# Permaculture Zone Planning Guide Zone planning is the foundational organizational system in permaculture design. Zones are areas classified by the frequency of human intervention required to maintain them, placed according to their distance from the center of human activity, typically the house. The concept was developed by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren as a way to efficiently allocate human energy across a landscape. By placing elements that need frequent attention closest to the center of activity and those needing less attention farther away, zone planning reduces wasted time, labor, and resources while increasing overall system productivity. ## Zone 0: The House Zone 0 is the house itself, or on a larger scale, a business or village. This is where you live, but it is also intimately connected to the garden and the greenhouse. It is where food from the garden is processed, where human waste is produced, and where the most time is spent. Everything else branches out from this zone. Elements in or immediately connected to Zone 0 include the kitchen, shade room, greenhouse, trellises with attached vines, composting toilet, and house pets. The house should ideally be positioned halfway up a slope for optimal drainage, solar exposure, and wind protection. Water storage should be located above the house on the slope to enable gravity-fed irrigation. Deciduous trees are placed on the sunny and eastern sides for summer shade and winter sun penetration, while evergreen trees protect from heat and wind on other sides year-round. ## Zone 1: The Intensive Garden Zone 1 is the area immediately surrounding the house, although it may extend down walkways or driveways if those places are visited frequently. This is where plants are grown very intensively in sheet-mulched garden beds. It may also include a small pond, frequently used outbuildings like a tool shed, dwarf fruit trees (or multi-grafted trees), and a low windbreak. Water in Zone 1 comes from a well or rainwater tank nearby. This zone requires daily attention for watering, harvesting, and general maintenance. Seedlings, herbs, salad greens, and frequently harvested vegetables dominate here. ## Zone 2: The Food Forest Zone 2 contains less frequently tended but still important elements: larger fruit trees, berry bushes, [[composting]] systems sit at the boundary between zones 1 and 2. Visits are needed several times per week rather than daily. This zone is often designed as a [[permaculture-designers-manual-animal-housing-detailed]], and large orchards. These areas need weekly or seasonal attention. Grazing animals, grain crops, and firewood plantations belong here. This zone connects the intensive homestead to the broader landscape. ## Zone 4: The Forage and Timber Zone Zone 4 is a semi-wild area managed for foraging, timber, and wildlife habitat. It requires only occasional management — perhaps a few visits per year for selective harvesting, trail maintenance, or [[permaculture-windbreak-design-species]] is placed in the wind sector, water-loving trees go in
flood-prone areas, and fire-resistant plantings buffer the fire sector.

## Creating a Zone and Sector Map

The practical process of zone planning begins with a detailed map of the
property showing water features, major existing plants, geological
formations, roads, paths, fences, structures, and power sources. Using the
zone system as a template, lines are drawn designating zones. They should
follow the contours and characteristics of the land rather than forming
perfect circles. Color coding aids visualization. Elements are arranged
beginning with Zone 1, with each element positioned relative to everything
else for maximum beneficial relationship. When starting out, it is important
to crowd everything in as close as possible. Cram plant life next to the
house and place [[comparison-passive-solar-vs-earth-sheltered]] heating, solar panel positioning, weather exposure, erosion
risk, and sunshine angle are all determined by slope. The top of a slope is
best for catching water despite erosion risk, the middle is ideal for the
house, and the bottom is best for gardens and animals though prone to
waterlogging. Integration with [[permaculture-design]] principle. Edges between zones should be maximized using
curved paths, branching waterways, and irregular plantings rather than
straight lines, following the [[holmgren-permaculture-twelve-design-principles-framework]] principle of "use edges and value the marginal."

## Urban and Small-Scale Zone Planning

Zone planning adapts readily to small urban and suburban properties.
On a quarter-acre lot, zones compress vertically and spatially.
Balconies and windowsills serve as Zone 0 extensions. A front yard
becomes Zone 1 with intensive raised beds. The backyard hosts Zone 2
elements like dwarf fruit trees and small chicken flocks. Community
gardens, nearby parks, and CSA farms function as distant Zone 3 and 4.
Even apartment dwellers can apply zone thinking: Zone 0 is the kitchen
with sprouting jars and fermentation projects, Zone 1 is the windowsill
herb garden, and Zone 3 is the farmers market. The key insight is that
zones are organized by access frequency, not by absolute distance — a
frequently visited community garden plot is functionally Zone 1 even if
it is physically distant from home.

## Common Mistakes in Zone Planning

The most frequent error is placing elements too far from where they are
used. Compost bins hidden in a far corner of the property become
neglected; they belong between the kitchen door (Zone 0) and the garden
(Zone 1). Herb gardens placed far from the kitchen stop being used for
daily cooking. Another common mistake is treating zones as concentric
circles on flat land — real sites have slopes, existing structures,
and access patterns that distort idealized zone shapes. A steep slope
may place Zone 3 pastures physically closer to the house than Zone 2
orchards if access paths make the orchard easier to reach. Ignoring
sector analysis when placing zones leads to planting wind-sensitive
crops in exposed locations or siting animal housing where winter
storms create extra work. Finally, failing to revisit and revise the
zone plan as the system matures means missing opportunities to
reorganize elements as trees grow, soil improves, and household
routines change.
