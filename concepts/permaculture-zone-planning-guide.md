# Permaculture Zone Planning Guide

Zone planning is the foundational organizational system in permaculture design.
Zones are areas classified by the frequency of human intervention required to
maintain them, placed according to their distance from the center of human
activity, typically the house. The concept was developed by Bill Mollison and
David Holmgren as a way to efficiently allocate human energy across a
landscape. By placing elements that need frequent attention closest to the
center of activity and those needing less attention farther away, zone
planning reduces wasted time, labor, and resources while increasing overall
system productivity.

## Zone 0: The House

Zone 0 is the house itself, or on a larger scale, a business or village. This
is where you live, but it is also intimately connected to the garden and the
greenhouse. It is where food from the garden is processed, where human waste
is produced, and where the most time is spent. Everything else branches out
from this zone. Elements in or immediately connected to Zone 0 include the
kitchen, shade room, greenhouse, trellises with attached vines, composting
toilet, and house pets. The house should ideally be positioned halfway up a
slope for optimal drainage, solar exposure, and wind protection. Water
storage should be located above the house on the slope to enable gravity-fed
irrigation. Deciduous trees are placed on the sunny and eastern sides for
summer shade and winter sun penetration, while evergreen trees protect from
heat and wind on other sides year-round.

## Zone 1: The Intensive Garden

Zone 1 is the area immediately surrounding the house, although it may extend
down walkways or driveways if those places are visited frequently. This is
where plants are grown very intensively in sheet-mulched garden beds. It may
also include a small pond, frequently used outbuildings like a tool shed,
dwarf fruit trees (or multi-grafted trees), and a low windbreak. Water in
Zone 1 comes from a well or rainwater tank and is fully controlled with pipes
and hoses. This area should be designed to produce most of the food a family
needs. Herbs are placed as close to the kitchen door as possible, typically
in an [[permaculture-poultry-orchard-integration]] and
[[permaculture-windbreak-design-species]] is placed in the wind sector, water-loving trees go in
flood-prone areas, and fire-resistant plantings buffer the fire sector.

## Creating a Zone and Sector Map

The practical process of zone planning begins with a detailed map of the
property showing water features, major existing plants, geological
formations, roads, paths, fences, structures, and power sources. Using the
zone system as a template, lines are drawn designating zones. They should
follow the contours and characteristics of the land rather than forming
perfect circles. Color coding aids visualization. Elements are arranged
beginning with Zone 1, with each element positioned relative to everything
else for maximum beneficial relationship. When starting out, it is important
to crowd everything in as close as possible. Cram plant life next to the
house and place animal housing as close as feasible. It is easier to thin
things out later than to wait for widely spaced elements to fill in. Roads
and trails should be integrated early, with the main road slightly sloped
uphill for drainage and built to follow land contours.

## Key Design Principles

Slope is the deciding factor for the placement of most elements. Water flow,
passive solar heating, solar panel positioning, weather exposure, erosion
risk, and sunshine angle are all determined by slope. The top of a slope is
best for catching water despite erosion risk, the middle is ideal for the
house, and the bottom is best for gardens and animals though prone to
waterlogging. Integration with [[permaculture-beekeeping-guide]],
and other elements should be considered from the outset, as the

## Practical Considerations

Successful implementation of Permaculture Zone Planning Guide requires attention to
several practical factors including environmental conditions,
resource availability, and timing. Careful monitoring and
adaptive management help optimize outcomes across varying
conditions. Integration with other system elements enhances
overall effectiveness and creates beneficial synergies that
improve resilience and productivity over time.
