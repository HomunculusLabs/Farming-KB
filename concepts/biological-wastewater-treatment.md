# Biological Wastewater Treatment

Biological wastewater treatment is a permaculture approach to processing sewage and contaminated water through living systems — plants, microorganisms, and ecological processes — rather than relying on chemical additives or energy-intensive mechanical filtration. Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* presents biological treatment as a productive, energy-yielding alternative to conventional sewage infrastructure, transforming waste into resources for food, fuel, and land rehabilitation.

## Key Principles

- **Waste is a resource**: Sewage contains nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) that can be captured by plants and cycled back into productive systems.
- **Biological processing over chemical**: Living organisms break down pathogens, organic compounds, and pollutants without toxic by-products.
- **Multi-functional design**: Treatment systems can simultaneously produce energy (methane, biomass), grow food or fodder, and restore degraded land.
- **Source separation**: Separating sewage into solids and liquids at the domestic level greatly simplifies treatment and allows each stream to be handled by its most appropriate biological pathway.

## Pathogen-Removal Species

Certain aquatic and semi-aquatic plant species are particularly effective at removing pathogens from wastewater. These species can be planted in flow-through gravel beds, lagoons, or constructed wetlands:

- **Alisma plantago-aquatica** (water plantain) — a common wetland species that filters and purifies water while hosting beneficial microbial communities on its root systems.
- **Mentha aquatica** (water mint) — provides vigorous root-zone filtration; aromatic oils may contribute additional antimicrobial properties.
- **Juncus effusus** (soft rush) — one of the most important treatment species; its extensive root mass and aerenchyma tissue (large pith cells with air channels) support oxygen transport to root-zone microbes that degrade contaminants.
- **Schoenoplectus lacustris** (lake clubrush) — similar aerenchyma structure to Juncus; effective in both pathogen removal and breakdown of complex organic compounds.
- **Spartina spp.** (cordgrasses) — salt-marsh species tolerant of brackish or saline waste streams; useful in coastal treatment systems.
- **Iris pseudacorus** (yellow flag iris) — deep-rooted, ornamental, and effective at nutrient uptake; commonly used in ornamental treatment wetlands.

## Treatment of Specific Contaminants

### Chlorinated Hydrocarbons

Chlorinated hydrocarbons — industrial solvents, pesticides, and persistent organic pollutants — are among the most difficult waste compounds to degrade. Rush-type plants with large **aerenchyma** cells (air-filled tissue that transports atmospheric oxygen down to the root zone) enable aerobic microbial communities to break these compounds down in the root rhizosphere. **Juncus effusus** and **Schoenoplectus spp.** are the primary species used for this purpose.

### Cyanide Compounds, Thiocyanates, and Phenols

These toxic industrial contaminants can be effectively treated in biological flow-through systems using **Juncus** species. Treatment times are relatively short — as little as **7+ hours** of contact time in planted gravel beds or lagoons can achieve significant reductions. This makes biological treatment competitive with or superior to many chemical neutralization approaches.

### Nitrogen Compounds

A critical finding is that **discharge of sewage to subsoils does not remove nitrogen compounds**. Nitrate and ammonium leach through soil profiles and contaminate groundwater. Surface-based biological treatment systems (planted beds, lagoons, constructed wetlands) are required to capture and assimilate nitrogen into plant biomass.

## System Types

### Flow-Through Gravel Beds

A constructed treatment bed consisting of a gravel substrate planted with purifying species. Wastewater flows horizontally or vertically through the gravel, where physical filtration combines with biological degradation in the root zone. These systems are compact, effective, and can be integrated into landscape design.

### Lagoons and Ditches

Open water bodies or earthen channels planted with treatment species. Lagoons provide longer retention times and larger treatment volumes. They can be multi-functional:

- **Sewage lagoons for livestock rearing**: The Werribee farm in Australia successfully reared beef cattle on sewage lagoons for over 35 years. A similar system operated in Hagerstown, Maryland. Nutrient-rich water supports pasture and forage growth.
- **Methane production**: Anaerobic zones within lagoon systems produce methane (biogas), which can be captured and used for heating or electricity generation.
- **Energy coppice**: Sewage-irrigated **coppiced poplar plots** can supply up to 60% of a town's energy use through fast-growing woody biomass harvested on short rotation cycles.

### Comfrey Beds

Comfrey (*Symphytum spp.*) beds are specifically used for absorbing faecal products from animal husbandry. Comfrey's deep taproots and rapid biomass accumulation make it an excellent nutrient sponge. The harvested comfrey can be composted, used as mulch, or fed to livestock ( wilted to break down pyrrolizidine alkaloids).

## Salinity Management

Water passing through towns and urban areas picks up salts, typically increasing by **300–400 ppm** in total dissolved solids. This salinity accumulation degrades downstream agricultural land and freshwater ecosystems. Permaculture approaches include:

- **Algae cultivation** in treatment lagoons to absorb and sequester salts.
- **Salt-tolerant plant production** (e.g., Spartina spp.) in discharge zones.
- **Evaporative concentration** in dedicated cells, with harvested salt used as a resource.

## Domestic Source Separation

Mollison advocates separating sewage into **solids and liquids at the domestic level** before any treatment. This separation:

- Reduces the volume and complexity of each waste stream.
- Allows solids to be composted or directed to biogas digesters.
- Enables liquids to be treated more efficiently in planted systems.
- Recovers valuable nutrients (especially urea/urine) for direct agricultural use.

## Urine as a Resource

Human urine is rich in nitrogen (primarily as urea) and can be a valuable agricultural input:

- **Foliar urea application**: A 2% urea solution sprayed on rice foliage increased grain protein content to 40%, demonstrating direct nitrogen bioavailability.
- **Fungal disease control**: Urine diluted to a 5% solution effectively controls moulds on cucurbits (cucumbers, squash, melons), providing both nutrient feeding and pathogen suppression.
