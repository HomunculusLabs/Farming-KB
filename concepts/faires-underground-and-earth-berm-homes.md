# Underground and Earth Berm Homes

Underground houses have been built for thousands of years in every climate. While dirt isn't a great insulator, the sheer quantity of earth acts as thermal mass, keeping homes warm in winter and cool in summer.

## Earth Berm Homes

Most modern North American underground homes are actually earth berm homes — covered in a man-made embankment. The front of the house is shaded with vines, and the roof can be part of the berm with plants growing on it.

In the desert, houses are set far into soil or rock faces. In temperate climates, only the back half may be earth-sheltered.

## Construction Method

The simplest and most fool-proof method:
1. Build with **steel framing or reinforced concrete** (in many places, the only legal method)
2. Use **circular or dome shape** for strength
3. Small is better — the enormous soil weight can crush poorly designed structures
4. Use pre-designed plans — worth the investment

## Waterproofing (Critical)

The home must be very waterproof due to soil moisture:
- Polyethylene sheeting (pond liner grade)
- Clay layers
- Commercial cement waterproofing products

Add insulation on top of the waterproof membrane (outside the house) to protect from freezing/damage:
- Polystyrene sheets
- Spray foam

Some people don't insulate inner walls, but it's wise where the frost line extends deep.

## Sustainability Trade-off

While earth is environmentally friendly, underground homes require significant unsustainable materials for waterproofing and structural safety. There are easier, cheaper, more sustainable options for amateur builders — particularly [[earthbag-construction]].

## Living Roofs

A living roof provides earth-sheltering benefits without full underground construction:

1. Roof must be very strong and well-supported
2. Moisture barrier between roof and sod
3. Beam along roof edge keeps sod from sliding
4. Sheet metal hangs off eaves and rolls under (simplifies gutter cleaning)
5. Plant selection depends on climate:
   - Wet areas: regular grass
   - Dry areas: local groundcover species
   - Some climates: bulbs and herbs work
6. Rainwater-fed watering system keeps plants alive during dry spells

If a full sod roof is too heavy, grow ivy — it will spread and cover the entire house.

## Cellars and Cold Storage

A cellar is an underground room maintaining 32-40°F (0-4°C) — optimal for food storage:

- Ensure water table is below floor year-round (or add drainage)
- Don't use pressure-treated wood for interior framing or masonry contact
- Use stone or reinforced concrete for support
- Leave floor as dirt (cement cracks and absorbs moisture; for food storage, air moisture is beneficial)
- Can be an extension of the basement or a separate structure

# Building Materials and Insulation

Natural and sustainable building materials range from ancient methods like cob and stone to modern innovations like earthbags and strawbale. Choosing the right material depends on climate, available resources, and building skill. As Dr. Stephen Kellert noted, humans need continuous and spontaneous affiliations with the biological world, and meaningful access to natural settings is as vital to the urban dweller as to any other.

## Structural Materials

**Bamboo:** Swift-growing grass. Used for framing, especially combined with adobe or cob walls. Not rot-resistant — must be kept off the ground with proper foundation. Traditional in warm/tropical climates. In North America it is more common to see bamboo used as framing combined with earthen wall systems.

**Wood:** Main structural material in North America. Use old growth from your own trees, not young trees (young trees are valuable for forest repopulation). If you decide to use your own trees, prioritize established old growth that can be harvested sustainably.

**Adobe/Cob:** Dirt mixed with clay and other materials, packed hard. Adobe = bricks stacked and plastered. Cob = wood framework filled with sculpted mud. Adobe has traditionally been used in hot desert climates. Cob is very similar but has a different mixture and has been used all over the world.

**Dirt:** Underground homes, earth berms, or earthbag construction. Modern dirt homes are sophisticated. Earthbags are filled with dirt and stacked with rebar framework. Underground and earth berm houses are very popular, but an even easier option is the earthbag — an extremely low-cost method that creates thermal mass and is incredibly strong.

**Canvas:** Yurts and tipis — cool in summer, warm in winter if built correctly. Commercial yurt kits available with various insulation options. Yurts are more often used as year-round houses with commercially made kits that provide all kinds of options and insulating capacities.

**Stone:** Available in most places. Well-insulated and extremely long-lasting. Best in storm-prone areas; not ideal in earthquake zones. Time-consuming to build, but the resulting structure is among the most durable available.

**Strawbale:** Tightly twined bales held with rebar framework. Thick bales provide excellent insulation despite straw's modest R-value. A modern popular alternative building method where bales must be tightly twined and are held together with a rebar framework.

**Cordwood:** Logs sliced into disks/circles, stacked with cement. Useful where wood is scarce. Better insulation than many modern methods. An old building method made popular again by natural homebuilders.

## Insulation Materials (R-values per inch)

| Material | R-value per inch | Notes |

|----------|-----------------|-------|

| Dirt | R-0.25 | Terrible insulator but excellent thermal mass; self-regulates over 12-hour cycles |

| Air | R-1.0 | Must be sealed (double-paned windows) |

| Sawdust | R-2.0 | Used historically in icehouses; needs vapor barrier and plastic bags |

| Balsa | R-2.0-3.0 | Quick-growing; seedpod cotton also usable |

| Paper/Cellulose | R-2.0-3.0 | Soak in 1 part borax : 10 parts water for fire resistance |

| Straw | R-2.4 | Used for centuries; straw bales or structural insulated panels |

| Cork | R-3.0 | Harvested from bark; tree stays alive and regrows |

| Cotton | R-3.4 | Recycled cotton + plastic fiber batts with flame retardant |

| Hemp | R-3.5 | Versatile, quick-growing, resists decay and rot |

| Plant fiber waste | R-3.5 | Any plant fibers matted into batts |

## Dirt as Thermal Mass vs. Insulator

Dirt is a terrible insulator (R-0.25/inch) but an exceptional thermal building material. It's terrible at transferring heat AND cold, self-regulating over 12-hour cycles (the sun's cycle). During the day it radiates nighttime coolness; at night it radiates daytime warmth.

This is why earth-sheltered and underground homes maintain stable temperatures year-round. While dirt isn't necessarily a great insulator, the sheer quantity of dirt used for an underground house acts as a thermal mass, keeping the house warm in the winter and cool in the summer.

## Passive Solar and Thermal Mass

Passive solar design relies on good insulation and thermal mass working together. Thermal mass is a large area of heat-absorbing material, usually stone or brick, which absorbs solar energy during the day and releases that heat at night. Wood, carpet, and furniture do not do this efficiently.
