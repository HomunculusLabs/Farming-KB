# Holzer Tamera Water Landscape Project

The Tamera Peace Research Centre in southern Portugal is one of Sepp Holzer's
most significant international projects. It demonstrates how a degraded,
drought-stricken landscape can be transformed into a water-rich, productive
paradise through the creation of a comprehensive water landscape system.

## The Site Before

Tamera is located in the Alentejo region of Portugal, an area characterised by
hot, dry summers and winter rainfall. The land was degraded, with little
vegetation, eroded soils, and no reliable water retention. Like much of
southern Portugal, the landscape had suffered from centuries of deforestation,
overgrazing, and poor water management.

## The Water Landscape Design

Holzer designed a system of interconnected water retention spaces following
contour lines across the property. Key elements include:

- Multiple ponds and lakes at different elevations, connected by natural
  overflow channels so water flows from higher to lower bodies.
- Terraces and banks that slow water movement and prevent erosion.
- Deep and shallow zones in each water body, creating the
  [[holzer-pond-shape-deep-shallow-zones]] for temperature
  regulation.
- Alignment of the longest pond axes with prevailing winds for natural
  aeration.
- Proper drainage using the [[holzer-pivoting-monk-pond-drainage]]
  system to manage overflow.

## Results

The transformation was dramatic. Within a short time, the water landscape:

- Raised the water table across the entire property. Springs that had been dry
  for decades began flowing again.
- Created a microclimate with increased humidity, reducing summer temperatures
  and enabling a wider range of plants to thrive.
- Stopped erosion and began rebuilding topsoil through organic deposition.
- Attracted diverse wildlife: amphibians, birds, insects, and fish.
- Enabled productive [[sepp-holzer-water-gardens-and-aquatic-plants]]
  with edible aquatic plants.
- Provided irrigation security for food crops without pumps or external water
  sources.

## The Rubbish Hugelkultur Workshop

Tamera also became the site where Holzer developed and tested the
[[holzer-urban-gardening-rubbish-hugelkultur]] technique.
During workshops with participants from 14 countries -- including occupied
Palestine, Colombia, Ecuador, Brazil, Kenya, and Latvia -- Holzer
demonstrated that food could be grown on degraded land using discarded
materials: old clothes, cardboard, paper, and kitchen waste as the core of
hugelkultur beds. Within two months, tomatoes, pumpkins, melons, cabbages,
radishes, and lettuces were growing in abundance.

## Significance

Tamera proves Holzer's central thesis: any region on earth can produce
abundant and healthy food when water is retained on the land. The project
became a magnet for permaculture practitioners worldwide and inspired similar
water landscape projects across Mediterranean climates.

## Design Principles and Hydrology

The water landscape at Tamera follows several core principles drawn from Holzer's decades of experience at the [[krameterhof-holzer-model-farm]] in Austria. The first principle is that water must be held on the land as high as possible and for as long as possible. Rather than allowing rainfall to run off into drainage channels and ultimately to the sea, the system captures water at multiple elevations, creating a cascade of retention spaces that slow, spread, and sink water into the soil profile. This approach mirrors natural hydrological patterns where beaver dams and fallen trees create step-pool sequences across watersheds.
The second principle is the creation of edge habitat. By designing irregular shorelines rather than simple geometric shapes, each pond maximizes the interface between water and land. These edge zones — where aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems meet — are the most biologically productive areas in any landscape. They provide nesting habitat for amphibians and birds, foraging grounds for beneficial insects, and nursery areas for juvenile fish. The [[holzer-pond-shape-deep-shallow-zones]] within each water body further increase ecological diversity by creating distinct thermal and oxygen gradients that support different communities of organisms.
The third principle is that every element serves multiple functions. The ponds provide irrigation water, but they also serve as firebreaks, reflective surfaces that increase light for adjacent crops, thermal regulators that moderate temperature extremes, and habitat for [[sepp-holzer-water-gardens-and-aquatic-plants]] such as water chestnuts, watercress, and Chinese water spinach. The overflow channels between ponds double as swales that infiltrate water into the soil along their entire length, and the earth excavated from ponds is used to build terraces and [[holzer-raised-bed-design-principles]].

## Implementation Timeline and Community Involvement

The Tamera water landscape was constructed between 2007 and 2009 with Sepp Holzer's direct guidance. The community organised several intensive workshop periods where Holzer supervised the earthworks, teaching participants the practical skills of water landscape construction. Work was carried out using a combination of excavators for the heavy earthmoving and manual labour for fine shaping and planting. The total system includes a large lake of approximately 1.2 hectares with a capacity of roughly 20,000 cubic metres, several smaller ponds at higher elevations, and an extensive network of swales and terraces covering approximately 30 hectares of the 330-hectare property.
Participants in the workshops came from conflict zones and developing regions, and Holzer deliberately designed the techniques to be replicable with minimal resources. The [[holzer-urban-gardening-rubbish-hugelkultur]] method demonstrated at Tamera was specifically developed to show that food production does not depend on expensive imported inputs or sophisticated technology. This accessibility principle is central to Holzer's teaching philosophy and aligns with the [[jadam-ultra-low-cost-agriculture]] movement developed by Youngsang Cho in Korea, which similarly emphasises self-sufficiency and locally sourced inputs.

## Ecological Outcomes and Biodiversity

The ecological recovery at Tamera has been documented through ongoing monitoring of flora and fauna. Within three years of completing the water landscape, over 80 species of birds were observed on the property, compared to fewer than 20 before the intervention. Amphibian populations, particularly frogs and newts, established themselves rapidly in the pond system, serving as natural bioindicators of ecosystem health. The increase in habitat diversity — from a relatively homogeneous degraded pasture to a mosaic of open water, marsh, wetland, terrace, and food forest — created niches for a wide range of species.
The water bodies also support fish populations that provide both ecological functions (nutrient cycling, mosquito control) and food for the community. Holzer's approach to [[holzer-pond-construction-and-aquaculture]] avoids artificial feeding and aeration, relying instead on natural productivity driven by the diverse aquatic plant communities and the influx of organic matter from the surrounding landscape. This self-regulating aquaculture model contrasts sharply with conventional fish farming, which typically requires imported feed, chemical treatments, and energy-intensive water management.

## Influence on Global Permaculture Practice

Tamera has become one of the most influential demonstrations of Holzer's methods outside Austria. The project has directly inspired water landscape installations in at least 15 countries, including projects in [[holzer-extremadura-water-paradise-project]], Morocco, Kenya, and Colombia. Several organisations have adopted Holzer's water retention approach as a core strategy for climate adaptation, recognising that restoring the natural water cycle is essential for building resilience against drought and desertification.
The Tamera model demonstrates that large-scale earthworks for water retention can be implemented rapidly — the primary infrastructure was completed within two years — and that the ecological benefits begin to accrue almost immediately. This rapid return on investment is a key argument for water landscape approaches in regions facing acute water scarcity. Unlike gradual reforestation or soil-building programmes, which may take decades to show measurable effects, a well-designed water landscape begins raising the water table and modifying the microclimate within the first season after construction.

## Challenges and Lessons Learned

The Tamera project has not been without difficulties. The initial earthworks required significant investment in heavy machinery and diesel fuel, raising questions about the scalability of the approach for communities with limited financial resources. Holzer has addressed this by emphasising that smaller-scale water retention can be achieved with hand tools and animal labour, citing examples from his own work at the Krameterhof where even small ponds and swales create meaningful hydrological benefits.
Water quality management has been an ongoing challenge. The nutrient-rich runoff from the surrounding agricultural landscape initially caused algal blooms in the shallower ponds, particularly during the warm summer months. The Tamera community addressed this by increasing the planting of aquatic vegetation in and around the ponds, establishing buffer strips of deep-rooted native grasses along the inflow channels, and introducing duck populations that graze on algae while adding fertility through their manure. These biological solutions, consistent with Holzer's philosophy of working with natural processes rather than against them, have progressively improved water quality and reduced maintenance requirements.
Legal and regulatory challenges have also been significant. Portugal's water management regulations, like those of many Mediterranean countries, were not designed to accommodate the kind of distributed, small-scale water retention that Holzer advocates. The Tamera team has had to navigate complex permitting requirements and engage with regulatory authorities to demonstrate that their water landscape does not deplete downstream water supplies but rather increases overall water availability by reducing runoff and increasing groundwater recharge.

## Food Production and Self-Sufficiency Outcomes

The water landscape has enabled Tamera to significantly increase its food production capacity. The community now produces a substantial portion of its own food on land that was previously considered agriculturally useless. Fruit trees — including figs, pomegranates, olives, citrus, and stone fruits — have been planted throughout the terraced areas surrounding the water bodies, benefiting from the improved moisture availability and moderated microclimate. The [[sepp-holzer-water-gardens-and-aquatic-plants]] in the ponds yield crops of water chestnuts, lotus roots, and water spinach that provide essential nutrients during the hot summer months when terrestrial vegetable production is most challenging.
Vegetable gardens established on the terraces above the water bodies have demonstrated that intensive organic food production is possible without irrigation in a region that receives less than 500 millimetres of annual rainfall. The key to this success is the raised water table created by the pond system, which provides subirrigation to the terraced gardens through capillary action in the soil. This passive irrigation system means that even during extended drought periods, the soil in the terraces remains sufficiently moist to support crop growth without supplemental watering. The combination of the water landscape with the [[holzer-urban-gardening-rubbish-hugelkultur]] beds has created a food production system that requires minimal external inputs and is resilient to the climate variability that characterises the Mediterranean region.

## Replication Guidelines for Other Sites
Holzer and the Tamera team have developed guidelines for replicating the water landscape approach in other degraded landscapes. The first step is always a thorough [[holzer-assessing-the-land]] — walking the property during and after rainfall to observe natural water flow patterns, identifying existing springs and seeps, and mapping the contour lines that will determine the placement of retention spaces. This assessment period should cover at least one full annual cycle to account for seasonal variations in rainfall and water flow.
The second step is to identify the highest practical elevation for the first (uppermost) water body. This pond serves as the "crown" of the system, capturing water before it can gain momentum on the slope and using overflow channels to distribute it to lower retention spaces. The size and depth of the upper pond should be determined by the catchment area above it and the local rainfall patterns. Holzer recommends starting smaller than seems necessary — it is easier to enlarge a pond than to reduce one that is too large.
Subsequent ponds and swales are then designed at intervals down the slope, with each one receiving overflow from the pond above and discharging to the pond below. The spacing between retention spaces depends on the slope gradient, soil type, and rainfall intensity. On steep slopes with low-permeability soils, retention spaces should be closely spaced; on gentle slopes with well-drained soils, they can be more widely spaced. The total water retention capacity of the system should ideally be sufficient to capture and infiltrate the runoff from a 1-in-50-year rainfall event, though even smaller systems that capture only a fraction of this capacity produce significant ecological benefits.

## Climate Adaptation Significance
The Tamera water landscape has taken on renewed significance in the context of climate change adaptation. The Mediterranean region is projected to experience increasing temperatures, decreasing rainfall, and more intense drought events over the coming decades. Climate models suggest that southern Portugal could experience a 20-40% reduction in summer rainfall by 2050, with corresponding increases in wildfire risk and desertification pressure. The water retention approach demonstrated at Tamera provides a practical, proven strategy for building landscape-level resilience to these changes.
The key insight from Tamera is that water scarcity in Mediterranean climates is not primarily a rainfall problem but a water management problem. Even in drought years, significant quantities of water fall on the landscape during winter storms; the problem is that this water runs off immediately rather than being captured and stored in the soil and in surface water bodies. By creating the conditions for water to infiltrate and be retained on the land, the water landscape approach effectively extends the period during which rainfall is available for biological use from a few hours (for runoff) to several months (for soil-stored water) or indefinitely (for groundwater recharge).
This climate adaptation potential has been recognised by organisations working in some of the world's most water-stressed regions. Projects inspired by the Tamera model have been established in the Sahel zone of Africa, in the arid regions of the Middle East, and in the drought-prone interior of Australia. In each case, the core principle is the same: hold water on the land as high as possible, for as long as possible, using the simplest and most natural means available. The [[holzer-extremadura-water-paradise-project]] in Spain represents the most direct replication of the Tamera approach in a similar Mediterranean climate, demonstrating that the principles are transferable across a range of specific site conditions.
