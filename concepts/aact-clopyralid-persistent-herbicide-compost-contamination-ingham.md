# AACT Clopyralid and Persistent Herbicide Contamination in Compost

Persistent herbicide residues in compost represent one of the most serious threats
to [[aact-brewing-ingredients-ingham]]. Chemicals such as **clopyralid**, **aminopyralid**, and
**picloram** survive the composting process entirely intact and contaminate any
aact brewed from that material. Dr. Elaine Ingham has been a vocal advocate
for awareness of this problem, which has caused widespread crop damage.

## How Persistent Herbicides Enter Compost

These chemicals enter the compost stream through common feedstock pathways:

- **Turf grass clippings** — Lawns treated with clopyralid (Confront, Lontrel,
  Stinger, Curtail) produce clippings carrying active residues. Homeowners and
  landscape companies deliver these to municipal composting facilities.
- **Hay and pasture forage** — Aminopyralid (Milestone) and picloram (Tordon,
  Grazon) are applied to pastures. Livestock pass the chemical through digestion
  and manure retains biologically active herbicide.
- **Manure** — Livestock do not metabolize these compounds, so contaminated
  manure is a direct vector. When composted and spread, residual herbicide
  damages sensitive broadleaf crops.
- **Chlordane and legacy pesticides** — Banned for decades but still persist in
  soils; can enter compost via plant material.

## Why Composting Does Not Break Down Clopyralid

**Standard composting temperatures do not degrade clopyralid or its chemical
relatives.** Thermophilic composting (131–170°F / 55–77°C) kills pathogens and
weed seeds but is inadequate for breaking the [[molecular-bonds-and-chemistry-for-gardeners]] in clopyralid,
aminopyralid, and picloram. These compounds are engineered for stability under
biological and thermal conditions in agricultural environments.

Clopyralid's soil half-life ranges from 30 to 300 days, but in low-biology
compost it persists far longer. The pyridine ring structure resists microbial
attack from standard compost organisms. Effective degradation requires specific
[[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] under controlled conditions.

## The Danger for AACT Brewers

When contaminated compost is used as the base in [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]], the
herbicide is extracted into the tea along with beneficial biology, causing:

- Curling, cupping, and distortion of new leaves on broadleaf plants
- Stunted growth and yield reduction; in severe cases, plant death
- particularly affects legumes, tomatoes, potatoes, sunflowers, and grapes
- Long-term soil contamination persisting for months to years

Because AACT is applied as foliar spray or soil drench, contamination affects
plants not growing in contaminated soil.

## Elaine Ingham's Position on Feedstock Sourcing

Dr. Ingham recommends a cautious, source-aware approach to compost feedstocks:

- **Avoid grass clippings from treated lawns entirely.** It is nearly impossible
  to verify whether a lawn has been treated with persistent herbicides.
- **Use hay, not straw — but verify the source.** Hay contains more diverse
  biology and nutrients than straw. However, hay from fields treated with
  aminopyralid or picloram is dangerous. Know your supplier and ask about
  herbicide applications specifically.
- **Be cautious with manure.** Ask livestock owners about deworming products
  and pasture treatments. Avoid manure from animals on recently treated pasture.

## Compost Temperature Monitoring as a Diagnostic

If [[query-how-to-build-a-compost-pile-that-works]] fails to reach expected thermophilic temperatures, or
temperatures drop abnormally and stay low, this may indicate inhibitory chemical
residues — including herbicides or pesticides — suppressing microbial activity.
A healthy pile with adequate feedstock diversity and moisture should heat
reliably. Persistent failure to do so warrants investigation.
