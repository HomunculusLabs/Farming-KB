# Oyster Mushroom Bagging Methods: Worldwide Comparison

## Overview

Bagging is the process of filling prepared substrate into containers before heat
treatment and inoculation. The method varies significantly by country, reflecting
local materials, climate, cost constraints, and tradition. This concept summarises
bagging approaches from six countries documented in the MushWorld handbook,
highlighting practical differences that affect colonisation speed, contamination
risk, and overall yield.

## General Principles

The prepared substrate mixture is usually filled into bags before heat-treatment,
though some growers pasteurise or sterilise in bulk and fill bags afterward. The
first method is recommended to minimise contamination risk.

After filling and compressing, a 2–3 cm diameter hole in the centre allows for
inoculation down at the bottom of the bag. This permits deeper inoculation and
better oxygen supply, encouraging faster colonisation. Growers who don't compact
the bags don't always need to make an inoculation hole.

Proper ventilated sealing is critical. Mycelia need oxygen to breathe, so
breathable plugs or stoppers with cotton balls or breathable micro-filters
provide free air exchange while filtering out contaminants.

## Country-Specific Methods

### Bangladesh

About 500 g of mango sawdust is mixed with wheat bran in a ratio of 4:1, then
watered and sterilised before being put into polypropylene bags measuring
25 × 20 cm. The small bag size suits the sawdust-based substrate and limited
scale of operations typical in Bangladeshi smallholder cultivation.

**Key features**: Mango sawdust substrate, small polypropylene bags, sterilised
rather than pasteurised, wheat bran supplementation at 20% of sawdust volume.

### Nepal

Before filling, polyethylene bags measuring 14 × 24 inches or 18 × 26 inches
are punched every two inches in rows all over the surface with a punching
machine. The conditioned straw is filled about 4 inches thick at a time,
pressed slightly, then another layer added. This is called the **"layer method."**

**Key features**: Pre-punched bags for aeration, layer-by-layer straw filling,
two available bag sizes for different scales of operation, polyethylene (not
polypropylene) suited to lower-temperature pasteurisation.

### Vietnam

Plastic bags measuring 20 × 30 cm or 18 × 25 cm serve as mushroom beds. Growers
open the bags and put a handful of straw inside, pressing it down tightly to make
a 3–5 cm thick layer at the bottom. Additional layers are added, with a final
layer of straw placed so its top sits 5–7 cm below the mouth. A clean piece of
cotton is placed in the mouth, the bag sealed, and bags tied together with nylon
rope (3–5 bags per rope) and hung.

**Key features**: Hanging bag system, cotton plug ventilation, multiple bags
strung on rope for space efficiency, compact layering method, no central
inoculation hole needed due to the hanging configuration.

### India

A wooden mould (frame) of 45 × 30 × 15 cm size is used — no top or bottom, with
a separate wooden cover of 44 × 29 cm. The frame is placed on a smooth floor
with jute ropes underneath (two vertical, one horizontal). A plastic sheet
previously sterilised by dipping in boiled water lines the frame. About 5 cm of
boiled straw is filled in and compressed with the wooden lid, then 4–6 layers
are added successively.

**Key features**: Wooden frame mould creates uniform blocks, jute rope binding
for structural integrity, pre-sterilised plastic liner, high layer count
(4–6 layers), creates free-standing blocks rather than hanging bags.

### South Africa

Two distinct approaches are used. Large-scale growers use clear or black
polyethylene ducting cut into 2-metre columns (50 cm diameter) with metal
support, holding approximately 50 kg of substrate. Small-scale growers use
small plastic bags containing about 20 kg of substrate with holes punched for
aeration and mushroom maturation.

**Key features**: Wide size range (20 kg bags to 50 kg columns), polyethylene
ducting for large operations, metal support structures, punched holes for
aeration rather than filtered plugs, dual-track approach for different scales.

### Hungary (From Related Documentation)

After heat treatment, the substrate is watered with a benomyl solution and
spawned at 4–5% of wet weight. While specific bagging details differ, the
Hungarian approach emphasises post-treatment fungicidal wash and relatively
high spawning rate compared to other countries.

**Key features**: Chemical treatment after pasteurisation (benomyl wash), high
spawning rate at 4–5% of wet weight, fungicidal protection against
contamination.

## Practical Considerations

1. **Bag material choice**: Polypropylene (PP) withstands autoclave
   sterilisation at 121°C; polyethylene (PE) is limited to pasteurisation
   temperatures. Choosing the wrong material leads to bag failure.
2. **Compression matters**: Proper compaction ensures good mycelial contact
   with substrate particles but must be balanced against aeration needs.
3. **Hanging vs. shelving**: Vietnam's rope-hanging method saves floor space
   but requires structural support; India's block method needs shelving.
4. **Scale-appropriate design**: The contrast between South Africa's large
   columns and Bangladesh's small bags shows how infrastructure investment
   shapes bagging choices.
