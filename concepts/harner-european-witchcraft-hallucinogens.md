# European Witchcraft and Solanaceous Hallucinogens

## The Overlooked Role of Hallucinogenic Plants

A prevalent attitude among historians is that late medieval and Renaissance witchcraft was essentially a fiction created by the Church. Yet once the use and effects of natural hallucinogens are understood, the major features of past beliefs and practices suddenly seem quite logical and consistent.

## The Solanaceous Hallucinogens

The single most important group of plants used by mankind to contact the supernatural belongs to the **order Solanaceae** (the potato family). Hallucinogenic members include:

- **Datura** (Jimson weed, devil's apple, thorn apple, mad apple, devil's weed, Gabriel's trumpet, angel's trumpet) -- used widely in shamanism, witchcraft, and vision quest in Europe, Asia, Africa, and among American Indian tribes
- **Mandrake** (*Mandragora*)
- **Henbane** (*Hyoscyamus*)
- **Deadly nightshade / Belladonna** (*Atropa belladonna*)

## Pharmacology

Each of these plants contains varying quantities of:
- **Atropine**
- **Hyoscyamine**
- **Scopolamine**

All are tropane alkaloids with hallucinogenic effects. They can be **extremely dangerous** in mental and physical effects, and their toxicity can result in death.

A critical pharmacological property: **atropine is absorbable even by the intact skin**. This has long been known in both Old and New Worlds and is of considerable significance for the study of shamanism and witchcraft.

## The Flying Ointment and the Witches' Flight

The witch on a broomstick represents a very serious and central aspect of European witchcraft:

1. European witches rubbed their bodies with a **hallucinogenic ointment** containing *Atropa belladonna*, *Mandragora*, and henbane
2. The atropine content was **absorbable through the skin**
3. The witch then took a "trip": the broomstick represents the **imagined aerial journey** to a rendezvous with spirits or demons, called a **Sabbat**

The broomstick or staff served as an **applicator** for the atropine-containing plant to the sensitive vaginal membranes, as well as providing the suggestion of riding.

## Historical Evidence

### Somerset Witches (1664)
Elizabeth Style confessed: "Before they are carried to their meetings, they anoint their Foreheads and Hand-wrists with an Oyl the Spirit brings them (which smells raw) and then they are carried in a very short time."

### Lady Alice Kyteler (1324)
Authorities found "a Pipe of oylment, wherewith she greased a staffe, upon the which she ambled and galloped through thick and thin."

### Bergamo (c. 1470-71)
"The vulgar believe, and the witches confess, that on certain days or nights they anoint a staff and ride on it to the appointed place or anoint themselves under the arms and in other hairy places."

### Nicole Ganette (Remy, 1596)
She put one foot into a basket after smearing it with ointment. Francis Fellet used the backward-bent twigs of a broom.

### Johannes Nider (1692)
A priest witnessed a woman step into a bowl, rub ointment on herself, and fall asleep -- then cry out, strike her hands, and injure herself falling, having dreamed so vividly of flying with Diana.

## Lewin's Account

The famous pharmacologist Lewin (1964 [orig. 1924]) writes:

> "Magic ointments or witches' philtres procured for some reason and applied with or without intention produced effects which the subjects themselves believed in, even stating that they had intercourse with evil spirits, had been on the Brocken and danced at the Sabbat with their lovers, or caused damage to others by witchcraft."

## The Sabbat as Drug-Induced Experience

The Sabbat -- the supposed gathering of witches with demons featuring orgies, feasting, and dancing -- becomes comprehensible as a drug-induced hallucinatory experience. The hallucinations evoked by solanaceous plants were so powerfully transmitted from the subconscious mind to consciousness that "mentally uncultivated persons believed them to be reality."

The Inquisition, at the cost of torture and execution of perhaps hundreds of thousands of believed and real witches, has supplied the bulk of our data on these practices, which were widespread throughout Europe and apparently known at least as early as Roman times.

## Connection to Shamanism

The use of solanaceous hallucinogens in European witchcraft represents a Western parallel to the use of *Banisteriopsis* and *Amanita muscaria* in shamanistic societies. The "witches' flight" is functionally equivalent to the **soul flight** of the shaman -- a journey to the supernatural world made possible by hallucinogenic substances.
