The two basic strategies of water conservation in runoff areas are the diversion of surface water to impoundments (dams, tanks) for later use, and the storage of water in soils. Both result in recharge of groundwater. Earthworks have specifically appropriate and inappropriate uses depending on climate, soil, and landscape context.

Small dams and earth tanks serve two primary uses. The minor use is providing watering points for rangelands, wildlife, and domestic stock, where modest, widely dispersed, and static systems suffice. The major use is containing surplus runoff water for domestic use or irrigation during dry periods.

These storages require careful design for safety, water harvesting, total landscape layout, outlet systems, draw-down, and placement relative to usage areas, preferably providing gravity flow.

A separate category of water storages includes ponds or wet terraces created specifically for aquatic polycultural systems of vegetation and mixed animal species, connecting to [[faires-aquaculture-pond-systems]].

BARRIER DAMS are constructed across flowing or intermittent stream beds. They require ample spillways, careful construction, and fish ladders on biologically important streams. They work best as part of a keyline series.

TURKEY'S NEST DAMS are above-grade tanks where water is pumped in, often by windmill or solar pump. Common in flatlands as stock water tanks or for low-head irrigation.

CHECK DAMS include barrier dams not intended to create water storages but to create flat areas where silt loads deposit, forming absorption beds during flood conditions. GABION DAMS use permeable barriers of rock-filled wire mesh baskets in drylands to create silt fields and water-spreading across eroding valleys.

## Dam Building Principles

Although dams can be built on any site given enough resources, storage dams should be located with respect to earth type (core samples for assessing clay fractions), grade behind wall (lower slopes give greatest capacity), downstream safety of structures, height above use points (gravity flow is desirable), and available catchment or diversion.

Tamped earth with clay fractions better than 50% is waterproof up to heights of 3.6 meters. Slopes to crest should be concave, with compaction every 25 cm.

Earth should be neither so dry as to crumble nor so wet as to slump. A key trench prevents shear and base seepage, needed on all walls 1.8 meters or higher. Slopes are safe at 3:1 inner and 2:1 to 2.5:1 outer, with 0.9 meters of freeboard and a key 0.6-0.9 meters deep.

Dam efficiency depends on the flatness of the area behind the wall. The key measure is the ratio of dam wall length to the length of water dammed. Short dams at constricted sites with flattish valleys behind them are the most cost-effective.

When fenced and planted to 30-60 meters of forest and fruit surround, small dams provide biologically clean water. As topsoil is returned and edges planted, mud decreases and water eventually clears. For water cleanliness and parasite control, cattle and sheep should be excluded from dams.

The spillway base should be surveyed 1 meter below crest and away from the wall, with a siphon or base outlet pipe fitted with baffle plates.

P.A. Yeomans estimated that if 10-15% of a normal humid lowland or foothill landscape were fitted with small earth storages, floods and drought or fire threat could be eliminated.

## Integrating Dams with Landscape Design

Dams should never be designed in isolation from the broader landscape plan. The full permaculture approach considers how dams relate to house sites, access roads, forest plantings, animal systems, and cropping areas. A well-placed dam serves multiple functions simultaneously: irrigating gardens via gravity flow, providing fire-fighting reserves, creating microclimate through reflected warmth, supporting aquaculture, watering livestock, and attracting wildlife.

Diversion drains can be cut at gradients of 1:250 to 1:500 rising from hilltops to direct runoff into saddle and ridge dams. These same drains, when placed as part of a [[permaculture-earthworks]] is fundamental. Swales, diversion drains, and dams work together as a coordinated water management system. Swales absorb water into soil on gentler slopes, while dams store surplus for later use. The combination of soil storage and surface storage maximizes the total water retention capacity of any landscape.
