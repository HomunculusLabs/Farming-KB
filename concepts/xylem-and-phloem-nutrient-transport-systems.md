# Xylem and Phloem Nutrient Transport Systems

Once nutrient ions enter the root, they must be distributed throughout the plant. Two distinct vascular systems accomplish this: the **[[xylem]]** moves water and dissolved nutrients primarily upward, while the **phloem** transports sugars, proteins, and other organic compounds bidirectionally. Together, they form the plant's circulatory system.

## Nutrient Movement Through Root Tissues

Nutrient ions travel from the root surface toward the xylem along the same pathways as water. The **[[apoplastic-pathway]]** through cell walls presents far fewer barriers to ions than the highly selective **symplastic pathway**. However, at the [[casparian-strip]] in the endodermis, all ions must enter the symplastic pathway by crossing the plasmalemma.

### From Symplast to Xylem

To reach the xylem, nutrient ions must exit the symplastic pathway and re-enter the apoplastic pathway, because xylem tracheids and vessel elements are dead cells with no cytoplasm, plasmalemma, or plasmodesmata. This transition is accomplished by specialized membrane proteins on xylem parenchyma cells, including proton pumps, ion channels, and carrier proteins that move ions and water across membranes and into the xylem cell walls.

### Driving Forces: Transpiration vs. Root Pressure

Two forces drive xylem sap flow:

**Transpiration pull** is the dominant force. As water evaporates from leaf stomata, cohesion between water molecules and adhesion to xylem surfaces creates tension that pulls the entire water column upward. This passive mechanism can lift water to the tops of the tallest trees.

**Root pressure** operates when stomata are closed or transpiration is very slow. Sap accumulates in xylem cells, water flows in by osmosis to dilute it, and the resulting internal pressure pushes sap upward. Root pressure can only push sap a few meters high. When pressure is extreme, sap may be forced out of leaf tips—a phenomenon called **guttation**.

## Xylem Transport

### Composition of Xylem Sap

Xylem sap is primarily water with dissolved nutrient ions. The concentration of ions varies with environmental conditions: during hot weather with high transpiration, ion concentrations are lower due to greater water volume moving through the plant. In spring, mineral uptake is generally higher than during summer heat.

### Spring Sap Flow and Syrup Production

In spring, the xylem plays an unexpected role: it transports large sugar molecules that were stored in roots over winter. These are converted into transportable sugars that move upward through the xylem to apical meristem tissues aboveground. The sugars create strong osmotic pressure, drawing water into xylem tubes and pushing the sweetened sap upward.

**Birch trees** rely on root pressure from osmotic water movement to generate this spring sap flow. **Maple trees** use a different mechanism involving freeze-thaw cycles. Carbon dioxide produced during cellular respiration of sugars creates gases in the xylem sap. At night, cooling temperatures dissolve these gases, lowering xylem pressure and pulling water in. If temperatures drop enough to freeze the xylem contents, gases become trapped in the ice. Morning warming causes these gases to expand, increasing internal pressure and pushing sap upward. This is the mechanism behind maple and birch syrup production.

Once leaves and buds form in later spring, xylem sap stops carrying sugars and the phloem takes over sugar transport.

### Hormone Transport in the Xylem

Certain hormones also travel through the xylem. Some provide chemical signals that trigger stomatal closure when water supply is insufficient. Others carry growth-related signals. Remarkably, just one or two hormone molecules in a cell can transmit a message, demonstrating the extraordinary sensitivity of plant signaling systems.

### Water Distribution from Xylem

Water exits the xylem and enters cells through multiple mechanisms: osmosis when passing cells with low water content, aquaporins for rapid water transport, and the apoplastic or symplastic pathways. The vascular tissue is arranged throughout the plant to ensure ample access to both water and nutrient supplies.

## Phloem Transport of Organic Compounds

### What the Phloem Carries

Plants are autotrophic—they synthesize everything they need from basic elements. The phloem delivers carbon-containing sugars from photosynthetic cells to non-photosynthetic tissues, along with other molecules picked up along the way. Phloem sap is approximately 80% sugar, with the remainder consisting of amino acids, proteins, hormones, organic acids, ATP, potassium ions, and even viruses.

Interestingly, glucose—the direct product of photosynthesis—is found in very low concentrations or is absent from phloem sap. Instead, approximately 90% of phloem sap compounds are more complex sugars. Some plant species transport sugar-alcohols (such as sorbitol) instead of sugars. The pH of phloem sap is typically alkaline (7.5–8.5), except in perennials during spring when it is acidic.

### Phloem Structure and Function

Phloem sieve tubes are atypical cells lacking nuclei, vacuoles, and most plastids, containing only limited mitochondria. Each sieve tube has a companion cell that controls its activities and supplies energy. Storage cells are associated with the phloem system, extending from leaf veins to roots.

### Root Exudates Travel via Phloem

[[root-exudates]]—the carbon-rich compounds plants release to attract beneficial microbes—travel from their site of synthesis through the phloem to the roots. These include organic acids such as citric, oxalic, piscidic, tartaric, acetic, lactic, and malonic acids. Each acid has different abilities to dissolve phosphate minerals and mobilize micronutrients like zinc, iron, and manganese. Plants tailor exudate composition to their specific nutrient needs.

### Messenger RNA in the Phloem

Modern analytical techniques have revealed that phloem sap contains messenger RNA and other proteins that function as a long-distance signaling system. These RNAs coordinate responses to virus attacks, set up defenses, respond to stress, regulate nutrient distribution, silence genes, and control development.

### Partitioning: Resource Allocation

**Partitioning** is the process by which plants divide up phloem-transported resources among competing sinks. All parts of the plant compete for sucrose, and growing parts also compete for proteins and hormones. Plants allocate resources with remarkable precision:

- Roots grow in proportion to the leaves they must support (which is why reducing leaf area should be accompanied by reduced watering)
- Flowers and seeds develop only after sufficient resources are available for their synthesis
- Sugar itself acts as a partitioning signal, stimulating root cell growth when delivered from leaves

If leaves produce more sugar than roots can use, sugar accumulates in leaves, photosynthesis slows or stops, and root growth ceases. This feedback mechanism maintains balanced growth between aboveground and belowground organs.

### Seasonal Phloem Dynamics

Phloem flow direction changes seasonally. In spring, stored starches in roots are mobilized and flow upward to support new growth. In autumn, leaf proteins are broken down and nitrogen moves downward to stems and roots for winter storage, along with carbohydrates. The nitrogen content of phloem sap can be especially high in young seedlings, where up to half of the sap solids may be nitrogen-based molecules.

### Xylem–Phloem Interaction

The two vascular systems interact functionally. When sugars are actively loaded into phloem sieve tubes at source tissues, the resulting high solute concentration causes water to move from adjacent xylem into the sieve tube via osmosis, creating the turgor pressure that drives phloem flow. At sink tissues, sugars are unloaded, water returns to the xylem, and transpiration carries it away.

## Key Takeaways

- The xylem is a unidirectional, mostly passive system driven by transpiration pull, supplemented by root pressure
- In spring, the xylem transports stored sugars upward; [[maple-syrup-production]] exploits the freeze-thaw mechanism that generates this flow
- The phloem is a bidirectional, energy-requiring system that transports sugars, amino acids, hormones, RNA, and organic acids
- Root exudates travel via the phloem and include specific organic acids tailored to mobilize particular nutrients
- Messenger RNA in phloem sap functions as a long-distance signaling system for defense, stress response, and development regulation
- Partitioning allocates phloem resources among competing sinks with sugar acting as a feedback signal
- Xylem and phloem interact: water moves from xylem into phloem at sources (driven by sugar loading) and returns from phloem to xylem at sinks
