# Mycoremediation of Dye and Textile Wastewater

Fungal decolorization and degradation of dyes represents an economical and feasible alternative for treating textile industry wastewater. During the past several decades, fungi have been demonstrated to degrade various classes of synthetic dyes, offering advantages over conventional physicochemical treatment methods.

## Classification of Synthetic Dyes

Major classes of synthetic dyes relevant to textile wastewater include:

- **Azo dyes**: The most versatile and largest class, containing one or more azo bonds (-N=N-) associated with aromatic systems. More than 2000 different azo dyes are used commercially. These are the most common class released into aquatic environments through textile effluents.
- **Phthalocyanine dyes**: Widely used in textile and dyestuff industries, but less studied for biodegradation.
- **Anthraquinone dyes**: Structurally related to the anthraquinone core found in some PAHs.
- **Triphenylmethane dyes**: Used extensively in various industries including textiles, paper, leather, and food.
- **Heterocyclic dyes, indigo dyes, and polymeric dyes**: Additional classes with distinct chemical structures and degradation characteristics.

Cationic dyes are more toxic, followed by anionic acid and direct dyes.

## Key Fungal Species for Dye Decolorization

### [[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]]

The model white-rot fungus degrades textile dyes through its lignin-degrading enzyme system. Under nitrogen-limited conditions, P. chrysosporium achieves 100% decolorization of Orange II (57 uM, 5 days), Azure B (16 uM, 1 day), and Congo Red (76 uM, 5 days). The fungus degrades dyes with bioaccessible groups such as guaiacol (2-methoxyphenol) and syringol (2,6-dimethoxyphenol). Degradation of azo dyes is enhanced by introducing guaiacol substituents into the dye structure.

### [[trametes-versicolor]]

T. versicolor achieves high decolorization rates across multiple dye classes. Strain CNPR 8107 shows 96% removal of both Remazol Blue RR and Remazol Red RR (60 mg/l, 6 days). T. versicolor does not require strict secondary metabolism conditions to produce ligninolytic enzymes. The fungus effectively decolorizes Acid Black 24 (97%), Reactive Blue (91%), and Indigo Carmine (93%) at 50 mg/l concentrations.

### Other White-Rot Fungi

- **Bjerkandera adusta**: Reduces toxicity of Hydrolyzed Reactive Violet 5 by reducing EC20 values from 72 to 10 after 7 days, with 95% decolorization.
- **[[pleurotus-pulmonarius]]**: Decolorizes Congo Red (93%), Trypan Blue (95%), and Remazol Brilliant Blue R (97%) during solid-state fermentation on corncob, with high laccase activity.
- **Irpex lacteus**: Achieves 90% decolorization of Reactive Black 5 (150 mg/l, 10 days) and over 90% decolorization of Reactive Blue 19.
- **Geotrichum sp.**: Degrades Reactive Black 5, Reactive Red 158, and Reactive Yellow 27 with greater than 99% decolorization through MnP, Mn-independent peroxidase, and laccase.
- **Cyathus bulleri**: Achieves 96% Crystal Violet and 100% Bromophenol Blue and Malachite Green decolorization through laccase activity.

### Non-White-Rot Fungi

Aspergillus foetidus shows high adsorption-based decolorization of Remazol dyes (greater than 98% for Red and Brown, greater than 99% for Dark Blue HR). [[aspergillus-niger]] achieves 100% Poly R-478 removal through adsorption. Penicillium ATCC 74414 removes 100% of Poly R-478 and Poly S-119 through combined adsorption and biodegradation.

## Mechanisms of Dye Decolorization

Two primary mechanisms operate in fungal dye removal:

1. **Biosorption**: Physical adsorption of dye molecules to fungal cell wall components, particularly effective for cationic dyes. This is a rapid process that does not involve enzymatic degradation.

2. **Biodegradation**: Enzymatic breakdown of dye molecules through the action of ligninolytic enzymes (LiP, MnP, laccase) and other oxidative systems. This process can involve azo bond cleavage, aromatic ring oxidation, and complete mineralization to carbon dioxide.

Of 26 white-rot fungi screened, 10 strains decolorize all structurally different dyes tested and produce laccase, lignin peroxidase, and manganese peroxidase on solid medium. Differences in decolorization efficiency are attributed to steric factors, electron distribution, and charge density of dye molecules.

## Enzyme-Catalyzed Decolorization

### Laccase-Catalyzed Decolorization

Laccase from Trametes villosa polymerizes bisphenol A. Dichomitus squalens and Ischnoderma resinosum use laccase and MnP for Orange G and Remazol Brilliant Blue R degradation (91-99%). [[pleurotus-ostreatus]] achieves 100% decolorization of Remazol Brilliant Blue R (50 uM) through laccase activity. Laccase immobilized on modified PVDF membranes has been used for pesticide-contaminated wastewater treatment.

### Peroxidase-Catalyzed Decolorization

Crude MnP preparations from P. chrysosporium decolorize Direct Green 6 (25%), Chrysophenine (31%), and Congo Red (33%). The enzyme system requires hydrogen peroxide and appropriate mediators for optimal activity.

## Factors Affecting Decolorization

Key factors include carbon source availability, nitrogen source and concentration, pH (optimal 4-6 for most fungi), temperature, initial dye concentration, inoculum size, and static versus agitated culture conditions. Higher initial dye concentrations generally decrease decolorization efficiency. Pretreatment of fungal biomass (e.g., Aspergillus niger with NaHCO3) can dramatically improve biosorption capacity.

## Sequential and Repeated-Batch Decolorization

Sequential dye decolorization involves treating effluents containing multiple dyes in successive steps or with different fungal species. This approach is necessary because single fungal species may efficiently decolorize certain dye classes while being ineffective against others. Repeated-batch mode using fungal pellets (e.g., Funalia trogii) can maintain decolorization efficiency over multiple cycles within 24 hours per cycle. Pretreatment with NaHCO3 of Aspergillus niger dead biomass removes nearly 90% of Congo Red in 42 hours at pH 6, demonstrating the effectiveness of simple chemical pretreatments.

## Industrial Effluent Applications

Highly colored diazo-linked chromophore industrial effluent from nitrated stilbene-sulfonic acid manufacturing has been successfully decolorized by white-rot fungi. The effluent contains high levels of sodium chloride and sodium sulfate, yet decolorization rates remain similar in both salted and unsalted portions. This tolerance to high salt concentrations suggests potential for treating chemical industry wastes alongside textile effluents.

## Yeast-Based Decolorization

Candida zeylanoides degrades several simple azo dyes in liquid aerated batch bioreactors, producing aniline as a metabolite from certain azo dyes and sulfanilic acid from others. Although less studied than white-rot fungi, yeasts offer advantages of faster growth rates and easier cultivation for certain wastewater treatment applications.
