# Charcoal Production and Biochar Soil Amendment

Bill Mollison's [[permaculture-designers-manual-bamboo]] discusses charcoal
production as both a traditional craft and a modern strategy for soil
improvement and carbon sequestration. The creation of biochar -- fine
charcoal intended as a soil amendment -- draws on indigenous Amazonian
practices (terra preta) and contemporary pyrolysis science to address
degraded soils and excess atmospheric carbon simultaneously.

## Traditional Charcoal Making

Mollison documents traditional charcoal production methods used
worldwide. The earth kiln, or mound kiln, is the simplest: wood is
stacked in a conical pile, covered with earth and turf, and slowly
burned from the top down. Air is restricted so that wood carbonizes
rather than fully combusting. This method has been practiced for
thousands of years and produces charcoal suitable for both fuel and
soil amendment.

The pit kiln variant involves burning wood in a shallow pit, which
offers better temperature control but is limited to small batches.
Mollison notes that traditional charcoal makers develop an intuitive
sense of when to seal the kiln and when to open it, reading smoke
color and flame behavior as indicators of the pyrolysis stage.

## Kilns and Retorts

More controlled production uses brick or metal kilns. Mollison
describes the Brazilian beehive kiln, a dome-shaped brick structure
that can carbonize several cubic meters of wood per cycle. These kilns
allow regulation of air intake and temperature, improving yield and
consistency.

Retorts represent a significant efficiency gain. In a retort system,
the charcoal is produced inside a sealed container heated by an
external fire. The volatile gases released during pyrolysis are routed
back into the fire as fuel, dramatically reducing wood consumption.
Modern retorts can achieve carbonization yields of 30-35 percent by
weight, compared to 15-20 percent in open kilns.

Mollison encourages the use of retorts that capture wood vinegar
(pyroligneous acid) and wood tar as valuable byproducts. Wood vinegar
serves as a natural pesticide and soil fungicide, while wood tar has
traditional uses in wood preservation and waterproofing.

## Terra Preta and Indigenous Soil Science

The discovery of terra preta (Amazonian dark earth) in the Amazon
basin revealed that indigenous peoples deliberately enriched poor
tropical soils with charcoal, pottery shards, bone, and compost over
centuries. These anthropogenic soils remain fertile today, thousands
of years after their creation, supporting [[soil-food-web]] microbial
communities far more diverse than surrounding unmodified soils.

Mollison presents terra preta as evidence that charcoal-amended soils
can be extraordinarily persistent. The charcoal provides a stable
carbon matrix with high internal surface area, creating habitat for
beneficial mycorrhizal fungi and bacteria. Nutrients adsorbed onto
charcoal surfaces resist leaching in high-rainfall tropical
environments, solving one of the primary challenges of tropical
agriculture.

## Biochar as Soil Amendment

For permaculture application, biochar is distinguished from fuel
charcoal primarily by its preparation. Effective biochar is:

- Finely crushed or screened to increase surface area
- Inoculated with compost tea, manure, or microbial cultures before
  application, because raw charcoal is biologically inert and can
  temporarily adsorb nutrients from soil
- Applied at rates of 5-20 tonnes per hectare for initial
  amelioration of degraded soils
- Mixed into the top 10-20 cm of soil or incorporated into
  [[vermicomposting]] windrows during the thermophilic phase

Mollison cautions that biochar is not a fertilizer substitute. It
improves soil structure, water retention, cation exchange capacity,
and microbial habitat, but must be combined with organic matter and
nutrient inputs to produce results.

## Carbon Sequestration

The climate implications of widespread biochar production are
significant. Charcoal is roughly 80-90 percent stable carbon by weight,
and when buried in soil, this carbon can remain sequestered for
centuries to millennia. Mollison frames biochar production as a
permaculture strategy that simultaneously addresses soil degradation
and atmospheric carbon accumulation.

In [[permaculture-principles]], this is an example of turning a
pollution problem (excess CO2) into a resource (soil amendment). The
energy released during pyrolysis can be captured for cooking, heating,
or electricity generation, making the process energetically productive
rather than purely consumptive.

## Integration into Permaculture Design

Biochar production fits naturally into [[permaculture-zone-planning]] as a Zone 2-3
activity. Kilns or retorts can be fueled by [[fruit-tree-pruning]] waste,
invasive woody species, and forestry residues that would otherwise be
burned openly or left to decompose. The resulting biochar feeds back
into [[zone-1-intensive-garden]] beds, orchard soils, and [[keyline-design]]
pasture improvement programs.

Mollison recommends establishing charcoal production as part of a
closed-loop system where woody biomass is converted to stable carbon,
the heat is used productively, the gases are captured, and the final
biochar returns to the soil that produced the original biomass.
