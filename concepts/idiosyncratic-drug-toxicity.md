## Overview
Idiosyncratic drug toxicity is an adverse reaction that occurs in only a small subset of
exposed patients and is not simply an extension of the drug's intended pharmacology.
It is often called type B toxicity because it is unusual, delayed, patient-specific, and
difficult to predict from ordinary dose response testing.
The liver, skin, and blood-forming tissues are frequent targets, but idiosyncratic
reactions can affect almost any organ system.
The concept is central to drug development because rare severe events may appear only
after thousands of patients have used a medicine.

## Distinction From Intrinsic Toxicity
Intrinsic toxicity is reproducible when exposure is high enough, as in acetaminophen
overdose or direct corrosive injury.
Idiosyncratic toxicity usually appears at therapeutic doses and in patients who tolerate
the drug for days, weeks, or months before injury begins.
Calling the reaction dose independent is misleading; every biological event has exposure
thresholds.
The practical point is that most patients will never experience the event at any
clinical dose, while susceptible patients may react within the therapeutic range.

## Chemical Bioactivation
Many idiosyncratic reactions begin with metabolic bioactivation, especially in the liver
where [[cytochrome-p450-enzyme-system]] activity is high.
A parent compound can be converted into an electrophilic intermediate such as a quinone
imine, epoxide, iminium ion, or acyl glucuronide.
Reactive intermediates may be detoxified by [[glutathione-biochemistry-and-redox-biology]], hydrolysis, or conjugation before they damage cells.
Risk increases when a metabolite persists long enough to covalently modify proteins,
lipids, nucleic acids, or transporter systems.

## Hapten and Neoantigen Models
Small drug molecules are often too small to be immunogenic by themselves.
The hapten model proposes that a reactive drug metabolite binds covalently to a host
protein, forming a new antigenic structure.
Antigen-presenting cells process the modified protein and display peptide fragments on
HLA molecules to T cells.
A related neoantigen model includes altered self-peptides, drug-modified peptides, and
drug-dependent changes in the shape of the peptide-HLA complex.

## Pharmacological Interaction Model
Some reactions do not require covalent binding.
In the pharmacological interaction model, a drug binds reversibly to an immune receptor,
HLA molecule, or T cell receptor and changes immune recognition directly.
Abacavir hypersensitivity is the classic example: the drug binds within HLA-B*57:01 and
changes the repertoire of self peptides presented to T cells.
This explains why a genetic marker can be highly informative for one drug yet fail to
predict most idiosyncratic reactions caused by other compounds.

## HLA and Patient Susceptibility
Human leukocyte antigen alleles are among the strongest known risk factors for some
idiosyncratic drug reactions.
Specific HLA associations exist for abacavir, carbamazepine, allopurinol,
flucloxacillin, and several forms of drug-induced liver injury.
However, carrying a risk allele is usually not sufficient; many carriers tolerate the
drug without clinically important toxicity.
T cell receptor repertoire, infection, inflammatory tone, age, sex, liver disease,
mitochondrial reserve, and detoxification capacity can all shift risk.

## Innate Immune Priming
Adaptive immunity usually requires a first signal from antigen recognition and a second
signal from activated antigen-presenting cells.
The danger hypothesis explains why silent cellular stress can convert a drug-protein
adduct into a true immune problem.
Mitochondrial stress, bile acid transporter inhibition, oxidative stress, or
endoplasmic-reticulum stress can release damage-associated molecular patterns.
These signals activate dendritic cells through pattern-recognition receptors and create
cytokine conditions that allow drug-specific T cells to expand.

## Drug-Induced Liver Injury
Idiosyncratic drug-induced liver injury is the most important clinical subtype because
it can lead to liver failure and drug withdrawal.
Hepatocellular injury shows high alanine aminotransferase relative to alkaline
phosphatase, while cholestatic injury shows disproportionate alkaline phosphatase and
bilirubin.
Mixed patterns occupy the middle ground and often reflect overlapping hepatocyte and
bile-duct stress.
Latency of one to three months is common, although some antibiotics and previously
sensitizing exposures can produce more rapid onset.

## Clinical Clues
A delayed onset after treatment begins is one of the strongest clues that immunity is
involved.
Fever, rash, eosinophilia, lymphadenopathy, or autoantibodies support immune
participation but are not required.
Rapid recurrence after rechallenge is dangerous and can be more severe than the original
episode.
Resolution after withdrawal is expected in many cases, yet some reactions continue
because immune activation, bile duct injury, or organ failure has become self-
sustaining.

## Screening and Prediction
Standard animal toxicology often misses idiosyncratic hazards because laboratory animals
lack the relevant human HLA backgrounds and patient-specific immune repertoires.
Medicinal chemists reduce risk by minimizing reactive metabolite formation, lowering
daily dose, and avoiding structural alerts when alternatives exist.
In vitro assays can detect covalent binding, glutathione adducts, mitochondrial
liability, bile salt export pump inhibition, and cytokine-amplified cytotoxicity.
No single assay predicts the syndrome reliably, so safety assessment uses converging
evidence rather than a decisive yes-or-no test.

## Management Principles
The first step is recognition and withdrawal of the suspected drug when the reaction is
clinically significant.
Supportive care, laboratory monitoring, and exclusion of viral, autoimmune, ischemic,
and obstructive causes are essential in liver injury cases.
Corticosteroids may help selected immune-mediated syndromes but are not a universal
antidote.
Genetic screening is valuable when a strong HLA association exists, as with abacavir and
HLA-B*57:01, but broad genetic prediction remains incomplete.

## Research Frontiers
Modern work links chemical toxicology, immunology, pharmacogenomics, and systems
biology.
Single-cell immune profiling can reveal which T cell populations expand during a
reaction and whether they recognize drug-modified antigens.
Proteomic methods identify the cellular proteins adducted by reactive metabolites in
vivo.
Better models of immune tolerance may explain why most exposed people remain healthy
even when a drug forms reactive intermediates.
