## Overview
Idiosyncratic drug toxicity is an adverse reaction that occurs in only a small subset of
exposed patients and is not simply an extension of the drug's intended pharmacology.
It is often called type B toxicity because it is unusual, delayed, patient-specific, and
difficult to predict from ordinary [[glutathione-biochemistry-and-redox-biology]], hydrolysis, or conjugation before they damage cells.
Risk increases when a metabolite persists long enough to covalently modify proteins,
lipids, nucleic acids, or transporter systems.

## Hapten and Neoantigen Models
Small drug molecules are often too small to be immunogenic by themselves.
The hapten model proposes that a reactive drug metabolite binds covalently to a host
protein, forming a new antigenic structure.
Antigen-presenting cells process the modified protein and display peptide fragments on
HLA molecules to T cells.
A related neoantigen model includes altered self-peptides, drug-modified peptides, and
drug-dependent changes in the shape of the peptide-HLA complex.

## Pharmacological Interaction Model
Some reactions do not require covalent binding.
In the pharmacological interaction model, a drug binds reversibly to an immune receptor,
HLA molecule, or T cell receptor and changes immune recognition directly.
Abacavir hypersensitivity is the classic example: the drug binds within HLA-B*57:01 and
changes the repertoire of self peptides presented to T cells.
This explains why a genetic marker can be highly informative for one drug yet fail to
predict most idiosyncratic reactions caused by other compounds.

## HLA and Patient Susceptibility
Human leukocyte antigen alleles are among the strongest known risk factors for some
idiosyncratic drug reactions.
Specific HLA associations exist for abacavir, carbamazepine, allopurinol,
flucloxacillin, and several forms of drug-induced liver injury.
However, carrying a risk allele is usually not sufficient; many carriers tolerate the
drug without clinically important toxicity.
T cell receptor repertoire, infection, inflammatory tone, age, sex, liver disease,
mitochondrial reserve, and detoxification capacity can all shift risk.
