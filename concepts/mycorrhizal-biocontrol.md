# Mycorrhizal Biocontrol

Mycorrhizal biocontrol refers to the ability of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and other mycorrhizal types to suppress plant diseases caused by soil-borne pathogens. This protective effect has been documented across numerous pathosystems and is one of the most practically important ecosystem services provided by mycorrhizal symbioses.

## Mechanisms of Biocontrol

### Induced Systemic Resistance (ISR)

Mycorrhizal colonization can prime plant defense responses, a phenomenon analogous to induced systemic resistance triggered by plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR). When AMF-colonized plants encounter pathogens, they mount a faster and stronger defense response. This "priming" effect involves:

- Activation of salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, and ethylene signaling pathways
- Enhanced production of pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins
- Increased activity of defense-related enzymes such as chitinases, glucanases, and peroxidases
- Upregulation of phenylpropanoid metabolism leading to accumulation of phenolic compounds

The defense priming triggered by mycorrhizal fungi shares molecular features with both systemic acquired resistance (SAR) and ISR, though the signaling pathways are not identical.

### Altered Root Exudates

AMF colonization changes the composition and quantity of root exudates, which can reduce the attractiveness of roots to pathogenic organisms. Specific mechanisms include:

- Reduced zoospore chemotaxis: In the Phytophthora nicotianae-tomato pathosystem, root exudates from AMF-colonized plants showed reduced ability to attract zoospores, decreasing infection rates
- Changes in amino acid and sugar profiles in exudates that are less stimulatory to pathogen propagule germination
- Increased production of secondary metabolites with antimicrobial properties

### Competition for Host Resources and Infection Sites

AMF and soil-borne pathogens compete for the same ecological niches within plant roots. Mycorrhizal colonization can physically block pathogen entry points and compete for photosynthate carbon. The Hartig net in ectomycorrhizal associations and the extensive intraradical hyphal network in AM associations create a physical barrier to pathogen invasion.

### Mycorrhizosphere Effects

The zone around mycorrhizal roots, termed the "mycorrhizosphere," supports a distinct microbial community. AMF hyphae influence bacterial populations in the hyphosphere, often promoting antagonistic microorganisms that contribute to pathogen suppression. This extended rhizosphere effect means that biocontrol extends beyond the root surface into the surrounding soil.

### Blumenol Accumulation

Specific compounds like blumenols accumulate in AMF-colonized roots and may contribute to defense. Blumenol accumulation follows a dynamic pattern during colonization, with levels increasing as mycorrhizal structures develop. These compounds are related to plant-derived sesquiterpenoids and may serve as both signaling molecules and direct antimicrobial agents.

|----------|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| Phytophthora nicotianae | Tomato | AMF (Glomus mosseae) | Reduced zoospore attraction, lower disease severity |
| Phytophthora cinnamomi | Pineapple | AMF | Decreased lesion development |
| Fusarium oxysporum f.sp. cubense | Banana | AMF | Suppressed wilt symptoms |
| Rhizoctonia solani | Various | AMF | Reduced damping-off |
| Pratylenchus vulnus | Plum | AMF (Glomus mosseae) | Reduced nematode damage |
| Radopholus similis | Banana | AMF (Glomus intraradices) | Decreased nematode reproduction |
| Meloidogyne incognita | Banana | AMF (Glomus mosseae) | Reduced gall formation |

## Limitations and Context-Dependence

The biocontrol effect of mycorrhizal fungi is not universal. Several factors influence efficacy:

- **Soil phosphorus level**: At high P availability, AMF colonization decreases and biocontrol effects may be diminished
- **Host plant cultivar**: Genotype-dependent mycorrhizal responsiveness affects the degree of protection
- **Timing of colonization**: Pre-colonization before pathogen exposure is generally more effective than simultaneous inoculation
- **Pathogen pressure**: Under very high inoculum pressure, mycorrhizal protection may be overwhelmed
- **Substrate type**: Peat-based substrates can reduce mycorrhizal effectiveness

## Practical Applications

### Micropropagation and Weaning

Micropropagated plants are particularly vulnerable during the weaning stage because they lack a protective rhizosphere microbiome. AMF inoculation during Stage 3 micropropagation (in vitro weaning) can provide biocontrol protection during this critical transition. Combined inoculation with PGPR (especially Pseudomonas and Bacillus species) often provides additive or synergistic protection.

### Agricultural Systems

In field conditions, mycorrhizal biocontrol is one component of integrated disease management. It is most effective when combined with other cultural practices and is most economically valuable in low-input and organic farming systems where chemical disease control is limited.
