## Tobacco: From Shamanic Plant to Commercial Cigarette — The Dilution Process

## Overview

The tobacco smoked by modern consumers bears little resemblance to the powerful
shamanic plant used by indigenous peoples of the Americas for millennia. Terence
McKenna, in Food of the Gods, traces the transformation of tobacco from Nicotiana
rustica — a potent, chemically complex, and potentially hallucinogenic species
central to Maya shamanism — to [[nicotiana-tabacum]], the milder commercial variety
that conquered Europe in the form of the cigarette. This transformation illustrates
a broader pattern McKenna identifies: the process by which powerful psychoactive
plants are diluted, domesticated, and commercialized as they pass through
successive cultures.

## The Chemical Complexity of Native Tobacco

Francis Robicsek, cited by McKenna, published extensively on the Mayan fascination
with tobacco and its chemical complexity. Robicsek emphasized that nicotine is by
no means the only bioactive substance in the tobacco leaf. Alkaloids of the harmala
group — harman and norharman — have been isolated from cured commercial tobaccos and
their smoke. These constitute a chemical group of beta-carbolines, which include
harmine, harmaline, tetrahydroharmine, and 6-methoxyharmine, all of which have
hallucinogenic properties.

While the native varieties of tobacco had not been fully analyzed for these
substances at the time of Robicsek's writing, it was considered a reasonable
supposition that their composition may vary widely depending upon the variety and
growing conditions, and that some native-grown tobaccos may contain relatively
high concentrations of these psychoactive beta-carbolines. Tobacco was and remains
the ever-present adjunct of the more powerful visionary hallucinogenic plants
wherever in the Americas they are used in a traditional and shamanic way.

## Maya Tobacco Shamanism and Enema Rituals

One of the most striking aspects of traditional tobacco use involved the New World's
invention of enema administration. Peter Furst researched the role of enemas and
clysters in Mesoamerican medicine and shamanism, revealing that the ancient Maya,
like the ancient Peruvians, employed enemas as a method of administering
intoxicating and hallucinogenic substances.

Enema syringes and narcotic clysters, and even enema rituals, were discovered to
be represented in Maya art. An outstanding example is a large painted vase dating
to approximately A.D. 600-800, on which a man is depicted carrying an enema syringe,
applying an enema to himself, and having a woman apply it to him. As a result of
this discovery, archaeologist Michael D. Coe was able to identify a curious object
held by a jaguar deity on another painted Maya vessel as an enema syringe.

If the enemas of the ancient Maya were, like those of the Peruvian Indians,
intoxicating or hallucinogenic, they might have consisted of fermented balche — a
very sacred [[mckenna-honey-mead-dionysian-cult]] beverage. Balche may have been fortified with tobacco or
with morning-glory seed infusions. Datura infusions and even hallucinogenic
mushrooms may have been administered in this way. A tobacco infusion alone could
also have been used. The variety of possible preparations underscores the
sophistication of Mesoamerican psychopharmacology.

## Tobacco as Quack Medicine in Europe

The introduction of tobacco into Europe was accompanied by the inevitable
association with quack medical theories. In 1661, the Danish physician Thomas
Bartholin recommended tobacco-juice enemas and even tobacco-smoke enemas to his
