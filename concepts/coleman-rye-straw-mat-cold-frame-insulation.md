## The French Intensive System

The maraichers of nineteenth-century Paris practiced what was arguably the most intensive and
productive vegetable-growing system ever devised. Working on small plots of two to five acres
within the city limits, they produced astonishing quantities of fresh vegetables year-round,
including through the winter months.

Their system combined several elements: glass-covered [[coleman-french-maraicher-horse-manure-hotbed-system]], glass bell jars called cloches for protecting individual plants,
and the straw mats that provided overnight insulation for all of these structures.

The scale of the operation was remarkable. Some growers managed up to three thousand
individual cloches, and the cold frames covered acres of growing beds.

## The Rye Straw Mats

The straw mats were made from rye straw, chosen for its durability, flexibility, and
resistance to decomposition. Each mat was approximately one inch thick — substantial enough
to provide meaningful insulation but light enough to be handled by a single worker.

The mats served as the equivalent of a blanket for the plants beneath. On cold nights, they
were unrolled across the glass covers of the cold frames, trapping the heat that had been
absorbed by the soil during the day and preventing it from radiating away into the cold night
air.

In the morning, the mats were rolled up and set aside, allowing sunlight to reach the plants
through the glass. This daily rhythm of covering and uncovering required enormous amounts of
hand labor, but it allowed the maraichers to maintain growing temperatures inside the frames
even during the coldest months.

## The Labor Requirement

The straw mat system was enormously labor-intensive. Every evening, workers had to unroll the
mats across every cold frame and cloche. Every morning, the mats had to be rolled up again.
