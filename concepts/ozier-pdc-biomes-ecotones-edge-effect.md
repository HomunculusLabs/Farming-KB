### When Edge is Counterproductive
Not all edges are beneficial:
- **Building walls** — More surface area means more heat loss
- **Fire-prone landscapes** — More forest-grassland edge increases fire risk

## Microclimates

**Microclimates** are smaller climatic niches within a local area. They are
created by topography, structures, vegetation, water bodies, and other
features that modify the ambient climate.

### Sources of Microclimates
- **Buildings** — All structures create shade, wind blocking, and thermal mass effects
- **Topography** — Valleys, hills, and ridges each create distinct conditions
- **Water bodies** — Ponds and streams moderate temperature
- **Vegetation** — Trees and shrubs modify light, wind, moisture, and temperature
- **Rocks** — Even small features create measurable microclimate differences

### Designing Microclimates
A skilled designer can identify existing microclimates, design new ones for
specific purposes, and support plants that wouldn't normally survive the
local climate by placing them in appropriate microclimates.

## Riparian Zones

**Riparian zones** are the ecological communities that form along rivers and
streams. They are among the most productive ecologies because water
supports dense vegetation, which creates rich habitat for diverse wildlife.

Key characteristics:
- Higher plant density than surrounding areas
- Greater wildlife concentration and distinct microclimates
- Can exist in any climate with surface water flow

## Monsoons
A **monsoon** is a reversal of prevailing winds causing noticeable changes
in rain patterns. Designers must account for monsoon patterns when sizing
water catchment, planning planting schedules, and designing flood management.

## Climate Research Checklist

Ozier recommends every designer research average temperatures, humidity,
prevailing wind directions and speeds, rainfall by month, local biome
identification, frost dates, and extreme weather history. This data forms
the foundation upon which all design decisions rest.
