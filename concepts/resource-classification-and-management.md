# Resource Classification and Management

In permaculture, resources are the useful reserves created when living components and non-living units translate incoming natural energies — sun, wind, rain — into stored, accessible forms. Understanding what resources are, how they behave under use, and how to manage them wisely is fundamental to sustainable design. Mollison's resource classification provides a framework for making ethical, practical decisions about resource use at every scale from household to nation.

## Defining Resources

Resources in a permaculture system are the reserves of energy and materials that the system accumulates and makes available for use. Some of these resources must be used by the system for its own purposes — stocks of fish must be maintained to produce more fish, soil organic matter must be retained to support plant growth. An ideal technology should at the very least fuel itself.

The surplus, over and above these system maintenance needs, is the **yield**. The way to obtain yield is to be conservative in resource use, for energy, like money, is much more easily saved than generated. Resource saving involves recycling waste, insulating against heat loss, and routing resources to their next use point. Before classifying resources by type, it is essential to understand the ethics of resource use that govern permaculture design.

## Two Categories of Ownership

Mollison distinguishes between two broad categories of resource ownership that affect management strategies:

- **Common heritage resources**: Forests, soils, air, water, sunlight, and seeds — resources that belong to all people and upon which all life depends. These are the foundation of any productive system and must be preserved for the benefit of present and future generations.

- **Developed resources**: Those fabricated, ordered, or otherwise developed by people through their work — buildings, tools, cultivated varieties, processed materials. These belong to individuals, families, or groups but are always made from the common resource, making the distinction between the two categories ultimately impossible to draw cleanly.

## The Five Resource Categories

Mollison's most useful analytical framework classifies resources by their response to use — what happens when we consume or interact with them. This use-and-results approach yields five distinct categories:

### Category 1: Resources That Increase by Modest Use

These resources become more productive or abundant when used at appropriate levels. Green browse is a primary example: if deer do not browse shrubs, the latter may become woody and unpalatable. A browsed biennial, unable to flower, may tiller out and become perennial. Seedling trees can be maintained at browse height for continued productivity, but if ungrazed will "escape" to unbrowsable height and shade out other palatable plants.

**Information** is another critical resource in this category. Information withers or becomes outdated if not used. Too little information impoverishes a system, but when freely used and exchanged, it flourishes and increases. This principle underpins permaculture's emphasis on knowledge sharing, open-source design, and community education.

The design implication is clear: identify these resources and ensure they receive regular, moderate use. Both overuse and underuse can degrade them, but modest use stimulates productivity and renewal.

### Category 2: Resources Unaffected by Use

These resources can be utilized without diminishing their availability or quality. Impalpable examples include views, good climate, and solar energy — using sunlight to grow food does not reduce the sunlight available to others. Palpable examples include diverting part of a river for hydroelectric generation or irrigation where the water is returned to the stream after use, stone piles used as mulch or heat stores, and well-managed ecosystems that maintain their productive capacity under harvest.

A well-managed ecosystem is perhaps the most important example in this category. When forests, grasslands, or wetlands are harvested at sustainable levels, they maintain their structure, function, and productivity indefinitely. This is the ideal state for all productive systems — moving them toward Category 2 behavior through careful design and management.

### Category 3: Resources That Disappear or Degrade If Not Used

These are time-sensitive resources that must be captured or they are lost entirely. An unharvested crop of an annual plant represents wasted productivity. Grass that could be stored for winter feed becomes useless if not cut and preserved. Irruptions of oceanic fish, swarms of bees or grasshoppers, ripe fruit, and water runoff during rains all represent fleeting surpluses that must be actively captured to yield any benefit.

The design implication is the need for storage systems and timely harvest strategies. Water storage, food preservation, seed saving, and efficient harvest scheduling are all responses to the challenge of Category 3 resources. Famine often results not from absolute scarcity but from failure to capture and store abundant seasonal surpluses.

### Category 4: Resources Reduced by Use

These resources diminish with each use cycle and cannot be fully renewed on human timescales. Examples include fish or game stocks that are unwisely harvested beyond their replacement rate, clay deposits, mature forests (which take centuries to replace), and fossil fuels like coal and oil. Using these resources at rates exceeding their natural renewal is a form of mining — drawing down stored capital rather than living on income.

The design strategy for Category 4 resources is twofold: use them sparingly and wisely, and use the energy or materials they provide to establish systems that reduce or eliminate dependence on them. Non-renewable resources should be used to conserve and generate energy in living (renewable) systems, creating a transition pathway toward full sustainability.

### Category 5: Resources That Pollute or Destroy Other Resources

These are resources whose use causes net harm to the broader system. Residual poisons in ecosystems, radioactive materials, super-highways, large buildings or areas of concrete, and sewers running pollutants to the sea all fall into this category. Their use destroys or degrades the common heritage resources upon which all life depends.

Mollison argues that these resources, if not used to produce permanent beneficial changes to the ecosystem, become pollutants — some of them permanent pollutants on the timescale of human civilization. The policy recommendation is unambiguous: a responsible human society bans the use of these resources entirely.

## The Sustainable Basis of Society

Categories 1 through 3 are those most commonly produced in natural systems and rural living situations, and are the only sustainable basis of society. Categories 4 and 5 result from urban and industrial development. A sane society manages Categories 1 through 4 wisely, bans the use of Category 5, and regulates all uses to produce sustainable yield. This approach has been successfully applied to some fish and animal populations, but seldom to the broader patterns of human settlement and consumption.

## The Principle of Enough

The key principle to wise resource use is the principle of "enough." Any system or organism can accept only that quantity of a resource which can be used productively. Any resource input beyond that point throws the system or organism into disorder; oversupply of a resource is a form of chronic pollution.

Both an over- and undersupply of resources have much the same effect on system health, except that oversupply has more grotesque results in life systems than undersupply. Undersupply can be coped with by reduced growth and wider spacing or dispersal of organisms, but oversupply can cause inflated growth, crowding, and sociopathy in social organisms. In people, both gross over- and under-nutrition are common, and ethical resource management is needed to balance out the pathologies of famine and obesity.

## Infrastructure and Hidden Costs

Mollison illustrates the consequences of ignoring resource management principles with stark economic examples. Superhighways and overpasses in Massachusetts alone need some $400 billion to repair, and the collapsing sewer systems of London and New York need some $80 billion. Neither jurisdiction can raise this money. This demonstrates that an unthinking historical development strategy can cripple a future society. Today's luxuries are tomorrow's disasters.

The failure to manage resources wisely brings societies down more surely and permanently than war itself. Programs of highway building and city expansion, the release of persistent biocides, and loss of soil destroy more productive land permanently than the loss of territory to a conqueror. Immorality of this nature is often termed "progress" and "growth" to confuse the ignorant and to supplant local self-reliance for the temporary ends of centralized power.

## Policy of Resource Management

A responsible resource management policy:

1. **Bans** the use of resources which permanently reduce yields of sustainable resources — pollutants, persistent poisons, radioactives, large areas of concrete and highways, sewers from city to sea.
2. **Regulates** all resource uses to produce sustainable yield rather than extraction beyond replacement.
3. **Prioritizes investment** in Category 1 and 2 resources that increase or are unaffected by use.
4. **Develops storage systems** to capture Category 3 resources before they are lost.
5. **Uses Category 4 resources** strategically to establish renewable alternatives.

These principles apply at both national and household levels. The investment priorities of a family — whether to spend on durable, productive assets or on consumptive, polluting conveniences — follow the same criteria as national resource policy.

## Resource Management in Practice

The distinction between Categories 1-3 (sustainable resources) and Categories 4-5 (depleting or polluting resources) has profound practical implications for how we design and manage productive systems.

### Investing in Category 1 Resources

Resources that increase with modest use deserve the highest investment priority. Information systems, educational programs, breeding programs for perennial crops, and soil-building practices all fall into this category. The more they are used, the more productive they become. A well-designed grazing system improves soil health, increases pasture diversity, and raises livestock productivity — all while the resource base grows rather than shrinks.

### Maintaining Category 2 Resources

Ecosystems that are unaffected by use represent the ideal state for all productive systems. Moving a degraded system toward Category 2 behavior — where harvest does not diminish productive capacity — is the primary goal of ecological restoration. This involves building soil organic matter, establishing diverse plant communities, restoring hydrological cycles, and ensuring that nutrient exports are balanced by nutrient inputs.

### Capturing Category 3 Resources

The failure to capture time-sensitive resources represents one of the greatest sources of waste in modern systems. Water running off during storms, seasonal fruit dropping unpicked, green manure crops not incorporated, and organic matter not composted all represent Category 3 resources being lost. Designing capture and storage systems for these fleeting surpluses is essential for closing resource loops and eliminating waste.

### Transitioning Away from Category 4 and 5

The long-term trajectory of any permaculture system must be toward eliminating dependence on Category 4 and 5 resources entirely. Category 4 resources (finite, depletable) should be used strategically to establish renewable alternatives — using fossil fuels to build infrastructure that then runs on solar energy, for example. Category 5 resources (polluting) should be eliminated from the system entirely.

## The Law of Return

Central to resource management is the law of return: what is taken from a system must be returned to it. Cities break this law by importing nutrients in food and exporting them as sewage to the sea. Industrial agriculture breaks it by exporting nutrients in harvests and replacing them with synthetic fertilizers derived from fossil fuels. Permaculture design restores the law of return by cycling all outputs back into productive use within the system.

When benefits are not returned or recycled, accumulations of unused benefits predict collapse at the production level, and then throughout all tiers of the system. The 4-6% of the population in essential production are, in Mollison's view, effectively enslaved while the remaining 94% derive secondary or tertiary benefits without adequate return to primary producers. This can only result in a weak economy, waste, and irresponsibility for life existence based on the expectation that the world owes politicians, students, and middlemen a living.

## Yield as the Measure of Resource Management

The ultimate measure of successful resource management is sustainable yield — the surplus of useful products and services that a system produces after meeting its own maintenance needs. Yield is not a fixed sum limited by circumstances that previously existed, but results from our behavior, knowledge, and the application of our intellect, skill, and comprehension. These can either limit or liberate the concept of yield.

Resource management that emphasizes Categories 1-3 creates conditions for unlimited, expanding yield. Resource management that depends on Categories 4-5 creates conditions for declining yield, increasing costs, and eventual system collapse. The choice between these trajectories is, fundamentally, a choice about resource ethics and design intelligence. A society that bans Category 5 resources and wisely manages Categories 1-4 builds a durable foundation for prosperity that does not depend on the impoverishment of future generations or the destruction of the ecological systems that sustain all life.
