# Endophytic Fungi — Sampling Methods, Surface Sterilization, Culture Protocols, and Selective Isolation

## Host Colonization Patterns — Systemic vs Limited Domains

The infection domain of an endophyte profoundly affects sampling efficiency and dictates the appropriate sampling strategy. Endophytes vary widely in how they colonize host tissue, ranging from systemic occupation of virtually all plant organs to highly restricted colonization of only a few cells.

### Systemic Endophytes

[[clavicipitaceous-grass-endophytes]] (e.g., *Epichloë* and *Neotyphodium*) are truly systemic — their hyphae colonize virtually all aerial tissues of the host plant and can be found in the seed coat and near the embryo, ensuring vertical transmission. This systemic distribution means that sampling efficiency is high; virtually any tissue segment will harbor the endophyte.

Nonsystemic "P-endophytes" of grasses, mainly *Phialophora* and *Gliocladium* species, have a more limited tissue distribution but can still be seed-borne (An et al. 1993), complicating their detection relative to the clavicipitaceous endophytes.

### Systemic Seed-Borne Endophytes in Nongrass Hosts

Scattered reports exist of systemic seed-borne endophytes outside the grass family:

- *Phomopsis casuarinae* in *[[casuarina-equisetifolia]]* (Bose 1947)
- Unidentified endophytes in *Helianthemum chamaecistus* (Boursnell 1950)
- Ericaceae (Rayner 1915, 1929)

These reports remain exceptional; most nongrass endophytes do not exhibit systemic colonization patterns.

### Limited Domain Colonization in Nongrass Hosts

Histological studies are available for only a few host species (Stone 1987; Suske and Acker 1987; Cabral et al. 1993; Viret and Petrini 1994). Where examined, nongrass endophyte colonization in healthy tissue is restricted, usually limited to no more than a few cells (Figs 12.1–12.4). This highly localized distribution means that intensive microdissection and careful placement of tissue segments are essential for adequate recovery. The distinction between systemic and limited domain colonization is the single most important factor governing sampling strategy.

## Microdissection and Distribution Patterns

Distribution patterns within host tissues have been investigated using microdissection and culture, revealing a striking mosaic of endophyte occupation:

- **Bissegger and Sieber (1994):** Phloem segments of 1 cm × 1.5 cm were divided into 25 units of 2 mm × 3 mm, yielding a mosaic of 8 endophyte species with discontinuous distribution. Some segments harbored multiple infections, demonstrating that even within a small tissue area, endophyte communities are highly heterogeneous.
- **Lodge et al. (1996a):** Leaves of *Manilkara bidentata* were sectioned into 5 mm × 20 mm panels and further cut into 1 mm × 2 mm fragments. Results were highly heterogeneous: 28 taxa were recovered, 21 were found on only 2 of 3 leaves, and some panels contained up to 15 taxa.
- **G. C. Carroll (unpublished; Carroll 1995):** Douglas fir needles examined on a still finer scale showed that infection incidence decreases precipitously with smaller sample units, underscoring the patchiness of endophyte distribution at microscopic scales.
- **Stone (1987):** *Rhabdocline parkeri* in Douglas fir exhibited densities ranging from 0.2 to more than 30 infections per mm².

These studies collectively demonstrate that endophyte distribution within individual host tissues is extremely heterogeneous. Maceration combined with serial dilution plating (Bills and Polishook 1994) may yield more accurate infection frequency estimates than direct plating of tissue segments.

## General Sampling Guidelines

Several broad principles emerge from decades of endophyte isolation work:

- **Smaller sampling units** yield greater recovery of diverse species and genotypes, but at increased labor and cost per sample.
- **Larger sampling units** may miss rare or slow-growing species and recover mixed genotypes, obscuring true diversity.
- **Older foliage** harbors greater species diversity. Perennials support more diverse endophyte communities than annuals, and evergreen hosts more than deciduous ones.
- **Mycobiota is relatively constant** across a host's geographic range, meaning that sampling many host species in one area is more efficient for biodiversity assessment than sampling one host species across its range.
- **Greatest diversity** is achieved through intensive selective sampling of limited tissue from ecologically varied sites, varying culture conditions, segment size, and medium composition.
- **Each host harbors unique endophyte species**, so total biodiversity is also a function of the number of host species sampled.

## Sample Collection and Storage

While rapid changes in endophyte communities are unlikely immediately after collection, material should be processed within 48 hours. During collection and transport:

- **Air-dry** samples to remove surface moisture.
- Keep samples **cool and dry** during transport.
- Use **cotton, Tyvek, or paper bags/envelopes** — these allow some airflow.
- **Do NOT use plastic bags**, which promote condensation and subsequent mold growth that can overgrow endophytes.

## Surface Sterilization and Culture Protocols

The size of the sampling unit and the [[sterilization]] procedures used vary considerably among investigators, host species, and tissue types. It is essential to experiment with these factors prior to detailed investigations (Petrini 1992; Schulz et al. 1993; Bissegger and Sieber 1994). For root tissues, serial washing is preferable to chemical sterilization (Summerbell 1989; Holdenrieder and Sieber 1992).

The most common approach is brief treatment with a strong oxidant or general disinfectant, followed by a sterile rinse.

### Common Disinfectants

| Disinfectant | Typical Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl, household bleach) | 2–10% | Most commonly used; always specify % hypochlorite/available chlorine and exposure duration |
| Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) | 3% | Widely available, effective oxidant |
| Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) | 2% | Strong oxidant |
| Peracetic acid | 0.03% | Effective at low concentrations |
| Ethanol | 70–95% | Used as wetting agent combined with sterilant, especially for hydrophobic or pubescent leaves |

**Tween 80** surfactant is sometimes combined with sterilants to improve surface penetration. After treatment, tissue is rinsed in sterile water or 70–95% ethanol.

### Less Common Sterilants

- **Silver nitrate (1%):** Used for grass roots and stems, particularly for *Gaeumannomyces graminis*. The silver is precipitated with 5% NaCl after treatment (Cunningham 1981).
- **Mercuric chloride (0.01%):** Historically used for *Acer*, *Eucalyptus*, and *Picea*. Seldom used now due to extreme toxicity and environmental concerns.
- **Formalin (30–50%):** Effective but requires careful handling due to fumes and toxicity.
- **Propylene oxide / Ethylene oxide:** Slow penetration makes them useful for sterilizing natural media, field equipment, and woody tissue. Both are explosive and toxic — handle with extreme care in ventilated areas.

### Serial Washing

For delicate tissues (especially roots), serial washing removes surface contaminants without chemical damage:

- **Harley and Waid (1955):** Classical method for roots, shoots, and leaves involving repeated water rinses.
- **C. Booth (1971):** Large vessel with vigorously agitating water.
- **Ultrasonic cleaning (Holdenrieder and Sieber 1992):** Provides the most complete surface contamination removal, suitable for root tissues with adhering soil particles.

## Media and Incubation

### Media Selection

Routine mycological media are suitable for endophyte isolation. [[malt-extract-agar]] (MEA) at 1–2% is the most widely used base medium, sometimes supplemented with yeast extract (0.1–0.2%). Colony-limiting agents and antibiotics are often added to suppress fast-growing contaminants and bacteria:

- **Bills and Polishook (1992):** 1% malt + 0.2% yeast + 50 ppm streptomycin + chlortetracycline yielded the highest species richness from *[[chamaecyparis-thyoides]]*.
- **Bills and Polishook (1991):** Fungal growth inhibitors added to media produced greater species richness from *[[carpinus-caroliniana]]* bark.
- **Water agar** can be used for initial isolations — it reduces bacterial and fungal contamination but produces more diffuse, spreading colonies that may be harder to transfer.

Fungi isolated on selective media should be **subcultured quickly** onto media without inhibitors to restore normal growth and sporulation.

### Incubation Conditions

- **Temperature:** 18–25°C is standard.
- **Duration:** Endophytes are characteristically slow to emerge; prolonged incubation (weeks to months) is needed to recover the full complement of species.
- **Plate sealing:** Sealing plates with Parafilm prevents desiccation but may inhibit sporulation. Slow desiccation of plates often promotes sporulation, particularly for coelomycetes.
- **Arrangement:** Tissue pieces placed on the agar surface in serial order allow investigation of positional and distribution effects within the original tissue.
- **Multiwell plates** help prevent cross-contamination among isolates.
- **Host tissue presence** on the plate often promotes sporulation of isolated endophytes.

### Practical Aids for Tissue Preparation

- **Glass microscope slides** can be used as guides for cutting uniform tissue segments.
- A **kitchen pasta cutter** works well for cutting thin leaf strips efficiently.
- **Alternative to cutting:** Tissue maceration, particle filtration, and dilution plating (Bills and Polishook 1994; Donegan et al. 1996) offer complementary approaches.

### Particle Filtration Protocol

Particle filtration is particularly useful for estimating infection frequencies and recovering a broader range of taxa:

1. Wash and disinfect tissue samples.
2. Macerate tissue in a sterile blender.
3. Filter homogenate through a wire mesh prescreen.
4. Pass fine particles through polypropylene mesh (105–210 μm), calibrated so that each particle yields one colony or none.
5. Resuspend particles in sterile water or 0.2% agar.
6. Plate on standard media.
7. Inspect plates daily and isolate new colonies as they emerge.

This approach provides a quantitative estimate of infection frequency and allows recovery of endophytes that might be missed by conventional segment-plating methods.

## Selective Isolation

Maximizing endophyte biodiversity recovery requires a multifaceted approach combining varied tissue segment sizes, different media compositions, and extended incubation periods. No single medium or protocol recovers all endophytes from a given host. The most comprehensive studies employ multiple media types (including selective and nonselective), multiple incubation conditions, and careful attention to emerging colonies over weeks to months. Each modification of protocol — segment size, sterilant strength, medium additives, incubation temperature, and humidity — selectively favors different subsets of the endophyte community.
