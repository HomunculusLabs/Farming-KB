## Gardening in Hard Times

Strategies for productive vegetable gardening when resources — water, fertilizer, money, energy — are limited or uncertain. Based primarily on Steve Solomon's "Gardening When It Counts."

## The Problem with Modern Gardening

Modern intensive gardening methods (raised beds, close spacing, frequent watering) have a hidden dependency:

- **Raised beds need constant watering** — they drain faster than in-ground beds
- **Close spacing demands constant fertility inputs** — more plants per area = more nutrients removed
- **Drip irrigation requires infrastructure** — pumps, filters, tubing, timers
- **Transplant production needs supplies** — potting mix, trays, grow lights, heat mats

When these inputs become expensive or unavailable, intensive gardens fail. Solomon advocates for **low-input, wide-spacing gardening** that produces more food per unit of effort and cost.

## Low-Input Gardening Principles

### 1. Wide Spacing
- Space plants 2-4x wider than intensive methods recommend
- Each plant has more root space to forage for water and nutrients
- Plants develop deeper, more drought-resistant root systems
- Less total water needed per unit of food produced
- Example: Tomatoes at 4-5 ft apart instead of 2 ft

### 2. In-Ground Beds (Not Raised)
- In-ground soil has a larger moisture reservoir
- Plant roots can access subsoil moisture
- Less surface area exposed = less evaporation
- No infrastructure needed (no lumber, screws, soil mixing)

### 3. Deep, Infrequent Watering
- Water deeply (to encourage deep rooting) rather than frequently
- Goal: water deeply enough that plants don't need irrigation for 1-2 weeks
- In dry climates, this may mean running water slowly for hours per bed
- Mulch heavily to retain moisture between waterings

### 4. Drought-Adapted Varieties
- Choose varieties bred for low-water conditions
- Look for "drought-tolerant," "dry-farmed," or regional heirloom varieties
- Some crops naturally need less water: beans, squash, corn (once established)
- Avoid water-demanding crops in dry climates (celery, lettuce in summer)

### 5. Complete Mineral Nutrition
- Even with wide spacing, plants need all 13 nutrients
- Use [[nutrient-dense-gardening]] COF recipe or similar
- Well-nourished plants are more drought-resistant and pest-resistant
- Poor nutrition + water stress = crop failure

## Vegetable Recommendations for Low-Input Gardens

### High-Return Crops (Grow These)
| Crop | Cal/ft² | Reason |
|------|---------|--------|
| Potatoes | High | Calorie-dense; stores well; low maintenance |
| Corn | High | Calorie-dense; stores as grain |
| Beans (dry) | High | Protein; stores indefinitely; fixes N |
| Winter squash | High | Stores for months without processing |
| Garlic | High | Stores forever; medicinal; easy |
| Onions | Medium | Stores well; essential flavor |
| Carrots | Medium | Stores well; calorie-dense for a vegetable |
| Cabbage | Medium | Stores well; high vitamin C |
| Tomatoes | Medium | Preserve as sauce; high yield potential |

### Low-Return Crops (Consider Skipping)
- Lettuce and salad greens (low calorie, high water, short season)
- Radishes (very low calorie return)
- Most herbs (low calorie; though valuable for flavor and medicine)

## Water Strategies

### Rainfall Gardening
In areas with >20 inches of annual rainfall, it's possible to garden without irrigation:

1. **Time planting to rainfall patterns** — plant main crops to mature during rainy season
2. **Use mulch** — 4-6 inches of organic mulch dramatically reduces evaporation
3. **Choose appropriate crops** — drought-adapted varieties
4. **Wide spacing** — reduces competition for limited water
5. **Build soil organic matter** — each 1% OM increases water-holding by ~20,000 gal/acre

### Graywater Use
- Divert washing machine, shower, and sink water to garden
- Use biodegradable soap only
- Apply through mulch basin, not directly on foliage
- See [[homestead-water-systems]] for details

## Fertility Without Purchased Inputs

If commercial fertilizers become unavailable:

1. **Comfrey and nettle liquid feed** — see [[dynamic-accumulators]]
2. **Compost** — from kitchen scraps, yard waste, animal manure
3. [[dynamic-accumulators]] — grow plants that mine deep minerals
4. **Animal manure** — chickens, rabbits, goats on the homestead
5. **Wood ash** — provides K, Ca, and raises pH (use sparingly)
6. **Cover crops** — legumes for N; deep-rooters for minerals; see [[cover-cropping-guide]]

## Seed Self-Reliance

- Learn to [[seed-saving-vegetables|seed saving techniques]] — open-pollinated varieties only
- Save seeds from your best-performing plants (local adaptation)
- Store seeds cool and dry; most last 3-5 years
- Maintain genetic diversity — save from multiple plants
- Focus on staples: beans, corn, squash, potatoes (true seed), grains

## Tools and Equipment

Minimal tool list for low-input gardening:

1. **Digging fork** — the most important tool; aerates without destroying structure
2. **Hoe** — for weed control between wide-spaced plants
3. **Shovel** — for moving compost and soil
4. **Rake** — for seedbed preparation and leveling
5. **Hose/watering can** — for deep watering
6. **Wheelbarrow** — for moving materials

No tiller needed. No raised bed lumber. No drip irrigation. No grow lights.
