# Permaculture Ecosynthesis and Novel Ecosystems

Ecosynthesis is the process by which novel ecosystems assemble from mixtures of indigenous and exotic species in response to new environmental conditions created by human activity. david holmgren argues in Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability that recognising and working with ecosynthesis, rather than fighting against it, is one of the most important and controversial implications of permaculture thinking for [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] planning.

## Humanity as Part of Nature

The idea that complex weedscapes, especially in urban landscapes, represent new evolving ecosystems of great importance in an energy descent future is a major theme of Holmgren's work over two decades. A broad understanding of successional models and evolutionary dynamics has helped him to see ecological pattern and order where others see meaningless and destructive chaos.

UNESCO's statement on the subject is definitive: "There is no fundamental difference between natural, wild or modified, semi-natural or developed, domesticated or purely artificial vegetations. The laws governing these ecosystems are identical." This recognition that human-influenced ecosystems are still ecosystems, governed by the same ecological laws as pristine ones, is the foundation of ecosynthesis thinking.

## The Chandler Floodplain Case Study

The Chandler floodplain in Melbourne's eastern suburbs provided a detailed case study of ecosynthesis in action. Researchers mapped and described the vegetation of the floodplain including channels, alluvial flats, riparian edges, and wetland sites, correlating this with recorded and anecdotal information about vegetation and land use in the past.

Despite the wide disparity and severity of land use impacts from grazing and farming to golf courses and stormwater drainage works, patterns of vegetation succession were evident. Of greatest value were areas where there had been no substantial management for decades: these places showed slowly emerging stages of vegetation succession that appeared to have some characteristics of a classic climax.

In the absence of fire, grazing, and clearing, the floodplain vegetation changed towards a forest dominated by river red gums with a closed canopy understorey of tall shrubs and small trees, including indigenous, Australian native, and exotic species. The robustness of this successional climax was given further weight by the fact that the vegetation pattern was emerging from diverse starting points including grazed woodland, cultivated fields, and freshly bulldozed areas.

## Factors Driving Novel Succession

Four factors important in driving the evolution of novel successional patterns in the urban Chandler floodplain ecosystem were identified. First, the abundance of water from both natural and stormwater sources. Second, the abundance of nutrients from farming, stormwater, and other sources. Third, the abundance of seed and vegetative propagules from diverse suburban garden, agricultural, and indigenous forest sources. Fourth, the absence of grazing animals and fire.

The absence of grazing animals and fire began the breakdown of the pre-European ecosystem, while the water and nutrients provided the energy for vigorous growth and decay cycles, as well as abundant food for birds and animals. The seed sources ensured a wide diversity of potential species from which systemic characteristics could develop.

## The Riparian Weedscapes of Hepburn Springs

The extensive riparian weedscape of Spring Creek and its tributary gullies in Hepburn Springs attracted Holmgren's attention when he moved to the area in 1985. Over the following decade, an informal group of local residents created tracks and planted trees over several kilometres of blackberry and willow-dominated stream corridor.

Management decisions were constrained by the great scale and power of the system and limited resources. Reading landscape skills and ecological theory helped guide a strategy for accelerating the succession to a low fire hazard, high amenity mixed corridor forest. A succession from primary weedscape of blackberry, gorse, and cape broom to deciduous forest of willow, hawthorn, and native blackwood was evident.

## Research Confirms Ecosynthesis

Two formal university research projects by members of the Hepburn group confirmed and quantified general observations and predictions. A PhD study comparing the streambed ecology of Spring Creek with a parallel stream dominated by eucalypts showed the willows were capturing forty times more sediment and ten times more phosphorus than the eucalypts. Most of the sediment was trapped in the extraordinary willow root mattresses that clothe the streambed.

The other study aged the willows, sycamores, and ashes along the creek and used statistical analysis and successional theory to predict that sycamore and ash would begin to dominate as the first generation of willows died. These results show the potential for more predictive ecological science applied to novel ecosystems.

## Implications for Revegetation Practice

At the time that novel successional patterns and other systemic characteristics were being identified in urban ecosystems, indigenous revegetation advocates were identifying all non-indigenous species as environmental weeds to be removed. After nearly two decades of intensive management, many of these areas have been turned into purely indigenous versions of the same forest type with massive inputs of herbicides and labour.

It is ironic that, despite the continued dominance of the ubiquitous red gum, the emerging wild systems and the intensively managed revegetation alternative bear little functional or structural relationship to the pre-European ecosystem: an open grassy woodland, much drier, more dependent on fire and grazing, and lower in fertility. The energy cost of maintaining a purely indigenous system may be far greater than working with the novel ecosystem that is actually emerging.

## Ecosynthesis and Energy Descent

The recognition of the reality of ecosynthesis is important at multiple levels. Knowing that ecosystems can evolve at remarkably fast rates in response to human and other influences, we can hope that the self-organisational power of nature can be enhanced and augmented to create functional human ecosystems in relatively few generations.

Thus permaculture and similar concepts such as Analogue Forestry, which design agricultural systems that mimic the structure and function of natural forest ecosystems, are realistic strategies for the rapid creation of productive and resilient systems. These novel systems will not be replicas of pre-European ecosystems but new creations that serve human needs while providing ecological functions.

## The Design Rules of Ecosynthesis

These examples suggest that the development of systemic properties in novel combinations of plants and animals is not so much the final outcome of slow bottom-up evolutionary processes. Instead they are the design rules that guide the cyclical and evolving dance of species toward a new nature. Understanding these design rules allows permaculture designers to work with the forces of ecosynthesis rather than against them.

The practical implication is that permaculture designers should spend less energy fighting against "weeds" and more energy understanding and guiding the successional processes that are already underway. The weeds are not the enemy; they are the pioneers of new ecosystems that may ultimately be more productive and resilient than the systems they replace.

## Controversy and Resistance

The ecosynthesis perspective is controversial within both the environmental movement and indigenous cultural resurgence. The identification of all non-indigenous species as environmental weeds to be removed has become a default position in many land management agencies. This position, while understandable as a reaction against the damage caused by some invasive species, fails to recognise the positive role that many exotic species play in building new ecosystems.

Holmgren acknowledges that some non-indigenous species are genuinely destructive, particularly those that create monocultures or fundamentally alter ecosystem processes in ways that reduce diversity and function. But the blanket identification of all exotic species as weeds prevents the recognition and utilisation of the positive contributions that many exotic species make to ecosystem function, especially in heavily modified landscapes where pre-European conditions cannot be restored.

## A New Nature

The ecological holocaust of the Central Victorian goldrush in the late nineteenth century was the release phase which allowed the piecemeal settlement processes since to lay down the energetic regimes and biological resources for ecosynthesis. Similar release phases have occurred throughout the world as industrial civilisation has transformed landscapes. In each case, the result has been not simply degradation but the creation of new ecological systems with their own emergent properties and potentials.

Permaculture's role is to understand these new natures and work with them to create productive, resilient, and sustainable human ecosystems. This does not mean abandoning the goal of ecosystem health but rather redefining what ecosystem health means in the context of novel species combinations and novel environmental conditions. It means being guided by function and process rather than by species origin.
