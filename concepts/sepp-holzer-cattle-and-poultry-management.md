Source: Sepp Holzer, *Sepp Holzer's Permaculture* (2010)

Beyond pigs, Sepp Holzer integrates wild cattle breeds and poultry into the Krameterhof permaculture system, each fulfilling multiple functions in the landscape.

## Wild Cattle and Old Domestic Breeds

Holzer has successfully bred old domestic and wild cattle breeds for decades. At peak, he kept a mixed herd of around 50 wild cattle in a 25-hectare paddock. Breeds include:
- European bison (Bison bonasus) -- critically endangered, bred in cooperation with zoos
- American bison (Bison bison)
- Yaks (Bos Poephagus mutus) -- thick hair makes them well suited to cold
- Water buffalo (Bubalus Bubalus arnee)
- Scottish Highland cattle
- Hungarian steppe cattle
- Dahomey miniature cattle

### Keeping Bovine Species
- Always kept using paddock rotation system
- Land never overused; soil and vegetation have time to recover
- Paddock system particularly important for cattle because their weight causes soil compaction
- Animals form small herds; different species can develop bonds
- Water buffalo tend to isolate themselves and stay close to one particular pond
- Social relationships between animals require careful attention
- Natural hierarchy should include a dominant bull; rivals should be significantly weaker
- Enclosures large enough to provide space to avoid each other
- Visual barriers (wooded areas, hedges) provide places to rest and hide
- Animals should not be "put on display" -- they need escape routes and minimal human disturbance
- Two-metre-high electrified game fences have given best results (wild cattle overcome normal fences through curiosity and instinct to play)

### Feed and Self-Medication
- Additional feeding limited to winter: turnips, fodder kale, Jerusalem artichokes, hay, grain, apple and pear pomace
- Animals with diarrhoea from parasites self-select poisonous plants: lupins, monkshood, male fern, buttercups, [[palmer-poisonous-mushrooms-deadly-species]]
- Animals with lush, diverse flora including poisonous mushrooms stay healthy and no longer need worming
- Confirmed by decades of veterinary inspection
- Key principle: "Food is also medicine! It must be varied, nutritious and healthy"
- Never mix herbs or poisonous plants into feed yourself -- only the animals know what they need

### Dehorning
Holzer strongly opposes dehorning:
- Unbelievably painful for the animals
- Animals act in a completely different and disturbed way afterwards
- Animals butt each other in the stomach, potentially causing premature or stillbirths in pregnant cows
- Animals may store and dispose of harmful substances in claws and horns
- Dehorning, docking tails, and cropping ears are "nothing more than mutilation"

## Poultry

### Bird Conservation
Holzer emphasises that birds are "incredibly useful and beneficial creatures" for [[mushroom-pests-insect-control-stamets]] and plant propagation. Even small gardens can contribute:
- Well-structured gardens with diverse hedges offer habitat and food
- Chemical use must be abandoned
- Greater plant diversity leads to greater insect diversity, providing balanced bird diet
- Recommended forage plants: elderberry, Guelder rose, wayfaring tree, wild cherry, bird cherry, fly honeysuckle, barberry, bramble, dog rose, wild privet, yew, ivy, spindle tree, dogwood, snowy mespilus, whitebeam, rowan

### Keeping Poultry Humanely
- Birds kept extensively, mostly self-sufficient
- Naturally hatched birds preferred (incubator-hatched birds lack maternal instincts)
- Good breeds: mallards, Indian runner and crested ducks, Styrian chickens
- Hardy and adaptable, help keep snail population down

### Predator Protection
- Thorny hedges provide shelter and nesting sites
- Recommended wild roses: multiflora rose (strong climber, abundant flowers), dog rose (medicinal, vitamin C-rich hips), Japanese rose (large red hips, tasty)
- Mobile nesting sites: two pieces of rough timber in thorny undergrowth
- Mobile sites hardly ever attacked -- moving them repels distrustful predators
- Duck and geese breeding ponds with islands accessible only by rickety wooden planks
- Islands planted with different willow varieties for additional protection

### Winter Feeding
- Birds do not need to be fed in a diverse permaculture system
- Forage plants with varied seed sizes attract multiple species
- Best winter feeding: do not harvest everything -- "untidy" brushwood piles and thick hedges harbour insects
- Only feed when ground is completely covered with snow
- Fat and feed mixture spread onto tree bark for woodpeckers, treecreepers, and nuthatches

### Economic and Self-Sufficiency Considerations

Holzer's approach to animal management is driven by self-sufficiency rather than maximum production. His mixed herd of wild cattle breeds serves multiple economic functions: meat production, breeding stock for conservation programs (particularly for endangered species like the European bison), and ecotourism — visitors come to the Krameterhof specifically to observe the unusual animal diversity. The feeding strategy reduces winter feed costs substantially by relying on stored root crops like Jerusalem artichokes and turnips that can be grown on-farm, rather than purchasing commercial feed. Poultry at the Krameterhof are largely self-sustaining, with natural hatching eliminating the need for incubators and the diverse ecosystem providing most of their food through insects, seeds, and forage plants. Holzer calculates that the value of pest control, fertilizer production, and plant propagation provided by free-ranging poultry far exceeds the value of eggs or meat they produce. For homesteaders looking to replicate this model, the initial investment in breeding stock and fencing is offset over time by reduced feed costs, veterinary expenses (due to robust health from diverse diets), and the elimination of chemical pest controls. ### Ecological Benefits of Mixed Herd Systems

The integration of multiple bovine species and poultry creates ecological synergies that conventional single-species livestock operations cannot achieve. Different grazing animals utilize different vegetation strata: cattle graze tall grasses, water buffalo prefer aquatic plants and lush margins, and poultry scratch through dung deposits to control parasite populations. This multi-species grazing mimics natural herd dynamics found in wild ecosystems and prevents any single plant species from dominating pasture. The hoof action of large animals breaks up compacted soil surfaces, improving water infiltration and seed germination, while their manure distributes nutrients across the landscape in patterns that benefit both plants and soil organisms. Poultry following behind cattle disrupt parasite lifecycles by scratching through manure patties, a natural form of biological parasite control that Holzer has relied on for decades without chemical dewormers. The paddock rotation system ensures that each area receives periodic intense disturbance (grazing and trampling) followed by extended rest periods, creating the pulse-and-rest dynamic that grassland ecologists recognize as essential for healthy pasture ecosystems. ### Health Monitoring and Veterinary Approaches

Holzer's approach to animal health diverges sharply from conventional veterinary practice, which relies heavily on routine chemical deworming, vaccinations, and antibiotics. At the Krameterhof, veterinary intervention is minimized through proactive management: diverse diets that allow animals to self-medicate, clean water from protected springs, adequate space to reduce stress and disease transmission, and mixed-species grazing that breaks parasite lifecycles. When health problems do arise, Holzer first addresses [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]] — is the paddock too small? Has the rotation been too fast? Is the diet lacking variety? He has found that most health problems resolve when these underlying conditions are corrected. The self-medication principle is central: when animals have access to the full diversity of plants in a permaculture system, including species that would be considered toxic in a conventional setting, they can select the compounds they need to maintain internal parasite balance and address minor ailments. Holzer notes that decades of veterinary inspections have confirmed the health of his animals without routine chemical treatments. For homesteaders, this approach offers significant advantages in reduced veterinary costs and the production of meat and eggs free from chemical residues. However, it requires careful observation skills and a willingness to let animals express natural behaviors that may seem alarming to conventionally trained stockpersons. See [[faires-livestock-integration-permaculture]] for integrating smaller-scale livestock systems.

### Manure Management and Soil Building

The integration of cattle and poultry into the Krameterhof's landscape creates a continuous cycle of [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] that eliminates the need for imported fertilizers. Holzer views animal manure not as a waste product to be managed but as a primary soil-building resource that should be returned to the land as efficiently as possible. In the paddock rotation system, cattle deposit manure across the landscape as they graze, distributing nutrients in the pattern of their natural movement. Poultry that follow or cohabit with cattle scratch through manure deposits, breaking them up and accelerating decomposition while controlling fly larvae and internal parasite eggs. Holzer constructs deep litter systems in winter shelters where layers of straw, leaves, and woody material absorb manure and create a rich compost over the winter months. This deep litter material is then spread on paddocks during spring, providing both organic matter and a slow-release nutrient source. The combination of cattle manure (high in nitrogen and phosphorus) and poultry manure (rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium) provides a balanced fertility profile. Holzer also utilizes duck and geese ponds, where accumulated organic material from waterfowl creates nutrient-rich muck that can be scooped out and applied to gardens. This integrated approach to manure management is fundamentally different from conventional concentrated animal feeding operations, where manure becomes a disposal problem requiring expensive handling infrastructure. In Holzer's system, manure is always an asset rather than a liability. See [[animal-manure-guide]] for [[solomon-manure-nutrient-variability-and-quality]] profiles and application guidelines.
