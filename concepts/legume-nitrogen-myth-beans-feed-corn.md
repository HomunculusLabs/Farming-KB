# Legume Nitrogen Myth: Do Beans Feed Corn?

The idea that beans and corn planted together form a mutually
beneficial partnership is one of the most persistent companion
planting myths in organic gardening. While the concept is appealing
and widely repeated, the scientific evidence does not support the
claim that beans meaningfully feed nitrogen to corn during the
current growing season.

## The Claim

Many gardening books recommend intercropping corn and beans so
that the nitrogen-loving corn will prosper from the association.
The thinking follows that legume roots take large amounts of
nitrogen from the air and make it available to nearby plants, as
stated in the Encyclopedia of Organic Gardening (Rodale Press,
1978). This interpretation oversimplifies how legumes actually
handle nitrogen.

## How Legumes Actually Use Nitrogen

During the growing season, nitrogen gathered by a legume's root
nodules is banked in a temporary savings account in the stems and
leaves. The nitrogen moves through the plant in a specific
pattern:

- Before flowering: approximately 60% of the plant's nitrogen is
  in the leaves, with only about half coming from root nodules
  and the rest absorbed from fertile soil.
- After seed pods form: only 8% remains in the leaves.
- At seed maturity: 70% of total nitrogen has accumulated in the
  seeds, while roots and nodules contain as little as 3 to 6% of
  the total.

In short, legumes hoard nitrogen for seed production. They offer
little nitrogen in a form other plants can use during the same
growing season.

## Why the Myth Persists Despite Evidence

There are almost no studies showing improved yield in corn grown
with beans in temperate climates. The studies that do show
benefits from legume-grass intercropping come from tropical
regions and are not applicable to the United States. However,
numerous studies do substantiate the well-known and traditional
practice of planting legumes in rotation with grains and corn,
which is a fundamentally different approach than intercropping.

Rotation works because the nitrogen benefit accrues over time as
legume residues decompose and become available to subsequent
crops. This is fundamentally different from expecting a living
bean plant to exude nitrogen to a neighboring corn plant in real
time.

## Can Legumes Share Nitrogen in the Current Season?

For a legume to contribute nitrogen to companion plants during
the same season, the nitrogen-fixing nodules must separate from
the legume's roots. Once released from their symbiotic
association, nodules decompose and release their nutrients into
the soil. However, nodule shedding is triggered by stress:

- Drought causes shedding, but also stunts the corn.
- Shading triggers shedding, but shade suppresses corn growth.
- Defoliation (from grazing or cutting) causes shedding.
- Plant death releases nodules, but too late for that season's
  crop.

There is no practical scenario where nodule shedding benefits a
companion crop without also harming the legume or the companion
itself.

## The Real Value of Legumes for Nitrogen

The nitrogen value of legumes is realized through incorporation,
not co-planting. Legume green manures tilled into soil before
flowering capture the peak leaf nitrogen content. Legume cover
crops left to decompose between seasons provide nitrogen for the
following crop. The range of nitrogen gathered per acre varies
from 40 pounds (field beans, peanuts) to 250 pounds (alfalfa),
making species selection critical.

## Practical Recommendations

Instead of interplanting beans with corn, gardeners should:

- Use legume cover crops in rotation with heavy-feeding crops.
- Till or cut legume green manures before flowering for maximum
  nitrogen return.
- Choose high-yielding nitrogen fixers like alfalfa (250 lb/ac)
  or sweet white clover (160 lb/ac) for cover cropping.
- Understand that the nitrogen benefit is for the next crop, not
  the current one.
- Plan rotations spanning multiple seasons for meaningful soil
  nitrogen improvement.
