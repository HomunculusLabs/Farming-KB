# How Should Nature Be Perceived?

From "The Natural Way of Farming" by [[masanobu-fukuoka]], Chapter 3 of "The [[fukuoka-natural-way-of-farming-theory-of-natural-farming]] Farming."

## Seeing Nature as Wholistic

The central truth [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] is that nothing need be done to grow crops.

I have learned this because non-discriminating knowledge has enabled me to confirm that nature is complete and crops more than capable of growing by themselves. This is not the theoretical hypothesis of a scholar in his study or the wishful thinking of an idler with an aversion to work; it is based on a total, intuitive understanding of the reality about self and nature.

This statement is the cornerstone of Fukuoka's entire philosophy, and it is perhaps the most difficult for the modern mind to accept.

We have been trained to believe that improvement requires effort, that progress requires intervention, that left to itself, nature tends toward disorder. Fukuoka asserts the opposite: that nature, left to itself, tends toward abundance, harmony, and health.

The role of the farmer, in Fukuoka's view, is not to impose order on nature but to recognize and align with the order that already exists.

This is not passive observation but an active, engaged form of attention -- what Zen practitioners call "beginner's mind," a state of openness that allows reality to reveal itself without the distortions of preconception.

## Examining the Parts Never Gives a Complete Picture

A scientist who wishes to know Mt. Fuji will climb the mountain and examine the rocks and wildlife.

After having conducted geological, biological, and meteorological research, he will conclude that he now has a full picture of Fuji. But if we were to ask whether it is the scientist who has spent his life studying the details of the mountain who knows it best, the answer would have to be no.

One can know Fuji by looking at it from afar. One must see it and yet not examine it, and in not examining it, know it.

Without the whole, the parts are lost, and without the parts, there is no whole. Both lie within the same plane. The moment he distinguishes between the trees and rocks that form a part of the mountain and the mountain as a whole, man falls into a confusion from which he cannot easily escape.

This paradox -- that the whole and the parts exist simultaneously on the same plane, yet cannot be grasped through the same mode of perception -- is central to Fukuoka's epistemology. It echoes the Buddhist teaching of nonduality and the Taoist concept of the Tao that can be named but is not the true Tao.

The scientist who dissects the mountain gains knowledge of its components but loses sight of the mountain itself. The poet who gazes at the mountain from a distance apprehends its wholeness but cannot name its rocks.

Fukuoka suggests that a higher form of perception exists in which both modes are transcended -- a perception that sees the parts within the whole and the whole within the parts, without separating them.

## Become One with Nature

Farming is an activity conducted by the hand of nature.

We must look carefully at a rice plant and listen to what it tells us. One should essentially put oneself in the place of the rice. In so doing, the self looking upon the rice plant vanishes.

This is what it means to "see and not examine and in not examining to know."

Laying aside one's ego is the quickest path to unity with nature.

Nature should not be taken apart. The moment it is broken down, parts cease being parts and the whole is no longer a whole. When collected together, all the parts do not make a whole.

"All" refers to the world of mathematical form and "whole" represents the world of living truth. Farming by the hand of nature is a world alive, not a world of form.

The distinction between "all" and "whole" is subtle but profound.

"All" is additive: it is the sum of parts, measurable and finite. "Whole" is emergent: it is a quality that arises from the relationship between parts, and it cannot be captured by addition.

A living organism is more than the sum of its cells. An ecosystem is more than the sum of its species. A farm is more than the sum of its soil, water, crops, and farmer.

## Natural Farming Begins with the Assumption That Nature Is Perfect

The only direct means for confirming the perfection of nature is for each individual to come into immediate contact with the reality of nature and see for himself.

[[natural-farming]] begins with the assumption that nature is perfect.

Natural farming starts out with the conviction that barley seeds which fall to the earth will send up sprouts without fail. If a barley sprout should emerge then later wilt in mid-growth, something unnatural has occurred and one reflects on the cause, which originates in human knowledge and action.

One never blames nature, but begins by blaming oneself.

There is no good or [[fukuoka-textdoc-no-good-or-evil-in-nature]]. Natural farming admits to the existence neither of [[duggar-mushroom-insect-pests-and-fungal-diseases]] nor of [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]]. If a pest outbreak occurs, one reflects that this was probably triggered by some human mistake.

Thus, [[mulching-in-natural-farming]], one always solves the problem by reflecting on the mistake and returning as close to nature as possible.

Those practicing [[fukuoka-illusions-scientific-farming-centrifugal-centripetal-model]], on the other hand, habitually blame insect infestation on the weather or some other aspect of nature.

## The Practice of Wholistic Perception

How does one actually develop this wholistic perception? Fukuoka offers no technique or method, because any technique would be a product of the discriminating intellect he seeks to transcend.

Instead, he suggests that wholistic perception arises naturally when the mind is quiet and the ego is set aside.

This is not a state that can be achieved through effort; effort itself is a form of interference. It is more like a state of receptivity -- like the way a mirror reflects whatever stands before it without distortion.

The farmer who has cultivated this receptivity sees his land as it is, not as he thinks it should be, and acts from that seeing rather than from theory.
