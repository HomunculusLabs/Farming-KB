# Natural Dye Plants

Natural dyeing transforms leaves, flowers, roots, and barks of plants into colorants for fiber, yarn, and fabric. This ancient craft aligns with homestead self-sufficiency by utilizing garden plants, weeds, and foraged materials for textile coloration.

## How Natural Dyeing Works

Natural dye molecules (pigments) bind to fiber through chemical bonds. The bond strength depends on the fiber type, the dye plant chemistry, and the mordant used. Without a mordant, most natural dyes will wash out or fade quickly.

### Fiber Types and Affinity

- **Cellulose fibers** (cotton, linen, hemp, ramie): Poor affinity for natural dyes. Require mordanting for any lasting color.
- **Protein fibers** (wool, silk, alpaca, mohair): High affinity for natural dyes. Take color readily, often with just alum mordant.

## Mordants

Mordants (from Latin "mordere" — to bite) are metallic salts that create a chemical bridge between fiber and dye. They also modify color outcomes.

### Common Mordants

| Mordant | Typical Use | Safety Notes |
|---------|------------|--------------|
| Alum (potassium aluminum sulfate) | Bright, clear colors; primary mordant | Safest option; food-grade available |
| Iron (ferrous sulfate) | Saddens/darkens colors; "iron modifier" | Use sparingly — damages fiber over time |
| Copper (copper sulfate) | Greens and blues; brightens yellows | Toxic — wear gloves, dispose properly |
| Tin (stannous chloride) | Bright reds and oranges | Toxic — use minimal amounts |
| Chrome (potassium dichromate) | Deep, fast colors | Highly toxic — avoid for home use |
| Tannin (oak gall, sumac, tea) | Pre-mordant for cellulose; browns | Safe; improves lightfastness |
| Alum + cream of tartar | Wool mordant; brighter colors | Standard professional combination |

### Mordanting Process (Alum for Wool)

1. Weigh fiber dry (WOF — weight of fiber)
2. Calculate: 15% alum WOF + 6% cream of tartar WOF
3. Dissolve in warm water, add to dye pot with fiber
4. Bring to 180°F, hold for 1 hour, stirring occasionally
5. Turn off heat, let fiber cool in mordant bath
6. Rinse and proceed to dyeing or dry for later use

### Mordanting Cellulose

Cellulose requires a two-step process:
1. Tannin bath (6-10% WOF tannin, simmer 1 hour)
2. Alum bath (15% WOF alum, simmer 1 hour)

Some dyers use aluminum acetate (5-8% WOF) as a single-step cellulose mordant with good results.

### Green

True green is rare in nature. Achieve by:
- Overdyeing yellow with indigo (dip in indigo after yellow dye)
- Using weld + iron combination
- Using foxglove leaves (variable results)

## Growing Dye Plants

Integrate dye plants into the homestead landscape:

- **Weld**: Biennial, full sun, well-drained soil. Direct sow spring, harvest flowering stems year 2.
- **Madder**: Perennial, needs 3+ years for root development. Plant in raised beds for easy harvest. Deep, sandy soil.
- **Indigo (annual varieties)**: Full sun, warm season crop. Start indoors 6 weeks before last frost. Harvest leaves repeatedly.
- **Woad**: Biennial, very cold-hardy. Direct sow fall or early spring. Harvest leaves from first-year rosettes.
- **Marigold**: Annual, easy from seed. Deadhead for continuous bloom. Dry flowers for year-round use.
- **Coreopsis**: Perennial, prolific. Deadhead to extend bloom season.

## Dye Extraction Methods

### Hot Extraction (Most Common)

1. Chop plant material finely
2. Cover with water in stainless steel pot (no aluminum or iron)
3. Bring to simmer (180°F), hold 1 hour
4. Strain out plant material
5. Add pre-mordanted fiber to dye bath
6. Simmer 30-60 minutes
7. Cool in bath for best color absorption

### Cold Extraction

For delicate pigments (some flowers, berries):
1. Soak plant material in water 24-48 hours
2. Strain and use liquid as dye bath
3. May require longer immersion times

### Fermentation Extraction

For indigo: leaves are fermented in alkaline water with a reduction agent to extract indican. See dedicated indigo resources for the complex vat process.

## Modifiers

Apply after dyeing to shift colors:

- **Iron water** (rusty nails in vinegar, 2 weeks): Saddens/browns yellows, turns pinks to purple
- **Ammonia**: Shifts some reds to brown-purple
- **Vinegar/acid**: Brightens yellows, shifts some colors
- **Copper after-bath**: Greens yellows
- **Baking soda (alkaline)**: Shifts reds toward purple

## Lightfastness and Washfastness

Not all natural dyes are equal in permanence:

- **Excellent**: Weld, madder, indigo, walnut hulls, cutch, cochineal
- **Moderate**: Marigold, coreopsis, logwood, onion skin
- **Poor**: Turmeric, berries (blackberry, elderberry), most flower petals without mordant

Test all dyes on sample skeins before committing to a large project. Tape samples to a sunny window for 2 weeks to test lightfastness.

## Sustainable Harvesting

- Harvest no more than 1/3 of any wild plant stand
- Leave roots of perennial dye plants to regenerate
- Grow dye plants in dedicated garden beds
- Compost spent dye plant material
- Collect onion skins, avocado pits, tea leaves, and coffee grounds as kitchen waste dye sources
- Use rainwater for dye baths when possible (mineral content varies with tap water)
