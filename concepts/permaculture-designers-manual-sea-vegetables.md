# Sea Vegetables

updated: 2026-04-18

From: [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]]

## Overview

Sea vegetables (edible seaweeds and marine algae) are a significant but often overlooked food and soil resource in permaculture systems. Mollison references seaweeds throughout the Designers Manual for their roles as soil amendments, trace element sources, livestock supplements, and traditional foods. The nutrient-rich coastal environments where seaweeds thrive offer parallels to the productive edge zones that permaculture design seeks to maximise.

## Nutritional and Soil Value

Seaweeds provide a rich source of trace elements and growth factors:
- **Selenium** - present in seaweeds; frequently deficient in agricultural soils
- **Boron** - present in seaweed concentrate; frequently deficient in cropped soils
- **Iodine** - deficiencies causing growth problems and goitre are remedied by fish, shellfish, and seaweed diet
- **Fluorine** - beneficial in small amounts, as in seaweed fertiliser, though a serious poison at high levels
- **Potassium** - abundant in seaweed; important for plant growth and often deficient on sandy, free-draining coastal soils

Seaweed teas sprayed on leaves, with seaweed concentrate, are absorbed by plants within 24-56 hours in humid weather. Manures add most trace elements except calcium; seaweed specifically provides the micronutrients that manures lack.

## Seaweed as Fertiliser and Mulch

In cold oceanic climates such as Alaska, Ireland, Scotland, and parts of Norway, shelter and added nutrients from seaweeds and manures yield rich meadows and heavy vegetable production during the long summer days. The small stone-walled fields of Ireland produce abundant sweet hay, root crops, and greens for storage, enriched by centuries of seaweed amendments. This traditional practice demonstrates the long-term soil-building potential of regular seaweed applications.

Seaweed can be applied as:
- Direct mulch on garden beds (after rinsing if salt content is high)
- Composted with other organic materials
- Liquid seaweed extract as foliar spray
- Seaweed meal mixed into potting soils
- Added to animal bedding for later composting

## Kelp and Coastal Ecology

Mollison describes miles-long drifts of sea kelp floating along coasts, carrying hundreds of tons of volcanic boulders held in their roots. Kelp strands are followed over 300 km, stranding on granite beaches and throwing boulders onto ancient basalt piles. Before migrating to Japan and Alaska, millions of petrels (Puffinus tenuirostris) annually fill their crop with Tasmanian bull kelp, demonstrating the vast ecological role of kelp forests as marine habitat and nutrient transport systems.

Kelp forests serve as:
- Habitat for thousands of marine species
- Nutrient cyclers from deep ocean to coastal zones
- Coastal protection through wave attenuation
- Carbon sequestration in marine ecosystems
- Food source for marine and terrestrial animals

## Common Sea Vegetables

### Brown Seaweeds
- **Kelp** (Laminaria and Ecklonia spp.) - large brown seaweeds for fertiliser, food, and mineral supplements
- **Wakame** (Undaria pinnatifida) - edible brown seaweed, popular in Asian cuisine
- **Bladderwrack** (Fucus vesiculosus) - traditional mineral supplement and fertiliser source

### Red Seaweeds
- **Dulse** (Palmaria palmata) - red seaweed, traditional food in northern Europe
- **Nori/Laver** (Porphyra spp.) - red seaweed for wrapping food, high in protein and vitamins
- **Irish moss** (Chondrus crispus) - source of carrageenan for food thickening

### Green Seaweeds
- **Sea lettuce** (Ulva lactuca) - green seaweed for salads and soups
- **Sea grape** (Caulerpa spp.) - tropical green seaweed

## Traditional and Modern Uses

Sea vegetables serve multiple functions in permaculture systems:
- **Food** - direct human consumption of edible seaweeds, rich in minerals and vitamins
- **Fertiliser** - seaweed mulch and liquid seaweed extracts for gardens and orchards
- **Animal feed** - seaweed meal as mineral supplement for livestock (providing iodine, selenium, and other trace elements)
- **Soil conditioner** - trace element and humic acid source for depleted soils
- **Compost activator** - seaweed stimulates compost decomposition and adds microbial diversity
- **Pest deterrent** - seaweed mulch may deter some soil pests through mineral content and salt

## Marine Impoundments

Throughout the Polynesian world, marine impoundments exist for the conservation and feeding of economically useful fish, shellfish, and marine plants such as mangroves, or to consolidate and protect shorelines with coral-block breakwaters. These traditional systems offer models for permaculture marine design, integrating sea vegetable cultivation with fish and shellfish production.

## Harvesting Considerations

- Harvest seaweed sustainably, leaving holdfasts and regrowth capacity intact
- Rinse salt from seaweed before applying to gardens if used as mulch
- Collect from clean beaches away from industrial pollution sources
- Compost fresh seaweed before direct application to avoid salt burn on sensitive plants
- Follow local regulations regarding seaweed collection from beaches and reefs
