# Fungal to Bacterial Biomass Ratio in Soil

The fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratio (F:B ratio) is a key indicator of [[soil-food-web-structure]] and health. It describes the relative abundance of fungal biomass compared to bacterial biomass in a given soil. This ratio is critically important because different plants have evolved to thrive in soils dominated by different [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]]. Understanding and managing the F:B ratio allows gardeners, farmers, and land managers to create soil conditions tailored to the plants they wish to grow.

## Overview

Soils contain vast quantities of microorganisms. Bacterial populations are remarkably consistent across soil types, typically ranging from approximately 100 million to 1 billion per teaspoon of soil — whether that soil comes from a garden, a prairie, or a forest. What changes dramatically across these environments is the fungal biomass. The shift from bacterially dominated to fungally dominated soils is driven primarily by increases in fungal biomass, not decreases in bacterial biomass.

Protozoa, which graze on bacteria, number in the hundreds of thousands per teaspoon of soil. [[fungal-hyphae]] lengths vary enormously by ecosystem: several yards per teaspoon in garden soils, but an astonishing 1 to 40 miles per teaspoon in conifer forest soils.

## Plant Succession and the F:B Ratio Gradient

[[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]] shift predictably along a gradient of ecological succession:

- **Bare soil / beach environments** — strongly bacterially dominated
- **Grasslands and prairies** — mixed or balanced communities
- **Old-growth forests** — strongly fungally dominated

This progression reflects the nature of the plant communities that establish at each stage. Pioneer species colonizing bare soil are typically short-lived annuals that benefit from rapid bacterial nutrient cycling. As plant communities mature into perennials, shrubs, and eventually trees, [[the-soil-food-web]] shifts toward greater fungal dominance.

Plants on the "beach side" of this spectrum prefer bacterially dominated soils. Plants on the "old growth" side prefer fungally dominated soils. Prairie plants occupy the middle ground, preferring a balance between bacterial and fungal communities — a condition analogous to that favored by lawn grasses.

## Why Fungal Dominance Increases with Succession

Several interrelated factors drive the increase in fungal biomass during succession:

- **Plant lifespan**: Longer-lived plants (perennials, trees) can sustain mycorrhizal partnerships, which require time to establish and maintain. Short-lived annuals gain little advantage from forming [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-and-mycorrhizal-relationships]] because the investment cannot be recouped within their brief lifespan.
- **Litter quality**: Seasonal (annual) plants produce litter composed mostly of cellulose, which is readily broken down by bacteria. Longer-lived plants produce litter containing both cellulose and lignin, and fungi are the primary decomposers of lignin-rich material.
- **Fungal growth characteristics**: Fungi are relatively fragile and slow-growing compared to bacteria. They require time and stable conditions to build biomass. Mycorrhizal fungi in particular depend on live root systems — they cannot persist indefinitely in fallow soil the way many free-living bacteria can.

## Plant Lifespan and Nitrogen Preference

A plant's position on the bacterial-to-fungal spectrum correlates with its preferred form of nitrogen:

| Plant Lifespan | Preferred Nitrogen Form | Soil Community |
|---------------|------------------------|----------------|
| Seasonal / annual | Nitrate (NO₃⁻) | Bacterially dominated |
| Perennial (1+ years) | Ammonium (NH₄⁺) | Fungally dominated |

Bacteria-dominated soils tend to produce nitrate through rapid mineralization, while fungally dominated soils retain more nitrogen in ammonium form and organic matter, releasing it slowly through fungal-mediated processes.

## F:B Ratio Requirements by Plant Category

The following tables summarize the fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratios preferred by various categories of garden and landscape plants, as reported in [[soil-food-web]] research.

### Vegetables and Field Crops

| Plant Category | F:B Ratio | Examples |
|---------------|-----------|----------|
| Leafy / cool-season vegetables | 0.3:1 to 0.8:1 | Carrots, lettuce, broccoli, cole crops |
| Warm-season vegetables and grains | 0.8:1 to 1:1 | Tomatoes, corn, wheat |

### Turf and Grasses

| Plant Category | F:B Ratio | Notes |
|---------------|-----------|-------|
| Lawns | 0.5:1 to 1:1 | Balanced bacterial and fungal communities |

### Trees

| Plant Category | F:B Ratio | Examples |
|---------------|-----------|----------|
| Conifers | 50:1 to 1,000:1 | Pine, spruce, fir, hemlock |
| Deciduous hardwoods | 10:1 to 100:1 | Maples, oaks, poplars |
| Orchard trees | 10:1 to 50:1 | Apple, pear, peach, cherry |
| Riparian trees (young) | Bacterially dominated | Alder, beech, aspen, cottonwood |
| Riparian trees (mature) | 5:1 to 100:1 | Same species, established |

Riparian trees are notable for shifting their soil community requirements over their lifespan. When young, they prefer bacterially dominated soils; as they mature, their preferred F:B ratio increases substantially.

### Flowers and Ornamentals

| Plant Category | F:B Ratio | Notes |
|---------------|-----------|-------|
| Annual flowers | Bacterially dominated | Short lifespan, cellulose-rich litter |
| [[hamilton-perennial-flowers-organic]] | Fungally dominated | Longer lifespan supports mycorrhizae |

### Shrubs

Shrubs generally require higher fungal dominance than perennial flowers. Within this category, the native habitat of the shrub determines its specific F:B requirements:

- **Conifer forest natives** (e.g., rhododendrons) — very high F:B ratios
- **Deciduous forest natives** (e.g., cotoneaster, lilac) — moderate F:B ratios, lower than conifer associates

## Managing the F:B Ratio

### Testing

Agricultural testing laboratories can analyze soil samples to determine the fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratio. This information is valuable for diagnosing why certain plants may be underperforming and for guiding soil amendment strategies.

### Increasing Fungal Biomass

To shift soil toward higher fungal dominance:

- Provide fungal foods such as woody materials, leaf litter, compost with high fungal content, and other lignin-rich organic matter
- Reduce or eliminate tillage, which physically disrupts fungal hyphae networks
- Maintain continuous live root systems to support mycorrhizal fungi
- Apply fungal-dominated compost teas or inoculants

### Fostering Bacterial Biomass

To shift soil toward bacterial dominance or maintain balanced communities:

- Provide bacterial foods such as green manures, fresh plant residues, and simple sugar sources
- Use controlled tillage where appropriate (tillage favors bacteria over fungi)
- Apply bacterial-dominated compost or compost teas
- Maintain adequate moisture and aeration for bacterial activity

## Practical Implications

Understanding the F:B ratio has direct practical applications:

- **[[crop-rotation-and-succession-planting]]** can be planned around microbial community requirements, ensuring that soil biology matches the needs of the next crop.
- **Compost quality** matters — composts differ in their F:B ratios depending on feedstock [[solomon-compost-tumbler-critique-and-turning-frequency]]. Less-frequently turned, woody composts tend to be more fungal.
- **Landscape design** should group plants with similar F:B requirements together, allowing for targeted soil management rather than attempting to maintain incompatible conditions in the same bed.
- **Disturbance recovery** takes time because fungal networks are slow to rebuild. Practices that minimize soil disturbance (no-till, perennial ground covers, mulching) preserve established fungal communities.

## Key Takeaways

- The F:B ratio reflects ecological succession: bacterial dominance in early-succession soils, fungal dominance in late-succession soils.
- Bacterial counts are relatively stable across ecosystems; the variable is fungal biomass.
- Plant lifespan, litter chemistry, and mycorrhizal dependence all influence a plant's preferred F:B ratio.
- Matching soil microbial communities to plant preferences improves nutrient availability, disease resistance, and overall plant health.
- Soil testing and targeted organic amendments allow deliberate management of the fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratio.
