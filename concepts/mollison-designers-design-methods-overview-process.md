# Permaculture Design Methods and Process

Permaculture design is a systematic methodology for creating sustainable
human settlements. Bill Mollison presents design as an iterative process of
observation, analysis, design, implementation, and evaluation. Unlike
conventional design approaches that impose predetermined solutions, permaculture
design emerges from the unique conditions of each site. The design process
applies [[permaculture-principles]] to create productive, resilient systems
that require minimal external inputs.

## The Design Process Overview

Mollison outlines the permaculture design process as a series of sequential
but iterative phases:

1. Observation and data collection
2. Site analysis and mapping
3. Design concept development
4. Detailed design and element placement
5. Implementation planning and scheduling
6. Implementation
7. Monitoring and evaluation
8. Adjustment and adaptation

Each phase feeds back into previous phases -- design is never truly "finished"
but continuously refined through observation and experience. This connects to
[[mollison-designers-site-analysis-mapping-observation]] and [[mollison-pattern-understanding]].

## Phase 1: Observation

The foundation of all permaculture design is protracted and thoughtful
observation. Mollison's first principle is to observe before acting. This
phase involves spending time on the site through all seasons, recording
natural patterns, and gathering climate, soil, water, and vegetation data.
The observer must suspend preconceptions and let the site reveal its
character. Key tools include journaling, photography, and systematic data
recording.

Observation extends beyond the physical site to include the broader community,
local economy, cultural context, and historical land use. Mollison emphasizes
that understanding people and community is as important as understanding the
land for effective design.

## Phase 2: Analysis

Site analysis transforms raw observation data into organized understanding.
Mollison uses several analytical frameworks:

**Sector analysis** maps incoming energies (sun, wind, fire, water, noise)
and determines appropriate design responses. See [[mollison-designers-sector-analysis-wind-sun-fire]].

**Slope analysis** classifies landforms and identifies appropriate uses for
each position in the landscape. See [[permaculture-slope-analysis-and-reading]].

**Zone planning** organizes elements by frequency of use and intensity of
management. See [[permaculture-zone-planning]].

**Need and yield analysis** identifies the inputs (needs) and outputs
(yields) of each proposed element, revealing opportunities for connections
between elements where one's yield meets another's need.

**Functional analysis** examines what each element does (functions) and what
could perform that function (functional alternatives). This ensures redundancy
and resilience -- each essential function is supported by multiple elements.

## Phase 3: Design Concept Development

The design concept integrates analytical understanding into a coherent vision.
Mollison describes several approaches to developing design concepts:

**Theme-based design**: Organizing the design around a central theme or
metaphor (e.g., "forest garden," "water harvesting landscape," "self-reliant
homestead"). The theme provides unity and direction.

**Functional flow design**: Tracing the flow of energy, water, nutrients,
and materials through the system and designing connections that minimize
waste and maximize cycling.

**Zone-based design**: Starting from Zone 1 (the home center) and working
outward, placing elements according to their frequency of use and
relationship to the center.

**Sector-based design**: Starting from the incoming energies identified in
sector analysis and designing responses that channel, deflect, or harvest
each energy flow.

Mollison recommends combining multiple approaches for robust design concepts
that address all site conditions.

## Phase 4: Detailed Design

Detailed design places specific elements on the map with precise locations,
sizes, species, materials, and connections. Mollison describes several
placement strategies:

**Relative location**: Place elements so that they support each other.
Chickens near the garden for pest control and manure. Compost between
kitchen and garden. Greenhouse attached to the house for thermal benefit.

**Multiple functions**: Every element should perform multiple functions.
A dam stores water, provides fire protection, creates habitat, grows fish,
and reflects light. A windbreak provides shelter, produces timber, fixes
nitrogen, and feeds livestock.

**Multiple elements for each function**: Important functions should be
supported by multiple elements for resilience. Food production from
gardens, orchards, foraging, and livestock. Water from tanks, dams, wells,
and rain harvesting.

**Energy efficient planning**: Place frequently-used elements close
together. Place rarely-used elements far away. Use gravity, animals, and
natural processes to move materials instead of fossil fuels.

## Design Methods

Mollison presents several formal design methods in the Designer's Manual:

**Flow diagrams**: Chart the movement of energy and materials through the
proposed system, identifying all inputs, outputs, and connections.

**Overlay mapping**: Transparent maps for each factor (water, access, sun,
wind, soil, vegetation) overlaid to identify optimal placement where
favorable conditions coincide. **Random assembly**: brainstorming element
lists and randomly combining them to discover unexpected synergies.
**Decision matrices**: systematic comparison of options against weighted
criteria for complex decisions.

## Phase 5-8: Implementation, Monitoring, Adjustment

Implementation planning sequences elements logically: water systems first,
windbreaks and pioneer trees early, access roads before earthworks, fencing
before livestock, soil-building before planting, and Zone 1 before outer
zones. Implementation remains flexible -- the real world always differs
