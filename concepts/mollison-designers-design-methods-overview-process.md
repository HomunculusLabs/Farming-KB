# Permaculture Design Methods and Process

Permaculture design is a systematic methodology for creating sustainable
human settlements. Bill Mollison presents design as an iterative process of
observation, analysis, design, implementation, and evaluation. Unlike
conventional design approaches that impose predetermined solutions, permaculture
design emerges from the unique conditions of each site. The [[hemenway-garden-design-process-observation-and-visioning]]
applies [[mollison-designers-site-analysis-mapping-observation]] and [[mollison-designers-sector-analysis-wind-sun-fire]].

**[[mollison-designers-slope-analysis-keyline-design]]** classifies landforms and identifies appropriate uses for
each position in the landscape. See permaculture-zone-planning.

**Need and yield analysis** identifies the inputs (needs) and outputs
(yields) of each proposed element, revealing opportunities for connections
between elements where one's yield meets another's need.

**Functional analysis** examines what each element does (functions) and what
could perform that function (functional alternatives). This ensures redundancy
and resilience -- each essential function is supported by multiple elements.

## Phase 3: Design Concept Development

The design concept integrates analytical understanding into a coherent vision.
Mollison describes several approaches to developing design concepts:

**Theme-based design**: Organizing the design around a central theme or
metaphor (e.g., "forest garden," "water harvesting landscape," "self-reliant
homestead"). The theme provides unity and direction.

**Functional flow design**: Tracing the flow of energy, water, nutrients,
and materials through the system and designing connections that minimize
waste and maximize cycling.

**Zone-based design**: Starting from Zone 1 (the home center) and working
outward, placing elements according to their frequency of use and
relationship to the center.

**Sector-based design**: Starting from the incoming energies identified in
[[mollison-designers-sector-analysis-wind-sun-fire]] and designing responses that channel, deflect, or harvest
each energy flow.

Mollison recommends combining multiple approaches for robust design concepts
that address all site conditions.

## Phase 4: Detailed Design

Detailed design places specific elements on the map with precise locations,
sizes, species, materials, and connections. Mollison describes several
placement strategies:

**Relative location**: Place elements so that they support each other.
Chickens near the garden for pest control and manure. Compost between
kitchen and garden. Greenhouse attached to the house for thermal benefit.

**Multiple functions**: Every element should perform multiple functions.
A dam stores water, provides fire protection, creates habitat, grows fish,
and reflects light. A windbreak provides shelter, produces timber, fixes
nitrogen, and feeds livestock.

**Multiple elements for each function**: Important functions should be
supported by multiple elements for resilience. Food production from
gardens, orchards, foraging, and livestock. Water from tanks, dams, wells,
and rain harvesting.

**[[cervantes-case-study-energy-efficient-sea-of-green]] planning**: Place frequently-used elements close
together. Place rarely-used elements far away. Use gravity, animals, and
natural processes to move materials instead of fossil fuels.
