## Homestead Record Keeping

Systematic record keeping transforms a homestead from a collection of guesswork into a data-driven operation. Tracking your activities lets you identify what works, what does not, and where your resources go. After 2-3 years of good records, you can make informed decisions that save money, increase yields, and reduce failures.

## Why Keep Records

- **Yield tracking** — know which varieties, planting dates, and methods produce the most food per square foot
- **Financial accountability** — understand true costs versus income from homestead products
- **[[seasonal-planning]]** — refer to past years to optimize planting, harvesting, and preservation schedules
- **Animal health** — track vaccinations, breeding, weight gain, feed consumption, and health events
- **Legal and tax** — support deductions, agricultural tax status, and insurance claims
- **Improvement measurement** — compare year-over-year progress toward your goals

## Record Categories

### Planting Records
For each crop, record:
- Variety name and source
- Planting date and method (direct seed, transplant, start date for indoor starts)
- Germination rate and date
- Spacing and location (garden bed number or map reference)
- Fertilizer/amendment applications (type, rate, date)
- Pest and disease observations
- First harvest date
- Total yield (weight or count)
- Taste notes [[mushroom-spawn-vitality-testing-and-quality-assessment]]
- Storage method and shelf life

A simple garden journal or spreadsheet handles this well. Include a garden map each year showing what was planted where — essential for crop rotation.

### Harvesting and Preservation Records
Track everything you preserve:
- Date
- Crop/variety
- Quantity harvested (raw weight)
- Preservation method (canning, freezing, dehydrating, fermenting, root cellaring)
- Quantity preserved (jars, pounds, bags)
- Shelf life observations
- Consumption tracking (when you open/use preserved goods)

This data tells you how much to plant next year. If you ate 40 quarts of tomato sauce last year, work backward: 40 quarts requires approximately 200 pounds of tomatoes, which requires roughly 15-20 plants depending on variety.

### Animal Records
For each animal or flock, maintain:

**Individual animal records:**
- ID/tag number, name, breed
- Birth date and parentage (if known)
- Purchase price and source
- Weight at purchase and periodic weigh-ins
- Vaccination and deworming dates (product, dosage)
- Health events (illness, injury, treatment, outcome)
- Breeding history (dates, sire, litter size)
- Production records (milk yield, egg count, weight gain)
- Butchering date and dressed weight
- Feed consumption (type, amount, cost)

**Flock/herd summaries:**
- Monthly feed costs
- Egg production (daily or weekly counts)
- Meat production (number processed, total weight, cost per pound)
- Mortality events and causes
- Veterinary expenses

### Financial Records

Track every dollar in and out of the homestead operation. Categories:

**Expenses:**
- Animal feed and supplies
- Seeds, plants, and garden supplies
- Fertilizer, amendments, and soil inputs
- [[coleman-small-farm-equipment-and-tools]] (with depreciation)
- Fuel and utilities
- Veterinary care and medications
- Building materials and repairs
- Insurance
- Processing supplies (jars, lids, freezer bags, etc.)
- Education and training

**Income:**
- Product sales (eggs, meat, vegetables, value-added products)
- Agricultural tax credits or subsidies
- Barter value (track estimated market value of goods exchanged)
- Labor savings (value of food produced versus cost to purchase)

Use a spreadsheet or accounting software. Even a simple notebook organized by month works if you are consistent.

### Weather and Environmental Records
- Daily high/low temperatures
- Rainfall (use a rain gauge)
- Frost dates (actual, not average)
- Significant weather events (storms, droughts, heat waves)
- Phenological observations (first bloom, leaf out, harvest dates for wild plants)

A weather station ($50-200 for a basic model) automates temperature and rainfall tracking.

## Tools and Systems

### Paper-Based
- Bound notebook with numbered pages (never lose a page)
- Index card system (one card per crop or animal, filed alphabetically)
- Wall calendar for quick date reference
- Clipboards in key locations (barn, kitchen, garden shed)

### Digital
- Spreadsheets (Google Sheets or Excel) — most versatile, free, and backed up
- Notion, Airtable, or Trello for relational data and visual boards
- FarmOS (open-source farm management software) — comprehensive but steeper learning curve
- Tiller or similar financial aggregator for expense tracking
- Photo documentation (date-stamped photos of garden stages, animal growth, building progress)

### Hybrid Approach (Recommended)
Use a pocket notebook for daily field notes, then transfer to a digital system weekly. This captures data in the moment without exposing electronics to weather, dirt, and livestock. Review and summarize monthly.

## Seasonal Planning Cycle

Build your record keeping around the annual cycle:

- **Winter** (Dec-Feb): Review previous year's records. Plan next year's garden, animal breeding, and projects. Order seeds and supplies.
- **Spring** (Mar-May): Record planting dates, soil amendments, weather observations. Begin animal production records.
- **Summer** (Jun-Aug): Track harvest yields, pest issues, watering schedules. Begin preservation records. Note what is thriving and what is struggling.
- **Fall** (Sep-Nov): Record final harvests, preservation totals, animal processing. Calculate annual financial summary. Note end-of-season observations for next year's planning.

## Tax and Legal Records

Maintain separate records for tax purposes:
- Receipts for all homestead expenses (store in envelopes by month)
- Mileage log for farm-related travel
- Income records from sales
- Depreciation schedules for equipment
