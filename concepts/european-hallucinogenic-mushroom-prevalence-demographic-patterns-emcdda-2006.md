### Young Adult Prevalence (15-24 years)
The highest prevalence estimates among young adults aged 15 to 24 years are found in the Czech Republic, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom. These countries share several characteristics that may explain their elevated rates:

- **Czech Republic**: Has a large indigenous population of Psilocybe bohemica and other psychoactive species. The cultural practice of mushroom foraging (a popular Czech pastime) combined with widespread availability of hallucinogenic species in nature contributes to high use rates. Czech dance party settings also show elevated use compared to other European countries.

- **Germany**: Large population, extensive dance music and club culture, and proximity to the Netherlands (where mushrooms were legally sold until 2007) all contribute. German cities like Berlin have well-established alternative drug scenes where hallucinogenic mushroom use is normalized.

- **Ireland**: High prevalence may be partly attributable to the cultural influence of the UK drug scene and the historical role of Amsterdam as a destination for Irish drug tourists. Ireland subsequently banned mushrooms in 2006 following a highly publicized death.

- **Netherlands**: As the epicenter of legal mushroom retail through smartshops from the late 1990s until the ban in 2007, the Netherlands naturally showed high prevalence. The legal retail infrastructure, including professional packaging and marketing, normalized mushroom use among young Dutch people.

- **United Kingdom**: The UK had one of Europe"s most extensive dance music and club cultures, with significant overlap between ecstasy use and mushroom use. The availability of mushrooms through both online retailers and natural foraging in the British countryside contributed to elevated prevalence.

## School Student Prevalence

### ESPAD Survey Data
The European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs (ESPAD) provides standardized data on drug use among 15-16 year old students across Europe. Among school students, the highest prevalence estimates for hallucinogenic mushroom use appear to be in the Czech Republic, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom.

The ESPAD data is particularly valuable because it allows cross-national comparison using a consistent methodology and age group. The concentration of high prevalence among the same countries that show elevated young adult rates suggests that use typically begins during adolescence, consistent with patterns observed for other recreational drugs.

### Demographic Correlates
School survey data reveals several demographic correlates of hallucinogenic mushroom use among European students:

- **Gender**: As with most recreational drugs, males generally report higher lifetime use rates than females, though the gender gap is smaller for hallucinogenic mushrooms than for substances like cannabis or amphetamines.

- **Urban vs. rural**: Use is generally higher in urban areas, where access to drug markets and exposure to dance music culture are greater. However, in countries with significant indigenous mushroom populations (Czech Republic, Poland, Baltic states), rural use may be elevated due to foraging access.

- **Socioeconomic status**: Some studies suggest that hallucinogenic mushroom use is slightly more common among middle and upper-middle class students, possibly reflecting the "natural product" and "alternative" associations that appeal to more affluent youth.

## Recreational Settings and Use Patterns

### Dance Music and Club Culture
Survey data consistently show that individuals who frequent dance music settings, especially those who use other illegal substances, are much more likely to use hallucinogenic mushrooms than those who do not. The overlap between mushroom use and dance culture is one of the most robust findings in European drug epidemiology.

Studies in France, the Czech Republic, Hungary, and the UK have found that mushroom use in dance settings is typically concentrated among experienced polydrug users rather than casual or first-time users. In the French TREND survey of 1,500 respondents in electronic music settings (Reynaud-Maurupt et al., 2004), hallucinogenic mushroom use was reported by a significant minority of respondents, almost all of whom also used ecstasy, cannabis, and other substances.

### Frequency and Intensity of Use
A key finding across all European data is that patterns of frequent or intensive hallucinogenic mushroom use are rare. Unlike cannabis, which many users consume daily or near-daily, or ecstasy, which some users take weekly during peak party seasons, mushroom use is predominantly episodic. Most users report consuming mushrooms only a few times per year, if that.

This pattern of infrequent use is likely attributable to several factors:
- The intensity of the mushroom experience itself, which most users find sufficiently powerful that they do not seek to repeat it frequently
- The unpredictable potency of mushrooms, which creates uncertainty about dose and effects
- The unpleasant physical effects (nausea, stomach discomfort) associated with consumption
- The lengthy duration of effects (typically 4-6 hours), which limits use to occasions where users have sufficient free time

### Route of Administration
Mushrooms are typically consumed either by chewing raw or dried fruiting bodies, or by brewing them in hot water to make a tea. The EMCDDA noted that unlike ecstasy, which is purchased and taken in a convenient tablet form, mushrooms require a more cumbersome route of administration — chewing unfamiliar-tasting material or drinking bitter tea. This inconvenient route of administration, combined with the unfamiliar and unpleasant taste, likely serves as a barrier to widespread or frequent use.
