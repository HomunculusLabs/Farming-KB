## Yield Theory and Strategies in Permaculture Design

## Defining Yield

Yields can be understood in multiple dimensions:

### Product Yield
The sum of primary and derived products available from, or surplus to, the system. Some products are intrinsic (precede design), while others are created by design. Product yield includes food, fibre, fuel, fodder, fertiliser, and pharmaceuticals—the "five F"s" of permaculture production.

### Energy Yield
The sum of conserved, stored, and generated energy surplus to the system, both intrinsic and created by design. Energy yield includes stored water, biomass, soil humus, thermal mass, and captured solar energy.

### Impalpable Yield
Yields related to health and nutrition, security, and a satisfactory social context and lifestyle. The search for these invisible yields most often drives people to seek good design or to take up life on the land. These are expressed in terms of values and ethics, which govern concepts of needs and set the limits of "enough."

## System Yield Definition

> System yield is the sum total of surplus energy produced by, stored, conserved, reused, or converted by the design. Energy is in surplus once the system itself has available all its needs for growth, reproduction, and maintenance.

This definition is broader than conventional agricultural or economic definitions. Some biologists define yield as a finite upper limit of surplus due to substrate conditions and available energy. Permaculture does not accept this passive approach, as it is inapplicable to active, conscious design or active management using fertilisers, windbreaks, or selected species.

Even more narrowly defined is the yield of agricultural economists, who regard a single product (peaches per hectare) as the yield. Mollison suggests it may be this approach itself which is the true limit to yield.

## True Cost Accounting

A true accounting of yield takes into consideration both **upstream costs** (energy) and **downstream costs** (health). The "product yield" may create problems of pollution and soil mineral loss, and cost more than it can replace. A system that appears productive in narrow terms may be degenerative when full costs are considered.

The very concept of surplus yield supposes either flow through or growth within the system. Coal and rock do not have yield in this sense; they have a finite product. Only life and flow can yield continually, or as long as they persist. Thus the energy stocks of any system are the flows and lives within it.

## The Role of Life in Yield

Living things, including people, are the only effective intervening systems to capture resources on this planet and to produce a yield. It is the sum and capacity of life forms which decide total system yield and surplus. Technology depends on the continuation of life, not the opposite.

Houses, farms, and cities have long been designed as energy-demanding, despite known strategies and techniques that could make them energy-producing. This condition is deliberately maintained by utilities, bureaucracies, and governments composed of those dependent on the consumption and sale of energy resources.

## Yield-Creating Strategies

Permaculture offers abundant strategies organized into broad categories:

### Foundational Strategies
- **Niche creation**: Providing a critical resource or habitat space
- **Soil rehabilitation**: Building the living substrate that underpins all production
- **Water diversion and recycling**: Maximizing the utility of water passing through the system
- **Structure-landscape integration**: Designing buildings and infrastructure as productive elements

### Biological Strategies
- **Low-maintenance cultivar selection**: Choosing species adapted to site conditions
- **Investigation of alternative species**: Exploring underutilized plants and animals for usable yields
- **Key nutrient supply**: Biological waste recycling through mulch, manure, and compost
- **Beneficial guilds**: Assembling cooperative assemblies of plants and animals

### Pattern and Spatial Strategies
- **Annidation**: "Nesting" or stacking one thing within another—like a bowl in a bowl, or a vine in a tree
- **Tessellation**: Forming or arranging a mosaic of parts that fit together without gaps
- **Edge and harmonics**: Using innovative spatial geometry to maximize productive edge
- **Material routing**: Directing materials or energy to next best use
- **Zone, sector, slope, orientation**: Site-specific placement strategies

### Sequential Strategies
- **Sequential annidation**: Interplanting and intercropping in time as well as space
- **Increased cyclic frequency**: More rapid cycling of nutrients and energy
- **Tessellation of cycles and successions**: Browsing sequences, rotational grazing patterns
- **Appropriate technology**: Using rehabilitative and energy-conserving tools

### Conservation Strategies
- **Resource routing**: Directing resources to next best use at every stage
- **High-level recycling**: Returning materials to their highest possible use before they degrade

### Structural Strategies
- **Durable systems**: Creating long-lasting, low-maintenance physical infrastructure
- **Energy-efficient structures**: Designing buildings that conserve and generate energy
- **Food storage**: Safe storage of food products through preservation and appropriate technology
- **No-tillage cropping**: Minimizing soil disturbance to preserve soil structure and biology

### Cultural Strategies
- **Removing barriers**: Breaking down cultural resistance to useful practices
- **Making unusual resources acceptable**: Introducing unfamiliar but valuable yields
- **Expanding choices**: Broadening the range of considered options

### Administrative Strategies
- **Removing impediments**: Addressing socio-legal barriers to resource use
- **Effective structures**: Creating organizations that aid resource management
- **Full cost accounting**: Costing and adjusting systems for all energy inputs and outputs
- **Cooperation**: Pooling resources, sharing equipment, forming cooperatives
- **Financial recycling**: Keeping money circulating within the local community
- **Positive replacement**: Actively removing and replacing impeding systems

## Yield as a Measure of Design Quality

> Yield is not a fixed sum in any design system. It is the measure of the comprehension, understanding, and ability of the designers and managers of that design.

Defined in this way, yield has no known limits. Designers cannot know all ways to conserve, store, and save energy, nor can they fail to improve any system they build and observe. There is always room for another plant, another cycle, another route, another arrangement, another technique or structure. Designers can continually shrink the area needed to survive. The critical yield strategy is in governing their own appetites.

## How Yield Can Be Decreased

Just as yield can be increased, it can be decreased. The perverse aims of some politicians, developers, and even religious dogmatists limit yield by disallowing certain products. Just as neighbours may refuse the snail and eat the lettuce, refuse the blackbird and eat the strawberry, people may only "allow" certain types of toilets or certain plants in gardens. Thus people are the main impediment to using their potential yields.
