## Farris Family Poisoning — Shenandoah, Pennsylvania, 1885

## Overview

The Farris family poisoning of August 1885 in Shenandoah, Pennsylvania, is a
landmark event in medical toxicology. Reported by Dr. S.E. Shadle, it constitutes
the **first documented case in human medicine** in which atropine was used
successfully as an antidote against amanitine (amanitin) poisoning. Five patients
consumed misidentified wild mushrooms, resulting in two deaths and three
recoveries. Shadle's report provided critical early evidence that atropine could
counteract the lethal effects of [[amanita-toxins-and-mushroom-poisoning]].

## Historical Context

In the late [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]], [[emcdda-wild-mushroom-foraging-misidentification-hazards]] was common in rural
Pennsylvania mining communities. Toxicological knowledge was still in its infancy;
Amanita species were poorly understood, and no reliable antidotal therapy existed.
The Shenandoah case illustrates both the danger of misidentifying wild fungi and
the evolving capacity of physicians to respond to novel poisoning events.

## The Five Patients

- **Mr. F., 29** — Coal miner, described as living an "intemperate life" (regular
  alcohol use), which may have influenced his recovery.
- **Mrs. F., 22** — Mr. F.'s wife, delicate constitution with neurotic tendencies.
  One of the two patients who died.
- **Mrs. R., 45** — Mrs. F.'s mother-in-law, small and nervo-bilious. Survived.
- **Thomas R., 13** — Mrs. F.'s brother-in-law, a well-developed youth. Despite
  his robustness, he was the first to die.
- **Mrs. B., 40** — A neighbor, tall and spare. Survived.

## The Incident: Sunday, August 30, 1885

Mr. F. and Thomas R. went foraging in nearby chestnut-dominated woods and gathered
a large quantity of "beautiful toadstools." Mrs. F. prepared approximately three
pints, stewing them in milk with butter, pepper, and salt. The group ate heartily
at supper. Critically, no discomfort was felt until daybreak Monday — a deceptive
latency period characteristic of [[bloomfield-amatoxin-poisoning-mechanism]].

## Mushroom Identification

The toadstools were identified as a mixed collection of two harmless Agaric species
and one **Amanita vernus** containing lethal amanitine toxin. This mixture of
[[classification-of-edible-and-poisonous-mushroom-families]] species in a single gathering is a classic and dangerous
pattern in wild mushroom poisonings.

## Timeline and Symptom Progression

Shadle described the presentation as "cholera morbus in its most aggravated form,"
unfolding in distinct phases:

### Phase 1 — Initial Onset (0–12 hours)
A significant latent period preceded all symptoms:
- Foul breath, coated tongue, stomach pain, persistent nausea
- Sickening sensation in the epigastrium

### Phase 2 — Gastrointestinal Crisis (~12 hours)

- Intense vomiting in four patients; violent retching in Mrs. R.
- Profuse fluid loss driving early dehydration

### Phase 3 — Advanced Gastrointestinal Distress (~30 hours)

- Severe ongoing gastrointestinal irritation
- Relaxed bowels with insufferable tenesmus
- Continued fluid depletion

### Phase 4 — Systemic Collapse

- Extreme prostration, stupor, cold extremities, complete indifference
- Face shrunken/wrinkled, eyes sunken, skin dusky, body dry and cold
- Pulse 120–140 bpm, soft and compressible, intermittent; temperature to 104°F
- Mild delirium (Mrs. F.); peculiar blindness sensations despite reactive pupils

### Phase 5 — Terminal Decline

- Thomas R. progressed to deep coma; died **56 hours** after ingestion
- Mrs. F. entered coma-vigil ~12 hours before death; died **63 hours** after
  ingestion
- Both deaths from profound asthenia — catastrophic cardiovascular collapse

## Treatment Regimen

### Supportive and Symptomatic Measures

- **Cracked ice** for thirst and burning; **emetics and mild purges** to evacuate toxin
- **Bismuth subnitrate, creosote, acacia mucilage, peppermint water** every 1–2 hours
- **Morphine sulfate** (1/8 grain) hypodermically for abdominal pain
- **Alcoholic stimulants** and **tincture of digitalis** orally and subcutaneously
- **Hot bricks and bottles** externally for peripheral circulation

### The Breakthrough: Atropine Sulfate

The critical intervention was subcutaneous injection of **sulphate of atropia**
(atropine sulfate) in doses of 1/60 to 1/50 grain. Shadle reported each injection
was "invariably followed by perceptible improvement" — heart's action strengthened,
pulse returned, respiration increased. This was the first documented successful
use of atropine as an antidote in human [[amanitine-poisoning-and-absorption-mechanisms]].

## Outcomes

| Patient   | Age | Outcome       | Time to Death |
|-----------|-----|---------------|---------------|
| Thomas R. | 13  | **Died**      | 56 hours      |
| Mrs. F.   | 22  | **Died**      | 63 hours      |
| Mr. F.    | 29  | **Survived**  | —             |
| Mrs. R.   | 45  | **Survived**  | —             |
| Mrs. B.   | 40  | **Survived**  | —             |

The overall recovery rate was **60%**. The two fatalities were the youngest and most
physically vulnerable patients, though delayed atropine administration may also
have contributed.

## Shadle's Clinical Conclusions

1. **Slow manifestation.** Amanitine poisoning has a deceptive latency period leading
   to false reassurance and delayed treatment.
2. **Death by asthenia.** The toxin destroys life through progressive weakness and
   cardiovascular collapse rather than acute organ failure.
3. **Atropine as antidote.** Atropine sulfate is effective and should be "pushed
   heroically from earliest inception" — administered aggressively without delay.

## Historical and Medical Significance

- **First documented human use of atropine for amanitine poisoning.** While atropine
  had been known since the 1830s, its application against Amanita toxins in human
  patients was unprecedented.
- **Clinical characterization of amatoxin syndrome.** Shadle's description of the
  latent period, gastrointestinal phase, and systemic collapse matches the modern
  understanding of the classic triphasic pattern.
- **Influence on practice.** Shadle's recommendation to administer atropine
  "heroically" presaged aggressive antidotal protocols, even as modern treatment
  shifted toward silibinin, penicillin G, and supportive liver care.
- **Educational value.** The case remains a cautionary example of the dangers of
  wild mushroom foraging and the difficulty of distinguishing toxic from edible
  species in mixed collections.
