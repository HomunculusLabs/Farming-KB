# Strain Selection, Sectoring, and Stock Culture Preservation

## Mycelial Growth Forms on Agar
As mycelium grows on nutrient agar, it displays remarkable
diversity of forms. Some mycelia are fairly uniform; others
are polymorphous at first and then develop into homogeneous-
looking mycelia. This is the nature of [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]],
to constantly change and evolve. When a mycelium grows from
a single inoculation site and several divergent types appear,
it is said to be "sectoring."

## Two Major Mycelial Types

### Rhizomorphic (Strandy) Mycelium
Characterized by thick, rope-like strands of interwoven
hyphae. Under a microscope, hyphae are larger and branch
less frequently. Rhizomorphic mycelia run faster, form more
primordia, and in the final analysis yield more mushrooms
than cottony mycelia. Primordia frequently form along
generatively oriented rhizomorphs.

### Tomentose (Cottony) Mycelium
Characterized by fluffy, aerial growth with fine branching.
Under a microscope, hyphae are smaller and branch more
frequently. Cottony mycelia are inferior performers. There
is an apparent correlation between cottony mycelia on agar
and later occurrence of "stroma," a dense mat-like growth
on casing that rarely produces mushrooms.

### Intermediate Linear Type
Grows longitudinally radial but lacks the twisted strands
of interwoven hyphae characterizing the rhizomorphic kind.
Linear mycelium can produce abundant primordia but this
usually occurs soon after it forms rhizomorphs.

## Causes of Sectoring
Sectors are common in [[cultivator-contaminants-of-mushroom-culture]]. Though little is
known about their cause, genetics, nutrition, and age of
the mycelium play important roles. According to Stoller
(1962), fluffy sectors are encouraged by broken and
exploded kernels that increase starch availability in
spawn media. Working with [[agaricus-brunnescens]], Stoller
noted that while mycelial growth is faster at high pH
(7.5) than slightly acidic pH (6.5), sectoring is more
frequent at higher pH.

## Stock Culture Preservation Methods

Once a pure strain is created and isolated, saving it as a
"stock culture" or "slant" is wise. Stock cultures are media-
filled glass test tubes sterilized and inoculated with mushroom
mycelium. A suitable size is 20mm x 100mm with a screw cap.
Every experienced cultivator maintains a "species bank"
collection.

### Slant Preparation
1. Mix [[growing-gourmet-agar-media-formulas-preparation]], fill
   test tubes one third full
2. Plug with cotton or screw on cap; cover with foil
3. Sterilize in [[pressure-cooker-sterilisation-fungal-substrate]] 30 minutes at 15 psi
4. Allow to cool to room temperature before opening
5. Gently shake to distribute media; lay at 15-30 degree
   angle to solidify

### Preservation Methods
- Refrigeration at 35-38 F for short-term storage (months to
  a few years)
- Immersion in liquid nitrogen (expensive but long-term)
- Inoculation of sterilized
  [[falconer-horse-manure-varieties]]/straw compost kept at
  36-38 F
- Inoculation of sawdust/bran media for wood-loving species

Multiple methods should be used simultaneously to safeguard
valuable strains. Regularly checking stock cultures for
viability and contamination ensures the species bank remains
a reliable genetic resource for future cultivation.

## Isolation Techniques
Strain isolation begins with obtaining a clean tissue sample
from a vigorous fruiting body. The interior of the mushroom
cap or stem is sterile and can be excised with a flame-
sterilized scalpel and transferred to agar media. Single
spore isolation provides genetically uniform strains by
germinating individual spores and selecting the most
vigorous colonies. Multispore germination on agar produces
a population of genetically diverse individuals, from which
the best-performing sectors can be isolated. Each approach
has advantages: tissue culture preserves the exact genetics
of a known high-performing mushroom, while spore isolation
allows selection of novel genetic combinations.

## Media Selection for Strain Evaluation
The choice of agar media significantly affects mycelial
morphology and the reliability of strain selection. Malt
extract agar (MEA) at 2 percent concentration is the most
widely used medium for general mycelial cultivation and
observation. Potato dextrose agar (PDA) supports vigorous
growth for many species but can mask sectoring due to its
rich nutrient profile. Minimal media such as yeast malt
agar (YMA) at reduced concentrations tend to reveal
differences between strains more clearly by making
[[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]] a limiting factor. Adding activated
charcoal to agar can absorb metabolic inhibitors and
improve mycelial recovery from stored cultures.

## Subculture Frequency and Maintenance
Stock cultures should be transferred to fresh media
periodically to maintain viability. Most mushroom species
retain vigor for 6 to 12 months on agar slants at
refrigeration temperatures. Transferring too frequently
increases the risk of contamination and genetic drift
through accumulated mutations. Transferring too
infrequently allows cultures to senesce and lose
viability. Best practice is to maintain two sets of
slants for each strain: one working set that is
regularly subcultured for active use, and one master
set that is kept untouched as a long-term backup.
Only the working set is used for routine inoculation.

## Contamination Monitoring
All stock cultures should be regularly inspected for
contamination. Bacterial contamination appears as slimy
or cloudy growth, often with an unpleasant odor. Mold
contamination from other fungal species is usually
obvious due to different colors, growth rates, or
morphologies. Hidden contaminants can lurk beneath or
behind the mushroom mycelium. Holding suspect cultures
at room temperature for several days often reveals
slow-growing contaminants that remain dormant at
refrigeration temperatures. [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] of
colony margins can detect early bacterial contamination
before it becomes visible to the naked eye.
