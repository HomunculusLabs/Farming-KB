# Mycoremediation of Environmental Contaminants

Mycoremediation is the use of fungi to degrade, sequester, or otherwise
neutralize environmental pollutants. Among fungi, [[pleurotus-species-guide]] species and
other white-rot basidiomycetes are particularly effective agents due to their
powerful extracellular enzyme systems capable of breaking down lignin — one of
the most recalcitrant organic polymers in nature. These same enzymes also
degrade a wide range of anthropogenic pollutants.

## Mechanisms of Mycoremediation

### Enzymatic Degradation

White-rot fungi produce three major classes of lignin-modifying enzymes:
- **Laccases** (EC 1.10.3.2) — multicopper oxidases that oxidize phenolic
  compounds using molecular oxygen as the electron acceptor. They have broad
  substrate specificity and can degrade phenols, aromatic amines, and
  polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons.
- **Manganese peroxidases (MnP)** (EC 1.11.1.13) — heme-containing enzymes
  that oxidize Mn²⁺ to Mn³⁺, creating a diffusible oxidant that can attack
  a wide range of organic compounds including lignin and many xenobiotics.
- **Lignin peroxidases (LiP)** (EC 1.11.1.14) — powerful oxidases that can
  directly oxidize non-phenolic aromatic compounds with high redox potentials.

These enzymes operate via a non-specific, radical-based mechanism. Rather than
binding to a specific substrate the way many enzymes do, they generate highly
reactive free radicals that indiscriminately attack nearby organic molecules.
This "shotgun" approach is what makes white-rot fungi such broad-spectrum
degraders.

### Biosorption

Fungal mycelium can bind and concentrate heavy metals and certain organic
pollutants through biosorption — adsorption to cell wall components including
chitin, glucans, and proteins. The cell wall provides abundant binding sites
through functional groups like carboxyl, amino, hydroxyl, and phosphate groups.
Biosorption is particularly effective for:
- Heavy metals: lead, cadmium, mercury, copper, zinc, chromium
- Metalloids: arsenic
- Radionuclides: uranium, cesium, strontium

Biosorbed metals can be recovered by desorption, or the metal-loaded biomass
can be safely disposed of or processed for metal recovery.

### Bioaccumulation

Unlike biosorption (a passive surface process), bioaccumulation involves active
uptake and intracellular concentration of contaminants. Some fungi actively
transport and concentrate metals, sometimes at concentrations orders of
magnitude above ambient levels. This can be useful for phytoextraction but
raises food safety concerns if fruiting bodies are consumed from contaminated
sites.

### Mycofiltration

Mycofiltration uses dense mats of fungal mycelium as biological filters to
capture and degrade contaminants from water. Techniques include:
- **Mycelial mats** placed in drainage paths to intercept agricultural runoff
- **Spent mushroom substrate** as biofilter media for treating wastewater
- **Constructed mycofiltration beds** for treating stormwater, greywater, or
  industrial effluent

Pleurotus species are particularly effective in mycofiltration due to their
dense, rhizomorphic mycelial networks and aggressive growth habits.

## Target Contaminants

### Petroleum Hydrocarbons

Petroleum contamination — including crude oil, diesel, gasoline, and motor oil
— is one of the most common and widely studied targets for mycoremediation.
P. ostreatus has demonstrated the ability to degrade 70-90% of total petroleum
hydrocarbons in contaminated soil within 8-12 weeks under optimized conditions.
The fungal mycelium colonizes the soil, secreting enzymes that break
hydrocarbon chains into smaller, more bioavailable molecules that can be
further metabolized by soil bacteria in a synergistic relationship.

### Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs)

PAHs are persistent organic pollutants produced by incomplete combustion of
organic matter. They are carcinogenic, mutagenic, and resistant to degradation.
High-molecular-weight PAHs (4-6 rings) are particularly recalcitrant to
conventional remediation. White-rot fungi can oxidize these compounds through
their ligninolytic enzyme systems. P. ostreatus has been shown to degrade
benzo[a]pyrene, pyrene, and phenanthrene in both soil and liquid culture.

### Pesticides and Herbicides

Organochlorine pesticides (DDT, lindane, endosulfan, chlordane) are
persistent in the environment due to their chemical stability and
lipophilicity. White-rot fungi can dechlorinate and oxidize these compounds.
Pleurotus species have shown significant degradation of DDT and its
metabolites, converting them to less toxic, more water-soluble products.

### Dyes and Textile Effluents

The textile industry generates large volumes of wastewater containing synthetic
dyes, many of which are toxic and resistant to conventional treatment.
Laccases from Pleurotus species can decolorize azo, anthraquinone, and
triarylmethane dyes effectively. This application has been extensively studied
and is among the closest to commercial deployment of mycoremediation
technology.

### Pharmaceuticals and Personal Care Products

Emerging contaminants including antibiotics, hormones, and other
pharmaceuticals are increasingly detected in waterways. Pleurotus species can
degrade several pharmaceutical compounds including diclofenac, carbamazepine,
and sulfonamide antibiotics through enzymatic oxidation.

## Factors Affecting Efficiency

### Environmental Conditions

- **Temperature**: Optimal ranges vary by species (typically 20-30°C)
- **pH**: Most white-rot fungi prefer slightly acidic conditions (pH 4-6)
- **Moisture**: Adequate moisture is essential; waterlogging limits oxygen
- **Nutrients**: Nitrogen and carbon affect growth and enzyme production

### Biological Factors

- **Strain selection**: Significant variation in degradative capability
- **Pre-adaptation**: Pre-exposed strains often show enhanced degradation
- **Microbial community**: Interactions can be synergistic or competitive
