# Sterile Cultivation Technique

Sterile cultivation technique is the foundational practice in mushroom growing that
prevents contamination by unwanted microorganisms. It encompasses every procedure
where exposed, uncolonized substrate or live culture is handled, from inoculation
through fruiting initiation. Mastery of sterile technique is what separates
successful cultivators from those who lose the majority of their work to molds
and bacteria.

## Why Sterility Matters

Mushroom mycelium grows relatively slowly compared to the bacteria and mold spores
present in virtually every environment. When you expose a nutrient-rich substrate,
you are creating an ideal breeding ground for competitors. A single bacterial cell
or mold spore landing on grain spawn can outcompete and overwhelm mushroom mycelium
within days. The goal of sterile technique is not to create a perfectly sterile
environment, which is practically impossible outside a laboratory, but to reduce
the number of contaminant organisms to a level where the mushroom mycelium can
establish itself and defend the substrate before invaders can take hold.

## The Work Area

A dedicated clean workspace is the first requirement. Many cultivators work inside
a still air box (SAB), which is a simple transparent container with arm holes.
The SAB works not by filtering air but by creating a still-air environment where
contaminant spores settle out of suspension rather than drifting onto your work.
More advanced setups use laminar flow hoods (LFH), which push HEPA-filtered air
across the work surface in a smooth, unidirectional stream, physically carrying
contaminants away from the work area.

Before any work session, the interior of the SAB or the surface of the LFH should
be wiped down with 70% isopropyl alcohol. All tools, jars, bags, and containers
placed inside should also be cleaned. Many cultivators spray the interior walls of
the SAB with soapy water to help capture any remaining airborne particles. The
area outside the SAB should be as clean as possible, and work should be done
during times of low air disturbance.

## Personal Preparation

Cultivators should shower and put on clean clothes before sterile work. Hair
should be tied back or covered. A surgical mask reduces the number of bacteria
expelled in breath, and nitrile gloves prevent contamination from skin. Washing
hands and forearms with antibacterial soap before gloving up is standard
practice. Avoid working near open windows, fans, or HVAC vents that create air
currents. Minimize talking and movement over the open work area, as both generate
airborne particles.

## Tool Sterilization

Metal tools such as inoculation loops, scalpels, and tweezers must be sterilized
before each use. The most common method is flame sterilization, where the tool
is heated in a butane or alcohol flame until it glows red-hot, then allowed to
cool briefly before contacting the culture. For tools that cannot withstand flame
heat, chemical sterilization with 70% isopropyl alcohol or a 10% bleach solution
is used, though chemical methods are less reliable than heat.

Between each individual transfer or inoculation, tools must be re-sterilized.
This is one of the most common points of failure. A scalpel used to cut one
transfer wedge and then used again without re-flaming will carry organisms from
the first transfer to the second. Develop the habit of flaming before every
single cut.

## Jar and Container Preparation

Glass jars used for grain spawn or liquid culture should be cleaned thoroughly
and then sterilized in a pressure canner at 15 PSI for 90 to 120 minutes. The
standard recommendation is 90 minutes for quart jars and up to 120 minutes for
larger volumes. The lids should be fitted with a gas exchange filter such as
polyfill, Tyvek, or a self-healing injection port. These filters allow gases to
move in and out while blocking contaminant spores.

Autoclave tape applied to the lids before sterilization provides a visual
indicator that proper temperature and pressure were achieved. After the cycle,
jars should be allowed to cool slowly inside the canner to prevent sudden
pressure changes that could draw unfiltered air through the filters.

## Inoculation Procedure

Inoculation is the moment of highest risk. The culture or spore syringe, the
recipient substrate, and every tool involved must be clean and sterile. Work
methodically and deliberately. Flame the needle of a spore syringe until it
glows, then allow it to cool for 15 to 20 seconds before injecting. Inject
through a self-healing port if available, or through a filter patch taped over
the injection site. For agar-to-grain transfers, flame the scalpel between each
wedge cut and each jar inoculation.

After inoculation, shake the grain jars to distribute the inoculum throughout
the grain. This creates multiple points of colonization and speeds up the
process. Do not shake again until colonization is roughly 20 to 30 percent
visible, as premature shaking can damage the fragile mycelial network.

## Common Sources of Contamination

The most frequent contamination vectors are unsterilized tools, contaminated
inoculant, poor-quality spore syringes, and airborne spores entering during
jar opening. Bacterial contamination often presents as a sour or rotten smell,
wet spots on grain, or a slimy texture. Mold contamination appears as colored
growth, most commonly green (Trichoderma), black (Aspergillus), or pink. If any
jar shows signs of contamination, it should be removed and disposed of
immediately to prevent spore release into the growing area.

## Contamination Rates and Expectations

Even experienced cultivators with excellent technique experience occasional
contamination. A rate below 5 percent is considered excellent, while beginners
should expect 20 to 50 percent failure rates as they develop their skills. Every
contamination event is a learning opportunity. Identifying where the breach
occurred and adjusting technique accordingly is how cultivators improve over
time. Patience, consistency, and meticulous attention to detail are the hallmarks
of good sterile technique.
