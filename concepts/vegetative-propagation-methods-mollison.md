# Vegetative Propagation Methods (Mollison)

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Bill Mollison, Sections 10-12 (pp. 284-430)

## Overview

Vegetative propagation is essential in permaculture for multiplying desired plants without relying on seed. Many of the most useful tree crops, berries, and perennial plants are propagated by cuttings, layers, division, or grafting rather than by seed, especially when specific cultivar characteristics must be preserved.

## Propagation from Cuttings

### Willow Cuttings and Rooting Hormones
Willows are among the easiest plants to propagate from cuttings. Willows are pollarded for propagation cuttings at nurseries. "Willow water" (fresh chips soaked in water) provides gibberellic acid for promoting root growth in the striking of cuttings. This natural rooting hormone solution can be used to improve strike rates for a wide range of difficult-to-root species.

### Willow Coppice for Multiple Products
Willow coppice crops serve multiple functions. They can be used for basketry, medicinals, forage, stickwood for radiant (mass) heaters, active charcoal filters, artist's charcoal, and the production of rooting hormone via willow water.

## Nursery Establishment

### The Essential Nursery
Every plantation needs a mulched, shaded nursery, no matter how modest. Shade is most cheaply provided by light-foliaged legumes at wide spacing (e.g. Acacia, Albizia). A plant nursery to serve gardens and forest is one of the first priorities in any permaculture development.

### Seed and Vegetative Propagule Supply
Combined plant nurseries and new species collections are essential for the provision of seed, bud, and scion materials, vegetative propagules, and grafted, layered, or tissue-cultured plants. Pioneer or innovative farmers are now often providers of plant materials to other people requiring sufficient plant materials to commence commercial operations.

### Nursery for New Species
In areas with no plantation history, it is perhaps wise to build up a small arboretum of many varieties, and select a range of cultivars suited to the end-product aims. In every country, the cooperation of local agricultural authorities, and their assistance with varietal selection will be needed.

## Division

### Grass and Perennial Division
Many perennials and grasses are propagated by divisions. Pampas grass, for instance, is both fast shelter for lambing ewes, and a preferred forage for most livestock; it is propagated by divisions and combines well with tagasaste strips.

### Bulb and Corm Propagation
Bulbs and corms are important storage organs that can be divided and replanted. Spring bulbs such as Narcissus and hyacinth are used in orchard guilds, flowering and dying back by early summer to create grass-free zones below trees.

### Tuber and Root Cuttings
Tubers such as potatoes, jerusalem artichoke (sunroot), and oca are planted from tuber divisions. Root cuttings of comfrey, horseradish, and other deep-rooted perennials can be taken and planted to establish new stands.

## Layering

### Natural Layering
Many plants naturally layer where branches touch the ground. Blackberry and related bramble fruits spread both by tip-layering and by seed. These natural processes can be encouraged by pinning branches to the ground.

### Grafted and Layered Plants
For orchard establishment, both grafted and layered plants are used. Apple, fig, quince, pear, and plum trees can be established from ungrafted whips planted in protective thickets, later selected for superior performers to graft.

## Grafting

### Multi-Graft Trees
Multi-graft trees allow several varieties to be grown on one rootstock, extending the harvest season and providing insurance against variety-specific failures. Trees can be multi-grafted and plants seedlings selected for later managed grafts.

### Bud and Scion Wood
The supply of bud and scion materials is a critical nursery function. Scion wood must be collected from disease-free, productive parent trees during the dormant season and stored properly until grafting time.

## Tissue Culture

### Meristem Culture
Almost all coconuts must be seed-grown, but even in seed-grown crop, meristem and single-cell mutations are very high. Tissue culture is used for mass propagation of selected cultivars in some species, though natural variation means that culling and selection will always be necessary.

## Transplanting and Hardening Off

### Reducing Transplant Shock
Transplant shock from nursery stock can be reduced when soil is properly prepared and seedlings are hardened off before planting. Mycorrhizal inoculants are used to great effect in nursery plants, row crops, new tree plantings in deserts, and in transplanting.

### Seedling Trays and Cloches
For cool areas, a small glasshouse for seedling trays, and a series of cloches for early transplants are invaluable. Even potatoes can be sprouted on old hessian bags indoors, or in any open shadehouse, and transplanted complete with leaves and roots by cutting up the bags into squares.

## Natural Propagation by Animals

Many plants came to rely on vegetative rather than seed propagation in rodent-rich meadows. Burrowing animals such as gophers carry bulbs and root cuttings into their tunnels, where they are sometimes forgotten and sprout later. Gophers industriously carry roots and bulbs to secret stores and sometimes forget their hoards, so that sunroot, gladioli, daffodils and hyacinths spring up in unexpected places above ground.

Foxes eat fruits, and defecate on gopher mounds, which are the dug-over areas for new trees. Even the regurgita of owls sprout meadow seed caught in the fur of their victims, while the neglected underground stores of their prey species are left uneaten, to sprout later.

## Planting Techniques

### Direct Planting from Pots
Plants can be purchased from a nursery or grown in pots on the site. Proper potting mixes, adequate drainage, and careful handling during transplanting all contribute to success rates.

### Dibble Planting
For transplanting seedlings into compost-rich soils, a dibble (a pointed stick) is used to create holes for the seedling roots, reducing root disturbance.
