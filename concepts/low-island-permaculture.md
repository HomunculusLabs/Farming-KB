## Low Island Permaculture

Low islands and atolls present some of the most challenging environments for
[[permaculture-design]]. Composed of coral, ground shell, and sand, they are built by the sea and
sustained by biomass from sea birds and migratory animals. Successful design
demands understanding of water table dynamics, soil chemistry, wind, and erosion.

## Atoll Water Table Dynamics

On low islands, fresh water sits as a **lens floating on salt water**. The top
of this freshwater table is typically only three to five feet below the surface,
with its bottom another four to five feet below. Given the island's area, the
available gallons of fresh water can be calculated. This lens is the island's
sole freshwater resource — if drawn down and replaced by salt water, plants die
and the island becomes uninhabitable. The water table must not be polluted;
even a few septic tanks can contaminate the entire lens. [[greywater-management-and-dry-toilets]] are an
absolute necessity.

## Platen Formation and Breaking

Phosphates and calcareous sands combine to form a concrete-like layer of
**calcium triphosphate** ("platen") about 18 inches below the surface, ranging
from nine inches to three feet thick. Rain follows the platen laterally and
runs off rather than percolating, restricting agriculture to shallow-rooted
species. Breaking the platen is the **first essential act** of island
[[permaculture]]: shovel away surface sand, smash through to brackish groundwater,
fill the hole with humus, and plant a tree — preferably coconut. The tree's
roots keep the platen cracked and permeable, establishing a local groundwater
system. Within twenty years, trees reach full production.

## Ring Garden Design

The **ring garden** exploits circle geometry for maximum growing efficiency.
Sand is heaped around the edge of a broken-platen circle, the center filled
with mulch, and vegetables planted with a single drip point. Because drip water
extends laterally about two to two and a half feet, a four-to-five foot ring
can contain roughly twelve running feet of row crop. The inner edge grows
cabbages, peas, and beans; cucumbers and beans climb reinforcing wire; arid
plants occupy the outer edge. The [[design-principle-integration]]: minimize pathway area and
maximize linear crop feet around the fewest drip points.

## Wick-Watering Systems

Sprinkler systems are **disastrous** on arid coral atolls — water is lost to
hot wind and surface evaporation, and sprinkling accelerates platen formation.
Instead, a **wick-watering system** uses a large wick of fibrous organic
material with a slow drip at center, keeping the area saturated as water
reaches roots through [[capillary-action-in-soils]]. This also mimics natural wicking when
a garden hole touches the water table.

## Gley Pond Construction

Small **gley ponds** lined with fast-fermenting leaves (papaw, grapes, banana)
and pumped with ground well water double available water in a profile.
Coconut logs line banks to prevent sandy collapse. Ponds can also self-seal:
a dry hole near the water table is planted, and rolled-down crop material
gradually creates a gley as it fills. Aquaculture should complement existing
seafood — prawns, ducks feeding prawns through the algae cycle, and
high-nutrient tropical water crops are excellent choices.

## Tree Establishment for Platen Management

Trees are the primary long-term platen [[savory-fire-as-management-tool]]. Each tree cracks the
platen downward from its trunk and around its circumference, creating permeable
zones for rain infiltration. The process is iterative: establish one tree, then
use it as anchor for more. Coconut palms are preferred pioneers. Large holes
filled with canoe-loads of mulch jump-start the self-sustaining process.

## Coastal Defense Plant Systems

Coastal vegetation is the island's primary erosion defense. Waves never cease
their attack, and islands can be entirely lost if shoreline vegetation is
removed. Vegetation alone can build an island up to about six feet elevation.

### Front-Line Oceanic Species

Three genera stand directly in the surf zone, growing to about fifteen feet:
**Pemphis**, **Tournefortia**, and **Scaevola**. Behind them, tall plants —
palms where climate permits — form a second line. Date palms thrive on
oceanic coastlines despite being considered oasis species.

### Needle-Leaved Front Line

A second set of front-line species — valued for mulch over yield — includes
**Casuarina**, **Tamarix**, **Monterey pine**, **[[norfolk-island-pine]]**, and
**Monterey cypress**. Most vegetables tolerate salt well, but chestnut, walnut,
almond, peach, apricot, apple, and pistachio cannot and need shelter.

## Island Windbreaks and Erosion Prevention

All oceanic islands experience two wind sets (typically NE-SW or NW-SE) with
calm periods. Atolls need windbreaks on exposed outer areas. The first tree
line provides windbreak with little yield; the second begins producing; by the
third line (within fifty feet), normal yields are achieved.

Removing even a strip of shoreline vegetation lets waves run inland in
streamlined wind with greater force. Vegetation lifting wind upward makes
waves break further out. **Airstrips** are particularly dangerous — removing
trees invites the sea to cut the strip apart. Airstrips should be contained
within vegetation, angled to winds, with trees along their length and ends.

## Nitrogen Cycling on Islands

Nitrogen turnover requires deliberate management. Many island legumes exist as
vines, ground covers, and trees. The **Moringa** ([[moringa-oleifera-horseradish-tree-permaculture]]) is
especially valuable: leaves, flowers, and pods are all edible, it serves as a
vegetable hedge, and grows 20-30 feet. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria may be absent
on sterile islands, so a handful of soil should be imported with nearly every
introduced plant. Some tree species also require native soil associates to
germinate from seed.

## Mangrove Ecosystem Importance

Mangroves are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth:

- **Highest biomass turnover** of any system — their leaf mulch feeds shrimps,
  anthropoids, diatoms, and the entire offshore food chain
- **Foundation of shrimp fisheries**: most offshore species depend on mangrove
  nursery habitat
- **Key genera**: Sonnerata, Rhizopodia, Aegiceras, Nipa palms, Avicenna (to latitude 40°)
- **Useful products**: Aegicera produces superb tropical honey; mangrove palms
  yield fruits, honey, and durable timber
- **Island extension**: mangroves established along log-and-sand barriers in
  atoll lagoons carry the island outward while creating fish-trap enclosures

Mangrove destruction through filling, draining, and clearing directly
correlates with declining fish populations worldwide.

## Island Settlement Layout

Settlement should be on the **periphery** of the island, drawing water from
central areas to keep waste away from the freshwater lens. Septic tanks, if
necessary, go at the outer perimeter where flow is outward. Excessive water
use draws salt to the edges (signalled by tree death); overpopulation pushes
pollutants to the intertidal zone, ruining beaches. A large central village
is "a disastrous technique."

## Key Technologies Summary

1. **Grow-hole technology**: pits with broken platen and humus for tree establishment
2. **Wick-watering gardening**: fibrous organic wicks with [[drip-irrigation]]
3. **Gley technology**: fermenting leaf-lined ponds for water and aquaculture
4. **Roof catchment**: rainwater from all small roofs, stored above ground
5. **Peripheral settlement**: housing at island edges protecting the freshwater lens
