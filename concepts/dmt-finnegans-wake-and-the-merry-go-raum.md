# DMT, Finnegans Wake, and the Merry Go Raum

## Joyce as Cartographer of Hyperspace

Terence McKenna's 1983 Esalen lecture on tryptamine hallucinogens and consciousness contains
a striking and often-overlooked literary reference. Describing the characteristic space
encountered at the peak of a DMT experience, McKenna borrows from James Joyce's *Finnegans
Wake* to name what he finds there. He describes arriving, approximately thirty seconds after
smoking DMT, in "a place that defies description, a space that has a feeling of being
underground, or somehow insulated and domed." He then identifies this space with Joyce's
concept of the "merry go raum," from the German *Raum* meaning "space." In *Finnegans Wake*,
Joyce created a language designed to operate on multiple levels simultaneously — phonetic,
semantic, symbolic, dreamlike, mythological — a language that aimed to capture the workings
of consciousness beneath the surface of rational thought. McKenna recognized in this literary
project a structural parallel to the DMT experience, where meaning is conveyed not through
linear language but through multivalent, overlapping, synesthetic forms that resist
translation into ordinary speech.

## Three Meanings in One Phrase

The phrase "merry go raum" collapses at least three meanings simultaneously, in a manner
entirely consistent with Joyce's own technique of multi-layered punning. First, it suggests a
merry-go-round — the spinning, circular quality of the experience where space itself appears
to rotate around the voyager, creating a proprioceptive sensation of being at the center of a
turning cosmos. Second, it reads as "the room that is going" — the space is not static but
alive, moving, transforming. The walls of ordinary spatial perception dissolve into a dynamic
field. The room has become a vehicle, a carousel, a ride. Third, the collision of German
*Raum* with English "merry" mirrors the collision of realities that characterizes the DMT
state. Just as Joyce puns across dozens of languages in *Finnegans Wake*, the DMT experience
presents a reality where the normal boundaries between categories — self and world, here and
there, now and then, one language and another — become permeable. This triple meaning is
itself a Joycean device. McKenna is not merely describing the DMT space but performing it,
using a multivalent phrase that operates the way the experience operates: on multiple levels
at once, resisting any single interpretation.

## Insulated and Domed Space

McKenna's phenomenological description of the DMT space as "insulated and domed" is
consistent across multiple reports from experienced DMT users. The sensation is one of
entering an enclosed, self-contained reality — not a vision overlaid on ordinary space but a
complete environment with its own physics, its own logic, its own inhabitants. This quality
distinguishes DMT from other psychedelics like LSD or psilocybin, which typically modify the
perception of the existing environment rather than replacing it entirely. The "domed" quality
suggests an architectural metaphor: the DMT space has a ceiling, a boundary, a shape. This
is not infinite cosmic space but a particular kind of chamber — one that feels ancient,
purposeful, and constructed. McKenna's connection to *Finnegans Wake* reinforces this sense
of an artfully constructed linguistic-architectural space. The merry go raum is a room that
has been built, not a void that has been discovered. The "insulated" quality is equally
important. The DMT space feels cut off from ordinary reality in a way that is protective
rather than claustrophobic. One is insulated from the noise, the demands, the expectations
of the ordinary world. This insulation is what allows the experience to unfold with such
intensity — nothing from outside can dilute or disrupt it.

## Joyce's Night-Language as Proto-Tryptamine

McKenna's appropriation of Joyce is not merely decorative. He saw in *Finnegans Wake* a
literary technology for achieving something analogous to the tryptamine state: the
dissolution of fixed meaning, the liberation of language from its referential function, the
emergence of a primal "Ursprache" (primal language) beneath the surface of ordinary speech.
Joyce's portmanteau words — his multilingual puns, his dream-narrative structures, his
collapsing of historical periods and mythological figures into a single continuous present —
all point toward a kind of consciousness that the DMT experience makes directly available. In
the DMT state, McKenna reports encountering "a language of alien meaning that is conveying
alien information that cannot be Englished." This translinguistic phenomenon — where meaning
is felt and understood without being translatable into any known human language — is
precisely the territory Joyce was mapping with his "night-language" in *Finnegans Wake*.
Joyce worked from the literary side, constructing a verbal approximation of the experience;
DMT provides direct access to the experiential reality behind the literary technique. The
book is the map; the molecule is the territory.

## The Logos and the Wake

McKenna connects the Joyce reference to his larger concept of the Logos — the transhuman,
hyperdimensional intelligence that he encountered through DMT and psilocybin. He notes that
psilocybin and DMT "invoke the Logos," working directly on the language centers of the brain
so that "an important aspect of the experience is the interior dialogue." This connection to
the language faculty is what makes the Joyce parallel so apt: *Finnegans Wake* is itself a
literary exploration of the Logos, an attempt to write the language that underlies language.
The title *Finnegans Wake* itself — with its suggestion of awakening from a sleep, of a
funeral that is also a resurrection, of an ending that is also a beginning — mirrors the
quality McKenna describes in the tryptamine state, where death and rebirth, ending and
beginning, collapse into a single moment.

## Childlike Consciousness in the Raum

Upon entering the merry go raum, McKenna reports feeling "like a child, though one has come
out somewhere in eternity." This quality of childlikeness — not childishness but genuine
openness, wonder, and receptivity — is a consistent feature of the DMT experience and
connects to McKenna's reading of Heraclitus fragment 24 ("The Aeon is a child at play with
colored balls"). The feeling of having "come out somewhere in eternity" locates the merry go
raum outside of ordinary temporal flow. The space is not in the future or the past but in a
kind of eternal present — McKenna's *nunc stans*, borrowed from Thomas Aquinas. The merry go
raum is where the moving image of time resolves into the static image of eternity, where
Joyce's "commodius, vicus of recirculation" becomes a direct experience rather than a
literary construct.

## The Raum as Cosmological Model

The merry go raum can be read as a cosmological model. If the DMT experience is, as McKenna
suggests, an encounter with the foundational level of reality — the quantum mechanical level
where mind and matter interact — then the qualities of the space (domed, insulated, turning,
alive) may reflect actual features of reality that are normally filtered out by ordinary
consciousness. The domed, insulated quality suggests that the DMT space is a kind of inner
chamber of the cosmos — a private room within the vast architecture of being. The turning,
merry quality suggests that this inner space is not static but dynamic, characterized by
play, motion, and transformation. Joyce's "chaosmos" — his vision of a universe that is
simultaneously chaos and cosmos — is the literary equivalent of this cosmological model.

## Literary Precedents and the Black Hole Effect

McKenna noted what he called the "total absence of the motifs of DMT" in any literary or
artistic tradition familiar to him. Despite this absence, his recourse to *Finnegans Wake*
suggests that Joyce came closer than any other writer to capturing something of the DMT
quality. The "black hole effect" that McKenna identified — where the more articulately one
describes the DMT experience, the less others can understand — may explain why Joyce's own
work, which attempts something similar through purely literary means, is notoriously
difficult and resistant to straightforward interpretation. The merry go raum thus stands as
both a phenomenological description and a hermeneutic warning: the space the DMT reveals is
real, but it resists the very language we would use to communicate it. Joyce's solution was
to break language itself; the DMT solution is to bypass language entirely and present the
experience directly, in three dimensions, as McKenna describes: "evolving surfaces" of
"light and color," "deployed fourth-dimensionally."
