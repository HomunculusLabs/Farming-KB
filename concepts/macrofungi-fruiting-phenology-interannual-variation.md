### Unusual Weather Events

Unusual weather events can trigger atypical fruiting patterns. In regions with dry summers, fruiting of species rarely observed in the region may follow a single summer shower. These episodic events mean that a single survey, no matter how thorough, may miss species that only fruit under unusual conditions.

## The Scale of Interannual Variation

### Low Overlap Between Years

The extent of year-to-year variation in macrofungal fruiting is remarkable and often underappreciated by non-specialists. In Olympic National Park, only 5% to 20% of ectomycorrhizal species encountered fruited in two successive years across eight sites. Even when combining data from all sites, only about 30% of species were detected in both years. This means that a two-year survey captures, at best, roughly one-third of the species actually present.

### Five-Year and Longer Studies

Winterhoff (cited in Arnolds, 1992) studied five sites for five years and found that from 8% to 88% of total reported species from a single site were detected in any given year. Lodge (1996) found that several Entolomataceae species fruited every second or third year in a wet subtropical forest in Puerto Rico, while a few species were found during only one year of a 13-year survey. Schmit et al. (1999) reported significant annual differences in species composition during a three-year study of macrofungal diversity in oak-dominated forests near Chicago.

### The 21-Year Swiss Study

Straatsma and colleagues (2001) conducted a remarkable 21-year study of fungal fruiting phenology in Switzerland. They found that some species fruited only during a single year that was particularly rich in species, and that fruiting abundance and species richness were correlated. Critically, species richness estimators did not stabilize during the full 21 years of observation, indicating that even two decades of sampling may be insufficient to fully characterize a macrofungal community.

## Species Accumulation and Sampling Duration

The cumulative species accumulation curve for macrofungi typically does not approach an asymptote for 8 to 12 years of regular sampling. This has profound implications for survey design: studies lasting fewer than five years will almost certainly underestimate species richness, and methods used to estimate total species richness will not perform well if no "shoulder" is present in the species accumulation curve.

## Phenological Windows and Survey Timing

Maximum richness of fruiting species occurs only during brief periods and differs among years. In a year when many species fruit simultaneously, specimens may decay before they can be adequately documented, resulting in significant data loss. This creates a cruel paradox: the years with the richest fruiting are precisely the years when the surveyor is most likely to miss species due to overwhelming abundance and specimen degradation.

## Fruiting Strategies and Ecological Guilds

Different ecological guilds of macrofungi exhibit distinct phenological patterns. Ectomycorrhizal fungi, which derive carbon from host trees, often show strong correlations between host phenology and fruiting timing. Saprotrophic species that decompose leaf litter tend to fruit later in the season than those that decompose wood, reflecting the different rates at which their substrates become available after the onset of autumn conditions. Coprophilous fungi associated with herbivore dung exhibit yet another pattern, fruiting in response to the deposition of fresh dung rather than to seasonal climate cues alone. Parasitic fungi that attack other fungi or plants may track the phenology of their hosts rather than responding directly to abiotic conditions.

### Multimodal Fruiting

Some macrofungal species are capable of fruiting multiple times per year or even year-round under favorable conditions. Species of Volvariella, for example, can fruit repeatedly during warm, wet periods. In tropical and subtropical regions, where seasonal temperature variation is minimal, moisture availability becomes the dominant driver of fruiting phenology, and some species may produce sporocarps nearly continuously. This contrasts sharply with temperate regions, where most species have a single, well-defined fruiting season.
