## The Tassili N'Ajjer Controversy

Perhaps the most famous claimed representation of a mushroom in prehistoric art
comes from the Tassili n'Ajjer plateau in the Algerian Sahara. Among the
remarkable rock paintings of this region, one image in particular has attracted
enormous attention: a figure known as the "bee-masked shaman," surrounded by
what appear to be rows of mushroom-shaped objects.

## Neolithic Mushroom Stones

The Neolithic stone formations of Europe have also been proposed as mushroom
references. The most prominent claim concerns the stones of various megalithic
sites, where certain carved forms have been interpreted as mushroom-shaped.

The archaeologist Michael Dames proposed that Silbury Hill, the massive
prehistoric mound in Wiltshire, England, was a giant mushroom effigy. This
reading, while imaginative, has not gained acceptance among mainstream
archaeologists, who note that the mound's shape is consistent with other
explanations including defensive, ceremonial, and astronomical functions.

## The Three Stages of Trance Model

In the 1980s, the South African archaeologists David Lewis-Williams and Thomas
Dowson developed their influential "three stages of trance" model. They argued
that certain images in Upper Palaeolithic cave art could be explained as
representations of the visual phenomena experienced in altered states of
consciousness, including the geometric patterns (phosphenes, entoptic phenomena)
that characterise the early stages of trance.

While this model does not specifically identify mushrooms in cave art, it opened
the door to interpreting prehistoric art as evidence of shamanic or
psychedelic practice. If the artists were depicting trance experiences, the
question naturally arises: what substances induced those experiences?

The model has been both influential and contested. Some anthropologists have
embraced it enthusiastically, while others argue that it risks reading
psychedelic experiences into art that may have had quite different motivations.

## Prehistoric European Claims

Various specific images in European prehistoric art have been proposed as
mushroom representations:

**Iberian mushroom figures**: Certain post-Stone Age rock carvings in Spain and
Portugal have been claimed to depict mushroom-headed figures or mushroom
deities. These identifications remain speculative.

**Scandinavian rock art**: Some Bronze Age rock carvings in Scandinavia have
been interpreted as mushroom-related, though alternative explanations are
generally preferred by specialists.

**Gundestrup Cauldron**: The spectacular Gundestrup Cauldron, a silver vessel
from the first or second century BCE found in Denmark, features elaborate Celtic
imagery that some have tried to connect with mushroom use, though the evidence
is thin.

## Medieval Manuscript Illustrations

Medieval European manuscripts contain numerous representations of mushrooms,
though these are overwhelmingly in the context of herbals and botanical
illustrations rather than religious or mystical imagery. The mushroom was
primarily of interest to medieval scholars as a botanical curiosity and a
potential source of food or poison.

## The Santa Marta de Cerquedo Mural

A particularly interesting Spanish example is the mural at the hermitage of
Santa Marta de Cerquedo in Burgos, which features a fresco showing the
Temptation of Eve. The serpent is coiled around a tree-like form that some
have interpreted as a mushroom, though art historians again favour a
stylised tree interpretation.

## Methodological Challenges

Identifying mushrooms in ancient art faces several fundamental challenges:

**Stylisation**: Ancient and prehistoric art is often highly stylised, making
it difficult to determine whether a particular form represents a mushroom,
a tree, a person, or an abstract design.

**Ambiguity**: Many natural forms (umbrellas, trees, fungi, pillars) share
basic visual characteristics. A dome-shaped cap on a stem could represent any
of these.

**Confirmation bias**: Once the idea of ancient mushroom use becomes
attractive, viewers tend to find mushrooms in ambiguous images, a form of
pareidolia.

**Cultural context**: Without supporting evidence from the same culture
(texts, archaeological remains, ethnographic parallels), it is very difficult
to establish that a particular image represents a psychoactive mushroom rather
than an ordinary fungus or an entirely different object.

## Current Scholarly Consensus

The mainstream archaeological consensus remains deeply sceptical of claims that
psychedelic mushrooms are represented in prehistoric European art. While it is
certainly possible that some prehistoric cultures used psychoactive fungi, the
visual evidence for this in art remains inconclusive at best.
