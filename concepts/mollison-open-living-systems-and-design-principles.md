# Open Living Systems and Permaculture Design Principles

## Overview

Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* frames the design challenge within the context of open living systems — complex, self-organizing biological systems that differ fundamentally from the closed thermodynamic systems studied in classical physics. The distinction between closed and open systems is central to understanding why conventional [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]] often fails and why permaculture design offers an alternative path grounded in ecological reality.

## Entropy and Living Systems

The laws of thermodynamics, while applicable to non-living or experimentally closed systems, are not necessarily applicable to living, open earth systems with which we are immersed. The concept of entropy — the tendency toward disorder in closed systems — does not fully describe biological systems, which constantly organize diffuse energy and materials into complex storages. These laws are more useful for navigating material technologies than for understanding the life-complexed world.

The key word in open systems is "exchange." Healthy natural systems are characterized by reciprocal exchange — outputs of one process become inputs for another. When exchange breaks down, systems degrade. Cities, for example, appear to be "open" at a local level but operate as one-way trade systems with respect to food resources. They import energy and materials while exporting wastes as pollutants to the sea, returning little to the systems that supply them. This breaks the basic "law of return" that characterizes healthy natural systems.

## The Manure Transformation Cascade

Life systems constantly organize and create complex storages from diffuse energy and materials, accumulating, decomposing, building, and transforming them for further use. We can use these effects in the design process by finding pathways by which life systems convert diffuse materials into those of most use. Mollison illustrates multi-function design through the manure transformation chain:

1. **Fermentation and distillation** to alcohol for fuel or industrial use
2. **[[anaerobic-digestion-biogas-production]]** in a biogas digester, where [[anaerobic-organisms-compost]] convert waste to methane for cooking, heating, or vehicle fuel
3. **Liquid effluent** routed to fields as nutrient-rich fertilizer
4. **Solid sludge** fed to worms, which convert it to rich horticultural soil
5. **Worms** used to feed fish or poultry, closing the nutrient loop completely

While leaving manure on a field achieves productive use of one function, routing it through this cascade achieves multiple yields from a single "waste" resource — the essence of permaculture design thinking. Each step produces a useful yield while the "waste" of one process becomes the input for the next.

## Birch's Six Principles of Natural Systems

Mollison cites six principles articulated by Birch that underpin natural system behavior and inform permaculture design:

1. **Nothing in nature grows forever.** There is a constant cycle of decay and rebirth. No organism or population expands indefinitely; limits and feedback loops constrain growth. Understanding these cycles allows designers to work within natural bounds rather than fighting them. Growth is always balanced by decomposition and renewal.

2. **Continuation of life depends on the maintenance of global biogeochemical cycles.** Carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, and phosphorus must cycle continuously through living systems. Designers must ensure these and other minor nutrients are cycled to stimulate growth and keep the atmosphere and waters of earth unpolluted. Disrupting these cycles — through pollution, deforestation, or monoculture — undermines the foundation of all production.

3. **Extinction risk is greatest at very high or very low population densities.** Both crowding and scarcity create vulnerability. Overcrowding leads to disease, competition, and resource depletion; underpopulation reduces [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] and reproductive success. This principle has direct implications for stocking rates, planting densities, and the design of polycultures that maintain populations within safe ranges.

4. **Species survival depends primarily on one or two key factors.** In the complex web of organism-environment relations, critical limiting factors can be identified. If we determine what these are, we can exclude some limiting factors by design (e.g., frost through shelter structures) and increase others (e.g., nest sites, food availability, water access). Design becomes the art of identifying and managing these leverage points.

5. **Human ability to change the earth outpaces ability to foresee consequences.** The folly of destroying life systems for short-term profit is self-evident but repeatedly ignored throughout history. This principle demands caution, humility, and long-term thinking in all design interventions. Every modification carries consequences that ripple through interconnected systems in ways we may not anticipate.

6. **Living organisms are both means and ends.** Beyond their instrumental value to humans and other organisms, they possess intrinsic worth — the life ethic thesis so often missing from otherwise ethical systems that assign value only through utility to humans. This principle establishes the ethical foundation for permaculture's care for all living systems.

## Mollison's Core Design Principles

From these natural system principles, Mollison distills several practical design directives that guide all permaculture work:

### 1. Work With Nature, Rather Than Against It

Natural successions proceed from grasses to shrubs to trees over time. Rather than slashing out pioneer species (weeds), use them to provide microclimate, nutrients, and wind protection for the desired exotic or native species being established. The pioneers create the conditions that later successional species need to thrive. We actively assist natural succession rather than fighting it.

[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s wisdom captures this principle perfectly: "If we throw nature out the window, she comes back in the door with a pitchfork." Spraying for pest infestations destroys both pests and their predators. The following year brings a pest explosion because no predators remain to control them. Heavier spraying puts things further out of balance. The surviving pests breed more resistant progeny — nature's pitchfork returning with increasing force.

### 2. The Problem Is the Solution

Everything works both ways — it is only perception that makes something advantageous or not. Cold wind can drive a wind generator or be directed to cool a food cupboard in a heated house. A corollary is that everything is a positive resource; it is up to the designer to work out how to use it as such.

A designer may recognize a site characteristic as either a problem or a unique feature capable of several uses. Jagged rock outcrops only become "problems" when a specific site pattern has already been decided that the rock interferes with. One friend incorporated natural boulders into her home with excellent physical, aesthetic, and economic benefit — the builder would have removed them as "problems" at great expense. Only our fixed attitudes are problems when dealing with site features.

### 3. Make the Least Change for the Greatest Possible Effect

Efficiency of intervention is the measure. When choosing a dam site, select the area yielding the most water for the least earth moved. Minimize disturbance while maximizing benefit. This principle favors careful observation over hasty action and strategic placement over wholesale modification of the landscape. Small, well-placed interventions often outperform large, poorly considered ones.

### 4. The Yield of a System Is Theoretically Unlimited

The only limit on the number of uses of a resource within a system is the limit of information and imagination of the designer. If you think you have fully planted an area, almost any other innovative designer can see ways to add a vine, a fungus, a beneficial insect, or a yield potential that has been ignored. Gahan Gilfedder at the Garden of Eden in Australia found an unsuspected market for cherimoya seed, required by nurseries as grafting stock — transforming a "waste" product from damaged fruit into a valuable resource.

### 5. Everything Gardens

Every organism has an effect on its environment — everything "makes its own garden." Rabbits create burrows and defecation mounds, scratch out roots, create short swards, and generate conditions favorable for certain weeds such as thistles (cardoons and globe artichokes). People build houses, dispose of sewage, dig up soils for gardens, and maintain annual vegetable patches. Understanding how plants and animals change ecosystems reveals many allies in the effort to sustain ourselves and other species.

## Practical Design Considerations

The systems we construct should:
- Last as long as possible with minimum maintenance
- Produce not only their own needs but the needs of the people creating or controlling them (true sustainability)
- Store or conserve more energy over their lifetime than was used to construct and maintain them

The long-term energy derived from the sun is available on earth and can renew our resources if life systems are carefully constructed and preserved. Designers must study whether resources and energy consumed can be derived from renewable or non-renewable sources, and how non-renewable resources can best be used to conserve and generate energy in living (renewable) systems.
