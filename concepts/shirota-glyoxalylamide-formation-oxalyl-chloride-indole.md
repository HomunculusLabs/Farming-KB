## The Two-Step Conversion

The conversion of 4-acetylindole to 3-dimethylaminooxalyl-4-acetylindole proceeds in two discrete steps within the same reaction sequence.

First, 4-acetylindole is acylated at the 3-position of the indole ring using oxalyl chloride. This introduces an oxalyl chloride group at the reactive 3-position, forming a glyoxalyl chloride intermediate.

Second, the glyoxalyl chloride intermediate is reacted with dimethylamine to replace the chloride with a dimethylamino group, producing the glyoxalylamide.

Both steps proceed in good yield, and the intermediate glyoxalyl chloride crystallizes as yellow crystals that can be isolated by filtration.

## Step One: Acylation with Oxalyl Chloride

The acylation of 4-acetylindole with oxalyl chloride exploits the well-known reactivity of the 3-position of the indole ring toward electrophilic substitution.

The 3-position of indole is the most nucleophilic site on the ring, and acylation at this position is a standard transformation in indole chemistry.

Shirota and colleagues dissolved 4-acetylindole (17.6 g, 100 mmol) in anhydrous [[diethyl-ether]] (100 mL) with stirring in an ice bath.

Oxalyl chloride (13 mL, 146 mmol) was added to the solution.

After stirring for 15 minutes, n-hexane (200 mL) was added to the reaction mixture.

The flask was placed in a freezer and stored overnight, during which the glyoxalyl chloride intermediate crystallized as yellow crystals.

The yellow crystals were separated from the solution by filtration, washed with cold solvent, and used directly in the next step without further purification.

## The Glyoxalyl Chloride Intermediate

The glyoxalyl chloride intermediate (compound 5 in the Shirota scheme) is formed as yellow crystals upon addition of n-hexane and overnight storage in the freezer.

The crystallization of this intermediate is advantageous for the overall synthesis because it allows isolation of a pure intermediate without chromatographic purification.

The use of n-hexane as an anti-solvent to induce crystallization is a standard technique in [[pericyclic-reactions-organic-chemistry]], but its effectiveness in this case is noteworthy.

The fact that the intermediate crystallizes readily reflects the relatively planar, rigid structure of the indole-glyoxalyl chloride system.

The intermediate is sufficiently stable to be isolated, handled, and stored, though Shirota and colleagues used it directly in the next step without prolonged storage.

## Step Two: Amidation with Dimethylamine

The isolated glyoxalyl chloride intermediate is dissolved in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (100 mL) with stirring in an ice bath.

A 2M solution of dimethylamine in THF (60 mL, 120 mmol) is added dropwise, followed by pyridine (10 mL, 123 mmol) as a base.

The pyridine serves to neutralize the hydrochloric acid generated during the amidation reaction.

The reaction mixture is stirred for 15 minutes at room temperature.

Additional anhydrous ether is added as needed to prevent solidification of the reaction mixture.

The glyoxalylamide product precipitates from the reaction mixture and is collected by filtration.

It is washed successively with n-hexane, ethyl acetate, and water to remove residual reagents and byproducts.

## Yield and Characterization

The two-step conversion from 4-acetylindole to the glyoxalylamide proceeds in over 80% yield.

Shirota and colleagues reported obtaining 22.0 g of the glyoxalylamide as an ivory white crystalline powder from 17.6 g of 4-acetylindole.

The product was characterized by proton and carbon-13 NMR and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry.

The proton NMR spectrum showed the characteristic N,N-dimethyl signals as two singlets at 3.02 and 2.92 ppm, confirming the presence of the dimethylamino group.

The carbon-13 NMR showed the two carbonyl carbons of the glyoxalylamide moiety at 185.4 and 170.9 ppm, along with the acetyl carbonyl at 168.4 ppm.

The ESI mass spectrum showed the expected molecular ion peaks, including [M + H]+ at m/z 275.1.

## Significance in the Synthetic Route

The glyoxalylamide intermediate is the direct precursor to psilocin in the Shirota synthesis.

Reduction of the glyoxalylamide with lithium aluminum hydride converts both the glyoxalyl carbonyl and the acetyl carbonyl to methylene groups, producing psilocin in a single operation.

This reduction step is remarkable because it simultaneously removes the acetyl protecting group from the 4-position and reduces the glyoxalylamide [[dimethylamine-amidation-tryptamine-side-chain-construction]] to the desired ethylamine.

The efficiency of this transformation is a key advantage of the Shirota route over alternative syntheses.
