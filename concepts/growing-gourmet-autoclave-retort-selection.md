# Autoclave and Retort Selection for [[contamination-mushroom-cultivation]]
The choice of [[sterilization]] vessel is one [[allegro-star-of-the-morning-venus-and-the-sacred-mushroom]] most
critical decisions [[bacteria-and-microorganisms-in-mushroom-fruiting]] cultivation. Home-style
pressure cookers are ideal for sterilizing agar media and
small batches of grain, but they have insufficient capacity
for the sterilization of bulk substrates needed for
commercial production. For [[growing-gourmet-spawn-laboratory-design]] that
integrates autoclave workflow, see that page.
## Pressure Cookers vs. Autoclaves
Home pressure cookers are the entry point for most
cultivators. The best models release pressure during the
sterilization cycle and ideally form a vacuum upon
cool-down. Old-fashioned pressure canners with weights
sitting upon a steam valve cause media to boil as steam
is vented, creating a mess.
If a pressure cooker does not form a vacuum upon cooling,
outside air laden with contaminant spores is sucked in,
potentially contaminating sterilized jars before the
cultivator has handled them. One precaution is to saturate
a paper towel with alcohol and drape it over the vent
valve to filter incoming air.
## Choosing an Autoclave or Retort
The pressurized steam autoclave is far better suited for
commercial production. The most useful autoclaves for
sterilizing bulk substrates are horizontal and have two
doors. Since the autoclave is the centerpiece upon which
the entire production process depends, many factors must
be considered in its acquisition.
Hospital autoclaves are typically smaller than needed by
commercial mushroom cultivators, measuring only 2 x 3 ft.
or 3 x 4 ft. by 3-6 ft. deep. Furthermore, they usually
have only one door, [[allegro-names-of-the-gods-and-their-fertility-origins]] pressure ratings have been
engineered to operate at 100 psi, far exceeding the needs
of most mushroom growers.
The most cost-effective vessels are those developed for
the canning industries. These are commonly called "retorts"
and are constructed of steel pipe, 1/4 to 3/8 inch thick,
and ideally fitted with doors at both ends. Quick-opening,
spider doors are popular and durable.
## Essential Autoclave Features
When equipping an autoclave [[cotter-substrate-preparation]], the
following features are recommended:
- Double doors (i.e. doors at both ends)
- Redundant pressure/temperature gauges (at least two)
- Pressure/vacuum gauge (+50 psi to -50 psi) with valves
- Electrical safety interlocks with warning lights
- Hand-operated vent valve on top for venting cold air
- 25 psi and 50 psi excess-pressure relief safety valves
- Hand-operated drain valve for drawing off condensate
- Heat-resistant, anti-corrosive paint coating
- At least four 1-inch and/or two 2-inch ports for inputs,
  exhausts, and sensors
- One-way gate valve in series with a vacuum gauge that
  allows drawing in clean-room air post-autoclaving
## Vacuum Capability
An important feature is the autoclave's ability to hold a
vacuum subsequent to the sterilization cycle. If the
autoclave cannot hold a vacuum as it cools, a valve should
be installed for the controlled intake of filtered air. If
the influx of air is not filtered, the contents can
contaminate after sterilization.
A microporous filter canister attached to a pipe equipped
with a gate valve, connected to a one-way check valve
leading directly into the autoclave, allows pressure to
be equalized without introducing contaminants. This
assembly should be located on the clean room side.
Commonly, an autoclave can swing in pressure from 20 psi
to -20 psi within several hours after steam injection has
stopped. This 40-psi pressure swing is devastating at the
cellular level, disabling any surviving endospores of
bacteria or conidia of contaminating molds.
## Boyle's Law and Sterilization Dynamics
When steam is injected into an autoclave, the displacement
of cold air by introducing steam and top-venting is
absolutely critical. Cold air, if not vented, gives a
false temperature/pressure reading. At 15 psi, the
temperature within the autoclave should be 252 degrees F
(121 degrees C). This relationship between temperature
and pressure is known as Boyle's Law.
When a cold mass is introduced into an autoclave or
pressure cooker, Boyle's Law does not come into play until
the thermal inertia [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] affected mass is overcome. The
duration [[allegro-fertility-philosophy-of-the-ancient-near-east]] autoclave run should not be timed until
temperature differentials have been largely eliminated.
## Sterilization Duration
Fresh hardwood sawdust needs 2-3 hours at 15 psi or
250 degrees F. The same mass supplemented with rice bran
needs 4-5 hours. One of the cardinal rules of mushroom
culture: as the percentage of cannabis-nitrogen|nitrogen supplements
increases, the greater the likelihood of contamination,
and thus the greater the need for full sterilization.
An autoclave filled tightly 5 bags high, 6 bags wide, and
8 bags deep (240 bags) requires exposure to steam pressure
for 5 hours at 18 psi to assure full sterilization. The
lower, central core is the slowest to heat up.
Over-sterilization occurs when wood substrates are
subjected to steam pressure for more than five hours. The
sawdust takes on a dark brown color, has a distinctly
different odor, and resists decomposition by mushroom
mycelium. Prolonged steam sterilization results in
complex chemical transformations.
## Unloading the Autoclave
Once the autoclave has achieved a vacuum, the pressure
must be returned to atmospheric before the door can be
opened. After opening, several hours of cooling are
necessary before bags can be handled freely. As the mass
cools, air is being drawn in, and if that air is full of
dust, contamination is likely.

autoclave is running. Suspicious cultures should be
removed, floors vacuumed and mopped, and countertops
wiped with alcohol. Selected personnel for laboratory
