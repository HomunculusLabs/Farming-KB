## The Mushroom Life Cycle in Cultivation

Although spores are the natural propagative bodies of mushrooms,
growers cannot employ them directly in production. Spores are difficult
to germinate even under optimal laboratory conditions. Under favorable
conditions, each spore produces a germ tube that ultimately develops
into a filamentous, thread-like growth known as mycelium. The mycelium
arises by subsequent branching and continued ramifications, and under
favorable conditions grows until the rapidly elongating filaments
penetrate the substratum in every direction.

This vegetative growth of the mycelium in any suitable substratum yields
the characteristic "spawn." Spawn refers to this visible phase of
mushroom growth in extensive quantity. It is appropriately called the
vegetative phase. Spawn may also be produced from fragments of living
tissue when pure culture methods are employed. The mycelium, pervading
the substratum in all directions, absorbs necessary water along with
organic and inorganic food materials for growth, accumulating a
considerable surplus that manifests in rapid mushroom production.

## Stages of Mushroom Development

When the spawn has developed sufficiently in the compost and conditions
for fruiting are satisfactory, minute cushion-like areas of growth
appear on the large threads. These become spherical in form, creating
snow-white pin heads, the first unmistakable signs of mushroom
production. In a suitable environment, pin heads grow rapidly and are
soon recognized as "buttons," ultimately maturing into full mushrooms.
Under the best conditions for commercial production, a week to ten days
elapses between pin heads and maturity. Commercial mushrooms are graded
highest if picked just before the veil breaks.

## Compost Preparation

The compost serves as both the physical substrate and nutritional
source for mushroom mycelium. Duggar emphasized that the quality of the
compost directly determines the success or failure of a mushroom crop.
The traditional compost is based on horse manure mixed with straw
bedding, though various formulations have been developed over time.

Key considerations for compost preparation include adequate moisture
content throughout the composting process, proper carbon-to-nitrogen
ratio in the starting materials, sufficient aeration during the
thermophilic (heat-generating) phase of composting, and adequate
curing time to allow partial decomposition and stabilization. The
compost must provide a balanced nutritional medium while being free
from competing organisms that could colonize before the mushroom
mycelium is established.

## Spawn Types and Production

Historically, mushroom spawn was produced through natural infection of
substrate materials, known as "mill track spawn" or "flake spawn."
Duggar documented the transition from these traditional methods to
pure culture spawn production, which he helped establish in the United
States. Pure culture methods involve growing the mycelium from a
single isolated strain under sterile conditions, ensuring genetic
consistency and freedom from contaminants.
