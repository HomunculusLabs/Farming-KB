# La Chorrera Experiment McKenna

## Overview

The La Chorrera experiment was a controversial and visionary
psychedelic research project conducted in 1971 by brothers Terence
and [[dennis-mckenna]] in the Colombian Amazon. The experiment, later
documented in their co-authored book "The Invisible Landscape:
Mind, Hallucinogens, and the I Ching" (1975), represented an
attempt to explore whether psilocybin mushrooms could act as a
catalyst for accessing hyperdimensional realities and whether
the tryptamine molecules found in these mushrooms might interact
directly with human DNA to produce observable phenomena.

## Context: The McKennas in the Amazon

[[terence-mckenna]] arrived in the Upper Amazon in early 1970, having
spent several years living in Asian societies. As he describes in
"Food of the Gods," he encountered psilocybin mushrooms for the
first time at San Augustine in the Alto Magdalena of southern
Colombia, and later encountered visionary brews made from
Banisteriopsis vines -- the ayahuasca -- near Florencia.

The McKennas were deeply immersed in the shamanic traditions of
the lower Rio Putumayo of Colombia. Terence's encounter with
Amazonian shamanism convinced him of the salvific importance of
hallucinogenic plants and the shamanic institutions their use
implies. The brothers' Amazonian experiences formed the
foundation for what would become a lifetime of advocacy for the
responsible use of psychedelic substances.

## The Experiment: Hypothesis and Method

The core hypothesis of the La Chorrera experiment was that
psilocybin, when combined with other psychoactive compounds
under specific conditions, could produce a phenomenon in which
hallucinatory experiences would become visible and tangible in
consensual reality -- essentially, that the boundary between
subjective experience and objective reality could be dissolved
through the intelligent use of tryptamine compounds.

The McKennas theorized that the tryptamine molecules present in
Stropharia cubensis mushrooms could interact with human genetic
material at a fundamental level. This hypothesis was rooted in
the observation that tryptamine hallucinogens -- including DMT,
psilocin, and psilocybin -- share structural similarities with
serotonin, a neurotransmitter that plays critical roles in mood,
consciousness, and possibly in neurogenesis and gene expression.

The experiment involved the ingestion of psilocybin mushrooms
combined with harmine and harmaline (beta-carboline alkaloids
derived from [[banisteriopsis-caapi]], the ayahuasca vine). The
beta-carbolines act as monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs),
which prevent the breakdown of DMT in the digestive tract and
dramatically prolong and intensify its effects -- the same
pharmacological principle underlying traditional ayahuasca.

## Stropharia Cubensis and the Amazon

The McKennas focused on Stropharia cubensis as their primary
psychedelic agent. As noted in "Food of the Gods," Stropharia
cubensis is a widely distributed psilocybin-containing mushroom
found throughout the warm tropics, thought to have originated
in Thailand. Richard Evans Schultes had documented its use among
Amazonian peoples, and Wasson had speculated about its possible
role as the original Soma of the Vedic peoples.

The mushroom contains psilocybin in concentrated form in its
mycelium and fruiting body, making it one of the most potent
naturally occurring psychedelic substances. Of all the mushroom
species known to the McKennas, only Stropharia cubensis was
considered to have the right combination of potency, availability,
and theoretical significance for their experiment.

## The Invisible Landscape

The results of the La Chorrera experiment, as documented in "The
Invisible Landscape," were extraordinary and deeply subjective.
The McKennas reported experiences of what Terence later called
"hyperspace" -- a dimension of reality that could be accessed
through the psychedelic experience and that seemed to possess its
own internal logic, inhabitants, and architecture.

Dennis McKenna, who brought a more rigorous scientific framework
to the project, attempted to develop a mathematical model --
the "timewave zero" or "novelty theory" -- based on the I Ching
that purported to map the [[ebb-and-flow]] of novelty (habit-breaking
change) throughout history. This theory, while controversial and
ultimately not scientifically validated, represented one of the
first serious attempts to integrate psychedelic experience with
mathematical modeling and systems theory.

## Legacy and Significance

The La Chorrera experiment stands as one of the most ambitious
and philosophically daring psychedelic experiments ever attempted.
Its significance lies less in its experimental methodology -- which
was informal, subjective, and unreplicable by conventional
scientific standards -- than in the questions it raised about the
nature of consciousness, reality, and the relationship between
mind and matter.

The experiment directly inspired Terence McKenna's later career as
one of the most influential advocates for psychedelic culture.
His concepts of "hyperspace," the "Transcendent Other," and the
role of psychedelics as tools for accessing what he called the
"Archaic Revival" all trace their origins to the La Chorrera
experience. Dennis McKenna went on to become a respected
ethnopharmacologist, contributing to the scientific understanding
of psychedelic compounds.

The McKennas also wrote "Psilocybin: The Magic Mushroom Growers
Guide" (published under pseudonyms O.T. Oss and O.N. Oeric),
which sold over a hundred thousand copies and made psilocybin
cultivation accessible to a broad audience, fundamentally
changing the landscape of psychedelic availability.
