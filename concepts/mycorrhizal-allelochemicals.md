### Non-Mycorrhizal Crops

Beets (*Beta vulgaris*), buckwheat (*Fagopyrum esculentum*), and members of
the Amaranthaceae, Chenopodiaceae, and Polygonaceae families do not form
mycorrhizae. When no host plants are present, mycorrhizal fungi cannot obtain
the carbon they need to survive. Spore populations decline, and reestablishing
arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi through

 can damage existing mycorrhizal
networks. Because arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi generally occur in the top 6 to
15 inches (15 to 38 centimeters) of soil, any practice that disturbs this zone
can reduce mycorrhizal
establishment.
When soil disturbance coincides with the planting of non-mycorrhizal or
allelopathic crops, the combined effect can devastate mycorrhizal populations.
Recovery requires either time (waiting for natural recolonization) or active
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-inoculum-production-best-practices]] as
needed.
## Research Gaps

The full extent of allelochemical impacts on mycorrhizal communities remains
an active area of research. Questions remain about the specific compounds
involved, their persistence in different soil types, and whether certain
mycorrhizal fungal species are more resistant to allelopathic effects than
others. As [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] complexity becomes better
understood, rotation design will likely incorporate more precise knowledge of
chemical interactions between plants, fungi, and soil
organisms.
