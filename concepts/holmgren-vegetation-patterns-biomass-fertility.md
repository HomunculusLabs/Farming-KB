# Vegetation Patterns and Biomass as Fertility Indicators

David Holmgren presents a detailed analysis of how vegetation patterns across
landscapes reflect underlying soil fertility, [[holmgren-mineral-balance-soil-fertility]], and ecological
potential. Understanding these patterns allows Permaculture designers to read
landscapes, select appropriate species, and avoid common misconceptions about
biomass as a measure of good land management.

## Biomass as a General Fertility Indicator

The relative amounts and types of plant and animal biomass in natural and managed
ecosystems can be used as a general indicator of mineral fertility. Although
substantial perennial plant biomass is important for sustainable agriculture,
there are understandable reasons why humans have tended to reduce woody and
perennial biomass in managed systems.

Ecosystems with large amounts of woody plant biomass often predominate on
infertile, leached soils in high-rainfall areas, whereas the archetypical human
habitats tended to be drier regions with more grassy vegetation. These regions
had higher mineral content supporting large numbers of animals and heavy
seed-producing grasses (parents of modern grains).

## The Fertility Gradient

There is a general ecological gradient from fertile grassland and open woodlands
on minerally rich soil through tall forest to stunted heathland on infertile
soil. This gradient reflects:

- Higher and balanced fertility: grasslands, grassy woodlands with large grazing
  mammals
- Moderate fertility with some leaching: tall forests with moderate animal
  populations
- Low and unbalanced fertility: heathy forests and heathlands with small
  herbivores and hard-leaved, toxic vegetation

## North American Landscapes

William Albrecht's studies of the American mid-west revealed clear patterns:

- Short-grass prairie: mineral balance favoured large herbivores, food crops,
  and people
- Tall-grass prairie: transitional zone with mixed characteristics
- Eastern deciduous forests: higher rainfall, more leached soils, less ideal
  for crops but abundant seed crops of acorns, beechnuts, and hickories
- Conifer forests: abundant woody biomass but few nutrients, large seeds, or
  animals -- on the lowest fertility soils

## Australian Landscapes

In Victoria, the drier and more fertile western volcanic plains were covered in
grasslands and open grassy woodlands, while higher-rainfall but more leached
sedimentary soils to the east supported tall eucalypt forests. The grasslands
supported more large herbivores (kangaroos) and Aboriginal people. Within the
native grasslands, the most nutrient-dense plants were small legumes, tuberous
wildflowers, and other relatively delicate herbaceous species mostly eliminated
by intensive sheep grazing.

This pattern was widespread in pre-European Australia and explained the
distribution of Aboriginal and early European settlement. It partially explains
our ancestral antipathy towards dense forests.

## Sydney: The Exception That Proves the Rule

On Sydney's north shore, the moderately fertile Ashfield shale loam soils
supported tall wet eucalypt forest, while the infertile Hawkesbury sandstone
supported stunted forest or low heathland. Early settlers clearing the tall
forests mistakenly assumed the soils would be more fertile. The experience around
Sydney may have led them astray elsewhere.

Although heathland may superficially look like grassland, it has few soft
herbaceous species palatable to grazing animals and a high proportion of
hard-leaved and toxic vegetation that tends to burn in fires even when green.

## Avoiding the Biomass Trap

These observations temper the tendency of some permaculturalists to become
"biomass junkies," regarding total plant biomass as the sole measure of good
land management. The Permaculture strategy of tree crops and food forests is not
about creating massive biomass forests; it focuses on trees with maximum potential
to feed people and livestock.

Ideal food-producing trees tend to be smaller, often deciduous, with soft leaves
that decompose readily and create non-acidic compost soils. They have large
fruits, seeds, nuts, or pods with high carbohydrate, protein, or oil content, and
are often fire retardant. Many people make the mistake of including tall forest
species, especially eucalypts and conifers, which can dominate systems without
contributing much of value.

## Fertility-Loving Versus Infertility-Loving Plants

Plants found naturally growing on infertile soils do not necessarily prefer those
conditions. It is their relative efficiency and competitive advantage on poor
soils that accounts for their [[brassinosteroid-natural-occurrence-plant-kingdoms]]. Most plants grow better at
higher fertility, but only plants evolved or bred to high-fertility soils are
reasonably efficient there.

Matching plants and animals to suit current conditions is an important strategy.
Local indigenous and other Australian native plant species make ideal shelter and
functional plants for unirrigated and unfertilised soils. When planting a new
garden, soil fertility may support only a limited range of vegetables, but over
time more demanding ones can be introduced.

## Grazing Animals and Energy Quality

The pattern of energetically optimal systems extends from plants to animals. Cows
produce more milk on good quality grass and clover pasture (high-quality energy),
but on poorer forage including woody shrubs (low-quality energy), goats are
relatively more productive. Cows may be best in high-rainfall [[greenhouse-cool-climates-mollison]] to
maintain healthy pasture, but goats are best for creating such pastures in the
first place.

When comparing cattle and sheep with kangaroos on semi-arid rangeland, kangaroos
are far more efficient. When available energy is rising, it is natural to search
for higher-yielding solutions; when energy availability is falling, efficiency
of energy conversion is more important -- a principle central to
[[permaculture-design]].
