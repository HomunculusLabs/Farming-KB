# Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF)

## Overview

Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) is a custom blended organic fertilizer formula developed by Steve Solomon for vegetable gardeners who need to grow nutrient-dense food without relying on chemical fertilizers or impractically large quantities of compost and manure. The formula is designed to provide a complete balance of macronutrients (NPK), secondary nutrients (calcium, magnesium, sulfur), and trace minerals that vegetables require for optimal growth and nutritional quality.

COF was created in response to Solomon's observation that most home gardeners cannot produce enough high-quality compost or obtain enough manure to meet the demands of medium and high fertility vegetables. Chemical fertilizers, while convenient, are incomplete — they supply NPK and sometimes sulfur, but lack calcium, magnesium, and the full spectrum of trace minerals. Vegetables grown with incomplete fertilizers are more susceptible to insects and disease, and they produce food with lower nutritional content.

## The Formula

All ingredients are measured by volume (scoops, bucketfuls, jarfuls) rather than by weight. The base recipe is:

### Protein Component (choose one):
- **4 parts** any kind of seedmeal (except coprameal)
- **OR** 3 parts any seedmeal except coprameal, plus 1 part tankage (blood-and-bone or meatmeal) for a higher-nitrogen option better suited to leafy crops in spring
- **OR** 4 parts less-potent coprameal, supplemented with 1 part tankage to boost nitrogen content

### Lime Component:
- **1/2 part** ordinary agricultural lime (finely ground)
- **1/2 part** gypsum (calcium sulfate)
- **1/2 part** dolomite lime

If gypsum is unavailable, double the quantity of agricultural lime.

### Phosphorus Component (choose one):
- **1 part** finely ground rock phosphate (hard or soft rock phosphate)
- **OR** bonemeal
- **OR** high-phosphate guano

### Trace Mineral Component:
- **1 part** kelpmeal
- **OR** 1 part basalt dust

## Key Ingredients Explained

### Seedmeal
Seedmeals are the byproduct of pressing vegetable oil from oilseeds such as cottonseed, linseed (flax), sunflower, canola (rapeseed), soybean, and sesame. They are typically analyzed at roughly 6-4-2 NPK, though coprameal (from coconut) is about one-third weaker. The general rule is to buy whichever seedmeal offers the most protein for the least cost. All seedmeals contain calcium, magnesium, and trace nutrients beyond their NPK analysis. Store seedmeals cool and dry in a tightly sealed container to prevent mold.

### Tankage
Tankage is a slaughterhouse byproduct — the whole animal minus the fats, oils, and hides, cooked, dried, and ground. It typically analyzes at about 10-4-0 NPK. Tankage is higher in nitrogen than seedmeal but has been harder to obtain in recent decades. It provides a broader nutrient profile than pure bloodmeal.

### Lime Varieties
Three types of lime are used in COF, each providing different forms of calcium:
- **Agricultural lime**: Relatively pure calcium carbonate
- **Dolomitic lime**: Contains both calcium and magnesium carbonates in roughly equal amounts
- **Gypsum (calcium sulfate)**: Provides calcium and sulfur without raising pH

Solomon recommends using a mixture of all three rather than choosing just one. Limes are inexpensive when bought in agricultural quantities (50-pound sacks). Importantly, Solomon advises ignoring soil pH testing — the COF formula automatically distributes the right amount of calcium and magnesium when applied at the recommended rate.

### Kelpmeal
Kelpmeal is the most expensive ingredient but adds considerable value. It provides growth regulators, natural hormones, and a full spectrum of trace minerals including iodine. Kelp contains significant amounts of phosphoric acid and compounds that enhance plants' resistance to cold, frost, and other stresses. Korean kelp is often less expensive than domestic sources. Basalt dust can substitute for kelpmeal, providing similar trace mineral content.

## Application Rates

Apply COF once per year, ideally in spring, at a rate of approximately 4 to 6 liters per 100 square feet of growing area (down each 50-foot row or atop each raised bed), creating a band 12 to 18 inches wide. Work the fertilizer into the top few inches of soil with a hoe or spade.

This base application provides sufficient fertility for **low-demand vegetables** (carrots, parsnips, beans, peas) to reach their full potential. It also adequately feeds most **medium-demand vegetables**. For maximum production of medium and high-demand vegetables, Solomon recommends additional side-dressings every few weeks after seedlings are established.

### Side-Dressing
After seedlings have emerged or been transplanted, sprinkle small amounts of COF around them in a band covering the area the root system will reach in the coming weeks. As plants grow, widen the band. A typical side-dressing rate is roughly 1 cup per 10 row feet for medium-demand crops like beets.

## How COF Works Differently from Chemical Fertilizers

Chemical fertilizers dissolve rapidly in soil water, causing an immediate growth spike followed by a nutrient sag that requires repeated applications. Their nutrients are highly water-soluble and can leach beyond the root zone if over-applied or if heavy rain follows application. Most importantly, inexpensive chemical fertilizers (analyzing around 12-12-12) are nutritionally incomplete — they lack calcium, magnesium, and trace minerals.

COF, like other organic amendments, releases nutrients only as soil microorganisms decompose the organic materials. This slow release means nutrients become available gradually over approximately two months in warm soil, matching the pace at which vegetables can absorb them. The decomposition rate roughly doubles for each 10°F increase in soil temperature. This biological release mechanism virtually eliminates leaching losses and provides steadier nutrition.

## Nutritional Impact on Food Quality

Solomon reports that vegetables grown with COF contain far more human nutrition — more protein, vitamins, enzymes, and minerals — than vegetables grown by any other method. This claim is supported by USDA data showing that the mineral content of American vegetables has declined about 2 to 3 percent per year over the past 25 years, as chemical fertilizers have displaced more complete organic fertility sources.

## Cost-Effectiveness

In 2005, Solomon's annual COF expenditure for a substantial garden came to under $300, which he estimated produced approximately $4,000 worth of vegetables at supermarket prices. Even accounting for the unfairness of that comparison (homegrown produce is superior in quality and freshness), the return on investment is exceptional. If COF ingredients become unavailable, Solomon notes that making high-quality compost supplemented with only a couple bags of lime (roughly $20) can produce nearly comparable results, albeit with significantly more labor.

## Fertigation and Foliar Feeding

For gardeners with irrigation systems, dissolving COF (or similar soluble organic materials) in irrigation water — a practice called fertigation — can boost results. Where water is too scarce for fertigation, foliar spraying with diluted fish emulsion and kelp tea provides a smaller but still meaningful benefit. Farmers in India discovered that half-strength Coca-Cola makes a surprisingly effective foliar spray due to its phosphoric acid and sugar content.

## Solubility and Soil Interactions

The ingredients in COF differ in their solubility rates. Seedmeals decompose over approximately two months in warm soil, providing a sustained nitrogen release. Rock phosphate and bone meal are very slow to dissolve — they become available to plants over years rather than weeks, which is why they must be incorporated into the soil rather than left on the surface. Lime and gypsum are moderately soluble and become available within a growing season. Kelpmeal decomposes at an intermediate rate.

This staggered release pattern is actually advantageous: nitrogen becomes available first (supporting leaf growth), followed by potassium and trace minerals (supporting fruiting and overall plant health), while phosphorus accumulates in the soil over successive seasons, building long-term fertility. Solomon notes that after several years of annual COF application, the soil develops a substantial phosphorus bank that can support demanding crops even if COF application is temporarily reduced.

## Application Timing

While Solomon recommends applying COF once annually in spring, the exact timing matters. Apply too early and some nitrogen may be lost before plants can use it. Apply too late and the slow decomposition means nutrients will not be available when seedlings need them most. The ideal timing is one to two weeks before the expected planting date for the main crop, allowing enough time for the initial microbial activation of the organic materials while minimizing nutrient losses.

In mild climates with year-round growing seasons, a split application may be beneficial: two-thirds of the annual COF quantity in early spring for the main summer crops, and one-third in late summer for fall and winter crops.

## Ingredient Substitutions and Availability

One of COF's strengths is its flexibility. If a specific ingredient is unavailable, reasonable substitutions exist:

- **Rock phosphate unavailable**: Use bone meal or high-phosphate guano instead. All three provide phosphorus, though at different release rates and with different trace mineral profiles.
- **Kelpmeal too expensive**: Basalt dust provides similar trace mineral content at lower cost. Azomite or other rock dusts can also substitute, though their exact mineral profiles differ.
- **No seedmeal available**: Use bloodmeal (much more concentrated — use at one-quarter the rate) or fish meal. Both are more expensive but effective.
- **Cannot obtain lime**: This is the hardest substitution to make. Vegetables require calcium and magnesium. Without lime, consider using crushed eggshells (very slow release) or calcium-rich wood ashes (but these raise pH rapidly and should be used sparingly).

Solomon emphasizes buying ingredients in bulk from agricultural supply stores rather than in small packages from garden centers. The price difference is often five to ten times per unit, and the ingredients store well when kept cool and dry.

## Interaction with Compost and Manure

COF is designed to supplement — not replace — organic matter additions. The ideal fertility program combines:

1. Annual incorporation of compost or well-rotted manure for humus, soil structure, and biological activity
2. COF for balanced mineral nutrition
3. Occasional green manure crops for nitrogen fixation and organic matter addition

When using both COF and substantial quantities of manure, Solomon advises reducing the COF rate by about one-quarter to avoid over-fertilization, particularly with nitrogen. Poultry manure is especially potent and should be used cautiously in combination with COF.
