# Neurotropic Fungi Taxonomy

Neurotropic fungi — also known as hallucinogenic, psychotropic, magic, sacred, psychedelic, or entheogenic mushrooms — encompass a diverse array of fungal species known for their effects on the central nervous system. The comprehensive taxonomic survey by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (2000) catalogued **216 known species** of neurotropic fungi worldwide, organized into four principal groups based on their chemical constituents and ethnographic significance.

## The Four Groups of Neurotropic Fungi

### Group 1: Psilocybin-Containing Fungi

The largest and most well-studied group comprises fungi containing **psilocybin** and related indole alkaloids (psilocin, baeocystin, norbaeocystin). This group contains the vast majority of neurotropic species, spanning 13 genera:

- **Psilocybe** — 116 species (54% of all neurotropic fungi)
- **Gymnopilus** — 14 species
- **Panaeolus** — 13 species
- **Copelandia** — 12 species
- **Hypholoma** — 6 species
- **Pluteus** — 6 species
- **Inocybe** — 6 species
- **Conocybe** — 4 species
- **Panaeolina** — 4 species
- **Gerronema** — 2 species
- **Agrocybe** — 1 species
- **Galerina** — 1 species
- **Mycena** — 1 species

It is important to note that for several species in this group — particularly among the Panaeoloideous fungi — no direct chemical studies have confirmed the presence of psilocybin. Their inclusion is based on taxonomic proximity to verified psychoactive species and circumstantial evidence of traditional use.

### Group 2: Ibotenic Acid-Containing Fungi

This smaller group contains species producing **ibotenic acid** and **muscimol** as their primary psychoactive compounds. The chemical pathway differs substantially from the tryptamine-based psilocybin group:

- **Amanita muscaria** — the iconic fly agaric, widely recognized by its red cap with white spots
- **Amanita pantherina** — the panther cap, known for its higher toxicity profile
- **Amanita regalis** — the brown king bolete agaric, less commonly encountered

These Amanita species are historically significant as sacred fungi among Siberian peoples, particularly the Chukchee and Koryak, and were documented by Wasson and Wasson (1957). Unlike psilocybin-containing species, Amanita intoxication can be genuinely dangerous due to the narrow margin between psychoactive and toxic doses.

### Group 3: Ergot Fungi

The ergot group comprises **Claviceps** and **Cordyceps** species that produce ergoline alkaloids, including ergotamine and lysergic acid derivatives:

- **Claviceps** — 5 species, most notably *C. purpurea*, the causal agent of ergotism
- **Cordyceps** — 2 species with documented neurotropic properties

These fungi are fundamentally different from the mushroom-forming species in Groups 1 and 2. Claviceps species are pathogenic on grasses and cereals, producing sclerotia (ergot bodies) rather than typical mushroom fruiting bodies. Their alkaloids have profound pharmacological significance as precursors to LSD and various pharmaceutical compounds.

### Group 4: Sacred Fungi Without Confirmed Chemistry

The fourth group encompasses fungi traditionally used in sacred or ceremonial contexts for which **no reliable chemical studies** have confirmed neurotropic properties:

- **Boletes** — two genera with 8 species total
- **Russula** — 6 species
- **Gasteromycetes** — 6 species across 3 genera (Lycoperdales and Phallales)

Heim (1963, 1966, 1978) initially reported certain boletes as hallucinogenic among New Guinea aborigines, but Ott (1993) later argued these species lack genuine neurotropic properties. The inclusion of puffballs (Lycoperdon species) in this category has been particularly controversial — Heim and Wasson (1962) reported *L. mixtecorum* and *L. marginatum* as narcotic among Mixtec Indians, but Ott et al. (1975) demonstrated these were common edible species likely confused with traditional psilocybin-containing mushrooms.

## Taxonomic Controversies and Confusion

Several factors complicate the taxonomy of neurotropic fungi:

### Misidentification in Traditional Use
Indigenous peoples often use the same common names for multiple fungal species. The Mazatec term "nise" (little bird) refers to both *Psilocybe mexicana* and the unrelated *[[guzman-chlorophyllum-molybdites-toxic-edible-paradox-yoruba-divinatory-use]]* illustrates this problem vividly. Called "a jegba ariwo-orun" (eat and hear voices from heaven) by the Yoruba tribe, experimental evidence from Adewusi et al. (1993) suggested neurotropic properties. However, this species is widely regarded as poisonous, with reports of poisoning from the USA, Argentina, Philippines, and East Africa. Singer (1969) noted that "apparently not all forms or races are poisonous," suggesting possible regional variation.

### Polypore Misclassification
Oldridge et al. (1989) classified polypores containing hordenine, N-methyltyramine, and tyramine as psychotropic fungi, citing *[[meripilus-giganteus]]*. These compounds can cause dizziness and disorientation but are fundamentally different from classical hallucinogens. Both species are widely consumed as edible mushrooms, and their inclusion as neurotropic fungi is not broadly accepted.
