Rust fungi (Class Urediniomycetes, Phylum Basidiomycota) comprise the largest and most ubiquitous group of obligately biotrophic fungi on vascular plants. The name "rust" is descriptive, referring to the rusty orange to brownish color of spores emerging en masse from pustules on the host plant. Most rust pustules measure only a few millimeters in diameter, although rusts associated with stem cankers on trees may sporulate in contiguous areas more than a meter long.

## Obligate Biotrophy and Host Dependence

Rust fungi are unable to grow saprobically in nature and thus are found only in environments suitable for their hosts. Their relationships with host plants, on which they are completely dependent, are highly specialized and reflect a long history of coevolution. Obligate biotrophs produce either intercellular or extracellular mycelia, often with specialized feeding structures called haustoria that penetrate and extract nutrients from the host plant. They require a living host, cannot survive on dead organic matter, and rarely are cultured in vitro.

## Taxonomy and Diversity

An estimated 168 rust genera and approximately 7,000 species are currently accepted, with more than half belonging to the genus Puccinia. While the majority of species in temperate regions of the northern hemisphere, Australia, and New Zealand have been well catalogued, many new genera and species are still expected to be found in tropical and subtropical regions such as South America, Africa, and Southeastern Asia. Buritica and Hennen collected 100 species of rusts in tropical South America, 20% of which were new taxa, suggesting vast underexploration.

## Economically Important Species

Although rust infections are not generally fatal to plants, they may severely limit growth and fruiting ability. Several species are notorious for their economic impact on agriculture and forestry:

- [[wheat-stem-rust-fungus|Wheat stem rust]] (Puccinia graminis)
- Coffee rust (Hemileia vastatrix)
- White pine blister rust (Cronartium ribicola)

These pathogens cause billions of dollars in crop losses annually and have shaped agricultural practices and breeding programs worldwide.

## Spore Stages and Life Cycle Complexity

Rust fungi can produce up to five different spore types (spermagonia, aecia, uredinia, telia, and basidia) in successive stages of reproduction. The various types of spores usually are produced at different times, although they sometimes may be found simultaneously on the same host. This remarkable complexity distinguishes rusts from most other fungal groups.

## Heteroecious and Autoecious Rusts

Some rust species require several seasons and two unrelated species of host plants, often in different families or orders, to complete their life cycles. In these heteroecious rusts, a spore stage produced on one host is only capable of infecting the other (alternate) host. A particular spore stage often has a narrow range of hosts and may be limited to one or two congeneric plant species.

Autoecious rusts, by contrast, complete their entire life cycle on a single host species. Species with reduced life cycles (microcyclic rusts) are more common in cool temperate to subarctic regions, where a short growing season selects for rusts that can survive perennially on the telial host.

## Seasonal Patterns and Detection

In temperate regions, aecial stages are generally most prevalent in the spring, with uredinial states developing midseason. Telia develop in the fall, usually overwintering in dead host tissue. Unlike other plant pathogens, rusts usually infect healthy, vigorously growing plants, so if infections are small and limited to certain plant parts such as foliage, they may be difficult to detect.

## Symptoms and Signs

Plants with severe rust infections may appear stunted, chlorotic (yellowed), or otherwise discolored. Heavy uredinial sporulation may turn the affected plant part orange. Rusts sporulate on leaves, shoots, fruits, and woody stems. Perennial, systemic infections may cause deformities such as [[witches-broom-fungal-disease|witches brooms]], cankers, or galls. The telia of microcyclic rusts simulate the habit of the parental macrocyclic forms, adding further complexity to field identification.

## Collection and Study Considerations

Both new and old infected tissues should be collected in case spore stages of early and advanced infections differ. This is particularly important with rusts that produce all five spore stages on a single host. The low number of records from the tropics likely reflects insufficient study rather than genuine absence, making tropical rust mycology a particularly promising area for future research.
