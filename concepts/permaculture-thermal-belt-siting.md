# Permaculture Thermal Belt Siting

Permaculture thermal belt siting is the practice of placing houses, gardens, and sensitive crops in the midslope thermal belt zone of hills and mountains, where temperatures are most moderate and frost risk is minimized. This is one of the most powerful yet often overlooked strategies in permaculture land design.

[[permaculture-design]] identifies the thermal belt as a critical energy-conservation strategy in all but tropical climates. The principle is that on hillsides, the coldest air settles in valley bottoms at night while hilltops are exposed to wind chill. The zone between these extremes, typically on midslopes, provides the most favorable growing conditions.

## The Physics of Thermal Belts

Cold air is heavier (more dense) than heated air. At night, radiation cooling causes air near the ground surface to become cold and dense, flowing downhill like an invisible river to pool in valleys and hollows.

This cold air drainage means that valley floors and flat areas experience the most frequent and severe frosts. Hilltops, while somewhat warmer than valley floors, are exposed to advected (wind-carried) cold and experience greater wind chill effects.

The thermal belt lies in the middle zone, above the valley cold air pool and below the exposed hilltop. Here, cold air drains away before it can accumulate, and the slope provides protection from wind.

Sun-facing slopes (north-facing in the southern hemisphere, south-facing in the northern hemisphere) receive maximum solar radiation, adding to the thermal advantage of midslope positioning.

## Elevation of Thermal Belts

The elevation of the thermal belt varies dramatically depending on the scale of the landscape and the climate.

In hill country and major mountains, thermal belts may lie at 1000-5000 meters elevation. On lower hill slopes they occur at 100-200 meters above the valley floor.

In hot deserts, frost levels may only reach 10-15 meters up the slopes of mesas, meaning the thermal belt is quite close to the valley floor.

In gentler terrain, the thermal belt effect is more subtle but still measurable. Even a slope of just a few meters can create a noticeable temperature difference compared to the adjacent flat ground.

## Identifying Thermal Belts on Site

Each site needs specific information to locate its thermal belt accurately. [[permaculture-design]] methodology uses several approaches for this assessment.

Local anecdotes from long-term residents often provide the best guide to frost-free and frost-prone areas on a property.

Observation of existing vegetation is another powerful indicator. Frost-susceptible species growing in a particular zone indicate that zone is in the thermal belt.

Dense autumn fogs are a visible indicator of winter frost extent and can be observed from high vantage points to map cold air drainage patterns.

Trial plantings of frost-susceptible species provide direct evidence of thermal belt location and frost risk at specific points on a site.

## Siting Houses in the Thermal Belt

In cold areas, earthbanks plus shelterbelt combined with a sun-facing aspect is a critical design strategy for house placement. [[permaculture-building-design]] prioritizes thermal belt siting for energy efficiency.

Shelterbelt including thick vine trellis around a house can effect a 20-30% saving in heating fuels in moderate to severe winters. Combined with thermal belt position and passive solar design, this can dramatically reduce or eliminate heating costs.

The [[energy-efficient-house-design]] approach integrates thermal belt siting with insulation, thermal mass, and solar gain to create comfortable living spaces with minimal external energy input.

## Advected Cold and Warm Air

Winds travelling from warmer to cooler regions bring advected warmth and cold to local regions. Advection fogs occur when warm moist air comes inshore from coasts.

Advection frosts occur when cold air flows down mountain slopes to pool in hollows. Understanding the direction of prevailing winds helps predict where advected cold will affect the site.

The [[permaculture-climate-adaptation]] framework incorporates thermal belt analysis alongside wind pattern assessment for comprehensive site planning.

## Integration with Other Design Elements

Thermal belt siting interacts with other permaculture design elements. [[permaculture-earthworks]] such as swales on contour can slow cold air drainage and create micro-thermal belts on gentle slopes.

Water bodies placed in or near the thermal belt add thermal mass that further moderates temperature extremes. [[permaculture-dam-design]] can incorporate thermal belt positioning for frost protection of downslope gardens.

[[windbreak-and-shelterbelt-design]] at the top of slopes prevents cold wind from reaching the thermal belt zone while allowing cold air drainage to continue beneath the canopy.

The [[permaculture-zone-planning]] system places the most frost-sensitive elements (Zone 1 gardens and Zone 2 orchards) in the thermal belt where possible.

## Deserts and Tropical Exceptions

In tropical climates, siting in shade or in cooling coastal windstreams is preferred over thermal belt positioning. The goal shifts from maximizing warmth to moderating heat.

In deserts, where advected wind-carried heat is the most severe effect on human comfort, shelterbelt trees serve to reduce ground temperatures up to 15 degrees Celsius. Here the thermal belt principle is inverted, with valley floors being more hospitable than exposed slopes.

The [[permaculture-climate-zones]] framework helps designers understand when thermal belt siting applies and when other strategies take precedence.
