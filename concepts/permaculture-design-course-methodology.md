# Permaculture Design Course Methodology

## Overview

The Permaculture Design Course (PDC) is the foundational educational program
in permaculture, created by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren as a packaged
set of lessons and standardized terminology that allows complex ecological
concepts to be communicated to others. The curriculum was developed through
their work codifying concepts of ecologically sound agriculture alongside
pioneers like Sepp Holzer, Howard Odum, and Rudolph Steiner. The PDC has
become the gateway to permaculture practice worldwide.

## Origins and Development

Mollison and Holmgren observed that for eons, ecological systems of nature
have provided food systems that fed billions of beings without machinery,
artificial inputs, or excessive labor. These systems grew stronger, more
productive, and more robust over time. Modern agricultural systems, by
contrast, require increasing inputs and labor while becoming weaker, less
productive, and destructive to local ecology. Industrial agriculture is
inherently self-extincting; a culture based on these systems would be as
well.

Permaculture was modeled on nature's systems to create food production that
maintains itself indefinitely and becomes stronger over time. Holmgren and
Mollison expanded this understanding to include architecture, business
practices, and social norms, creating a system of [[pdc-urban-permaculture-design-principles]] that form the core
of PDC instruction. Understanding the underlying logic matters more than
memorizing exact wording:

1. **Apply self-regulation and accept feedback**: Nature self-regulates.
   Humans must consciously choose to regulate themselves within ecological
   limits. Without accepting feedback there can be no self-regulation, and
   without self-regulation there can be no ecological coherence.

2. **[[permaculture-principle-creatively-use-and-respond-to-change]]**: Change is constant. Rather
   than resisting it, [[permaculture-principle-design-from-patterns-to-details]] to details**: Begin with broad patterns (water
   flow, sun angles, wind patterns) before working on specific elements.
   The overall structure determines success more than individual components.

5. **[[holmgren-observe-and-interact-detailed]]**: Careful observation over time reveals patterns
   and relationships that inform better design decisions.

7. **[[holmgren-obtain-a-yield-detailed]]**: Every element should produce something useful. A
   system that does not yield is not sustainable.
