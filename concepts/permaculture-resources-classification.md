## Resource Classification in Permaculture

[[bill-mollison]]'s framework for classifying resources provides designers with an ethical and practical lens for evaluating what we use, how we use it, and what consequences follow. This classification is central to [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]] in any permaculture system.

## The "Everything Gardens" Principle

The foundation of permaculture's resource thinking begins with the observation that every organism modifies its environment. This principle — known as permaculture resources classification — recognizes that no living thing exists in passive relation to its surroundings. Every species acts as a gardener of sorts, shaping the world it inhabits through its biological activity.

Rabbits, for example, create burrows that aerate soils, produce defecation mounds that fertilize ground, scratch at roots disturbing plant communities, and through their grazing patterns create and maintain lawns. People build houses, dispose of sewage, and dig into soils — all acts of environmental modification. The critical insight is that organisms can function as **allies** in sustaining systems when their environmental effects are understood and directed rather than ignored or suppressed.

Understanding how every element in a system modifies its environment allows designers to place elements so that their "gardening" effects serve the broader system rather than undermining it.

## Defining Resources and Yield

In permaculture, a **resource** is defined as a useful reserve that has been translated and stored from incoming energies — whether from the sun, wind, rain, or other natural flows. Resources are not static deposits; they are the product of energy capture and conversion by living systems and geological processes.

**permaculture resources classification** is defined as the surplus produced by a system above and beyond what the system itself needs to maintain its functions. A healthy system produces yield that can be shared or harvested without degrading its capacity to continue producing. The concept of yield is inseparable from resource thinking — resources are the stores, and yield is what flows from those stores when they are well-managed.

## Ethics of Resource Use

Permaculture imposes a clear ethical framework on resource use. Resources are not merely commodities to be exploited; they carry obligations of care and stewardship. The ethical use of resources requires that designers consider:

- The source and renewability of any resource drawn upon
- The effects of extraction or use on the broader system
- Whether use preserves or degrades the resource base for future generations
- Whether alternatives exist that serve the same function with less impact

These ethical considerations lead directly into the classification system that follows.

## Two Categories of Ownership

Mollison distinguishes between two broad categories of resources based on their nature and rightful stewardship:

### Common Heritage Resources

These are resources that belong to all people and to future generations. They cannot ethically be privatized or monopolized. Common heritage resources include:

- **Forests** — standing ecosystems that provide for countless species and processes
- **Soils** — the living skin of the earth built over millennia
- **Air** — the atmosphere shared by every living organism
- **Water** — the hydrological cycle that sustains all life
- **Seeds** — the genetic heritage of plants, evolved over ages

These resources are held in trust. Their degradation or privatization is an ethical violation because they are the common foundation upon which all life and all human systems depend.

### Personal or Group Resources

These are resources that have been fabricated, developed, or improved through human labor and creativity. They include tools, buildings, artworks, bred varieties, and other products of deliberate human effort. Personal and group resources carry legitimate claims of ownership because they represent applied human energy and intelligence.

## The Five Resource Categories by Use-and-Results

The most practical dimension of Mollison's classification sorts resources by what happens to them when they are used. This framework guides designers toward resources that are beneficial or benign to use and away from those that are destructive.

### Category 1: Resources That Increase by Modest Use

These resources become more abundant or more valuable when they are used wisely. Examples include:

- **Green browse** — pruning stimulates new growth, increasing the overall productivity of the plant
- **Information** — sharing knowledge does not diminish it; it multiplies its value
- **Pasture** — properly grazed pasture is more productive than ungrazed
- **Seed** — planting seed leads to multiplication

These are the ideal resources for any system. Designers should seek to identify and maximize Category 1 resources wherever possible.

### Category 2: Resources Unaffected by Use

These resources remain stable regardless of whether or how they are used. They can be enjoyed without concern for depletion. Examples include:

- **Views** — looking at a landscape does not diminish it
- **Climate** — living within a climate does not degrade it
- **Well-managed ecosystems** — a balanced system maintains itself

Category 2 resources are benign and can be freely utilized as part of a design.

### Category 3: Resources That Disappear If Not Used

These are perishable or time-sensitive resources that are lost entirely if left unused. Examples include:

- **Annual crops** — if not harvested, they rot or reseed unpredictably
- **Grass** — uncut or ungrazed grass becomes rank and less productive
- **Fish irruptions** — periodic population surges that collapse if not harvested
- **Ripe fruit** — falls and spoils if not picked

Category 3 resources demand timely use. Systems should be designed so that these resources are captured and utilized rather than wasted.

### Category 4: Resources Reduced by Use

These resources are diminished with every use and may eventually be exhausted. Examples include:

- **Fish and game stocks** — overharvesting depletes populations
- **Clay** — extracted and used, finite in any given location
- **Forests** — timber harvesting reduces standing biomass
- **Coal and oil** — fossil fuels consumed and gone forever

Category 4 resources require careful management. Use should be limited, regeneration supported where possible, and alternatives sought. The goal is to minimize dependence on Category 4 resources and transition toward Categories 1 and 2.

### Category 5: Resources That Pollute If Used

These resources cause environmental harm simply by being used. Their application or consumption introduces negative effects into the system. Examples include:

- **Residual poisons** — pesticides, herbicides, and persistent toxic chemicals
- **Radioactives** — nuclear materials and waste
- **Highways** — paving over living landscapes
- **Concrete** — sealing soils and disrupting hydrology
- **Sewers** — concentrating and misdirecting nutrient flows

The permaculture policy on Category 5 resources is unequivocal: **they should be banned from use** wherever possible. No design should rely on or incorporate resources whose use inherently degrades the environment. This is not merely a preference but a principled stand grounded in the ethics of earth care.

## The Principle of "Enough"

Underlying the entire resource classification is the principle of **enough** — the recognition that there is a sufficiency point beyond which additional consumption does not improve well-being and often diminishes it. Permaculture designs around meeting genuine needs rather than maximizing extraction or consumption. "Enough" is not austerity; it is the intelligent calibration of use to actual need, ensuring that systems remain productive, diverse, and resilient over the long term.

The principle of enough connects directly to yield: a system that produces enough for its inhabitants while maintaining its health is a successful system. Surplus beyond "enough" can be shared, stored, or cycled back into the system, but it should not be pursued at the expense of Category 4 and 5 resource degradation.

## Summary

Mollison's resource classification provides a decision-making framework for designers:

| Category | Effect of Use | Design Response |
|----------|--------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Increases | Maximize and prioritize |
| 2 | Unaffected | Use freely |
| 3 | Lost if unused | Design for timely capture |
| 4 | Reduced | Minimize, manage carefully |
| 5 | Pollutes | Eliminate entirely |

By classifying resources this way and applying the principle of enough, permaculture designers create systems that are regenerative, ethical, and capable of sustained productivity over generations.
