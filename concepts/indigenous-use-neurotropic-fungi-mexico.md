# Indigenous Ceremonial Use of Neurotropic Fungi in Mexico

## Overview

Mexico possesses the world's richest tradition of ceremonial neurotropic fungi
use, with 76 neurotropic species — more than any other country. This tradition
spans multiple indigenous groups across the central and southern regions,
including the Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Chinantec, Trique, Nahua, and others.
The practice was documented by Spanish chroniclers as early as the 16th century
and rediscovered by [[fukuoka-road-critique-of-modern-science]] in the 1950s.

## Historical Documentation

### The Spanish Chronicles

The earliest known documentation comes from **Bernardino de Sahagún** (1569–1582),
who recorded the Nahuatl word **"teonanácatl"** — literally "[[edaphic-factors]]" or
"flesh of the gods." This term has since been indiscriminately applied to all
Mexican hallucinogenic fungi, though it originally referred to specific species.

### Rediscovery by Modern Science

**[[richard-evans-schultes]]** (1939) was the first modern researcher to take
serious interest in the [[gartz-wasson-mexican-mushroom-cult-rediscovery]] cults. His work was followed by:

- **R. [[gordon-wasson-comparative-method-ethnomycology]]** and **[[shroom-cultural-history-western-discovery-valentina-wasson]]** — whose 1957 *Life* magazine
  article brought the topic to global attention
- **Roger Heim** — French mycologist who cultivated several species in Paris
- **Rolf Singer** — who conducted extensive taxonomic work
- **Gastón Guzmán** — who became the world's foremost authority on *Psilocybe*

## Indigenous Groups and Their Practices

### Mazatec (Oaxaca)

The Mazatec are perhaps the best-known mushroom-using group, largely due to the
work of Wasson and Heim. María Sabina, a Mazatec curandera, became famous
after allowing Wasson to participate in a *velada* (night ceremony) in 1955.

The Mazatec use *[[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]]* (called "nise" — little bird) and
occasionally *P. caerulescens*. Confusingly, they also call *Schizophyllum
commune* by the same name "nise," though this species has no neurotropic
properties — a case of shared nomenclature without shared pharmacology.

### Mixtec (Oaxaca)

The Mixtec have a particularly complex fungal pharmacopeia. They use:
- Standard psilocybin-containing *Psilocybe* species
- *Lycoperdon* puffballs (*L. mixtecorum*, *L. marginatum*) — though Ott et al.
  (1975) showed these are actually mixtures of at least nine non-neurotropic
  species, probably confused with active species collected simultaneously
- *Scleroderma verrucosum* — experimentally proven poisonous, yet used
  ceremonially

### Trique (Oaxaca, Alta Mixteca)

The Trique use *Cordyceps* species in special **nocturnal ceremonies**, eaten
either alone or together with *[[psilocybe-muliercula]]*. A specimen of *Elaphomyces*
(truffle) — the host fungus of the *Cordyceps* — is placed in the center of the
ceremony room as a "director." The Trique also use *Elaphomyces* species to
treat wounds and for "rejuvenation of the organism."
