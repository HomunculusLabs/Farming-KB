# Permaculture Designers Manual - Grazing Animals

updated: 2026-04-18

Grazing animals -- sheep, goats, cattle, deer, and their range systems -- are covered in Sections 12.12-12.14 of Chapter 12, covering grasslands, pastures, lawns, and rangelands. Mollison presents grazing as a skilled management task requiring constant observation, in stark contrast to factory farm systems regulated by recipes and automation.

## Grasslands (Section 12.13)

Perennial grasslands (veldt, prairie, meadow, steppe, herbal ley, pasture, heathlands) are a feature of mesothermal climates. Many are treeless areas established during periods of icecap retreat that have never established as forests, though both soils and climate would permit forest establishment. Others are maintained by fire against forest invasion.

Many aboriginal peoples managed their environments to maintain herd species, berry crop, nettle crop, or fire-succession ecology for food supply. The management of natural grasslands by fire is skilled; too frequent or too hot fires destroy soil structure and tip the balance from perennial bunch grasses to fire-loving annuals of low nutrient value.

### Biomass Comparison

Natural grasslands are very stable systems. While a forest may have a visible standing crop of 207 t/ha and grasses only 7 t, below ground forests produce 184 t/ha of organic matter compared to 345 t/ha for natural prairie. Grasses and their associates act more on the near soil surface, producing copious litter and shallow root masses (195 t/ha in top 30 cm versus a forest's 106 t/ha).

The critical difference is regeneration time: decades for trees versus weeks for grasses. Grasses offer for our use only a little part of what they have, while forests make most of themselves visible and available.

## The Lawn Problem (Section 12.12)

Mollison delivers a sharp critique of lawn culture, estimating 16 million acres devoted to lawn in the USA by 1978, requiring 573 kilocalories per square metre to maintain -- more than corn or vegetables. Lawns used 15-20% of annual US fertiliser production, equal to total Indian food agriculture. In California, 44% of domestic water consumption serves lawns.

Perhaps 13% of lawns have any use in recreation, sport, or rest. Most are purely cosmetic. Mollison states: "every society that grows extensive lawns could produce all its food on the same area using the same resources, and world famine could be totally relieved if we devoted the resources of lawn culture to food culture in poor areas."

## Rangelands (Section 12.14)

Brown (1972) estimates standing crop of large herbivores on the African savannah at about 500 kg/ha, similar to improved temperate pastures. Instead of one or two species sustained by oil energy, the savannah hosts guilds of browsers (5 species), grazers (11 species), and 4 species that alternately graze or browse, each with specific food preferences.

### Stock Units

The standard stock unit is a European cow and calf (450 kg liveweight): 2 African cows; 5 European sheep; 20 African sheep; 25 Thompson gazelles; 125 dikdik antelope. Ten standard stock units equal 1 elephant; 4 black rhino; 15 zebras; or 27 hartebeest.

### Range Diet Balance

Animals balance their range diets in different ways, requiring variety of species for full utilisation:
- Elk: 65% browse, grasses, mosses, 3% forbs
- Antelope: 74% browse, 4% grasses, 22% forbs

Some animals are sedentary, some range widely. Deer may utilise 92% of range area while cattle exploit only 52%. Deer convert food to flesh at efficiencies 2-3 times greater than cattle.

Cattle range only 3-7 km from water; deer, kangaroo, goats, and small antelope can exist without free surface water except in extreme drought.

### Multi-Species Grazing

A judicious selection of animal species enables far greater production from range than one species alone. Different species feed at different browse levels. One European cow browse year equals 6 sheep, 18 kangaroo, or 385 ground squirrels.

Kangaroo has far higher assimilable protein content (58%) than cattle (35%), making them more productive per unit from a protein standpoint. A judicious balance of species will always exceed a monoculture in yield and keep rangeland in better productive condition.

## Rangeland Degradation

The plant cover on range profoundly affects water infiltration:
- 5% vegetative cover: 1.2% rainfall soaks in (first 30 minutes)
- 15% cover: 1.3% soaks in
- 25% cover: 1.7% soaks in
- 35% cover: 2.4% soaks in

After 9 years of stocking, Stoddart (1955) reports soil pore space was reduced 44% in the top two inches and 60% in the lower 24-inch layer. Infiltration is correspondingly reduced and runoff increased with resultant soil loss.

Any species not harvested (or where predators are removed) can expand to damage rangelands. Canadian workers found direct correlation between range depletion and grasshopper outbreaks. Rangelands infested with grasshoppers, plague locusts, or mormon crickets can lose 67% of browse to insects.

## Design for Sustainable Grazing

Key principles:
- Maintain diverse species assemblages rather than monoculture
- Provide critical resources (water, shelter) to enable species to utilise the full range
- Use multi-species grazing to utilise all browse levels
- Preserve wetlands and ponds for insect control and waterfowl
- Rest paddocks to allow recovery; rotational systems essential
- Monitor ground cover, soil pore space, and preferred species indicator
- Match stocking rates to seasonal carrying capacity
- Include insectivorous birds, fish, and waterfowl in range management for plague control
