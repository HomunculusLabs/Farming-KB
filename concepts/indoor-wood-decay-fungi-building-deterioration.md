---# Indoor Wood-Decay Fungi and Building Deterioration

Wood-decay fungi that colonize buildings represent a significant threat to
built structures worldwide. Among these, the dry [[meruliporia-incrassata]] causes extensive [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], while [[bioremediation-fenton-chemistry-brown-rot-fungi]] form two types of root-like structure for colonization and
water transport:

- **Strands (chords)**: develop as assemblages of hyphae with a thick
  'parent' hypha at the core, surrounded by thinner hyphae. Strands begin
  developing in response to nutrient exhaustion and act as exploratory
  structures sent out from the colony in search of new food sources. Serpula
  forms strands but does not produce true rhizomorphs.

- **Rhizomorphs**: larger, waterproofed structures that enclose a central
  pipe for transmission of water and dissolved nutrients. Rhizomorphs can
  grow for many metres, piping water from wet soil at their base to their
  tip buried in dry wood or masonry. Air can also pass along rhizomorphs,
  allowing the fungus to avoid suffocation while exploring anoxic interiors.
  Meruliporia generates both strands and rhizomorphs.

Once in a basement, hyphae of dry rot fungi digest cellulose walls, transforming
springy lumber into parched brown cubes that crumble when touched (brown
cubical rot). The brown colour results from progressive concentration of
dark-pigmented lignin left behind as the fungus digests cellulose. A single
well-established mycelium can colonize an entire home without forming a single
spore if it can conduct enough water through rhizomorphs.

## Fruiting Bodies and Spore Production

In advanced decay, basidiomes as large as surfboards burgeon as crusts on beam
surfaces. These structures are yellow to orange-brown with a white margin and
produce massive quantities of rusty spores. A single healthy basidiome can
shed billions of spores. Fruiting bodies can extend along entire beams,
creating colossal platforms for spore production. Individual fruiting bodies
measuring 6 square metres have been documented, with total spore-producing
surfaces of 78 square metres in a single property.

## Historical Impact

Dry rot has caused catastrophic damage throughout history. Samuel Pepys,
as Secretary of Admiralty Affairs in the 1680s, discovered that many Royal
Navy ships 'lie in danger of sinking at their very Moorings', with planks
'perished to powder'. The average ship life fell from 25-30 years in the
seventeenth century to 12 years in the eighteenth century, and to 'no
duration' after the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805. The 110-gun battleship
Queen Charlotte deteriorated so swiftly during construction that the navy
was forced to rebuild her in 1810 before she could set sail, at a cost that
would translate to approximately US$2.5 billion today.

The dry rot epidemic was linked to the use of imported unseasoned timber from
Eastern Europe, often soaked for weeks by floating in rivers or already
rotten on arrival. The possible biblical reference to dry rot appears in
Leviticus 14:33-53, describing a 'fretting leprosy of the house' with
greenish or reddish hollow streaks in walls.
