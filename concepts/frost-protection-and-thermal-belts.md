### Why the Thermal Belt Works

- Sun-facing slopes absorb more heat during the day
- Cold air drains downhill at night, away from midslope positions
- The belt sits above valley frost pockets but below exposed hilltops

### Thermal Belt Altitudes by Region

### Locating the Thermal Belt

Each situation requires specific local information, obtainable through:
- **Local anecdotes** and traditional knowledge from long-term residents
- **Observation of existing vegetation** — frost-sensitive species indicate the thermal belt
- **Trial plantings** of frost-susceptible species as bioindicators

## Design Principles for Frost-Prone Sites

### Site Selection
- In subtropical to cool climates, choose house sites that maximise solar radiation
- Position housing and gardens in the thermal belt on sun-facing slopes
- In tropical climates, site in shade or cooling coastal windstreams instead

### Plant Selection
- Trees with high biomass store heat in their mass (which is mainly water)
- Fairly small clearings within forest may be frost-free even in cold climates
- **Dark evergreen trees** absorb and radiate heat effectively — choose these for cool districts
- **White-barked, shiny, or light-coloured trees** reflect heat — useful on forest edges and where light itself is a limiting factor

### Heat Storage Materials
- **Water and stone** are excellent heat storages due to high specific heat
- Bodies of water moderate temperature extremes
- **Air** has low specific heat and is a poor conductor — therefore an excellent insulator
- Most insulation systems work by trapping air (cork, sawdust, wood fibre)

### Community and Plant Health
Areas of severe direct or diffuse radiation — especially where atmosphere is thin (mountains) or albedo is high (snow, granite, white sand, hayfields in summer) — can produce:
- Severe radiation burns on plants
- Skin cancers in humans
- Temporary or permanent blindness

Young plants must be screened against sunburn by partial shade and white paint on stems. Older plants may suffer bark damage but will generally survive.

### Cross-Ventilation Systems
Excess heat in one area of a house can be used in arid and tropical areas to drive a cross-ventilation system, essential in humid tropics for cool dry air intake. Designers should always look for opportunities to:
- Convert light to heat
- Reflect more heat onto cool areas
- Light dark areas by reflection or skylight placement
- Store heat below insulated slab floors
