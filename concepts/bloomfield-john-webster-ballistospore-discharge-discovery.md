## The Egg Hunter of Dartmoor

John Webster, a mycologist at the University of Exeter, was the
scientist who finally solved one of mycology's most enduring puzzles:
the mechanism by which mushrooms launch their spores. His field
work on Dartmoor in Devon, England, provided the raw material for
decades of laboratory investigation. Webster visited a favorite
collecting site on the edge of the moors every year, tramping up
steep slopes through pine needles damp with morning mist, searching
for specimens for his undergraduate classes.

had eluded explanation since the microscopic structure of the
basidium was first described. Victor Fayod, a French scientist,
noted in 1889 that a droplet of fluid formed at the base of each
spore before discharge, but more than a century of subsequent
research failed to explain how this droplet propelled the spore from
its attachment point on the basidium. The problem was not merely
academic: understanding spore discharge was essential to explaining
how mushrooms colonize new substrates, spread disease, and maintain
their vast geographical distributions.

A. H. Reginald Buller, professor of botany at the University of
Manitoba, had devoted much of his career to the problem. His
seven-volume masterwork Researches on Fungi remains one of the
monumental achievements of mycological literature. Buller described

vaulting from a cliff edge and almost instantaneously reaching a

## Legacy and Influence

Webster's elucidation of the ballistospore [[bloomfield-fungal-biology-and-hyphal-growth]]. The
discovery unified decades of fragmentary observations into a coherent
physical model and opened the door to quantitative studies of spore
dispersal in field conditions. His laboratory at Exeter became a
center for mycological research, producing numerous students who
carried his rigorous field-and-laboratory approach into their own
careers. The story of the spore discharge mechanism, from Fayod's
initial observation of the droplet in 1889 through Buller's
pioneering researches to Webster's final resolution, spans more
than a century and exemplifies how seemingly simple biological
questions can resist explanation for generations until the right
combination of patience, technology, and insight converges.
