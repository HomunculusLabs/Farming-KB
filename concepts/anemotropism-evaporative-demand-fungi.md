# Anemotropism and Evaporative Demand in Fungi

Anemotropism is the growth response of an organism to wind or air currents. In fungi, this tropism has been most thoroughly studied in the sporangiophores of *Phycomyces blakesleeanus* and the basidiocarps of *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]*. The prevailing mechanistic explanation involves differential evaporative demand across the fungal structure, driven by asymmetric boundary layer redistribution in the presence of airflow.

## The Boundary Layer Mechanism

When wind flows past a cylindrical structure like a mushroom stipe, it creates an asymmetric boundary layer:

- **Windward side**: The boundary layer is thin, and airflow remains close to the surface, increasing the rate of moisture evaporation
- **Leeward side**: The boundary layer is thicker and more turbulent, reducing the effective evaporative demand

This asymmetry creates a **moisture gradient** across the stipe surface. The cells on the windward side experience greater drying, which appears to stimulate differential growth rates. The result is a bending curvature — in *Psilocybe cubensis*, the stipe curves **toward** the wind (into the airflow).

## The Four-Factor Evaporative Demand Model

The differential evaporative demand driving anemotropism is a function of four interacting environmental variables:

1. **Wind velocity**: The primary driver; establishes the boundary layer asymmetry
2. **Humidity**: Higher ambient humidity reduces the overall evaporative demand magnitude but does not eliminate the gradient
3. **Light**: Can heat the mushroom surface, increasing local evaporation; may become the dominant factor of evaporative demand if sufficiently strong
4. **Temperature**: Affects the capacity of air to hold moisture and the rate of evaporation from the fungal surface

Badham (1982) demonstrated this in *P. cubensis* using a closed-circuit wind tunnel (43 × 30 × 20 cm working area) with controlled conditions: 9 ± 3 cm/sec wind velocity, 94 ± 3% relative humidity, 24.5 ± 1.5°C, and cool white fluorescent lighting at 2685 lux.

## Parallel in Phycomyces

The zygomycete *Phycomyces blakesleeanus* exhibits both anemotropism and an avoidance response (bending away from barriers), and the mechanisms appear related:

- **Johnson and Gamow (1971)** demonstrated that wind and water vapor are both necessary for these tropic responses
- **Cohen et al. (1975)** characterized the full range of sporangiophore responses to environmental stimuli
- **Russo et al. (1977)** suggested that **ethylene** may mediate the autochemotropic component, where the sporangiophore responds to gases it produces

The stipe curvature mechanism in *Psilocybe cubensis* is thought to be analogous, though the potential role of ethylene in [[basidiomycetes]] has not been confirmed.

## Evidence from Rotation Experiments

Critical evidence for the boundary layer mechanism comes from rotation experiments:

- When *P. cubensis* primordia were rotated perpendicular to the airflow at 3 rpm, stipe growth was **upward** (negatively geotropic) rather than anemotropic
- When rotated parallel to the airflow at 3 rpm, stipe elongation and pileus expansion occurred **horizontally**, into the wind
- Horizontal rotation equalizes the gravitational stimulus, revealing that in the absence of a constant directional gravity cue, the mushroom responds to the evaporative gradient

This demonstrates that the anemotropic response is masked by geotropism under normal conditions and can be unmasked by rotating the organism to neutralize gravity.

## The Role of Light in Evaporative Tropisms

Light's influence on [[mushroom-tropisms]] may operate through two pathways:

1. **Direct evaporative effect**: Light heats the illuminated surface, increasing evaporation. However, at experimental flux densities (~1/100th of field conditions), this direct effect was minimal
2. **Hormonal triggering**: Small quantities of light may trigger hormonal responses that alter growth patterns — this is the more likely mechanism for [[phototropism]] at low light levels

If light acts primarily through evaporative demand, then phototropism and anemotropism share the same fundamental mechanism, with different stimuli (radiative heating vs. convective drying) creating analogous moisture gradients across the stipe.

## Force vs. Evaporative Hypotheses

An alternative to the boundary layer/evaporative demand model is that the mushroom responds directly to the **mechanical force** of wind:

- Dennison (1961) showed that *Phycomyces* sporangiophores respond to both gravity and centrifugal force
- Physical bending of the stipe by wind pressure could theoretically trigger a thigmotropic or stress-response growth pattern

However, the evaporative demand hypothesis is generally favored because:
- It explains the interaction with humidity and temperature
- It accounts for the phototropic component through a shared mechanism
- It is consistent with the demonstrated role of water vapor in *Phycomyces* responses

## Adaptive Significance of Anemotropism

Anemotropic growth serves two primary ecological functions:

1. **Optimizing [[spore-dispersal]]**: By growing into the airflow, the stipe extends the pileus into the wind stream, maximizing the distance and efficiency of spore release
2. **Spacing cespitose fruiting bodies**: In clustered (cespitose) growth, directed growth toward evaporative demand helps separate individual stipes, reducing competition and improving spore dispersal from each cap

The transition from anemotropic to geotropic growth at maturity ensures that the final pileus orientation is horizontal — ideal for basidiospore release from downward-facing lamellae.
