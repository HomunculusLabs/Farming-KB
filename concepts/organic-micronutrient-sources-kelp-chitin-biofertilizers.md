# Organic Micronutrient Sources, Kelp, Chitin, and Biofertilizers

## Overview

While macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium) dominate most fertilizer discussions, micronutrients are equally essential for plant health. Organic gardening relies on natural sources of these trace minerals, with kelp products and chitin-rich materials being among the most versatile options. Additionally, a growing category of inputs known as biofertilizers harnesses living microorganisms to produce or mobilize nutrients directly in the soil.

## Phosphorus Sources in Organic Systems

### Animal Bone Meal (N–P–K 3–15–0)

Bone meal is produced by steam-processing and grinding animal bones. The phosphorus it contains is very readily available and persists for 1 to 4 months in the soil. A critical limitation is that bone meal does not work effectively unless soil pH is below 7.0. Labels may list either phosphorus or phosphate content (phosphate being the norm). Bone meal can attract animals until the odor dissipates, typically 1 to 2 weeks after application.

### Bat Guano (N–P–K 3–10–1)

Bat guano (feces and urine of cave-dwelling bats) comes in high-nitrogen and high-phosphorus formulations. Because phosphorus is rapidly immobilized in soil, high-phosphorus bat guano lasts only 1 to 4 months. Mycorrhizal fungi are the best releasers of phosphorus from guano, but excessive phosphorus application limits mycorrhizal colonization. Gardeners relying on mycorrhizal fungi should inform their [[solomon-soil-testing-laboratory-analysis-low-input-gardening]] so recommendations account for this biological pathway. Guano is best used when a testing laboratory specifically recommends it as a phosphorus source, particularly in new gardens without prior preparation time.

### Colloidal Rock Phosphate (N–P–K 0–2.5–0)

Also called soft rock phosphate, this material consists of clay particles surrounded by phosphate. The clay component improves the soil's cation and anion exchange capacities. Colloidal rock phosphate releases phosphorus slowly over years — some gardeners report it works better in the second and third years after application. It remains available for 3 to 5 years depending on rainfall and watering patterns. Placement is critical: rock phosphates perform best when positioned where roots will directly intercept the particles, as they rely on mycorrhizal hyphae or diffusion to reach root surfaces.

### Crab Shell Meal (N–P–K 2–3–0)

A less common but valuable phosphorus source, crab shell meal also provides calcium, trace elements, and chitin. The chitin content makes it an excellent fungal food and can help control high nematode populations. Chitin stimulates chitinolytic bacteria and fungi in the soil, which can suppress plant-parasitic nematodes by degrading their eggshells.

## Potassium Sources

### Greensand (N–P–K 0–0–7)

Also known as New Jersey Greensand or glauconite, this ancient seabed mineral contains over thirty elements including calcium, magnesium, iron, and other micronutrients alongside potassium. It requires weathering to release nutrients, making it slow-acting with a 2 to 3 year effective duration.

### Wood Ashes (N–P–K 0–1–3)

Used for centuries as a potassium source (historically called "pot ash"), wood ashes are free for those with fireplaces. Care is essential: ash from treated charcoal or chemically treated wood must be avoided due to harmful residue. Ashes are alkaline and will raise soil pH, so they should not be applied to already-alkaline soils. Effective duration is 1 to 2 months.

### Sulfate of Potash (N–P–K 0–0–2)

Often labeled Sul-Po-Mag, this mineral salt is highly water-soluble and contains 23% sulfur, 22% potash, and 11% magnesium. Some organic gardeners consider it borderline because it doesn't add organic bulk to soils. Its solubility makes it fast-acting but short-lived (approximately 2 months).

## Calcium Sources

### Calcitic Limestone (N–P–K 0–0–0)

Derived from sedimentary rock containing calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), calcitic limestone breaks down when powdered and exposed to water and carbon dioxide (which form carbonic acid). Duration in soil depends heavily on pH, organic matter, and clay content. A practical limitation is that pH can only be raised about one point per growing season. Autumn application is optimal for the following growing season.

### Dolomitic Limestone (N–P–K 0–0–0)

Dolomite (CaMg[CO₃]₂) provides both calcium and approximately 10% magnesium. However, it tends to bind soil particles, and excess magnesium can impair uptake of other nutrients. Soil testing should confirm actual magnesium deficiency before application. As with calcitic limestone, efficacy relates to existing pH, water, and CO₂ levels.

## Micronutrient Sources

### Shrimp Shell Meal (N–P–K 5–8–15)

Made from ground shrimp heads and shells, this material provides phosphorus, potassium, approximately 15% calcium, and about 20% chitin. Ocean-derived, it contains abundant trace elements. As a slow-release fertilizer, it persists for 3 to 6 months.

### Kelp Meal (N–P–K 0–0–0 to 1–0–4)

Kelp meal is made from seaweed and contains up to sixty elements, including all trace minerals required by plants. It also contains natural plant growth hormones (cytokinins, auxins, gibberellins). Nutrients become available approximately 1 to 2 months after application, with slow release continuing over 4 to 5 months as the meal decomposes. A caveat is that many of these trace minerals may already be present in soil; they are needed only in minute quantities. Sustainable harvesting of kelp is an important consideration.

### Kelp Powder

Finely ground kelp meal is more soluble and readily available to soil microbes, which quickly make nutrients accessible to plants. Effective duration is up to 1 month.

### Liquid Kelp (N–P–K 0–0–0 to 1–0–4)

Liquid kelp provides instantly available micronutrients when applied. Most brands use enzymatic digestion, which preserves more plant growth hormones than heat-based or chemical extraction methods. Persistence in soil is 1 to 3 weeks.

## Biofertilizers

Biofertilizers are living agents that produce or facilitate the production of plant nutrients. While they don't fit current legal definitions of fertilizers (because N–P–K content is difficult or impossible to quantify), they represent a growing category of inputs that go beyond simple decomposition [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]].

### Nitrogen-Fixing Biofertilizers

**Rhizobia and Frankia** are symbiotic nitrogen fixers that can produce substantial quantities of usable nitrogen. Rhizobia are well-established for legume cultivation (soybeans, locust trees, wisteria). Frankia were discovered more recently and their use is still developing. Both exhibit incredible specificity between plant species and microbial strain — this must be considered when deploying them.

**Azotobacter and Azospirillum** are free-living (non-symbiotic) nitrogen-fixing bacteria frequently used with cereal crops. They produce nitrogen independently of plant hosts. Researchers are developing mixed formulations for general gardening use.

### Phosphorus-Solubilizing Organisms

Certain bacteria (*Bacillus megaterium* var. *phosphaticum*, *B. subtilis*, *B. circulans*, *Pseudomonas striata*) — collectively called phosphobacterins — can liberate insoluble phosphorus, making it directly available to plants or placing it into the diffusion stream.

Similarly, fungi (*Penicillium* species, *Aspergillus awamori*) free phosphorus indirectly by producing organic acids during their metabolic activity. These acids break the chemical bonds that immobilize phosphorus in soil.

### Mycorrhizal Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots, exchanging root exudates (carbon, which fungi cannot produce themselves) for soil-acquired nutrients. Their extensive hyphal networks extend root surface area by hundreds of times, dramatically increasing nutrient interception capacity.

Research suggests mycorrhizal fungi can deliver approximately:
- 80% of a plant's phosphorus
- 60% of its copper
- 25% of its nitrogen and zinc
- 10% of its potassium

Over 90% of plant species form mycorrhizal relationships in nature. Mycorrhizae are a primary reason trees thrive with minimal intervention.

### Plant Growth-Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPR)

PGPR are root-colonizing bacteria (including *Pseudomonas fluorescens* and many others) that form symbiotic relationships with plants. They aid in nutrient synthesis, promote root growth, and enhance plant nutrition. A major studied function is assisting mycorrhizal fungi with phosphorus acquisition — some PGPR produce alkaline phosphatase, an enzyme that breaks down phosphate bonds.

## Compost and Earthworm Castings

### Compost

Technically a soil amendment rather than a fertilizer, compost contains nutrients because organic matter and clay contribute to its [[cation-exchange-capacity]]. Compost should ideally be tested for both nutrient and microbial content. A critical caveat: incompletely composted material is not compost. Partially decomposed organic matter can tie up nitrogen as the composting process completes adjacent to plant roots rather than in a dedicated pile.

### Earthworm Castings

Worm castings concentrate nutrients dramatically compared to source material — approximately 10× more potassium, 5× more nitrogen, 7× more phosphorus, 3× more magnesium, 1.5× more calcium, and 1.4× more humus. Worms primarily target bacteria, protozoa, and fungi; they digest some and process the rest into nutrient-dense castings. Providing a guaranteed N–P–K analysis is nearly impossible, which is why castings are classified as "almost fertilizer."

## Maximizing Nutrient Availability

Several factors affect nutrient availability from organic fertilizers, all relating to the rate at which minerals are decomposed to their ionic forms by soil food web organisms (bacteria, Archaea, and fungi):

1. **Soil temperature**: Microbes that release nutrients are more active at warmer temperatures
2. **Soil pH**: Correct pH maximizes the number of unencumbered ions available for plant uptake
3. **Soil testing**: The single most important practice — test before establishing a feeding program, retest at season's end or start of the next season, and thereafter every 2 to 3 years

## Key Takeaways

- Kelp products (meal, powder, liquid) provide the widest spectrum of trace minerals plus plant growth hormones
- Chitin-containing materials (crab shell meal, shrimp shell meal) serve dual roles as nutrient sources and nematode suppressants
- Biofertilizers represent a paradigm shift from adding nutrients to adding the organisms that produce or mobilize them
- Mycorrhizal fungi are arguably the most important biofertilizer, delivering the majority of several essential nutrients
- Phosphate-solubilizing bacteria and fungi unlock otherwise unavailable phosphorus reserves
- Complete composting is essential — partial compost causes more problems than it solves
