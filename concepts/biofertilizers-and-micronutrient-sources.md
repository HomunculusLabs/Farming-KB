# Biofertilizers and Micronutrient Sources in Organic Systems

Biofertilizers represent a paradigm shift in plant nutrition—rather than applying mineral nutrients directly, gardeners introduce living organisms that produce, solubilize, or transport nutrients to plant roots. Combined with natural micronutrient sources, these biological approaches form the foundation of sustainable organic fertility.

## What Are Biofertilizers?

Related topics: [[organic-micronutrient-sources-kelp-chitin-biofertilizers]] [[organic-fertilizer-sources-and-biofertilizers]] [[micronutrient-deficiency-symptoms]]
Biofertilizers are living agents that help produce plant foods. While they do not fit the current legal definitions of fertilizers (because their N–P–K content is difficult or impossible to quantify), the microorganisms involved are intimately connected to nutrient production. They go beyond simple decay and cycling microbes—biofertilizer organisms actively manufacture or facilitate the delivery of nutrients to plants.

## Nitrogen-Fixing Biofertilizers

### Rhizobia and Frankia (Symbiotic Nitrogen Fixers)

Both Rhizobia and Frankia are bacteria that form symbiotic relationships with plants to fix atmospheric nitrogen. Rhizobia associate with legumes (soybeans, locust trees, wisteria, clover, peanuts) in root nodules, and their use is a well-established agricultural practice. Frankia were discovered more recently and their application is still developing—they associate with actinorhizal plants.

The pinkish color inside legume root nodules comes from leghemoglobin, a compound similar to hemoglobin in red blood cells, which helps maintain the low-oxygen environment nitrogenase enzymes need to fix nitrogen.

**Specificity is critical**: there is incredible specificity between plant species and their microbial partners among nitrogen-fixing diazotrophs. Gardeners must ensure a correct match when employing these organisms. The best approach is often to collect nodules from established plants of the same species and use the pink interior material to inoculate seeds or soil.

### Azotobacter and Azospirillum (Free-Living Nitrogen Fixers)

These two bacterial genera fix nitrogen without entering into symbiotic relationships with plants. They are free-living in the soil and are frequently used in cereal crop production. Researchers are actively developing garden-use formulations. While they produce less nitrogen per unit than symbiotic fixers, their advantage is that they associate with any plant root zone without requiring specific host relationships.

## Phosphorus-Solubilizing Organisms

### Phosphate-Solubilizing Bacteria (Phosphobacterins)

Certain non-symbiotic bacteria can free up insoluble phosphorus from soil minerals, making it directly available to plants or placing it into the diffusion stream that carries phosphorus to roots. Key species include:

- *[[bacillus-megaterium]]* var. *phosphaticum*
- *[[bacillus-subtilis]]*
- *Bacillus circulans*
- *Pseudomonas striata*

### Phosphate-Solubilizing Fungi

Fungi including *Penicillium* species and *Aspergillus awamori* also liberate phosphorus through indirect mechanisms—they produce organic acids as metabolic byproducts, and these acids break the chemical bonds that tie up phosphorus in soil minerals. This acidification approach complements the direct enzymatic action of phosphobacterins.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi form the most important nutrient-delivery symbiosis in terrestrial ecosystems. They form mycorrhizae (intimate associations) with plant roots, exchanging carbon from plant exudates for nutrients scavenged from the soil.

### Nutrient Delivery Capacity

Research suggests mycorrhizal fungi can deliver:
- Up to **80%** of a plant's phosphorus
- **60%** of its copper
- **25%** of its nitrogen
- **25%** of its zinc
- **10%** of its potassium

This nutrient delivery is essentially free—both economically and in terms of labor. Mycorrhizal fungi are a primary reason why trees and native plants thrive with so little human intervention.

### How They Work

Their extensive hyphal networks extend root surfaces by hundreds of times, dramatically increasing the interception zone for immobile nutrients like phosphorus. Over 90% of plant species in nature enter into mycorrhizal relationships, making this the norm rather than the exception.

### The Phosphorus Paradox

There is an important tension: while mycorrhizal fungi are the best releasers of phosphorus from organic [[plant-nutrients-and-mineral-sources]], excessive phosphorus fertilizer application actually suppresses mycorrhizal colonization. Gardeners who over-apply phosphorus inadvertently destroy the very organisms that deliver it. Soil test recommendations should account for mycorrhizal dependence.

## Plant Growth-Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPR)

PGPR are root-colonizing bacteria that form beneficial relationships with plants. The most well-known species is *Pseudomonas fluorescens*, but PGPR encompass a diverse community. Their major studied functions include:

- **Aiding phosphorus acquisition**: They help mycorrhizal fungi obtain phosphorus, possibly by assisting in breaking phosphate bonds
- **Producing alkaline phosphatase**: An enzyme that breaks down phosphate bonds, liberating phosphorus
- **Stimulating root growth**: By producing hormones and other signaling compounds that positively influence root architecture, indirectly improving nutrient uptake

## Compost as a Biological Amendment

While not technically a fertilizer, compost is the cornerstone of [[deep-organic-soil-management]]. It provides:

- A full community of microbes and humus
- An ideal environment for microbial activity needed to cycle organic materials into plant-available nutrients
- Nutrient content influenced by its CEC ([[cation-exchange-capacity]]) from organic matter and clay
- Improved soil structure, water retention, and disease suppression

Compost should ideally be tested for both nutrient content and microbial populations so gardeners understand what they are applying and how it affects soil test recommendations.

**Critical caveat**: compost must be fully composted. Partially composted material (common in home piles) can cause problems including nitrogen tie-up, as the composting process completes in the garden bed rather than in the pile, competing with plants for available nitrogen.

## Earthworm Castings

Worm castings are concentrated nutrient sources produced when earthworms ingest organic matter (primarily targeting bacteria, protozoa, and fungi). Compared to the source soil, castings contain:

- **10×** more potassium
- **5×** more nitrogen
- **7×** more phosphorus
- **3×** more magnesium
- **1.5×** more calcium
- **1.4×** more humus

Castings also harbor diverse microbial communities and plant growth hormones. Like compost, they are difficult to assign a guaranteed N–P–K analysis, but their nutrient concentration and biological activity make them invaluable.

## Micronutrient Sources

### Shrimp Shell Meal (N–P–K 5–8–15)

Made from grinding shrimp heads and shells. Contains abundant trace elements from the marine environment, approximately 15% calcium, and about 20% chitin. The chitin content makes it an excellent fungal food and nematode suppressant. It is a slow-release fertilizer lasting 3 to 6 months.

### Kelp Meal (N–P–K 0–0–0 to 1–0–4)

Kelp (seaweed) meal contains up to sixty elements including all trace minerals plants need, plus natural plant growth hormones (cytokinins, auxins, gibberellins). Nutrients become available 1 to 2 months after application and continue releasing during the 4 to 5 months the meal takes to decompose.

While effective for hydroponics, many kelp-derived nutrients may already be present in garden soil. Trace minerals are essential but needed in minute quantities, so excess application provides diminishing returns. Sustainable harvesting practices should be verified.

### Kelp Powder

Further ground kelp meal, making it more soluble and readily available to microbes. Nutrients become available quickly but the product lasts only about 1 month.

### Liquid Kelp (N–P–K 0–0–0 to 1–0–4)

Provides instantly available micronutrients and hormones when applied. Most quality brands use enzymatic digestion, which preserves more plant growth hormones than heat-based or chemical extraction processes. Persists in soil for only 1 to 3 weeks, making it best for quick corrections or regular foliar/soil drench programs.

## Manures as Soil Amendments

Livestock manures require careful assessment in modern agriculture:

- May contain antibiotics, hormones, and medicines that do not break down during composting
- Can lead to salt and heavy metal buildup with repeated application
- If improperly composted, can be a source of *E. coli* and other pathogens
- Must be composted at minimum 131°F (55°C) for at least 72 hours before garden use
- Vary enormously in N–P–K content depending on animal species, diet, and bedding

## Maximizing Nutrient Availability

The availability of nutrients from organic fertilizers depends on [[the-soil-food-web]]'s ability to decompose organic materials into ionic forms. Key factors include:

1. **Soil temperature**: Microbes that release nutrients are more active at warmer temperatures
2. **Soil pH**: Must be in the correct range to maximize unencumbered ions and minimize nutrient lock-up
3. **Soil testing**: The single most important step—know what nutrients are already present before applying anything
4. **Retesting**: Test at the end of the season or start of the next, then every 2 to 3 years to track trends

## Key Principles

- Biofertilizers work with the soil food web rather than replacing it
- Mycorrhizal fungi deliver the majority of phosphorus, copper, and zinc to most plants
- Over-applying phosphorus destroys mycorrhizal partnerships—the very organisms that deliver phosphorus
- Compost must be fully finished to avoid nitrogen tie-up and pathogen risks
- Earthworm castings concentrate all major nutrients plus humus relative to source material
- Kelp provides the widest spectrum of [[soil-trace-elements-and-micronutrients]] natural growth hormones
- Soil testing before, during, and after fertilization programs is non-negotiable for intelligent organic management
