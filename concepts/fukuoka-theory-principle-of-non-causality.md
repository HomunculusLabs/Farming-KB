# Fukuoka's Principle of Non-Causality in Agriculture

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] argued that the causal relationships between factors in
nature are too entangled for humans to unravel through research and analysis.
This principle of non-causality forms one of the most radical philosophical
foundations of his critique of scientific agriculture and justification for
natural farming.

## The Problem of Identifying True Causes

Consider a farmer who attributes a poor harvest to poor weather. A specialist
offers more detail: tiller formation was good, grain count per head was good,
but insufficient sunlight after heading slowed maturation. This second
explanation appears closer to truth. Surely insufficient sunlight and poor
maturation are causally related.

Yet one cannot claim that lack of sunlight was the decisive factor. Insufficient
sunlight means leaves did not receive enough light. The cause of drooping
leaves may have been excessive vegetative growth, caused by overapplication of
nitrogenous fertilizers, or a shortage of some other nutrient, or stem
weakness from silica deficiency, or excess leaf nitrogen from inhibited
conversion to protein. Behind each cause lies another cause.

## The Infinite Web of Causation

When we discuss causes, we refer to a complex web of organically
interrelated causes: basic causes, remote causes, contributing factors,
predisposing factors. This is why no brief, simple explanation can capture
the true cause of poor maturation. The poor harvest might be attributed to
insufficient sunshine, excess nitrogen, poor starch transport, or low
temperatures. It is impossible to determine the real cause.

The conclusion that poor harvest resulted from a combination of factors is no
more meaningful than saying it was written in the stars. The scientist may be
pleased with a detailed explanation, but it makes no difference whether we
analyze reasons carefully or throw all analysis to the winds; the result is
the same.

## The Futility of Year-to-Year Analysis

Scientists believe that analyzing one year's harvest will benefit growers the
following year. Yet the weather is never the same, so growing conditions next
year will differ entirely. Because all factors of production are organically
interrelated, when one factor changes, all others are affected. Rice will be
grown under entirely different conditions, rendering this year's experience
totally useless. Although useful for examining results in retrospect, the
explanations of yesterday cannot set tomorrow's strategy.

## Mutual Cancellation of Improvement Measures

Many people believe that planting a variety bearing large heads, ensuring lots
of sunlight, applying plenty of fertilizer, and carrying out thorough pest
control will guarantee good yields. Fukuoka demonstrates how each measure
undermines the others:

- Varieties bearing large heads usually have fewer heads per plant, defeating
  dense planting for light exposure
- Heavy fertilizer application causes excessive vegetative growth, again
  reducing light exposure
- Efforts to obtain large stems and heads weaken the plant and increase
  disease and insect damage
- Thorough pest control measures result in lodging of rice plants
- Water-conserving cultivation to improve light exposure may actually reduce
  available light due to weed growth
- Attempting to raise photosynthetic efficiency may lower photosynthetic
  ability itself

The more improvement measures are combined, the more they cancel each other
out, producing an indefinite result. There is no way to join all these into
one overall method that works correctly.

## The Equilibrium of Nature

This property of mutual cancellation derives from the equilibrium of nature.
Nature inherently abhors the unnatural and makes every effort to return to its
true state by discarding human techniques for increasing harvests. A natural
control operates to hold down large harvests and raise low harvests,
approaching the natural yield without disrupting balance.

## The Impossibility of Comprehensive Knowledge

To resolve poor maturation alone, one must simultaneously address weather,
biological environment, cultivation methods, soil, fertilizer, disease and
pest control, and human factors across every field of study. This simultaneous
solution is extraordinarily difficult and fraught with contradiction.

Since the basic causes of actions and effects at any particular time and place
cannot be known to man, he can have no true understanding of causal
relationships. There is no way to know the true effectiveness of any technique.
Although he knows no grand conclusion is forthcoming, man persists in believing
his partial conclusions are effective overall.

It is utterly impossible to predict what effects will arise from actions
undertaken using the human intellect. Man only thinks the effects will be
beneficial. He cannot know. Only God is capable of erecting comprehensive
measures applied completely on all counts. Man's understanding can at best be
myopic and uncertain. After causing meaningless confusion, his efforts cancel
each other out and are eventually buried in nature.

## The Breeding Paradox

The breeding specialist believes endeavors will produce a variety meeting the
needs of the age. But an improved variety with three good features will also
have three bad features, and one with six strengths will have six weaknesses.
Any variety thought better will probably be worse, because new contradictions
defying solution lie within it.

Although each improvement conceived by agricultural scientists may appear
fine individually, when seen collectively they cancel each other out and are
totally ineffective. There being infinite causes for an event, there are
infinite solutions as well, deepening and broadening to infinite complexity.

## Implications for Agricultural Practice

This principle leads to a profound practical conclusion: if human
intervention in complex natural systems inevitably produces unpredictable and
often contradictory results, then the wisest course is to minimize
intervention and allow nature's inherent balance to operate. This is not
passive resignation but an active recognition that nature's comprehensive
intelligence exceeds any partial understanding humans can achieve.
