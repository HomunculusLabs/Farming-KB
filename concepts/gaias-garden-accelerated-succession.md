# Accelerated Succession in Ecological Gardening

Ecological succession is the natural process by which ecosystems
develop and mature over time. Toby Hemenway presents accelerated
succession as one of the overarching strategies of [[ecological-gardening-principles]],
demonstrating how gardeners can work with nature's momentum to
create productive, mature landscapes far faster than would occur
without intervention.

## What Is Ecological Succession?

When plants first colonize bare earth, whether an abandoned farm,
a landslide scar, or a freshly tilled garden, a predictable
progression begins. Certain annual grasses, herbs, and flowers
arrive first. Because of their speedy colonization ability, they
are called pioneer plants. They are well adapted to invading
disturbed soil and mantling floral emptiness with green.

We know most of these fast-colonizing plants as weeds: crab
grass, dandelion, sheep sorrel, pigweed, plantain, chicory,
wild lettuce, and many more. These pioneers have a job to do:
sheltering bare soil from erosive rains, and ferrying nutrients
from deep in the soil to the surface where they can be used.
These fast-growing, short-lived pioneers preserve and restore the
fertility of disturbed ground.

## The Stages of Succession

If left alone, the short early annuals are crowded and shaded
out by a taller, mostly perennial crew. In the northern half of
the United States, these include asters, fireweed, goldenrod,
perennial grasses, and many others. The dense foliage, branching
stems, and many textures of the tall weeds offer more niches
for insects and birds to shelter, breed, and feed. The amount of
living matter, called biomass, increases.

In five to fifteen years the weedy field is clothed with
perennial shrubs. With enough rain and fertility, in two or
more decades the shrubs give way to a young forest. Though
succession is nearly irresistible, it is not smoothly linear.
Fire, wind, lightning, plowing, or other disturbance can set it
back to an earlier phase. Most landscapes are a mosaic of many
successionary stages at many scales.

## Why Conventional Gardens Are Immature

Conventional gardens mimic immature ecosystems. They are usually
dominated by early-succession plants. Most grasses, flowers,
and especially annual vegetables are pioneers. This means that
in our love of lawns and orderly gardens, we are attempting to
keep our yards at an early stage of ecological development.

A lawn of grass edged with flowers is an ecological cousin to
prairie. The archetypal turf dotted with occasional trees and
shrubs mimics a savanna. Neither prairie nor savanna flourish
under typical suburban conditions, which lack the low rainfall,
heavy grazing, and frequent fire these ecosystems require.

An immature ecosystem like a lawn demands that we expend time,
energy, and materials to wrench back the hands of the ecological
clock, holding the land at prairie phase with mowing and
weeding. With sprinkler and fertilizer we are tromping on the
accelerator, yet with tiller and pruning saw we are slamming on
the brake. No system runs well under that schizophrenic regime.

## Accelerating the Process

Since landscapes have an irresistible tendency to mature, why
not hop on board the successionary freight train and take
advantage of nature's momentum? With a nudge here and a tweak
there, we can actually accelerate succession, using nature to
help a garden mature much faster than it otherwise would.

In the ecological garden, we create well-developed, productive,
and lush landscapes very quickly by riding the tracks already
laid down by nature. This is what the Bullock brothers did on
Orcas Island, transforming a once-scrubby, blackberry-entangled
parcel into a rich landscape of walnut trees, bamboo groves,
and vegetable beds winding into the woods.

## Immature vs Mature Ecosystems

As a landscape matures, several key shifts occur:

- **Organic matter** builds up in the form of plants, animals,
  and rich soil
- **Nutrient sources** shift from nonliving (rocks, rainfall)
  to biological (plants, animals, humus)
- **Mineral cycles** become closed through recycling rather
  than open with many imports
- **Species diversity** increases dramatically
- **Plant structure** becomes multilayered rather than a single
  flat plane
- **Niche availability** expands, supporting more species

In the annual garden, soil is bare many months of the year.
Climate is harsh and varies wildly. The nutrient cycles are open
in straight lines, into the garden and then out. Fertility must
be imported to replace all that is lost to leaching, erosion,
and the near-total removal of plants.

In contrast, a mature woodland holds soil in place year-round.
The canopy moderates temperature extremes. Nutrients are cycled
internally through decomposition. Little fertility needs to be
imported because most of what is used is returned.

## Techniques for Acceleration

Several techniques can speed succession in a garden:

- **Plant perennials instead of annuals** to jump directly to
  later successionary stages
- **Use [[gaias-garden-nurse-plants]]** to shelter young trees
  and shrubs through vulnerable early years
- **Build soil rapidly** with [[gaias-garden-sheet-mulching]],
  compost, and organic matter additions
- **Install [[gaias-garden-food-forest-layers]]** that mimic
  the structure of mature forests
- **Choose fast-growing pioneer species** that prepare the
  ground for slower, longer-lived plants
- **Design [[gaias-garden-interplanting-polyculture]]** systems
  that fill all available niches, leaving no room for unwanted
  pioneer weeds

## The Role of Weeds in Succession

Understanding succession changes our relationship with weeds.
Weeds are simply pioneer plants, molded by millions of years
of evolution to quickly cover disturbed, open ground. They will
do that relentlessly in the bare ground of a garden.

Rather than fighting weeds, the ecological gardener can
recognize them as indicators of ecosystem immaturity and work
to accelerate past the stage where weeds dominate. The solution
is not to battle pioneer plants but to create conditions that
favor more mature ecosystems.
