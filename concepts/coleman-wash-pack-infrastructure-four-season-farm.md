## The Cool House as Wash Station

From the beginning of comm [[coleman-greenhouse-heating-options-winter-production]] duction at Four Season
Farm, Coleman dedicated one large greenhouse to serve dual purposes:
minimal supplementary heat fo [[mushroom-spawn-production-and-laboratory-techniques]] ion and on-farm wash and
pack infrastructure. The cool house was built because Coleman
recognized that a greenhouse would be the least expensive type of
covered area for washing and packing produce, compared to building
a separate insulated structure.

One-quarter of the cool house has a concrete floor with space
allocated for washing and packing facilities, a walk-in cooler,
and for starting s [[psilocybin-in-the-counter-culture]] gs in the spring. The remaining three-
quarters of the house is used fo [[coleman-semi-hibernating-winter-crops-harvest-extension]] nter crops that
benefit from the minimal supplementary heat.

The propane heater thermostat is set low, just above 32°F (0°C).
The primary purpose of this heat is to prevent the vegetable
washing system from freezing during winter operations, not to
warm the growing space to any significant degree. This minimalist
approach to heating reflects Coleman's broader philosophy of
minimizing inputs while maximizing output.

## The Walk-In Cooler

A walk-in cooler is housed within the cool house structure,
providing cold storage for harvested produce before it goes to
market. The cooler allows Coleman to harvest ahead of market
days and store produce at optimal temperatures, maintaining
freshness and extending shelf life. The integration of the cooler
within the greenhouse structure avoids the need for a separate
insulated building while taking advantage of the existing
concrete floor and power infrastructure.

## Baby-Leaf Salad Wash and Pack Process

The salad mix is composed only of whole baby leaves that are
washed, mixed, and packed directly after harvesting. Coleman
emphasizes that leaves should ideally be no more than three
inches in length, ensuring they fit easily onto a fork and into
the mouth. This quality standard extends to all salad mix
components without exception.

The wash process is designed to remove field grit and debris
while preserving leaf quality. Coleman does not chop mature
plants into small pieces as is common in supermarket mixes.
Instead, each component is grown specifically for the baby-leaf
stage and harvested at the correct size. This approach produces
a superior product but requires careful harvest timing and
frequent successions to maintain adequate supply.

## Quality Standards for Packing

Quality is the primary concern in the baby-leaf salad operation.
Coleman works to set rather than follow quality standards. The
farm does not sell salad mix ingredients that are too large,
bitter, tough, stringy, or frost-damaged. If the team is not
completely satisfied with any component, it is left out of the
mix entirely until conditions improve.

The evaluation process has led to several important management
decisions: moving claytonia from colder edge beds to warmer
inner beds for midwinter harvest; increasing succession plantings
of minutina and narrow-stem chard to maintain young leaf supply;
cutting back red-oak lettuce earlier because regrowth is hardier
than initial leaves; and conducting continuous trial plantings
of both standard and potential new crops.

## Carrot Washing and Presentation

For root crops like carrots, Coleman's approach emphasizes
presentation quality. Carrots are washed thoroughly and presented
with attention to visual appeal. The careful washing and packing
process adds value to each bunch, making the product attractive
at market and justifying premium pricing over mass-produced
alternatives. Field grit is removed completely, and the carrots
are bunched or packed in ways that highlight their quality.

## Efficiency Considerations

The integration of washing and packing into the cool house offers
several efficiency advantages. Harvested produce moves directly
from the growing area to the wash station without exposure to
outdoor winter temperatures. Workers can harvest and pack in a
single pass through the greenhouse, reducing handling time and
labor costs. The concrete floor provides a durable, washable
surface that can be hosed down easily.

The compact layout also means that crated produce can be moved
short distances between harvest, wash, cooler, and market
loading. This streamlined workflow is essential for a small
farm where labor efficiency directly impacts profitability.

## Infrastructure Investment Philosophy

Coleman's approach to wash-pack infrastructure reflects his
broader philosophy of minimal investment for maximum return.
Rather than building a dedicated post-harvest facility, he
integrates washing and packing into the existing greenhouse
structure. The concrete floor section serves multiple purposes
(wash, pack, cooler, seedling starting), maximizing the utility
of every square foot of expensive covered space.

This integrated approach is particularly well-suited to small
diversified farms where capital is limited and flexibility is
essential. The wash station can be reconfigured seasonally as
needs change, and the space serves different functions at
different times of year.
