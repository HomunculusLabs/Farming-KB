## Tropical Earth-Shaping Techniques

Earth-shaping (land-forming) is a foundational permaculture practice in tropical
agriculture. By moulding the soil surface into ridges, mounds, furrows, basins,
trenches, or log boxes, growers create micro-environments that improve water
management, root development, mulch retention, and crop yields. The technique
chosen depends on rainfall patterns, crop type, and local soil conditions.

## Benefits of Earth Shaping

Regardless of the specific form, earth shaping delivers five core benefits:

- **Water infiltration and runoff control** — patterned ground captures wet-season
  rainfall for dry-season use, serving as the cheapest form of irrigation.
- **Free root run** — loosened, mounded, or ridged soil reduces resistance and
  gives roots greater volume to explore for nutrients and water.
- **Mulch retention in wind** — depressions, furrows, and boxed beds hold mulch
  that would otherwise be blown or washed away on flat ground.
- **Better drainage in over-wet areas** — ridges and mounds elevate the root zone
  above waterlogged soils, preventing rot in humid tropics.
- **Microclimate benefits** — shaped surfaces provide wind shelter and improve
  ground warmth, extending the range of sensitive species.

## Trenching

Trenching is the preferred technique for **dry tropics**. Excavating channels
and heaping soil to one side concentrates limited rainfall into planting zones
and creates wind-sheltered microsites. Trenches capture run-off and allow water
to percolate deeply, making moisture available long after surface wetting ceases.

## Ridges

Ridges are the most widely used earth-shaping form in tropical agriculture.
Mollison specifies ridges of **0.5 m high × 1 m wide**, which increase yields
in cassava, sweet potato, potato, and yam. **Pineapple** and **ginger** also
prefer ridges in wet areas, where the elevated root zone prevents waterlogging
while friable soil promotes tuber enlargement.

Ridges create a natural two-zone intercropping system. *Leucaena* planted on
ridge tops provides nitrogen and mulch, while maize and green mulch crops (beans)
occupy the sheltered hollows between ridges. The ridge form also permits **deep
mulching** for low crops like pineapple, protecting shallow roots from sun and
erosion.

## Mounds

Mounds are the default earth-shaping form for **root crops and cucurbits in
humid tropics**. Elevating soil prevents waterlogging that rots tubers and
suffocates roots in high-rainfall areas.

**Volcano-shaped mounds with hollow centres** are excellent cucurbit and melon
sites. The design: form a mound with a central depression, enrich with manures,
place one or two **stones** inside the hollow (thermal mass absorbs daytime heat
and radiates it at night to reach germination temperature), then plant seeds in
the enriched, warmed hollow. This is especially valuable where soil temperatures
are marginal for warm-season germination.

## Furrows

Furrows are **depressions** that concentrate water and mulch, serving the
opposite function of ridges. They are particularly valuable for **ginger and
pineapple in dry areas**. Furrows should be covered with mulch to retain soil
moisture, suppress weeds, and gradually feed the crop as the mulch decomposes.
Mulched furrows also carry **subsurface water seepage lines**, distributing
moisture laterally through the soil profile along the entire furrow length.

## Basins

Even **shallow basins** significantly aid moisture-loving crops in dryland
conditions. Basins are effective for dryland taro, banana, and Chinese water
chestnut. The basin shape causes rainfall to pool and infiltrate rather than run
off, saturating the root zone more easily. Combined with **deep mulch**, basins
dramatically reduce water stress on crops requiring high soil moisture.

## Palm Trunk Boxes

Boxes constructed from **palm trunks** are ideal mulch-holders for yams, banana,
vanilla orchid, vines, and borders of beds in home gardens. Log boxes are built
by arranging palm trunk sections to form rectangular enclosures, stacked **1–3
logs high** depending on mulch depth needed, then filled with mulch. Benefits
include greatly simplified weeding (weeds cannot establish through deep mulch),
slow decomposition of log walls adding organic matter, and clearly defined
growing areas for easier garden management.

## Crop Succession in Palm Systems

Light, space, and microclimate shift as palms mature, requiring planned
intercrop succession. Mollison's timeline:

| Phase | Years | Conditions | Suitable Crops |
|-------|-------|------------|----------------|
| Establishment | 1–2 | Open ground, full sun | Short-term grains, vegetables, legumes |
| Frond Closure | 2–4 | Fronds damage understory and shade ground | Shade-tolerant herbs, reduced intercrop |
| Stem Formation | 4–6 | Trunk forms, crown rises above understory | Transition to perennial species |
| Mature Canopy | 6–14 | Multi-layer system, filtered light | Complex perennial intercrop |
| Full Maturity | 14+ | Established understory niche | Stable polyculture, shade crops |

In **clump systems** (multiple palms from a single base), early ground-shading
is less pronounced, allowing intercropping to continue longer in early years.

## Economics

Nair (1975) demonstrated that irrigation alone increases yield by ~50%, while
complex intercropping of two or more species triples yield (doubling cash
return). Adding three species increases net yield 3–9 times while raising costs
by only 3.1 times. Mollison emphasises that **the cheapest irrigation is to
pattern the ground to hold wet-season run-off for dry-season tree crop use**.

## Design Considerations

1. **Rainfall regime** — dry tropics favour trenches and basins; humid tropics
   favour ridges and mounds.
2. **Crop sensitivity** — waterlogging-prone crops need elevation;
   drought-sensitive crops need depressions.
3. **Slope** — contour-aligned earthworks prevent erosion and maximise capture.
4. **Materials** — palm trunk boxes require local palms; stone-heated mounds
   require accessible rock.
5. **Maintenance** — ridges and furrows need seasonal reshaping; log boxes and
   permanent basins are more stable long-term.
