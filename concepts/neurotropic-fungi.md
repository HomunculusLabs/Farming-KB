# Neurotropic Fungi

Neurotropic fungi, also called hallucinogenic, psychotropic, magic, sacred,
psychedelic, or entheogenic mushrooms, are a diverse group of fungi known to
produce psychoactive effects on the central nervous system. The term
"neurotropic" (acting on the nervous system) was used by Guzmán, Allen, and
Gartz in their landmark 1998 taxonomic survey of these organisms. The paper
catalogued **216 known species** across four major chemical groups, drawing on
more than 450 bibliographic references.

## Classification

The neurotropic fungi are divided into four primary groups based on their
active compounds:

1. **Psilocybin and related indole alkaloids** — The largest group,
   encompassing the majority of known neurotropic species. The principal
   compounds are [[psilocybin-compound]], [[psilocin]], [[baeocystin]], and
   [[norbaeocystin]]. These are tryptamine derivatives that act primarily
   as serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonists.

2. **Ibotenic acid and muscimol** — Found in species of Amanita, notably
   [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]], [[amanita-pantherina]], and
   [[amanita-regalis-entity]]. These GABAergic compounds produce a
   fundamentally different psychoactive profile compared to the psilocybin
   group, often described as deliriant rather than psychedelic.

3. **Ergot fungi** — Including Claviceps purpurea|Claviceps species (5)
   and Cordyceps species (2). These produce ergotamine and related
   ergoline alkaloids, which are the precursors to [[lysergic-acid-amide]].

4. **Sacred fungi of uncertain chemistry** — Species used traditionally in
   indigenous ceremonies for which no reliable chemical analyses exist. This
   includes several Boletus|boletes (2 genera, 8 species), Russula
   (6 species), and gasteromycetes (6 species in 3 genera including
   Lycoperdon and Phallales).

## Taxonomic Challenges

The study of neurotropic fungi is complicated by several persistent problems:

**Misidentification** is widespread in the literature. Many chemical studies
were performed on misidentified material. Guzmán documented cases where
researchers analyzed mixed collections containing multiple species identified
as a single taxon. For example, material used by Leslie and Repke to isolate
psilocybin was later found to contain a mixture of Panaeolus spp.,
[[psilocybe-mexicana]], and [[psilocybe-coprophila-profile]], all
erroneously labelled as P. coprophila.

**Specimen age** significantly affects alkaloid content. Repke et al. (1977)
demonstrated that [[psilocybe-baeocystis-profile]] and
[[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]] showed no detectable indole compounds
when analyzed 20 to 60 days after collection. Fresh analysis is essential for
accurate chemical profiling.

**Conflicting chemical results** are common. [[panaeolina-foenisecii]] has been
reported both with and without psilocybin by different researchers, leading
to ongoing debate about whether it is truly psychoactive or merely toxic.

## Historical Context

The formal study of neurotropic fungi began with Schultes (1939) and was
greatly advanced by Singer (1949-1960), Heim (1956-1967), and Wasson
(1957-1980). The "rediscovery" of traditional mushroom use among indigenous
peoples of Mexico in the 1950s sparked a revolution in mycology. Guzmán
divided Mexican mycological history into two eras: before and after the
Wasson-Heim-Singer studies of 1954-1958, so transformative was their impact.

## Global Distribution

Mexico possesses the highest diversity of neurotropic fungi with 76
species (44 of which are Psilocybe, representing 39% of the world total).
The majority of psilocybin-containing species occur in or near the Austral
hemisphere, concentrated in subtropical humid forests. See
[[distribution-of-neurotropic-fungi]] for detailed geographic analysis.
