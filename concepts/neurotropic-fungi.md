# Neurotropic Fungi Neurotropic fungi, also called hallucinogenic, psychotropic, magic, sacred, psychedelic, or entheogenic mushrooms, are a diverse group of fungi known to produce psychoactive effects on the central nervous system. The term "neurotropic" (acting on the nervous system) was used by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz in their landmark 1998 taxonomic survey of these organisms. The paper catalogued **216 known species** across four major chemical groups, drawing on more than 450 bibliographic references. ## Classification The neurotropic fungi are divided into four primary groups based on their active compounds: 1. **Psilocybin and related indole alkaloids** — The largest group, encompassing the majority of known neurotropic species. The principal compounds are [[psilocin]], [[norbaeocystin]]. These are tryptamine derivatives that act primarily
   as serotonin 5-HT2A [[cannabinoid-receptor-agonists-antagonists-synthetic-reference-blesching]].

2. **Ibotenic acid and muscimol** — Found in species of Amanita, notably
   [[amanita-pantherina]], and
   [[lysergic-acid-amide]].

4. **Sacred fungi of uncertain chemistry** — Species used traditionally in
   indigenous ceremonies for which no reliable [[guzman-allen-gartz-conflicting-chemical-analyses-neurotropic-fungi]] exist. This
   includes several Boletus|boletes (2 genera, 8 species), Russula
   (6 species), and gasteromycetes (6 species in 3 genera including
   Lycoperdon and Phallales).

## Taxonomic Challenges

The study of neurotropic fungi is complicated by several persistent problems:

**Misidentification** is widespread in the literature. Many chemical studies
were performed on misidentified material. Guzmán documented cases where
researchers analyzed mixed collections containing multiple species identified
as a single taxon. For example, material used by Leslie and Repke to isolate
psilocybin was later found to contain a mixture of Panaeolus spp.,
[[psilocybe-coprophila-profile]], all
erroneously labelled as P. coprophila.

**[[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybin-volatility-specimen-age-degradation]]** significantly affects alkaloid content. Repke et al. (1977)
demonstrated that [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]] showed no detectable indole compounds
when analyzed 20 to 60 days after collection. Fresh analysis is essential for
accurate chemical profiling.

## Mechanism of Action

Psilocybin itself is pharmacologically inactive; it is rapidly dephosphorylated
by alkaline phosphatase enzymes in the body to produce psilocin, the primary
psychoactive compound. Psilocin is a structural analog of serotonin
(5-hydroxytryptamine) and acts primarily as a partial agonist at serotonin
5-HT2A receptors in the prefrontal cortex. This receptor activation modulates
the default mode network, a set of interconnected brain regions associated
with self-referential thinking, introspection, and ego-boundary maintenance.
The disruption of default mode network connectivity correlates strongly with
the subjective experience of ego dissolution reported by users.

Additional receptor activity contributes to the full spectrum of effects.
Psilocin also binds to 5-HT1A, 5-HT2B, 5-HT2C, and 5-HT7 receptors, each
contributing to different aspects of the experience including mood changes,
visual perception alterations, and changes in body temperature regulation.
The onset of effects occurs within 20-60 minutes of ingestion, peaks at
2-3 hours, and gradually subsides over 4-6 hours, depending on dose and
individual metabolism.

## Historical and Cultural Context

The use of neurotropic fungi spans millennia. Rock art from the Sahara
suggests ritual mushroom use dating back 7,000-9,000 years. In Mesoamerica,
stone carvings known as "mushroom stones" from Guatemala and Mexico,
dating to approximately 1000 BCE, depict figures holding or emerging from
fungi with distinctive morphological features matching Psilocybe species.

The Aztecs called these mushrooms teonanacatl, meaning "flesh of the gods."
