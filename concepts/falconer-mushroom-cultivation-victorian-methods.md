# Falconer Mushroom Cultivation - Victorian Methods

William Falconer's "Mushrooms: How to Grow Them" (1891), published by
Orange Judd Company, was a practical treatise on [[mazatec-mushroom-culture-and-velada]] for
profit and pleasure, written from extensive experience at the Dosoris
estate on Long Island, New York, and from correspondence with leading
growers in America and England.

## Intended Audience

Falconer addressed multiple classes of cultivators:

- **Market Gardeners**: For whom mushrooms were a [[coleman-sunlight-and-winter-crop-growth-rates]] filling
  the gap when outdoor operations were at a standstill. Manure used
  first for mushrooms was thoroughly rotted and ideal for subsequent
  spring crops of tomatoes, lettuce, cabbages, and cauliflowers.

- **Florists**: Who could [[query-how-can-i-grow-mushrooms-alongside-garden-vegetables]] under greenhouse benches.
  A 100-foot greenhouse could yield 500 square feet of bed producing
  250 pounds at fifty cents per pound net ($125), with the additional
  expense being only labor and spawn price.

- **Private Gardeners**: [[duggar-mushroom-growing-history-and-methods]] was routine duty in
  pretentious gardens. Failing a dedicated mushroom house, beds could
  be made in tool-sheds, potting-sheds, wood-sheds, stoke-holes,
  fruit-rooms, or under greenhouse benches.

- **Farmers**: Who had cellar space, fresh manure, and loam at home.
  The labor was simple enough for the commonest farm hand: turning
  manure every day or two for three weeks, building beds, spawning,
  and then mainly maintaining temperature and gathering crop for
  10-12 weeks.

- **Village and Suburban Residents**: Even a single horse stable
  afforded opportunity. A bed could be made in a cow-house,
  horse-stable, carriage-house, wood-shed, house-cellar, or even a
  large box.

## The Secrecy Problem

Falconer emphasized that market growers guarded everything about
mushroom-growing "from the outside world, and even from one another;
in fact, in some cases their next-door neighbors and life-long
intimate friends have never been inside their mushroom cellars."
He attributed the aura of mystery to European traditions compounded
by conspicuous amateur failures.

## Manure Preparation

The standard procedure involved turning fresh [[falconer-horse-manure-varieties]] once every
day or two for about three weeks before making beds. Falconer
described several successful growers' methods:

### Mr. Gardner (Jobstown, N.J.)
Gardner shipped fresh manure 70+ miles by rail from New York. He gave
it no preparatory treatment, spreading and packing it solid into beds
the same day it arrived. On one occasion, manure arriving July 8
yielded first mushrooms the second week in September -- two months
from stable to harvest. He added loam to the bed immediately, which
became charged with "steam" from the fermenting manure. He maintained
a box of steaming fresh horse-droppings in one end of his cellar,
tossing them with a dung-fork once or twice daily to raise a
nutrient-rich "steam" that fattened the mushrooms.

### Mr. Denton (Woodhaven, L.I.)
Denton hauled manure from Brooklyn stables, prepared it in an open
shed, then added one-third bulk of ordinary field loam. This mixture
counteracted overheating, allowed firmer shelf-bed construction, and
he reported better success than with manure alone -- despite most
authors denouncing manure-fatted loam in mushroom beds.

## Spawn Types and Use

Two principal spawn types were available:

- **English Brick Spawn**: Sold by American seedsmen. Broken into
  pieces 1-2 inches square.

- **French [[french-flake-spawn-vs-brick-spawn]]**: Less commonly used in America.

Gardner's distinctive method was to draw drills 18 inches apart and
2.5-3 inches deep with an angular-pointed hoe, then sow the broken
spawn pieces in rows "as if sowing peach stones or walnuts or snap
beans." Most other growers inserted pieces 2-3 inches under the
surface at 9-inch intervals.

Gardner observed marked differences between French flake and English
brick spawn results but never observed distinct varieties from the
same kind of spawn. Occasional differently-formed mushrooms he
attributed to cultural conditions rather than true varietal
differences.

## Temperature

The most suitable temperature was 57 F. Gardner maintained this
without fire-heat by mulching beds with straw. Falconer noted that
marketmen increasingly preferred mushrooms gathered young, before
the veil (frill between cup and stem) broke apart, rather than the
older practice of waiting until heads opened flat.

## Watering

Water at 90 F was applied gently through a fine-spraying pot when
beds got dry, but never enough to penetrate the casing into the
manure. Gardner preferred maintaining a moist atmosphere over direct
watering. After beds had borne for a while, a half-inch top-dressing
of fine soil was applied -- soil that had been kept near steaming-hot
manure to become thoroughly charged with mushroom food.

## Economic Returns

Gardner's beds bore from early November to early May (six months).
After May, mushrooms became so infested with maggots as to be
worthless. Denton marketed 1,700-2,500 pounds annually from two
cellars, using 400 pounds of brick spawn per season.

## London and Paris Methods

Falconer drew extensively on William Robinson's descriptions of the
London market gardens and Paris caves. London growers started beds in
August or September outdoors. The Paris caves used ridge beds 2 feet
wide at bottom, 2 feet high, 6-8 inches wide at top, with 1-foot
alleys between.
