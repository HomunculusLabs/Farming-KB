# Hallucinogenic Mushroom Prevalence and Use Patterns in Europe

## Data Landscape

Systematic data on hallucinogenic mushroom use in the European Union has been
historically limited. Most drug surveys collected information on LSD consumption
or a general "hallucinogenic drugs" category rather than specific data on
mushroom use. By the mid-2000s, most school surveys and some general population
surveys had begun including specific questions about hallucinogenic mushrooms,
providing the first comprehensive prevalence picture across EU member states.

## General Population Prevalence

Among young people aged 15-24, lifetime prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom
use across 12 EU member states ranged from less than 1% to 8%. The Netherlands,
Czech Republic, UK, Germany, and Ireland reported the highest prevalence
estimates, while Lithuania, Hungary, and France reported the lowest. A 2003
survey of 17-18 year old French residents showed 4.3% lifetime prevalence, 2.9%
last-year prevalence, and 1% last-month prevalence.

Prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use was consistently much lower than for
cannabis, where lifetime prevalence among 18-36 year olds ranged from 15%
(Poland) to 45% (Denmark). Importantly, the proportion of current users among
those who had ever used hallucinogenic mushrooms was lower than for both
cannabis and ecstasy, suggesting limited repeat use. The effects of the mushrooms
themselves appear to limit the appeal of regular consumption.

## School Survey Findings (ESPAD 2003)

The ESPAD (European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs) 2003 survey
covered 22 EU member states, Norway, and four candidate countries (Croatia,
Bulgaria, Turkey, and Romania). Among 15-16 year old students, lifetime
prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use ranged from 0% to 8%. The Czech
Republic, Netherlands, France, and Belgium had the highest estimates, while
Cyprus, Finland, and Romania reported zero lifetime prevalence.

A striking finding was that lifetime use of hallucinogenic mushrooms equaled or
exceeded lifetime use of ecstasy in nine countries. This suggests that mushrooms
occupied a position in the recreational drug hierarchy comparable to or above
that of MDMA among school-age populations in parts of Europe.

## Correlation with Other Drug Use

Analysis across 11 EU member states plus Norway and Croatia demonstrated that
school students aged 15-16 were much more likely to have used hallucinogenic
mushrooms if they had also used LSD, ecstasy, amphetamines, or cocaine, compared
to those who had only used cannabis or legal substances. This positioned
mushroom use firmly within the "harder" end of the recreational drug spectrum
in terms of user profiles, despite their natural origin.

Male students generally showed higher prevalence rates than females, consistent
with broader gender distributions observed for illegal drug use. The gender gap
was most pronounced among users of LSD and other hallucinogens, where male
lifetime prevalence significantly exceeded female rates.

## Clubbing and nightlife populations

Non-probability surveys conducted in dance music settings across Belgium, France,
Hungary, Italy, the Netherlands, and UK revealed dramatically higher prevalence
estimates than general population surveys. Lifetime prevalence among clubbers
ranged from 9% in Budapest to 55% in France, depending on the specific setting,
country, and survey methodology.

The consistent finding across all non-probability clubbing surveys was that drug
prevalence estimates were substantially higher than those found in general or
school populations. Within club samples, polydrug use tended to cluster in the
same individuals. In a 2000 survey of 760 clubbers from six south-east England
nightclubs, overall lifetime prevalence for mushroom use was 24%, but among
current drug users (those who had used any drug in the past month), prevalence
rose to 44% — compared to only 11% among 16-19 year olds in the general
population.

## Trend Data

The only longitudinal trend data came from six consecutive annual readership
surveys by the UK clubbing magazine Mixmag (2000-2005). Lifetime prevalence
estimates increased between 2003 and 2004, and last-month prevalence increased
significantly from 2.4% in 2002 to 18% in 2004. However, these findings require
cautious interpretation due to changes in non-probability sampling methods across
years and variable sample characteristics.

The average age for first use was 18-19 years according to the 2004 Mixmag
sample. Among 305 individuals who had used hallucinogenic mushrooms, over half
planned to take them again, with "Mexican" variety being the most commonly used.
Qualitative research from the Netherlands indicated that users generally did not
find mushroom use particularly pleasant, lacking the positive mood-altering
(entactogenic) effects of ecstasy.

## Perceived Availability

ESPAD 2003 data on perceived availability showed that rates of 15-16 year old
students who considered hallucinogenic mushrooms "very" or "fairly easy" to
obtain ranged from 4% to 28%. Under 10% reported easy access in Cyprus, Finland,
Greece, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Romania, and Turkey, while over 20% reported
easy access in the Czech Republic, Ireland, Italy, Poland, and the UK. Despite
the lack of legal sanctions, only 16% of Dutch school students reported easy
access. Following the 2005 UK legal change, 67% of Mixmag survey respondents
reported mushrooms were less available than previously.

## Telephone Helpline Data

FESAT reports from 26 drug telephone help lines across 16 European countries
showed no significant increase in calls regarding hallucinogenic mushrooms during
2004. Belgium reported 1% of all Druglijn inquiries were mushroom-related in
both 2003 and 2004. The Swedish Poisons Information Centre saw calls peak at 96
in 1998, then stabilize at 30-40 annually in subsequent years, suggesting a
stable rather than growing pattern of acute incidents.

## Barriers to Diffusion

Several factors appear to limit the diffusion of hallucinogenic mushroom use:
unpredictable potency, negative effects including nausea and panic attacks, lack
of sociable effects compared to ecstasy, limited repeat use appeal, and the
inherently challenging nature of the psychedelic experience. The Dutch risk
assessment noted that mushrooms tend to be taken experimentally, with people
generally discontinuing use after a few experiences.
