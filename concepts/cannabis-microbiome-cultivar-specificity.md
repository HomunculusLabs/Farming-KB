# Cannabis Microbiome Cultivar Specificity

The Cannabis microbiome is shaped by a two-tier selection process where soil type determines the broad microbial community composition across bulk soil, rhizosphere, and endorhiza compartments, while plant cultivar genotype fine-tunes the specific community structure within the endorhiza (root interior). This was the first comprehensive characterization of the endorhiza, rhizosphere, and bulk soil-associated microbiomes of distinct Cannabis cultivars, conducted by Winston et al. (2014).

## The Two-Tier Selection Model

The two-tier selection model describes how microbial communities shift as they colonize from soil into plant roots:

**Tier 1: Soil-driven filtering.** Edaphic (soil) factors, particularly nitrogen, carbon, water content, salinity, and pH, determine the composition of the bulk soil microbiota. These microbes form the source pool for colonization of the rhizosphere, the nutrient-rich zone immediately surrounding roots. The strongest predictor of community composition across all sample types was soil type, explaining 32% of community variation.

**Tier 2: Host genotype-driven selection.** Following rhizosphere colonization, migration into plant root tissues is governed by host plant genotype. Different cultivars selectively recruit or enrich specific bacterial taxa from the available soil-derived pool into the endorhiza. This second tier acts primarily on community structure (relative abundance of OTUs) rather than composition (presence/absence).

## Sample Compartments

Three distinct microbial habitats were characterized around Cannabis roots:

### Bulk Soil
The soil at a distance from plant roots, representing the ambient microbial community. Alpha diversity (chao1) is highest in bulk soil, ranging from approximately 4300 to 5600 depending on soil type. Community composition is dominated by the full spectrum of soil bacteria, including Acidobacteria that typically dominate in acidic forest soils.

### Rhizosphere
The soil tightly adhering to root surfaces after removal from the ground. This zone is enriched by rhizodeposits (exudates, mucilage, sloughed cells) that create a nutrient gradient, shifting community composition toward bacteria adapted to metabolize plant-derived carbon compounds. Alpha diversity shows a slight reduction from bulk soil. Communities are intermediate between bulk soil and endorhiza in composition.

### Endorhiza
Bacteria colonized within root tissue. This compartment shows the most dramatic community shifts from bulk soil, with a significant reduction in Acidobacteria and an increase in Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria. Alpha diversity drops dramatically, with chao1 values of approximately 3300. This compartment shows the strongest cultivar-specific effects, as the plant immune system and root tissue chemistry exert selective pressure on which bacteria gain entry.

## Cultivar-Specific Communities

### First Experiment (Sour Diesel, Bookoo Kush, Burmese)

Three Cannabis cultivars were grown in similar soils in Vista, California. Endorhiza communities showed significant cultivar-specific clustering (ADONIS: R2 = 0.59, p = 0.004 for weighted UniFrac). The abundance of Methylophilus explained a significant portion of the inter-cultivar difference, comprising 13% of the endorhiza community in Bookoo Kush, only 0.13% in Burmese, and was absent entirely in Sour Diesel.

### Second Experiment (White Widow, Maui Wowie)

Two cultivars were grown in two distinct soil types (Mo-Bio soil and Orange County soil) with significant edaphic differences in total carbon, nitrogen, salinity, and water content. Community beta diversity was significantly different between soil types, sample types, and strains. The prevalence of Sphingomonas wittichii distinguished the Maui Wowie endorhiza. This bacterium can metabolize phenazine-1-carboxylic acid and has been implicated in increased survival in soil environments, potentially conferring advantages to its host plant.

### Core Endorhiza Community

Despite cultivar-specific differences, all endorhiza samples shared a core bacterial community including Pseudomonas, Cellvibrio, Oxalobacteraceae, Xanthomonadaceae, Actinomycetales, and Sphingobacteriales. With the exception of the aerobic cellulytic bacterium Cellvibrio, all prevalent core community members are well-known endophytic bacteria, primarily within the orders Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria.

## Edaphic Factors and Microbial Community Structure

Soil physicochemical properties were the strongest determinants of microbial community structure across all sample types. In order of importance:

1. **Nitrogen** (strongest effect, r-stat: 0.465 weighted, 0.630 unweighted)
2. **Salinity** (r-stat: 0.437 weighted, 0.620 unweighted)
3. **Total carbon** (r-stat: 0.330 weighted, 0.512 unweighted)
4. **Water content** (r-stat: 0.281 weighted, 0.466 unweighted)
5. **pH** (weakest of the five, r-stat: 0.221 weighted, 0.292 unweighted)

The optimal combination of three edaphic factors (nitrogen, carbon, and water) explained 63.2% of community variation (BEST analysis). This finding suggests that manipulating soil conditions could be the most effective approach to shaping the Cannabis microbiome for agricultural purposes.

## Phylum-Level Shifts

Consistent with the two-tier selection model, the following shifts were observed from bulk soil to endorhiza:

- **Acidobacteria**: Dramatically decreased in endorhiza, with the order iii1-15 showing the most significant reduction (Bonferroni-corrected ANOVA: p = 1.12e-7). Acidobacteria are oligotrophic soil bacteria poorly adapted to the carbon-rich root environment.
- **Proteobacteria**: Increased in endorhiza, particularly orders Rhizobiales, Pseudomonadales, Burkholderiales, and Sphingomonadales. These copiotrophic bacteria are well-adapted to metabolizing plant exudates.
- **Actinobacteria**: Increased in endorhiza, consistent with their known role as common plant endophytes and producers of bioactive compounds.
- **Bacteroidetes**: Orders Sphingobacteriales and Flavobacteriales contributed to strain-level differences, suggesting cultivar-specific recruitment.

## OTU Sharing Between Compartments

Beta distances confirmed that rhizosphere and bulk soil communities are more similar to each other than either is to endorhiza communities. Testing the two-step model directly, White Widow plants grown in two different soils shared significantly more OTUs between endorhiza and their own soil (mean = 2934) than between endorhiza and the foreign soil (mean = 2162), supporting the prediction that endophytic microbes are derived from and selected by the surrounding soil.

## Cannabinoid Correlations

Cannabinoid concentration and composition showed significant correlation with endorhiza community structure. However, because higher THC composition occurred in plants from one particular soil type, and THC was also significantly correlated with soil edaphic variables, the association between cannabinoids and microbiota could not be cleanly disentangled from soil physicochemical effects. This represents an important area for future research with controlled experimental designs.

## Post-Harvest Root Decay

The first experiment sampled plants 8 weeks after harvest, revealing evidence of root decay. The relative abundance of the cellulytic bacterium Cellvibrio was 16.9% in post-harvest endorhiza versus only 0.095% in plants sampled 2 weeks before harvest. This dramatic difference suggests root tissue senescence and decomposition were occurring, highlighting the importance of sampling timing in microbiome studies.

## Functional Roles of Endorhiza Bacteria

The bacteria that successfully colonize the Cannabis endorhiza are not passive residents. They may perform several beneficial functions:

- **Nutrient cycling**: Endophytic bacteria can fix atmospheric nitrogen, solubilize phosphate, and chelate micronutrients, making them more available to the plant.
- **Disease suppression**: Certain endophytes produce antimicrobial compounds that protect the host plant from pathogens. Pseudomonas species are particularly well-known for producing antibiotics and siderophores that inhibit soil-borne diseases.
- **Growth promotion**: Some endophytes produce phytohormones such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), cytokinins, and gibberellins that directly stimulate plant growth.
- **Stress tolerance**: Endophytic bacteria can induce systemic resistance in plants, helping them cope with abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and heavy metals.

The cultivar-specific recruitment patterns observed suggest that different Cannabis varieties may selectively enrich for bacteria that confer specific advantages relevant to their particular growth characteristics or environmental adaptations.

## Methodological Considerations

The study employed 16S rRNA gene sequencing on the V4 region using barcoded primers and Illumina MiSeq sequencing. Sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity. Alpha diversity was calculated using the chao1 index, and beta diversity was assessed using both weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances.

One limitation noted was the potential confounding of post-harvest sampling effects in the first experiment, where plants had been harvested 8 weeks prior to root sampling. This introduced the confounding variable of root tissue decomposition, which was addressed by conducting the second experiment with pre-harvest sampling.

## Implications for Cannabis Agriculture

Understanding the Cannabis microbiome has practical implications for cultivation:

- **Soil selection** is the primary lever for influencing the microbial community, more so than cultivar choice
- **Cultivar-specific microbes** may contribute to plant fitness, disease suppression, or metabolite production
- **Endorhiza communities** could potentially be manipulated through inoculation strategies, though the strong soil-driven filtering means such approaches must account for local soil conditions
- **Timing of sampling** is critical; post-harvest samples show degraded microbial communities not representative of active plant-microbe interactions
