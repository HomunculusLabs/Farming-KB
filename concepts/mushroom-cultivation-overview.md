# Mushroom Cultivation Overview Mushroom cultivation is the practice of growing fungi for food, medicine, or research. The process transforms spores or tissue cultures into fruiting bodies through a series of controlled stages. This overview covers the complete lifecycle from spore to harvest. ## The Mushroom Life Cycle Mushrooms are Basidiomycetes -- fungi that produce spores on club-shaped cells called basidia. The life cycle proceeds as follows: 1. **Spore germination**: A basidiospore germinates to form a monokaryotic (single-nucleus) hypha.2. **Mating (somatogamy)**: Two compatible monokaryotic mycelia fuse to form a dikaryotic mycelium containing two nuclei per cell. Most psilocybin mushrooms are tetrapolar heterothallic, meaning compatibility is governed by two sets of mating factors (A and B).3. **Vegetative growth**: The dikaryotic mycelium grows indefinitely, absorbing nutrients. This is the main assimilative stage and the primary stage used in cultivation.4. **Fruiting (basidiocarp formation)**: Given appropriate environmental triggers, the mycelium differentiates into a mushroom (basidiocarp). Karyogamy (nuclear fusion) occurs in the basidia on the gills, followed by meiosis, producing four haploid basidiospores per basidium.5. **Spore release**: Spores disperse to restart the cycle. ## The Cultivation Pipeline Cultivation typically follows these stages: ### Stage 1: Spore Collection and Germination Spore prints are collected from mature caps. ## Contamination Prevention
Contamination is the single most common cause of failure in mushroom
cultivation. Contaminants include molds (Trichoderma, Aspergillus,
Penicillium), bacteria (bacillus, pseudomonas), and competitor fungi. The
spores of these contaminants are ubiquitous in the environment -- a single
cubic meter of indoor air can contain thousands of mold spores. Successful
cultivation depends on creating a window of opportunity where the substrate is
sterile and the desired mycelium can colonize it before competitors
arrive.

Key prevention measures include working in a still-air box or laminar
flow
hood for all inoculation steps, flame-sterilizing tools between uses, wearing
gloves and masks, and maintaining clean work surfaces with 70 percent
isopropyl alcohol. Grain jars should be filtered with synthetic filter disks
or polyfill to allow gas exchange while excluding spores. If contamination is
detected early, the affected jar or tray should be removed immediately and
disposed of outdoors -- never opened indoors, as releasing a cloud of mold
spores can contaminate the entire grow
space.

## Harvesting and Post-Harvest

Mushrooms should be harvested just before or as the veil beneath the
cap tears, which is when potency and texture are optimal for most
species. Harvesting is done by gently twisting the stem at the base
rather than cutting, which reduces the likelihood of leaving a stump
that could harbor contaminants. After harvest, mushrooms can be
fresh-dried using a food dehydrator at 95-115°F (35-46°C) until
cracker-dry. Properly dried mushrooms retain potency for years when
stored in airtight containers with desiccant, away from light and heat.
