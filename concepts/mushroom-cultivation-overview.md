# Mushroom Cultivation Overview Mushroom cultivation is the practice of growing fungi for food, medicine, or research. The process transforms spores or tissue cultures into fruiting bodies through a series of controlled stages. This overview covers the complete lifecycle from spore to harvest. ## The Mushroom Life Cycle Mushrooms are Basidiomycetes -- fungi that produce spores on club-shaped cells called basidia. The life cycle proceeds as follows: 1. **Spore germination**: A basidiospore germinates to form a monokaryotic (single-nucleus) hypha.2. **Mating (somatogamy)**: Two compatible monokaryotic mycelia fuse to form a dikaryotic mycelium containing two nuclei per cell. Most psilocybin mushrooms are tetrapolar heterothallic, meaning compatibility is governed by two sets of mating factors (A and B).3. **Vegetative growth**: The dikaryotic mycelium grows indefinitely, absorbing nutrients. This is the main assimilative stage and the primary stage used in cultivation.4. **Fruiting (basidiocarp formation)**: Given appropriate environmental triggers, the mycelium differentiates into a mushroom (basidiocarp). Karyogamy (nuclear fusion) occurs in the basidia on the gills, followed by meiosis, producing four haploid basidiospores per basidium.5. **Spore release**: Spores disperse to restart the cycle. ## The Cultivation Pipeline Cultivation typically follows these stages: ### Stage 1: Spore Collection and Germination Spore prints are collected from mature caps. Spores are germinated on mushroom-spore-syringe-and-inoculation for details on spore syringe preparation. ### Stage 2: Agar Culture and Strain Isolation Germinated spores produce a multispore culture containing many genetic substrains. Through successive transfers, individual pure substrains can be isolated for more uniform and productive flushes. Tissue from promising fruitbodies can also be cloned onto agar. ### Stage 3: Grain Spawn Preparation Pure mycelium is transferred from agar to sterilized grain (rye, brown rice, millet) to expand the mycelial mass. See mushroom-substrate-recipes for substrate formulations. ### Stage 5: Fruiting Once fully colonized, the substrate is introduced to fruiting conditions -- appropriate temperature, humidity, fresh air exchange (FAE), and light. A casing layer (non-nutritive soil mix) may be applied to induce pinning for some species. See mushroom-lighting-and-environmental-requirements. ### Stage 6: Harvest and Preservation Mushrooms are harvested before or just as the veil tears for maximum potency. They are dried using desiccant or a dehydrator and stored airtight. ## Major Cultivation Methods ### PF TEK (Psilocybe Fanaticus Method) A beginner-friendly method using brown rice flour and vermiculite in half-pint jars. The substrate is steam-sterilized (no pressure cooker needed), inoculated with a spore syringe, and fruited directly from the jar in a humidified terrarium. No grain spawn or casing layer is required. The entire cycle takes 4-6 weeks. ### Rye Grain to Bulk Mycelium is grown on sterilized rye grain, then mixed with a pasteurized bulk substrate (coir, manure, straw) in a larger container. Higher yields than PF TEK but requires a pressure cooker and more [[outdoor-wood-lover-cultivation]].
### Panaeolus Cultivation
[[stamets-panaeolus-cyanescens-copelandia-identification-ecology]] and P. tropicalis require a dung-based substrate (dried
cow dung, straw, vermiculite), casing, and very high fresh air exchange. See
[[psilocybin-mushroom-identification]].

## Key Principles

- **Sterility is paramount**: Contamination is the primary cause of failure. All inoculation steps must be performed under sterile conditions.
- **Genetic diversity vs. uniformity**: Multispore inoculation preserves full genetic diversity but produces variable flushes. Cloned isolates produce more uniform results.
- **[[eclss-environmental-control-life-support]]**: Fruiting requires specific combinations of temperature, humidity, CO2 levels, light, and air exchange that vary by species.
- **Substrate matching**: Different species require different nutritional bases -- dung, grain, wood, or compost.

## Contamination Prevention
Contamination is the single most common cause of failure in mushroom
cultivation. Contaminants include molds (Trichoderma, Aspergillus,
Penicillium), bacteria (bacillus, pseudomonas), and competitor fungi. The
spores of these contaminants are ubiquitous in the environment -- a single
cubic meter of indoor air can contain thousands of mold spores. Successful
cultivation depends on creating a window of opportunity where the substrate is
sterile and the desired mycelium can colonize it before competitors
arrive.

Key prevention measures include working in a still-air box or laminar
flow
hood for all inoculation steps, flame-sterilizing tools between uses, wearing
gloves and masks, and maintaining clean work surfaces with 70 percent
isopropyl alcohol. Grain jars should be filtered with synthetic filter disks
or polyfill to allow gas exchange while excluding spores. If contamination is
detected early, the affected jar or tray should be removed immediately and
disposed of outdoors -- never opened indoors, as releasing a cloud of mold
spores can contaminate the entire grow
space.

## Environmental Parameters by Growth Phase
Each phase of mushroom cultivation requires specific environmental conditions.
During colonization ([[mushroom-spawn-run-thermogenesis-cotter]]), the substrate should be kept at the species'
optimal colonization temperature, typically between 70-78°F (21-26°C) for P.
cubensis. Higher CO2 concentrations (5,000-10,000 ppm) during colonization
actually promote faster [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]], so containers should be sealed or
only loosely filtered. Light is unnecessary during this phase and can even
promote premature
pinning.

During fruiting, the environment shifts dramatically. CO2 must be
reduced
below 800 ppm through fresh air exchange (FAE), humidity raised to 85-95
percent, and indirect light introduced on a 12/12 cycle. A temperature drop of
5-10°F from the colonization temperature often serves as the primary pinning
