Vermicomposting is the process of using earthworms to convert organic materials
into nutrient-rich castings, and in *Teaming With Microbes*, Lowenfels and Lewis
present it as a valuable complement to traditional
[[comparison-bokashi-vs-hot-composting-method]] but
requires less space and physical effort.

## Feedstocks for Vermicomposting

Good starting materials for vermicompost include food wastes (excluding fats and
meats), paper, cardboard, leaves, and [[mushroom-cultivator-compost-pile-management]] work, with some precautions. Weedy materials
should be thermally composted first before adding to the worm bin to prevent
unwanted seedlings. Brown materials need to be shredded or broken up so worms
can ingest them more quickly.  Placing bins outdoors encourages microarthropod
and insect activity that helps physically [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]] in the
finished product.

## Vermicastings: Nutrient Profile

The castings produced by vermicomposting are extraordinary soil amendments. They
are 50 percent higher in [[lowenfels-bacterial-fungal-dominance]], making
vermicompost an excellent amendment for these plantings. It can be applied as a
top dressing, mixed into planting holes, or used as an ingredient in
[[lowenfels-compost-tea-brewing]]. Its high bacterial content makes it ideal for producing
bacterial teas for lawns, vegetables, and annuals. When used in compost tea,
proportionately less vermicompost is needed compared to regular compost because
of its concentrated microbial populations. The bacteria and nutrients in
vermicompost tea are readily available to plant roots, providing a quick boost
to soil biology when applied to the rhizosphere.

## Comparison to Hot Composting

Vermicomposting and hot composting each have advantages. Hot composting is
faster, producing finished compost in weeks, and it reaches temperatures that
kill pathogens and weed seeds. Vermicomposting is slower but requires less
space, no turning, and can be done indoors year-round. Hot compost can be
tailored to be either bacterial or fungal by adjusting the carbon- to-nitrogen
ratio of inputs, while vermicompost is inherently bacterial.  Both produce
valuable soil amendments, and using both methods provides gardeners with
flexible options for managing organic waste and building soil biology.

## Summary

Vermicomposting uses earthworms and their gut bacteria to convert organic waste
into exceptionally nutrient-rich castings that are bacterially dominated.
Vermicastings exceed surrounding soil in phosphate, potash, nitrogen, magnesium,
and calcium availability. They support bacterial populations needed by
vegetables, annuals, and lawns, and are excellent for compost tea production.
Combined with traditional hot composting, vermicomposting provides a
comprehensive approach to recycling organic waste and building healthy soil
biology.
