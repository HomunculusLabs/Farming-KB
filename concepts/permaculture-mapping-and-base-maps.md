## Permaculture Mapping and Base Maps

Mapping is the foundational design tool in permaculture. William Ozier Williams provides detailed guidance on creating and using base maps in his PDC, describing the base map as the starting point from which all analysis and design decisions flow.

## What Is a Base Map?

The base map is a scaled drawing of the entire site within the legal property boundaries. It shows the existing conditions as they are, before any design modifications. It serves as the blank canvas onto which all analytical data (zones, sectors, water flow, soil types) and design elements are placed.

Williams states: "The Base Map is the map of the entire site within the legal property limits."

## Acquiring or Creating a Base Map

### Existing Maps

The easiest starting point is to obtain an existing map:

- **County or city offices**: Tax maps, survey maps, plat maps, and topographic maps are often available from local government
- **Surveyor's plot plan**: If available from the property purchase
- **Google Earth**: Useful for creating a base map when no survey exists; can measure distances and areas, export imagery
- **USGS topographic maps**: Show contour, water features, and surrounding landscape context

### Creating Your Own

If no suitable map exists, you can create one:

1. **Measure the property boundaries** using a long tape measure, laser rangefinder, or GPS
2. **Establish a scale**: Common scales for residential properties:
   - 1:100 (1 cm = 1 meter) for small urban lots
   - 1:200 (1 cm = 2 meters) for typical suburban properties
   - 1:500 (1 cm = 5 meters) for larger rural properties
   - 1:1000 (1 cm = 10 meters) for large acreages
3. **Measure and locate all existing features**: Buildings, trees, paths, fences, water features, utilities, slopes
4. **Draw the map**: Graph paper or tracing paper at the chosen scale; or use digital tools

## What to Include on the Base Map

### Property Information
- Property boundaries and dimensions
- North arrow
- Scale bar
- Date of survey
- Legal description or address
- Contour lines (if topographic information is available)

### Built Features
- All buildings with footprints and heights
- Driveways, roads, and parking areas
- Paths and walkways
- Fences and walls
- Utilities: water lines, power lines, gas lines, septic systems
- Wells, water meters, power connections
- Outdoor structures: sheds, greenhouses, barns, pergolas

### Natural Features
- Trees (with species and approximate canopy spread)
- Existing garden beds and plantings
- Lawns and meadows
- Rocks and outcrops
- Water features: ponds, streams, springs, ditches, wet areas
- Slope indicators (if not using contour lines)
- Erosion areas
- Shade patterns (at least approximate)

### Access
- Entry points to the property
- Vehicle access routes
- Pedestrian routes
- Rights of way and easements

## Working With the Base Map

### Keep an Original

Williams strongly recommends keeping one clean original of the base map as a permanent reference. Never draw on the original.

### Create Working Copies

Make multiple copies for different analytical layers:

- **Tracing paper method**: Overlay tracing paper on the base map. Put all analytical data on one sheet if possible, as multiple layers become difficult to read.
- **Transparency method**: Create separate transparent overlays for related data groups (e.g., one for sectors and zones, another for water flow and soil types).
- **Digital method**: Use software (CAD, GIS, drawing programs) to create layered digital base maps where each data type is a separate toggleable layer.

### Recommended Data Groupings for Overlays

1. Zones and sectors
2. Surface water flow and soil types
3. Vegetation and existing plant communities
4. Microclimates
5. Access and circulation
6. Buildings and infrastructure

## Mapping Techniques

### Finding Contour

On sloped land, mapping contour lines is essential:

- **A-frame level**: Simple, inexpensive tool made from two legs and a crossbar with a plumb bob
- **Bunyip water level**: Flexible tube with water; level at both ends indicates same elevation
- **Laser level**: More precise, requires power
- **GPS**: Can provide elevation data but may lack precision for detailed design work

### Measuring Slope

- Clinometer or inclinometer for slope percentage
- Rise over run measurement with a level and tape measure
- Online topographic maps for approximate data

### Mapping Water Flow

After mapping contours, chart surface water flow:

- Observe the site during and after rain
- Map where water flows, pools, and exits the property
- Note infiltration rates in different areas
- This data is essential for designing [[swale-construction-guide|swales]], [[permaculture-pond-and-water-garden|ponds]], and other water management features

## Scale Considerations

Choose a scale appropriate to your property size and the level of detail needed:

- Too small a scale (too zoomed out) and you cannot show design details
- Too large a scale (too zoomed in) and the map becomes unwieldy with multiple sheets
- The scale should allow you to show the smallest detail you need to design while fitting the entire property on one sheet
