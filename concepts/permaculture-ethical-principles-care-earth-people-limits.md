# Permaculture Ethical Principles: Care for Earth, People, and Limits

Ethics are the moral principles guiding action toward good outcomes and away from bad ones. In permaculture, ethics function as constraints on survival instincts and self-interest — culturally evolved mechanisms for enlightened self-interest, a more inclusive view of "us," and a longer-term understanding of consequences. This page explores the three core permaculture ethical principles in depth, their philosophical foundations, and their relationship to energy descent.

## The Three Core Ethics

### 1. Care for the Earth
### 2. Care for People
### 3. Set Limits to Consumption and Reproduction, and Redistribute Surplus

These principles were distilled from research into community ethics adopted by older religious and cooperative groups. The third (and even the second) can be seen as derived from the first. They are common to all indigenous tribal peoples and reflect cultures that have existed in relative balance with their environment longer than any recent civilization.

## Ethics in Context: Energy and Power

The greater the power of human civilization (due to energy availability) and the greater the concentration of power within society, the more critical ethics become in ensuring long-term cultural and even biological survival. This ecologically functional view of ethics makes them central to developing a culture for energy descent.

Beliefs and values that developed with a rising energy base are likely to be dysfunctional — even destructive — in a world of limited and declining energy. We should be suspicious of seeing individualism as the source rather than outcome of material well-being.

## Philosophical Frameworks

### Critique of Reductionism

- **Cartesian dualism** separates mind and body, humanity and nature, thought and action
- **Reductionist science** seeks fundamental causes in simple constituents
- Reductionism both explained the industrial world and reflected its ideology
- Despite substantial philosophical critiques, it held sway because it is powerful in dealing with an increasingly disintegrated world
- Now an impediment to human survival despite its past achievements

### Productive Alternatives

- **Dialectical materialism** — understanding through contradictions and synthesis
- **Systems theory** — understanding through relationships and feedback
- **Design science** — understanding through purposeful creation

### Permaculture as Design Science

[[bill-mollison]] described permaculture as integrated design science. It is applied science concerned with improving long-term material well-being by drawing together strategies from modern and traditional cultures, seeking wholistic integration of utilitarian values through an ecological perspective.

## Spiritual Dimensions

### The Science-Spirituality Convergence

- The success of science and materialism has led to unprecedented disharmony and discontent
- A shift to more spiritually based values may be essential for survival
- The more we understand through systems thinking and ecology, the more we see wisdom in spiritual perspectives
- Jungian psychology shows the same convergence
- [[rudolf-steiner]]'s spiritual science has borne practical fruits in Waldorf education and biodynamics

Holmgren acknowledges being drawn toward spiritual awareness through permaculture practice, despite an atheist, humanist upbringing. He interprets permaculture's ethical principles on rational and humanist foundations while accepting the evidence of spiritual dimensions.

## Principle 1: Care for the Earth

### Spaceship Earth vs. Gaia

**Spaceship Earth** (Stewart Brand, 1960s-70s): Powerful for galvanizing understanding of global crisis, but implies we have power and wisdom to manage the earth — an abstraction separate from us.

**Gaia Hypothesis** (James Lovelock, Lynn Margulis): Earth as a self-organized system. Evidence of 4 billion years shows that if we seriously affect fundamental life-support systems, we will be "neutralized" by co-evolutionary mechanisms (climate change, disease). The Gaia hypothesis spawned a countercultural revival of the indigenous view of earth as living, all-powerful mother.

### Living Soil

In the most grounded sense, Care for the Earth means caring for **living soil** as the source of terrestrial life:

- The state of soils is the best measure of future health and well-being of society
- Builds on both scientific and ethical traditions of organic/biological agriculture
- Reverence for soil tends to be forgotten in excitement about "sexier" issues
- Careless and profligate soil use can result in rapid loss of its life-supporting capacity

### Stewardship vs. Connection

Wendell Berry criticized planetary stewardship as a product of arrogant disconnection from nature. The real question is not how to care for the earth in general, but how to care for the particular places we inhabit and understand. Bioregionalism — the concept of caring for home, place, country, or territory — acknowledges individual and collective responsibility for particular natural resources about which we have understanding and power.

## Principle 2: Care for People

Care for people means attending to human needs starting from ourselves and our immediate community, then extending outward. The second principle can be derived from the first — caring for the earth requires caring for the people who depend on it and whose actions affect it.

This principle recognizes that sustainable systems must provide for human material and non-material needs. A design that neglects human well-being will ultimately fail because people will not maintain systems that do not serve them.

## Principle 3: Set Limits and Redistribute Surplus

The third principle has multiple dimensions:

### Setting Limits to Consumption

- Recognizing that infinite growth is impossible on a finite planet
- Self-regulation of consumption based on ecological understanding
- Accepting that energy descent requires reduced material throughput

### Setting Limits to Reproduction

- Population must eventually balance with renewable resource base
- Voluntary restraint is preferable to nature's involuntary checks

### Redistributing Surplus

- Surplus time, money, energy, and skills should be directed toward rebuilding natural capital and community resilience
- This is not charity but enlightened self-interest — investing in the systems that support us

## Indigenous Ethics as Foundation

The [[permaculture-ethics]] are common to all indigenous tribal peoples. Permaculture's focus on learning from [[ethnobotanical-use-of-sacred-mushrooms-in-indigenous-cultures]] is based on evidence that these cultures have:

- Existed in relative balance with their environment
- Survived longer than any recent experiments in civilization
- Developed sophisticated understandings of local ecology
- Maintained social structures that reinforced ethical behavior

This does not mean romanticizing indigenous cultures or ignoring their problems, but recognizing that their longevity provides evidence of sustainable design principles.

## Key Takeaways

1. Ethics function as ecologically functional constraints on self-interest — not abstract ideals
2. The three ethics are hierarchical: care for earth is primary, care for people derives from it, limits derive from both
3. Energy availability determines the criticality of ethics — more power requires more ethical constraint
4. Reductionist science, while powerful, is now an impediment to understanding whole systems
5. The convergence of scientific and spiritual understanding is a positive trend that permaculture embodies
6. Care for the earth is most concretely expressed through caring for living soil and specific places
7. Indigenous cultures provide evidence that ethics based on ecological reality can sustain societies for millennia
