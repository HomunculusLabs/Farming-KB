# PF TEK Fruiting Chamber Temperature and Heating Principles

The dual [[dual-chambered-terrarium-design]] fruiting environment operates on a
counterintuitive principle: direct heating works against humidification and
adds a drying influence. This is one of the most commonly violated rules in
home [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]], and Psylocybe Fanaticus was explicit that heating
cables, heat pads, heat blankets, and direct light should never be used in
the fruiting chamber.

## The Drying Problem with Direct Heat

When heat is applied directly to a fruiting chamber, it accelerates evaporation
of the water supply (whether perlite, spray-generated moisture, or substrate
moisture). The warmer air can hold more water vapor, but this water is drawn
from the limited reservoir inside the sealed terrarium. The result is a net loss
of available humidity at the mushroom surface, even if the relative humidity
briefly spikes.

The fungus requires bathing in a "shroud of floating water molecules" at or
near 100% humidity. Molecularized water (evaporated humidity) is what the fine
[[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]] need. Direct water droplets from spraying can actually drown
the delicate fungal structures and inhibit or contaminate growth.

### The Physics of Sealed Chamber Heating

The physics are straightforward: warmer air holds more water vapor before
reaching saturation. In an open system like a [[mollison-designers-tropical-pasture-grass-species-and-establishment]], warm air
continuously draws moisture from the surrounding environment. In a sealed
terrarium, warm air draws moisture only from the surfaces inside until the
internal moisture reservoir is exhausted. At that point the relative humidity
drops, even though the air temperature remains elevated.

This creates a paradoxical situation: the cultivator applies heat intending to
create tropical conditions, but instead creates a progressively drier
environment that kills the very humidity the fungus depends on.

## Optimal Temperature Ranges

Psilocybe cubensis fruits well at temperatures as low as 60F (15C). PF
reported seeing the fungi growing perfectly at temperatures even cooler than
60F. The fungi grow more slowly at cooler temperatures but develop normally.
When grown at warm or heated room temperature, they grow very fast. The
recommended range is between 65F and the upper 80sF (18-31C).

The biological rationale is that in nature, Psilocybe cubensis fruits during
warm, humid conditions in subtropical and tropical grasslands. However, the
cultivated environment of the terrarium is fundamentally different from the
open air of a tropical pasture. In nature, warm air carries more moisture from
the surrounding environment. In a sealed terrarium, warm air only draws
moisture out of the limited internal reservoir.

## Heating Methods (and Why to Avoid Them)

Several heating methods are commonly attempted by new cultivators, all of
which violate the no-heat principle. Heating pads placed under the terrarium
create a hot spot that dries the lower portion of the chamber. Aquarium
heaters submerged in the perlite water heat the entire moisture supply and
accelerate evaporation across all surfaces. Heat cables wrapped around or
under the chamber provide uneven heat distribution with the same drying
effect.

Positioning the terrarium near a heat vent or radiator creates fluctuating
temperatures that stress the developing fungi. Leaving the terrarium in direct
sunlight provides both radiant heat and light that is too intense. All of
these methods share the same failure mechanism: they increase the rate of
moisture loss from the sealed system without providing any mechanism for
moisture replenishment.

## Thermostat Placement and Monitoring

If temperature monitoring is desired, the thermostat or thermometer should be
placed at the level of the mushroom cakes inside the terrarium, not on the
floor or near any potential heat source. A simple digital thermometer with a
remote probe works well. The cultivator should verify that the reading
represents the actual conditions the mushrooms experience, not the conditions
near the terrarium wall or lid.

The ideal monitoring strategy is minimal and passive. Check temperature once
daily at most. If the room ambient falls within the 65-80F range, no
adjustment is needed. If the room is consistently below 60F, the cultivator
should warm the entire room rather than the terrarium directly.

## Humidity-Temperature Balance

The fundamental tradeoff is that any action that raises temperature in a
sealed terrarium will reduce humidity, and any action that raises humidity
will tend to lower temperature (through [[bloomfield-mushroom-evaporative-cooling-fungal-frigidity]]). The cultivator
cannot optimize both simultaneously through active intervention. The correct
approach is to accept whatever [[jadam-ambient-temperature-principle]] the room provides and focus
all effort on maintaining the humidity system.

If the room is cool, the mushrooms will grow more slowly but will develop
normally and maintain higher alkaloid concentrations per gram of tissue. If
the room is warm, the humidity system must work harder through more frequent
spraying, larger perlite volume, or a larger terrarium with more internal
moisture reservoir relative to surface area.

## The Overheating Failure Mode

A terrarium that is too hot will produce lots of spreading mycelium but no
fruiting. This is a common and often confusing failure mode: the cultivator
observes vigorous white growth across the cakes and assumes conditions are
excellent, when in fact the elevated temperature is suppressing the transition
from vegetative mycelial growth to reproductive fruiting.

The mycelium may spread across the entire cake surface and even begin climbing
the walls of the terrarium, giving the appearance of vigorous health while
failing to produce a single mushroom. This can persist for weeks if the
cultivator does not recognize the overheating problem.

Signs of overheating include vigorous mycelial spreading without pin
formation, excessive condensation that quickly drips away, rapid substrate
moisture loss visible as shrinking or cracking cakes, and the terrarium
feeling warm to the touch. The solution is always to remove the heat source.

## Incubation Versus Fruiting Temperature

There is an important distinction between the incubation phase and the fruiting
phase, and the temperature principles are essentially reversed. After
inoculation, jars should be kept around 70F (21C) for incubation. Overhead
light shining on jar tops is an acceptable heating technique during incubation
(the jars are sealed, so moisture loss is minimal), but the temperature must be
monitored to avoid overheating.

Once cakes are birthed into the terrarium, the principle shifts entirely. The
mycelium has already colonized the substrate and accumulated sufficient
resources to trigger fruiting. The organism now needs stable, cool, humid
conditions to form primordia and develop fruit bodies. Active heating is
explicitly prohibited at this stage.
