# PF TEK Fruiting Chamber Temperature and Heating Principles

The dual chambered terrarium fruiting environment operates on a counterintuitive principle: **direct heating works against humidification and adds a drying influence.** This is one of the most commonly violated rules in home mushroom cultivation, and Psylocybe Fanaticus was explicit that heating cables, heat pads, heat blankets, and direct light should never be used in the fruiting chamber.

## The Drying Problem with Direct Heat

When heat is applied directly to a fruiting chamber, it accelerates evaporation of the water supply (whether perlite, spray-generated moisture, or substrate moisture). The warmer air can hold more water vapor, but this water is drawn from the limited reservoir inside the sealed terrarium.

The result is a net loss of available humidity at the mushroom surface, even if the relative humidity briefly spikes.

The fungus requires bathing in a "shroud of floating water molecules" at or near 100% humidity. Molecularized water (evaporated humidity) is what the fine mycelial networks need. Direct water droplets from spraying can actually drown the delicate fungal structures and inhibit or contaminate growth.

Heating disrupts the delicate balance between water available in the substrate, water in the air, and water condensing on surfaces inside the terrarium.

In the PF TEK system, humidity is generated and maintained by spraying water into the primary chamber of the dual chambered terrarium. The spray strikes the acrylic shield and is broken into a fine mist that flows around the shield into the secondary chamber where the cakes are located.

This molecularized water provides the humidity the fungus needs. Any heat source introduced into this system will accelerate the evaporation of this finite moisture supply, and because the terrarium is sealed (to retain humidity), the dried-out air cannot be replenished from the surrounding room.

### The Physics of Sealed Chamber Heating

The physics are straightforward: warmer air holds more water vapor before reaching saturation. In an open system like a tropical pasture, warm air continuously draws moisture from the surrounding environment.

In a sealed terrarium, warm air draws moisture only from the surfaces inside — the perlite, the cake substrates, the condensation on the walls — until the internal moisture reservoir is exhausted. At that point the relative humidity drops, even though the air temperature remains elevated.

This creates a paradoxical situation: the cultivator applies heat intending to create tropical conditions, but instead creates a progressively drier environment that kills the very humidity the fungus depends on.

## Ideal Temperature Range

Psilocybe cubensis fruits well at temperatures as low as 60°F (15°C). PF reported seeing the fungi growing perfectly at temperatures even cooler than 60°F. The fungi grow more slowly at cooler temperatures but develop normally.

When grown at warm or heated room temperature, they grow very fast. The recommended range is between 65°F and the upper 80s°F (18–31°C).

The biological rationale is that in nature, Psilocybe cubensis fruits during warm, humid conditions in subtropical and tropical grasslands. However, the cultivated environment of the terrarium is fundamentally different from the open air of a tropical pasture.

In nature, warm air carries more moisture from the surrounding environment. In a sealed terrarium, warm air only draws moisture out of the limited internal reservoir. This is why the natural temperature preference of the organism does not translate directly to the optimal temperature for the artificial growing environment.

## The Overheating Failure Mode

A terrarium that is too hot will produce lots of spreading mycelium but no fruiting. This is a common and often confusing failure mode: the cultivator observes vigorous white growth across the cakes and assumes conditions are excellent, when in fact the elevated temperature is suppressing the transition from vegetative mycelial growth to reproductive fruiting.

The organism continues to invest energy in vegetative expansion rather than forming primordia because the environmental signals — particularly humidity at the cake surface — are not indicating favorable fruiting conditions. The mycelium may spread across the entire cake surface and even begin climbing the walls of the terrarium, giving the appearance of vigorous health while failing to produce a single mushroom.

This can persist for weeks if the cultivator does not recognize the overheating problem.

### Recognizing Overheating

Signs of overheating in the fruiting chamber include:

- Vigorous mycelial spreading across the cake surface without pin formation
- Excessive condensation on the terrarium lid that quickly drips away (rather than forming a stable fog)
- Rapid substrate moisture loss from the cakes, visible as shrinking or cracking
- The terrarium feeling warm to the touch when placing a hand near it

The solution is always to remove the heat source and allow the chamber to equilibrate to ambient room temperature.

## Lighting Considerations

A small low wattage fluorescent plant light makes the phototropic mushrooms grow upwards and can be left on continuously. However, the light must be kept at a safe distance from the terrarium.

Direct light shining down into the terrarium provides radiant heat that can raise the internal temperature above the fruiting threshold.

Indirect room light (including indirect sunlight) is sufficient for fruiting and poses no heating risk. The mushrooms use light primarily for directional guidance (growing upward) rather than as an energy source, so intensity requirements are minimal. A room with a window providing ambient daylight is ideal.

## Incubation Versus Fruiting Temperature

There is an important distinction between the incubation phase and the fruiting phase, and the temperature principles are essentially reversed.

### Incubation Phase

After inoculation, jars should be kept around 70°F (21°C). Spore germination begins within 3–5 days at this temperature. Overhead light shining on jar tops is an acceptable heating technique during incubation (the jars are sealed, so moisture loss is minimal), but the temperature must be monitored with a thermometer to avoid overheating.

The rule during incubation is "never overheat" — cool temperatures are manageable but hot temperatures kill or stall the mycelium.

### Fruiting Phase

Once cakes are birthed into the terrarium, the principle shifts. The mycelium has already colonized the substrate and accumulated sufficient resources to trigger fruiting. The organism now needs stable, cool, humid conditions to form primordia and develop fruit bodies.

Active heating is explicitly prohibited at this stage.

## Relationship to Humidity Symptoms

Low humidity symptoms can be confused with temperature problems. When humidity drops, mushrooms may develop fuzzy white mycelium on their caps (a "crown of white hair") or show deformed, convoluted, and withering growth.

These symptoms can be caused either by inadequate spraying, by overheating that has dried the moisture supply, or by a combination of both.

Addressing temperature first — removing any heat source — often resolves humidity problems as well, because the moisture already present in the terrarium is no longer being driven off by heat. Only after temperature has been normalized should the cultivator increase spraying frequency to compensate for any remaining humidity deficit.

## Practical Temperature Management

The simplest approach is to place the terrarium in a room where the ambient temperature naturally falls within the 65–80°F range. Most homes maintain this range without any active climate control.

If the growing space tends to be warm, positioning the terrarium near the floor (where air is cooler) or against an exterior wall can help.

The key insight from the PF TEK is that the cultivator's effort should go into maintaining humidity, not temperature — the mushrooms are remarkably tolerant of cool conditions but unforgiving of dry conditions.

## The Temperature-Humidity Tradeoff in Practice

The fundamental tradeoff can be summarized as follows: any action that raises temperature in a sealed terrarium will reduce humidity, and any action that raises humidity will tend to lower temperature (through evaporative cooling). The cultivator cannot optimize both simultaneously through active intervention.

The correct approach is to accept whatever ambient temperature the room provides and focus all effort on maintaining the humidity system — regular spraying, clean perlite, intact seal. If the room is cool, the mushrooms will grow more slowly but will develop normally and maintain higher alkaloid concentrations per gram of tissue.

If the room is warm, the humidity system must work harder. More frequent spraying, larger perlite volume, or a larger terrarium (more internal moisture reservoir relative to surface area) can partially compensate. But there is a temperature ceiling beyond which no amount of spraying can maintain adequate humidity — the moisture simply evaporates too fast.

## Common Temperature Mistakes

The most frequent temperature-related mistakes in PF TEK cultivation are:

- Placing heating pads under the terrarium, which creates a hot spot that dries the lower portion of the chamber
- Using an aquarium heater in the perlite water, which heats the entire moisture supply and accelerates evaporation
- Positioning the terrarium near a heat vent or radiator, which creates fluctuating temperatures
- Leaving the terrarium in direct sunlight, which provides both radiant heat and light that is too intense

All of these share the same mechanism of failure: they increase the rate of moisture loss from the sealed system without providing any mechanism for moisture replenishment. The solution in every case is the same — remove the heat source.
