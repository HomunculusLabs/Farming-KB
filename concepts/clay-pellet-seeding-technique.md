# Clay Pellet Seeding Technique

Clay pellet seeding (also known as seed balls or nendo dango) is a direct
seeding method developed by [[masanobu-fukuoka]] as part of his natural
farming system. Seeds are encased in balls of clay and soil, which
protect them from predation and desiccation until conditions are suitable
for germination. The technique is primarily associated with Fukuoka's
ambitious proposal for [[fukuoka-aerial-seeding-clay-pellets-desert-revegetation-technique]], though it has applications in
agriculture, [[guerrilla-gardening-techniques]], and [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]].

## Mechanism and Purpose

The clay pellet serves multiple protective functions. The hard clay
exterior shields seeds from being eaten by birds, rodents, and insects.
It also prevents premature germination by blocking moisture until the
clay is softened by sustained rainfall. Once sufficient rain falls, the
clay dissolves gradually, exposing the seeds to moisture and allowing
them to germinate at the optimal time.

Fukuoka also incorporated soil and microorganisms into the pellets. One
gram of soil from his farm contained approximately 100 million
nitrogen-fixing bacteria and other soil-enriching microbes. He believed
that these microorganisms, distributed along with the seeds, could
initiate the biological processes necessary to restore degraded soils.

## Desert Revegetation Proposal

Fukuoka's most ambitious application of clay pellet seeding was his
proposal to revegetate the world's deserts. He argued that deserts do
not form because there is no rain, but rather rain ceases to fall because
vegetation has disappeared. Restoring plant cover would, in his view,
bring back the rains by restoring the local water cycle.

The proposed seed mixture included [[comparison-green-manure-guide-vs-mulch-types-and-effects]] trees such as black
wattle ([[acacia-mearnsii]]) capable of growing in areas with annual rainfall
under two inches, along with clover, alfalfa, bur clover, and other green
manure plants mixed with grain and vegetable seeds. The mixture was
coated first with soil, then with clay, to form microbe-containing
pellets.

These finished pellets could be scattered by hand over deserts and
savannahs. After one year, the surviving plants would indicate which
species were suited to the local climate and soil. Fukuoka envisioned
blanketing deserts rapidly with green cover to restore rainfall patterns.

## Preparation Method

The basic clay pellet preparation involves several steps. Seeds of
desired plant species are mixed together in proportions suited to the
target environment. This seed mixture is combined with moist soil
containing [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]]. The seed-soil mixture is then
coated with clay to form small balls, typically marble-sized or slightly
larger.

The pellets are allowed to dry in the sun until the clay hardens into a
protective shell. The drying process must be controlled to avoid killing
the seeds with excessive heat. Once dried, the pellets can be stored for
extended periods and scattered whenever conditions permit.

For agricultural use in his rice-barley rotation, Fukuoka encapsulated
rice seeds individually or in small groups within clay pellets before
broadcasting them over the straw-covered field in winter. This protected
the seeds until spring flooding triggered germination.
