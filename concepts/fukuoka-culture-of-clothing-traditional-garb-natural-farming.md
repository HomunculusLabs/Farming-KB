## The Problem with Synthetic Farm Clothing

After World War II, Japanese farmers adopted Western clothing made of nylon and
other synthetic fibers. Fukuoka observed that these materials were fundamentally
unsuited to agricultural work:

- Synthetic fabrics do not breathe, causing heat buildup and stuffiness
- Tight-fitting modern work clothes restrict movement and cause stiff shoulders
- Static electricity buildup in synthetic fabrics may contribute to physical
  discomfort during long hours of field work
- Rubber raincoats prevent water penetration but trap humidity, leaving the
  wearer drenched in sweat
- Modern work hats made from petroleum-based materials bake the head in sun
  and feel dull and heavy in rain

Fukuoka contrasted this with the traditional sedge hats and straw raincoats made
from hemp palm, which he considered superior in every practical dimension. The
traditional jika-tabi (split-toe cloth shoe) was, in his view, one of the finest
Japanese inventions for farm work — so effective that American carpenters had
begun adopting them.

## The Doi: A New Traditional Garment

Fukuoka worked with tailors to develop a garment called the doi, combining
features of the haori (jacket) and hakama (skirt-like pants). Made of navy blue
cotton fabric, it was:

- Loose-fitting enough for comfort during heavy field work
- Presentable enough for formal occasions, bridging work and social life
- Connected to the spirit of the farmer's work as service to God

He argued that the farmer swinging a scythe and the samurai wielding a sword
must be identical in spirit and attitude. In this framing, the farmer's work
clothes are the proper dress before God — a radical elevation of agricultural
labor from mere economic activity to spiritual practice.

## The European Tour as Clothing Philosophy

When Fukuoka arrived at Anchorage airport wearing his traditional garb, he was
greeted as "Hanasaka-Jiisan" — the honest old man from Japanese folklore who
makes flowers bloom. Throughout Europe, people responded warmly to his
appearance. In Italy, visiting a natural farming commune, he used the example of
footwear to make a deeper ecological point:

Italian farmers wore heavy legionnaire boots that compacted and damaged the soil.
Fukuoka suggested that the soft, traditional geta he wore were part of why the
ground in Japan remained soft and fertile — farmers there trod lightly. When he
suggested this, the Italian farmers raised their feet and asked about the geta.

The episode illustrates Fukuoka's broader principle: the tools and materials of
daily life are not neutral. They shape our relationship with the land in ways
that are usually invisible. Hard-soled boots compact soil; synthetic fabrics
create a barrier between body and environment; petroleum-based materials
disconnect the farmer from the biological world.
