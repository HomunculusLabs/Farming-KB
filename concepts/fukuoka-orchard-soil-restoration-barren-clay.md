# Fukuoka: Orchard Soil Restoration from Barren Clay

## The Barren Red Clay Mountain

After the war, Fukuoka started with 1 3/4 acres of [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]]. Conventional farming had stripped the soil of its [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] and biological life, leaving behind a sterile, compacted clay that could support no vegetation. Most farmers would have considered this land worthless. Fukuoka saw it as an opportunity to demonstrate the power of natural soil restoration.

## The Failure of Deep Cultivation

The technique of deeply cultivating a [[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-establishment-bare-hillside]] and digging holes for adding organic matter was being encouraged. Fukuoka tried doing this in his own orchard. After a few years he came to the conclusion that this method is not only physically exhausting, but, as far as improving the soil is concerned, is just plain useless.

At first, he buried straw and ferns which he had brought down from the mountain. Carrying loads of 90 pounds and more was a big job, but after two or three years, there was not even enough humus to scoop up in his hand. The trenches he had dug to bury the organic matter caved in and turned into open pits.

This experience was crucial in shaping Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] approach.

## The Morishima Acacia -- Multi-Purpose Tree

In the orchard, there are pines and cedar trees, a few pear trees, persimmons, loquats, Japanese cherries, and many other native varieties growing among the citrus trees. One of the most interesting trees, though not a native, is the Morishima acacia.

The wood is hard, the flowers attract bees, and the leaves are good for fodder. It helps to prevent insect damage in the orchard, acts as a windbreak, and the rhizobium bacteria living within the roots fertilize the soil. This tree was introduced to Japan from Australia some years ago and grows faster than any tree Fukuoka has ever seen. It sends out a deep root in just a few months and in six or seven years it stands as tall as a telephone pole. In addition, this tree is a nitrogen fixer, so if 6 to 10 trees are planted to the [[fukuoka-agriculture-tomorrow-quarter-acre-vision]], soil improvement can be carried out in the deep soil strata and there is no need to break your back hauling logs down the mountain.

The Morishima acacia exemplifies Fukuoka's approach to [[fukuoka-white-clover-ground-cover]] and alfalfa on the barren ground. It was several years before they could take hold, but finally they came up and covered the orchard hillsides. He also planted [[fukuoka-soil-self-improvement-without-tillage]] -- nature builds soil from the surface down and from below up simultaneously.

## Twenty-Five Years of Transformation

As a result of this thick weed/clover cover, over the past twenty-five years, the surface layer of the orchard soil, which had been hard red clay, has become loose, dark coloured, and rich with earthworms and organic matter.

With the green manure fertilizing the topsoil and the roots of the Morishima acacia improving the soil deep down, you can do quite well without fertilizer and there is no need to cultivate between the orchard trees. With tall trees for windbreaks, citrus in the middle, and a green manure cover below, Fukuoka found a way to take it easy and let the orchard manage itself.

This transformation from barren clay to rich, living soil is one of the most compelling demonstrations of [[fukuoka-living-soil]]: the soil is not an inert substrate but a living organism capable of self-renewal.
