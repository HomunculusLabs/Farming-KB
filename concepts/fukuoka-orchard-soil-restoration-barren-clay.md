# Fukuoka: Orchard Soil Restoration from Barren Clay

## The Barren Red Clay Mountain

After the war, Fukuoka started with 1 3/4 acres of [[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-establishment-bare-hillside]] and 3/8 acre of rice fields, but now the citrus orchards alone cover 12 1/2 acres. He came by this land by taking over surrounding hillsides which had been abandoned. The face of this mountain was bare red clay, so hard you could not stick a shovel into it. A good deal of the land around here was like that. People grew potatoes until the soil was exhausted and then the fields were left abandoned.

This abandoned mountain land represented the typical pattern of [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]]. Conventional farming had stripped the soil of its organic matter and biological life, leaving behind a sterile, compacted clay that could support no vegetation. Most farmers would have considered this land worthless. Fukuoka saw it as an opportunity to demonstrate the power of natural soil restoration.

## The Failure of Deep Cultivation

The technique of deeply cultivating a citrus orchard and digging holes for adding organic matter was being encouraged. Fukuoka tried doing this in his own orchard. After a few years he came to the conclusion that this method is not only physically exhausting, but, as far as improving the soil is concerned, is just plain useless.

At first, he buried straw and ferns which he had brought down from the mountain. Carrying loads of 90 pounds and more was a big job, but after two or three years, there was not even enough humus to scoop up in his hand. The trenches he had dug to bury the organic matter caved in and turned into open pits.

This experience was crucial in shaping Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-critique-soil-cultivation]]. The conventional wisdom held that deep cultivation with added organic matter was the best way to improve soil. Fukuoka's experiment showed that this approach was not just laborious but fundamentally flawed. The buried organic matter decomposed too quickly in the disturbed soil, and the cultivation itself destroyed the soil structure that would have retained the resulting humus.

## Wood Is Better Than Straw for Soil Building

Next, he tried burying wood. It seems that straw would be the best aid for improving the soil, but judging from the amount of soil formed, wood is better. This is fine as long as there are trees to cut. However, for someone without trees nearby, it is better to grow the wood right in the orchard than to haul it from a distance.

The reason wood outperforms straw is that it decomposes slowly, providing a steady supply of organic matter over many years rather than a burst of decomposition followed by rapid oxidation. Wood also creates physical structure in the soil as it breaks down, leaving channels for air and water movement. This insight led Fukuoka toward the principle of growing soil-building materials in place rather than importing them -- a key element of his [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] approach.

## The Morishima Acacia -- Multi-Purpose Tree

In the orchard, there are pines and cedar trees, a few pear trees, persimmons, loquats, Japanese cherries, and many other native varieties growing among the citrus trees. One of the most interesting trees, though not a native, is the Morishima acacia.

The wood is hard, the flowers attract bees, and the leaves are good for fodder. It helps to prevent insect damage in the orchard, acts as a windbreak, and the rhizobium bacteria living within the roots fertilize the soil. This tree was introduced to Japan from Australia some years ago and grows faster than any tree Fukuoka has ever seen. It sends out a deep root in just a few months and in six or seven years it stands as tall as a telephone pole. In addition, this tree is a nitrogen fixer, so if 6 to 10 trees are planted to the quarter acre, soil improvement can be carried out in the deep soil strata and there is no need to break your back hauling logs down the mountain.

The Morishima acacia exemplifies Fukuoka's approach to [[fukuoka-natural-orchard-management]]: instead of fighting nature with imported inputs and heavy labor, work with nature by introducing multi-purpose plants that perform many functions simultaneously. The acacia provides wind protection, habitat for beneficial insects, nitrogen fixation, fodder, and wood -- all from a single tree that requires no maintenance once established.

## Surface Layer Restoration

As for the surface layer of the soil, Fukuoka sowed a mixture of [[fukuoka-white-clover-ground-cover]] and alfalfa on the barren ground. It was several years before they could take hold, but finally they came up and covered the orchard hillsides. He also planted [[fukuoka-rice-dry-field-cultivation]]. The roots of this hearty vegetable penetrate deeply into the soil, adding organic matter and opening channels for air and water circulation. It reseeds itself easily and after one sowing, you can almost forget about it.

As the soil became richer, the weeds started to make a comeback. After seven or eight years, the clover almost disappeared among the weeds, so he tossed out a little more clover seed in late summer after cutting back the weeds with a scythe.

The combination of deep-rooted daikon radishes, nitrogen-fixing clover, and returning weeds created a multi-layered soil-building system. The daikon opened deep channels in the hard clay, the clover fixed nitrogen at the surface, and the weeds contributed diverse root systems and organic matter at every soil depth. This is the essence of [[fukuoka-soil-self-improvement-without-tillage]] -- nature builds soil from the surface down and from below up simultaneously.

## Twenty-Five Years of Transformation

As a result of this thick weed/clover cover, over the past twenty-five years, the surface layer of the orchard soil, which had been hard red clay, has become loose, dark coloured, and rich with earthworms and organic matter.

With the green manure fertilizing the topsoil and the roots of the Morishima acacia improving the soil deep down, you can do quite well without fertilizer and there is no need to cultivate between the orchard trees. With tall trees for windbreaks, citrus in the middle, and a green manure cover below, Fukuoka found a way to take it easy and let the orchard manage itself.

This transformation from barren clay to rich, living soil is one of the most compelling demonstrations of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] in action. No compost was imported, no fertilizer was applied, no cultivation was performed. The soil was restored entirely through the action of plants growing in place, each contributing its unique function to the soil-building process. The key was patience -- it took years for the clover to establish, years for the soil to soften, years for the ecosystem to reach self-sustaining equilibrium.

## Philosophical Significance

The orchard soil restoration story is a parable of Fukuoka's entire philosophy. The hard, barren clay represents the human condition when we try to force nature to conform to our will: exhausted, lifeless, resistant to improvement through conventional means. The restoration process represents the path of non-intervention: trust nature, provide the minimal conditions for life to flourish, and then get out of the way.

Fukuoka's observation that the hardest, most barren soil could be transformed into rich, living earth without any imported inputs challenges the fundamental premise of modern agriculture. Conventional science holds that soil fertility must be maintained through external inputs -- fertilizer, compost, cultivation. Fukuoka demonstrated that the soil itself, when covered with living plants, generates its own fertility from within. This is the principle of [[fukuoka-living-soil]]: the soil is not an inert substrate but a living organism capable of self-renewal.

## Connection to Natural Farming Principles

The orchard restoration demonstrates all four of Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming-detailed]]:

1. No cultivation: The soil was never tilled. Improvement came from plant roots and surface mulch working from above and below.

2. No chemical fertilizer: The Morishima acacia and white clover provided all the nitrogen needed. Other nutrients were cycled through the decomposition of plant material.

3. No pesticides: The diversity of plant species in the orchard created a balanced ecosystem where no single pest species could dominate.

4. No weeding: Weeds were welcomed as contributors to soil health, not enemies to be eliminated.

The layered design of the orchard -- tall windbreak trees, citrus in the middle, ground cover below -- creates a multi-story ecosystem that maximizes the use of sunlight, water, and nutrients. This is [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] that mimics the structure and function of a natural forest while producing food.
