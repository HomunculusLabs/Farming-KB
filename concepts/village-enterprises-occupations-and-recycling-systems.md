# Village Enterprises, Occupations, and Recycling Systems

A self-reliant [[permaculture]] village generates livelihoods internally by meeting its own needs for food, energy, transport, health, and shelter, while also producing surplus goods and services for trade with surrounding districts. Mollison provides a detailed analysis of how village occupants can self-employ in service to both the village and nearby areas, along with practical models for [[community-recycling-and-waste-recovery-systems]].

## Enterprise Planning Context

The three highest cost areas for any community are **food, energy, and transport**. In a hypothetical 50-household (100 adults) village, residents can earn their living by addressing these core needs directly. Much depends on establishing a **[[permaculture-village-development]] credit union** under Trust B to serve village financial needs.

## Essential Occupations

### Food Provision (~5–7 families)

About 20 adults can support 1 adult providing food supply, meaning 5–7 families can earn a living from food-related enterprises:

- **Open-air market gardens** — Fresh produce for village and district
- **Glasshouse crops** — [[coleman-protected-cultivation]] for year-round production
- **Cooperative store** — Local trade hub with regional purchasing power
- **Domestic livestock and fishery** — Chickens, goats, pigs, sheep, rabbits, aquaculture
- **Cafe and food processing** — Part-time livelihood from prepared foods and baked goods
- **Village market** — Surplus sold to visitors and locals from outside the village

### Energy Services (~2–5 families)

To establish energy-efficient systems, plans must include excellent house design, retrofit capability for inefficient houses, insulation, and quality appliances. Most needs require capital to start but amortize within 3–7 years:

- **House design and retrofit** — [[comparison-passive-solar-vs-earth-sheltered]], insulation, thermal mass (with contract work extending outside the village)
- **Hardware and appliance sales** — Supplying the village and district
- **Installation and maintenance** — Village energy system installation, repair, and monitoring
- **Manufacturing** — Over time, local appliance manufacturing enables balanced trade

### Vehicle and Transport Services (~2–4 families)

Vehicles are perhaps the most costly item and need careful planning, ideally on a village network level rather than a single-village system:

- **Service and repairs** — Vehicle maintenance for village fleet
- **Bulk fuel and oil supply** — Coordinated purchasing and distribution
- **Fuel production** — Growing, distilling, or fermenting fuels for engines
- **Fleet lease and insurance** — Coordinated vehicle management
- **Special vehicle and tractor lease** — Shared heavy equipment
- **Manufacturing** — Village alliances can establish special engine or chassis manufacture

### Financial Services (~2 people)

Handling income, loans, and enterprise accounting is essential for village viability:

- **Credit union management** — A community credit union holding insurance money and incomes can fund enterprises such as energy systems, dairies, and food processing centres
- **Accounting and management** — Computer-based bookkeeping, forward planning, and enterprise coordination

### Medical and Pharmaceutical Services (~1–2 people)

- **Primary care** — Prescription, massage, counselling, and local treatment
- **Herbal and local medicine** — Medications grown or manufactured on site, with wider sales potential
- **Prevention-based health** — Community health should always prioritize illness prevention

### Building and Maintenance (~1 person)

- **Multi-skilled tradesperson** — A plumber-builder-mechanic serves establishment and ongoing maintenance needs
- **Woodworking** — Producing useful furnishings and sale items for extra income
- **Furniture making** — Craft production for village use and external sale

### Coverage Summary

These essential occupations cover shelter, food, economy, energy, transport, and health—initially funding about **20 of the 50 households**, with more employment developing as manufacturing and enterprises grow.

## Extended Enterprise Opportunities

Beyond essential occupations, numerous potential village enterprises can develop from land resources and local skills:

### Land-Based Enterprises
- **Specialty crops** — Cut flowers, herbs, pharmaceutical plants
- **Aquaculture** — Fish and water-based food production
- **Processed dairy products** — Cheese, yogurt, butter
- **Nursery operations** — Plant materials for site use, fire control, orchards, and forage crops ([[comfrey]], [[tagasaste]])
- **Animal breeding** — Special poultry, pigeon, sheep, or goat breeds as a small stud
- **Beekeeping** — Honey, pollination services, wax products

### Skill-Based Enterprises
- **Education** — Teachers for children, adult education, and applied workshops on site
- **Workshops** — Craft, medical, or design workshops yielding 1–2 livings
- **Business service centre** — Small business support employing 3–4 people
- **Consultancy** — Architecture, landscape design, and implementation for other sites
- **Computer services** — Programming, databases, and network services placed in homes
- **Publishing** — Computer word processing and typesetting services

### Trade and Commerce
- **Distribution rights and wholesale** — Regional trade networks
- **Import-export operations** — Village-level international trade
- **Village trade networks** — Inter-village cooperative commerce
- **Cooperative store** — Primary outlet for goods and services
- **Craft products** — Metal or wood workshops, pottery, and art
- **Tourism** — Modest income from guests, visitors, and site tours
- **Educational services and accommodation** — Much in demand in rural districts
- **Physical therapy and paramedical services** — Massage and stress-related treatments find plenty of customers in rural areas

## Capital and Management Requirements

All enterprises depend on two basic factors:

### Capital
Enough money to start up and develop. Key considerations:

- Many small enterprises can **pre-sell their wares** for initial capital
- Many others need **no capital to start** but require time to develop
- Some (massage, paramedical) need **only skills** to begin
- Others (market garden, orchard, furniture making) need **skills, capital, and time**
- **Workshop income** provides reliable early capital, though it fades as students gain their own skills

### Management
Careful oversight including:

- Accounting and bookkeeping
- Forward planning and market research
- Product development and costing
- Correct lease and rental agreements
- Appropriate [[permaculture-designers-manual-legal-structures]]
- Staffing and sensible staffing decisions

A **small business service centre** is economical and necessary when a village has 20 or more businesses.

## The Job-Sharing Principle

Mollison advocates that the long-term aim of any village should be to own and operate its own employment enterprises. In past times, it was unusual for a villager to hold just one job—the banker was also a part-time barber, trader, and perhaps gardener.

### Benefits of Job-Sharing

- **Total failure is unlikely** — Diversified income reduces risk
- **Unemployment is minimized** — Multiple occupational shares provide backup
- **Holidays are manageable** — Indoor work fills wet days
- **Skills diversification** — Individuals develop multiple competencies

Mollison recommends that in every occupation, **job-sharing should be the rule, not the exception**. Each individual should have occupational shares in 2–3 enterprises. Although the total village structure is complex, the work of any individual is simple—just as a single plant in a polyculture plays a simple role within a complex ecosystem.

## Development Priorities

A careful assessment of skills and available capital indicates priorities:

1. **Income-earning for local development** is the first priority
2. **Capital-intensive schemes** (e.g., commercial pottery) should wait until capital accumulates
3. **Information-based income** through workshops and classes provides reliable early revenue
4. **Educational centre development** is possible if enough skilled people are attracted to the village

## Community Recycling: The Devonport Model

The borough of Devonport in Auckland, New Zealand, operates a total solid waste-recycling system that returns a **cash benefit to households** (versus the cash payment or rates paid by households in non-recycling areas).
