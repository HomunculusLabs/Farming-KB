# Hongo Distribution Types Applied to Neurotropic Fungi

## Background

Akira Hongo (1978) proposed nine types of geographical distribution for Japanese fungi, a biogeographic framework that Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998) applied to neurotropic fungi worldwide. This classification system provides a structured way to understand the [[guzman-global-distribution-patterns-neurotropic-fungi]] [[health-risks-of-hallucinogenic-mushrooms]] and related species.

## The Nine Distribution Types

### Type 1: Cosmopolitan Species

Cosmopolitan species are found across multiple continents in diverse habitats. In the neurotropic fungi, this category includes:

- *Panaeolus spp.* — Most species in this genus have wide distribution, particularly *P. subbalteatus* and *P. cyanescens* (note: not the same as *Psilocybe cyanescens*).
- *[[guzman-panaeolina-foenisecii-psilocybin-controversy-conflicting-chemical-analyses]]* — The "mower's mushroom," one of the most widespread fungi in the world, found in lawns and grasslands globally. Despite contradictory chemical analyses over the decades, it is likely more toxic than neurotropic.
- *Claviceps purpurea* — Ergot, found on cereal grasses worldwide and the original source of ergot alkaloids including lysergic acid (the precursor to LSD).

Cosmopolitan species often share a close ecological association with human agricultural activities — they grow on dung, cultivated grasses, or disturbed ground, giving them a distribution pattern that tracks human settlement and agriculture.

### Type 2: Northern Hemisphere Species

Species restricted to the temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere:

- *Amanita muscaria* and *A. pantherina* — Both form mycorrhizal associations with *Pinus* (pine) and *Betula* (birch) in boreal and temperate forests. *A. muscaria* is the iconic fly agaric, used ceremonially by Siberian peoples including the Chukchee and Koryak.
- *Cordyceps spp.* — Parasitic fungi on insects and other fungi, found in temperate forest ecosystems.
- *Psilocybe pelliculosa* — Pacific Northwest North America.
- *Psilocybe silvatica* — Temperate coniferous forests of North America.

### Type 3: Eurasian Species

Species found across the Eurasian landmass:

- *Amanita muscaria* is primarily Eurasian in origin, with its center of diversity in the boreal forests of Scandinavia, Siberia, and northern Europe.
- Several *Inocybe* species with reported neurotropic properties are primarily Eurasian.

### Type 4: North American and Eastern Asiatic Species

This disjunct distribution type reflects ancient connections between the mycobiotas of [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] and eastern Asia, separated by the intervening dry grasslands and deserts of central North America and central Asia:

- *Psilocybe graveolens*, *P. muliercula*, *P. pintonii*, and *P. zapotecorum* from the Americas are very closely related to *P. argentipes* and *P. subcaerulipes* from Japan. This pattern parallels well-known plant disjunctions (e.g., ginseng, tulip trees) between eastern North America and eastern Asia.

### Type 5: Far Eastern Species

Species endemic to Japan and the Korean peninsula, from the perspective of Hongo's original Japanese-centered framework:

- Several species of *Panaeolina* described by Hongo from Japan.
- *Psilocybe subcaerulipes* — endemic to Japan's temperate forests.

### Type 6: Southeastern Asiatic Species

Species from the tropical and subtropical forests of Southeast Asia:

- *Psilocybe subaeruginascens* var. *subaeruginascens* — Known from Japan and Java, Indonesia.
- *Psilocybe subaeruginascens* var. *septentrionalis* — Restricted to Japan only.
- *Psilocybe samuiensis* — From Koh Samui, Thailand.

### Type 7: Tropical and Subtropical Species

Species restricted to warm climates between approximately 23.5°N and 23.5°S, though some extend into warm temperate zones:

- *Psilocybe cubensis* — The quintessential pantropical neurotropic fungus, found in cow pastures throughout the tropics.
- *Psilocybe subcubensis* — Closely related to *P. cubensis*, also tropical.
- *Copelandia cyanescens* — With some exceptions, primarily tropical and subtropical.
- *Copelandia tropicalis* — As the name suggests, tropical distribution.
- *Gerronema fibula* — Reported from Malaysia, New Guinea, Solomon Islands, and South America, also known from Europe, representing a complex distributional history.

### Type 8: Arctic and Alpine Species

Species of extreme cold environments:

- *Psilocybe aztecorum* var. *aztecorum* — Restricted to subalpine and alpine habitats in the high mountains of central Mexico, growing at elevations up to 4,000 m. This species represents a remarkable example of neurotropic fungi adapting to extreme conditions.
- *Panaeolus moellerianus* and *P. olivaceus* from the Faeroe Islands — Examples of northern cold-adapted species.

### Type 9: Endemic Species

Species known from only a single country, region, or very restricted geographic area:

**Mexico endemics:**
- *Conocybe siliginoides* — Collected only once in 1955 by Wasson in Oaxaca; never re-found despite extensive searching.
- *Hypholoma naematoliformis*
- *Psilocybe muliercula*, *P. chiapanensis*, *P. laurae*, and many others.

**Colombia endemics:**
- *P. columbiana*, *P. guatapensis*, *P. pintonii*.

**Brazil endemics:**
- *P. brasiliensis*, *P. paulensis*.

**Europe endemics:**
- *Psilocybe hispanica* — Spain.
- *Psilocybe serbica* — Central Europe.

**Other regional endemics:**
- *P. portoricensis* — Puerto Rico.
- *Panaeolina* species described by Hongo from Japan and by Natarajan and Raman from India.

## Amphitropical and Bipolar Distributions

Beyond Hongo's nine types, several neurotropic fungi show amphitropical distributions (found in both Northern and Southern temperate zones but not in the tropics between):

- *Psilocybe cyanescens*, *P. fimetaria*, *P. pelliculosa*, *P. semilanceata*, and *P. silvatica* are common in both North America and Europe.
- *Psilocybe stuntzii* is only known in the Pacific Northwest of North America.
- *Psilocybe coprinifacies* and *P. serbica* are only European.

## Biogeographic Implications

The application of Hongo's distribution types to neurotropic fungi reveals several important patterns:

1. **Austral hemisphere dominance**: Despite more extensive [[guzman-allen-gartz-mycological-exploration-bias-and-underexplored-regions]] in the Northern Hemisphere, the majority of neurotropic *Psilocybe* species occur in the Southern Hemisphere or near it. This suggests that the evolutionary center of diversity for the genus may be in subtropical Gondwanan regions.

2. **Mesoamerican convergence**: Mexico sits at the crossroads of Northern and Southern Hemisphere mycobiotas, with species from both hemispheres reaching their distributional limits in its diverse mesophytic forests. This convergence explains Mexico's extraordinary species richness.

3. **Gondwanan connections**: Close relationships between species in Mesoamerica/South America and Southeast Asia/Australasia support the hypothesis of ancient Gondwanan distributions disrupted by continental drift, as documented by Horak (1983) for agarics and boletes.

4. **Human-mediated dispersal**: Cosmopolitan species like *P. cubensis* and *Panaeolina foenisecii* may owe their wide distributions partly to human activities — cattle ranching (for dung-inhabiting species) and lawn cultivation (for grassland species).
