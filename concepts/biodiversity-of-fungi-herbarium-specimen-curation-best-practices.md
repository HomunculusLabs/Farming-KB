# Herbarium Specimen Curation

Herbaria serve as **conservatories of voucher specimens** that document the occurrence of fungi at specific places and times. They are indispensable infrastructure for mycological research, providing the physical evidence upon which taxonomic, ecological, and biogeographic conclusions rest. Unlike living culture collections, [[biodiversity-fungal-herbarium-collections]] remain scientifically valuable and accessible over centuries. This page summarises those best practices and highlights their importance for fungal biodiversity documentation.

## The Urgency of Fungal Documentation

Accelerated deforestation and habitat destruction worldwide are creating an urgent need for **rapid biodiversity inventories**. Fungi — many of which are narrowly endemic or associated with particular host species — are especially vulnerable to habitat loss. Because the vast majority of fungal species remain undescribed, each uncollected population may represent a lineage that will never be documented. ## Types of Herbarium Specimens

Fungal herbaria accommodate several specimen formats, each with distinct curatorial requirements:

- **Dried herbarium specimens** — the standard format for macrofungi (agarics, polypores, puffballs) and many microfungi, dried by heat or desiccant and stored in sealed packets or boxes.
- **Spirit collections** — fungi preserved in ethanol, formalin, or FAA solution, essential for taxa whose morphology collapses upon drying (e.g., gelatinous fungi, truffles in some groups).
- **Microscopic slide collections** — permanent mounts of sectioned tissue in resin or mounting medium, often filed alongside the dried voucher.
- **Frozen tissue collections** — material stored at −80 °C specifically for molecular work, increasingly maintained as a parallel resource to dried vouchers.

## Specimen Processing

Rigorous processing preserves both the physical integrity of specimens and the informational content that makes them scientifically useful. Errors or shortcuts at this stage can permanently degrade a specimen's research value, regardless of how well it is stored thereafter.

### Field Work and Initial Handling

- **Specimen preparation** begins at the collection site: fungi should be photographed in situ, described fresh (colour, odour, texture, bruising reactions, spore print colour), and assigned a unique collector number linked to a field notebook or electronic data sheet.
- **Field labels and notes** must record locality (with GPS coordinates when possible), date, collector name and affiliation, habitat description, substrate identity, elevation, and any associated organisms such as host plants or symbiotic partners.
- Fresh material intended for molecular work may be dried separately with silica gel or placed directly into CTAB buffer; these subsamples should share the same collector number as the voucher specimen to maintain an unbroken chain of custody between the physical voucher and any derived sequences.

### Accessioning and Storage

- Specimens are **packeted, boxed, or mounted** using **archival-quality supplies** — acid-free paper, lignin-free boxes, and inert adhesives — to prevent chemical degradation over decades of storage. Non-archival materials accelerate oxidation and embrittlement.
- Packet labels carry full accession data and are cross-referenced to field notes, spirit collections, microscopic slide collections, and electronic records to ensure that no information is orphaned from its physical specimen.
- **Filing systems** follow standard taxonomic arrangements (often alphabetically by genus within families) so that related material is physically collocated for comparison during revisionary studies and monographic work.

## Specimen Maintenance

Long-term preservation requires controlled environmental conditions and active pest management. Neglect at this stage can destroy decades of curatorial investment within months, as dermestid beetles and mould can rapidly consume or degrade entire folders of specimens.

### Facilities

- **[[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] (IPM)** employs monitoring traps, regular inspections, quarantine protocols for incoming material, and environmental controls rather than blanket chemical treatments that may themselves damage specimens.
- **Insect hormones** (pheromone-based lures) attract and capture specific pests such as cigarette beetles (*Lasioderma serricorne*) and drugstore beetles (*Stegobium paniceum*) before they colonise collections.
- **Freezing** — cycling specimens through −20 °C or colder for 48–72 hours — is a highly effective, non-chemical treatment for established infestations and is now standard practice at major institutions worldwide.

## Use of Specimens

Herbaria are working research collections, not static museums. Several mechanisms facilitate access while protecting physical integrity and ensuring specimens remain available for future study:

- **Outgoing loans** ship duplicated or non-type material to requesting researchers under formal loan agreements specifying care conditions, insurance, and return deadlines. Type specimens are rarely loaned and are increasingly digitised instead to reduce handling risk.
- **Incoming specimens** (gifts, exchanges, type deposits) are accessioned following the same [[fungal-destructive-sampling-herbarium-dna-extraction]]** for DNA extraction, [[mycorrhiza-bioassays-ecological-studies]]** — correlating fungal occurrence with host plants, soil types, successional stage, and [[biodiversity-fungal-community-assembly]] and functional roles.
- **Distributional studies** — mapping species ranges, identifying disjunctions, and tracking range shifts over time in response to climate change or land-use conversion.
