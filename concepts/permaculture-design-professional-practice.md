## Professional Permaculture Design Practice

Professional [[permaculture-design]] is a structured discipline with its own ethics, business practices, training pathways, and reporting methodology. The designer's function is not to tell people how to garden or build, but to place things in the environment so that multiple functions are served, low energy inputs produce high yields, and stability is achieved.

## The Designer's Ethics

Eight foundational ethics govern professional [[permaculture]] design:

1. **Cooperation, not competition**: Designers cooperate as a group and do not duplicate each other's work.
2. **Individual designs**: As far as possible, create and use unique designs rather than copying.
3. **Client responsibility**: Care for clients and remain responsible to them.
4. **Earth restoration**: The ultimate end is the reforestation of the Earth and restoration of its soils to health.
5. **Environmental stewardship**: In all design work, side with the "super client" — Gaia, the living Earth.
6. **Appropriate energy**: Seek the best, most appropriate energy paths.
7. **Highest-level recycling**: Everything is a resource; use what you have. Make the least change produce the most effect.
8. **Surplus divestment**: Divest yourself of everything surplus to your needs.

## The Designer's Role

The professional permaculture designer's core function is **placement** — knowing where to put things and why. This encompasses:

- Using multiple functions of every element.
- Creating low-energy inputs for high yield and stability.
- Acting as a creative observer who recognizes and develops potential uses in nature for human benefit.
- Building implementation groups of regional people who can carry out designs.

## Training and Certification

### Pathway to Design Diploma

- Work as a designer for **one to two years** before applying to the [[permaculture-institute]] for a design diploma.
- Rural backgrounds are not necessary, but discipline and experience are essential.
- Courses should be run specifically for people who intend to become designers.

### Gender Balance

The movement has historically been short of women designers. Half of [[permaculture-design-course-methodology]] participants must be women. Hosts are required to recruit at least half women students, with scholarships used to assist women where payment is a barrier. All hosts must offer free or affordable childcare.

### Scholarship Structure

- People who want to work as designers without charge do not pay course fees, but must cover the host group's basic costs.
- Typically one to two scholarship students per course.
- When a scholarship recipient begins charging for work, they should repay the fee to those who trained them.
- Courses with 30 people can afford four scholarship students.
- Indigenous people should be contacted and informed of scheduled courses.
- One full scholarship per six paid tuitions is the target ratio.

### Course Types

- **3-week [[mollison-permaculture-design-course-curriculum]]**: Covers many topics broadly.
- **10-day Design Intensive**: Qualifies people to design their own homes; provides more design experience than the full course and serves as preparation for or post-graduate follow-up to the design course.
- **Weekend Workshops** (Friday night through Sunday afternoon): Short enough for broad attendance, long enough for design experience.

## Business Practices

### Finding Work

1. Conduct weekend workshops (jobs emerge from these).
2. Lecture publicly.
3. Write articles for local journals.
4. Only advertise as a permaculture designer once established with a backup team.
5. Start modestly and gain experience before scaling.

### Free Work Strategy

Almost without exception, a free job can be organized alongside a paid job. Do free work when already in the area for paid work, keeping personal costs down.

### Fee Structure

#### Standard Jobs (Up to ~40 Acres)

- **Daily rate** for one day on site plus a typed, documented, illustrated report (15–20 pages).
- Reports include numerous small pictures, details, book references.
- Always finish with an invitation for the client to inquire further at no cost.
- Inform clients of any second thoughts in a letter.

#### Large Jobs (Over 100 Acres)

- Quote on a time (hourly) basis.
- Group work may be appropriate for large properties.
- Always provide a **pre-design report** outlining what is generally possible — never attempt total design immediately.
- Give the client a brief from which they can decide how to proceed.

#### Design Fees

- Generally tied to local landscape architect rates.
- Design cost: 1.5% to 2% of total investment the client will make in implementation.
- Determine what the client proposes to spend on improvements, then calculate fees at 1–2% of that amount.
- Approximately $35 per hour for professional permaculture design work (1981 baseline; should be adjusted for inflation).
- Quote a fixed price for standard jobs; use hourly rates for extended engagements.

### Additional Services

| Service | Description | Fee |
|---------|-------------|-----|
| Property Finding | Find specific property for client (not like real estate — charge the buyer, not seller) | 0.5% of purchase price (finding) + 1.5% on purchase |
| Property Management | Ongoing oversight of land the client doesn't live on | $2,000–$5,000/year or ~$5,000/year for 3–5 properties |

## Finding Property for Clients

### Six Criteria

1. **On-site water supply potential**: The client must have their own water.
2. **Suitability for client's goals**: The site must match what they want to do.
3. **Current undervaluation**: The site in its present usage should be undervalued (e.g., strip-mined land).
4. **Multi-titled or unrestricted**: Preferably multi-titled or without limitations on structures and occupancy.
5. **Improvement potential**: You must be confident you can improve the property to a high level.
6. **Not subdivision-valued**: Avoid land valued for subdivision (speculation has inflated the price beyond productive use).

## The Caution: Designers Must Not Become Managers

A critical tension exists in professional practice: as designers take on management roles, they stop designing. Every designer lost to management is one fewer designer available. The priority must remain training more designers, not absorbing them into ongoing management situations. Education, long-term urban development, and management all draw designers away from design work.

## Building a Network

Successful designers build networks of:
- Horticulturists
- Architects
- Agricultural engineers
- Real estate agents
- Public relations people
- Business management people
- Other clients (whose properties new clients can visit)

At least 80% of real estate agents are reported to support permaculture approaches. Printing handouts for real estate agents about permaculture is recommended.

## Demonstration Sites

Designers should establish much of their work as demonstration sites. When looking for land, persuade clients to share concern for rehabilitation. A nursery on a small separate title can provide ongoing site improvement and supply materials for future design jobs.

The ultimate vision is to become developers — acquiring and preserving wild areas, converting destructive land uses (cattle and sheep on dry lands) to productive permaculture systems, and demonstrating that [[regenerative-agriculture]] can outperform extractive land use by [[event-classification-and-orders-of-magnitude]].
