# Human Diversity and Multiculturalism in Permaculture

Human biological and cultural diversity represents one of the most complex applications of [[permaculture]]'s diversity principle. The diversity we see in human populations — at racial, ethnic, and individual levels — has been used throughout history to include and exclude people from social groups. Understanding how this diversity arises, and how it relates to ecological systems thinking, is essential for applying [[permaculture-ethics]] to the human domain.

## Multiculturalism as Transitional Diversity

The wider community has come to value diversity through multiculturalism, though the modern form differs significantly from the carefully maintained balance between ethnic groups that characterized pre-industrial cities. Ancient cities functioned like beehives — dense but human-scale — where different ethnic groups mixed freely in public life while maintaining their own culture through separate language, education, religion, and even professions. These diverse human ecosystems mostly worked well, despite periods of violence in the historical record.

Modern multiculturalism contains the same paradox as the permacultural use of biological diversity: the process of valuing and making use of nature's diversity contributes to changing it. Acknowledgement of the value of differing traditions goes hand in hand with promiscuous hybridisation to create new local cultures of place, which contributes to the eventual dissolution of those original ethnically diverse cultures.

This is analogous to the permacultural combination of biotic diversity in pioneering combinations, rather than the ancient stability of pristine biodiversity. Modern multiculturalism can only represent a transitory stage in the evolution of new cultures of place.

## The Language Extinction Crisis

There are approximately 6,800 remaining languages in the world, and most face extinction over the next century. The question of whether multiculturalism can stem this mass extinction is open. A rapid and wholesale collapse of industrial civilisation would probably save much more of this vast cultural diversity than the combined effects of multicultural tolerance and pro-active linguistic conservation.

This raises an impossible question: which loss would be greater for the long-term survival of humanity — thousands of distinct languages and cultures of place maladapted to a world changed forever by industrialisation, or the vast internal diversity of industrial culture maladapted to declining energy? Rather than succumbing to paralysis, the permacultural response is to get on with the practical business of creating local cultures of place, which will eventually create new global biological and cultural diversity.

## Human Biological Diversity

### Genetic Unity and Surface Variation

Modern genetic evidence shows that even strong features such as skin colour are relatively superficial responses to environment and social selection pressures, operating on a common human genetic pool that is relatively fixed and long-standing. It is even possible that some so-called ancestors thought to be other species, such as the Neanderthals, were no more than racial selections.

In this view of *Homo sapiens*, the diversity we see is akin to the diversity in breeds of domesticated animals. Humans applied selection pressure through a diverse range of cultural preferences and taboos, which interacted with natural selection pressures to produce the physical and other characteristics of tribes and races. We probably began this process before we did the same to livestock — thus humans were the first domesticated animals.

These selection pressures continue to influence human characteristics through aesthetic preferences, though cultural change is so fast that a selection pressure for, say, blue eyes may not be maintained for enough generations to create a new global culture. The important point about human self-selection is that it operates through cultural co-evolutionary processes not controlled by any person or power structure.

### The Eugenics Legacy and Its Lessons

In the 1920s and 1930s, eugenics — the science of "breeding better people" — was a respectable field of research that influenced public policy and laws in the United States and Germany. The Nazi experiments in breeding a "super-race" and systematic elimination of "inferior" peoples gave eugenics its lasting notoriety. Since the Holocaust, it has been widely recognised that meddling with the diversity of humankind risks horrific and dysfunctional outcomes.

Key unanswered questions about human fitness and diversity include:

- Has inter-breeding of previously separated races and ethnic groups injected hybrid vigour into *Homo sapiens*, especially over the last few hundred years?
- Is the disappearance of ethnic and racial identity inevitable with inter-breeding?
- Have medicine and other benefits of the modern world reduced human fitness in the way over-breeding has reduced fitness in animal breeds?
- Is the re-emergence of eugenics inevitable if biotechnology continues to provide new ways of intervening in human reproduction and genetics?
- Have technology and rapid change made conventional notions of fitness irrelevant?
- What will be the effect of declining energy on old and new selection pressures?

### Energy Descent and Human Diversity

Using an ecological systems perspective to consider the global situation over the last and next few thousand years, the rapid growth in the human energy base has broken down old biological and cultural diversity, while simultaneously leading to proliferation of human numbers and individual diversity. The climaxing and decline of energy will inevitably result in a reduction of human numbers, and possibly individual diversity, while stimulating the re-emergence of localised biological and cultural diversity.

The details of how this could take place range from the benign and enlightened to the horrific and genocidal. While human self-selection through cultural forces will continue in some form, the wisdom of the ages has been to value human diversity as a gift of nature or of God. We need to acknowledge that we have neither the power nor the wisdom to conserve every aspect of human individual or ethnic diversity, any more than we can conserve all the planet's lifeforms. Even conserving the diversity built up over millennia in cultivated plants and domesticated animals may be beyond our capacity.

## Applying the Third Ethic

In accepting this situation, we return to the third permaculture ethic: recognising diversity as a form of abundance to be cherished, while accepting that forces larger than ourselves will set the limits to that abundance. This perspective provides a framework for engaging with human diversity that is neither naive preservationism nor careless disregard, but a realistic appreciation of diversity's role in systemic health and resilience.
## Theoretical Framework

The theoretical basis for this concept draws from systems thinking,
ecology, and design science. It integrates principles of energy flow,
nutrient cycling, and community dynamics to create practical frameworks
for sustainable design and management.
## Implementation Strategies

Implementation follows a structured approach: site analysis, design
development, incremental implementation, and adaptive management.
Each phase builds on previous observations, allowing the system to
evolve in response to changing conditions and emerging feedback.
## Case Studies

Documented examples demonstrate successful application across diverse
climates and scales. Common success factors include thorough site
analysis, community engagement, appropriate technology selection,
and long-term monitoring and adaptation.
## Sustainability Metrics

Measuring outcomes involves tracking ecological indicators such as
soil health, biodiversity indices, water quality, and carbon
sequestration. Social metrics include food security, community
resilience, and economic sustainability indicators.
## Challenges and Limitations

Key challenges include scaling from pilot projects to broader
implementation, adapting general principles to specific local
conditions, and maintaining long-term commitment. Addressing these
requires flexible approaches and supportive policy frameworks.
