# Global Psilocybin Mushroom Distribution

Jochen Gartz's worldwide survey reveals that psychoactive mushrooms
containing psilocybin and related indole alkaloids are found on every
inhabited continent. Their distribution follows patterns determined by
climate, substrate availability, and the historical movement of people and
animals. From the highlands of Mexico to the pastures of Scotland, from
the bark mulch of Japanese gardens to the cow dung of Australian
pastures, these fungi have colonized an extraordinary range of habitats.

## North America: The Pacific Northwest

The Pacific Northwest of North America -- stretching from northern
California through Oregon, Washington, and into British Columbia --
represents one of the world's richest regions for psychoactive mushroom
diversity. [Psilocybe semilanceata is the most widely
used species, with habitat expanding east of the Cascade Mountains.
[Psilocybe cyanescens, a strongly bluing species
common in parks and on bark mulch, is the second most important species.
The newly described Psilocybe azurescens, found in Oregon, is unusually
large and very potent due to high psilocybin content.

Psilocybe stuntzii, discovered at the University of Washington in 1972,
fruits on bark mulch in parks, on golf courses, football fields, and
gardens. It can fruit year-round under favorable conditions. By the early
1980s, experts estimated that the number of hallucinogenic mushroom users
in the United States outnumbered LSD users for the first time, with an
estimated one million consumers across North America.

Psilocybe cubensis is common across the American South, and Panaeolus
subbalteatus grows across the Southwest. California alone was estimated
to have 100,000 "Magic Mushroom People." The 1979 British Columbia High
Court ruling that only pure psilocybin was prohibited -- not the mushrooms
themselves -- represented a landmark in the legal history of these fungi.

## Central and South America: The Heartland

Mexico remains the historical center of psychoactive mushroom culture. The
[Psilocybe species of Mexico -- including P. mexicana,
P. caerulescens, and P. aztecorum -- have been used in sacred ceremonies
for centuries, a tradition made famous by Albert Hofmann, R. Gordon Wasson, and Maria Sabina in the 1950s. Since then,
the influx of "hippie" tourism has damaged the cultural fabric of
indigenous mushroom worship in many areas.

In South America, [Psilocybe cubensis and Panaeolus
cyanescens are the most commonly used species. Colombia's archaeological
park at San Augustin near Bogota has been dubbed the "heart of magic
mushroom land," and golden mushroom-shaped pendants found in the region
suggest centuries of traditional use. Communes were established in the
Andes Mountains, the most famous known as La Miel ("honey"). The species
also occur throughout Amazonia where Colombia, Peru, and Brazil share
borders.

## Australia: The Fifth Continent

Australia's psychoactive mycoflora was first documented scientifically
by J. Cleland, who in 1927 described [Psilocybe subaeruginosa, a bluing species found in New South Wales, Victoria, and
South Australia. Psilocybe cubensis grows abundantly along the sunshine
coast of Queensland and in open valleys around Brisbane, likely introduced
by early settlers. Panaeolus cyanescens, known colloquially as "Blue
Meanies," is widespread throughout northern and southern Australia.

The summer of 1969 brought a "mushroom epidemic" to a 4,000-hectare region
of coastal Queensland after heavy rains. Collectors flocked to the area,
and special transport companies were founded to deliver mushrooms to
major cities. Dried mushrooms sold for approximately one US dollar per
fruiting body on the black market, and in Tasmania, gelatine capsules
containing ground mushroom powder sold for six dollars each. By 1972, LSD
had almost disappeared from the Australian black market, largely replaced
by locally available psychoactive mushrooms.

## Europe: From Skepticism to Widespread Use

[Psilocybe semilanceata use in Europe was first
reported scientifically in September 1976, when the British High Court
ruled that "psilocybin is a chemical and mushrooms are mushrooms,"
acquitting a man of possession charges. Usage spread rapidly through
Britain, Norway, Finland, Denmark, the Netherlands, Germany, Austria,
Switzerland, and eventually Italy, the former Czechoslovakia, and Russia
near St. Petersburg.

In Britain, poison center statistics showed a dramatic rise: 33 cases
requiring treatment in 1978, rising to 142 in 1981, with the average
user aged 15 to 19. Despite these numbers, there was not a single fatality
among 318 reported poisoning cases. British colloquial names for the
mushroom include Liberty Cap, Magic Mushroom, Blue Legs, and Pixie Caps.
In Norway, usage began in 1977 and spread through the fjord areas. In
Czechoslovakia, [Psilocybe bohemica was used more
frequently than Psilocybe semilanceata.

## Japan and East Asia

Japan has a long tradition of mushroom cultivation dating back 2,000
years. The famous medieval tale of the "Laughing Mushroom" (waraitake) and
"Dancing Mushroom" (maitake) describes lumberjacks encountering Buddhist
nuns who had consumed psychoactive fungi and could not stop laughing and
dancing. The species Stropharia venenata (now classified within
[Psilocybe), first described by Imai in 1932, was
responsible for documented intoxications as early as 1929. Other Japanese
psychoactive species include Psilocybe subcaerulipes and Psilocybe
argentipes, both of which have been chemically confirmed to contain
psilocybin.

## Africa: The Ancient Rock Art

The African continent, considered the cradle of humanity, has yielded
evidence of what may be the oldest known mushroom cult anywhere in the
world. Rock paintings in the Sahara -- dating from 9,000 to 7,000 years
ago, when the region was still a fertile garden -- depict anthropomorphic
beings with mushroom-shaped heads engaged in ecstatic dancing. Dashed lines
connecting the mushrooms to the centers of the heads suggest a flow of
energy or consciousness, consistent with psychoactive mushroom use. The
mushroom shapes depicted resemble [Psilocybe semilanceata and Psilocybe species with umbonate caps. Despite this
ancient heritage, the African psychoactive mycoflora remains largely
unstudied.
