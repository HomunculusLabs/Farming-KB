# Global Psilocybin Mushroom Distribution

Jochen Gartz's worldwide survey reveals that [[ceremonial-shamanic-use-psychoactive-mushrooms]]
containing psilocybin and related indole alkaloids are found on every
inhabited continent. Their distribution follows patterns determined by
climate, substrate availability, and the historical movement of people
and animals. From the highlands of Mexico to the pastures of Scotland,
from the bark mulch of Japanese gardens to the cow dung of Australian
pastures, these fungi have colonized an extraordinary range of habitats.

## North America: The Pacific Northwest

The Pacific Northwest of North America -- stretching from northern
California through Oregon, Washington, and into British Columbia --
represents one of the world's richest regions for psychoactive mushroom
diversity.

[[psilocybe-semilanceata]] is the most widely used species, with habitat
expanding east of the Cascade Mountains. [[psilocybe-cubensis]], a
strongly bluing species common in parks and on bark mulch, is the second
most important species. The newly described *Psilocybe azurescens*, found
in Oregon, is unusually large and very potent due to high psilocybin
content.

*Psilocybe stuntzii*, discovered at the University of Washington in 1972,
fruits on bark mulch in parks, on golf courses, football fields, and
gardens. It can fruit year-round under favorable conditions.

By the early 1980s, experts estimated that the number of hallucinogenic
mushroom users in the United States outnumbered LSD users for the first
time, with an estimated one million consumers across North America.
Psilocybe cubensis is common across the American South, and
*[[gartz-panaeolus-subbalteatus-taxonomy-and-intoxication-history]]* grows across the Southwest. California alone
was estimated to have 100,000 "Magic Mushroom People."

The 1979 British Columbia High Court ruling that only pure psilocybin was
prohibited -- not the mushrooms themselves -- represented a landmark in
the legal history of these fungi.

## Central and South America: The Heartland

Mexico remains the historical center of psychoactive mushroom culture.
The *Psilocybe* species of Mexico -- including *P. mexicana*,
*P. caerulescens*, and *P. aztecorum* -- have been used in sacred
ceremonies for centuries, a tradition made famous by Albert Hofmann,
R. Gordon Wasson, and Maria Sabina in the 1950s. Since then, the influx
of "hippie" tourism has damaged the cultural fabric of indigenous
mushroom worship in many areas.

In South America, [[psilocybe-cubensis]] and *[[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]]* are
the most commonly used species. Colombia's archaeological park at
San Augustin near Bogota has been dubbed the "heart of magic mushroom
land," and golden mushroom-shaped pendants found in the region suggest
centuries of traditional use. Communes were established in the Andes
Mountains, the most famous known as La Miel ("honey"). The species also
occur throughout Amazonia where Colombia, Peru, and Brazil share borders.

## Australia: The Fifth Continent

Australia's psychoactive mycoflora was first documented scientifically by
J. Cleland, who in 1927 described *[[psilocybe-subaeruginosa]]*, a bluing
species found in New South Wales, Victoria, and South Australia.
Psilocybe cubensis grows abundantly along the sunshine coast of
Queensland and in open valleys around Brisbane, likely introduced by
early settlers.

*Panaeolus cyanescens*, known colloquially as "Blue Meanies," is
widespread throughout northern and southern Australia. The summer of 1969
brought a "mushroom epidemic" to a 4,000-hectare region of coastal
Queensland after heavy rains. Collectors flocked to the area, and
special transport companies were founded to deliver mushrooms to major
cities. Dried mushrooms sold for approximately one US dollar per
fruiting body on the black market, and in Tasmania, gelatine capsules
containing ground mushroom powder sold for six dollars each.

By 1972, LSD had almost disappeared from the Australian black market,
largely replaced by locally available psychoactive mushrooms.
