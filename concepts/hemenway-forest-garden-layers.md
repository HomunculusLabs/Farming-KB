# Forest Garden Layers

The forest garden is the ultimate expression of ecological gardening, mimicking
the structure and function of a natural forest while producing food, medicine,
and other products for people. At its core is the concept of vertical layering,
where multiple species occupy different heights and niches within the same
space, dramatically increasing productivity per square foot.

## Why Layers Matter

In nature, forests are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth. From open
sky to earth, forest vegetation ranges in many layers: high canopy, low trees,
shrubs, tall herbs, ground-hugging rosettes and creepers, and vines that span
the whole range. Amid all this varied habitat are hundreds of niches for
insects, birds, and other creatures. Food webs are complex, with plants,
grazers, predators, top carnivores, and decomposers entwined in a many-partnered
dance.

An ecological garden with many layers creates varied niches for creatures,
provides diverse products, and offers plenty of visual interest. Compared to a
conventional garden with only one layer about one to three feet high, the
multi-storied approach captures far more sunlight, holds more water, builds
more soil, and supports far more life.

## The Seven Layers of the Forest Garden

### 1. High Canopy (Overstory Trees)
The tallest layer, typically 40 feet and up. In a forest garden, these are
large nut and fruit trees such as oaks, walnuts, chestnuts, hickories, and
standard-size fruit trees. They provide shade, timber, nuts, and wildlife
habitat. In smaller gardens, this layer may be absent or represented by just
one or two trees placed where their shade benefits the rest of the garden.

### 2. Low Tree Layer
Trees from about 15 to 40 feet, including semi-dwarf fruit trees, smaller nut
trees, and ornamental or productive trees like persimmon, pawpaw, mulberry,
and serviceberry. These are often the primary food-producing trees in home
forest gardens. See [[hemenway-food-forest-design]] for specific tree
recommendations.

### 3. Shrub Layer
Woody plants from roughly 3 to 15 feet tall. This productive layer includes
berry bushes (blueberry, raspberry, blackberry, currant, gooseberry), nitrogen-
fixing shrubs (Russian olive, goumi, seaberry, buffaloberry), and ornamental
or flowering shrubs that attract pollinators and provide habitat. The shrub
layer is where a tremendous diversity of food and function can be packed into
a small area.

### 4. Herbaceous Layer
Non-woody plants including perennial vegetables, herbs, and ornamental
flowers. Perennial vegetables such as asparagus, artichokes, rhubarb, Good
King Henry, perennial kale, French sorrel, and many others replace the labor
of annual planting. Culinary and medicinal herbs like rosemary, thyme, sage,
oregano, mint, and bee balm fill gaps and attract beneficial insects.

### 5. Ground Cover Layer
Low-growing plants that carpet the soil, protect it from erosion, suppress
weeds, and maintain soil moisture. Clover, creeping thyme, strawberries,
vypergrass, and other living mulches serve this function. A dense ground cover
also provides habitat for ground-dwelling beneficial insects and spiders.

### 6. Root Layer (Rhizosphere)
Plants whose primary edible or useful portion grows underground. These include
potatoes, sweet potatoes, Jerusalem artichokes, daikon radish, garlic, onions,
and other root crops. In a mature forest garden, root crops are often placed
in the sunnier edges where canopy gaps allow light penetration.

### 7. Vine Layer
Climbing plants that ascend into the other layers, using trees, trellises, or
other structures for support. Grapes, kiwis, hardy passionflower, hops,
climbing beans, cucumbers, and flowering vines like clematis and honeysuckle
all occupy vertical space that would otherwise go unused. A grape arbor over a
seating area or chicken coop is a classic example of stacking functions with
vines.

## Advantages of Layered Systems

Multiple, layered systems offer several key advantages over single-layer
gardens:

- **Increased yield per area:** By occupying all seven layers, a forest
  garden produces far more total food per square foot than a conventional
  garden
- **Reduced maintenance:** Shade from upper layers suppresses weeds, and the
  dense vegetation retains moisture, reducing irrigation needs
- **Pest and disease resistance:** Complex food webs with many predator
  species keep pests in check naturally
- **Soil building:** Constant leaf drop and root turnover from all layers
  builds deep, rich soil
- **Extended seasons:** Different layers create varied microclimates,
  extending the growing season for understory plants
- **Habitat creation:** Layered vegetation supports far more wildlife than
  monocultures

## Designing with Layers

Not every forest garden needs all seven layers. In a small urban yard, you
might have just three or four layers: a small fruit tree, a few berry bushes,
herbaceous plants, and ground cover. The key principle is to stack as many
productive layers as your space allows, and to ensure that each layer receives
appropriate light levels.

In sunny climates, large trees can be closely spaced to provide shade for
understory crops. In cooler or grayer zones, trees should be spread out to
allow ample light and warmth to reach lower layers.
