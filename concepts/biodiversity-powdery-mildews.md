# Powdery Mildews

The Erysiphaceae, or powdery mildews, is a family of
[[biodiversity-ascomycete-diversity-taxonomy]] whose
species are obligate parasites on the above-ground parts of higher
plants. They are nourished by haustoria and develop a characteristic
white powdery conidial stage on the surface of the host. Powdery
mildews are among the most recognizable and economically important
groups of plant pathogenic fungi.

## Taxonomic Overview

Depending on the authority, there are from 19 to 22 currently
accepted genera and an estimated 400 species, the majority of
which are in the genus Erysiphe. Although the morphological
features of the conidial states are sufficient to separate four
form genera, the perithecia must generally be present for species
determination using current taxonomic treatments. Hirata (1986),
in his massive worldwide treatise, lists close to 4,000
publications on powdery mildews, including regional monographs.

## Host Range and Distribution

Powdery mildews attack 9,838 angiosperm host species dispersed
among 44 orders, 169 families (roughly half of the known
angiosperm families), and 1,617 genera. Of the hosts, 93 percent
are dicotyledons. Of the 662 known monocotyledonous hosts, 634
are in the Gramineae. No powdery mildews are known on gymnosperms,
pteridophytes, or lower plants such as mosses and liverworts.

The areas of greatest abundance and greatest damage are California
and Israel, both characterized by warm, rain-free summers and
intensive agriculture. Hirata divided the world into 10 geographic
regions and noted that north-temperate regions had far larger
numbers of host species than tropical, subtropical, arctic, or
subarctic regions.

## Species-to-Host Ratio

Hirata calculated that the ratio of species of powdery mildews to
angiosperm species is 4.5 percent, based on an estimated total
of 220,000 angiosperm species. The number of herbaceous species
(8,250) hosting powdery mildews was far larger than the number
of tree hosts (2,645). This difference probably reflects the
growth habit of the fungus, which generally is limited to leaves
and succulent shoots.

Host ranges vary from large to small. Because species concepts
generally have become narrower and more restricted in recent
years, comparisons of species numbers reported in older regional
monographs with those in modern treatments is difficult.

## Field Recognition

Powdery mildews can be recognized in the field by the white,
dusty, conidial state, which coats leaf and small stem surfaces.
Infected tissues may be stunted or chlorotic. Conidial states
develop first and may be the only stage present on young
infections. Perithecia are more likely to occur in older,
well-established infections on senescing leaves.

On close examination with a hand lens, black, globose, mature
perithecia less than a millimeter in diameter may be observed
scattered over the mycelium. Immature perithecia are yellowish
to orange. The nonostiolate perithecia (sometimes called
cleistothecia) are ornamented with various types of hyphal
appendages, which may be simple or ornamented with hooks,
branches, or other structures.

## Identification

Genera are delimited based on features of the appendages plus
the number of asci per perithecium. Because many species of
powdery mildews are host-specific, correct host identification
is essential. If the diseased host is unidentified, healthy
foliage and flowers or fruit should be collected at the same
time as the fungus for later identification.

Perithecia provide the most reliable characters for species
determination. Dark, mature fruiting bodies should be collected
when possible. The appendage morphology, number of asci, and
ascus structure are critical taxonomic characters.

## Ecological Role

As obligate biotrophs, powdery mildews depend entirely on living
host tissue for nutrition. Their haustoria penetrate host epidermal
cells to absorb nutrients without killing the cells outright. This
parasitic strategy places powdery mildews among the most specialized
of [[biodiversity-fungi-plant-pathogens]].

In natural ecosystems, powdery mildews generally cause little
lasting damage to host plants, serving as natural regulators of
plant populations. In agricultural and horticultural settings,
however, they can cause significant yield losses and reduce crop
quality. Their impact is particularly severe in Mediterranean
and similar climates with warm, dry growing seasons.

## Relationship to Sooty Molds

Sooty molds are superficial black mats on living leaves and
stems and are particularly abundant in the tropics. They use
honeydew secreted by plants or sap-sucking insects such as aphids
and scale. They show no host preference, and colonies may consist
of mixed populations of eight or more species. Sooty molds form
a thin network of hyphae, a velvety growth, or a dark crust on
leaves and smaller twigs. On trunks and larger branches, growth
is usually more robust.

Sooty molds predominate along continental margins of the Americas,
in Central America, and on islands in the Western Hemisphere.
They occur in some form in most vegetation types but are prevalent
around the margins of rain forest communities. Unlike powdery
mildews, sooty molds are not host-specific parasites but rather
epiphytic saprobes growing on insect exudates.
