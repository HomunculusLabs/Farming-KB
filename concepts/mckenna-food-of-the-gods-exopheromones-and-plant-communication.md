# Exopheromones and Plant Chemical Communication

A concept from Terence McKenna's *Food of the Gods* (1992) proposing that
hallucinogenic alkaloids are not passive byproducts of plant metabolism but
active chemical messengers — "exopheromones" — enabling cross-species
communication between plants and animals, particularly primates.

## The Problem of Alkaloid Function

Classical biochemistry long treated alkaloids — including the powerful
hallucinogenic compounds found in fungi and plants — as inert secondary
metabolites. In this conventional view, they were considered accidental
molecular waste products with no meaningful biological role.

McKenna challenged this assumption directly. He observed that alkaloids exist
in dynamic states: their concentrations fluctuate over time, they undergo
metabolic decay, and they are produced in greatest quantities within the
tissues that are most metabolically active overall. This pattern is
inconsistent with the notion of passive waste. Something more purposeful is
occurring.

## Exopheromones: Chemical Messengers Across Species Lines

McKenna proposed the term "exopheromone" to describe a class of chemical
messenger that operates across species boundaries. Where pheromones mediate
communication between members of the same species, exopheromones allow
individuals of one species to influence the behavior, consciousness, or
development of members of an entirely different species.

This is a radical extension of the known science of [[plant-volatile-communication-chemical-ecology]]. If
exopheromones exist, they imply a web of biochemical communication that
links otherwise separate species into a single informational network.

Crucially, McKenna suggested that some exopheromones possess outsized
leverage: a small group of individuals producing or carrying these compounds
can affect an entire community or even a biome. This amplification effect
makes exopheromones potentially among the most powerful tools in nature's
repertoire for coordinating complex ecological systems.

## Nature as an Organismic Whole

The exopheromone hypothesis rests on a broader vision of nature as an
organismic whole — not a collection of competing individuals, but a
coordinated entity that mediates its own development through chemical
messages passed between species.

This view directly challenges the classical Darwinian picture of nature as
an arena of "tooth and claw" competition. McKenna noted that most
evolutionary biologists had long regarded this classical view as incomplete.
Nature, in his reading, is better understood as "an endless dance of
diplomacy" — mediated not by overt conflict but by language-like chemical
signaling systems that organisms use to negotiate their relationships.

## Cooperation Over Competition

If chemical communication pervades the natural world, then the dominant
evolutionary strategy is not raw competition but mutual cooperation and
coordination. McKenna argued that the strategy most likely to ensure
successful breeding is one of becoming indispensable to other organisms —
achieved through communication and sensitivity to signal processing.

Nature, he observed, tends to act with economy. Once an evolutionary
response is developed, it is applied again and again in appropriate
situations. The chemical [[plant-signaling-pathways]] that make exopheromones possible
would be no exception: a successful communication channel between species
would be reused and refined over evolutionary time.

## Hallucinogens as Interspecies Messengers

McKenna brought these ideas to bear directly on the question of
hallucinogenic compounds. If exopheromones are real, then hallucinogens may
function as interspecies chemical messengers — enabling the transfer of
information from plants (and fungi) to primates and other animals.

The implications are striking. The "Transcendent Other" that human beings
encounter under the influence of hallucinogenic plants may not be a
subjective hallucination at all. It may be nature correctly perceived — as
alive, as intelligent, and as actively communicating through the very
molecules that occasion the experience.

This reframes the [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]] from a purely neurochemical event
to a genuine instance of cross-species information exchange.

## Psilocybin and the Bootstrapping of Culture

McKenna traced particular significance to psilocybin, the active compound
in *Psilocybe* mushrooms. Its effects, he argued, are especially potent in
the domain of language. Psilocybin excites vocalization, empowers
articulation, and — under the right conditions — transmutes language into
something that feels visibly beheld, almost tangible.

In the presence of hallucinogens, McKenna proposed, human culture can be
quickly bootstrapped to higher states of self-reflection. Novel
information, unexpected sensory input, and unconventional behaviors
generated during psychedelic experiences feed back into the cultural
substrate, accelerating the development of language, social organization,
and abstract thought.

## Fungi as Evolutionary Catalysts

McKenna also highlighted the fact that the most powerful mutagens in the
natural environment occur in molds and fungi. This biological detail
strengthens the case for viewing fungal chemistry as an active force in
evolutionary change — not a passive backdrop but a dynamic driver of
genetic and cultural innovation alike.

## Summary

McKenna's exopheromone hypothesis proposes that hallucinogenic alkaloids
are active [[query-how-do-plants-use-chemical-signals-to-defend-against-insects]] enabling communication across species
boundaries. This view casts nature as a communicative organismic whole,
challenges purely competitive models of evolution, and reframes the
psychedelic experience as a genuine encounter with an intelligent,
chemically articulate natural world.
