# Indigenous Use of Sacred Mushrooms

**Tags:** ethnobotany, sacred-mushrooms, mexico, new-guinea, indigenous-culture, entheogen

## Overview

The ceremonial and ritual use of psychoactive fungi spans multiple continents and millennia of human history. While the most extensively documented traditions are found in Mexico and New Guinea, evidence suggests that sacred mushroom use may have been considerably more widespread than current records indicate. The rediscovery of these traditions in the mid-twentieth century fundamentally altered the modern understanding of human-fungal relationships and catalyzed the establishment of ethnomycology as a scholarly discipline.

## Rediscovery in Mexico

The modern rediscovery of traditional mushroom use in Mexico is credited to the collaborative efforts of R. Gordon Wasson, Roger Heim, and Rolf Singer during the 1950s. Wasson, a vice president at J.P. Morgan, became fascinated with the cultural significance of mushrooms after learning of their ceremonial use among the Mazatec people of Oaxaca. His subsequent fieldwork, conducted with Heim (a French mycologist) and Singer (an Argentine-American mycologist), revealed that the pre-Columbian practice of consuming psychoactive mushrooms had survived centuries of Spanish colonial suppression and continued as a living tradition among several ethnic groups in central and southern Mexico.

Their publications in *Life* magazine (1957) and in the scientific literature brought these traditions to worldwide attention and sparked intense interest in psychoactive fungi within both the scientific community and the broader public.

## The Term Teonanácatl

The Nahuatl word **teonanácatl**, meaning "divine mushroom" or "flesh of the gods," holds a central place in the ethnomycological literature. It was first documented in the historical record by Bernardino de Sahagún, a Franciscan friar who conducted extensive ethnographic studies of Aztec culture between 1569 and 1582. Sahagún's *Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva España* contains detailed descriptions of the Aztec use of psychoactive mushrooms in [[entheogenic-folk-religious-ceremonies]].

Richard Evans Schultes later brought the term to wider scholarly attention in his 1939 publication. Today, teonanácatl has become indiscriminately applied to virtually any Mexican hallucinogenic fungus, though its original Nahuatl usage referred specifically to the mushrooms used in Aztec and other central Mexican religious ceremonies, where they served as sacramental conduits to the divine.

## Common Names in Mexico

Mexico's extraordinary diversity of sacred mushroom traditions is reflected in the more than 200 common names documented for psychoactive fungi. Many of these names exist in indigenous languages, reflecting both the depth and the cultural specificity of these traditions:

- **Nahuatl names:** apipiltzin (kid/child), teotlaquilnanácatl (divine fungus that paints or describes)
- **Mazatec names:** di nizé taaya (fungus of the genius), shi thó (that eruptions thing)
- **Zapotec names:** atkad (mayor/leader), reflecting the social authority of the ceremonial practitioner
- **Spanish-language names:** san isidros (after San Isidro Labrador, patron saint of agriculture), pajaritos (little birds, referring to the visual form of dried specimens), derrumbes (landslides, referring to the destabilizing subjective effects)

This linguistic richness reflects both the deep cultural integration of mushroom use and the diversity of ethnic groups — including Mazatec, Zapotec, Mixtec, Chinantec, Nahuatl, and others — that maintain these traditions across central and southern Mexico.

## Mexico as the Center of Traditional Use

Mexico represents the preeminent center of sacred mushroom use worldwide on multiple metrics. It possesses the highest number of documented psychoactive fungal species of any country, the most thoroughly studied indigenous groups using these fungi, and the longest continuous documented history of ceremonial mushroom use stretching back to pre-Columbian civilizations. The main ethnic groups known to use sacred mushrooms are concentrated in the central and southern regions of the country, particularly in the states of Oaxaca, Puebla, Michoacán, and Mexico.

## New Guinea Traditions

New Guinea represents the second major center of documented sacred mushroom use. The ceremonial use of psychoactive fungi — including species of *Psilocybe*, *Russula*, and Boletaceous fungi — was first reported by Singer (1958, 1960a) and subsequently documented by Heim & Wasson (1965) and Heim (1965a, 1978). These traditions are found among various Highland cultures where mushrooms are incorporated into ritual contexts that parallel, in certain [[metzner-shamanic-ceremony-versus-psychotherapy-structural-features]], the Mexican ceremonies — including the use of mushrooms in divination, healing, and community bonding practices.

The diversity of fungal genera used in New Guinea is notably broader than in Mexico, where *Psilocybe* predominates. The inclusion of *Russula* and Boletaceous fungi suggests a more generalized cultural conception of "sacred fungi" that extends beyond the tryptamine-containing species. The specific psychoactive properties, if any, of the non-Psilocybe fungi used in these ceremonies remain poorly characterized and represent an important area for future ethnomycological investigation.

New Guinea mushroom traditions remain among the least studied of any known entheogenic practices, and much work remains to document the full extent of their cultural significance and the specific species involved. The remote and linguistically diverse nature of New Guinea communities presents significant logistical challenges to ethnobotanical research in the region.

## Possible Use in Other Regions

Evidence hints at sacred mushroom traditions beyond the well-documented centers of Mexico and New Guinea:

- **Colombia:** Schultes & Bright (1979) documented gold pectorals and other artifacts from pre-Columbian cultures that appear to depict mushroom motifs, including small mushroom-shaped ornaments that may represent psychoactive fungi. These findings suggest possible ancient ceremonial use of psychoactive mushrooms in northern South American cultures, though direct evidence of consumption has not been recovered.

- **Africa:** Samorini (personal communication cited by Guzmán, Allen & Gartz) has suggested possible indigenous use of psychoactive mushrooms on the African continent. Given Africa's fungal biodiversity and the presence of neurotropic species such as *P. natalensis*, this possibility warrants systematic investigation.

- **Siberia:** The Chukchee and Koryak peoples of Siberia have a well-documented tradition of using *Amanita muscaria* (fly agaric) as an intoxicant and ceremonial substance, described in detail by Wasson & Wasson (1957). Although this involves a different fungus with different active compounds (muscimol and ibotenic acid rather than psilocybin), it represents an important parallel tradition of psychoactive mushroom use in a northern hemisphere indigenous context.

## Non-Psilocybe Fungi in Mexican Ceremonies

[[guzman-allen-gartz-indigenous-mexican-mushroom-nomenclature-sacred-names]] mushroom traditions extend well beyond *Psilocybe* to encompass a variety of other fungi used in ceremonial, medicinal, and ritual contexts:

### Ganoderma lobatum Cult

In Chignahuapan, Puebla, a unique cult venerates *Ganoderma lobatum*. A church was constructed in honor of this fungus, and its interior is decorated with sketches depicting Christ alongside the sun and the moon — a syncretic blend of Catholic iconography and pre-Hispanic cosmological symbols. Although *Ganoderma lobatum* possesses no neurotropic properties, the cult exists in a region where *Psilocybe* use is well documented, suggesting a broader cultural framework of fungal veneration that encompasses both psychoactive and non-psychoactive species. This cult may represent a cultural "fossil" — a practice that originally centered on psychoactive fungi but shifted to a non-psychoactive surrogate under colonial pressure, preserving the ritual structure while changing the focal organism.

### Lycoperdon Puffballs

In Mixtec ceremonies, at least nine species of *Lycoperdon* (puffball) fungi are used indistinctly as narcotic fungi. Their specific psychoactive properties, if any, remain poorly characterized, and their inclusion in ceremonial practice may reflect broader symbolic associations with fungi rather than direct pharmacological effects.

### Cordyceps Species

*Cordyceps capitata* and *C. ophioglossoides*, known locally as "hombrecitos" (little men) and "mujercitas" (little women), are used in ceremonies alongside *Psilocybe muliercula* at the Nevado de Toluca, a high volcanic peak in central Mexico. The anthropomorphic names reflect the indigenous tendency to perceive personhood or spiritual agency in these fungi.

### Elaphomyces (Deer Truffles)

*Elaphomyces* species (deer truffles) serve a distinctive ceremonial role as the "director" of mushroom ceremonies, guiding and structuring the ritual experience without being consumed themselves. Among the Trique Indians, *Elaphomyces* is additionally used medicinally for treating wounds and for purposes described as "rejuvenation," reflecting a broader understanding of fungi as sources of both spiritual and physical healing.

## The Ceremony: Structure and Context

Traditional mushroom ceremonies in Mexico typically follow a structured ritual format that has been transmitted across generations. A curandero or curandera (healer) serves as the ceremonial guide, selecting appropriate specimens, determining dosage, and managing the overall course of the experience. The ceremony commonly takes place at night, in darkness or candlelight, and may involve the chanting of specific prayers, the burning of copal incense, and the invocation of Catholic saints alongside pre-Columbian deities — reflecting the syncretic religious framework that characterizes much of indigenous Mexican spirituality.

Participants typically consume the mushrooms in pairs or small groups, often accompanied by the ritual leader. The subjective effects are interpreted within a cultural framework that attributes visionary content to spiritual entities, natural forces, or divine communication, rather than regarding them as merely pharmacological phenomena. The integration of the experience into daily life through subsequent discussion with the healer is considered an essential component of the practice.

## Entheogenic Use vs. Recreational Degeneration

The indigenous use of sacred mushrooms is fundamentally ceremonial and integrative, embedded within cosmological frameworks that govern their purposeful application. The concept of entheogenic use — derived from the Greek *enthos* (divine) and *genesthai* (to generate) — was proposed to distinguish these traditional practices from the recreational consumption that became widespread during the 1960s following the Wasson revelations.

Indigenous use emphasizes healing, divination, communal bonding, and spiritual insight, typically conducted within structured ritual settings under the guidance of experienced practitioners who serve as cultural interpreters of the visionary experience. Recreational patterns, by contrast, often lack the cultural structures that traditionally moderate and contextualize the experience, potentially leading to what some observers have described as a "degeneration" of the original sacred context — a process in which the profound spiritual dimensions of the experience are reduced to mere sensory novelty.

## Colonial Suppression and Survival

The survival of sacred [[sacred-mushroom-traditions-in-mexico]] Mexico is itself remarkable given the intensity of Spanish colonial efforts to eradicate indigenous religious practices. Following the conquest, Catholic missionaries identified mushroom ceremonies as particularly threatening examples of "pagan idolatry" and targeted them for systematic suppression. The Inquisition recorded numerous cases of mushroom use among indigenous peoples and punished practitioners severely.

Despite these pressures, the traditions survived in remote communities where colonial authority was weakest and where indigenous cultural practices could be maintained with relative discretion. The geographic isolation of many Mazatec, Zapotec, and Mixtec communities proved crucial to the preservation of these practices into the modern era, where they were ultimately documented by Wasson, Heim, and Singer.

## Five Historical Periods of Neurotropic Fungi Study

The scholarly study of neurotropic fungi can be divided into five historical periods spanning from early colonial observations through modern phytochemical and clinical research:

1. **Colonial period (16th–18th centuries):** Initial documentation by Spanish chroniclers, particularly Sahagún, who described teonanácatl use among the Aztecs.

2. **Early botanical period (19th century):** Specimens were collected and described taxonomically, but their psychoactive properties were not yet recognized by Western science.

3. **Ethnobotanical rediscovery (1930s–1950s):** Schultes identified teonanácatl as a psychoactive mushroom; Wasson, Heim, and Singer documented living ceremonial traditions.

4. **Chemical and pharmacological period (1960s–1980s):** Hofmann identified [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]]; clinical research explored their effects before legal restrictions intervened.

5. **Modern research period (1990s–present):** Renewed scientific interest, clinical trials for depression and end-of-life anxiety, and advances in neuroimaging of psychedelic states.

Each successive period brought expanding understanding of both the cultural significance and the pharmacological properties of these remarkable organisms.

## Spanish Colonial Impact on Mushroom Traditions

The Spanish conquest of Mexico brought devastating consequences for indigenous mushroom traditions. Catholic missionaries viewed the ceremonial use of psychoactive fungi as a direct challenge to their evangelization efforts and a manifestation of "diabolical" practices. The Inquisition actively persecuted practitioners, and mushroom ceremonies were driven underground or adapted to incorporate Catholic elements as a protective disguise.

Despite this sustained suppression over nearly five centuries, the traditions demonstrated remarkable resilience. The remote geography of many indigenous communities in Oaxaca, Puebla, Michoacán, and adjacent states provided a degree of isolation that shielded ceremonial practices from complete eradication. The syncretic blending of Catholic and pre-Columbian religious elements — visible in the simultaneous invocation of saints and mushroom spirits — represents both a survival strategy and a genuine cultural synthesis that characterizes much of contemporary indigenous Mexican spirituality.

## Contemporary Relevance

The indigenous use of sacred mushrooms has gained renewed significance in the 21st century as clinical research into psychedelic-assisted therapy has expanded dramatically. Modern clinical trials investigating psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression, end-of-life anxiety, and substance use disorders have drawn explicit inspiration from traditional ceremonial frameworks, particularly the emphasis on set (mindset), setting (environment), and integration (post-experience processing) that are hallmarks of indigenous practice.

This intersection of traditional knowledge and modern science raises important questions about intellectual property, cultural appropriation, and the ethical responsibilities of researchers working with psychoactive substances that have deep roots in [[ethnobotanical-use-of-sacred-mushrooms-in-indigenous-cultures]]. Several indigenous communities and organizations have called for greater recognition of their contributions to the understanding of these fungi and for equitable participation in the benefits of commercial and therapeutic applications.
