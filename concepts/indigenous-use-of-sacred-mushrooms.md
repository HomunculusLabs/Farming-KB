## Mexico as the Center of Traditional Use

Mexico represents the preeminent center of sacred mushroom use worldwide on multiple metrics. It possesses the highest number of documented psychoactive fungal species of any country, the most thoroughly studied indigenous groups using these fungi, and the longest continuous documented history of ceremonial mushroom use stretching back to pre-Columbian civilizations. The main ethnic groups known to use sacred mushrooms are concentrated in the central and southern regions of the country, particularly in the states of Oaxaca, Puebla, Michoacán, and Mexico.

## New Guinea Traditions

New Guinea represents the second major center of documented sacred mushroom use. The ceremonial use of psychoactive fungi — including species of *Psilocybe*, *Russula*, and Boletaceous fungi — was first reported by Singer (1958, 1960a) and subsequently documented by Heim & Wasson (1965) and Heim (1965a, 1978). These traditions are found among various Highland cultures where mushrooms are incorporated into ritual contexts that parallel, in certain [[guzman-allen-gartz-indigenous-mexican-mushroom-nomenclature-sacred-names]] mushroom traditions extend well beyond *Psilocybe* to encompass a variety of other fungi used in ceremonial, medicinal, and ritual contexts:

### Ganoderma lobatum Cult

In Chignahuapan, Puebla, a unique cult venerates *Ganoderma lobatum*. A church was constructed in honor of this fungus, and its interior is decorated with sketches depicting Christ alongside the sun and the moon — a syncretic blend of Catholic iconography and pre-Hispanic cosmological symbols. Although *Ganoderma lobatum* possesses no neurotropic properties, the cult exists in a region where *Psilocybe* use is well documented, suggesting a broader cultural framework of fungal veneration that encompasses both psychoactive and non-psychoactive species. This cult may represent a cultural "fossil" — a practice that originally centered on psychoactive fungi but shifted to a non-psychoactive surrogate under colonial pressure, preserving the ritual structure while changing the focal organism.

### Lycoperdon Puffballs

In Mixtec ceremonies, at least nine species of *Lycoperdon* (puffball) fungi are used indistinctly as narcotic fungi. Their specific psychoactive properties, if any, remain poorly characterized, and their inclusion in ceremonial practice may reflect broader symbolic associations with fungi rather than direct pharmacological effects.

### Cordyceps Species

*Cordyceps capitata* and *C. ophioglossoides*, known locally as "hombrecitos" (little men) and "mujercitas" (little women), are used in ceremonies alongside *[[sacred-mushroom-traditions-in-mexico]] Mexico is itself remarkable given the intensity of Spanish colonial efforts to eradicate indigenous religious practices. Following the conquest, Catholic missionaries identified mushroom ceremonies as particularly threatening examples of "pagan idolatry" and targeted them for systematic suppression. The Inquisition recorded numerous cases of mushroom use among indigenous peoples and punished practitioners severely.

Despite these pressures, the traditions survived in remote communities where colonial authority was weakest and where indigenous cultural practices could be maintained with relative discretion. The geographic isolation of many Mazatec, Zapotec, and Mixtec communities proved crucial to the preservation of these practices into the modern era, where they were ultimately documented by Wasson, Heim, and Singer.
