# Nematode-Trapping Fungi

Soils contain a diverse range of fungi that are parasites on nematodes,
encompassing more than 200 species across all major taxonomic groups including
deuteromycetes, basidiomycetes, chytridiomycetes, and zygomycetes.
[[nematophagous-fungi]] are found worldwide, from the tropics to Antarctica, in
agricultural and forest soils. Among these, the nematode-trapping fungi are
the best-known group, owing to their remarkable morphological adaptations for
capturing prey and their dramatic infection of nematodes. These fungi
represent an important but often overlooked component of soil food webs and
[[gadd-fungal-nutrition-metabolism]].

## Classification and Diversity

Based on infection mechanisms, three broad groups are recognized among
nematophagous fungi: nematode-trapping fungi and endoparasitic fungi that
attack free-living nematodes using specialized structures, and egg- and cyst-
parasitic fungi that infect these stages with their hyphal tips. The
nematode-trapping fungi are the most extensively studied group. With few
exceptions, including the mushroom Hohenbuehelia (asexual state Nematoctonus),
most identified species belong to a monophyletic clade among the apothecial
ascomycetes, demonstrating that the predatory lifestyle has a single
evolutionary origin within this group.

Phylogenetic analysis of 18S rDNA sequences confirms that nematode-trapping
fungi form a monophyletic clade among an unresolved cluster of apothecial
ascomycetes. Importantly, the phylogenetic pattern within this clade is
concordant with the morphology of the trapping structures, suggesting that
trap morphology is evolutionarily conserved and phylogenetically informative.

## Trap Morphology and Diversity

Nematode-trapping fungi grow as saprophytes in soils and enter the parasitic
stage by developing specific morphological structures called traps. Traps
develop from hyphal branches and can be formed spontaneously or induced in
response to signals from the environment, including peptides and other
compounds secreted by host nematodes. There is large variation in trap
morphology, even between closely related species:

**Adhesive branches**: An erect branch covered with adhesive material.
**Adhesive nets**: Complex three-dimensional networks of adhesive hyphae, as
in the well-studied [[arthrobotrys-oligospora]].
**Adhesive knobs**: Morphologically distinct cells, often produced on the
apex of a slender hyphal stalk, with a layer of adhesive polymers not
present on the support stalks. Monacrosporium haptotylum uses this type.
**Constricting rings**: Mechanical traps that capture nematodes through
rapid hyphal constriction.

Following trap development, infection proceeds through a sequence of events:
attachment of trap cells to the nematode surface, cuticle penetration,
digestion, and assimilation of nutrients from the killed nematode.

## Model Species and Molecular Tools

Two model species have been particularly important: Arthrobotrys oligospora
(adhesive nets) and Monacrosporium haptotylum (adhesive knobs). For A.
oligospora, the function of a serine protease (PII, a subtilisin) and a
carbohydrate-binding protein (AOL, a lectin) have been examined in detail.
PII deletion mutants produced fewer infection structures and had lower
virulence, while over-expressing mutants developed more traps and killed
nematodes more rapidly. AOL appears to function in nitrogen storage, being
synthesized in large amounts during nematode digestion and transported from
the infected nematode to other parts of the mycelium.

For M. haptotylum, an EST database of 8,463 sequences was assembled into
3,121 contigs representing putative unique genes. Between 5% and 37% showed
homology to known proteins, while 38% to 60% were orphans with no known
homologues, the proportion being highest in trap (58%) and infection (60%)
libraries. From this resource, a cDNA microarray of 3,518 clones was
constructed for transcriptional profiling.

## Transcriptional Profiling of Trap Development

Comparison of gene expression in trap cells (knobs) and vegetative mycelium
of M. haptotylum revealed substantial differences. Using mixed-model ANOVA
with a significance threshold of p < 0.05, 23.3% (657 of 2,822) of
putative genes were differentially expressed. Genes with roles in
transcription, cellular transport, signal transduction, cell rescue and
defence, and cell growth were expressed at lower levels in knobs than in
mycelium, reflecting the transition from active vegetative growth to a
specialized infection structure.

The trap represents a shift in cell polarity, and several differentially
expressed genes are homologues of those known to modify cell polarity in
fungi. Key components include actin-binding proteins profilin and cofilin,
and members of the rho and ras families of small GTPases that regulate the
actin cytoskeleton. During apical growth, actin polarizes through cortical
patches and cables at the tip; during isotropic growth of the knob, these
proteins adopt a more diffuse distribution.

## Convergence with Plant Pathogenic Fungi

Remarkable similarities exist between the knobs of nematode-trapping fungi
and the appressoria of plant pathogenic fungi. Both are specialized
infection structures developing as spherical cells at hyphal tips, both
contain adhesive layers for host attachment, and both penetrate hosts using
physical force combined with extracellular enzymatic activities.

Transcriptional profiling of knobs shows convergence in gene regulation with
appressoria of [[magnaporthe-grisea]] and Blumeria graminis. Stress and defence
response genes, including cyclophilins, peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans
isomerases, metallothionein, and thioredoxins, are differentially expressed
in both systems. Genes involved in protein synthesis and degradation
(ribosomal proteins, ubiquitin, proteasome components) are also differentially
regulated, suggesting that infection structure development in both types of
fungi involves extensive protein turnover.

The serine protease (subtilisin) gene is up-regulated in both knobs and
appressoria, highlighting the shared importance of proteolytic enzymes in
host penetration. In M. grisea, physical penetration force is generated by
turgor pressure from glycerol accumulation; a similar mechanism may operate
in nematode-trapping fungi.

## Ecological Significance

Nematode-trapping fungi play important roles in soil ecosystems by
regulating nematode populations, particularly in agricultural and forest
soils. Their predatory activity links the fungal decomposer community to the
faunal food web. The ability to switch between saprotrophic and predatory
lifestyles gives them ecological flexibility, allowing them to exploit both
organic matter and living nematode prey.

## Biocontrol Potential

The ability of nematode-trapping fungi to capture and kill plant-parasitic
nematodes has generated interest in their use as biological control agents.
Species such as Arthrobotrys oligospora and Duddingtonia flagrans have been
investigated for suppression of gastrointestinal nematodes in livestock and
plant-parasitic nematodes in crops. Understanding the molecular basis of trap
formation through functional genomics will enhance the development of these
fungi as effective biocontrol agents, complementing approaches used in
[[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs]] and gadd fungal biodegradation  bioremediation.
