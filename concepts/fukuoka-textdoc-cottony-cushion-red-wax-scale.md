## Cottony-Cushion Scale and Red Wax Scale — Natural Biological Control History

Fukuoka used the histories of two major citrus pests in Japan—the cottony-cushion
scale and the red wax scale—to illustrate a fundamental principle of natural
farming: human intervention with powerful pesticides often creates the very
problems it claims to solve, while leaving nature alone allows biological control
systems to function effectively. Both pests were once considered among the three
major citrus pests in Japan, and both were brought under control by natural
predators before postwar pesticide use reversed decades of [[hamilton-beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]].

In Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-natural-orchard-development]], where he did not use strong pesticides, the
cottony-cushion scale continued as before to serve as prey for several types of
ladybugs. He saw almost no damage from this pest, demonstrating that the
original biological control system was still functional wherever it had not been
destroyed by chemical intervention.

## The Red Wax Scale

The red wax scale (*Ceroplastes rubens*) was another of the three major citrus
pests in Japan. Before the war, it had to be destroyed by spraying a pine rosin
mixture, a relatively mild treatment that did not eliminate the natural enemies
of the scale. Then, in what Fukuoka described as perhaps a stroke of good luck,
the wartime shortage of rosin material forced orchardists to discontinue the pine
rosin applications. During this pesticide-free interval, [[parasitic-wasps-biological-pest-control]] emerged
that preyed on the red wax scale, making it no longer necessary to exterminate
them.

This sequence of events is a textbook example of how nature fills ecological
vacancies. When the pesticide pressure was removed, the parasitic wasps that had
always been present at low levels were able to increase their populations and
establish effective control over the scale insect. The system reached a new
equilibrium in which the pest was suppressed without chemical inputs.

## The Fluorine Pesticide Disaster

After the war, although the red wax scale was no longer much of a problem,
farmers began using a potent fluorine pesticide reputed to be effective against
the scale. This was a classic case of treating a problem that no longer existed.
The introduction of the fluorine pesticide had immediate and severe consequences:
serious outbreaks of the red wax scale arose at once.

The fluorine pesticide was highly toxic and was responsible for a number of
local deaths among farmworkers and nearby residents. Because of these human
casualties, its use was later banned. Once the fluorine pesticide was banned,
infestation by the red wax scale declined almost immediately. The parasitic wasps
recovered, and the biological control system reestablished itself.

Fukuoka pointed to this episode as clear evidence that the most intelligent way
of controlling the red wax scale was simply not to spray. The entire episode of
outbreak, poisoning, and recovery demonstrated that pesticide use was not merely
unnecessary but actively counterproductive. The natural enemy complex was fully
capable of managing the pest without human intervention.

## The Pattern of Pesticide-Created Problems

Together, the stories of the cottony-cushion scale and the red wax scale
illustrate a recurring pattern that Fukuoka observed throughout his farming
career. When a pest problem exists, the first impulse [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]], and
pesticides have drawn the farmer away from nature. Although these useless products
of human manufacture do not raise the yields of his land, because they are
promoted as tools for making profits and boosting yields, he labors under the
illusion that he needs them. Their use has wrought great destruction on nature,
robbing it of its powers and leaving man no choice but to tend vast fields by his
own hand. This in turn has made large machinery, high-grade compound fertilizers,
and powerful poisons indispensable. And the same vicious cycle goes on and on
without end."

## Implications for Natural Farming

These case histories demonstrate one of the [[permaculture-core-principles]] of Fukuoka's approach
to pest management: if man had left the pests alone, they would never have become
major problems. In his own orchard, he never had any serious problems with mites
or scales in his citrus trees. Or if he did, the problem solved itself through
the action of natural enemies.

Fukuoka did not claim that all pests can be ignored without consequence. He
acknowledged that certain [[solomon-insects-and-diseases-integrated-approach]] present special problems in some
types of fruit trees, and that some attention must be given to specific pests
through [[coleman-winter-greens-variety-selection]] and environmental management. But his experience with
the cottony-cushion scale and the red wax scale strongly suggested that the
starting point for pest management should be the protection and encouragement of
natural enemies, not the application of poisons.

## Broader Significance

These two examples also illustrate a larger philosophical point that runs through
Fukuoka's work. Man's belief that he can improve upon nature through
intervention is, in Fukuoka's view, the fundamental error of modern civilization.
Nature already contains within itself the mechanisms for maintaining balance.
When man disrupts these mechanisms through pesticide use, he creates problems that
he then must struggle to solve, always falling further behind. The only way to
escape this cycle is to stop interfering and allow nature's self-regulating
processes to function.
## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
