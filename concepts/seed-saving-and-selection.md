# Seed Saving and Selection

Seed saving is the practice of harvesting seeds from mature crops for replanting in subsequent seasons. Steve Solomon's *Gardening When It Counts* treats seed saving as an essential skill for self-sufficient gardeners, arguing that dependence on commercial seed suppliers is a vulnerability during hard times when seed may become unavailable or unaffordable.

## The Importance of Seed Saving

In normal times, purchasing seeds is convenient and reliable. Commercial [[spanish-cannabis-seed-companies]] offer tested varieties with known germination rates and disease resistance. However, Solomon points out several risks in seed dependence:

- **Supply disruption:** Seed companies may face crop failures, shipping delays, or business closures
- **Variety discontinuation:** Popular varieties can be dropped from catalogs without warning
- **Genetic narrowing:** Commercial breeding focuses on uniformity and transportability, often at the expense of flavor and regional adaptation
- **Cost:** For gardeners growing significant areas, seed costs can become substantial

Home-saved seeds, when properly managed, become increasingly adapted to local growing conditions over generations. This local adaptation is a form of plant breeding that no commercial operation can replicate for every microclimate.

## Open-Pollinated vs. Hybrid Varieties

The distinction between open-pollinated (OP) and hybrid (F1) varieties is fundamental to seed saving:

### Open-Pollinated Varieties

Open-pollinated varieties reproduce true to type from saved seed. When pollen from one plant fertilizes another of the same variety, the offspring closely resemble the parents. This stability is the result of many generations of selection for consistent traits.

OP varieties are the foundation of seed saving. Solomon recommends that gardeners serious about food self-sufficiency transition entirely to OP varieties, as hybrids cannot be reliably saved and reproduced.

### Hybrid (F1) Varieties

F1 hybrids are produced by crossing two distinct inbred parent lines. The resulting seeds exhibit "[[cannabis-hybrid-vigor-and-phenotypes-clarke]]" — often greater uniformity, yield, or disease resistance than either parent. However, seeds saved from F1 plants do not breed true. The F2 generation (saved seed replanted) segregates into a wide range of types, most of which are inferior to the F1.

Solomon does not dismiss hybrids entirely — they can be useful for specific purposes — but he insists that hybrid-dependent gardeners are not truly self-sufficient. If seed companies stop producing a hybrid, the gardener cannot reproduce it.

## Basic [[seed-saving-techniques]]

### Dry Seed Crops

Dry-seeded crops (beans, peas, grains, lettuce, brassicas, onions, carrots) produce seeds that dry naturally on the plant. Harvest when seed pods are brown and brittle, or when seed heads are fully dry. Thresh to separate seeds from chaff, then dry further in a well-ventilated area out of direct sunlight.

Solomon recommends harvesting slightly before full maturity if bird predation or weather is a concern, then finishing the drying process indoors. Seeds should be stored at cool, dry conditions. A simple test for adequate dryness: hit a seed with a hammer — properly dry seeds shatter rather than mash.

### Wet Seed Crops

Wet-seeded crops (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, squash, melons) produce seeds encased in fleshy fruit. Harvest fully ripe fruit, extract seeds, and process to remove the gelatinous coating that inhibits germination.

For tomatoes, Solomon recommends the fermentation method: squeeze seeds and pulp into a container, add a small amount of water, and let ferment for 2–3 days. The good seeds sink while the gelatinous material and non-viable seeds float. Pour off the floaters, rinse the sunk seeds, and dry on a screen or paper towel.

For peppers, seeds can be simply scraped from ripe fruit and dried. Cucumbers, squash, and melons require rinsing to remove the sac surrounding each seed.

## Maintaining Genetic Purity

Cross-pollination between different varieties of the same species produces offspring that are genetic mixtures, not true to either parent. Seed savers must prevent unwanted cross-pollination to maintain variety purity.

Solomon provides isolation distances for common vegetable species:

- **Self-pollinating (minimal isolation needed):** Tomatoes, peppers, lettuce, peas, beans (most types), and okra naturally pollinate themselves before flowers open. Different varieties can be grown side by side with minimal crossing.
- **Insect-pollinated (moderate isolation):** Squash (1/2 mile), cucumbers (1/2 mile), brassicas (1/4 mile), and onions (1/4 mile) require significant isolation or physical barriers.
- **Wind-pollinated (maximum isolation):** Corn (1/2–1 mile), beets, and spinach require the greatest isolation distances.

For home gardeners unable to maintain full isolation distances, Solomon recommends hand-pollination with bagged flowers, or simply accepting some genetic mixing and selecting for desired traits in subsequent generations.

## Selection and Local Adaptation

The power of seed saving lies not just in reproduction but in selection. By choosing which plants to save seed from, the gardener becomes a plant breeder, gradually shaping varieties to local conditions.

Solomon recommends selecting for:

- **Vigor and health:** Only save seed from the healthiest, most productive plants
- **Disease resistance:** If some plants resist a disease that affects others, save their seed
- **Early maturity:** In short-season climates, saving seed from the earliest plants extends the effective [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]
- **[[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]]:** During dry years, the plants that perform best are the ones to save from
- **Flavor and quality:** Taste matters — save seed from the best-tasting specimens

Over 5–10 generations, this selection pressure can produce varieties remarkably well-suited to a specific garden. Solomon calls this "backyard plant breeding" and considers it one of the most undervalued skills in home gardening.

## Population Size Considerations

[[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] within a seed-saving population is important for long-term variety health. Very small populations (fewer than 20 plants) lose genetic diversity through random chance, potentially leading to [[cervantes-hybrid-vigor-inbreeding-depression]] — reduced vigor, fertility, and disease resistance over generations.

Solomon recommends minimum population sizes:

- **Self-pollinating crops:** Save from at least 10–20 plants
- **Cross-pollinating crops:** Save from at least 50–80 plants for good genetic health
- **Maximum diversity:** Include as many plants as practical, especially for rare varieties
