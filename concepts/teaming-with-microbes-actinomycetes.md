Actinomycetes are a remarkable group of soil bacteria that bridge the
gap between bacteria and fungi in both form and function. Belonging to
the order Actinomycetales (with Streptomyces as the best-known genus),
they play essential roles in decomposing resistant organic compounds and
shaping the character of healthy soil.

## Fungal-Like Growth Form

Actinomycetes are different from other soil bacteria in a striking way:
they grow filaments, almost like fungal hyphae. These branching
filamentous structures give actinomycetes several advantages:

- **Physical reach**: Like fungi, they can extend across soil particles
  to access organic matter that ordinary bacteria cannot reach
- **Predator defense**: Their filamentous growth, combined with
  connecting soil particles, makes them too large to be eaten by
  their natural predators, the [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-types-amoeba-flagellate-ciliate]] that would
  engulf and ingest single bacterial cells
- **Soil binding**: Their filaments may help connect and bind soil
  particles, contributing to
  [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]]

## The Smell of Good Soil

Perhaps the most recognizable contribution of actinomycetes is the
characteristic earthy aroma of healthy soil. Streptomyces species produce
enzymes that include volatile chemicals responsible for this clean, fresh
scent -- the smell that every experienced gardener associates with "good
soil." This same earthy smell is the hallmark of well-made compost. The
aroma comes from a compound called geosmin, a metabolic byproduct of
Streptomyces activity.

## Specialization in Recalcitrant Compounds

Actinomycetes are particularly adept at decomposing difficult-to-digest
carbon compounds that most other bacteria cannot handle:

### Cellulose Decomposition

Cellulose constitutes half the mass of plant bodies. While specialized
bacteria like Cellulomonas carry cellulose-breaking enzymes, actinomycetes
are also important cellulose decomposers. They attack cellulose during
both early decomposition and the later maturation stages of composting.

### Chitin Decomposition

[[fungal-chitin-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]] is the
structural polysaccharide found in fungal cell walls and arthropod
exoskeletons. Most bacteria cannot digest chitin, but actinomycetes
excel at it. This makes them critical players in the turnover of fungal
biomass and the recycling of nutrients from dead insects, mites, and
other arthropods.

### Lignin Decomposition

In the maturation stage of composting, actinomycetes continue their
attack on the most resistant plant remnants, including lignin. Their
filamentous growth allows them to penetrate and break apart lignified
tissue that would otherwise persist for years. Working alongside
basidiomycete fungi, they complete the decomposition of the toughest
plant materials.

## Environmental Adaptability

Actinomycetes are adapted to live in a wider range of pH than most other
bacteria, tolerating conditions from acidic to alkaline. This broad
tolerance allows them to function in diverse soil environments where
other decomposers may be inhibited.

## Role in Composting

Actinomycetes are particularly important during the thermophilic and
maturation stages of composting:

### Thermophilic Stage (104-150F)

During the hot phase of composting, Streptomyces and other actinomycetes
become prominent along with Arthrobacter and Pseudomonas species. Their
metabolic activity contributes to the heat generation that kills
pathogens and weed seeds.

### Maturation Stage

As temperatures cool, actinomycetes are among the primary organisms
attacking the remaining resistant plant material -- lignin, chitin,
cellulose, and proteins. The products of their decomposition activity
contribute to [[fungal-roles-soil-food-webs]] formation.

## Antibiotic Production

Streptomyces species are renowned for producing antibiotics --
streptomycin (the first antibiotic effective against tuberculosis) is
named after them. In soil, these natural antibiotics help suppress
pathogenic organisms, contributing to the disease-suppressive qualities
of healthy, biologically active soil. This is one reason why compost-
amended soils tend to have fewer disease problems.

## Relationship to the Soil Food Web

Actinomycetes occupy a unique position in the
[[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]]:

- They are decomposers at the second trophic level, breaking down
  resistant organic matter
- Their filamentous growth provides some protection from protozoan
  grazing, giving them longer lifespans than typical bacteria
- By decomposing chitin, they recycle nitrogen from fungal biomass and
  arthropod exoskeletons
- Their broad pH tolerance allows them to function across the bacterial-
  fungal dominance continuum
