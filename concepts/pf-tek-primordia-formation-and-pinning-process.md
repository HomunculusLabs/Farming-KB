# PF TEK Primordia Formation and Pinning Process

The transition from vegetative mycelial growth to reproductive fruiting is the most critical phase in the PF TEK cultivation cycle. Understanding the sequence of primordia formation, pinning, and fruit body development is essential for optimal timing of cake birthing and harvest.

## Colonization to Fruiting Trigger

After the substrate turns completely white with mycelium (typically 2–3 weeks after inoculation at 70°F), the jars are left in indirect light. The mycelium continues to infiltrate the substrate, accumulating nutrients and reaching the biomass threshold needed to trigger the fruiting cycle.

This post-colonization waiting period can range from less than a week to a few weeks.

The cultivator should not rush this stage. Premature birthing of under-colonized cakes results in poor or no fruiting, because the mycelium has not yet accumulated sufficient energy reserves. The substrate must be fully colonized — white throughout, with no visible uncolonized substrate — before the shift to reproductive mode begins.

## Stages of Fruiting Initiation

### 1. Pinning

The first visible structures are tiny white pin-like growths called "pins." These are the initial hyphal knots that have aggregated into dense masses, signaling the shift from vegetative to reproductive growth.

Pinning is triggered by a combination of factors: full substrate colonization, exposure to light, high humidity, and fresh air exchange. In the PF TEK, these conditions are provided by transferring the cake to the dual chambered terrarium, where the spray shield system maintains near-100% humidity while ventilation during spraying provides gas exchange.

Pins are typically white, very small, and may appear in clusters across the cake surface. Their appearance is the first definitive sign that the cultivation has succeeded and that the organism is preparing to reproduce.

### 2. Primordia Development

Soon after pinning, small round fungus growths appear that begin to turn yellow. These develop into "primordia," described by PF as tiny worm-like structures with tiny reddish heads.

The primordia are the first recognizable mushroom formations. They represent the earliest stage of the sporocarp (fruit body) and are characterized by a discernible cap and stem differentiation, even at miniature scale.

The reddish coloration on the head is an early indication of the pigments that will develop more fully as the cap matures. At this stage the primordia are extremely small — typically just a few millimeters — but they contain the full developmental potential of a mature mushroom.

### 3. Normal Sporocarp Development

From the primordia, normal fruit bodies develop with conical caps, white stems, and light-colored gills on the underside.

As the mushroom matures, the cap expands and flattens, the veil beneath the cap stretches and eventually tears, and the gills darken from light to deep brown as spores mature. Purple spore deposits begin appearing on the stem as sporulation proceeds.

The mature mushroom resembles an umbrella with upturned edges — this is the signal that the specimen has entered its sporulation cycle and is past its prime for consumption.

## Aborts and Mutants

Not all primordia develop into mature fruit bodies. Over half of the small primordia that initially form will "abort" — they cease growing, becoming convoluted in cap shape with gnarly stems and stunted growth.

Additionally, mutant forms appear as blobs of fungus with little or no cap.

Both abhorts and mutants are significant because they are exceptionally high in psychoactive alkaloid concentration.

### Identifying Aborts

Abhorts are characterized by:

- Convoluted, gnarly caps that never fully expand
- Stunted stems compared to normal specimens developing nearby
- Cessation of growth while neighboring mushrooms continue developing normally
- A tendency for the cap to turn black if left unharvested too long

Mutant blobs lack a differentiated cap structure entirely, appearing as undifferentiated fungal masses. Despite their atypical appearance, both forms are described by PF as "primo in magic alkaloids" — among the most potent material the cake produces.

The tiny "baby mushroom" abhorts that stop growing at the earliest stage are likewise high potency.

## Optimal Birthing Timing

The best time to remove the fungus cake from the jar is when primordia (tiny worm-like structures with reddish heads) are visible on the cake surface while still in the jar.

Birthing at this stage confirms the mycelium has accumulated sufficient resources for fruiting and minimizes the risk of damaging early-forming fruit bodies. It also reduces the lag time between birthing and first harvest.

Care must be taken not to damage the primordia during the birthing process. The cake is removed by placing an old jar lid over the jar mouth, inverting the jar, and lightly slamming it down on a table cushioned with a magazine.

The cake slides onto the jar cap, which functions as a base. The cake should be handled as little as possible, as bruising (visible as blue discoloration) damages the mycelial network, though the fungus is resilient and can tolerate moderate handling.

Immediately after birthing, any water droplets on the cake surface should be dabbed away with a piece of loose tissue paper. The cake should then be placed directly into the dual chambered terrarium for the fruiting cycle.

## Harvest Timing and Potency

The secret to potent mushrooms lies in harvesting at the correct stage of maturity. Scientific evidence from Jochen Gartz shows that Psilocybe cubensis can achieve approximately 1% psilocybin content (similar to Psilocybe semilanceata), but only when grown on brown rice, harvested young (pre-sporulation), and cool-dried with desiccant.

The PF TEK harvest strategy prioritizes quality over quantity.

### Harvest Categories

- **Abhorts**: Harvest as soon as they can be identified as ceased-growing. Pick before their heads turn black. A pointed knife blade works well. Several cakes will produce a surprising pile of these small specimens.
- **Mutant blobs**: These small capless growths are also high in alkaloids and should be harvested when identified.
- **Normal fruit bodies**: Harvest before the veil under the cap breaks. At this stage, caps are still roundish and conical, gills are light-colored, and sporulation has not yet begun.
- **Mature specimens**: After the veil tears, the cap flattens and spreads, gills darken to deep brown, and purple spore deposits appear on the stem. These are past their prime for consumption but ideal for spore collection.

As the mushroom cap expands like an umbrella, the mushroom's size increases but its potency by weight decreases. By harvesting only the young and abortive specimens, total yield is significantly lower but quality approaches that of wild-collected liberty caps.

## Eating Recommendations

PF advised that eating the fungi is best done as a ritual — deliberate, controlled, and methodical. The entire dose should be consumed at one sitting rather than piece-mealed, because the lag time in response can defeat the peak performance of the psychedelic power if the dose is spread over too long a period.

## Successive Flush Cycles

After the first flush of mushrooms is harvested, the cake will typically produce additional flushes. The second flush usually appears within a week to ten days after the first harvest, provided the cake remains in the humid terrarium environment.

Each successive flush tends to produce fewer but sometimes larger mushrooms. The abort rate may increase with later flushes as the substrate's nutrient reserves are depleted. Most cultivators observe 2–4 productive flushes before the cake is exhausted and should be discarded.

Between flushes, the cake should remain in the terrarium with continued humidity maintenance. No special treatment is needed — the mycelium will recover from the harvest stress and begin forming new primordia when it has accumulated sufficient energy reserves.

## Pinning Troubleshooting

When fully colonized cakes fail to pin, the problem typically traces to one of three causes:

- **Temperature too high**: The most common cause. Mycelium spreads but no pins form. Solution: remove any heat source and lower ambient temperature.
- **Humidity too low**: Pins form slowly or not at all, and existing pins may abort. Solution: increase spraying frequency and verify terrarium seal integrity.
- **Insufficient fresh air exchange**: Carbon dioxide accumulates and suppresses pinning. Solution: ensure regular spraying provides adequate gas exchange.
