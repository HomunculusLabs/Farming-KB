# Parisian Market Gardening

Parisian market gardening (French: *culture maraîchère*) was a system of intensive vegetable
production developed in and around Paris between approximately 1850 and 1900. It represented the
highest achievement of pre-industrial horticulture — a system that fed the entire city of Paris
with fresh, locally grown produce year-round using nothing but horse manure, hand tools, glass
covers, and extraordinary horticultural skill.

## The System's Core Principles

### Intensive Land Use

The average Parisian market garden was one to two acres. A leading grower stated his first principle
as: "Always tend the smallest amount of land possible, but tend it exceptionally well." The system
achieved this through:

- Extremely narrow access paths (only 10 inches wide — too narrow for wheelbarrows)
- [[plant-micronutrients-and-their-essential-functions]]:

1. **Heat source**: Fresh manure was packed into deep hotbeds (up to 2 feet deep) beneath cold
   frames. As the manure decomposed, it generated heat (120-150°F internally) that protected
   crops from freezing. This allowed production of fresh salads in January and early cucumbers
   and melons in May.

2. **Soil amendment**: Once the hotbed manure had spent its heat and decomposed into crumbly
   [[compost]], it was shoveled out and applied to the beds. Composted horse manure and straw was
   considered by French growers to be the only suitable manure for vegetable growing.

3. **Sustainability**: The system recycled a waste product of urban transportation. The cycle was
   elegant: vegetables were delivered to city markets by horse-drawn wagons; the returning wagons
   brought back manure from the city stables to the gardens. Despite intense production, soil
   fertility increased from year to year.

### Protected Cultivation

Two primary structures provided season extension:

- **Cold frames and hotbeds**: Glass-covered wooden frames placed over heated manure beds. These
  were the primary production surfaces for winter and early spring crops. Frames could be
  individually ventilated by propping up one edge with a notched stick.

- **Cloches**: Bell-shaped glass jars, 18 inches in diameter, placed over individual plants or
  rows. Some growers owned up to 3,000 cloches. Each had to be individually vented on sunny days
  and covered with straw mats on cold nights. The labor required was staggering.

- **Straw mats**: One-inch-thick mats woven from rye straw were rolled out over cold frames and
  cloches for overnight insulation. These could add several degrees of frost protection.

## Intensive Planting Techniques

The Parisian growers achieved remarkable productivity through sophisticated intercropping. A single
hotbed might be sown with radish and carrot seed broadcast together, then interplanted with
lettuce transplants. The radishes would be harvested first, making room for the carrots growing
between the lettuces. Once lettuces were harvested, young cauliflower transplants would be set out
among the carrots. After carrots were pulled, cauliflowers had the frame to themselves.

This relay system meant that the same piece of ground was continuously productive with no fallow
periods. The grower had to understand the growth rates, space requirements, and compatibility of
multiple crops simultaneously.
