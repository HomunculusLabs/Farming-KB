# Nutrient Storage and Cycling in Terrestrial Ecosystems

Nutrient storage and cycling in terrestrial ecosystems is a central concern in David Holmgren's treatment of the principle "Catch and Store Energy." He argues that understanding how natural landscapes have evolved to capture and retain mineral nutrients, water, and organic carbon is fundamental to designing productive human systems that can persist through energy descent.

## The Geochemical Background

Mineral nutrients are largely invisible, yet they control the productivity of every ecosystem. The essential elements of carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, and nitrogen are abundant in the atmosphere, supplied through photosynthesis and associated plant processes. But the mineral nutrients — calcium, magnesium, potassium, phosphorus, sulphur, and trace elements — occur in small and varying amounts in diverse rock types that make up the earth's crust.

Plants can easily absorb these nutrients in water-soluble forms, but solubility also results in leaching beyond plant reach. Consequently, soil ecosystems have evolved to catch and store plant nutrients in non-soluble but biologically available forms.

Ecosystems develop against a geochemical background of nutrient imbalance and deficiency. They seek to overcome these through mechanisms that:

- Mine bedrock and other inert sources using root exudates and microbial action
- Catch nutrients leaking from adjacent systems through interception by vegetation and soil organisms
- Capture atmospheric transfers in dust, smoke, pollen, and other lifeforms
- Recycle nutrients internally through decomposition and mycorrhizal networks

## Long-Term Nutrient Loss

Over geological time, all ecosystems lose mineral nutrients through gravity and leaching, as well as periodic fires, droughts, floods, and other natural disasters. Additionally, chemical bonding of nutrients into highly unavailable forms progressively reduces plant access. Unless a landscape can mine or catch more than it loses, there is a progressive decline in productivity.

Species requiring high levels of nutrients are replaced by those adapted both to low levels and to chronic imbalances. In Australia, much of the exquisite biodiversity results from endless adaptations to low and unbalanced mineral nutrients. Humans, by contrast, are completely dependent on very high and balanced mineral fertility in their foods. Without adequate balanced mineral nutrition, human societies from hunter-gatherers to great civilisations have crumbled because their soils could not provide the quantity or quality of food required.

## Soil as the Primary Nutrient Storage

Soil is the most important storage for nutrients in temperate climates. Humus is, in Holmgren's words, "perhaps the greatest invention of nature" because it increases the capacity of soils to store mineral nutrients, water, and carbon simultaneously.

Humus formation follows a cascade:

1. Plant materials fall to the ground as litter
2. Worms and other soil organisms process organic matter
3. Fast-turnover products (polysaccharides, proteins) support soil micro-organisms and plant life
4. Much carbon cycles back to atmosphere as carbon dioxide within a season from microbial respiration
5. Some carbon is distilled into complex organic compounds — humic and fulvic acids — that are stable for hundreds or thousands of years

Under favourable conditions these humus storages can be stable for centuries. Wes Jackson of the Land Institute in Kansas described the loss of this ancient humus from American prairie cropping soils as "the mining of young coal." The rebuilding of humus in the world's cropping soils should be seen as one of the two great tasks for humanity, alongside reforestation of catchments and degraded rangelands.

## Humus and Mineral Balance

The value of humus goes beyond simple carbon storage. Humus increases the cation exchange capacity (CEC) of soils — their ability to hold mineral nutrients in plant-available forms without leaching. The relationship between humus and mineral balance is central to soil fertility:

- Humus acts as a sponge, holding nutrients that would otherwise leach away
- Mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective root zone, mining minerals from beyond the plant's direct reach
- Well-structured soils with high humus content allow better water infiltration, supporting deeper root growth
- The balance of soil chemistry (pH, calcium:magnesium ratio, trace elements) affects both nutrient availability and soil structure

Holmgren emphasises that many of the problems of modern agriculture can be traced to the loss of humus and consequent mineral imbalances. The common response of applying more soluble fertilisers often accelerates the decline by further degrading soil structure and biological activity.

## Tropical vs Temperate Storage Strategies

The relative importance of different storage mechanisms varies with climate:

**Wet tropics**: Rates of oxidation and leaching are so high that natural systems rely more on storage of nutrients in living plants. Long-lived trees are the most abundant and stable living store of nutrients. Soil humus is rapidly broken down and leached. Even in temperate ecosystems, plants and especially trees represent an important store of nutrients which can be recycled to the soil by annual leaf drop, browsing by insects and herbivores, or fire.

**Temperate climates**: Soil humus provides the dominant long-term storage. Plants and especially trees represent important but secondary stores that recycle nutrients to soil through annual leaf drop, browsing by insects and herbivores, or fire.

**Dry climates**: Physical soil structure, mineral content, and water-holding capacity become the dominant factors. Carbon storage is limited by low rainfall, but deep-rooted perennials can access and cycle nutrients from subsoil layers unavailable to annual crops.

## Carbon Storage in Biomass

Ecosystems with actively growing plants can accumulate several tonnes of carbon per hectare per year. Trees are especially significant because they keep accumulating carbon as wood for hundreds or even thousands of years. This long-lived storage in woody biomass is one of the best measures of an ecosystem's capacity to continue catching and storing energy and resist seasonal variability and disturbances.

Holmgren notes that the contemporary focus on carbon sequestration as a "sink" for unwanted carbon dioxide is "back-to-front" — it focuses on the problem rather than on carbon as a source of fuel for new life. Carbon in living plants and soil humus is literally "the staff of life." Much larger quantities of carbon in fibrous fodder plants sustain grazing animals, which provide renewable products from protein-rich foods and wool to horsepower.

## The Role of Trees in Nutrient Cycling

Trees play multiple roles in nutrient storage and cycling:

- **Deep mineral mining**: Tree roots penetrate subsoil layers inaccessible to annual crops, bringing minerals to the surface through leaf fall
- **Buffer against leaching**: Tree canopies intercept rainfall, reducing soil erosion and nutrient runoff
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Leguminous trees (acacias, alders, carobs) fix atmospheric nitrogen, enriching surrounding soil
- **Mycorrhizal networks**: Tree root systems host extensive fungal networks that trade minerals for carbohydrates
- **Buffer against fire and drought**: Mature trees moderate microclimate and maintain soil moisture
- **Biomass accumulation**: Trees can accumulate carbon for centuries, far exceeding the capacity of annual crops

Timber and fuel forests can be grown on marginal soils which lack the depth, structure, or fertility to support human food crops. This is "the single most important reason why the storage of carbon by perennial plants, and especially trees, is central to permaculture strategies for catching and storing energy."

## Geological Context of Productivity

Geologically young regions with recent mountain building and vulcanism tend to be much more biologically productive and have supported large populations despite vulnerability to natural disasters. Geologically old regions (like most of Australia) tend to have low biological productivity and supported fewer people.

Long periods of climatic and geophysical stability actually result in a rundown of the energy available to ecosystems and people. The biological productivity of young landscapes derives from their ability to capture and hold water, essential minerals, and the organic matter that their resultant ecosystems generate.

## The Abundance of Modern Wastes as Nutrient Sources

In a transition to energy descent, the enormous nutrient flows currently being wasted through sewage systems, landfill, and agricultural runoff represent underutilised resources. Organic matter from agriculture, forestry, and industry can be used for soil improvement, fuel, and animal feed. Holmgren emphasises that these manufactured resources are not necessarily renewable, but they can be tapped during the transition to low-energy systems.

## The Four Key Landscape Storages

Using these understandings of how nature catches and stores energy, Holmgren groups permaculture strategies for rebuilding natural capital in landscapes under four broad headings:

**Water**: The value of catching and storing water is well understood, especially in Australia. By building reservoirs, dams, swales, tanks, cisterns and other structures, people increase the biological potential of landscapes. Small catchment dams have more benefits and less adverse environmental effects than large reservoirs in major river valleys.

## From Theory to Practice: Melliodora as Example

Holmgren and his partner Su Dennett have lived these principles at Melliodora, their home and permaculture demonstration site in central Victoria, Australia. At Melliodora, they have made use of all five major energy sources — solar, wind, biomass, run-off water, and wastes — to establish and maintain the system. The particular mix and ways in which they do so are unique to their site and situation.

The site demonstrates how energy circuit thinking translates into practical design: passive solar mud-brick house for thermal storage, swales and dams for water storage, food forests for carbon and nutrient storage, and seed saving for genetic storage. Each element is positioned to maximise feedback between storages and sources.

## From EMERGY to Real Wealth

All biological and mineral resources can be considered as embodied energy. The tools, infrastructure, and technology needed to support human society derive from primary energy sources collected from the natural environment. The more useful and durable forms into which we transform those sources are stores of high-quality energy — or, in Holmgren's phrase, "real wealth."

This energetic view challenges conventional economic notions of value. Because of a major disconnection between biophysical sciences and social sciences, the energetic view has little impact on normal understandings of wealth. Understanding of these energy laws was fundamental to the development of the permaculture concept itself.
