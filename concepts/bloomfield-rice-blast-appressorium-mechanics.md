The rice blast fungus [[magnaporthe-grisea]] (also known as Magnaporthe
oryzae) employs one of the most extraordinary infection mechanisms in
all of biology. Its appressorium, a specialized infection structure,
generates pressures exceeding 80 atmospheres, making it the most
powerful fungal pathogen on the planet and capable of puncturing
Kevlar. Nicholas P. Money provides a detailed account of this
remarkable biological mechanism in "Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard." For related
[[fungal-ecology]] topics, see [[bloomfield-mycorrhizal-symbiosis-and-fungal-ecology]].

## The Infection Sequence

Trouble begins for the rice plant when a pear-shaped conidium drifts
onto its surface. Impact triggers the release of a sticky globule of
mucilage from one end of the spore that binds the fungus to the waxy
cuticle. If the leaf is wet with dew, the conidium germinates, and a
germ tube travels a short distance from the spore before adhering at
its tip and swelling into a bulbous structure called the appressorium.
This is an infection apparatus and one of the more remarkable
structures formed by fungi.

## Melanization and Pressurization

Within a few hours the appressorium becomes blackened with melanin,
fills itself with glycerol, becomes highly pressurized, and plunges
straight through the leaf surface. The mechanism of pressurization is
osmotic: water diffuses into the cytoplasm in response to the rising
glycerol content, and the melanin layer traps glycerol from diffusing
out of the cell. Most cell walls are quite leaky structures that allow
small molecules to pass freely in either direction, but a melanized wall
turns the cell into an impregnable container.

This melanin layer is critical for the rice blast fungus because it
must accumulate and retain tremendously high concentrations of glycerol
and other compounds that allow it to become pressurized. If the
formation of this layer is inhibited using a chemical fungicide that
targets the biochemical pathway used to manufacture melanin, the
appressorium will never penetrate the leaf. Similarly, the transparent
appressoria of albino mutants of Magnaporthe are ineffective at plant
penetration. Clearly, the change in color is linked to the mechanism
of invasion.

## Force Generation and Measurement

Pressure inside the cell has been measured using a number of different
methods. A group of German researchers allowed appressoria to indent a
metal sandwich filled with breast-implant gel, using a laser to measure
the precise depth of the indentation and calculating the force from the
force needed to indent the gel. This ingenious experiment revealed that
the force exerted against the rice leaf is considerably greater than
the pressure exercised by the toe of a 400-pound dancer engaged in a
brief pirouette.

The geneticist John Hamer compared the force exerted by the fungus to
the effect of a lighter woman wearing stiletto heels walking on
someone's back. The pressure inside an appressorium can exceed 80
atmospheres, and a back-of-the-envelope calculation shows this is
sufficient to penetrate bulletproof vest material, a discovery made by
Richard Howard at the DuPont Company.

## Penetration and Colonization

As the base of the appressorium begins to yield under the immense
internal pressure, a slender hypha is forced into the underlying leaf.
This penetration hypha enters the epidermal cells and begins to feed
on the plant's cytoplasm. The fungus usually infects the leaves of the
rice plant and spreads as an invasive mycelium, consuming the plant's
tissues and starving the developing grain.

Sometimes the neck or panicle of the rice plant is ravaged by the
fungus, becomes bleached by the sun, and withers to a dry straw.
Because the grains normally ripen on the panicle, crop loss due to
"neck blast" is particularly severe. Blast is most prevalent in areas
of intensive agriculture where the soil is supplemented with nitrogen
fertilizers; crops grown by subsistence farmers who rely upon natural
soil fertility are less likely to be devastated by blast. This connects to
broader discussions of [[coleman-deep-organic-farming]] and soil health.

## Genome and Genetics

Magnaporthe has been studied intensively by geneticists for many years.
It possesses seven chromosomes containing about three times more DNA
than saccharomyces-cerevisiae and 80 times less than a human. The
relationship between the amount of DNA in each nucleus and the
complexity of an organism is quite messy; many plants and amphibians
have much more DNA than humans.

The reason so much effort is being expended on sequencing the rice
blast genome is that there is a high probability of finding distinctive
Magnaporthe genes whose products have not been found in any other
organisms. Because Magnaporthe has been engaged as a cereal predator
for millions of years, we can anticipate genes that orchestrate the
intimate relationship between the fungus and its hosts: genes that
control the timing of conidium germination and melanin synthesis,
genes turned on only when the fungus penetrates the epidermis, and
others expressed when it begins to feed on the cytoplasm.

## O-Ring Glue Secretion

Once the appressorium has stuck itself to the leaf by secreting an
"O-ring" of glue, it deposits a veneer of pigment molecules against
the inner surface of its cell wall. Within a couple of hours, a
distinct melanin-rich layer has been added. The glue ensures that the
appressorium maintains firm contact with the leaf surface during the
pressurization process, preventing the structure from being pushed away
by the enormous internal forces it generates.

## Melanin as a Universal Fungal Tool

The role of melanin in Magnaporthe appressoria illustrates a broader
theme in [[biodiversity-of-fungi-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]]. Melanin is used by diverse fungi for multiple
purposes: protecting spores from ultraviolet radiation, detoxifying
reactive oxygen species produced by host defenses, and trapping
metabolites within cells to generate osmotic pressure. The rice blast
fungus has simply co-opted this ancient fungal pigment for its own
specialized purpose: becoming the most mechanically powerful pathogen
in the fungal kingdom. Similar melanin-dependent mechanisms may operate
in other fungal pathogens, suggesting that targeting melanin synthesis
could be a broadly effective antifungal strategy.

## Implications for Disease Control

Understanding the precise mechanism of appressorium function has
important implications for controlling rice blast disease. Fungicides
that target melanin biosynthesis are already used in some rice-growing
regions. The identification of genes specific to the infection process
may lead to new targets for chemical or biological control agents.
Additionally, understanding how the fungus generates such extreme
pressures could inspire engineering applications, as the fungal
mechanism accomplishes what human engineers can only achieve with
complex mechanical systems.
