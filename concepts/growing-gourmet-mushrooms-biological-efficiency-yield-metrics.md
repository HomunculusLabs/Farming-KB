Biological Efficiency (BE) is the foundational metric for evaluating and
comparing mushroom cultivation yields across different species, substrates, and
growing systems. Developed to provide a standardized basis for comparison, BE
allows growers to assess whether a particular strain or cultivation method is
performing optimally, regardless of the raw materials used. Paul Stamets treats
BE as the single most important number in commercial and amateur mushroom
cultivation alike.

## Defining Biological Efficiency

The formula for Biological Efficiency is straightforward:

    BE (%) = (weight of fresh mushrooms harvested / dry weight of substrate) × 100

A BE of 100% means that the total weight of fresh mushrooms harvested equals
the dry weight of the substrate on which they were grown. This benchmark is
considered excellent performance for most species. Since fresh mushrooms are
typically 85-92% water, a BE of 100% implies that the mushrooms have converted
roughly 8-15% of the substrate dry mass into actual fungal tissue, with the
remainder being water absorbed from the substrate and the growing environment.

The genius of this metric lies in its normalization across substrates. Whether
a grower is using wheat straw, hardwood sawdust, coffee grounds, or
agricultural waste, BE provides a common language for comparing results. A
grower achieving 120% BE on straw and 80% BE on sawdust can meaningfully
evaluate which substrate offers better returns per unit of dry material
invested.

## BE Benchmarks for Common Species

Different mushroom species have characteristic BE ranges reflecting their
inherent growth efficiency. Stamets provides benchmark data for the major
cultivated species:

- **Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.)**: 100–200% BE on pasteurized straw.
  Oyster mushrooms are among the most efficient cultivars, with aggressive
  strains sometimes exceeding 200% BE under optimal conditions on supplemented
  substrates. Their rapid colonization and broad enzymatic capability make them
  the standard for high-yield cultivation.

- **Shiitake (Lentinula edodes)**: 50–100% BE on [[grifola-frondosa]])**: 30–70% BE on supplemented sawdust. Maitake
  is slower to fruit and more sensitive to environmental conditions, but
  commands premium market prices that compensate for lower biological efficiency.

- **Reishi ([[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-hericium-erinaceus]])**: 50–100% BE on supplemented hardwood
  sawdust. This prized culinary and medicinal species performs well with proper
  supplementation and humidity management.

## Factors Affecting Biological Efficiency

BE is influenced by a complex interplay of variables. Stamets identifies
several critical factors that growers must optimize:

**Spawn rate**: Higher spawn rates (typically 5-10% by weight for grain spawn,
or 20-40% for sawdust spawn) generally increase BE by ensuring rapid
colonization that outcompetes contaminants. However, there are diminishing
returns, and excessive spawn rates become economically inefficient.

**Substrate moisture content**: The ideal moisture range for most substrates is
60-75% by weight. Substrates that are too dry slow mycelial growth. Substrates
that are too wet create anaerobic pockets that promote bacterial contamination.
Achieving proper moisture content requires careful measurement and often
moisture metering equipment.

**Supplementation**: Adding nitrogen-rich materials such as wheat bran, rice
bran, or soy meal to a base substrate can dramatically increase BE.
Supplementation provides amino acids and vitamins that accelerate mycelial
growth and support larger fruiting bodies. However, supplemented substrates
typically require full sterilization (121°C for 60-90 minutes) rather than
simple pasteurization, because added nutrients also accelerate competitor
organisms.
