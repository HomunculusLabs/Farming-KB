# Water Conservation in Sewage Systems

[[bill-mollison]] identified that up to 40% of domestic water use goes to toilet flushing alone, and advocated for practical alternatives ranging from handbasin-diversion toilets to dry [[composting-systems]], alongside a critical analysis of the chemical pollutants introduced through wastewater.

## Handbasin Flush Toilets

The simplest and most effective conservation measure is the handbasin flush toilet, where water from handwashing is diverted directly into the toilet cistern. No clean water need be used to flush toilets if this diversion is in place. In Australia, handbasins moulded into toilet flush tanks are commercially available.

It is essential to use low-flush toilet bowls with these systems. Standard bowls used with reduced water volumes flush incompletely, leading to a buildup of heavy pathogenic bacteria populations in the bowl and plumbing. When properly implemented, handbasin diversion solves approximately 40% of domestic water misuse and encourages hand cleanliness rather than the false cleanliness of toilet flushing for its own sake.

The holding tank can contain several flushes, and excess water drains to the toilet. This simple technology requires no special maintenance and can be retrofitted to existing plumbing in most cases.

## Dry Toilets

Dry (composting) toilets eliminate water use for sewage entirely. In Sweden, their use is compulsory in remote, unsewered, or environmentally unsuitable areas. By contrast, in the USA, UK, and Australia, homeowners often have to fight regulatory authorities to gain permission to install them, as municipal water and sewerage systems represent vested commercial interests that depend on continued water throughput and charges.

Dry toilets are specifically useful where:

- No methane digester system is used
- Sewage is not applied to plant production systems
- Local soils do not suit septic tank drainage
- Cities face critical water supply problems

However, dry toilets are not universally appropriate. On farms or in well-drained soils, they are unnecessary. Wherever sewage can be directed to anaerobic digestion for methane production, dry toilets actually reduce the potential utility of that sewage resource. Mollison drew an analogy: dry toilets reduce the potential uses of sewage just as compost is a reduction in the potential use of mulch — both processes narrow the range of applications.

## Greywater Chemistry and Risks

When wastewater from kitchens, bathrooms, and laundries is directed to gardens and soils, it is critical to understand the chemicals being released. A typical analysis of powdered detergent or soap (from Kevin Handreck, CSIRO Division of Soils, 1979) includes:

- **Surfactants**: Sodium or potassium salts, polysulphates
- **Builders**: Silicates, sulphates, bicarbonates, borates
- **Biocide residues**: Concentrated in animal fats — DDT, Dieldrin, Hexachlor from dairy cattle
- **Additives**: Resins (hardeners), scents, dyes, optical brighteners
- **Biological contaminants**: Faecal bacteria, viral and worm pathogens from showers and washing

Most of these substances can be processed by soil organisms, but several pose serious risks:

- If the basic water supply is already saline, sodium and potassium salts compound the problem, deflocculating soil clays and damaging leaves at concentrations exceeding 1,000 ppm
- Borates at >0.5 ppm create excessive boron concentrations in soils; above 1.0 ppm they become harmful to soil life and plants
- Residual biocides concentrate in animal fats and persist through wastewater pathways

The recommended approach is to use plain soaps on food crops where possible, and to route more complex pollutants to tree systems (woody perennials), which can cope better than garden vegetables and allow more time for the decomposition of long-term pollutants. In critical areas, especially arid or delicate environments, it may be necessary to create both special soaps (from unpolluted oils, potash, or sodium) and plant special crops that remove excesses (many aquatic plants are effective accumulators) before passing greywater onward to soils and streams.

## Problem Contaminants in Drinking Water

Mollison identified six major categories of contaminants most likely to affect drinking water supplies:

1. **Turbidity**: Silt and fine particles suspended in water, reducing light penetration and harbouring bacteria
2. **Bacterial and organic pollution**: From sewage and decay products — *E. coli*, disease organisms, viral and protozoan pathogens, parasitic worm eggs
3. **Metallic pollutants**: Chromium, cadmium, lead, and mercury from industrial discharge
4. **Biocides**: Organophosphates and halogenated hydrocarbons including Aldrin, Dieldrin, 2,4-D, 2,4,5-T, dioxin, and PCB
5. **Excessive fertiliser**: Nitrogenous compounds, phosphates, sodium and potassium salts causing eutrophication
6. **Acids and acid-forming compounds**: A pH below 5.5 increases the solubility and toxicity of metallic pollutants

## Contaminant Interactions

These contaminants interact in complex and often synergistic ways:

- **Acid rain** dissolves poisonous forms of aluminium, mercury, lead, cadmium, and selenium from rocks and soils. It also leaches copper, nickel, and lead from drinking tanks, tea urns, and hot water systems
- **Biological conversion**: Organisms can convert inorganic mercury to organic methylmercury forms (as occurred in the Minamata disaster in Japan), which are readily absorbed by the human body. Sewage in water aids this conversion to biologically active metal species
- **Biocide synergy**: Aldrin prevents DDT from being excreted by organisms — the combination is deadly. DDT is a stable residual poison that co-distills with water, meaning conventional distillation cannot remove it
- **Asbestos piping**: Many miles of asbestos cement pipe in public water supply systems pose a definite threat of both stomach and bladder cancer from asbestos particles in drinking water

## Aluminium Toxicity in Water Treatment

Ferric and aluminium sulphate, salt, and lime are commonly added to water supplies to cause fine particles to flocculate and settle out. However, this treatment itself introduces risks:

- As pH increases due to acid rain, aluminium goes into solution and may bind to proteins in vegetables and meat, especially those that are boiled or steamed
- Cooking can increase the water content of metals by a factor of 5 due to this protein binding, making bound metals easy to assimilate in the body
- Cooking acidic substances in aluminium pots worsens the problem significantly
- Aluminium from acidic rain leaching is now considered a major cause of tree death and lake acidification
- Even adding salt to cooking water to decrease these effects results in levels far exceeding the 30 μg/L allowable for those with kidney problems

Ferric sulphate may be safer to use, especially if the water is initially or reasonably alkaline. Mollison stressed that any inorganic or metallic salt deserves very cautious use in water treatment, and that these effects need far more study than they have received.

## The Technological Fix Problem

The fundamental issue is that 30-40 additives are commonly introduced into water supplies, many of which are themselves pollutants to the increasingly sensitive portion of the population developing allergies to modern contaminants. These additives represent the endpoint of what Mollison called the "technological fix" — pollution is addressed by introducing further pollution.

The only long-term insurance of good water supply to a settlement is rigorous control of a forested catchment, including a total ban on biocides and metallic processing. With few such clean areas remaining globally, house roof tanks may have to suffice for the foreseeable future. The duty of every settlement is to release only water of sufficiently good quality to be safely usable by downstream communities — for the wastewaters of upstream settlements are the drinking waters of downstream areas.
