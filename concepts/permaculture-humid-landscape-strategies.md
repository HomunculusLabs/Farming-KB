# Permaculture Humid Landscape Strategies (Design Course)

**Source:** Permaculture Design Course, Bill Mollison (1981, Pamphlet II)
**Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Overview

Pamphlet II covers permaculture strategies for humid landscapes, where water is abundant and the primary challenge is managing water flow, preventing erosion, and capturing nutrient runoff.

## Key Principles

### Water as the Primary Design Element
In humid landscapes, water is the principle design element. All water that leaves the property represents a loss of nutrient, soil, and potential. The designer's task is to slow, spread, and sink water movement across the landscape.

### Keyline Design
- Paul Yeomans' keyline concept is central to humid landscape management
- The keypoint is the point in a valley where the slope changes from convex to concave
- Keyline cultivation follows the natural contours just below the keypoint
- Plowing parallel to keyline (not to contour) naturally moves water from valleys to ridges
- This rehydrates ridges without requiring dams, pipes, or pumps
- Keyline dams sited above the keypoint provide gravity-fed irrigation to all land below

### Swale Systems
- Swales are level trenches on contour that intercept water runoff
- Water infiltrates along the entire length of the swale rather than concentrating
- Swales convert surface runoff to subsurface flow, recharging groundwater
- Trees planted below swales have permanent access to moisture
- Swales can be very small (hand-dug) to very large (bulldozer-scale)

### Dam and Pond Siting
- Dams in humid landscapes fill quickly — choose sites carefully
- Multiple small dams are more useful and stable than one large dam
- Site dams to cascade — overflow from one feeds the next
- Dam walls should be compacted earth, never concrete (cracks)
- Use geese in dam systems — they fertilize water and control vegetation

## Forest Systems in Humid Landscapes

### The Role of Forests
- Forests are the highest expression of humid landscape design
- They maximize water capture, storage, and slow release
- Forest floor acts as a giant sponge — absorbing rainfall and releasing it slowly
- Even 1% increase in forest cover significantly affects stream flow

### Edge Effects
- Forest edges are the most productive zones (highest diversity, most light)
- Design for maximum edge length in food forests
- Spiral, crenellated, and lobate edge patterns increase productivity

## Stream and Valley Management

- Never clear-fell valley bottoms — maintain riparian vegetation
- Streams should be heavily vegetated on both banks
- Use leaky weirs (not solid dams) in streams to slow water without blocking fish passage
- Natural sequence: upper slope (forest) → mid slope (mixed tree crops) → lower slope (garden, pasture)
