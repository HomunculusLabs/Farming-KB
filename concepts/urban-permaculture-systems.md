# Urban Permaculture Systems

Urban permaculture transforms cities from wasteful consumers into productive food-generating ecosystems. By reclaiming vacant land, recycling organic waste, retrofitting buildings for energy efficiency, and using every available surface for growing food, urban permaculture demonstrates that high-density settlement and food production are not only compatible but mutually reinforcing.

## Community Gardens and Urban Farms

### Green Guerillas (The Bronx, New York)
The Green Guerillas organization transforms vacant land in urban wastelands into productive, beautiful, and recreational spaces as city farms. This grassroots approach demonstrates that community action can reclaim degraded urban landscapes and create both food security and social cohesion in neighborhoods experiencing urban decay.

### Schreber Gardens (West Germany)
The productive Schreber gardens adjoin almost every settlement in Germany, providing food and much-needed recreation in cities. People are permitted to spend weekends on these mini-farms. This model represents a systematic approach to greening cities through small-scale, distributed food production integrated into residential areas.

### Glide Farm (South Bronx, New York)
This urban farm provides 8% of New York's herbs while providing employment for many people in an area of urban decay. Glide Farm demonstrates that urban agriculture can operate at meaningful commercial scale, contributing significantly to a city's food supply while creating economic opportunities in disadvantaged neighborhoods.

## Urban Waste Recycling

### Composting Operations
The Bronx Frontier group operates a small composting business that represents the only example of productive use of New York's food waste at the time documented. Small mountains of compost are sold off-site to create meadows over rubble-filled wastelands, transforming urban waste streams into productive soil.

This model of urban waste cycling addresses multiple problems simultaneously:
- **Waste reduction:** Diverts organic material from landfills
- **Soil creation:** Converts waste into growing medium for urban agriculture
- **Land restoration:** Enables revegetation of contaminated or degraded urban sites
- **Economic activity:** Creates green jobs and sellable products

## Building Retrofits and Energy Conservation

### Project WARM (Louisville, Kentucky)
Project WARM volunteers retrofit and insulate old folks' homes by the thousands, sharply reducing energy costs and deaths from cold. The cost amortizes in just two winters. This approach demonstrates that:

- Energy conservation is always cheaper than energy generation
- Modest investments in building efficiency can eliminate the need for dangerous or dirty power-generating systems
- Community volunteer efforts can address systemic infrastructure problems at scale
- Such retrofits make many proposed power systems (including nuclear power) obsolete

## Buildings as Agricultural Space

### Wall and Vertical Growing Systems
West- or sun-facing walls in cities and towns provide radiant heat for marginal crops. Espaliered fruit trees can grow well on urban building walls, "softening" hard architectural surfaces while producing food. This principle extends the concept of urban agriculture:

- Most urban buildings can be regarded as additional agricultural space
- Trellis systems over alleys, shopping complexes, and building walls create vertical growing surfaces
- The thermal mass of buildings creates favorable microclimates for heat-loving crops
- Wall-mounted fruit production requires no additional land area

## Design Principles for Urban Permaculture

### Maximize Edge and Surface Area
Urban environments offer extraordinary edge effects—the interfaces between buildings, pavement, and soil create diverse microclimates. Permaculture design in cities exploits these edges:

- **Vertical surfaces:** Walls, fences, and trellises multiply growing area
- **Rooftops:** Flat and sloped roofs can support turf, herbs, and even food crops
- **Balconies and window boxes:** Extend growing space into the building envelope
- **Street trees:** Provide both food and microclimate modification

### Close the Waste Loop
Urban areas concentrate both waste and demand. Permaculture systems close the loop between these:

- Food waste → compost → soil → food
- Grey water → irrigation → food production
- Building heat → protected growing space → extended seasons
- Organic materials → mulch → soil building → increased yields

### Community Integration
Urban permaculture succeeds when it integrates with community needs:

- **Food security:** Production of fresh, local food in food deserts
- **Economic opportunity:** Jobs in farming, composting, and retrofits
- **Social cohesion:** Community gardens as gathering and recreational spaces
- **Education:** Living demonstrations of sustainable food production
- **Health:** Access to fresh produce and physical activity through gardening

### Multi-Function Design
Every element in an urban permaculture system should serve multiple functions:

- **Compost:** Waste management, soil creation, heat generation, economic product
- **Trees:** Food production, shade, air quality improvement, stormwater management
- **Walls:** Structure, thermal mass, vertical growing surface, wind protection
- **Rooftops:** Shelter, water collection, growing space, insulation

## The Urban-Rural Connection

Urban permaculture does not exist in isolation from broader food systems. Urban farms and gardens complement rural production by:

- Supplying perishable herbs and vegetables that are best consumed fresh and local
- Processing and adding value to rural products
- Recycling urban organic waste back into agricultural productivity
- Reducing the energy and environmental costs of food transportation
- Maintaining food production knowledge and skills within urban populations

## Challenges and Opportunities

Urban permaculture faces unique challenges including contaminated soils, limited space, regulatory barriers, and air pollution. However, these challenges also create opportunities:

- Contaminated sites can be remediated through composting and phytoremediation
- Limited space drives innovation in intensive growing methods
- Regulatory barriers can be addressed through community advocacy and demonstration
- Air pollution effects can be mitigated by the very plants grown in urban systems

The fundamental insight of urban permaculture is that cities are not inherently wasteful or unsustainable—they become so through design choices that can be reversed. By applying permaculture principles to urban environments, cities can become net producers of food, clean water, and biological resources while reducing their ecological footprint.
