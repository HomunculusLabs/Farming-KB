# Comparative Photoreception in Plants and Animals

Plants and animals have independently evolved sophisticated light-sensing
systems that, while differing in their anatomical organization, share
remarkable similarities at the molecular level. Both kingdoms use
light-absorbing proteins coupled to signalling cascades that convert
photonic energy into biochemical information. Understanding these parallels
reveals deep evolutionary convergence in how [[savory-living-organisms-as-tools]] perceive and
respond to their light environment, and challenges the anthropocentric
assumption that vision is uniquely the domain of animals.

## The Human Visual System as Baseline

Human vision relies on photoreceptor cells in the retina, which contains
approximately 125 million rods and 6 million cones in an area the size of
a passport photo. Rods are sensitive to all visible wavelengths and enable
low-light vision without colour discrimination. Cones come in three types
responding preferentially to red, green, and [[chamovitz-julius-von-sachs-blue-light-phototropism]], enabling colour
vision. Each photoreceptor contains a light-absorbing chemical: rhodopsin
in rods and photopsins in cones. These chemicals have specific molecular
structures tuned to absorb particular wavelengths. Blue light at
approximately 450nm is the shortest visible wavelength, while [[phytochrome-red-far-red-light-switch]] at
approximately 700nm is the longest. Once absorbed, light energy triggers a
conformational change in the photoreceptor protein that initiates a
neural signal processed by the brain into a coherent visual image.

## Plant Photoreceptor Families

Plants lack eyes, brains, and neural tissue, yet they possess multiple
families of photoreceptor proteins that collectively provide a
comprehensive picture of their light environment. The [[ascomycota-phylogeny-major-classes-lineages]] include
phytochromes, which detect red and far-red light and regulate flowering,
[[cannabis-seed-germination-methods]], and shade avoidance; phototropins, which detect blue
light and mediate phototropic bending, chloroplast movement, and stomatal
opening; cryptochromes, which detect blue and UV-A light and regulate
circadian rhythms and de-etiolation; and UV-B receptors (UVR8), which
detect ultraviolet-B radiation and activate protective responses. Each
photoreceptor family uses a different chromophore molecule to absorb light,
analogous to how human rods and cones use different opsins for different
colours.

## Convergent Molecular Architecture

Despite the evolutionary distance between plants and animals, their
photoreceptor proteins share fundamental architectural principles. Both use
a chromophore molecule covalently or non-covalently bound to a protein
apoprotein. Light absorption by the chromophore causes an isomerization or
conformational change that alters the protein's interaction with downstream
signalling partners. In animals, photoactivated rhodopsin activates a G
protein (transducin) that triggers a phosphodiesterase cascade altering
cGMP levels and ultimately closing ion channels. In plants, photoactivated
phytochrome undergoes a Pr-to-Pfr conformational change that enables
nuclear translocation and transcription factor regulation. While the
specific molecular components differ, the general logic of chromophore
absorption leading to protein conformational change leading to cellular
response is conserved across kingdoms.

## From Rhodopsin to Phytochrome

The evolution of photoreceptors traces back to an ancient common ancestor.
Photolyase enzymes, which repair UV-damaged DNA using blue light energy,
are considered the evolutionary precursors of both cryptochromes and
phototropins in plants. Phytochromes appear to have evolved from bacterial
phytochrome-like proteins. Rhodopsins, the visual pigments of animals,
share structural homology with microbial rhodopsins found in bacteria and
archaea. This deep evolutionary history means that the fundamental
capacity to detect light predates the divergence of plants and animals by
hundreds of millions of years. The diversity of photoreceptor systems in
modern organisms represents divergent elaboration upon ancient molecular
themes.
