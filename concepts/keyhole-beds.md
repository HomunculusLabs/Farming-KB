# Keyhole Beds

Keyhole beds are a space-saving garden layout that minimizes the area
sacrificed to paths while maximizing plantable growing space. Named for
the small, keyhole-shaped path that provides access to the center of a
horseshoe-shaped bed, keyhole beds are a staple of permaculture Zone 1
and Zone 2 garden design.

## The Problem with Paths

In a conventional garden with single rows of plants and paths between
each row, paths consume roughly half of the soil area. Raised beds
improve this ratio, sacrificing only about 30 percent to walkways. But
by bending a rectangular raised bed into a horseshoe or circle with a
narrow path to the center, the path shrinks dramatically. Less than a
quarter of the ground is surrendered to paths in a keyhole bed, compared
to half in a traditional row garden.

## Construction

To create a keyhole bed, begin with a circle of soil about 8 to 12 feet
in diameter, pierced on one side by a path to the center. Keyhole beds
can be created by shoveling fertile topsoil into a horseshoe shape, or
by layering up lasagna-style with leaves, compostable organic matter,
newspaper, and soil using the [[sheet-mulching]] technique.

The plantable zone is about 3 to 5 feet across, similar to a standard
raised bed. The access path into the bed can be narrow, about a foot
wide, but the central circle of path needs to be large enough to turn
around in, about 18 to 24 inches in diameter.

## Zone-Based Planting

You can plant a keyhole bed using the [[zone-system]] concept. Put the
most frequently picked plants closest to the center: herbs, greens, and
other daily-eaten vegetables should border the central path. Behind
these, place plants picked regularly over the growing season: tomatoes,
peppers, bush peas and beans, eggplants. At the back of the bed, install
long-term crops and those harvested only once, such as potatoes and
carrots, plus single-harvest vegetables like cauliflower and cabbage.

For back-row plants that may be out of reach, stepping briefly onto the
bed once per season is acceptable. Alternatively, lay down a board to
stand on, which limits soil compaction.

## Creative Possibilities

A whole circle could be dedicated to tomatoes with companion culinary
herbs such as basil or chives at the inner margin. The circular geometry
can balance sun and shadow: place shade-tender crops east of taller
sun-lovers. Tall sturdy crops such as artichokes or sunflowers on the
windward side serve as windbreaks. If the central path faces south with
tall plants at the northern edge, the bed creates a U-shaped sun bowl
that traps warmth, ideal for tender or heat-loving varieties.

## Margins and Corners

The triangular corners between a round bed and a square yard are not
wasted space. They are ideal for insect-attracting flowers, perennial
[[nitrogen-fixing-plants]] such as Dutch clover, wind-and-weed barriers,
robust mulch-providers such as crotolaria or comfrey, or small fruit
trees. The bed can also be expanded to fill the corners, since a
keyhole bed can be square rather than round. It is the central path
that defines it.

## Multiple Keyhole Beds

Several keyhole beds can extend to the left and right of a central
walkway. An undulating path flanked by keyhole beds can wrap around a
house to make an attractive Zone 1 garden.

## Mandala Gardens

A mandala garden is a set of four to eight keyhole beds arranged in a
circle with one more bed in the center, and a path entering from one
side. A mandala garden combines beauty and efficiency to create a
magical effect. Few designs can fit more growing space into less area,
and the pattern brings a spiritual quality to a piece of ground.

## Herb Spirals

An herb spiral is a further evolution of pattern-based garden design. It
coils 20 to 30 linear feet of pathside herbs into a roundish spiral
about 5 feet across, built as a mound of soil about 3 feet high with
rocks placed in a spiral pattern from bottom to top, leaving about a
foot of soil between tiers.

The spiral creates multiple [[gaias-garden-microclimates]]: the south-facing slope is
hotter, the east side dries earlier, the bottom stays wetter, and the
top is driest. Heat-loving herbs like oregano, rosemary, and thyme go on
the sunny south side near the top. Cool-loving parsley and chives find a
home on the north side. Coriander, which bolts in hot afternoon sun, can
be stationed on the east side.

Building tips include placing rocks or subsoil at the base to save
topsoil, running irrigation tubing inside the mound, and sinking a small
pond at the bottom for watercress and other edible aquatics.

## Natural Patterns in Garden Design

Keyhole beds, mandala gardens, and herb spirals all demonstrate how
nature's patterns can be applied to garden design to create synergistic
effects. Nature never takes the shortest distance between two points.
Instead, nature meanders, drifting in lazy undulations. Bringing curves
into a garden eliminates the monotonous quality of ruler-straight beds
and rows while being more efficient and space-conserving.

The spiral and its relative the helix (a spiral stretched into three
dimensions, like a corkscrew) are particularly abundant in nature: snail
shells, sunflower seed patterns, ram's horns, hurricanes, and galaxies
all form spirals. The pattern of leaves or branches extending from a
stem often unwinds in a helix, minimizing shade cast by each leaf on the
one below. Spirals are often the result of growth or expansion.

Branching patterns are also useful for gardeners. A branching path
system, modeled on leaf veins, uses a large central path for carts and
wheelbarrows, smaller paths branching off for frequent foot traffic, and
tiny tracks from those into each bed. This saves space and creates a
natural flow pattern while reaching all points in a large area with the
shortest total path distance.

Clever use of natural patterns in garden design often generates
delightful synergistic effects: unexpected benefits that neither partner
alone can offer. The herb spiral, for instance, saves space AND creates
multiple microclimates AND provides easy access AND forms an attractive
focal point, all from a single design decision.
