# Brazilian Ayahuasca Churches

Ralph Metzner identified three organized religious churches in Brazil that use
[[ayahuasca-tourism-peru-retreat-landscape]] as their principal sacrament: Santo Daime, Uniao do Vegetal
(UDV), and Barquinha. Each was founded in the 1950s by rubber tappers working
in the Amazon region who came into contact with the hallucinogenic vine
through mestizo or Indian ayahuasceros. The founders of each church reported
significant visions that instructed them to organize a church using the brew
as the central sacrament. These churches represent a distinct paradigm in
Metzner's four-part framework of entheogenic practice, bridging indigenous
[[shamanism]] and organized religion.

## Historical Origins

All three churches emerged from the same cultural matrix of Amazonian rubber
tapping in the mid-twentieth century. Rubber tappers, predominantly of mixed
European, African, and indigenous ancestry, spent long periods in remote
forest areas where they encountered indigenous and mestizo healers working
with ayahuasca. The foundational visions that led to each church's
establishment followed a similar pattern: a profound entheogenic experience in
which the drinker received explicit instructions to create an organized form
of worship centered on the [[ayahuasca-tourism-peru-retreat-landscape]] brew.

The 1950s founding period means these churches predate the major wave of
psychedelic exploration in the West by roughly a decade. They developed their
ceremonial forms, hymns, and organizational structures independently, though
all draw on the same Amazonian ayahuasca tradition as their source material.
The churches represent a uniquely Brazilian synthesis of indigenous Amazonian
practices, Afro-Brazilian spiritual traditions, and elements of popular
Catholicism.

## The Three Churches

### Santo Daime

Santo Daime was founded by Raimundo Irineu Serra in the Brazilian state of
Acre. The church's ceremonies, called trabalhos (works), involve the ritual
consumption of ayahuasca (called daime within the church) while participants
sing collected hymns received through [[entheogenic-ceremony]] visions. The ceremonies
are highly structured, with specific sequences of hymns, dances, and prayers
that can last for many hours. Santo Daime has expanded significantly beyond
Brazil, establishing centers in North America, Europe, and other parts of the
world. The hymnal itself is considered a living document, as new hymns
continue to be received by practitioners during entheogenic ceremonies and
added to the collection.

### Uniao do Vegetal (UDV)

The Centro Espirita Beneficente Uniao do Vegetal was founded by Jose Gabriel
da Costa. UDV ceremonies, called sessoes (sessions), are typically conducted
in a seated circle with participants drinking ayahuasca while engaging in
structured periods of concentration, reflection, and discussion. Unlike Santo
Daime, UDV sessions involve verbal sharing of experiences and insights after
the entheogenic phase. The UDV places particular emphasis on mental discipline
and the pursuit of self-knowledge through the entheogenic experience. The
church has developed a sophisticated organizational hierarchy and has been at
the center of important legal battles establishing religious freedom
protections for entheogenic practice.

### Barquinha

Barquinha is the smallest and least internationally known of the three
churches. Its ceremonies incorporate more elements of Afro-Brazilian spiritual
traditions, including spirit possession and mediumship, alongside the
ayahuasca ritual. The name Barquinha (little boat) references the spiritual
vessel that carries participants through their visionary experiences.
Barquinha ceremonies are often described as the most syncretic of the three
churches, drawing most heavily on the African spiritual traditions that
arrived in Brazil through the transatlantic slave trade.

## Legal Framework in Brazil

All three churches are officially recognized by the Brazilian government, and
the religious use of [[ayahuasca-tourism-peru-retreat-landscape]] is legal within their institutional
framework. This legal protection was established after extensive governmental
review, including studies by Brazilian medical and scientific institutions
that confirmed the safety and social benefits of controlled ceremonial
ayahuasca use. The Brazilian legal precedent is significant because it
represents one of the few jurisdictions worldwide where an otherwise
controlled psychoactive substance is legally protected for religious use.

The legal recognition process involved multidisciplinary investigation by a
federal commission that examined the pharmacological, psychological, and
social dimensions of ayahuasca use within the churches. The commission's
findings supported the conclusion that ceremonial ayahuasca use, when
conducted within the structured context of these religious institutions, posed
no significant public health risk and provided measurable social benefits to
participants and their communities.

## Community and Social Functions

The ayahuasca churches serve several important social functions that Metzner
identified as distinct from both indigenous [[shamanism]] and Western
[[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]]:

### Community Cohesion and Protection

The organized church structure provides members with social cohesion and a
degree of legal and social protection. As recognized religious institutions,
they offer a shield against both the encroachments of Christian missionaries
and the pressures of Western consumer culture. This protective function
parallels the role that indigenous ceremonial traditions play in maintaining
cultural identity, but within an urban, modernizing Brazilian context. The
churches provide a sense of belonging and participation that counteracts the
social atomization of modern urban life.

### Reduction of Substance Abuse

Participation in the ayahuasca churches tends to reduce the incidence of
alcoholism and drug addiction among members. This has been documented in both
the Native American Church in the United States and the Brazilian
ayahuasca churches. The mechanism appears to involve both the direct
pharmacological effects of the ayahuasca experience, which often produces a
profound reorientation of values and priorities, and the community support
structure that provides social accountability and meaningful participation.
Members who have struggled with alcohol or drug dependence frequently report
that their entheogenic experiences provided the insight and motivation needed
to change their relationship with intoxicants.
