# Preface to the English Edition: The Road Back to Nature

Fukuoka opens the English edition of "The Road Back to
Nature" by clarifying what he means by "returning to
nature." To him, this represents the effort to reunify God,
nature, and man -- which have been split apart by mankind.
He warns that man cannot succeed in returning to nature
unless he knows what true nature and God are. See also the [[fukuoka-road-japanese-preface]].

## The Soul of Nature Is Not Found by Dissection

Some people argue that nature ravaged by man is still
nature, that wasted desert lands left in the wake of human
civilization are nature nonetheless. But Fukuoka insists
that however far man may wander through such fields and
mountains, he can only gaze upon nature's outer shell; he
will never access its true inner heart and soul.

The soul of nature is also the will of God who dwells in
nature. This is not something that scientists can find by
dissecting nature. Scientists are incapable even of knowing
that they are in no position to understand the soul of a
flower in the meadow.

Geneticists, believing they explore the root of life,
extract and synthesize genes present in living cells. But
nature's soul does not lie hidden within DNA. Nor is this
where God resides.

## The Limits of Physics and Quantum Theory

Fukuoka notes that physicists claim the Eastern concept of
nothingness (Mu) appears close to resolution within quantum
theory, and that astronauts floating in space sense God's
presence in a weightless state. But God and nature lie
beyond the grasp of the human intellect. No matter how
much man dissects nature or denies the existence of human
intelligence and calls everything "Mu," he only moves
further from both nature and God.

## The Rampage of Science

The fragmented and diffusive development of knowledge,
expanding outward without aim or direction, has brought
human thought to the extremes of confusion, recklessly
splitting apart God, nature, and man -- originally one
indivisible -- and leaving only a legacy of incoherent
chaos. Science has gone on a wild rampage, and the global
disruption this has caused is only now becoming clear.

Fukuoka frankly states that there no longer seems any hope
that man will succeed in returning to nature and reuniting
with God.

## A Personal Confession

Fukuoka reveals that fifty years earlier, he received a
revelation of God for one moment. He was so taken aback by
the vision that he failed to advance along the road he
should have. Instead, he turned his back on God and tried
to follow quietly the path of a solitary farmer. In time,
he came to call the road he had passed over "the road back
to nature" and, growing arrogant, professed to practice [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-impact]].

He acknowledges that all he really did was to haphazardly
conceive of a form closest to the image of nature and grow
crops in accordance with that form. He felt the purpose
should always be the revival of nature and the
manifestation of God hidden deep within nature, but there
was little hope that he, having once ignored the will of
God, should find a proper way.

## The Orchard as Paradise

Even so, Fukuoka expresses gratitude that when spring
arrives at his farm, for several days the cherry, plum,
peach, and pear trees in his orchard, and the semi-wild
vegetables growing beneath them, all break out into bloom.
Visitors call this an Eden, a paradise on Earth. But as
soon as they finish clicking their camera shutters, they
hurry off home. Even though they call this place beautiful,
people no longer have the time or ease of mind to immerse
themselves in such sensibility. They find greater
attraction in nature as an object for self-expression.

## Reason Versus Understanding

Fukuoka identifies the human intellect and reason as the
greatest enemies responsible for man's loss of his native
aesthetic sense. Reason and understanding are mutually
antagonistic. The intellect attempts to open up nature but
succeeds only in closing it down because human knowledge
is nothing more than a cumulation of judgment by the human
intellect. See also [[fukuoka-european-civilization-standstill]] and [[fukuoka-food-civilization-critique]].

The flower perceived innocently is itself divine nature,
but when examined with the intellect, this is transformed
into the cold flower of reason, the heart of which nature
shuts out.

## God Came of His Own Choosing

Fukuoka offers a crucial theological insight: God did not
create heaven, earth, and the cosmos. Rather, when the
Earth was born and the meadow flowers bloomed, the
butterflies fluttered about, and the birds sang, God came
of his own choosing to dwell there.

Instead of praying to God as a mighty power reigning over
the heavens, man should have frolicked innocently with this
wonderful sprite, this angel inhabiting the fields. That
was the shortest road back to nature and at once the Great
Way back to the side of God.

The preface closes with a poem Fukuoka wrote in April
1986:

  Look how beautiful, the flowers of the earth!
  This is the land where live the gods;
  A perfect, faultless, natural paradise.

  Now in the deep slumber of spring in my Eden,
  I dream a private dream of returning to nature.
  Here there is nothing that must be done;
  No effort is required, not even courage.

  But no one even bothers to look back.
  Will the road to nature fade again
  Into the mists?
