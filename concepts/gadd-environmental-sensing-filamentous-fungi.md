## Chemical Sensing and Chemotropism

Chemical sensing is perhaps the most important environmental detection system
in filamentous fungi. Hyphal tips grow toward nutrient sources (positive
chemotropism) and away from toxic compounds (negative chemotropism) by
detecting concentration gradients of specific molecules. The hyphal tip
contains a structure called the Spitzenkorper, which acts as a vesicle supply
center that directs growth toward favorable conditions. When a nutrient
gradient is detected, the Spitzenkorper shifts position, redirecting hyphal
extension toward the source. This mechanism allows fungi to efficiently
explore their environment and locate resources even at considerable distances
from the colony center.
## Light Sensing and Photoreception

Many filamentous fungi can sense light quality, intensity, and direction, and
modify their growth and reproductive behavior accordingly. Fungi use blue
light receptors (similar to the cryptochrome and phototropin families found in
plants), red light receptors (phytochrome-like proteins), and in some species
opsins for light detection. Light sensing influences diverse fungal behaviors
including the direction of hyphal growth (phototropism), the timing and
morphology of reproductive structure formation, pigment production, circadian
rhythms, and the regulation of metabolic pathways. The bread mold Neurospora
crassa has been a [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]], driving the infection peg
through the plant cuticle. This mechanosensing capability is essential for
successful infection.
