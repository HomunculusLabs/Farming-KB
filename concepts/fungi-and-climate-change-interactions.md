# Fungi and Climate Change Interactions

Climate change — driven primarily by rising atmospheric CO₂ concentrations and associated temperature increases — has profound and complex effects on fungal communities and their ecosystem functions. Fungi mediate critical responses to climate change through their roles in decomposition, nutrient cycling, mycorrhizal symbiosis, and soil carbon storage.

## Effects on Primary Production and Mycorrhizal Dynamics

### The Dighton and Jansen Model

Dighton and Jansen (1991) proposed an influential model predicting how climate change would affect ectomycorrhizal plant communities through three interacting scenarios:

**Scenario A — Elevated CO₂ alone:**
- Enhanced photosynthetic activity provides a larger carbohydrate pool
- Greater carbon supply to roots supports increased mycorrhizal development, fruiting, community diversity, and extraradical hyphal soil exploitation
- Higher C:N ratio in plant litter increases the importance of mycorrhizal fungi as saprotrophs for nutrient acquisition from organic sources
- Mycorrhizal diversity is maintained, favoring enzyme-producing species capable of accessing organic nutrients

**Scenario B — Elevated temperature effect:**
- Increased saprotrophic microbial activity accelerates plant litter decomposition
- Enhanced nutrient mineralization increases available nutrient pools
- This "fertilizer effect" reduces plant dependence on mycorrhizae
- Leads to decline in mycorrhizal diversity and extraradical hyphal exploration

**Scenario C — Combined CO₂ and temperature with altered litter chemistry:**
- Enhanced nitrogen content in leaf litter improves resource quality
- Mycorrhizal fungi must compete more intensely with the saprotrophic community
- Forces mycorrhizal fungi to increase diversity and extraradical hyphal exploitation

### Predicted Geographic Shifts

Climate change will alter the geographical distribution of plant species along with their associated mycorrhizal fungi. Most increases in net primary productivity are predicted to occur at higher latitudes, distant from the Equator. Cool-adapted plants are expected to show the greatest increases in active carbon pool size and translocation rates. This means:

- **Ectomycorrhizal and ericoid mycorrhizal communities** (dominant in boreal and temperate regions) will be more affected than **arbuscular mycorrhizal communities** (dominant in tropical and grassland systems)
- As plants colonize new habitats or retreat from formerly optimal ones, the role of **fungal pathogens** in reducing populations and individual fitness may increase
- The role of **mycorrhizal fungi** in preventing pathogen attack and accessing scarce nutrients will likely increase in importance

## Below-Ground Responses to Elevated CO₂

### Inconsistency in Soil Organism Response

The effect of enriched atmospheric CO₂ on soil organisms is notably inconsistent across studies:

- Kandeler et al. (1998) and Bardgett et al. (1999) found that elevated CO₂ and temperature increased microbial carbon but decreased the metabolic quotient when temperature alone was raised
- Zak et al. (2000) reviewed 47 publications on soil C and N cycling under elevated CO₂, finding coefficients of variation between 80–800% among responses
- Despite variability, two general patterns emerged: (1) greater plant growth with more carbon entering below-ground systems, and (2) greater metabolic activity of soil microbial communities

### Root and Mycorrhizal Responses

Numerous researchers have documented responses of roots and mycorrhizae to elevated CO₂, though no consistent universal pattern exists:

- Dhillion et al. (1996) showed that a Mediterranean *Bromus madritensis* monoculture responded to doubled CO₂ (350→700 μmol mol⁻¹) with:
  - 31% increase in root biomass
  - 88% increase in root length
  - 42% increase in soil microbial biomass
  - 20% increase in soil fungal hyphal length
  - 57% increase in total arbuscular mycorrhizal root colonization
- These fungal biomass increases were reflected in elevated fungal-feeding nematode populations, suggesting enhanced energy transfer through fungal-based food webs at elevated CO₂ (Hungate et al., 2000)

## Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling

### Leaf Chemistry Changes

Gifford et al. (2000) reviewed effects of elevated CO₂ on leaf chemistry and found mixed results with little consensus, though a general trend toward ~15% increase in C:N ratio under doubled CO₂. Specific findings include:

- De Angelis et al. (2000) showed elevated CO₂ increased C:N and lignin:N ratios in oak leaf litter in a Mediterranean forest, reducing decomposition rates by 5–8% in field experiments and 12.5% in microcosms
- Cotrufo et al. (1998) showed nitrogen content reduction in elevated-CO₂ leaves ranged from 50% in sweet chestnut (*Castanea sativa*) to 19% for sycamore (*Acer platanoides*)
- Reduced leaf litter quality stimulated grazing by isopods *Oniscus asellus* and *Porcellio scaber* (Hättenschwiler et al., 1999)

### Litter Quality and Saprotrophic Activity

The overall conclusion from decomposition studies is that reduced leaf litter resource quality in elevated CO₂ environments diminishes saprotrophic community activity, including fungal decomposition. However, the relationship is not straightforward:

- Significant increases in C:N (45.6→72.7) and lignin:N (16.3→22.4) ratios in beech twigs grown under elevated CO₂ did **not** change decomposition rates or nitrogen/lignin dynamics (Cotrufo and Ineson, 2000)
- Gorisen and Cotrufo (2000) cautioned that changes in leaf chemistry may not correlate with decomposition rates, as additionally available carbon in elevated CO₂ conditions is preferentially translocated to roots (26% contribution) rather than remaining in leaves (6%)

### Below-Ground Carbon Contribution

Gorisen and Cotrufo's (2000) decomposition study over 222 days revealed that plant root material is potentially more important than leaf litter for below-ground carbon storage. This finding has significant implications for understanding how elevated CO₂ may alter soil carbon dynamics through the mycorrhizal pathway.

## Mycorrhizal Carbon Drain and Plant Responses

### Carbohydrate Investment

Mycorrhizal symbiosis represents a significant carbon drain from plant shoots:

- Douds et al. (1988) showed 5–6× greater carbon translocation to mycorrhizal than nonmycorrhizal root systems in Carrizo citrange seedlings
- Wang et al. (1989) demonstrated that mycorrhizal roots in split-pot experiments caused:
  - 20% increase in carbon exchange rate in *Panicum* plants
  - 45% increase in photosynthate storage in leaves
  - More than doubled sink activity (movement to roots)

### Species-Specific Mycorrhizal Responses

The response to elevated CO₂ varies dramatically among mycorrhizal fungal species:

- **Laccaria laccata** (nitrogen-rich environments): Additional carbohydrate translocated to roots was largely **respired**
- **Suillus bovinus** (nitrogen-poor environments): Extra carbon was incorporated into root and fungal **biomass**, with plant N contents reaching twice the level of Laccaria-colonized trees

These results support the concept that carbon use for nitrogen acquisition is more critical in mycorrhizal symbioses characteristic of N-limiting ecosystems.

### Ectomycorrhizal Community Shifts

Rygiewicz et al. (2000) found that Douglas fir seedlings in low-nitrogen soil showed:
- Increased total number of mycorrhizae under elevated CO₂
- Increased ectomycorrhizal **diversity** resulting from elevated temperature, with *Rhizopogon* and *Cenococcum* morphotypes dominating

### Enhanced Growth with Mycorrhizae under Elevated CO₂

O'Neill et al. (1987) demonstrated that seedling oak and pine trees produced 66% (pine) and 56% (oak) greater biomass due to increased mycorrhizal colonization under double-ambient CO₂, suggesting that enhanced photosynthesis more than offsets the carbon drain imposed by mycorrhizal colonization.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Hyphal Responses

- Sanders et al. (1998) found a **five-fold increase** in arbuscular mycorrhizal extraradical mycelium under elevated CO₂
- Rillig et al. (2000) showed that mycorrhizal root colonization and soil hyphal length increased linearly (r² = 0.47 and r² = 0.76, respectively) along a natural CO₂ gradient near cold CO₂ springs in New Zealand
- Staddon et al. (1999), however, found that elevated CO₂ had similar growth-promoting effects on both mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal *Plantago lanceolata*, with no effect on carbon partitioning between roots and shoots

## Feedback Loops

Fitter et al. (2000) proposed a positive feedback loop: increased plant carbon fixation → enhanced mycorrhizal growth → improved nutrient (P for AM, N for ECM) capture → alleviation of nutritional stress → further increased plant growth. However, high turnover of extraradical hyphae would increase soil respiration, potentially counteracting enhanced C fixation. The net effect likely depends on the balance between mycorrhizal fungal species composition and their turnover rates.
