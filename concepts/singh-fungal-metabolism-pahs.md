# Fungal Metabolism of Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (Singh)

## PAHs in the Environment

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are ubiquitous environmental contaminants formed during incomplete combustion of organic materials. Major sources include:
- Fossil fuel combustion (vehicles, power plants)
- Industrial processes (steel, coke, aluminum production)
- Wood burning and forest fires
- Waste incineration
- Oil spills and petroleum contamination
- Agricultural burning

PAHs are classified by ring number:
- **2-3 rings** (naphthalene, anthracene, phenanthrene, fluorene): More soluble, more readily degraded
- **4 rings** (pyrene, chrysene, fluoranthene): Intermediate solubility and persistence
- **5-6 rings** (benzo[a]pyrene, benz[a]anthracene): Highly hydrophobic, persistent, and carcinogenic

## Fungal Metabolism Pathways

### Cytochrome P450 Monooxygenase Pathway
The primary fungal PAH metabolism pathway involves:
1. **Epoxidation**: Cytochrome P450 introduces an epoxide group across an aromatic double bond
2. **Hydration**: Epoxide hydrolase converts epoxide to trans-dihydrodiol
3. **Dehydrogenation**: Dihydrodiol dehydrogenase converts to catechol
4. **Ring cleavage**: Ortho- or meta-cleavage of the catechol ring
5. **Further metabolism**: Side-chain oxidation and entry into central metabolism

### Ligninolytic Enzyme Pathway
White-rot fungi degrade PAHs through non-specific [[fungal-ligninolytic-enzyme-systems-overview]]:
- **Lignin peroxidase (LiP)**: One-electron oxidation of PAH aromatic rings to radical cations, initiating non-enzymatic reactions leading to ring opening
- **Manganese peroxidase (MnP)**: Generates diffusible Mn(III) chelates that oxidize PAHs at distance from fungal hyphae
- **Laccase**: Oxidizes PAHs with mediator compounds (ABTS, HBT) extending substrate range to higher-molecular-weight PAHs

## Metabolism of Specific PAHs

### Naphthalene (2 rings)
- Most studied fungal PAH substrate
- Degraded via initial epoxidation at 1,2-position by cytochrome P450
- Metabolites include 1,2-dihydroxynaphthalene, salicylaldehyde, and salicylic acid
- Mineralization to CO2 demonstrated for several fungal species

### Anthracene (3 rings)
- Degraded by both cytochrome P450 and ligninolytic enzyme pathways
- Key metabolite: 1,2-dihydroxyanthracene
- White-rot fungi achieve significant mineralization

### Phenanthrene (3 rings)
- Most abundant 3-ring PAH in contaminated environments
- Fungal metabolites include 1-hydroxy-2-naphthoic acid and phthalic acid
- Both cytochrome P450 and ligninolytic pathways active
- Partial mineralization commonly observed

### Pyrene (4 rings)
- More recalcitrant than 3-ring PAHs
- Degraded primarily by ligninolytic enzyme systems
- Key metabolites: 1-hydroxypyrene, pyrene quinones
- Co-metabolism requires additional carbon source

### Benzo[a]pyrene (5 rings)
- Highly carcinogenic priority pollutant
- Most recalcitrant PAH for biological degradation
- Degraded by white-rot fungi through ligninolytic enzymes with mediators
- Extremely slow degradation; typically only partial transformation achieved

### Fluoranthene (4 rings)
- One of the most abundant PAHs at contaminated sites
- Degraded by several white-rot fungi species
- Laccase-mediator systems particularly effective
- Metabolites include fluoranthene dihydrodiols and hydroxyfluoranthenes

### Chrysene (4 rings)
- Degraded by both fungal and bacterial systems
- Fungal degradation primarily through ligninolytic enzymes
- Limited mineralization; primarily initial oxidation products detected

## PAH Degradation in Soil

### Influence of Co-substrates and Surfactants
- **Co-substrates**: Lignocellulosic materials (straw, sawdust, wood chips) stimulate white-rot fungal growth and ligninolytic enzyme production, enhancing PAH degradation
- **Surfactants**: Non-ionic surfactants (Triton X-100, Tween 80) increase PAH solubility and bioavailability. Cyclodextrins are biodegradable alternatives that form inclusion complexes with PAHs
- **Nutrient amendments**: Nitrogen and phosphorus (biostimulation-techniques|biostimulation) enhance overall microbial activity

### Fate of PAH Disappearance

**Mineralization**: Complete conversion to CO2 and water. Rarely achieved for high-molecular-weight PAHs. Measured using radiolabeled (14C) PAHs.

**Bound residue formation**: PAH metabolites become covalently incorporated into soil organic matter (humus). Bound residues are less bioavailable and less toxic but may persist for years. Fungal transformation contributes significantly to bound residue formation through oxidative coupling reactions.

### Factors Affecting Biodegradation
- **PAH molecular weight**: Higher molecular weight = lower solubility = slower degradation
- **Soil organic matter**: High organic matter content increases PAH sorption, reducing bioavailability
- **Soil aging**: PAHs become increasingly sequestered over time, reducing bioavailability
- **Oxygen**: Aerobic conditions required for both cytochrome P450 and ligninolytic enzyme pathways
- **Moisture**: Optimal moisture for fungal growth (40-60% water holding capacity)
- **pH**: Most fungi prefer slightly acidic conditions (pH 4-7)
- **Temperature**: 20-30 C optimal for mesophilic fungi

## Fungal-Bacterial Co-cultures

Combined fungal and bacterial approaches are more effective than either alone:
- **Fungi** partially oxidize high-molecular-weight PAHs, producing intermediates accessible to bacteria
- **Bacteria** mineralize lower-molecular-weight PAHs and fungal intermediates more completely
- **Synergistic interactions**: Fungal hyphae improve soil structure and oxygen diffusion, creating conditions favorable for bacterial activity
- **Consortia design**: Optimal pairing depends on PAH mixture, soil type, and environmental conditions

## Compost Bioreactors for PAH Removal

Composting contaminated soil with organic amendments provides:
- Thermophilic conditions enhancing degradation rates
- Nutrient-rich environment supporting diverse microbial communities
- White-rot fungi in compost degrade PAHs while contributing to organic matter stabilization
- See [[staycare-fungal-agricultural-waste-conversion]] for composting fundamentals

## Biotechnology and Bioengineering

Emerging approaches for enhancing fungal PAH degradation:
- **Genetic engineering**: Overexpression of ligninolytic enzyme genes
- **Enzyme engineering**: Modified laccases and peroxidases with broader substrate ranges
- **Immobilized enzyme reactors**: Packed-bed reactors with immobilized fungal enzymes for water treatment
- **Fungal strain improvement**: Selection and adaptation of high-performance PAH-degrading strains
- **Nanotechnology**: Enzyme-nanoparticle conjugates for enhanced stability and activity
