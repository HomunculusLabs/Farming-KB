# Soil Fungal Community Composition

Soil fungal communities are among the most diverse and functionally
important biological assemblages on Earth. The composition of these
communities is regulated strongly by climatic and edaphic factors,
with ecological similarity among habitats superseding geography as
the primary determinant of community structure. An experienced
microfungal taxonomist can predict the prevailing vegetation and
climate of an area from the list of its most abundant soil fungi.

## Climatic and Edaphic Regulation

The influence of environment on occurrence of soil fungi is
manifested when certain genera or species are encountered more
commonly in one biome than others. Distinctive assemblages of
soil fungi characterize different vegetation types. Cylindrocarpon,
Chrysosporium, Mortierella, Oidiodendron, and certain Penicillium
species are common in Antarctic, arctic, alpine, and boreal
regions. Aspergillus species are isolated more often from desert,
subtropical, and tropical soils, sometimes accounting for up to
20% of total species isolated.

Soil fungal similarity is an outstanding indicator of environmental
similarity. Soils of beech forests in North America and Europe
show striking similarities in their principal saprobic fungal
species. This pattern of ecological similarity overriding
geographic distance has been documented across multiple continents
and vegetation types.

## Vertical Stratification

Fungal communities change with depth through the soil profile.
The litter and humus layers harbor distinct mycotas. The litter
is a transition area between the living plant community and the
soil, and its composition is tied closely to the plant community.
Assemblages of plant-associated species intergrade with soil
species at the humus-soil interface. This litter-humus profile
is exceptionally species rich.

Fungi exhibit succession downward through litter and soil
horizons. Stable organic compounds such as waxes, lignins, and
phenolics may persist for hundreds of years and are colonized
by characteristic groups of fungal species. In the humid tropics,
species richness declines rapidly with increasing depth in
mineral soil. Relict fungal communities characteristic of past
climatic conditions have been found at depth in soils.

## Seasonal Dynamics

Relative abundances of common soil fungal species in forest and
grassland soils vary seasonally. Such variation is less
conspicuous in agricultural soils and some boreal conifer
forests. Soil mycobiotae also change over successional time.
Intensive and extensive sampling is necessary to inventory
principal soil fungi both spatially and temporally. Sampling
from a representative mosaic of vegetation types in a single
season should precede sampling designed to examine seasonal
variation.

## Agricultural and Disturbed Soils

In agricultural soils, Acremonium, Arthrinium, Cladosporium,
Drechslera, Fusarium, Periconia, Microdochium bolleyi, and
Phoma eupyrena frequently are recovered. Twelve species of
Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Penicillium are characteristic of
temperate American grassland soils, and nine species of
Fusarium may occur consistently in grasslands worldwide.

## Tropical Forest Soils

Less is known about characteristic assemblages in humid tropical
forest soils. Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Trichoderma species
usually are abundant. Among Aspergillus species, a large group
of typically tropical species exists. Tropical soils contain
many minor or rare genera that appear primarily tropical in
distribution and commonly are associated with overlying litter.

## Cold Environment Soils

In cold bogs and coniferous swamps, yeasts, yeastlike forms,
Tolypocladium, Trichoderma species, sterile dematiaceous
forms, and certain penicillia and eurotialean fungi prevail.
Desert soils are characterized by low propagule densities but
high species diversity. Mitosporic fungi with melanized,
thick-walled conidia and fungi producing chlamydospores,
bulbils, or sclerotia are well represented in desert soils.

## Chytridiomycetes in Soil

Chytridiomycetes are distributed widely in soils from the Arctic
to the tropics and appear abundantly when specialized enrichment
methods are used. Their species diversity increases along a
gradient from higher latitudes toward equatorial regions. The
distributions of chytrids in soils are sensitive to moisture,
ion content, pH, and temperature. Different soil types harbor
totally different assemblages of chytrid taxa.

## Estuarine and Marine Soils

Filamentous fungi isolated from soils, sands, and sediments of
estuaries, mangroves, and marine habitats are predominantly
terrestrial soil fungi. Terrestrial fungal propagules accumulate
and form an inactive spore bank in marine sediments because of
the mycostatic effect of seawater. Only a few facultative marine
mitosporic fungi such as Asteromyces cruciatus and Dendryphiella
arenaria appear to be true inhabitants of marine sediments.

## Canopy Soils

No data exist on fungal communities of aerial soils resulting
from humus formation on large limbs in forest canopy trees.
Fungal species composition in these specialized soils may be
quite different from mineral soils because of the lack of
mineral input, extreme fluctuations in water content,
distinctive microfauna, and the generally exposed environment
in the canopy.

## Assessment Methods

Species-abundance curves and species-isolate curves are the
mycological analogues of species-area curves in plant ecology.
Asymptotes for species-isolate curves range from about 60 to
150 species for 600-1000 isolates from temperate or tropical
soils. New species continue to be encountered indefinitely
with continued sampling. Determination of all taxa present in a
given soil type seems impossible.
