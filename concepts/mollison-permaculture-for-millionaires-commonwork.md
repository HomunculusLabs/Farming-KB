# Mollison on Permaculture for Millionaires (Commonwork)

**Source:** Bill Mollison, Permaculture Design Course, Pamphlet XV (Permaculture for Millionaires), 1981
**Last Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Overview

Mollison proposes a strategy for redirecting investment capital into large-scale biological resource systems through "commonwork" -- functional stacking where investors own only the product they're interested in while all other yields support families and communities.

## The Commonwork Concept

- "Functional stacking": like laying one painting on top of another, laying on functions
- Investor buys right to one product (e.g., alcohol from toddy palms); all other products (fish, honey, grain, vanilla, cattle) belong to the people living on site
- Permaculture Institute holds and manages rights; everybody receives "eternal right to their part of production"

## Case Study: Babassu Palm (India)

- Grows under worst conditions on exposed Indian coast
- Produces 10,000-12,000 liters of fuel sap per acre annually
- Can be heavily intercropped; provides shade, building/thatch material
- Keyline dam system upslope; automatic harvest (liquid sap flows downhill by gravity)
- Stacking: toddy palms + cattle grazing + green-leaf + bean crops + honey production + aquaculture
- Investor earns return from alcohol production; doesn't need to own site

## Case Study: Australian Desert

- Land available: $120,000 for 700 sq miles; $200,000 for 1,000 sq miles
- 500 miles of excellent date washes with nothing else on them
- Deserts have constant 15 mph winds -- sail freighters possible (100-foot wide sown strip)
- Everything produced in desert is self-stored (dry storage)

## Strategy for Engaging Investors

1. Investors have nobody to advise them on environment ("nobody is informed")
2. Their own people are graduates of economic/management schools -- unequipped for ecological advice
3. They are not immune from acid rain or environmental disaster
4. The "alternative" has set up no structures that integrate with business
5. Permaculture now provides that structure: Institute --> Community --> Trading Trust --> Investment Trust

## Key Arguments for Investors

- Companies are immortal; can plan 70-year investments
- Can't turn off coal overnight; two paths: suppress pollutants OR rapidly generate biological resources to replace coal
- "Let the meek inherit the Earth, just so long as I have the mineral rights" (Gulbenkian)
- Once investor sees acid rain data checked in 4 days: "absolutely frightening"
- "No opposition in high echelons" -- just lack of information

## Implementation Challenges

- Principals must be involved (underlings resist change threatening their position)
- Need thousands of qualified designers for $2-3 million to $2-3 billion installations
- Takes many designers full-time for many years to adjust, tune, and extend systems
- Goal: show it can be done, return money, leave improved real estate and happy people

## On Large Dams

- Aswan-type dams: mostly negative
- Reduce river fertility below by trapping silt
- Increase disease (tropical)
- Change fisheries; wipe out species for miles downstream (cold water release)
- Give rise to centralized power and polluting industries
- "Generally speaking, they are a disaster"
## The Commonwork Village Model

Mollison extends the commonwork concept beyond single-investor projects to envision entire villages structured around functional stacking. In a commonwork village, each household or family unit manages a specific function -- one family might manage the fish ponds, another the orchards, another the livestock, another the [[mollison-biogas-and-methane-production-from-sewage]] -- while all families share in the total production of the system. No single household needs to manage the full complexity of a diverse productive landscape. Instead, each specialises in one or two functions and trades with neighbours. This creates a resilient local economy where the total productive output far exceeds what any single household could achieve alone. The village as a whole functions as an integrated biological system, with wastes from one function becoming inputs for another, following the same principles that govern [[mollison-aquatic-polyculture-and-pond-culture]] in aquaculture.
## Ethical Investment Principles

Mollison's approach to engaging investors is grounded in the [[mollison-ethical-investment-movement]] and the permaculture ethics of earth care, people care, and fair share. The commonwork model offers investors genuine returns not through speculation or extraction, but through productive biological systems that improve the land over time. Unlike conventional investments that externalise environmental costs onto communities and ecosystems, permaculture investments internalise those costs by design -- the investor's return depends on the health and productivity of the living system. This alignment of financial return with ecological health is a fundamental innovation. Mollison also emphasises that investors in commonwork projects gain a form of security that conventional investments cannot offer: productive land that becomes more valuable as biological systems mature, independent of market volatility or currency fluctuations.
## Legal Trust Structures

The commonwork model requires innovative legal structures to separate investment interests from land ownership and management rights. Mollison proposes a layered trust system: a Community Trust holds the land in perpetuity for the benefit of the community living on it; a Trading Trust manages the commercial operations and product sales; and an Investment Trust manages the financial interests of external investors. The [[mollison-permaculture-community-legal-structure]] ensures that no single entity can sell the land, that community residents have secure tenure regardless of investment changes, and that investors have enforceable rights to their contracted share of production. This separation of legal interests protects all parties: the community cannot lose their land, the investor cannot lose their capital, and the land itself is protected from being sold or degraded for short-term profit.
## Scaling Strategies

Mollison identifies a critical scaling challenge: moving from successful demonstration sites to widespread adoption. The commonwork model requires thousands of qualified permaculture designers to plan, install, and manage systems at the scale of hundreds or thousands of hectares. Each large installation requires a team of designers working full-time for years to establish, tune, and optimise the biological systems. The [[mollison-permaculture-design-philosophy]] provides the framework, but the bottleneck is trained designers. Mollison proposed that the Permaculture Institute serve as the coordinating body, matching investor capital with design teams and suitable land, while maintaining quality control through peer review of designs and ongoing monitoring of established systems. The goal is to demonstrate that large-scale biological resource systems can outperform conventional investments in both financial return and ecological impact, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of investment and implementation.
## Failure Modes and Risk Management

Mollison is candid about the risks of the commonwork model. The most common failure mode is investor impatience: biological systems take years to reach full productivity, and investors accustomed to quarterly returns may pressure managers to accelerate timelines at the expense of system health. Another risk is the departure of key designers and managers: the complexity of established permaculture systems means that losing the people who understand the design logic can be catastrophic. Mollison addresses this through extensive documentation and through training multiple people in every critical function. Political and regulatory risks also threaten commonwork projects: changes in land use regulations, tax policy, or agricultural subsidies can undermine the economic viability of a system designed under different conditions. The [[mollison-permaculture-trusts-and-legal-structures-for-community-land]] provide some protection against these risks by separating the land holding from the commercial operations, making it harder for any single political change to destroy the entire system. Despite these risks, Mollison believed the commonwork model offered better risk-adjusted returns than conventional investments precisely because its biological foundation is inherently diversified and resilient.
## The Role of Demonstration Sites

Mollison considered demonstration sites essential to the commonwork strategy. Before approaching major investors, the permaculture movement needed working examples that proved the economic viability of large-scale biological resource systems. The [[krameterhof-holzer-model-farm]] in Austria and other established permaculture demonstration farms serve this function, showing that productive, profitable agriculture can coexist with ecological restoration. Mollison argued that a small number of well-documented, financially transparent demonstration projects would be more persuasive to investors than any amount of theoretical argument. Each successful demonstration site also trains the designers and managers needed for the next, larger project, creating a self-expanding network of capable practitioners.

## Comparison with Conventional Agricultural Investment

The commonwork model differs fundamentally from conventional agricultural investment in its time horizon, risk profile, and relationship to the land. Conventional agricultural investments typically focus on single-commodity production with annual return expectations, externalised environmental costs, and land valued primarily for its extraction capacity. Commonwork investments, by contrast, focus on diverse biological production with multi-year return expectations, internalised environmental costs, and land valued for its productive capacity that increases over time as biological systems mature. The [[mollison-permaculture-appropriate-technology]] emphasis in permaculture means that commonwork projects use low-capital, locally adaptable technologies rather than expensive imported equipment, reducing both initial investment requirements and ongoing maintenance costs. This difference in approach means that commonwork investments are inherently more resilient to market volatility, climate disruption, and resource scarcity than conventional monoculture investments, even though they may offer lower returns in the short term.
## Bioregional Application of Commonwork

The commonwork model must be adapted to the specific ecological and economic conditions of each bioregion. In tropical regions, the functional stacking approach can take advantage of year-round growing seasons and rapid biological productivity, with systems centred on perennial tree crops, aquaculture, and multi-species livestock integration. In arid regions, commonwork investments might focus on [[mollison-dryland-strategies]] including water harvesting earthworks, drought-adapted perennial species, and rotational grazing systems that restore degraded rangeland while producing livestock. In temperate regions, commonwork projects can combine [[mollison-farm-forestry-and-woodlot-design]] with seasonal crops, livestock, and value-added processing. The [[mollison-bioregional-organisation-and-resource-index]] principles that Mollison developed emphasise that each region has unique combinations of climate, soil, water, species, and human culture that must be understood and respected in the design process. A commonwork investment in one bioregion will look very different from one in another, but the underlying principles of functional stacking, community benefit, and ecological restoration remain constant.
## The Investor as Ecosystem Steward

Mollison's most radical insight in the commonwork framework is the reconceptualisation of the investor not as a passive capital provider seeking maximum financial return, but as an active participant in ecosystem restoration whose financial return is linked to ecological health. In this model, the investor becomes what Mollison called a "steward of biological capital" -- someone whose wealth is held not in abstract financial instruments but in living, productive landscapes that generate continuous yields while improving in productivity and resilience over time. The [[mollison-ethical-basis-of-an-alternative-nation]] requires exactly this kind of reimagining of economic relationships, where investment serves ecological and social functions rather than merely extracting financial returns. Mollison believed that many wealthy individuals and institutions would find this model more satisfying than conventional investment, because it offers tangible, visible results: forests growing, soils improving, communities thriving, and biological systems maturing in ways that no stock portfolio or bond certificate can match.
## Legal Structures for Commonwork Projects

The legal and financial structures supporting commonwork projects require careful design to protect both the investors and the land. Mollison recommends the use of [[mollison-permaculture-trusts-and-legal-structures-for-community-land]] and community land trusts as vehicles for holding land in perpetuity while allowing productive use and investment returns. These structures separate the ownership of the land (held in trust for ecological purposes) from the ownership of the productive enterprises (held by investors and operators), creating a legal framework that prevents the land from being sold or degraded for short-term profit. The trust structure also provides tax advantages in many jurisdictions, as charitable or conservation trust status can reduce property taxes and provide income tax benefits for donors and investors. Mollison emphasised that the legal structure must be established before investment begins, and that the trust documents must clearly articulate the ecological covenants and management principles that govern the use of the land.
## Transition Strategies for Commonwork

Establishing a commonwork project on a degraded or conventionally managed property requires a phased transition strategy that balances the need for immediate income generation with the long-term goal of building productive biological systems. Mollison recommends beginning with the installation of water management infrastructure and the establishment of tree crop systems, which take the longest to mature but provide the highest long-term returns. Annual crops and livestock can generate income during the establishment phase, while the [[mollison-designers-food-forest-succession-design]] process gradually transforms the landscape from a simple monoculture to a complex, stacked polyculture. The transition period typically spans 5-10 years, during which the investor must be prepared for below-market returns while the biological systems develop. Mollison's key insight is that the transition phase itself can be productive and profitable if designed well, with each stage of succession generating its own yields while building the foundation for the next, more productive stage.
## Commonwork and Local Currency Systems

Mollison saw commonwork projects as natural anchors for local economic systems, including local currencies and mutual credit arrangements. Because commonwork produces a diverse range of goods and services, participants can trade among themselves using local exchange systems that reduce dependence on national currency and the external economy. The [[mollison-local-currency-and-informal-economy]] concepts that Mollison explored include LETS (Local Exchange Trading Systems), time banking, and community-supported agriculture arrangements where members pre-purchase shares of the commonwork's production. These economic innovations strengthen the commonwork model by creating stable local markets for its products, reducing the financial risk of market volatility, and building the social relationships of trust and mutual obligation that sustain cooperative enterprises over time. A commonwork project integrated with a local currency system becomes more than a farm -- it becomes the economic engine of a resilient local community.
