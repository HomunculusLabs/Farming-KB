# Edible Mushroom Species Identification

## Introduction to Species Identification

Palmer's practical approach to [[mushroom-identification]] focused on observable characteristics that could be learned through direct experience. He emphasized that descriptions in books were inherently limited and that "the hand and eye acquire a delicacy of analysis, impossible to communicate in words." This section covers the specific edible species Palmer described in detail, along with the field marks he considered most reliable.

## Meadow Mushroom and Horse-Mushroom

The meadow mushroom (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*) and the horse-mushroom (*[[agaricus-arvensis]]*) were treated by Palmer as closely related varieties that "run into each other so imperceptibly" that even experienced collectors could ignore any difference between them. Palmer endorsed the theory that the horse-mushroom was propagated from spores of the meadow mushroom after passing through the digestive system of a horse.

### Field Characteristics
- At first growth, resembles a puffball
- Quickly discloses a veil or web under the cap that ruptures to expose free gills
- Gills begin as faint pink, deepening through purple into black
- When pink, the ring around the stem is quite perfect; older specimens may lose all trace of ring or veil
- Cap color ranges from pure white to deep brown
- Taste and smell are peculiar to the species — "there is not much danger of deception when once you have made their acquaintance"

Palmer advised amateurs to "have one identified by an expert, if possible" for these species, as their variability made verbal description unreliable.

## Maned Mushroom (Coprinus comatus)

The maned mushroom was Palmer's "first fungus we dared to eat on our own recognition." He described it as "the most easily-recognized agaric" and noted it has "no poisonous counterpart."

### Field Characteristics
- When fit for food, shaped like a turtle's egg — ovate and alike at each end
- Top is brown and smooth
- Lower portion has a white mane or fringe of concentric layers (the "mane")
- Stem is hollow; when cut, the cross-section appears like macaroni
- Ring around the stem is movable in well-developed plants
- Very soon after full growth, gill tips turn black (sometimes passing through pink)
- Opens umbrella-like and melts into an inky fluid (deliquescence)

### Warnings
Should never be eaten when stale or when the gills have turned black. The inky deliquescence is a natural process of [[mushroom-veil-function-wind-light-protection-prior-to-spore-release]] and decomposition. Palmer noted that "the manuscript of this article is written with the result of such deliquescence," demonstrating the copious ink-like fluid the mushroom produces.

## Elm-Tree Mushroom (Agaricus ulmarius)

The elm-tree mushroom grows only in autumn on elm trees, making it relatively easy to identify by its substrate alone.

### Field Characteristics
- Thick, solid stem (sometimes set eccentrically to one side)
- Broad, white gills
- Firm white flesh
- Light-yellow top, smooth at first, becoming spotted and cracked with age
- Older specimens develop yellow gills and leathery caps
- Most commonly found in a cleft or where a limb has been sawn off
- Often reappears yearly in the same spot

Palmer stated: "I know of no other large fungus with gills and white flesh growing from the elm."

## The Russulae Family

Palmer considered the Russulae "quite a safe one for beginners," owing to their distinctive brittle texture. The key diagnostic feature separating them from other gilled mushrooms was brittleness: "the milk-mushrooms and the Russulae are very brittle, the gills do not mat down... they break into small pieces while the whole fungus snaps suddenly on being bent."

### Identification Protocol
1. Check for brittleness (the gills and flesh break crisply rather than bending)
2. If milk or juice exudes, reject the specimen (these are *Lactarius* species, which Palmer advised novices to avoid cooking)
3. If perfectly dry, taste a small piece of the stem
4. If not an edible russula, it will "bite and sting the tongue"
5. All esculent varieties are "tasteless, mild, or with the flavor of chestnuts"

### Problematic Species
Palmer highlighted the difficulty of distinguishing *[[russula-alutacea]]* (bright red top, buff-colored gills, stout short stem — edible) from *[[russula-emetica]]* and *[[russula-rosacea]]* (which resemble it closely). His personal practice was to put a small piece of each stem into his mouth while washing them, "in order to avoid all possibility of mistake."

## The Boleti

Palmer provided clear rules for distinguishing edible from dangerous Boleti:

### Favorable Signs
- Tubes or pores are brown, yellowish, or greenish
- Top is russet-color or any shade of brown
- Flesh remains white when cut
- Grows from the ground with a distinct stem
- Tubes are easily separable from each other

### Warning Signs
- Brilliant coloring (red or yellow)
- Turns blue when bruised or cut
- Tubes are red at the orifice
- Grows on wood with an abortive stem (likely *Polyporus*, not *Boletus*)

## Puffballs

Palmer called puffballs "the safest mushrooms for the beginner." The giant puffball (*[[lycoperdon-giganteum]]*) in its infant state has a smooth skin and perfectly white interior. The skin color varies from pure white to nearly black, but the critical test is the interior: "If the knife leaves a stain of yellow, it is too old to eat." Several smaller varieties exist, which Palmer ate "indiscriminately."

Key rule: "There is no poisonous fungus resembling them." However, puffballs change rapidly after gathering and should only be eaten in their freshest state. All white puffballs were considered safe if cooked immediately.

## Spreading Hydnum (Hydnum repandum)

The primary representative of the tooth-bearing fungi that Palmer encountered. Field characteristics: usually yellow, sometimes reddish, always firm-fleshed, with a deformed, indistinct, or eccentric stem. One side of the cap is frequently much higher than the point diametrically opposite. The teeth or spines (which easily rub off in young specimens) are distinctive enough to prevent confusion with other fungi.

Palmer cited Smith's authority that "all the species of any size enjoy a good character" among the Hydnei.
