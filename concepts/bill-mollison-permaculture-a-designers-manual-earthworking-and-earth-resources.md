# Earthworking and Earth Resources in Permaculture

## Overview

Earthworking is one of the most consequential and irreversible actions [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]] applies: **make the least change for the greatest possible effect**. When choosing a dam site, select the area where the most water is stored for the least amount of earth moved. Every earthwork should serve multiple functions — a dam stores water, raises the water table for downslope plantings, creates an aquaculture opportunity, provides fire protection, and serves as a reflective microclimate modifier.

### Slope Measurement

Accurate slope measurement is essential before any earthworking begins. Mollison describes several methods:

- **A-frame level**: A simple wooden A-frame with a plumb bob, used to find the [[comparison-hugelkultur-vs-keyline-design]], developed by P.A. Yeomans, uses the "keypoint" or "keyline" — a specific contour line in the landscape — to guide the placement of earthworks for maximum water distribution across broadacre land.

## Types of Earthworks

### Swales

Swales are shallow, broad channels dug on contour to catch, slow, and infiltrate surface water runoff. Unlike drains, swales do not carry water away; they hold it on the landscape long enough to percolate into the soil. Key characteristics of swale design:

- Dug on the exact contour line, level across their length
- The excavated soil is placed on the downslope side as a berm
- The berm is planted immediately with trees, shrubs, and groundcovers
- Swales can be interconnected to form a water-harvesting network
- They reduce erosion, recharge groundwater, and create productive growing zones

Swales are particularly important in dryland and sloping landscapes where water runoff is rapid and infiltration is low.

### Dams and Ponds

Dams are among the most significant earthworks in permaculture, providing water storage for irrigation, stock, aquaculture, fire control, and [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]]:

- Roads should follow the contour or have a very slight grade (1-5%) to shed water into swales or vegetated areas
- Never place roads directly in a valley floor or natural watercourse
- Crown the road surface so water drains to both sides
- Stabilize road surfaces with gravel, grass, or other appropriate materials
- Place roads on ridge tops where possible, as they shed water to both sides and minimize erosion

## Earth Resources

### Soil as a Resource

Soil is the most critical earth resource, and Mollison treats it as a living system rather than an inert substrate. Earthworking must minimize soil compaction, preserve topsoil, and avoid exposing subsoil to erosion. When topsoil is stripped during dam or road construction, it should be set aside and replaced after the earthwork is complete.

### Rock and Stone

Rock is a valuable earth resource used for walls, gabions, drainage, mulch, heat storage, and [[mollison-designers-dam-construction-and-water-storage]] pond sealing. Clay's impermeability makes it ideal for liners, core walls in dams, and earthen ponds. Understanding the clay content of local soils is a prerequisite for earthworking design.

## Rehabilitation After Earthworks

Disturbed earth must be rehabilitated immediately. Bare soil is vulnerable to erosion, weed invasion, and structural failure. Mollison emphasizes:

- **Immediate planting**: Stabilize all exposed surfaces with fast-growing groundcovers, grasses, or mulch within days of earthwork completion
- **Pioneer species**: Use nitrogen-fixing and pioneer species to establish initial vegetation cover, then successionally replace with productive species
- **Water management**: Ensure that all water flows from the earthwork are controlled and directed to vegetated areas or storage
- **Monitoring**: Regularly inspect earthworks, especially dams and spillways, for erosion, seepage, or structural problems during the first few years after construction

## Designers' Checklist

- Has the site been accurately surveyed for slope, aspect, and soils?
- Are all earthworks designed on contour or with appropriate drainage?
- Does each earthwork serve at least two functions?
- Has topsoil been preserved for respreading after construction?
- Is there a rehabilitation plan for all disturbed areas?
- Are dams equipped with properly designed spillways?
- Have access roads been placed to minimize erosion?
- Have existing rock outcrops and natural features been incorporated into the design?
