# Earthworking and Earth Resources in Permaculture

## Overview

Earthworking is one of the most consequential and irreversible actions [[mollison-lawn-reform-and-alternatives-in-permaculture-design]]. Chapter 9 of Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* dedicates extensive treatment to the planning, execution, and rehabilitation of earthworks, emphasizing that poor earthworking can take decades or centuries to repair. Earthworks include swales, dams, ponds, terraces, access roads, and any modification to the land surface that alters water flow, soil stability, or microclimate.

## Planning Earthworks

### The Design Imperative

Mollison stresses that earthworks must be planned with the utmost care before any machinery is brought to the site. The fundamental [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]] applies: **make the least change for the greatest possible effect**. When choosing a dam site, select the area where the most water is stored for the least amount of earth moved. Every earthwork should serve multiple functions — a dam stores water, raises the water table for downslope plantings, creates an aquaculture opportunity, provides fire protection, and serves as a reflective microclimate modifier.

### Slope Measurement

Accurate slope measurement is essential before any earthworking begins. Mollison describes several methods:

- **A-frame level**: A simple wooden A-frame with a plumb bob, used to find the [[comparison-swale-vs-contour-line-water-management]] across a slope. By marking where the two legs sit at the same level, a contour can be traced across the landscape.
- **Water tube level**: A long flexible tube filled with water, where the water level at both ends indicates equal elevation. Suitable for longer distances than the A-frame.
- **Laser levels and dumpy levels**: More precise instruments for larger earthworks and professional installations.

Understanding slope is critical because it determines water flow speed, erosion potential, and the placement of swales, dams, and roads.

### Levels and Levelling

Contour lines — lines of equal elevation across a slope — are the foundation of earthworking design. Swales are constructed on contour to intercept and infiltrate water. Roads and tracks are placed off-contour (with a slight grade) to shed water rather than concentrate it. [[comparison-hugelkultur-vs-keyline-design]], developed by P.A. Yeomans, uses the "keypoint" or "keyline" — a specific contour line in the landscape — to guide the placement of earthworks for maximum water distribution across broadacre land.

## Types of Earthworks

### Swales

Swales are shallow, broad channels dug on contour to catch, slow, and infiltrate surface water runoff. Unlike drains, swales do not carry water away; they hold it on the landscape long enough to percolate into the soil. Key characteristics of swale design:

- Dug on the exact contour line, level across their length
- The excavated soil is placed on the downslope side as a berm
- The berm is planted immediately with trees, shrubs, and groundcovers
- Swales can be interconnected to form a water-harvesting network
- They reduce erosion, recharge groundwater, and create productive growing zones

Swales are particularly important in dryland and sloping landscapes where water runoff is rapid and infiltration is low.

### Dams and Ponds

Dams are among the most significant earthworks in permaculture, providing water storage for irrigation, stock, aquaculture, fire control, and [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]]. Mollison outlines several considerations for dam placement:

- **Site selection**: Choose the narrowest point of a valley or swale for the dam wall, where the most water is stored per unit of earth moved
- **Catchment area**: The dam must have sufficient contributing catchment to fill within a reasonable timeframe
- **Soil suitability**: Clay-rich soils are ideal for dam construction; sandy or rocky soils may require geomembrane liners or compacted clay cores
- **Depth-to-surface-area ratio**: Deep dams lose less water to evaporation than shallow ones with the same volume
- **Spillway design**: Every dam must have a properly designed spillway to safely pass excess water without eroding the dam wall

### Terraces

Terraces convert sloping land into a series of level steps, each supported by a retaining wall or earthen berm. Terraces:

- Prevent soil erosion on steep slopes
- Create level planting surfaces for crops
- Facilitate irrigation by allowing water to pond and infiltrate
- Have been used for millennia in rice cultivation, vineyards, and hillside agriculture
- Require significant initial earth-moving but create permanently productive land

Terraces can be bench terraces (broad, flat platforms), channel terraces (with water channels), or broad-base terraces with gentle slopes.

### Access Roads and Tracks

Roads are earthworks that, if poorly placed, become major conduits for erosion. Permaculture road [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]]:

- Roads should follow the contour or have a very slight grade (1-5%) to shed water into swales or vegetated areas
- Never place roads directly in a valley floor or natural watercourse
- Crown the road surface so water drains to both sides
- Stabilize road surfaces with gravel, grass, or other appropriate materials
- Place roads on ridge tops where possible, as they shed water to both sides and minimize erosion

## Earth Constructs

### Banks and Berms

Earth banks serve multiple functions: windbreaks, privacy screens, sound barriers, frost pockets, and microclimate modifiers. A north-facing bank in the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]] (south-facing in the Northern) captures and stores solar heat. Banks planted with trees become living windbreaks that also produce forage, timber, or fruit.

### Ramps and Mounds

Ramps provide access between levels, useful for terraced landscapes and dam walls. Mounds create elevated planting areas that improve drainage for moisture-sensitive plants, [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] season by improving [[hemenway-cold-air-drainage-and-frost-management]] (reducing frost risk), and provide wind protection.

## Moving the Earth

Mollison discusses both manual and mechanized approaches to earthworks:

- **Manual**: Spades, mattocks, and wheelbarrows for small-scale earthworks, swales, and garden beds. Labor-intensive but precise.
- **Animal-powered**: Oxen, horses, or mules with plows and scoops for medium-scale work. Still practical in many parts of the world.
- **Mechanized**: Bulldozers, excavators, and graders for large-scale earthworks. Efficient but require skilled operators and careful supervision to avoid over-excavation or compaction.

The choice of method depends on scale, budget, site accessibility, and the designer's philosophy regarding [[appropriate-technology]].

## Earth Resources

### Soil as a Resource

Soil is the most critical earth resource, and Mollison treats it as a living system rather than an inert substrate. Earthworking must minimize soil compaction, preserve topsoil, and avoid exposing subsoil to erosion. When topsoil is stripped during dam or road construction, it should be set aside and replaced after the earthwork is complete.

### Rock and Stone

Rock is a valuable earth resource used for walls, gabions, drainage, mulch, heat storage, and [[holzer-climate-zone-microclimate-creation]]. Mollison encourages designers to work with existing rock outcrops rather than removing them — "the problem is the solution."

### Clay

Clay soils are essential for [[mollison-designers-dam-construction-and-water-storage]] pond sealing. Clay's impermeability makes it ideal for liners, core walls in dams, and earthen ponds. Understanding the clay content of local soils is a prerequisite for earthworking design.

## Rehabilitation After Earthworks

Disturbed earth must be rehabilitated immediately. Bare soil is vulnerable to erosion, weed invasion, and structural failure. Mollison emphasizes:

- **Immediate planting**: Stabilize all exposed surfaces with fast-growing groundcovers, grasses, or mulch within days of earthwork completion
- **Pioneer species**: Use nitrogen-fixing and pioneer species to establish initial vegetation cover, then successionally replace with productive species
- **Water management**: Ensure that all water flows from the earthwork are controlled and directed to vegetated areas or storage
- **Monitoring**: Regularly inspect earthworks, especially dams and spillways, for erosion, seepage, or structural problems during the first few years after construction

## Designers' Checklist

- Has the site been accurately surveyed for slope, aspect, and soils?
- Are all earthworks designed on contour or with appropriate drainage?
- Does each earthwork serve at least two functions?
- Has topsoil been preserved for respreading after construction?
- Is there a rehabilitation plan for all disturbed areas?
- Are dams equipped with properly designed spillways?
- Have access roads been placed to minimize erosion?
- Have existing rock outcrops and natural features been incorporated into the design?
