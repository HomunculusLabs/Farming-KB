# Aquaculture and Water Systems

Aquaculture and water systems represent some of the most productive and
space-efficient components of permaculture design. Bill Mollison devoted
extensive coverage to the design and management of ponds, dams, and
integrated aquatic polycultures in *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*. Water
systems in permaculture serve multiple functions including food production,
irrigation storage, fire protection, wildlife habitat, microclimate moderation,
and recreation. These systems connect to [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-water-harvesting-and-earthworks]]
for infrastructure, [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-edge-effect-and-ecotone-design]] for productivity
optimization, and [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-animal-systems-in-permaculture]] for integrated livestock
components.

## Pond Design Principles

Mollison outlined several key principles for pond design. Ponds should be
sited where they can fill naturally from runoff, springs, or pumped water
while fitting into the overall [[zone-and-sector-analysis]] plan. The ideal
pond shape maximizes shoreline edge through lobate and irregular contours,
as described in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-edge-effect-and-ecotone-design]]. Depth should vary from
shallow margins to deeper zones, creating multiple habitat niches. Ponds
should include islands for wildlife refuge and additional edge. The pond
floor should be graded with ledges for planting aquatic vegetation at
different depths. Shallow areas warm quickly in spring, stimulating
biological activity that cascades through the entire aquatic food web.

## Aquatic Polyculture

The core of permaculture aquaculture is the polyculture approach, where
multiple species are raised together in mutually beneficial combinations.
This contrasts with industrial monoculture aquaculture that raises single
species in intensive conditions requiring heavy feed and chemical inputs.
Mollison described several polyculture models inspired by traditional Asian
and European aquaculture systems.

### The Five-Species Pond Model

One of the most well-known models integrates five types of aquatic organisms
that occupy different ecological niches within a single pond. Surface-feeding
fish consume insects and floating plants. Mid-water fish feed on zooplankton
and organic particles. Bottom-feeding fish consume detritus and benthic
organisms. Shellfish filter algae and organic particles from the water column.
Aquatic plants like duckweed, water hyacinth, and water chestnut produce
oxygen, provide habitat, and serve as food for fish and livestock. Each
species fills a niche without competing directly with the others, creating a
balanced ecosystem that produces multiple yields.

### Duckweed Systems

Duckweed (Lemna species) is one of the fastest-growing plants on Earth and
forms the foundation of many permaculture aquaculture systems. Duckweed
doubles its biomass every two to three days under favorable conditions, and
its protein content can exceed thirty percent of dry weight. Mollison
described duckweed ponds that produce livestock feed, human food, and water
purification simultaneously. Duckweed absorbs nutrients from the water,
preventing algal blooms and improving water quality. Harvested duckweed can
feed chickens, ducks, pigs, and fish, making it a critical link between
aquaculture and [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-animal-systems-in-permaculture]].

## Chinampas

Mollison drew inspiration from the chinampas of pre-Columbian Mexico, an
ancient system of artificial islands built in shallow lake beds. Chinampas
are constructed by piling mud and vegetation between narrow canals, creating
extremely fertile raised beds surrounded by water. The canals provide
irrigation, nutrient-rich sediment, and aquatic habitat while the raised beds
support intensive crop production. Mollison considered chinampas among the
most productive agricultural systems ever devised, producing multiple crops
per year with no external fertilizer inputs. Modern adaptations of chinampas
can be built in marshy areas, ponds, and even in urban settings using
containers and water channels.

## Integrated Rice-Fish-Duck Systems

In tropical and subtropical regions, Mollison described integrated systems
combining rice cultivation with fish and duck production. Rice paddies serve
as aquatic ecosystems where fish consume mosquito larvae and pests, ducks
control weeds and snails, and both animals fertilize the rice crop with
their manure. The rice provides shade and habitat for the animals while
suppressing weed growth through flooding. This system produces rice, fish,
duck meat, and duck eggs from the same land area while reducing or
eliminating the need for chemical pesticides and fertilizers.

## Pond Ecosystem Management

Mollison emphasized that productive aquaculture requires understanding and
managing the pond as a complete ecosystem. The food web begins with sunlight
driving photosynthesis in algae and aquatic plants. Zooplankton graze on
algae. Small fish feed on zooplankton. Larger fish prey on smaller fish.
Detritivores break down organic matter on the pond floor. Each level of the
food web depends on the health of the levels below it. The designer's role is
to establish balanced populations at each trophic level and avoid the
overstocking that leads to oxygen depletion, disease, and system collapse.

## Water Quality Management

Maintaining water quality is essential for productive aquaculture. Mollison
described several biological approaches to water quality management. Aquatic
plants absorb excess nutrients that would otherwise cause algal blooms.
Freshwater mussels and clams filter suspended particles from the water.
Oxygenating plants release oxygen during photosynthesis. Aeration through
wind-powered or solar-powered devices supplements natural oxygen production
in deeper ponds. [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-water-harvesting-and-earthworks]] design ensures that
fresh water enters the system while controlled outlets allow for periodic
water exchange.

## Multi-Function Design of Water Systems

Every water system in permaculture should serve multiple functions beyond
aquaculture. A dam stores irrigation water for [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-broadacre-and-rural-design]]
and provides fire protection. A pond moderates the temperature of nearby
areas through evaporative cooling, benefiting [[faires-climate-zones-and-microclimate-design]].
The pond edge provides habitat for frogs that control insect pests in
surrounding gardens, supporting [[integrated-pest-management]]. Water
features reflect light into adjacent buildings and growing areas. The
sediment that accumulates behind dams is nutrient-rich material that can be
excavated and applied to gardens as fertilizer, connecting to
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]].

## Small-Scale Aquaculture

Aquaculture is not limited to large rural properties. Mollison described
small-scale systems suitable for [[urban-permaculture-trees-water-cycling]] including barrel
ponics, backyard ponds, and container-based fish systems. A single barrel
or bathtub can support a small population of fish combined with water plants
for water purification. These systems provide educational value, food
production, and the aesthetic benefits of water features in confined spaces.
Waste water from aquaculture can be used to irrigate garden beds, creating
a nutrient-rich water source that closes the loop between water systems and
plant production.
