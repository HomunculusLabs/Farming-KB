## Tropical Hedgerow and [[tropical-house-design-cooling-strategies|design|Shelterbelt]] Design

Tropical hedgerow and shelterbelt design is a fundamental permaculture practice for establishing wind protection, microclimate creation, soil stabilization, and wildlife habitat on tropical and subtropical farms. While hedgerow traditions are well-developed in temperate zones (where they arose from enclosures of common lands), the rapid degradation of tropical landscapes has made pioneer hedgerow a priority theme for tropical coasts and hill country.

## Overview

Due to ideal growing conditions in tropical climates, hedgerows establish quickly once properly designed and planted. A deliberately mixed hedgerow provides multiple functions simultaneously: windbreak, forage source, wildlife corridor, soil stabilization, mulch production, and structural material supply. The design principles differ from temperate hedgerows in species selection, establishment speed, and the range of products they yield.

## Establishing a Classical Tropical Hedgerow

### Site Preparation

The procedure for establishing a tropical hedgerow follows these steps:

1. **Cultivate and manure** the hedgerow site
2. **Place a dripline** along the entire hedgerow length for irrigation
3. **Set out concurrently** a multi-layer row of complementary species

### Species Composition

The classical tropical hedgerow consists of four layers planted simultaneously:

| Layer | Species | Function |
|-------|---------|----------|
| **Tall grass/clump bamboo** | *Pennisetum* species (usually) | Immediate wind protection, structural material |
| **Quickset cuttings** | *[[casuarina-equisetifolia|Casuarina equisetifolia]]*, sea grape (*Coccoloba unifera*), coastal shrubs (*Scaevola*, *Tournefortia*, *Pemphis*), mangroves, and *Barringtonia* trees

### Caliche and Calcrete

A thin coralline sand layer typically overlies a hardpan of caliche or calcrete. **Important**: Calcrete is worsened by the application of superphosphate. Seabird guano usually provides sufficient phosphates if colonies of terns, gulls, boobies, frigate birds, and shearwaters are protected or encouraged. Where seabirds are absent, domestic poultry (pigeon, quail, pheasant, geese, ducks, chickens) can serve as phosphate sources, as feral predators are usually absent on islands.

## Water Lens Protection on Atolls

### The Freshwater Lens

The sole natural water sources on low islands are biological storages (baobabs and coconuts — about 12 coconut trees provide one person's drinking water for a year) and the **water lens** trapped in sand below the caliche, floating on seawater.

Rain absorbs into surface soils, percolates through caliche, and recharges the water lens, which slowly leaks to sea between or below tide levels. With the beach berm at 2–4 m above sea level and the inner atoll at 2–3 m, the delicacy of the water lens is evident.

### Pollution Prevention

To protect the water lens:
- **Settlement, toilet areas, processing, livestock, and even gardens should be kept to the periphery** of the island
- **The interior should be devoted to dense natural stands of food and native trees**, kept free of pesticides, industrial pollutants, and fuels
- A polluting source at the center of the island can affect the entire water lens, all plants, and all people
