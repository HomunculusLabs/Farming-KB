# Nature's Fundamental Perfection

[[fukuoka-road-critique-of-modern-science]]. Knowledge that treats self and object as a
unified whole is "non-discriminating knowledge" — the highest wisdom.

The "discriminating adult" is inferior to the "non-discriminating
child," for the adult plunges into ever-deepening confusion. A child
raised in the city fears the muddy rice field because his mother told
him dirt is full of germs. Yet hundreds of millions of microorganisms
crowd each gram of soil — harmful and beneficial alike — and a child
who rolls in the dirt grows up healthy. The knowledge that "there are
germs in the soil" is more ignorant than ignorance itself.

## Nature as Paradise

Fukuoka describes nature as a place where flowers bloom and birds sing,
spiritually and materially replete with the greatest possible wealth. It
is a paradise where joy and contentment reign. Nature is never one
moment late; it moves freely and innocently as God wills it.

This is not romanticism. Fukuoka acknowledges nature can appear to
collapse when viewed from a limited perspective. But from a macroscopic
vantage, nature does not destroy itself — it adopts many different
forms. What appears as destruction has almost always been precipitated
by man upsetting the course of nature at some earlier point.

## The Bushman and the Barren Land

Fukuoka uses the San Bushmen of southern Africa to illustrate the gap
between primitive existence and truly natural conditions. The Bushman
himself appeared fine — his bright, cheerful face suggesting a natural
man immersed in joy. But his environment was barren and scarce.
