# Mushroom Genetics and Strain Selection

Understanding [[fungal-genetics]] is essential for selecting productive, stable cultures and maintaining genetic quality over generations. Unlike plants, fungi have unique reproductive strategies — including heterokaryosis and clamp connections — that shape how traits are inherited and expressed.

## Fungal Genetics Fundamentals

### Dikaryotic Mycelium

Most basidiomycete fungi (the club fungi that produce mushrooms) are dikaryotic during their vegetative growth phase:

- Each cell contains **two genetically distinct nuclei** (n + n)
- These nuclei pair up and divide synchronously (conjugate division)
- The dikaryotic state is maintained by **clamp connections** — small hook-like bridges on septa that ensure each new cell receives one nucleus of each type
- Fruiting (mushroom formation) only occurs from dikaryotic [[mycelium]]
- At the basidium (spore-producing cell), the two nuclei finally fuse (karyogamy), undergo meiosis, and produce haploid spores

### Monokaryon vs. Dikaryon

- **Monokaryon**: Mycelium with a single nucleus per cell (n). Results from spore germination. Thin, wispy, slow-growing. Cannot fruit
- **Dikaryon**: Mycelium with two nuclei per cell (n+n). Results from mating of compatible monokaryons. Robust, rhizomorphic, fast-growing. Can fruit
- A single spore germinates into a monokaryon. Two compatible monokaryons must mate to form a dikaryon

## Mating Systems

See [[fungal-sexual-reproduction]] for deeper coverage of mating type genetics.

### Bipolar Mating

- One mating type locus with two or more alleles (e.g., A1, A2, A3...)
- Two mycelia can mate if they carry different alleles at this locus
- Approximately 50% of spores from a single fruiting body are compatible with each other
- Common in: Coprinus, Agaricus, Panaeolus

### Tetrapolar Mating

- Two unlinked mating type loci (A and B), each with multiple alleles
- Both loci must differ for successful mating: A1B1 x A2B2 works; A1B1 x A1B2 does not
- Only ~25% of spore pairings from a single mushroom are compatible
- More genetic diversity maintained in natural populations
- Common in: Schizophyllum, Pleurotus, Psilocybe, most wood-decay fungi

### Homothallism

- Self-fertile: a single spore can produce a fertile dikaryon
- [[agaricus-bisporus]] is predominantly homothallic (secondary homothallism) — most spores contain two compatible nuclei
- Simpler breeding but less genetic diversity

## Strain Isolation

### From Spores (Multispore)

- [[spore-print]] or [[spore-syringe]] contains thousands of haploid spores
- When inoculated onto agar or grain, spores germinate into many monokaryons that pair randomly
- Result: a patchwork of many different dikaryotic strains
- **Pros**: Maximum genetic diversity; potential for novel traits; inexpensive
- **Cons**: Unpredictable results; variable potency and yield; may contain incompatible or poor performers
- Each multispore grow is genetically unique — like siblings, not clones

### From Tissue (Cloning)

- Take a small piece of living tissue from inside the cap or stem of a desired mushroom
- Transfer to agar under sterile conditions
- The tissue is already dikaryotic — it grows into a genetically identical copy
- **Pros**: Reproduces exact traits of the parent mushroom; consistent results
- **Cons**: No genetic novelty; accumulates mutations over time; susceptibility to the same pathogens
- Always clone from a healthy, well-formed specimen. Avoid mushrooms near contamination or past prime

### Sector Isolation

- Even "clean" mycelium on agar may contain multiple genetic strains growing in different sectors
- Isolate the fastest-growing, most rhizomorphic sector by cutting it away from slower sectors
- Repeated isolation over 3-5 transfers stabilizes the culture
- Document growth rate, morphology, and performance at each transfer

## Genetic Stability and Degradation

### Senescence

- Fungal mycelium has a finite lifespan before genetic degradation occurs
- Each cell division accumulates mutations; after many divisions, vigor declines
- Signs: slowing growth, reduced yield, abnormal morphology, increased contamination susceptibility
- Mitigation: store master cultures on slants at refrigeration temps; minimize subculturing

### Anastomosis

- When two compatible dikaryotic mycelia meet, they can fuse (anastomose)
- Nuclei exchange between the two networks — genetic recombination without fruiting
- This can improve or degrade culture quality depending on the partners
- In multispore grows, anastomosis among siblings can produce "sector dominance" by the fittest strain

### Epigenetics

- Environmental conditions can affect gene expression without changing DNA sequence
- The same genetics can produce different results under different conditions
- Temperature, substrate, light, and CO2 levels influence morphology and potentially alkaloid production

### Selection Criteria

- **Colonization speed**: Faster colonization means less time for contamination
- **Rhizomorphic growth**: Rope-like, organized mycelium correlates with vigorous fruiting
- **Yield**: Total [[biological-efficiency]] (fresh weight of mushrooms / dry weight of substrate)
- **Potency**: For active species, alkaloid content varies significantly between strains
- **Environmental tolerance**: Some strains handle temperature or humidity fluctuations better
- **Appearance**: Cap shape, color, stem thickness — aesthetic preference
- **Contamination resistance**: Some genetics outcompete contaminants better

## Working with Agar for Strain Selection

1. **Germinate spores** on agar plates (MEA, PDA, or DFA)
2. **Observe sectors**: Different strains appear as distinct growth zones with different textures
3. **Isolate sectors**: Transfer the most vigorous, rhizomorphic sector to a new plate
4. **Compare isolates**: Run parallel test grows of different isolates on identical substrates
5. **Select the best**: Keep the isolate with the best combination of speed, yield, and quality
6. **Store master culture**: Create slants of the selected strain for long-term storage

## Genetic Tools

### Microscopy

- Spore size and shape can indicate species and sometimes strain
- Basidium morphology, cheilocystidia shape — taxonomic identification
- Requires compound microscope (400-1000x magnification)

### DNA Barcoding

- ITS (Internal Transcribed Spacer) region is the standard barcode for fungi
