# Ethical Basis of an Alternative Nation

The concept of an alternative nation, as developed by Bill Mollison in Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14), rests on the fundamental premise that a people united by a shared ethical framework constitute a nation regardless of geographic location or political boundaries. This idea challenges the conventional understanding of nationhood based on territory, language, or ethnicity, replacing it with a definition grounded in common purpose and ecological responsibility.

## Core Principles of Sustainable Societies

Mollison identifies several foundational principles that distinguish sustainable societies from exploitative ones:

### Duty to Nature Equal to Duty to People

Sustainable societies extend ethical obligations beyond human-to-human relationships to include responsibilities toward all lifeforms and elements of the landscape. Codes of conduct that address only interpersonal relationships allow people to evade accountability for environmental damage, which ultimately harms other people as well. A truly ethical framework must encompass the entire resource base upon which life depends.

### The Seventh Generation Principle

Drawing from the Iroquois Nation, Mollison highlights the concept that decisions should be evaluated in terms of their consequences for descendants seven generations into the future (approximately 100 years). This long-term perspective explains why tribally managed lands historically supported rich biological resources, while contemporary management has depleted much of what was inherited. The principle demands that every action be weighed against its intergenerational impact.

### Right Livelihood

People must adopt an ethic of right livelihood, recognizing that labour directed toward destructive systems makes them complicit in destruction. The argument extends beyond personal ethics to economic structures: individuals should not serve as passive workers within systems that degrade ecological and social health. Instead, they can redirect their skills and energy toward investing in life-sustaining systems. There is a fundamental inconsistency—and self-damage—in professing one ethic while living by another.

### Integrated Learning

Knowledge should be studied and applied within a total integrated system framework, with awareness of how every action and piece of understanding permeates all systems. The fragmentation of knowledge into isolated academic disciplines creates conditions where practitioners can claim ignorance of downstream effects. However, people remain responsible for the distant consequences of their actions and should actively work to produce foreseen benefits.

### Conserver Societies

The development of conserver societies requires close attention to recycling, waste avoidance, and the use of durable technologies that extend useful life. Critically, Mollison warns against discarding older, proven technologies simply because newer ones become available. A new windmill for electricity generation should not come at the expense of allowing a dependable older grain mill to fall into disrepair. Similarly, the availability of a forage harvester should not lead to losing the skill of using a scythe on steep terrain.

The art of conservation lies in developing a mosaic of small, well-managed, and effective systems. Such modest systems are unlikely to cause widespread disruption and are less vulnerable to external or unethical control.

### Meaning Through Action

Meaning in life is not found through the pursuit of status or future glory but through action directed toward a common ideal. Service to the whole—according to one's physical, mental, educational, and spiritual capacities—provides genuine fulfillment. As Mollison states, "fair words plant no cabbages": good intentions must translate into effective action.

### Security Through Sharing

Security arises from the renunciation of ownership over people, money, and real assets. Insecurity and unhappiness result from attempting to acquire, retain, or protect resources that others legitimately need. A lending library enables self-education, while a locked book collection serves only its owner. The principle extends to land, tools, knowledge, and biological resources.

### Leisure as a Resource

When an ethical community establishes a durable, dependable, and waste-free resource base, leisure time becomes abundant. This time can be used for cultural enrichment, personal development, and evolving more effective ways to assist systems and people. However, emphasis on spectator recreation wastes the time gained, leading to the professionalization of arts, sciences, and education as passive observers replace active participants.

### Teaching as the Foundation of Security

The best preparation for security is teaching others the strategies, ethics, and practices of resource management. When others feel secure and possess the knowledge to manage their own environments, there is no need to guard against them. Aid and education should be extended wherever possible.

## The Starting Point

Mollison acknowledges that achieving a fully repaired world may not occur within his lifetime or his children's lifetimes. Soil erosion, deforestation, famine, and poverty will persist for generations. However, he identifies a clear direction: spending one's life toward earth repair. Until humanity demonstrates the ability to establish a productive and secure earth society, it has not earned the right to explore space or oceans, nor would it be welcome elsewhere.

## Practical Application

The ethical framework translates into specific personal and community actions:

- **Evaluate every decision** against its impact seven generations forward
- **Refuse destructive employment** and redirect skills toward regenerative work
- **Maintain diverse technologies** rather than replacing older proven methods wholesale
- **Build small, resilient systems** rather than large, vulnerable ones
- **Share resources freely** to build collective security
- **Teach others** rather than hoarding knowledge or skills
- **Act locally** while maintaining global awareness and connections

The strategies described are starting points rather than endpoints. Given the extent of ecosystem damage and the resulting rehabilitative work needed, there will be productive restorative work for several generations to come.

## Philosophical Foundations
These concepts draw on indigenous wisdom, ecological ethics, and
systems thinking to propose alternative governance and social structures
that work with natural processes rather than against them.

## Practical Applications
Community-level implementation involves consensus decision-making,
resource sharing agreements, and long-term stewardship planning.
These approaches emphasize local self-reliance and ecological integrity.

## Related Movements
These ideas connect to broader movements including bioregionalism,
ecological economics, and indigenous sovereignty frameworks.
