# Mushroom Fruiting Body Development Without Meristems

Mushrooms are produced by the elongation and branching of innumerable
hyphae, a fundamentally different developmental strategy from that of
plants or animals. While plants grow by repeated division of specialized
groups of cells called meristems, and animals develop from distinct tissue
types, mushrooms are constructed from tangled masses of essentially
identical cells — yet they achieve remarkably complex forms.

## The Hyphal Construction Principle

At first glance through the eyepieces of a microscope, a mushroom slice
appears like pavement made up of roughly spherical cells that flatten
wherever they make contact. An apple or potato looks very similar under
the microscope. But this resemblance is superficial. Apples and potatoes
grow by repeated division of meristems situated at different positions
within the plant, which throw off cells that form the tissues of leaves,
stems, roots, flowers, and fruits.

Fungi do not have meristems. Instead, mushrooms are produced by the
elongation and branching of innumerable hyphae. The reason that the
tissues of apples and mushrooms look so similar is that it is impossible
to cut along a hypha very far before passing through the other side.

## The Rattlesnake Bundle Analogy

Imagine you have tied twenty or more rattlesnakes side by side with some
strong cord. A fruiting body can contain millions of hyphae, and when its
stalk or cap is cut, you will always see ellipses and circles rather than
cylinders. This hyphal construction is a unique feature of fungi. Nothing
else in nature develops in the same fashion. The cross-section of any
[[hplc-analysis-psilocybin-psilocin-mushroom-tissue]] reveals not differentiated cell layers but the cut ends
of innumerable hyphal tubes packed tightly together.

## Embryonic Mushroom Formation

The first sign of fruiting body genesis is a cluster of hyphae that
converge as a white pellet the size of a pinhead. This is the embryonic
mushroom. Even at this early stage, the hyphae have arranged themselves
into a tiny [[cap-and-stem-alkaloid-distribution-in-psilocybe-cubensis-strains]] — a homuncular ghost of the mature fruiting body.
Following the embryonic phase, hyphae continue to grow, their nuclei
multiplying by mitosis and tips extending and branching.

Much of the rapid phase of mushroom expansion occurs by inflation of
existing hyphae rather than further increase in the number of cells. In
comparison with animal tissues, there is little if any differentiation
among cell types in most of the mushroom.

## Every Cell Is a Stem Cell

If a piece of stem or cap tissue is placed on agar, the hyphae will grow
outward, revert to their invasive behavior, and regenerate a mycelium.
Every mushroom cell behaves like an animal stem cell. Something about the
position of hyphae within the fruiting body and relative to one another
is critical in determining the way that each cell grows, but only while
it stays in that place. This positional information, rather than genetic
differentiation, governs the form of the mushroom.

This means that the cap, stem, gills, and other structures of a mushroom
are not composed of different cell types as in plants and animals, but
are formed by the same hyphal cells growing in different directions and
inflating to different degrees depending on their position within the
developing structure.

## Hymenium Differentiation

The only obvious differentiation, other than variations in cell length
and diameter in the cap and stem, occurs within the fertile hymenium,
where the spores are produced. In the majority of mushroom species, all
hyphal apices that emerge at the gill surface become basidia. Basidia must
be spaced evenly across the landscape of the gill to ensure that they
offer their spores unobstructed launch pads. For this reason, successive
generations of basidia arise on the same gills in most species, new ones
replacing those that deflate after discharging their quartet of spores.

## Ink Cap Gill Architecture

Ink-cap gills are more complex than those of most mushrooms. They house
three distinct types of cells: basidia, sausage-shaped paraphyses that
establish a sterile pavement between them, and enormous cells called
cystidia that inflate across the gap to connect with the opposing gill.
The cystidia act as braces, preventing the gills from tearing apart as
the cap expands. This elegant structural solution is unique to the
Coprinus genus and its relatives, and represents one of the few clear
examples of cellular differentiation in basidiomycete [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]].

## Mutant Analysis and Developmental Genetics

We have reached the limits of observational research on mushrooms, and
hopes of understanding how fruiting bodies demarcate the cap and stem,
establish the spacing of gills before the cap expands, and decide which
cells become basidia rest on the analysis of mutants. Mutant strains of
the ink-cap Coprinus cinereus have been created by disrupting normal
genes with short lengths of DNA that are inserted at randomly selected
sites in the genome.

Fungi that have emerged from this abuse develop bonsai fruiting bodies,
form caps or stems that do not expand, or fail to produce spores. One of
the great challenges of [[bloomfield-buller-experimental-mycology]] lies in elucidating the
source of the disabilities suffered by these mutants.

## Comparison With Other Organisms

The developmental strategy of fungi contrasts sharply with that of other
organisms. In the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, researchers have
documented every cell division that converts a fertilized egg into an
adult worm, determining the developmental origin of every cell. But the
same path of inquiry has not been productive for mycologists. Unlike a
worm or a plant, in which numerous types of tissue are built from distinct
kinds of cells, fruiting bodies consist of nothing but tangled masses of
identical cells that assume different forms through positional cues
rather than genetic programming.
