# Permaculture Design Course -- Earthworks and Swales

Much of the ecology is affected by the shape of the land. Just as soup is contained within a bowl, the ecology is contained within geological features -- hills, valleys, grottos, soil composition. The shape of the ground affects the local hydro-cycle, creates micro-climates, determines what plants can grow where, and through these effects, the animal populations. Human-made geomorphology is referred to as **earthworks**.

## Purpose of Permaculture Earthworks

In Permaculture, you are not just moving earth for its own sake. The point is to create geomorphological conditions that increase the ecological health of the site. This can be accomplished through many design strategies.

## Swales

A **swale** is a trench dug on contour to catch and slow rainwater flowing downhill. They are one of the most fundamental earthworks in Permaculture.

### How to Build a Swale
1. Dig down into the earth creating a trench about the depth and width of your shovel head
2. Collapse in the sides to create a V-shaped trench with gentle slopes
3. Fill with mulch (cut grasses, leaves, sticks, twigs)
4. Plant with summer and winter perennial ground covers, possibly edibles
5. Use a level (architect's level with bricks, A-frame level, or water level) to ensure the swale is level

Swales cut across the path of rainwater flowing downhill. They should be on contour -- perfectly level -- so water spreads evenly along the entire length rather than rushing to one end.

## Berms

A **berm** is built on top of the ground (as opposed to a swale, which is dug into it). Typically a berm and swale are made at the same time: the earth dug out of the swale is piled up to form a berm. Often the berm is placed on the downhill side of the swale to help catch rainwater. Berms on the downhill side of plants help catch and hold water for plant use.

## Terracing

Terraces help reduce water flow and create garden space on slopes and hills. Use berms and swales to slow surface water flow, and terraces when you need to create garden space or a large flat plane. Terraces are essentially a series of stepped flat areas carved into a slope.

## Bio-Swales

A bio-swale is like a swale but planted with species chosen specifically for their function as bio-remediators rather than as food crops. They combine water management with ecological cleansing.

## Earthworks for Erosion Control

Earthworks can slow erosion and create proper conditions for plants to grow. Berms, swales, and keylines are used to slow surface water flow. Earthworks can also serve as **fire breaks** -- roads, pathways, and swales (if moist during fire season) can protect sites from wildfire.

## Flood Control

Berms and raised pathways can reduce destruction from minor flooding. Most flood damage is caused by minor floods (a few inches), not major events. Earthworks designed to handle minor flooding will address 90% of flood disasters.

## Caution: Earthworks Are the Most Impactful Element

More than any other element, earthworks are the single most transformative -- or destructive -- element in any design. As you put in berms, swales, and foundations, you must constantly be aware of the effect on the overall ecological health of the site. Always design for overflow: where does water go when it doesn't infiltrate?

## Design Process for Earthworks

1. Chart surface water flow across the site (from topographical base map)
2. Determine infiltration rates of soils
3. Design swales, berms, and terraces to slow, spread, and sink water
4. Account for overflow -- direct it toward pre-existing water courses or create new ones
5. Consider secondary functions: fire breaks, access paths, growing space, micro-climate creation

## Earthworks as Transformative Elements

Earthworks are the most transformative element you can incorporate into a Permaculture design. They can turn a barren landscape into a lush garden by storing water in the soil, slowing and spreading water flow, and creating microclimates. The scale of earthworks can range from small swales in a backyard garden to massive contour banks on broadacre properties.

Key principles for earthworks design:
- Always work on contour to ensure water is evenly distributed across the landscape
- Observe the land during heavy rain events to understand natural water flow patterns before designing
- Start small and simple -- even a single swale can have dramatic effects
- Earthworks should be integrated with plant systems (e.g., swales planted with trees and ground covers)
- Consider access roads and pathways when planning earthworks to avoid interrupting water flow

### Swale Depth and Spacing

Swale depth and spacing depend on slope, soil type, and rainfall patterns. On steeper slopes, swales should be closer together and deeper. On gentle slopes, they can be farther apart. A common guideline is to space swales at a vertical interval equal to approximately 10-20% of the slope distance between them.

### Soil and Water Testing

Before implementing earthworks, perform a perc test (percolation test) to understand how quickly water moves through your soil. This will inform swale depth and overflow design. Clay soils hold water longer and may require broader, shallower swales. Sandy soils drain quickly and may need deeper swales or additional water-holding strategies.

## Learning Exercise

Take a landscape site with a slope and identify three locations where you would place earthworks. For each location, describe:
1. The type of earthwork (swale, berm, pond, etc.) and its dimensions
2. Why you chose that location (water flow, soil type, existing vegetation)
3. What you would plant on or near the earthwork
4. How the earthwork connects to the broader water management system of the site
