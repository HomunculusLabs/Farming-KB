# Woodlovers Outdoor Alder Chip Method

Alder (Alnus spp.) is one of the premier [[fungal-succession-wood-substrates]] for outdoor cultivation of wood-loving Psilocybe species. In its native [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] range, alder is the dominant hardwood associated with natural P. cyanescens fruitings, and it performs well for P. azurescens cultivation too.

## Why Alder

Alder offers several advantages as a cultivation substrate:

- **Native association**: P. cyanescens is most commonly found on decaying alder chips in the Pacific Northwest; this is its natural substrate
- **Nitrogen-fixing tree**: Alder roots host nitrogen-fixing actinomycetes (Frankia spp.), resulting in wood with relatively higher nitrogen content than many other hardwoods
- **Soft hardwood**: Alder is a softer hardwood that decomposes relatively quickly, making it readily colonized by [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]]
- **Widely available**: Common in riparian zones across [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]]; often available as arborist waste or municipal mulch
- **Moisture retention**: Alder chips hold moisture well while maintaining adequate [[growing-gourmet-growing-room-ventilation-air-exchange]]

## Alder vs. Other Hardwoods

| Wood Species | Colonization Speed | Availability | Notes |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------|-------|
| **Alder** | Fast | Excellent (PNW); good elsewhere | Natural P. cyanescens substrate; higher nitrogen |
| **Beech** | Moderate | Widely available as animal bedding | Recommended in both source guides |
| **Hazelnut** | Fast | Good (Europe) | Specifically recommended for P. azurescens |
| **Oak** | Slow | Good | Denser wood, slower decomposition |
| **Maple** | Moderate | Good | Acceptable hardwood |
| **Poplar** | Fast | Good | Soft hardwood, quick colonization |

## Preparation Method

### Chip Sourcing
- Fresh arborist chips are ideal
- Avoid chips mixed with conifers, walnut, or treated wood
- Chip size matters: smaller chips for spawn, larger for beds
- For spawn: chips should be small enough to fit in jars or bags with good mixing

### Soaking Protocol
1. Submerge alder chips in water for **24-48 hours**
2. Do not exceed 48 hours or competing fungi may begin growing on the wood
3. Drain thoroughly — no standing water
4. Target moisture content of 60-65% if measuring (wood should feel damp but not dripping)

### Supplementation (Optional)
For spawn bags, adding a nitrogen supplement accelerates colonization:
- 1/2 cup oatmeal per bag
- Or boiled [[disposable-tray-casing-method-grass-seed-substrate]] (same quantity)
- This is particularly beneficial for alder since it already has elevated nitrogen; the supplement pushes growth speed further

### Sterilization
- Load soaked (and optionally supplemented) chips into autoclavable bags or jars
- **Jars**: Sterilize at 121C for 1+ hours
- **Bags**: Sterilize at 121C for 3 full hours
- The longer bag sterilization is necessary due to the greater [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]] and less uniform heat penetration

### Inoculation and Incubation
1. Inoculate cooled bags/jars with [[comparison-grain-spawn-vs-grain-to-grain-transfer]] or agar wedges
2. Shake to distribute inoculum
3. Incubate at 20C for 3-4 weeks
4. Spawn is ready when fully white, compact, and mushroom-scented

## Outdoor Bed with Alder Chips

### Bed Construction
1. Select a half-shaded site under shrubs or at a garden edge
2. Dig a shallow hole 15-20 cm deep
3. Soak coarse alder chips for 24 hours, drain
4. Mix colonized alder chip spawn with fresh chips at 1:5 to 1:20 ratio
5. Fill hole with the mixture
6. Water thoroughly (several liters for a standard bed)
7. Cover with wet cardboard and perforated plastic

### Seasonal Maintenance
- **Spring**: Establish the bed; keep humid for 2 weeks
- **Summer**: Minimal watering; check weekly under the plastic
- **September**: Remove plastic, increase watering to 1-2x weekly
- **Late autumn**: Fruit at 7-10C for azurescens; 10-15C for cyanescens
- **Winter**: Add fresh alder chips on top for next year

### Alder-Specific Considerations
- Alder decomposes faster than beech or oak, meaning beds may need topping up with fresh chips sooner
- The faster decomposition rate also means potentially faster colonization and earlier fruiting
- In regions where alder is native, using locally sourced alder may introduce beneficial native microorganisms that support the mycelium
