# Ergot of Barley and Wheat — Alkaloid Composition

**Source:** The Road to Eleusis, Chapter II — Albert Hofmann

## Overview

Hofmann's laboratory analysis of ergot growing on wheat and barley — the grains cultivated on the sacred [[rarian-plain-sacred-grain-eleusis]] — confirmed that these Mediterranean cereals hosted ergot containing both water-soluble psychoactive alkaloids and the insoluble medicinal alkaloids of the ergotamine/ergotoxine group. This finding was essential to establishing the [[wasson-hofmann-ruck-collaboration]].

## Laboratory Findings

"No rye grew there [in ancient Greece], but wheat and barley did and Claviceps purpurea flourishes on both. We analyzed ergot of wheat and ergot of barley in our laboratory and they were found to contain basically the same alkaloids as ergot of rye, viz. alkaloids of the ergotamine and ergotoxine group, ergonovine, and sometimes also traces of lysergic acid amide."

## Two Categories of Alkaloids

The ergot alkaloids fall into two pharmacologically distinct categories:

1. **Water-soluble psychoactive alkaloids**:

   - Lysergic acid amide (ergine)

   - Lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide

   - Ergonovine (ergometrine)

   - These are closely related to LSD and possess hallucinogenic properties

2. **Water-insoluble medicinal alkaloids**:

   - The ergotamine and ergotoxine groups

   - These are primarily uterotonic and vasoconstrictive

   - Not hallucinogenic in normal use

## The Critical Extraction Property

"Ergonovine and lysergic acid amide, both psychoactive, are soluble in water whereas the other alkaloids are not." This differential solubility was the key to the [[kykeon-composition-and-ergot-alkaloid-hypothesis]]: "With the techniques and equipment available in antiquity it was therefore easy to prepare an hallucinogenic extract from suitable kinds of ergot."

A simple water extraction would selectively dissolve the psychoactive alkaloids while leaving the non-psychoactive, potentially toxic alkaloids behind in the grain matrix. This would have been achievable with Bronze Age technology — grinding grain, soaking in water, and straining.

## Geographic and Temporal Variability

"Ergot differs in its chemical constituents according to its host grass and according to geography." Hofmann acknowledged: "We have no way to tell what the chemistry was of the ergot of barley or wheat raised on the Rarian plain in the 2nd millennium BC."

But he argued: "it is certainly not pulling a long bow to assume that the barley grown there was host to an ergot containing, perhaps among others, the soluble hallucinogenic alkaloids."

## The Paspalum Parallel

The same water-soluble alkaloids found in cereal ergot also occur in [[claviceps-paspali-alkaloids]] growing on Paspalum distichum, a wild Mediterranean grass, and in [[ololiuhqui-morning-glory-ergot-alkaloid-connection]] (Mexican morning glory seeds). This pharmacological convergence across three different plant hosts on two continents supports the plausibility of ergot-based preparations in antiquity.

## Historical Context of Ergot in Mediterranean Agriculture

Ergot has been a constant companion of cereal agriculture since the Neolithic revolution. The sclerotia — the dark, hardened fungal bodies that replace individual grain kernels — would have been visible to ancient farmers harvesting barley and wheat by hand. Unlike modern mechanical harvesting, which can remove ergotized grains, ancient hand-harvesting methods would have left sclerotia mixed with the grain.

In the context of the [[rarian-plain-sacred-grain-eleusis]], the sacred grain fields dedicated to Demeter, ergot contamination would have been an annual occurrence. The hierophantic families managing these fields may have learned to identify and selectively harvest ergotized grain, developing specialized knowledge about which contaminations produced desirable effects and which were dangerous.

## Ergot in Classical Greek Medicine

Ancient Greek medical writers were aware of ergot's effects on reproduction, though they did not identify the fungus itself. The uterotonic properties of ergot — primarily from the ergotamine group alkaloids — were exploited in folk obstetrics throughout the Mediterranean world. Midwives likely used crude ergot preparations to hasten labor, though at sub-psychoactive doses.

This medical tradition meant that the basic technology for processing ergotized grain — grinding, steeping, and straining — was already familiar in the ancient Greek world. The [[eleusinian-hierophantic-families-eumolpidae-kerykes]] would have had access to this practical knowledge, adapted and refined for their specific ritual purposes.

## Comparison with Modern Ergot Analysis

Modern analytical chemistry has confirmed and extended Hofmann's findings. Gas chromatography and mass spectrometry have revealed that ergot alkaloid profiles vary significantly depending on the host plant species, geographic location, and even the specific strain of Claviceps purpurea. Some populations of the fungus produce predominantly water-soluble alkaloids, while others produce mainly the insoluble ergotamine/ergotoxine group.

This variability actually strengthens the [[kykeon-potion-and-ergot-hypothesis-at-eleusis]]: over the two millennia of the Eleusinian Mysteries, the hierophants could have selected and cultivated ergot strains with favorable alkaloid profiles, much as modern agricultural science selects crop varieties for desired characteristics.
