## Domestic [[herb-spiral]] and Kitchen Garden

The herb spiral and kitchen garden represent zone 1 in [[permaculture-design]] --
the areas of highest use and most intensive management, located immediately
adjacent to the home. Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*
emphasizes that zone 1 elements must be convenient, productive, and designed
for daily interaction. The herb spiral is a compact, three-dimensional growing
structure that exemplifies permaculture's approach to maximizing yield in
small spaces through [[mollison-designers-triazonal-cultivation-system]] and
edge effect principles.

## Zone 1 Kitchen Garden Layout

Zone 1 is the area most frequently visited, where the gardener passes daily
to harvest herbs, salad greens, vegetables, and flowers for immediate use.
Mollison recommends positioning the kitchen garden on the path between the
home entrance and the most commonly used outdoor area, ensuring that
harvesting becomes a natural part of daily movement through the landscape.

Key layout principles for the zone 1 kitchen garden include:

- **Proximity**: Place the garden within 10 to 20 meters of the kitchen door,
  ideally visible from the kitchen window for monitoring and inspiration.
- **Access**: Design beds no wider than 1.2 meters for easy reach from both
  sides without stepping on soil. Pathways should be at least 60 centimeters
  wide for comfortable passage.
- **Efficiency**: Arrange crops by frequency of use, with daily-harvest items
  closest to the door and less-frequently accessed crops further away.
- **Microclimate**: Use walls, fences, and structures to create favorable
  microclimates with reflected heat, wind protection, and modified light.

## [[herb-spiral-design]] Principles

The herb spiral is a mound of soil built in a rising spiral approximately
1.5 to 2 meters in diameter and 0.5 to 1 meter tall at the center. It
creates a range of microclimates within a small footprint: the top of the
spiral is dry, sunny, and well-drained, while the base is moist, shaded,
and cool. The south-facing side (northern hemisphere) receives more sun and
heat, while the north-facing side remains cooler.

This microclimate gradient allows the gardener to grow herbs with widely
different environmental preferences in a single structure. Mediterranean herbs
such as rosemary, thyme, and oregano thrive at the hot, dry top. Moisture-
loving herbs like mint, parsley, and chervil grow at the cool, damp base.
Sun-loving basil occupies the sunny side, while shade-tolerant cilantro and
chives grow on the cooler side.

Construction begins with a foundation of rocks, bricks, or other durable
material laid in a spiral pattern. The spaces between rocks are filled with
good quality soil enriched with [[mollison-designers-humus-building-and-soil-organic-matter]].
The spiral should be oriented so that the warm, sunny face corresponds to the
equator-facing direction in the local hemisphere.

## Culinary and Medicinal Herb Selection

Mollison advocates selecting herbs based on both culinary use and ecological
function. Culinary herbs provide daily harvest value, while many also serve as
insectary plants, pest repellents, and dynamic nutrient accumulators. A well-
designed herb garden integrates species from several functional categories:

**Culinary essentials**: Basil, thyme, rosemary, oregano, sage, parsley,
cilantro, chives, mint, dill, tarragon, and marjoram form the core of most
kitchen herb collections. These should be positioned for convenient daily
harvest.

**Medicinal herbs**: Echinacea, calendula, chamomile, lemon balm, valerian,
and yarrow provide traditional remedies. These may be positioned slightly
further from the kitchen door as they are harvested less frequently.

**Insectary herbs**: Dill, fennel, yarrow, borage, and clover attract
pollinators and predatory insects that provide biological pest control for
the entire garden system.

**Pest-repellent herbs**: Wormwood, tansy, pyrethrum daisy, garlic, and
rue deter specific pests through volatile oils and allelopathic compounds.

## [[seasonal-planning]]

Effective kitchen garden design accounts for seasonal variation in production.
In temperate climates, a well-planned zone 1 garden provides fresh produce
year-round through a combination of:

- **Spring**: Peas, lettuce, radishes, spinach, asian greens, and early
  herbs such as chervil and cilantro.
- **Summer**: Tomatoes, peppers, beans, cucumbers, basil, and summer
  savory, with succession plantings of salad crops.
- **Autumn**: Root vegetables, brassicas, kale, and late-harvest herbs
  such as sage and rosemary.
- **Winter**: Hardy greens (kale, mizuna, corn salad), overwintering
  alliums, and protected crops under cloches or cold frames.

Season extension structures such as cold frames and cloches should be
integrated into the zone 1 layout, positioned for convenient access during
cold weather.

## Intensive Bed Design

Intensive raised beds maximize production in zone 1 by providing deep,
well-drained, nutrient-rich soil in a format optimized for high-density
planting. Beds are typically 15 to 30 centimeters raised, filled with a mix
of topsoil, compost, and organic matter. The raised format improves drainage,
reduces soil compaction, and brings the growing surface closer to the
gardener for easier access.

[[square-foot-gardening]], biointensive methods, and [[mollison-designers-triazonal-cultivation-system]]
principles all apply to intensive bed design. Spacing is closer than
conventional row gardening, with plants arranged in offset patterns rather
than straight rows to maximize light interception and root space utilization.
Interplanting fast and slow-growing crops ensures continuous harvest from the
same bed area.

## Integration with Other Systems

The kitchen garden connects to broader permaculture systems. Compost bins
should be located in or adjacent to zone 1 for convenient processing of
kitchen scraps. [[mollison-designers-water-purification-and-storage-principles]]
systems can supply irrigation from greywater. Small poultry systems can be
integrated for pest control and manure production. The herb spiral and
intensive beds demonstrate the [[mollison-designers-intensifying-small-farm-production]]
principles at the smallest practical scale.

## Summary

> **Definition:** The domestic herb spiral and kitchen garden are zone 1
> permaculture elements designed for intensive daily food production
> immediately adjacent to the home. The herb spiral creates multiple
> microclimates for diverse herb species within a compact spiral structure,
> while the broader kitchen garden integrates intensive raised beds, seasonal
> planning, culinary and medicinal herbs, and connections to composting,
> water systems, and small livestock. These elements maximize the yield and
> convenience of the most frequently accessed growing areas.
