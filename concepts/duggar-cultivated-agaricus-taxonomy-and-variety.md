# Cultivated Agaricus Species Taxonomy and Variety Classification

The taxonomy of cultivated agarics has been a persistent source of
confusion since the earliest days of commercial mushroom growing. Duggar's
1915 examination of the problem reveals the difficulty of applying botanical
classification to organisms that have been selected and maintained through
vegetative propagation for centuries. The question of how many distinct
species are actually under cultivation remains partly unresolved even today.

## The Species Problem in Cultivation

The original mushroom grower obtained "virgin" spawn almost annually from
spontaneous occurrences of mushrooms in fields and pastures, or from spawn
appearing in compost heaps. If there are many "varieties" in cultivation,
these same varieties must in large part exist in the open. Duggar found it
difficult to agree with those who maintained that cultivated varieties had
originated from wild types by mutation. Instead, he was convinced that
from a half dozen to a dozen distinct forms were frequently used in
cultivation, and that these must represent several species.

This perspective challenged the prevailing view that the single species
[[agaricus-campestris-morphology-and-life-cycle]] encompassed all
cultivated forms. The reality is considerably more complex, involving at
least two and possibly several species with numerous intergrading forms.

## Principal Species in Cultivation

Botanists have long distinguished "typical" Agaricus campestris, the Field
Mushroom, from A. arvensis, the Horse Mushroom. Characteristic forms of
both species are widely cultivated. Intergrading forms occur as well, and
it is often very difficult to determine whether these are varieties of the
two recognized species or constitute separate forms deserving specific rank.
Some authorities assigned specific rank to forms like A. magnificus and A.
Rodmani.

The typical form of A. campestris possesses gills that are at first bright
pink, changing through bright reddish brown to brown-black. The annulus is
single and relatively thin. The cap is convex or plane, varying from white
to brownish, and the stem is equal and solid. However, under the vigorous
production conditions of cellars and mushroom houses, spawn derived from
the typical field species may yield specimens in which color and ring
characteristics are not distinguishable from those commonly identified as
A. arvensis.

## The Horse Mushroom: Agaricus Arvensis

Agaricus arvensis, formerly more commonly known as the Horse Mushroom, is
found in situations similar to those inhabited by A. campestris. Typical
field forms are larger and stouter than the Field Mushroom, and are
particularly distinguished by the possession of a double ring. Several
varieties in cultivation may properly be referred to this species. One
especially interesting thin-capped brown variety possessed a clearly double
ring, yet one much more delicate than usual. Under cultivation, Duggar
found A. arvensis equal to or better than A. campestris in flavor.

## Trade Classification by Color

In the absence of any practical way for growers to distinguish between
species on structural grounds, Duggar recommended a classification based
on color for trade purposes. The smaller, practically pure white form in
cultivation was to be called "Alaska," the large cream-colored form
(sometimes grayish) "Columbia," and the typical brown variety of the
English brick spawn "Bohemia." This simple classification assisted in
directing grower attention to the necessity for distinguishing between
varieties, though Duggar acknowledged it was no longer adequate.

## The Almond Mushroom: Agaricus Fabaceus

Agaricus fabaceus (A. subrufescens) is the almond-flavored and
almond-fragrant mushroom, reported as springing up in greenhouses and
flower beds. It is readily distinguished from allies of A. campestris by
the long-persistent membranous veil, whose lower surface is covered with
soft frosty scales; the red-brown to gray-brown pileus; paler gills; and
the enlarged lower part of the stem. It has been cultivated, but requires
more completely fermented [[mushroom-compost-bed-systems-duggar]], a higher
temperature, and may not bear for several months. It is also less gregarious
and less prolific, though it deserves extended trial.

## The Flat-Capped Woodland Agaricus

Agaricus placomyces is a woodland species found from early summer until
late fall. The cap is large, flat, and thin, appearing smoky above from
numerous small dark scales more closely aggregated near the center. The
veil is double like that of A. arvensis but not so stout. This species
represents the forest-adapted end of the Agaricus spectrum and may hold
promise for cultivation on non-traditional substrates like deciduous leaf
compost.

## Environmental Influence on Morphology

The appearance of cultivated agarics is profoundly influenced by growing
conditions. Specimens produced when spawn is just beginning to run, and
therefore not very vigorous, may more closely resemble the typical field
form. Those produced at the end of the season may again seem more typical.
Exposure, including the action of light and wind, intensifies color up to a
certain point. Forms producing perfectly white mushrooms in moist, protected
places may yield plants in the open that would not be recognized as the same
species or variety.

## European Counterparts

In Europe, A. pratensis (A. praticola) and A. villaticus are recognized as
related species. Types corresponding to these also occur in the United
States. A comprehensive taxonomic study would require growing all forms
simultaneously under similar conditions, contrasting specimens produced
under both favorable and unfavorable growing conditions.
