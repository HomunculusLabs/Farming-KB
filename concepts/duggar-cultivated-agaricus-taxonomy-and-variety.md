## The Species Problem in Cultivation

The original mushroom grower obtained "virgin" spawn almost annually from
spontaneous occurrences of mushrooms in fields and pastures, or from spawn
appearing in compost heaps. If there are many "varieties" in cultivation,
these same varieties must in large part exist in the open. Duggar found it
difficult to agree with those who maintained that cultivated varieties had
originated from wild types by mutation. Instead, he was convinced that
from a half dozen to a dozen distinct forms were frequently used in
cultivation, and that these must represent several species.

This perspective challenged the prevailing view that the single species

it is often very difficult to determine whether these are varieties of the
two recognized species or constitute separate forms deserving specific rank.
Some authorities assigned specific rank to forms like A. magnificus and A.
Rodmani.

The typical form of A. campestris possesses gills that are at first bright
pink, changing through bright reddish brown to brown-black. The annulus is
single and relatively thin. The cap is convex or plane, varying from white
to brownish, and the stem is equal and solid. However, under the vigorous
production conditions of cellars and mushroom houses, spawn derived from
the typical field species may yield specimens in which color and ring
characteristics are not distinguishable from those commonly identified as
A. arvensis.

## The Almond Mushroom: Agaricus Fabaceus

Agaricus fabaceus (A. subrufescens) is the almond-flavored and
almond-fragrant mushroom, reported as springing up in greenhouses and
flower beds. It is readily distinguished from allies of A. campestris by
the long-persistent membranous veil, whose lower surface is covered with
soft frosty scales; the red-brown to gray-brown pileus; paler gills; and
the enlarged lower part of the stem. It has been cultivated, but requires
