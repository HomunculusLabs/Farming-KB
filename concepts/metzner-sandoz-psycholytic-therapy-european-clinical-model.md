# Sandoz Psycholytic Therapy and the European Clinical Model

## Overview

When Albert Hofmann discovered the "fantastically potent mind-
altering qualities" of LSD at Sandoz laboratories in Switzerland
during World War II, the compound was initially characterized as
"psychotomimetic" and "psycholytic." The early Sandoz research
papers described LSD as bringing about "seelische Auflockerung"
— "psychic loosening or opening." This became the dominant model
for LSD-assisted psychotherapy in Europe, the **psycholytic
approach**, which differed fundamentally from the psychedelic
therapy model that developed in Anglo-American circles.

Ralph Metzner's comparative review of [[hallucinogenic-drugs-in-psychotherapy-and-shamanism]]
[[metzner-hallucinogens-psychotherapy-and-shamanism]] provides a detailed account of both
[[european-psycholytic-therapy-1960s-1970s]] and Anglo-American [[metzner-amplifier-microscope-metaphors-psychedelic-therapy]]
models, their historical development, and their shared fundamental
principles that distinguish hallucinogen-assisted therapy from
conventional pharmacological treatment.

## The Psycholytic Model

In psycholytic therapy, neurotic patients suffering from anxiety,
depression, obsessive-compulsive, or psychosomatic disorders were
given LSD in a series of sessions at gradually increasing doses,
while undergoing more or less standard analytic interactions using
a Freudian perspective. The rationale was that through
"psycholysis" — the loosening of psychological defenses — the
patient would become more vividly aware of previously unconscious
emotional dynamics and reaction patterns acquired in early family
interactions, and such insight would bring resolution of inner
conflicts.

The psycholytic approach was methodical and gradual, using multiple
sessions with incrementally increasing doses, allowing patients
to gradually access deeper unconscious layers — analogous to
peeling an onion, each session revealing more fundamental
psychological structures.

## Grof's Discovery: Perinatal Matrices

The Czech psychiatrist Stanislav Grof, working within the
psycholytic model, made a startling discovery that expanded the
model's scope significantly. In a series of sessions involving
increasing doses, patients who had resolved their Freudian-
dynamics of early childhood would find themselves reliving the
significant sensory-emotional features of their birth experience.
Grof gave the name **perinatal matrices** to these patterns,
identifying four distinct matrices corresponding to different
stages of birth. This discovery suggested the unconscious contains
layers deeper than those accessible through conventional
psychoanalysis. The birth experience represented a previously
unrecognized domain of psychological material with profound
implications for understanding psychological distress and the
potential depth of therapeutic transformation.

## The Psychedelic Therapy Model

More or less simultaneously with the European psycholytic
approach, the **psychedelic model** became preferred in Anglo-
American psychological and psychiatric circles. English
psychiatrist Humphrey Osmond, working in Canada with Abram Hoffer
on LSD treatment of alcoholism (and who provided Aldous Huxley
with his first mescaline experience, immortalized in *The Doors
of Perception*), introduced the term "psychedelic" in an
exchange of letters with Huxley.

First used in alcoholism treatment, where it was thought to
simulate the life-changing "bottoming out" experience, psychedelic
therapy involved one or a small number of high-dose sessions in
which unconscious contents manifested as vivid hallucinatory
imagery leading to insight and transformation.

## Harvard and Consciousness Expansion

The term "psychedelic" was adopted by Timothy Leary, Frank Barron,
Richard Alpert and the [[hofmann-leary-case-and-harvard-research]] project. Leary's work
focused not on treatment but on exploring psychedelic possibilities
for "normals" — mostly graduate students — as well as artists,
musicians, poets and writers, in relatively unstructured but
supportive, home-like settings.
