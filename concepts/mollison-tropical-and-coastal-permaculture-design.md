# Mollison's Humid Tropics Design

Chapter 10 of Permaculture: A Designers' Manual addresses the humid tropics, encompassing the river basins and wet coasts from latitudes 0 to 25 degrees. This chapter covers three climatic subtypes: wet tropics, wet-dry tropics, and monsoon tropics, each requiring distinct design responses but sharing fundamental principles of multi-storied polyculture, continuous biomass production, and careful water management.

## The Fundamental Challenge

In the wet tropics, heat and high rainfall leach most mobile nutrients from soils, except for the biomass of the great variety of plants, which contain 80 to 90 percent of available nutrients. Humus is an essential soil fraction, and humus creation must be given considerable emphasis as prerequisite to sustainability. Inappropriate strategies include bare-soil cultivation and intensive clearing and burning in short cycles (less than 8 years). Appropriate strategies involve complex, multi-storied plant systems designed to yield basic staples, create mulch, and preserve soil nutrients.

## Lessons from Failure

Mollison cites James Fox's analysis of social changes following abandonment of ancient palm polycultures in Indonesia. The FAO admits to failure in transferring mechanised monocultural systems to fragile African or tropical soils. The clearing and cultivation of tropical soils has for decades proved disastrous, and most ecologists would have predicted this failure by the early 1950s. Complex perennial fodder and food systems are known to be stable but are not yet part of officially funded agriculture.

## Climatic Subtypes

### Wet Tropics

Temperature fluctuates little at about 21 to 32 degrees Celsius (70 to 90 degrees Fahrenheit). Humidity is constantly high, frost unknown. Rainfall ranges from 152 to 328 cm (60 to 129 inches), with rain most days and frequent thunderstorms (75 to 150 per year). The vegetation is luxurious broadleaf rainforest with lianas and epiphytes, very mixed as to species with up to 800 tree species per square kilometre. Staple foods include plantain, banana, cassava, yams, coconut, corn, taro, rice, ducks, pigs, poultry, and fish.

### Wet-Dry Tropics

Winter is the dry time with clear skies and intense sunlight taking temperatures to 38 degrees Celsius (100 degrees Fahrenheit) or more. Summer resembles the wet tropics but with more episodic flooding. Rainfall ranges from 25 to 152 cm (10 to 60 inches). These regions contain vast savannah grasslands with thorn-bush and flat-topped Acacia trees, enormous herds of zebra, antelope, and associated carnivores. Design essentials include small domestic water storage, hedgerows against wind, in-crop tree legumes, mulch use of grasses, decreased fire frequency, and keyline techniques of flood control.

### Monsoon Tropics

Influenced by continental land masses and oceanic winds, these areas extend from India through Southeast Asia to northern Australia. Late summer heating causes onshore winds and heavy rains; the dry season reverses winds from cool interiors. About 60 percent of rain falls in summer, but totals vary erratically from 102 to 1016 cm (40 to 400 inches). Floods and droughts are equally unpredictable.

## Soils and Mulch

Tropical soils are inherently fragile. Most nutrients are held in living biomass, not soil. Clearing and cultivation rapidly depletes soil fertility, leading to erosion and formation of insoluble iron and aluminum oxides. Only terraces, floodplains, and new volcanic areas maintain soil fertility. Designers must emphasize continuous mulch production, composting, and the preservation of existing tree cover. Mulch sources include bamboo, pampas grass, lemongrass, leguminous ground covers, and chop-and-drop pruning of tree crops.
