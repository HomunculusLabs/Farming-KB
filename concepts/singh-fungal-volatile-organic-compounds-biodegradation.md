# Fungal Volatile Organic Compounds Biodegradation

## Overview

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) represent a major class of air
pollutants from industrial processes, fuel combustion, and chemical
manufacturing. Singh's discussion of [[mycoremediation-pahs]] highlights that
fungi offer significant advantages over bacteria for VOC biodegradation,
particularly for hydrophobic compounds that partition poorly into aqueous
phases. Fungal biofiltration systems leverage the aerial mycelial growth
form providing direct contact with gas-phase pollutants.

## VOC Pollutants of Concern

### BTEX Compounds

BTEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene) are monoaromatic
hydrocarbons derived from petroleum:

- **Benzene**: Known carcinogen (Group 1 IARC); most difficult BTEX
  compound to biodegrade due to ring stability
- **Toluene**: Most readily biodegradable; serves as primary carbon
  source for many VOC-degrading fungi
- **Ethylbenzene**: Intermediate degradability; ethyl side chain
  provides initial attack point
- **Xylene**: Three isomers (ortho, meta, para); biodegraded via
  initial methyl group oxidation

Fungi typically degrade BTEX through cytochrome P450 monooxygenases,
followed by ring cleavage by [[laccase-mediator-system-dye]] and [[singh-white-rot-fungi-lignin-modifying-enzymes-bioremediation]].

### Other Target VOCs

- **Styrene**: From plastics manufacturing; degraded by Cladosporium
  sphaerospermum and Exophiala jeanselmei
- **Halogenated VOCs**: Chloroform, dichloromethane, trichloroethylene;
  more resistant due to carbon-halogen bond strength
- **Aliphatic hydrocarbons**: Hexane, heptane, octane from fuel vapor
- **Oxygenated VOCs**: Ethanol, methanol, acetone, formaldehyde
- **Terpenes**: Alpha-pinene, limonene from wood processing

## Advantages of Fungi Over Bacteria

Fungi possess inherent advantages for VOC biodegradation:

1. **Aerial hyphal growth**: Mycelium extends into the gas phase of
   biofilters, providing direct contact with hydrophobic VOCs
2. **Hydrophobic cell surfaces**: Chitin and hydrophobins in cell walls
   enhance sorption of hydrophobic VOCs
3. **Nonspecific enzyme systems**: [[singh-white-rot-fungi-lignin-modifying-enzymes-bioremediation]] have
   broad substrate specificity for co-metabolic VOC degradation
4. **Low moisture tolerance**: Fungi tolerate lower water activity than
   bacteria, reducing biofilter drying risk
5. **Acid tolerance**: Fungi tolerate acidic conditions better; fungal
   VOC oxidation generates acidic intermediates inhibiting bacteria

## Fungal Biofiltration Systems

### Biofilter Design and Operation

Biofilters consist of:

- **Filter bed**: Packed with organic (peat, compost, wood chips) or
  synthetic media (ceramic beads, polyurethane foam) colonized by fungi
- **Humidification chamber**: Pre-humidifies incoming gas stream
- **Gas distribution system**: Ensures even flow through filter media
- **Leachate collection**: Captures metabolic byproducts

Key operating parameters:

| Parameter | Optimal Range | Notes |
|-----------|--------------|-------|
| Moisture content | 40-70% (wet basis) | Critical for activity |
| Temperature | 20-35 degrees C | Mesophilic range |
| pH | 3.0-7.0 | Fungi tolerate wider range |
| EBRT | 30-120 seconds | Longer = higher removal |

### Biotrickling Filters

Biotrickling filters circulate a liquid phase through the filter bed:

- Enable better pH control and nutrient supply
- Suitable for water-soluble VOCs and chlorinated compounds
- Fungal-dominated systems show superior performance for hydrophobic VOCs

### Fungal Immobilization Strategies

- **Spore inoculation**: Direct seeding with subsequent colonization
- **Pre-colonized media**: Inoculated before installation for immediate
  treatment capacity
- **Mixed cultures**: Combining fungi with bacteria for complementary
  degradation pathways

## Key Fungal Genera for VOC Biodegradation

- **Cladosporium**: C. sphaerospermum degrades toluene, benzene, styrene
- **Exophiala**: Black yeast species adapted to VOC-rich environments
- **Fusarium**: F. oxysporum degrades aromatic and aliphatic VOCs via
  cytochrome P450 systems
- **Trametes**: T. versicolor degrades VOCs through [[laccase-mediator-system-dye]] and
  [[manganese-peroxidase-mechanism]]
- **Aspergillus**: A. niger degrades oxygenated VOCs and short-chain
  aliphatic compounds
- **Scedosporium**: S. apiospermum shows excellent biofilter performance

## Indoor Air Quality Applications

Fungal bioremediation of VOCs has promising indoor air applications:

- **VOC sources**: Building materials, furniture, cleaning products
- **Biological air filters**: Wall-mounted or portable units with fungal
  biofilters for continuous VOC removal
- **Living wall systems**: Green walls incorporating VOC-degrading fungi
- **Tolerance to intermittent loading**: Fungi maintain viability during
  low VOC concentration periods

## Degradation Pathways

Fungal VOC degradation proceeds through several routes:

1. **Cytochrome P450 oxidation**: Initial monooxygenation of alkyl side
   chains on aromatic rings
2. **Laccase-mediated coupling**: Oxidative coupling of phenolic
   intermediates, reducing VOC toxicity
3. **Ring cleavage**: [[singh-white-rot-fungi-lignin-modifying-enzymes-bioremediation]] cleave aromatic rings
4. **Terminal oxidation**: Complete mineralization to CO2 and H2O via
   TCA cycle intermediates

## Challenges and Limitations

- **Incomplete mineralization**: Some partial oxidation products may be
  more toxic than parent compounds
- **Biofilter clogging**: Excessive fungal growth can obstruct gas flow
- **Sporulation and health concerns**: Spores may pose allergen risks
- **Process control**: Maintaining optimal conditions at large scale
  requires sophisticated monitoring
