## Price Spectrum

The economics of ayahuasca tourism span a wide range, from subsistence-level community practice to premium international packages:

### Local Peruvian Ceremonies (Baseline: ~$2)

The baseline cost of participating in community ayahuasca practice in Peru is approximately two dollars per person.

This price reflects the economics of a practice embedded in daily life, where the brew is prepared communally, ceremonies are held regularly, and costs are distributed among participants who share kinship and community ties.

At this tier, ayahuasca functions as a community religious practice rather than a commercial service.

The low price point reflects both the local abundance of raw materials and the communal nature of preparation and participation.

### Tourist-Oriented Village Ceremonies ($10-$30)

When the same practitioners host foreign tourists, prices rise to ten to thirty dollars per session.

In [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] facilitators offer four-star accommodations alongside ceremonial experiences.

While the article does not specify exact prices for these retreats, they are described as "more expensive" and likely cost hundreds or thousands of dollars per week.

The higher price point covers not only the ceremony itself but also international marketing, staff salaries, facility maintenance, insurance, and intermediary fees.

### Specialty Guided Tours (Premium Pricing)

High-end experiences led by established guides like Peter Gorman command premium prices for their reputation, curated itineraries, and depth of Amazonian knowledge.

These tours occupy a distinctive market position for tourists willing to pay for expertise and adventure beyond the standard retreat model.

## Income Disparity and Economic Distortion

### Practitioner Wealth vs. Community Poverty

Tourism income creates dramatic local economic inequality.

One shaman in Yarina earns "a couple hundred dollars per month catering to tourists" -- a substantial income in a context where "his neighbors cannot even afford a thirty cent taxi ride."

This income disparity illustrates how tourism injects disproportionate wealth into individual practitioners" hands without necessarily benefiting the broader community.

The fifteen-fold pricing differential between foreign and local participants shows how foreign demand inflates prices beyond what local economies would naturally support.

### Entrepreneurial Incentives

The economic incentives of tourism can reshape practitioner motivations.

The article describes one shaman's nephew as "an enterprising shaman" whose tourism income dwarfs that of his community.

The word "enterprising" is significant -- it frames his success in commercial rather than spiritual terms.

When tourism income vastly exceeds what can be earned serving local populations, the economic pressure to prioritize foreign clients becomes difficult to resist, potentially at the expense of community-oriented practice.
