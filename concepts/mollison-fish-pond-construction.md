# Mollison: Fish [[permaculture-pond-construction]] and Configuration

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 13, Section 13.5)

## Overview of Pond Sizing

Mollison provides a detailed framework for pond construction across all scales, from tiny garden ponds to commercial aquaculture operations.

The fundamental principle is that every size order of pond has specific appropriate uses, and the design must match scale to purpose.

As few fish ponds exceed 2-3 metres in depth, earth, clay, and stone-faced earth walls generally suffice to hold water.

Still-ponds require an essential compact clay base, while tidal and diversion ponds can tolerate some seepage losses.

## Pond Construction Basics

New ponds need careful assessment for factors of evaporation, seepage, water sources, sealing, and stability.

Pond or terrace construction on the small scale (up to 0.2 hectares) can be hand-tuned for drainage, levelling, and spillways.

Larger constructions arise from either many years of hand labour or modern survey and machine methods.

The most productive, common, and economical ponds are clay-based.

A build-up of organic mulch and algae in ponds assists sealing, so that even ponds in sandy loams may gradually evolve to reduce water losses over time.

Long uncompacted canals, however, need good clay bases to prevent water loss in transit.

## Sealing Leaky Ponds

Mollison describes several methods for sealing ponds that do not hold water naturally:

### Gley Method

Gley is a layer of mashed, wet, green, sappy plant material sealed off from air.

Cattle manure is preferred, but shredded sappy vegetation also works.

The material is laid as a continuous 15-23 cm layer over the base and gently sloping sides (1:4 ratio), then covered completely with earth, cardboard, thick wet paper, plastic sheets, or rolled clay.

This ferments anaerobically and produces a bacterial slime that permanently seals soil, sand, or small gravels.

Modifications include penning cattle in a dry dam until the bottom is manurial pug, strewing bales of green hay on slightly leaking ponds to produce sealing algae, or sowing green crop in the dry dam and feeding it off with cattle.

### Bentonite

Bentonite is a slippery clay-powder derived from volcanic ash that swells when watered and will seal sandy or gravelly soils.

It is spread as a dry powder over the pond base and wetted, or applied as a slurry.

### Clay

Carted clay can be used for a whole core wall in suspect soils, providing complete impermeability.

### Impermeable Membranes

Plastic or rubber liners provide immediate sealing but are more expensive and may degrade over time.

## Orders of Pond Size

### Ponds from 1-10 Square Metres

Very small garden ponds of 2-60 cm depth can be made from old baths, stock tanks, or plastic-lined holes.

Suitable crops include Chinese water chestnut, kangkong, watercress, taro, frogs, and small fish.

A square metre of taro yields 20-30 kg of starchy food.

At 10 square metres and about 2 metres deep, clear fibreglass ponds at the New Alchemy Institute produced fish and products valued at $4.50 to $17 per square foot (1984), amortising costs in 3-5 years.

These ponds provide useful heat storage and can be used in greenhouses or outdoors for much of the year.

### Ponds from 10-100 Square Metres

Ponds of this size rear fish fry for sale or stocking, breed forage fish, produce vegetable crops, supply aquaculture nurseries, create fire-breaks, water reserves, and heat moderation.

For a family, an intensively-managed 100 square metre fish pond comes close to providing a full protein and vegetable resource if carefully designed and stocked with beneficial polyculture.

Realistic yields range from 300-2,000 kg of protein.

Aeration via photovoltaic cell and air pump may be necessary for high yields.

### Ponds from 100-500 Square Metres

Chakroff (1982) and others regard ponds of this size as ideal for fish culture.

Easily harvested, netted, or drained, and capable of holding specific age or size assemblies, such ponds give management flexibility.

Even one 500 square metre pond can provide income from baitfish, aquarium fish, special plant crops, and intensively managed prawns.

Incomes of about $30,000 were possible from open-air prawn ponds of this size in Hawaii (1983).

These ponds can form part of a total wastewater system, growing constant masses of green manure for garden mulch or biogas recycling.

### Ponds from 500 Square Metres to 5 Hectares

About 5 hectares provides a full affluent family income in high-value product.

Ten to twenty prawn ponds with ducks, mussels, and edge plants represent 2 full-time jobs.

Many successful enterprises never exceed this family-farm scale.

The scale permits multiple use, supplies a large area of land crop, and forms a complete fire-break for homesteads.

## Orders of Depth

Mere films of water or wet mud patches suffice for green salad and root crops, and allow bees to safely drink.

From 2-6 cm depth, smaller floating plants take root and scuds and small crustaceans flourish.

From 10-100 cm depth, true floating and anchored plants separate niches and most fish species can be reared.

The maximum commonly used depth for fish culture is 2 metres, above which unstable thermoclines can develop.

From 2-15 metres, lakes of clear water are potentially productive.

Below 15 metres, less biological cycling takes place.

## Pond Arrangements

Ponds can be arranged isolated, in parallel, in consecutive series, in order sequences, or as any combination.

They can be annidated (nested) one within each other, and some features permit linear arrangements as canals or waterways.

Depth and plants together serve as breeding refuges for the food organisms of omnivores and carnivores.

The configuration of levels at different depths has a profound effect on total yield of food organisms.

## Wall Construction

Economical sealers for pond walls include compacted clay every 20-30 metres along the wall.

Where clay is in short supply, a tamped clay cut-off core provides effective water retention.
