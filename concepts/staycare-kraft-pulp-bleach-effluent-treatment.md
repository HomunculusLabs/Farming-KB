# Kraft Pulp Bleach Effluent Treatment by White Rot Fungi

The pulp and paper industry releases large volumes of intensely colored
bleach plant effluents (BPEs) that contain chlorophenols, chlorolignols,
resin acids, guaiacols, and other environmentally harmful pollutants.
White rot fungi have shown significant promise for the biological
treatment of these complex waste streams, offering an alternative or
supplement to conventional physical and chemical treatment methods.

## Composition of Bleach Plant Effluents

Bleach plant effluents from kraft pulping contain multiple classes of
pollutants. Chlorinated compounds include chlorophenols and chlorolignols
that are toxic and persistent. Non-chlorinated resin acids, which are
diterpenoid carboxylic acids from wood, account for 0.2-0.8% of total
wood weight and contribute to effluent toxicity. Guaiacols, by-products
of the bleaching process, are persistent pollutants of both terrestrial
and aquatic ecosystems.

The high temperature and alkaline pH of these effluents during
production can kill most contaminating microorganisms, providing a
natural degree of sterility. Some effluents also contain sodium sulfite,
which has antimicrobial properties. These characteristics may favor
white rot fungi, which tolerate conditions inhibitory to many bacteria.

## Enzyme Systems in BPE Decolorization

### Phanerochaete chrysosporium

The ligninolytic white rot fungus P. chrysosporium has been known to
decolorize BPEs for some time. Manganese peroxidase (MnP) plays the
primary role in BPE decolorization by this organism. The oxidation of
BPE is mediated by Mn(III) chelated to lactate or other organic acids.
Purified P. chrysosporium MnP catalyzed BPE decolorization in the
presence of lactate, Mn(II), and hydrogen peroxide, confirming that
Mn(III)-organic acid chelates are primarily responsible for BPE
decolorization in vivo.

### Trametes versicolor

Laccases play the primary role in BPE decolorization by T. versicolor.
This organism produces laccases that, in the presence of phenolic
substrates, generate Mn(III) chelates similar to those produced by MnP.
Several laccases of T. versicolor further dechlorinate toxic
polychlorinated phenols, which are major constituents of BPEs.

## Resin Acid Degradation

Resin acids are diterpenoid carboxylic acids found in pines, spruces,
and firs that are released during pulping and contribute to effluent
toxicity. Fungi require an additional carbon source (metabolic
substrate) to degrade resin acids effectively. Most detoxification
reactions occur through hydroxylation reactions.

Mortierella isabellina can hydroxylate dehydroabietic acid, abietic
acid, and isopimaric acids. Chaetomium cochliodes transforms
dehydroabietic acid. However, transformations by several fungi may be
incomplete. Pretreatment of wood chips with fungi before pulping may
prove useful for removing toxic resins prior to the pulping process,
representing a preventive rather than remedial approach.

## Guaiacol Degradation

Guaiacols are persistent bleaching process by-products in both
terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems. The dechlorination pathway of
tetrachloroguaiacol by laccase of Coriolus (Trametes) versicolor begins
with a demethylation step producing tetrachlorocatechol. This is
subsequently dechlorinated through a series of intermediates including
2,3,5-trichloro-6-hydroxy-p-benzoquinone, 2,5-dichloro-3,6-dihydroxy-
p-benzoquinone, and dichloro-6-hydroxy-p-benzoquinone.

## Biomimetic System Potential

The elucidation of enzyme systems responsible for BPE degradation by
P. chrysosporium and T. versicolor may lead to the design of effective
biomimetic systems capable of generating chelated Mn(III) for
degradation of BPEs. Such systems could combine the advantages of
biological specificity with the reliability and controllability of
chemical processes, potentially offering industrial-scale treatment
solutions.

## Bioreactor Applications for BPE Treatment

Multiple bioreactor configurations have been applied to BPE treatment.
Packed bed bioreactors using Pycnoporus cinnabarinus achieved effective
decolorization of pigment plant effluents. Fluidized bed reactors with
immobilized fungal pellets have shown good performance for continuous
treatment. The ability to maintain active fungal biomass over prolonged
periods is important for commercial viability.

Eaton et al. considered that the combination of temperature, pH, and
nutrient limitation in kraft effluent treatment systems would be enough
to inhibit growth of most competing organisms, potentially allowing
non-aseptic operation. If competing microbes are present, they may use
added carbon sources and deny them to the slower-growing white rot
fungi.

## Advantages of Fungal Treatment

Fungal treatment of BPEs offers several advantages: simultaneous
decolorization and detoxification through multiple enzyme systems;
ability to degrade both chlorinated and non-chlorinated components;
potential for enzyme reuse and biomass regeneration; and compatibility
with existing treatment infrastructure through various bioreactor
designs. White rot fungi can also reduce chemical oxygen demand (COD)
along with color removal, addressing multiple effluent quality
parameters simultaneously.
