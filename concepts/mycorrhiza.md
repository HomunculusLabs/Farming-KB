# Mycorrhiza

Mycorrhiza (plural: mycorrhizae) is a symbiotic association between a fungus
and the roots of a plant. The term, derived from the Greek words for "fungus"
and "root," describes one of the most widespread and ecologically important
mutualisms on Earth. Approximately 90 percent of all plant species form
mycorrhizal associations, making this symbiosis fundamental to terrestrial
ecosystem function and plant evolution. The mycorrhizal symbiosis is ancient,
with fossil evidence suggesting it originated over 400 million years ago,
coinciding with the colonization of land by early plants.

## Types of Mycorrhiza

Several distinct types of mycorrhizal associations exist, classified by their
anatomical and taxonomic characteristics:

**Arbuscular Mycorrhiza (AM)** is the most common type, formed by fungi in the
phylum Glomeromycota. AM fungi penetrate root cortical cells, forming
treelike structures called arbuscules that provide the primary interface for
nutrient exchange. They also form vesicles, lipid-filled storage structures.
AM associations are found in the majority of herbaceous plants, many shrubs,
and some tropical trees. The association is estimated to be over 400 million
years old, potentially playing a role in the initial colonization of land by
plants.

**Ectomycorrhiza (ECM)** involves fungi from diverse basidiomycete and
ascomycete lineages that associate primarily with woody plants in temperate and
boreal forests. Unlike AM fungi, ECM fungi do not penetrate root cells.
Instead, they form a dense sheath (mantle) around root tips and a network of
hyphae (Hartig net) between root cortical cells. ECM fungi produce the
familiar mushroom fruiting bodies seen in forest floors. Important ECM genera
include Amanita, Boletus, Russula, Lactarius, and Cortinarius.

**Ericoid Mycorrhiza** is formed by fungi in association with plants in the
order Ericales (heaths, heathers, blueberries, rhododendrons). These fungi
penetrate root cells and form coils within them. This association is
particularly important for plants growing in nutrient-poor, acidic soils where
mineral availability is extremely limited.

**Orchid Mycorrhiza** is essential for orchid germination and early growth.
Orchid seeds lack endosperm and cannot germinate without fungal infection.
The fungal partner provides carbon and nutrients to the seedling, and in some
species, this dependency continues throughout the plant's life. Some orchids
remain entirely dependent on their fungal partners for nutrition (myco-
heterotrophy).

## Nutrient Exchange

The mycorrhizal symbiosis is fundamentally a nutrient exchange partnership:

**Plant to fungus**: The host plant supplies the fungus with carbohydrates
(primarily glucose and sucrose) produced through photosynthesis. In some
mycorrhizal associations, the plant may transfer up to 20 percent or more of
its total photosynthate to the fungal partner, representing a significant
carbon investment.

**Fungus to plant**: The fungal mycelium, which can extend far beyond the
plant's root zone, accesses soil minerals and water that would otherwise be
unavailable to the plant. The most significant contribution is phosphorus,
which is relatively immobile in soil. The fungal hyphae, being much thinner
than roots, can explore soil micropores and access phosphate that plant roots
cannot reach. Mycorrhizal fungi also supply nitrogen, potassium, calcium,
magnesium, and micronutrients such as zinc and copper.

## Ecological Significance

Mycorrhizae influence ecosystem function at multiple scales:

- **Plant community dynamics**: Mycorrhizal networks mediate plant
  competition and coexistence. Common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) can
  redistribute resources among plants of different species, potentially
  reducing competitive exclusion and maintaining diversity.
- **Forest health**: ECM fungi are critical for tree health and forest
  regeneration. Seedlings establishing near mature trees benefit from shared
  mycorrhizal networks that provide access to water and nutrients.
- **Soil structure**: Mycorrhizal hyphae bind soil particles together and
  produce glomalin, a sticky glycoprotein that contributes to soil aggregate
  stability and carbon sequestration.
- **Carbon cycling**: Mycorrhizal fungi are a major sink for plant carbon.
  Recent estimates suggest that mycorrhizal pathways transfer more carbon
  into soil than all other soil processes combined.

## Agriculture and Horticulture

In agricultural systems, mycorrhizal associations can reduce fertilizer
requirements, improve drought tolerance, and enhance disease resistance.
However, intensive agricultural practices including tillage, high phosphorus
fertilization, and monoculture cropping can reduce mycorrhizal colonization.
Interest in mycorrhizal inoculants as sustainable agricultural amendments
has grown significantly, with commercial products available for various crop
types.

## Evolution and Paleobotany

The mycorrhizal symbiosis has a deep evolutionary history. Fossil evidence
from the Rhynie Chert (approximately 400 million years old) shows that early
land plants such as Aglaophyton already possessed arbuscular mycorrhiza-like
associations with fungi. This ancient partnership likely played a critical
role in enabling plants to colonize terrestrial environments, as the fungal
partner would have provided access to mineral nutrients in the nutrient-poor
soils of early land surfaces.

The evolution of ectomycorrhiza is more recent, estimated at approximately
150 to 200 million years ago, coinciding with the diversification of
conifers and the rise of forest ecosystems in temperate regions. The
independent evolution of ECM associations in multiple fungal lineages
represents a remarkable example of convergent evolution toward a similar
ecological function.
