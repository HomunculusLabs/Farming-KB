## Mycorrhiza

Mycorrhiza (plural: mycorrhizae) is a symbiotic association between a fungus and the roots of a plant.
The term, derived from the Greek words for "fungus" and "root," describes one of the most widespread and ecologically important mutualisms on Earth.
Approximately 90 percent of all plant species form mycorrhizal associations, making this symbiosis fundamental to terrestrial ecosystem function and plant evolution.
The mycorrhizal symbiosis is ancient, with fossil evidence suggesting it originated over 400 million years ago, coinciding with the colonization of land by early plants.

## Nutrient Exchange

The mycorrhizal symbiosis is fundamentally a nutrient exchange partnership.
The host plant supplies the fungus with carbohydrates (primarily glucose and sucrose) produced through photosynthesis.
In some mycorrhizal associations, the plant may transfer up to 20 percent or more of its total photosynthate to the fungal partner, representing a significant carbon investment.
The fungal mycelium extends far beyond the plant's root zone, accessing soil minerals and water that would otherwise be unavailable.
The most significant contribution is phosphorus, which is relatively immobile in soil.
The fungal hyphae, being much thinner than roots, can explore soil micropores and access phosphate that plant roots cannot reach.
Mycorrhizal fungi also supply nitrogen, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients such as zinc and copper.

## Evolution and Paleobotany

The mycorrhizal symbiosis has a deep evolutionary history.
Fossil evidence from the Rhynie Chert (approximately 400 million years old) shows that early land plants such as Aglaophyton already possessed arbuscular mycorrhiza-like associations with fungi.
This ancient partnership likely played a critical role in enabling plants to colonize terrestrial environments.
The evolution of [[ectomycorrhiza]] is more recent, estimated at approximately 150 to 200 million years ago, coinciding with the diversification of conifers.
The independent evolution of ECM associations in multiple fungal lineages represents a remarkable example of convergent evolution toward a similar ecological function.

## Climate Change Implications

Climate change may significantly alter mycorrhizal function and distribution.
Elevated atmospheric CO2 generally increases carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi, potentially enhancing nutrient uptake.
However, warming temperatures and altered precipitation patterns can shift the composition of mycorrhizal communities, with unknown consequences for ecosystem productivity.
Understanding these dynamics is critical for predicting how forests and grasslands will respond to ongoing environmental change.
