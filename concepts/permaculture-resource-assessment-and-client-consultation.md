# Permaculture Resource Assessment and Client Consultation

Resource assessment is one of the most valuable services a permaculture designer provides, often more impactful than the design itself. Before any layout or planting plan is drawn, the designer must systematically inventory every resource available on and around the site — earth resources, biological resources, energy resources, and social resources. Mollison states that a thorough resource assessment alone justifies the design fee, even before any layout work begins. "You will earn your fee."

## Categories of Site Resources

### Earth Resources
- **Water**: Potable water is a saleable commodity. Clients with clean springs or wells may bottle and sell water, especially as city consumers increasingly purchase bottled water from rural sources. Mollison observes city health enthusiasts "staggering upstairs with two great bottles of water that they bought from some farmer." However, in areas affected by acid rain, "to sell water would be selling acid." Water quality must be tested before any commercial exploitation. Water rights must also be verified — hillside landowners typically own the water on their property, but this should be confirmed legally.
- **Clay and Minerals**: Clay deposits have commercial value for ceramics, building, and landworks. Designers should advise clients to secure miner's rights to clay and any other subsoil minerals before external interests claim them. The cost of staking a claim is minimal compared to the value of the resource.
- **Topsoil and Rock**: Even seemingly unproductive features like quarries can be transformed. Mollison suggests building "a hanging garden in his quarry — another wonder of Ninevah." Rock serves [[bamboo-as-building-material]], thermal mass, and landscape feature.
- **Gold and Mining Deposits**: A client of Mollison's discovered he had a gold mine and another mine on his property that he thought was "just a hole in the ground." The leases had lapsed through the mines department. The client might "do a little part-time gold mining, while making his pond." This illustrates how even ostensibly known properties can harbor unrecognized resources.

### Energy Resources
- **Wind Power**: Wind sites are valuable, and in some jurisdictions (notably the United States), wind power is classified similarly to mining resources. Multinational companies can stake out wind sites on private land. Designers must ensure clients file permits to exploit wind resources on their own property before external entities claim them. "It costs little to do this."
- **Biomass**: Forests represent stored energy. Managed forests produce far more biomass than unmanaged ones held at "a crowded standstill, with a slight accretion value." Removing dead wood by hand preserves soil structure (fire removes topsoil by up to 12 inches), while also reducing catastrophic fire risk. Additional management through swaleing and selective planting can produce "a lot more biomass" than the natural state.
- **Wood, Oil, and Gas**: Any combustible or extractable energy resource on the property should be catalogued and its exploitation rights secured.

### Biological Resources
- **Plant Resources**: Existing vegetation — from timber to medicinal herbs — represents capital. The designer must help the client see the value in plants they may take for granted. Forest management can dramatically increase the standing biomass and productive capacity of woodlands.
- **Animal Resources**: Wildlife and livestock potential should be assessed for both current use and future enterprise. Even wild animal populations represent a resource (as food, as pest controllers, as indicators of ecosystem health).
- **Insect Life**: Pollinators [[hemenway-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insects]] constitute a critical and often overlooked resource. Their presence indicates ecological health and supports all plant production.

### Social and Extrinsic Resources
Resources outside the property boundary are frequently missed by consultants. Mollison calls these "resources extrinsic to the site, resources that lie in the district":
- **Market resources**: District-level demand for products. Mollison notes the local area was "short of tanks" — anyone selling tanks the following year could do well. The same logic applies to permaculture equipment, soil conditioners, and other useful goods. "Just print a pamphlet and leave one here and there; you don't have to have a tank."
- **Waste products**: Nearby industrial or agricultural wastes that could serve as inputs (compost materials, greywater, organic matter). As the designer "comes and goes to the job," these district-level resources should be noted.
- **Unfulfilled demands**: Gaps in the local economy the client could fill. "There might be a need for such an item as a soil conditioner."
- **Distribution and manufacturing rights**: Securing rights to distribute or manufacture useful equipment provides additional income streams beyond the land itself. "At least, have the distributor's rights; also, if possible, the manufacturing rights."

## The Designer's Approach to Resource-Blind Clients

Many clients fail to see the resources on their own land, instead engaging in "little enterprises that bring him only a few dollars a year." The designer's first job is to open their eyes to what they already possess. Even clients who choose not to live from their land should understand its resource value — "somebody else might care to lease them." The client should "take out the rights to the resources on his property" as a protective measure, regardless of whether they intend to exploit those resources immediately.

## The Rich Client Warning

Mollison offers blunt advice about wealthy clients: "If you get a rich client, watch it! Keep your mouth shut until you get things together and can present your report properly." A rich client with a bulldozer can cause enormous damage in hours if given premature advice. Mollison recounts a colleague who casually mentioned a diversion drain; the client immediately "got out his bulldozer and charged uphill at about 25 degrees and put an incredibly bad canal around the hill." Design recommendations must be presented as complete, considered plans — never as offhand suggestions to clients who can act on them instantly.

## Ethical Boundaries

If a client refuses to follow [[mollisonian-permaculture-principles]], the designer should recommend they hire someone else — "like the agriculture department, or a forestry commissioner, who will tell him how to cut his forest." Permaculture designers work on an ethical basis: increasing soil fertility, building stored energy, and working with natural systems rather than extracting from them. The designer's role is to "look out for the site, increasing the number of ways in which the site stores energy, increasing soil productivity, and [[building-soil-fertility-long-term]], rather than taking it out." "If your client won't go along with some of these things, you can tell him that he wants somebody else."

## Legal Considerations

The designer must investigate and advise on:
- **Water rights**: Does the client need a license to sell water? Does he own runoff on his hillside? "If he doesn't, he had better take out a right to the water on his land before he gets bypassed."
- **Mineral rights**: Have previous leases lapsed? Could dormant mining charters be revived by external parties? "I doubt it" that a license is needed to sell water from one's own land, but certainty is worth establishing.
- **Seashore rights**: Clients on coasts may need special permits to collect seaweed, driftwood, or shells.
- **Covenants**: "A good lawyer" should verify no restrictive covenants exist on land before purchase.
- **Permits**: Building permits, water extraction permits, wind energy permits — all should be secured proactively.

## Forest Management as Resource Strategy

Forest management offers a case study in resource thinking. Whether dead wood is removed for use or left to burn in catastrophic fire is a design decision with enormous consequences. Hand removal preserves soil (no 12-inch drop), provides fuel or mulch, and reduces fire intensity. Active management through swaleing, selective planting, and biomass harvesting produces far more than the natural "crowded standstill." The forest becomes not a static resource but an actively growing one. "You can allow the forest to remain, as at present, at a crowded standstill... or you can manage it to produce much more biomass."

## Increasing the Ways a Site Stores Energy

The overarching goal of resource assessment is to identify every avenue by which the site captures, stores, and cycles energy. Each resource identified is a potential energy pathway — water flowing through swales stores gravitational energy, forests store solar energy as biomass, wind represents kinetic energy that can be harvested. The designer's task is to "increase the number of ways in which the site stores energy" by identifying and connecting these pathways into an integrated system.
