## Homestead First Aid Guide

Homesteads present a wider range of hazards than typical households: power tools, chainsaws, livestock, [[jadam-soil-compaction-heavy-machinery]], venomous animals, open flames, and remote locations where professional help may be delayed. A comprehensive first aid approach is essential.

## Building a Homestead First Aid Kit

Your kit should be larger and more comprehensive than a standard household kit. Store it in a waterproof container, clearly labeled, and keep a smaller version in vehicles and outbuildings.

### Wound Care
- Sterile gauze pads (4x4 and larger abdominal pads)
- Adhesive bandages (multiple sizes)
- Medical tape (paper and cloth)
- Elastic bandages (ACE wraps, 2, 3, 4 inch)
- Butterfly closures and Steri-Strips
- Wound irrigation syringe (60cc with 18ga tip)
- Betadine or chlorhexidine solution
- Triple antibiotic ointment
- Sterile saline for irrigation
- Tourniquet (CAT tourniquet) and training on its use
- Hemostatic gauze (QuikClot or equivalent)

### Burns
- Burn gel and burn dressings
- Sterile non-adherent pads
- Wraps for securing dressings

### Fractures and Sprains
- SAM splint (moldable aluminum)
- Triangular bandages
- Instant cold packs
- Ace bandages (various widths)

### Medications
- Ibuprofen and acetaminophen
- Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) — for allergic reactions and insect stings
- Epinephrine auto-injector — if anyone on the property has known severe allergies
- Hydrocortisone cream
- Loperamide (anti-diarrheal)
- Oral rehydration salts
- Aspirin (for suspected heart attack — 325mg chewable)

### Tools and Supplies
- Trauma shears
- Tweezers (pointed and slant)
- Thermometer
- CPR pocket mask
- Disposable gloves (nitrile, non-latex)
- Biohazard bags
- Flashlight with extra batteries
- Emergency blankets (mylar)

## Common Homestead Injuries

### Cuts and Lacerations
The most common workshop injury. Stop bleeding with direct pressure. Irrigate thoroughly — the most important step for preventing infection. Close with Steri-Strips or butterfly closures if the wound edges approximate cleanly. Deep wounds, wounds that will not stop bleeding, or wounds over joints require professional evaluation.

### Chainsaw and Axe Injuries
High risk of deep tissue damage. Apply direct pressure immediately. Tourniquets are appropriate for arm or leg wounds with uncontrolled bleeding. These injuries almost always require professional medical attention. Know the location of the nearest emergency room and the fastest route before you need it.

### Animal Injuries
Livestock kicks, bites, and crushing injuries are common. Clean wounds thoroughly — animal mouths carry heavy bacterial loads. Watch for signs of infection (redness, swelling, warmth, red streaking) for 72 hours after any animal-related injury. See  for species-specific handling safety.

### Burns
Common around wood stoves, [[rocket-stove]], [[mud-oven]], and during canning. Cool burns with running water for 20 minutes (not ice). Do not pop blisters. Cover with a non-adherent dressing. Burns larger than 3 inches, on hands/face/genitals, or that appear charred or white need immediate medical attention.

## Herbal and Natural Remedies

Many homesteaders incorporate [[childrens-herbal-medicine]] as a complement to conventional first aid. These are not replacements for professional care but can be useful for minor issues:

- **Plantain (Plantago spp.)** — crush leaves and apply to insect stings, minor wounds, and rashes. Anti-inflammatory and mildly antimicrobial.
- **Yarrow ([[achillea-millefolium]])** — dried leaves or powder sprinkled on wounds to stop bleeding. Has documented astringent and antiseptic properties.
- **[[calendula-salve-making]]** — promotes wound healing and reduces inflammation. Apply to minor cuts, scrapes, and burns after initial treatment.
- **Honey (raw, medical grade)** — applied topically to wounds, honey has antibacterial properties and promotes healing. Manuka honey has the most research behind it.
- **Lavender [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]]** — diluted and applied to minor burns for pain relief. A few drops on a cloth can help with headache or mild anxiety.
- **Echinacea tincture** — may support immune response at early signs of infection. Not a substitute for antibiotics when clearly indicated.

Always document any herbal or natural remedy use alongside conventional treatments in your health records.

## Snake Bites

Know the venomous snakes in your region before you need this information. In [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]]: rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths, and coral snakes are the primary concerns.

**Do:**
- Call 911 or your local emergency number immediately
- Keep the person calm and still
- Keep the bitten limb at or slightly below heart level
- Remove rings and watches from the affected limb (swelling will occur)
- Note the snake's appearance if safely possible (color, size, head shape)

**Do NOT:**
- Apply a tourniquet
- Cut the wound or attempt to suck out venom
- Apply ice
- Use commercial snake bite kits (they do more harm than good)
- Attempt to catch or kill the snake

Antivenom is the only effective treatment. Time is critical — get to a hospital. Many hospitals in snake-prone areas stock antivenom.

## When to Call for Help

On a homestead, you may be farther from help. Err on the side of calling:

- Uncontrolled bleeding after 10 minutes of direct pressure
- Signs of internal bleeding (coughing blood, dark tarry stool, abdominal rigidity)
- Suspected fractures that cannot be splinted or that involve the spine
- Chest pain, difficulty breathing, or signs of stroke (FAST: Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call)
- Severe allergic reaction (throat swelling, difficulty breathing) — administer epinephrine if available
- Deep puncture wounds (especially from animal bites or dirty objects)
- Any wound that shows signs of infection
- Snake bite, spider bite from a known dangerous species, or multiple bee stings
- Heat stroke (core temp above 104F, confusion, loss of consciousness)

## Preparedness Context

A well-stocked first aid kit is one pillar of overall [[emergency-preparedness]]. Pair your medical supplies with training and a clear communication plan. For tools and environments where injuries commonly occur, see [[homestead-workshop-tools]] for safety equipment recommendations.
- Hypothermia (shivering stops, confusion, drowsiness)

## Training Recommendations

- **Wilderness First Responder (WFR)** — 70-80 hour course, gold standard for remote area medicine. Certifications typically last 2-3 years.
- **Stop the Bleed** — free 1-hour course on hemorrhage control with tourniquets
- **CPR/AED certification** — renewal every 2 years, widely available through Red Cross and American Heart Association
- **First Aid** — basic 4-8 hour course, minimum baseline knowledge

Keep certification cards accessible and post emergency numbers (911, poison control, nearest hospital, veterinarian) at every phone and in every outbuilding.
