# Permaculture Plant Species Botanical Index (A–M)

## Why Botanical Nomenclature Matters in Permaculture

Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual* organizes its extensive species appendix by scientific (botanical) name rather than common name. This is not mere academic formality — it is a practical necessity. Common names vary wildly across regions, languages, and cultures: "macadamia" means nothing to a Brazilian farmer, while "nogueira" tells a Portuguese speaker exactly which tree is meant. Botanical names provide a universal, stable reference that transcends these barriers, ensuring that designers worldwide can communicate unambiguously about the species they deploy.

Beyond clarity, botanical nomenclature encodes **evolutionary relationships**. The genus name groups species that share fundamental traits — growth habit, root structure, flower morphology, and ecological role. When a permaculture designer learns that *Acacia* species are nitrogen-fixing legumes, that knowledge transfers to every member of the genus. Understanding these family-level relationships is essential for guild design, succession planning, and predicting how plants will interact in polycultures.

## Major Plant Families Represented (A–M)

The first half of Mollison's index spans an enormous range of functional groups. The most heavily represented families include:

### Fabaceae (Legume Family)

Legumes are arguably the most important family in permaculture due to their nitrogen-fixing symbiosis with Rhizobium bacteria. The A–M section is dense with them:

- **Acacia** — A vast genus of nitrogen-fixing trees and shrubs. *A. melanoxylon* (Blackwood) provides premium timber; *A. dealbata* (Silver wattle) yields flowers for bee forage and bark for tanning; *A. mangium* and *A. auriculiformis* are fast-growing tropical species used in agroforestry and land reclamation; *A. sophorae* (Boobala) stabilizes coastal dunes.
- **Albizia** — Fast-growing leguminous shade trees, valuable in tropical agroforestry as canopy nitrogen contributors.
- **Caragana arborescens** (Siberian pea shrub) — A cold-hardy, wind-tolerant legume producing edible seeds and fixing nitrogen in temperate systems. A staple in windbreak hedgerows.
- **Cajanus cajan** (Pigeon pea) — A multipurpose tropical legume providing food, fodder, green manure, and erosion control. Deep taproots break compacted subsoil.
- **Calliandra** — Tropical leguminous shrub used extensively in alley cropping for biomass production and soil improvement.
- **Leucaena leucocephala** — Among the most productive multipurpose legume trees, used for fodder, fuelwood, mulch, and shade. Its leaf protein content makes it a critical livestock feed in the tropics.
- **Gliricidia sepium** — A live-fencing staple and shade tree for tropical crops. Fast-growing, coppices readily, and fixes significant nitrogen.
- **Lablab purpureus** — A vigorous climbing legume used as a cover crop, green manure, and edible bean in tropical systems.
- **Lupinus** (Lupin) — Temperate nitrogen-fixers used as green manures, with some species producing edible seeds rich in protein.
- **Glycine max** (Soya bean) — The world's most important grain legume; fixes nitrogen and provides high-protein food and fodder.
- **Lens culinaris** (Lentils) — Cool-season food legumes that improve soil nitrogen while producing a protein-dense staple crop.
- **Crotolaria** (Rattle pod) — Often used as a green manure and cover crop; some species are valuable in pest-disruption strategies.

### Poaceae (Grass Family)

Grasses form the backbone of ground-cover strategies, erosion control, and grazing systems:

- **Bambusa / Dendrocalamus** (Bamboo) — Among the most versatile plants in permaculture: building material, erosion control, windbreak, edible shoots, biomass production, and water management. Bamboos are technically grasses and demonstrate the extraordinary productivity possible within this family.
- **Agropyrum elongatum** (Tall wheatgrass) — Salt-tolerant perennial grass for rehabilitation of saline soils.
- **Agrostis** (Bent grass) — Fine turf and groundcover species used in wetland edges and lawns.
- **Ammophila** (Beach grass) — Primary dune stabilizer; its rhizomatous growth traps sand and builds coastal landforms.
- **Brachiaria mutica** (Para grass) — High-yielding tropical forage grass suited to wet, poorly drained sites.
- **Buchloe dactyloides** (Buffalo grass) — Low-input native turf grass for dryland North American systems.
- **Chloris gayana** (Rhodes grass) — Drought-hardy tropical pasture grass, excellent for erosion control on slopes.
- **Cortaderia selloana** (Pampas grass) — Ornamental and windbreak grass; provides biomass and wildlife habitat.
- **Cymbopogon citratus** (Lemongrass) — Culinary herb and companion plant that repels certain pests in tropical gardens.
- **Dactylis glomerata** (Cockfoot/Orchard grass) — Productive temperate pasture grass, tolerant of shade and drought.
- **Digitaria decumbens** (Pangola) — Creeping tropical grass for erosion-prone banks and grazing.
- **Hordeum** (Barley) — Grain crop and quick-growing green manure; sea barley species handle saline conditions.
- **Imperata cylindrica** (Blady grass) — Notorious invasive in some contexts but a thatching and erosion-control grass where managed.
- **Lolium** (Rye grass) — Important temperate pasture species; fast-establishing for sward repair.
- **Imperata cylindrica** (Blady grass) — A problematic grass in some contexts but useful for thatch and land stabilization where controlled.

### Myrtaceae

- **Eucalyptus** — A massive genus of Australian trees used for timber, windbreaks, bee forage, and essential oils. Species selection is critical: some are aggressive water users while others are appropriate for specific site conditions. River red gum and Blue gum are widely referenced.
- **Corymbia/CALLIANDRA relatives** — The Myrtaceae family includes many aromatic, nectar-rich species important for pollinator support.

### Cucurbitaceae (Cucumber/Gourd Family)

- **Cucurbita** (Pumpkin/Squash) — Heavy-producing ground covers in the "three sisters" polyculture; large leaves suppress weeds and fruits provide calorie-dense storage food.
- **Cucumis sativus** (Cucumber) and **C. melo** (Melons) — Warm-season climbing or trailing vegetables.
- **Lagenaria** (Gourds) — Hard-shelled gourds used as containers, floats, and musical instruments; a multi-century permaculture utility plant.

### Solanaceae

- **Lycopersicon lycopersicum** (Tomato) — One of the most widely grown food plants; demonstrates the importance of crop rotation away from other Solanaceae to avoid soil-borne disease buildup.
- **Capsicum** (Peppers) — Often intercropped with legumes and basil in tropical guilds.

### Arecaceae (Palm Family)

- **Cocos nucifera** (Coconut) — The quintessential multi-use tropical palm: food, oil, fiber, timber, thatch, and microclimate creation.
- **Areca catechu** (Betelnut palm) — Cultural and economic significance in South/Southeast Asia.
- **Arenga pinnata** (Sugar palm) — Produces sugar, sap, and fiber; a productive understory palm in tropical food forests.
- **Elaeis guineensis** (Oil palm) — Important for oil production in tropical systems, though large-scale monocultures raise ecological concerns. In permaculture, integration with mixed agroforestry is preferred.
- **Hyphaene thebaicus** (Doum palm) — Desert-adapted palm providing food, fiber, and shade in arid African systems.

## Companion Herbs and Accumulators

The index includes many smaller species critical to guild composition:

- **Allium** species (Onion, Garlic, Chives, Leeks, Shallots) — Pest-repellent companions; many permaculture guilds place alliums at the herbaceous layer to deter aphids and other pests.
- **Artemisia absinthium** (Wormwood) — Aromatic pest deterrent; used as a boundary plant and companion herb.
- **Lavandula** (Lavender) — Bee forage; pest-repellent aromatic; hedgerow component.
- **Coriandrum sativum** (Coriander) — Culinary herb that attracts beneficial insects when allowed to flower.
- **Anethum graveolens** (Dill) — Attracts predatory wasps; companion to brassicas in pest-management guilds.
- **Alyssum** — Groundcover that attracts hoverflies, key predators of aphids.
- **Laurus nobilis** (Laurel/Bay) — Culinary evergreen; hedgerow component in Mediterranean climates.
- **Tagasaste** (*Chaemocytisus palmensis*) — Fast-growing leguminous shrub from the Canary Islands; high-protein fodder and bee forage.

## Aquatic and Edge Species

Several species in the A–M section serve specialized roles in water management zones:

- **Eichhornia crassipes** (Water hyacinth) — Extremely productive aquatic plant for nutrient stripping from greywater and sewage treatment systems. Must be managed carefully to prevent escape.
- **Colocasia esculenta** (Taro) — Edible tuber crop for wetland edges; a staple in tropical aquatic polycultures.
- **Alisma plantago-aquatica** (Water plantain) — Edge-zone plant supporting water filtration and wildlife habitat.
- **Cyperus** (Sedge/Nutgrass) — Wetland stabilizer; some species produce edible tubers (e.g., *C. esculentus*, chufa).
- **Eleocharis** (Water chestnut) — Edible aquatic tuber crop; grown in pond margins.
- **Carpobrotus** (Ice plant) — Coastal groundcover for dune stabilization and erosion control.
- **Juncus** (Rushes) — Riparian zone plants for water filtration and bank stabilization.

## Using Botanical Relationships in Guild Design

Understanding which species share a genus or family allows designers to:

1. **Predict nutrient needs** — All Brassicaceae (*Brassica* species) are heavy sulfur feeders; plan rotations accordingly.
2. **Anticipate pest and disease pressure** — Solanaceae share many pathogens; avoid consecutive plantings of tomatoes, peppers, and potatoes.
3. **Select nitrogen fixers** — Knowing the full range of leguminous genera (Acacia, Albizia, Caragana, Cajanus, Calliandra, Leucaena, Lupinus, Glycine, Lens, etc.) allows designers to choose climate-appropriate fixers for every layer and zone.
4. **Design for redundancy** — Multiple species within a genus can fill the same functional niche, providing resilience. If one fails, another may succeed.
5. **Build succession pathways** — Fast-growing pioneer legumes (Leucaena, Acacia) prepare sites for slower climax species (Chestnut, Walnut, Citrus).

## Key Permaculture Principles Reflected

The species in this index embody core permaculture principles: **every element performs multiple functions** (coconut provides food, fiber, and microclimate), **each function is supported by multiple elements** (nitrogen fixation is handled by diverse legume genera across all climate zones), and **systems are designed for biological redundancy and resilience** rather than monoculture dependency.

The A–M section alone encompasses over 130 genera spanning food crops, timber trees, fiber plants, medicinals, nitrogen fixers, pest deterrents, aquatic filters, ground covers, and livestock fodder — illustrating the extraordinary biodiversity that permaculture design draws upon.
