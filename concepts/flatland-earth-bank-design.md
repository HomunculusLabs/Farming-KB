## Flatland Earth Bank Design in Permaculture

## Overview

Earth bank design is a foundational technique for applying [[permaculture-principles]] on flat terrain where natural slopes are absent. On flat lands, designers must **create artificial slopes** using excavated earth to achieve the [[water-management]], [[cervantes-wind-protection-outdoor-cannabis]], privacy, and thermal benefits that sloped sites provide naturally. [[bill-mollison]] emphasized that rather than focusing solely on water surfaces in flat areas, designers should pay primary attention to the earth they move and what earth banks can accomplish.

## Core Principle: Creating Slopes Where None Exist

The fundamental challenge of flatland [[permaculture]] is the absence of natural elevation change. On a site with as little as a two-foot drop across 300 acres, there is virtually no natural water movement, no gravity-fed systems, and no downhill advantages. The permaculture solution is to **manufacture topography**:

- **Throw up earth banks** to create windbreaks, privacy screens, and [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]]
- **Excavate ponds and tanks** below grade, using the removed earth to build up banks and peninsulas
- **Construct swales** even on nearly level ground to direct and retain water
- **Raise tanks and roofs** above house level to enable gravity-fed water systems

Mollison"s guiding rule: *"Make your slopes, throw up your roofs, and throw up your tanks, while you keep your house low."*

## Earth Bank and Swale Construction

### The Fastest Windbreak

The fastest way to establish a windbreak is to grade up an **earth bank and [[swale]]**. A four to six foot earth bank provides immediate wind protection, while plantings on top of the bank (such as pampas grass or bamboo) grow quickly because of the moisture collected in the swale. This gives a "racing start" to [[windbreak-establishment-and-benefits]] compared to planting trees alone on flat ground.

### Swales on Minimal Slope

Swales function effectively even on very flat sites. The fall across a site may be minute, but the swale still intercepts water, slows its movement, and allows infiltration. Combined with earth banks, swales create micro-elevation that transforms a flat plane into a managed water landscape.

### Net and Pan Systems

On gentle slopes, a **net and pan** system can be employed. Very shallow guide lines for drainage are hand-cut, vegetation is cleared at junctions, and small flat planting platforms are created. This is a low-technology, small-scale approach suitable for areas where machinery is impractical.

## Site Planning Principles for Flat Terrain

### Wind Analysis

Before designing, determine the directional characteristics of winds on the site:

- **Winter cold winds** — typically come from off-sun directions (northeast or northwest depending on continent)
- **Summer hot winds** — come from on-sun directions (interior, southwest in New England)
- **Cooling winds** — arrive at roughly 45 degrees from coastal summer winds
- Look for any **deflection** of these patterns caused by local topography

Local knowledge from longtime residents is invaluable for this assessment.

### Fire Considerations

Flat areas are not immune to fire. Earth banks serve as excellent **radiation shields** from fire. Dams with earth banks provide dual function as water sources and fire barriers. Key fire defense elements on flat sites include earth walls, water access roads, and short grazing systems. Note that electrical pumps are typically among the first things to fail during a fire, making gravity-flow water systems critical.

## House Placement Strategies

### Single-Story House Surrounded by Earth Bank

The classic flatland solution: a single-story house set low, surrounded by earth banks on all sides. This provides:

- Complete privacy from surrounding landscape
- Excellent noise insulation (especially from roads)
- Wind shelter from all directions
- Thermal moderation from earth mass
- A protected courtyard environment

### Two-Story House on a Peninsula

When views are desired (e.g., of distant mountains on the shade side), a two-story house can be placed on a raised peninsula built from excavated earth. Key design points:

- **Upper rooms** should be bedrooms or low-activity spaces; **lower rooms** are service areas
- The house roof becomes a **water collector** feeding tanks elevated above the house
- The elevated position provides views without requiring tree clearing
- Surrounded on three sides by water, the house gains **light reflection** and cooling
- A shaded second-story veranda provides outdoor living space above the hot ground plane
- A two-story house is noted as "a very efficient structure for insulation"

### Cave Houses Integrated with Ponds

An innovative solution for flat alluvial plains:

- A backhoe excavates a **cave beneath the house site**
- The house is built above the cave
- The cave extends beyond the house at both ends, opening to a pond at water level
- One end is **glassed over** and trellised, creating an [[underground-greenhouse-walipini]] and shower
- The opposite end (next to the pond) serves as a **cool cave** for root [[root-vegetable-storage-guide]]
- The cave sits about a foot above dam level, keeping it dry
- The deck above overlooks the water, entered through the cave

This design provides natural heat control, with the ability to duct warm or cool air through the structure.

## Privacy and Noise Insulation

Privacy and noise can be significant problems in flat, open landscapes. Earth banks are highly effective solutions:

- A well-insulated house with earth bank protection can be "near a fairly noisy system and be quite quiet"
- Vegetation alone requires approximately **100 meters** to meaningfully absorb noise (especially traffic noise)
- Earth banks provide **immediate** noise reduction without waiting years for plantings to mature
- The combined effect of earth bank plus vegetation is far more effective than either alone

### The Road-to-Bank Rule

One main rule governs earth bank placement relative to roads: from the **crown of the road to the top of the earth bank**, there should be a straight-line deflection that clears the roof of the house. This ensures that road noise and views are fully intercepted.

## Water Diversion and Collection Systems

### Diversion Drains

Water coming onto a flat site from roadways or higher ground can be diverted around living areas using earth banks. The diverted water can be routed:

- Around ponds and back off-site
- Into below-grade tanks or lakes
- Through the system to flush out and refresh standing water

### Earth Banks as Silt and Mulch Collectors

Earth banks serve a critical water quality function:

- Floodwater flowing across the landscape carries **silt and leaf material**
- Earth banks intercept this material, accumulating it at the outside of garden walls
- The intercepted organic material becomes excellent **mulch** for gardens
- This simultaneously protects dams from silting up and provides free fertilizing material
- An "enormous amount of water" diverts through earth bank systems during rain events

### Below-Grade Water Storage

On flat sites, water is best stored **below ground in tanks** rather than in elevated dams. The excavated earth from these tanks is used to build the banks, peninsulas, and raised areas that define the site plan.

## Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Earth Bank Enclosure

A flat site with minimal fall was transformed by surrounding a single-story house with earth banks on all sides. The design included:

- Complete wind shelter from summer hot winds and winter cold winds
- Pond reflection for cooling
- Access from living areas to workshops routed through the earth bank
- Water garden integrated into the annual garden area
- A tall barn with elevated water tank completing the water system

Result: "An elegant site, a highly admired site" with total privacy and no noise.

### Case Study 2: Gully Cave House

A flat site with an eroded gully and noisy surroundings. The client had already built a dam across the gully. The permaculture solution:

- Excavated a cave beneath the house site using a backhoe
- Cave opens at both ends, one to a trellised greenhouse, one to the pond
- House built over the cave, with deck overlooking water
- Cave provides cool storage space and shower facilities
