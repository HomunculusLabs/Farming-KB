# Cloudburst and Stormwater Management in Permaculture

Intense rainfall events, known as cloudbursts, represent one of the most
destructive natural forces that permaculture designers must address. In the
Permaculture Designers Manual, Mollison devotes considerable attention to
the management of extreme water flows, recognising that a system designed
only for average rainfall will fail catastrophically during storm events.
Effective stormwater management integrates [[mollison-designers-rainwater-harvesting-swale-systems]]
principles with specific strategies for safely receiving, slowing,
spreading, and storing extreme runoff.

## Understanding the Problem

Cloudbursts deliver extraordinary volumes of water over short periods,
often exceeding the infiltration capacity of even healthy soils. On
degraded, compacted, or bare landscapes, the results are predictable:
surface runoff accelerates, concentrates in drainage lines, and carries
topsoil, organic matter, and pollutants downstream. Erosion gullies form,
infrastructure is damaged, and the water that could have sustained the
landscape through dry periods is lost to floodwater.

Mollison frames stormwater not as waste to be disposed of but as a
resource that the landscape has temporarily lost the capacity to absorb.
The design task is to restore that absorption capacity while providing
safe overflow pathways that prevent damage during events exceeding the
system's storage capacity. This philosophy underpins the entire
permaculture approach to [[mollison-designers-keyline-pattern-plow-technique]].

## The Five S's of Stormwater Management

Mollison outlines a systematic approach to stormwater based on five
principles: **Stop** the water at the highest point possible, **Slow** its
movement across the landscape, **Spread** it over the maximum area,
**Sink** it into the soil profile, and **Store** it for later use. Each
principle can be implemented through specific earthworks, planting
strategies, and structural elements.

Stopping water at the top of the catchment prevents the accumulation of
momentum and erosive energy that makes downhill water so destructive.
Swales placed on contour are the primary tool for this purpose, intercepting
runoff before it can concentrate. Slowing is achieved through roughened
surfaces, vegetated drainage ways, and check dams that reduce flow velocity.
Spreading distributes concentrated flows across broad areas, increasing
infiltration opportunity. Sinking refers to maximising soil infiltration
through aeration, organic matter addition, and [[mollison-designers-humus-building-and-soil-organic-matter]].
Storing captures excess in dams, tanks, and soil moisture reserves.

## Swales and Contour Earthworks

Swales are level trenches excavated on contour, with the excavated soil
placed on the downhill side as a berm. During normal rainfall, swales
capture and infiltrate water along their entire length. During extreme
events, they serve as distributed detention systems that reduce peak flow
rates downstream. Mollison emphasises that swales must be designed with
adequate capacity for the expected maximum rainfall intensity, with safe
spillways that direct overflow to the next swale downslope.

Swale spacing depends on slope, soil type, and rainfall intensity. On
steep slopes, swales are closer together; on gentle slopes with permeable
soils, they can be more widely spaced. The berm is planted immediately
with deep-rooted grasses, shrubs, and trees whose roots stabilise the
earthwork and enhance infiltration. Poorly designed or maintained swales
can themselves become erosion hazards if water overtops the berm
concentrated at a single point.

## Detention Basins and Retention Ponds

Where swales alone are insufficient, detention basins provide temporary
storage of excess runoff during storm events. These are shallow, broadly
excavated areas designed to fill during rain and drain slowly through
infiltration or controlled outlets. Unlike permanent dams, detention
basins are normally dry and can serve multiple functions: grazing,
recreation, or seasonal cropping during dry periods.

Retention ponds hold water permanently and provide both flood mitigation
and water supply. Mollison integrates retention ponds into the broader
landscape water plan, connecting them to swale systems, irrigation
channels, and [[mollison-designers-pond-construction-liner-sealing]] systems.
The key design principle is that all overflow pathways must be clearly
defined and vegetated to prevent erosion during extreme events that exceed
the design capacity.

## Vegetated Drainage and Riparian Buffers

Natural and constructed drainage lines must be stabilised with vegetation
to handle high-velocity flows without eroding. Mollison recommends planting
riparian buffers along all watercourses with dense, deep-rooted native
species that bind soil, filter sediments and pollutants, and slow water
movement. Bamboo, willow, and various fast-growing native grasses are
particularly effective for this purpose.

On larger properties, vegetated waterways serve as broad, shallow drainage
channels that convey overflow from swales and detention basins to the
primary drainage network. These waterways are designed to carry water as
sheet flow rather than concentrated channel flow, minimising erosive
energy. The combination of vegetated drainage with contour earthworks
creates a cascading system that progressively reduces runoff energy as
water moves down the catchment.

## Urban and Settlement Applications

Stormwater management is equally critical in urban and peri-urban
permaculture design. Impervious surfaces generate rapid, high-volume
runoff that overwhelms conventional drainage systems. Mollison advocates
for retrofitting urban landscapes with rain gardens, infiltration
trenches, green roofs, and permeable paving that restore infiltration
capacity to developed areas. At the building scale, roof catchment
systems connected to tanks and infiltration gardens capture a significant
proportion of rainfall before it reaches the stormwater network. Swales
can be incorporated into roadside verges, parking areas, and public open
space, transforming drainage infrastructure into productive landscape
elements. These urban strategies connect to
[[mollison-designers-settlement-village-design]] at the community scale.

## Emergency Overflow and Fail-Safe Design

All stormwater systems must include fail-safe overflow pathways. Mollison
insists that designers must anticipate the failure of any single element
during an extreme event and ensure that such failure does not cause
catastrophic damage. This means providing emergency spillways on all dams
and detention basins, designing swale berms with reinforced overflow points,
and ensuring that concentrated water has a clear, non-erosive path off the
property if infiltration capacity is exceeded.

The principle of controlled failure is essential: it is better for a swale
to overflow gently at a designed point than for water to find an
uncontrolled path around the end of the berm, carving a new erosion
gully through valuable productive land.
