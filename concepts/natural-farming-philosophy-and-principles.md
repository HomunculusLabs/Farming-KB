# Natural Farming Philosophy and Principles

Natural farming is a holistic agricultural philosophy that works with nature rather than against it. It encompasses several traditions worldwide, united by the principle of minimizing human intervention and respecting natural ecological processes.

## Core Philosophy

Natural farming recognizes the abundance of nature and utilizes indigenous resources for production. Its basic philosophy is to maximize the inborn potential of a life form and its harmony with the environment by not interfering with their growth and development or forcing crops to yield more than what they can. Natural farmers believe that the best way to achieve top quality yield is to respect the nature of life.

Natural farming uses methods that observe the laws of nature and utilizes natural materials and products. It is based on the principle of interdependence among all living things, aiming to have a nurturing impact on the environment, in sharp contrast to the disadvantageous effects of modernized and commercialized agriculture.

## The Four Principles (Fukuoka)

[[masanobu-fukuoka]], the Japanese farmer and philosopher, articulated four foundational principles of natural farming:

1. **No cultivation** -- Soil should not be turned or disturbed. Instead, soil is aerated and loosened by natural means only (earthworms, roots, microbial activity, etc.)
2. **No fertilizer** -- No chemical or prepared organic fertilizers are applied. Soil fertility is maintained through natural decomposition and the return of plant residues.
3. **No weeding** -- Weeds are not eliminated but managed. If a weed is not harming the desired crop, it is left alone. Weeds often serve ecological functions like protecting soil and providing habitat for beneficial organisms.
4. **No pesticides** -- No synthetic or natural pesticides are applied. Pest balance is maintained through biodiversity and healthy soil ecology.

These principles do not mean soil is never touched -- rather, disturbance comes from natural processes rather than mechanical or chemical intervention.

## Key Concepts

### Non-Intervention
Natural farming is not about doing nothing; it is about recognizing what nature already does well and allowing those processes to operate. The farmer's role shifts from controller to observer and facilitator.

### Soil as a Living System
Living soil is the basis of soil fertility. What makes a living soil is the existence of life forms like microorganisms. Without them, soil fertility and health cannot be created or sustained. In natural farming, microorganisms are fed and nurtured, not the plants directly -- the soil nurtures the plants.

### Interdependence
Natural farming applies the principle of interdependence in which people nurture nature, instead of surmounting it or undermining it. All elements of the farm ecosystem -- soil, plants, animals, microorganisms, water, sunlight -- are seen as interconnected.

### Local Adaptation
Natural farming emphasizes using locally available materials and indigenous organisms adapted to the specific regional conditions. What works in one climate or soil type may not work in another, so farmers are encouraged to experiment and observe.

### Farming with Air, Water, and Sunlight
Ninety-six percent of plant and animal growth is attributed to air, water, and sunlight. Only four percent can be attributed to nutrients and other elements. Natural farming recognizes that optimizing access to these fundamental elements is more important than nutrient supplementation.

## Strengths and Benefits

- **Environment-Friendly**: Inputs are made from natural materials, protecting crops from harmful chemicals
- **Higher Yield**: Following natural cycles with precise timing nourishes the soil with the right amounts of nutrients when plants or animals need it
- **Low Cost**: Use of homemade materials and farm resources lessens financial burden
- **High Quality**: Non-usage of harmful chemicals produces better-quality crops and livestock
- **Adaptable**: Can be replicated in any region using natural resources distinct to each ecosystem
- **Farmer/User Friendly**: Methods are free of toxic chemicals, dangerous to user health
- **Respect for Life**: Gives utmost respect and care to crops and animals following the natural cycle

## Natural Farming vs Conventional Farming

| Conventional Farming | Natural Farming |
|---|---|
| Chemical fertilizers | Indigenous microorganisms (IMOs) |
| Mechanical tillage (deep plowing) | Nature's tillers (bacteria, fungi, earthworms) |
| Expensive technology for livestock | Microbes as simple, cost-effective management |
| Pesticides to kill pests | Natural inputs to distract pests away |
| Imported/artificial microorganisms | Locally adapted indigenous microorganisms |

## Relationship to Permaculture and Organic Farming

Natural farming is similar to organic farming in that both use non-chemical ingredients. However, natural farming has its own systematic approach ranging from theory to practice. Dr. [[cho-han-kyu]], founder of [[korean-natural-farming]], is ranked alongside Masanobu Fukuoka, Rudolf Steiner, and Bill Mollison as a post-modern agriculture sage.

## Ecological Succession and Natural Patterns

Natural farming works with ecological succession rather than against it. When ground is cleared in nature, it repopulates through predictable stages -- from pioneer species to climax communities. Permaculture and natural farming seek to accelerate these natural succession processes, establishing productive ecosystems more quickly than would occur through unassisted natural regeneration.

Mathematical patterns recur throughout nature and can inform farm design. The Fibonacci sequence appears in the spiral of snail shells, the arrangement of seeds in sunflower heads, the branching patterns of oak leaves, and even in the structure of DNA. Understanding these patterns helps farmers design systems that work with natural efficiency rather than against it.

## The Sustainability Crisis

The need for natural farming arises from a broader sustainability crisis driven by population growth and industrial resource extraction. Modern industrial agriculture depletes soils, pollutes waterways, and depends on finite fossil fuel inputs. Natural farming offers an alternative that builds soil health over time, reduces external inputs, and creates more resilient food systems.

## Core Values

Three core values underpin natural farming philosophy:
- **Self-sufficiency** -- the farm should produce its own inputs (fertilizers, pest controls, seeds) rather than depending on purchased products
- **Minimal intervention** -- work with natural processes rather than imposing artificial controls; the best farming is often that which does the least
- **Observation over action** -- the farmer's primary tool is careful observation of natural systems, intervening only when necessary and in the gentlest possible way

## Indigenous Microorganisms (IMO)

A distinctive practice in Korean Natural Farming is the collection and cultivation of Indigenous Microorganisms (IMOs) -- naturally occurring bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms found in the local environment. These are collected from forest soils, leaf litter, and other natural sources, then multiplied through simple fermentation processes. IMOs are applied to soil, compost, and plant surfaces to enhance nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and overall soil health. This approach recognizes that healthy soils are living ecosystems, not inert substrates.
