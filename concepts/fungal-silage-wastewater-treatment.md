# Fungal Silage Wastewater Treatment

Silage wastewater is one of the most potent agricultural wastes, with a biological oxygen demand (BOD) that can be 300 times greater than raw domestic sewage. Fungal treatment of silage effluent represents an application of [[fungal-wastewater-treatment]] that simultaneously reduces pollution and produces potentially valuable fungal biomass.

## Background and Environmental Impact

During winter, controlled fermentation of crops of high moisture content, such as forage maize and grass, leads to the production of silage. Plant juices are expelled from the ensiled crop, and together with surface water, form silage wastewater.

The National Rivers Authority reported silage effluent to be 300 times more polluting than raw domestic sewage. Some studies indicate four times faster degradation of silage effluent than sewage, and it can be 1000 times more potent as a pollutant.

Silage wastewater is considered one of the most potent agricultural wastes, with a very high BOD value. The pH of silage effluent ranges from 3 to 5, which poses problems of corrosion to concrete and steel infrastructure.

When discharged into water bodies, the most acidic silage effluent can decrease pH and kill fish and other aquatic species. The impact of such pollution is even higher when discharge occurs in small streams with low flow and low dilution rates. Scorching of grass or other crops results from spreading of the effluent, followed by depletion of oxygen in the surrounding soil. The effluent can also find its way into surface water or groundwater.

Well-preserved silage effluent can be fed to animals as it deteriorates in quality rapidly. Feeding is recommended within 3 to 4 days after production or by anaerobic storage.

## Legislative Context

In the UK, the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food estimated the cutting of 42.3 million tons of forage crops for silage production, resulting in an estimate of 2,115 million liters of effluent. In dry years, effluent production is much lower.

The variable production of effluent causes differences in the number of pollution incidents: 25% of total agricultural water pollution in wet years compared to 14% in dry years. Between 1995 and 1998, there were 114 to 234 pollution incidents of silage effluent, accounting for 7% of organic pollution incidents.

In the UK, fines of up to 20,000 pounds or a 3-month prison sentence can be imposed for polluting water courses with silage effluent, underscoring the seriousness of this environmental problem.

## Alternative Treatment Approaches

Aerobic treatment of silage effluent results in a reduction in BOD and a change in pH. The application of membrane filtration to silage effluent has also been examined, though this is considered only a part of an integrated waste facility treatment.

Farm waste pollution has been described as a significant environmental concern requiring effective treatment solutions. The search for biological treatment methods that can handle the high pollutant load and low pH of silage effluent has led to the investigation of fungal treatment approaches.

## Fungal Treatment Performance

Several fungal species have been evaluated for silage wastewater treatment:

The yeast Torulopsis (Candida) glabrata cultivated on grass silage effluent achieved an 85% reduction in carbohydrate content. However, this yeast was dropped from further consideration due to its inability to utilize lactic acid and potential pathogenicity.

Candida utilis and T2B (Galactomyces geotrichum) are the most effective fungi identified for reducing the pollutants of silage effluents of variable age and composition. These fungi achieved significant reductions across multiple effluent types:

### COD Reduction

Both fungi achieved COD reductions of 74 to 95% across all effluent types tested. The percentage reduction was highest (95%) in more dilute and fresher effluents. In 50% diluted effluent, T2B achieved 79-95% COD reduction and C. utilis achieved 74-92% COD reduction.

### TOC Reduction

Both fungi exhibited TOC removal of 60 to 87%, with the greatest reduction in the fresher effluent. T2B achieved 78-84% TOC removal and C. utilis achieved 60-86% TOC removal across different effluent types.

### Lactic Acid Removal

Both yeasts assimilate both d- and l-lactic acids. Both yeasts removed 100% of l-lactic acid in 50% and 25% silage effluents. Both organisms removed 50 to 98% of d-lactic acid in 25% effluent and approximately 90% for C. utilis and T2B, respectively, in 50% effluent.

### Other Pollutant Removal

Both yeasts reduced varying degrees of phenolic compounds in the most acidic effluent. The increase in pH due to the removal of lactic acids and volatile fatty acids (VFAs) occurs in all treatments.

Both yeasts were effective in 82 to 99% removal of phosphate in both 25% and 50% dilutions of all silage effluents. C. utilis and T2B removed about 95 to 100% of acetic and propionic acids and 70 to 100% of four higher VFAs.

## Dominant Fungi in Silage

Penicillium roqueforti is most prevalent on bales of grass silage in Ireland, representing 52% of all isolates. Malt-yeast extract-sucrose agar (MYSA) is the medium of choice for the enumeration and isolation of fungi from silage.

## On-Farm Treatment Approach

The feasibility of a simple on-farm treatment process for silage effluent has been demonstrated. The process involves the addition of a large inoculum of yeast followed by vigorous aeration.

The dominating yeast T2B can survive and compete easily with the native microflora. The higher reductions in COD and TOC indicate the potential of a yeast treatment process to protect human health and the environment.

However, the process may not reduce the residual COD in the effluent sufficiently to discharge the effluent directly to a water course. Further treatment is required before final direct discharge. Biomass produced during this process can be considered for use as a fertilizer or soil conditioner, contributing to [[fungal-single-cell-protein-production]] from agricultural waste.

## Biomass Production

The protein content for C.utilis grown on silage effluent is approximately 160 mg/g dry weight, and for T2B is approximately 74 mg/g. Elemental analyses of the biomass grown on the more dilute effluent result in higher concentrations of nitrogen and phosphorus compared to biomass grown on more concentrated effluent.

The simultaneous production of protein-rich fungal biomass during effluent treatment adds economic value to the process and supports the principles of waste valorization in [[fungal-bioremediation]].
