## Poisonous Gilled Fungi (Lactarius, Russula, Entoloma, Hebeloma, Volvaria, Inocybe)

> Based on *Poisonous Fungi* by [[john-ramsbottom]] (1945), Keeper of Botany at the British Museum
> ([[stropharia-cubensis-identification-and-natural-history]]). Dimensions are given as cap diameter, stem height, stem width.

This page covers the non-Amanita poisonous or suspect gilled fungi described in Ramsbottom"s
survey. While the deadly *Amanita* species account for the vast majority of fatal poisonings,
several other genera contain species that cause significant illness and deserve attention from
foragers and mycologists alike. A recurring theme across these genera is the distinction between
genuine toxicity (substances that poison regardless of preparation) and irritancy (acrid or
bitter principles that are destroyed by cooking but make raw specimens dangerous). Ramsbottom
stressed that the only safe approach is to learn each species individually rather than relying on
any rule-of-thumb method.

## Lactarius torminosus — Woolly Milk Cap

**Dimensions:** Cap up to 6", stem 2", width 1"

*[[lactarius-torminosus]]* is a conspicuous milk-cap named for the dense whitish woolly felt covering its
cap when young. It exudes a white latex when broken, distinguished by a very acrid taste that
immediately warns against eating it raw. The cap is convex with a strongly inrolled margin
(concealing the gills in youth), becoming depressed at the centre with age, viscid when moist, and
ranges from rosy pink to pale orange-red (sometimes almost white) with darker concentric zones and
a paler margin. The gills are thin, decurrent, and yellowish-pink. The pale pink stem is firm and
brittle, sometimes with small superficial pits, finally hollow; the flesh is pinkish.

It fruits in summer and autumn in woods and on heaths, frequently under birches — an association
that is so reliable it serves as a [[amanita-muscaria-physical-description-field-identification]] aid. Despite its fearsome reputation among
British foragers, it is **not truly poisonous**. The acrid principle irritates the digestive tract
when eaten raw — the epithet *torminosus* means "griping" — but cooking destroys it entirely.
In Finland it is a favourite toadstool, boiled and eaten at once or salted down for winter storage.
It is also consumed in neighbouring Scandinavian and Baltic countries. In Norway it is strongly
roasted and added to coffee, a testament to the cultural acceptance of species that British
mycological tradition has wrongly condemned. This discrepancy underscores Ramsbottom"s point that
edibility data from continental Europe were often ignored in Britain due to excessive caution.

## Russula — Genus Overview

The genus *Russula* closely resembles *Lactarius* in its brittle, crumbly flesh, but **no milk is
exuded** when the fruiting body is broken — the single most reliable field character separating the
two genera. Many species are brightly coloured — reds, yellows, greens, and purples — making them
among the most visually striking woodland fungi and a frequent source of confusion for novice
foragers who assume bright colours indicate danger (a myth Ramsbottom thoroughly debunked).

The majority of *Russula* species are mild-tasting and edible, some excellent. However, acrid-
tasting species are considered poisonous and cause severe gastrointestinal irritation if consumed.
Ramsbottom noted that although the irritant substance is dispersed by thorough cooking, acrid
russulas are best avoided by casual foragers. The risk is compounded by the difficulty of
distinguishing acrid from mild species in the field without tasting — a practice that carries its
own dangers. Some classifications of the genus use taste as a primary division, reflecting its
importance as a taxonomic character. Crucially, none of the truly dangerous fungi (the deadly
*Amanita* species) has a sharp taste, so the presence of acridity, while unpleasant, does not
indicate the most lethal forms of poisoning.

## Russula emetica — The Sickener

**Dimensions:** Cap up to 4", stem 3", width 1"

*[[russula-emetica]]* is one of the best-known [[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]], its epithet meaning "emetic" — a
direct warning. The cap is convex at first, then flattened or depressed, brilliant bright red —
at first matt then shiny, fading with age to pink, yellow, or white. The cap edge is smooth when
young, later furrowed; the skin peels easily and is one of the species that "peels," demonstrating
that this character has no bearing on toxicity (since *[[amanita-phalloides]]* also peels). The gills
are shining white, more or less free and somewhat crowded; the stem is rigid, white, sometimes
pink-tinged. The flesh is firm and white, pink under the cap skin. The taste is very acrid after
a few seconds and persistent — this delayed onset of acridity is characteristic and distinguishes
it from *R. fragilis*; the odour is pleasant, which further illustrates Ramsbottom"s dictum that
smell is no guide to safety. It grows in woods through summer and autumn, with different forms
associated with beeches and conifers.

## Russula fragilis — Fragile Russula

**Dimensions:** Cap up to 2.75", stem 2", width 3/8"

*Russula fragilis* closely resembles *R. emetica* but is generally smaller with a wider colour
range and a characteristically striate cap edge that aids identification. The stem is spongy then
hollow and very fragile — the origin of its epithet — making it delicate to handle in the field.
Unlike *R. emetica*, the flesh is **not** pink under the cap skin, a useful differentiating
character. The acrid taste is immediate (though less durable than *R. emetica*), providing another
point of contrast, and the smell is pleasant. It occurs in woods during summer and autumn; the
commonest form has a red cap, but purple, yellow, and mixed colourations are also seen, some of
which have been given varietal names.

## Russula fellea — Bitter Russula

**Dimensions:** Cap up to 4", stem 2", width 3/4"

*Russula fellea* is distinguished by its entirely pale ochraceous colouration. The cap is convex then flattened, viscid, with a darker centre and thin striate margin. The gills are adnate, thin, and crowded, white at first, characteristically exuding watery drops. The stem is cylindrical and white; the flesh is firm, whitish then pale ochraceous. The taste is both very acrid and intensely bitter — among the most disagreeable of all russulas — while the odour is pleasant. Found in beech woods from late summer through autumn.

## Russula drimeia — Pungent Russula

**Dimensions:** Cap up to 4", stem 3", width 3/4"

*Russula drimeia* bears a bright purple to brown-violet cap, darker in the middle, prone to decolouration by rain. It is convex with an incurved margin at first, becoming flat or depressed, slightly viscid. The gills are adnate and somewhat crowded, citron-yellow then sulphur-yellow. The stem is solid, firm, concolorous with the cap but paler. The flesh is compact, yellowish then white, purplish under the skin. The taste is very acrid with a pleasant smell. It grows in coniferous woods in autumn. The variety *Queletii* has white gills.

## Entoloma lividum — Leaden Entoloma

**Dimensions:** Cap up to 5", stem 3", width 1"

The genus *Entoloma* is recognised by its pink spores and typically sinuate gills — characters
that immediately separate it from superficially similar species in other genera. *E. lividum* has
a tawny or greyish cap turning white in patches, hard and compact in texture, roundish and regular
at first with a thin, mealy, strongly inrolled edge, then flattened except for the central fleshy
disk, with the edge becoming irregularly raised. The skin is finely silky. The gills are yellowish
then pink, often retaining a yellow edge — a useful field mark. The shining white stem is firm,
somewhat swollen above or below, with a mealy apex bearing fine striations. The flesh is white and
fragile, with an initially agreeable meal-like smell and taste — another illustration of the
danger of relying on pleasant organoleptic characters to judge safety, as the pleasantness does not
persist.

It grows in grassy places in autumn, especially near deciduous woods. It is a **genuinely
dangerous mushroom**, causing violent sickness and diarrhoea, with occasional fatalities. Unlike
the acrid russulas, its poisonous effects are not mitigated by cooking — Ramsbottom attributes
this to "definite resinoid substances which act on the mucous lining of the intestine." Near Dijon
it is known as *le grand empoisonneur de la Côte d"Or* — "the great poisoner of the Côte d"Or" —
a local name that speaks volumes about its reputation in a region with a long mycological tradition.

## Hebeloma crustuliniforme — Crustlike Hebeloma

**Dimensions:** Cap up to 4.25", stem 2.25", width 1"

The genus *Hebeloma* has brown spores and typically sinuate gills. *H. crustuliniforme* has a convex then spreading cap with a flattened central boss, pale yellow to brown or reddish, darker at the centre, viscid when young. The gills are crowded with an uneven edge, progressing from white through clay-coloured to date-brown; in wet weather they characteristically exude drops from their edges. The short whitish stem is stuffed then hollow, thickened at the base, with white squamules at the apex turning brownish from spores. The flesh is white and firm with a distinctive radish-like smell and taste. It grows in rings in grassy woods in autumn, resembling "rolls from a baker"s oven." It is very indigestible; the radish taste persists even after cooking.

## Volvaria gloiocephala — Sticky Volvaria

**Dimensions:** Cap up to 4.5", stem 7", width 1/2"

The genus *Volvaria* is characterised by pink spores, free gills, and a membranous volva at the stem base. *V. gloiocephala* has a campanulate then spreading cap with a central prominence, glutinous in texture, smoky grey or white with a greyish disk and striate edge. The gills are crowded, white becoming salmon-pink. The long stem is white then grey or tawny, mealy at first, cylindrical, narrowed above and swollen at the base with a white to grey persistent volva. The flesh is soft and white with an unpleasant taste and smell.
It grows on grass covering rubbish heaps, straw, and manure in summer and autumn, occasionally on thatched roofs and vegetable remains in sand dunes. **Formerly regarded as deadly, it is now known to be harmless** and much eaten in Portugal and Algeria — a notable example of corrected mycological understanding.

## Inocybe patouillardi — Red Staining Inocybe

The genus *Inocybe* contains species with brown spores and fibrillose or scaly caps that are
easily overlooked on the forest floor. They are generally small, drab-coloured, and difficult to
identify without [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] — traits that make [[psilocybin-accidental-ingestion-australia]] a significant
risk. *I. patouillardi* is among the more dangerous members, noted by Ramsbottom as containing
substances that **paralyse the nervous system**, a pharmacological action it shares with *Amanita
muscaria* and *A. pantherina* in his three-category classification [[history-of-poisonous-fungi-knowledge]]. The species
bruises or stains reddish when handled, hence the common name "Red Staining Inocybe."
