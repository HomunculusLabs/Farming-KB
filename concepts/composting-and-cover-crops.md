## Composting and [[query-which-cover-crops-are-best-for-breaking-hardpan-and-compacted-soil|Cover]] Crops

## Overview

Composting and cover cropping are two of the most important practices for building and maintaining soil fertility in sustainable gardens. In *[[gardening-when-it-counts]]*, Steve Solomon treats these as complementary strategies: composting converts organic waste into stable humus and available nutrients, while cover crops (green manures) protect and improve fallow soil between vegetable crops. Together, they form the backbone of a self-renewing soil fertility system.

## Composting Fundamentals

### The Carbon-Nitrogen Ratio (C/N)

The **C/N ratio** is the single most important concept in successful composting. Different materials have different ratios:

- **High carbon (brown)** materials: straw, dry leaves, wood chips, sawdust, paper (C/N ratios of 50:1 to 500:1 or higher)
- **High nitrogen (green)** materials: fresh manure, kitchen scraps, grass clippings, green weeds (C/N ratios of 10:1 to 30:1)
- **Ideal blend**: approximately 25-30:1 overall C/N ratio

A chart comparing C/N ratios of common compostable materials is provided at page 194. Getting the ratio right ensures rapid decomposition without odor problems. Too much carbon slows decomposition to a crawl; too much nitrogen produces ammonia odors and nutrient loss.

### The Decomposition Process

Composting involves three overlapping phases:

1. **Mesophilic phase**: Moderate-temperature bacteria break down readily available sugars and proteins. The pile warms to roughly 30-40°C (86-104°F).
2. **Thermophilic phase**: Heat-loving bacteria raise pile temperatures to 50-65°C (122-149°F), killing weed seeds and pathogens. This is the active "cooking" stage.
3. **Curing phase**: As easily decomposed materials are consumed, temperatures gradually decline. Fungi and actinomycetes dominate, converting remaining materials into stable humus.

The entire process, from raw materials to finished compost, typically takes 6-18 months depending on management intensity, materials used, and climate conditions.

### Making Low-Grade Compost (Pages 194-197)

Low-grade compost is the simplest approach, suitable for gardeners who want to recycle organic waste without intensive management:

- **Materials**: Grass clippings (C/N ~20:1), mixed with higher-carbon materials like straw or autumn leaves
- **Method**: Pile materials in a heap, optionally turning occasionally. No precise measurements needed.
- **Uses**: General soil improvement, mulching, filling raised beds. Not suitable as a primary fertilizer source due to variable and often low nutrient content.

### Making Medium-Quality Compost (Pages 197-203)

Medium-quality compost requires more attention to materials and proportions:

- **Key additions**: Manure (chicken, horse, steer), comfrey leaves, coffee grounds as nitrogen boosters
- **Comfrey as compost activator**: Comfrey (page 203-204) is exceptionally valuable because its deep taproot mines subsoil minerals and concentrates them in its leaves. Adding comfrey to compost piles significantly raises mineral content.
- **Coffee grounds** (page 203): A readily available nitrogen source that also adds trace minerals. Can be obtained free from coffee shops.
- **Grinders** (page 196-197): Shredding or grinding coarse materials before composting dramatically speeds decomposition by increasing surface area.
- **Uses**: Can partially substitute for [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof]] (COF) in moderate-demand vegetable beds.

### Making Highest-Quality Compost (Page 207)

The highest-quality compost approaches the nutrient density needed to fully support vegetable growth. This requires:

- Careful balancing of C/N ratio using measured inputs
- Inclusion of mineral-rich materials (rock dusts, kelp meal, comfrey)
- Proper [[mushroom-bed-moisture-management]] throughout the process
- Adequate aeration through turning or ventilation
- Complete thermophilic decomposition to stabilize nutrients

### Sheet Composting (Pages 186-189, 190)

Sheet composting (also called sheet mulching or lasagna gardening) bypasses the pile entirely:

- Organic materials are spread directly on the soil surface in layers
- Green and brown materials alternate, mimicking the layered C/N approach of pile composting
- Decomposition occurs in place, gradually building soil organic matter
- Particularly useful for converting sod or weedy ground to garden beds without digging

Sheet composting is less labor-intensive than pile composting but slower, and the nutrient release is more gradual and less predictable.

### Compost Teas (Pages 155-156, 204, 236-237)

Compost tea is a liquid extract made by steeping finished compost in water:

- **[[actively-aerated-compost-tea]]**: Brewed with forced aeration to encourage beneficial microorganism growth
- **Non-aerated tea**: Simple steeping, useful primarily as a mild liquid fertilizer
- **Kelp teas** (pages 24, 161, 220, 236): Add [[comparison-seaweed-extract-vs-fish-hydrolysate]] for trace minerals and natural growth stimulants
- **Applications**: Foliar feeding (spraying on leaves), fertigation (applying through irrigation), or direct soil drench

Elaine Ingham's work on soil microbiology (referenced at page 236-237) has advanced understanding of how compost teas can introduce beneficial [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] to soil and plant surfaces.

### Wood Compost (Pages 198, 202)

Composting woody materials (tree prunings, wood chips, sawdust) requires special consideration:

- Very high C/N ratios (200:1 to 500:1+) mean extremely slow decomposition
- Best composted separately or in very small proportions with high-nitrogen materials
- Nitrogen robbery: adding raw wood chips to soil can temporarily immobilize soil nitrogen as microbes decompose the wood
- Partially decomposed wood makes excellent long-lasting mulch for perennial plantings

### Manure in Composting (Pages 189-190, 199, 200, 204-207)

Manure is one of the most valuable compost ingredients, but different types vary significantly:

| Manure Type | NPK (approx.) | C/N Ratio | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chicken | 1.1-1.5 / 0.8 / 0.5 | 7-10:1 | Hot; rich in nitrogen; may burn if used fresh |
| Horse | 0.5-0.7 / 0.3 / 0.6 | 20-25:1 | Good all-around; may contain weed seeds |
| Steer | 0.7 / 0.3 / 0.5 | 15-20:1 | Widely available; moderate nutrient content |
| Rabbit | 2.0 / 1.0 / 0.8 | ~15:1 | Very concentrated; excellent for compost |
| Humanure | Variable | Variable | Requires careful thermophilic composting |

**Sawdust** (pages 188, 194, 198, 206-207, 231-232) is often mixed with manure from stabled animals, [[query-which-drought-tolerant-legumes-are-best-for-cover-crops-and-grain|which]] helps absorb moisture and ammonia while providing carbon.

## Cover Crops (Green Manures) (Pages 209-215)

### What Cover Crops Do

Cover crops serve multiple simultaneous functions in the garden:

1. **Soil protection**: [[query-how-do-i-garden-on-a-slope-and-prevent-erosion]] from wind and rain during fallow periods
2. **Weed suppression**: Dense growth shades out weed seeds and competing vegetation
3. **Nutrient capture**: Deep-rooted species (like buckwheat) mine leached nutrients from subsoil
4. **Nitrogen fixation**: Leguminous cover crops (clovers, peas, beans) convert atmospheric nitrogen to plant-available forms through symbiosis with rhizobia bacteria
5. **Organic matter addition**: When turned under, cover crop biomass decomposes to add humus
6. **Soil structure improvement**: Root growth creates channels for water infiltration and root penetration
7. **Pest disruption**: Breaking crop cycles with unrelated cover crops reduces pest and disease carryover

### Common Cover Crop Species

- **Buckwheat** (pages 213-214): Fast-growing summer cover; excellent for quick soil improvement; attracts [[beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]]; dies at first frost, leaving easy-to-incorporate residue
- **Clovers** (pages 209, 214): Perennial or annual legumes; fix atmospheric nitrogen; low-growing varieties can serve as living mulch
- **Grass** (page 209, 214): Perennial grass sod can be used as a long-term cover; provides erosion control but may require significant effort to kill before replanting

### Integration with Vegetable Cropping

Cover crops fit into garden rotations in several ways:

- **Winter cover**: Sown after summer vegetable harvest, protects soil through winter, turned under in spring before planting
- **Summer cover**: Grown between spring and fall vegetable crops; buckwheat is ideal for short summer windows of 4-6 weeks
- **Inter-cropping**: Low-growing covers grown between or beneath vegetable rows
- **Fallow year recovery**: A full season of cover crop growth to restore depleted soil (page 283, 301)

### Turning Under Cover Crops

The method and timing of incorporating cover crops affects nutrient release:

- **Timing**: Turn under 2-4 weeks before planting the next crop to allow partial decomposition
- **Method**: Use a sharp spade to cut and invert the cover crop, or mow and till
- **[[green-manure-crops]]** (pages 209-215): Leguminous cover crops are most valuable when turned under at peak flowering, when biomass and nitrogen content are highest

## Compost and Insects (Pages 183, 185, 198)

A healthy compost pile supports a complex ecosystem of beneficial organisms:

- **Predatory insects** in compost (ground beetles, rove beetles, centipedes) help control garden pests by providing habitat and alternative food sources
- Properly managed hot composting kills most pest organisms and disease pathogens through sustained high temperatures
