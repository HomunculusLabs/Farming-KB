# Winter Harvesting Philosophy and the Movable Greenhouse

## Core Philosophy

Coleman's winter-harvest system at Four Season Farm is built on the insight that fresh vegetables can be produced year-round [[query-what-is-succession-planting-and-how-do-i-plan-a-succession-planting-schedule]]** — sowing vegetables multiple times from late summer through late fall to provide continual harvest

This prevents the buildup of pests, diseases, and excess soil nutrients that plague permanent greenhouses.
- **Green manure opportunity**: During the uncovered year, growers can plant long-term, deep-rooting leguminous [[mobile-greenhouses]] because of their enormous potential for soil health.

Coleman calls the movable greenhouse "the best new gardening idea of the twenty-first century" — or more accurately, a "rediscovered new idea."

## The Second Spring: Succession Planting Timeline

Coleman's winter-harvest system begins planting on August 1, which he calls the "second spring." The planting season for winter harvest extends from August 1 through late fall and into winter. This reversed timing — where day length is contracting rather than expanding and temperatures are falling rather than rising — makes the choice of precise sowing dates far more crucial than for spring planting. Dates are also very crop-specific.

The guiding principle is to get plants almost to maturity before the day length becomes shorter than ten hours per day. Once the ten-hour threshold is crossed, plant growth slows dramatically. The strategy then shifts to maintaining mature plants in a harvestable state through the coldest months, relying on the twice-tempered climate of the greenhouse plus row cover to keep them alive.

During midwinter, the vigorous regrowth on cut-and-come-again crops (like spinach, arugula, and claytonia) provides the harvest, while late-fall and winter-sown crops slowly reach productive size. Coleman aims to never leave a greenhouse bed unplanted — within twenty-four hours after a crop is harvested, the residues are removed, the soil is re-prepared, and the bed is replanted.

## Protected Cultivation: The Double-Layer System

The technical foundation of the winter harvest is the combination of an outer greenhouse layer and an inner row-cover layer, creating a twice-tempered climate. The outer structure consists of standard plastic-covered gothic-style hoop houses. Inside, floating row-cover material is supported by flat-topped wire wickets at about 12 inches above the soil, spaced every 4 feet along 30-inch-wide growing beds.

This double coverage produces a dramatic temperature differential. When the outdoor temperature drops to -15°F (-26°C), the temperature under the inner layer drops only to 15°F to 18°F above zero (-10°C to -8°C) on average. The cold-hardy vegetables can easily survive temperatures down to 10°F (-12°C) or lower as long as they are not exposed to the additional stresses of outdoor conditions — especially the desiccating effects of cold winter winds.

The double coverage also increases relative humidity in the protected area, offering additional protection against freezing damage. Any type of lightweight floating row cover that allows light, air, and moisture to pass through is suitable for the inner layer.

## The Cool House: Minimal Supplementary Heat

Coleman explored one variant — a "cool house" maintained at just above freezing (32°F / 0°C) using a propane heater. This minimal heat accelerates plant growth enough to harvest two more crops per winter than in the unheated houses (five to six crops per year versus three to four). The cool house also enables year-round production of crops like baby turnips and crisp radishes, which are limited by freezing in unheated houses, and brings early spring carrots to harvest six weeks earlier.

However, Coleman ultimately decided not to pursue the cool-house option further, preferring the simplicity and resource-efficiency of unheated production. The original inspiration was to achieve as much as possible without heating, and the unheated houses proved more than adequate for baby-leaf salads, braising mixes, spinach, leeks, Asian greens, and winter carrots.

## Cold-Hardy Crop List

Coleman has grown approximately thirty different vegetables in winter-harvest greenhouses: arugula, beet greens, broccoli raab, carrots, chard, chicory, claytonia, collards, dandelion, endive, escarole, garlic greens, kale, kohlrabi, leeks, lettuce, mache, minutina, mizuna, mustard greens, pak choi, parsley, radicchio, radish, scallions, sorrel, spinach, tatsoi, turnips, and watercress.

The eating quality of these cold-hardy vegetables is unrivaled during the cooler months. They reach a higher level of perfection without the heat stress of summer — many become sweeter, more tender, and more flavorful after exposure to cold temperatures. This is not a limitation of winter growing; it is one of its greatest advantages.

## Economic Model

The winter-harvest system was designed to solve a real economic problem: extending the season to the whole year means growers can hold their markets, keep their crew employed, and provide a more balanced year-round income. Simplicity, low external inputs, and high-quality outputs have always been the guiding criteria. The system avoids the cost of supplemental heat entirely, relying instead on crop selection, timing, and simple structures.

## Wind Protection and Microclimate

Coleman emphasizes that wind is a greater enemy of winter crops than cold temperatures alone. Cold winter winds strip moisture from plant tissues and increase [[coleman-wiggle-wire-channel-greenhouse-plastic-attachment-system]] adds significant R-value, further reducing heat loss during the coldest periods. Coleman recommends monitoring snow accumulation and gently removing it from structures that show signs of stress, but otherwise allowing snow to remain as a natural insulating layer. The thermal mass of snow against the greenhouse can raise interior temperatures by several degrees compared to an exposed structure.

## Labor Efficiency and Harvest Planning

Winter harvest systems require careful labor planning. Harvesting in freezing conditions is slower and more physically demanding than summer work. Coleman recommends harvesting during the warmest part of the day when plants are least brittle and workers are most comfortable. Cut-and-come-again crops like arugula and claytonia are harvested with scissors or knives rather than pulling entire plants, allowing for multiple harvests from a single sowing. Planning harvest schedules around predicted weather patterns — harvesting before major cold snaps — helps maintain product quality. The low light conditions of midwinter slow regrowth, so over-harvesting during this period can deplete the stand.

## Season Extension Timeline Summary

Coleman's system creates a continuous production calendar. August through September serves as the primary planting window for winter crops. October brings the transition as greenhouses move to their winter positions. November through January is the deep winter period when harvest slows and plants are maintained in a semi-dormant state. February through March sees accelerating growth as day length increases. April through May provides the last winter harvests before the transition to summer crops. This year-round rhythm eliminates the traditional off-season gap and transforms winter from a period of scarcity into a period of premium-quality production.
