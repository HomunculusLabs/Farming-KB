# Cannabis for Alcohol Dependence

Alcohol abuse is one of the most common contributing factors to a great deal of pain and suffering. A neurological and mental illness, alcohol abuse causes or exacerbates domestic violence, child abuse, homicide, suicide, accidents, fatal overdose, loss of work, and increased poverty. Alcohol abuse affects the entire body, with cardiovascular disease named as the primary cause of death associated with alcohol abuse.

## Disease Characteristics

Other health problems connected to alcohol abuse include poor food absorption, cancer, liver cirrhosis, pancreatitis, gastritis, alcohol poisoning, nervous system damage, depression, dementia, seizures, psychosis, and withdrawal delirium tremens. Long-term alcohol abuse induces neurochemical changes in brain physiology and structure that reinforce psychological and physical need for alcohol.

Orthodox medicine suggests no cure for chronic alcoholism. Patients remain alcoholics for life and must learn to manage the urge and maintain abstinence. Therapy is two-fold: detoxification with thiamine (vitamin B1) support, followed by peer-group therapy.

## Evidence Base

The Cannabis Health Index (CHI) rates the evidence for cannabis in alcohol dependence at a CHI value of 9, based on 4 studies reviewed. The evidence comes from clinical case studies and animal research with intriguing implications.

## Clinical Case Study Evidence

In 1970, Tod H. Mikuriya, MD, published a case involving an alcohol-dependent patient who significantly benefited from cannabis' ability to reduce his alcohol consumption. Thirty-four years later, Dr. Mikuriya followed up with a larger study incorporating 92 alcoholic patients.

Of the 92 patients, 45 found cannabis treatment "very effective" for reducing alcohol consumption, 38 found it "effective," and 9 patients reported they had been able to give up alcohol altogether. These results suggest that cannabis may serve as a harm reduction tool and potential substitute for alcohol in dependent individuals.

## CB1 Receptor and Alcohol Preference

Studies conducted on mice in 2005 and 2008 suggest that blocking the CB1 receptor appears to effectively reduce the preference for alcohol and actual alcohol intake. Researchers speculate that medications targeting CB1 receptors may be beneficial for treating alcoholism.

This finding suggests that the endocannabinoid system is directly involved in the reward pathways associated with alcohol consumption. Modulating CB1 receptor activity could therefore influence alcohol-seeking behavior and consumption patterns.

## CBD Neuroprotection Against Binge Drinking

A multidisciplinary research team from Bethesda, Maryland (2005), designed an experiment inducing rats to binge-drink alcohol, an activity causing substantial neurodegeneration in the brain, especially the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex. Concurrently, rats received CBD, a non-psychoactive cannabinoid.

Results indicated that CBD could protect nerve cells from alcohol-induced toxicity in a dose-dependent manner. This neuroprotective finding is significant because alcohol-related brain damage is one of the most serious long-term consequences of alcohol abuse.

## Harm Reduction Potential

The clinical evidence suggests cannabis may function as a harm reduction tool for alcohol-dependent individuals. By reducing alcohol consumption and potentially serving as a less harmful substitute, cannabis could decrease alcohol-related health damage even if complete abstinence is not achieved.

This harm reduction approach aligns with public health strategies that prioritize reducing overall harm rather than requiring immediate complete abstinence, which has low success rates in alcohol dependence treatment.

## Substitution Hypothesis

The observation that some patients were able to give up alcohol entirely when using cannabis suggests a substitution effect. If cannabis can satisfy some of the same psychological and physiological needs that alcohol addresses (anxiety reduction, social facilitation, mood alteration), it may serve as a safer substitute given cannabis' lower toxicity profile.

Unlike alcohol, cannabis has no known lethal dose and does not cause the organ damage, particularly liver cirrhosis, associated with chronic alcohol use.

## Strain Considerations

The CB1 receptor research suggests that CB1 modulation is relevant to alcohol dependence. THC binds relatively equally to both CB1 and CB2 receptors. Sativa or sativa-dominant strains with higher THC content may be more effective for substitution purposes.

For neuroprotection, CBD-dominant strains may be more appropriate. A balanced THC:CBD preparation could potentially address both the substitution and neuroprotection aspects simultaneously.

## Safety Considerations

Using cannabis to manage alcohol dependence should be done under professional supervision, particularly during acute withdrawal from alcohol. Cannabis should not be used during acute alcohol withdrawal, which can be life-threatening and requires medical management.

Patients should be aware that cannabis is not a cure for alcoholism but may serve as a harm reduction tool. Concurrent use of alcohol and cannabis may impair judgment and motor function more than either substance alone.

## Key Research Summary

Dr. Mikuriya's studies (1970, 2004) showed 45 of 92 alcoholic patients found cannabis "very effective" and 9 gave up alcohol entirely. Animal studies (2005, 2008) found CB1 receptor blockade reduces alcohol preference and intake. A 2005 Bethesda study demonstrated CBD protects nerve cells from alcohol-induced brain damage in a dose-dependent manner.

## Limitations and Future Directions

The clinical evidence comes from uncontrolled case studies without placebo groups. The CB1 blockade studies used antagonists (which block the receptor) rather than agonists (which activate it like THC does), creating a complex picture for clinical translation. Large controlled trials examining cannabis as a harm reduction tool and substitute for alcohol are urgently needed. The neuroprotective potential of CBD against alcohol-induced brain damage deserves clinical investigation.
