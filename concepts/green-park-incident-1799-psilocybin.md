# The Green Park Incident of 1799: First Documented Psilocybin Intoxication in Europe

On October 3, 1799, in London's St. James's Green Park, an impoverished family inadvertently consumed [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] (liberty cap) mushrooms, producing what is now recognized as the earliest documented case of psilocybin intoxication in Europe. The detailed clinical account published by E. Brande in the Medical and Physical Journal provides a remarkable window into how European medicine first encountered the effects of psychoactive mushrooms.

## The Incident

### Collection and Preparation

J.S., a father aged forty, gathered mushrooms early in the morning of October 3, 1799, in St. James's Green Park, London. He collected what he supposed to be small edible mushrooms. The mushrooms were stewed with "the common additions" in a tinned iron saucepan. The total quantity did not exceed a tea saucerful. The father and his four children ate the mushrooms first thing, about eight o'clock in the morning, "as they frequently had done without any bad consequence." They afterwards took their usual breakfast of tea.

### The "Common Additions" Note

Brande specifically notes the use of a tinned iron saucepan, adding: "This accuracy may seem trivial, but I have met with people who supported the following symptoms might have arisen from the use of a copper vessel." This attention to detail -- ruling out copper poisoning as an alternative explanation -- shows a commendable scientific rigor for the era.

## Clinical Symptoms

### Edward (Eight Years Old)

Edward had eaten a large proportion of the mushrooms. His symptoms were the most severe and developed first:

- **Laughter:** "Attacked with fits of immoderate laughter, nor could the threats of his father or mother restrain him"
- **Vertigo:** Followed the laughter
- **Stupor:** A great degree of stupor, from which he was roused by being called or shaken, but immediately relapsed
- **Pupils:** "Dilated to nearly the circumference of the cornea, and scarcely contracted at the approach of a strong light"
- **Breathing:** Quick
- **Pulse:** Very variable -- "at times imperceptible, at others too frequent and small to be counted; latterly, very languid"
- **Extremities:** "Feet cold, livid, and contracted"
- **Abdominal pain:** "Sometimes pressed his hands on different parts of his abdomen, as if in pain, but when roused and interrogated as to it, he answered indifferently, yes, or no, as he did to every other question, evidently without any relation to what was asked"

### The Father (Aged Forty)

The father also developed symptoms, though less severe:

- **Vertigo:** "Attacked with vertigo"
- **Visual disturbance:** "Complained that everything appeared black"

### Other Children

The account mentions four children in total, though detailed symptoms are provided only for Edward, who had eaten the largest proportion.

## Brande's Analysis

### Species Identification

Brande described the mushrooms as small, consistent with Psilocybe semilanceata's small size (typically 1-5 cm cap diameter). The location (Green Park -- a grassy parkland) is also consistent with P. semilanceata's preferred habitat of rich grasslands, particularly in autumn.

Within the context of Sowerby's book from the same period, only the variety of mushrooms distinguished by their cone-shaped caps were believed to cause intoxication. Sowerby's contemporaries knew the mushroom as "Agaricus glutinosus Curtis," and its descriptions are fully compatible with current knowledge about Psilocybe semilanceata.

### Clinical Interpretation

Brande interpreted the incident as a case of accidental mushroom poisoning, which is consistent with the mycophobic framework of European medicine at the time. However, his detailed clinical observations -- particularly the pupillary dilation, variable pulse, and the characteristic alternating between stupor and brief periods of responsiveness -- are entirely consistent with modern descriptions of psilocybin intoxication.

The "immoderate laughter" is the hallmark symptom that connects this case specifically to psilocybin-containing mushrooms rather than to other types of mushroom toxicity. Laughter is not a typical symptom of most mushroom poisons (which more commonly cause gastrointestinal distress, liver failure, or neurological damage).

## Historical Context

### Pre-Wasson Knowledge

This incident occurred 158 years before R.G. Wasson's 1957 expedition to Mexico that brought psychoactive mushrooms to widespread Western attention. It demonstrates that European encounters with psilocybin mushrooms long predated the "discovery" of Mexican mushroom cults.

### The "Fool's Mushroom" Tradition

The Green Park incident fits within a broader European tradition of encounters with psychoactive mushrooms, known by various folk names:

- **Hungary:** "Bolond gomba" / German "Narrenschwamm" (fool's mushroom)
- **Slovakia:** Also documented with similar folk names
- **Poland:** Referenced by poet Vaclav Potocki (1625-1699)
- **England:** Parkinson's "Theatricum Botanicum" (1640) mentions a "foolish mushroom"
- **Austria:** The expression "He ate those madness-inducing mushrooms"

### The 1799 Report vs. Modern Understanding

Comparing Brande's 1799 observations with modern pharmacological knowledge:

| Brande's Observation | Modern Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Immoderate laughter | Serotonin 5-HT2A receptor activation |
| Dilated pupils | Sympathetic nervous system activation |
| Variable pulse | Autonomic effects of psilocybin |
| Stupor alternating with arousal | Characteristic psilocybin consciousness fluctuation |
| Cold, livid extremities | Peripheral vasoconstriction |
| Answering without relation to questions | Dissociative/cognitive effects |
| Everything appeared black (father) | Visual disturbance/alteration |

## Significance

### Earliest Clinical Description

The Green Park incident represents the earliest known clinical description of psilocybin intoxication in the medical literature. The level of detail in Brande's account -- including pupil size, pulse characteristics, and behavioral observations -- makes it a valuable historical document for understanding how the effects of psilocybin were first encountered and interpreted by European medicine.

### The Safety Profile

Despite the alarming symptoms described, the family recovered completely. This is consistent with [[psilocybin-toxicity-and-safety-profile]] -- no deaths have ever been conclusively attributed to psilocybin mushroom ingestion alone. The fact that an eight-year-old child who ate a "large proportion" of the mushrooms recovered without lasting harm is particularly noteworthy.

### The Mycophobic Frame

The incident is framed entirely within a pathological model -- as "poisoning" rather than as an "experience." This framing reflects the broader European pattern of interpreting psychoactive mushroom effects as symptoms of mental illness rather than as potentially meaningful altered states of consciousness. See [[mycophilia-mycophobia-european-mushroom-history]] for the full analysis.

### A "Normal" Family Activity

Notably, the family had gathered and eaten mushrooms in Green Park "frequently" without ill effects, suggesting that they were experienced mushroom foragers who simply misidentified the liberty caps on this particular occasion. This indicates that psychoactive mushrooms were present in London parklands in the late 18th century and that foraging was a common activity among the poor.
