# Fungal Biofilms and Quorum Sensing

**Source:** Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, *Fungi in the Environment* (2006), Chapters 3, 10 (Read; Liu et al.)

## Overview

Filamentous fungi form complex multicellular structures through hyphal aggregation, producing tissues analogous to biofilms in bacteria. These aggregated structures include fruit bodies, sclerotia, rhizomorphs, and mycelial cords. Quorum sensing (cell density-dependent signaling) regulates key developmental transitions in fungi, including dimorphism, spore formation, and hyphal fusion. The extracellular matrix surrounding fungal structures in symbiotic interactions shares functional properties with bacterial biofilm matrices.

## Hyphal Aggregation and Multicellular Development

### From Hyphae to Tissues
Filamentous fungi achieve multicellularity through a fundamentally different mechanism than animals or plants:
1. Hyphae aggregate and become glued together
2. Aggregated hyphae form "conglutinate cells" (from Latin 'conglutinare', to stick together)
3. These form either pseudoparenchymatous tissue (hyphal appearance lost) or prosenchymatous tissue (original hyphae still recognizable)
4. The resulting hyphal aggregates contain numerous discrete uninucleate and multinucleate cell types

### Complexity of Differentiated Structures
The model fungus [[neurospora-crassa]] has 28 morphologically distinct cell types, all derived from aggregated hyphae. Examples of hyphal aggregates include:
- **Fruit bodies:** Ascomata, basidiomata, conidiomata
- **Survival structures:** Sclerotia (resist adverse environments)
- **Transport structures:** Rhizomorphs and mycelial cords (long-distance transport of water and nutrients)
- **Reproductive structures:** Spore-producing aggregates

### Septal Pore Occlusion
Physical isolation of adjacent hyphal compartments is a prerequisite for cell specialization. Specialized septal pore plugs (e.g., basidiomycete dolipore septa) may play roles in selective cell-cell communication during multicellular development.

## Autoregulatory Signals and Quorum Sensing

### Extracellular Chemical Signals
The least understood biotic signals influencing filamentous fungi are the autoregulators -- extracellular chemical signals produced by the fungus itself. They play critical roles in regulating:

**Colony Organization:**
- Hyphal avoidance (preventing self-overgrowth)
- Growth directionality (away from colony center)
- Hyphal homing (prelude to hyphal fusion)

**Developmental Regulation:**
- Germination self-inhibitors
- Germ-tube emergence patterns from spores
- Regulation of asexual and sexual reproduction
- Choice between sexual and asexual reproduction

### Quorum Sensing in Fungi
Some autoregulatory signals function as quorum (cell density) sensors:

**[[candida-albicans]]:**
- Farnesol is a quorum-sensing molecule that regulates yeast-hyphal dimorphism
- Tyrosol is a quorum-sensing molecule (Chen et al., 2004)
- Release from quorum-sensing molecules triggers morphogenetic transitions (Enjalbert & Whiteway, 2005)
- The two-component signal transduction protein Chk1p regulates quorum sensing responses
- Cell cycle dynamics are linked to quorum sensing (Martin et al., 2005)

**Filamentous Fungi:**
- Quorum sensing regulates yeast-hyphal dimorphism (Chen et al., 2004; Hornby et al., 2004; Kruppa et al., 2004)
- Chlamydospore formation is quorum-regulated (Martin et al., 2005)
- Conidial fusion is regulated by quorum-like signals (Roca et al., 2005)

### Sex Pheromones
Fungus-derived signals regulating sexual reproduction include:
- **Trisporic acid:** Used in Mucorales
- **Peptide pheromones:** Used by filamentous ascomycetes and basidiomycetes

## Extracellular Matrix in Symbiotic Fungi

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Interface
In AM symbiosis, the arbuscule (the fungal nutrient-exchange structure) develops within an extracellular matrix compartment:
- The plant plasma membrane extends around hyphal branches as the arbuscule develops (four-fold membrane length increase)
- An extracellular matrix is deposited around the arbuscule as cell wall material continues to be synthesized
- Matrix composition includes: pectins, xyloglucans, non-esterified polygalacturonans, arabinogalactans, and hydroxyproline-rich glycoproteins (HRGP)
- H+-ATPase activity demonstrated in this matrix compartment
- The matrix creates an apoplastic interface between fungus and plant for nutrient exchange

### Functional Analogies to Bacterial Biofilms
The fungal extracellular matrix in mycorrhizal interfaces shares key features with bacterial biofilms:
- Polysaccharide-rich matrix embedding cells
- Facilitation of cell-cell communication
- Protection of enclosed organisms
- Mediation of nutrient exchange with the environment

## Signal Transduction Machinery

### Complexity in Filamentous Fungi
Filamentous fungi possess more extensive sensing and signaling capabilities than yeasts, reflecting their need to respond to varied and changing microenvironments:

**G-Protein Coupled Receptors (GPCRs):** Overall increase in number compared to yeasts

**Two-Component Signaling Systems:**
- N. crassa possesses complex two-component systems uncommon in prokaryotes
- 11 histidine kinases identified (vs. 1 in S. cerevisiae, 3 in S. pombe)
- Involved in hyphal development, light sensing, and environmental responses
- Conserved downstream elements (1 histidine phosphotransferase, 2 response regulators) suggest networking capabilities

**Calcium Signaling:** Overall increase in protein machinery compared to yeasts

**MAP Kinase Cascades:** Multiple pathways involved in stress responses, mating, and filamentation

## Environmental Signal Categories

Fungi respond to four types of environmental signals:
1. **Abiotic and global:** Light (intensity, wavelength, periodicity), temperature, humidity
2. **Abiotic and local:** Nutrients, oxygen, CO2, pH, chemical signals, mechanical stimuli, osmotic shock
3. **Biotic from other organisms:** Chemicals from fungi, bacteria, plants, animals that promote/inhibit growth or modulate metabolism
4. **Biotic from self (autoregulators):** Quorum signals, pheromones, germination inhibitors, hyphal avoidance/homing signals
