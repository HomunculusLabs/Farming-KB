# Sclerotia Production Timeline and Dark Incubation for Psilocybe tampanensis and P. mexicana

## Overview

Psilocybe tampanensis and Psilocybe mexicana are unique among cultivated psilocybin-containing mushrooms for their ability to produce sclerotia — dense, hardened masses of mycelial tissue that function as long-term survival structures. Unlike fruit body production, which requires environmental triggers including light and fresh air exchange, sclerotia form while the mycelium is still colonizing the substrate inside sealed jars, without the need for casing, fruiting chambers, or complex environmental controls. Understanding the production timeline, optimal incubation conditions, and harvest windows is essential for maximizing sclerotia yield.

## What Are Sclerotia

Sclerotia (singular: sclerotium) are compact, hardened aggregates of densely packed mycelium that serve as a long-term survival mechanism for certain fungi. In nature, sclerotia allow the organism to persist through unfavorable conditions such as drought, extreme temperatures, or nutrient depletion. When conditions improve, sclerotia can remain dormant for extended periods and later germinate to produce fruiting bodies or resume vegetative growth.

In cultivation, sclerotia are valued as a product for several reasons:

- They can be produced entirely within sealed jars, requiring no fruiting chamber
- No casing soil, humidity control, or air exchange systems are needed
- The production process is passive and requires minimal intervention after inoculation
- Sclerotia are durable and have a reasonable shelf life
- They can be harvested at the cultivator's convenience rather than at a precise moment

## Species Comparison

### Psilocybe tampanensis

- **Sclerotia production**: Robust, reliable, and prolific; this is the primary cultivation method for P. tampanensis
- **Common name**: Known commercially as "Philosopher's stones" or "magic truffles" in markets where they are legal
- **Sclerotia quality**: Dense, firm-textened, with good potency and pleasant flavor profile
- **Growth rate**: Moderate and steady; continuous increase in size throughout the incubation period
- **Fruiting difficulty**: P. tampanensis is relatively difficult to fruit as mushrooms, making sclerotia production the preferred approach
- **First discovered**: Originally collected near Tampa, Florida in 1977; the species has only been found in the wild on a few occasions

### Psilocybe mexicana

- **Sclerotia production**: Less prolific than P. tampanensis but still reliable and worthwhile
- **Fruiting**: P. mexicana also produces mushrooms more readily than P. tampanensis when cased and given fruiting conditions
- **Flush pattern**: Usually produces one large flush and a smaller second flush when cultivated for mushrooms
- **Dual production**: Can produce both sclerotia and fruit bodies; sclerotia form in both uncased jars and in the casing layer of cased trays
- **Historical significance**: One of the traditional ceremonial mushrooms of Mesoamerican cultures, used by Aztec and Mazatec peoples
- **Natural habitat**: Native to subtropical and tropical regions of Mexico and Central America

## Production Timeline

### Maximum Duration: 4 Months After Inoculation

Cultivation experience with P. tampanensis indicates that sclerotia will continue to enlarge until approximately 4 months after inoculation. After this point, growth essentially plateaus and further incubation provides diminishing returns. The 4-month timeline represents the practical maximum for jar-based sclerotia production under standard conditions.

### Early Stage: Weeks 1-4 (Colonization Phase)

During the first month, the mycelium focuses on colonizing the grass seed substrate:

- Mycelium grows outward from the inoculation point, consuming the seed nutrients
- The grass seed substrate gradually transitions from loose individual kernels to a consolidated white mass
- Initial sclerotia primordia may begin to form as small white knots visible through the jar wall
- Full substrate colonization is typically achieved by the end of this period
- No sclerotia of harvestable size have formed yet
- The jar should not be shaken after initial colonization, as this would disrupt early sclerotia formation

### Weeks 5-12 (Active Growth Phase)

The second and third months show the most active sclerotia growth:

- Sclerotia increase rapidly in size, becoming visible as hardened structures through the glass
- Individual sclerotia press against jar walls, creating visible bulges
- Mycelium becomes denser and more rhizomorphic as it reorganizes for sclerotia production
- The substrate mass consolidates and shrinks around growing sclerotia
- Color may shift from white to slightly yellowish or tan as sclerotia mature

### Weeks 13-16 (Maturation Phase)

The final month shows continued but slowing growth:

- Growth rate decreases as sclerotia approach maximum size
- Sclerotia become firm and resistant to gentle pressure through the jar wall
- Substrate may show significant shrinkage as nutrients are consumed
- Some sclerotia may darken slightly as they fully mature

### Beyond 4 Months: Diminishing Returns

Extending incubation beyond 4 months is generally not recommended:

- Little to no additional sclerotia growth occurs
- The substrate may begin to dry out, reducing sclerotia quality
- Risk of contamination increases with extended storage of nutrient-rich substrate
- Sclerotia may begin to degrade if left in the jar too long
- Harvest is recommended no later than 4 months post-inoculation for best quality

## Dark Incubation Conditions

### Why Darkness Is Important

Sclerotia production does not require light and is actually optimized in complete darkness. Unlike fruit body formation, which is triggered by light exposure and fresh air exchange, sclerotia formation is a vegetative process that proceeds optimally when the mycelium is undisturbed and kept in dark conditions.

Light exposure during sclerotia production can trigger the mycelium to begin forming fruit body primordia instead of, or in addition to, sclerotia. Since the goal of dedicated sclerotia production is to maximize sclerotial biomass, excluding light ensures that the fungus directs all available resources toward sclerotia formation rather than fruiting.

### Recommended Incubation Setup

- **Dark location**: Closet, cabinet, drawer, or box covered with opaque material. Complete darkness preferred; brief exposure during jar checks is harmless.
- **Clean environment**: Essential throughout the long incubation — contamination at month 1 destroys the batch by month 4.
- **Temperature**: 20-25°C room temperature. No cycling, cold shock, or manipulation needed. Consistent temperature is best.
- **No disturbance**: Do not shake jars after colonization. Shaking disrupts developing sclerotia and may cause them to abort.
- **Loose lids**: Allow gas exchange while preventing contamination. Tight lids create anaerobic conditions.

### Ventilation Considerations

While sclerotia production jars are sealed, some gas exchange is necessary for the mycelium to respire:

- Loose lids or lids fitted with filter tape allow CO2 to escape and O2 to enter
- Polypropylene lids with self-healing injection ports and gas exchange filters are ideal
- Complete sealing will eventually lead to anaerobic conditions and mycelial death
- However, excessive air exchange increases contamination risk over the long incubation period

## Sclerotia Formation in Cased Substrates

Sclerotia can also form in the casing layer of cased trays, not just in sealed jars. When colonized grass seed is cased for mushroom production, sclerotia may develop simultaneously with or instead of fruit bodies. This dual production has been observed with both P. tampanensis and P. mexicana.

However, jar-based production is generally preferred for dedicated sclerotia cultivation because the process is simpler, harvest is cleaner (no casing soil mixed in), the controlled environment produces more consistent results, and contamination risk is lower with sealed jars.

## Substrate Considerations for Sclerotia

Grass seed is the preferred substrate for sclerotia production. Standard preparation applies: 110 g grass seed + 180 ml water per 720 ml jar, sterilized at 15 psi for 1 hour (2 hours for larger jars). Proper moisture content is especially critical because jars must maintain hydration throughout the 4-month incubation without additional watering.

## Harvesting

Sclerotia are harvested by opening the jar and manually separating the hardened masses from the colonized substrate:

- Use clean hands or gloved hands to break apart the substrate
- Sclerotia are firm and separate easily from the surrounding mycelium and seed
- Gently brush off any adhering substrate particles with a soft brush or fingers
- Rinse briefly under cold water if needed (not strictly necessary)
- Pat dry with a clean paper towel

Post-harvest options include refrigeration (2-4°C for several weeks), drying for long-term storage, or immediate processing.

## Yield Expectations

Yields vary based on strain genetics, substrate quality, incubation conditions, and harvest timing. P. tampanensis typically produces 10-30% of the initial dry substrate weight as fresh sclerotia over a 4-month incubation period. P. mexicana generally produces lower sclerotia yields but may also produce a flush of mushrooms if the remaining substrate is cased after sclerotia harvest.
