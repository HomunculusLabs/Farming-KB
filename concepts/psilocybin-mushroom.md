# Psilocybin Mushroom

Psilocybin mushrooms, commonly known as magic mushrooms, are fungi that
contain the psychoactive compounds psilocybin and psilocin. Belonging
primarily to the genus Psilocybe, these mushrooms have been used for
thousands of years in Mesoamerican religious ceremonies and have become
significant both as objects of scientific research and as widely used
recreational substances. Their rediscovery by Western science in the
mid-twentieth century opened new avenues for understanding consciousness and
treating mental illness.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

Psilocybin (4-PO-DMT, O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) is a
prodrug that is rapidly dephosphorylated by alkaline phosphatase enzymes into
psilocin (4-HO-DMT) after ingestion. Psilocin is the pharmacologically active
compound, acting primarily as a partial agonist at the 5-HT2A serotonin
receptor. It also interacts with other serotonin receptor subtypes including
5-HT1A, 5-HT2B, and 5-HT2C, as well as dopamine and adrenergic receptors at
higher concentrations.

The onset of effects typically occurs within 20 to 60 minutes of ingestion,
with peak effects between 60 and 120 minutes. The total duration of the
experience is approximately 4 to 6 hours, depending on dose, individual
metabolism, and whether the mushrooms are consumed on an empty stomach.
Psilocybin and psilocin are primarily metabolized in the liver and excreted
through the kidneys, with total elimination typically complete within 24 hours.

## Major Species

Over 200 species of mushrooms across multiple genera are known to contain
psilocybin or related tryptamine alkaloids. The most commonly encountered
species include:

- **Psilocybe cubensis**: The most widely cultivated species, native to
  subtropical regions. Easily identified by its golden-brown cap that bruises
  blue when damaged. Strains like Golden Teacher, B+, and Ecuador are popular
  among cultivators due to their vigor and consistent yields.
- **Psilocybe semilanceata** (liberty cap): Found in temperate grasslands of
  Europe and North America. Considered one of the most potent naturally
  occurring species. Fruits in autumn, often in association with livestock
  grazing areas.
- **Psilocybe cyanescens** (wavy caps): Known for high potency, found in
  woody debris and mulched areas in temperate coastal regions. The cap has a
  distinctive wavy margin when mature.
- **Psilocybe azurescens**: Among the most potent psilocybin-containing
  mushrooms, native to the Pacific Northwest coast of North America. Found
  primarily in coastal dune grasses.
- **Psilocybe mexicana**: The species first identified by R. Gordon Wasson,
  used in Mazatec ceremonies and the source of "teonanacatl" (flesh of the
  gods) in Aztec tradition.
- **Psilocybe allenii**: Found along the Pacific coast, commonly appearing in
  wood chip landscaping in urban areas, particularly in the San Francisco Bay
  Area.
- **Psilocybe baeocystis**: Known as "knobby tops," found in the Pacific
  Northwest. Contains baeocystin in addition to psilocybin.

## Identification and Foraging

Accurate identification of psilocybin mushrooms requires careful attention to
several morphological features. Key identifying characteristics include the
blue-bruising reaction (caused by the oxidation of psilocin), spore print
color (typically dark purple-brown), gill structure, cap shape and texture,
and the presence or absence of a partial veil or annulus. The blue bruising
reaction, while characteristic, is not unique to psilocybin mushrooms and
should not be used as the sole identification criterion.

Foraging carries significant risks, as several toxic mushroom species can
resemble psilocybin-containing species. Galerina marginata, which contains
deadly amatoxins, can grow alongside Psilocybe species and shares some
superficial similarities. Proper identification requires experience and
careful examination of multiple features.

## Cultivation

The cultivation of psilocybin mushrooms was revolutionized by the publication
of the PF Tek (Psilocybe Fanaticus technique) in the 1990s, which made home
cultivation accessible using widely available materials. The basic process
involves preparing a sterilized substrate of brown rice flour and vermiculite,
inoculating with spore syringes, incubating for colonization, and providing
appropriate fruiting conditions of high humidity, fresh air exchange, and
ambient light.

More advanced cultivation methods use grain spawn (typically rye or millet)
transferred to bulk substrates such as coco coir, manure, or straw. These
methods can produce significantly higher yields. Many jurisdictions now permit
the sale of psilocybin mushroom cultivation supplies and spores for
microscopy purposes, though the legal status of cultivation varies widely.

## Dosage

Psilocybin mushroom potency varies considerably between species, individual
specimens, and growing conditions. A typical dried P. cubensis mushroom
contains approximately 0.5 to 1.0 percent psilocybin and psilocin by weight.
Approximate dried dosage ranges for P. cubensis are:

- **Light dose**: 0.5 to 1.0 grams, producing mild perceptual changes
- **Moderate dose**: 1.0 to 2.5 grams, clear hallucinogenic effects
- **Strong dose**: 2.5 to 5.0 grams, intense psychedelic experience
- **Heroic dose**: 5.0+ grams, potentially overwhelming full ego dissolution

## Ethnobotanical History

The use of psilocybin mushrooms in Mesoamerican cultures extends back
thousands of years. The Aztecs, Maya, and other civilizations incorporated
these mushrooms into religious ceremonies, divination, and healing practices.
Spanish conquistadors attempted to suppress their use, but the traditions
survived in remote indigenous communities. The rediscovery of these practices
by R. Gordon Wasson and his wife Valentina in the 1950s, when they
participated in a Mazatec mushroom ceremony led by Maria Sabina, brought
psilocybin mushrooms to the attention of Western science and the broader
public, ultimately leading to the identification of psilocybin by Albert
Hofmann in 1958.
