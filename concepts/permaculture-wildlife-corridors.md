# Permaculture Wildlife Corridors

Wildlife conservation is integral to permaculture design. Mollison's Manual treats [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] not as a separate reserve but as an essential component of productive landscapes, integrated through corridors, refuges, and deliberate design for biodiversity.

## Wildlife in the Permaculture Landscape

### Water Bodies as Wildlife Reserves

Wherever precipitation exceeds the demands of transpiration and evaporation, small dams, wetlands, and swamps can proliferate. All of these act as long-term water and wildlife reserves in the total landscape. Many Australian farms have become "drought-proof" due to sensible investment in Keyline or similar water conservation systems that simultaneously create wildlife habitat.

### Boulders as Wildlife Refuges

Boulders serve as coarse mulch, wildlife refuge, and walling/windbreak material which gains and radiates heat. Rock piles, stone walls, and boulder clusters provide habitat for reptiles, small mammals, insects, and birds throughout the designed landscape.

### Forest Systems

Multi-strata tree systems provide habitat complexity that supports diverse wildlife. The design of windbreaks, shelterbelts, and forest gardens always includes wildlife habitat as a co-benefit. Pioneer species and wildlife indicator species are noted -- aspen strips, for instance, indicate avalanche history in mountainous terrain.

## Wildlife Corridors

### Design Function

Wildlife corridors connect isolated habitat patches across the landscape, allowing genetic exchange and seasonal migration. In permaculture land trust design, wildlife corridors are listed among the primary aims alongside water conservation, developing forest, wetlands, and special species reserves.

### Integration with Productive Systems

Corridors are not set aside from productive use but integrated with:

- Riparian zones along waterways (providing both filtration and movement corridors)
- [[windbreak-and-shelterbelt-design]] plantings (serving farm animals, crops, and wildlife simultaneously)
- Hedgerow systems between fields (habitat, windbreak, and fodder production)
- Steep slope forests ([[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]], timber, and wildlife movement)
- Road and track verges (planted with wildlife-friendly species)

### Land Trusts and Conservation

Land in trust can be developed to include garden clubs, commonworks, and leased land for specific purposes. Many trusts have wildlife corridors, wetlands, and special species reserves as primary aims. Land can be gifted to trusts for purposes including wildlife refuges, economic botanical gardens, or examples of good land use.

Reasons people gift land include:
- To continue land in its use as a wildlife refuge
- Because they believe in trusteeship rather than private ownership
- Land is surplus to their needs and a cost
- As a bequest to establish wildlife areas or demonstration farms
- Because they want more people on the land in socially rich arrangements

## Design Principles for Wildlife

1. Every design should include provisions for native wildlife
2. Habitat elements (water, food, shelter, nesting sites) should be distributed across zones
3. Corridors should connect habitat patches to prevent genetic isolation
4. Productive elements should double as wildlife habitat where possible
5. Predatory and pest species should be managed through balanced ecosystem design rather than eradication
6. Seasonal variation in food and shelter availability should be planned for

## Zonation of Wildlife Habitat

- **Zone 4-5**: Large forest reserves, wetlands, and primary wildlife areas
- **Zone 3**: Integrated orchard and pasture systems with hedgerow corridors
- **Zone 2**: Garden ponds, bird boxes, insect habitat plantings, small rock piles
- **Zone 1**: [[cervantes-companion-planting-pest-deterrent]] [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]], small water features
