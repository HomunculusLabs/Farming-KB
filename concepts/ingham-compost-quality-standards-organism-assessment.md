# Compost Quality Standards and Organism Assessment

**Source:** The Field Guide I for [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] Tea — Elaine Ingham (2008)

## Core Concept

Not all compost is suitable for making actively aerated compost tea (AACT).
The quality of the compost directly determines the quality, diversity, and
effectiveness of the tea. Ingham establishes specific organism-level
benchmarks that compost must meet before it can be used as a tea base. These
standards focus on the total biomass and diversity of [[ingham-aact-beauveria-fruit-fly-beneficial-bacteria-inoculant]],
fungi, protozoa, and nematodes present in the finished compost.

## The Minimum Data Set for Compost Assessment

Ingham's [[ingham-compost-quality-assessment-visual-microbial-indicators]] protocol requires direct microscopy
examination at 400x magnification. The minimum data set includes:

### Total and Active Bacteria (µg/g)
- **Minimum total bacterial biomass**: 100-150 µg/g compost
- **Active bacterial biomass**: should be 10-15% of total (10-20 µg/g)
- Active bacteria are identified by their brightness under fluorescence
  staining (FDA or fluorescein diacetate) — only metabolically active cells
  fluoresce
- The ratio of active to total bacteria indicates compost maturity and
  biological health

### Total and Active Fungi (µg/g)
- **Minimum total fungal biomass**: 100-200 µg/g compost for bacterial-
  dominant tea, 200-500+ µg/g for fungal-dominant tea
- **Active fungal biomass**: should be 10-20% of total
- Hyphal diameter matters: beneficial saprophytic fungi typically have
  diameters > 2.5 µm, while many opportunistic/pathogenic fungi are < 2.5 µm
- A diverse range of hyphal diameters indicates a healthy fungal community

### Protozoa (numbers/g)
- **Flagellates**: minimum 10,000/g compost
- **Amoebae**: minimum 10,000/g compost
- **Ciliates**: present but not dominant (< 10% of protozoan community)
- Protozoa indicate nutrient cycling capacity — they consume bacteria and
  release plant-available nitrogen through their excretion

- **[[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]]**: minimum 100/g
- **Fungal-feeding nematodes**: minimum 50/g (for fungal-dominated systems)
- **Predatory nematodes**: desirable but not required as minimum
- Nematodes are the top predators of the soil food web and indicate a
  mature, functional compost ecosystem

## Why Organism Levels Matter

The organism levels in compost determine what happens when tea is brewed
and applied. During brewing, organisms multiply. During application, they
establish on leaf surfaces (phyllosphere) or in soil (rhizosphere) and
begin performing their ecological functions:

1. **Nutrient cycling**: [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] consume bacteria and fungi,
   mineralizing nutrients locked in microbial biomass and making them
   available to plants
2. **Disease suppression**: A diverse community of beneficial organisms
   occupies ecological niches that would otherwise be available to pathogens
3. **Soil structure improvement**: Fungal hyphae and bacterial exopolymers
   bind soil particles into stable aggregates
4. **Plant growth promotion**: Beneficial organisms produce hormones,
   vitamins, and other growth-promoting compounds

If the starting compost lacks adequate organism levels, the tea will not
contain sufficient beneficial organisms to achieve these functions, regardless
of how well the tea is brewed.

## Compost Maturity Indicators

Beyond organism counts, several indicators distinguish mature, high-quality
compost from immature or poorly made compost:

### Visual Assessment
- Dark brown to black color (indicates humification)
- Crumbly, uniform texture (no recognizable feedstock particles)
- Pleasant earthy smell (no ammonia, sour, or putrid odors)
- Moist but not wet (60% moisture content is ideal)

### Biological Maturity
- Active-to-total organism ratio of 10-20% (higher ratios suggest
  instability; lower ratios suggest dead compost)
- Presence of all four trophic groups (bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes)
- Absence of indicator pathogens (observed during routine microscopy)
- Ciliates should be present but not dominant — dominant ciliates suggest
  anaerobic conditions during composting

### Chemical Indicators
- pH between 6.5 and 7.5
- Electrical conductivity below 3 dS/m
- C:N ratio between 15:1 and 25:1
- Nitrate significantly higher than ammonium (indicates maturity)

## Thermal vs. Vermicompost for Tea

Ingham distinguishes between thermally composted material and vermicompost:

### Thermal Compost
Produced at temperatures reaching 55-65°C (131-149°F) through microbial
metabolism. This process:
- Kills weed seeds and most human/animal pathogens
- Produces a bacterial-dominated compost (good for annual crops and
  vegetable gardens)
- Requires careful management of moisture, aeration, and C:N ratio
- Typically takes 6-12 months to full maturity

### Vermicompost
Produced by earthworms at ambient temperatures. This process:
- Does not reach temperatures high enough to kill all pathogens
- Produces compost with higher fungal diversity and more stable humus
- Often has higher protozoan and nematode populations due to worm activity
- Takes 3-6 months but requires less management

Both can produce excellent tea bases. The choice depends on the target crop
and desired bacterial-to-fungal ratio.

## Testing Frequency and Methodology

### Direct Microscopy
Ingham strongly advocates direct microscopy over plate counts. Plate counts
detect only 0.1-10% of actual microbial diversity because most organisms are
unculturable. Direct microscopy with fluorescein diacetate staining reveals
total biomass, active fraction, morphological diversity, and potential
pathogens — information plate counts cannot provide.

### When to Test
- Before brewing: verify compost meets minimum standards
- After brewing: verify adequate organism levels for application
- After application: monitor organism establishment in soil or on foliage

## Consequences of Poor Compost Quality

Using compost that does not meet organism-level standards leads to:
- Tea with insufficient beneficial organism diversity and biomass
- Poor disease suppression despite application
- No measurable improvement in soil structure or nutrient cycling
- In worst cases, introduction of [[ingham-anaerobic-organisms-role-soil]] or pathogens that
  can harm plants
- Wasted brewing effort with no beneficial result

The single most important step in producing effective compost tea is starting
with compost that meets these organism-level standards.
