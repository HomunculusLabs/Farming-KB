# Neurotropic Puffballs and Vascellum Species — Lycoperdales as Psychoactive Fungi

## Overview

Among the 216 [[mexico-center-neurotropic-fungal-biodiversity]] species catalogued by Guzmán, Allen & Gartz
(1998), one of the most surprising [[coprophilous-fungi-taxonomic-groups-distribution]] is the Lycoperdales — the
puffballs. While most people associate psychoactive mushrooms with gilled
agarics (like Psilocybe) or boletes, the catalogue includes four species of
puffball-like fungi (#211-214) that have been reported as neurotropic. These
species, particularly those in the genus Vascellum, challenge conventional
assumptions about which types of fungi can produce psychoactive effects.

## Species Listed in the Guzmán Catalogue

The Lycoperdales species listed as neurotropic in the Guzmán, Allen & Gartz
(1998) catalogue are:

- **#211. Lycoperdon candidum** Pers. (= L. marginatum Vittad.)
- **#212. Lycoperdon oblongisporum** Berk. & M.A. Curtis
- **#213. Vascellum pratense** (Pers. emend. Quél.) Kreisel
- **#214. Vascellum intermedium** A.H. Sm. (= Lycoperdon cruciatum s. auct.,
  non s. Rostk.; Vascellum cruciatum s. Ponce de León)
- **#215. Vascellum qudenii** (Bottomley) P. Ponce de León (= Lycoperdon
  mixtecorum R. Heim)

Additionally, one species from the related order Phallales is listed:
- **#216. Dictyophora indusiata** (Vent. ex Pers.) Desv. (= D. phalloidea
  Desv.), with three varieties documented by Guzmán et al. (1990)

## Vascellum qudenii and the Mixtec Connection

Perhaps the most culturally significant of the neurotropic puffballs is
Vascellum qudenii, originally described as Lycoperdon mixtecorum by Roger
Heim. The species epithet "mixtecorum" refers to the Mixtec people of Oaxaca,
Mexico, indicating that this puffball was used by [[guzman-allen-gartz-indigenous-mexican-mushroom-nomenclature-sacred-names]] communities
in a manner analogous to the use of Psilocybe species by the Mazatec and other
groups.

The documentation of a puffball species used as a psychoactive fungus by the
Mixtec people is remarkable for several reasons:

1. **Taxonomic breadth**: It demonstrates that indigenous Mexican communities
   recognized psychoactive properties in fungi well beyond the agaric genera
   commonly associated with ceremonial use.
2. **Nomenclature**: The original name Lycoperdon mixtecorum directly
   acknowledges the indigenous knowledge that led to its scientific discovery.
3. **Taxonomic revision**: The species was later transferred to the genus
   Vascellum by Ponce de León, reflecting advances in puffball systematics.

## Vascellum pratense — A Widespread Species

Vascellum pratense (species #213) is described in the catalogue as "a species
very common," noted by Phillips (1981) and many other authorities. Its listing
as neurotropic is notable because this is a widespread, common puffball species
found across Europe and other temperate regions. If confirmed as psychoactive,
V. pratense would represent one of the most accessible [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]] due
to its abundance and broad distribution.

The species has been documented across numerous European countries in the
distribution section of Guzmán et al. (1998), suggesting that its neurotropic
properties, if genuine, have been widely overlooked in the European mycological
tradition.

## Vascellum intermedium — The "Cruciate" Puffball

Vascellum intermedium has a complex taxonomic history. Originally described
as Lycoperdon cruciatum by various authors (though not by Rostkius, whose
interpretation was different), it was later recognized as Vascellum cruciatum
by Ponce de León and ultimately treated as V. intermedium. The synonymy
reflects the challenges of puffball taxonomy, where species distinctions often
rely on subtle differences in spore morphology and peridium (outer wall)
structure.

## Lycoperdon candidum and L. oblongisporum

Two species of Lycoperdon s.s. (true puffballs) are also listed as
neurotropic. Lycoperdon candidum (= L. marginatum) is a relatively common
puffball species, while L. oblongisporum (described by Berkeley and Curtis)
is less widely known. The listing of these species in the neurotropic
catalogue suggests that psychoactive properties may be more widespread among
gasteroid fungi (puffballs and their relatives) than commonly recognized.

## Dictyophora indusiata — The Basket Stinkhorn

While not a puffball, Dictyophora indusiata (species #216) is included in the
same section of the catalogue as the Lycoperdales. This distinctive fungus,
known as the "basket stinkhorn" or "veiled lady mushroom," is a member of the
Phallales. It is notable for its striking appearance, featuring a white,
lacy "indusium" (skirt-like structure) hanging from beneath the cap.

D. indusiata has a long history of use in traditional Chinese medicine and
cuisine, where it is valued for its nutritional and purported medicinal
properties. Guzmán et al. (1990) documented three varieties of this species.
Its inclusion in the neurotropic fungi catalogue raises interesting questions
about its pharmacological properties beyond its traditional uses.

## Chemistry and Verification Status

The psychoactive chemistry of the Lycoperdales species listed by Guzmán et al.
(1998) is not well characterized in the literature. Unlike the agaric
neurotropic fungi where psilocybin and psilocin are well-established as the
primary active compounds, the specific substances responsible for any
neurotropic effects in puffballs remain to be definitively identified. It is
possible that:
- Some puffball species contain psilocybin or related indole alkaloids
- The neurotropic effects may be due to entirely different compound classes
- Some reports may reflect misidentification or confusion with co-occurring
  psychoactive agaric species

The verification of these species' neurotropic status represents an important
area for future phytochemical research.

## Ethnobotanical Significance

The documentation of puffball species as neurotropic fungi expands our
understanding of the breadth of indigenous knowledge about psychoactive
organisms. While the use of Psilocybe species in Mesoamerican ceremonies is
well documented, the recognition of puffballs (Vascellum qudenii/L.
mixtecorum) as psychoactive by the Mixtec people demonstrates that indigenous
communities had a sophisticated and comprehensive understanding of fungal
pharmacology that extended across multiple fungal orders.
