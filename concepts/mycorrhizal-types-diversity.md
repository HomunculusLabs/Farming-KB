## Mycorrhizal Types and Diversity Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the vast majority of terrestrial plants, but these relationships are far from uniform. Rather than a single type of partnership, mycorrhizae encompass a diverse array of morphological and functional categories, each adapted to specific plant hosts, soil conditions, and ecological niches. Their host range is enormous, spanning most herbaceous plants, many shrubs, and some tropical trees. These plants represent about 5% of terrestrial plant species and grow characteristically in acidic, peat-rich soils where few other plants can survive.

A fine veil of hyphae grows over the root surface, thinner than an ectomycorrhizal mantle. The dominant fungal partners are ascomycetes, which have adapted to the fine root system of ericaceous plants. These roots possess only a one- or two-cell-layered cortex, and fungal hyphae penetrate the cortical cell wall to form dense coils called pelotons. ## Arbutoid Mycorrhizae

Arbutoid mycorrhizal fungi belong to the Basidiomycota and associate primarily with plants in the Ericaceae family, including the Pacific Northwest madrone tree (Arbutus menziesii), from which the arbutoid name derives, and manzanitas and bearberries (Arctostaphylos spp.).

Structurally, arbutoid mycorrhizae combine features of ecto- and endomycorrhizae. A fungal mantle surrounds the host root, and intercellular Hartig nets are formed between cortical cells, similar to ectomycorrhizae. However, arbutoid fungi can also penetrate the root cell wall and form tiny coils within outer cortical cells, a feature more characteristic of endomycorrhizae. This dual structural strategy allows for efficient nutrient transfer between fungus and host. Further details are at [[linum-album-piriformospora-indica-co-culture-system]], discovered in desert soils of northwest India. Laboratory experiments demonstrated that P. indica can successfully colonize several crop plants, including corn, barley, wheat, parsley, and poplar. Inoculated plants show increased resistance to root pathogens and diseases, suggesting substantial agricultural potential. Sebacinoid fungi also colonize Eucalyptus marginata, one of the most common native eucalyptus species in Australia.

## Multi-Mycorrhizal Plants

Some plants can form more than one type of mycorrhizal association simultaneously or at different life stages. Most poplars (Populus spp.) can form either ectomycorrhizal or [[comparison-orchid-mycorrhizae-vs-ericoid-mycorrhizae]] represent more specialized adaptations to particular ecological niches, such as acidic soils and nutrient-poor environments.

The existence of ectendomycorrhizae and sebacinoid mycorrhizae, which blur the boundaries between categories, suggests that the distinction between mycorrhizal types is not absolute but represents points along a continuum of plant-fungal partnership strategies. Multi-mycorrhizal plants further demonstrate that these evolutionary solutions are not mutually exclusive but can coexist within a single host, providing flexibility in the face of environmental change.
