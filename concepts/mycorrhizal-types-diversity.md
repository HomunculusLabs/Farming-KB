# Mycorrhizal Types and Diversity

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the vast majority of terrestrial plants, but these relationships are far from uniform. Rather than a single type of partnership, mycorrhizae encompass a diverse array of morphological and functional categories, each adapted to specific plant hosts, soil conditions, and ecological niches. Understanding this diversity is essential for grasping the full scope of [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and their roles in terrestrial ecosystems.

At least seven major types of mycorrhizae have been identified, plus the intriguing phenomenon of multi-mycorrhizal plants that can host more than one type simultaneously. These types differ in their fungal partners, structural features, host specificity, and ecological functions. The classification reflects not just taxonomic differences but also distinct evolutionary solutions to the challenge of plant-fungal nutrient exchange.

## Structural Features for Classification

The key structural differences among mycorrhizal types revolve around three anatomical features: the presence or absence of a fungal mantle (sheath) around the root, the formation of a Hartig net (intercellular hyphal network), and the degree of intracellular penetration into root cortical cells.

Ectomycorrhizal types, including arbutoid and monotropoid mycorrhizae, typically form a dense fungal mantle around the root surface and a Hartig net between cortical cells but do not generally penetrate the cells themselves. Endomycorrhizal types, including arbuscular, ericoid, and orchid mycorrhizae, lack a substantial mantle and instead penetrate directly into root cells, forming specialized intracellular structures. The [[ectomycorrhizal-morphological-structures]] page provides detailed descriptions of mantle and Hartig net architecture.

Some types blur these boundaries. Ectendomycorrhizae combine features of both groups, forming a thin mantle while also penetrating cortical cells. Sebacinoid mycorrhizae are remarkably versatile, capable of forming endomycorrhizal, ectomycorrhizal, ericoid, or orchid-type associations depending on the host.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizae

Arbuscular mycorrhizae are the most widespread and ancient type, associated with roughly 80% of terrestrial plant species. Formed by fungi in the phylum Glomeromycota, these endomycorrhizae produce characteristic tree-like structures called arbuscules inside root cortical cells, which serve as the primary site of nutrient exchange. They also form vesicles for lipid storage. Their host range is enormous, spanning most herbaceous plants, many shrubs, and some tropical trees. See [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] for comprehensive coverage of this type.

## Ectomycorrhizae

Ectomycorrhizal fungi, associated with ascomycetes and basidiomycetes, are more evolutionarily recent than endomycorrhizal types, having evolved approximately 250 million years ago. They associate exclusively with woody plants, including about 25,000 tree species such as pines (Pinus spp.), beeches (Fagus spp.), birches (Betula spp.), and oaks (Quercus spp.).

Formation begins with fungal contact below the apex of young, actively growing roots. A fungal mantle develops around the root surface, and hyphae penetrate between cortical cells to form the Hartig net. Only a small active zone about 1 to 3 millimeters below the root tip is colonized at any time; as the root grows, this zone moves outward while older Hartig net tissue dies. External hyphae can merge into rhizomorphs, hollow structures that transport water and nutrients over considerable distances, functioning much like root systems. For ecological details, see [[ectomycorrhizal-fungal-ecology]].

## Ericoid Mycorrhizae

Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic relationships with members of the Ericaceae (heath family), which includes rhododendrons, azaleas, blueberries, and cranberries. These plants represent about 5% of terrestrial plant species and grow characteristically in acidic, peat-rich soils where few other plants can survive.

A fine veil of hyphae grows over the root surface, thinner than an ectomycorrhizal mantle. The dominant fungal partners are ascomycetes, which have adapted to the fine root system of ericaceous plants. These roots possess only a one- or two-cell-layered cortex, and fungal hyphae penetrate the cortical cell wall to form dense coils called pelotons. Ericoid fungi specialize in obtaining nitrogen from organic matter in nitrogen-limited environments by producing strong acids that break down organic material. This acidification would be harmful to most plants, but ericoid fungi enable their hosts to thrive precisely in these acidic conditions. More on these specialized ecosystems is available at [[ericoid-mycorrhiza-heathland-ecosystems]].

## Orchid Mycorrhizae

Approximately 10% of Earth's plant species are orchids (family Orchidaceae), and most depend on specialized endomycorrhizal fungi from the Basidiomycota at some point in their life cycle. Orchid seeds are extremely small and contain virtually no stored nutrients, making the fungal association essential for germination and early development.

When an orchid seed germinates, it sends out a few hairs that are immediately colonized by fungi. The hyphae grow into the root epidermis and cortex, which is thicker than in ericaceous plants, allowing for complex networks of pelotons. These pelotons live for only a few days before being digested and absorbed by the plant. The association can be temporary, disappearing as the plant matures, or it may persist in species that lack chlorophyll and remain fully dependent on fungal partners for nutrition throughout their lives. See [[orchid-mycorrhiza-protocorm-development]] for details on early orchid-fungal interactions.

## Arbutoid Mycorrhizae

Arbutoid mycorrhizal fungi belong to the Basidiomycota and associate primarily with plants in the Ericaceae family, including the Pacific Northwest madrone tree (Arbutus menziesii), from which the arbutoid name derives, and manzanitas and bearberries (Arctostaphylos spp.).

Structurally, arbutoid mycorrhizae combine features of ecto- and endomycorrhizae. A fungal mantle surrounds the host root, and intercellular Hartig nets are formed between cortical cells, similar to ectomycorrhizae. However, arbutoid fungi can also penetrate the root cell wall and form tiny coils within outer cortical cells, a feature more characteristic of endomycorrhizae. This dual structural strategy allows for efficient nutrient transfer between fungus and host. Further details are at [[arbutoid-mycorrhiza]].

## Monotropoid Mycorrhizae

Monotropoid mycorrhizae were once classified within the arbutoid group but are now recognized as distinct. They colonize non-photosynthetic plants of the Monotropaceae family, including Indian pipes (Monotropa spp.). Unlike arbutoid mycorrhizae, monotropoid fungi do not penetrate plant cell walls in the same manner.

These fungi form a dense sheath or mantle around the root and produce Hartig nets that surround but do not penetrate root cells. Some individual hyphae form fungal pegs that do penetrate the cortex, living inside the host for approximately two weeks before dying. The host plant then absorbs the dead fungal hyphae to obtain carbon. Crucially, the fungal partner simultaneously forms an ectomycorrhizal association with nearby trees such as beech, oak, and cedar, effectively serving as a carbon bridge from photosynthetic trees to the non-photosynthetic monotropoid host. See [[monotropoid-mycorrhiza]] for more on this tripartite relationship.

## Ectendomycorrhizae

Though rare, ectendomycorrhizae exhibit characteristics of both endo- and ectomycorrhizae. They are associated primarily with deuteromycetes (imperfect fungi) and form in pot-grown nursery seedlings and with some tree seedlings, mostly pines, after forest fires. Hosts can be either coniferous or deciduous.

A thin mantle is formed around the root, and the hyphae of the Hartig net penetrate the cortex cells without crossing the plasma membrane. As the seedling matures, these associations typically morph into conventional ectomycorrhizae. This transitional nature suggests that ectendomycorrhizae may represent an intermediate or developmental stage rather than a permanently distinct type.

## Sebacinoid Mycorrhizae

Sebacinoid mycorrhizae are formed by a special group of basidiomycetes in the order Sebacinales. They are remarkably diverse in terms of host range and functional type, capable of forming endomycorrhizal, ectomycorrhizal, ericoid, or orchid-type associations depending on the host plant. This versatility makes them one of the most functionally plastic mycorrhizal groups known.

A particularly significant sebacinoid is Piriformospora indica, discovered in desert soils of northwest India. Laboratory experiments demonstrated that P. indica can successfully colonize several crop plants, including corn, barley, wheat, parsley, and poplar. Inoculated plants show increased resistance to root pathogens and diseases, suggesting substantial agricultural potential. Sebacinoid fungi also colonize Eucalyptus marginata, one of the most common native eucalyptus species in Australia.

## Multi-Mycorrhizal Plants

Some plants can form more than one type of mycorrhizal association simultaneously or at different life stages. Most poplars (Populus spp.) can form either ectomycorrhizal or arbuscular mycorrhizal relationships depending on the species, but quaking aspen (P. tremuloides) has been documented hosting both types at the same time. This dual capacity also occurs in other members of the willow family (Salicaceae), as well as alder (Alnus spp.) and eucalyptus. Less commonly, ericaceous plants may also exhibit multi-mycorrhizal behavior.

This phenomenon is not well studied due to its relative rarity, but it raises fascinating questions about plant-fungal coevolution. A host plant maintaining multiple mycorrhizal types may be experimenting with or testing different fungal partners, potentially optimizing nutrient acquisition across varying environmental conditions. The concept of [[mycorrhizal-specificity-and-host-range]] explores how plants select and maintain these associations.

## Evolutionary Relationships

The diversity of mycorrhizal types reflects deep evolutionary history. Arbuscular mycorrhizae are the most ancient, dating to the earliest land plants over 400 million years ago. Ectomycorrhizae evolved more recently, approximately 250 million years ago, as plants diversified and moved into new terrestrial environments. Ericoid and orchid mycorrhizae represent more specialized adaptations to particular ecological niches, such as acidic soils and nutrient-poor environments.

The existence of ectendomycorrhizae and sebacinoid mycorrhizae, which blur the boundaries between categories, suggests that the distinction between mycorrhizal types is not absolute but represents points along a continuum of plant-fungal partnership strategies. Multi-mycorrhizal plants further demonstrate that these evolutionary solutions are not mutually exclusive but can coexist within a single host, providing flexibility in the face of environmental change.
