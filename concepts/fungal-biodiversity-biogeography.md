# Fungal Biodiversity and Biogeography

Species diversity, distribution patterns, endemism, and the challenges of documenting fungal biodiversity. Based on Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, *Fungi in the Environment* (2006). See also [[fungal-ecology-extreme-environments]], [[fungal-ecology-decomposition-cycles]], and [[fungal-nutrient-cycling-mineral-transformations]].

## Overview

Fungi represent one of the most diverse kingdoms of life, yet their biodiversity remains poorly documented. The disconnect between estimated and described species reflects the historical difficulty of studying organisms that are mostly microscopic, often hidden, and fruit only sporadically. Fungal biogeography -- the study of fungal distribution patterns -- is undergoing a revolution due to molecular tools that reveal previously invisible diversity.

## The Scale of Fungal Diversity

### Species Estimates

- **Described species**: Approximately 70,000-150,000 species have been formally described (the number varies depending on whether lichen-forming fungi are included)
- **Estimated total**: 1.5 million to 5.1 million species globally (Hawksworth, 1991, 2001). The most commonly cited estimate is ~1.5 million.
- **The gap**: If the 1.5 million estimate is accurate, only 5-10% of fungal species have been described. At current rates of description (~1,000-1,500 new species per year), cataloging fungal diversity would take centuries.
- **Temperate vs tropical**: The ratio of described to estimated species is much lower in tropical regions, suggesting tropical fungi are dramatically undercollected and understudied.

### Why Are Most Fungi Undescribed?

Multiple factors contribute to the gap:

1. **Microscopic size**: Most fungi exist only as hyphae or spores, invisible without microscopy
2. **Sporadic fruiting**: Many species fruit only under specific environmental conditions (particular temperature, moisture, and substrate combinations)
3. **Cryptic species**: DNA sequencing reveals that morphologically identical "species" often contain multiple genetically distinct lineages
4. **Inaccessibility**: Many fungi live in canopy soils, deep soil, wood, leaf litter, or aquatic sediments -- difficult habitats to sample
5. **Expertise shortage**: There are relatively few trained fungal taxonomists worldwide, and their numbers are declining

## Fungal Biogeography

### Distribution Patterns

Historically, many fungi were assumed to have cosmopolitan distributions (found everywhere), primarily because spores can disperse over long distances. Molecular studies have challenged this assumption:

- **Endemism**: Many fungal species show restricted geographic distributions, especially in association with particular plant hosts or habitat types. Ectomycorrhizal fungi, in particular, often show strong geographic structure.
- **Disjunct distributions**: Some species show "amphi-Pacific" or "amphi-Atlantic" distributions, found on both sides of an ocean but absent in between. These patterns may reflect ancient continental connections or long-distance dispersal.
- **Latitudinal gradients**: Like most organisms, fungal diversity generally increases toward the tropics, but the pattern is less pronounced than for plants or animals, partly because sampling has been biased toward temperate regions.

### Dispersal Limitation vs Ubiquity

A fundamental debate in fungal biogeography:

- **Ubiquity hypothesis** (Finlay, 2002): Microorganisms, including fungi, are globally distributed, and local environmental conditions select for particular species from a global spore bank. "Everything is everywhere, but the environment selects."
- **Dispersal limitation hypothesis**: Fungal distributions are constrained by geographic barriers, limited dispersal ability, and historical factors. Many fungal species cannot cross oceans or mountain ranges.
- **Current consensus**: Both patterns exist. Some fungal groups (especially those with wind-dispersed spores and broad host ranges) approach cosmopolitan distributions. Others (especially mycorrhizal fungi with specific host requirements) show strong dispersal limitation and endemism.

### Molecular Approaches

DNA-based methods are transforming our understanding of fungal biogeography:

- **Environmental DNA (eDNA)**: Extracting DNA directly from soil, water, or air samples reveals the presence of fungal species that have never been cultured or observed as fruiting bodies. eDNA studies consistently find 2-10x more fungal OTUs (operational taxonomic units) than traditional surveys.
- **DNA barcoding**: The ITS (internal transcribed spacer) region of ribosomal DNA has been adopted as the standard fungal DNA barcode. This enables rapid identification and comparison across studies.
- **Metagenomics**: High-throughput sequencing of environmental samples provides comprehensive community profiles without the need for culturing.
- **Phylogeography**: Combining phylogenetic analysis with geographic data to reconstruct the evolutionary history of fungal lineages and their spread across landscapes.

## Major Fungal Phyla and Their Diversity

### Basidiomycota

- **Estimated diversity**: ~30,000 described species; likely 100,000-200,000 total
- **Key groups**: Agaricales (gilled mushrooms), Polyporales (bracket fungi), Boletales (boletes), Russulales, Cantharellales
- **Ecological roles**: Dominant wood decomposers, all ectomycorrhizal fungi, many plant pathogens (rusts, smuts), some lichen-forming fungi
- **Ecological significance**: Primary decomposers of lignin, major mycorrhizal partners in boreal/temperate forests

### Ascomycota

- **Estimated diversity**: ~30,000-65,000 described species; likely 200,000-500,000 total (the largest fungal phylum)
- **Key groups**: Pezizomycetes (cup fungi, truffles), Eurotiomycetes (Penicillium, Aspergillus), Sordariomycetes (Neurospora, Magnaporthe), Dothideomycetes (largest class, many plant pathogens), Lecanoromycetes (largest group of lichenized fungi)
- **Ecological roles**: Endophytes, plant pathogens, saprotrophs, lichen formers, fermenters, antibiotic producers
- **Ecological significance**: Include most known endophytic fungi, many important plant pathogens, all truffle-forming species

### Glomeromycota

- **Estimated diversity**: ~250 described species; molecular studies suggest higher diversity
- **Key groups**: Glomeraceae, Gigasporaceae, Acaulosporaceae, Archaeosporaceae
- **Ecological role**: Exclusively arbuscular mycorrhizal -- the most widespread plant-fungal symbiosis
- **Ecological significance**: Form symbioses with ~70% of terrestrial plant species; critical for plant nutrition and soil carbon storage

### Zygomycota (sensu lato)

- **Estimated diversity**: ~1,000-1,600 described species
- **Key groups**: Mucorales (bread molds), Entomophthorales (insect pathogens), Zoopagales (nematode-trapping fungi)
- **Ecological roles**: Rapid colonizers of organic matter, food spoilage organisms, insect pathogens
- **Note**: The traditional Zygomycota has been reclassified into multiple phyla (Mucoromycota, Zoopagomycota) based on molecular phylogeny

### Chytridiomycota

- **Estimated diversity**: ~1,000 described species; molecular studies suggest much higher diversity
- **Key groups**: Chytridiales, Blastocladiales, Neocallimastigales (rumen fungi)
- **Ecological roles**: Primarily aquatic; decompose chitin, keratin, and cellulose; some parasites (e.g., *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*, the chytrid fungus causing amphibian declines)
- **Unique features**: Only fungi with flagellated zoospores; represent the earliest-diverging fungal lineage

## Habitat-Specific Diversity

### Soil Fungi

Soil harbors the greatest fungal diversity of any habitat:

- **Forest soils**: Rich in both saprotrophic and mycorrhizal species. ECM-dominated forests show higher fungal diversity than AM-dominated forests.
- **Agricultural soils**: Typically less diverse than natural ecosystems, but can harbor significant diversity depending on management practices. No-till systems maintain higher fungal diversity than tilled systems.
- **Grassland soils**: AM-dominated communities with diverse *Glomus* and *Funneliformis* species.
- **Depth distribution**: Fungal diversity generally decreases with soil depth, but some species are adapted to deep soil layers.

### Wood-Inhabiting Fungi

Dead wood supports highly diverse fungal communities that change with decomposition stage:

1. **Fresh wood**: Primary colonizers (Ascomycetes, some Basidiomycetes) that tolerate high C:N ratios and secondary metabolites
2. **Partially decomposed**: Brown-rot and white-rot fungi become dominant, breaking down cellulose and lignin
3. **Advanced decay**: Specialized fungi utilizing remaining nutrients; succession toward humus formation
4. **Very decayed**: Transition to soil fungal community as wood becomes incorporated into humus

### Aquatic Fungi

Freshwater and marine environments contain diverse but understudied fungal communities:

- **Freshwater**: *Ingoldian* fungi (hyphomycetes that produce conidia adapted for underwater dispersal), chytrids parasitizing algae, yeasts in surface films
- **Marine**: Over 500 species described from wood and mangrove substrates; deep-sea fungi associated with organic falls; sea ice fungi
- **Leaf decomposition in streams**: Aquatic hyphomycetes are the primary decomposers of autumn-shed leaves in forest streams, a process critical for stream food webs

## Conservation of Fungal Biodiversity

### Threats to Fungal Diversity

- **Habitat loss**: Deforestation, wetland drainage, and urbanization destroy fungal habitats. Old-growth forests with their complex woody debris support the highest fungal diversity.
- **Air pollution**: Nitrogen deposition alters fungal communities, favoring nitrophilous species and reducing diversity of sensitive species. This is a major threat to ectomycorrhizal fungi in Europe.
- **Climate change**: Altered temperature and precipitation patterns shift fungal distributions, potentially eliminating locally adapted populations.
- **Over-collection**: Some prized edible fungi (matsutake, chanterelles, truffles) are threatened by commercial harvesting.
- **Invasive species**: Introduction of non-native fungi (e.g., *Amanita phalloides* in North America) can alter native fungal communities.

### Fungal Conservation

- **Red List assessments**: The IUCN has assessed relatively few fungal species for threatened status. Many species are "Data Deficient" due to lack of distribution and population data.
- **Habitat protection**: The most effective fungal conservation strategy is protecting the habitats (forests, grasslands, wetlands) that support diverse fungal communities.
- **Fungal reserves**: Some countries have established specific fungal reserves (e.g., in Switzerland and Italy) where collection is restricted.
- **Monitoring**: Long-term monitoring of fungal fruiting patterns (e.g., the UK's National Fungus Day surveys) provides data on distribution changes and population trends.
