## Cool Climate Home Garden and Seed Saving

The home garden in cool temperate climates faces unique challenges centered on the winter food gap — the period when growth effectively ceases from late autumn to early spring. Designing a productive cool-climate garden requires careful planning of planting periods, storage systems, and techniques for extending the growing season.

## The Winter Food Gap

One special feature of cool area home gardens is the need to grow and store crops (in the field or home) over the winter months. The middle of spring is known as the "hungry gap," when winter crops are finished and new crops are not yet yielding. The winter food gap is bridged by growing root crops, cucurbits, and subsequently processed crops for preservation. These crops mature in late summer and are stored or preserved, with some crops also left in the ground.

## Staple Storage Crops

Staple root crops include potato, parsnip, carrot, and minor species such as oca, sunroot ([[green-manure]]) can be sown before winter using oats, tares, or fava beans, then slashed or dug-in in spring.

## Storage Methods

Cellar or pit storage is extensively used in cool areas to store crops:

- **Potatoes**: Stored in straw or ash in dark boxes or humid cellars
- **Carrots and parsnips**: Tops removed, stored in mounds of sand or ash
- **Apples**: Separately wrapped or spread out on attic floors (never stored with root crops due to ethylene production, which causes root crops to shoot)
- **Cabbage or kale**: Stored uprooted, covered with hay in the open, fermented as sauerkraut in large jars, or as sterilised or frozen storage

Many crops today are stored frozen in deep freezers and retain good nutritional value.

## Garden Layout Planning

Planning the garden means small beds for the spring planting with main crops of potato, tomato, and sweet corn, then a reversion to larger mass beds in the autumn planting for winter storage crops (potatoes, carrots, parsnips, turnips, cabbage, fava beans, peas, Brassicas). Chard and parsley are important all-year foods and several successions can be sown at irregular intervals so that they are always in the garden.

Mulch is appropriate for most crops in cool areas. The sole exception may be for spring carrot planting, when mulch is pulled from the seedbed to let it warm up early. All other crops planted in mulch catch up with bare-soil crops and mature at the same time.

## Season Extension

For cool areas, a small glasshouse for seedling trays and a series of cloches for early transplants are invaluable. Even potatoes can be sprouted on old hessian bags indoors or in any open shadehouse, and transplanted complete with leaves and roots by cutting up the bags into squares.

## Perennialising Annuals

Several techniques can turn annuals into perennial crops:

- **Carrots**: Cut a disc off the top, sprout it on soft wet paper in a shallow tray, and replant. One gardener reported eating the same carrots for 9 years using this method.
- **Leeks**: Let one in four go to seed and dry off, then remove the small bulbils from the base and replant. When harvesting, cut off the leek 2 cm below ground level — the central shoot quickly regrows. There is also a perennial bunching leek (pearl onions).
- **Lettuce**: Pick single leaves, allow to go to seed, scatter seed. Mignonette variety is a good self-seeder, as are some Italian varieties.
- **Cabbage**: Cut the head 8 cm above the ground, then deeply crosscut the stem. Each quarter of the stem regrows a small cabbage.
- **Self-seeders**: Garden fennel, chard, parsley, and parsnip self-seed freely if allowed to go to seed. Judicious thinning, weeding, and transplanting keeps them productive.

## Seed Saving

When saving seed from any umbelliferous plant, collect the strong, mature seed from the terminal panicle only — this germinates best. Side panicles contain immature and small seeds and can be pruned or rejected.

## Trellis Systems

Trellis crop in mesothermal climates is important. Many peas, beans, and cucurbits will trellis, as will varieties of tomatoes. Unlike in deserts, trellis over the garden is inappropriate in cool climates; instead, vertical zigzag, inverted "V," or house wall and garden fence trellis is best, aligned north/south where possible for even sun effects.

## Small Fruit

Small fruit such as currants, gooseberry, raspberry, or trellis blackberry is a feature of cool-area gardens. Six to fifteen plants of 7–8 species yields a great deal of fresh or frozen food of high vitamin C value. Many such rows can act as windbreaks in the garden.

## Seed Sovereignty

Seed, especially the 20–30 basic food crop seeds of grains and legumes, is today subjected to a concerted effort at total control by a few agro-chemical multinationals. In response, people and organizations everywhere have set up seed exchanges, seed libraries, open-pollinated and non-patented seed companies, and hundreds of thousands of growers have studied home seed-saving systems.

Despite the enormous loss of locally adapted seed (estimated at 85% of European varieties) that resulted from the monopoly control of the seed market by patenting, the encouraging result is that most gardeners or farmers today can assemble more species and varieties of seed than ever before in history, by using seed exchanges and local collections instead of patented hybrid seeds.

Every gardener who preserves a few valuable varieties — many heirloom or locally reliable traditional food plants specific to site, climate, or regional preference — contributes to a vast range of potential food crops. A region in any climate can escape external food control via a local, regional, and national network of seed exchanges and open-pollinated, non-patented seed. Perennials and trees providing staple food have largely usurped seed patenting, offering additional paths to food sovereignty.
