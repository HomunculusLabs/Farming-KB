Mix with clean hands sprayed with 70 percent isopropyl alcohol, or use latex or

## Container Filling Best Practices

Fill containers densely to avoid air pockets where mushrooms may form but
become trapped and unable to mature. Aborted mushrooms rot and attract flies,
harbor molds, and create bacterial blooms. For plastic bags, fill until they
stretch just before breaking. As mycelium consumes media, contents diminish and
air pockets may form, so a tight fit at the onset minimizes this problem.
Slam bags onto the ground or use a tamping tool to pack down media. Stacked
bags or pots can be compressed by pressing upper ones down onto lower ones.

Use opaque containers for production with a few clear "spy bags" for monitoring
contamination and spawn run progress. Label all containers with mushroom species
and inoculation date. Poke holes for gas exchange immediately after filling and
tying off. If using reusable containers, clean them a day ahead with diluted
bleach and allow time to dry. Keep a cultivation journal recording dates of
inoculation, growing media, spawn source, inoculation rate, and observations.

## Inoculation Rates and Spawn Handling

Using too much spawn is wasteful and expensive, while too little slows
colonization and increases contamination risk. Inoculation rates vary by case
and require experimentation. The species' resiliency, speed, and aggressiveness
play a major role. More spawn provides more surface area and inoculation
points, making mycelia more likely to find each other quickly. For bulk
inoculation of substrates, you create thousands of individual colonies that are
genetically identical, all racing to colonize the substrate before competitors
establish themselves.

Handle spawn carefully before inoculation. Oyster mushroom spawn, for example,
is extremely fast-growing and can become matted or overcolonized if left too
long. Break apart matted spawn during inoculation as you spread it through the
media. Record the details of each batch in a cultivation journal including
