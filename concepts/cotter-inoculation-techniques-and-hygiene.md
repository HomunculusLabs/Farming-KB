# Inoculation Techniques and Hygiene

Inoculation, the process of introducing mushroom spawn into prepared growing
medium, is the most difficult step to master in mushroom cultivation. Sanitation
measures are critical since the grower is in direct contact with both prepared
media and pure culture spawn. Clean tools, clean hands, clean floors, clean
containers, and clean everything: it takes only one set of dirty hands to ruin
an entire day or more of work.

## Setting Up the Inoculation Area

For pasteurized substrates, inoculation can occur in a nonsterile environment
provided the mixing surface is clean, such as a lightly bleached tarp or table.
For sterilized substrates, a clean room with HEPA filtration and laboratory
space is required. Pasteurized substrates are easier to work with, less prone to
contamination, and require lower cost and energy input, though not all mushrooms
will thrive on them. Locate the inoculation area at or next to the pasteurization
or sterilization room to minimize the distance and time media are exposed.

Dedicated inoculation rooms should have sweepable floors (concrete or
landscaping fabric), minimal airflow with prefiltered incoming air, and
surfaces designed for easy cleaning. Larger structures can be partitioned with
plastic curtains to reduce air movement between work areas. Organize all
materials before starting: clean surfaces, sanitize tools, stock gloves, rubbing
alcohol, and twist ties. Keep dedicated tools in the inoculation area and never
remove them for other purposes.

## Inoculation Methods

For bulk substrates, inoculation entails mixing spawn into the substrate,
transferring the mixture to containers, and moving containers to the spawn run
area. A basic method for oyster mushrooms on shredded straw is to cool media on
a clean tarp, mix spawn thoroughly, and fill containers immediately. Spread
media in an even layer 3 to 4 inches deep, break spawn into the smallest
fragments possible, broadcast evenly across the surface, and lightly rake in.
Repeat layering for larger volumes.

Mix with clean hands sprayed with 70 percent isopropyl alcohol, or use latex or
tight-fitting rubber gloves for better dexterity. On larger scales, use a clean
rake designated solely for mushroom cultivation that has been dunked in diluted
bleach (50 percent) or wiped with alcohol. Keep a tray of bleach solution
nearby for dipping the rake. If the rake accidentally touches anything other
than the medium, sanitize it before returning. When done mixing, push
inoculated media into a pile for easier bagging or tray filling.

## Container Filling Best Practices

Fill containers densely to avoid air pockets where mushrooms may form but
become trapped and unable to mature. Aborted mushrooms rot and attract flies,
harbor molds, and create bacterial blooms. For plastic bags, fill until they
stretch just before breaking. As mycelium consumes media, contents diminish and
air pockets may form, so a tight fit at the onset minimizes this problem.
Slam bags onto the ground or use a tamping tool to pack down media. Stacked
bags or pots can be compressed by pressing upper ones down onto lower ones.

Use opaque containers for production with a few clear "spy bags" for monitoring
contamination and spawn run progress. Label all containers with mushroom species
and inoculation date. Poke holes for gas exchange immediately after filling and
tying off. If using reusable containers, clean them a day ahead with diluted
bleach and allow time to dry. Keep a cultivation journal recording dates of
inoculation, growing media, spawn source, inoculation rate, and observations.

## Inoculation Rates and Spawn Handling

Using too much spawn is wasteful and expensive, while too little slows
colonization and increases contamination risk. Inoculation rates vary by case
and require experimentation. The species' resiliency, speed, and aggressiveness
play a major role. More spawn provides more surface area and inoculation
points, making mycelia more likely to find each other quickly. For bulk
inoculation of substrates, you create thousands of individual colonies that are
genetically identical, all racing to colonize the substrate before competitors
establish themselves.

Handle spawn carefully before inoculation. Oyster mushroom spawn, for example,
is extremely fast-growing and can become matted or overcolonized if left too
long. Break apart matted spawn during inoculation as you spread it through the
media. Record the details of each batch in a cultivation journal including
growing media formulas, spawn rates, temperatures, and yield data. This
information is invaluable for refining your process over time.

## Sterile Inoculation for Laboratory Work

Inoculation of sterilized substrates or agar plates requires a much higher
standard of cleanliness. Work inside a laminar flow hood or near a Bunsen
burner flame. All tools must be sterilized, typically by flaming with alcohol
between each transfer. The work area should be cleaned with 70 percent
isopropyl alcohol before and after each session. Wear clean gloves and avoid
talking or breathing directly over open containers or plates.

For agar-to-agar transfers, use a sterilized scalpel to cut a small section of
mycelium from the leading edge of a colonized plate and transfer it to a fresh
plate. Work quickly to minimize exposure time. Seal plates with tape or
Parafilm after transfer. For grain-to-grain transfer, use a sterilized spoon
or scoop to move colonized grain from a donor jar to a recipient jar. The
recipient jar is opened only briefly near the laminar flow hood, grain is added,
and the lid is replaced immediately.

## Automation Considerations

Automating inoculation is possible but not cheap. Commercial equipment includes
ribbon or paddle mixers, substrate elevators, and conveyor belts that save
labor but also magnify problems like low-yielding formulas, strain
degeneration, and contamination. Before committing to automation, analyze each
step of production and eliminate potential failure points. Used agricultural
machines can be retrofitted: used paddle mixers can be fitted with steam
inputs, and concrete mixing trucks have been modified with injection ports for
bulk media pasteurization.

## Key Points

- Inoculation is the most contamination-sensitive step in mushroom cultivation
- Pasteurized substrates can be inoculated on clean tarps; sterilized substrates
  require HEPA-filtered clean rooms
- Break spawn into smallest fragments and broadcast evenly for best distribution
- Fill containers densely to prevent trapped air pockets and aborted mushrooms
- Use 70 percent isopropyl alcohol or 50 percent bleach for surface sanitation
- Keep dedicated tools in the inoculation area; never remove for other uses
- Label all containers with species, date, and spawn source; maintain a journal
- Pack plastic bags until they stretch; slam or tamp to eliminate air pockets
- Automation magnifies both production volume and production problems equally
