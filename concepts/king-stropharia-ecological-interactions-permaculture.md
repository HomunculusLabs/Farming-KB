## The Bee-Mycelium Interaction

### Discovery of the Phenomenon

The interaction between honey bees and King Stropharia mycelium was discovered inadvertently at Stamets' farm. Over a period of six weeks during one summer, bees were observed attacking a King Stropharia bed, excavating the sawdust and exposing the mycelium to air. The mystery was resolved: the bees were not interested in the sawdust itself but in the underlying sweet mushroom mycelium that had colonized the sawdust. This explanation connected a widespread but poorly understood bee behavior to a specific biological mechanism — the attraction of bees to the sugar-rich cytoplasm of fungal mycelium.

### Nutritional Significance for Bees

The mycelial cytoplasm of King Stropharia contains simple sugars, amino acids, and other soluble nutrients that are readily accessible to foraging bees. In periods when nectar sources are scarce, mycelial feeding may provide an important supplemental energy source for bee colonies. The ecological significance of this interaction may be particularly relevant in agricultural landscapes where diverse floral resources have been reduced by monoculture farming practices, potentially contributing to bee colony health and resilience.

The sugar-rich cytoplasm is exposed when bees excavate the substrate and break the mycelial cords, creating wounds from which the cellular contents leak. This suggests that the bees are actively harvesting rather than passively consuming surface exudates, and that the energetic reward is sufficient to justify the considerable excavation effort documented in the field observations.

### Implications for Apiary Design

The discovery suggests that establishing King Stropharia beds near apiaries could provide supplemental forage for honey bees. The sawdust or wood chip substrate required for King Stropharia cultivation can be placed in close proximity to hives without interfering with normal beekeeping operations. If the bees' attraction to mycelial cytoplasm proves to be nutritionally significant, this could become a simple, low-maintenance strategy for supporting pollinator health in integrated farming systems.

## King Stropharia as Fish Food

### The Accidental Discovery

The use of King Stropharia as fish food was discovered during silver salmon aquaculture. When mature King Stropharia mushrooms were thrown into a fish-holding tank, they floated on the surface. Fly larvae that had proliferated inside the developing mushrooms soon emerged, struggling in the water. The fish struck the large mushrooms to dislodge the swollen larvae, which were eagerly consumed. After several days of feeding mushrooms to the fish, the salmon learned to anticipate the succulent, squirming larvae and would excitedly strike at the King Stropharia as soon as the mushrooms hit the water.
