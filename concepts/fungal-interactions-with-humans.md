# Fungal Interactions with Humans

Human activities increasingly affect fungal diversity, activity, [[fungal-biodiversity-and-ecosystem-function]]. This concept explores the multifaceted interactions between anthropogenic environmental changes and fungal-mediated processes, focusing on acidifying pollutants, heavy metals, radionuclides, and elevated atmospheric CO₂.

## The Human Footprint on Fungal Ecosystems

As global population increases, the effects of human activities on ecosystems escalate. While climate change, acid rain, nitrogen deposition, and stratospheric ozone depletion dominate public discourse, the impacts of these environmental changes on fungi rarely receive attention in popular media. Yet fungi are fundamental mediators [[fungi-as-regulators-of-ecosystem-processes]] — nutrient cycling, decomposition, plant-microbe symbioses, and soil food web dynamics — making their response to anthropogenic change critically important for ecosystem health.

## The Acidifying Pollutant Story

The interaction between acidifying pollutants and fungi represents a compelling case study in the evolution of scientific thought. The narrative began with the industrial revolution in Europe, where industrially-related changes in ecosystems and organisms were attributed to pollution. Early observations included the evolution of a dark morph of the peppered moth (*Biston betularia*) on soot-coated tree bark, and dramatic declines in lichen diversity and abundance near industrial centers.

### From Canopy Damage to Root-Level Effects

The identification of central European forest decline ("Waldsterben") in the 1970s shifted attention to belowground processes. Soil ecologists discovered that acid rain was affecting both root growth and the mycorrhizal status of trees. The realization emerged that the pollutant sulfur dioxide (SO₂), released from combustion of high-sulfur coal, combines with atmospheric water to form sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), which deposits onto forest canopies and soils.

Subsequent research expanded to include:
- **Ozone (O₃)** in the lower atmosphere, which damages cuticular waxes and reduces photosynthetic capacity
- **Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)** from fossil fuel combustion, leading to nitrogen deposition and forest soil nitrogen saturation
- Recognition that these pollutants never operate in isolation — ecosystems experience complex mixtures of acidifying agents

### The Dighton and Jansen Two-Directional Impact Model

Dighton and Jansen (1991) proposed a two-directional impact model of acidifying pollutants on mycorrhizal development:

**Direction 1 — Aboveground pathway:** Pollutant-induced reduction in photosynthetic capacity of the tree canopy reduces allocation of photosynthate to roots and their mycorrhizae. Reduced energy supply decreases overall mycorrhizal colonization and selects for fungal species that survive on low carbohydrate supplies.

**Direction 2 — Belowground pathway:** Acidifying pollutants reduce soil pH, making toxic metals (aluminum, manganese, magnesium) more soluble and plant-available. Increased toxicity leads to reduced root growth, root dieback, and reduced mycorrhizal fungal growth and root colonization.

This dual-pathway model explains why both aboveground canopy damage and belowground soil acidification contribute to the loss of [[mycorrhizal-diversity-and-ecosystem-function]] function.

## Critical Loads and Biological Indicators

### Using Macrofungal Fruit Bodies as Indicators

In Europe, changes in the composition and abundance of macrofungal fruit bodies, particularly ectomycorrhizal Basidiomycotina, have been adopted as biological indicators for terrestrial forested ecosystems. The ratio of saprotrophic to ectomycorrhizal fruit bodies provides a diagnostic tool for assessing forest health:

- **Latent disturbance:** Ectomycorrhizal fungi decrease to 40%, while lignicolous species increase to >30%
- **Acute disturbance:** Ectomycorrhizal species decrease to <40%, lignicolous species increase to >40%
- **Lethal disturbance:** Ectomycorrhizal species decrease to <20%, lignicolous species rise to >55%

In healthy forest ecosystems, fruit bodies of mycorrhizal fungi typically form 45–50% of all fruit bodies found. In polluted stands, this drops to approximately 10%.

### The Caveat of Fruiting vs. Root Colonization

A critical complication in interpreting these patterns is that the appearance or non-appearance of fruit bodies may bear little relation to the actual abundance of that mycorrhizal morphotype on root systems. Molecular analyses by Jonsson (1998) demonstrated only 1–4% correspondence between the species of fruit bodies and mycorrhizae observed on roots in the NITREX experiment, adding complexity to the interpretation of environmental monitoring data.

## Nitrogen Deposition and Mycorrhizal Community Change

### Shifts in Fungal Community Composition

Nitrogen deposition from atmospheric sources has profound effects on mycorrhizal fungal communities:

- **Arnolds (1989a,b)** documented changes in fungal species composition in a nutrient-poor acidic-soil Dutch pasture under long-term fertilizer application. While total species numbers remained stable between 1974 and 1980, the community structure shifted dramatically — species like *Hygrocybe ceracea* and *Geoglossum glutinosum* were lost, while *Marasmius oreades* and *Panaeolus acuminatus* increased 6- to 400-fold.
- **Brandrud (1995)** in the Swedish NITREX experiment showed that dominant ectomycorrhizal genera *Cortinarius* and *Russula* were reduced by additional nitrogen, *Lactarius* showed little change, and specific species like *Paxillus involutus* and *Lactarius rufus* increased.
- **Rühling & Tyler (1991)** found that adding three times ambient nitrogen deposition to beech woodlands caused near-complete cessation of mycorrhizal fungal fruiting, while many saprotrophic species increased fruiting.

### Mechanisms of Nitrogen Effects on Mycorrhizae

The effects of nitrogen operate through several mechanisms:

1. **Reduced plant dependency:** When nitrogen is no longer limiting, plants reduce carbohydrate allocation to mycorrhizal partners, diminishing the mutualism
2. **Nutrient imbalance:** Chronic nitrogen addition induces limitations of other nutrients (potassium, phosphorus), supporting Liebig's "Law of the Minimum"
3. **Phosphorus interactions:** Changes in the relative availabilities of N and P strongly influence mycorrhizal community structure, promoting species with high phosphatase-producing potential and suppressing those capable of acquiring nitrogen from organic sources
4. **Direct toxicity:** At extreme levels, nitrogen saturation leads to nitrate leaching, increased N₂O and CH₄ production, and greenhouse gas amplification

### Species-Specific Responses to Aluminum Toxicity

Acidification increases aluminum solubility, which differentially affects ectomycorrhizal fungal species:
- **Thompson & Medve (1984)** showed that 146 mM aluminum suppressed growth of *Cenococcum*, *Pisolithus*, and *Thelephora*, but *Suillus* showed no growth reduction below 1000 mM
- The ratios Al:Ca, Al:Mg, and Al:PO₄ are important determinants of fungal growth
- Improved growth at low Al:PO₄ ratios, even at high Al concentrations, was explained by aluminum absorption and immobilization into polyphosphate droplets in fungal hyphae

### Ozone Effects on Mycorrhizal Structure

Ozone exposure alters the nature (not degree) of root colonization in arbuscular mycorrhizae. In sugar maple (*Acer saccharum*), Duckmanton and Widden (1994) found that ozone reduced the number of arbuscules but increased vesicles and hyphal coils within the root cortex. This is interpreted as a stress response — vesicles are storage structures requiring less energy than arbuscules to produce.

## Mycorrhizal Diversity and Ecosystem Function

### The Diversity-Function Debate

The question of whether mycorrhizal species diversity per se, or the composition of the species assemblage, determines ecosystem function has received considerable investigation:

- **Van der Heijden et al. (1998)** suggested that plant productivity is highest when [[singh-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-diversity-grassland-ecosystems]] is greatest
- **Baxter & Dighton (2001)** showed that ectomycorrhizal diversity per se was a better determinant of improved birch seedling nutrient content than actual species composition or colonization rates, with stronger effects on phosphorus than nitrogen uptake
- Much more work remains to fully explain the consequences of changes in both mycorrhizal and saprotrophic [[mineralogical-controls-fungal-community-structure]] on ecosystem-level processes

## Conservation Implications

The sensitivity of fungal communities to anthropogenic pollution has led to significant conservation actions:

- **"Red data" lists** for fungal species conservation have been adopted, following Arnolds' documentation of species declines
- In the United Kingdom, fungi and non-vascular plants have been included in species conservation lists
- These actions result from recognition that fungal species are being lost due to anthropogenic influences, with cascading consequences for ecosystem function
