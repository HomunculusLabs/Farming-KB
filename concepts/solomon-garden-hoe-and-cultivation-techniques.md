# Solomon Garden Hoe and Cultivation Techniques

In "Gardening When It Counts," [[garden-hand-tools-solomon]] for
low-input [[home-vegetable-garden-planning]]. Of all hoes in his collection, only one
gets regular use -- the swanneck hoe, properly fitted and kept sharp.

## The Swanneck Hoe

The ordinary garden hoe has a rectangular blade attached via a swanneck
-- a mild steel rod connecting blade to handle. The swanneck must be
bent so the blade is parallel to the soil when held comfortably with the
user standing upright. At this angle, the blade rapidly cuts weeds just
below the surface while loosening soil and breaking crusts. This action
is called "cultivation."

If the angle is wrong, you must hold the hoe too far from your body to
correct the blade, causing fatigue. Many cheap hoes have poorly bent
swannecks that force awkward posture.

## Fitting the Hoe to the User

The swanneck should be bent to suit the individual. Since it is mild
steel, it can be bent with fair force. The blade should be parallel to
ground at a natural standing height. A properly fitted hoe makes
cultivation nearly effortless; a poorly fitted one turns weeding into
backbreaking labor.

## Sharpening the Hoe

File the blade to a sharp, uniform single bevel of about 20 degrees,
like a chisel:

1. **Initial sharpening**: Place the hoe on the ground or in a bench
   vise, press down near the socket, and file with controlled strokes.
2. **Maintenance**: After the first sharpening, touch-ups take about
   ten strokes. Sharpen each spring.
3. **The cost of dullness**: Solomon learned this with a cheap hoe that
   dulled by the end of each 100-foot row. It takes three times the
   effort to weed with a slightly dull hoe; a really dull one is
   unbearable. He carried a file in his back pocket, resharpening after
   each row.

## Hoe Quality: What to Look For

A quality hoe sharpened each spring stays sharp through half the summer.
The most reliable indicator is how the swanneck attaches:

- **Cheap hoes**: Ferrule (thin metal sleeve) around the handle. The
  hole gradually enlarges from stress, the swanneck loosens, and the
  head wobbles. The owner tries wedges and improvisations until a new
  handle is needed.
- **Quality hoes**: The swanneck end is forged (or welded) into a
  strong, slightly tapered socket affixed with screws or a bolt. Any
  manufacturer making a forged socket almost certainly uses quality
  metal for the blade.

## Weeding Technique for Soft Ground

1. Hold the handle with both hands.
2. Pull the blade toward you, cutting about half an inch (1.25 cm) below
   the surface.
3. Most weeds die within hours. Survivors almost surely die when cut
   again a week later.
4. Weeds from tubers or bulblets may need six to eight cuttings over
   six to eight weeks. But Solomon asserts no weed survives repetitive
   leaf removal -- even horseradish or comfrey eventually succumbs.

## Weeding Frequency

If weeded once weekly during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]], there will never be
large, resistant weeds. All are killed rapidly when small and tender.
Solomon reports weeding 2,000 square feet (200 sq m) of beds and paths
in about one concentrated hour, once weekly.

Spring requires the most frequent attention -- weeds grow rapidly and
wet weather may keep you out of action, allowing weeds to reach the
size demanding exhausting chopping rather than easy scraping. As summer
wanes, weeding frequency decreases.
