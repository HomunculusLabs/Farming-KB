# Liming and pH Management

Before deciding on a soil-feeding regime, you must first discover
whether the soil is acid or alkaline. This will have a major effect
not only on your cultivation technique but also on the plants you
choose to grow. Acidity or alkalinity of soil is determined by its
lime content and is measured in pH units on a scale of 1 to 14,
where neutral soil has a pH of 7. Most garden plants thrive in a
pH of about 6.5, though individual crops have specific preferences.

## Testing Soil pH

Testing the lime content of your soil is simple and can be done at
home using widely available pH test kits. These use a chemical
solution that changes color when mixed with soil in a test tube.
Yellow or orange indicates acid soil, dark green shows alkaline
soil, and bright green means neutral pH. Always conduct a pH test
when you take over a new garden and repeat every year, particularly
if you are trying to alter the pH level. Matching the sample against
a color chart provides a more precise idea of your soil's pH.

## Raising Soil pH

Making an acid soil more alkaline is achieved by adding lime, but
do not expect dramatic changes overnight. The effect is slow, so
apply small amounts regularly rather than large quantities at once.
Excessive lime can chemically lock up some plant nutrients,
particularly trace elements, making them unavailable to roots. Like
other chemicals, lime will gradually be washed through the soil by
drainage, and applications of manure and compost have an acidifying
effect that counteracts liming over time.

## Types of Lime

Slaked lime (calcium oxide), sometimes sold as garden lime, is the
most readily available form and lasts longer in soil than builder's
lime. [[cervantes-secondary-nutrient-deficiency]]. Calcified
seaweed contains several plant foods as well as lime and lasts in
soil for two to three years.

## When to Apply Lime

Apply lime several weeks before sowing or planting. Ideally, dig
manure into the soil in the fall and apply lime in the spring.
Never apply lime to soil that has just been manured because it will
combine with the manure to form ammonia gas, releasing nitrogen

## pH and Nutrient Availability

Soil pH affects the availability of many plant nutrients. At very
low or very high pH values, essential nutrients become locked in
forms that plant roots cannot absorb. This is why maintaining pH
around 6.5 is important for most crops. See
