# Soil Building in Permaculture

Soil is the foundation of [[permaculture-design]]. Both Mollison and Holmgren treat soil not as a substrate for growing plants but as a living ecosystem -- the most complex and biologically diverse system on Earth. Building soil is simultaneously the first task and the ongoing practice of any permaculture design.

## Mollison's Approach: Practical Soil Strategies

Mollison's soil building strategies are embedded throughout the design course. Key techniques include:

### Mulching Systems

Mulch is the primary soil-building tool. Mollison describes multiple mulch strategies:
- **Sheet mulching**: Layering organic material directly on the soil surface to suppress weeds, retain moisture, and build humus. "Spot-mulched crops" in zone 2 use thin, targeted mulch rather than heavy applications.
- **Living mulch**: Plants like comfrey and Siberian pea tree that can be cut repeatedly to provide surface mulch. Comfrey is particularly valued for its deep taproot that mines minerals from subsoil.
- **Green manure crops**: Clover, sunflower seed, and other plants sown specifically to be cut and returned to the soil.
- **Pit mulching**: Burying organic material in pits for rapid anaerobic decomposition, especially effective in [[fire-control-in-permaculture]] as a fuel reduction strategy.

### Soil Conditioning

Mollison describes the use of the Wallace soil conditioner -- a sophisticated implement that opens compacted soil to a depth of nine inches, creating crevices for water storage and root penetration. The results are dramatic:
- "A fantastic amount of water storage within the landscape"
- Soil temperatures 15-19 degrees Fahrenheit above surrounding soils in winter
- "Soil conditioning sharply decreases frost" and extends the growing season at both ends
- Trees that might bear in 17-18 years "will normally bear within three years in conditioned soil"

The conditioner includes a seed box that drips seeds at pre-regulated rates into the crevices, allowing direct conversion of pasture to crop without cultivation. On stubborn compacted soils, the process is repeated: go down four inches, let grass recover, then recondition to nine inches.

### Swales and Earthworks

[[permaculture-earthworks-guide]] serve a dual function: water infiltration and soil building. Swales catch runoff water, allowing it to infiltrate and build soil depth on slopes. The earth banks thrown up from swale construction accumulate leaf litter and silt, creating mulch that can be transferred to gardens.

The swale is fundamental to water storage: "We store most of our water in our soil." Swales create ribbed systems that "run water out and store it up." Roots follow the crevices right down and then "going on further down, again making channels for water for even further penetration."

### Biological Soil Opening

Where mechanical soil conditioning is not available or affordable, Mollison recommends biological alternatives:
- **Daikon radish**: Broadcast daikon radish to spike soil to about two feet deep. Being biennial, they rot in place and never need pulling. "We accomplish it biologically."
- **Pioneer tree species**: Western red cedar and other deep-rooted trees act as "soil spikes," starting the process of opening compacted ground.
- **Log planting**: In small areas, dig holes, put in rotting logs, and plant vegetables where the logs decompose underground.

### Animal Manures

Animals are the nutrient cyclers of the [[permaculture-zones-system]]. The [[forage-systems-animals-permaculture]] in zone 2 generates high-nitrogen, high-potash mulch that "rots quickly in the garden." Mollison designs animal shelters with mesh floors at the edge of zone 1 so manure is always available for garden transfer without carting. "Everybody has been very pleased with them. They always have dry, stored manure and shredded bedding ready."

### Seaweed

Mollison recommends dried seaweed, powders, and concentrates for water retention in dry soils. Seaweed works "on the surface film itself" and greatly assists water storage and plant resistance to wilt.

### Hardpan Problems

Mollison warns against using superphosphate on calcareous soils: "When you put superphosphate on top, the rain carries it down to certain depths; then summer comes and the moisture evaporates and an insoluble tri-calcium phosphate forms in a concrete block 15 inches down." He calls this a "type one error" -- a mistake that perpetuates difficulty forever. Instead, use phosphate rock or biological methods.

## Holmgren's Approach: Soil Science and Energy Accounting

Holmgren provides a deeper scientific framework for understanding soil. Drawing on the work of William Albrecht and others, he emphasizes:

### Cation Exchange Capacity

The ability of soil colloids (clay and humus) to hold and exchange positively charged nutrient ions (cations) is fundamental to soil fertility. High cation exchange capacity means the soil can hold more nutrients in plant-available form. Building humus through organic matter additions increases CEC far more than adding clay.

### The Soil Food Web

The [[soil-food-web-structure]] -- bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms -- is the engine of nutrient cycling. Mycorrhizal fungi form the most critical link, extending root systems by orders of magnitude and facilitating nutrient exchange between plants. Holmgren notes that "a rainforest clearing with abundant sunlight and fertility will grow a profusion of competing annual weeds, vines and trees; in the mature forest, adjacent plants are more likely to have mutual or symbiotic relationships, such as trees that exchange nutrients via mycorrhizal fungi."

### Energy Descent and Soil

Holmgren argues that [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] will force a return to biological soil fertility as chemical fertilizers become expensive and unavailable. This is not a regression but an opportunity: biological soil building creates soils that are more fertile, more resilient, and more self-maintaining than chemically managed soils.

## Integration with Design

Soil building is not a separate activity but integrated into every element of the design:
- [[forests-in-permaculture]] build soil through leaf litter and root turnover
- [[aquaculture-in-permaculture]] produces nutrient-rich mud for garden application
- [[composting-systems]] close the nutrient loop between household waste and garden fertility
- [[keyline-design]] distributes water to maximize soil development across the landscape

## The Nutrient Vortex

Mollison describes the zone system as a "biological funnel" or "nutrient vortex" -- deliberately concentrating nutrients from outer zones into inner zones through animal collection and cycling. "Everything farms for itself, does exactly the same thing that we are doing. Animals do the same."
