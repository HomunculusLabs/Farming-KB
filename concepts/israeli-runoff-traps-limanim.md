# Israeli Runoff Traps and Aquifer Intake Areas

This concept covers passive water harvesting and groundwater recharge techniques from arid and semi-arid landscapes, as detailed in [[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual*. The methods described — Israeli runoff traps ([[water-management]] wisdom applicable to dryland [[permaculture-design]] worldwide.

## Israeli Runoff Traps (Limanim)

[[limanim]] are a class of dry dam structures developed in Israel for harvesting surface runoff in arid and semi-arid regions — among the most well-documented passive water harvesting systems for establishing trees and crops in desert environments.

### Structure and Dimensions

Each liman functions as a small, flat-bottomed impoundment bounded by dry stone walls:

- **Footprint**: 0.1 to 0.5 hectares per unit
- **Wall height**: 1–2 meters
- **Wall width**: 2–5 meters (dry stone construction)
- **Base**: graded flat to ensure even water distribution across the impoundment floor
- **Stone support**: placed on the inside base of the bank to prevent scouring and structural failure during flood events

The dry stone construction is deliberate — water seeps through slowly, recharging the soil profile rather than evaporating from a permanent surface pond.

### Hydraulic Design

Limanim are designed as a **cascading series**. Sills constructed at the ends of each wall act as spillways, releasing water at a depth of 40–60 cm. Excess water flows over the sill and is directed to the next liman downstream, creating a chain of impoundments across a slope. The staged approach ensures no single structure receives the full force of a flood, water is distributed across the landscape, and sediment loads are managed progressively as each liman traps a portion of suspended material.

### Catchment-to-Impoundment Ratio

The runoff contributing area is typically **30 to 500 times** the impoundment area, reflecting the low rainfall and high runoff coefficients of desert surfaces where bare compacted soils generate rapid surface flow. Designers must calculate expected runoff from the catchment based on rainfall intensity, ground cover, and soil type.

### Water Delivery Methods

The method of delivering runoff water to limanim significantly affects their longevity:

- **Low-slope diversion drains** convey water gently, minimizing the kinetic energy that carries sediment
- **Sheet flow** (unguided overland flow) is the gentlest delivery method and produces the least silting
- High-velocity channelized flow should be avoided, as it delivers large sediment loads that rapidly fill impoundments

### Soil and Vegetation Management

- **Tillage** of the impoundment floor keeps the soil profile open and receptive to infiltration
- **Livestock compaction** must be strictly prevented — hooves seal the soil surface and destroy infiltration capacity
- **Trees** are established directly in the impoundments, where their roots access the stored soil moisture
- **Catch-crops** can be grown in wet years when impoundments receive above-average fill

## Aquifer Intake Areas

Aquifer intake areas are landscape features where surface water enters groundwater storage through open subsurface channels. Identifying and protecting these zones is critical for maintaining spring flow, river baseflow, and well yields in dryland regions.

### Landscape Settings

Aquifer intakes are found predominantly in hill country, at locations including:

- **Ridges and plateaus** with fractured or porous substrates
- **Detritus slopes** (scree and colluvium) where open voids between rock fragments allow rapid percolation
- **Dune fields** where deep sand profiles act as massive infiltration zones

### Geological Types

Effective aquifer intake geologies include:

- Loose gravel ridges with high interstitial porosity
- Shattered or soluble rock formations (karst limestone, fractured basalt)
- Dune sands with deep profiles
- Forested ridges where root channels and organic matter maintain soil structure
- Porous limestone with solution channels

### Aquifer Behavior

Groundwater travels distances ranging from **meters to kilometers** before emerging at springs, rivers, or artesian basins. Some aquifer systems can **flow uphill** relative to local terrain — a function of confined pressure in artesian conditions. Bore pipes into such systems produce hydraulic heads of **0.5 to 7 meters**, enabling water to flow to the surface without pumping.

### Flatland Aquifers

On flat terrain, perched aquifers form where sand layers sit above impermeable desert pavement or clay. These shallow systems are productive for local supply but vulnerable to contamination and clogging.

### Threats to Intake Function

Aquifer intakes are degraded by:

- **Volcanic ash** deposition, which fills pore spaces
- **Desert dust** accumulation, forming surface crusts that shed water
- **Blown sand** from overgrazed areas, which fills voids in gravel and scree
- **Deforestation**, which removes root channels, reduces organic matter inputs, and allows soil compaction

### Restoration and Enhancement

Intake areas can be actively enhanced through several strategies:

- **Planting trees on ridges and slopes** to maintain root channels and organic soil structure
- **Deep interceptor drains** to capture runoff and direct it into permeable substrates
- **Ripping rock pavement** to fracture sealed surfaces and restore infiltration
- **Leading drains to wells** in shattered rock to maximize recharge
- **Boulder banks and pits** in gravel deposits to slow flow and increase infiltration residence time
- **Windbreaks** and **protected reserves** to reduce erosion and prevent compaction

## Quanats

Quanats are ancient underground water galleries, thousands of kilometers of which were constructed across Middle Eastern deserts. Each consists of a gently sloping tunnel tapping into an aquifer (often at the base of a mountain) and conveying groundwater by gravity to settlements and agricultural land at lower elevations.

### Characteristics

- **Construction**: A mother well reaches the water table, then a tunnel extends downhill at a slight gradient
- **Permanence**: Once built, quanats are effectively permanent and cannot be easily diverted
- **Regional utility**: Well-suited to Southwest Asian geology where mountain aquifers provide reliable baseflow
- **Secondary benefit**: Cool air vented from tunnel openings was historically used to cool adjacent houses

While primarily of historical interest today, their principle of gravity-fed groundwater transport remains relevant for modern dryland design.

## Infiltration Rates by Soil Type

Understanding infiltration rates is essential for sizing water harvesting earthworks. The following rates describe the time to infiltrate **25 mm of rainfall** under various soil conditions:

### Sand and Loess Soils

- **Minimum rate**: 1–7 mm/hr
- **Time for 25 mm**: 2–3 hours
- Accept water rapidly but may have limited water-holding capacity without deep profiles

### Clay-Loam Soils

- **Minimum rate**: 4–1 mm/hr (decreasing as clay content increases)
- **Time for 25 mm**: 6–20 hours
- Moderate infiltration with better retention than sands, but prone to surface sealing if bare

### Alkaline Swelling Clays

- **Minimum rate**: approximately 1 mm/hr
- **Time for 25 mm**: up to 24 hours
- Most problematic for infiltration — they swell when wet, sealing surface pores and shedding water

### Engineered Infiltration Surfaces

Mollison describes several techniques for creating effective infiltration surfaces over alkaline clay pans:

1. **Sand blanket method**: Spreading neutral or acidic sands to a depth of 0.5 meters or more over alkaline clay pans creates an excellent infiltration surface. The sand layer accepts water rapidly and stores up to **1 meter of water** for extended periods, while the clay pan beneath prevents deep percolation losses.

2. **Deep swale method**: Deep swales excavated in alkaline soils and backfilled with sands and gravels create linear infiltration galleries that accept and store water along contour lines.

3. **Impermeable barrier method**: An impermeable layer (clay, plastic, gley, or tar) is buried beneath deep sands, with drainage slits cut every 100–200 meters. This creates a subsurface water storage system that holds water in the sand layer above the barrier, allowing slow lateral movement to the drainage slits for extraction or controlled deep percolation.

## Design Integration

These four systems form a comprehensive toolkit for dryland water management. In an integrated permaculture design:

- **Limanim** capture and store surface runoff at the landscape scale
- **Aquifer intake protection** ensures that natural groundwater recharge continues to function
