# Fungal Edibility Classification Key

A classification key for determining the edibility of large, fleshy fungi was
developed by Julius A. Palmer in his pioneering American mycological work. This
key organized mushrooms and toadstools into broad divisions based on the nature
of their spore-bearing surface, then provided sub-classifications by color,
reaction to bruising, and other observable characteristics. It was one of the
first original classification systems published in America for this purpose.

## The Four Grand Divisions

Palmer's system begins with the most fundamental distinction: the type of
surface that bears the reproductive spores. Any toadstool will fall into one of
four categories when the cap is inverted and examined.

### 1. Gills (Agaricini)

The largest family of mushrooms bears thin plates set on their edges, radiating
from a common center like spokes of a wheel. These "spokes" are called gills
(lamellae), with the stem serving as the hub. This family includes both the
common meadow mushroom and the deadly Amanita species. Because harmless and
deadly species can look nearly identical, Palmer regarded the Agaricini as the
least safe for the amateur collector.

### 2. Pores and Tubes (Boleti and Polyporei)

Instead of gills, some fungi have a spongy surface composed of fine tubes. When
the stem is distinct, of ordinary length, and the tubes are easily separable
from each other, the fungus is a Boletus. When the tubes are not separable and
the stem is abortive or absent, it is a Polyporus (bracket fungus). The
edibility rules for this group are relatively straightforward:

- Tubes brown, yellowish, or greenish with a russet or brown cap, and white
  flesh that does not change color when cut — edible or at least harmless
- Brilliant red or yellow coloring, or flesh that turns blue when bruised —
  best rejected
- Tubes red at the orifice — doubtless poisonous

### 3. Teeth or Spines (Hydnei)

The third group bears small points or spines instead of gills or pores. The
spreading hydnum (*[[hydnum-repandum]]*) is the most commonly encountered species.
It is usually yellow, sometimes reddish, with firm flesh and a deformed or
eccentric stem. Palmer considered teeth-bearing fungi safe for amateur
experimentation, noting that all species of any size were regarded as having a
good character by the leading authorities of his time.

### 4. Enclosed Spores (Puffballs)

Puffballs bear their spores internally rather than on external surfaces. When
young, the interior is filled with firm, white tissue. When mature, the
contents are discharged as a cloud of dust when squeezed. The giant puffball
(*[[lycoperdon-giganteum]]*) is the most notable edible species. Palmer considered
all white puffballs safe if cooked promptly, noting that no poisonous fungus
resembles them.

## The Series Classification

Palmer expanded the four grand divisions into a more detailed key organized by
series, each with specific edibility determinations.

### Series 1: Pore-Bearing Fungi

This series distinguishes Boleti (ground-growing, distinct stem, separable
tubes) from Polyporei (wood-growing, abortive stems, inseparable tubes). The
key proceeds through characteristics such as tube color, flesh reaction to
air exposure, and cap coloration to arrive at an edibility verdict.

### Series 2: Gill-Bearing Fungi

The gill-bearing series is the most complex. Sub-classifications consider:
- Gill color progression (pink to purple to black suggests edibility)
- Presence or absence of a ring (annulus) on the stem
- Volva or cup at the base (presence suggests dangerous Amanita species)
- Milk or latex exudation when cut
- Acridity when raw versus mildness
- Flesh brittleness (brittle flesh suggests Russula or Lactarius)
- Cap coloration and surface texture

### Series 3: Morels and Cup Fungi

The morel group is characterized by a honeycomb-pitted or wrinkled cap
resembling a head or trumpet. The common morel (*Morchella esculenta*) and its
relatives are generally considered safe edibles, though Palmer cautioned that
proper identification was still essential.

## Color Change as an Indicator

One of the most reliable field indicators in Palmer's system is color change
upon bruising or cutting. Many Boleti turn blue when their flesh is exposed
to air — a reaction caused by the oxidation of variegatic acid and other
compounds. While some blue-staining species are edible, Palmer generally
advised rejecting any bolete that turned vividly blue, as this reaction was
associated with a higher likelihood of toxicity.

Similarly, some agarics turn red, brown, or black when bruised. While
color change alone is not definitive, it serves as one data point in the
broader identification process.

## Palmer's Key vs. Modern Systems

Palmer's key was original to his work and represented one of the first
attempts at a practical field guide for American fungi. Modern mycological
keys are far more precise, incorporating microscopic features (spore size and
shape, cystidia, pileipellis structure) and DNA-based taxonomy. However,
Palmer's system was remarkable for its accessibility to non-specialists and
its emphasis on observable macroscopic features.

The key notably omitted several important groups, including:
- The Truffle (*Tuber* species), which grows entirely underground
- Coral fungi (*Clavaria* species)
- Jelly fungi (*Tremella* species)
- The smaller and more obscure genera

## The Bibliography as Foundation

Palmer provided a bibliography of mycological works that served as the
foundation for American study. The list included works by the four leading
British mycologists — Berkeley, Cooke, Smith, and Badham — as well as
continental authors such as Tulasne, Fries, and Paulet. American
contributions were sparse, consisting mainly of the reports of Charles H.
Peck, the New York State Botanist, whose catalogues and annual reports
contained valuable classification work.

Palmer specifically criticized United States Government pamphlets for relying
on the "earlier and incomplete labors of Worthington G. Smith" and for failing
to distinguish between the genuinely deadly Amanita and the merely acrid
Lactarius piperatus — a distinction of critical importance for public safety.
