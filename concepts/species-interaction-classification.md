## Species Interaction Classification

Species interaction classification is a systematic framework for categorizing the relationships between pairs of species based on how each is affected by the other. Originally developed by Haskell (1970), this taxonomy provides a critical analytical tool for [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] with plant roots, and pollinator/flower relationships. This is the gold standard in guild design — whenever mutualism can be arranged, it should be prioritized.

**Commensalism (+0):** The actor benefits while the other is unaffected. An epiphyte like vanilla growing on a coconut trunk is a classic example. The epiphyte gains structural support and access to light while the host tree is neither helped nor harmed.

**Allotrophy (0+):** The actor is unaffected while the other benefits. Teacher-student relationships and charitable giving fit this category. In nature, a large tree providing shade to understory seedlings without cost to itself represents allotrophy.

### Negative Interactions

**Synnecrosis (--):** Both species lose — a "lose-lose" situation. War between human societies is the most dramatic example, but battles between plants for light, nutrients, and space, and chemical warfare between competing species, also qualify. This must be avoided in designed systems.

**Amensalism (-0):** The actor is harmed, not the other. A butterfly attacking a rhinoceros illustrates the futility, as does a wasp parasite that becomes glued to the tree it attacks, as occurs with some pine trees and Sirex wasps.

**Allolimy (0-):** The actor is unaffected but the other is harmed. Walnut trees growing beside apple trees yield well themselves, but the juglones secreted by walnut roots kill or weaken the apple tree. Similarly, grasses weaken most deciduous fruit trees growing nearby.

**Parasitism (+-):** The actor (parasite) benefits while the other loses. All pathogens and parasites tend to weaken or take from their hosts. In [[acacia]] near walnut produces (0+): walnut benefits from Acacia proximity

## Building Guilds from Co-Action Analysis

The critical insight of co-action analysis is that **negative two-species interactions can be resolved by introducing a third species as an intervenor, buffer, or arbitrator**. This is the foundation of guild construction:

**Example — Resolving Apple-Walnut Conflict:**
- Apple + Walnut = (0-): undesirable, apple sickens
- Apple + Mulberry = (+0): good
- Mulberry + Walnut = (00): neutral
- **Result:** Apple–Mulberry–Walnut arrangement yields (+00), effectively cancelling the negative interaction

**Extended Guild:**
- Adding Acacia: Acacia + Walnut = (0+), Acacia + Mulberry = (0+)
- **Result:** Apple–Mulberry–Acacia–Walnut = (++0+), a strongly positive four-species array

This systematic approach goes far beyond accepting primary conflicts as inevitable. By isolating and arranging guild species to maximize (++), (0+), and (+0) effects, the designer creates productive polycultures from what would otherwise be incompatible pairings.

## The Role of Configurations in Guilds

Guild design must account for **distance-dependent interactions** between species. While some interactions (like shade or root competition) operate at close range, others function over considerable distances:

- Territorial birds may display or call a mile or more apart while remaining in clear interaction
- Plants interact via pollen and spores over significant distances
- Chemical "messengers" (allelopathic compounds, pheromones) can affect organisms far from the source
- Mammals communicate territory through scent marking at great range

**Configuration strategy:** Assess the distance across the interaction boundary where effects occur, then arrange guild species to obtain maximum positive edge interactions. For grain-legume intercrops, the beneficial effect extends 1.5–2 meters into the crop, allowing spiral or strip-planting designs for total positive edge interaction.

These careful guild analyses and configurations form the basis of species planning in permaculture, transforming competitive natural systems into cooperative designed ones.
