## McKenna"s Dominator vs Partnership Culture Model

[[terence-mckenna]]'s *Food of the Gods* proposes a grand historical thesis: that human culture can be divided into two fundamentally different social organizational patterns — the **partnership model** and the **dominator model** — and that the transition from one to the other was catalyzed by changes in humanity's relationship with psychoactive plants. This framework, influenced by Riane Eisler's *The Chalice and the Blade*, provides the organizing principle for McKenna's entire narrative of human cultural evolution.

## The Two Models Defined

### Partnership Culture

Partnership culture, in McKenna's formulation, is characterized by:

- **Gender egalitarianism**: No systematic subordination of women; social power distributed between sexes
- **Egalitarian social structure**: Hierarchies are minimal and based on competence rather than force
- **Connection to nature**: The natural world is viewed as a partner and teacher, not a resource to be exploited
- **Use of psychoactive plants**: Entheogenic plants and fungi are central to religious and social life, providing access to transcendent experience
- **Emphasis on community**: Social cohesion maintained through shared ritual and collective experience rather than coercive authority
- **Artistic and expressive culture**: Visual art, music, and dance are integrated into daily life and spiritual practice

McKenna associates partnership culture with the prehistoric societies of the Tassili Plateau, Çatalhöyük, and other Neolithic sites where archaeological evidence suggests relatively egalitarian social organization and the presence of psychedelic mushroom iconography.

### Dominator Culture

Dominator culture is characterized by:

- **Patriarchy**: Systematic male dominance over women in social, political, and religious life
- **Hierarchical social structure**: Power concentrated at the top of steep social pyramids
- **Nature as resource**: The natural world is viewed as raw material for human exploitation
- **Suppression of psychoactive plants**: Entheogens are replaced by alcohol, which McKenna sees as a "dominator drug" that reinforces social hierarchy rather than dissolving it
- **Emphasis on control**: Social cohesion maintained through coercion, law, and military force
- **Obsession with death**: Warfare, human sacrifice, and monuments to male power become central cultural expressions

McKenna associates dominator culture with the rise of kingship, organized warfare, and the patriarchal religions that emerged in the Bronze Age and have dominated Western civilization ever since.

## The Historical Transition

McKenna argues that the transition from partnership to dominator culture was not gradual but relatively abrupt, occurring roughly 4,500 to 5,000 years ago in the context of:

- **Population growth and resource competition**: As societies grew larger and more complex, competition for resources intensified
- **Loss of the [[psilocybin-mushroom]]**: As pastoral nomadism replaced hunter-gatherer lifestyles in some regions, access to psychedelic mushrooms diminished
- **Rise of the "king":** The emergence of hereditary rulership concentrated power in the hands of individuals who used religion to legitimize their authority
- **Replacement of entheogens by alcohol**: Alcohol, which is easier to produce and control than psychedelic mushrooms, became the dominant psychoactive substance

The crucial turning point, in McKenna"s narrative, was the **fall of Çatalhöyük** — the Anatolian settlement that represents for him the apex of partnership culture — and the rise of the "Age of Kingship" that followed. Çatalhöyük"s elaborate art, absence of defensive fortifications, and apparent gender equality stand in sharp contrast to the fortified, hierarchical societies that succeeded it.

## Alcohol as the Dominator Drug

One of McKenna's most provocative arguments is that alcohol is the quintessential dominator drug, while psychedelic plants are the quintessential partnership drugs. He bases this distinction on the different social effects of the two substance classes:

**Alcohol**:
- Reinforces existing social hierarchies (drunk people become louder versions of themselves)
- Promotes aggression and violence, particularly in men
- Creates dependency and addiction that can be socially controlled
- Is easily produced in quantity, making it suitable for taxation and regulation
- Suppresses introspection and promotes conformity

**Psychedelic plants** (particularly psilocybin mushrooms):
- Dissolve social hierarchies (the psychedelic experience is inherently egalitarian)
- Promote empathy, connection, and peaceful introspection
- Do not create physiological addiction
- Are difficult to control (they grow wild and cannot be monopolized)
- Promote deep self-examination and questioning of social norms

McKenna sees the replacement of psychedelics by alcohol as one of the most consequential events in human history — a chemical revolution that fundamentally altered the trajectory of civilization.

## The Tassili Plateau and the Round Head Civilization

McKenna devotes significant attention to the Tassili Plateau in the Algerian Sahara, where rock paintings dating to 7,000-9,000 years ago depict humanoid figures with mushroom-shaped heads. He interprets these "Round Head" figures as evidence of a psychedelic-influenced culture that practiced a form of partnership social organization.

The Tassili paintings, which also depict elaborate communal rituals, dancing, and apparently peaceful social interaction, represent for McKenna a window into the "high plains of Eden" — a period when humanity lived in relative harmony with nature and with each other, mediated by the regular use of psychoactive mushrooms.

## Gender and the Dominator Model

McKenna"s analysis of gender is one of the more compelling aspects of his framework. He argues that the suppression of entheogenic religion was closely linked to the suppression of feminine power:

- Early goddess-worshipping cultures (like those at Çatalhöyük) used psychoactive plants in rituals that celebrated fertility, nature, and the feminine principle
- The rise of sky-god religions (which McKenna associates with pastoral nomadism and dominator culture) replaced earth-centered spirituality with hierarchical, male-dominated theologies
- The witch hunts of medieval and early modern Europe can be understood as the final suppression of the remaining partnership-culture practices, including the use of psychoactive plants by midwives and herbalists

This analysis parallels but extends Riane Eisler's work, adding the dimension of psychoactive plant use to the partnership/dominator framework. McKenna argues that the "pathological monotheism" of dominator religions — their insistence on a single male deity, their hostility to nature worship, their persecution of heresy — can be understood as the ideological expression of a culture that has severed its connection to the psychedelic experience and the partnership values it fostered.

## Iboga and the Fang: A Window into Partnership Culture

McKenna examines the use of **iboga** (*Tabernanthe iboga*) among the Fang people of Central Africa as a surviving example of partnership culture. Iboga, a powerful psychedelic root bark, is used in the **Bwiti** religion in all-night ceremonies that involve communal dancing, singing, and visionary experience.

What distinguishes Bwiti from dominator religious practices, in McKenna"s analysis, is its egalitarian structure and its integration of the feminine principle. Both men and women participate in ceremonies; the visionary experience is considered accessible to all; and the plant itself is treated as a teacher and ally rather than a commodity to be controlled by a priestly caste.

McKenna sees the survival of Bwiti as evidence that partnership culture has not been entirely extinguished, and that the entheogenic connection to the "transcendent Other" can persist even under the pressure of dominator cultural forces. The contrast between Bwiti"s egalitarian, nature-revering spirituality and the hierarchical, nature-exploiting religions of dominator culture encapsulates the choice that McKenna believes humanity faces.

## The Archaic Revival as Return to Partnership

McKenna's concept of the "archaic revival" — the title of one of his other major works — is essentially a call for a return to partnership values mediated by a renewed relationship with psychoactive plants. He argues that the psychedelic renaissance of the 1960s represented a brief opening in dominator culture's suppression of entheogenic experience, and that the continued persecution of psychedelic substances represents dominator culture's defense against the partnership values they promote.

The archaic revival is not a call to literally return to prehistoric social organization but to recover the partnership values — egalitarianism, nature connection, gender balance, communal ritual — that McKenna believes were central to early human culture and that are desperately needed in the face of ecological crisis.

## Criticisms and Limitations

Several serious criticisms have been leveled at McKenna's partnership/dominator model:

- **Oversimplification**: Binary frameworks rarely capture the complexity of real human societies; most cultures contain elements of both partnership and dominator organization
- **Romanticization of prehistory**: The archaeological evidence for peaceful, egalitarian prehistoric societies is ambiguous; prehistoric violence is well-documented at many sites
- **Deterministic drug theory**: Reducing vast cultural transformations to changes in psychoactive substance use is reductive and difficult to support with evidence
- **Selective evidence**: McKenna emphasizes archaeological findings that support his thesis while downplaying contradictory evidence
- **Lack of cross-cultural analysis**: The model is heavily focused on Western Eurasian history and may not apply to other cultural trajectories

## Legacy and Influence

Despite these criticisms, McKenna's framework has been influential in several domains:

- **Psychedelic culture**: The partnership/dominator model provides an intellectual foundation for the modern psychedelic renaissance's critique of mainstream culture
- **Ecofeminism**: The connection between patriarchy, environmental destruction, and the suppression of plant wisdom resonates with ecofeminist thought
- **Cultural history**: Even scholars who reject McKenna's specific claims acknowledge that he raises important questions about the role of psychoactive substances in cultural evolution
- **Alternative spirituality**: The model has been adopted by various neo-pagan and entheogenic spiritual movements
