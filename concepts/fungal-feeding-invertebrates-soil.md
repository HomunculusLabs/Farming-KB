# Fungal-Feeding Invertebrates and Mycelial Grazing

Updated: 2026-04-18

Mycelial networks are highly nutritious and serve as food for many soil invertebrates. The interactions between fungi and their invertebrate grazers profoundly shape fungal community structure, nutrient cycling, and ecosystem processes in terrestrial environments.

## Major Fungal-Feeding Invertebrate Groups

Several groups of soil invertebrates use fungi as a food source, either grazing directly on mycelia or indirectly by ingesting mycelium within decomposing litter (Maraun et al., 2003):

- **Collembola (springtails)**: The most studied fungal grazers; multiple species show different grazing preferences and intensities
- **Termites**: Major consumers of fungal material in tropical and subtropical ecosystems
- **Nematodes**: Both fungal-feeding and fungivorous nematodes are abundant in soil
- **Diptera larvae**: Consume fungi during their soil-dwelling developmental stages
- **Mites (Acari)**: Oribatid mites in particular are significant fungal consumers in forest soils and contribute to the dispersal of fungal spores through their feces and body surfaces

The collective grazing pressure of these invertebrate groups represents one of the primary regulatory mechanisms controlling fungal biomass and community composition in soil ecosystems.

## Grazing Intensity and Effects on Mycelial Morphology

High-intensity invertebrate grazing can completely destroy mycelia in the laboratory, but less intense grazing results in dramatic changes in fungal growth and activity (van der Drift and Jansen, 1977; Hedlund et al., 1991; Bengtsson et al., 1993; Hedlund and Augustson, 1995; Harold et al., 2005).

### Collembola Grazing Effects on Hypholoma fasciculare

When H. fasciculare growing in trays of non-sterile soil was subject to varying grazing intensities by three collembolan species (Folsomia candida, Hypogastrura cf. tullbergi, and Proisotoma minuta), extra-resource mycelium changed from slow, dense exploitative growth to less dense explorative growth during grazing. Effects varied depending on:

- Grazing intensity (density)
- Collembolan species (F. candida having the largest effects)
- Resource status of wood-block inocula (size, state of decay, nutrient status)

### Species-Specific Grazing Patterns

Different cord-forming fungi are preferentially grazed in different regions (Tordoff et al., 2006):

| Fungal Species | Preferred Grazing Region |
|---|---|
| Hypholoma fasciculare | Hyphal tips at growing margins |
| Resinicium bicolor | Indiscriminate on cords and fine mycelium, generally close to wood inoculum |
| Phanerochaete velutina | Fine mycelium within colony and hyphal tips at growing margin |

Intense grazing (80 Collembola per 24 x 24 cm tray) by F. candida resulted in complete removal of hyphal tips around large parts of the circumference of Phanerochaete velutina mycelia, yielding much smaller systems than ungrazed controls.

## Physiological Responses to Grazing

Grazing triggers physiological changes distant from the grazing site:

- Ligninolytic enzymes were switched on by Phanerochaete velutina in the presence of nematodes (Dyer et al., 1992)
- The soil-inhabiting zygomycete Mortierella isabellina showed differences in protease expression during grazing (Hedlund et al., 1991)
- Changes in enzyme expression occurred distant to the grazing site, suggesting systemic signalling within the mycelial network

A major unanswered question concerns the extent to which soil mesofauna affect re-allocation of nutrients within mycelial networks. Mineral nutrients are certainly released during grazing of litter (Anderson et al., 1983), but effects on translocation within mycelia need further investigation.

## Reversed Predation: Fungi That Kill Invertebrates

Some basidiomycete species have evolved the ability to kill invertebrates by immobilizing them with toxins, with hyphae subsequently penetrating and feeding on the cadavers:

- **Pleurotus species** (wood-rotting oyster mushrooms) feed on nematodes (Barron and Thorn, 1987)
- **Laccaria bicolor** (ectomycorrhizal) preys on Collembola (Folsomia candida) (Klironomos and Hart, 2001)
- Some fungi produce specialized structures that trap nematodes (Tzean and Liou, 1993; Luo et al., 2004)

These fungivorous relationships have obvious analogies with insectivorous plants (sundew, Venus flytrap) and insect parasitoids.

## Nutritional Effects on Mycelia

Invertebrates can exert direct nutritional effects on basidiomycete mycelia through nitrogen inputs in their feces. Grazing also stimulates morphological changes -- grazed mycelia often show points of more rapid outgrowth as cords with fanned margins, analogous to the way plants produce multiple shoots when their apical buds are removed by herbivores.

## Mycelial Resilience Through Network Architecture

The ability of mycelial networks to withstand grazing relates to their connectivity. Highly connected networks with many loops are more resilient to invertebrate grazing, because even though some connections are severed, others remain (Fricker et al., 2006). Resilience also depends on the ease and efficiency with which the network can reconnect by regrowth following damage. The thickened arterial hyphae of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are much less palatable to collembolans than saprotrophic fungi (Gange, 2000), contributing to their recalcitrance and role in soil carbon sequestration.

## Collembola Selectivity and Fungal Community Shaping

Collembola are not indiscriminate grazers; they demonstrate marked selectivity among fungal species, preferring certain taxa over others based on palatability, nutritional quality, and the presence of defensive compounds. This selective grazing pressure acts as a significant driver of [[fungal-fungal-community-assembly]] in soil. Species of fast-growing, opportunistic fungi (often Zygomycota such as Mucor and Rhizopus) are generally preferred over slower-growing basidiomycetes and ascomycetes with tougher hyphal walls and higher melanin content.

However, grazing preferences can shift depending on the collembolan species and the ecological context. Some collembola selectively avoid mycorrhizal fungi, possibly due to the recalcitrant nature of arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphal walls, while others graze ectomycorrhizal mantles when alternative food sources are scarce. The implications for plant-fungal mutualisms are significant: selective collembola grazing can suppress beneficial mycorrhizal associations under certain conditions, potentially reducing plant nutrient uptake. Conversely, collembola that preferentially graze saprotrophic competitors may indirectly benefit mycorrhizal fungi by reducing competition for root colonization sites.

## Termite-Fungus Mutualisms

In tropical and subtropical ecosystems, termites maintain one of the most sophisticated fungus-growing mutualisms on Earth. Macrotermitinae termites cultivate Termitomyces fungi within their nest structures on substrates of plant litter and woody material that the termites collect. The fungus comb — a complex structure of mycelium and partially digested plant material — serves as both a food source and a decomposition engine. The Termitomyces fungus breaks down lignocellulose that the termites cannot digest alone, converting recalcitrant plant material into nutrient-rich fungal tissue that the termites consume.

This mutualism has profound effects on carbon and nutrient cycling in tropical soils. Termite mounds serve as hotspots of decomposition and nutrient mineralization, creating localized patches of enhanced fertility. The fungal comb processes enormous quantities of plant litter, and termite foraging activity distributes nutrients across the landscape. The evolutionary sophistication of the termite-fungus mutualism — including vertical transmission of fungal symbionts through termite queen lineage and coordinated caste-based management of the fungal garden — rivals that of leafcutter ant agriculture and represents one of the most remarkable examples of fungal-animal coevolution. Recent molecular phylogenetic studies have shown that the mutualism between Macrotermitinae and Termitomyces is approximately 30 million years old, making it one of the oldest known examples of insect agriculture.

## Nematode-Fungal Interactions and Trophic Cascades

Fungal-feeding nematodes (fungivores) represent a critical link between the fungal decomposer community and higher trophic levels in soil food webs. Aphelenchoides, Aphelenchus, and Ditylenchus species are among the most abundant fungal-feeding nematodes in agricultural and forest soils. Their grazing on fungal hyphae regulates fungal biomass and activity, releasing nutrients immobilized in fungal tissue back into the soil solution for plant uptake.

The interaction between fungal-feeding nematodes and their fungal prey can trigger trophic cascades with consequences for plant community composition and primary productivity. When nematode grazing suppresses fungal decomposition rates, litter accumulates and nutrient mineralization slows, potentially limiting plant growth. Conversely, moderate nematode grazing can stimulate fungal activity and nutrient turnover by preventing fungal biomass from reaching senescence, analogous to the way grazing mammals stimulate grassland productivity. The [[fungal-interactions-soil-fauna]] create complex feedback loops that determine whether fungal decomposition contributes primarily to carbon sequestration or nutrient mineralization.

## Implications for Soil Carbon Storage

The balance between fungal decomposition and invertebrate grazing has direct implications for soil carbon sequestration. When mycelial networks grow and are subsequently protected from grazers by soil aggregation, physical barriers within soil pores, or chemical defenses (melanin, calcium oxalate crystals), the carbon contained in fungal cell walls (rich in chitin and glucans) can persist in soil for years to decades. The [[mycorrhizal-carbon-cycling-soil-sequestration]] is partly attributable to the recalcitrance of their hyphal residues to decomposition and their relative protection from invertebrate grazers compared to saprotrophic fungi.

Conversely, intensive invertebrate grazing accelerates carbon turnover by breaking down fungal biomass into more labile forms that are rapidly mineralized by bacteria. Understanding these grazing dynamics is essential for predicting how soil management practices — including tillage, organic amendments, and cover cropping — affect long-term carbon storage in agricultural and forest soils.

## Defense Mechanisms of Fungi Against Invertebrate Grazing

Fungi have evolved diverse strategies to deter or resist invertebrate grazing, forming an evolutionary arms race that shapes fungal community structure. Chemical defenses include production of toxic secondary metabolites such as styrylpyrones (produced by Hypholoma species), triterpenes, and volatile organic compounds that repel or poison grazers. Physical defenses include melanization of hyphal walls, which reduces digestibility and nutritional value, production of calcium oxalate crystals on hyphal surfaces, and formation of hardened sclerotia or resistant spores that pass through invertebrate guts unharmed.

The [[biodiversity-fungicolous-mycoparasitic-fungi]] represent yet another dimension of fungal defense, where one fungus parasitizes another, sometimes using invertebrate vectors for dispersal. Some entomopathogenic fungi such as Beauveria and Metarhizium species bridge the gap between fungal-feeding and fungi-that-kill, infecting and killing insect herbivores while also contributing to nutrient cycling through decomposition of insect cadavers. The ecological complexity of these multi-trophic interactions underscores the difficulty of predicting the effects of soil management changes on fungal community composition and ecosystem function.

The study of fungal-feeding invertebrates has important implications for soil management in agriculture and horticulture. Conservation tillage and organic matter inputs that promote fungal-dominated soil food webs tend to increase the abundance and diversity of fungal grazers, creating positive feedback loops that further enhance soil health and nutrient cycling. The [[hamilton-soil-biology-and-microorganisms]] interact with both fungi and their invertebrate grazers, adding further complexity to these belowground food webs.

These [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] mediated by fungal grazers are particularly important in no-till and reduced-tillage agricultural systems, where fungal hyphal networks remain intact and can support larger invertebrate populations than in frequently disturbed soils. The shift from bacterial-dominated to fungal-dominated soil food webs under reduced tillage is one of the most consistent findings in [[soil-food-web-management-for-farmers]] research.
