## Termite-Fungus Mutualisms

In tropical and subtropical ecosystems, termites maintain one of the most sophisticated fungus-growing mutualisms on Earth. Macrotermitinae termites cultivate Termitomyces fungi within their nest structures on substrates of plant litter and woody material that the termites collect. The fungus comb — a complex structure of mycelium and partially digested plant material — serves as both a food source and a decomposition engine. The Termitomyces fungus breaks down lignocellulose that the termites cannot digest alone, converting recalcitrant plant material into nutrient-rich fungal tissue that the termites consume.

This mutualism has profound effects on carbon and nutrient cycling in tropical soils. Termite mounds serve as hotspots of decomposition and nutrient mineralization, creating localized patches of enhanced fertility. The fungal comb processes enormous quantities of plant litter, and termite foraging activity distributes nutrients across the landscape. The evolutionary sophistication of the termite-fungus mutualism — including vertical transmission of fungal symbionts through termite queen lineage and coordinated caste-based management of the fungal garden — rivals that of leafcutter ant agriculture and represents one of the most remarkable examples of fungal-animal coevolution. Recent molecular phylogenetic studies have shown that the mutualism between Macrotermitinae and Termitomyces is approximately 30 million years old, making it one of the oldest known examples of insect agriculture.

## Nematode-Fungal Interactions and Trophic Cascades

Fungal-feeding nematodes (fungivores) represent a critical link between the fungal decomposer community and higher trophic levels in soil food webs. Aphelenchoides, Aphelenchus, and Ditylenchus species are among the most abundant fungal-feeding nematodes in agricultural and forest soils. Their grazing on fungal hyphae regulates fungal biomass and activity, releasing nutrients immobilized in fungal tissue back into the soil solution for plant uptake.

The interaction between fungal-feeding nematodes and their fungal prey can trigger trophic cascades with consequences for plant community composition and primary productivity. When nematode grazing suppresses fungal decomposition rates, litter accumulates and nutrient mineralization slows, potentially limiting plant growth. Conversely, moderate nematode grazing can stimulate fungal activity and nutrient turnover by preventing fungal biomass from reaching senescence, analogous to the way grazing mammals stimulate grassland productivity. The [[mycorrhizal-carbon-cycling-soil-sequestration]] is partly attributable to the recalcitrance of their hyphal residues to decomposition and their relative protection from invertebrate grazers compared to saprotrophic fungi.

Conversely, intensive invertebrate grazing accelerates carbon turnover by breaking down fungal biomass into more labile forms that are rapidly mineralized by bacteria. Understanding these grazing dynamics is essential for predicting how soil management practices — including tillage, organic amendments, and cover cropping — affect long-term carbon storage in agricultural and forest soils.

## Defense Mechanisms of Fungi Against Invertebrate Grazing

Fungi have evolved diverse strategies to deter or resist invertebrate grazing, forming an evolutionary arms race that shapes fungal community structure. Chemical defenses include production of toxic secondary metabolites such as styrylpyrones (produced by Hypholoma species), triterpenes, and volatile organic compounds that repel or poison grazers. Physical defenses include melanization of hyphal walls, which reduces digestibility and nutritional value, production of calcium oxalate crystals on hyphal surfaces, and formation of hardened sclerotia or resistant spores that pass through invertebrate guts unharmed.
