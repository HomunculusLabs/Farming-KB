## Soil Health and Conservation

Soil is the foundation of all terrestrial life and the most critical resource in permaculture design. Despite centuries of study, the world is losing topsoil at an ever-increasing rate. Australia, for example, has perhaps only 30% of its original soils in fair condition. The rest have been washed or blown away, or sadly depleted in structure and yield — a pattern true of most countries where extractive agriculture and forestry occurs.

## The Nature of Soil

Soils defy precise treatment. Their structure, permeability, organic content, gaseous components, mineral content, pH, and water status change from hour to hour with soil depth, treatment, and micro-elevations. Many soils are complex mixtures derived from a variety of rock types with long and varied histories. Nobody can be dogmatic about any natural system — soils are always a matrix or mosaic in constant variation in place and time.

### Soil Formation Factors

Soils can be **residual** (resting in place over their parent rock) or **transported** by water, ice, gravity, and wind. Key formation factors are:

- **Rock type** — the parent material determines mineral availability
- **Climate** — temperature and rainfall drive weathering rates
- **Topography/landform** — slope affects erosion, drainage, and deposition

Water plays a central role in rock breakdown, combining with common minerals such as feldspars to swell and fracture rock, then hydrating feldspars to kaolin, clay, and potassium carbonate. Atmospheric oxygen dissolves in rain to oxidise iron minerals, forming haematite and sulphuric acid, which further dissolves metals. Even without invoking ice expansion or glacial grinding, water is an effective rock decomposer.

### Soil Formation vs. Soil Loss

Topsoil builds at approximately **2–4 tonnes per hectare per year** as uncompacted soil. However, we remove it at rates of **40–500 tonnes per year** in cropping and soil tillage. In the worst cases, a single erosion season — one sequence of flood or strong wind over cultivated soils — can remove 2,000 years of accumulated soil.

## Where Soils Are Conserved or Increased

Only four environments consistently conserve or build soil:

1. **Uncut forests** — permanent root systems, continuous litter fall, and minimal disturbance
2. **Lakes and ponds** — quiet water allows sediment accumulation and organic matter deposition
3. **Prairies and meadows of permanent plants** — deep root systems and continuous organic input
4. **Mulched or no-tillage systems** — soil protected from erosion while organic matter accumulates

These are the core subjects of sustainable societies. Notably, they are not the subjects most taught in agricultural colleges or forestry courses, nor do they occupy the minds of politicians, investment bankers, or media personalities.

## Values in Soil Management

Soil management can be assessed from several different value frameworks:

### Health

We must be careful and conservative in approach, especially regarding biocides and high levels of artificial fertilisers. Our ancestors lived to a ripe old age on home-grown produce without herbicides, pesticides, or artificial fertilisers. Beyond diet, gardening provides mild regular exercise, meditation, meaning and purpose ("right livelihood"), and lifelong learning — every day and season brings change and something new to observe.

### Yield

The once-off yield of a ploughed, fertilised monoculture supported by chemicals and large energy inputs can out-yield almost every other production system. But the critical questions are: at what public cost? For how long maintained? With what improvement in nutrition? With what guarantee of sustainability? There is abundant proof that forced yields are temporary, and that plough cultures destroy soils and societies.

### Life in Soil

Soil organisms are a major soil factor with myriad perceptible and profound effects on pH, mineral content and availability, soil structure, and erosion. The living component of soil — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, earthworms, insects, and plant roots — is what distinguishes living soil from inert dirt.

### Ethics

It is not the purpose of people on earth to reduce all soils to perfectly balanced market gardens. The largest job is restoring soils and forests for a healthy earth itself — not clearing or ruining any more land, but first putting in order what has been destroyed.

## Soil Pollution

Industrial and agricultural chemicals have contaminated soils worldwide. Major sources include:

- **Orchards** — arsenicals, copper salts, and persistent organochlorines such as Aldrin and Dieldrin
- **Sugarcane, cotton, and tobacco** — heavy biocide loads including Aldrin, DDT, and dioxins
- **Lead** — deposited from vehicle exhausts along roadsides and in urban areas
- **Groundwater contamination** — persistent biocides leaching into aquifers

Persistence is the critical problem. Apple orchards in Western Australia were still producing unsafe levels of Aldrin and Dieldrin 18–20 years after application ceased. These chemicals do not simply go away — they bioaccumulate and move through food chains, presenting long-term health risks far beyond their intended agricultural purpose.

## Strategies for Polluted Soils

Where contamination is identified, permaculture offers practical responses:

- **Raised beds with clean compost** — isolating food crops from contaminated subsoil by building up with imported, tested organic matter
- **Forestry conversion** — planting trees on contaminated land, where deep root systems can sequester or gradually break down pollutants without entering human food chains
- **Phytoremediation** — using specific plants to extract or neutralise contaminants
- **Site avoidance** — redirecting sensitive food production to uncontaminated zones

## Soil Rehabilitation Timelines

For soil rehabilitation, we start with what is there. Most gardeners and farmers who have developed sustainable soil systems allow:

- **3–4 years** for building a productive garden
- **5–15 years** to restore a devastated soil landscape

This applies only to physical restoration and the development of appropriate plant systems. Far more lengthy processes are required where past chemical pollution has occurred. Key practices include composting, mulching, cover cropping, minimal tillage, and inoculating with beneficial microorganisms.

## Water Integration and Soil Health

Soil health cannot be separated from water management. The designer's water checklist connects directly to soil conservation:

- **Identify all water sources** and analyse quality and quantity
- **Reserve sites for tanks, swales, and dams** early in the design process
- **Use slope benefits** for gravity-fed irrigation, reducing energy inputs
- **Select plant species** adapted to unirrigated conditions where possible

### Earthworks for Soil and Water

- **Soil samples** should test for 40%+ clay content before earth-dam construction
- **Keyline systems** manage water movement across slopes to maximise infiltration
- In **arid areas**, infiltrate run-off via soil conditioning, rip-lines, swales, pits, and sandfield soakages
- **Forested ridges, windbreaks, and in-crop trees** significantly reduce water loss through evapotranspiration
