# Banana Circles

updated: 2026-04-18

From: [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]]

## Overview

The banana circle is a permaculture design for intensive food production in small spaces, particularly suited to tropical and subtropical gardens. It forms a central element of the mandala garden design and serves as a greywater and mulch absorption system. The design originates from the work of the East-West Institute in Hawaii and the Samaka gardens of the Philippines, with the layout refined as a purely permaculture concept by Bill Mollison, who named his version "Gangamma's Mandala" after a Karnataka (India) design graduate.

## Construction Steps

1. At the centre of a 100 square metre (1075 square foot) or larger area, describe a circle 2 m (6 feet) across and excavate the topsoil (or subsoil) to a dish shape, ridged on the perimeter, about 0.6-1 m (2-3 feet) deep from hollow to rim.

2. The whole circle is then covered with wet paper or wet cardboard, banana leaves, or any mulch material. The hollow is filled (or over-filled as a dome) with rough mulch of short logs, coarse twigs, hay, rice husks, and sawdust -- any humus-creating materials. A little scatter of manure, ash, lime, dolomite, or fertiliser can be added. If stones are available, bank them to the outside of the rim.

3. The rim is then planted to 4-5 papaya (a tall variety), 4 or so bananas (dwarf types), and 8-10 sweet potato. If available, yams or taro can be placed inside the rim. Later, beans can be planted to climb the papaya and banana stalks.

## Integration with Garden Design

Banana circles receive greywater from hand basins and kitchen sinks. In the total garden design, they are positioned as element "B" and can also function as papaya trees or mixed papaya/banana/pigeon pea/tomato/yam circles. They are watered from hand basin and kitchen sink drainage and mulched from the surrounding hedge.

### The Total Garden Layout
The garden design integrates banana circles with:
- **A** - wet food patch receiving clean water, growing kangkong, watercress, Chinese water chestnut, with dry staples (yam, sweet potato, cassava) on the bank
- **B** - banana circles (also papaya circles or mixed circles), watered from hand basin and kitchen sink
- **C** - greywater patch with vigorous, damp-tolerant, manure-tolerant green forage (comfrey, banana grass, lemongrass, legumes), sunken and surrounded by a bank planted to pigeon pea, papaya, and banana
- **D/D*** - avenue crops of maize, corn, or other main crop in spiral or zigzag layouts, self-mulched intercrop systems
- **E** - low, raggedly-trimmed hedge of forage-mulch species with dense weed barrier
- **G** - keyhole garden beds for preferred vegetables and greens

### Community Scale
In a larger system designed for a community kitchen at a rural centre in Karnataka state (India), a core assembly of 4-5 banana circles is used with 8-12 keyhole beds. In this case, a keyhole accesses the banana circles. Any one of these banana circles can contain a small pond.

## Species Combinations

Banana circles are versatile polyculture systems. Suitable species include:
- Bananas (dwarf types preferred for stability)
- Papaya (tall varieties for vertical layer)
- Sweet potato (ground cover on the rim)
- Yam and taro (inside the rim, partial shade)
- Climbing beans (on papaya and banana stalks)
- Pigeon pea (on surrounding banks)
- Tomato (in mixed circles)
- Cassava (on earth bank edges)

## Water and Mulch Function

The sunken bowl shape of the banana circle captures and retains water from household greywater outlets. The mulch fill absorbs moisture and nutrients, breaking down over time to feed the heavy-feeding banana and papaya plants. This creates a self-fertilising system that recycles household wastewater into productive food crops. The circular raised rim creates a warm microclimate and provides well-drained planting positions.

## Keyhole Integration

Around each keyhole, a bed 1.5-2 m (5-6 feet) wide is first edge-banked with soil 10-20 cm (4-8 inches) high to prevent water run-off, and the beds are then papered and mulched (as for the banana circle). The whole garden creates a pattern of connected circular and keyhole beds maximising edge and access.

## Climate Suitability

Banana circles are primarily suited to:
- Humid tropics and subtropics
- Areas with adequate rainfall or household greywater supply
- Frost-free climates

In cooler climates, the concept can be adapted using different plant assemblages in a similar sunken, mulch-filled circular bed, though true bananas will not thrive. The principle of sunken mulch gardens with greywater recycling applies across climates.

## Design Principles Applied

- Each element serves multiple functions: food production, greywater treatment, mulch creation
- The circle shape maximises edge effect for planting diversity
- Nutrient cycling from household waste to food
- Zone 1 placement for frequent harvest access
- Integration of water, soil, and plant systems
- Every function is served in multiple ways (greywater treated by plants AND mulch)
