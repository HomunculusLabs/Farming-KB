Ingham's field guide describes the use of compost tea as a delivery system for
specific [[query-how-do-i-use-beauveria-bassiana-for-pest-control]] fruit fly

targeted biocontrol inoculation, creating a dual-action pest management strategy
that addresses both above-ground and below-ground pest pressures.

## Beauveria bassiana for Fruit Fly Control

Beauveria bassiana is an entomopathogenic fungus that attacks and kills insect
larvae, including fruit fly larvae. Ingham recommends incorporating Beauveria
into [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] that supports
Beauveria spore germination and persistence in the soil. The beneficial bacteria
and fungi in the tea create favorable conditions for Beauveria establishment,
while the aeration during brewing ensures the Beauveria spores remain viable and
do not succumb to anaerobic conditions.

For sourcing Beauveria inoculant, Ingham provides international contacts: in
Europe, the SFI Europe lab in Holland (K.L. Eigenraam); in the US, Holmes
Enviro; in Australia and New Zealand, Bio-Start. This global network of suppliers
reflects the widespread adoption of Beauveria-based biocontrol in orchard and
vineyard systems.

## Beneficial Bacteria Species Selection

A significant portion of Ingham's correspondence addresses the selection and use
of specific beneficial bacteria species as inoculants in compost tea. She draws
an important distinction between laboratory plate-culture behavior and actual
soil performance: there is a huge difference between what happens on plate media
in the lab and what happens in the complex soil environment.

Some beneficial bacteria species ENCOURAGE the growth of beneficial fungi which
occupy the space that disease fungi would otherwise use. This is a very different
mechanism from direct antibiosis and means that the bacteria work through
ecological displacement rather than [[ingham-aact-disease-suppression-through-food-competition]] competitive exclusion. The foliar spray
approach is particularly important for fruit trees where the economic damage from
fruit fly is significant and chemical insecticides have undesirable residues.

## Timing and Application Rates

The seasonal timing of Beauveria applications is critical. The autumn soil
drench targets late-season fruit fly larvae that are preparing to overwinter in
the soil. The spring application targets early-season larvae before they pupate
and emerge as adults. These two applications bracket the period of maximum larval
vulnerability and provide the longest window of soil-level protection.

The 150 liters per hectare rate provides adequate soil coverage without
excessive dilution. For smaller operations, this rate scales down proportionally.
Ingham emphasizes that consistent annual applications are more effective than
occasional high-rate applications, because the goal is to establish a persistent
Beauveria population in the soil rather than achieve a one-time population spike.

## Cost-Effectiveness and Accessibility

Ingham acknowledges that adding specific inoculants to compost tea increases
cost, and recommends that growers hedge their bets by adding inoculum if it is
inexpensive enough. The SFI assay provides the data needed for informed
decisions about which species to add. Most of the time, if a grower is dealing
with a healthy ecosystem, the beneficial species will already be in the compost
base, and no additional purchase is necessary. This makes the approach accessible
to both large commercial operations and small-scale growers who may not have
budgets for commercial biocontrol products.

The economic case for compost tea-based biocontrol strengthens over time as soil
biology improves and pest pressure decreases. Initial years may show modest
returns while the soil foodweb is being established, but by year three to five,
many growers report significant reductions in both pest damage and input costs
compared to conventional chemical-based programs.

## Relationship to the Soil Foodweb

The Beauveria and beneficial bacteria approach is fully integrated with Ingham's
soil foodweb philosophy. Rather than targeting individual pest species with
narrow-spectrum chemicals, the strategy builds a diverse and resilient microbial
community that provides multiple layers of protection. The compost tea delivers
the full soil foodweb to the plant's environment, and specific inoculants add
targeted capabilities to an already functional biological system. This is
fundamentally different from conventional pest management, which typically
destroys soil biology with broad-spectrum chemicals and then attempts to manage
pests with increasingly specific synthetic products.
