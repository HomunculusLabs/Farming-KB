## Action Potentials in Venus Flytrap

The Venus flytrap ([[plant-cell-membrane-transport-proteins]].
This potential propagates through the trap tissue at speeds of up to
20 centimeters per second. A single stimulation generates one action
potential, but the trap only closes if two action potentials occur within
approximately 20 seconds. This dual-trigger requirement prevents the
plant from wasting energy closing on raindrops or debris.

The action potential is generated by rapid ion movement across cell
membranes. When a trigger hair bends, stretch-activated ion channels
open, allowing calcium ions to flow into the cell. This calcium influx
triggers additional channel openings, causing rapid membrane
depolarization. The depolarization wave spreads through the symplast —
the continuous cytoplasmic network connecting adjacent cells through
plasmodesmata — causing the trap to snap shut through a sudden change in
cell [[mimosa-pudica]], commonly known as the sensitive plant, provides another
striking example of plant electrical signaling. When touched, shaken, or
exposed to heat, Mimosa rapidly folds its leaflets and droops its
petioles — a response called thigmonasty. This movement is driven by
action potentials propagating from the stimulation site through the
plant's vascular tissue.

The mechanism involves rapid changes in cell turgor pressure in
specialized motor cells called pulvini at the bases of leaflets and
petioles. The action potential causes potassium and chloride ions to
rush out of motor cells, followed by water exiting through osmosis. The
water loss deflates the cells, and the volume change drives visible
leaf movement. Recovery occurs over 15-30 minutes as ions and water are
pumped back. Remarkably, Mimosa can habituate to repeated harmless
stimuli, learning to ignore irrelevant signals — a phenomenon Chamovitz
discusses in the context of plant memory.

## Calcium Ion Channels: The Trigger Mechanism

Calcium ions play a central role in initiating electrical signals in
plants. Cytosolic calcium concentration is normally kept extremely low
(approximately 100 nanomolar) compared to the extracellular space
(approximately 1 millimolar), creating a steep concentration gradient.
When mechanical stimulation or herbivore damage is detected, calcium
channels open, allowing calcium to rush into the cytosol. This rapid
increase — a calcium signature — triggers downstream responses including
gene activation, hormone synthesis, and action potential generation.

The specificity of calcium signaling arises from the amplitude, duration,
frequency, and spatial localization of the calcium transient. Different
stimuli produce distinct calcium signatures, decoded by calcium-binding
proteins such as calmodulin and calcium-dependent protein kinases. This
allows plants to generate appropriate responses to diverse environmental
challenges using a single ion as the primary messenger.
