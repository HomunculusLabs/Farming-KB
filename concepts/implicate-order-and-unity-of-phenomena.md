# Implicate Order and the Unity of Phenomena

## Overview

The concept of **implicate order**, drawn from physicist David Bohm's *Wholeness and the Implicate Order* (1980), provides a [[fukuoka-spiritual-philosophical-foundation]] for understanding how all natural phenomena are interconnected. In [[permaculture]] design:

- **Duality is misleading**: Concepts like "pest vs. beneficial insect" or "weed vs. crop" are artificial distinctions. In reality, each organism plays multiple roles within the whole system.
- **Reductionism is limited**: Studying parts in isolation cannot reveal the emergent properties of the whole system. Holistic observation is essential.
- **Process over substance**: Substance itself may be nothing more than process — ongoing transformation and flow.

## Genealogy and Pattern Streams

Mollison extends the concept of implicate order to genealogy and time. Rather than seeing ancestry as a linear chain of separate individuals, [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]] reveals that we are simultaneously defined by our ancestors and our descendants. We exist within a **pattern stream** — a flowing continuum where past, present, and future are interwoven.

This perspective has practical consequences for permaculture:

- **Design for continuity**: When designing food forests, water systems, or animal husbandry plans, consider multi-generational timeframes rather than single-season yields.
- **Acknowledge interdependence**: Every element in a design is both shaped by and shaping every other element, much like organisms exist in continual exchange with their animate and inanimate surroundings.

## All Is Streams, Particles, Waves

Pattern understanding reveals that natural phenomena manifest as streams, particles, and waves simultaneously — each defining the other. This is not merely an abstract observation; it is a design principle:

- **Streams** represent flow (water, nutrients, energy, information)
- **Particles** represent discrete elements (plants, animals, structures)
- **Waves** represent cyclical patterns (seasons, tides, [[fukuoka-textdoc-microclimate-mite-scale-population-dynamics]])

Effective design works with all three modalities. A [[swale]], for example, is a structure (particle) that directs water flow (stream) and operates on seasonal cycles (wave).

## Lovelock and the Gaia Hypothesis

[[gaia-hypothesis]]** (1979) provides a scientific framework consistent with implicate order. Earth behaves as a self-regulating, self-generated construct — akin to a single organism or thought process. The conditions that make life possible are balanced about such fine tolerances that many processes appear to exist specifically to preserve this equilibrium in dynamic stability.

For [[bill-mollison-permaculture-designers-manual]], this means:

- **Earth-care is self-care**: Damaging the biosphere damages the system that sustains all life, including human life.
- **Self-regulation over control**: Design systems that regulate themselves (through biological feedback loops, succession dynamics, and closed nutrient cycles) rather than systems requiring constant external management.
- **Fine tolerances**: Small changes can have large effects. Design with sensitivity to the narrow thresholds within which biological systems function.

## Birch, Cobb, and the Field of Events

Biologists [[charles-birch]] and John Cobb, in *The Liberation of Life* (1984), argue against the existence of truly individual organisms or separate events. All exist within a "field of events" or as expressions of one life force. Organisms exist only as inseparable parts of their event environments, in continual exchange with surrounding animate and inanimate entities.

This framework, as reviewed by Warwick Fox, leads to the assertion that "we must view the cosmos as an infinite complex of interrelated events" — all things are "enduring societies of events."

## Conflict with Current Systems

These unified worldviews stand in direct conflict with the ethics governing contemporary political, educational, and economic systems, which are built on:

- **Individualism**: The primacy of the isolated self or nation
- **Reductionism**: Breaking problems into disconnected parts
- **Competition**: Zero-sum games rather than cooperative systems
- **Short-term thinking**: Discounting long-term consequences

Permaculture's three core ethics — earth care, people care, and fair share — emerge from this understanding of unity. They are not arbitrary moral rules but practical necessities derived from the recognition that all phenomena are interrelated.

## Applications in Permaculture Design

### Pattern Perception

The first skill in applying implicate order is **perception** — learning to see the patterns that already exist in landscapes and social systems. This requires:

- Observation of natural processes before intervention
- Recognition of feedback loops and interconnections
- Understanding that local patterns are expressions of larger patterns

### Pattern Imposition

The second skill is the **imposition of pattern** on sites to achieve specific ends. This is not about imposing artificial geometry but about working with natural patterns to create functional designs:

- Using spiral forms for compact herb gardens (herb spirals)
- Using branching patterns for water distribution
- Using net patterns for garden layouts
- Using circular patterns for efficient irrigation

### Sensible Land Division

Mollison contrasts the rectilinear grid patterns of centralized societies (designed to suit survey instruments) with the bioregional approaches of traditional peoples. Hawaiian villagers, for example, used natural ridgelines as boundaries, creating stable, resource-rich landscapes spanning from cloud forests to outer reefs. This approach:

- Aligns boundaries with natural water catchments
- Maintains clean water supplies
- Captures lost nutrients in shallow ocean enclosures
- Preserves offshore reef and marine environments

Zulu and American Indian societies adopted circular or zonal modes in their plains settlements — patterns that served environmental and social needs rather than arbitrary geometric convenience.

## The Role of the Designer

The permaculture designer, informed by implicate order, operates not as an engineer imposing external solutions but as a **pattern reader** who:

1. **Observes** existing patterns in the landscape and social context
2. **Understands** the connections between elements
3. **Works with** natural processes rather than against them
4. **Designs** interventions that harmonize with the existing pattern language
5. **Anticipates** the emergent properties of the designed system

## Key Takeaways

- All phenomena are interconnected phases of a single unfolding process
- Isolating elements from their context yields incomplete understanding
- Design must work with streams, particles, and waves simultaneously
- Self-regulating systems are more resilient than controlled systems
- Pattern perception and pattern imposition are complementary design skills
- Traditional [[sensible-land-division-patterns]] often outperform rectilinear grids in both function and aesthetics
- The unity of phenomena demands an ethic of earth-care, without which human existence has no meaning in the larger cosmic context
