## Stock Management and Personal Survival in Wildfire

## Overview

Effective preparation for [[wildfire]] extends beyond structural design to encompass livestock [[livestock-grassland-management-strategies]] and personal survival protocols. Pre-planning is the single most important factor in reducing stock losses and human casualties during fire events. This concept covers practical, field-tested approaches to protecting animals and people in fire-prone rural landscapes.

## Stock Protection Strategies

### Preparing Fire Refuges for Livestock

Good stock managers can significantly reduce losses through pre-planning. The ideal approach involves creating dedicated refuge paddocks:

1. **Close-cropped paddocks**: Small areas close-cropped from late winter (or late summer in monsoon areas) through early summer carry no inflammable fuel and serve as general refuges during fire. These are most effective when prepared well before fire season.

2. **Bare soil refuge areas**: Even more effective is placing a water trough in a deliberately bare soil area where topsoil and shrubs have been bulldozed into a surrounding earth bank for protection against radiant heat. These areas serve as permanent, always-open refuges accessible from 4–6 prepared range paddocks.

3. **Temporary electric fencing**: Stock enclosed by temporary electric fences can be directed to clean off rocky knolls of grasses before the fire period. These grazed knolls then serve as additional emergency refuges.

### Using Grazing Animals for Fuel Reduction

Tethered goats or sheep effectively reduce patches of fuel near houses. Other close grazers that contribute to fuel management include:
- Geese
- [[wallaby]]
- Rabbits

Wild wallaby or geese can be encouraged to create "marsupial lawns" or "rabbit swards" by pollarding (cutting back) areas and feeding animals in. These naturally grazed areas provide fire breaks around settlements.

### Forest Refuges for Stock

A forest of young, deciduous trees — fig, mulberry, or oak — makes an effective stock refuge if close-browsed before fires are expected (during the dry grass period). Short swards are fairly quickly developed by close grazers if the area is first slashed, then limed and fertilised with phosphate. Over time, such fire refuges become popular places with stock, who learn to seek them out.

### Chickens and Poultry

Chickens survive well in solid housing. They often create bare areas around chicken sheds naturally, reducing fuel load. For additional protection, chicken sheds can be part-buried in earth banks to provide complete fire protection.

### Integration with House Firebreak Systems

Close-grazed or mown areas around houses form part of the upwind or downslope firebreak system. During fire-danger periods:
- Stock can be fed in or mustered
- Animals can be secured in these prepared areas or within the settlement itself
- The combination of fuel reduction and animal shelter creates a mutually reinforcing safety system

## Personal Survival During Wildfire

### Clothing and Shelter
The main survival factor for people is to:
- Cover the body with wool or cotton clothing
- Shelter from fire-front radiation behind a tree, car, house, or in a trench
- Cape the whole body in a wet blanket if possible

After flames have passed, move cautiously onto burnt ground. Never attempt to outrun a fire front.

### The Critical Danger of Oxygen Depletion
In firestorms, oxygen is periodically exhausted and the fire briefly goes out. People cannot breathe and will faint. **Critical safety warning**: Never take refuge in small water tanks, dams, or rivers — unconscious people will drown. Only the sea and large rivers of approximately 100 metres or more across are safe. Even then, be aware that the skin needs oxygen too, and fainting from lack of oxygen is a real risk.

### Hydration
Drink plenty of water to prevent dehydration. Ensure children and stock also receive water to replace lost body fluids. Dehydration compounds all other fire-related health risks.

## Pre-Fire Preparation Checklist

Advance preparation dramatically improves survival outcomes:

### Outdoor Preparations
- Build or maintain fish ponds and fill metal buckets with water
- Remove doormats (inflammable)
- Hose down the garden thoroughly
- Fill roof gutters (or plug and fill them)
- Tie up or pen stock in safe refuge areas

### Indoor Preparations
- Prepare blankets and important papers to be picked up by the rear door
- Prepare woollens to wear
- Take in drying clothes
- Fill baths and sinks with water

### Community and Civil Authority Response

For civil authorities, effective wildfire response requires:
- Radio stations keeping constant reports on fire direction
- Clear communication of open escape routes
- Family location centres and designated refuge areas
- Constant instructions to householders and travellers

### Post-Fire Operations
Just as the fire passes:
- Well-equipped ground teams preceded by a bulldozer should clear roads
- Teams should put out spot fires in unoccupied houses
- Police should roster guards or well-disciplined volunteers to prevent looting until the area is re-occupied and services reconnected
- Services must be on standby at the fire periphery — it is suicidal to put forward teams in the path of wildfire

## Psychological Aftermath

Survivors often face significant psychological challenges:
- **Noise and confusion**: The experience of a [[firestorm]] is overwhelmingly loud and chaotic
- **Sense of isolation**: Even in populated areas, fire creates profound feelings of being cut off
- **Survivor guilt**: The question "why should I have survived when people around me have died?" can persist for weeks, months, or even years
- **Desolation**: The losses sustained can create long-term psychological and social effects

Recovery is greatly aided by having assisted others before, during, and after the fire — knowing that one did their best provides meaningful psychological resilience. This principle applies to all catastrophes, not just fire.

## Rebuilding After Fire

A critical [[design-principle-integration]]: if a house burns down, do not rebuild another "just the same" as most people do. Instead, build one designed to survive the next disaster. This means incorporating all the fire-resistant [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]] — proper siting, fire-resistant materials, radiation shields, water storage, and defensible space — from the start.

The overarching philosophy is to have realistic expectations about fire risk, act on those expectations, have planned moves ready, and prepare better for next time. Every large wildfire in settled areas will result in loss of life, property destruction, and persistent psychological and social effects — but the severity of all three can be dramatically reduced through systematic preparation.
