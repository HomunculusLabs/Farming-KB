# Diversity and Stability in Permaculture

## Overview

Diversity and stability are two of the most fundamental — and most misunderstood — concepts in [[permaculture-design]]. [[bill-mollison]] devoted significant attention to dismantling the popular assumption that simply adding more species to a system automatically produces resilience. Instead, he argued that the *connections* between components, not their mere count, determine the stability and productivity of any designed ecosystem.

## Defining Diversity

Diversity is formally defined as the number of different components or constructs within a system — an enumeration of elements and their parts. Importantly, diversity has no intrinsic relationship to the connections between those components, and bears little relation to the self-regulating capacity of a real system (within the boundaries of too few or too many components). Simply maintaining a diverse collection of species in a garden does not guarantee either stability or yield.

### The Maintenance Burden

In practice, human effort is an integral part of maintaining diversity in [[permaculture]] systems. Few species cultivated by people persist beyond the lifetime of those species if the site is abandoned. Mollison cites the example of Australian mining and port towns built in forested areas: when abandoned, they are obliterated by forest within 30–80 years, with perhaps only a few hardy survivor trees — dates, mulberries, and figs — persisting in dryland locations. These survivor species are critical reference points for planning long-term stability in a given region.

### Richness, Diversity, and Evenness

Peter Moon (New Scientist, 28 Feb 1985) differentiates between three related but distinct measures:

- **Richness**: the number of species per unit area
- **Diversity**: the relative abundance of species
- **Evenness**: how species contribute to the biomass total

Richness in plants may actually decrease as systems age, when shade and competition reduce annuals or weaker species. However, richness in animals — particularly decomposers — may increase due to the development of a greater range of niches and microclimates. More animals live in ungrazed or uncut grasslands, but fewer plant species survive.

### Energy Use and Species Richness

Richness of tree species has been correlated to the energy use of the plant community, as measured by evapotranspiration (New Scientist, 22 Oct 1987). Species-rich regions are linked more to the basic productivity of a region than to latitude, prior glaciation, or fire history. Within this framework, local niches or a range of altitudes can create additional diversity. These measures refer to present climate, not past conditions.

## The Stress Mosaic

Some disturbance or "moderate stress" — such as that achieved in cultivated gardens — provides the richest environment. Designers can actively create a **stress mosaic** by allowing some undisturbed (low-stress) islands of vegetation while mowing or digging in other areas (high stress), thus maximizing species diversity across the site. Managing grazing pressures locally further allows control of species abundance.

### Stress and Harmony Defined

- **Stress** may be defined as either prevention of natural function, or forced function.
- **Harmony** is defined as the integration of chosen and natural functions, and the supply of essential needs.

## The Principle of Stability

> It is not the number of diverse things in a design that leads to stability; it is the number of beneficial connections between these components.

This is one of the most cited principles in permaculture. Adding a technology or living species "just to have it there" has no value. Adding it to supply a need or consume an otherwise wasted resource — to perform a useful function — makes the system more stable. Often, however, designers lack functional information about components and may therefore leave out technologies or species that would have been useful.

## Information as the Critical Resource

> Information is the critical potential resource. It becomes a resource only when obtained and acted upon.

In the real world, resources are energy storages; in the abstract world, useful information or time. Watt's categories of resources include time and diversity, but diversity of itself is not a resource — a diversity of beneficial functional connections certainly is. Complexity, in the sense of powerful interconnections between species, is what permaculture designers seek in food systems. Such complexity follows its own rules, expressed through polyculture recommendations and guilds of mutually assisting plants and animals.

## Stability in Ecosystems

### Self-Regulation vs. Climax

The meaningful definition of stability in an ecosystem is self-regulation rather than climax (end-point) stability. Nothing in nature remains forever — not soil, hills, or forests. For the foreseeable future, we can have dynamic life-support systems, as tribal people have demonstrated worldwide, sometimes for thousands of years of constructive regulation.

Stability is not the stability of a concrete pylon; it is the process of constant feedback and response, like riding a bicycle.

### The Fragility of Old Systems

Very old systems may also be fragile, having been long in a state of maintenance. [[john-seymour]] documented the slow loss of nutrients in an old stable dune system at Cooloola in Australia, where climax is a passing phase as virgin dunes lose nutrient status to fire and deep water filtration. [[daniel-goodman]] noted that "wild fluctuations" may occur in tropical forests or savannah grasslands, with epidemics of pathogens affecting individual species. While these natural fluctuations are less severe than human-induced disturbances, persistent nutrient loss remains the most damaging threat.

All these effects are under some degree of human control in a developed permaculture system: protection from fire, positive nutrient supply, and managed long-term evolution are possible during human occupancy.

## Time, Yield, and System Age

Old systems store their energy in bulky, unproductive forms. An old forest has large trunks, roots, and limbs; old fish are "on maintenance." Ancient systems composed of large individuals need energy just to maintain their health, leaving less available solar energy for productive flow. Therefore, the yield — or turnover of matter — is less.

### Species Richness as a Counterbalance

Against this aging factor, species diversity (richness) works to maximize the use of incoming energy. Carlander showed that the standing crop of fish in reservoirs is an increasing function of the number of species present. This pattern holds in most wild systems, where complexity and standing crop are both much greater than in the simple cultivated ecologies that replace them.

### Death as Renewal

Death in over-mature systems is seen as essential renewal of life — not in the negativistic sense of the fatalist, but in a positivistic and natural way. It is better that elements die and are renewed by other species than that the system simplifies to extinction. Life is then understood as a preparation for succession and renewal, rather than a journey to extinction.

### Time as a Resource

Time, as Watt notes, is a resource. Like all resources, too much becomes counterproductive. A system in which too much time has accumulated becomes chronically degraded, just as a system with insufficient time is below peak yield. A strawberry seedling and an old strawberry bush are equally unproductive, as are the very young and the very old in society. A mixed-age stand is the best insurance against complete failure or epidemic disease.

## Practical Applications

1. **Design for connections, not collections**: Select each component for its functional role in the system, not for diversity alone.
2. **Create stress mosaics**: Combine undisturbed areas with actively managed zones to maximize richness.
3. **Note survivor species**: Identify which species persist after abandonment in your region — these inform long-term stability planning.
4. **Maintain mixed-age stands**: Avoid monocultures of a single age class to prevent age-specific diseases and failures.
5. **Plan for succession**: Design systems that renew themselves through natural cycles of growth, death, and replacement.
6. **Seek complexity over diversity**: Focus on beneficial functional connections between species rather than simply counting species.
