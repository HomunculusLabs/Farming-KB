## Diversity and Stability in Permaculture

## Overview

Diversity and stability are two of the most fundamental — and most misunderstood — concepts in [[bill-mollison]] devoted significant attention to dismantling the popular assumption that simply adding more species to a system automatically produces resilience. Instead, he argued that the *connections* between components, not their mere count, determine the stability and productivity of any designed ecosystem.

## Defining Diversity

Diversity is formally defined as the number of different components or constructs within a system — an enumeration of elements and their parts. Importantly, diversity has no intrinsic relationship to the connections between those components, and bears little relation to the self-regulating capacity of a real system (within the boundaries of too few or too many components). Simply maintaining a diverse collection of species in a garden does not guarantee either stability or yield.

### The Maintenance Burden

In practice, human effort is an integral part of maintaining diversity in [[john-seymour]] documented the slow loss of nutrients in an old stable dune system at Cooloola in Australia, where climax is a passing phase as virgin dunes lose nutrient status to fire and deep water filtration. [[daniel-goodman]] noted that "wild fluctuations" may occur in tropical forests or savannah grasslands, with epidemics of pathogens affecting individual species. While these natural fluctuations are less severe than human-induced disturbances, persistent nutrient loss remains the most damaging threat.

All these effects are under some degree of human control in a developed permaculture system: protection from fire, positive nutrient supply, and managed long-term evolution are possible during human occupancy.

## Time, Yield, and System Age

Old systems store their energy in bulky, unproductive forms. An old forest has large trunks, roots, and limbs; old fish are "on maintenance." Ancient systems composed of large individuals need energy just to maintain their health, leaving less available solar energy for productive flow. Therefore, the yield — or turnover of matter — is less.

### Species Richness as a Counterbalance

Against this aging factor, species diversity (richness) works to maximize the use of incoming energy. Carlander showed that the standing crop of fish in reservoirs is an increasing function of the number of species present. This pattern holds in most wild systems, where complexity and standing crop are both much greater than in the simple cultivated ecologies that replace them.

### Death as Renewal

Death in over-mature systems is seen as essential renewal of life — not in the negativistic sense of the fatalist, but in a positivistic and natural way. It is better that elements die and are renewed by other species than that the system simplifies to extinction. Life is then understood as a preparation for succession and renewal, rather than a journey to extinction.

### Time as a Resource

Time, as Watt notes, is a resource. Like all resources, too much becomes counterproductive. A system in which too much time has accumulated becomes chronically degraded, just as a system with insufficient time is below peak yield. A strawberry seedling and an old strawberry bush are equally unproductive, as are the very young and the very old in society. A mixed-age stand is the best insurance against complete failure or epidemic disease.

## Practical Applications

1. **Design for connections, not collections**: Select each component for its functional role in the system, not for diversity alone.
2. **Create stress mosaics**: Combine undisturbed areas with actively managed zones to maximize richness.
3. **Note survivor species**: Identify which species persist after abandonment in your region — these inform long-term stability planning.
4. **Maintain mixed-age stands**: Avoid monocultures of a single age class to prevent age-specific diseases and failures.
5. **Plan for succession**: Design systems that renew themselves through natural cycles of growth, death, and replacement.
6. **Seek complexity over diversity**: Focus on beneficial functional connections between species rather than simply counting species.
