# Perennial Vegetables for the Ecological Garden

The ecological garden imitates a mature ecosystem by emphasizing
perennials rather than annuals. While the most obvious perennial food
plants are berries, fruits, and nuts, a growing roster of perennial
vegetables can significantly reduce the gardener's workload while
improving soil health and ecosystem function.

## Why Perennials Matter

Perennials eliminate seed-starting, tilling, and the weed opportunities
that tilling creates, slashing three major chores at one stroke. They
need less water and fertilizer than annuals because their deep root
systems tap into moisture and nutrient pockets that annuals cannot reach.
As year-round plants, perennials also offer dependable habitat to
wildlife and beneficial insects.

The ecological advantages run deeper still:

- **Standing biomass**: Perennials retain permanent branches and large
  root systems. When you harvest leaves from perennial kale, you remove
  only a tiny fraction of the plant, leaving most biomass in place to
  keep nutrients cycling. Harvesting annuals, by contrast, resembles
  small-scale clear-cutting
- **Soil integrity**: Perennials hold soil in place year-round with
  their roots and prevent erosion with nearly continuous cover. Their
  roots often go far deeper than annuals, accessing water and nutrients
  from deep soil horizons
- **Ecosystem maturity**: Ample standing biomass is a quality of mature
  ecosystems such as forests. Perennials are a feature of mature
  ecosystems; annuals, of immature, pioneer systems like vacant lots
  and conventional farmland
- **Reduced disturbance**: Planting annuals every year means disturbing
  soil, which damages soil life and brings weed seeds to the surface.
  Tilling flushes the soil with oxygen, causing microbes to burn
  organic matter and consume nutrients

## Common Perennial Vegetables

The most widely known perennial vegetables in temperate North America
are asparagus, rhubarb, and artichoke. But the list extends well beyond
these staples:

- **Egyptian or walking onions** (Allium x proliferum): Set
  shallot-like bulbs above ground on stalks; self-propagate by
  topsetting
- **Nine-star perennial broccoli** (Brassica oleracea var. botrytis):
  Must be picked before setting seed or it may die
- **Perennial kale or tree collards** (Brassica oleracea var. ramosa):
  Reaches 5 feet in height with a thick trunk; provides continuous
  harvest of leaves
- **Scarlet runner beans**: Perennial in mild climates (USDA Zone 8 and
  above), producing both edible beans and attractive flowers
- **Bamboo**: Edible shoots in spring; also provides garden stakes and
  windbreak material

## Less Familiar Perennial Vegetables

A broader palette of perennial vegetables deserves wider attention:

- **Garlic chives** (Allium tuberosum): Tangy leaves and flowers;
  attractive ornamental
- **Perennial groundcherry** (Physalis heterophylla): Wild relative of
  the tomato, hardy to at least minus 20 degrees F, with tangy sweet
  golden berries
- **Groundplum milkvetch** (Astragalus crassicarpus): Plum-like pods
  with a pealike flavor and purple flowers; also fixes nitrogen
- **Sea kale** (Crambe maritima): Broccoli-like heads and blanchable
  shoots; edible leaves
- **Lovage** (Levisticum officinale): Old European vegetable with
  celery-flavored stalks, seeds, and leaves; huge flowers attract
  beneficial insects
- **Mitsuba or Japanese parsley** (Cryptotaenia japonica): Perennial
  parsley for moist, shady spots; hardy to minus 20 degrees F or below
- **Ramps or wild leeks** (Allium tricoccum): Prized spring delicacy
  for shady, moist woodlands
- **French sorrel** (Rumex acetosa): Self-seeding perennial green with
  a lemony tang
- **Good King Henry** (Chenopodium bonus-henricus): Perennial spinach
  relative

## Perennial Greens and Self-Seeders

Many salad greens are either perennial or effectively self-seeding,
meaning they return each year without replanting. Perennial options
include French sorrel, perennial kale, and Good King Henry. Effective
self-seeders include arugula, chard, red mustard, and lettuce. These
bridge the gap between true perennials and annuals, reducing the need
for formal seed-starting and transplanting.

## Integration into Garden Design

Perennial vegetables integrate naturally into [[hemenway-food-forest-design]]
and [[hemenway-forest-garden-layers]]. They fit into the herbaceous layer
beneath fruit trees and shrubs, forming part of the multi-storied
canopy described in the ecological garden model. They also pair well
with [[hemenway-vegetable-polyculture-design]] systems, where perennial
greens can serve as long-lived components alongside annual vegetables.

Perennial vegetables are particularly valuable in [[hemenway-zone-and-sector-planning]],
where low-maintenance food crops near the home (Zone 1 and 2) reduce
the gardener's workload while providing regular harvests. They also
support [[hemenway-soil-food-web-garden]] by maintaining undisturbed root
systems that harbor beneficial microbes and fungi year-round.

The overarching goal is to shift the garden from an immature,
disturbance-dependent system toward the stability and self-reliance of a
mature ecosystem, as described in [[hemenway-ecological-principles-niche-succession-biodiversity]].
