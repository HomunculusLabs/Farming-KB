# Types of Earthworks — Banks, Benches, and Terraces

## Overview

Earthworks [[ecological-succession-in-permaculture-design]] encompass a range of structural forms, each suited to specific site conditions and purposes. The three primary types discussed here — banks, benches, and terraces — are fundamental building blocks for landscape modification, [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]], access creation, and productive land use on slopes.

## Banks

### Principles

It is cheaper to construct a bank than to step or retain the earth, provided the bank can be made stable. Wherever a four-way bucket (Bobcat or drott) or power shovel can be used to dish the bank, the result will remain stable at greater slopes than a straight cut, because the dished profile frustrates soil slump by giving it the profile it would naturally achieve had it slumped.

### Stability Considerations

- Banks or cuts more than 4 metres high need careful stabilisation
- Unlike dam walls, cuts for roads are not normally compacted, nor do they necessarily have much pressure on them
- The greatest cause of slump is water flow through or over banks and the lie of strata in shaly ground
- All normal soils with some clay and stony banks are fairly stable if the angle of rest is preserved, banks are cut somewhat concave, and drainage is fitted

### Slump Control Essentials

Multiple methods can prevent slump from cuts above terraces or roads, and vegetation assists all of these systems:

- Proper drainage to prevent water accumulation behind or within banks
- Concave bank profiles that mimic natural stable slopes
- Appropriate slope angles for the soil type
- Vegetation on bank faces and at the base
- In severe slump areas, re-routing of roads or expensive drainage and concrete retaining walls may be last resorts

## Benching

### Definition and Purpose

A bench is a flat, near-contoured cut made in a slope. Benches serve multiple functions:

- Creating roads and house sites on steep terrain
- Providing access for planting, maintenance, and harvest in forestry operations
- Enabling productive land use on slopes that would otherwise be inaccessible

### Applicable Slopes

Very severe slopes of 30–40° can be bench-cut if a bulldozer can start on safe ground (typically from a ridge of 20° or so). Benches are particularly valuable in long-term forestry if a steep hill is benched every 100 metres or so, greatly aiding access, planting, and eventually harvest.

### Construction Method

1. Survey line pegged every 6 metres (20 feet)
2. Bulldozer with blade set to side-cast (angled) follows the peg line
3. A run with a raised blade shaves back or slightly steps the uphill bank
4. In stable soils, benches can slope into the hill to form swales for water infiltration

### Tree Shelf Benches

Narrow (1.5–3.0 metre) benches on steep slopes are not so much roads as tree shelves — quickly over-hung by tree crop, shaded, stable, and accessible. A quiet traverse with a donkey or cart serves to distribute plants, fertiliser, or mulch and to gather in the crop.

### Design Details

- **Cross-walls** every 20–30 metres prevent gutter flow along the bench
- Benches on very steep slopes can slope slightly outwards to drain
- Benching should not continue across water runnels unless pipes, gabions, or culverts are fitted
- Small watercourses can be bridged
- Watercourse crossings are the greatest long-term expense and need to be well-made and durable

### Kick-Down Systems

Small-holders on very steep hillsides can work with slopes using a cascading system: orchard and mulch above poultry above garden. If the ridge or hill above is planted to mulch-producing trees such as Casuarina, pine, oak, and beech, mulch and bedding can be thrown downhill as greenfeed and seed for chickens, who kick down to the lower fence where the gardener accepts the manured and shredded mulch for terraced [[natural-farming-guide-hugelkultur-mounded-garden-beds]].

### Machine Efficiency

- Bulldozers can loosen and pile up material but not load it
- They can bench but not trench (or at least not smaller than blade width)
- Much faster and better work can be done cross-slope and downhill than upslope
- Expensive for a bulldozer to travel far pushing a load (about 6–9 machine lengths is maximum)
- Side-casting (blade angled to direction of travel) is efficient — no dirt is carried, just cast to one side
- A site carefully pegged to suit the machines called in saves time, fuel, money, and timber
- On complex jobs, two or more machines may be needed

## Terracing

### Potential and Stability

Terraced lands, given a reserve of local [[cover-crops]] or composts and adequate water, are potentially very stable production systems. Terraces have [[community-supported-agriculture-csa]] for thousands of years across Asia, the Mediterranean, the Andes, and elsewhere.

### Conditions for Failure

Terracing fails when:

- **Unstable soils or sediments** are used — attempting to terrace in materials that cannot maintain structural integrity
- **Hydraulic pressure** builds up on hill slopes from impounded or infiltrated water without adequate drainage
- **Terrace bunds or wall faces** are unstable at their designed angle or construction quality
- **Terracing extends as annual crop over too large an area** without adequate maintenance capacity
- **Drainage is inadequate** — water accumulation behind terrace walls creates hydrostatic pressure that can cause catastrophic failure

### Successful Terracing Requirements

- Stable base soils with adequate clay content for cohesion
- Proper drainage behind and through terrace walls
- Bund or wall faces constructed at appropriate angles for the soil type
- Integration with perennial vegetation, especially trees, for long-term root stabilisation
- Maintenance regime for wall repair, drainage clearing, and soil renewal
- Appropriate scale — matching terrace extent to available labour and materials for upkeep

## Integration of Earthwork Types

In practice, banks, benches, and terraces are often combined within a single site design. A hillside development might use benched roads for access, terraced gardens for production, and banks for [[cervantes-wind-protection-outdoor-cannabis]] and water management. The designer's task is to select the appropriate earthwork type for each location based on slope, soil, water flow, access needs, and intended use, while ensuring that all elements work together as a stable, productive system.

The true finesse of earthworks is best entrusted to experienced machinery drivers, but designers need to designate stages, spoil areas, topsoil stores, bank slopes, and sequencing — and above all, to imagine themselves as the driver to make it as easy as possible to cut the shapes desired.
