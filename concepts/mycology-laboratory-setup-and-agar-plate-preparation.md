## Mycology Laboratory Setup and Agar Plate Preparation

A milestone in any mushroom grower"s development is the construction and maintenance of a sterile culture laboratory capable of producing spawn of many different species for personal use or small-scale commercial production. While hobby growers may prefer ordering spawn from supply companies, growers who consistently cultivate several species may be ready to upgrade to a home laboratory. The primary benefits are cost savings and having a consistent, fresh supply of spawn made in small batches as needed.

## Setting Up a Laboratory

Almost any enclosed space can be converted into a basic mycology lab -- a small closet, extra room, or even a partitioned section of a room. Partitions can be constructed with metal or PVC frames enclosed with clear plastic, similar to greenhouse walls. The entry should be well sealed to maintain interior air cleanliness; zippered entries work well in plastic partition walls. Floors must be smooth -- concrete, tile, linoleum, or any hard surface that can be swept and mopped daily. Carpeted or textured floors are unsuitable. Walls should be smooth and capable of withstanding weekly wipe-downs. As a last resort, a spare bathroom can be thoroughly sanitized and used with a portable air filter or ionizer running for an hour before work begins.

## Positive Pressure and Gas Exchange

A positive-pressure ventilation unit, while not strictly necessary at first, significantly improves lab conditions. This consists of a small fan fitted with a HEPA filter that pulls clean fresh air into the lab space. The incoming air "inflates" the room (positive pressure), so if any leak exists, clean air pushes out rather than contaminated air coming in. Fresh air is also important for developing spawn, which generates carbon dioxide, and for the operator"s safety.

Commercial spawn laboratories often have positive-pressure air lock entries to ensure the lab space is never open to the outside. A length of ribbon hanging from the door serves as a visual cue confirming airflow direction is outward from the lab.

## Making Agar Plates

An agar plate is a petri plate containing agar, a gelatinous [[comparison-seaweed-extract-vs-fish-hydrolysate]] used as a substrate for tissue cultures. Glass petri plates can be washed and reused, sterilizing between uses. Agar powder is available from mycological supply companies and most Asian markets. Lab-grade agar becomes more economical as production scales up.

### Step 1: Preparing Agar Gel

Slice a small potato (unpeeled) into small chunks or grate it. Place the pieces in a small pot with 3-4 cups of water and boil for one hour, being careful not to let it foam or boil over. The water will look like "potato tea" but will not thicken. Add water if the level drops significantly. Strain the potatoes from the liquid, measure the hot broth, and pour it into an Erlenmeyer flask or wide-bottomed glass container. Add 1 teaspoon (approximately 5 grams) of agar powder for every 2 cups of potato broth. Always add agar to hot broth to prevent clumping. Professional labs use hot plate stirrers; swirling the agar into hot water by hand also works.

### Step 2: Sterilizing the Agar

A pressure cooker capable of maintaining 12-15 PSI is required. Lower PSI means longer cook times and lower sterilization temperatures. Metal-to-metal seal models are superior to rubber gasket models, though gasketed units work for developing skills before investing in better equipment. The cooker must be tall enough to accommodate the flask.

Fill the pressure cooker with water per manufacturer directions, place the flask upright in the inner pan (stabilize with aluminum foil wads or a wire basket if needed), plug the flask top with cotton or polyester fill, and cover with aluminum foil compressed tightly. Process at 15 PSI for twenty minutes, then turn off the heat and allow cooling.

Critical timing: Open the cooker only when internal pressure is slightly above 0 PSI, in a clean room or in front of the laminar hood. If pressure drops below 0 PSI, a vacuum forms that sucks in contaminated air, requiring resterilization. If opened at too high pressure, the sudden change causes the agar to boil out of the flask. The safe window is 2-3 PSI for moving the cooker and slightly above 0 PSI for opening.

Remove the hot flask using a paper towel saturated with 70 percent isopropyl alcohol as a mitt, or a sanitized jar lifter. Never touch sterilized containers with bare hands, as skin oils can be colonized by bacteria and compromise every subsequent transfer.

### Step 3: Pouring Agar Plates

Monitor the agar temperature with an infrared temperature gauge. The agar will be extremely hot when removed (up to 200 degrees F / 93 degrees C) but cools quickly. Check every ten minutes and swirl occasionally to mix. Do not touch the flask with bare hands; wear sterile gloves. The pouring temperature is around 100 degrees F (38 degrees C). If antibiotics are being added, do so between 105-110 degrees F (41-43 degrees C).

Prepare sterile petri plates at the laminar hood by wiping the outside of the packaging sleeve with rubbing alcohol and sanitizing scissors. Trim the bottom off the sleeve, invert the stack, set it upright, and remove the sleeve by tugging upward. Divide into four or five smaller stacks for efficient pouring.

Hold the flask in one hand and lift the lid of the bottom petri plate with the other, raising the entire stack while leaving the base on the tabletop. Pour agar until the plate is about half full, then lower the stack to replace the lid. Slide to the next plate and repeat. Keep filled stacks level to prevent sloshing. Two cups of agar fills approximately twenty to twenty-five plates. With practice, an entire sleeve can be poured in one to two minutes.

After approximately one hour, check for gelling by tilting a few plates. Properly gelled agar should be firm and elastic. Wet or improperly gelled agar is prone to contamination and difficult for spore streaking.

### Step 4: Labeling and Storing

Label plates with date, recipe, and additives. A color-coding system simplifies this -- for example, green marks denote penicillin G, with one mark for 1 ml and two marks for 2 ml. Dragging a colored marker up an entire stack of gelled plates codes them quickly and efficiently.

Cultured plates should be sealed with tape or Parafilm at the seam between base and lid to prevent separation and air infiltration. Unused plates are sealed in new ziplock bags in packs of three or four, labeled with date and formula code, and refrigerated for one to two months or longer. Plastic bags are very clean inside since they were heat-injected during manufacturing, minimizing contamination risk. Pre-poured plates are invaluable when unexpected mushroom specimens are found during field collection. Always inspect stored plates for contamination -- circular green, yellow, or black powdery growth means the bag and its contents should be discarded.

## Alternative Agar Bases

Potato agar is difficult for bacteria to grow on, making it an excellent starter medium. However, picky mushroom species may need more complete or specific food sources. Alternative bases include powdered brewer's malt, dog and rabbit food, tomato juice, cattle feed, cornmeal, and beet pulp. Different ecotypes of the same species can differ in their enzymatic ability to break down compounds, so customizing agar formulas improves culture health and performance. Keep a journal tracking which species perform best on which formulas. Small quantities of supplements can be mixed into agar, but increased supplementation raises the risk of cultivating contaminants -- the goal is finding the supplementation range that yields the best formulas while preventing molds and bacteria from outcompeting mushroom cultures.
