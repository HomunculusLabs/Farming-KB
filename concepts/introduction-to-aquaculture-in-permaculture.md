# Introduction to Aquaculture in Permaculture

Aquaculture — the cultivation of aquatic organisms in natural or artificial water systems — represents one of the most productive and efficient food [[system-yield-and-production-strategies]] available to [[permaculture]] designers. Water-based systems consistently outperform terrestrial agriculture in yield per unit of input, making aquaculture a critical component of integrated land design.

## Definition and Scope of Wetlands

The term **wetlands** encompasses both natural and artificial impoundments of water:
- Lakes and ponds
- Bogs, swamps, and marshes
- Shallow water and intertidal areas of estuaries
- Marine marshes
- Artificial impoundments, canals, and raceways

In [[permaculture-design]], the emphasis is on those wetland areas used by people for foraging and fish-farming, and on the design principles that integrate water systems into productive landscapes.

## Productivity Advantages of Aquatic Systems

[[fungal-roles-aquatic-environments]] are among the most naturally productive systems on Earth. Tropical rainforests and shallow-water aquatic environments share the distinction of having the greatest natural yields. Mangrove swamps, marshes, and estuaries produce prodigious biomass of great complexity.

### Why Aquatic Systems Outperform Terrestrial Agriculture

Given equivalent inputs in energy or nutrients, aquatic systems can produce **4-20 times the yield** of adjoining land. This remarkable productivity advantage results from several factors:

1. **Constant water supply** for plant and animal growth — no drought stress, no irrigation overhead
2. **Soluble, easily assimilable nutrients** — plants in water access nutrients directly without the soil chemistry limitations of terrestrial systems
3. **Water and nutrient flow** — a continuous factor in [[biodiversity-fungi-freshwater-aquatic-habitats]] that dramatically boosts production, absent in static field systems
4. **Reduced energy expenditure** — aquatic organisms (fish, shellfish) waste little energy in movement, being largely free of gravitational effects and weight disadvantages
5. **Three-dimensional medium** — light, nutrients, and plants occupy a volume rather than a surface, with complex edges, surfaces, and conditions supporting a variety of species
6. **Polyculture from inception** — unlike terrestrial agriculture, which has been dominated by monoculture, water cultures have recognized the advantages of polyculture from the beginning
7. **Eliminated cultivation energy costs** — energies lost in ploughing, cultivating, and harvesting land crops are reduced or eliminated
8. **Multiple products from impounded water** — beyond food production, water systems provide energy, recreation, irrigation, and transport

## Historical Aquaculture Civilizations

Throughout history, some of the most productive and sustainable human civilizations have been built around integrated water systems:

### Deltaic and Riverine Cultures
The deltaic mazes of the Fly, Chao Phraya, Mekong, Nile, and Ganges rivers support rich cultures with access to marine, estuary, freshwater, mangrove, and land organisms. These civilizations demonstrate the productive potential of integrated multi-habitat systems.

### Terraced Padi Systems
Cultures based on terraced padi (rice and taro) serve much of Asia and Oceania, with water providing the primary medium for staple crop production plus subsidiary crops, fish, and aquatic plants.

### The Aztec Chinampas
The Aztecs at Lake Tenochtitlan in Mexico developed what is possibly the most productive system of polyculture yet devised, combining:
- **[[chinampa]] crop** production on artificial islands
- **Waterfowl** culture
- **Reedbed** systems for forage, boats, housing, and materials
- **Fish culture** integrated with crop production

### Reed-Based Civilizations
On the Euphrates and Lake Titicaca in Peru, whole cultures based on reed beds (Typha, Phragmites, Cyperus) derive forage, boats, housing, bread, and meat from wetland systems.

### Island Cultures
Island cultures such as the Hawaiian ohana synthesis neatly combine water [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-earthworking-and-earth-resources]], demonstrating the integration potential of aquatic systems in diverse environments.

## The Concept of Bioregion and Watershed

The concept of **bioregion** is fundamentally based on **watershed** — an ancient natural division of tribes and languages. Cooperative communities and bioregional democracies have historically been based on water rights, as have hydraulic tyrannies. Both forms of water-based social organization continue to exist today.

There is an intimate and indissoluble link between the health of a river and the health of its catchment. This reveals the literal truth of "upstream and downstream costs" — activities in one part of a watershed directly affect all downstream systems.

## The Pollution Threat to Aquatic Systems

Pollution most rapidly permeates landscapes and societies via water. This creates a critical vulnerability:

- **Biocides used in catchments** cause death in rivers and seas, extending to reef areas offshore
- The **death of coral reef areas** is closely correlated with the use of 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T herbicides inland
- **Preservation of water habitat cleanliness and variety** is as critical to the survival of nations as soil conservation
- Both water quality and soil health are in peril today

This underscores the importance of organic and biological farming practices in the entire catchment area — pollution at any point in the watershed affects all downstream aquatic productivity.

## Species Selection: Local vs. Introduced

Historically, aquacultures have had a predominantly local flavour and species composition. Key principles:

- **Indigenous species** are typically better adapted, more productive, and uniquely suited to local conditions than introduced species
- **Exotic species** should only be considered when no local species exist, or when all trials using local species prove fruitless
- The pre-1960s global aquarium trade widely distributed plants, molluscs, fish, and disease organisms, many of which became naturalized
- Government-assisted distribution of salmonid fish as a "preserve of the idle and the affluent" has caused significant ecological disruption
- A few fish species, notably **Tilapia** and **carp**, have been successfully introduced as basic farmed food for terrace rice and taro culture

## Modern Aquaculture Systems

Contemporary aquaculture now ranges from extensive to highly intensive:

- **Open-water cage and ring-net systems** for marine species
- **Highly intensive tank or channel-flow cultures** for freshwater species
- **Innovative systems** on islands, atolls, and in deserts where local species were absent
- **Polycultural systems** — unlike terrestrial cultures, many aquaculture developments have been polycultural from their inception

As pelagic fisheries are overexploited, island and coastal peoples have been forced to develop alternative food systems, with aquaculture being one of the primary solutions.

## The Case for Expanding Aquaculture

Several converging factors drive the expansion of aquaculture:

### Declining Wild Fisheries
Over-fishing of marine resources means we can no longer harvest sufficient fish, molluscs, and plants from natural water systems. This creates an imperative for cultivated aquatic production.

### Changing Food Habits
Global travel and information exchange have shifted dietary preferences from fatty and red meats toward fish and shellfish, and created demand for variety in foods that can be eaten raw or lightly cooked.

### Proven Stability
Water cultures have demonstrated long-tested and undoubted stability — many have persisted without external inputs for thousands of years. The stability and productivity of aquaculture systems are superior to the terrestrial culture systems so far developed.

### Multi-Product Yields
Impounded water produces a great variety of products beyond food: energy production, recreation, irrigation, and transport. This multi-functionality makes aquaculture investments particularly valuable.

### Barriers to Adoption
Despite these advantages, terrestrial farmers have been slow to convert to aquaculture, partly because:
- Supporting infrastructure of storage, transport, and sales has been lacking
- Many farmers are unfamiliar with aquatic system management
- Traditional agricultural skills and inhibitions (particularly monocultural thinking) do not transfer directly

However, as infrastructure develops, it becomes increasingly worthwhile to shift from surplus terrestrial commodities to the more stable and specialized products of aquaculture.

## Design Implications for Permaculture

For [[bill-mollison]], aquaculture offers:
- **Highest per-hectare productivity** when combining species of different feeding habits
- **Integration potential** with terrestrial systems (irrigation, nutrient cycling, fire protection)
- **Diversified production** from a single water resource
- **Polyculture advantages** that are inherent rather than requiring deliberate design
- **[[water-conservation]]** benefits that extend to the entire property
- **Multiple yield streams** from food, energy, recreation, and ecological services

The designer's task is to assess local water resources, select appropriate species combinations, and integrate aquatic systems into the broader property design for maximum productivity and resilience.
