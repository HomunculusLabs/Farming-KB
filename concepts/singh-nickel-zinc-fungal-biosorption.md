# Nickel and Zinc Biosorption by Fungi

Nickel and zinc are common heavy metal contaminants in industrial
wastewaters from mining, electroplating, battery manufacturing, and
metal finishing operations. Fungal biosorption provides an effective and
economical approach for removing these metals from solution. Singh's
review documents numerous fungal species capable of nickel and zinc
biosorption, the mechanisms involved, and the behavior of these metals
in single and multi-metal competitive systems.

## Nickel Biosorption Mechanisms

Nickel biosorption by fungi involves multiple mechanisms including surface
binding, intracellular accumulation, and in some cases, precipitation as
metal oxalates. Analytical electron microscopy of [[aspergillus-niger]]
biomass reveals the localization of nickel in the form of small
rectangular crystals associated with the cell wall and inside the cell
(Magyarosy et al., 2002). These crystals are identified as nickel oxalate
dihydrate, representing an intracellular detoxification mechanism.
Electron microscopy reveals the localization of nickel on the cell
surface of Rhizopus sp. (Mogollon et al., 1998). FTIR spectra of
protonated and Ni(II)-saturated yeast show that biosorption occurs
primarily in the sugar and nucleic acid regions, involving carboxyl and
amino groups (Padmavathy et al., 2003b).

## Nickel Uptake Capacities

Aspergillus niger demonstrates 98% removal of Ni(II) at 6.5 mM and pH
4.75 over 100 hours (Magyarosy et al., 2002). [[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]]
achieves Ni(II) uptake of 11.4 mg/g at 1000 mg/l, pH 6.75, within 20
to 60 minutes (Padmavathy et al., 2003a). Live biomass removes nickel
more effectively than does alkali-boiled biomass (Kapoor et al., 1999),
indicating that both passive and active uptake mechanisms contribute to
nickel removal. Bjerkandera adusta and [[trametes-versicolor]] have also
demonstrated significant nickel reduction from aqueous solutions. The
binding affinity of metal ions to yeast cells follows the order
Pb(II) > Cu(II) > Ni(II), with nickel showing moderate affinity relative
to other divalent cations.

## Zinc Biosorption Mechanisms

Chitin and chitosan of the fungal cell wall are the predominant targets
for zinc biosorption by [[rhizopus-arrhizus]] (Zhou, 1999). In Penicillium
simplicissimum, adsorption of zinc is accompanied by the production of
citric acid (Franz et al., 1991), suggesting that both passive surface
binding and active metabolic processes contribute to zinc removal. Zinc
uptake has been summarized by Ross (1994), noting that Zn(II) uptake
by the yeast-mycelium dimorphic fungus [[aureobasidium-pullulans]] grown
in chemostat culture correlated with saturation kinetics (Krogh et al.,
1998). Zinc uptake affected the yeast-mycelium transition of Candida
albicans (Sabie and Gadd, 1990), indicating that zinc can influence
fungal morphology at elevated concentrations.

## Zinc in Multi-Metal Systems

Metal ions in both distilled water and urban wastewater were adsorbed in
the order Pb(II) > Cu(II) > Zn(II) > Cd(II) > Ni(II) using biomaterials
obtained by thermal alkali treatment of Phomopsis sp. (Saiano et al.,
2005). All binary systems containing zinc show mutual inhibition, and an
unequal inhibition is noted in systems containing lead. The respective
sorption order of Streptoverticillium cinnamoneum and Penicillium
chrysogenum in multimetal systems is Pb(II) > Zn(II) > Ni(II) > Cd(II).
Lead uptake by nonliving R. nigricans was reduced in the presence of
co-cations such as Zn(II) and Fe(III) ions at pH 4.0. The ubiquitous
presence of zinc in contaminated environments means it frequently
competes with target metals for biosorption sites.

## Multi-Metal Competitive Biosorption

Toxic metals can be removed from aqueous mixtures containing copper,
nickel, and zinc in the presence of existing co-cations such as calcium
and sodium ions (Zouboulis et al., 1999). In multimetal ternary systems
using R. arrhizus in batch-stirred reactors, the adsorption capacities
displayed the preferential order Pb(II) > Ni(II) > Cu(II) at pH 5.0
(Sag et al., 2000c). Experimental and model-predicted data of lead,
nickel, and copper concentration profiles have been compared in ternary
systems on R. arrhizus in a continuous-flow stirred tank reactor (CFST)
under optimum operating conditions (Sag et al., 2000b). The equilibrium
uptake was 48.79 mg Pb(II)/g, 25.78 Cu(II)/g, and 41.44 mg Ni(II)/g.
Nearly 50% removal of cadmium, nickel, and zinc occurred for 8, 7, and
8 bed volumes, respectively, using immobilized Mucor rouxii in a
polysulfone matrix (Yan and Viraraghavan, 2001).

## White-Rot Fungi and Metal Tolerance

White-rot fungi exposed to nickel and zinc trigger defense mechanisms to
deter ill effects on metabolism. Heavy metals cause a reduction in growth
followed by changes in morphology. Metals cause oxidative damage to
proteins by binding to amino acid residues in enzymes. In white-rot
fungi, extracellular enzymes are affected at the transcriptional and
translational levels. Low levels of essential metals are necessary for
the production of ligninolytic enzymes. Manganese plays an important
role in the expression of manganese peroxidase and laccase, while copper
serves as a cofactor for laccase. The interplay between metal tolerance
and enzyme production affects the overall remediation capability of
white-rot fungi in metal-contaminated environments.

## Oxalic Acid and Metal Leaching

Oxalic acid is a leaching agent for a variety of metals including zinc,
forming soluble metal oxalate complexes. Metal oxalates are produced by
a wide range of fungi, including mycorrhizas and [[lichenicolous-fungi]].
The white-rot fungi Bjerkandera fumosa, Phlebia radiata, and Trametes
versicolor and the brown-rot fungus Fomitopsis pinicola produce oxalate
crystals on ZnO substrates. One-third of the isolates of soil fungi are
able to solubilize at least one toxic metal compound including ZnO, and
10% solubilize all three tested metal compounds (Sayer et al., 1995).
This fungal leaching capability has implications for both metal recovery
and soil bioremediation strategies.

## Bioreactor Applications

Continuous-flow stirred tank reactors can handle large concentrations of
multimetal solutions with greater biosorption yields and are applicable
for the continuous treatment of large quantities of wastewaters.
Crossflow filtration can be used to remove heavy metal ions from water
using yeast cells as carriers. In the presence of binary or ternary
mixtures, rejection of individual metals is generally reduced, except
for lead. The economic viability of nickel and zinc biosorption is
enhanced by the availability of fungal biomass as a waste by-product of
large-scale industrial fermentation processes.
