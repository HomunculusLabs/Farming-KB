# Animal Days and Forage Measurement

In [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]],
accurate measurement of forage consumption is essential to effective
[[savory-livestock-grazing-planning]]. The system relies
on the concept of Animal Days (AD) and Animal Days per Acre (ADA), which
provide a practical language for planning, monitoring, and adjusting
grazing operations across diverse environments and conditions.

## The Animal Day Concept

Given unfettered access to good grazing, animals will eat a predictable
amount in a day, depending on their size, weight, reproductive status, and
other factors. In feedlots and confined situations, managers easily compute
the amount of feed needed to carry a given number of animals for a given
number of days.

The common American practice of basing grazing fees on Animal Unit Months
(AUMs) parallels this relationship, but the AUM is too crude a figure for
detailed and well-planned grazing. Instead, Savory uses the Animal Day. An
Animal Day can be based on any animal type: cow, sheep, donkey, or other
stock.

You can think of an Animal Day as the forage one animal harvests in a day.
Ignoring the volume of forage present, the amount animals take from a
given paddock depends on two factors:

1. The number of animals
2. The number of days of grazing

## Computing Total Animal Days

The calculation is straightforward: multiply the number of animals by the
number of days they graze in a paddock. For example, 100 animals grazing
for 5 days equals 500 animal days. Given adequate and similar forage, an
equal number of animals will harvest the same amount in the same time
regardless of paddock size.

## Animal Days Per Acre (ADA)

Naturally, equal treatment depletes a smaller paddock more than a larger
one. To compare grazing pressure across paddocks of different sizes, divide
total animal days by the acreage. If 500 animal days are taken from an
83-acre paddock, the ADA is 6.0. If the same 500 animal days are taken
from a 250-acre paddock, the ADA is only 2.0.

ADA shows the amount of forage that each acre of land will have to render
on average. This metric enables managers to compare grazing pressure across
paddocks of varying size and to plan future operations with precision.

## Applications of ADA

The grazing aspects of holistic management require a thorough understanding
of ADA and how to use them. Since Andre Voisin first developed the concept
in his pasture work, Savory and others have found ways to apply it in many
contexts:

- Planning [[savory-drought-planning-and-reserves]]
- Setting [[savory-stock-density-vs-stocking-rate]]
- Reducing competition with [[savory-wildlife-livestock-integration]]
- Relating past grazing pressure to present forage condition
- Planning future operations in complex situations

Its most powerful application comes in planning future operations. In the
biological planning process, computing the ADA allows managers to foresee
grazing needs at all times of the year and decide which paddocks will
supply them.

## More Paddocks, Higher Daily Impact

Increasing the number of paddocks does not change the total grazing
pressure on a grazing cell. In general, only a change in the number of
animals or the time they spend in a cell will change the total forage
harvested. However, as paddock size decreases, the daily ADA per acre
increases dramatically.

In a six-paddock cell with 300 animals, about 0.9 animal days per acre
are consumed each day the herd remains in a paddock. In a sixty-one-paddock
cell, 9.1 animal days per acre are consumed each day. One extra day in the
smaller paddock takes ten times as much from each acre as an extra day in
the bigger paddock.

This means a twenty-four-hour mistake in a small-paddock cell could mean
extreme depletion of forage, loss of selectivity, and a drop in animal
performance. Management discipline becomes more critical with more
paddocks, not less.

## Relationship to Stock Density

Stock density, which reflects the concentration of animals on a unit of
land at a given moment, is directly related to ADA calculations. As
paddock size decreases with a constant herd size, stock density increases.
Higher stock density generally increases [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]], while shorter time on the land reduces
[[savory-holistic-resource-management-overgrazing]] risk.

Remember that stock density always equals the amount of forage taken from a
paddock divided by the paddock area. As paddock size decreases, stock
density increases, causing better distribution of grazing, dunging, and
trampling.

## Standard Animal Units

While the basic Animal Day provides a workable measure, more accurate
measurements called Standard Animal Units can be used to allow for the
weight and growth rate required or breeding state of the animals. These
refinements are addressed in Savory's companion planning materials.

## Planning with ADA

The biological planning process uses ADA to plan grazing needs at all
times of the year. Managers can know in advance if the land can provide
the forage needed. They can plan for enough margin to feed wildlife and
buffer the risk of drought. They can plan, and later evaluate, the
performance of every paddock and make allowances for conflicting uses,
poison plant seasons, winter cover, and many other considerations related
to production and landscape goals.
