# [[permaculture]] Goat Raising
Goats are exceptionally good at clearing pasture and can effectively handle the toughest brambles and unwanted vegetation in a [[permaculture-design]] system. They are kept primarily for milk and meat production.
## Role in the System
Goats clear the toughest unwanted vegetation and can be temporarily used by penning them or tying them with a halter, moving them from place to place. However, they can be so destructive that keeping more than one goat per family member is unnecessary. If using lactating goats to clear pasture, supplement with grain to maintain milk production.
## Housing and Fencing
- Fence at least 4.5 feet (1.4 meters) high
- Quarter acre (0.1 hectare) per goat
- Wrap trees with chicken wire to prevent bark stripping
- No gaps wider than 8 inches (20 cm); if goats can't see through, they won't try to escape
- Goats can unlock standard latches with their tongue -- padlocks may be necessary
- If a goat tries to escape, use a Y-shaped yoke temporarily
- Housing: any sturdy three-sided shed in their pasture, or 36 square feet (3.3 square meters) per goat in a barn with clean hay bedding
## Feeding
Each goat needs about 4-5 pounds (1.8-2.3 kg) of hay per day of mixed grass and legumes such as permaculture-composting|alfalfa -- about half a bale per 10 goats, twice a day. Goats on overgrown pasture will eat less hay. They also need salt and water at all times. Goats won't lick a salt block, so provide loose mineralized salt.
Goats themselves control their own worms by eating higher leaves first and working their way down, wandering far distances over pasture. The tree method of [[permaculture-pasture-management]] management is particularly valuable for them. Rotate to new pasture every three weeks (the lifespan of a stomach worm). Keep them in very clean conditions with fresh water always available.
### Poisonous Plants to Avoid
Milkweed, [[nightshade-plants-entheogen]], buckthorn, cowbane, dog's mercury, foxglove, greater celandine, hemlock trees, henbane, ragwort, rhododendron, rhubarb leaves, spindle, water dropwort, yew, iris, azalea, beet leaves, evergreen trees.
## Breeding
### Breeding Schedule
- Start October 1st: feed breeding does a quarter pound of grain per day
- Increase quarter pound per week until November 1st (1 pound/day total)
- This increases chances of twins and triplets
- Taper off grain starting December 15th for healthy older does
- Bring grain back up February 15th (six weeks before kidding)
- First-time breeders keep eating grain throughout
Breed does 149 days (5 months) before you want kids. Ideal kidding: April 1st, so breed November 1st. Don't breed does under 70 pounds or younger than 2 years. One buck can service 50 does (30 are easier to handle). Does show heat by sniffing, tail-wagging toward the buck pen, and making noise.
## Kidding
When a doe is close to kidding, check every morning. Provide a barn floor with dry bedding. Clean the kid's nose and mouth at birth, otherwise leave her alone unless: water breaks with no progress after 2 hours, great pain with nothing after 30 minutes, or total exhaustion with nothing after 15 minutes.
Kids should stand up right away and nurse within 15 minutes. If they can't, hold the teat in their mouth. If they still won't suck, squirt milk in their mouth and try again in 3-4 hours. Never feed a kid cold milk. Don't keep a kid away from the mother for more than 6 hours or she may reject it. Keep the family separate from the herd for 3-5 days.
## Milking
An average doe produces about 3 quarts of milk per day (1.5 quarts goes to kids, 1.5 for you). Stainless steel seamless equipment is best. Check for mastitis weekly: strange milk texture (flakes, lumps, strings), tumors, hard areas, or abscesses. To prevent "goaty" taste, keep a pan of baking soda full in the feed trough. Any goat eating grain should have baking soda to avoid acidosis.
## Goat Health and Common Diseases
Goats are generally hardy animals, but they are susceptible to several conditions that require regular monitoring and preventive care.
Vaccination essentials:
- CD-T vaccine (Clostridium perfringens types C and D plus tetanus): give to all goats annually; especially critical for kids
- Pneumonia vaccine: recommended in herds with respiratory disease history or in wet, [[permaculture-cold-climate-strategies]]s
- Caseous lymphadenitis (CL) and sore mouth vaccines: situational based on herd risk
Parasite management is the single largest health challenge for goat keepers. Goats browse rather than graze, and their feeding behavior (eating upward from ground level) naturally reduces parasite exposure compared to sheep or cattle. However, heavy stocking and warm, wet conditions can still cause dangerous worm burdens.
Parasite control strategies:
- Practice rotational grazing with a minimum 3-week rest period between paddocks
- Use FAMACHA scoring to identify anemic goats needing treatment rather than blanket deworming
- Rotate dewormer classes (benzimidazoles, macrocyclic lactones, imidazothiazoles) to prevent resistance
- Provide clean water and minimize fecal contamination of feed and water sources
- Copper oxide wire particles can help reduce barber pole worm (Haemonchus contortus) loads
Common diseases and conditions:
- CAE (Caprine Arthritis Encephalitis): viral disease spread through milk; test all new goats and feed kids heat-treated colostrum or pasteurized milk
- CL (Caseous Lymphadenitis): bacterial infection causing abscesses; quarantine affected animals and maintain closed herds
- Mastitis: check udders at every milking; hot, swollen udders or abnormal milk require immediate attention
- Enterotoxemia (overeating disease): prevent through proper vaccination and avoiding abrupt feed changes
- Urinary calculi: blockage in male goats caused by excess phosphorus; balance calcium-to-phosphorus ratio in feed (2:1 ideal)
- Foot scald and footrot: common in wet conditions; trim hooves regularly and provide well-drained walking surfaces
## Cheese Making
Goat milk cheese (chevre) is one of the most rewarding and profitable products a small-scale goat keeper can produce. Goat milk has smaller fat globules than cow milk, making it naturally homogenized and easier to digest.
Fresh chevre (simple farm cheese):
- Heat 1 gallon of goat milk to 86°F (30°C), remove from heat
- Add 1 packet mesophilic starter culture (or 2 tablespoons cultured buttermilk), stir gently, cover, and let sit 1 hour at room temperature
- Add 2-4 drops of liquid rennet diluted in 1/4 cup cool water, stir gently for 30 seconds, cover, and let set 12-18 hours at 72°F (22°C)
- When curds have separated from whey (clean break), ladle curds into a cheesecloth-lined colander, drain for 6-12 hours
- Add salt to taste (start with 1 teaspoon); mix in fresh or dried herbs if desired
- Stores refrigerated for 2 weeks
Aged goat cheeses:
- Feta-style: press fresh curds, brine in a salt solution for 4-8 weeks
- Chevres coated in ash, herbs, or wax age for 1-3 months and develop complex flavors
- Hard cheeses like gouda or cheddar can be made from goat milk but require a press and aging cave or refrigerator
Profit potential:
- One gallon of goat milk yields approximately 1-1.5 pounds of fresh chevre
- Farm-fresh goat cheese sells for $12-20 per pound at farmers markets
- A single doe producing 3 quarts per day generates roughly 2-3 pounds of cheese per week during lactation
## Fiber Production
While dairy and meat are the primary reasons for keeping goats, fiber breeds offer an additional yield that integrates well with a diversified permaculture homestead.
Fiber goat breeds:
- Angora goats: produce mohair, a lustrous, strong fiber; sheared twice per year (spring and fall); adult goats produce 4-8 pounds of mohair per shearing; kid mohair is the finest and most valuable
- Pygora goats: a Pygmy-Angora cross producing three fleece types (type A resembles mohair, type B is a blend, type C resembles cashmere); excellent for small-scale fiber production
- Cashmere goats: not a distinct breed but any goat carrying the cashmere gene; downy undercoat is combed out during spring molt; yields 4-6 ounces of raw cashmere per goat per year
Fiber processing steps:
- Shear or comb fiber in spring when the winter coat naturally loosens
- Skirt the fleece to remove debris, short fibers, and stained sections
- Wash in hot water with mild detergent; rinse thoroughly and dry flat on a screen
- Card or comb the fiber to align fibers for spinning
- Spin into yarn using a drop spindle or spinning wheel
- Handspun goat fiber yarn sells for $20-40 per skein at fiber festivals and craft markets
