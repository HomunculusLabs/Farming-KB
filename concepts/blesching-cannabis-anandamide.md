# Anandamide and Endogenous Cannabinoids

Anandamide (N-arachidonoylethanolamine or AEA) is the primary endogenous
cannabinoid, meaning it is a cannabinoid-like molecule produced naturally
within the human body. Along with 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG), anandamide
serves as the body's own cannabis-like signaling molecule, binding to and
activating cannabinoid receptors throughout the nervous and immune systems.

## Overview

The discovery of anandamide in 1992 by Raphael Mechoulam and his colleagues
at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem represented a landmark in neuroscience
and pharmacology. The identification of this endogenous cannabinoid molecule
provided a biological explanation for the existence of cannabinoid receptors
in the human body: receptors exist because the body produces its own
cannabinoid-like compounds. This discovery fundamentally transformed the
understanding of the endocannabinoid system and its role in human health and
disease.

Anandamide is sometimes referred to as the bliss molecule due to its role in
regulating mood, pleasure, and emotional well-being. It binds primarily to
CB1 receptors in the central nervous system, where it modulates
neurotransmitter release, pain perception, appetite, memory, and emotional
responses. Anandamide levels are influenced by physical exercise (contributing
to the phenomenon known as runner's high), social bonding, meditation, and
other activities associated with positive psychological states.

The name anandamide is derived from the Sanskrit word ananda, meaning
joy, bliss, or delight. This etymology reflects the molecule's association
with positive emotional states and its role in the neurobiology of happiness
and well-being. Anandamide is synthesized on demand in response to cellular
needs and is rapidly degraded by the enzyme fatty acid amide hydrolase
(FAAH), which means its effects are typically short-lived and localized.

## Anandamide Biochemistry and Function

Anandamide belongs to a class of lipid signaling molecules known as
N-acylethanolamines. It is synthesized from the membrane phospholipid
N-arachidonoyl-phosphatidylethanolamine through a multi-step enzymatic
process. Unlike classical neurotransmitters that are stored in synaptic
vesicles and released in response to electrical signals, anandamide is
synthesized and released on demand, making it a retrograde signaling
molecule that travels backward across the synapse to regulate
neurotransmitter release.

The rapid degradation of anandamide by fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH)
serves as an important regulatory mechanism. When FAAH activity is high,
anandamide is quickly broken down, resulting in lower endocannabinoid tone.
When FAAH activity is low, anandamide persists longer in the synaptic space,
producing enhanced endocannabinoid signaling. Genetic variations in FAAH
enzymes can produce significant inter-individual differences in endocannabinoid
tone, which may explain why some people are naturally more resistant to stress
and anxiety while others are more vulnerable.

Anandamide plays a critical role in several physiological processes including
pain modulation, appetite regulation, memory formation, mood stabilization,
and the sleep-wake cycle. It also contributes to neuroprotection,
embryonic development, and immune function. The broad range of physiological
processes influenced by anandamide helps explain why cannabis, which mimics
anandamide by binding to the same receptors, has such diverse therapeutic
applications.

## 2-AG: The Second Endogenous Cannabinoid

2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) was identified as an endogenous cannabinoid in
1995, three years after the discovery of anandamide. While anandamide tends
to receive more public attention, 2-AG is present at significantly higher
concentrations in the brain and body and may be responsible for the majority
of CB1 and CB2 receptor activation under physiological conditions.

2-AG is a full agonist at both CB1 and CB2 receptors, meaning it produces
maximal receptor activation, while anandamide is a partial agonist with
lower efficacy. 2-AG is degraded by monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) rather
than FAAH, and this different degradation pathway provides an additional
point of therapeutic intervention. Inhibitors of MAGL can elevate 2-AG levels
and have shown promise in preclinical models of pain, inflammation, and
neurodegenerative disease.

The interplay between anandamide and 2-AG in the endocannabinoid system
creates a complex signaling network with built-in redundancy and
fine-tuning. While both molecules activate the same receptor types, they
are produced in different cellular contexts, degraded by different enzymes,
and may serve somewhat different functional roles. Together, they maintain
endocannabinoid tone, the baseline level of endocannabinoid signaling that
is critical for homeostatic balance.

## Cannabis and Endogenous Cannabinoid Interaction

When cannabis is consumed, its phytocannabinoids interact with the same
receptors that endogenous cannabinoids normally activate. THC binds directly
to CB1 and CB2 receptors, mimicking and amplifying the effects of anandamide
and 2-AG. CBD does not bind directly to cannabinoid receptors but instead
modulates their activity and inhibits the degradation of anandamide by FAAH,
effectively increasing endogenous anandamide levels. This mechanism of action
helps explain why CBD produces therapeutic effects without psychoactivity
and why it can modulate the effects of THC.
