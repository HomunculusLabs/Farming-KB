# Mycoparasitic Fungi as Biocontrol Agents

Mycoparasitic fungi — fungi that parasitize other fungi — represent a major class of [[mushroom-cultivation-fundamentals]] agents used against plant-pathogenic fungi. Approximately 2,175 species of fungicolous fungi are known across all major fungal groups, with biocontrol applications documented for species in the Hypocreales, Zoopagales, Tremellales, and various mitosporic genera. This diversity makes fungicolous fungi one of the most important functional groups for biological disease management in agriculture.

## Types of Mycoparasitism

### Necrotrophic Mycoparasites
Necrotrophic mycoparasites kill host cells before or during nutrient absorption. They are relatively unspecialized and tend to have a broader host range than biotrophs. Two principal necrotrophic interfaces exist:

1. **Contact necrotrophic**: The parasite damages the host through [[fungal-mycoparasitism-and-biocontrol-interactions]] without penetrating host mycelium. Examples include Arthrobotrys superba and Tilletiopsis species, which contact and kill cells of powdery mildews.

2. **Invasive necrotrophic**: Hyphae penetrate the host cell wall and show considerable growth within host hyphae. Examples include Talaromyces flavus, which aggressively parasitizes sclerotia of Verticillium dahliae, producing the enzyme glucose oxidase that releases hydrogen peroxide toxic to the pathogen.

### Biotrophic Mycoparasites
Biotrophs maintain a balanced relationship with living host cells without causing immediate harm. They often form specialized infection structures:

- **Haustorial biotrophs**: Species in the Zoopagales and Dimargaritales form haustoria that penetrate host cells. Piptocephalis species form small enucleate haustoria delimited from host cytoplasm by electron-transparent wall material. Some species stimulate host growth (mutualistic relationship), while others inhibit it.
- **Biotrophic contact parasites**: Gonatobotrys simplex grows on Alternaria and Cladosporium, contacting hosts via fingerlike branches. Melanospora zamiae can withdraw all required nutrients from washed host mycelium.
- **Mycotrophein-dependent species**: Some biotrophs require mycotrophein (a mixture of tetraethyleneglycol and pentaethyleneglycol mono-nonylphenyl ethers) found in fungal extracts for axenic growth.

### Mixed Strategies
Many species shift between biotrophic and necrotrophic modes. Hypomyces chrysospermus causes necrosis in mushroom hosts but can grow biotrophically inside cells of Botrytis cinerea. Pythium oligandrum develops different contact structures on different hosts and plays a dual role as both mycoparasite and "secondary sugar fungus" that profits from cellulolytic activity of other fungi.

## Key Biocontrol Genera

### Trichoderma Species
Trichoderma is the most commercially important genus of mycoparasitic biocontrol agents. T. harzianum and T. virens are widely deployed against soil-borne pathogens:

- **Trichoderma harzianum**: Interacts with hosts through parasitic contact, direct cell penetration, and production of diffusible toxic agents. It efficiently suppresses Pythium ultimum in cotton fields and attacks sclerotia of Sclerotinia sclerotiorum. Strains beneficial as biocontrol agents are microscopically indistinguishable from aggressive competitors of cultivated mushrooms.
- **Trichoderma virens**: Produces gliotoxin, a highly specific fungitoxic compound. Efficiently suppresses Pythium ultimum and destroys sclerotia of Sclerotinia. T. virens does little harm to arbuscular mycorrhizal Glomales, unlike T. harzianum which can damage them.
- **Secondary metabolites**: Trichoderma species produce diverse secondary metabolites including peptaibols (potent polypeptide antibiotics) and volatile alkyl pyrones that antagonize host fungi, causing cell vacuolization, collapse, and cytoplasmic disintegration.

### Clonostachys rosea (Gliocladium rosea)
A ubiquitous mycoparasite whose hyphae penetrate and destroy many host fungi including Mucorales, Ceratocystis fimbriata, and numerous sclerotial pathogens. C. rosea f. catenulata is an efficient destructive parasite of Sclerotinia sclerotiorum and several Fusarium species, contacting hosts with pseudoappressoria. It is among the most efficient parasites of Phymatotrichopsis omnivora sclerotia buried deep in soil.

### Coniothyrium minitans
The most common and best-studied parasite of Sclerotinia species, distributed worldwide. It does not form appressoria but significantly inhibits S. sclerotiorum on nutrient-poor media. When applied in combination with Talaromyces flavus, it achieves nearly equivalent disease suppression to either agent alone, demonstrating the value of multi-agent biocontrol strategies.

### Lecanicillium (Verticillium sect. Prostrata)
Species in this genus exhibit high levels of chitinase activity and have great potential as entomogenous, nematophagous, and fungicolous biocontrol agents. They interact with Rhizoctonia solani hyphae through appressed growth, coiling, and sometimes penetration.

### Laetisaria arvalis
A sclerotial, strand-forming basidiomycete that is a potent biocontrol agent against Rhizoctonia solani and Pythium ultimum. It suppresses R. solani growth by hyphal interference and competition for nutrients and space.

## Control of Sclerotial Pathogens

Sclerotia of soil-borne ascomycetes and basidiomycetes provide rich nutrient sources for mycoparasites. Key biocontrol agents targeting sclerotia include:

| Agent | Target Pathogen | Mechanism |
|-------|----------------|-----------|
| Coniothyrium minitans | Sclerotinia sclerotiorum | Pycnidial infection, no appressoria |
| Sporidesmium sclerotivorum | Sclerotinia species | Branched haustoria, specialized biotroph |
| Talaromyces flavus | Verticillium dahliae | Glucose oxidase (H2O2), glucanase, chitinase |
| Clonostachys rosea | Multiple sclerotial fungi | Hyphal penetration, pseudoappressoria |
| Trichoderma virens | Sclerotinia, Verticillium | Mycolytic enzymes, secondary metabolites |
| Dictyosporium elegans | Sclerotinia sclerotiorum | Active in Australian field conditions |
| Paecilomyces lilacinus | Aspergillus spp., Sclerotinia | Colonizes and shortens sclerotial survival |

Sporidesmium sclerotivorum, Teratosperma oligocladum, and Laterispora breviramosa are specialized biotrophic hyphomycetes that penetrate host sclerotial cells via branched haustoria. None infects sclerotial fungi in other families, demonstrating a degree of host specificity unusual among sclerotial parasites.

## Control of Soil-Borne Oomycetes and Basidiomycetes

Soil-borne Pythium and Phytophthora species are affected by numerous antagonistic fungi:

- **Trichoderma virens** efficiently suppresses Pythium ultimum in cotton fields
- **Geomyces pannorum** varieties antagonize Pythium ultimum in cucumber cultivation
- **Pythium oligandrum** attacks Rhizoctonia solani, suppressing its cellulolytic activity and sclerotium formation
- **Schizophyllum commune** is a potent mycoparasite of many plant-pathogenic fungi, coiling around and penetrating host hyphae before destroying them
- **Penicillium glabrum** has antifungal substances rendering it suitable as a biocontrol agent against Monilinia laxa peach twig blight

Oospores of Pythium and Phytophthora, because of their persistence in soil, are a special substratum for diverse mycoparasites. Conidial fungi of Dactylella and Trinacrium have been described from Pythium oospores, and Microdochium fusarioides was found in Phytophthora syringae oospores.

## Effects on Mycorrhizal Fungi

Resting spores of arbuscular mycorrhizal Glomales are particularly susceptible to necrotrophic parasites. Large proportions of field-extracted spores are parasitized and nonviable, their walls perforated by fine radial canals. Both amoebae and fungi create these pores. Heavy infestation with Phlyctochytrium can reduce soil populations of Glomus macrocarpum and Gigaspora gigantea. This parasitism may limit mycorrhizal populations in wet soils, with implications for [[mycorrhizal-biocontrol]] and plant nutrition.

## Practical Considerations

The biocontrol efficacy of mycoparasitic fungi depends on environmental conditions (soil temperature, moisture, organic matter), formulation type, application timing, and compatibility with other management practices. Some agents (e.g., Trichoderma harzianum) can damage beneficial mycorrhizal fungi, necessitating careful strain selection. The distinction between biocontrol strains and mushroom competitors of the same species is often impossible without molecular markers, creating regulatory and quality-control challenges for commercial products.
