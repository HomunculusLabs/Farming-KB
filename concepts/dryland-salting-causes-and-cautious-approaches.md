# Dryland Salting — Causes and Cautious Approaches

## Overview

[[cyclic-salt]] deposition amounts to 27–150 kg per hectare per year.

Over immense periods — roughly 100,000 years at an average deposition rate of 50 kg/ha/year — this steady rainwater input built up the extraordinary salt reserves found in Western Australian soils. Soils formed over granites and gneiss in Western Australia hold between 3,400 and 19,000 tonnes of salt per hectare to a depth of 20 metres.

### Cyclic Salt and Connate Salt

Under natural forested conditions, this salt remains locked deep in the soil profile. Stream salt loss in undisturbed catchments roughly equals rainfall input — a balanced system known as "cyclic salt." The salts arrive via rain and are carried away by streams in roughly equivalent quantities.

However, once the land is cleared, the equation changes dramatically. Farmed areas discharge salt at rates approximately 20 times higher than the rainfall input. This enormous increase is the result of connate (ancient, trapped) salt being mobilised by rising groundwater tables that follow deforestation. The cyclic salt input is minor by comparison to the geological salt reserves being unlocked.

## The Role of Forest Cover and Deforestation

Forested country is notably free from salting. Forests play a critical hydrological role: evergreen trees transpire significantly more water than annual crops, keeping the water table well below the surface throughout the year. Their deep root systems also promote superior rain infiltration compared to compacted agricultural soils, allowing rainfall to penetrate deeply without excess water accumulating above impermeable layers.

When forests are cleared and replaced with shallow-rooted crops and pastures, excess water percolates past the root zone, causing the water table to rise and progressively dissolve stored salts. As these salts move upward with the rising water table, they enter the root zone and eventually the soil surface.

### Deforestation Statistics

The scale of deforestation across affected regions reveals the severity of the problem:

- **Western Australia**: approximately 0.7% tree cover remaining
- **South Australia**: approximately 3% tree cover remaining
- **Victoria**: approximately 13% tree cover remaining
- **Ethiopia**: approximately 4% tree cover remaining

These figures closely correlate with the severity of dryland salting observed in each region. Agricultural clearing and industrial wood-chipping have effectively converted once-productive landscapes into expanding salt deserts.

## Surface Indicators and Progression of Salting

Salting announces itself through a predictable sequence of surface changes that astute land managers can learn to recognise early. Identifying these indicators before significant damage occurs is critical to successful intervention.

### Early Warning Signs

- **Vegetation shifts**: Brownish patches of sea barley grass (*[[soil-collapse]] can create sunken off-stream salt lakes — dramatic landscape features that represent the end stage of the salting process. Forests in drylands also excrete salts that accumulate in surface soils over time. This effect is further concentrated by soil collapse, which physically concentrates salts into depressions, accelerating the formation of barren salt pans.

## Evidence Summary — Five Key Points

The body of evidence on dryland salting can be summarised in five key observations:

1. **Soil collapse** is a primary mechanism that concentrates salts into surface depressions and off-stream lake beds, creating self-reinforcing saline zones.

2. **Deforested areas** are overwhelmingly the landscapes affected by dryland salting; forested country remains free from salting, demonstrating the protective role of tree cover.

3. **Trees are highly effective** at controlling water tables through transpiration and improving infiltration, making revegetation a cornerstone of salinity management.

4. **Interceptor banks** have shown promise in some conditions but remain insufficiently trialled, particularly in Victoria, where their effectiveness is not yet well established.

5. **The scale of the problem** exceeds the capacity of mechanical solutions like pumping and tile drainage, which work for wetland salting but cannot address the vast areas affected by dryland salting.

## Trees Versus Interceptor Banks

Different landscape conditions demand different responses. There is no single solution applicable to all situations.

### When Trees Alone May Suffice

Trees alone can be effective where salting is moderate and the primary mechanism is excess recharge caused by shallow-rooted vegetation replacing deep-rooted perennials. In these cases, re-establishing tree cover lowers the water table through transpiration and improves rainfall infiltration.

### When Interceptor Banks Are Needed

Interceptor banks — earthworks designed to intercept and redirect subsurface water flow before it reaches salt-affected areas — may be necessary where groundwater movement follows defined subsurface pathways or where the rate of salinisation is too rapid for biological solutions alone.

In Victoria, interceptor banks have not been widely trialled, leaving their effectiveness in many soil types unproven. The prudent approach is to assess site conditions carefully using backhoe pits and recognise that neither method is universally applicable.

## Cautious Approaches to Land Management

Given the severity and near-irreversibility of dryland salting once established, a cautious, incremental approach to land management is essential.

### Start Small and Monitor

Always begin with small trial systems. Monitor their effects carefully over time before extending interventions to larger areas. Rushing into large-scale earthworks or broadacre revegetation without site-specific data risks wasting resources or, worse, exacerbating the salting problem.

### Assess Before Acting

Use backhoe pits to examine local soil profiles, water table depths, and salt distribution before designing any intervention. This ground-truthing is far more valuable than relying on regional generalisations. Every site has unique geology, hydrology, and salt dynamics that must be understood before committing to a management strategy.

### Water Management Principles

Water management in salinity-prone landscapes requires a paradoxical awareness:

- **Store no runoff** in locations where tree roots cannot reach and transpire the stored water, as this will contribute directly to water table rise and salt mobilisation.

- **Lessen infiltration** where the groundwater table is already shallow — additional water only adds to the problem by raising the water table further.

- **Increase infiltration** where soils are free-draining and the water table is deep enough to safely absorb extra moisture without mobilising salt.

- **Use ridge trickle irrigation** and allow floodwaters to flush furrows periodically to prevent salt accumulation at the root zone.

- **Favour roof tanks and swales** over wells and bores. Surface water harvesting does not contribute to groundwater recharge, whereas extraction from bores can inadvertently alter groundwater dynamics.

### Work With Natural Yields

Rather than forcing conventional agricultural production on marginal or salting land, assess what the landscape can sustainably yield without contributing to degradation. Natural yield systems — such as managed populations of saiga antelope, kangaroo, or any of the approximately 140 large animal species of the African savannas — represent productive land uses compatible with fragile dryland environments.

Observe naturally high-yield patches already present in the landscape. These areas serve as living indicators of safe, sustainable approaches that can be studied and extended cautiously to surrounding areas.

## Critical Strategic Priorities

The three most urgent strategies for addressing dryland salting at a landscape scale are:

1. **Forbid further clearing** of remaining tree cover. Every hectare of forest retained is a hectare protected from future salting. This is the single most important preventative measure.

2. **Trial interceptor banks** in representative landscape positions to build a local evidence base for their effectiveness under specific conditions.

3. **Revegetate uplands** as a priority. Upland clearing is often the source of the excess recharge that drives lowland salting, so strategic upland revegetation can protect large areas downstream.

These measures, combined with the cautious site-specific approaches outlined above, represent the most responsible and permaculture-aligned path forward for managing and reversing dryland salting.
