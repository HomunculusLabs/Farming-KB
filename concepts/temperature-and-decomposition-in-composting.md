# Temperature and Decomposition in Composting

Temperature is the single most important variable controlling the speed and quality of compost decomposition. The relationship between temperature and decomposition rate is not linear — it is geometric. Understanding this relationship, and managing the temperatures your compost heap reaches, is the difference between producing potent, nutrient-rich compost and creating a pile of partially decomposed waste that loses most of its value.

## The 10°F (5°C) Rule

The fundamental law of decomposition: **for every 10°F increase in temperature, the speed of the reaction approximately doubles.**

### Geometric, Not Linear

This increase is geometric, not linear:
- At 60°F: speed = 1x
- At 70°F: speed = 2x
- At 80°F: speed = 4x
- At 90°F: speed = 8x
- At 100°F: speed = 16x
- At 110°F: speed = 32x
- At 120°F: speed = 64x
- At 130°F: speed = 128x
- At 140°F: speed = 256x

A compost heap at 130°F decomposes **256 times faster** than one at 60°F. This is why hot composting works and cold composting doesn't.

## Organic Enzyme Temperature Limits

### Lower Limit

Most organic enzymes become progressively less active below about 50°F (10°C). Below freezing, decomposition essentially stops.

### Upper Limit

Most organic enzymes are destroyed at around 120°F (50°C). A few thermophilic enzymes — like those found in a hot compost heap — can handle temperatures as high as 155°F (68°C).

### The Danger Zone: Above 155°F (68°C)

Above this temperature, enzymatic reactions slow dramatically and then stop. The heap may continue to be hot from residual biological activity, but the quality of decomposition degrades:

- Nutrients are lost as gases (ammonia, methane, [[nitrous-oxide]])
- The remaining material is essentially humus — depleted of the nutrients that would have fertilized plants
- Research shows conclusively that heaps heated above 150°F (65°C) lose the bulk of their nutrients

## Optimal Temperature Ranges

### For Maximum Potency: 125-135°F (52-57°C)

This range produces the most nutrient-rich compost. Decomposition is fast (128-256x baseline rate) but not so fast that nutrients are lost as gases. Most organic enzymes are still active. Microbial diversity is high.

### For Fast Decomposition: 135-150°F (57-65°C)

Faster decomposition but some nutrient loss begins. Acceptable if speed is more important than maximum potency.

### Too Cool: Below 110°F (43°C)

Decomposition is slow. The heap may sit for months without finishing. Nutrients are conserved but may not be released in a useful timeframe.

### Too Hot: Above 155°F (68°C)

Significant nutrient loss. Compost will be mostly humus with low fertilizing value.

## How Temperature Affects Different Composting Methods

### Mulch Decomposition

Mulched soil might be no more than 75°F (24°C) on average. Decomposition is slow at that temperature. A six-inch (15 cm) layer of hay or grain straw might take a year to decompose [[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]] because:

- Low average temperature
- Only the material in direct contact with soil decomposes at significant speed

### Manure Decomposition

If the same hay passes through a cow's gut first, the process is completed in only a few months, even in chilly spring soil. Why?

- The material is thoroughly mixed with digestive enzymes
- Inoculated with intestinal microorganisms
- In close contact with the soil
- The C:N is much lower (manure vs. raw vegetation)

### Heap Composting

By concentrating materials and managing moisture and aeration, heap composting achieves temperatures in the optimal range (125-135°F), completing decomposition in weeks to months rather than years.

## Climate Effects on Compost Temperature

### Maritime and Cool-Summer Climates

Soils never get very warm. The release of nutrients from compost depends on soil temperature. In these climates:

- You need larger quantities of more potent compost
- Spring and early summer gardens tend to get going slowly
- COF or well-decomposed manure should supplement compost for high-demand crops

### Hot Climates

[[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] rots quickly. In really warm soil:

- Compost nutrients are released rapidly
- You can get acceptable results growing high-demand vegetables with only medium-quality compost
- Risk of over-decomposition — organic matter may disappear too fast

### Temperate Climates

The balanced approach: compost is reliable and predictable. Standard methods and [[compost-tea-application-rates]] work well.

### Cold-Winter Climates

- Autumn-built heaps may freeze and stop working during winter
- When they thaw in spring, wait a month, turn, and moisten
- The heap should promptly heat back up
- May need three turns instead of two

## Managing Temperature in Your Heap

### If Too Cool

- Check moisture — dry heaps don't heat
- Check C:N — too high means insufficient nitrogen
- Check size — heaps under 3 feet wide may not reach [[critical-mass]]
- When you turn the heap, blend in more strong stuff (manure, seedmeal)

### If Too Hot

- This is uncommon in home composting
- Usually caused by too much low C:N material (fresh manure, seedmeal)
- The heap will cool naturally as the nitrogen source is consumed
- Don't add water to cool it — this just creates [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]]

### The Hand Test

Push your hand deep into the heap:
- **Uncomfortably hot but tolerable for 5-10 seconds**: About right (125-135°F)
- **So hot you must pull your hand out immediately**: Too hot (>155°F)
- **Warm but not uncomfortable**: Too cool — needs more nitrogen or moisture

## Temperature and Nutrient Loss

### Ammonia Volatilization

At high temperatures, proteins break down into ammonia gas. Without soil in the heap to capture the ammonia (converting it to nitrates), this nitrogen escapes to the atmosphere permanently.
