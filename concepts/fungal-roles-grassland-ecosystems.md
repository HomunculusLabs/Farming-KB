Fungal Roles in Grassland Ecosystems

Fungi play fundamentally different roles in grassland ecosystems compared
to forests, reflecting differences in organic matter inputs, soil structure,
mycorrhizal associations, and decomposition pathways. In grasslands, the
dominant organic inputs come from belowground root turnover rather than
surface litter, creating distinct fungal communities and decomposition
dynamics.

Grassland Soil Structure and Organic Matter

In grassland ecosystems, the input of dead root tissue to deeper soil
layers is the most important organic input, while the addition of aerial
plant parts is relatively minor. This results in soil profiles where organic
matter is more intimately mixed with mineral soil, unlike the clearly defined
organic horizons (Ao, A) characteristic of forest soils. The absence of a
thick surface litter layer means that decomposition occurs primarily within
the soil matrix.

Where decomposition is rapid, as in agricultural soils and grasslands, a
mineral soil profile develops with low organic matter content that is
incorporated to a greater degree into the mineral component. The fertility
of grassland soils is closely linked to the rate of organic matter turnover,
with rapid decomposition producing nutrient-rich, mineral-associated organic
matter.

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Dominance

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) are the dominant mycorrhizal type in
temperate and tropical grasslands. AMF associations with grass roots are
ubiquitous and influence grassland productivity, species composition, and
nutrient cycling. In grassland ecosystems, AMF contribute to soil aggregation
through their extraradical hyphal networks and glomalin-related soil protein
production.

Griffiths et al. (1995) showed that mycorrhizal root colonization and soil
hyphal length increased linearly with decreasing phosphorus availability in
grassland soils. The conversion of pasture (with 90% stable macroaggregates)
to tilled tomato crop reduced aggregate stability, demonstrating the
importance of mycorrhizal fungal networks for soil structure maintenance.

Saprotrophic Fungal Communities

Grassland saprotrophic fungal communities differ from forest communities in
several important ways:

- Greater proportion of microfungi (ascomycetes and mitosporic fungi)
relative to basidiomycetes
- Higher fungal biomass per unit organic matter due to rapid root litter
  turnover
- Less diverse basidiomycete communities due to absence of woody litter
- Stronger seasonal fluctuations linked to root growth and dieback cycles

The diversity of litter types in grasslands is lower than in forests, with
dominant inputs from grass leaves, stems, and roots. This lower chemical
heterogeneity supports a less diverse decomposer community, but one that
is highly efficient at processing the dominant grass litter types.

Fungal Biomass and Hyphal Length Patterns

Kjoller and Struwe (1982) measured fungal hyphal length and biomass across
ecosystem types, finding that hyphal length is a more important fungal
investment in cooler environments and where resources are more recalcitrant,
while biomass with less hyphal extension is more important in warmer
environments with higher-quality resources.

In grasslands, fungal hyphal lengths typically fall between forest and
agricultural values, reflecting intermediate resource quality and
decomposition rates. The relationship between fungal investment strategy
(hyphal extension vs. biomass accumulation) and ecosystem conditions
suggests that grassland fungi balance exploration and exploitation
differently than their forest counterparts.

Nitrogen Cycling in Grasslands

Fungi play important roles in grassland nitrogen cycling through:
- Decomposition of root litter and soil organic matter
- Nitrogen immobilization in fungal biomass during periods of high
  resource availability
- Facilitation of nitrogen mineralization through enzyme production
- AMF-mediated nitrogen transfer among plants
- Contribution to nitrogen retention during periods of leaching potential

In semi-arid prairie meadows, fungal contributions to decomposition and
nutrient cycling are particularly important because bacterial activity is
limited by moisture stress. Fungi, with their ability to transport water
and nutrients through hyphal networks, maintain decomposition activity
under conditions that restrict bacterial growth.

Grassland Restoration and Fungi

Prairie restoration efforts have demonstrated the importance of fungal
communities for ecosystem recovery. Studies of changes in soil characters
under prairie restoration show that fungal biomass and hyphal length
increase as restored grasslands mature, contributing to improved soil
aggregation, nutrient retention, and plant community development.

The conversion of grassland to agricultural use typically reduces fungal
biomass by 50-80% and disrupts hyphal networks, reducing soil aggregate
stability and nutrient retention capacity. Recovery of fungal communities
following agricultural abandonment is a slow process that may take decades,
particularly for mycorrhizal associations.

Seasonal Dynamics

Grassland fungal communities show strong seasonal dynamics linked to plant
phenology. Root growth and dieback cycles create pulses of organic matter
input that drive fungal biomass fluctuations. Peak fungal biomass typically
coincides with periods of active root growth and moderate soil moisture.
Drought periods reduce fungal biomass and activity, while wet periods
stimulate both fungal growth and nutrient cycling.

The coupling of root production and fungal activity creates tight linkages
between aboveground plant productivity and belowground fungal processes in
grasslands, more so than in forest ecosystems where decomposition of
aboveground litter can be decoupled from current plant productivity.
