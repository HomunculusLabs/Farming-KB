# Effective Working Groups and the Troika System

## Overview

[[bill-mollison]] identifies effective group organization as one of the most critical — and most frequently botched — aspects of [[holmgren-community-design-and-intentional-communities]]. Drawing on decades of observation of communes, cooperatives, and ecovillages, he argues that most failures stem not from poor design of physical systems but from poor design of *social* systems. The troika system, group-size limits, and the principle of commonwork together form a coherent framework for building resilient, functional human organizations.

## The Troika System

The troika (or "committee of three") is the basic decision-making unit in Mollison's recommended governance model. Each essential function — food production, finance, building maintenance, education, water systems, and so on — is assigned to a **troika of one to three people** who hold full authority and responsibility for that domain.

### Core Principles

- **No boards of directors.** Boards aggregate power in a way that diffuses accountability and invites political maneuvering. The troika replaces the board entirely.
- **No consensus meetings.** Whole-group consensus works only for tiny groups making trivial decisions. For any function of consequence, the responsible troika decides. Others may advise but do not veto.
- **Overlapping membership.** One person may sit on two or three troikas, creating cross-links that keep the system integrated without requiring plenary meetings.
- **Rotational service.** Troika membership rotates on a regular cycle (commonly annually), preventing power concentration and spreading skills throughout the community.

The troika model abolishes the two most common forms of "meeting tyranny": the endless consensus process where one holdout can paralyze action, and the authoritarian board where a charismatic leader dominates. Both represent the same error — centralizing decisions that should be distributed.

## Group Size Dynamics

Mollison provides specific numeric ranges for functional group sizes, each tied to a different purpose:

| Size | Purpose | Notes |
|------|---------|-------|
| **1–3** | Decision-making | The troika range. Fast, accountable, personal. |
| **4–6** | Work groups | Effective for hands-on tasks. Communication stays direct. |
| **7–20** | Social gatherings | The Dunbar-adjacent range for friendship and celebration. Beyond this, social cohesion degrades. |
| **30–40** | Minimal viable community | Large enough to support division of labor and skill diversity. Small enough for everyone to know each other. |
| **200–300** | Genetic variability hamlet | The minimum population for long-term genetic health in an isolated settlement. |
| **300–600** | Identity limit | The largest group in which every member can recognize every other member. Beyond this, anonymity and alienation set in. |
| **1,000–5,000** | Bioregional scale | The appropriate scale for governance, trade networks, and shared infrastructure among linked hamlets. |

These ranges are not arbitrary. They correspond to well-documented thresholds in anthropology, genetics, and organizational psychology. Communities that ignore them — attempting, for example, to run a 30-person farm by consensus of all members — reliably fail.

## Volunteer Allotment vs. Rostered Maintenance

Mollison draws a sharp distinction between two types of community work:

- **Volunteer allotment:** Passion-driven, creative work that people choose because it aligns with their interests and skills. This includes teaching, gardening innovation, design work, and artistic contributions. Volunteer work thrives on autonomy and should never be mandated.
- **Rostered maintenance:** The unglamorous but essential tasks that keep systems running — cleaning, repairs, basic food processing, infrastructure upkeep. These should be scheduled on a roster, shared equitably, and treated as non-negotiable community obligations.

The error most communities make is trying to run *everything* by volunteer enthusiasm (which leads to burnout and resentment) or scheduling *everything* on a roster (which kills motivation and treats people as interchangeable labor units). The solution is to clearly distinguish between the two categories and manage each differently.

## Work-Netting, Not Networking

Mollison coins "work-netting" to distinguish productive interconnection from the superficial contact-trading of conventional networking. Work-netting means creating actual functional linkages between people's skills and needs — not collecting business cards or LinkedIn connections, but weaving a web of mutual obligation and exchange where every node performs real work.

In a [[permaculture]] community, work-netting manifests as: the builder who also teaches apprentices, the grower who supplies the kitchen, the accountant who maintains shared ledgers, the educator who coordinates with the food forester. Each person's portfolio occupation connects them to multiple troikas and multiple community members through concrete, useful relationships.

## Portfolio Occupations and Right Livelihood

Right livelihood — work that is ethical, ecologically sound, and personally fulfilling — is best achieved through **portfolio occupations** rather than single-career employment. A portfolio occupation combines several [[homesteading-income-streams]] and work roles:

- Primary productive work (growing food, building, making goods)
- Teaching or mentoring (passing on skills)
- Community service (troika responsibilities, rostered tasks)
- Creative or intellectual pursuits (writing, research, design)
- Trading or exchange (selling surplus, barter, [[mollison-local-currency-systems-and-community-banking]])

This diversification provides resilience against economic shocks, prevents the monotony of repetitive single-role work, and aligns with the balanced life principle. Portfolio occupations also make people more self-reliant and less dependent on any single employer or market.

## Commonwork

Commonwork is Mollison's term for the integrated system of productive and maintenance activities that sustain a community. It encompasses:

- The full range of tasks necessary for community survival and flourishing
- The organizational structures (troikas, work groups, rosters) that coordinate those tasks
- The ethical framework of right livelihood that gives the work meaning
- The skill-sharing and education systems that ensure continuity

Commonwork is to a community what a guild is to a garden — a set of mutually supporting relationships where each element contributes to and benefits from the whole. It is the social analog of permaculture's [[williams-pdc-ecological-design-principles]].

## The Balanced Life

Mollison insists that any community design must support a balanced life across three domains:

- **Physical:** Manual work, outdoor activity, bodily health, connection to land and seasons. Sedentary intellectual work must be balanced with hands-on productive labor.
- **Intellectual:** Learning, problem-solving, design, research. Communities need ongoing education and the freedom to pursue ideas.
- **Emotional:** Relationships, celebration, art, rest, solitude. The social and 7–20 person gathering range serves this domain.

Neglecting any of these three produces dysfunction. Communities that glorify physical labor breed resentment in intellectually oriented members. Communities that privilege intellectual work become disconnected from their landbase. Communities that emphasize social life to the exclusion of productive work cannot sustain themselves.

## Self-Defined vs. Employed States

Mollison challenges the conventional binary of employed/unemployed. In a properly designed community with commonwork and portfolio occupations, people exist in a **self-defined state** — they choose their mix of activities based on their skills, interests, and community needs, rather than being slotted into a job by an employer or rendered idle by lack of one.

The self-defined state eliminates the psychological damage of unemployment (loss of purpose, identity, social standing) and the alienation of wage labor (working solely for money at tasks one did not choose). It requires, however, that the community's economic design provide enough diversity of roles and enough basic security to make genuine choice possible.

## Primary Errors in Community Systems

Mollison catalogs the seven most common failures in intentional communities:

1. **Group consensus tyranny:** Attempting to make all decisions by full-group consensus, resulting in paralysis, factionalism, and the tyranny of the most obstinate member.
2. **Group leadership tyranny:** Concentrating power in a single charismatic leader or a dominant faction, creating dependency and eventual collapse when that leader departs or abuses authority.
3. **Lifestyle rules:** Imposing rigid codes of behavior, dress, diet, or belief that drive away diverse contributors and create a culture of conformity and surveillance.
4. **Large group households:** Housing more than 6–8 adults together in a single domestic unit, which reliably generates interpersonal conflict over cleanliness, noise, schedule, and privacy.
5. **Overscheduling:** Filling every hour with meetings, work sessions, and communal activities, leaving no time for solitude, spontaneous creativity, or unstructured social interaction.
6. **Neglect of income:** Assuming that good intentions and shared labor will somehow pay the bills, without designing realistic income streams or accounting for external costs.
7. **Poor accounting:** Failing to track resources, labor contributions, and financial flows with the same rigor applied to any viable enterprise, leading to hidden debts and eventual crisis.

Each of these errors can be prevented by applying the principles outlined above: troika governance, appropriate group sizing, clear distinction between volunteer and rostered work, portfolio occupations, and the balanced life framework.

## Key Takeaways

- Governance should be distributed through small troikas (1–3 people per function), not centralized in boards or consensus meetings.
- Group sizes must match their purpose — decision-making is not a plenary activity.
- Volunteer creative work and rostered maintenance work require different management approaches.
- Portfolio occupations provide resilience, diversity, and alignment with right livelihood.
- Commonwork integrates all productive and maintenance activities into a mutually supporting system.
- The balanced life demands attention to physical, intellectual, and emotional domains.
- The self-defined state replaces the employed/unemployed binary with genuine occupational choice.
- The seven primary community errors are all preventable through proper social design.
