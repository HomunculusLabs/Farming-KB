# Forest Succession and Food Forests

Forests are among the most productive and stable ecosystems on Earth. By
understanding how forests structure themselves and evolve over time,
permaculture designers can create food-producing systems modeled on forest
principles. These food forests combine high yields with ecological
resilience.

## The Seven Forest Layers

All forests follow the same basic seven-layer structure, occupying
vertical space from canopy to subsoil:

1) **Canopy layer**: The tallest trees, forming the overarching structure
   of the forest. In natural forests these are species like Doug fir and
   oak. In food forests they become fruit and nut trees.

2) **Understory layer**: Trees shorter than the canopy, shaded by the
   overstory. Made up of smaller versions of overstory trees and
   shade-tolerant species such as dogwood or hornbeam. In food forests,
   shorter fruit or nut-bearing trees fill this layer.

3) **Shrub layer**: Woody shrubs such as gooseberry, coralberry, and
   highbush cranberry. Berry-producing shrubs occupy this niche in food
   forest designs.

4) **Herbaceous layer**: The most diverse layer, consisting mostly of
   non-woody species including grasses, sedges, and wildflowers. In food
   forests, herbs and vegetables fill this layer.

5) **Ground layer**: The forest floor holds soil builders and stabilizers
   including fungi, mosses, liverworts, and [[permaculture-ethics]].

## Designing Food Forests

A food forest modeled after natural forest structure would be composed of
many three-dimensional layers containing various plants, animals, insects,
fungi, and microbes in dynamic harmonious balance. It would provide
shelter for varied animals, be powered by renewable energy, maximize
space usage through layering and guilding, recycle all resources, produce
as much food as possible, and maintain hydrological and nutrient loops.

The concept of food forests ties directly to the principle of using edges
and valuing the marginal: vertical space is often marginalized instead of
utilized. By stacking production from canopy to root layer, food forests
achieve some of the highest yields per square foot of any agricultural
system.

## Practical Considerations

Successful implementation of Forest Succession and Food Forests requires attention to
several practical factors including environmental conditions,
resource availability, and timing. Careful monitoring and
adaptive management help optimize outcomes across varying
conditions. Integration with other system elements enhances
overall effectiveness and creates beneficial synergies that
improve resilience and productivity over time.
