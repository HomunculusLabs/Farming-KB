# Mollison: Local Currency Systems and Community Banking

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14, Section 14.11)

## Establishing a Local Currency

For currency to be valid and usable, preconditions are necessary:
1. It must be backed up by a real, objective resource.
2. Other people must have confidence in it (which is why it is backed up by a local resource and can therefore be traded).
3. There must be a demand for its use, and a place to exchange it for other currency.

## Steps to Create a Community Currency

1. Find and lease a resource that can be pledged to the exchange institution as a redeemable asset. If timber, the currency is based on "a cord of timber, cut and stacked in the woodyard at such-and-such a place"; if clean water, "a gallon of water bottled and redeemable at a certain place." This is the reserve and gives confidence.

2. Print the currency itself, preferably in a solid local material difficult to duplicate or forge elsewhere, numbered, dated, and counted. The value so printed should not exceed the value of the reserve by a factor of more than 3 times. Reserve pledges need to be publicly available and assessed. Notes can exceed reserves if used for procreative assets only (e.g. forests). Forests represent firewood, but reserves are never cut except in dire emergencies.

3. Educate local businesses and banks (exchanges) in the use of the currency, which can be changed for national currency at the bank, or for other regional currencies. Post exchange rates publicly.

4. Start satisfying demand (e.g. for homes) by lending the currency to builders, who can exchange it at the bank for other currency needed in trade, or locally for products and services.

5. Producers of local goods accept local currency only; this creates demand, and other regions must "buy" such currency to obtain local goods.

6. To prevent hoarding, notes can be dated and a new issue made every 4 to 5 years; this is also a check on unwarranted accumulation and possible forgeries.

7. When most needs of the region are met, the currency can be collapsed. Many small towns funded their public works this way in the 1930s.

## Community-Based Loan Systems

### S.H.A.R.E. (Self-Help Association for a Regional Economy)

A local nonprofit corporation formed to help encourage small businesses producing necessary goods and services for the community. It works in conjunction with a local bank. Members open a SHARE joint account receiving only 6% interest (enabling small loans at 10%). The borrower must collect references from people who know them as responsible, and show that the proposed business will attract customers.

### C.E.L.T. (Community Enterprise Loans Trust)

A New Zealand-wide charitable trust to promote and support small businesses and cooperatives. CELT helps people form and run cooperatives by providing advice, training sessions, and loans. Services funded by subscriptions ($5), donations, and government schemes. Depositors receive 0 to 12% interest per year. CELT has achieved bank status.

### Southern Cross Capital Exchange (Australia)

A nonprofit organisation bringing together borrowers for socially conscious projects and lenders. Loans are not secured but guaranteed by the Exchange and by "guarantee circles" that spread risk.

## The Revolving Loans Office: Critical Personnel

1. Assessor-designers: People skilled in house, factory, farm design, energy budgeting, and appropriate technology -- the "permaculture" team.
2. Accountants: Skilled in setting up appropriate accounting systems to monitor progress and profit.
3. Broker-bankers: Handling cash flow, assessing reserves, operating banking and insurance functions.
4. Lawyer-trustees: Packaging legal strategies for community groups, advising on taxation, export-import, trust and leasing documents, labour exchange agreements.
5. Real estate or realty: Handling bookkeeping, serving local industry, supplying goods, advising on land design and nursery services.

## The Small Business Service Centre

A key organisation in a bioregion, itself a small business. Facilities offered:
- Secretarial: Letters, prospectus, submissions, filing, mail order.
- Bookkeeping: Banking, cost accounting, summary analyses, billing and collecting.
- Accounting: Tax assessment, broad strategies, trust advice.
- Legal: Legal forms, leases, representation, land conveyancy.
- Communication: Phone, telex, fax, photocopy, answering services.
- Gift, tithe, and public service deductions.
- Education: Seminars, inviting speakers, instructing new members.
- Skills register: Key people available for special advice.
- Research services: Retrieval and basic research for region and projects.
- Lease of seminar rooms, warehousing, equipment, office space.

## Specific Local Currency Models

### LETS (Local Exchange Trading System)

LETS, created by Michael Linton in 1983 in the Comox Valley of British Columbia, is one of the most widely implemented local currency models in the world. A LETS system operates as a mutual credit network: members list goods and services they can offer and those they need. Transactions are recorded in a central directory, with each participant's account going into debit or credit as they buy and sell. No physical currency is necessarily issued — the system runs on a ledger of mutual obligation.

The key innovation of LETS is that money is created at the point of transaction by both parties. When Alice does work for Bob, Bob's account is debited and Alice's is credited by the agreed amount. No interest is charged on negative balances, which means the system does not experience the debt-driven scarcity that characterizes conventional money. LETS systems have been established in dozens of countries, with varying degrees of success. The model works best in communities with strong social bonds and a culture of mutual aid.

### Ithaca Hours

Ithaca Hours, launched in 1991 in Ithaca, New York, by Paul Glover, is perhaps the most famous example of a successful local paper currency in the United States. Each Ithaca Hour was valued at $10 US (based on the idea that the average hourly wage in Tompkins County was approximately $10). The currency was printed on high-quality paper with local artwork and circulated through a network of hundreds of participating businesses.

At its peak, Ithaca Hours had an estimated circulation equivalent to over $100,000 US, supported by a quarterly directory listing accepting businesses. The system facilitated local trade, kept wealth circulating within the community, and became a powerful symbol of local economic self-determination. Though its circulation has declined since the early 2000s, Ithaca Hours remains a landmark case study in community currency design and inspired dozens of similar initiatives across North America.

### Time Banks

Time banking, developed by Edgar Cahn in the 1980s, is a model in which the unit of exchange is one hour of labor, regardless of the type of service provided. An hour of gardening equals an hour of legal advice equals an hour of elder care. This egalitarian approach values all forms of useful work equally and builds social solidarity across class and skill divides.

Time banking has proven particularly effective in communities with large elderly or disabled populations, where conventional economic transactions may not capture the full value of mutual aid. The system also addresses a common failure mode of local currencies: the tendency for trade to stagnate because participants cannot find mutual needs. Because time banks use a universal unit (one hour), they facilitate multi-party exchanges — Alice helps Bob, Bob helps Carol, Carol helps Alice — without requiring direct barter pairing.

## Case Studies of Successful Community Currencies

The Wära currency, issued in the German town of Schwanenkirchen during the Great Depression, demonstrated the power of local currencies to revitalize stagnant economies. The Wära was a "stamp scrip" currency — notes required periodic stamps (purchased for a small fee) to remain valid, which discouraged hoarding and encouraged rapid circulation. Despite its success in stimulating local trade, the Wära was banned by the German government in 1931 under pressure from the central bank.

The Chiemgauer, launched in 2003 in the Chiemgau region of Bavaria, is a more recent and ongoing success story. It operates on a similar demurrage model — notes lose a small percentage of their value each quarter unless stamped — and has achieved a circulation of over €5 million. Approximately 600 businesses accept the currency, and the demurrage revenue is donated to local nonprofits, creating a direct link between economic activity and community benefit.

In Switzerland, the WIR Bank (Wirtschaftsring-Genossenschaft) has operated a complementary currency system since 1934. Though it has grown far beyond a local experiment — the WIR currency now facilitates billions of francs in annual transactions among small and medium businesses — it demonstrates that complementary currencies can function at scale over extended periods without undermining the national currency. The WIR system has been credited with helping stabilize the Swiss economy during periods of financial crisis.

## Local Currencies and Permaculture Economics

Local currency systems are a natural extension of permaculture's core ethics: earth care, people care, and fair share. By keeping economic value circulating within a bioregion rather than being extracted by distant corporations and financial institutions, local currencies strengthen the economic foundations of sustainable land stewardship. A farmer who can trade directly with local consumers using community currency is less dependent on volatile global commodity markets and more able to adopt regenerative practices that may require shorter supply chains and local knowledge.

Mollison's vision of community currency was explicitly tied to the permaculture principle of small-scale, locally adapted systems. Just as permaculture design works with local climate, soil, and ecology rather than imposing generic solutions, local currencies work with local economic conditions, resources, and social networks. A forest-backed currency in the Pacific Northwest, a water-backed currency in an arid region, or a food-backed currency in an agricultural community — each reflects the unique productive capacity of its bioregion.

The relationship between local currencies and permaculture also operates at the level of community resilience. Diverse, locally controlled economic systems are more resistant to systemic shocks — financial crises, supply chain disruptions, energy shortages — than centralized, globally dependent ones. In permaculture terms, local currencies are an economic form of the principle of "each important function supported by multiple elements": if the national currency fails or becomes scarce, the local currency provides an alternative means of exchange, keeping essential economic functions operating.

## Demurrage and the Velocity of Money

A key concept in community currency design is demurrage — a negative interest rate that penalizes hoarding and encourages rapid circulation. Mollison referenced this idea through his suggestion that notes be dated and reissued every four to five years. The economic logic is straightforward: if money loses value over time, holders are incentivized to spend or invest it quickly, increasing the velocity of money and stimulating local economic activity.

The demurrage concept was most thoroughly developed by Silvio Gesell in the early twentieth century and was successfully implemented in the Wära and Wörgl experiments during the Great Depression. Mollison incorporated this principle into his design framework as a practical tool for ensuring that community currencies function as circulating media of exchange rather than as stores of value — a distinction that aligns with permaculture's emphasis on active, productive systems over static accumulations.

## Digital Complementary Currencies

While Mollison wrote before the widespread adoption of digital technology, the principles he outlined for community currency systems have found new expression in digital complementary currencies and blockchain-based platforms. Projects such as the Regenerative Network, Circles UBI, and various community token systems extend Mollison's vision by using digital infrastructure to reduce the transaction costs of local exchange, enabling mutual credit systems to operate at larger scales with lower administrative overhead.

These digital adaptations preserve the core permaculture economics principles that Mollison articulated — local backing, community governance, resistance to hoarding, and support for bioregional trade — while addressing some of the practical limitations of physical paper currencies, particularly the challenges of counterfeiting prevention and transaction tracking that Mollison addressed through numbered, dated notes and publicly posted exchange rates.

## Direct Marketing and Group Labels

Direct marketing cuts out the retailer and enables products to be sold at lower prices. A group label for products -- "Another Product from [Village]" -- generates interest in community products and gains a valuable reputation for quality, durability, or taste.
