# Permaculture Principle 11 — Use Edges and Value the Marginal

The eleventh of David Holmgren's twelve permaculture design principles. The icon of the sun rising over the horizon with a river in the foreground shows us a world composed of edges. Edges are where the most interesting, productive, and diverse events take place in any system.

> "Don't think you're on the right track just because it's a well-beaten path." — David Holmgren

## Core Concept

Within every terrestrial ecosystem, the living soil — which may only be a few centimeters deep — is an edge or interface between non-living mineral earth and the atmosphere. For all terrestrial life, this is the most important edge of all. Deep, well-drained, and aerated soil is like a sponge, a great interface that supports productive and healthy plant life.

Holmgren reminds us that whatever the object of our attention, it is at the edge of any thing, system, or medium that the most interesting events take place. Design that sees edge as an opportunity rather than a problem is more likely to be successful and adaptable.

## Landscape Edges

### Ecotones

An ecotone is the edge between two bioregions where the distribution of species from both regions overlaps, creating greater biodiversity than in either respective region. Holmgren describes the Towamba Valley on the far south coast of New South Wales as a three-way ecotone between warm temperate, cool temperate, and cold dry plateaus — 13 species of eucalypts on less than 75 hectares, compared to the typical 2-8 species.

### Coastlines and Waterways

Coastal ecosystems are diverse and productive interfaces between terrestrial and oceanic domains. While deep oceans are ecological deserts, shallow coastal waters fed by land nutrients support huge diversity. Tidal estuaries are complex interfaces functioning as ecological trade markets between land and sea.

Rivers, lakes, and wetlands support diverse freshwater ecosystems. Riparian vegetation (along waterways) is often more diverse in species and greater in density than vegetation further from water.

### Land Systems

Changes in soil type, slope (break of slope), or aspect (ridges) can create rapid transitions in vegetation types over distances as small as a metre. Edges between forest and grassland support a greater number of bird species than either vegetation system because the resources of both are available.

## Micro-Scale Edge

Within living organisms, edges are where action happens:

- **Lungs**: The ultimate fractal pattern of tissue allowing maximum gaseous exchange
- **Roots**: Surprisingly little mass but enormous surface area (edge) for osmotic absorption of water and nutrients
- **Soil**: Clay and humus provide complex surfaces (cation exchange capacity) that hold and release nutrients. A huge part of what makes soil productive is an open, friable structure that amounts to maximum edge

## Edge in Cultivated Landscapes

Holmgren describes how traditional English farm landscapes — with their irregular fields bordered by hedgerows, woods, copses, streams, and ponds — were full of carefully maintained edges. The change to large-scale monocultures eliminated much of this landscape complexity. The removal of hedgerows reduced wildlife habitat, wild food, and landscape amenity.

Mediterranean terracing (14th-16th centuries) increased agricultural productivity through heroic construction of landscape edge on degraded hillsides. Holmgren notes that the amount of structural edge in a landscape can be seen as a "leading indicator" of biological and resource diversity.

## Increasing Edge as Growth

Increasing edge is one important way to increase system intensity and productivity without gross expansion. This feeds into the principle of small scale — rather than expanding the area of a garden, we can increase its productivity by increasing the complexity and length of its edges. A pond with lobes and bays has far more edge than a round pond of the same area, supporting far more plants and habitat.

## The Value of Marginal Systems

Holmgren extends this principle beyond physical edges to social and cultural margins. Wild foods, rundown neighborhoods, and countercultural movements often exist at the margins of society but can be sources of innovation and resilience. The proverb reminds us that the most common, obvious, and popular is not necessarily the most significant.
