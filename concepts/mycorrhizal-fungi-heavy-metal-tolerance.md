# Mycorrhizal Fungi and Heavy Metal Tolerance

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] play a critical role in enabling plants to survive
and thrive in soils contaminated with heavy metals. Through
extracellular barriers, intracellular sequestration, and biochemical
transformations, these symbiotic fungi buffer host plants from toxic
metal concentrations, with profound implications for ecological
succession on contaminated sites, mine reclamation, and our
understanding of [[fungal-evolution]].

## Overview of Metal Tolerance in Mycorrhizal Fungi

Heavy metal contamination of soils—arising from mining, industrial
activity, and atmospheric deposition—poses a severe threat to plant
establishment [[fungal-biodiversity-and-ecosystem-function]]. Mycorrhizal associations
(ectomycorrhizae, [[arbuscular-mycorrhizae]], and [[ericoid-mycorrhizae]])
can significantly ameliorate these pollutants' effects. Tolerance
varies widely among species and even isolates of the same species,
making community composition a key determinant of plant performance on
contaminated sites. All three mycorrhizal types contribute through
different mechanisms with varying effectiveness depending on metal,
concentration, and host plant.

## Mechanisms of Plant Protection

Mycorrhizal fungi protect host plants through multiple, complementary
mechanisms operating at different spatial scales within the symbiosis.

### Extracellular Adsorption and Binding

Metals adsorb onto fungal structures before reaching the plant root.
Denny and Wilkins (1987a,b), using electron microscopy with EDAX,
demonstrated adsorption onto **extraradical hyphae** (soil filaments
with vast surface area), the **fungal sheath (mantle)** (dense hyphal
layer acting as a physical/chemical filter at the root tip), and the
**Hartig net** (hyphal interface between cortical cells). The
extracellular slime layer also binds metal ions, adding a diffusion
barrier between soil and root surface.

### Intracellular Compartmentalization

Metals penetrating fungal cells are handled through **polyphosphate
granule complexation**—granules that complex metal ions, rendering them
inert—and **vacuolar sequestration**, isolating metals from cytoplasmic
metabolism by concentrating them in the central vacuole. These pathways
allow substantial metal accumulation without damage.

### Quantitative Distribution in Mycorrhizal Roots

The protective redistribution is illustrated by *[[paxillus-involutus]]*
data. With colonization, zinc shifts: stele 26→15, cortex 50→20,
mantle 0→24, and extraradical hyphae 0→192 mmol/g. The fungus
intercepts metals in peripheral tissues, shielding vascular and
cortical tissues from the bulk of the burden.

## Ectomycorrhizal Fungi and Metal Tolerance

### Pisolithus tinctorius: A Model Tolerant Species

*Pisolithus tinctorius* is among the most tolerant ectomycorrhizal
species. EC50 values (Tam 1995) show broad tolerance: Al, Fe, Cu, Zn
at ~200 mg/L; Ni, Cd, Cr, Hg at 1–10 mg/L. Marx (1975, 1980)
demonstrated that *P. tinctorius* inoculation produced tree volumes
**250% greater** than natural inoculum on mine spoil soils.

### Comparative Tolerance Among Species

Blaudez et al. (2000) screened 39 isolates: *P. tinctorius*, *S.
luteus*, and *S. variegatus* showed greater Cu/Cd/Zn tolerance, while
*Paxillus* was more Ni-resistant. *Scleroderma* tolerates Al/Pb at
200 mg/L but not Cd (10 mg/L) or Hg (1 mg/L). Cd tolerance varies
dramatically within *Suillus*: Hartley et al. reported EC50 of 0.008
mmol/m³ for *S. variegatus* versus 12.6 for *S. granulatus*—over
three [[event-classification-and-orders-of-magnitude]]. Cd EC50: *P. involutus* 3.7, *S.
variegatus* 2.3 mg/g.

## Lead Translocation Through Mycelia

Vodnik et al. found Pb uptake of 1.1% for *P. tinctorius*, 6.2% for
*[[laccaria-laccata]]*, and 5.4% for *Suillus bovinus*. However, 45% of Pb
taken up by *L. laccata* was released elsewhere (away from the root
zone), compared to only 10% for *S. bovinus*. Some species actively
transport metals away from host roots through mycelial networks.

## Effects of Metals on Mycorrhizal Colonization

Heavy metals suppress colonization in metal-specific ways: lead reduces
ectomycorrhizal colonization of loblolly pine, while cadmium is most
toxic to Scots pine mycorrhizae. However, certain metal combinations
show ameliorative effects through competitive binding or physiological
antagonism. The overall metal profile—not individual concentrations
alone—determines mycorrhizal function.

## Ericoid Mycorrhizae and Metal Protection

[[singh-ericoid-mycorrhizal-fungi-remediation]] protect Ericaceae plants (*Calluna*,
*Vaccinium*, *Rhododendron*) on contaminated soils. Bradley et al.
showed ericoid plants grew in Cu/Zn at 0–150 mg/L **only when
colonized**—uncolonized plants failed entirely. *Oidiodendron maius*
produces enzymes even under metal stress, suggesting active biochemical
defenses beyond physical barriers alone.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizae in Polluted Soils

Call and Davies (1988) showed AM inoculation on lignite overburden
increased grass survival from 86% to 97% and biomass from 15 to 25
g/plant, reflecting enhanced nutrient uptake, soil stabilization, and
direct metal detoxification. AM fungi are valuable for associating
with herbaceous plants used in initial site stabilization.

## Evolutionary Routes for Metal Coping

At **low metal concentrations**, specific mechanisms like siderophore
production evolved to manage trace metal availability—siderophores are
high-affinity iron-chelating compounds that also bind other metals. At
**high concentrations** (e.g., mine spoils), non-specific mechanisms—
thick slime layers, polyphosphate granules, vacuolar compartmentalization,
and enhanced efflux—provide broader-spectrum defense. This explains why
some species show narrow tolerance while others exhibit broad
cross-tolerance. Fungal communities on naturally metal-rich soils
(serpentine, ultramafic) may harbor strains pre-adapted for reclamation.

## Applications in Ecological Restoration

The metal-tolerant properties of mycorrhizal fungi have direct
restoration applications: **targeted inoculation** with species like
*P. tinctorius* improves tree establishment on degraded lands;
**site-specific matching** of fungal species to metal profiles
optimizes outcomes; **metal redistribution** through mycelial networks
may contribute to longer-term remediation; and **community-level
approaches** using diverse inocula with complementary tolerances
maximize protection across all site metals.

## Summary

Mycorrhizal fungi provide multi-layered protection against heavy metal
toxicity through adsorption on hyphal surfaces, intracellular
complexation (polyphosphate granules, vacuoles), and altered metal
distribution within root tissues. *Pisolithus tinctorius* is
exceptionally tolerant across multiple metals, while ericoid and
arbuscular mycorrhizae extend protection to additional plant families.
