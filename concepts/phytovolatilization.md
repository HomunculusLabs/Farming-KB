# Phytovolatilization

Phytovolatilization is a phytoremediation mechanism in which plants absorb contaminants from soil or water and subsequently release them into the atmosphere as volatile compounds through transpiration. This process is one of the five principal mechanisms of [[phytoremediation-mechanisms]], alongside [[rhizofiltration]], phytoextraction, phytostabilization, and rhizodegradation.

Phytovolatilization applies primarily to certain metal and metalloid contaminants, as well as some volatile organic compounds, that can be converted to less toxic gaseous forms during plant metabolism. The process represents a unique pathway among [[bioremediation-monitoring-and-assessment]] strategies because it transfers contaminants from the subsurface to the atmospheric compartment rather than destroying or immobilizing them in place.

## Mechanism of Phytovolatilization

The phytovolatilization pathway begins with root uptake of dissolved contaminants from the soil solution. Contaminant ions or molecules enter root cells through membrane transporters or diffuse passively across cell walls, depending on the chemical properties of the contaminant.

Once absorbed, the contaminant travels through the xylem with the transpiration stream to the leaves. Within leaf tissues, biochemical transformations may convert the contaminant into a volatile species. This conversion can involve enzymatic reduction, methylation, or other conjugation reactions that increase the vapor pressure of the compound.

The volatile product then diffuses through stomatal pores and enters the atmosphere. The rate of volatilization is directly proportional to transpiration rate, which depends on environmental conditions including temperature, humidity, light intensity, and wind speed.

For metals and metalloids, this conversion often involves methylation or reduction reactions catalyzed by plant enzymes. Selenium, for example, can be taken up as selenate or selenite and metabolically converted through the sulfur assimilation pathway to selenocysteine and selenomethionine. These amino acid analogs are subsequently methylated to dimethyl selenide and dimethyl diselenide, volatile compounds with toxicity roughly 500 to 700 times lower than inorganic selenate.

Similarly, arsenic can be biomethylated through a pathway analogous to arsenic detoxification in microorganisms. Inorganic arsenate (As(V)) is reduced to arsenite (As(III)) and then sequentially methylated to produce monomethylarsonic acid (MMA), dimethylarsinic acid (DMA), and ultimately trimethylarsine (TMA), a volatile gas.

Mercury phytovolatilization involves the enzymatic reduction of ionic mercury (Hg2+) to elemental mercury (Hg0), which has a significant vapor pressure at ambient temperatures. The reduced mercury vapor then exits the leaf through stomata. This pathway mirrors bacterial mercury resistance mechanisms involving the mer operon.

## Contaminants Amenable to Phytovolatilization

Selenium is the most extensively studied contaminant for phytovolatilization and the one with the strongest evidence for practical application. Certain plant species, particularly members of the Brassicaceae family such as Indian mustard (Brassica juncea), can accumulate and volatilize substantial quantities of selenium from both selenate and selenite sources.

Field studies in seleniferous soils of California's San Joaquin Valley have demonstrated that managed phytovolatilization can reduce soil selenium loads significantly while producing harvested plant biomass with lower selenium concentration than would occur without volatilization. The volatilized dimethyl selenide disperses rapidly in the atmosphere and undergoes photochemical degradation.

Mercury phytovolatilization has attracted considerable research interest because of mercury's global cycling and toxicity. Aquatic plants and certain wetland species have shown natural capacity for mercury reduction and volatilization. Duckweed (Lemna minor), water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes), and poplar trees have all demonstrated mercury volatilization in laboratory studies.

Arsenic phytovolatilization has been observed in fern species and several angiosperms. The brake fern (Pteris vittata), well known as an [[rhizofiltration]], also volatilizes a fraction of absorbed arsenic as arsine or methylated arsine gases. The proportion volatilized is generally small compared to the total arsenic accumulated, typically less than one percent, but the pathway remains significant for understanding arsenic biogeochemical cycling.

Volatile organic contaminants represent another important class of compounds amenable to phytovolatilization. Trichloroethylene (TCE), tetrachloroethylene (PCE), benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene (BTEX compounds), and methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) can be passively taken up from contaminated groundwater and transpired by deep-rooted trees. Hybrid poplar trees have demonstrated significant capacity for TCE uptake and release, with transpiration-driven volatilization accounting for the majority of mass removal in some field studies.

## Plant Species for Phytovolatilization

Poplar (Populus spp.) and willow (Salix spp.) are the most widely used species for VOC phytovolatilization because of their fast growth, deep root systems, and high transpiration rates. Individual poplar trees can transpire 50 to 350 liters of water per day during the growing season, processing large volumes of potentially contaminated groundwater.

Indian mustard (Brassica juncea) and canola (Brassica napus) are effective selenium volatilizers that have been tested at field scale. Indian mustard has been the primary model species for selenium phytovolatilization research and produces dimethyl selenide as the dominant volatile product.

Brake fern (Pteris vittata) demonstrates both arsenic hyperaccumulation and limited arsenic volatilization. Other fern species including Pteris cretica and certain wetland grasses also show capacity for arsenic transformation and volatilization in hydroponic and soil systems.

Tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) and cottonwood (Populus deltoides) have been investigated for mercury phytovolatilization. Transgenic lines expressing bacterial mercury reductase (merA) genes have demonstrated enhanced mercury volatilization rates in laboratory and greenhouse studies, though field deployment of transgenic remediation species faces significant regulatory hurdles.

## Advantages of Phytovolatilization

The principal advantage of phytovolatilization is that the contaminant is removed from the soil or groundwater without requiring harvest and disposal of contaminated plant biomass. This eliminates the secondary waste management costs associated with [[phytoextraction-mechanisms]] and related harvest-based approaches.

Phytovolatilization can treat contamination at greater depth than most alternative phytoremediation mechanisms, limited only by root penetration depth. Deep-rooted trees such as poplars can access groundwater contamination at depths of three to ten meters, providing treatment capacity that would be expensive to achieve with excavation or engineered systems.

The technology is aesthetically pleasing and provides ancillary ecosystem benefits including carbon sequestration, habitat creation, and reduction of wind and water erosion. Treatment sites can remain productive or recreational during remediation, unlike conventional excavation or containment approaches.

Phytovolatilization operates continuously during the growing season, driven by solar energy through the transpiration process. This makes it a low-energy, low-maintenance remediation approach once plants are established.

## Limitations and Environmental Concerns

The transformation of a contaminant from one environmental compartment to another raises important regulatory and ethical questions that must be carefully evaluated for each application. Atmospheric release of volatile arsenic, selenium, or mercury species may pose inhalation risks to nearby populations or contribute to regional atmospheric deposition.

The toxicity reduction achieved through biomethylation must be verified for each contaminant and each set of environmental conditions. While dimethyl selenide is substantially less toxic than selenate, trimethylarsine is more toxic than inorganic arsenic in some exposure scenarios, and the environmental behavior of organoselenium and organoarsenic gases is not fully understood.

Efficiency of phytovolatilization depends on many plant-specific and site-specific factors. Transpiration rate, root depth and distribution, soil contaminant bioavailability, and the plant's enzymatic capacity for contaminant transformation all influence the overall mass removal rate. Seasonal variation in transpiration can cause substantial fluctuation in volatilization rates, with minimal activity during winter dormancy in temperate climates.

Climate and weather conditions directly affect performance. Temperature, humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation all influence stomatal conductance and transpiration rates. Phytovolatilization is most effective in warm climates with long growing seasons and consistent water availability.

## Monitoring and Integration with Other Strategies

Monitoring phytovolatilization requires measurement of both contaminant uptake by plants and volatile emission rates to the atmosphere. Enclosed chamber methods can quantify volatile flux from individual plants or small plots, while micrometeorological techniques such as eddy covariance provide landscape-scale emission estimates.

Tissue analysis complements flux measurements by tracking contaminant distribution and transformation products within plant tissues. Understanding the mass balance between uptake, accumulation, transformation, and volatilization is essential for evaluating overall treatment efficiency.

Environmental fate assessment must account for atmospheric transport, chemical transformation, and deposition of volatilized contaminants. Dimethyl selenide released by plants can undergo photochemical oxidation in the atmosphere, producing aerosol-phase selenium that may deposit on downwind ecosystems. Similarly, volatile elemental mercury can undergo oxidation to reactive gaseous mercury and contribute to mercury contamination of distant water bodies.

Phytovolatilization is most appropriately viewed as one component of an integrated phytoremediation strategy. Combining phytovolatilization with [[constructed-wetlands-wastewater-treatment]] and [[mycorrhizal-fungi-bioremediation]] can enhance overall contaminant removal while maintaining ecological function of treated sites. The process may also complement [[constructed-wetlands-wastewater-treatment]] systems where wetland plants volatilize selenium and other metalloids from contaminated surface waters.

## Research Directions and Future Prospects

Ongoing research in phytovolatilization focuses on several key areas. Genetic engineering approaches aim to enhance the enzymatic capacity of plants for contaminant transformation, particularly for mercury and selenium. Expression of bacterial merA genes for mercury reduction and enhancement of plant methyltransferase activity for selenium volatilization have both shown promising results in controlled environments.

Understanding the complete biochemical pathways of contaminant volatilization in plants remains an active area of investigation. Proteomic and metabolomic approaches are being applied to identify the specific enzymes responsible for arsenic and selenium methylation in various plant species, which could enable targeted breeding or engineering of improved phytovolatilizers.

Scale-up from laboratory and greenhouse studies to field deployment requires better predictive models of volatilization rates under variable environmental conditions. Process-based models that integrate plant physiology, soil chemistry, and micrometeorology are being developed to support site-specific design of phytovolatilization systems.

Integration with [[bioaugmentation-strategies]] and [[biostimulation-techniques]] offers potential for enhancing the rhizosphere microbial communities that support plant health and contaminant uptake. Microbial partners may contribute directly to contaminant transformation or may enhance plant growth and stress tolerance in contaminated environments.

The role of [[mycorrhizal-fungi-rhizosphere-remediation]] in phytovolatilization is increasingly recognized. Mycorrhizal associations can extend the effective root zone for contaminant access, improve plant nutrient status in marginal soils, and potentially contribute directly to contaminant transformation through fungal metabolic activities.
