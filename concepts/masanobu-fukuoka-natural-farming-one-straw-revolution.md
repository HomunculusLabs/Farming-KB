# Masanobu Fukuoka's Natural Farming — The One-Straw Revolution

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] (1913–2008) developed a method of [[natural-farming]] that requires **no ploughing, no chemical fertilizer, no prepared compost, no weeding by tillage or herbicides, and no dependence on chemicals**. His rice and winter grain yields of ~22 bushels (1,300 lbs) per quarter acre match or exceed the top yields in Ehime Prefecture, Japan — one of the prime agricultural areas — yet his fields have not been ploughed for over 25 years.

## Origins

Fukuoka was a plant pathologist at the Yokohama Customs Bureau, working under [[eiichi-kurosawa]] (who first isolated gibberellin, the plant growth hormone). After a near-death experience with acute pneumonia at age 25, he had a transformative insight while resting against a tree overlooking Yokohama harbor at dawn on May 15th:

> "In this world there is nothing at all... Humanity knows nothing at all. There is no intrinsic value in anything, and every action is a futile, meaningless effort."

He resigned the next day, wandered Japan for several years, then returned to his father's citrus farm on Iyo, Ehime Prefecture in 1938 to put his philosophy into practice. His first attempt at "natural farming" — simply abandoning the orchard — resulted in two acres of mandarin orange trees withering from insect attack and tangled branches. He learned that **abandonment is not natural farming** — it took 30 years of refinement to reach true simplicity.

## The Four Principles

### 1. No Cultivation
The earth cultivates itself naturally through plant root penetration and the activity of microorganisms, small animals, and earthworms. Cultivation destroys soil structure, brings deep weed seeds to the surface, and disrupts the natural soil ecosystem. Japan practiced agriculture without ploughing until the Tokugawa Era (300–400 years ago); deep ploughing came with Western agriculture.

### 2. No Chemical Fertilizer or Prepared Compost
When left to itself, soil maintains fertility naturally through the orderly cycle of plant and animal life. Organic remains accumulate on the surface, decompose via bacteria and fungi, and nutrients move deep into the soil with rainwater. Plant roots draw nutrients back up. Fukuoka uses:
- White clover as a leguminous ground cover (~1 lb per quarter acre)
- All straw returned to the field after threshing
- A small amount of poultry manure to help decompose straw

When he rented a field that had been used as a chicken manure dump over winter, the rice came up too thickly and was attacked by blast disease — demonstrating that **too much fertilizer causes problems**.

### 3. No Weeding by Tillage or Herbicides
Weeds play their part in building soil fertility and balancing the biological community. They should be **controlled, not eliminated**. Key strategies:
- **Straw mulch** — completely covering the field with uncut straw stops weed germination
- **[[white-clover-ground-cover]]** — interplanted with crops
- **Timing** — sowing seeds while the preceding crop is still standing gives grain a head start on weeds. No interval between crops means weeds never get established
- **Temporary flooding** — weakens weeds and clover while allowing rice to sprout

Fukuoka notes: "The farmer who tries to control weeds by cultivating the soil is, quite literally, sowing the seeds of his own misfortune." Cultivation stirs up deep weed seeds that would never otherwise germinate.

### 4. No Dependence on Chemicals
Disease and insect imbalance became problems only because of unnatural practices like ploughing and fertilizing, which produced weak plants. Nature left alone is in perfect balance. Harmful insects and plant diseases are always present but do not reach problematic levels. The approach is to **grow sturdy crops in a healthy environment**.

## The Annual Cycle

Fukuoka's rice/winter-grain succession method:

| Time | Action |
|------|--------|
| **Early October** | Broadcast white clover among ripening rice; broadcast winter grain (rye/barley) seeds mid-month |
| **Early November** | Harvest rice; broadcast next year's rice seed (in clay pellets); spread rice straw over field |
| **Mid-Nov to Mid-December** | Broadcast clay pellets containing rice seed among young barley/rye; spread thin layer of chicken manure |
| **Winter** | Clover and winter grain grow; rice seeds (in pellets) lie dormant beneath straw |
| **May (~20th)** | Harvest winter grain; thresh; spread all straw over field |
| **Late May** | Flood field for 7–10 days (weakens weeds/clover, allows rice to sprout) |
| **June–July** | Rainwater sufficient; no standing water needed |
| **August** | Run fresh water through field about once weekly (without standing) |
| **Autumn** | Harvest rice; cycle begins again |

**Seeding rates:**
- White clover: ~1 lb per quarter acre
- Winter grain: 6.5–13 lbs per quarter acre
- Rice: 4.5–9 lbs per quarter acre

The entire year's planting can be finished by New Year's Day if rice is sown in autumn along with winter grain.

## Clay Pellet Seed Protection

Rice seeds sown in autumn are vulnerable to mice, birds, and rot. Fukuoka encloses them in clay pellets (~0.5 inch diameter):

1. Spread unhulled rice seed on a flat pan
2. Dust with fine powdered clay
3. Add thin mist of water
4. Form pellets by shaking in circular motion

Alternatively: soak seeds, mix with moist clay by kneading, push through chicken wire screen, dry 1–2 days, roll into pellets. One person can make enough pellets for several acres in a day.

## The Role of Straw

Straw is "fundamental to my method" — connected with fertility, germination, weed control, sparrow deterrence, and water management. Key points:

- **Spread uncut**, tossed randomly (not neatly arranged like garden mulch)
- **Cross-species mulching:** Rice straw for winter grain; winter grain straw for rice. This prevents disease transmission (rice diseases won't infect winter grain, and straw decomposes before rice sprouts the following spring)
- Fresh rice straw is safe for other grains; fresh straw of one winter grain species should not be used for other winter grain species (disease risk)
- **All straw and hulls** from threshing should be returned to the field
- Surface soil enriched to >4 inches of humus over 20+ years of returning all straw

## Natural Ecosystem Balance

Fukuoka's fields support a balanced ecosystem: dragonflies, moths, honeybees, insects, spiders, frogs, lizards, moles, earthworms. Plant disease may sweep through the area leaving his crops unaffected.

**Spider phenomenon:** Occasionally, after rice harvest, fields become completely covered with spider webs "as though with silk." Several spiders per square inch, millions in a quarter acre. After 1–2 days, strands break off and young spiders sail away on the wind "like dandelion fluff." This natural drama is destroyed instantly by any chemical application — even harmless wood ash caused all spider webs to disintegrate and spiders to disappear.

**Pest control insight:** The Kochi Prefecture insect specialist found that while harmful insects were few in Fukuoka's fields, their natural predators were far more numerous than in sprayed fields. The fields were maintained by natural balance among insect communities. Different predators dominate in different years (spiders one year, toads another, frogs another). Specialized research cannot grasp these complex interrelationships.

## Rice Paddy Water Management

- **Winter:** No water in field
- **After winter grain harvest (May):** Flood for 7–10 days to weaken weeds/clover
- **June–July:** Rainwater only; no standing water
- **August:** Run fresh water through weekly without standing
- **Autumn harvest:** Drain before harvesting rice

This differs from conventional paddy rice, which maintains standing water throughout the growing season.

## Why Natural Farming Hasn't Spread

Fukuoka identifies several barriers:

1. **Overspecialization** — researchers see only their specialty (insect specialist sees only insects; nutrition specialist sees only plant vigor)
2. **Compromise** — "experts" always suggest mixing natural and scientific methods ("Wouldn't it be more convenient to harvest by machine?"), missing the fundamental point
3. **Institutional inertia** — testing stations say "it's too early" and want years of research before approval
4. **Misunderstanding** — some see it as primitive, others as the pinnacle of achievement; few grasp that it "arises from the unmoving and unchanging centre of agricultural development"

## Applicability to Other Regions

Fukuoka believes natural farming could be applied to other areas with indigenous crops:
- Where water is scarce: upland rice, buckwheat, sorghum, millet
- Alternative ground covers: other clover varieties, alfalfa, vetch, lupine instead of white clover
- The method "takes a distinctive form in accordance with the unique conditions of the area"

## Philosophy

Key insight: "Humanity knows nothing at all." The irony is that "science has served only to show how small human knowledge is." An object seen in isolation from the whole is not the real thing. True nature is seen by infants, who see without thinking, "straight and clear."

The method evolved through asking not "How about trying this?" but **"How about not doing this? How about not doing that?"** — progressively eliminating unnecessary practices until reaching maximum simplicity. "It has taken me over thirty years to reach this simplicity."
