# Basidium and Spore Production in Mushrooms

The basidium is the definitive spore-producing cell of the
Basidiomycetes, the class of fungi that includes most gourmet
and medicinal mushrooms. Understanding basidial development
and spore release mechanisms is fundamental to mushroom
biology and cultivation.

## Structure of the Basidium

Basidia are club-shaped cells that arise from a genetically
rich, dense surface layer on the gills called the hymenium.
They typically form four "arms" at their apices known as
sterigmata. These sterigmata project upwards and elongate,
with each tip eventually swelling to form a small globular
cavity that becomes a spore. (See Stamets, Figures 47-52.)

Each young basidium contains two haploid, sexually paired
nuclei. These nuclei fuse through a process called karyogamy,
forming one diploid nucleus containing a full complement of
chromosomes. Immediately thereafter, meiosis (reduction
division) occurs, resulting in four haploid nuclei. These
haploid nuclei are elastic in form, squeezing up the
sterigmata to be deposited in their continually swelling
tips.

## Spore Formation and Arrangement

Once residing in the newly forming spore cavity, the spore
casing enlarges. Each spore is attached to the end of each
sterigma by a nipple-like protuberance called the sterigmal
appendage. With many species, the opposite end of the spore
is dimpled with a germ pore.

The four spores of the basidia emerge diametrically opposite
one another. This arrangement ensures that the highly viscous
spores do not touch. Should a young spore come into contact
with another before their outer shells harden, they fuse and
development is arrested. The spores become pigmented at
maturity and are released in sets of paired opposites.

Most mushrooms have four-spored basidia, though comparatively
few species have two-spored basidia. The spore-producing
hymenium is nestled between two layers, with the gill trama
composed of larger interwoven cells acting as channels for
feeding the hymenial layers with nutrients.

## Spore Ejection Mechanism

The method of spore ejection has been a subject of much study
and yet still remains largely a mystery. At the junction
between the spore and the basidium's sterigma, an oily gas
bubble forms and inflates. This bubble swells to capacity
and explodes, ejecting spores with a force calculated to
represent more than 6 atmospheres of pressure.

After ejaculation, the basidium collapses, making way for
neighboring basidia, until now dormant, to enlarge.
Successions of basidia mature in ever increasing quantities
until peaking at the time of mushroom maturity. The well
organized manner by which populations of basidia emerge
from the plane of the gill optimizes the efficiency of
spore dispersal.

## Spore Release Dynamics

After peak spore production, spores cover the gill face
several layers deep, hiding the very cells from which they
arose. At this stage, spore release actually declines as
the battery of basidia has been largely exhausted and/or
because the basidia are rendered dysfunctional by the sea
of overlying spores.

Cap orientation is foremost affected by the direction of
air currents, then by light, and finally by gravity (Badham
1985). Beneath the cap, the gill plates radiate outwards
from a centralized stem like spokes on a wheel. This
arrangement maximizes the surface area available for spore
production and dispersal.

## Spore Production Volume

With the larger agarics, the numbers become astronomical.
A single mushroom can produce from tens of thousands to
one hundred million spores. This massive output is
necessary because mushrooms reproduce through spores, and
the success of the mushroom life cycle depends upon their
production and dispersal.

Populations of spores decrease with distance from the parent
mushroom. When spores germinate, the mycelium grows out
radially, away from the site of origin. Often, the next
hospitable environment may be far away. Spores, taken up
by wind or carried by insects and mammals, are dispersed
to habitats well distant from the parent mushroom.

## Role in Cultivation

For cultivators, understanding basidial biology has
practical implications:

- Spore color and [[mushroom-spore-printing-and-storage]] characteristics are key
  identification features
- The timing of spore release affects harvest decisions
  since heavy spore production accelerates mushroom
  decomposition
- Strains selected for reduced spore load have extended
  shelf life
- The germ pore presence and spore ornamentation are
  important microscopic identification features

The basidium represents the culmination of the mushroom
life cycle, from spore germination through mycelial
colonization to fruiting body formation. Each spore
carries one half of the genetic material necessary for
propagation, and upon germination, compatible hyphae
must fuse to form the dikaryotic mycelium capable of
producing mushrooms.
