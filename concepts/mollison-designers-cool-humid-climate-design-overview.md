# Cool Humid Climate Design Overview

Mollison devotes extensive attention to the cool humid [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America, southern South America,
and highland regions globally. This is the most agriculturally productive
[[hemenway-cold-air-drainage-and-frost-management]]:** Cold air is denser and flows downhill like water,
  pooling in valleys, hollows, and behind barriers. Mollison calls these
  "frost pockets" and advises against placing frost-sensitive plants or
  animal shelters in them.

- **Frost line on slopes:** The altitude above a valley floor at which frost
  no longer pools depends on terrain shape. On a typical hillside, the frost
  line sits roughly 10-30 metres above the valley floor. Above this line,
  air drainage keeps temperatures more moderate. Mollison recommends
  identifying the frost line through observation before siting houses,
  orchards, or animal shelters.

- **Inversions:** Clear, calm nights produce temperature inversions where
  cold air pools below warmer air. Cloudy or windy nights prevent this by
  mixing air layers. Design for the worst case -- the still, clear night.

- **Microfrost management:** Stone walls, water bodies, and dense vegetation
  moderate local frost. A pond uphill of an orchard can raise temperatures
  by 2-3 degrees C through thermal radiation on still nights.

## Windbreak Essentials

Windbreaks are critical in cool humid climates for three reasons:

1. Wind chill dramatically increases effective cold stress on plants and
   animals
2. Desiccating winter winds damage evergreens and cause soil erosion
3. Windbreaks reduce heating costs for structures by 20-40%

Mollison's windbreak [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] accumulation:** The combination of adequate moisture
  and cool temperatures slows decomposition, allowing thick humus layers to
  develop. This is an asset -- these soils are naturally fertile.
