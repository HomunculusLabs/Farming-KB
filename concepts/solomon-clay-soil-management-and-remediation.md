## Understanding Clay Soil Problems

Clay soils contain a high proportion of fine mineral particles (less than 0.002 mm) that pack tightly together. This creates several interrelated problems:

- **Poor drainage**: Water moves slowly through compacted clay, leading to waterlogging after rain and root suffocation during wet periods
- **Hardpan formation**: Repeated tilling at the same depth creates a compacted layer (plow pan) that roots cannot penetrate
- **Late warming**: Clay's high thermal mass means spring soils stay cold longer, delaying planting and slowing early growth
- **Difficult cultivation**: Wet clay sticks to tools; dry clay becomes brick-hard and resists digging
- **Cracking**: As clay dries, it shrinks and cracks, damaging root systems and creating channels that drain too quickly

## The Gypsum Question

Many gardening books recommend gypsum (calcium sulfate) to improve clay soil structure by promoting clay particle aggregation. Solomon is skeptical of this advice for most home gardeners:

- Gypsum is effective primarily in sodic soils (high sodium) where it displaces sodium with calcium, improving structure
- Most garden clay soils are not sodic; they are simply fine-textured
- The calcium in gypsum is less available to plants than calcium from limestone
- Adding gypsum without a clear need may create nutrient imbalances

Solomon recommends applying gypsum only if a soil test indicates high sodium levels or if the soil is known to be sodic.
