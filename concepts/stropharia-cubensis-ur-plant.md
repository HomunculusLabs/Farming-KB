# Stropharia Cubensis as the Ur Plant

## Overview

In *Food of the Gods*, Terence McKenna presents the hypothesis that *Stropharia cubensis* (also known as *Psilocybe cubensis*, the familiar "magic mushroom") was the "Ur plant" — the primary psychoactive catalyst in the emergence of human consciousness and culture. Through a process of elimination from known African hallucinogen candidates, McKenna argues that this coprophilic mushroom, growing in the dung of zebu cattle across tropical grasslands, was the most likely vehicle for the transformative encounter between early humans and [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]].

The designation "Ur plant" — borrowing the German prefix *Ur-* meaning "proto-" or "original" — signals the primordial status McKenna assigns to this mushroom in the story of human becoming.

## Weeding Out the Candidates

McKenna's argument proceeds by systematically eliminating African hallucinogenic plant candidates as plausible catalysts for the emergence of human consciousness. The candidates considered include iboga, LSD-type compounds, [[mckenna-food-gods-peganum-harmala-soma]], and the [[mckenna-tryptamine-hallucinogens-and-consciousness]].

**Iboga** (*Tabernanthe iboga*): There is absolutely no evidence of its use before the early [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]]. It was never mentioned by the Portuguese, who had a long history of trade and exploration in West Africa. Furthermore, it is not a grassland plant but a native of the tropical forest, rarely found growing outside of cultivation. This makes it an unlikely companion to early hominids expanding onto the open grasslands.

**LSD-type compounds**: No plants containing LSD-type compounds are known to have been used in Africa, nor are there any striking examples of plants rich in these compounds native to the African continent.

**Peganum harmala** (the giant Syrian rue): Rich in the beta-carboline harmine, it occurs wild across the arid portions of Mediterranean North Africa. However, there is no record of its use in Africa as a hallucinogen, and in any case it must be concentrated and/or combined with DMT to activate its visionary potential — something beyond the technical reach of early human beings.

This process of elimination leaves the tryptamine hallucinogens as the only viable candidate class, and among them, only the coprophilic mushrooms could deliver effective doses in a format accessible to pre-technological peoples.

## The Tryptamine Hallucinogens

By process of elimination, McKenna arrives at the tryptamine-type hallucinogens: psilocybin, psilocin, and DMT. In a grassland environment these compounds might be expected to occur in either a dung-loving (coprophilic) mushroom containing psilocybin or in a grass containing DMT. But unless the DMT were extracted and concentrated — something beyond the technical reach of early human beings — these grasses could never supply sufficient amounts of DMT to provide an effective hallucinogen. By process of elimination, McKenna concludes that a mushroom was the most likely candidate.

When our remote ancestors moved out of the trees and onto the grasslands, they increasingly encountered hooved beasts who ate vegetation. These beasts became a major source of potential sustenance. Our ancestors also encountered the manure of these same wild cattle and the mushrooms that grow in it.

## Why Stropharia Cubensis

Several grassland mushroom species contain psilocybin: *Panaeolus* species and *Stropharia cubensis*, also called *Psilocybe cubensis*. Of these mushroom species, only *Stropharia cubensis* meets all the criteria McKenna deems necessary:

1. **Concentrated psilocybin content**: It contains psilocybin in amounts sufficient to produce profound visionary experiences.
2. **Free of nausea-producing compounds**: Unlike many other psychoactive mushrooms, it does not contain compounds that cause nausea, making it practical for regular use.
3. **Pandemic distribution**: It occurs throughout the [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]], at least wherever cattle of the zebu (*Bos indicus*) type graze, giving it an enormous geographical range coincident with early human habitation.

## Botanical History and Distribution

The first specimen of *Psilocybe cubensis* was collected by the American botanist Earle in Cuba in 1906, but current botanical thinking places the species' point of origin in Southeast Asia. At an archaeological dig in Thailand at a place called Non Nak Tha, which has been dated to 15,000 B.P., the bones of zebu cattle have been found coincident with human graves. *Stropharia cubensis* is common in the Non Nak Tha area today.

The Non Nak Tha site is highly significant for McKenna's argument. It suggests that mushroom use was a human trait that emerged wherever human populations and cattle evolved together. The coincidence of zebu bones with human graves at a 15,000-year-old site implies a deep cultural association between humans, cattle, and psilocybin-containing mushrooms that extends far back into prehistory.

The implications of this finding are substantial. If the association between humans, zebu, and *Stropharia cubensis* was established by 15,000 years ago in Southeast Asia, it likely existed in Africa for much longer, given that the genus *Homo* originated on that continent. The mushroom's presence wherever zebu graze tropically means that the human-mushroom encounter was not a one-time event but a recurring, generation-spanning relationship that could exert sustained evolutionary pressure on human cognition and culture.

## The Question of Zebu Specificity

McKenna raises several important questions about the relationship between *Stropharia cubensis* and zebu cattle. Does the mushroom occur exclusively in the manure of zebu, or can it occur in the manure of other cattle? How recently has it reached its various habitats? These questions remain partially open, but the correlation between zebu distribution and mushroom presence across the tropics is striking and suggestive of a very long co-occurrence.

The zebu cattle (*Bos indicus*) represent a critical link in McKenna's argument. These humped cattle, domesticated in South Asia but with ancient presence across tropical Africa and Southeast Asia, provided the dung substrate in which *Stropharia cubensis* grows. Wherever early human pastoralists followed their zebu herds across tropical grasslands, the mushrooms would have been present in abundance, making regular encounter and consumption virtually inevitable for any omnivorous primate exploring its environment.

## The Tripartite Relationship

McKenna emphasizes that the relationship between humans and mushrooms necessarily included a third party: cattle. The mushrooms grow only in bovine dung, and the cattle create the only source of the mushrooms. This tripartite relationship — human, cattle, and mushroom — forms an ecological and cultural complex that shaped the trajectory of human evolution. Cattle provided sustenance, mushrooms provided transcendence, and humans provided the [[ayahuasca-pharmacology-and-cultural-context]] in which the mushroom experience could be interpreted, shared, and integrated into social life.

This three-way relationship also helps explain the elevation of the cow to sacred status in many [[entheogens-in-ancient-cultures]], particularly in Vedic India where the cow remains sacred to this day. The mushroom that grew in cattle dung may have been the original source of the bovine veneration that later became codified in religious law and taboo.

## The Ur Plant

McKenna declares that "ample evidence supports the notion that *Stropharia cubensis* is the Ur plant, our umbilicus to the feminine mind of the planet, which, when its cult, the Paleolithic cult of the Great Horned Goddess, was intact, conveyed to us such knowledge that we were able to live in a dynamic equilibrium with nature, with each other, and within ourselves."

The phrase "Ur plant" conveys the primordial status McKenna assigns to this mushroom — not merely as one psychoactive among many, but as the original source of humanity's encounter with the transcendental dimensions of consciousness. The "umbilicus to the feminine mind of the planet" is a characteristically McKennan metaphor that links the mushroom to the partnership model of culture, the Great Horned Goddess, and the maternal intelligence of the natural world.

## A Deep-Rooted Custom, Not Biological Symbiosis

McKenna is careful to distinguish the human-mushroom relationship from true biological symbiosis. The relationship is probably altogether no more than a million years old, for the era of the nomadic human hunter dates from that time. The last 100,000 years is probably a more than generous amount of time to allow for the evolution of pastoralism from its first faint glimmerings.

Since the entire relationship extends no further than a million years, McKenna argues, "we are not discussing a biological symbiosis that might take many millions of years to evolve. Rather, we are talking about a deep-rooted custom, an extremely powerful natural habit."

This distinction is important for McKenna's argument because it grounds the hypothesis in cultural and behavioral evolution rather than requiring the much longer time scales of genetic coevolution. The mushroom did not need to literally rewire human DNA — it needed only to be consumed regularly enough to create behavioral and cultural consequences that were then subject to natural selection.

[[health-risks-and-harm-reduction-for-hallucinogenic-mushroom-use]] evolved as a kind of natural habit with behavioral and evolutionary consequences. This relationship between human beings and mushrooms necessarily included cattle, the creators of the only source of the mushrooms. Whatever we call the human interaction with *Stropharia cubensis*, it has not been a static relationship, but rather a dynamic through which humanity has been "bootstrapped to higher and higher cultural levels and levels of individual self-awareness."
