# Fungal Diversity in Mangrove and Estuarine Ecosystems

Mangrove and estuarine ecosystems represent transitional habitats between marine and terrestrial environments, supporting distinctive fungal communities adapted to fluctuating salinity, tidal inundation, and substrate characteristics. These ecosystems are among the most productive and ecologically important coastal habitats worldwide.

## Habitat Characteristics

Mangroves and estuaries present unique environmental conditions for fungal colonization:

- **Fluctuating salinity**: from freshwater to full marine salinity on tidal cycles

- **Periodic inundation**: substrata alternately submerged and exposed

- **Anaerobic sediments**: waterlogged soils create low-oxygen conditions

- **High organic matter**: productive ecosystems with abundant plant debris

- **Chemical complexity**: tannins, phenolics, and other plant secondary compounds from mangrove foliage

## Fungal Communities in Estuarine Soils

Various investigators have isolated filamentous fungi from soils, sands, and sediments of estuaries, mangroves, and marine and freshwater habitats (Tubaki et al. 1975; Cooke 1979; Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer 1979). Almost all taxa isolated from those habitats, with the exception of a few facultative marine mitosporic fungi (e.g., Asteromyces cruciatus, Dendryphiella arenaria, and D. salina), appear to be terrestrial soil fungi.

Terrestrial fungal propagules are thought to accumulate and form an inactive spore bank in marine sediments because of the mycostatic effect of seawater (Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer 1979). This suggests that many fungi found in estuarine sediments are allochthonous (introduced from terrestrial sources) rather than truly marine or estuarine.

## True Marine and Estuarine Fungi

True marine fungi are those that grow and sporulate exclusively or preferentially in marine or estuarine habitats. Facultative marine fungi can complete their life cycle in both marine and terrestrial environments. The distinction is important for understanding fungal biodiversity in coastal ecosystems.

Mangrove ecosystems are particularly rich in [[marine-estuarine-fungi]], with many species showing host specificity or preference for particular mangrove tree species. The decaying wood of mangroves (especially Rhizophora, Avicennia, and Bruguiera species) supports diverse communities of marine ascomycetes and their anamorphs.

## Mangrove Wood-Decomposing Fungi

Decomposing mangrove wood is one of the best-studied substrata for marine fungi. Key groups include:

- **Marine Ascomycota**: numerous genera including Lulworthia, Halosphaeria, and Savoryella, which are largely restricted to marine and estuarine environments

- **Marine mitosporic fungi**: anamorphic stages of marine ascomycetes and facultative marine species

- **Basidiomycota**: less common in marine environments but some species contribute to wood decomposition

## Sampling Challenges

Studying fungal diversity in mangrove and estuarine ecosystems faces particular challenges:

- Tidal access requires careful timing of field work

- Salinity gradients complicate isolation and culture

- Standard mycological media may not support marine fungi

- Many marine fungi grow slowly and may be overgrown by fast-growing terrestrial contaminants

- Species identification often requires specialist expertise

Sampling protocols for mangrove fungi should include:

- Collection of wood, leaves, and roots at different tidal heights

- Use of seawater-based isolation media

- Incubation at appropriate temperatures and salinities

- Documentation of precise salinity and tidal position at collection sites

## Ecological Roles

Fungi in mangrove and estuarine ecosystems contribute to:

- Decomposition of mangrove wood and leaf litter, releasing nutrients

- Carbon cycling in coastal food webs

- Symbiotic associations with mangrove roots ([[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-network-visualization-anastomosis]] occur in some mangroves)

- Pathogenic interactions affecting mangrove health

- Food web support through fungal biomass consumed by invertebrates

## Biogeographic Patterns

Mangrove fungal communities show distinct biogeographic patterns related to temperature, latitude, and mangrove species composition. Tropical mangroves harbor higher fungal diversity than temperate ones, paralleling the general latitudinal gradient in fungal species richness. The [[fungal-biodiversity-marine-ecosystems-coastal-deep-sea]] of marine fungi is influenced by ocean currents, which can disperse fungal propagules across vast distances. Endemic species are often associated with specific mangrove tree genera, reflecting coevolutionary relationships.

## Conservation Concerns

Mangrove ecosystems are among the most threatened coastal habitats globally, with significant losses due to aquaculture, coastal development, and climate change. The loss of mangrove forests directly impacts their associated fungal communities, including many species that may be restricted to these habitats. [[fungal-biodiversity-conservation-status]] requires integrated approaches that protect both the fungal communities and their mangrove habitat.
