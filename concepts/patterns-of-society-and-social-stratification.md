# Patterns of Society and Social Stratification

## Overview

Permaculture extends its pattern analysis beyond the natural world to encompass human social structures. Research conducted in Tasmania from 1969 to 1974 examined the behavioral patterns of social animals and human societies, revealing consistent hierarchical structures that can be mapped using pattern language. Understanding these social patterns is essential for designing human settlements that work with, rather than against, natural social dynamics.

## The "Boat" Pattern of Social Hierarchy

### Research Basis

Studies surveying approximately 35,000 people across several organizations — including the local army, a multinational company, churches, and many small businesses — revealed a consistent pattern of social stratification. When plotted, the occupational hierarchy of society takes on a characteristic "boat" shape.

### Structure of the Pattern

The central axis of the boat represents the orders or castes of occupational level (status), while the width represents the number of people at each level. Even in rigorously stratified hierarchies such as the military, there are seldom more than seven major occupational levels.

### The Four Occupational Levels

**1. Low Occupational (Resource) Area — Manual and Unskilled Urban**

- General dearth of material resources
- Low social status
- Part-time or insecure occupations
- Remarkable preponderance of male births and survived male children (approximately 140 males per 100 females)
- Large families
- Serial polyandry is common or acceptable

**2. The Central or Most Populated Level — The "Middle Class"**

- Adequate resources
- Nine-to-five employment with some job tenure
- "Normal" birth ratio of approximately 104 males per 100 females
- Mixed white collar and skilled technical workers
- Average family sizes
- Monogamy as an ideal, but often expressed as serial monogamy

**3. The Upper Levels**

- Few people with extensive resources
- Flexible and often self-set schedules
- High proportion of female children (approximately 100 females to 70 males or fewer)
- Urban professionals or managers typify this group
- Small families
- Effective polygyny via concubines or mistresses

**4. Very High Levels**

- Executive directors and landed nobility
- Variable family sizes with a preponderance of female children (similar to level 3)
- Habit of lateral intermarriage for economic alliances
- Facilitated by exclusive schools and resorts

### Genetic Streaming Between Levels

The imbalance of sex ratios across strata ensures a genetic turnover or diffusion between classes. This creates a streaming of genetic material between levels over generations, driven by the surplus of one sex in each stratum seeking partners from adjacent levels.

## Implications for Permaculture Design

### Designing for Social Reality

Understanding these social patterns allows permaculture designers to create settlements and systems that accommodate rather than resist natural social dynamics:

1. **Resource distribution**: Settlements should provide pathways between resource levels that allow natural social mobility
2. **Housing diversity**: Design should accommodate the varying family sizes, resource levels, and lifestyle patterns characteristic of different occupational levels
3. **Community structure**: The seven-level hierarchy suggests that even in small communities, differentiation of roles occurs naturally

### Genetic and Cultural Flow

The genetic streaming between social levels represents a form of system resilience. Just as genetic diversity strengthens biological systems, social mobility strengthens cultural systems. Permaculture designs that create rigid barriers between social levels work against this natural flow.

## The Arts in the Service of Life

### Art as Public Mnemonic

Art, in the forms of song, dance, sculptural or painted objects, or designs, is an ancient preoccupation of all peoples. Most tribal art serves quite specific ends — it functions as a public and ever-renewed mnemonic, or memory-aid. Apparently simple spiral or linear designs can combine thousands of bits of information in a single, deceptively simple pattern.

### Modern Art vs. Tribal Art

Much modern art is individualistic and decorative. Some "motif" art is plagiarized from ancient origins but no longer carries educational or sacred function. The modern "monoculture" — based on advertising jingles and purely decorative patterns, with education divorced from relevant long-term observations of the natural world — represents a significant loss of the art-as-knowledge tradition.

### Destruction of Indigenous Art Systems

Most tribal art has been burnt, looted, destroyed, and broken by invading systems — by those seeking secret power rather than open knowledge, or by those who are merely destructive. Book-burning and image-breaking represents the reaction of the alienated to the accumulated wisdom of their ancestors.

### The Challenge to Artists and Educators

The challenge to artists is to study and portray knowledge in a compact, memorable, and transmissible form, to research and recreate surviving art forms that retain their meaning, and to reintegrate art with science and society's functions and needs.

### Flowform Systems as Applied Art

The "Flowform" system developed at the Virbella Institute, Emerson College, Kent, UK, serves both an aesthetic and a water-oxygenation function. These sculptural forms induce three distinct mixing effects: vertical overturn, figure-8 lateral flow, and an interactive rhythm that pulses water side to side. They have practical use in sewage treatment, pond oxygenation, and as instructive hydrological pattern models.

## Universal Belief Systems

Many world beliefs share an essential core derived from contemplation of natural events. They indicate a way of life and a philosophy rather than a dogma. When searching for the roots of belief, one returns repeatedly to the oneness underlying science, word, song, art, and pattern — "The jewel in the heart of the lotus."

## Relevance to Permaculture

1. **Settlement design**: Communities should be designed with awareness of natural social stratification patterns
2. **Information systems**: Permaculture should incorporate mnemonic art forms that make knowledge accessible to all
3. **Cultural preservation**: Understanding indigenous knowledge reinforces the permaculture ethic of preserving traditional ecological knowledge
4. **Applied aesthetics**: Functional beauty models the permaculture principle that every element should serve multiple functions
