## Identification and Safety of Edible Mushroom Families

## The Problem of Mushroom Poisoning

Julius A. Palmer Jr. documented numerous cases of mushroom poisoning that resulted from ignorance rather than from inherent danger of all wild fungi. In September 1879, a New York family was badly poisoned; two persons died, and a survivor sent a specimen to the *Scientific American* claiming they had been poisoned by the common [[classification-of-cultivated-mushroom-species]] — a conclusion Palmer demonstrated was erroneous. The survivor could not reliably identify which species they had actually consumed.

Palmer emphasized that a gentleman once sent him specimens from three independent genera, all "as unlike in properties and appearance as currants from grapes," yet blamed them all for his illness. Probably none had caused his indisposition. The key principle: one must know precisely what species one is consuming, just as with any other food.

## Why Mushrooms Cause Illness

Palmer identified three reasons mushrooms may be unfit for food:

1. **Tasteless alkaloids**: The most dangerous category. These compounds, such as [[amanitine]] from *[[amanita]]* species, produce no immediate taste or symptoms but cause irreversible organ damage. No chemist of Palmer"s era could detect the cause, and few medical men would suspect it.
2. **Acrid, bitter, or nauseous principles**: Unpleasant but generally not life-threatening. The non-edible principle, like that in eggplant, can often be removed by salt and water treatment.
3. **Simple digestive incompatibility**: Some individuals may find particular edible species disagreeable, just as with any food. This category should be avoided by sensitive individuals but does not indicate the species is poisonous.

A critical principle Palmer established: "A variety once found edible never changes its properties; that which is true of other plants is true of these." This controverted theories from authorities like Berkeley of England and Cordier of France.

## Systematic Testing Methodology

Palmer developed a careful protocol for testing unknown mushrooms:

1. Take a small piece of flesh from the cap, clear of the spore-bearing surface (gills or pores)
2. Eat it first thing in the morning on an empty stomach
3. If no ill effects follow, increase the dose daily
4. Continue until a whole specimen has been consumed
5. Never reverse a decision once a species has passed testing

This methodology, combined with careful botanical identification, provided a safety framework that Palmer followed throughout his career without incident.

## The Role of Amanitine in Poisoning

Palmer noted that the identification of an *Amanita* among consumed mushrooms, combined with the length of time between eating and the onset of symptoms, could inform treatment. [[amanitine-poisoning-and-absorption-mechanisms]] required specific intervention — atropine — while less virulent poisons from other genera could be treated with milder remedies. The delay between ingestion and symptoms was diagnostic: amanitine acts slowly and insidiously, while other fungal toxins typically produce faster reactions.

## The Puff-ball Family (Lycoperdaceae)

Puff-balls are among the safest [[query-what-are-the-most-common-edible-wild-mushrooms-for-beginners]] novice foragers. Palmer"s rule was simple: wherever a puff-ball is cut open and found perfectly white throughout, it may be eaten. There are no poisonous varieties in this family. The key warning signs are:

- Any discoloration (yellow, purple, or dark) inside indicates the specimen is past its prime
- The interior must be uniformly white and firm
- Specimens should be gathered before they begin producing spores (which turn them dark and powdery inside)

## The Boleti

The Boleti represent a large group of fleshy mushrooms distinguished by their underside, which is composed of a porous or sponge-like stratum rather than gills. Key characteristics:

- The pore surface (underside of the cap) is typically white, yellow, olive, or brown
- Many Boleti turn blue or red when bruised or cut — a change Palmer explicitly noted does NOT indicate poison
- Italian immigrants were observed harvesting American Boleti, using the same species identification they practiced in Europe
- Palmer found them "generally delicious"

The most famous edible bolete is the porcini (*[[boletus-edulis-and-pore-fungi-polyporaceae]]*), though Palmer worked with many American species. The pore surface should be checked for insect damage, as Boleti are particularly attractive to larvae.

## The Russulae

Palmer made an early and significant contribution by segregating the Russula family from its neighbors based on consistent morphological traits:

- **Brittle flesh**: The [[cap-and-stem-alkaloid-distribution-in-psilocybe-cubensis-strains]] break cleanly and crisply, unlike the fibrous texture of many other mushrooms
- **No juice**: Unlike the related Lactarii, Russulae produce no latex when cut
- **Equal gills**: The gills are attached to the stem and are of uniform length (not forking or arranged in tiers)

Palmer"s insight came from observing forest animals — squirrels holding Russulae as they held nuts, snakes and land tortoises feeding on them. Despite textbooks characterizing the family as "of doubtful use, and frequent danger," Palmer concluded that all pleasant-tasting Russulae were edible. His correspondent Casamajor confirmed this by gathering basketfuls with his children, excluding anything that was not recognizably Russula, then taste-testing each specimen before cooking.

## The Lactarii (Milk Mushrooms)

The Lactarii are closely related to Russulae but are distinguished by the milky latex they exude when cut or broken. This is an albuminous juice holding resin in solution. Key points:

- When the milk is naturally mild (not bitter or acrid), the mushroom is directly edible
- When the milk is acrid or peppery, salt and water treatment changes its nature while leaving the mushroom substance intact
- The family is "very properly called the Lactarii"
- Palmer acknowledged it was possible that dangerous members existed among Lactarii and Russulae, but stated he had never discovered such in years of experience

## The Clavaria (Coral Fungi)

Coral fungi appear in autumn forests as club-shaped or branching, coral-like groups growing from a common base. They range from a few inches to two feet in height. Palmer"s assessment was enthusiastic:

- "More delicate food than cauliflower"
- "Nicer in soup or stew than vermicelli"
- Should be cut off just below the top
- Only young, hard specimens should be chosen — they quickly become full of maggots
- Specimens should be gathered perfectly clean

They are generally safe when not bitter, though some species may cause mild gastrointestinal upset in sensitive individuals.

## Color Changes as a Safety Indicator

The widespread belief that color change indicates poison is one of the most persistent and dangerous myths in mycology. Palmer documented several edible species that change color dramatically:

- ***[[boletus-cyanescens]]***: Turns entirely blue when cut or bruised — edible
- Many other Boleti: Assume lighter blue shades — edible
- One unidentified species: Turns blood red — edible

Meanwhile, the deadly *Amanita* "remains the purest white under all conditions." The most reliable field mark for distinguishing the deadly *Amanita* from its closest edible lookalike is that the edible species" gills turn pink or red upon exposure to fire (cooking heat).

## Building Discrimination Skills

Palmer noted that even children could rapidly develop reliable [[mushroom-identification]] skills. He described one young woman in her teens who was fond of eating raw Russulae and whose "discrimination was unerring" — she consistently avoided dangerous species "although clothed in the snowy semblance of purity." For the majority of people without such natural aptitude, Palmer advised selecting twenty easily recognized edible varieties and leaving all others alone.
