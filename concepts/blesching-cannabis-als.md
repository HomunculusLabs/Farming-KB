# Cannabis and Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

## Overview

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as Lou Gehrig's disease, is a
chronic degenerative neurological illness associated with the selective loss of
motor neurons in the brain and spinal cord. The name derives from Greek:
"a-myo-trophic" (no muscle nourishment), "lateral" (referring to affected
nerves on either side), and "sclerosis" (hardening). Without neuronal
nourishment, muscles waste away along with the ability to initiate movement.

## Disease Profile

ALS typically begins spontaneously in otherwise healthy adults with symptoms
ranging from isolated muscle weakness and twitching to difficulty speaking,
muscle cramps, spasms, and episodes of uncontrollable laughter or crying. These
progress to advanced-stage symptoms including difficulty swallowing. Most ALS
patients eventually die of respiratory failure. While late-stage patients may
descend into total paralysis, their minds largely remain unaffected.

- Incidence: approximately 2 per 100,000 in the U.S.
- Median survival: 3-5 years after onset; some survive up to 10 years
- No cure exists in orthodox medicine
- Elevated glutamate levels in spinal fluid are a hallmark finding
- Pharmaceutical riluzole may extend lifespan by up to 2 months

## Cannabis Health Index Assessment

- Total Number of Studies Reviewed: 7
- CHI Value: 19

## Mechanisms of Action

Researchers suggest that cannabis, with its pain-killing, muscle-relaxing,
bronchodilating, saliva-reducing, appetite-stimulating, sleep-inducing,
antioxidative, and neuroprotective properties, may serve as a practical
therapeutic agent in ALS management. A landmark 2010 Seattle trial on mice led
scientists to propose an optimal ALS treatment regimen that would include
glutamate antagonists, antioxidants, centrally acting anti-inflammatory agents,
microglial cell modulators (including TNF-alpha inhibitors), antiapoptotic
agents, neurotrophic growth factors, and mitochondrial function-enhancing
agents. Remarkably, cannabis appears to have activity in all of those areas.

## Key Research Findings

- A 2010 Swiss trial with 27 ALS patients receiving 5 mg THC twice daily
  showed no change in cramps (CHI 0)
- A 2010 University of Washington mouse study demonstrated that cannabinoids
  prolonged neuronal cell survival, delayed onset of ALS symptoms, and slowed
  disease progression (CHI 2)
- A 2010 Italian review confirmed cannabinoids regulate immune responses and
  protect nerve cell function and integrity (CHI 4)
- A 2005 University of Washington study found CBN delivered via implanted
  osmotic mini-pumps significantly delayed disease onset by more than two
  weeks (CHI 2)
- A 2004 survey of 131 ALS patients at the University of Washington found
  cannabis moderately effective at reducing appetite loss, depression, pain,
  spasticity, and drooling (CHI 3)
- A 2001 preclinical review suggested ALS patients may benefit from analgesia,
  muscle relaxation, bronchodilation, saliva reduction, appetite stimulation,
  sleep induction, and neuroprotective effects (CHI 4)

## Strain-Specific Considerations

The reviewed studies generally suggest full-spectrum cannabinoids that bind
with both CB1 and CB2 receptors. CBN has proven effective in delaying symptom
onset in mice, though no human trial has yet confirmed these findings. Some
ALS patients prefer indica or indica-dominant strains with higher CBD/CBN:THC
profiles. Others prioritize symptoms and select strains accordingly.

## Mind-Body Medicine and ALS

A study of 56 ALS patients and caregivers concluded that clinical depression
is not an inevitable outcome of life-threatening illness, even with major
disability. Lou Gehrig himself expressed only gratitude in his 1939 farewell
speech. Stephen Hawking, the renowned physicist who lived with ALS for decades,
stated: "I have lived with the prospect of an early death for the last 49
years. I'm not afraid of death, but I'm in no hurry to die."

The widening rift between diminishing body and functioning mind in ALS may
offer insight into the condition's deeper message. Some neurologists refer to
this as "locked-in syndrome." Aggravating factors may include military service
exposure and environmental toxins.
