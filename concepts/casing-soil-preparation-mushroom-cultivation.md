# Casing Soil Preparation and Application for Mushroom Cultivation

Casing is the process of applying a non-nutritive soil layer on top of fully colonised substrate to stimulate and support fruiting body formation. Unlike the nutritious substrate below, the casing provides a microclimate of moisture and humidity at the surface while triggering environmental cues that initiate [[fruiting-body-development]]. The technique is used in both commercial mushroom farming and amateur cultivation, and is particularly important for species that do not fruit readily from exposed grain.

## Purpose of Casing

The casing layer serves several critical functions:

- **Moisture reservoir**: Holds water at the substrate surface, maintaining the high humidity primordia (pinheads) require. Without it, exposed grain dries out rapidly and fails to support pin formation.
- **Microclimate creation**: Provides a buffer zone of stable temperature and humidity between substrate and ambient air, reducing the impact of room condition fluctuations.
- **Fruiting trigger**: The physical and chemical properties simulate the natural soil surface, signalling mycelium to shift from vegetative growth to reproductive mode. Changes in CO2 concentration, humidity, and surface texture all contribute.
- **Contamination barrier**: Properly prepared casing provides some protection against airborne contaminants landing directly on mycelium.

## Casing Soil Composition

The recommended recipe uses three components in specific ratios refined through extensive experimentation:

**10 parts peat** provides the organic matrix and water-holding capacity. Peat is preferred over garden soil for its low nutrient content and excellent water retention. Sphagnum peat moss is standard. Peat naturally has a pH of 3.5-4.5 — too acidic for mushroom cultivation and requiring correction.

**5 parts vermiculite** improves aeration and moisture retention. It absorbs and slowly releases water, maintaining consistent moisture levels. It also prevents peat from compacting into a dense, airless layer that would suffocate developing mycelium.

**2 parts limestone (marl)** raises pH to approximately 7.0-7.5. Most contaminant moulds prefer acidic conditions, while mushroom mycelium tolerates near-neutral pH. The limestone provides a selective advantage to cultivated species. Calcium also serves as a structural component for fruiting bodies.

## Preparation Method

### Dry Mixing

Combine all three ingredients in dry form and mix thoroughly. Even limestone distribution is critical — acidic pH pockets become contamination sites that spread. Use a large clean bucket or tub. The mixture should be homogenous in appearance and colour before water is added.

### Hydration

Add water gradually while stirring. Target "field capacity" — the maximum water content without the mixture becoming muddy. When squeezed firmly, a handful should hold together but drip no water. Releasing should cause the clump to break with a gentle tap.

Water quantity depends on the peat's initial moisture, which varies between sources, seasons, and storage conditions. Add incrementally and test frequently.

### Correction for Over-Watering

If too wet, add more dry ingredients (in the 10:5:2 ratio) to restore proper moisture. Spreading thin to dry is ineffective and risks contamination.

## Sterilisation Debate

### Arguments Against Sterilisation

Some authors advise against sterilising casing, arguing it kills beneficial microorganisms that suppress competitor moulds, break down organic matter, and create natural soil ecology. Pasteurisation (60-70°C for several hours) is sometimes recommended as a compromise preserving heat-sensitive beneficials while killing pathogens.

### Arguments For Sterilisation

The source documentation reports consistently bad experiences with untreated or pasteurised casing soils. [[Sterilisation]] at 121°C for one hour in a [[pressure cooker]] provides the most reliable results. The pragmatic view: while beneficial organisms may help theoretically, untreated soil's contamination risk outweighs any benefit. The documentation states: "We just tell what works best for us!"

### Practical Implementation

Place casing soil in oven bags (nylon), autoclave bags (polypropylene), or jars. Sterilise at 15 PSI for one hour. Cool to room temperature before use. Prepared casing can be stored sealed for several days.

## Application Technique

When [[grass-seed substrate]] is fully colonised:

1. Shake colonised grain loose and pour into 1-litre disposable plastic trays (one jar per tray)
2. Break up lumps using the clean jar rim for an even grain bed
3. Level the grain surface evenly with a clean utensil
4. Apply casing to 1.5-2.0 cm depth using a large spoon and fork
5. Create an even but rough surface — small valleys and hills increase surface area and create pinning micro-environments
6. Cover with aluminium foil; place at 20-25°C in a clean location
7. Wait for mycelium to grow visibly through the casing before exposing to air and light

The rough surface is important: smooth casings lead to uneven pinning, while micro-topography creates multiple humidity zones at the soil-air interface.

## Timing and Fruiting Initiation

Mycelium typically breaks through the casing within a few days. Exposure timing depends on strain and species. Too early causes poor pinning; too late causes excessive overlay — a dense mycelial mat inhibiting pin formation. Some strains fruit immediately upon breakthrough; others need several more days of casing colonisation before triggering.

## Casing Maintenance During Fruiting

Once the [[fruiting-body-development]] cycle has been initiated, the casing requires ongoing maintenance:

- **Misting**: The casing surface should be misted daily to maintain moisture. Never spray directly — use a fine mist from above and allow droplets to settle gently.
- **Avoid over-watering**: After a flush is picked, watering should be temporarily increased because maturing mushrooms pull significant moisture from the casing. However, the casing should never be allowed to become waterlogged.
- **Clean the surface**: Remove dead pinheads (aborts) promptly, as they can become sites for mould colonisation on the casing surface.
- **Flush management**: Well-maintained casings can produce 3-6 flushes depending on species and care. [[Psilocybe mexicana]] typically produces one large flush and a smaller second flush, while other species produce more consistent yields across multiple flushes.

## Fruiting Environment

The source documentation describes a simple but effective fruiting setup using clear plastic bins covered with polyethylene sheeting. These bins are stackable and space-efficient. Key features include:

- Several small holes melted in the sides for air exchange, optionally covered with mesh to exclude flies
- Five cased trays per bin — evaporation from the casing surface provides sufficient humidity
- Daily misting to maintain casing moisture without waterlogging
- Cold-shocking harder-to-fruit strains by placing them in a refrigerator overnight before putting them in the bins
- Pinheads appearing 6-15 days after introducing casings to the bins, with mushrooms maturing in 5-7 days

Notably, [[Psilocybe mexicana]], [[Psilocybe tampanensis]], and [[Panaeolus subbalteatus]] do not benefit from cold-shock treatment. Only certain difficult strains require it.

## Simplicity as a Design Principle

A notable theme in the source documentation is the emphasis on simplicity over sophistication. The authors explicitly state that elaborate setups with humidifiers and cool-mist devices are unnecessary. Their approach uses basic clear plastic bins with polyethylene sheeting, relying on natural evaporation from the casing surface to maintain humidity. This philosophy extends throughout the document — from substrate preparation (simple grass seed rather than complex nutrient formulations) to sterilisation (standard pressure cooker rather than laboratory autoclave) to fruiting (basic bins rather than climate-controlled chambers. This pragmatic approach makes the techniques accessible to cultivators with limited resources while still producing reliable results.

## Species-Specific Fruiting Behaviour

Different species exhibit distinct fruiting patterns when cased on grass seed substrate:

- **[[Psilocybe cubensis]]** strains vary considerably. The documentation mentions "Ecuador cubensis" as an easy-fruiting strain that does not require cold shock, while other strains benefit from the treatment. First flushes are typically the largest.
- **[[Psilocybe mexicana]]** usually produces one large flush followed by a noticeably smaller second flush. Total flush count is typically lower than cubensis.
- **[[Psilocybe tampanensis]]** fruits less prolifically than cubensis but produces sclerotia as a supplementary harvest from both the jar and the casing layer.
- **[[Panaeolus subbalteatus]]** has specific environmental requirements and does not benefit from cold shock.

The documentation notes that developing a "feeling" for the watering regime is essential — explicit directions are difficult to provide because optimal moisture varies with species, strain, ambient conditions, and casing depth. This skill develops with experience across multiple grows.
