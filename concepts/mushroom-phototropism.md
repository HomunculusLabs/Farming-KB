# Mushroom Phototropism

Phototropism in mushrooms is the directional growth response to light. Unlike phototropism in plants — which is predominantly positive (growth toward light) and mediated by auxin redistribution — mushroom phototropism involves multiple distinct responses to light at different developmental stages and intensities. At least four different light responses have been described for mushrooms (Eger-Hummel, 1980), and *[[basidiocarp-four-stage-development-classification-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]]* exhibits a particularly complex relationship with light that intersects with its anemotropic and geotropic behaviors.

## Light Responses in Mushroom Development

Mushrooms respond to light at multiple stages of their life cycle, and these responses serve different functions:

### 1. Basidiocarp Initiation

Light is required for the initiation (formation) of basidiocarps in many mushroom species. In *P. cubensis*, Badham (1980) demonstrated that basidiocarp initiation is light-dependent, with the fungus requiring a light stimulus to trigger the transition from vegetative mycelial growth to reproductive [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]]. Without light, fully colonized substrates may remain in vegetative state indefinitely.

### 2. Stipe Elongation

Light influences the rate and direction of stipe elongation. In *P. cubensis*, light exerts an inhibitory effect upon stipe elongation — mushrooms grown in higher light tend to have shorter, thicker stipes than those grown in lower light or darkness. This is consistent with observations in *Coprinus congregatus* and other species.

### 3. Pileus Expansion and Spore Production

Light is necessary for normal pileus expansion [[mushroom-geotropism-and-spore-formation]]. In *P. cubensis*, mushrooms kept in darkness produce few or no spores and do not develop fully expanded caps. The light requirement for sporulation appears to be relatively low — Badham used only 0.002 calories/cm²/min in the wind tunnel experiments, which was approximately 1% of the light intensity expected in natural field conditions.

### 4. Geotropic Sensitivity

The negative [[spore-formation-geotropic-response-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]] (upward curvature) that emerges at Stage 2 of development requires light. This could be because:
- Light is directly required for the geotropic sensing mechanism
- Light is indirectly required because it is necessary for spore production, and spore production is linked to the geotropic shift
- Light triggers hormonal changes that enable geotropic sensitivity

The observation that sporeless mutants do not exhibit the geotropic shift (even in light) supports the second explanation.

## Phototropic Directional Response

In Badham's (1982) wind tunnel experiments, the phototropic response of *P. cubensis* was examined by varying light direction:

### Light from Above

With light incident from above and wind from the side:
- Stage 1–2: Stipe grows into the wind (anemotropism dominates)
- Stage 2+: Pileus turns upward (geotropism dominates), regardless of light position

### Light from Below

With light incident from below and wind from the side:
- The geotropic response still occurred at Stage 2+, with the pileus turning upward
- The mushroom grew against the light direction to orient vertically
- This confirms that at Stage 2+, geotropism overrides phototropism

### Constant Light

Under constant illumination (no dark period):
- Growth and pileus expansion continued, but the specific tropic interactions were modified
- Mushrooms rotated parallel to the airflow showed stipe elongation and pileus opening into the wind, suggesting that continuous light may alter the balance between tropic stimuli

## Light Intensity and Evaporative Demand

Badham proposed that light's influence on mushroom tropisms may be mediated through its effect on evaporative demand rather than through a dedicated photoreceptor pathway:

- Light heats the mushroom surface, increasing water evaporation
- If strong relative to wind and humidity, light becomes the dominant factor in evaporative demand
- This could be the mechanism underlying phototropism: the illuminated side of the stipe dries faster, creating a differential growth response

However, the light intensity used in the experiments (0.002 cal/cm²/min) was very low — approximately 1% of natural field conditions. At this low intensity, the direct heating effect is minimal, which raises the question of whether:

1. Light at natural intensities acts primarily through evaporative demand (a physical mechanism)
2. Light at low intensities triggers hormonal responses (a biological mechanism)
3. Both mechanisms operate simultaneously, with the dominant one depending on intensity

## Blue Light Photoreception

Eger-Hummel (1980) reviewed blue light photomorphogenesis in fungi, establishing that many fungal light responses — including those in mushrooms — are mediated by blue light receptors rather than the red/far-red phytochrome system used by plants. Key features of fungal blue light responses:

- Peak sensitivity is in the blue region of the spectrum (approximately 450–480 nm)
- "Cool white" fluorescent tubes (used by Badham) emit significant blue light, making them suitable [[grass-seed-substrate-for-mushroom-cultivation]]
- The photoreceptor molecules have not been definitively identified in most mushroom species, though flavin-based photoreceptors are suspected

## Light's Role in Geotropic vs. Anemotropic Balance

The relationship between light, wind, and gravity responses creates a three-way interaction:

| Condition | Stage 1–2 Response | Stage 2+ Response |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Light above + wind | Anemotropic (into wind) | Geotropic (upward) |
| Light below + wind | Anemotropic (into wind) | Geotropic (upward, against light) |
| Dark + wind | Anemotropic (into wind) | No geotropic shift (few spores) |
| Constant light + rotation parallel to wind | Stipe elongation into wind | Pileus opens into wind |

The consistent observation is that **anemotropism operates independently of light**, while **geotropism requires light** (indirectly, through the light requirement for sporulation).

## Practical Implications for Cultivation

### Light Spectrum

"Cool white" [[cervantes-compact-fluorescent-lighting]] provides appropriate blue light wavelengths for mushroom development. Full-spectrum LED grow lights with significant blue output are also suitable. The key is providing adequate blue light (450–480 nm) rather than high total light intensity.

### Light Intensity

Mushrooms do not require high light intensities. Badham's study used only 0.002 cal/cm²/min — far below photosynthetic light levels. For cultivation:

- Too much light inhibits stipe elongation, producing short-stemmed mushrooms
- Too little light delays or prevents basidiocarp initiation and reduces spore production
- Indirect ambient room lighting is often sufficient; direct light is rarely needed

### Light Duration

A 12-hour photoperiod (used in Badham's study) is standard for *P. cubensis* cultivation. The dark period is important — constant light modifies tropic responses and may not be optimal for normal development.

### Light Direction

For straight stem growth, light should come from directly above. Side lighting can induce curvature, though in *P. cubensis* this effect is modest compared to wind-induced curvature. The fact that geotropism overrides phototropism at maturity means that light direction primarily affects early vegetative growth orientation.

## Comparison with Coprinus

*Psilocybe cubensis* is similar to *Coprinus congregatus* in its light responses, showing at least four distinct phases of light sensitivity. Both species require light for fruiting body initiation, show light-modulated stipe elongation, need light for normal cap development, and exhibit light-influenced tropic behavior. This convergence suggests that the underlying photoreceptor and signaling systems are conserved across agarics.
