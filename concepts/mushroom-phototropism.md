# Mushroom Phototropism (Light Response)

Phototropism is the growth response of an organism to light. In mushrooms,
light does not serve as an energy source (mushrooms are heterotrophic), but
it acts as a critical environmental signal that influences multiple stages of
the life cycle including [[light-basidiocarp-initiation-psilocybe-cubensis-research]], stipe elongation, pileus
orientation, and spore production. Studies on [[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]] have
identified at least four distinct light-mediated responses.

## Overview

Mushrooms exhibit complex phototropic behaviors that differ from the simple
phototropism seen in photosynthetic plants. Rather than growing toward light
for energy, mushrooms use light as an informational cue to coordinate their
development with environmental conditions favorable for spore production and
dispersal.

## Four Light Responses in Mushrooms

Eger-Hummel (1980) described at least four different responses to light in
mushrooms, and P. cubensis is similar to Coprinus congregatus in exhibiting
all of them:

### 1. Basidiocarp Initiation

Light is required for the initiation of basidiocarps (fruitbodies). Badham
(1980) demonstrated that P. cubensis requires light exposure to trigger the
formation of primordia from colonized substrate. Without light, mycelium
continues to grow vegetatively but does not produce mushrooms.

The light intensity required for initiation is relatively low. Badham's
experiments used cool white fluorescent light at 2685 lux with a 12-hour
photoperiod, though the threshold for initiation may be considerably lower.

### 2. Stipe Elongation Control

Light exerts an **inhibitory effect** upon stipe elongation. In the presence
of light, stipes tend to be shorter and more robust. In darkness, stipes
elongate excessively, producing tall, thin, etiolated fruitbodies with
underdeveloped caps. This response ensures that mushrooms develop normally
only when conditions are appropriate for eventual spore dispersal.

### 3. Pileus Orientation

Light provides directional information that influences pileus orientation,
though this effect interacts strongly with geotropism and anemotropism. When
light is the dominant directional cue (in the absence of strong wind and with
gravity equalized by rotation), mushrooms grow toward the light source.

### 4. Spore Formation

Light is necessary for normal spore production in P. cubensis. When
basidiocarps are grown in darkness, few or no spores form. This links the
phototropic response to the reproductive function of the mushroom — there is
no adaptive value in producing spores in dark environments where dispersal
would be ineffective.

## Light Quality: Blue Light Response

Eger-Hummel (1980) noted that mushroom photomorphogenesis is primarily a
**blue light response**. The photoreceptors involved are most sensitive to
wavelengths in the blue region of the spectrum (approximately 400–500 nm).
This is consistent with the use of fluorescent "cool white" lighting in
cultivation, which has a strong blue component.

Blue light receptors in fungi differ from the chlorophyll-based
photoreceptors of plants and may involve flavin-based photoreceptors similar
to the White Collar complex first characterized in Neurospora crassa.

## Light as a Modifier of Evaporative Demand

Badham proposed that light's influence on tropisms may be mediated partly
through its effect on evaporative demand:

1. Light heats the surface of the mushroom, increasing water evaporation
2. If strong relative to wind and humidity, light could become the dominant
   factor in evaporative demand
3. The resulting moisture gradient could drive differential cell growth on
   opposite sides of the stipe

However, the light flux density in Badham's experiments (0.002 cal/cm²/min)
was only about 1/1000 of natural sunlight, suggesting that direct heating
effects were minimal. This leaves open the possibility that light triggers
hormonal responses rather than (or in addition to) thermal effects.

## Interaction with Geotropism

The relationship between light and geotropism in P. cubensis is indirect:

- Light is necessary for spore production
- Spore production is linked to the onset of negative geotropism
- Therefore, light is indirectly necessary for the geotropic response, but
  only through its role in enabling sporulation

This was demonstrated by the fact that sporeless mutants did not show
negative geotropism even under normal lighting conditions.

## Cultivation Lighting Recommendations

Based on the photobiology of P. cubensis and related species:

- **Light source**: Cool white fluorescent or LED with blue spectrum
  component is ideal
- **Photoperiod**: 12 hours on / 12 hours off is standard and effective
- **Intensity**: Moderate (1000–3000 lux); excessive light is unnecessary
  and may be inhibitory
- **Direction**: Light from above promotes normal upright growth; side
  lighting can cause bending
- **Duration**: Light is needed for [[chen-maitake-primordia-initiation-fruiting]] and throughout
  fruiting for normal cap development and spore production
- **Dark periods**: Essential; constant light is not optimal and may stress
  the organism

## Phototropism in Other Fungi

- **Coprinus congregatus** — shows four light responses similar to P. cubensis
- **Phycomyces blakesleeanus** — has an extremely sensitive phototropic
  response studied extensively as a model system for fungal photobiology
- **[[agaricus-bisporus]]** — requires light for normal fruiting, though less
  dramatically than tropical species
- **[[schizophyllum-commune]]** — phototropic and geotropic responses vary
  between normal and mutant fruitbodies (Schwalb and Shanler, 1974)
- **[[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]** — shows strong positive phototropism, growing
  toward light to orient the gill surface for spore release
