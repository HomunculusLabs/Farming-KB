# Tagasaste as Pioneer Fodder Tree in Permaculture

Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus palmensis), also known as tree lucerne, is a
nitrogen-fixing legume native to the Canary Islands and one of the most
useful [[permaculture-pioneer-species]] available to permaculture designers. It belongs to the family
Fabaceae/Papilionaceae and serves multiple functions including livestock
fodder, bee forage, nurse cropping, and firewood production.

## Botanical Description and Growth Habit

Tagasaste is generally a fairly short-lived plant, although regular
cutting or browsing can considerably extend its life expectancy. It grows
to a height of up to 7 metres and is capable of productive growth in
areas of low rainfall (as little as 300 mm). Drought tolerance is
improved when phosphorus is readily available, and liberal application of
rock phosphate at planting time works well. The nitrogen-fixing bacteria
does not generally need introduction with the plant, as it is usually
present in the soil from other [[nitrogen-fixing-trees]] species including Acacia spp, Albizia spp,
and Cassia spp.

Today tagasaste is a naturalised coloniser of Australian wastelands,
capable of surviving on a wide range of soils. In Western Australia,
50,000 hectares of mostly marginal farmland had been planted to tagasaste
by 1996, with an estimated 1.5 million hectares of Australian farms
identified as potentially benefiting from the species.

## Nutritional Value as Stock Feed

Tagasaste is a fast-growing, highly productive stock feed with high
nutrition levels, low toxicity, and a high degree of palatability. See [[forage-and-fodder-trees-permaculture-systems]] for a broader look at fodder tree systems. Its
foliage compares favourably with common stock feeds:

- Crude protein: 17-31% (compared to 21% for lucerne)
- Carbohydrate: 38-58%
- Crude fat: 2-6%
- Crude fibre: 14-30%
- Calcium: 0.48-1.62%
- Phosphorus: 0.12-0.41%

## Stock Carrying Capacity

Research at the Martindale Research Project in Western Australia
demonstrated remarkable results on near-useless sandy soils capable of
yielding only 700 kg of wheat. Tagasaste yielded the feed equivalent of
1,100 kg of wheat. By year 6, tagasaste-only systems supported 35
sheep/ha compared to just 1 sheep/ha on pasture alone. Combined
tagasaste and pasture systems supported 37 sheep/ha by year 6.

Ewes on tagasaste over summer/autumn grew 30% more wool with the same
yield but slightly broader micron compared with traditionally managed
flock mates. The ovulation rate and twinning rate were also significantly
higher. One farmer claimed tagasaste lifted profit from $6/ha to $30/ha.

## Fodder System Design

To maximise stock feed potential, trees should be trimmed at a very
early age to encourage a bushy rather than erect habit. This keeps
foliage within reach of stock and makes ring-barking virtually
impossible. Trees are ideally arranged into lock-up [[forage-and-fodder-trees-permaculture-systems]] to allow recovery from grazing and to avoid the time-consuming
cut-and-carry method. Well-fenced hedge rows can be constantly grazed.

Tagasaste is readily eaten by all types of stock including pigs, horses,
and rabbits, and can comprise a major portion of their diet. The seed
is also valuable as free-range [[permaculture-designers-manual-animal-forage-systems]], with 25% crude protein content in the seed.

## Bee Forage Value

Tagasaste is a valuable source of feed for bees and flowers in winter,
a time when little else blooms. Cutting different plants at different
times can significantly extend the flowering period of a tagasaste stand.

## Nurse Crop Function

Tagasaste can be used as a [[tagasaste-nurse-crop-fodder-systems]] for other trees.
In trials in New Zealand with various fruit and nut trees, trees growing
in close association with tagasaste (as close as 1 m) consistently grew
better than controls without tagasaste.

When grown close to timber trees, tagasaste forces them upward for
light, producing straight, long timber. For fruit or nut trees where a
tagasaste should be clipped and used as
[[comparison-sheet-mulching-vs-chop-and-drop]] around the nursed tree.

## Other Uses

The wood is a good firewood and has potential as a hard, light wood for
tool handles and craft work. It is a prolific producer of seed. The
The plant serves as an excellent demonstration of the [[mollison-pioneer-species-and-accelerated-succession]] pattern described in permaculture design.

## Companion Plants

Tagasaste grows well with Albizia lophantha and Acacia decurrens as
fellow nurse plants. Other pioneers in the same ecological niche include
Cassia spp, Cytisus spp, and Prosopis spp. For more on integrating
tagasaste with other species, see [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]].

## Propagation

Propagation is typically by seed, which is produced abundantly. The seed
requires scarification to germinate, which can be achieved by soaking in
boiling water.

## Key Design Considerations

- Planting density: approximately 1,000 trees/ha for fodder systems
- Edible yield reaches 19 tonnes dry matter/ha/yr by year 6
- Minimum rainfall requirement: 300 mm annually
- Soil adaptability: wide range, including sandy and marginal soils
- Frost tolerance: moderate; young seedlings need protection
