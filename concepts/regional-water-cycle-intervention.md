# Regional Water Cycle Intervention

## Overview

Humans are not powerless in the face of water scarcity. A range of strategies exist for increasing local precipitation, enhancing soil water storage, and modifying regional water cycles. These interventions range from cloud seeding technologies to strategic tree planting on ridgelines, and from soil conditioning to earthwork systems that maximise infiltration. ## Soil Storages and Conditioning

### Soil Treatment Effects

Soil conditioning or "ripping" (deep ploughing to break compacted layers), when followed by tree plantation, trace element additions, and non-destructive agriculture, increases the ability of soils to hold and infiltrate water by factors of **up to 70–85%**. Areas of up to 85% run-off can be converted to **zero overland flow** through a combination of soil conditioning, swales, and water spreading to forests.

### The Soil as the Largest Water Storage

Soils can contain many times the water of open storages or streams. As both throughflow, baseflow, and plant-available water increase, the cycling of water via evapotranspiration and rainfall also increases. Soil treatments need to precede tree planting over almost every area that has been used by contemporary agriculture.

Trees are essential to prevent water-logging of soils and soil salting in the long term.

## Infiltration via Earthworks

Cheap broadscale earthwork systems and many minor forms of earthworks can aid the infiltration of overland water flow:

### Primary Earthwork Types for Infiltration

- **Pitting**: small excavations that catch and hold overland flow
- **Swales**: level or near-level channels on contour that allow water to sink into the soil
- **Water spreading**: diversion of surface flow to broader areas for infiltration

### Supporting Systems

- Diversion of surface flow to sand basins, dune fields, swamps, and soakage beds in earth-bermed fields
- Diversion drains with associated valves, slides, cross-walls, intakes, and irrigation systems
- These enable effective water harvesting, dependable storage, and fast emergency use
- They recharge sand basins and swales from otherwise wasted overland flow and can damp out wildfires

## Biological Water Storages

The great forests and biological water storages in the form of fruits and nuts (such as the coconut) are the basis for the proliferation of life forms where no "free" water otherwise exists. On atolls and arid islands of free-draining sands, biological reserves are the **main water reserves**.

Many plants have specific tissues or organs for storing water:

- **Cactus** — succulent stems with water-storing parenchyma
- **Palms** — particularly coconut, storing litres of water in the nut
- **Agaves** — thick, water-retaining leaves

In the local microclimate, water in vegetation greatly moderates heat and cold extremes, and both releases water to and absorbs water from passing air streams. Essential crops such as cassava produce yield as a result of the humidity provided by surrounding vegetation.

## Key Principles

- Cloud seeding can increase precipitation by 8–30% but requires pre-existing ground storage infrastructure
- Cross-wind ridges planted with trees act as condensers, significantly boosting local rainfall
- Deforestation of ridgelines can induce drought hundreds of kilometres inland
- Soil conditioning can reduce runoff from 85% to zero
- The soil is the largest water storage system in any landscape
- Biological water storages (fruits, nuts, succulent tissues) sustain life where free water is absent
- Every intervention in the water cycle should be accompanied by adequate storage and distribution infrastructure
