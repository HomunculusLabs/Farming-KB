# Container Growing for Vegetables and Herbs

Container growing allows you to cultivate vegetables and herbs in small spaces, on patios, balconies, or even windowsills. In the organic garden, containers are particularly valuable for tender plants that need winter protection and for crops that can be given ideal growing conditions.

## Pot and Container Types

**Clay pots:** Porous and "breathe" through the sides, allowing air circulation around roots. However, they absorb moisture from compost so plants need more frequent watering. They harbor disease and are difficult to clean thoroughly. Best for alpines and plants needing very good drainage. Use with soil-based composts.

**[[query-fabric-pots-vs-plastic-pots-vs-air-pots-for-cannabis]]:** Light, cheap, easy to sterilize with boiling water. Plants need less frequent watering. Best for most plants, especially with peat-free composts which dry quicker. Disadvantage: lighter weight can cause top-heavy plants to tip over.

**Seed trays:** Shallow trays, 2-3in (5-7cm) deep. Traditional wood or modern plastic/styrofoam. Plastic is easier to clean and sterilize.

**Module trays:** Divided into individual cells. Seed is sown in individual cells; when germinated, seedling and compost can be pushed out together without disturbing roots -- eliminating [[query-how-to-prevent-transplant-shock-when-moving-cannabis-plants]]. Particularly suitable for multiple sowing. Styrofoam has excellent heat retention.

**Peat-free blocks:** Compressed coir discs that reconstitute in water to fill propagation pots. Stand in seed trays to keep them stable. Sow directly into coir and transplant before too much root shows.

**Improvised containers:** Any shallow container can work -- styrofoam meat trays, margarine tubs, yogurt cups, cut-off plastic bottles. Must be cleaned and have adequate drainage holes.

## Composts for Containers

**Soil-based composts:** Soil retains moisture longer and is easier to rewet than peat. Contains trace elements. Best for plants grown outside in containers (where they tend to get forgotten). Loam must be fibrous and crumbly. For sowing: 2 parts loam, 2 parts peat, 1 part coarse sand + bone meal and lime. For potting: 7 parts loam, 3 parts peat, 2 parts sand + lime and blood, fish, and bone meal.

**Peat-free composts:** Mix peat 50:50 with green compost + [[cervantes-dolomite-lime-ph-buffering]]. Add pelleted chicken manure and kelp liquid feed. Moisten with half-strength solution before sowing.

**Worm-worked compost** mixed with peat, or leaf mold mixed with vermiculite.

**Homemade growing bags:** Fill old compost bags with worm-worked compost or animal manure. Tape up the end and cut holes in the top. A cheap alternative to commercial growing bags.

## Repotting

When roots completely fill the pot, repot into a slightly larger container. Never repot in winter (dormant period). Do not move plants into pots very much larger -- this surrounds roots with cold, wet compost and inhibits root growth.

**Plant mold technique:** Pack fresh compost around a discarded pot in the new container, forming a mold into which the root ball fits without damage. Water repotted plants and leave to settle.

## Watering

Container-grown plants rely entirely on you for water. In summer they need a lot. Use a hose reaching to the container area. In early spring, let water warm to [[jadam-ambient-temperature-principle]] before using.

**Automatic options:**
- **Capillary matting:** Stand pots on moist absorbent matting with one end in water. Plants absorb water as needed.
- **Trickle irrigation:** Narrow-gauge tubing from an overhead tank, fixed into each pot with staples. Adjustable drip rate.

## Feeding

Base fertilizer in composts lasts about 8 weeks. After that, container plants need regular feeding:
- Liquid kelp feed contains the full range of nutrients needed
- Homemade animal-manure tea
- Feed every 1-2 weeks during active growth
- Heavy-cropping plants (tomatoes, cucumbers) need feeding every watering at peak season
- Overfeeding does more damage than underfeeding

## Vegetables Suitable for Containers

- **Tomatoes:** Bush varieties ideal for pots; upright types need support
- **Peppers and eggplants:** Good in 10-12in pots; need warmth and regular feeding
- **Lettuce and salad crops:** Successional sowings in troughs or large pots
- **Radishes:** Quick crop, suitable for small containers
- **Carrots:** Short-rooted varieties in deep containers (at least 8in deep)
- **Beans:** Dwarf varieties in pots, climbing on strings or wigwams
- **Cucumbers:** Bush types in large pots; climbing types need support
- **Potatoes:** Special potato bags or large containers; plant 1-2 tubers per bag

## Herbs in Containers

Many herbs thrive in containers, especially those that are invasive in the garden:
- **Mint:** Best grown in containers to prevent invasiveness
- **Basil:** Needs warm, sunny position; bring indoors in winter
- **Parsley:** Good in pots on a windowsill
- **Chervil:** Shade-loving; suitable for a shadier spot
- **Thyme, rosemary, sage:** [[query-best-mediterranean-herbs-for-beginner-herb-garden]] need excellent drainage
- **Bay:** Tender; must be brought into frost-free greenhouse in winter

## Overwintering

Tender perennials (fuchsias, geraniums, pelargoniums, bay) must be moved into a frost-free greenhouse or cool indoor room for winter. Reduce watering and stop feeding during the dormant period. Check regularly for pests.
