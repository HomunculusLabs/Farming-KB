## Soil Biology and Underground Life

The living organisms within soil constitute one of the most complex and least appreciated ecosystems on Earth. Before humans learned to cut open the soil with ploughs, it was thoroughly dug, aerated, and overturned by multitudes of industrious burrowers. The unploughed meadows of Europe and America were as soft as a great mattress, well aerated by the moles, gophers, worms, prairie dogs, rodents, and larvae eternally at work below ground, even beneath winter snows. Understanding this underground biology is essential for any designer working with soil, as these organisms are the true builders and maintainers of soil structure.

## The Burrowers as Gardeners

### Termites and Ants

Termites and ants function as the [[compost]] piles. In some of these mounds, they cultivate fungi to feed their colonies. The upthrown earth from ant mounds and mole hills forms a specific niche — a zone of disturbed, aerated soil — where annual plants can readily seed and wind-blown pioneer trees can establish. If birds are the seed-scatterers of the forest, burrowers are its gardeners.

### Earthworms and Crustaceans

Worms and soil crustaceans, in their damp and sometimes semi-liquid burrows, move up and down like billions of pump plungers. They suck in and expel air (and thus nitrogen) to root zones, giving the soil its daily breath. This piston-like action is functionally equivalent to the alveoli of lungs in an animal body — the burrows of spiders, gophers, and worms serve the same respiratory function for soil that alveoli serve for the human body.

### Mammalian Burrowers

Gophers and moles industriously carry roots and bulbs to secret underground stores and sometimes forget their hoards. This accidental planting is how comfrey and sunroot spread across landscapes despite their lack of viable seed — they depend not on bees but on moles and gophers for their increase. Foxes eat fruits and defecate on gopher mounds, which are the freshly dug-over areas perfectly prepared for new tree establishment.

Wombats may tunnel, overturn, and even topple many hectares of trees, leaving richly manured, open, and fertile beds for new forest succession. Rabbits industriously garden thistles, and their tunnel systems provide shelter to possums, squirrels, bandicoots, snakes, and frogs, creating underground habitat networks that support diverse communities.

### Building Organisms

Many creatures mix special mudbrick soils with body secretions, and from swallows to termites, organisms create homes from stabilized soils. These structures — termite mounds, ant hills, wasp nests — represent concentrated zones of soil modification where minerals are brought to the surface, organic matter is concentrated, and the soil structure is fundamentally altered.

## Underground Fungal Networks

Underground fungal spores eaten by squirrels or wallabies are activated by digestive enzymes. They break hibernation to occupy new ground and help the new roots of acorns and eucalypts convert soil minerals and liquids into food. This relationship between animals, fungi, and plant roots represents a three-way symbiosis that is essential for nutrient cycling and plant establishment in natural systems.

Seeds and spores are buried, excavated, hidden, activated, and forgotten by burrowers, and recycled to life or [[eucalyptus-globulus]]) can easily embrace 1.5 hectares underground. Forests are so complex and even intergrafted below ground that their aerial canopy seems simple by comparison.

Many desert plants lead long and sturdy underground lives while appearing thin, straggly, and ephemeral above ground. Their below-ground biomass often vastly exceeds what is visible, representing an investment in water and nutrient storage that enables survival through extended drought.

### Root Space Scheduling

Root space sharing is temporally scheduled in productive ecosystems. Spring bulbs feed, flower, and die back before tree roots begin their upward thrust for nutrients and water. Tap-rooted late starters such as thistles and comfrey reach deep for late summer moisture. A few plants and fungi take advantage of autumn rains for flowering and dispersal, occupying root zones vacated by summer-active species.

### Allelopathic Root Interactions

Some species secrete phenols or creosotes to inhibit other plants, including bracken, tamarisk, members of the Juglandaceae family, and Brassicas. Others encapsulate or surround competitors, including Eucalyptus, willows, and tamarisks. Some plants actively trap nematodes and other root predators, or poison them out — marigolds, certain fungi, and Crotalaria species are notable examples.

### Mineral Deposition by Roots

Around the roots of dune trees, calcium and other minerals are deposited as stone-like secretions by root-associated fungi and bacteria. This mineralization process represents a significant pathway for the concentration and fixation of nutrients in the root zone.

### Special Root Forms

Where there is no season of cold death, as in low latitudes, aerial roots may develop. Strangler figs send down roots from high in the crotches of other trees to the earth, building great buttresses as they progressively strangle their host tree in a root well. These aerial root systems demonstrate the remarkable adaptability of roots to local conditions.

## Insects with Extended Underground Phases

Some insects spend the vast majority of their lives underground. Swift moths (Hepialidae) spend 7–8 years underground as large bardi grubs, with only a few days of nocturnal, foodless life in air — mating and laying eggs before disappearing again to root sheaths and soil. Their near cousins, ghost moths and witchetty grubs, follow similar patterns in aerial stems.

The bardi grubs open thousands of shafts to the air and cycle tons of nutrient underground. Their predators follow these burrowers and hunt them in darkness and secrecy, creating additional channels and nutrient pathways through the soil profile.

## Design Applications of Underground Biology

### Using Burrowers Productively

For designers, many of these biological effects can be put to deliberate use. It is as valid to plant an Acacia for the considerable by-product of swift moth or ghost moth larvae as it is to plant a mulberry for silkworms. Moles can substitute for mole ploughs. Gophers serve as unpaid daffodil gardeners. Even on shores and lake bottoms, burrowers carry nutrients below to roots and bring up fresh minerals for decomposition.

### Root Diversity as a Design Tool

The diversity of roots in soil can be used as effectively as the diversity of crowns and canopies in above-ground design:

- **Slope stabilization**: Unstable slopes are pegged with the great root "tree nails" of chestnut, pine, oak, and walnut. The steep slopes of Tasmania are only beginning to collapse a century after forest clearing, and could still be saved by pines, Acacias, or chestnuts.
- **Landslide control**: Bamboo not only holds landslides but provides an earthquake-proof mattress of roots for light structures.
- **Wetland stabilization**: Root mats of swamp vegetation save bulldozers from watery graves during earthworks.
- **Wind protection**: The fibrous web of prairie grass roots defeats wind erosion across vast areas.

### The Fungal and Microbial Dimension

Fungi and bacteria associated with root systems play critical roles in nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and soil aggregation. Mycorrhizal associations extend the effective root zone of plants by orders of magnitude, while various bacterial species fix nitrogen, solubilize phosphorus, and produce growth-promoting compounds. The designer who understands and supports these underground partnerships can create productive systems that require far fewer external inputs than conventional approaches.

## The Navajo Warning

A traditional Navajo saying captures the essential relationship between burrowing animals and landscape health: "If you kill off the prairie dogs, there will be no one to cry for rain."

In the 1950s, amused scientists recommended the extermination of all burrowing animals in desert areas planted to rangelands, believing there was no conceivable relationship between prairie dogs and rainfall. Today, the area near Chilchinbito, Arizona, has become a virtual wasteland due to fierce runoff, soil compaction, and lack of fresh seedbed. The prairie dog burrows had served as water sinks, causing runoff to flood down into them and germinating stored underground seed — a function that their extermination eliminated entirely.

On the Page ranch, where prairie dog burrows were instead used as water infiltration points and runoff was directed into them, the opposite effect was achieved: the land became productive dryland pasture. This example illustrates how understanding and working with underground biology can make the difference between landscape degradation and landscape productivity.
