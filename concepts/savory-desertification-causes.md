# Causes of Desertification

Desertification is the deterioration of land, water, and biological resources. Most people recognize it only by its terminal symptom—blowing sand—but the same syndrome of declining health and productivity occurs wherever the four  blocks are failing, regardless of whether sand dunes ever appear.

## The Fundamental Misunderstanding

For millennia, humanity has believed that land damaged by grazing animals could be restored by simply leaving it undisturbed. This assumption seems logical and satisfies the intuition of cause and effect. It does apply to the stable environments of northern Europe and the eastern United States, where modern agricultural science developed. But it is catastrophically wrong for [[savory-brittle-environments]], which cover over half the earth's land surface.

The term desertification describes not just advancing sand dunes but the progressive failure of all [[fungal-ecosystem-processes-overview]]: succession declining toward simplicity, water cycles becoming ineffective, mineral cycles breaking down, and energy flow diminishing. These processes are interconnected, and failure in one accelerates failure in all.

## Overgrazing: A Function of Time, Not Numbers

The most common misunderstanding is attributing land damage to "too many animals." Historical records show that vast herds of wild animals once existed on lands that are now desertifying—herds that vastly outnumbered the domestic livestock that later replaced them. Springbok migrations in South Africa were so vast they trampled everything in their path, yet the environment was more abundant than anything modern inhabitants can imagine.

André Voisin proved that overgrazing bears little relationship to the number of animals but rather to the **time plants are exposed to animals**. If animals remain in any one place too long, or return before plants have recovered, they overgraze plants. This is a function of management, not of animal numbers.

Wild herds moved constantly, seldom occupying any area longer than two or three days, after which the land had opportunity to recover. Domestic livestock, confined by fencing and denied the [[savory-predator-prey-and-herding-behavior]] induced by predators, often remain in the same area for weeks or months, causing severe overgrazing of preferred plants.

## Overrest: The Hidden Destroyer

If overgrazing is the commonly recognized cause, **overrest** is the unrecognized one—and in brittle environments, often the more damaging. When grasses in brittle environments are not periodically grazed or trampled, old material accumulates and blocks sunlight from reaching basal growing points. The plants weaken and eventually die.

On the Sevilleta Wildlife Refuge in New Mexico, fifty years of total rest in a brittle nine-inch-rainfall area produced dying perennial grasses with large bare spaces opening between plants. Despite years of seed production, no seedlings established on the bare, undisturbed surface. Similar patterns were documented at Crescent Lake Wildlife Refuge in Nebraska, where twelve years of rest showed weakened grasses and tap-rooted weed invasion, progressing to widespread grass death after fifty years.

Overrest occurs in two forms:
- **Total rest**: Complete absence of large animals
- **Partial rest**: Small numbers of widely scattered livestock or game that fail to significantly disturb most plant life and soil surface

Both are widespread policies. Most grazing land worldwide is managed in a way that paradoxically produces both partial rest AND overgrazing—too few animals scattered too widely to provide adequate disturbance, while remaining long enough to overgraze preferred plants.

## Fire as a Cause

Fire has long been used to maintain grasslands and clear brush. While it can temporarily increase forage production and species diversity, in brittle environments its long-term effects are overwhelmingly negative:

- **Soil exposure**: Fire removes plant cover, leaving bare soil vulnerable to capping and erosion
- **Destruction of litter**: The material that slows water flow and maintains soil structure is consumed
- **Damage to soil microenvironment**: The biological communities that drive decomposition are damaged
- **Erosion acceleration**: Bare soil after fire is rapidly eroded by wind and water

Repeated fires progressively reduce species diversity, slow mineral and water cycles, and ultimately reduce energy flow. In the tsetse fly eradication areas of Zimbabwe, fire alone (without grazing animals) caused serious grassland deterioration despite the absence of any livestock.

## Bare Soil: The Central Problem

All causes of desertification converge on one critical symptom: **exposure of bare soil**. Bare soil between plants is the earliest and most reliable indicator of ecosystem deterioration. Yet it is routinely ignored in range management, which traditionally focuses on plant species composition rather than soil surface condition.

Savory documented ranches that had received awards for "best management" where transects revealed 97 percent of the soil surface between plants was bare, exposed, and visibly eroding. Another supposedly "perfectly managed" ranch showed serious erosion on 95 percent of its area.

The chain of degradation runs: bare soil → raindrop impact destroys crumb structure → fine particles seal the surface (capping) → water runs off rather than infiltrating → less water available to plants → less plant cover → more bare soil → accelerating deterioration.

## The Role of Reduced Animal Impact

When predators were eliminated and wild herds diminished or were replaced by scattered domestic livestock, the beneficial disturbance that herding animals once provided was lost. The soil surface, no longer churned by concentrated herds, became capped. Old plant material, no longer trampled into mulch, remained standing and oxidizing. New seedlings, without the broken seedbed that hooves once provided, could not establish.

The result: plant spacings widened, soil became exposed, and the desertification process accelerated—even as animal numbers decreased. We blamed the damage on too many animals and reduced numbers further, which only increased the bare ground and deterioration.

## The Compound Effect

Desertification is rarely caused by a single factor but by the interaction of overgrazing, overrest, fire, soil exposure, and the removal of natural disturbance regimes. These factors reinforce each other in a self-accelerating cycle. The key to reversal lies not in attacking individual symptoms but in restoring the processes that naturally maintain ecosystem health—particularly through planned grazing that provides adequate but timed animal impact in brittle environments.

## The Scale of the Problem

Desertification affects approximately two-thirds of the world's land surface. It is not confined to arid regions—much of what is classified as productive agricultural land is in fact desertifying, with declining soil organic matter, increasing erosion, rising input requirements, and falling water tables. The process is often invisible to people who see it daily because the changes are gradual.

In the United States, for example, soil erosion rates remain high despite decades of conservation programs. The underlying problem is that most conservation efforts address symptoms (planting trees, building terraces, reducing animal numbers) without addressing the root cause: the breakdown of the four ecosystem foundation blocks due to management that fails to account for brittleness and the time dimension of grazing.

## Reversal Through Holistic Management

Reversing desertification requires restoring effective function of all four ecosystem foundation blocks simultaneously. The holistic management approach does this by:

1. Defining a holistic goal that describes the desired future landscape
2. Using planned grazing to provide the periodic disturbance that brittle environments require
3. Managing stock density and recovery periods to prevent overgrazing while ensuring adequate animal impact
4. Monitoring soil surface conditions as the earliest indicator of change
5. Adapting management based on [[savory-replanning-and-monitoring-feedback]]

Documented results from ranches applying holistic management show rapid improvements in water infiltration, plant density, soil organic matter, and forage production—often within three to five years of implementation. These improvements occur not by adding inputs but by restoring the natural processes that maintained ecosystem health for millions of years.
