# Spreader Banks and Irrigation Bays

Spreader banks (also called spreader broads) and irrigation bays are earthwork
systems designed to distribute surplus water efficiently across broad landscapes.
These structures convert concentrated flow into thin, even sheet flow, serving
both irrigation and erosion [[mollison-permaculture-two-arid-water-harvesting-and-erosion-control|control]] purposes — particularly in arid and semi-arid
environments.

## Spreader Banks (Broads)

Spreader banks are earthworks that spill a thin sheet of surplus overflow water
down broad grassy slopes. Their primary functions are irrigation and the
prevention of channel scour and gullying in desert landscapes.

### Design and Construction

- Spreader banks take overflow from **swales and dams**, and can also receive
  water from minor streams.
- They range in length from tens to hundreds of metres.
- The **lower side is dead level**, ensuring even water distribution across the
  full length of the structure.
- The lower side may be compacted earth or concreted for durability.
- Water enters from a dam, swale, or minor stream at a single point or
  distributed inlet, and leaves as a **thin sheet flow** running downslope.
- **Spoil from excavation is piled uphill**, preferably formed into mounds,
  [[query-which-cover-crops-are-best-for-breaking-hardpan-and-compacted-soil|which]] themselves can serve as useful planting zones or windbreaks.

The key principle is converting concentrated, high-energy water flow into a
broad, shallow, low-energy sheet that irrigates vegetation without eroding
soil. In desert environments this is critical — channelised flow carves gullies
and wastes water, while sheet flow nurtures widespread plant cover.

## Irrigation Bays (Flatland Systems)

Irrigation bays are the flatland counterpart to spreader banks, designed for
level or gently sloping terrain where gravity-fed sheet flow must be carefully
controlled and directed.

### Components

- A main bank is **pierced by dead-level pipe outlets** that feed water into
  individual irrigation bays.
- Each bay is **confined by low side walls** called **steering banks**, which
  guide the sheet of water across the bay area.
- A **tail drain** at the lower end of each bay leads off excess water to a
  stream or to secondary storage, preventing waterlogging.
- The **head race** is the uphill primary feeder drain, equipped with
  **cross-slides** (gates) that control flow into individual pipe outlets and
  bays.

### Efficiency

Using these gravity-fed systems, **one or two people can water 200–240
hectares in a single morning**. This extraordinary labour efficiency makes
irrigation bays one of the most productive water distribution strategies
available in [[permaculture-design]].

## Interceptor [[spreader-banks-and-interceptor-drains|Drains]]

Interceptor drains were designed by **Harry Whittington of West Australia** to
address overland waterflow and waterlogging in desert soil catenas — the
sequences of different soil types found along slopes in arid regions.

### Design Specifications

- Interceptor drains are always **1.5–2.5 metres deep**, ensuring they cut
  well below the surface flow layer.
- They are **carefully spaced** according to slope, soil type, and expected
  rainfall intensity.
- Subsoil layers within the drain are **rammed and compacted** to prevent
  water from seeping back into adjacent soils.
- They **cut degraded croplands into blocks** ranging from **100 metres to a
  maximum of 300 metres wide**.

### Functions

- **Prevent overland waterflow** from reaching lower slopes during heavy rain.
- **Eliminate waterlogging** by intercepting subsurface and surface flows
  before they saturate productive areas.
- **Isolate soil from flooding** and from salty cascade flow that moves
  downslope through soil catenas, carrying dissolved salts that poison plant
  roots.
- Restore degraded land by breaking the cycle of erosion, waterlogging, and
  salinisation.

## Diversion Drains

Diversion drains run from streams to dams, or collect overland flow and direct
it to storage. They serve as the primary water-harvesting input channels that
feed the spreader bank and irrigation bay systems described above.

## Slide Gates and Flow Control

Effective water control requires reliable gate mechanisms:

- **Fixed slide gates** are typically made of concrete and set at permanent
  positions in head races and distribution channels.
- **Moveable slide gates** use plastic sheets weighted with chain — known
  colloquially as **"flags"** — that can be positioned or removed by hand.
  These are inexpensive, easily adjustable, and well-suited to the scale of
  [[permaculture-earthworks]].

## Wildfire Control

Dams and sheet irrigation systems serve a dual purpose as **wildfire
defence**. When equipped with infrared sensors and automatic spill-gates, these
earthworks can be triggered to release water across broad areas ahead of an
approaching fire front. The saturated vegetation and wet ground surface act as
effective firebreaks.

## Earthworks: Rehabilitative vs Damaging

Mollison emphasises that earthworks are not inherently good or bad — their
impact depends entirely on how they are used:

- **Rehabilitative use**: Earthworks supporting perennial browse systems,
  forests, and diverse plantings that build soil, reduce evaporation, and
  restore hydrological cycles.
- **Damaging use**: Earthworks installed to support high water-demand crops
  (cotton, rice, sugarcane) in drylands, which deplete aquifers, cause
  salinisation, and ultimately degrade the landscape.

The distinction is critical. A spreader bank irrigating a mixed food forest
rehabilitates; the same earthwork feeding a monocrop field in a desert may
accelerate degradation.

## Water Conservation in Sewage Systems

Permaculture design addresses the enormous waste of potable water in
conventional sewage systems.

### Handbasin Flush Toilets

A simple but effective innovation is the **handbasin moulded into the toilet
flush tank**. Wash water from the handbasin drains directly into the cistern,
reusing it for the next flush. This reduces household water consumption
significantly with no change in user behaviour.

**Low-flush toilet bowls are essential** when using these systems, as the
reduced water volume from the handbasin may be insufficient for older
high-volume bowls.
