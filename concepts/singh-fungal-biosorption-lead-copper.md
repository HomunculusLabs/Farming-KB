# Fungal Biosorption of Lead and Copper

Lead and copper represent two of the most significant metallic
contaminants targeted by fungal biosorption technologies. Lead is a
potent neurotoxin with no biological function, while copper is an
essential trace element that becomes toxic at elevated concentrations.
Both metals are widespread industrial pollutants amenable to fungal
removal through biosorption and bioaccumulation mechanisms.

## Environmental Context of Lead and Copper Contamination

Lead contamination originates from battery manufacturing, smelting,
paint, gasoline additives, and plumbing systems. Copper enters the
environment through mining, electroplating, agricultural chemicals, and
antifouling agents. Singh's review identifies lead and copper, along
with cadmium, as the metals of highest concern after uranium in
biosorption priority, due to strong toxic environmental effects. Copper
is used in many applications and is increasing in the environment, while
lead remains one of the most problematic heavy metal contaminants in
industrial wastewater.

## Lead Biosorption by Filamentous Fungi

Filamentous fungi demonstrate exceptional lead uptake capacities.
Aspergillus niger achieves an impressive 580 mg/g of Pb(II) removal at
200 mg/l concentration within 20 minutes, representing one of the
highest reported fungal lead biosorption values. Phellinus badius
removes 170 mg/g of Pb(II) at pH 5.0 within 15 minutes. Penicillium
chrysogenum displays 116 mg/g Pb(II) uptake at pH 4.0-5.0. Rhizopus
nigricans shows 68 mg/g Pb(II) removal from 500 mg/l solutions within
120 minutes, with desorption achievable using HCl, HNO3, or EDTA.
Phomopsis sp. demonstrates 870 microM/g Pb(II) uptake in multimetal
systems. Metal ions in both distilled water and urban wastewater are
adsorbed in the order Pb(II) > Cu(II) > Zn(II) > Cd(II) > Ni(II) using
thermally alkali-treated Phomopsis sp. biomaterials.

## Copper Biosorption by Filamentous Fungi

Copper biosorption by fungi is particularly interesting because copper
also serves as an essential cofactor for laccase production in many
white-rot species. Aspergillus niger removes 0.52 mg Cu(II)/g dry
weight, while A. pendulus removes 0.09 mg Cu(II)/g dry weight.
Penicillium chrysogenum displays 9 mg/g Cu(II) uptake. Rhizopus arrhizus
achieves 0.60 mM/g of Cu(II) removal. Phomopsis sp. demonstrates 390
microM/g Cu(II) uptake capacity. Scanning electron microscopy coupled
with x-ray energy dispersion analysis indicated that K(I) and Ca(II) are
replaced by Pb(II) on the cell wall of Mucor rouxii, illustrating the
ion exchange mechanism underlying copper and lead biosorption.

## Copper as a Laccase Cofactor

A unique aspect of copper in fungal systems is its dual role as both
a pollutant and an essential enzyme cofactor. Copper is a cofactor for
the enzyme laccase, and its addition has a positive effect on laccase
production in numerous white-rot species including Ceriporiopsis
subvermispora, Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus ostreatus, P. sajor-caju,
Trametes trogii, T. pubescens, T. multicolor, T. hirsuta, T. gibbosa,
Ganoderma applanatum, Polyporus ciliatus, and Panus tigrinus. Copper
enhances the production of all isozymes of laccase in P. ostreatus.
Metal-response elements (MRE) have been identified in laccase genes
in P. ostreatus that interact with Cu-responsive transcriptional
factors. This relationship creates a complex dynamic where copper
pollution may actually enhance the fungal enzymatic machinery useful
for degrading organic pollutants.

## Lead and Copper Effects on Fungal Morphology

Heavy metals cause significant morphological changes in fungi.
Schizophyllum commune forms black mycelial pellets in Pb-containing
media. In white-rot fungi, the concentrations of heavy metal ions in
soil and contaminated sites are generally higher than those in wood.
These metals affect growth, reproduction, metabolic activity, mycelial
morphology, and enzymatic activities. Metal ions accumulated in
Daedalea quercina decrease in the preferential order Zn > Cu > Pb > Al.
Ganoderma lucidum follows a toxicity order of Hg > Cd > Cu > U > Pb >
Mn = Zn. Lead uptake by nonliving R. nigricans was reduced in the
presence of co-cations such as Zn(II) and Fe(III) ions at pH 4.0.

## White-Rot Fungi in Lead and Copper Biosorption

White-rot fungi have demonstrated significant potential for lead and
copper biosorption. Polyporus versicolor removes 25% of Pb(II) and 5%
of Cu(II) from 150 mg/l solutions within 6 hours. Phanerochaete
chrysosporium removes 25% of Pb(II) and 10% of Cu(II) under similar
conditions. P. chrysosporium achieves 100% removal of Cu(II) from 100
mg/l solutions within 4 hours, with the biomass regenerable using 1 N
HCl for up to 6 cycles. Resting cells of P. chrysosporium show much
higher Pb(II) uptake (80 mg/g dry cell) compared to live cells (9 mg/g)
at pH 3-4, while dead cells achieve 20 mg/g at pH 5.0.

## Aspergillus niger in Soil Bioremediation

A. niger plays a dual role in lead and copper remediation, functioning
both as a biosorbent and as a soil bioremediation agent. The fungus
biomass produces weak organic acids, mostly citric acid, for leaching
heavy metals from contaminated soils. A process to bioremediate three
types of soils contaminated with heavy metals has been developed using
A. niger. Several types of metals are leached at different levels in
loam, clay loam, and sandy clay loam soils. These contaminated soils
can be remediated to Quebec Standards for heavy metals after 20 to 25
days at a cost of approximately $5.00 per cubic meter, applicable for
in situ or ex situ mycoremediation.

## Competitive Biosorption in Multimetal Systems

Real wastewater typically contains multiple metal ions simultaneously.
In ternary systems using Rhizopus arrhizus, the adsorption capacities
display the preferential order Pb(II) > Ni(II) > Cu(II) at pH 5.0.
Lead adsorption by A. niger is more sensitive to the presence of copper
than of cadmium. The binding affinity of metal ions to yeast cells
follows Pb(II) > Cu(II) > Ni(II). In the presence of binary or ternary
mixtures, rejection of individual metals is reduced, except for Pb(II),
which maintains strong biosorption even in competitive conditions.
This preferential lead uptake makes fungal biosorption particularly
effective for lead-contaminated mixed-waste streams.
