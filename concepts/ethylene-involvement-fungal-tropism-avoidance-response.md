# Ethylene Involvement in Fungal Tropism and Avoidance Responses

## Overview

Ethylene (C₂H₄), a simple gaseous plant hormone well-known for its roles in fruit
ripening, senescence, and stress responses in higher plants, has also been implicated
in the tropic responses of fungi. Research on *Phycomyces blakesleeanus* and related
observations in *Psilocybe cubensis* suggest that ethylene may serve as an endogenous
signaling molecule in fungal avoidance responses and directional growth, functioning
through mechanisms analogous to—but distinct from—those in plant systems.

## Ethylene as a Fungal Signaling Molecule

The hypothesis that ethylene participates in fungal tropism emerged from studies of
the sporangiophore avoidance response in *Phycomyces blakesleeanus*. Russo, Halloran,
and Gallori (1977) demonstrated that ethylene is involved in the autochemotropic
(avoidance) response of *Phycomyces*, providing the first direct evidence that this
gaseous hormone functions as a tropic signal in the fungal kingdom.

Key observations supporting ethylene's role in fungal tropism include:

- **Autochemotropism**: *Phycomyces* sporangiophores bend away from obstacles and
  from each other. This avoidance response depends on the redistribution of gases
  around the growing structure, with ethylene identified as a candidate mediator.

- **Gas redistribution**: When wind or physical barriers alter the boundary layer
  around a [[fungal-fruiting-body]], the local concentration of volatile compounds
  including ethylene becomes asymmetric, potentially creating a differential growth
  signal.

- **Cross-kingdom conservation**: While the biosynthetic pathways for ethylene
  differ between plants (via ACC synthase and ACC oxidase) and fungi, the molecule's
  physical properties—small size, high diffusivity, and lipid solubility—make it an
  effective intercellular signal in any organism.

## Mechanism in Phycomyces Avoidance Response

In *Phycomyces blakesleeanus*, the sporangiophore (the stalk bearing the spore
capsule) exhibits a sophisticated avoidance response:

1. When a sporangiophore encounters an obstacle or another sporangiophore, it
   detects the resulting change in the local gas environment.

2. The side facing the obstacle experiences reduced gas exchange due to the
   boundary layer effect, while the opposite side maintains normal diffusion.

3. Ethylene, continuously produced by the growing tissue, accumulates asymmetrically.
   Higher concentrations on the obstacle-facing side inhibit cell wall extensibility
   or reduce elongation rates.

4. The resulting differential growth causes the sporangiophore to bend away from the
   obstacle—a classic avoidance response.

This mechanism shares conceptual parallels with the anemotropic (wind-directed)
response observed in *Psilocybe cubensis* basidiocarps, where the redistribution of
water vapor and potentially other gases across the stipe surface creates differential
evaporative demand that drives curvature.

## Connection to Psilocybe Cubensis Tropisms

Badham (1982), studying tropisms in *Psilocybe cubensis*, noted that the mechanism
of stipe elongation may be similar to that of *Phycomyces*. The wind tunnel
experiments demonstrated that:

- Stipe growth was directed into the airflow during early developmental stages,
  consistent with a boundary layer redistribution mechanism.
- The evaporative demand gradient across the stipe (windward vs. leeward side)
  could differentially affect cell elongation rates.
- While water vapor was identified as the primary candidate gas, Badham explicitly
  noted that "the wind could also cause a redistribution of gases other than water
  vapor," citing Russo et al.'s work on ethylene in *Phycomyces*.

The parallel between these systems suggests that ethylene—or a similar volatile
compound—may contribute to the tropic responses of mushroom basidiocarps alongside
water vapor, potentially explaining some of the more subtle aspects of directional
growth that cannot be accounted for by evaporative demand alone.

## Broader Context: Ethylene in Fungal Biology

Ethylene production has been documented in numerous fungal species beyond the model
organisms:

- **Saprotrophic fungi**: Many wood-decay and litter-decomposing fungi produce
  ethylene during substrate colonization, potentially regulating hyphal branching
  patterns and interactions with competing organisms.

- **Plant pathogenic fungi**: Ethylene production by pathogenic fungi can manipulate
  host plant physiology, promoting susceptibility to infection. Some pathogens
  exploit the plant's own ethylene signaling pathways.

- **Mycorrhizal fungi**: Ethylene modulation appears to play a role in the
  establishment and maintenance of ectomycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal
  symbioses, affecting root architecture and fungal colonization patterns.

- **Edible and medicinal mushrooms**: Ethylene exposure has been shown to affect
  [[fruiting-body-development]] in cultivated species, with implications for commercial
  mushroom production.

## Biosynthetic Pathways in Fungi

Fungal ethylene biosynthesis differs from the plant Yang cycle. The primary routes
include the KMA (2-oxoglutarate-dependent) pathway and methionine-dependent
pathways, with some species also producing ethylene non-enzymatically via lipid
peroxidation during oxidative stress.

## Evidence Gaps and Future Directions

Despite suggestive evidence, several questions remain about ethylene's precise role
in fungal tropism:

1. **Quantitative measurements**: Direct measurement of ethylene gradients around
   growing fungal structures during tropic responses has not been performed. Modern
   gas-sensitive microelectrodes or laser-based detection methods could address this.

2. **Receptor identification**: While ethylene receptors are well-characterized in
   plants, no definitive fungal ethylene receptor has been identified. The signaling
   cascade downstream of ethylene perception in fungi remains speculative.

3. **Interaction with other signals**: How ethylene interacts with other tropic
   signals—gravity, light, water vapor, and touch—in determining the net growth
   direction of fungal structures is not well understood.

4. **Species-specific variation**: The extent to which ethylene-mediated tropism
   occurs across diverse fungal groups, from zygomycetes like *Phycomyces* to
   basidiomycetes like *Psilocybe*, warrants systematic investigation.

5. **Applied implications**: If ethylene plays a significant role in mushroom
   development, manipulation of ethylene levels in cultivation environments could
   provide a novel tool for optimizing fruiting body morphology and yield.
