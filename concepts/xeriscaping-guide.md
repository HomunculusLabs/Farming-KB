## Xeriscaping Guide

Xeriscaping is a landscaping approach that minimizes or eliminates supplemental irrigation by using drought-tolerant plants, efficient water management, and thoughtful design. The term comes from the Greek "xeros" (dry) and "landscape." It is not "zeroscaping" — xeriscapes are lush, diverse, and vibrant, not barren gravel yards.

## Core Principles

1. **Plan and design**: Assess site conditions, create a water-use zones map, group plants by water needs
2. **Improve the soil**: Add compost to increase water retention (but ensure good drainage)
3. **Limit turf areas**: Replace lawn with drought-adapted ground covers, pathways, or native plantings
4. **Select appropriate plants**: Choose species adapted to local rainfall patterns and soil
5. **Irrigate efficiently**: Drip irrigation, soaker hoses, or no irrigation after establishment
6. **Mulch heavily**: 3-4 inches of organic or inorganic mulch to reduce evaporation
7. **Maintain appropriately**: Minimal but consistent — weed control, pruning, occasional deep watering

## Drought-Tolerant Landscaping

### Hydrozoning

Group plants by water requirements into irrigation zones:

- **Oasis zone** (near house or high-use areas): Higher water use, edible plants, container gardens
- **Transition zone**: Moderate water use, native ornamentals, fruit trees
- **Dry zone** (outer areas, slopes, edges): No irrigation after establishment, drought-adapted natives

This approach reduces overall water use by concentrating irrigation where it matters most.

### Soil Improvement for Water Retention

- Incorporate 2-4 inches of compost into planting areas to [[query-how-do-fungi-improve-soil-structure]]
- Compost acts as a sponge, holding several times its weight in water
- Avoid excessive tilling, which destroys soil structure and accelerates moisture loss
- In very sandy soils, add biochar (charged with compost tea) for improved water retention
- In clay soils, add coarse sand or expanded shale to improve drainage and root penetration

## Rock Gardens

Rock gardens combine drought-tolerant plants with stone elements to create attractive, low-water landscapes.

### Design Elements
- **Stone selection**: Use local stone for natural appearance; vary sizes from boulders to gravel
- **Placement**: Bury large stones 1/3 to 1/2 into the ground for a natural, anchored look
- **Topography**: Create mounds and swales to direct rainwater to planting areas
- **Gravel mulch**: 2-3 inches of crushed gravel or river rock around plants suppresses weeds and reflects heat

### Rock Garden Plants
- **Sedums and succulents**: Hens and chicks (Sempervivum), stonecrop, ice plant
- **Alpine plants**: Cushion spurge, alpine forget-me-not, dwarf iris
- **Native wildflowers**: Penstemon, blanket flower, prairie clover
- **Ornamental grasses**: Blue fescue, little bluestem, maiden grass
- **Herbs**: Lavender, thyme, rosemary, oregano (many Mediterranean herbs thrive in rock gardens)

### Construction Tips
- Start with the largest stones and work down in size
- Create pockets of deeper soil between rocks for larger plants
- Slope the garden slightly for drainage
- Incorporate [[rain-garden-design]] principles by directing runoff into the rock garden

## Native Dryland Plants

Native plants adapted to dry conditions are the backbone of successful xeriscaping. Selection depends on your region:

### Arid Southwest (Zones 7-10)
- Agave, yucca, desert marigold, brittlebush, creosote bush
- Palo verde, mesquite (as accent trees)
- Red yucca (Hesperaloe), blackfoot daisy, damianita

### Great Plains and Midwest (Zones 3-7)
- Little bluestem, big bluestem, switchgrass
- Coneflower, black-eyed Susan, blanket flower
- Yarrow, prairie dropseed, blue sage
- Leadplant, prairie clover, rattlesnake master

### Mediterranean and West Coast (Zones 8-10)
- Manzanita, ceanothus, buckwheat
- Salvia, penstemon, monkey flower
- California poppy, lupine, native grasses
- Toyon, coast live oak (as specimens)

### General Drought-Tolerant Ornamentals
- Russian sage (Perovskia atriplicifolia)
- Coneflowers ([[echinacea-purpurea]] and hybrids)
- Black-eyed Susan ([[rudbeckia-fulgida]])
- Yarrow ([[achillea-millefolium]])
- Catmint (Nepeta faassenii)
- Ornamental onions (Allium spp.)
- Sedum 'Autumn Joy'

## Irrigation Reduction Strategies

### Efficient Irrigation Systems
- **Drip irrigation**: Delivers water directly to root zones, 90% efficient vs 50% for sprinklers
- **Soaker hoses**: Porous hoses that weep water along their length, simple and effective
- **Olla irrigation**: Unglazed clay pots buried in soil, filled with water that seeps to roots
- **[[comparison-drip-irrigation-vs-rainwater-harvesting]]**: Collect roof runoff in barrels or cisterns for supplemental irrigation

### Water-Saving Practices
- Water deeply and infrequently (once per week in summer, less in cooler months)
- Water early morning (4-7 AM) to minimize evaporation
- Use a rain gauge to track natural rainfall — skip irrigation after rain events
- Add a 2-inch layer of organic mulch to all planting beds
- Use soil moisture sensors to avoid overwatering
- Choose drought-tolerant turf alternatives: buffalo grass, blue grama, creeping red fescue

### Lawn Alternatives
- **Buffalo grass**: Native prairie grass, needs 1-2 inches of water per month
- **Blue grama**: Short native grass, minimal water, mow once per year
- **Creeping thyme**: Low-growing, foot traffic tolerant, flowers
- **White clover**: Nitrogen-fixing, stays green with minimal water, attracts bees
- **No-mow fescue blends**: Low-maintenance, drought-tolerant once established

## Maintenance

- Weed control in the first 2-3 years (once established, drought-tolerant plants outcompete weeds)
- Prune to maintain shape and remove dead wood in early spring
- Replenish mulch annually (organic mulches decompose; stone mulches stay put)
- Monitor for [[query-how-do-endophytic-fungi-protect-plants-from-pests-and-disease]] — stressed plants are more vulnerable
- Deep water occasionally during extended droughts, even for established drought-tolerant plants
- Divide overgrown perennials every 3-5 years
