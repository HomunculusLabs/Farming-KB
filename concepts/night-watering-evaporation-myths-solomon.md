# Night Watering and Evaporation Myths

## Overview

Watering at night is widely believed to be harmful to plants, with
concerns about disease promotion and fungal growth from wet foliage. (see [[wide-spacing-dry-gardening-low-irrigation-solomon]]).
However, Solomon argues that for gardeners with heavy clay soils and
proper low-rate sprinkler systems, night watering may actually be the
best time to irrigate — more efficient than morning watering and more
practical than midday watering. The key factors are soil type,
application rate, and wind conditions. (see [[soil-moisture-management-and-root-growth]]).

## The Common Belief

The conventional gardening wisdom holds that watering in the evening
leaves foliage wet overnight, promoting fungal diseases. This belief
is so deeply entrenched that many gardening authorities categorically
advise against any irrigation after mid-afternoon. The concern has
some basis — prolonged leaf wetness can promote certain pathogens — but
the blanket prohibition ignores important nuances about soil type,
application rate, and natural dew patterns.

## Why Night Watering Can Work

### Plants Are Already Adapted to Nighttime Moisture

Plants are naturally dampened by dew for much of the night during the
growing season. During summer, dew forms as air temperatures drop and
relative humidity rises toward saturation. Plants quickly dry after
morning sunlight arrives. Watering plants just before dark mimics this
natural pattern. If plants are not harmed by natural dew — and they are
not — they will not be harmed by irrigation that follows the same
timing. This single observation undermines the entire argument against
night watering for most climates.

### Minimal Evaporation Loss

When sprinkling is done during calm, low-light conditions, little or
no water is lost to evaporation before it enters the soil. Daytime
watering, especially during hot sunny weather, can lose a substantial
fraction of the applied moisture to evaporation before it ever reaches
the root zone. Night watering eliminates this waste entirely, meaning
less total water is needed to achieve the same soil moisture level.
For gardens on limited well water, this efficiency gain is critical.

### The Leaching Threshold

The critical requirement for safe night watering is that the sprinkler
application rate must be low enough to prevent leaching. Solomon
provides specific guidance:

- Clayey soils growing larger vegetables with root systems about two
  feet (60 cm) deep can easily accept 1 to 1.5 inches (2.5 to 4 cm)
  of water without danger of leaching
- If the sprinkler system applies less than 0.5 inches per hour, it
  is possible to water a clay soil all night long without risk of
  nutrient leaching
- The water simply fills the soil profile to field capacity and any
  excess drains slowly through the clay matrix without carrying
  soluble nutrients beyond the root zone

This means that for an eight-hour night window with a 0.45 in/hr
nozzle, the total application would be approximately 3.6 inches —
more than enough to fully recharge even a very dry clay soil profile
without any leaching risk.

## The Practical Advantage: Well Water Competition

For rural homes with limited-output wells, nighttime offers a crucial
practical advantage: there is no competition for water from household
use. No one is taking showers, running dishwashers, flushing toilets,
or washing clothes. The full well output is available for irrigation.

The optimal window runs from bedtime to breakfast. During this period
there is rarely any wind, temperatures are at their lowest, and
household water demand is at its minimum. This convergence of favorable
conditions makes nighttime the most efficient time to irrigate.

## When Night Watering is Not Ideal

Night watering works best under these conditions:

- The soil is heavy (clay or clay-loam) with good water retention
- The sprinkler system has a low application rate (under 0.5 in/hr)
- The air is calm with minimal wind drift
- The crop canopy is not exceptionally dense (adequate airflow)

For sandy or fast-draining soils, night watering with low rates may not
deliver sufficient moisture to the root zone. Lighter, more frequent
applications or daytime watering may be preferable. In humid climates
with poor airflow and dense crop canopies, the disease risk from
prolonged leaf wetness may outweigh the efficiency gains.

## The Evaporation Criticism of Sprinkler Irrigation

Most criticism of sprinkler irrigation — particularly from drip
irrigation enthusiasts — is based on misuse of the method. Running
sprinklers during hot, sunny, windy daytime conditions wastes water
through evaporation and produces poor distribution uniformity. When
sprinklers are used correctly during calm, low-light conditions, the
efficiency difference between sprinkler and drip systems narrows
considerably, while sprinklers offer simpler maintenance and broader
coverage.

## Nursery Industry Practice

The nursery industry has long understood the benefits of watering at
dusk. Commercial nurseries routinely irrigate in the evening to take
advantage of calm conditions and minimal evaporation. This professional
practice contradicts the amateur gardening advice to always water in
the morning.

## Summary

Night watering is not inherently harmful to plants. For gardeners with
heavy clay soils and low-application-rate sprinkler systems, watering
from bedtime to breakfast during calm conditions is the most efficient
and practical approach. Plants are already adapted to nighttime moisture
from dew, and the absence of sun and wind eliminates evaporative losses
while freeing well capacity from household competition.
