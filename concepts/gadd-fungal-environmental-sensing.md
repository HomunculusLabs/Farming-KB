# Fungal Environmental Sensing

Filamentous fungi are exquisitely sensitive organisms that perceive and
respond to an extraordinary range of environmental signals. Their non-motile,
heterotrophic lifestyle has driven the evolution of sophisticated sensing and
signalling systems that enable them to explore, exploit, and survive in
highly heterogeneous microenvironments. The molecular machinery underlying
fungal environmental perception represents a complex network of receptors,
second messengers, and downstream effectors that coordinate growth, metabolism,
and development.

## Consequences of the Fungal Lifestyle

Animal cells are motile and heterotrophic, whereas plant cells are non-motile
and autotrophic. Fungi combine features of both by being non-motile and
heterotrophic, though phylogenetically they are more closely related to
animals than plants. The possession of [[fungal-hyphal-organization]] has allowed fungi to combine
these attributes to great effect.

Being non-motile and heterotrophic has introduced selection pressures during
evolution that have played a crucial role in imparting many characteristic
features of filamentous fungi. Hyphae respond extremely sensitively and
quickly to the myriad of signals within heterogeneous microenvironments.
Growth direction and branching patterns can change very rapidly, and hyphae
can selectively grow from unfavourable to more favourable environments.

## Categories of Environmental Signals

Filamentous fungi respond to a phenomenal range of environmental signals,
which can be broadly classified into four types:

### Abiotic and Global Signals
These include light (varying in intensity, wavelength, and periodicity,
especially diurnal rhythmicity), temperature, and relative humidity. Such
signals affect the entire organism simultaneously and often trigger
developmental switches such as sporulation or fruiting body formation.

### Abiotic and Local Signals
In their normally heterogeneous microenvironments, fungi often experience
external signals at the microscopic level in the form of gradients. These
local signals include nutrients, oxygen, carbon dioxide, pH, other chemical
signals, mechanical stimuli, and osmotic shock. The ability to sense and
respond to nutrient gradients is fundamental to the exploratory and
exploitative strategies of [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]].

### Biotic Signals from Other Organisms
Other organisms produce a wide variety of chemical and physical signals that
can influence fungal growth, behaviour, metabolism, and gene expression.
These include chemicals that promote or inhibit fungal growth, compounds
that modulate [[gadd-fungal-nutrition-metabolism]], and physical and chemical
signals from plant or animal hosts used as cues for invasion and penetration.

### Biotic Signals from the Fungus Itself
Extracellular chemical signals produced by the fungus itself, collectively
called autoregulators, play critical roles in regulating filamentous
organization and differentiated structures. Some act as germination
self-inhibitors, others influence germ-tube emergence patterns, and still
others cause hyphal avoidance, growth away from colony centres, and hyphal
homing as a prelude to hyphal fusion.

## Quorum Sensing and Autoregulation

Some autoregulatory signals function as [[gadd-fungal-biofilms-quorum-sensing]] and
play roles in regulating yeast-hyphal dimorphism, chlamydospore formation,
and conidial fusion. Fungus-derived signals also regulate asexual and sexual
reproduction. Self-produced compounds can stimulate asexual reproduction or
regulate the choice between sexual and asexual pathways.

Sex pheromones include trisporic acid used in the Mucorales and a variety
of peptide pheromones used by filamentous ascomycetes and basidiomycetes.
These [[fungal-pheromones-and-sexual-signaling]] systems enable communication between mating partners and
coordinate the complex developmental programme of sexual reproduction.

## Signal Transduction Machinery

### Two-Component Signalling Systems
Two-component signalling is used extensively in prokaryotes and also found
in plants, slime moulds, yeasts, and filamentous fungi but not in animals.
Neurospora crassa possesses two-component signalling of a complex type
uncommon in prokaryotes, consisting of hybrid proteins with both histidine
kinase and response regulator domains. N. crassa has eleven histidine
kinases, compared with one in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and three in
Schizosaccharomyces pombe, reflecting the greater complexity of sensing
required by filamentous growth.

Two histidine kinases are involved in hyphal development and three others
appear to be involved in light sensing. The conservation of downstream
elements (one histidine phosphotransferase and two response regulators)
suggests that these proteins integrate multiple signalling inputs from many
histidine kinases to evoke proper cellular responses.

### G-Protein Coupled Receptors (GPCRs)
N. crassa possesses 35 predicted GPCRs and GPCR-like proteins, whereas
S. cerevisiae has only six. Ten of the N. crassa GPCRs fall into the five
traditional classes: microbial opsins, pheromone receptors, glucose sensors,
nitrogen sensors, and a class showing similarity to cAMP receptors in
Dictyostelium discoideum.

A novel sixth class of GPCR-like proteins (PTH11-related proteins) was
identified in Magnaporthe grisea with 25 members also found in N. crassa.
G-protein signalling plays roles in hyphal growth, conidiation, female
fertility, and [[fungal-stress-responses]].

### MAP Kinase Cascades
Mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinases are critical components of a central
intracellular signalling switchboard. They consist of three types of
serine/threonine protein kinase: MAP kinase kinase kinase (MAPKKK), MAP
kinase kinase (MAPKK), and MAP kinase (MAPK), which act sequentially. N.
crassa possesses a substantial complement of MAP kinase components, enabling
it to coordinate responses to diverse environmental stimuli.

### cAMP Signalling
cAMP is an intracellular second messenger involved in regulating aerial
hyphal growth, aerial hyphal formation, conidiation, and thermotolerance
in N. crassa. cAMP activates protein kinase A, for which N. crassa possesses
two catalytic subunits and one regulatory subunit.

### Calcium Signalling
Calcium signalling is expanded in filamentous fungi compared with yeasts,
with multiple calcium channels, transport proteins, and calmodulin-regulated
processes. Calcium functions as a versatile second messenger in responses to
environmental stress, hyphal tip growth, and mechanosensation. See also [[fungal-calcium-signalling]].

### Ras-like GTPases
Five Ras/Ras-related GTPases are present in N. crassa, functioning as
molecular switches that control a wide range of cellular processes including
signal transduction, hyphal growth, and [[fungal-conidia-and-spore-types]].

## Expansion of Sensing Capacity in Filamentous Fungi

Compared with yeast cells, filamentous fungi live in more complex,
heterogeneous environments, and the environmental signals they have to
respond to are more varied and changing as hyphae grow through their
environments. Genome analysis has revealed that filamentous fungi possess
a more extensive array of sensing and signalling capabilities than yeasts.
