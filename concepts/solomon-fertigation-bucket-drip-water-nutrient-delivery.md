# Fertigation Technique — Bucket Drip for Water and Nutrient Delivery

**Source:** Gardening When It Counts (Solomon, 2005)

## Overview

Fertigation is Solomon's preferred method for delivering both water and
nutrients to vegetable plants during dry periods. The technique uses a simple
bucket with a small drain hole, placed next to each large plant, to slowly
drip fertilizer solution directly into the root zone. Solomon's dry-gardening
trials in Oregon demonstrated that fertigated [[cannabis-grow-nutrient-deficiency-problem-solving]] induced by dry
surface soil — when plant nutrients are located in the plowed surface layer
but roots have extended into dry subsoil seeking moisture, the plants cannot
access the nutrients they need.

Fertigation solves both problems simultaneously: it delivers water and
dissolved nutrients directly to the root zone.

## The Fertigation Bucket Setup

### Equipment
- A standard bucket (5 gallon / 20 liters)
- A small drain hole drilled in the bottom
- The drain hole size determines drip rate and is calibrated to soil type

### Drain Hole Sizing
The speed of water coming out of the bucket determines how widely moisture
spreads. The drain hole must be sized to match your soil:

- **Sandy soil:** Accepts moisture rapidly; water goes deep and narrow. Use a
  slightly larger hole. Apply ~2.5 gallons (10 liters) every 10 days.
- **Clay soil (20%+ clay):** Accepts moisture slowly; water spreads broadly.
  Use a smaller hole. Apply ~5 gallons (20 liters) every 3 weeks.

The goal is that water sinks straight in, making a wet spot no larger than the
drip point. This way moisture penetrates deeply into the subsoil rather than
spreading across the surface.

## Fertigation Solutions

### Compost or Manure Tea (Primary Method)
1. Fill a barrel (or garbage can) with water
2. Dump in a bucketful of fresh manure or compost
3. Allow it to brew for a week, stirring every few days
4. Dip out buckets of concentrated tea
5. Dilute according to the strength of the compost/manure
6. Refill barrel with fresh water and add more material when depleted

### Comfrey Leaf Tea
Armloads of comfrey leaves make an excellent fertigation brew. Comfrey is
high in potassium and [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]] as well as NPK are
also highly effective. When applied in the highly dilute form used in
fertigation, they do not damage soil life. However, they do not replace the
need for regular additions of [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]].

## Which Plants Benefit Most

Fertigation is most useful for large, moisture-demanding plants:
- Tomatoes, melons, corn, squash, pumpkins
- Brussels sprouts, large cabbage, broccoli
- Pepper bushes
- Any plant showing drought stress despite adequate rainfall

Smaller plants and low-demand vegetables generally do not need fertigation.
