# Three Ways of Farming Compared

In "The Natural Way of Farming," Masanobu Fukuoka presents three distinct
paradigms of agriculture that represent fundamentally different relationships
between humanity and nature. Understanding these three paths reveals why
modern agriculture has arrived at its current crisis and why natural farming
offers the most viable way forward.

## Natural Farming (Mu Farming)

Natural farming is based on a nature free of human meddling and intervention.
It originates in the philosophy of "Mu" (nothingness) and returns to a
"do-nothing" nature. The objective is non-action and a return to nature;
the method is centrifugal and convergent. Natural farming is described as
the "methodless method of nature, the unmoving way of Bodhidharma."

Its core characteristics:

- **No tillage**: Soil is worked by microorganisms and small animals, not
  by plows. No plant is so weak as to germinate only in plowed soil.
- **No fertilizer**: Soil enriches itself year in and year out. Straw and
  green manure replace all external inputs.
- **No pesticides**: A thousand diseases attack plants, but nature strikes
  a balance. Natural enemies emerge in proportion to pest populations.
- **No weeding**: Weeds are not arbitrarily designated as enemies. Weed
  diversity contributes to soil health and ecological balance.
- **No pruning**: Trees grow best in their natural form; branches do not
  tangle, sunlight falls on every leaf, and the tree bears fully each year.

Natural farming produces approximately 130 pounds of rice (200,000
kilocalories) per man-day of labor without any outside material inputs.
This is roughly 100 times the daily caloric intake of a farmer on a
natural diet, making it by far the most energy-efficient farming method.

## Traditional Farming

Traditional Japanese farming occupied a middle ground between nature and
human intervention. Farmers devoted themselves to serving the land that
grows crops. They used animal power, prepared compost from grass cuttings
and straw, and practiced continuous rice-barley rotation. The rice paddy
was seen as the "arbor of the soul," and the farmer did not believe he
"grew" the rice but rather that nature did, and people partook of its
blessings.

Traditional farming required approximately ten times the energy input of
natural farming, primarily through animal labor and compost preparation.
Yields peaked at about 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre, limited by
the labor-intensive nature of compost preparation and tillage.

While far more sustainable than modern scientific farming, traditional
farming still involved significant human intervention: plowing, compost
production, weeding, and crop management. It was a stepping stone that
preserved ecological wisdom but fell short of the "do-nothing" ideal.

## Scientific (Modern) Farming

Scientific farming breaks away from nature with the expansion of human
wants and desires. It is centripetal and divergent — an outward expansion
that cannot be stopped. Scientific farming treats agriculture as a
processing industry that uses oil energy in the form of fertilizers,
pesticides, and machinery to manufacture synthetic food products that are
poor imitations of natural food.

The energy progression tells the story: traditional farming used ten
times the energy of natural farming. Small-scale mechanization doubled it
again. Large-scale mechanization doubled it yet another time. Today's
energy-intensive methods represent a geometric progression of waste.

Key features of scientific farming:

- **Mechanization**: Rather than reducing labor, it enslaves the farmer
  to equipment. Machinery is "the largest domestic animal of all — a
  great guzzler of oil, a consumer good rather than a capital good."
- **Chemical dependency**: Killing soil with plow and chemical fertilizer
  creates weak, diseased plants that require more chemicals — a vicious
  cycle without end.
- **Fragmented understanding**: Science inserts its scalpel into the
  unified harmony of nature and finds only "a complex and horrendously
  chaotic array of elements."
- **Yield illusion**: High-yield practices do not actually exceed natural
  productivity. They merely restore a fraction of the productivity that
  was lost when nature was hamstrung.

## The Trajectory

Japan's rice cultivation illustrates the progression: primitive farming
(1940), animal-powered agriculture (1950), scientific farming with
mechanization (1960), and agribusiness with energy-intensive systems
(1970). With each stage, the farmer became further alienated from the
land and from the spiritual dimension of agriculture.

The critical insight is that scientific farming is doomed to extinction
because its outward expansion cannot be halted. Each new technology only
makes it more complex and diversified, generating ever-increasing expense
and labor. Natural farming, by contrast, is simple, economical, and
labor-saving — and remains current and applicable in any age.

## The Convergence Point

Fukuoka suggests that the three ways of farming represent stages in a
historical trajectory, not equally valid alternatives. Primitive farming
gave way to traditional farming, which gave way to scientific farming.
But scientific farming, by its nature of endless expansion, cannot
sustain itself. It must eventually exhaust either the resources it
consumes or the patience of those who suffer its consequences.

The exit from this trajectory is not a return to primitive conditions
but a leap forward to natural farming — which is simultaneously the
oldest and the newest way of growing food. Ancient because it reflects
the processes that sustained ecosystems for hundreds of millions of
years before humans appeared. New because its principles have never
been consciously and systematically applied to food production at scale.

Natural farming is not about abandoning knowledge but about recognizing
the limits of knowledge and learning to work within them. It is about
trusting the processes of nature that have proven themselves over
evolutionary time, rather than substituting human cleverness for natural
wisdom.
