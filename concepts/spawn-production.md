# Spawn Production

Spawn is the mycelial "seed" material used to inoculate bulk substrates in mushroom
cultivation. It is the critical bridge between a pure culture and a productive fruiting
substrate. Without high-quality spawn, contamination rates soar and yields collapse.
The entire commercial mushroom industry depends on reliable spawn production, making it
one of the most important technical disciplines in mycology.

## Principles of Spawn Making

The goal of spawn production is to expand a small amount of pure mycelium into a large
volume of carrier material while maintaining genetic purity. This is done through a
series of staggered expansions called "generations." A typical commercial operation uses
three generations: stock culture (first generation or G1), mother spawn (G2), and
planting spawn (G3). Each generation represents roughly a 10- to 100-fold increase in
biomass. The mycelium must be vigorous, fast-colonizing, and free of competing organisms
at every stage.

## Carrier Materials

Different carriers suit different scales and species. The most common include:

- **Cereal grains**: Rye, wheat, millet, sorghum, and milo are the standard carriers
  for most gourmet and medicinal species. Grains are nutritious, have a large surface
  area for colonization, and break apart easily for even distribution. Rye is widely
  considered the best all-around grain spawn medium.
- **Wood-based spawn**: Sawdust or wood chip spawn is preferred for wood-decomposing
  species like shiitake, maitake, and reishi. Hardwood sawdust (oak, beech, alder)
  supplemented with bran provides an excellent matrix for these species.
- **Supplemented sawdust**: A blend of sawdust with wheat bran (typically 5-20%
  bran by dry weight) dramatically increases mycelial growth rates and final spawn
  vigor. This is the industry standard for shiitake and other log-grown species.
- **Liquid culture**: Mycelium grown in a nutrient broth (malt extract, dextrose, yeast)
  can be used to inoculate grain or sawdust directly. Liquid culture is fast and
  scalable but requires strict sterility and offers shorter shelf life than grain spawn.

## Sterilization

Spawn production demands absolute sterility. Grain spawn is typically sterilized by
autoclaving at 15 PSI (121 degrees C) for 60 to 90 minutes, depending on bag size and
grain volume. Sawdust spawn requires longer sterilization cycles (90-120 minutes)
because wood particles insulate bacterial endospores. Some producers use tyndallization
(fractional sterilization) for sawdust, running three consecutive 60-minute steam
cycles with 24-hour rest periods between them to allow endospores to germinate and
then be killed.

## Inoculation

Inoculation is performed in a sterile environment, either a laminar flow hood or a
still-air box. Grain-to-grain transfer is the fastest method: fully colonized grain is
poured into fresh sterilized grain bags or jars and mixed. A single jar of G1 grain
spawn can inoculate 10 jars of G2, and those 10 can each inoculate 10 bags of G3,
yielding 1,000 planting spawn units from one stock culture. Liquid culture can be
injected through self-healing injection ports for even faster expansion.

## Quality Control

Spawn quality is assessed by colonization speed, mycelial appearance, and absence of
contamination. Good spawn should fully colonize its carrier within 10-21 days
depending on species and temperature. The mycelium should appear thick, white, and
ropy. Any off-colors (green, pink, black, slimy yellow) indicate contamination and
the batch must be discarded. Spawn that smells sour or sweet (rather than earthy and
mushroomy) is likely bacterial. Some producers do test grows of each spawn batch to
verify fruiting performance before large-scale use.

## Storage

Properly made grain spawn can be stored at 2-4 degrees C for 2-6 months depending on
the species. Oyster mushroom spawn has the shortest shelf life (2-3 months), while
shiitake and reishi spawn can last 4-6 months. Sawdust spawn generally stores longer
than grain spawn because the lower nutrient density slows senescence. Liquid culture
should be used within 30 days and ideally within 2 weeks of preparation.

## Common Problems

Contamination is the primary challenge. It usually results from incomplete
sterilization, poor inoculation technique, or compromised filter patches on spawn bags.
Slow or stalled colonization can indicate overly wet grain, insufficient gas exchange,
old or senescent mycelium, or suboptimal incubation temperature. "Bacterial wet spot"
is a frequent issue with grain spawn, caused by excess moisture creating anaerobic
pockets where bacteria thrive. The solution is better grain hydration (soak, not boil)
and proper drying before bagging.
