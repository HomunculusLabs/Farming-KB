## The Feedback Principle

### Self-Regulation Through Feedback

The principle of apply self-regulation and accept feedback is one of the most important yet most challenging [[mollison-designers-climate-change-adaptation-strategies]]

Effective adaptation requires:
- Regular observation of the system and its environment
- Honest assessment of what is working and what is not
- Willingness to change course when feedback indicates problems
- Flexibility in design -- elements that can be modified, moved, or removed
- Redundancy -- multiple pathways to meet each need, so if one fails others remain
- Diversity -- a wide range of species and strategies to buffer against change

## Ecological Coherence

### What Is Ecological Coherence?

Ecological coherence means that a design functions in harmony with the natural systems of its site. The elements of the design support and enhance rather than degrade the ecological processes that sustain them. A coherent design:

- Works with natural forces rather than against them
- Uses the patterns and processes of the local ecosystem
- Maintains or improves soil health, water quality, and biodiversity
- Produces yields without degrading the productive capacity of the system
- Creates conditions where natural regulation reduces the need for human intervention

### The Checklist for Ecological Design

The Williams course provides a comprehensive checklist for ecological design:

- Use as little energy as possible
- Provide comfortable shelter for habitation
- Generate and/or harness as much energy as possible
- Provide as much clean water as possible
- Provide as much food as possible
- Provide space for social interaction and meaningful interaction with nature
- Have the appropriate support structures for any endeavours planned
- Include habitat for other species as much as possible
- Be easily maintained by the inhabitants
- Be designed for maintenance, upgrade, and disposal/obsolescence
- Manage all of its own waste
- Follow the Triple Bottom Line (social, environmental, economic sustainability)

### The Problem Is the Solution

The Williams course expands on the principle that the problem is the solution:

A problem represents a significant amount of embodied energy in your system. The principles tell us to catch and store that energy. The principles also tell us to produce no waste. If you encounter a problem and do not repurpose it into a beneficial element, you have wasted an opportunity. A problem is only something that has been de-valued and marginalised.

This principle calls for imagination and adaptability on the part of the designer. The first instinct with a problem is to remove it. What this principle calls for is dropping the judgement of a thing and finding its potential within the design.
