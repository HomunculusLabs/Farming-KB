# pdc adaptation feedback and ecological coherence

The concepts of adaptation, feedback, and ecological coherence form the dynamic core of [[permaculture-design-methodology]]. Unlike conventional design approaches that create static plans, permaculture recognises that all systems exist within constantly changing environments. The ability to observe feedback, accept it honestly, and adapt the design accordingly is what separates a resilient permaculture system from a rigid, fragile one. The Williams PDC course returns to these themes repeatedly throughout its treatment of principles, design methods, and community processes.

## The Feedback Principle

### Self-Regulation Through Feedback

The principle of apply self-regulation and accept feedback is one of the most important yet most challenging [[permaculture-principles]]. The Williams course explains it directly:

Part of the process of self-regulation is accepting the feedback from your actions and changing in response to that feedback. Without accepting feedback there can be no self-regulation, without self-regulation there can be no true ecological coherence. From analysis, to concept, to implementation, to ongoing usage, the permaculture designer must be open to accepting feedback and self-regulating the actions and impacts of designs and habits.

### The Human Challenge

The course acknowledges that humans have the capacity to NOT self-regulate when it comes to achieving a harmonious balance with the environment. The problem with this attitude is that eventually nature wins, one way or the other. When humans ignore the natural balance, the methods by which nature achieves equilibrium can be very painful and devastating to both the human and natural world.

The goal of permaculture is to achieve a harmonious balance with nature that is healthy and positive for both humans and the Earth. This requires conscious self-regulation in accordance with the limits of the natural environment.

### Types of Feedback

Feedback in a permaculture system comes in many forms:

Ecological feedback: Changes in soil health, water patterns, biodiversity, plant growth, and animal behaviour that indicate whether the system is moving toward or away from ecological balance.

Yield feedback: The quantity and quality of outputs from the system. Declining yields signal problems; increasing yields indicate maturation and improvement.

Social feedback: The experience and satisfaction of the people living within the design. If a design element is not used because it is inconvenient or doesn't meet real needs, that is important feedback.

Economic feedback: The costs and benefits of the system over time. If maintenance costs exceed the value of yields, the design needs adjustment.

Observational feedback: Direct observation of how the design interacts with existing site patterns. If a design disrupts patterns on the site in unexpected ways, that is feedback that the design is not compatible with the ecology of the site.

## Adaptation and Change

### Creatively Using and Responding to Change

The principle of creatively use and respond to change recognises that change is inevitable and constant. The Williams course connects this to several other principles:

- Reading patterns and determining the proper pattern language may enable a design to produce the greatest yield
- If you want to creatively use and respond to change, you must understand what patterns exist and what patterns they can be changed into with the energy available on the site
- When a designer does not understand the patterns on their site, waste will almost always be the result

### Ecotones and Adaptation

The course discusses ecotones -- the transitional zones between different ecosystems -- as areas of particularly high diversity and adaptation potential. Ecotones are one of the manifestations of adaptation. Understanding ecotones helps designers create edge effects that maximise diversity and productivity.

### [[mollison-designers-climate-change-adaptation-strategies]]

Effective adaptation requires:
- Regular observation of the system and its environment
- Honest assessment of what is working and what is not
- Willingness to change course when feedback indicates problems
- Flexibility in design -- elements that can be modified, moved, or removed
- Redundancy -- multiple pathways to meet each need, so if one fails others remain
- Diversity -- a wide range of species and strategies to buffer against change

## Ecological Coherence

### What Is Ecological Coherence?

Ecological coherence means that a design functions in harmony with the natural systems of its site. The elements of the design support and enhance rather than degrade the ecological processes that sustain them. A coherent design:

- Works with natural forces rather than against them
- Uses the patterns and processes of the local ecosystem
- Maintains or improves soil health, water quality, and biodiversity
- Produces yields without degrading the productive capacity of the system
- Creates conditions where natural regulation reduces the need for human intervention

### The Checklist for Ecological Design

The Williams course provides a comprehensive checklist for ecological design:

- Use as little energy as possible
- Provide comfortable shelter for habitation
- Generate and/or harness as much energy as possible
- Provide as much clean water as possible
- Provide as much food as possible
- Provide space for social interaction and meaningful interaction with nature
- Have the appropriate support structures for any endeavours planned
- Include habitat for other species as much as possible
- Be easily maintained by the inhabitants
- Be designed for maintenance, upgrade, and disposal/obsolescence
- Manage all of its own waste
- Follow the Triple Bottom Line (social, environmental, economic sustainability)

### The Problem Is the Solution

The Williams course expands on the principle that the problem is the solution:

A problem represents a significant amount of embodied energy in your system. The principles tell us to catch and store that energy. The principles also tell us to produce no waste. If you encounter a problem and do not repurpose it into a beneficial element, you have wasted an opportunity. A problem is only something that has been de-valued and marginalised.

This principle calls for imagination and adaptability on the part of the designer. The first instinct with a problem is to remove it. What this principle calls for is dropping the judgement of a thing and finding its potential within the design.

## The Design as a Living System

### Evolution Over Time

A [[permaculture-design]] is not a static blueprint but a living system that evolves. The Williams course describes succession as a natural process that designs can work with:

- Systems change over time as plants grow, soil improves, and relationships between elements develop
- The designer must anticipate and plan for these changes
- What works in the first year may not work in the fifth year
- The design should include mechanisms for transition between phases

### The Triple Bottom Line

Every design decision should be evaluated against three criteria:
1. Environmental sustainability: Does it support or degrade ecological health?
2. Social sustainability: Does it support or degrade community wellbeing?
3. Economic sustainability: Is it financially viable over the long term?

A design that fails on any of these three dimensions is not ecologically coherent.

## Practical Application

### Monitoring Protocol

To enable effective feedback and adaptation, establish a regular monitoring protocol:

Weekly: Walk the entire site, observe changes, note any problems or opportunities

Monthly: Record observations, compare with previous months, identify trends

Seasonally: Assess yields, review system performance, plan adjustments

Annually: Comprehensive review of the entire design, major adjustments if needed

### Decision-Making Framework

When feedback indicates that adjustment is needed, use this framework:
1. Identify the specific problem or opportunity
2. Understand the root cause (not just symptoms)
3. Generate multiple possible responses
4. Evaluate each response against [[permaculture-design-principles]]s
5. Choose the response that best addresses the root cause while maintaining ecological coherence
6. Implement the change using small and slow solutions
7. Monitor the results of the change
8. Accept the feedback from the change and continue adapting
