- **Size**: Never more than two rooms wide; never more than 1.5x longer than wide. Smaller footprint = easier to heat and cool.
- **Orientation**: Long side faces sun (south in Northern Hemisphere, north in Southern Hemisphere). Bedrooms on shady side; kitchen and living areas on sunny side.
- **Windows and eaves**: Angled so sun hits heat-absorbing materials (tile, brick, stone) in winter but is blocked in summer. This is the fundamental principle of [[faires-hugelkultur-and-forest-garden-strategy]] transitions seamlessly from the home's edge. Key integrations include:

- **Attached greenhouse**: Better attached to the house than built separately. Can meld with the bathroom (Japanese bath design). Base insulated 3 feet into ground. Double-paned windows. Water containers on shelves for thermal mass. Ventilation with electric fan is critical -- greenhouses can overheat even in winter.
- **Shaderoom**: Trellised patio on the shady side with grape vines and misting system. Opening the shaderoom door to the house creates a cooling draft in summer. Can hold a water tank for passive thermal storage. In hot climates, it may extend around the entire house.

## Kitchen Design

The kitchen is the heart of a homesteading house and should be designed around food processing workflows:

- Work and storage room attached to the kitchen
- Large tub sink for washing produce from the garden
- Cupboards designed to hold drying racks and mushroom growing setups
- Pantry for preserved food (canning, drying, fermenting)
- Garden equipment and harvest containers stored near the exit door
- The mudroom to pantry to kitchen flow allows immediate processing of harvests

## Bathroom Design

The bathroom can integrate with the greenhouse, especially in a Japanese-style design:

- Stone, brick, or tile surfaces for passive heat absorption
- Japanese bath approach: quick shower first to get clean, then soak in a deep tub. Water is reused because everyone entering is already clean.
- Attached greenhouse provides humidity, warmth, and a connection to growing plants
- Greywater from the bathroom can irrigate nearby gardens

## Greenhouse Design Details

The attached greenhouse is often the most important part of a home in cold climates:

- Well sealed with ventilation at both top and bottom
- 20-gallon water containers on shelves provide thermal mass (absorb heat during day, release at night)
- Double-paned windows for insulation
- Dark curtains or shutters closed at night to retain heat
- Bunnies or chickens housed in the greenhouse add supplementary heat through body warmth
- Compost bins inside the greenhouse generate additional warmth

## Climate Adaptations

### Very Cold Climates

- Small footprint to minimize surface area for heat loss
- Steep roof to shed snow
- Multiple stories or attached to a warm building (barn)
- Option to close off the greenhouse from the main house during extreme cold
- Grow winter food in the greenhouse using supplementary heat from animals and compost

### Very Hot Climates

- Palm trees naturally shade the roof
- Grass groundcover (not pavement) to reduce heat reflection
- Separate outdoor kitchen near the shaderoom
- White painted roof with steep angle for rain collection
- Deep underground anchors for hurricane zones
- Covered barbecue area with screens and bed netting for open windows

### Desert Climates

- House set into mesa rock for natural thermal mass and insulation
- Flat roof with shaderoom and clothesline
- Water gravity-fed from a dam or tank at the top of the slope
- Rainwater harvesting is critical -- see [[faires-water-purification-methods]]
