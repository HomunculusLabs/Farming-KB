# House Design and Retrofit

An energy-efficient home integrates with its environment, serving as part of the Zone 1 garden system rather than an isolated box. Faires emphasizes passive design, appropriate materials, and retrofitting existing homes. Houses are not just for eating and sleeping -- they also serve as workspaces for pottery, carpentry, seed packaging, computer work, photography, and health services. Space-saving innovations are essential.

This page covers the house as Zone 0. See also [[permaculture-zone-0-house-design]] and [[faires-zone-and-sector-planning]] for how the house connects to the broader property layout.

## Energy-Efficient Design Principles

The core shape and orientation of the house determine most of its energy performance. See [[passive-solar-design]] for the underlying physics.

- **Size**: Never more than two rooms wide; never more than 1.5x longer than wide. Smaller footprint = easier to heat and cool.
- **Orientation**: Long side faces sun (south in Northern Hemisphere, north in Southern Hemisphere). Bedrooms on shady side; kitchen and living areas on sunny side.
- **Windows and eaves**: Angled so sun hits heat-absorbing materials (tile, brick, stone) in winter but is blocked in summer. This is the fundamental principle of [[passive-solar-design]].
- **Window placement**: Small windows on the east (morning light); tiny or no windows on the west and shady side.
- **Insulation**: Extremely well insulated throughout. For a concrete slab foundation: install 2-inch foam insulation 3 feet deep around the entire perimeter to prevent heat loss to frozen ground.

## Entryway and Mudroom

The entryway is the transition between outdoor work and indoor living:

- Stone or brick pathway leading to the door (not dirt, which tracks inside)
- A second entry door creating an airlock against temperature loss
- Designated place for muddy boots with a floor drain
- Hooks for outdoor work clothing

The ideal flow pattern for a homesteading house is: mudroom to pantry to kitchen, so harvests can be processed immediately without tracking through living spaces.

## House as Part of Zone 1

The house is not separate from the garden. Vines and plants grow on walls and possibly the roof. A [[faires-hugelkultur-and-forest-garden-strategy]] transitions seamlessly from the home's edge. Key integrations include:

- **Attached greenhouse**: Better attached to the house than built separately. Can meld with the bathroom (Japanese bath design). Base insulated 3 feet into ground. Double-paned windows. Water containers on shelves for thermal mass. Ventilation with electric fan is critical -- greenhouses can overheat even in winter.
- **Shaderoom**: Trellised patio on the shady side with grape vines and misting system. Opening the shaderoom door to the house creates a cooling draft in summer. Can hold a water tank for passive thermal storage. In hot climates, it may extend around the entire house.

## Kitchen Design

The kitchen is the heart of a homesteading house and should be designed around food processing workflows:

- Work and storage room attached to the kitchen
- Large tub sink for washing produce from the garden
- Cupboards designed to hold drying racks and mushroom growing setups
- Pantry for preserved food (canning, drying, fermenting)
- Garden equipment and harvest containers stored near the exit door
- The mudroom to pantry to kitchen flow allows immediate processing of harvests

## Bathroom Design

The bathroom can integrate with the greenhouse, especially in a Japanese-style design:

- Stone, brick, or tile surfaces for passive heat absorption
- Japanese bath approach: quick shower first to get clean, then soak in a deep tub. Water is reused because everyone entering is already clean.
- Attached greenhouse provides humidity, warmth, and a connection to growing plants
- Greywater from the bathroom can irrigate nearby gardens

## Greenhouse Design Details

The attached greenhouse is often the most important part of a home in cold climates:

- Well sealed with ventilation at both top and bottom
- 20-gallon water containers on shelves provide thermal mass (absorb heat during day, release at night)
- Double-paned windows for insulation
- Dark curtains or shutters closed at night to retain heat
- Bunnies or chickens housed in the greenhouse add supplementary heat through body warmth
- Compost bins inside the greenhouse generate additional warmth

## Climate Adaptations

### Very Cold Climates

- Small footprint to minimize surface area for heat loss
- Steep roof to shed snow
- Multiple stories or attached to a warm building (barn)
- Option to close off the greenhouse from the main house during extreme cold
- Grow winter food in the greenhouse using supplementary heat from animals and compost

### Very Hot Climates

- Palm trees naturally shade the roof
- Grass groundcover (not pavement) to reduce heat reflection
- Separate outdoor kitchen near the shaderoom
- White painted roof with steep angle for rain collection
- Deep underground anchors for hurricane zones
- Covered barbecue area with screens and bed netting for open windows

### Desert Climates

- House set into mesa rock for natural thermal mass and insulation
- Flat roof with shaderoom and clothesline
- Water gravity-fed from a dam or tank at the top of the slope
- Rainwater harvesting is critical -- see [[backyard-water-harvesting]]

## Retrofitting Existing Homes

Even a typical inefficient house can be improved without major construction. Follow these steps in order of impact:

1. **Seal** all windows and doors with weatherstripping; install double-paned windows if affordable
2. **Insulate** the attic and roof (easier and cheaper than walls); wrap the exterior if possible
3. **Add a greenhouse** -- even a small one against one wall, ideally opening into a stone or brick bathroom
4. **Add passive thermal mass** -- brick or rock wall facing windows, tile on living room floor
5. **Attach a shaderoom** on the shady side with a doorway connecting to the house for cooling draft
6. **Switch appliances** to energy-efficient models; add solar hot water heater or on-demand water heater; use clotheslines to replace the dryer

At minimum, even a skylight can bring significant passive solar benefit to a dark house.

## Temperature Management Without Energy

- Heavy curtains: closed at night in winter to retain heat, closed during day in summer to block sun
- Summer strategy: open windows at night to let cool air in, close before morning heat arrives; bamboo blinds outside windows
- Cross ventilation: open all doors and vent the greenhouse top to push hot air out
- Ceiling fan uses significantly less electricity than air conditioning
- Tile, brick, and stone floors and walls: absorb sun heat in winter (radiate warmth at night), absorb cool night air in summer (stay cool during day)
- Cook and bake at night or outside to avoid heating the house
