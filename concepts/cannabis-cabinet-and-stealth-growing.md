# Cannabis Cabinet and Stealth Growing

Cabinet growing is a method of cultivating cannabis inside enclosed furniture or purpose-built boxes that conceal the grow from casual observation. This approach is used by growers who need to hide their cultivation from housemates, landlords, or visitors. Cabinet grows require careful planning to manage the unique challenges of growing in confined spaces with limited ventilation, light, and root volume.

See [[cannabis-indoor-growing-basics]] for fundamental indoor growing principles, [[cannabis-lighting-guide]] for selecting appropriate lighting, and [[cannabis-environment-control]] for managing heat and humidity in enclosed spaces.

## Cabinet Design Principles

A successful cabinet grow must address four critical factors: light containment, ventilation, odor control, and access for maintenance. The cabinet must be completely light-proof to prevent light leakage during the 12-hour dark period that would disrupt flowering. All seams, joints, and openings must be sealed with light-proof material.

Ventilation is typically the biggest challenge in cabinet growing. Enclosed spaces heat up rapidly under HID lighting, and without adequate air exchange, temperatures quickly exceed the safe range for cannabis. Active ventilation using an exhaust fan and passive or active intake is essential. The cabinet should have at least one complete air exchange per minute during light hours.

Odor control can be achieved with a carbon filter on the exhaust fan. Carbon scrubbers effectively remove the characteristic cannabis smell from exhausted air, making the cabinet undetectable by scent. The carbon filter must be properly sized for the cabinet's airflow volume.

Access for maintenance means the grower must be able to reach all plants for watering, training, pruning, and eventually harvesting. Hinged doors, removable panels, or front-opening designs work better than top-access designs for this purpose.

## Lighting for Cabinet Grows

Light selection for cabinet growing is constrained by heat generation. HID lights produce excellent results but generate significant heat that is difficult to manage in a small enclosed space. A 250W HPS is typically the maximum practical HID wattage for a cabinet, and even this requires robust ventilation.

LED grow lights have become the preferred choice for cabinet grows because they produce less heat while still providing full-spectrum light suitable for all growth stages. Low-power LED panels (100-200 watts actual power draw) can illuminate a cabinet effectively while generating minimal heat that can be managed with a small exhaust fan.

Compact fluorescent (CFL) lights are the most budget-friendly option and produce very little heat. However, their lower light intensity limits yield potential. Multiple CFL bulbs positioned around the canopy can partially compensate for the lower per-bulb output.

## Strain Selection

Strain selection is critical for cabinet success. Only compact, short-stature Indica or Indica-dominant strains should be considered. Pure Sativa strains and tall hybrids will quickly outgrow even the largest cabinets. Autoflowering strains are well-suited to cabinet growing because of their small size and predictable, short life cycle, though they typically produce lower yields than photoperiod strains.

Specific strains known for compact growth include Northern Lights, Lowryder and its derivatives, and various "dwarf" or "mini" strains bred specifically for small-space cultivation. When selecting any strain for cabinet growing, research its expected final height and choose accordingly.

## Training for Small Spaces

Cabinet growing almost always requires plant training to keep plants within the available height. [[cannabis-pruning-and-defoliation-guide]] during the vegetative stage creates a shorter, bushier plant. Low-stress training (LST) bends branches horizontally to fill the available width rather than growing tall.

Screen of Green (ScrOG) works exceptionally well in cabinet grows because the screen keeps all growth at a uniform, controlled height beneath the light. See [[cannabis-scrog-and-sog-methods]] for detailed ScrOG and SOG techniques adapted to confined spaces.

The main limitation of cabinet growing is height. Most cabinets offer 3-5 feet of usable vertical space. After accounting for the container, the light, and the gap between the light and the canopy, only 2-3 feet may be available for actual plant growth. This severely limits the size of plants and consequently the yield per plant.

## Container Size and Root Management

Small cabinets require small containers, which means small root systems. Plants in small containers need more frequent watering and feeding than plants in larger containers. A 1-3 gallon container is typical for cabinet grows, though some micro-grow setups use containers as small as half a gallon.

Root-bound plants are a common problem in cabinet growing because the limited container size restricts root development. When a plant becomes root-bound, growth slows and nutrient problems develop. The grower must either transplant to a larger container before flowering or accept the yield limitation imposed by the small root mass.

Fabric pots (smart pots) are recommended for cabinet grows because they air-prune roots, preventing the circling that occurs in solid containers. This maximizes the efficiency of the limited root space available.

## Yield Expectations

Cabinet yields are inherently limited by the small growing space. A well-managed cabinet grow with proper lighting, ventilation, and training might produce 1-4 ounces per grow cycle, depending on cabinet size and strain. This is significantly less than a dedicated grow room but can still provide a meaningful supply for personal use.

Maximizing yield in a cabinet requires filling every available cubic inch of the growing space with healthy plant canopy. ScrOG training, multiple small plants in SOG configuration, or a single well-trained plant can all be effective approaches depending on the cabinet dimensions.

## Common Cabinet Problems

Heat is the most common problem. Without adequate ventilation, cabinet temperatures can exceed 100 degrees F, which stresses plants and reduces yield. Solutions include switching to cooler lighting (LED), increasing exhaust fan capacity, adding an intake fan, or using air-cooled light fixtures.

Odor is the second most common problem. Carbon filters must be properly sized and maintained. A filter that is too small for the airflow volume will not effectively remove odor. Carbon filters also lose effectiveness over time and should be replaced every 6-12 months depending on use.

Noise from exhaust fans can be a concern in stealth situations. Inline fans can be muffled with duct insulation and by mounting them on rubber vibration-dampening pads. The cabinet itself can be lined with acoustic foam to reduce fan noise transmission.

## Converting Furniture to Grow Cabinets

Many growers convert existing furniture into grow cabinets. Old refrigerators, wardrobes, dressers, and storage cabinets can all be adapted with proper modifications. The key requirements are light-proof lining (Mylar or white paint), ventilation holes with fans, a carbon filter for odor, and electrical access for lighting and timers.

Purpose-built grow cabinets are also commercially available and offer the advantage of integrated ventilation, lighting, and odor control systems. These pre-built solutions are more expensive but save the time and effort of custom construction.

## Watering Challenges in Cabinets

Watering in a confined cabinet space requires care to avoid spills on electrical equipment and to reach all plants without disturbing the canopy. Long-necked watering cans, squeeze bottles, or small watering wands help direct water precisely to the root zone. A waterproof tray beneath all containers catches runoff and prevents water damage to the cabinet floor.

Runoff water must be managed. In soil grows, a tray beneath each container catches excess water that drains during watering. In hydroponic systems, the reservoir must be accessible for nutrient changes and level checks. Cabinet designs should include easy access to the reservoir and drainage system.

Self-watering systems using wicks or capillary mats can reduce the frequency of manual watering visits, which is useful for growers who cannot access the cabinet daily. However, these systems require careful setup to avoid overwatering, which is the most common cause of plant death in small-container grows.

## Automation and Monitoring

Small environmental controllers that monitor temperature and humidity are valuable additions to any cabinet grow. These devices can trigger alarms or automatically adjust fans when conditions go out of range. For growers who cannot check their cabinet daily, a timer-based watering system or drip irrigation setup ensures plants receive consistent moisture.

Hygrometers and thermometers placed at canopy height provide the most relevant readings for plant health. Temperature at the canopy is often several degrees higher than the room ambient temperature, particularly under HID lighting. Monitoring both values helps identify heat problems before they damage plants.
