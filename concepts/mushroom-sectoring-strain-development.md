# Mushroom Sectoring and Strain Development

Sectoring is one of the most important visual tools available to the mushroom cultivator working with [[mushroom-genetics-and-mating-systems]] on agar. The appearance of wedge-shaped zones of differing mycelial characteristics within a colony provides critical information about strain purity, vigor, and fruiting potential. Understanding sectoring is essential for [[mushroom-strain-selection-genetics]] and [[agar-culture-work]].

This technique is described in detail in Paul Stamets' The Mushroom Cultivator and remains a foundational skill for any cultivator developing new strains from [[mushroom-spore-printing-and-storage]].

## What Is Sectoring?

Sectoring occurs when a colony of mycelium produces wedge-shaped areas (sectors) that differ from the surrounding, predominant mycelia in growth rate, texture, color, or density. The term is defined solely in contrast to the surrounding growth pattern — a sector is any region that deviates from the main colony's characteristics.

Two major classes of mycelial sectors are recognized: rhizomorphic (strandy, rope-like) and tomentose (cottony, fluffy). These two growth forms represent fundamentally different levels of metabolic activity and organization within the [[fungal-growth-dynamics]].

Sectors are common in [[query-how-to-make-liquid-culture-for-mushroom-cultivation]] and although little genetic research has specifically addressed the causes of sectoring, experienced cultivators have developed reliable interpretations.

## Causes of Sectoring in Young Cultures

In young multispore cultures, sectoring has a clear genetic explanation. When a spore print is germinated on [[mushroom-agar-media-recipes]], many different strains grow simultaneously. Some are products of legitimate matings between compatible monokaryons, while others are illegitimate dikaryons that look convincing but cannot produce fruitbodies. Each strain has unique characteristics — different growth rates, textures, fruiting capabilities, and preferred conditions.

According to Stoller (1962), the growth of fluffy (tomentose) sectors is encouraged by broken and exploded grain kernels during [[mushroom-grain-spawn-preparation]], by high sugar concentrations in the media, and by elevated temperatures. At slightly acidic pH levels around 6.5, sectoring is more frequent. Sectors on grain can sometimes be reversed by transferring to lower-sugar agar media.

The different strains formed from [[mushroom-genetics-and-mating-systems]] naturally sort themselves into sectors as the colony expands. This self-segregation is the cultivator's primary tool for isolating pure, fruiting-capable strains.

## Rhizomorphic vs. Tomentose Growth

Rhizomorphic mycelium appears as thick, strand-like cords that radiate outward with defined directionality. It is generally considered the more desirable growth form because it correlates strongly with fruiting potential.

Tomentose mycelium appears cottony, fluffy, and undifferentiated. It covers the agar surface evenly but without the organized strand structure. While it can colonize substrates, it typically produces fewer and less vigorous fruitbodies.

The relationship between these two forms is not always fixed. A predominantly rhizomorphic culture may begin producing tomentose sectors, and vice versa. However, when rhizomorphic and tomentose sectors are isolated from the same plate and each is used to inoculate separate substrates, the rhizomorphic sector typically forms abundant primordia while the tomentose sector does not — an observation common across many species grown on agar.

## Practical Sectoring Protocol

When working with a multispore germination plate, the cultivator should follow a systematic approach to strain development.

First, observe the plate carefully. Note which sectors are rhizomorphic and which are tomentose. Different strains also vary in color — some mycelia are bright white, others take on brownish, bluish, or yellowish hues. Color varies with both strain and the [[mushroom-agar-media-recipes]] used.

Second, always propagate only rhizomorphic sectors and avoid cottony ones. Use a sterilized scalpel or inoculating loop to transfer small wedges from the leading edge of rhizomorphic sectors to fresh plates. Each transfer should go to its own plate to prevent cross-contamination between strains.

Third, repeat this process through multiple transfers. A strain that initially sectors may stabilize after two or three rounds of selection — meaning the colony grows uniformly without producing new sectors of differing characteristics. This stabilization indicates a genetically pure strain.

Fourth, once a stable strain is obtained, it should be preserved as a [[fungal-culture-preservation]] (slant) before any further testing. Multiple slants per strain insure against loss.

## Sectoring in Old Cultures: Senescence

A different type of sectoring occurs in old, pure cultures. As cultures age and undergo repeated subculturing, random mutations accumulate. These mutations eventually express themselves as sectors of differing growth characteristics. A strain that has reached this condition is no longer pure and should not be used for cultivation.

This senescence-related sectoring is why [[fungal-culture-preservation]] is so important. Every subculturing event provides accumulated mutations an opportunity to express. The solution is to minimize subculturing by maintaining stock cultures and going back to the original slant whenever possible.

Perrin (1979) found that all 30 wood-inhabiting species stored under mineral oil for 27 years produced viable cultures. Strains stored under mineral oil for the same period showed distinct signs of degeneration when repeatedly subcultured, while those same strains maintained on original slants remained vigorous. This underscores the importance of the hierarchy: use stock cultures to produce working cultures, never the reverse.

## Strain Selection Criteria

When evaluating isolated strains for cultivation potential, Stamets identifies several key criteria.

First, rhizomorphic growth is the primary indicator of vigor. Strains that grow fast and strand-like on agar typically perform better on substrate.

Second, purity — the absence of cottony sectors — indicates genetic stability.

Third, colonization speed on mushroom-substrates|substrate matters. The fastest colonizer usually has the competitive advantage against contaminants.

Fourth, and most importantly, actual fruiting ability must be confirmed. A strain that looks excellent on agar may fail to fruit, while an unremarkable-looking strain may be prolific. This is why [[growing-gourmet-tree-species-guide-mushroom-cultivation]] are essential before committing to large-scale cultivation.

Fifth, the quality and quantity of the mushroom crop should be evaluated. Some strains produce many small mushrooms while others produce fewer large ones. The cultivator must decide which profile best suits their market and methods.

## Integration with Breeding Programs

For cultivators interested in developing new varieties, sectoring provides the starting point for a systematic [[mushroom-genetics-and-mating-systems]]. By isolating monokaryotic sectors from different parent strains and cross-mating them on the same plate, new hybrid dikaryons can be created. The [[mushroom-genetics-and-mating-systems]] provides a way to physically separate spores so individual monokaryotic cultures can be isolated.

The key challenge in breeding is that in tetrapolar species, only one quarter of random dikaryons will be fully fertile. This means that large numbers of crosses must be attempted, and each must be tested for fruiting ability — a time-consuming process that is nevertheless the foundation of commercial mushroom sectoring strain development.
