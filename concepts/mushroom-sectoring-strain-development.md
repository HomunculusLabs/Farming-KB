## Rhizomorphic vs. Tomentose Growth

Rhizomorphic mycelium appears as thick, strand-like cords that radiate outward with defined directionality. It is generally considered the more desirable growth form because it correlates strongly with fruiting potential.

Tomentose mycelium appears cottony, fluffy, and undifferentiated. It covers the agar surface evenly but without the organized strand structure. While it can colonize substrates, it typically produces fewer and less vigorous fruitbodies.

The relationship between these two forms is not always fixed. A predominantly rhizomorphic culture may begin producing tomentose sectors, and vice versa. However, when rhizomorphic and tomentose sectors are isolated from the same plate and each is used to inoculate separate substrates, the rhizomorphic sector typically forms abundant primordia while the tomentose sector does not — an observation common across many species grown on agar.

## Sectoring in Old Cultures: Senescence

A different type of sectoring occurs in old, pure cultures. As cultures age and undergo repeated subculturing, random mutations accumulate. These mutations eventually express themselves as sectors of differing growth characteristics. A strain that has reached this condition is no longer pure and should not be used for cultivation.
