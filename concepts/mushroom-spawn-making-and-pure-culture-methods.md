The practical consequence of this difficulty was that, for many years, it was nearly
impossible to start a new [[mushroom-spawn-production-grain-inoculation]]. Drawing on the principles of aseptic technique
that had been developed by Pasteur, Koch, and other pioneers of microbiology,
researchers found ways to isolate and propagate mushroom mycelium in pure form, free
from contamination by other organisms.

Pure culture methods typically begin with the selection of a superior mushroom — one
exhibiting desirable characteristics of size, shape, color, yield, or vigor. A small
piece of tissue is taken from the interior of the selected mushroom and placed on a
sterile nutrient medium in a Petri dish or culture tube. Because the interior of a
healthy mushroom is essentially sterile, this tissue provides a clean starting point
for culture. The tissue fragment is incubated under controlled conditions, and the
mycelium that grows out from it is a pure culture of the selected strain.

Alternatively, pure cultures can be started from spores, though this is more difficult.
If spores can be successfully germinated — perhaps with the aid of specialized media
or germination stimulants — the resulting mycelium represents a new genetic individual.
