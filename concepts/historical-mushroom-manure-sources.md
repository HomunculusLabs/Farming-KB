# Historical Mushroom Manure Sources and Selection

## The Primacy of Horse Manure

In the [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]], there was no known substitute for horse manure as a basis for commercial mushroom production.

[[agaricus-campestris]] was cultivated on a large scale, fresh horse dung was the starting material.

The quality of the manure directly determined the quality and quantity of the [[sustaining-mushroom-crop-flushing-harvesting]].

Falconer devoted an entire chapter of his treatise to the subject of manure for mushroom beds, reflecting the central importance of this material.

All other materials — loam, leaves, spent hops — were secondary supplements.

The fundamental requirement was always horse manure in sufficient quantity and of suitable quality.

## Freshness as the Critical Factor

The freshness of horse manure was the single most important quality criterion.

Fresh manure from city stables, gathered daily, was preferred above all other sources.

Around New York City, fresh stable manure could always be had in any quantity at a reasonable rate.

Market gardeners would haul vegetables to market and bring back a load of manure on the return trip.

Others made arrangements with teamsters or specialized agents to deliver manure.

The whole matter of city manure was handled deftly by agents who made a special business of it.

Any quantity could be obtained, from a 500-pound bale to an unlimited number of loads, delivered near or far, at a fairly moderate price.

It was city stable manure that nearly all large market growers used for their mushroom beds.

## Entire Horses Versus Other Animals

There was a widespread belief that the manure of entire (stallion) horses was superior to that of other horses for [[mazatec-mushroom-culture-and-velada]].

Parisian growers insisted upon this difference, especially in the case of hard-worked animals engaged in heavy carting.

However, practical American growers were more skeptical.

Mr. John G. Gardner, at the Rancocas Farm, had abundant opportunity to test this question.

He gave it a fair trial and was unable to find any difference in the quality or quantity of mushrooms.

Beds made from the manure of entire horses and those made from the manure of other equally well-fed animals produced equivalent results.

Some practical men asserted there was no difference whatsoever.

This question remained unresolved in Falconer's time, with European tradition favoring entire horses and American experience being more equivocal.

## The Carrot-Feeding Controversy

One of the most persistent dogmas in mushroom literature was the condemnation of manure from carrot-fed horses.

Nearly every book on mushrooms repeated this warning.

Falconer challenged this orthodoxy with direct practical evidence.

At the Dosoris establishment, there were two shelf beds in one cellar, each thirty feet long, three feet wide, and nine inches deep.

Both were bearing a very thick crop of mushrooms.

The manure had been saved from stables on the property in November 1888.

At the time the manure was being saved, the horses were only very lightly worked.

Each horse was fed, in addition to hay and some oats and bran, about a third of a bushel of carrots per day.

The beds bore well until the middle of April, producing mushrooms averaging three-fourths to one ounce apiece.

When asked whether manure from carrot-fed horses was as good as manure from animals fed no [[permaculture-plants-root-crops-and-tubers]], Falconer answered: decidedly not.

While not the best, at the same time it was good, and anyone having plenty of it could also have plenty of mushrooms.

The complete denunciation of carrot-fed horse manure was not always founded on fact.

## Mule Manure — An Excellent Alternative

Mule manure was regarded as being next in value to that of entire horses.

Some French growers went so far as to say it was quite as good.

Mr. Gardner told of an extraordinary crop of mushrooms from beds made of manure from mule stables.

The heaviest crop Falconer ever saw was at Mr. Wilbur's place at South Bethlehem, Pennsylvania.

These beds were made of clean mule droppings from the coal mines.

Mule manure could be obtained in quantity at mule stock yards in nearly every large city in the Middle and Southern States.

Getting it from the mines cost more than it was worth, except as a fancy article.

The miners would not collect and save it for any reasonable price.

But in cities with street railways and other operations using mules, the manure was readily available.

## Cellar Manure

Many stables had cellars under them into which manure and urine were dropped at every day's cleaning.

These cellars were not generally cleaned out before a good deal of manure had accumulated.

This might be a few weeks', a few months', or a winter's gathering.

The manure was commonly well moistened by the urine.

If this manure had not become too dry and "fire-fanged" in the cellar, it was splendid for mushrooms.

Falconer bought a good deal of it, but was particular to reject the very dry and white-burned parts.

Sometimes manure from both cow-stables and horse-stables was dropped together into the cellar.

In that case Falconer would pay less, especially if the cow manure predominated.

Cow manure kept too cold and wet and pasty in the working.

But if there was not enough cow manure to give the mass a pasty character, it would make capital mushroom beds.

Pigs often had the run of the manure-cellar, as was generally the case in farmyards.

Falconer would not use any part of this mixed pig manure.

Mycelium avoided hog manure; besides it was impure and malodorous, and a propagating bed for noxious insect vermin.

## Bedding Materials and Their Effects

The kind of bedding used in the stable significantly affected the quality of the manure for mushroom culture.

Straw was the traditional and preferred bedding material.

Field hay was also excellent.

German peat moss was a comparatively new material in America, used in place of straw for bedding horses.
