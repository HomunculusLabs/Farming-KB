# Spawn Preparation Spawn is the living fungal mycelium propagated on a nutritious substrate, serving as the "seed" for [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]] because the fungus cannot access dissolved nutrients
efficiently.

Common hydration methods include:

- **Boil and drain**: Boil grains for 20-40 minutes, then spread on a
  screen to steam-dry until no moisture pools when pressed.
- **24-hour soak**: Soak grains in cold water overnight, which allows
  endospores of contaminating bacteria to germinate so they are killed
  during sterilization.
- **Fractional hydration**: Boil a measured volume of water, add grains,
  and simmer until water is fully absorbed.

After hydration, grains are loaded into spawn jars or bags. Quart mason
jars with four-hole polyfill lids are the standard for hobbyists. Unicorn
bags with filter patches are preferred for larger operations.

## Sterilization

Spawn must be sterilized to eliminate all competing organisms. Pressure
canning at 15 PSI (121 C / 250 F) for 90-120 minutes is the minimum for
grain spawn. Larger bags require longer cycles — up to 2-3 hours for
5-pound grain bags — because heat penetration through the grain mass
takes time.

Autoclaves provide the most reliable sterilization for commercial
operations. They cycle faster than pressure cookers, maintain more
consistent pressure, and can process many bags simultaneously. For
hobbyists, a standard 23-quart [[comparison-liquid-culture-vs-grain-spawn]] syringes offer
a faster, lower-risk alternative since the jar lid is only briefly
opened.

After inoculation, jars are shaken once to distribute the mycelium, then
stored at the appropriate temperature for the species. Most gourmet and
medicinal species colonize best between 20-26 C (68-79 F). Periodic
shaking at 20-30% colonization can accelerate the process by spreading
actively growing mycelium to fresh grain surfaces.

## Contamination Recognition

Healthy mycelium appears uniformly white and grows in organized,
rhizomorphic strands. Common contaminants include:

- **Trichoderma** (green mold): Fast-growing, turns bright green when
  sporulating. The most common and destructive grain contaminant.
- **Bacillus** (wet spot / sour rot): Causes grains to appear slimy and
  smell sour or rotten. Indicates insufficient sterilization.
- **Aspergillus** species: Yellow, green, or black powdery growth,
  often with a distinctive musty odor.
- **Cobweb mold**: Gray, wispy growth that spreads rapidly across the
  grain surface.
