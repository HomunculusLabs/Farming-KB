# Hallucinogenic Mushroom Chemistry: Psilocybin, Psilocin, and 5-HT2A Receptor Pharmacology

## Overview

The psychoactive effects of [[emcdda-eu-legislative-timeline-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-2001-2006]] are primarily
attributable to psilocybin and its dephosphorylated metabolite psilocin,
both of which belong to the tryptamine class of indolealkylamines —
the same broad chemical family as serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT)
and LSD. The EMCDDA (2006) provided a concise pharmacological overview
of these compounds, their receptor interactions, and their relationship
to the broader classification of hallucinogenic substances.

## Chemical Structure and Classification

Hallucinogens can be classified by chemical structure into three major
groups. [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] fall within the indolealkylamine
(tryptamine) group, alongside LSD, DMT, and 5-MeO-DMT. The other two
major groups are phenylethylamines (e.g., mescaline) and cannabinoids.

**Psilocybin** (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, 4-PO-DMT) is
the phosphate ester of psilocin. It is more stable in air and water-soluble,
making it the primary storage form in fungal tissue. **Psilocin**
(4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, 4-HO-DMT) is the pharmacologically
active compound that is produced when psilocybin is converted in the body
through dephosphorylation.

Both compounds are structurally similar to the neurotransmitter serotonin
(5-HT), which explains their ability to interact with [[psilocybin-pharmacology-and-serotonin-receptors]]
in the central nervous system. The structural similarity is not coincidental:
the indole ring system that characterizes both serotonin and the
psilocybin/psilocin molecules is a key feature that enables receptor
binding.

## Additional Tryptamine Alkaloids

Beyond psilocybin and psilocin, two further tryptamines may be present in
hallucinogenic mushrooms: **baeocystin** (4-phosphoryloxy-N-methyltryptamine)
and **norbaeocystin** (4-phosphoryloxytryptamine). These are considered
to be less active than psilocybin and psilocin, though their exact
pharmacological contributions remain incompletely understood.

The relative concentrations of these four compounds vary between species,
between individual specimens, and according to the age and condition of
the mushrooms. Younger specimens generally contain higher concentrations
of active alkaloids, and drying methods that use heat can degrade the
compounds. Repke et al. (1977) demonstrated variations in baeocystin
content according to specimen age, while Ohenoja et al. (1987) detected
decreased psilocybin in [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]] with increasing age of
collection.

## Mechanism of Action: 5-HT2A Receptor

Psilocin acts on the serotonin system as a 5-HT2A post-synaptic agonist
or partial agonist. The 5-HT2A receptor is a G-protein coupled receptor
widely distributed in the cerebral cortex, particularly in layer V
pyramidal neurons. Activation of these receptors modulates glutamate
release and alters thalamocortical signaling, producing the characteristic
effects on perception, mood, and cognition.

The agonist activity at 5-HT2A receptors is the primary mechanism
responsible for the hallucinogenic effects, though psilocin also has
affinity for other serotonin receptor subtypes including 5-HT1A, 5-HT2C,
and 5-HT2B, which may contribute to the overall experiential profile.
The partial agonist nature of psilocin means that it does not fully
activate the receptor, which may explain the generally manageable
intensity of mushroom experiences compared to full agonists.

## Comparison with Other Hallucinogen Classes

The Amanita species (A. muscaria, A. pantherina) contain an entirely
different set of active compounds — muscimol, ibotenic acid, and
muscarine — acting primarily on GABAergic systems. These carry
substantial toxicity risks, partly explaining their lower recreational
popularity despite wider legal availability in some jurisdictions.

Ergot fungi ([[ergot-fungi-claviceps-and-cordyceps-as-neurotropic-organisms]]) produce ergot alkaloids rather than
tryptamines, with substantially different pharmacological profiles.

## "Hallucinogenic" vs "Psychedelic": Terminology

The EMCDDA discussed the confusing and often controversial terminology
surrounding hallucinogenic drugs. These substances have been called
"psychedelic" (mind-opening, mind-expanding), "psychotomimetic"
(resembling psychosis), "psychodysleptic" (mind-disrupting),
"hallucinogenic," and "phantastica" or "oneirogenic," depending on the
perspective and purposes of those using the terms.

The scientific community has largely adopted "hallucinogens," however
inaccurate it may be, while users generally prefer "psychedelic." The
The term "hallucinogens" is misleading because these drugs do not generally
cause true hallucinations (sensory perceptions without external stimuli).
The effects are more accurately described as perceptual distortions, though
changes in thought, mood, and self-awareness are equally important.

## Chemical Stability and Degradation

Psilocybin is more stable than psilocin, which is why it serves as the
primary storage compound in the fungal organism. Both compounds are
described as volatile by Guzmán, who experienced involuntary
hallucinations from airborne exposure while sleeping in a room filled
with fresh neurotropic Psilocybe specimens in Oaxaca, Mexico. The
hallucinations ceased when he left the room and breathed fresh air,
suggesting that psilocybin and/or psilocin can volatilize under certain
conditions of warmth and humidity.

The practical implication is that dried mushrooms lose potency over time.
The Mexican Indians traditionally discarded specimens kept for more than
one year, considering them no longer effective. Ohenoja et al. (1987)
quantified this degradation in herbarium specimens of Psilocybe
semilanceata, finding 0.014%, 0.67%, and 0.84% psilocybin dry weight
in specimens from 1869, 1954, and 1976 respectively.

## Species Variation in Alkaloid Content

Different species of hallucinogenic mushrooms contain widely varying
concentrations of psilocybin and psilocin. [[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybe-semilanceata-temperate-cosmopolitan-distribution]]
(liberty cap) averages approximately 1% psilocybin/psilocin content
by dry weight, making it one of the more potent naturally occurring
species. Psilocybe cubensis, the most commonly cultivated species,
generally contains lower concentrations but compensates with larger
fruiting body size.

Psilocybe azurescens and P. cyanescens are among the most potent
species known, containing the highest documented concentrations of
psilocybin and psilocin. At the other extreme, some species reported
in the literature as neurotropic have yielded contradictory chemical
results, with some studies finding active compounds and others failing
to detect them. These discrepancies may reflect genuine variation
between populations, differences in analytical methodology, or
taxonomic misidentification of the studied material.

## Pharmacokinetics

After ingestion, psilocybin is rapidly dephosphorylated to psilocin
by alkaline phosphatase in the digestive system and liver. Psilocin
crosses the blood-brain barrier due to its lipophilic properties.
Onset occurs within 20-60 minutes, with peak effects at 60-120 minutes
and total duration of 4-6 hours.
