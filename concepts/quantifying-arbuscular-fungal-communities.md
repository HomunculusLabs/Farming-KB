# Quantifying Arbuscular Fungal Communities

Quantifying the composition, abundance, and relative importance of arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungal species in natural communities presents unique methodological challenges. Unlike plants or animals, the functionally important parts of the fungus — the mycelium, arbuscules, and vesicles or auxiliary cells — cannot be distinguished among species co-inhabiting a mycorrhizal root system. This chapter covers the approaches, indices, and practical considerations for characterizing AM fungal communities.

## Fundamental Challenges

### The Spore Problem

All current routine methods for quantifying AM fungal communities rely on counting and identifying spores extracted from soil. This creates several inherent limitations:

- **Sporulation bias**: Only sporulating species are detected; nonsporulating species may be dominant colonizers but remain invisible
- **Seasonal variability**: Different species sporulate at different times; single-date samples may misrepresent community composition
- **Dead spore contamination**: In some soils and seasons, dead intact spores can outnumber live spores (Lee and Koske 1994)
- **Identification difficulties**: Field-collected spores may be degraded, parasitized, or intergraded morphologically, preventing accurate identification

### Incompatibility with Standard Ecological Indices

Many standard ecological diversity indices (e.g., Shannon-Weiner Index, Dominance Index) assume each counted unit represents an individual organism. This assumption is not met with AM fungi because the units being sampled are spores or sporocarps, not individual organisms. These indices therefore assess only resource allocation to sporulation, not true abundance or ecological importance.

## Measuring Species Abundance and Importance

### Primary Variables

The relative importance of individual AM fungal species can be estimated by measuring two variables:

1. **Spore abundance**: Number of spores per 100 cm³ of soil
2. **Frequency of occurrence**: Proportion of samples in which a species is found

### Derived Indices

Additional measures calculated from these primary variables include:

- **Relative abundance**: Proportion of total spores belonging to each species
- **Relative frequency**: Proportion of total occurrences belonging to each species
- **Spore biovolume**: Volume of spores per 100 cm³ of soil — the most biologically meaningful approximation of carbon resources transferred to each fungal species
- **Dominance**: Based on either abundance or frequency
- **Importance Value (IV)**: Combined index, calculated by summing frequency and abundance, or frequency, abundance, and spore volume (Koske 1987)

### Spore Volume as a Carbon Proxy

Spore volume is the most easily measured approximation of the amount of host carbon transferred to a colonizing fungus, which converts it to cytoplasm, lipids, and cell-wall components. Volume is calculated using:

- **Spherical spores** (most species): V = 4/3πr³
- **Oblong spores** (e.g., *Scutellospora erythropa*, *S. pellucida*): V = (l × w²)/6

Example comparison: A *Gigaspora gigantea* spore of 300 μm diameter has a volume of 1.4 × 10⁷ μm³, whereas a *Glomus caledonium* spore of 150 μm diameter has a volume of only 1.8 × 10⁶ μm³ — nearly an 8-fold difference.

Caveats: Spore volume is not consistently correlated with root colonization (niche occupation), season, or fungal species (Gazey et al. 1992). Estimates based on mean diameters may not reflect the actual range and distribution of sizes.

## Importance Value and Rank-IV Plots

**Rank-IV plots** (borrowed from plant ecology; Whittaker 1975) are useful for visualizing community structure:

1. Calculate an IV for each species
2. Plot each IV on a graph with logarithmic Y-axis
3. Rank species from highest to lowest IV on the X-axis

**Interpreting slope**:
- **Steep slope**: Associated with habitats dominated by severe stress at the plant community level (drought, cold, reduced photosynthetic activity) — one or few species dominate
- **Flat slope**: Indicates more equitable habitats where competition is important in determining community structure — species are more evenly distributed

The method of IV calculation affects slope and interpretation, so consistent methodology is essential for comparisons.

## Species Richness Assessment

### Field Sampling vs. Trap Cultures

Species richness is measured as the number of species whose spores are extracted from soil samples, with occasional exceptions for species whose mycorrhizal roots are sufficiently well-characterized for identification (McGee 1989; Abbott and Gazey 1994).

Key considerations:
- A minimum level of fungal biomass (mycorrhizal colonization) is needed before reproduction commences (Gazey et al. 1992)
- Many fungi may colonize roots at low levels and spread through hyphal growth among plants without sporulating
- Trap cultures typically recover more species than one-time field samples (out of 600+ trap cultures from multiple habitats, species diversity usually exceeded field collections)
- In arid or wet habitats, trap cultures are essential because sporulation may be absent for most of the year

### Sampling Intensity

The species-increment curve approach determines how many samples are needed to adequately characterize a site. In New England sand dunes, the likelihood of recovering additional species decreased after the eleventh sample (Tews and Koske 1986). Similar calculations are needed for each new study site.

## The Rare Species Problem

Rare species constitute large proportions of AM fungal communities in both temperate and tropical regions (Koske 1987; Johnson and Wedin 1997). They present two main challenges:

1. **Insufficient specimens**: Many species in *Glomus* and *Gigaspora* cannot be identified with fewer than 40–50 spores, even when all diagnostic characters are intact
2. **Statistical limitations**: Large variation in spore counts precludes meaningful statistical comparisons among associated rare species

### Abundance vs. Frequency: A Case Study

In sand dune sampling, *Scutellospora pellucida* was found in only 1 of 11 samples but was abundant in that sample, while *S. calospora* occurred at lower numbers in 5 samples. Based on average abundance alone, *S. pellucida* would appear overwhelmingly dominant. Yet statistical tests revealed no significant difference. **Frequency of occurrence was the more informative parameter** for rare species.

This illustrates the critical importance of combining abundance and frequency when calculating indices of importance.

## Viability Assessment

For most studies, only live spores are informative. Viability is determined based on:
- Healthy appearance (intact spore wall and internal flexible walls in described color range)
- Clear lipid globules in spore lumen

Vital stains (e.g., MTT, FDA) have been used but results are unreliable (Meier and Charvat 1993; Walley and Germida 1995). Visual assessment by experienced observers remains the most practical approach.

## Emerging Molecular Approaches

Some progress is being made using selective enrichment of PCR-amplified DNA to probe for both sporulating and nonsporulating fungal colonizers of roots (Clapp et al. 1995; Vandenkoornhuyse et al. 2002). These molecular methods may eventually resolve the fundamental limitation of spore-based community analysis, but they are not yet routine or practical for most surveys.
