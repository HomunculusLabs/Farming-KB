# Psilocybe cubensis Cap vs Stem Alkaloid Distribution

## Overview

The distribution of psychoactive alkaloids between the cap (pileus) and
stem (stipe) of Psilocybe cubensis is not uniform, and the pattern of
distribution varies significantly between different strains. Bigwood and
Beug (1982) provided the first controlled quantitative analysis of this
phenomenon, finding that caps generally contain roughly twice the
psilocybin concentration of stems, while psilocin shows a strain-
dependent localization pattern that contradicts simple expectations.
Understanding these [[guzman-global-distribution-patterns-neurotropic-fungi]] has practical implications
for users seeking to optimize or standardize their dosage.

## Bigwood and Beug Findings

The study analyzed cap and stem tissues separately from three distinct
strains of Psilocybe cubensis cultivated on rye-grain substrate. All
samples were from first-flush fruitings, providing a consistent baseline
for comparison. The results revealed notable strain-dependent differences
in how alkaloids partition between cap and stem tissues:

### M.R. Strain

- **Caps**: 6.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0 mg/g psilocin
- **Stems**: 4.2 mg/g psilocybin, 0.35 mg/g psilocin

The M.R. strain showed approximately 1.6 times more psilocybin in caps
than in stems. Notably, all detectable psilocin was found exclusively in
the stem tissue, with none present in the caps.

### Equadorian Strain

- **Caps**: 7.6 mg/g psilocybin, 0 mg/g psilocin
- **Stems**: 4.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0.4 mg/g psilocin

The Equadorian strain exhibited a similar pattern to the M.R. strain,
with approximately 1.6 times more psilocybin in caps. Again, psilocin
was present exclusively in stem tissue.

### Amazon Strain

- **Caps**: 5.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0.1 mg/g psilocin
- **Stems**: 5.7 mg/g psilocybin, 0 mg/g psilocin

The Amazon strain showed a strikingly different pattern from the other
two strains. Psilocybin was distributed equally between caps and stems
at 5.7 mg/g each. Psilocin, present only in trace amounts, was found
in the cap rather than the stem—the reverse of the pattern observed
in the M.R. and Equadorian strains.

## General Pattern: Caps Concentrate Psilocybin

Across all three strains, caps consistently contained equal or greater
amounts of psilocybin compared to stems. In two of the three strains,
caps contained approximately 60% more psilocybin than stems. This
finding aligns with the general mycological understanding that the cap
is the primary site of spore production and [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]]
synthesis in agaric mushrooms. The reproductive structures of the cap
appear to concentrate biosynthetic activity, including the production
of tryptamine alkaloids.

## Psilocin Localization: A Strain-Dependent Paradox

The psilocin distribution pattern proved more complex and strain-
dependent than the psilocybin pattern. In the M.R. and Equadorian
strains, psilocin was found exclusively in the stem despite being
absent from the cap. In the Amazon strain, the reverse was true: a
trace of psilocin appeared in the cap with none in the stem. This
inconsistency suggests that the enzymatic dephosphorylation of
psilocybin to psilocin is regulated differently in different genetic
lineages, and that the stem and cap tissues possess different complements
of phosphatase enzymes depending on the strain.

## Implications for Dosage and Preparation

These findings have practical implications for mushroom consumers:

- **Whole mushroom consumption** provides the most consistent experience
  because it averages the variable concentrations across tissues.
- **Cap-only preparations** would generally deliver more psilocybin per
  gram of dry material than stem-only preparations in most strains.
- **Stem-only consumption** might provide a slightly different qualitative
  experience in strains where psilocin localizes to the stem, though the
  total psilocin quantities are small relative to psilocybin.
- **[[blesching-cannabis-strain-selection-receptor-targeting]]** matters significantly for alkaloid distribution
  patterns, and results from one strain cannot be generalized to others.

## Methodological Considerations

It is important to note that all tissue distribution data in this study
came from first-flush fruitings, when psilocin levels are typically at
their lowest. The distribution patterns observed may differ in later
flushes when psilocin concentrations are higher. Additionally, all
mushrooms were cultivated on rye-grain substrate, and different
substrates such as compost or manure might produce different distribution
patterns. The freeze-drying and cold storage protocol used in the study
helped preserve alkaloid integrity, but different drying methods
(air-drying, oven-drying, [[mushroom-desiccant-drying-methods]]) could potentially alter the
measured ratios.

## Relationship to Biosynthetic Pathway

The differential distribution of psilocybin and psilocin between tissues
supports the hypothesis that psilocin is produced primarily through
enzymatic dephosphorylation of psilocybin rather than through independent
biosynthesis. The strain-dependent localization of [[mycorrhizal-phosphatase-activity-elf97-detection]]
suggests that the genes encoding these enzymes are differentially
expressed in cap and stem tissues across genetic lineages. This adds
another layer of complexity to the already substantial variability in
alkaloid content across flushes documented in the broader
[[psilocybin-psilocin-variation-across-flushes|flush variation study]].
