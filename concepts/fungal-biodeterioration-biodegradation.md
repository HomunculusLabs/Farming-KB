# Fungal Biodegradation and Biodeterioration

Fungi play dual roles in environmental processes: as agents of biodeterioration that degrade materials and structures, and as agents of biodegradation that can be harnessed for [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]] of contaminated environments. Understanding the relationship between these two processes is fundamental to applying fungal biology for environmental benefit.

## Biodeterioration by Fungi

Fungal biodeterioration refers to the undesirable degradation of materials caused by fungal activity. This is a significant economic problem across many industries and material types. Fungal biodeterioration of various types of library materials, for example, is a well-documented problem affecting cultural heritage collections worldwide.

Fungi attack materials through several mechanisms: enzymatic decomposition of organic substrates, production of organic acids that corrode metals and degrade stone, mechanical penetration of hyphae into porous materials, and production of pigments and stains that disfigure surfaces.

Wood decay represents one of the most extensively studied forms of fungal biodeterioration. [[brown-rot-fungi-biodegradation]] primarily degrade cellulose while leaving lignin largely modified but not removed. White-rot fungi, conversely, can degrade all components of wood including lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose. Soft-rot fungi typically attack wood under conditions too wet for brown-rot or too dry for white-rot activity.

The same enzymatic systems that cause biodeterioration of wood, particularly the [[ligninolytic-enzymes-bioremediation]] of white-rot fungi, are the basis for fungal bioremediation capabilities. Lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and laccase produced by these fungi can degrade a wide range of recalcitrant organic pollutants.

## Biodegradation Capabilities

The biodegradation potential of fungi derives from their diverse metabolic capabilities and their ability to produce extracellular enzymes that attack complex organic molecules. Fungi are among the major decomposers in any ecosystem and have been harnessed by humans for diverse applications including [[fungal-bioremediation]].

[[white-rot-fungi-bioremediation]] are particularly important due to their nonspecific ligninolytic enzyme systems. These enzymes, especially lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase, have low substrate specificity, enabling them to oxidize a wide variety of structurally diverse pollutants.

Fungal biodegradation extends to many pollutant classes including [[fungal-oil-petroleum-bioremediation]], [[fungal-pah-degradation-mechanisms]], [[fungal-biodegradation-of-synthetic-pollutants]], [[fungal-degradation-of-textile-dyes]], [[fungal-degradation-of-pesticides]], and [[fungal-heavy-metal-biosorption-detailed]].

## Fungal Attack on Coal

The role of fungi in the transformation of coal has been recognized for several decades. Of more than 750 fungal strains screened, only six have been shown to modify the physicochemical properties of pieces of hard coal, indicating the specialized nature of this capability.

The attack by Coprinus sclerotigenis C-142-1 on powdered hard coal releases 2-hydroxybiphenyl, alkylated benzenes, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Fusarium oxysporum and Trichoderma atroviride are able to solubilize low-rank coal (LRC). Strong evidence exists for the existence of components of a ligninolytic system in coal-degrading fungi comparable to that of Phanerochaete chrysosporium.

Trichoderma atroviride secretes a partly inducible heat-sensitive agent with hydrolytic properties that assists in the solubilization of Rhenish lignite. This mold also produces an unusual esterase for the cleavage of ester bonds in lignite, as well as alkaline substances and chelators that assist in the dissolution of coal humic materials.

Trichoderma sp. M2 and Penicillium sp. can modify and solubilize hard coal without the formation of tarlike products. An indigenous culture of Aspergillus sp. eliminated 70 to 80% of total sulfur from high-sulfur coal from the coal fields of Assam in India.

Bioconversion of brown coal occurs through solubilization, which is typical for molds, yeasts, actinomycetes, and pseudomonad bacteria. Depolymerization is largely limited to ligninolytic Basidiomycetes (wood-decaying and litter-decomposing fungi).

Wood-rotting fungi and their oxidases are found to solubilize, polymerize, depolymerize, and decolorize macromolecules derived from low-rank coal. Phanerochaete chrysosporium depolymerizes and decolorizes alkali-soluble acid-precipitate LRC macromolecules to a form unrecoverable by alkali washing. Nitrogen-limited media with hyperbaric oxygen enhance the transformation, partly due to oxidation by manganese peroxidase and lignin peroxidase.

## Enzymatic Mechanisms in Coal Transformation

Ligninolytic Basidiomycetes produce three enzymes — lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and laccase — that appear to be involved in the depolymerization of coal. The basidiomycete strain RBS 1k produces MnP, and strain RBS 1b secretes oxidative enzymes with laccase and peroxidase activity before the onset of coal solubilization.

However, cell-free peroxidase of RBS 1k or laccase of RBS 1b in vitro is unable to promote water solubility of low-rank coals, suggesting that living fungal metabolism or synergistic interactions between multiple enzyme systems are required.

MnP from Clitocybula dusenii catalyzes and optimizes the depolymerization of coal humic substances. The South American white-rot fungus Nematoloma frowardii and four other fungi have been shown to depolymerize low-rank-coal-derived humic acids, releasing fulvic acid-like compounds. Nematoloma frowardii and other fungi produce high levels of laccase mRNA in the presence of coal humic substances in the medium.

## Extremophilic Fungi in Bioremediation

The majority of fungi are mesophilic and grow in the temperature range of 5 to 35 degrees Celsius, with optimum growth between 25 and 35 degrees. Some fungi are psychrophilic and capable of growth near or below 0 degrees Celsius. Certain psychrotolerant fungi can survive at temperatures as low as minus 40 degrees Celsius.

Thirty-two cold-adapted, psychrophilic, cold-tolerant yeast strains from alpine habitats have been characterized for their taxonomy, growth temperature profiles, and their potential to degrade phenol and phenol-related compounds. These extremophilic fungi represent an untapped resource for bioremediation in cold environments.

[[thermophilic-fungi]] can grow above 40 degrees Celsius and are widespread in terrestrial habitats. They can be cultivated in minimal media with growth yields and metabolic rates comparable to those of mesophilic fungi. These organisms can grow in heavy metal concentrations, variable pH, or extremes of environmental conditions. Heat-tolerant fungi are known for their ability to decompose plant polymers and their potential in composting. Unlike white-rot fungi, many enzymes of thermophilic fungi remain to be discovered for use in [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]].

About 20% of all known fungal species are obligate symbionts in lichens. The role of lichen-forming fungi in waste and wastewater treatment remains largely unexplored but represents a potential area for future research.

## Bridging Biodeterioration and Bioremediation

The connection between biodeterioration and bioremediation lies in the fundamental metabolic capabilities of fungi. The same enzymatic systems that fungi use to decompose wood, paper, textiles, and other natural materials in biodeterioration processes can be redirected to degrade anthropogenic pollutants.

This principle has been most successfully applied with white-rot fungi, whose lignin-degrading system represents one of the most powerful oxidative enzyme systems in nature. Lignin, a complex aromatic polymer, is structurally similar to many recalcitrant environmental pollutants. The nonspecific nature of ligninolytic enzymes means they can attack a broad spectrum of xenobiotic compounds.

In [[fungal-wastewater-treatment]], the biodeterioration capability of fungi is exploited to remove organic pollutants from industrial effluents. Fungi can simultaneously degrade pollutants and produce valuable biomass, converting waste streams into resources through [[fungal-single-cell-protein-production]].

The challenge in applied mycoremediation is to direct fungal metabolic activity specifically toward target pollutants while minimizing undesirable biodeterioration of infrastructure and non-target materials. This requires careful selection of fungal species, optimization of growth conditions, and appropriate containment strategies in bioreactor systems.

## Ecological Context

Fungi occupy diverse ecological niches and their biodegradation activities are influenced by environmental factors including temperature, moisture, pH, nutrient availability, and the presence of competing microorganisms. In natural ecosystems, fungal biodegradation is part of essential nutrient cycling processes that break down complex organic matter and release simpler compounds for uptake by plants and other organisms.

In the context of [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]], understanding how fungi interact with their environment helps predict and optimize their performance in remediation applications. The ability of fungi to form extensive mycelial networks allows them to explore large volumes of soil and access pollutants that may be poorly available to bacteria.

Fungi also produce a wide range of secondary metabolites, including organic acids, solvents, and surfactants, that can modify the bioavailability of contaminants and influence the activity of other microorganisms in contaminated environments. [[bioremediation-using-fungi]] produced by fungi can enhance the solubilization and accessibility of hydrophobic pollutants.

## Future Directions

Research on fungal biodegradation continues to expand in several directions. [[genetic-engineering-fungal-bioremediation]] offers the potential to enhance the degradation capabilities of fungi or to introduce novel metabolic pathways. The discovery of new ligninolytic enzymes from thermophilic fungi, extremophilic species, and previously uncharacterized fungal taxa represents a promising frontier.

The role of fungal-bacterial interactions in biodegradation is increasingly recognized as important, with [[bioremediation-fungal-vs-bacterial]] approaches showing enhanced degradation of recalcitrant compounds compared to single-species systems.

The application of molecular tools, including [[ligninolytic-enzymes-bioremediation]], enables better monitoring of fungal communities during bioremediation and provides insights into the dynamics of fungal populations in contaminated environments.
