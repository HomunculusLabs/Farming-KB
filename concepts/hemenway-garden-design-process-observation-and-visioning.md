# Garden Design Process: Observation and Visioning

Toby Hemenway lays out a five-step design process for creating
an ecological garden: observation, visioning, planning,
development, and implementation. This process draws from
[[permaculture-zone-planning-detailed]] design methodology but is adapted for home
gardeners working at any scale. The first two steps, observation
and visioning, establish the foundation upon which the entire
design rests.

## The Design Sequence

The full sequence is: observation, visioning, planning,
development, and implementation. Each step builds on the
previous one, and Hemenway cautions against skipping ahead.
Gardeners eager to start planting often skip the observation
phase, but this leads to designs that fight rather than work
with the site's existing conditions.

## Step One: Observation

Observation goes far beyond simply noting the objects on the
site. At its best, observation means being immersed in the
place. Hemenway asks: Who lives there? When do various birds
and other animals come and go? What do they eat or otherwise
use at the site? What interactions take place among the plant,
animal, and human inhabitants?

The first step is to list observations without immediately
analyzing them. It is important to retain a childlike quality
of wonder without moving instantly to analysis. Analysis
channels thinking and reduces options. When we leap from
"this plant's leaves are turning yellow" to "it needs more
nitrogen," we immediately think of fertilizer as the only
solution. But by remaining with the simple observation, we
open many options: Should we keep this plant? Should we add
companion plants? Is this an appropriate species for this
location?

Hemenway provides a detailed designer's checklist of what
to observe:

- Areas of shade and sun throughout the day and seasons
- Wind direction, intensity, and seasonal changes
- Temperature highs and lows, dates of first and last frosts
- Points of sunrise, sunset, and seasonal solar positions
- Microclimates: cool, hot, wet, or dry spots
- Rainfall amounts and seasons, snow, and hail patterns
- Soil conditions: drainage, texture, fertility, stability
- Water movement: creeks, gullies, flooding zones during rain
- Rock outcrops and other geological features
- Views and aesthetic considerations
- Neighbor activities: noise, pets, traffic patterns
- Utilities: power, phone, sewer, and gas lines
- Zoning restrictions, easements, and building codes
- Existing vegetation: species, health, invasives, rare plants
- Animal life: native and introduced species, pests
- History of the land from local knowledge and old maps
- Neighborhood resources: sources of organic matter, plants

## Step Two: Visioning

Once the site has been thoroughly observed, the gardener dreams
about what the place can become. Visioning asks several key
questions:

- What do the human inhabitants want and need from the
  landscape? Food, herbs, wildlife habitat, cut flowers,
  privacy, income, play space, or all of these?
- What does the landscape and region need? Has previous
  abuse caused problems that good design can correct? Does
  the soil need rejuvenating? Are rare native plants that can
  be nurtured?
- What are your skills, resources, limitations, likes and
  dislikes? How much time and money are available?
- How should the new landscape feel? Like a forest, a meadow,
  a sanctuary?
- What products can the land provide sustainably?

Hemenway's crucial insight during visioning is to think in
terms of function rather than static objects. Rather than
writing down "fence," call it a "barrier," which invites far
more possibilities: a hedge, rock wall, trench, or moat.
Instead of "compost pile," think "organic matter recycling."
Listing design elements by function rather than inert
description keeps far more options open.

## Step Three: Planning

The planning phase has three segments: priorities, analysis,
and layout.

Priorities involve sorting through observations and visions
to identify the most urgent problems or desires. Analysis
involves collecting design elements: plants, structures,
tasks, and functions that will make the vision real. Layout
uses the [[hemenway-zone-and-sector-planning]]
to organize elements spatially.

Using a [[permaculture-mapping-and-base-maps]] and
sketching ideas on overlays of tracing paper, designers can
arrange pieces to connect sensibly. Hemenway also describes
a [[hemenway-random-assembly-design-technique]]
technique using lists of elements and connecting words to
break creative blocks and discover unexpected but useful
combinations.

## Connecting Observation to Design

The entire design process rests on the quality of observation.
Hemenway emphasizes that observation is not a one-time event
but a continuing practice. Even after the garden is installed,
prolonged observation of how plants, animals, and elements
interact will reveal opportunities for refinement and
improvement that no amount of initial planning could predict.
