## Antimicrobial Compounds from Mushroom Mycelium

## Mycelial Exudates

Living mycelium continuously secretes a range of [[calvatia-gigantea]]).

**Armillarin** from honey mushrooms (Armillaria mellea).

**Campestrin** from meadow mushrooms (Agaricus campestris).

**Coprinol** from inky caps (Coprinus species).

**Coriolin** from turkey tail mushrooms ([[coriolus-versicolor]]).

**Cortinellin** from shiitake (Lentinula edodes).

**Ganomycin** from reishi ([[piptoporus-betulinus]]), and agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis)
— demonstrated more than 99.99% inhibition of both Escherichia coli
and Staphylococcus aureus within 48-72 hours.

## Selective Bacterial Influence

Stamets hypothesizes that mushrooms actively select bacterial
communities in their environment. Primary saprophytic mushrooms — the
first to colonize fresh debris — release antibiotics that dramatically
shape the bacterial populations surrounding them. This bacterial
selection may favor microbial communities that support the mushroom's
nutritional needs, suppress its competitors, or facilitate spore
germination and mycelial establishment.

The interrelationships between mushrooms, their associated bacteria,
viruses, and bacteriophages represent a largely unexplored frontier.
Studying these interactions may reveal medically significant
antibiotics and deepen our understanding of soil microbial ecology.

## Antiviral Properties

Medicinal mushrooms have been ingested for hundreds to thousands of
years, suggesting they are generally safe for human consumption.
Research has identified several mushroom-derived compounds with direct
antiviral activity:

**Shiitake** (Lentinula edodes): Suzuki et al. (1990) discovered a
water-soluble antiviral lignin extracted from mycelium grown on rice
bran and sugarcane bagasse.

**Turkey tail** (Trametes versicolor): Collins and Ng (1997) identified
a polysaccharopeptide with activity against HIV type 1 infection.
