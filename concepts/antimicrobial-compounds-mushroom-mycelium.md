# Antimicrobial Compounds from Mushroom Mycelium

## Mycelial Exudates

Living mycelium continuously secretes a range of [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]]
through its cell surface — compounds known in mycology as exudates.
These include enzymes for breaking down organic matter and antibiotics
for defending against microbial competitors. The mycelial cell surface
"sweats" these compounds into the surrounding environment. Since
mycelium has only a single cell wall protecting it from millions of
hostile microbes per gram of soil, this chemical defense system is
essential for survival.

Mushroom mycelium resists bacterial and fungal rot until it releases
spores, ages, and dies. After sporulation, the fruiting body becomes
vulnerable to the very organisms its mycelium once suppressed. Each
mushroom species may predetermine which bacterial and fungal colonies
can colonize it after death.

## Antibiotic Compounds from Mushrooms

A comprehensive survey by Merck researchers in Spain (Suay et al. 2000)
assessed antibacterial activities of 204 basidiomycete mushroom species.
The results showed that many mushrooms selectively target specific
bacterial species — they do not act as broad-spectrum biocides but
rather exhibit targeted antimicrobial activity.

Notable antibiotics isolated from mushrooms include:

**Calvacin** from giant puffballs ([[calvatia-gigantea]]).

**Armillarin** from honey mushrooms (Armillaria mellea).

**Campestrin** from meadow mushrooms (Agaricus campestris).

**Coprinol** from inky caps (Coprinus species).

**Coriolin** from turkey tail mushrooms ([[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]], formerly
[[coriolus-versicolor]]).

**Cortinellin** from shiitake (Lentinula edodes).

**Ganomycin** from reishi ([[ganoderma-lucidum]]).

**Agaricin** from agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis).

**Sparassol** from cauliflower mushrooms (Sparassis crispa).

Cold-water extracts from living mycelium of the most antibacterially
active species — oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus), birch
polypore ([[piptoporus-betulinus]]), and agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis)
— demonstrated more than 99.99% inhibition of both Escherichia coli
and Staphylococcus aureus within 48-72 hours.

## Selective Bacterial Influence

Stamets hypothesizes that mushrooms actively select bacterial
communities in their environment. Primary saprophytic mushrooms — the
first to colonize fresh debris — release antibiotics that dramatically
shape the bacterial populations surrounding them. This bacterial
selection may favor microbial communities that support the mushroom's
nutritional needs, suppress its competitors, or facilitate spore
germination and mycelial establishment.

The interrelationships between mushrooms, their associated bacteria,
viruses, and bacteriophages represent a largely unexplored frontier.
Studying these interactions may reveal medically significant
antibiotics and deepen our understanding of soil microbial ecology.

## Antiviral Properties

Medicinal mushrooms have been ingested for hundreds to thousands of
years, suggesting they are generally safe for human consumption.
Research has identified several mushroom-derived compounds with direct
antiviral activity:

**Shiitake** (Lentinula edodes): Suzuki et al. (1990) discovered a
water-soluble antiviral lignin extracted from mycelium grown on rice
bran and sugarcane bagasse.

**Turkey tail** (Trametes versicolor): Collins and Ng (1997) identified
a polysaccharopeptide with activity against HIV type 1 infection.

**Reishi** (Ganoderma lucidum): Eo et al. (1999, 2000) found
methanol-soluble fractions that selectively inhibited herpes simplex
1 and 2, and vesicular stomatitis virus.

**Oyster mushroom** (Pleurotus ostreatus): Wang and Ng (2000) isolated
a novel ubiquitin-like glycoprotein that inhibited HIV.

**Gypsy mushroom** (Cortinarius caperatus): Piraino and Brandt (1999)
discovered derivatives that inhibited varicella zoster (shingles),
influenza A, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV).

**Hoof fungus** (Fomes fomentarius): Aoki et al. (1993) demonstrated
inhibition of tobacco mosaic virus.

## Dual Antiviral and Antibacterial Activity

Some mushrooms possess both antiviral and antibacterial properties,
making them particularly valuable. This dual activity is clinically
relevant because viral respiratory infections often lead to secondary
bacterial pneumonia. A mushroom compound that simultaneously inhibits
both the primary viral infection and opportunistic bacterial
complications could address a major cause of mortality in
immunocompromised patients.

## Immune Activation

Beyond directly killing or inhibiting pathogens, mushroom-derived
compounds activate innate immune responses in mammalian cells. Beta-
glucans and other polysaccharides from medicinal mushrooms stimulate
macrophage activity, enhance natural killer cell function, and promote
the production of cytokines and other immune signaling molecules. This
immunomodulatory effect boosts the body's own resistance to microbial
infection.

The combination of direct antimicrobial activity and immune
stimulation may explain why many traditional medicinal mushroom
preparations show clinical benefit across a range of infectious
conditions, even when the specific pathogen varies.

## Implications for Drug Discovery

With an estimated 140,000+ mushroom species, of which only a fraction
have been screened for bioactive compounds, mushrooms represent a
vast and largely untapped resource for pharmaceutical discovery.
The fact that mushrooms share a closer evolutionary history with
animals than with plants means that fungal bioactive compounds may be
more likely to interact productively with human biological systems
than plant-derived compounds.

Stamets argues that fungi from old-growth forests, which have evolved
complex chemical arsenals over millions of years of competition, may
be particularly promising sources of new medicines — including agents
active against emerging infectious diseases, antibiotic-resistant
bacteria, and potential bioterrorism threats.
