# Humic Acids: Extraction, Function, and Soil Application

## Overview

Humic acids are complex organic molecules formed through the long-term
decomposition of plant and animal residues. In the Ingham soil food web
framework, humic substances represent the stable end-product of microbial
decomposition and play critical roles in [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]], soil
structure, and microbial habitat. Understanding humic acids is essential
for producing effective compost teas and building long-term soil health.

## Definitions: Humus, Humic Acids, and Fulvic Acids

Humus is the broad term for the total stable [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]] soil, giving
fertile soils their characteristic dark color. It is not a single compound
but a complex mixture of molecules at various stages of decomposition.

Humic acids are a specific fraction soluble in alkaline solutions but
insoluble under acidic conditions (below pH 2). These large, high-molecular-
weight molecules with complex aromatic ring structures are typically dark
brown to black and constitute the majority of humic substances in most soils.

Fulvic acids are the smaller fraction that remains soluble at all pH levels.
Their smaller size allows them to penetrate plant tissues more readily. They
are typically yellow to yellow-brown and are more biologically active per unit
mass due to a greater density of functional groups (carboxyl, phenolic
hydroxyl, and quinone groups).

## Chelation and Mineral Availability

The primary function of [[teaming-with-microbes-humic-and-fulvic-acids]] acids in soil fertility is their
role as natural chelators. Chelation is the process by which organic
molecules bind to mineral ions (particularly micronutrients like iron,
manganese, zinc, copper, and boron), holding them in a plant-available form
that resists precipitation and lock-up in the soil.

Without humic substances, many micronutrients would quickly form insoluble
compounds with soil minerals, becoming unavailable to plants. Humic acids
wrap around these mineral ions, keeping them dissolved in the soil solution
and accessible to plant roots. This chelation effect is particularly
important in alkaline soils where iron and other micronutrients commonly
become unavailable.

Fulvic acids are especially effective chelators due to their small size and
high density of functional groups (carboxyl, phenolic hydroxyl, and quinone
groups). They can transport chelated minerals directly across cell membranes,
delivering nutrients into plant root cells.

## Extraction from Compost

Humic acids can be extracted from finished compost through alkaline
extraction. The process involves soaking mature compost in a solution
adjusted to pH 10-12 using potassium hydroxide (KOH) or sodium hydroxide
(NaOH). After 12-24 hours of agitation, the dark humic-acid-rich liquid is
separated from the solid compost material by filtration or settling.

In compost tea production, humic acids are extracted passively during the
brewing process. The aeration and gentle agitation of AACT brewing help
solubilize humic and fulvic acids from the compost particles into the tea
solution. This is one reason why Ingham emphasizes using mature, finished
compost with well-developed humus for tea production — immature compost
has not yet built sufficient humic acid content.

## Role in Soil Structure and Water Holding Capacity

Humic acids dramatically improve soil physical properties by binding soil
particles into stable aggregates, creating a range of pore sizes that improve
both drainage through macropores and water retention through micropores. Humic
substances can hold many times their weight in water due to their hydrophilic
functional groups, making soils more resilient during both drought and excess
rainfall.

## Interaction with Microbial Communities

Humic acids serve as both habitat and food source for soil microorganisms.
Their complex molecular structures provide surface area for bacterial and
fungal colonization. While most microorganisms cannot fully decompose humic
acids (which is why humus is stable), they can metabolize the simpler
side-chains and functional groups, slowly releasing energy.

In compost tea, humic acids serve an additional function by providing food
for beneficial fungi. Fungal-dominant teas benefit from humic acid additions
because the complex carbon molecules selectively feed fungi over bacteria.
This is why Ingham recommends humic acid additions when brewing teas for
perennial crops and woody plants.

## Root Growth Stimulation

Humic and fulvic acids directly stimulate root development by enhancing cell
membrane permeability, stimulating root cell elongation and division, and
modulating auxin-like hormone activity that promotes lateral root formation.
Research consistently shows increased total root mass, branching density, and
root hair development, with effects most pronounced in stressed plants and
low-organic-matter soils.

## Humic Acid Sources

Leonardite is an oxidized form of lignite coal and is the most common
commercial source of concentrated humic acids. It typically contains 40-60%
humic acid by weight. Leonardite-derived humic acids are effective but may
lack the full spectrum of biological activity found in compost-derived
humus.

Compost-derived humic acids come from well-finished compost and contain a
more biologically diverse mixture of humic substances. While their total
humic acid concentration is lower than leonardite (typically 10-20%),
they carry a living [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] that commercial humic acid products
lack. For compost tea production, Ingham recommends compost-derived sources
over mined humic acid products when possible.

## Application Rates and pH Considerations

For compost tea brewing, Ingham recommends adding 1-4 tablespoons of humic
acid powder per 5 gallons of tea. For direct soil application, rates vary
from 1-10 pounds per 1,000 square feet depending on soil conditions and
crop needs. [[ingham-foliar-applications-compost-tea-disease-suppression]] typically use 0.5-2% humic acid solutions.

Humic acids are most effective in soils with pH between 6.0 and 8.0. In
strongly acidic soils (below 5.5), humic acids may begin to precipitate
and lose effectiveness. In very alkaline soils (above 8.5), other
amendments may be needed to address broader nutrient availability issues.
