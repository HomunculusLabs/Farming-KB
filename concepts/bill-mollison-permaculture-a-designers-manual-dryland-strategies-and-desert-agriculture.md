# Dryland Strategies and Desert Agriculture in Permaculture
## Overview

Chapter 11 of Mollison's manual addresses one of permaculture's most critical challenges: designing productive systems in arid and semi-arid environments. Drylands cover over 40% of the Earth's land surface and support some of the world's most vulnerable populations. Permaculture dryland strategies focus on maximizing water capture, minimizing evaporation, building soil rapidly, and selecting adapted plant and animal systems. The fundamental principle is that water, not soil, is the primary limiting factor — manage water and everything else follows.

## Understanding Dryland Environments

### Climate Classification

Drylands are classified by the ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration:

- **Hyper-arid** — less than 100mm annual rainfall; virtually no agriculture without irrigation
- **Arid** — 100-250mm annual rainfall; nomadic pastoralism possible, limited cropping
- **Semi-arid** — 250-500mm annual rainfall; dryland farming feasible with water management
- **Dry sub-humid** — 500-750mm annual rainfall; transitional to humid zones

### Key Characteristics

Dryland environments share several features that the designer must work with:

- **High evapotranspiration** — water loss exceeds precipitation for most of the year
- **Intermittent rainfall** — rain often comes in intense, short bursts causing runoff
- **Low organic matter** — slow [[dighton-litter-quality-fungal-decomposition-rates]] limit soil humus accumulation
- **Temperature extremes** — hot days and cold nights stress both plants and animals
- **High solar radiation** — intense UV damages exposed soil and plants
- **Wind exposure** — desiccating winds accelerate evaporation and soil erosion

## Water Harvesting Techniques

### Swales and Contour Banks

Swales are level trenches dug on contour to intercept and infiltrate surface runoff. In drylands, swales are critical infrastructure:

- **Spacing** — swales are spaced according to slope: closer together on steep land (5-15m apart), further apart on gentle slopes (20-50m apart)
- **Construction** — excavated soil is placed on the downhill side as a berm, which is immediately planted
- **Infiltration** — even a single large storm can fill swales and recharge groundwater for months
- **Tree planting** — fruit and fodder trees planted on swale berms have reliable moisture access

### Microcatchments

For individual [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]], small-scale water harvesting is appropriate:

- **Semi-circular bunds** — crescent-shaped earth walls behind each tree capturing runoff
- **Negarim microcatchments** — square bunds with inlet channel, each serving a single tree
- **Zai pits** — traditional West African technique of planting in small compost-filled pits

### Keyline Design

water [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-water-harvesting-and-earthworks]] applies keyline principles in drylands by placing the keyline (point of maximum valley convexity) as the primary water distribution channel, spreading water across the landscape rather than allowing it to concentrate in valleys, and subsoiling along the keyline to break hardpans.

## Soil Management in Drylands

### Mulching

Mulch is the single most important soil management tool in drylands. Bare soil in arid environments can reach temperatures of 70°C or more, killing soil organisms and baking the surface into an impermeable crust. Mulch functions to:

- Reduce soil surface temperature by 10-20°C
- Reduce evaporation by 50-80%
- Prevent crusting and improve water infiltration
- Provide habitat for soil organisms
- Add organic matter as it decomposes

In drylands, rock mulch (stones placed around plants) is often more practical than organic mulch, as stones absorb heat during the day and release it at night, moderating temperature extremes.

### Soil Building

soil [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]] in drylands requires specific approaches:

- **Green manuring** — planting and incorporating leguminous cover crops during brief rainy seasons
- **Compost tea** — liquid compost extracts applied as a soil drench to jumpstart soil biology
- **Biochar** — charcoal incorporated into soils to increase water-holding capacity [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]]
- **Gypsum** — applied to sodic soils to improve structure and water penetration

### Wind Erosion Control

Wind is a major force in drylands. Strategies include:

- **Windbreaks** — multi-row plantings of drought-tolerant trees and shrubs (see below)
- **[[natural-farming-guide-contour-farming-strip-cropping]]** — alternating bands of perennial vegetation with annual crops
- **Emergency cover** — quick-growing annuals like sorghum sown after disturbance
- **Roughening the surface** — leaving furrows perpendicular to prevailing winds

## Plant Strategies for Drylands

### Windbreak Design

Windbreaks in drylands serve multiple functions: reducing evapotranspiration, preventing wind erosion, providing shade, and producing fodder, fuel, and timber. [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]] include:

- **Orientation** — perpendicular to prevailing hot/dry winds
- **Density** — 40-60% porosity for optimal wind reduction; too dense creates turbulence downwind
- **Species selection** — deep-rooted, drought-tolerant species like Casuarina, Prosopis, Acacia, Tamarix, and Olea
- **Multiple rows** — 3-5 rows with a mix of tall and low species for maximum effectiveness
- **Length** — at least 10-12 times the height of the tallest tree for adequate protection zone

### Drought-Adapted Plant Guilds

Productive dryland guilds center around deep-rooted, drought-tolerant species:

- **Date palm overstory** — with pomegranate, fig, and grape understory
- **Mesquite/Acacia overstory** — with prickly pear, agave, and desert legumes
- **Olive/carob overstory** — with rosemary, lavender, and annual grains beneath
- **Ziziphus (jujube)** — extremely drought-tolerant fruit tree for the hottest zones

### Waffle Gardens

Traditional Zuni waffle gardens are small square or rectangular depressions surrounded by low earthen walls. These create microenvironments where:

- Water is concentrated from the surrounding clay walls
- Wind is reduced at plant level
- Soil stays cooler and more moist
- Intensive [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]] is possible even in arid conditions

## The Desert House

Mollison describes the desert house as an integrated system where the dwelling itself contributes to the productive landscape:

- **Thermal mass** — thick earth or stone walls absorb heat during the day and release it at night
- **Underground or earth-sheltered construction** — stable temperatures year-round
- **Internal courtyards** — shaded outdoor living spaces with water features for cooling
- **Greywater recycling** — all household water is directed to [[query-how-do-i-build-and-manage-a-greywater-system-for-garden-irrigation]]
- **Roof catchment** — even small roofs in arid areas can capture significant water during rare storms
- **Shade structures** — pergolas, ramadas, and trellises create filtered shade for living and growing spaces

## Desertification Prevention

Desertification — the degradation of drylands to desert — is addressed through:

- **Restocking controls** — managing grazing animals within the [[fukuoka-textdoc-land-requirements-per-diet-type-carrying-capacity]] of the land
- **Revegetation** — establishing perennial vegetation on degraded land using succession principles
- **Salinity management** — preventing the rise of saline water tables through appropriate planting and drainage
- **Fire management** — controlling fire frequency to allow woody perennial establishment
- **[[ozier-pdc-stewardship-mandala-community-organization]]** — Mollison emphasizes that dryland management requires collective action, as individual efforts are easily overwhelmed by landscape-scale degradation

## Design Checklist for Drylands

- Map all water flows, including intermittent streams and runoff patterns
- [[swale-design-water-harvesting]] infrastructure before any planting
- Select plant species from the most arid zone they can survive in
- Plan for wind from all directions, not just the prevailing wind
- Design redundancy into water systems — always have backup storage
- Integrate animal systems carefully; overstocking causes desertification
- Use [[zone-and-sector-analysis]] to place high-water-use elements where water concentrates
