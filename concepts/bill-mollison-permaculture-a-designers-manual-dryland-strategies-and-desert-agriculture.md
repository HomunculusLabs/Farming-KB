# Dryland Strategies and Desert Agriculture in Permaculture
## Overview

Chapter 11 of Mollison's manual addresses one of permaculture's most critical challenges: designing productive systems in arid and semi-arid environments. Drylands cover over 40% of the Earth's land surface and support some of the world's most vulnerable populations. Permaculture dryland strategies focus on maximizing water capture, minimizing evaporation, building soil rapidly, and selecting adapted plant and animal systems. The fundamental principle is that water, not soil, is the primary limiting factor — manage water and everything else follows.

## Understanding Dryland Environments

### Climate Classification

Drylands are classified by the ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration:

- **Hyper-arid** — less than 100mm annual rainfall; virtually no agriculture without irrigation
- **Arid** — 100-250mm annual rainfall; nomadic pastoralism possible, limited cropping
- **Semi-arid** — 250-500mm annual rainfall; dryland farming feasible with water management
- **Dry sub-humid** — 500-750mm annual rainfall; transitional to humid zones

### Key Characteristics

Dryland environments share several features that the designer must work with:

- **High evapotranspiration** — water loss exceeds precipitation for most of the year
- **Intermittent rainfall** — rain often comes in intense, short bursts causing runoff
- **Low organic matter** — slow [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]], small-scale water harvesting is appropriate:

- **Semi-circular bunds** — crescent-shaped earth walls behind each tree capturing runoff
- **Negarim microcatchments** — square bunds with inlet channel, each serving a single tree
- **Zai pits** — traditional West African technique of planting in small compost-filled pits

## Plant Strategies for Drylands

### Windbreak Design

Windbreaks in drylands serve multiple functions: reducing evapotranspiration, preventing wind erosion, providing shade, and producing fodder, fuel, and timber. [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]] is possible even in arid conditions

## The Desert House

Mollison describes the desert house as an integrated system where the dwelling itself contributes to the productive landscape:

- **Thermal mass** — thick earth or stone walls absorb heat during the day and release it at night
- **Underground or earth-sheltered construction** — stable temperatures year-round
- **Internal courtyards** — shaded outdoor living spaces with water features for cooling
- **Greywater recycling** — all household water is directed to [[fukuoka-textdoc-land-requirements-per-diet-type-carrying-capacity]] of the land
- **Revegetation** — establishing perennial vegetation on degraded land using succession principles
- **Salinity management** — preventing the rise of saline water tables through appropriate planting and drainage
- **Fire management** — controlling fire frequency to allow woody perennial establishment
- **[[swale-design-water-harvesting]] infrastructure before any planting
- Select plant species from the most arid zone they can survive in
- Plan for wind from all directions, not just the prevailing wind
- Design redundancy into water systems — always have backup storage
- Integrate animal systems carefully; overstocking causes desertification
- Use [[zone-and-sector-analysis]] to place high-water-use elements where water concentrates
