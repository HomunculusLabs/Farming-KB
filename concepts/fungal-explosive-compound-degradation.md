     9|# Fungal Explosive and Nitroaromatic Compound Degradation
    10|
    11|## Overview
    12|
    13|Nitroaromatic compounds are used in the manufacture of explosives,
    14|pesticides, pharmaceuticals, dyes, and plastics. They are common
    15|contaminants of groundwater and soil near production sites and
    16|military facilities. Many nitroaromatics are highly toxic, carcinogenic,
    17|or mutagenic. White rot fungi, particularly Phanerochaete
    18|chrysosporium, have demonstrated significant ability to transform and
    19|degrade these recalcitrant compounds through both ligninolytic and
    20|non-ligninolytic enzyme pathways. The fungal approach offers advantages
    21|over bacterial methods because extracellular fungal enzymes can attack
    22|highly oxidized nitroaromatic structures that bacteria often cannot
    23|access.
    24|
    25|## Trinitrotoluene (TNT) Degradation
    26|
    27|2,4,6-trinitrotoluene (TNT) is the most widely studied explosive
    28|contaminant. Regular exposure to TNT causes liver damage and anaemia.
    29|P. chrysosporium degrades 30 to 50% of added TNT when concentrations
    30|remain below 20 mg per litre. Higher TNT concentrations inhibit
    31|degradation because intermediates, including 2-hydroxylamino-4,6-
    32|dinitrotoluene, 4-hydroxylamino-2,6-dinitrotoluene, and
    33|4-hydroxylamino-4,6-dinitrotoluene, accumulate and themselves inhibit
    34|further TNT breakdown. These inhibitory intermediates, not TNT itself,
    35|cause the cessation of degradation. Addition of TNT to ligninolytic
    36|cultures results in the disappearance of lignin peroxidase (LiP)
    37|activity, another effect of metabolites rather than TNT itself.
    38|
    39|## TNT Degradation Pathway
    40|
    41|P. chrysosporium transforms 2,4-dinitrotoluene through a proposed
    42|pathway involving oxidative, reductive, and methyl transfer reactions.
    43|The pathway includes multiple steps of nitro group reduction and
    44|oxidative ring transformation. An MnP from Nematoloma frowardii
    45|has been shown to catalyze TNT transformation. The degradation of
    46|TNT involves initial reduction of nitro groups to amino or
    47|hydroxylamino intermediates, followed by further oxidative or
    48|condensative reactions. The complexity of the pathway reflects the
    49|highly oxidized nature of TNT, which has three electron-withdrawing
    50|nitro groups on the aromatic ring.
    51|
    52|## Other Nitroaromatic Explosives
    53|
    54|Beyond TNT, fungi can transform a range of nitroaromatic explosives.
    55|These include 2,4-dinitrotoluene, 2-amino-4,6-dinitrotoluene,
    56|1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene, 2,4-dichloro-1-nitrobenzene, and
    57|1,3-dinitrobenzene. Nitrotoluene residues from explosive
    58|manufacturing are common pollutants at military facilities. The
    59|broad substrate range of ligninolytic enzyme systems enables white
    60|rot fungi to attack diverse nitroaromatic structures through
    61|free radical mechanisms, providing a general approach to explosive
    62|contaminant remediation regardless of specific molecular structure.
    63|
    64|## Ligninolytic versus Non-Ligninolytic Pathways
    65|
    66|Fungal degradation of nitroaromatics can proceed through both
    67|ligninolytic and non-ligninolytic mechanisms. The ligninolytic
    68|pathway involves peroxidases (LiP and MnP) and laccases acting on
    69|nitroaromatic substrates directly or through mediated oxidation.
    70|However, some nitroaromatic degradation by P. chrysosporium occurs
    71|independently of ligninolytic enzymes. This dual pathway capability
    72|is significant because it means degradation can continue even when
    73|ligninolytic conditions are not maintained, and it suggests
    74|alternative enzyme systems in these fungi remain underexplored.
    75|The relative contribution of each pathway depends on growth
    76|conditions, nitrogen availability, and the specific compound.
    77|
    78|## Challenges with TNT in Soil
    79|
    80|Soil bioremediation of TNT presents specific challenges. Fungal
    81|growth of P. chrysosporium can be completely inhibited by small
    82|amounts of TNT-contaminated soil (equivalent to 24 ppm TNT) added
    83|to liquid culture. The soil environment introduces additional
    84|complexity through sorption of TNT and its metabolites to soil
    85|organic matter, variable pH, moisture, and competition from
    86|indigenous microorganisms. In soil systems, complete mineralization
    87|of TNT to carbon dioxide is rarely achieved; instead, bound
    88|residues and transformation products dominate. These bound residues
    89|may be less bioavailable and therefore less toxic, but regulatory
    90|frameworks typically measure total TNT rather than bioavailable
    91|fractions.
    92|
    93|## Chemical Warfare Agent Degradation
    94|
    95|Fungal enzyme systems have shown potential for degrading chemical
    96|warfare agents. Nerve agents VX and Russian VX (RVX), along with
    97|the insecticide analog diisopropylamiton, contain phosphothiolate
    98|bonds. Pleurotus ostreatus rapidly and completely oxidized these
    99|compounds in the presence of ABTS as a laccase mediator. This finding
   100|extends the relevance of fungal bioremediation beyond conventional
   101|explosive contaminants to include military chemical agents, suggesting
   102|potential applications for demilitarization and cleanup of chemical
   103|warfare agent disposal sites.
   104|
   105|## Fungal Species for Nitroaromatic Degradation
   106|
   107|While P. chrysosporium remains the most extensively studied organism,
   108|other white rot fungi have shown significant nitroaromatic degradation
   109|capability. Bjerkandera adusta, Trametes versicolor, and Pleurotus
   110|species have all demonstrated explosive compound transformation.
   111|Screening programs have identified additional species from diverse
   112|ecological niches, including tropical forest fungi, that may possess
   113|superior degradation capabilities. The ecological diversity of
   114|ligninolytic fungi suggests that many more species with useful
   115|degradative properties await discovery, particularly from
   116|underexplored environments.
   117|
   118|## Integration with Other Remediation Approaches
   119|
   120|Fungal nitroaromatic degradation can be combined with other
   121|remediation strategies for enhanced effectiveness. Sequential
   122|treatment, where fungi first transform recalcitrant intermediates
   123|and bacteria subsequently mineralize products, has been advocated.
   124|Soil amendments including straw, corn cobs, and compost improve
   125|fungal growth and enzyme production in contaminated matrices.
   126|Surfactant addition may increase bioavailability of hydrophobic
   127|nitroaromatic compounds. Composting of explosive-contaminated soil
   128|with fungal inoculation has shown success on a laboratory scale,
   129|creating conditions favorable for sustained ligninolytic activity.
   130|
   131|## Monitoring and Assessment
   132|
   133|Monitoring fungal degradation of nitroaromatics requires tracking
   134|both parent compound disappearance and transformation product
   135|formation. Techniques include radiolabelled substrate studies,
   136|HPLC analysis of nitroaromatics and their metabolites, and
   137|enzyme activity assays for LiP, MnP, and laccase. In situ monitoring
   138|of fungal gene expression via mRNA extraction and quantitative
   139|reverse transcriptase PCR allows assessment of fungal activity
   140|in soil without destructive sampling. Image analysis of hyphal
   141|growth in soil provides complementary information on fungal
   142|colonization extent.
