The classic batch toilet consists of a toilet seat mounted over a container
(a 20-litre bucket or larger bin). After each use, the deposit is covered
with a generous layer of dry carbonaceous material such as sawdust, straw,
rice hulls, or shredded leaves. This cover material controls odour, absorbs
moisture, and provides the carbon necessary for effective composting. When
the container is full, it is sealed and set aside to compost while a fresh
container is put into service.

### Continuous Systems

Continuous compost toilets maintain a single composting chamber where fresh
deposits enter at the top and finished compost is removed from the bottom.
These systems are more complex, requiring proper ventilation, drainage of
excess liquid, and mechanical agitation or raking to maintain aerobic
conditions. They are suited to permanent installations with higher use rates.

### Vermicomposting Toilets

Vermicomposting systems use composting worms (typically Eisenia fetida,
red wigglers) to process humanure. Worms accelerate decomposition, reduce
volume, and produce vermicast, a high-quality soil amendment. Vermicomposting
toilets work best in warm climates where worms remain active year-round.

## Cover Materials

The choice and quantity of cover material is critical to successful
composting toilet operation. Cover material serves several functions:

- Absorbs excess moisture to maintain aerobic conditions
- Provides carbon to balance the high nitrogen content of humanure
- Controls odour by creating a biological filter
- Prevents fly access to fresh deposits

Suitable cover materials include sawdust (especially hardwood), [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-coco-coir-and-peat-moss]],
rice hulls, straw, shredded newspaper, coconut coir, and dry leaves. The
cover material should be dry, carbon-rich, and applied generously after
each use. Mollison notes that sawdust is often the most readily available
and effective cover material in rural and forested areas.
