# Constructed Wetlands for Wastewater Treatment

Constructed wetlands are engineered systems designed to replicate the natural water purification processes of wetland ecosystems for the treatment of wastewater, stormwater, and various contaminated water streams. They use the combined physical, chemical, and biological interactions of wetland vegetation, substrates, and associated microbial communities to remove pollutants including organic matter, nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus), suspended solids, heavy metals, pathogens, and emerging contaminants.

Constructed wetlands represent one of the most widely implemented and cost-effective phytoremediation technologies, with thousands of systems operating worldwide for municipal wastewater treatment, industrial effluent polishing, agricultural runoff management, and acid mine drainage treatment.

## Types of Constructed Wetlands

Constructed wetlands are classified into two primary types based on their hydrology and flow regime. Each type has distinct design characteristics, treatment mechanisms, and suitable applications.

### Surface Flow (Free Water Surface) Wetlands

Surface flow constructed wetlands (SF CWs) consist of shallow basins with emergent aquatic vegetation growing in saturated soil or sediment. Water flows horizontally across the surface through the standing water column and dense plant stands at relatively low velocities.

The water surface is exposed to the atmosphere, allowing atmospheric oxygen transfer, algal photosynthesis, and volatilization of certain contaminants. Treatment occurs through sedimentation of suspended solids, microbial degradation of organic matter in the aerobic water column and anaerobic sediments, plant uptake of nutrients and metals, and adsorption to substrate and root surfaces.

SF CWs provide excellent habitat value for wildlife and are visually appealing, making them popular for stormwater treatment and tertiary polishing of municipal wastewater. However, they require larger land areas than subsurface flow systems and may present concerns about mosquito breeding in standing water.

### Subsurface Flow Wetlands

Subsurface flow constructed wetlands (SSF CWs) route water through a porous medium such as gravel, sand, or crushed rock planted with wetland vegetation. Water flows horizontally or vertically through the substrate matrix below the surface, with no exposed standing water.

Horizontal subsurface flow (HSSF) wetlands are the most common type. Water enters at one end, flows horizontally through the gravel bed, and is collected at the opposite end. The saturated gravel provides a large surface area for microbial biofilm development, and the subsurface flow prevents mosquito breeding and reduces human exposure to contaminated water.

Vertical flow (VF) wetlands distribute water over the surface of a gravel bed, allowing it to percolate vertically through the substrate. intermittent dosing creates alternating [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-aerobic-and-anaerobic-composting]] conditions that enhance nitrification-denitrification processes for nitrogen removal. VF systems typically achieve better oxygen transfer and ammonia removal than HSSF systems.

Hybrid systems combining HSSF and VF stages in series leverage the strengths of each type for more comprehensive treatment of nitrogen, organic matter, and other contaminants.

### Floating Treatment Wetlands

Floating treatment wetlands (FTWs) use buoyant platforms supporting plants whose roots hang freely in the water column below. The plant roots provide extensive surface area for biofilm attachment and direct contact with the water column. FTWs can be deployed in existing water bodies such as ponds, lakes, and retention basins without the need for dedicated land area or substrate construction.

## Treatment Mechanisms

Constructed wetlands remove contaminants through a complex network of interacting processes involving physical filtration, chemical adsorption and precipitation, plant uptake, and microbial transformation. The relative importance of each mechanism varies with wetland type, design, and the target pollutants.

### Physical Processes

Physical removal mechanisms include sedimentation of suspended solids as water velocity decreases in the wetland, filtration of particles as water passes through substrate media or dense vegetation stands, and volatilization of certain compounds from the water surface. Surface flow wetlands provide more opportunity for sedimentation and volatilization, while subsurface flow wetlands excel at physical filtration through the substrate matrix.

### Biological Processes

Microbial processes are the primary drivers of contaminant transformation in constructed wetlands. Aerobic degradation of organic matter (BOD/COD removal) occurs in oxygenated zones near plant roots and at the water surface. Anaerobic processes including denitrification, sulfate reduction, and methanogenesis occur in anoxic sediments and deeper substrate layers.

Wetland plants play a crucial role by transporting atmospheric oxygen through their aerenchyma tissue to their root zones, creating aerobic microsites within otherwise anaerobic substrate. This oxygen leakage (radial oxygen loss) supports aerobic microbial communities in the rhizosphere, enabling simultaneous nitrification (aerobic) and denitrification (anaerobic) within close proximity.

Nitrification-denitrification is the primary nitrogen removal pathway in most constructed wetlands. Ammonia is oxidized to nitrate by nitrifying bacteria (Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter) in aerobic zones near plant roots, and nitrate is subsequently reduced to nitrogen gas by denitrifying bacteria (Pseudomonas, Paracoccus) in anoxic zones. Phosphorus is removed primarily through adsorption to substrate media and uptake by plants and microorganisms, though adsorption capacity is finite and eventually saturates.

### Plant-Microbe Interactions

The plant rhizosphere in constructed wetlands supports microbial populations that are orders of magnitude higher than bulk water or substrate. Root exudates provide carbon sources that sustain diverse microbial communities capable of degrading a wide range of organic contaminants.

Fungi play an important but often overlooked role in constructed wetland treatment. Studies have identified up to 40 fungal species belonging to 24 genera from contaminated wetland ecosystems, with many demonstrating efficient degradation of organic pollutants including [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]] hydrocarbons (PAHs). White-rot fungi and other ligninolytic species produce extracellular enzymes (laccase, [[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]]) that can degrade recalcitrant organic compounds.

Wastewater decolorization in constructed wetlands depends on the decomposition capabilities of diverse microbial communities. Pilot-scale constructed wetlands have been successfully employed to treat PAH-contaminated water, with fungal species degrading high percentages of fluoranthene and anthracene in the wetland environment.

### Chemical Processes

Chemical removal mechanisms include adsorption of metals and phosphorus to substrate media (especially gravel, sand, and manufactured media with high surface area), precipitation of metal hydroxides and sulfides, and chemical oxidation-reduction reactions. The choice of substrate media is particularly important for phosphorus removal, as different materials vary widely in their adsorption capacity and longevity.

## Design Considerations

Key design parameters for constructed wetlands include hydraulic loading rate, which determines the volume of water treated per unit area per day, organic loading rate, which affects the balance between aerobic and anaerobic processes, water depth and flow path length, which influence hydraulic retention time, and substrate media selection and depth.

Climate affects treatment performance, particularly in cold regions where biological activity slows during winter. Wetland vegetation must be selected for local climate conditions, water tolerance, treatment performance, and ease of establishment. Common species include Phragmites australis (common reed), Typha latifolia (cattail), Scirpus species (bulrushes), Juncus species (rushes), and Iris species.

Land area requirements are a significant design consideration. Surface flow wetlands typically require 5 to 10 times more area than subsurface flow systems for equivalent treatment. The area requirement depends on the target effluent quality, influent concentration, hydraulic loading rate, and climate.

## Applications

Constructed wetlands treat municipal wastewater at various levels from primary to tertiary treatment, particularly effective in small communities and rural areas where conventional treatment is cost-prohibitive. They provide [[fungal-industrial-wastewater-treatment]] treatment for sectors including food processing, textile manufacturing, pulp and paper, and mining.

Stormwater management systems use constructed wetlands to remove nutrients, sediments, metals, and hydrocarbons from urban runoff. Agricultural applications include treatment of animal waste lagoon effluent, feedlot runoff, and irrigation return water. Acid mine drainage treatment is a well-established application that leverages the sulfate-reducing and metal-precipitating capabilities of wetland systems.
