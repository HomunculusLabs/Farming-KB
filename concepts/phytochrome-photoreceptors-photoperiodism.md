# Phytochrome Photoreceptors and Photoperiodism in Plants

## Overview

Phytochrome is a photoreceptor pigment in plants that mediates responses to red and far-red light, playing a central role in photoperiodism—the ability of plants to measure day length and regulate flowering accordingly. Discovered through a series of elegant experiments beginning in the 1940s, phytochrome functions as a molecular light-activated switch that allows plants to distinguish between red light (which activates it) and far-red light (which deactivates it). This binary switching mechanism enables plants to determine the length of continuous darkness, which in turn controls critical developmental transitions including flowering, seed germination, [[phototropism-and-shade-avoidance]].

## Historical Discovery

### Maryland Mammoth Tobacco

The story of photoperiodism begins with an agricultural mystery. In 1906, farmers in southern Maryland noticed a new tobacco strain that grew to four and a half meters in height and produced nearly a hundred leaves, but rarely flowered. This "Maryland Mammoth" tobacco kept growing vegetatively and only stopped when winter frosts set in. While its vigorous growth seemed beneficial, the lack of flowering meant farmers could not harvest seed for the next year's crop.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of Agriculture set out to solve this mystery. They planted Maryland Mammoth in pots and conducted a simple but ingenious experiment: one group remained outside in natural conditions, while the other was moved to a dark shed each afternoon, artificially shortening the day. The shortened-day plants stopped growing and began flowering, revealing that Maryland Mammoth was a "short-day" plant—it required long nights (short days) to trigger flowering. This [[maryland-mammoth-tobacco-and-the-discovery-of-photoperiodism]] showed for the first time that plants actively measure the duration of light and darkness.

### The Night-Break Experiments

Following Garner and Allard's discovery, scientists around World War II made another critical finding: they could manipulate flowering by briefly turning lights on and off during the night. A short-day plant like soybean could be kept from flowering during naturally short autumn days if lights were turned on for just a few minutes in the middle of the night. Conversely, a long-day plant like iris could be induced to flower in winter if given a brief light pulse during the night.

This proved that plants measure not the length of the day but the length of the **continuous period of darkness**. A momentary flash of light during the night resets the plant's [[chamovitz-circadian-rhythm-internal-clock-plants]], as if the dark period had never occurred. This finding had immediate practical applications: chrysanthemum farmers could prevent flowering throughout autumn and winter by giving brief night-time light pulses, then stop the treatment two weeks before Mother's Day to trigger simultaneous flowering just in time for the spring market.

### Color Specificity

Scientists next asked what color of light the plants were detecting during these night-break experiments. The answer was surprising and elegant: only red light was effective at resetting the photoperiodic clock. Blue or green flashes had no influence on flowering, but just a few seconds of red light in the middle of the night was sufficient to disrupt the plant's measurement of continuous darkness. This color specificity pointed to a dedicated red-light photoreceptor, distinct from the blue-light receptors involved in phototropism, and ultimately led to the identification of phytochrome.

This discovery had immediate practical applications for greenhouse agriculture. By manipulating the duration and color of light exposure, growers could control the flowering time of commercially important crops. The technique of night-break lighting—using brief red-light pulses to prevent flowering in short-day plants—became standard practice in the chrysanthemum and poinsettia industries, allowing year-round production of seasonal crops.

### The Far-Red Reversal

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues at the same USDA lab where Maryland Mammoth was studied made the remarkable discovery that far-red light—wavelengths slightly longer than bright red, most visible at dusk—could completely cancel the effect of red light. If irises (long-day plants) were given red light at night, they flowered. If far-red light followed immediately, they did not flower. If red light followed the far-red, they flowered again. This on-off switching could be repeated multiple times, with the plant always responding to the **last color it saw**.

This led Warren L. Butler and colleagues to propose that a single photoreceptor was responsible for both effects, which they named **phytochrome** (from Greek: phyton = plant, chroma = color).

## Phytochrome Mechanism

### Two Forms

Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms:
- **Pr (P660)**: The inactive form that absorbs red light (~660 nm wavelength)
- **Pfr (P730)**: The active form that absorbs far-red light (~730 nm wavelength)

When Pr absorbs red light, it converts to Pfr. When Pfr absorbs far-red light, it converts back to Pr. In nature, sunlight contains both red and far-red wavelengths, with red dominating during full daylight. At dusk, the light shifts toward far-red as shorter wavelengths are scattered by the atmosphere. This means that during the day, phytochrome accumulates in the active Pfr form, and at night it gradually reverts to the inactive Pr form. The rate of this reversion provides the plant with a biochemical timer for measuring the duration of darkness.

### Ecological Logic

This mechanism has elegant ecological logic. The last light a plant sees at the end of the day is far-red, signaling that darkness is coming and the plant should "turn off" its photoperiodic responses. In the morning, red light dominates and activates phytochrome, "waking up" the plant's light-sensing system. By measuring how long ago it last saw red light, the plant can determine the season and regulate its development accordingly.

### Location in the Plant

Unlike phototropism, where the "eye" is at the shoot tip, photoperiodic sensing is distributed across the leaves. Experiments showed that illuminating any single leaf is sufficient to regulate flowering in the entire plant. If all leaves are removed but the stem and apex remain, the plant becomes blind to light pulses. Phytochrome in the leaves receives light cues and initiates a mobile signal—later identified as the protein FT (FLOWERING LOCUS T)—that propagates throughout the plant and induces flowering at the shoot apical meristem.

## Photoperiodic Plant Categories

Plants are classified by their [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] to day length. This classification reflects the evolutionary adaptation of different species to specific latitudes [[solomon-climate-zones-and-growing-seasons]], ensuring reproductive success under favorable environmental conditions.

- **Short-day plants**: Flower when the continuous dark period exceeds a critical length. Examples include chrysanthemums, soybeans, Maryland Mammoth tobacco, and Cannabis (under natural conditions). These plants typically evolved at lower latitudes where day length variation is less dramatic.
- **Long-day plants**: Flower when the continuous dark period is shorter than a critical length. Examples include irises, barley, spinach, and lettuce. These plants tend to originate from higher latitudes where long summer days signal favorable conditions for reproduction.
- **Day-neutral plants**: Flowering is not regulated by day length. Examples include tomatoes, rice, and maize. These plants rely on other environmental cues or autonomous developmental programs to time their reproductive transition.

The photoperiodic response ensures that plants flower at the appropriate time of year for successful reproduction—when pollinators are available, temperatures are suitable, and [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] provides sufficient time for seed maturation.

## Phytochrome Beyond Flowering

While photoperiodic control of flowering is the most dramatic phytochrome-mediated response, phytochrome also regulates multiple other developmental processes that are critical for plant survival and competitive ability in natural environments:

- **Seed germination**: Many seeds require red light to break dormancy, with far-red light reversing this effect. This prevents germination when seeds are buried too deep or shaded by leaf litter. The phytochrome system acts as a crude proximity sensor, allowing germination only when sufficient light reaches the seed—indicating it is close enough to the soil surface for successful seedling establishment.

- **[[shade-avoidance-syndrome]]**: Plants growing under a canopy receive more far-red than red light because canopy leaves absorb red light for photosynthesis while transmitting or reflecting far-red light. The resulting low Pfr/Pr ratio triggers stem elongation, petiole extension, and leaf repositioning—collectively called the shade avoidance syndrome—that helps the plant reach better light conditions. This response has significant implications for agriculture, as densely planted crops can trigger shade avoidance responses that reduce yield.

- **De-etiolation**: When a seedling emerges from soil into light, phytochrome activation triggers the transition from etiolated (pale, elongated, with closed cotyledons) to green, photosynthetically competent growth. This involves inhibition of stem elongation, opening and expansion of cotyledons, development of chloroplasts, and synthesis of chlorophyll and protective pigments.

## Modern Understanding

Since its discovery, phytochrome has been found to be a family of related proteins (phyA through phyE in Arabidopsis) with distinct but overlapping functions. Different phytochromes have different sensitivities to light intensity, duration, and quality, allowing plants to mount sophisticated, multi-layered responses to their light environment. The molecular signaling cascade downstream of phytochrome activation involves changes in gene expression mediated by transcription factors such as PIFs (Phytochrome Interacting Factors) and ultimately leads to the developmental responses observed at the whole-plant level.

## Molecular Details of Phytochrome Signaling

At the molecular level, phytochrome is a dimeric protein with a covalently attached chromophore called phytochromobilin, an open-chain tetrapyrrole related to the chlorophyll and heme pigments. The chromophore is responsible for light absorption and the conformational change that switches the protein between its Pr and Pfr forms.

When phytochrome converts from Pr to Pfr, it translocates from the cytoplasm into the nucleus, where it interacts with multiple signaling partners. In the nucleus, Pfr interacts with PIF transcription factors, targeting them for degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Since PIFs generally promote skotomorphogenic (dark-growth) development, their removal allows light-adapted gene expression programs to proceed. This signaling cascade affects hundreds of genes simultaneously, coordinating the massive physiological transition from dark to light growth.

PhyA is the primary phytochrome responsible for de-etiolation responses and is particularly sensitive to very low light intensities, making it the phytochrome that detects the first light a seedling encounters upon emerging from soil. PhyB is the predominant phytochrome in light-grown plants and mediates most red/far-red reversible responses including shade avoidance and photoperiodic flowering. The other phytochrome family members (phyC, phyD, phyE) have more specialized roles and often function redundantly with phyB.

## Photoperiodism and Cannabis

Cannabis is generally classified as a short-day plant, flowering when the dark period exceeds approximately 12 hours. This photoperiodic response is the basis for the standard [[cannabis-sativa-indoor-cultivation-techniques]] practice of switching from an 18/6 (light/dark) vegetative cycle to a 12/12 flowering cycle. The phytochrome system detects this change in day length and triggers the floral transition.

Understanding the phytochrome mechanism has practical implications for Cannabis growers. Light leaks during the dark period—even brief exposures to red light—can reset the phytochrome clock and delay flowering or cause plants to revert to vegetative growth. This sensitivity is particularly pronounced during the first two weeks of the 12/12 cycle, when the floral transition is being established. Once flowering is firmly initiated, the plant becomes somewhat less sensitive to occasional light interruption, though chronic exposure can still cause problems including hermaphroditism.

The color spectrum of supplemental lighting also matters: red-dominant LED fixtures are more likely to affect phytochrome-mediated responses than blue-dominant fixtures, which primarily drive photosynthesis through chlorophyll absorption rather than phytochrome activation. Some cultivators deliberately manipulate phytochrome using end-of-day far-red treatments to accelerate the floral transition, exploiting the natural Pfr-to-Pr dark reversion to signal an earlier onset of the critical dark period.

## The Concept of Plant Memory

The far-red reversal experiments revealed something profound about plant perception: the plant "remembers" the last color of light it saw. This is not memory in the neurological sense but rather a persistent biochemical state—the ratio of Pr to Pfr—that encodes recent light history. This molecular memory allows plants to integrate information over time and make developmental decisions based on accumulated light cues rather than responding only to instantaneous conditions.

This concept extends [[chamovitz-plant-photoreceptors-beyond-phytochrome]]. Plants exhibit various forms of "memory" including priming (enhanced defense responses after initial pathogen attack), stress memory (altered responses to repeated abiotic stresses), and [[plant-epigenetic-memory-gene-based-remembering]] (heritable changes in gene expression without changes to the DNA sequence). The phytochrome Pr/Pfr ratio represents one of the simplest and most elegant examples of how plants encode environmental information in biochemical form, and it serves as a conceptual bridge to understanding more complex forms of plant information processing discussed in contemporary plant neurobiology.
