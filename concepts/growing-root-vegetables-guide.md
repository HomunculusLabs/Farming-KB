# Growing Root Vegetables - Carrots Parsnips and Radishes

Updated: 2026-04-18

[[steve-solomon]] provides detailed guidance on biennial [[permaculture-plants-root-crops-and-tubers]], explaining their survival strategy and the specific techniques needed to grow them successfully in a low-input garden.

## The Biennial Root Crop Strategy

Solomon describes the biennial root crop's strategy as follows: sneak a deep tap root through the opposition; then begin drawing on moisture and nutrition located deeper than neighbors can reach. With a supply of raw materials assured, the plant fills a storage chamber with surplus food, rests over the winter, and in spring starts with a huge nutritional storehouse that it uses to rapidly overtop its neighbors and shade them out, then begins making seeds.

This strategy means that proper spacing and loose soil are critical -- the root must be able to penetrate deeply without obstruction.

## Carrots

### Soil Requirements

- Carrots need loose soil, especially a top foot (30 cm) that graciously allows the root to swell as fast as it wishes.
- In hard soil, the root pushes the soil aside, becoming tougher and irregularly shaped.
- If soil tends to become compacted, dig in a large quantity of well-rotted manure or compost (perhaps a two-inch layer).
- If the layer of soil the carrot grows in is too fertile, roots can become hairy and fork. Carrots discover most of their nutrients in the subsoil.

### Sowing Technique

Solomon's method for even spacing:

1. Thoroughly blend a heaping half teaspoon (2.5 ml) of strong carrot seed into a quart (liter) of fine sand or compost.
2. Uniformly sprinkle this mixture into the bottom of 25 feet (8 meters) of furrow.
3. You'll end up with evenly spaced seedlings about half an inch (1.25 cm) apart.
4. If they come up thicker, do a preliminary thinning right after germination.

### Thinning

- Providing steady moisture has more to do with thinning than with watering.
- Thin progressively during their first month.
- About the time tops are 3-4 inches (7-10 cm) tall, there should be no more losses to diseases.
- Then thin the row so each plant has its final spacing -- depends on variety size.

### Variety Selection

- **Nantes types**: tender, juicy, for fresh eating. Don't have enough strength to push aside resistant earth or grow large without cracking.
- **Danvers, Chantenay, Berlicum, Flakkee**: stronger, more fibrous types for heavier soils and winter storage.
- Nantes types need soils where carrots can push aside resistant earth when roots are filling out.
- Fiber types are better for storing well and for growing in heavier soils.

### Harvest and Storage

- If you have a younger carrot patch coming along, harvest by removing every other one in the row.
- You'll be amazed at how large some varieties can become while still tender and sweet with little root-zone competition.
- In mild climates, carrots overwinter in the earth. Cover crowns by hilling up a few inches of soil.
- Where soil freezes, root-cellar in barrels of moist sand.

### Seed Saving

- The wild carrot is Queen Anne's lace; domesticated carrots cross freely with it.
- If none is growing in the vicinity, grow seed safely. Overwinter selected roots and replant in spring.
- Need at least 50 plants because the species is bee-pollinated and depends on crossing for vigor.
- A 25-foot-long bed with a double row can produce enough seed for a decade.
- Carrot seed that did not get rained on and fully matured will last many years if stored dry.

## Parsnips

### Differences from Carrots

- Most varieties are longer than carrots, needing a deeper layer of loose topsoil.
- Slightly lower need for fertility and slower growth.
- Don't crack when quite big, as some carrot varieties do.
- Biggest difficulty: getting short-lived parsnip seeds to germinate (most seed lasts only two years after harvest).

### Planting

- Start on or shortly after the summer solstice so they size up when growth gets cooler.
- Closer to the equator, start closer to the equinox and consider them a cool-season crop.
- Sow thickly because germination is often below 25%.
- Cover seed with compost instead of soil to help.
- Shade may also help with germination in hot weather.

### Flavor

- Flavor of parsnips improves after they go through some serious frosts.
- Start them on the solstice, and they size up during the cooling weather.
