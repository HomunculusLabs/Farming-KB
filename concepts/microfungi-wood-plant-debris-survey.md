# Microfungi on Wood and Plant Debris — Survey Overview

Microfungi are defined as fungi with microscopic spore-producing structures. They comprise an unnatural but practical grouping that cuts across classification schemes for Fungi and fungus-like organisms.

## Taxonomic Composition

The microfungi on wood and plant debris include:

- **Ascomycota:** Largest division, ~3250 genera with 32,250 recognized species; most microfungi are ascomycetes
- **Anamorphic Fungi (Deuteromycetes):** ~2600 genera, 15,000 species, characterized by mitotic spore production (conidia). Most have teleomorphs in the Ascomycota
- **Microscopic Basidiomycota:** ~1400 genera, 22,250 species; many have minute fruit bodies or lack them completely. Tulasnellales are only microscopically visible
- **Zygomycota (Mucorales):** ~56 genera, 300 species, usually sampled via soil or dung protocols

## Nutritional Strategies

The most important factor influencing fungal distribution is their heterotrophic nutrition. Fungal growth is confined to environments with suitable supplies of organic compounds:

- **Saprotrophy:** Using nonliving organic material from a plant the fungus did not kill
- **Necrotrophy:** Killing living plant tissues and then using them; frequently involves processes similar to saprotrophy
- **Biotrophy:** Exploiting only living host cells; much less common among debris-colonizing microfungi

Many species exhibit nutritional metamorphoses — a large proportion of endophytic fungal species become saprobic on host death, allowing immediate utilization of dead tissue. Some enter an intermediate necrotrophic stage as host tissue becomes moribund.

## Habitat Extent

Microfungi grow on plant debris in every conceivable habitat, from Arctic and Antarctic to deserts and rain forests. The nature and quantities of nutrients, physical features of substratum, water availability, humidity, and temperature determine colonization success, survival, and community composition.

## Factors Influencing Diversity

### Site-Level Factors
- Temperature, water relations, seasonality, and physical factors
- Plant diversity and distribution
- Degree of habitat variety

### Substratum Characterization
- Origin: roots, stems, trunks, bark, branches, twigs, leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds
- Condition: age, fragmentation, decomposition degree, chemical composition
- Location: superficial, buried, immersed, litter layer, canopy

### Ecological Strategies
Three primary strategies (Grime's CSR system adapted for fungi):
- **Ruderal:** Rapid colonization of disturbed or newly available substrata
- **Competitive:** Domination of stable, resource-rich environments
- **Stress-Tolerant:** Persistence in resource-poor or physically stressful conditions

## Sampling Strategies

### Direct Observation
Careful examination of substrata in the field and laboratory

### Culture Techniques
Isolation on artificial media; essential because many microfungi are identified only from their anamorphic (asexual) states in culture

### Moist Chamber Incubation
Placing substrata in humid chambers to induce sporulation

### Washed Litter Methods
Washing and filtering litter particles to concentrate fungal spores and propagules

## Limitations

- **Size:** Microfungi are easily overlooked without careful microscopic examination
- **Teleomorph-Anamorph connections:** Most anamorphs have no known link to sexual states
- **Ecology:** Little is known about the ecology of most species
- **Species concepts:** Varying species concepts among taxonomists complicate diversity estimates
- **Taxonomic expertise:** Severe shortage of trained microfungal taxonomists worldwide
