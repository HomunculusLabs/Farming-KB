# Microfungi on Wood and Plant Debris — Survey Overview

Microfungi are defined as fungi with microscopic spore-producing
structures. They comprise an unnatural but practical grouping that
cuts across classification schemes for Fungi and fungus-like
organisms. Despite their small size, microfungi are enormously
important in nutrient cycling, plant pathology, and ecosystem
function. Their roles are part of the broader [[fungal-ecosystem-services]] framework, and their diversity on decomposing material is catalogued in [[biodiversity-microfungi-litter-wood-debris]].

## Taxonomic Composition

The microfungi on wood and plant debris include representatives from
multiple major [[fungal-diversity-leaf-litter-coarse-woody-debris|fungal]] lineages:

- **Ascomycota:** Largest division, approximately 3,250 genera with
  32,250 recognised species. Most microfungi are ascomycetes,
  including the vast majority of leaf litter and wood-inhabiting
  species.
- **Anamorphic Fungi (Deuteromycetes):** Approximately 2,600 genera
  and 15,000 species, characterised by mitotic spore production
  (conidia). Most have teleomorphs in the Ascomycota.
- **Microscopic Basidiomycota:** Approximately 1,400 genera and
  22,250 species. Many have minute fruit bodies or lack them
  completely. Tulasnellales are only microscopically visible.
- **Zygomycota (Mucorales):** Approximately 56 genera and 300
  species, usually sampled via soil or dung protocols.

## Nutritional Strategies

The most important factor influencing fungal distribution is their
heterotrophic nutrition. Fungal growth is confined to environments
with suitable supplies of organic compounds:

- **Saprotrophy:** Using nonliving organic material from a plant the
  fungus did not kill. This is the dominant strategy among debris-
  colonising microfungi and drives the decomposition process that
  recycles nutrients in all terrestrial ecosystems.
- **Necrotrophy:** Killing living plant tissues and then using them.
  Frequently involves processes similar to saprotrophy. Necrotrophic
  fungi are important plant pathogens responsible for cankers, leaf
  spots, and root diseases.
- **Biotrophy:** Exploiting only living host cells. Much less common
  among debris-colonising microfungi but includes important
  endophytic species that switch to saprotrophy upon host death.

Many species exhibit nutritional metamorphoses: a large proportion of
endophytic fungal species become saprobic on host death, allowing
immediate utilisation of dead tissue. Some enter an intermediate
necrotrophic stage as host tissue becomes moribund, blurring the
boundary between parasitism and saprotrophy.

## Habitat Extent

Microfungi grow on plant debris in every conceivable habitat, from
Arctic and Antarctic to deserts and rain forests. The nature and
quantities of nutrients, physical features of substratum, water
availability, humidity, and temperature determine colonisation
success, survival, and community composition.

Key environmental gradients include:
- Moisture availability: from permanently saturated to xeric
- Temperature: from permanently frozen to thermophilic
- pH: from strongly acidic leaf litter to alkaline wood ash
- Substrate quality: from nutrient-rich fresh litter to recalcitrant
  heavily decomposed wood

## Factors Influencing Diversity

### Site-Level Factors
- Temperature, water relations, seasonality, and physical factors
- Plant diversity and distribution across the landscape
- Degree of habitat variety (microhabitats increase diversity)
- Disturbance history and successional age of the ecosystem

### Substratum Characterization
Factors that determine which fungi colonise a given piece of debris:
- **Origin:** roots, stems, trunks, bark, branches, twigs, leaves,
  flowers, fruits, seeds
- **Condition:** age, fragmentation, decomposition degree, chemical
  composition (C:N ratio, lignin content, tannins)
- **Location:** superficial, buried, immersed, litter layer, canopy
- **Size:** fine twigs support different communities than coarse
  woody debris

### Ecological Strategies
Three primary strategies following Grime's CSR system adapted for
fungi:
- **Ruderal:** Rapid colonisation of disturbed or newly available
  substrata. These species grow quickly, produce many spores, and
  are often displaced by slower-growing competitors.
- **Competitive:** Domination of stable, resource-rich environments.
  These species grow steadily and can exclude ruderals through
  resource capture and antagonistic interactions.
- **Stress-Tolerant:** Persistence in resource-poor or physically
  stressful conditions. These species grow slowly but survive where
  competitors cannot, such as on extremely dry or chemically
  challenging substrata.

## Succession on Decomposing Wood

Microfungal communities on decomposing wood follow predictable
successional patterns:
1. **Initial colonisers (weeks):** Primarily yeasts and
  sugar-tolerant fungi exploiting simple carbohydrates
2. **Primary decomposers (months):** Cellulose-degrading fungi
  become dominant as simple sugars are exhausted
3. **Secondary decomposers (years):** Lignin-degrading fungi
  appear as cellulose is depleted — these include [[brown-rot-fungi-cellulose-degradation-wood-decay|brown-rot]] and white-rot fungi that are key agents of wood decomposition
4. **Late-stage specialists (decades):** Stress-tolerant species
  persist on recalcitrant substrates

## Sampling Strategies

### Direct Observation
Careful examination of substrata in the field and laboratory using
hand lenses and compound microscopes. Sporulating structures on
substrate surfaces are identified directly. This method captures
only fungi currently producing spores.

### Culture Techniques
Isolation on artificial media is essential because many microfungi
are identified only from their anamorphic (asexual) states in
culture. Dilution plating, direct plating, and [[particle-filtration-dilution-plating-microfungal-isolation|particle]] filtration
are standard approaches. Media selection affects which species are
recovered.

### Moist Chamber Incubation
Placing substrata in humid chambers (typically Petri dishes with
moist filter paper) to induce sporulation. Incubation at ambient or
controlled temperatures for days to weeks allows fungi to fruit on
the substrate surface.

### Washed Litter Methods
Washing and filtering litter particles to concentrate fungal spores
and propagules. Particularly useful for quantifying fungal biomass
and detecting rare species missed by direct observation.
