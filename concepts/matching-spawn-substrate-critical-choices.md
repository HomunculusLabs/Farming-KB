# Matching Spawn to Substrate for Mushroom Cultivation

## Overview

Once mushroom spawn has been created, the cultivator arrives at a critical crossroad in the cultivation process. The choice of how to transition from spawn to fruiting substrate involves several possible paths, each with distinct advantages and risks. The selection of spawn type relative to the target substrate directly affects colonization speed, crop yield, disease resistance, and the likelihood of contamination. This decision represents one of the most consequential choices in the mycelial path from laboratory to harvest.

## The Fundamental Decision: Grain vs. Sawdust Spawn

Grain spawn and sawdust spawn serve overlapping but distinct roles in mushroom cultivation. Grain spawn, typically made from rye, wheat, millet, or milo, is highly nutritious and provides a strong nutritional boost to the substrate it inoculates. Each kernel of grain becomes a mycelial capsule, a platform from which mycelium can leap into the surrounding substrate. Sawdust spawn, created by inoculating sterilized hardwood sawdust with grain spawn, is less nutritious but provides many more points of inoculation per unit of mass.

The choice between grain and sawdust spawn depends on the target substrate and the species being cultivated. For Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.) grown on cereal straws, grain spawn is preferred because it boosts the nutritional base of the straw, radically improving yields compared to using an equal mass of sawdust spawn. Although sawdust may provide more individual points of inoculation, the nutritional advantage of grain results in substantially higher biological efficiency.

## The Recommended Path for Wood-Degrading Species

For high-yield indoor cultivation of Shiitake (Lentinula edodes), Enokitake (Flammulina velutipes), Maitake (Grifola frondosa), Kuritake (Hypholoma sublateritium), Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum), and similar species on sterilized substrates, a three-stage progression is recommended:

1. **Grain spawn**: The first stage, created from agar cultures, provides vigorous mycelium on a nutritious carrier.
2. **Sawdust spawn**: Grain spawn inoculates sterilized sawdust, creating a second-generation inoculum already acclimated to wood-based substrates.
3. **Enriched sawdust fruiting blocks**: Sawdust spawn inoculates supplemented sawdust formulations designed for maximum fruiting.

Each transfer step results in an expansion of mycelial mass, typically by a factor of 5 to 10, and takes one to two weeks to fully colonize. This progressive acclimation ensures that the mycelium has already adapted to the lignocellulosic environment before encountering the final, supplemented fruiting substrate.

## Risks of Skipping Intermediate Steps

More direct methods can be used but carry significant risks. For example, using grain spawn of Shiitake to directly inoculate enriched sawdust, skipping the intermediate sawdust spawn stage, results in several observable problems:

- **Fewer inoculation points**: Sawdust spawn provides many more points of contact with the substrate than grain, leading to faster and more even colonization.
- **Slower recovery**: The mycelium has already acclimated to the sawdust substrate when sawdust spawn is used, so the "leap off" is faster from sawdust than from grain.
- **Thermogenesis**: Enriching the substrate with grain spawn increases its nitrogen content, which accelerates biochemical reactions and produces two main by-products: heat and carbon dioxide.

## Understanding Thermogenesis

Thermogenesis is the self-heating that occurs when mycelium colonizes a substrate. This phenomenon is directly related to the metabolic activity of the growing fungus. When grain spawn is used to inoculate a supplemented sawdust substrate, the increased nitrogen content causes metabolic reactions to accelerate. Internal temperatures within the substrate bags can rise rapidly.

If internal temperatures exceed 100 degrees Fahrenheit (38 degrees Celsius) in the core of each bag, latent contaminants, especially thermophilic bacteria and black pin molds (Aspergillus, Rhizopus, and Mucor), can spring forth, contaminating every bag. These same bags, incubated at 75 degrees Fahrenheit (24 degrees Celsius), would otherwise be successfully colonized without issue.

The arena of thermogenesis problems is largely avoided by using sawdust spawn for inoculation into supplemented sawdust substrates rather than grain spawn. Thermogenesis is reduced to a more manageable level, colonization is faster and more even, and the cultivator gets more "mycelial mileage" from the overall process.

## Substrate Testing with Agar Media

A useful technique for matching a mushroom strain with an available substrate is to place a small sample of the substrate directly into the agar media formula. Upon exposure, the mushroom mycelium generates enzymes and acids to break down the proposed food source. Once acclimated, the mycelium carries a genetic memory of the end substrate to which it is destined.

For Shiitake, Enokitake, Maitake, and Reishi, Paul Stamets recommends acquainting the mushroom mycelium with the host substrate by introducing a 1 to 2 gram sample of sawdust directly into the liquid fermentation vessels. The resulting liquid inoculum is then used to generate grain spawn. This method of pre-acclimation empowers the mushroom mycelium with enzyme pathways specifically suited to the target substrate.

## Exceptions and Special Cases

Two notable exceptions exist where sawdust spawn outperforms grain on straw: Hypsizygus ulmarius and H. tessulatus (the Elm Oyster and Buna-Shimeji). Both species benefit when sawdust spawn is used to inoculate wheat straw, contrary to the general rule that grain is superior for straw-based cultivation.

For those who wish to create plug spawn for inoculating stumps and logs, the recommended path is grain spawn to sterilized sawdust, and once fully grown out, to sterilized wooden dowels. This multi-step acclimation ensures the mycelium is well-adapted to wood before encountering the outdoor log environment.

## Success Criteria

The success of any spawn-to-substrate matching strategy is measured by three primary criteria:

1. **Speed and quality of colonization**: How quickly and completely the mycelium permeates the substrate.
2. **Crop yield**: The biological efficiency achieved, measured as the weight of fresh mushrooms relative to the dry weight of the substrate.
3. **Resistance to disease**: The ability of the colonized mycelium to resist competitor organisms during the vulnerable colonization phase.
