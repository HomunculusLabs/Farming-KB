The permaculture response to pest problems is not to reach for a spray but to
ask: what systemic factor is allowing this pest to proliferate? The answer is
usually found in design deficiencies -- lack of diversity, poor plant health,
absent habitat for beneficial organisms, or inappropriate species selection.

## Design for Pest Prevention

**Diversity:** The primary defense. A diverse polyculture of 10-20+ species
in any given area makes it difficult for any single pest to find sufficient
hosts in proximity. Interplant aromatic herbs (rosemary, thyme, basil, mint)
among crops to mask host plant odors. Mollison recommends at least 30% non-crop
vegetation in any production area.

**Plant Health:** Healthy plants resist pests. Proper nutrition (especially
balanced mineral availability), adequate water, appropriate spacing, and
suitable microclimate selection produce vigorous plants less susceptible to
attack. Over-fertilized nitrogen-rich growth is particularly attractive to
aphids and other sucking insects.

**Timing:** Stagger plantings, use succession cropping, and choose varieties
with different maturity dates to avoid creating large uniform populations of
susceptible plants at any one time.

**Resistant Varieties:** Select crop varieties with known pest resistance as
the first line of defense. Regional heirloom varieties often carry resistance
to local pest populations through centuries of co-evolution.

## Habitat for Beneficial Organisms

Designing habitat for predatory and parasitic insects, birds, bats, frogs, and
spiders is a central IPM strategy. Mollison calls these the "biological
controls" and treats their habitat requirements as a design specification.

**Insectary plants:** Species that provide nectar and pollen for adult
predatory insects (ladybirds, lacewings, hoverflies, parasitic wasps). Key
genera include: umbellifers (dill, fennel, coriander, Queen Anne's lace),
Asteraceae (daisies, yarrow, cosmos), and Lamiaceae (mint family). Maintain
continuous bloom throughout the growing season by including early, mid, and
late-flowering species.

**Permanent habitat features:** Rock piles, log piles, bare soil patches,
hedgerows, and ponds provide overwintering sites, nesting habitat, and drinking
water for beneficial organisms. A [[trap-cropping]]

Trap crops are sacrificial plantings that attract pests away from main crops.
The principle exploits the pest's preference for specific host plants. Examples:

- Nasturtiums trap aphids and caterpillars away from brassicas
- Radishes attract flea beetles, protecting eggplant and cabbage
- Marigolds can trap nematodes (though effectiveness varies by species)
- Chervil attracts slugs away from lettuce and other tender greens

Trap crops must be managed actively -- monitor them for pest populations and
destroy or treat the trap crop before pests complete their lifecycle and move to
main crops. Position trap crops between the pest source (usually a fencerow or
wild area) and the main crop.

## Biological Controls

**Predatory insects:** Ladybirds (Coccinellidae) for aphids; lacewings
(Chrysopidae) for aphids, mites, and thrips; ground beetles (Carabidae) for
slugs and soil-dwelling pests; predatory mites (Phytoseiidae) for spider mites.

**Parasitoids:** Parasitic wasps (Braconidae, Ichneumonidae, Trichogrammatidae)
attack caterpillars, aphids, and insect eggs. Tachinid flies parasitize
caterpillars and beetles.

**Microbial agents:** Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) is a soil bacterium that
produces toxins lethal to certain insect larvae (caterpillars, mosquito larvae).

**Sanitation:** Remove diseased plant material, destroy infected debris, and
clean tools to prevent pathogen carryover.

**Physical barriers:** Row covers, netting, and insect exclusion mesh protect
crops during critical growth stages. Copper tape and diatomaceous earth deter
slugs and snails.

## Monitoring and Thresholds

Mollison advocates systematic observation over reactive intervention. Weekly
monitoring allows early detection before populations reach damaging levels.
Establish action thresholds: the pest population level at which economic or
ecological damage justifies intervention. In permaculture systems, the threshold
for intervention is typically higher than commercial agriculture because some
cosmetic damage is acceptable. Yellow sticky traps, pheromone traps, and light
traps indicate pest species presence and population trends.

## When Intervention Is Needed

When natural controls fail to keep pest populations below threshold, Mollison
recommends the following escalation:

1. Hand-picking or mechanical removal
2. Targeted biological sprays (Bt, neem oil, insecticidal soap)
3. Botanical sprays (pyrethrum, rotenone) as a last resort
4. Synthetic pesticides only in extreme circumstances and never as routine
   practice
