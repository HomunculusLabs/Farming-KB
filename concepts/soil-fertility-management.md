# Soil Fertility Management

Steve Solomon's "Gardening When It Counts" provides a comprehensive framework for managing soil fertility, especially for low-input homestead gardeners who cannot afford or choose not to use [[teaming-with-nutrients-natural-vs-synthetic-fertilizers]]. The core thesis: you must understand the actual mineral needs of your crops and provide them systematically from organic or mineral sources.

## The Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF)

Solomon's signature contribution is a balanced, custom organic fertilizer recipe designed to provide complete NPK plus essential trace minerals. It is far more effective than applying single-nutrient amendments like manure or compost alone.

### Standard COF Recipe (for initial ground preparation)

| Ingredient | Amount | Provides |
|------------|--------|----------|
| Seed meal (canola, soy, cottonseed, or linseed) | 4 parts | Nitrogen, phosphorus, some trace minerals |
| Agricultural lime (ag lime) | 1 part | Calcium, raises pH |
| Rock phosphate (phosphate rock, colloidal phosphate) | 1/2 part | Slow-release phosphorus |
| Gypsum | 1/2 part | Calcium + sulfur (does not raise pH) |

**Application rate**: 4-6 quarts per 100 sq ft, worked into top 4-6 inches of soil before planting.

### Key Principles of the COF

- **Seed meal is the N source**: Choose the cheapest available seed meal in your region. Canola is widely available. Cottonseed is common in cotton-growing areas.
- **Lime vs. gypsum matters**: Ag lime raises pH AND supplies calcium. Gypsum supplies calcium AND sulfur WITHOUT raising pH. On already-alkaline soils, use gypsum instead of lime.
- **Rock phosphate is slow**: It releases phosphorus over years, not days. Think of it as building long-term phosphorus reserves.
- **This is a complete fertilizer**: It provides N, P, K, Ca, S, and trace minerals in one application.

### COF Variations

- **For acid soils**: Use full ag lime portion
- **For alkaline soils**: Replace ag lime with gypsum (same amount) — supplies calcium without further raising pH
- **Side-dressing during growth**: Use COF at half rate (2-3 quarts per 100 sq ft) as a side-dress when plants are established
- **For heavy feeders** (tomatoes, corn, brassicas): Full rate plus additional seed meal side-dressing

## Understanding NPK in Context

### Nitrogen (N)
- Drives leafy growth; most plants show dramatic response to N
- **Deficiency**: Pale yellowing leaves (chlorosis), stunted growth
- **Excess**: Excessive foliage, delayed flowering, soft growth susceptible to pests
- **Organic sources**: Seed meal (most concentrated), blood meal, fish meal, well-rotted manure, legume [[cover-cropping-guide]]
- Seed meal provides 5-7% N — the highest practical concentration from organic sources

### Phosphorus (P)
- Essential for root development, flowering, fruit/seed production
- **Deficiency**: Poor root growth, stunted plants, dark green or purplish leaves
- **Very slow to become available** in organic systems — rock phosphate takes years to break down
- **Organic sources**: Rock phosphate, bone meal, well-rotted manure
- Rock phosphate at 30% P2O5 is the standard slow-release source

### Potassium (K)
- Essential for disease resistance, fruit quality, drought tolerance, cell wall strength
- **Deficiency**: Weak stems, poor fruit quality, yellowing leaf edges
- **Most soils have adequate K** — deficiency is less common than N or P
- **Organic sources**: Wood ash, kelp meal, greensand, sulfate of potash (mined)
- **Wood ash**: 3-7% K2O, also supplies calcium and raises pH (use cautiously on alkaline soils)

## pH: The Master Variable

### Why pH Matters
Soil pH (0-14 scale, 7 = neutral) determines [[fungal-enzyme-activity-nutrient-availability]]. Most garden vegetables prefer slightly acid soil (pH 6.0-6.8). At the wrong pH, even abundant nutrients become "locked out" and unavailable to plants.

### Key pH Relationships
- **Below 5.5**: Aluminum and manganese become toxic; phosphorus, calcium, and magnesium become unavailable
- **6.0-6.8**: Optimal range for most vegetables — maximum nutrient availability
- **Above 7.5**: Iron, manganese, zinc, and boron become unavailable; chlorosis common
- **Every 1.0 pH point change** = 10x change in hydrogen ion concentration

### Testing and Adjusting pH
- Test soil annually with a lab test (cheap and thorough) — home test kits are unreliable
- **To raise pH** (make more alkaline): Add agricultural limestone. Fineness matters — the finer the lime, the faster it acts. Dolomite lime adds magnesium too (use only if Mg is also needed)
- **To lower pH** (make more acidic): Add elemental sulfur, peat moss, or organic matter (compost naturally acidifies slightly as it decomposes)
- **Never add lime and sulfur at the same time** — they cancel each other

### Regional pH Patterns
- Areas with high rainfall tend toward acidic soils (rain leaches calcium)
- Arid regions tend toward alkaline soils
- Know your starting point before amending

## Compost: Excellent but Not Complete

Solomon cautions against relying on compost alone as a fertility source:

- **Compost is a soil conditioner first, a fertilizer second** — it improves tilth, water retention, and microbial life, but its NPK content is low and unpredictable
- Typical finished compost: ~1-1.5% N, ~1% P, ~1% K (variable by inputs)
- To supply adequate nitrogen from compost alone, you would need to apply impractical volumes (several inches per year)
- **Best use of compost**: As a soil conditioner applied at 1/4-1/2 inch per year, plus the COF for actual nutrient supply
- Compost from manure is more nutrient-dense than compost from yard waste only
- See [[vermicomposting]] for worm-based [[composting-methods-comparison]]

## The Importance of Calcium

Calcium is arguably the most underappreciated nutrient in home gardening:

- Essential for cell wall structure — deficiency causes blossom end rot in tomatoes, tip burn in lettuce
- **Not very mobile in plants** — the plant cannot move calcium from old leaves to new growth
- **Soil calcium levels matter more than calcium in the fertilizer** — adequate soil calcium allows steady uptake
- **Ag lime** is the primary calcium source (also raises pH)
- **Gypsum** (calcium sulfate) provides calcium without pH change — essential on alkaline soils
- Most vegetables need calcium at 60-75% of base saturation

## Organic Matter and Soil Structure

### The Role of Organic Matter
- Improves water retention (holds 4-5x its weight in water)
-decompositioninage in clay soils (binds particles into aggregates)
- Provides cation exchange sites (holds nutrients in root zone)
- Feeds [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] microorganisms
- Buffers pH changes
- Releases nutrients slowly through microbial decomposition

### Building Organic Matter
- [[cover-cropping-guide]] with legumes adds both N and organic matter
- Incorporating crop residues (not removing them)
- Applying compost at 1/4-1/2 inch annually
- Green manures (grown and tilled in before maturity)
- Mulching with organic materials that decompose in place

### Solomon's Practical Note
- Don't try to build organic matter levels above 5-8% — this is plenty
- Excessive organic matter can cause waterlogging, [[green-cannabis-soil-flushing-nutrient-leaching]], and weed problems
- Focus on maintaining steady levels rather than dramatic increases
