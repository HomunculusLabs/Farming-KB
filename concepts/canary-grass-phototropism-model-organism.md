# Canary Grass as a Model Organism in Phototropism Research

**Canary grass** (*Phalaris canariensis*) occupies a distinguished position in the
history of plant physiology as the primary experimental organism used by Charles
Darwin and his son Francis in their landmark studies of phototropism. Their
experiments with this unassuming grass seedling, published in *The Power of
Movement in Plants* (1880), established foundational principles about how plants
perceive and respond to light that remain influential more than a century and a
half later.

## The Species and Its Botanical Qualities

*Phalaris canariensis* is an annual grass native to the Mediterranean region but
widely cultivated as a birdseed crop, which is the origin of its common name. The
seedlings possess a prominent coleoptile, which is the protective sheath that
encloses the emerging shoot tip of grasses and other monocots. The coleoptile of
canary grass is straight, cylindrical, and relatively uniform in diameter, making
it an ideal subject for experiments that require precise localisation of light
stimuli and surgical manipulation.

Several practical qualities made canary grass an excellent choice for phototropism
research. The seedlings grow quickly under [[psilocybe-cubensis-strain-potency-variability-controlled-conditions-bigwood-beug]], their coleoptiles
are long enough to allow clear observation of bending responses, and their
phototropic sensitivity is both rapid and pronounced. These characteristics enabled
Darwin to observe measurable bending within just three hours of exposing dark-grown
seedlings to a directional light source, even when that source was as dim as a gas
lamp positioned twelve feet (3.5 metres) away from the plants.

The coleoptile architecture of canary grass was particularly well suited to the
experimental manipulations Darwin had in mind. Its elongated, cylindrical shape
made it straightforward to apply surgical treatments such as tip removal with a
sharp blade, and to position small barriers like caps and tubes at precise
anatomical locations. The bending response occurred consistently at a specific
region approximately two centimetres (one inch) below the tip, providing a clear
and reproducible anatomical reference point for all observations.

## Darwin's Dark-Room Protocol

Before each experiment, Darwin and his son grew canary grass seedlings in total
darkness for several days. This dark-adaptation period was essential to the
[[lightproof-cap-experimental-design-phototropism]]: it ensured that the plants had not been previously exposed to
directional light, which could have primed or desensitised their phototropic
response mechanism. Only after the seedlings had been sufficiently dark-adapted
were they exposed to the experimental light source.

The light source itself was a gas lamp maintained at an extremely low intensity.
Darwin noted that it was so dim that the experimenters themselves could not see the
seedlings, nor could they read a pencil line drawn on paper held near the plants.
This detail is significant because it demonstrates that the phototropic response was
operating through a dedicated light-sensing system entirely separate from
photosynthesis. The light levels were far too low to drive meaningful energy
production, proving that canary grass possessed a specialised perceptual capability
for detecting light direction and quantity.

After only three hours of exposure to this barely perceptible light, the canary
grass coleoptiles had bent unmistakably toward the lamp. The bending always
occurred at the same anatomical location, approximately two centimetres below the
tip of the coleoptile, providing a consistent and measurable endpoint for the
experiments.

## The Five-Condition Experiment

The most celebrated of Darwin's canary grass experiments involved five seedlings
grown under identical dark conditions and then subjected to different surgical or
barrier treatments before being exposed to the same directional light source:

**Condition 1: Untreated control.** The first seedling received no treatment at
all. It bent normally toward the light, confirming that the dark-room conditions
and light intensity were conducive to phototropism and establishing the baseline
response for all subsequent comparisons.

**Condition 2: Tip removed.** The second seedling had its coleoptile tip pruned
off with a sharp blade. Despite being exposed to the same light, it did not bend
at all. This demonstrated that the tip was essential for the phototropic response,
though this result alone was ambiguous because removing the tip could have damaged
the plant in ways unrelated to [[plant-vision-and-light-perception-mechanisms]].
