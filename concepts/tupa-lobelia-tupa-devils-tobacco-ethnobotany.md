# Tupa (Lobelia tupa)

Tupa, known scientifically as Lobelia tupa, is a tall, variable plant of
the high Andes, also called tabaco del diablo ("devil's tobacco") in
reference to its use as a smoking mixture. In Chile, the Mapuche Indians
smoke the dried leaves of this beautiful red-flowered plant for their
narcotic effects. Whether these effects are truly hallucinogenic has not yet
been established. The plant contains the alkaloid lobeline and several
derivatives of it. The same alkaloid occurs in some North American species
of Lobelia, especially L. inflata, known locally as Indian tobacco.

## Botanical Description

Lobelia tupa is a striking perennial plant native to the high Andes of Chile
and Peru. It is a tall, robust herbaceous plant that can reach considerable
height, with large, soft leaves and spectacular red flowers that make it one
of the most visually impressive members of its genus. The specific epithet
"tupa" derives from the indigenous Mapuche name for the plant, reflecting
its long history of use by the native peoples of central and southern Chile.

The plant belongs to the bluebell family, Campanulaceae (also spelled
Campanuloceae in older texts). This family includes approximately 300
species of Lobelia distributed across tropical and subtropical regions
worldwide, with some species extending into temperate zones. Many Lobelia
species are highly prized as garden ornamentals, valued for their attractive
flowers and often vibrant coloration. Despite its ornamental qualities,
L. tupa is also known for its toxicity and psychoactive properties.

## Mapuche Use: Smoking the Leaves

The Mapuche Indians of Chile smoke the dried leaves of Lobelia tupa for
their narcotic effects. The practice of smoking this plant rather than
preparing it as a tea or other oral preparation is noteworthy and is
reflected in the Spanish common name tabaco del diablo ("devil's tobacco").
The name likely arose from the plant's use as a tobacco substitute or
admixture, combined with the perception that its effects are more intense
or dangerous than those of ordinary tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum).

Smoking as a method of administration allows rapid absorption of active
compounds through the pulmonary mucosa, producing effects more quickly than
oral ingestion. This route of administration also permits precise titration
of dosage, as the user can stop smoking when the desired effects are
reached. The Mapuche practice of smoking tupa parallels other indigenous
smoking traditions in the Americas, including the use of Nicotiana species
and the smoking of various psychoactive plant mixtures.

## Chemistry: Lobeline and Derivatives

The principal alkaloid identified in Lobelia tupa is lobeline, along with
several derivatives of this compound. Lobeline is a piperidine alkaloid that
acts primarily as a nicotinic receptor agonist, though its pharmacological
profile is complex and not fully elucidated. Lobeline has been used
medicinally in various contexts, most notably as a smoking deterrent due to
its ability to partially substitute for nicotine while producing unpleasant
effects at higher doses.

The same alkaloid occurs in some North American species of Lobelia,
especially L. inflata, known locally as Indian tobacco. L. inflata was
widely used in Native American medicine and was adopted into the
Anglo-American pharmacopeia as a treatment for respiratory ailments,
including asthma and bronchitis. Lobeline was formerly included in
commercial preparations as an expectorant and respiratory stimulant. The
presence of lobeline in both South American and North American species of
Lobelia suggests that this alkaloid is a characteristic secondary metabolite
of the genus as a whole.

## Lobeline: Pharmacology and Effects

Lobeline's pharmacological action is complex. It acts on nicotinic
acetylcholine receptors but also affects dopamine, serotonin, and other
neurotransmitter systems. At low doses, lobeline produces stimulant effects
similar to nicotine, including increased heart rate, elevated blood
pressure, and heightened alertness. At higher doses, it can cause nausea,
vomiting, dizziness, and more serious toxic effects including respiratory
depression.

The narcotic effects described by the Mapuche likely result from the
combined action of lobeline and its derivatives present in Lobelia tupa. It
is possible that the South American species contains higher concentrations
of lobeline or additional alkaloid compounds not found in North American
species, which could account for the more pronounced psychoactive effects
reported by the Mapuche compared to the milder medicinal use of North
American Lobelia species.

## Is Tupa Truly Hallucinogenic?

Schultes notes that whether the effects of Lobelia tupa are truly
hallucinogenic has not yet been established. The classification of tupa as a
narcotic rather than a confirmed hallucinogen is significant. Lobeline and
its derivatives are not typically classified as hallucinogenic compounds,
and their primary pharmacological effects are on the autonomic nervous
system rather than on serotonin or other neurotransmitter systems associated
with classical hallucinogens.

It is possible that the Mapuche experience of smoking tupa includes sensory
alterations or distortions at higher doses that might be described as
hallucinatory, even if the primary pharmacological mechanism is not that of
a classical hallucinogen. Alternatively, the "narcotic" effects described
may primarily involve sedation, euphoria, or altered consciousness without
true hallucinations.

## The Genus Lobelia

The genus Lobelia comprises approximately 300 species distributed across
tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions worldwide. The genus is
remarkably diverse in growth form, including small annual herbs, perennial
herbaceous plants, and even large shrubs and small trees in tropical
montane environments. Many species are cultivated as ornamental plants for
their attractive flowers, which come in shades of blue, purple, red, pink,
and white.

Several Lobelia species have documented ethnobotanical uses beyond their
ornamental value. L. inflata (Indian tobacco) was widely used in Native
American medicine and entered the formal pharmacopeia. L. siphilitica was
used by Native Americans to treat syphilis. Various African and Asian
species have traditional medicinal applications as well, demonstrating that
the genus has a rich history of human use across multiple continents.

## Comparison with Other Andean Smoking Plants

The Mapuche use of Lobelia tupa as a smoking mixture places it within a
broader South American tradition of smoking psychoactive plants. Other
examples include the smoking of Nicotiana species (tobacco), the use of
Banisteriopsis bark in some Amazonian snuff preparations, and the smoking
of various Datura preparations. The practice of smoking psychoactive
substances is deeply rooted in indigenous American cultures and predates
European contact by many centuries.
