# Zone and Sector Planning

Zones and sectors are the organizing framework for placing elements on a permaculture property. Zones organize space by frequency of human use, while sectors map external energies (sun, wind, water, fire) that flow through the property. This system is foundational to [[permaculture-zones-and-sectors]] and [[zone-and-sector-analysis]].

## Zone 0: The House

The house is where the most time is spent, and everything branches outward from here. In permaculture design, the house is not separate from the garden -- it is part of the system. See [[permaculture-zone-0-house-design]] and [[faires-house-design-and-retrofit]].

Zone 0 includes the kitchen, [[comparison-passive-solar-vs-earth-sheltered]], greenhouse, trellises, and composting toilet. The house should integrate with Zone 1 through attached greenhouses, vine-covered walls, and direct garden access.

## Zone 1: Daily Intensive Care

Immediately surrounding the house. This is the area you walk through every day and should produce most of the food a family needs. City dwellers may have only 10 feet; rural people might have half an acre.

Contains: intensive sheet-mulched garden, small pond, outbuildings, dwarf fruit trees, low windbreak, greenhouse, shaderoom, herb spirals, salad beds, compost bin, clothesline, rabbit or quail pens.

Key design considerations: access paths, water sources, fencing and hedges, animal housing, irrigation, and drainage from paths. Fully controlled water via [[backyard-water-harvesting]] systems.

## Zone 2: Orchard and Small Livestock

The perimeter of Zone 1, slightly beyond the intensive gardens. Contains major crops (grains, potatoes), fruit and nut trees, compost heap, hedges, and trellising. Worms, rabbits, chickens, ducks, and fish in a small yard.

For suburban properties, Zone 2 may be as far as the land extends. On larger land, this zone includes goats and a milk cow. Animals can forage here once trees are established (chickens at 1-3 years, pigs at 3-7 years, sheep after 7 years, cattle after 15 years). See [[chicken-husbandry-in-permaculture]] and [[chicken-integration-permaculture-guide]].

## Zone 3: Forest Garden and Pasture

Less frequently visited. Contains larger unpruned orchards, windbreak trees, goat pens, beehives, living mulches, animal fodder, and firebreaks. Water managed through swales, small dams, and ditches. Barn and cash crops. Pastures, woodlots, larger livestock, grain fields, and ponds.

This zone requires less intensive management but still regular attention. Main crop areas and [[faires-hugelkultur-and-forest-garden-strategy]] elements thrive here.

## Zone 4: Forage and Woodlot

Visited occasionally. Long-term development zone: woodlot, dam, extensive tree planting. Windmills, large animal stock, large-scale water harvesting. Pigs work well in the forest zone, foraging on acorns and roots.

No mulches are applied -- hardy edible plants are foraged from edges. Contains grazing, timber, wild foraging, and watershed management. See [[faires-aquaculture-pond-systems]] for pond integration in this zone.

## Zone 5: Wild Ecosystem

Left completely unmanaged. Provides a genetic reservoir, wildlife habitat, reference ecosystem, and spiritual connection to wild nature. Wildlife corridors and forest growth are protected here.

In urban settings, Zone 5 might be as small as a back corner left to birds and bunnies. On rural properties, it can be extensive forest or wilderness preserve. This zone serves as a benchmark for understanding local ecology.

## Sector Mapping

Sectors map the external energies that cross your property regardless of zone boundaries:

- **Wind sector**: Wind direction and intensity. Identify prevailing winds for windbreak placement. Wind-shaped trees reveal prevailing patterns.
- **Water sector**: Rainfall amount and distribution. Map natural water flow, drainage, swampy or flooding areas, and [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]] opportunities.
- **Sun sector**: Sun direction and seasonal duration. Track summer and winter sun angles for [[passive-solar-design]].
- **Fire sector**: Fire danger directions. Assess wildfire risk and plan firebreaks accordingly.
- **Privacy sector**: Prying neighbors and noise. Views to preserve or block with plantings.
- **Topography sector**: Hills, slopes, cliffs, and rocks. Slopes affect water movement, erosion, and microclimates.
- **Wildlife sector**: Track animal movement corridors and habitat needs.

## The Six Rules of Design

1. **Appropriate scale** -- Elements should be sized to their function
2. **Right order** -- Establish infrastructure before planting
3. **Minimize wasted space** -- Edge effect increases productivity
4. **Edge effect** -- The interface between two ecosystems is most productive
5. **Simple species first** -- Start with hardy, easy species
6. **Appropriate patterns** -- Use natural patterns (spirals, branches, waves) as described in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]]

## Phase 1 Implementation

When starting, prioritize:
1. Access (roads, paths, gates)
2. Water (storage, irrigation, diversion)
3. Power (energy systems)

Place elements in relation to each other initially, crowding them more than ideal. They can be thinned later once relationships are observed. This iterative approach is part of the [[permaculture-design-process]].

## Connecting to Element Analysis

Zone and sector planning works hand-in-hand with [[faires-element-analysis-method]]. Once elements are analyzed for their inputs, characteristics, and outputs, they are placed in appropriate zones based on frequency of use. Sector analysis then refines placement by accounting for sun, wind, water, and other external factors. Together they form a complete [[permaculture-site-analysis]].
