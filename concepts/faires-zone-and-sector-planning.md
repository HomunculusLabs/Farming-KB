## Zone 1: Daily Intensive Care

Immediately surrounding the house. This is the area you walk through every day and should produce most of the food a family needs. City dwellers may have only 10 feet; rural people might have half an acre.

Contains: intensive sheet-mulched garden, small pond, outbuildings, dwarf fruit trees, low windbreak, greenhouse, shaderoom, herb spirals, salad beds, compost bin, clothesline, rabbit or quail pens.

Key design considerations: access paths, water sources, fencing and hedges, animal housing, irrigation, and drainage from paths. Fully controlled water via [[chicken-husbandry-in-permaculture]] and [[faires-hugelkultur-and-forest-garden-strategy]] elements thrive here.

## Zone 4: Forage and Woodlot

Visited occasionally. Long-term development zone: woodlot, dam, extensive tree planting. Windmills, large animal stock, large-scale water harvesting. Pigs work well in the forest zone, foraging on acorns and roots.

No mulches are applied -- hardy edible plants are foraged from edges. Contains grazing, timber, wild foraging, and watershed management. See [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]] opportunities.

## Phase 1 Implementation

When starting, prioritize:
1. Access (roads, paths, gates)
2. Water (storage, irrigation, diversion)
3. Power (energy systems)

Place elements in relation to each other initially, crowding them more than ideal. They can be thinned later once relationships are observed. This iterative approach is part of the [[faires-element-analysis-method]]. Once elements are analyzed for their inputs, characteristics, and outputs, they are placed in appropriate zones based on frequency of use. Sector analysis then refines placement by accounting for sun, wind, water, and other external factors. Together they form a complete [[permaculture-zones-in-practice]]
