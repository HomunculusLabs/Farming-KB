# Organic Rose Care and Growing

updated: 2026-04-18

There are many types of roses and their classification can be confusing. Species roses are the original ancestors of modern, hybrid roses and should be grown in the same way as other shrubs. The modern, hybrid roses can be grown as bushes or standards -- standard roses are simply bush roses budded onto a long stem to make a short tree. Many varieties of climbing and rambling roses are also available.

## Choosing Roses

With the wide variety of shapes and colors available, choosing roses can be difficult. Bush standards tend to be too formal for mixed borders in very small gardens, though "weeping standards" (rambler roses on a long stem with branches allowed to hang down) fit into a mixed border quite well.

Consider the following when selecting:
- Flower form (single, semi-double, fully double)
- Color (plan for combinations with surrounding plants)
- Scent (many modern hybrids have sacrificed scent for flower form)
- Disease resistance (some varieties are more resistant to mildew and black spot)
- Habit (bush, climbing, rambling, [[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-ground-cover-cultivation]], standard)

## Buying Roses

Roses can be bought bare root in the winter or container-grown at any time of the year. Check all plants for signs of mildew and black spot before buying. Bare root plants are generally more robust and establish better than container-grown plants.

## Planting

Unlike other shrubs, bush roses should be planted a little lower than they were grown at the nursery in order to encourage the growth of new shoots from below ground. About 1 inch (2.5cm) deeper is generally sufficient, but make sure that the point at which the variety is budded onto the rootstock is below ground. If you are planting roses in the fall, they should be pruned back hard immediately after planting.

### Planting Steps

1. Dig a hole large enough for the roots or root ball
2. Mix the dug-out soil with a bucketful of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] (well-rotted manure or compost)
3. If container-grown, remove the plastic container carefully without damaging roots
4. Water the plant well
5. Place in the hole, ensuring the bud union is below soil level
6. Sprinkle a little fine soil over the roots
7. Refill the hole and press the soil down
8. Mulch around the rose with a layer of organic matter to conserve moisture and inhibit weed growth

## Maintenance

### Feeding

Feed roses like other shrubs, mulching annually with well-rotted manure or compost. If no mulch is available, apply blood, fish, and bone meal in [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] and kelp meal every three years.

### Watering

Water well in the first year after planting. In subsequent years ensure the roots do not dry out in [[stamets-cultivator-agaricus-bitorquis-warm-weather-cultivation]]. Deep soaking at the base is better than frequent light sprinkling.

### Pruning

Pruning is carried out every year on hybrid bush roses in early spring, just before the bushes start into their [[cho-plant-growth-stage-treatment-protocols]], as you will be able to assess the amount of frost damage and cut it out.

The principle of pruning is that the harder you cut back, the stronger the branch will grow. Get into the habit of always cutting back weak branches further than the stronger-growing ones to balance the bush. Weak branches should be pruned to leave one or two buds while stronger ones can have three or four.

Cut all shoots back to an outward- or downward-facing bud to encourage them to grow outward, away from the center of the bush.

Bush standards are pruned just like the ordinary bush hybrids, while weeping standards are pruned after flowering, merely removing very old, diseased, or overcrowded wood.

### Removing Flower Heads

Removing the flowers of continuous-flowering varieties as they fade will ensure a supply of blooms all the way through summer and fall and often into early winter too. Break the stem about half an inch (1cm) below the seedhead with your thumb and forefinger.

### Suckers

Rose varieties are usually grown by being grafted onto a rootstock. Occasionally the rootstock will send out a vigorous shoot, known as a sucker, which has no ornamental merit. These are easy to spot as they are light green and are usually covered with more thorns than the variety. If allowed to develop, suckers will sap the energy of the variety so they must be removed.

To remove a sucker: scrape away a little soil where the sucker arises from the root, and pull it off if you can. If not, cut it as near to the main stem as possible.

### Pests and Diseases

Check all roses regularly for signs of pest or disease attack, particularly:
- **Mildew**: White powdery coating on leaves and stems; improve [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]] and avoid overhead watering
- **Black spot**: Black spots on leaves with yellowing; remove affected leaves and mulch well
- **Greenfly (aphids)**: Colonies on new growth; encourage ladybirds and other predators; spray with insecticidal soap if severe

Rugosa roses are particularly disease-resistant and make excellent hedging plants, forming a dense, wind-resistant barrier with attractive orange hips in fall.
