# Fukuoka's Critique of Inductive and Deductive Reasoning in Agriculture

Masanobu Fukuoka's *The Natural Way of Farming* mounts a systematic philosophical attack on the foundations of scientific agriculture, targeting the epistemological methods of inductive and deductive reasoning that underlie all agricultural research. He proposes intuitive reasoning, rooted in direct observation of nature without preconceptions, as the only path to understanding true agriculture.

## The Limits of Inductive Experimentation

Fukuoka illustrates the failure of inductive reasoning through the example of livestock feed research. Scientific farming gives various formulations to cows chained in a barn, representing a certain set of environmental conditions, and judges the mixture producing the best results to be superior. From this, it draws conclusions about cattle feed that it believes to be the truth.

The problem is that this approach produces only conditional truth, not absolute truth. The conclusions are valid only within the specific experimental conditions and cannot be generalized. When the temperature, humidity, or any other variable changes, the conclusions may no longer hold.

Fukuoka contrasts this with natural farming, which begins by examining the cow from a standpoint that disregards environmental conditions. It asks how the cow lives in open nature, taking a broader perspective on how a cow is born and grows. By paying too much attention to what the cow feeds on, we lose a broader understanding of how it lives and what its needs are. More is required to sustain life than just food. Nor are problems of sustenance resolved by food alone. Many other factors relate to life: weather, climate, living environment, exercise, sleep, and more.

## The Deductive Trap: Premises That Lead Astray

Deductive reasoning in agriculture begins with premises drawn from inductive experiments and extends these to new situations. Fukuoka argues that this compounds the errors of induction because the premises themselves are flawed. When a scientist plans to cultivate high-yielding rice on a field, he sees the weeds growing at his feet only as pests that will rob sunlight and nutrients. This premise leads him to the conclusion that total weed eradication will maximize yields, when in fact the opposite is true.

The very notion that hot and cold exist in nature is, for Fukuoka, a false premise. Although high and low temperatures exist, the notions of hot and cold do not. One will never be wrong in starting with the assumption that the temperature and humidity are everywhere and at all times just right. Although cattle, horses, pigs, sheep, chickens, and ducks all know heat and cold, with Japan's temperate climate there was never a need to worry about whether summer heat or winter cold was good or bad for raising animals.

## Conditional Truth vs. Unconditional Truth

Fukuoka draws a sharp distinction between two types of truth:

**Conditional truth** is what scientific farming produces. It depends on specific experimental conditions, assumptions about time and space, and the prior framework of the researcher. It is always subject to revision and cannot be generalized beyond its narrow parameters.

**Unconditional truth** is what natural farming seeks. It is found through experiments free of conditions, assumptions, and notions of time and space. It represents the laws and principles in force at the true source of nature.

Scientific farming is content to think of conditional truth as the truth, but natural farming makes every effort to discard all premises and conditions and seek out a truth without conditions. Unchanging truths can be found only through experiments free of conditions, assumptions, and notions of time and space.

## Intuitive Reasoning: The Third Way

Fukuoka proposes intuitive reasoning as the path beyond both induction and deduction. This approach involves:

1. Rejecting all assumptions, knowledge, and action, not thinking, not seeing, not doing.
2. Following closely on nature's heels, observing without preconceptions.
3. Recognizing that one thing is all things. To resolve one matter, one must resolve all matters. Changing one thing changes all things.
4. Understanding that nature is God.

This is not mysticism in the ordinary sense but a rigorous discipline of perception. Fukuoka arrived at his farming methods not through experimentation but through what he calls intuitive reasoning. Once he decided to sow rice in the fall, he found he could also stop transplanting, plowing, applying chemical fertilizers, preparing compost, and spraying pesticides. This was not a series of independent discoveries but a single intuitive insight that revealed the interconnected nature of all farming practices.

## The Holistic Principle: One Thing Is All Things

Central to Fukuoka's epistemology is the principle that all aspects of agriculture are organically interrelated. In rice cultivation, preparing the field, sowing the seed, tilling, covering the seed with soil, fertilizing, weeding, and disease and pest control are all organically interrelated. No problem in any one area is truly solved unless a common solution is found for all areas.

Scientific farming divides rice cultivation into narrow specialties: experts on germination attend to problems of seed germination, specialists in tillage address tilling problems, and likewise with seeding, transplanting, and other areas. Solving these independently is totally meaningless. Natural farming treats everything as part of a whole.

## The Illusion of Measurable Progress

Fukuoka argues that scientific agriculture gives the appearance of progress while actually destroying the foundation on which agriculture depends. The post-season cultivation program in Kochi Prefecture, which represented the most advanced agricultural technology of its time, succeeded only in preventing further losses. This demonstrated that since the purpose of scientific agriculture is always and everywhere convenience to man, no matter how large and complete the technology amassed, it will never amount to more than a temporary expedient.

The incident taught Fukuoka not to rely on human action and strengthened his resolve to move toward a natural way of farming. Even in the selection of a cultivar, scientific farming chose a variety suited to an artificially chosen period. The cultivar selected was merely one that would not reduce yields when planted in July; in no way was it capable of positively raising yields.

## The Problem of Causal Attribution

Fukuoka repeatedly warns against arbitrarily settling upon causal relationships based on subjective human notions. When the first plant dies in a land turning to desert, it may have been triggered by a microorganism. But botanists rarely study microorganisms because they lie outside their field of specialization. The chain of interwoven events and factors is beyond man's comprehension.

Similarly, when farmers in Kochi concentrated all efforts on eradicating the visible rice borer pest, they doubled back before finding the true cause. The connection between rice stubble and chaotic planting practices was never clearly established, and the deeper reasons why yellow rice borers were numerous in the region remained uninvestigated. There must have been other reasons why the yellow rice borer, rice stem borer, and other insect pests were so numerous in Kochi Prefecture. The cause had less to do with the environment than with poor methods of cultivating rice.

## The Maze of Relative Subjectivity

Fukuoka extends his critique to the fundamental nature of human perception. Even nature stripped of all human action and influence is still only nature as seen through man's relativity, a nature clothed in the subjective notions of man. To follow the path of natural farming, one must tear the robes of human action from nature and remove the innermost garments of subjectivity.

The scientific researcher always takes a single subject and applies a number of variable conditions to it while making some prior assumption about the results. Natural farming, however, pushes aside all conditions, and knocking away the precepts from which science operates, strives to find the laws and principles in force at the true source.

This is why Fukuoka repeatedly emphasizes that the first thing a farmer must do is discard all preconceptions. Before looking at a pest or a weed or a disease, the farmer must set aside the assumption that these things are problems. Only by approaching nature without the filter of human judgment can the true order of nature be perceived.

## The Nondiscriminating Mind

Fukuoka links his epistemology to Buddhist concepts of nondiscriminating awareness. The child who has not yet learned to distinguish between crops and weeds, between beneficial and harmful insects, between beautiful and ugly plants, perceives nature more accurately than the trained agricultural scientist. The process of education and specialization progressively narrows perception until the expert can see only the small fragment of nature that falls within his specialty.

Fukuoka tells the parable of Okuninushi's sack, in which the god of the land carries a sack that contains all the knowledge of nature. But when a specialist reaches into the sack, he pulls out only one small piece and declares it to be the whole truth. This is the pitfall of all specialized knowledge: it mistakes a fragment for the whole.

## The Three Ways of Knowing

Fukuoka categorizes all approaches to agriculture into three fundamental ways of knowing:

1. **The discriminative way of scientific farming**, which breaks nature into components, studies each in isolation, and attempts to reassemble the knowledge into techniques. This produces conditional truth that is always limited by its experimental parameters.

2. **The intuitive way of natural farming**, which apprehends nature as a whole through direct observation without preconceptions. This leads toward unconditional truth but requires the discipline of setting aside all assumptions.

3. **The way of ignorance**, which is the recognition that human knowledge is inherently limited and that the wisest course is often to do nothing. This is not passive resignation but active trust in nature's self-organizing capacity.

The path from the first to the second passes through the third. Only by recognizing the limitations of discriminative knowledge can one begin to perceive nature directly. Only by accepting ignorance can one discover what nature already knows.

## Implications for Agricultural Research

Fukuoka's epistemological critique has profound implications:

1. Research that isolates variables inevitably misses the holistic nature of agricultural systems.
2. Experiments that control for conditions produce only conditional truth, not universal principles.
3. The farmer who observes nature directly without preconceptions gains more practical knowledge than the researcher who designs controlled experiments.
4. The complexity of natural systems means that any intervention, however well-intentioned, produces consequences that cannot be fully predicted or controlled.
5. The only sustainable path is to work within nature's patterns rather than trying to impose human designs upon them.
6. The goal of agricultural research should be to understand nature's integrated systems, not to extract techniques that can be applied in isolation.
7. The scientist who believes he has discovered a universal law of agriculture has merely found a pattern that holds under a specific set of conditions.

## The Limits of Scientific Method in Understanding Nature

Fukuoka's philosophy of natural farming rests on a profound epistemological critique: both inductive and deductive reasoning, the twin pillars of the scientific method, are fundamentally incapable of grasping the true nature of nature. While scientists proceed by gathering data through observation (induction) and then formulating general laws or theories (deduction), Fukuoka argues that this entire process operates within a framework of "discriminating knowledge" that fractures the organic wholeness of living systems.

The scientific method demands that the investigator stand apart from the object of study, dissect it into component parts, and analyze each part in isolation. This approach has produced extraordinary technological achievements, but Fukuoka insists it has also produced a civilization that is fundamentally insane — one that destroys the very biosphere upon which it depends while congratulating itself on its mastery over nature. He writes that whether one views the modern world as insane may serve as a criterion of one's own sanity.

## Inductive Reasoning: The Accumulation of Error

Inductive reasoning begins with specific observations and builds toward general principles. A scientist observes that applying nitrogen fertilizer increases rice yields, repeats the experiment across multiple locations, and concludes that nitrogen is a limiting factor in rice production. This seems reasonable, but Fukuoka points out several fatal flaws in this approach.

First, the act of observation itself changes what is being observed. When a scientist clears a field, removes all organic matter, kills the soil microbiome through repeated tillage, and then discovers that fertilizer improves yields in this dead medium, he is not learning about rice or soil — he is learning about the consequences of his own destructive interventions. The "discovery" that fertilizer helps plants grow is only meaningful in the artificial conditions the scientist himself has created.

Second, inductive reasoning can never achieve certainty. No matter how many times an experiment is repeated, the scientist can never observe all possible conditions. The conclusion that nitrogen increases yields is valid only within the narrow parameters of the experimental design. When these results are generalized into agricultural policy applied across millions of acres, the accumulated errors of induction compound into ecological catastrophe.

Third, and most fundamentally for Fukuoka, inductive reasoning treats nature as a collection of separate, measurable phenomena rather than as an indivisible whole. The scientist who studies nitrogen uptake in rice roots, the one who studies soil microbiology, and the one who studies pest ecology each work in isolation, producing fragmented knowledge that can never be reassembled into a true understanding of the living system.
