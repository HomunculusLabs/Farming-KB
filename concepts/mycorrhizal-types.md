# Mycorrhizal Types

Mycorrhizal fungi are divided into several categories based on how they associate with plant roots. Understanding these types is essential for choosing the right management practices. See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for the practical overview and [[mycorrhizal-networks]] for how networks function.

## Endomycorrhizae (Penetrate Root Cells)

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF)
The most common and agriculturally important type. Associated with ~70% of all terrestrial plants.

- **Phylum:** Glomeromycota (~230 species, but colonize 400,000+ plant species)
- **Hosts:** Most vegetables, annuals, grasses, legumes, shrubs, many fruit trees
- **Mechanism:** Hyphae penetrate root cell walls (not the plasma membrane), forming arbuscules (tree-like exchange structures) inside root cells
- **Also form:** Vesicles (storage organs inside roots) and extraradical hyphae extending into soil
- **Reproduction:** Large spores (50-1000 micrometers), do not produce mushrooms
- **Key nutrient:** Phosphorus — provide plants up to 4x more P than roots alone
- **Signaling:** Plant releases strigolactones (sesquiterpene hormones) when soil P is low, attracting fungal hyphae. Fungi respond with lipo-chitooligosaccharide (Myc factors) — same chemical family as rhizobia Nod factors. Both nodulation and mycorrhizae use similar chemicals and common genes
- **Spore viability:** Spores have ~7-10 days to reach a root before internal carbon supply runs out
- **Network capacity:** Extraradical hyphae enable a plant to access 100,000x more soil volume than roots alone
- **Nutrient transporters:** High-affinity transporter proteins in the interfacial apoplast (acidic H+ environment) move phosphate, ammonium (NH4+), nitrate (NO3-), and H+-ATPases. The plant membrane controls what enters — full plant control over nutrient transfer
- **Special compound:** Produce [[glomalin]], contributing 27-30% of soil carbon
- **Carbon exchange:** Plants provide ~20% of photosynthate to fungi; fungi convert to lipids for transport
- **Signal:** Plants release strigolactones to attract fungi; fungi release Myc factors (lipo-chitooligosaccharides) to prevent plant defense response
- **Relevant to cannabis:** This is the mycorrhizal type used by cannabis/hemp

### Ericoid Mycorrhizae
- **Hosts:** Ericaceae family — rhododendrons, azaleas, blueberries, cranberries (~5% of terrestrial plants)
- **Fungal partners:** Ascomycetes
- **Mechanism:** Hyphae penetrate cortical cells forming dense coils (pelotons)
- **Specialty:** Obtain nitrogen from organic matter in acidic, peat-rich soils; produce strong acids that break down organic matter
- **Note:** Acidify soil, helping plants survive in low-pH conditions
- **Commercial inoculation:** Difficult — use unsterilized peat from colonized plant root zones

### Orchid Mycorrhizae
- **Hosts:** Orchidaceae (~10% of Earth's plant species)
- **Fungal partners:** Basidiomycota
- **Mechanism:** Hyphae form pelotons in root cortex, absorbed by plant after a few days
- **Critical role:** Orchid seeds cannot germinate without mycorrhizal support — seeds lack stored nutrients
- **Some orchids:** Fully heterotrophic (no chlorophyll), relying entirely on fungi for life

## Ectomycorrhizae (Surround Root, Don't Penetrate)

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (ECM)
- **Evolved:** ~250 million years ago
- **Hosts:** Woody plants — pines, oaks, beeches, birches, myrtles (~5% of terrestrial plants but 25,000+ tree species)
- **Fungal partners:** Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes (several thousand species)
- **Mechanism:** Form dense mantle around roots + Hartig net between cortical cells
- **Features:** Often produce mushrooms (porcini, chanterelles, truffles, fly agaric)
- **Prefer:** Acidic soils with low pH
- **Rhizomorphs:** Form hollow transport structures resembling roots
- **Soil pH preference:** More acidophilic than endomycorrhizal fungi

## Specialized Types

### Arbutoid Mycorrhizae
- **Hosts:** Madrone (Arbutus menziesii), manzanitas, bearberries (Arctostaphylos)
- **Fungal partners:** Basidiomycota
- **Mechanism:** Mantle + Hartig net, sometimes penetrating cell walls (like endomycorrhizae)

### Monotropoid Mycorrhizae
- **Hosts:** Indian pipes (Monotropa), other non-chlorophyllous plants
- **Mechanism:** Fungi form ectomycorrhizae with trees, then transfer tree carbon to the non-photosynthetic host
- **Note:** All host plants lack chlorophyll

### Ectendomycorrhizae
- **Hosts:** Some pine seedlings, especially after forest fires
- **Mechanism:** Characteristics of both endo- and ectomycorrhizae; thin mantle + some cell penetration

### Sebacinoid Mycorrhizae
- **Fungal partners:** Basidiomycetes in order Sebacinales
- **Note:** Very diverse — can form endo-, ecto-, ericoid, or orchid mycorrhizae depending on host
- **Research interest:** Piriformospora indica (discovered in Indian desert soils) colonizes many crops and confers disease resistance

## Multi-Mycorrhizae

Some plants (poplars, alders, eucalyptus) can form both ecto- and arbuscular mycorrhizae simultaneously or switch between them depending on conditions.

## Plants Without Mycorrhizae

These families do not form mycorrhizae and have alternative strategies:
- **Brassicaceae:** Mustards, kale, cabbage — extensive root systems with fine root hairs
- **Chenopodiaceae:** Amaranths, goosefoot — quick-growing roots
- **Polygonaceae:** Knotweed, buckwheat — some release allelopathic chemicals that inhibit mycorrhizal fungi
- **Proteaceae:** Proteas, lupines — cluster roots with phosphate-solubilizing bacteria
- **Cyperaceae:** Sedges — dauciform roots covered in root hairs
