# Fungal Species Abundance and Diversity Indices

Quantifying [[fungal-biodiversity]] requires standardized approaches to
measuring species richness, diversity, and evenness. These quantitative
indices provide the foundation for comparing [[biodiversity-fungi-soil-fungal-communities]] across
habitats, assessing the impact of environmental disturbances, and
tracking changes [[staycare-surfactant-enhanced-bioavailability-in-fungal-bioremediation]] diversity over time. The principles and
methods discussed here apply broadly to both macrofungi and microfungi.

## Species Richness

Species richness is the most widely used parameter for evaluating
[[biodiversity-fungal-biodiversity-estimation-methods]], defined as an enumeration of species in a
particular sample, area, habitat, or substratum. Three kinds of species
richness can be distinguished:

- **Numerical species richness**: species where biomass or number
  of individuals has been standardized.
- **Species density**: species where area, volume, or weight of
  the sampling unit has been standardized.
- **Total species richness**: cumulative species from a series
  of samples from a habitat or substratum.

Environmental variation effects on species density and numerical
species richness are scale-dependent. Most ecological studies of
fungi cover species densities as a consequence of sampling design.

## Species-Effort Relationships and Collector's Curves

The total number of species identified increases with sampling effort
but eventually attains an asymptote. Three mathematical relations
describe how total species richness (S) increases with effort (A):

- **Logistic model**: S = B/(C + A)-z. Approaches an asymptote,
  most appropriate when the domain is circumscribed geographically.
- **Power model**: S = CAz. Increases monotonically without
  asymptote; appropriate for heterogeneous landscapes.
- **Exponential model**: S = C + z ln A. Also increases
  monotonically; figures in island biogeography theory.

The value of S at the logistic asymptote is the best estimate of true
species richness. Comparisons at effort levels below the asymptote can
lead to spurious conclusions.

## Rarefaction

When sample sizes are unequal, rarefaction (Magurran 1988) facilitates
comparison of species richness among areas as if based on a
standardized sample size. The expected number of species E(S) in a
rarified sample of n individuals is calculated from total individuals
(N) and number per species (Ni). Rarefaction curves plotting cumulative
species against sampling effort are essential for evaluating sampling
completeness.

## Diversity Indices

Diversity combines two distinct attributes: species richness and
species evenness (equitable distribution of individuals among species).

### Simpson's Index

Simpson (1949) proposed the first diversity index used in ecology.
It varies from 0 to 1 as a dominance measure, influenced strongly by
the abundance [[allegro-star-of-the-morning-venus-and-the-sacred-mushroom]] most common species. The reciprocal form (1/D)
ensures the index increases with diversity.

### Shannon Index

The Shannon Index (H') is the most popular diversity index in community
ecology. It measures the average uncertainty in predicting the identity
of a randomly chosen individual. H' equals zero only with a single
species and reaches its maximum when all species are equally abundant.
Values are usually between 1.5 and 3.5, rarely exceeding 4.5.

### Other Indices

Additional useful indices include:
- **McIntosh's index (U)**: reflects Euclidean distance [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] sample
  point from the origin of an S-dimensional hypervolume. Good
  discriminant ability but influenced by sample size.
- **Berger-Parker index (d)**: reflects proportional importance of
  the most abundant species. Poor discriminant ability.
- **Fisher's log-series alpha**: first mathematical model of species-
  abundance relationships. Good discriminant ability, low sensitivity
  to sample size, but unaffected by evenness distribution.
- **Hill's numbers (N0, N1, N2)**: effective numbers of species.
  N0 = total species; N1 = number of abundant species (e to H');
  N2 = number of very abundant species (1/D). Most interpretable.

## Evenness Indices

Each diversity index has a corresponding evenness index measuring the
degree to which a community evinces maximal diversity possible given the
observed richness: e = Diversity(observed) / Diversity(max), where
Dmax is calculated with each taxon assuming 1/S of total abundance.
The Shannon evenness index (E = H'/ln S) and Hill's evenness
(E = N2/N1) are widely used.

## Species-Abundance Distributions

Species-abundance models provide the most complete [[staycare-ecotoxicological-assessment-of-fungal-bioremediation]]
diversity by using all available data. Four main models are recognized:

- **Geometric series**: abundances proportional to resources utilized;
  dominant species preempts the largest resource portion. Associated
  with species-poor, nonequilibrial habitats.
- **Logarithmic series**: governed by one or a few factors; propagules
  arrive at random intervals. Describes small assemblages in
  nonequilibrial conditions.
- **Lognormal distribution**: governed by many independent factors;
  characterizes large, varied, equilibrial communities.
- **Broken-stick model**: random division of resources along a continuum.
  Represents small samples of related organisms with stable populations
  and long life cycles.

Rank-abundance plots are the usual graphic method for presenting
species-abundance data. Each model corresponds to specific biological
scenarios, making species-abundance distributions valuable tools for
inferring ecological processes from diversity data.

## Alpha, Beta, and Gamma Diversity

Whittaker's framework distinguishes three scales of diversity: alpha
(within-habitat), beta (between-habitat turnover), and gamma (regional
diversity across multiple habitats). [[beta-diversity-root-soil-compartments-cannabis]] can be measured
using binary data, such as the Whittaker index. These concepts are
fundamental to understanding how fungal communities vary across
[[biodiversity-of-fungi-biodiversity-patterns-ecosystems]].

## Practical Recommendations

When assessing [[biodiversity-of-fungi-biodiversity-patterns-ecosystems]], investigators should:
define the scale and attribute of richness being evaluated; generate
collector's curves to determine adequate sample sizes; use rarefaction
for unequal sample comparisons; select diversity indices appropriate to
the question and data; and complement index-based approaches with
species-abundance distribution analysis.
