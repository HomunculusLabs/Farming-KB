# Pattern Understanding in Permaculture

Pattern understanding is the ability to read the physical
and behavioral patterns that exist in nature and human
systems, enabling designers to create coherent designs
that work with natural forces rather than against them.

## Designing from Patterns to Details

As Permaculture Principle 4 says, design from patterns to
details. Nature works on a vast array of simple patterns
which result in highly complex systems. For example, DNA is
based on very simple patterns of four nucleotides that
combine in simple ways, giving rise to a double helix and
all the complexity of life. Simple patterns are the stuff
of nature. A permaculture designer must be able to read the
patterns that exist in nature to both understand existing
systems and design systems of their own.

## Physical and Behavioral Patterns

In nature there are two types of patterns. Physical patterns
are how things are shaped, such as trees growing in a branch
pattern. Behavioral patterns are how things behave under
given conditions, such as when the temperature drops below
a certain level the tree drops its leaves and grows them
back when it warms.

A freeway system is based on a physical pattern that fits
the parameters of cars traveling at 60 mph. Rush hour on
the same freeway is a behavioral pattern. When does rain
fall, how does water flow down a hill, how do plants grow
in shade, what plants attract birds, mating seasons,
migrations: these are all patterns. The ecology of Earth is
one vast interaction of material and behavioral patterns.

## Reading Patterns

A permaculture designer must learn to read patterns.
Principle 6 calls for us to observe and interact, both parts
are necessary to properly read patterns. Observation allows
you to see the pattern, and interaction allows you to
understand how the pattern changes in response to external
stimulus. If you do X to the existing patterns, what new
patterns emerge on the site, what happens to the energy of
the old patterns?

## Creating a Pattern Language

A designer not only reads patterns but learns to create a
pattern language to aid in the design process. By creating
a pattern language a designer can make simple, small patterns
that when combined can create a larger more complex design.
The pattern language becomes like building with legos, taking
simple repeating patterns and connecting them together to
create larger intricate structures. In permaculture design
the legos will be ecological.

## Applying Pattern Language to Design

By reading the patterns of a site and creating a pattern
language a designer can figure out how design elements can
be inserted into the existing site in a way that is coherent
with the permaculture principles. For example, to catch and
store energy one has to understand the patterns of energy on
the site, and what physical and behavioral patterns will
best catch and store that energy.

Reading patterns and determining the proper pattern language
may enable a design to produce the greatest yield. If you
want to creatively use and respond to change it would help
to understand what patterns exist and what they can be
changed into with the energy available on the site.

## Patterns and Feedback

When a designer does not understand the patterns on their
site, waste will almost always be the result. Observing the
effects that your designs have on existing patterns on the
site may be one of the best methods of obtaining feedback.
If your design disrupts the patterns on your site in
unexpected ways, that is likely the feedback that your
design is not compatible with the ecology of your site.

## The Pattern Language as Design Tool

To design it is recommended that the designer create a
pattern language to help them design. For example a designer
may conclude that an average person requires a six by six
foot garden space and a five hundred square foot structure
facing south. You then plug in the number of people and
that tells you how much total garden space and structure
your design should have. Of course the elements of this new
pattern language must mesh into the existing patterns of the
site. Design interventions will alter the patterns of a site,
and the designer must ensure new patterns maintain ecological
harmony.

## Patterns in Human Systems

Humans, being products of nature, are themselves ruled by
patterns. Even the artificial systems of humanity form
patterns: when do you go to work, how often does your car
need gas, what games do children play, all patterns. A
permaculture designer who understands pattern understanding
can read and design for human behavioral patterns just as
effectively as natural ones, creating designs that work with
the rhythms of daily life rather than against them.

## Recommended Reading

A Pattern Language: Towns, Buildings, Construction by
Christopher Alexander, Sara Ishikawa, and Murray Silverstein
is the foundational text on pattern language as a design
methodology. While focused on architecture and urban design,
its principles apply directly to permaculture design and are
essential reading for any serious student of ecological
design patterns.
