# Phoenicoid Fungi — Fire-Associated Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes

Phoenicoid fungi (also called pyrophilous, anthracophilous, or
carbonicolous fungi) are a specialized group of primarily ascomycetes and
basidiomycetes that fruit on soil, plant material, and burned wood
following forest fires, prairie fires, or fireplace use. They also appear
on steam-sterilized soil in greenhouses.

## Ecology

### Why Fire Promotes Fruiting

- Forest fires cause 3-5 unit pH increase from ash deposition
- Soil heating may stimulate dormant propagules
- Elimination of competitors frees resources
- Changes in soil chemistry following combustion

### Abiotic Factors

- Soil heating stimulates some species to fruit
- Changes in soil pH after fire trigger others
- Sporocarp production in undisturbed forest soils can be stimulated by
  lime application (mimicking ash effects)

## Fruiting Groups After Fire (Dix and Webster 1995)

### Group 1: Early Fruiting (7-80 weeks)

- **Pyronema species**
- **Trichophacea abundans**
- Appear as early as 7 weeks post-fire
- No later than 80 weeks

### Group 2: Intermediate Fruiting (10-190 weeks)

- Ascobolous carbonarius
- Peziza trachycarpa, P. praetervisa
- Rhizinia undulata
- Tephrocybe carbonaria
- Begin 10-15 weeks after burn; may continue up to 190 weeks

### Group 3: Late Fruiting (20-200 weeks)

- Peziza endocarpoides
- Trichophaea hemisphaerioides
- Appear 20-50 weeks post-fire; continue 130-200 weeks

### Group 4: Latest Fruiting (50-150+ weeks)

- Myxomphalia maura
- Neotiella hetieri
- Ripartites tricholoma
- Include apothecial forms associated with moss carpet development
- Generally not until 50+ weeks post-fire

## Habitat and Geographic Patterns

### Moist Tropics and Subtropics

- Pyrenomycetes dominate phoenicoid communities
- Common on charred vegetation

### Temperate Forests

- Operculate discomycetes (Pezizales) dominate
- A few agarics also present

### Prairie Ecosystems

- Pyrenomycetes are dominant phoenicoid group

## Mount St. Helens Case Study

### Six Weeks Post-Eruption (1980)

- Anthracobia melaloma apothecia appeared within tephra
- Gelasinospora reticulospora perithecia present

### One Year Post-Eruption

Large discomycetes and agarics common:

- Peziza species
- Rhizinia undulata
- Trichophaea hemisphaerioides
- Coprinus plicatilis
- Pholiota carbonaria
- Psathyrella carbonicola

## Cultivation and Study Methods

Phoenicoid fungi present unique challenges for cultivation and laboratory
study due to their specialized ecological niche and dependence on fire-
modified substrates.

### Simulating Fire Conditions

Researchers have developed several methods to induce fruiting of
phoenicoid fungi without actual fire. The most reliable approach is
autoclaving or steam-sterilizing natural soil samples, which mimics the
heat and moisture conditions of a fire event. Adding wood ash or lime to
sterilized soil can further simulate the pH changes caused by fire.

Some species, particularly Pyronema omphalodes, fruit readily on
heat-treated soil in laboratory culture. Others require more specific
conditions, such as charred wood substrates or specific mineral
supplements. [[thermophilic-fungi]] share some of these heat-adapted
traits, and comparative studies of both groups illuminate the role of
temperature in fungal ecology.

### Collection and Documentation

[[psilocybin-mushroom-field-collection-techniques]] of phoenicoid fungi requires visiting burned sites at
regular intervals. The [[macrofungal-sampling-design-plots-transects]]
methodology can be adapted for burn sites by establishing permanent
transects across the burn perimeter and into unburned control areas.

Specimens should be collected with substrate (charcoal, soil, or wood)
attached. Note the time since fire, burn severity, and dominant
vegetation. Photograph the fruiting habit and habitat. Voucher specimens
should be dried and deposited in herbaria, as many phoenicoid species are
poorly represented in collections.

### Molecular Identification

Many phoenicoid fungi are difficult to identify morphologically. DNA
barcoding using ITS regions has become essential for accurate species
determination. Researchers studying
[[biodiversity-of-fungi-soil-fungal-communities-agriculture]] have found
that metabarcoding of soil samples from burn sites reveals a much higher
diversity of fire-associated fungi than fruiting body surveys alone.

implications for conservation and fire management policy.
