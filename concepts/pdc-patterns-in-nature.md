# PDC [[permaculture-pattern-literacy]]s and Edge Effect

In the 1981 [[permaculture-introduction-and-definitions]] Design Course, Bill permaculture-design|Mollison devotes substantial attention to natural patterns and the concept of "edge" as a fundamental design tool. This material appears primarily in Pamphlet II (Humid Landscapes) and represents some of Mollison's most original theoretical contributions.

## Defining Edge

"Where things join, there are edges. Those edges can be more or less complex." Edge includes:
- The steel-strong film between water and air
- The zone around a soil particle where water bonds with force
- Shorelines between land and water
- The interface between forest and grassland
- The scrub transitional zone
- The border between frost and non-frost on a hillside
- The border of the desert

## The Edge Effect

"Everything I know of pauses there. I have never seen anybody with any sensibility walk straight from the forest onto the plain." Edge creates a pause zone where species from both systems mingle plus species unique to the edge itself.

"In natural systems we find within edge a kind of species or productivity that sharply increases, and potential interactions increase. Therefore the energy flux is greater through the edge."

Examples of edge richness:
- Tidal marshes
- Mangrove systems
- Barrier reefs
- Light edges at forest margins

## The Problem with Agricultural Science

"All good agricultural scientists never gather samples from the edge. They will discard the first two meters and walk into the crop and sample within crop. Why? Because edge figures are often biased toward much higher production." Scientists treat edge as an anomaly rather than a principle to exploit.

"It works on a per-acre basis" -- the scientific mind reduces everything to uniform units, missing the productivity boost that edge provides.

## Pitjantjatjara Pattern Revelation

Mollison describes his discovery of edge harmonics through Aboriginal Australian women:

"What the women were doing, and what they knew they were doing, was to describe aspects of desert ecology in terms of story and myth. These are ecological maps. They are accurate -- the spacings are precise. One woman talking to another woman a great distance away can send her to a single stone and a single salt pan at a distance, to a place where the other woman had never been and she will accurately locate it."

Only when he went up in a light plane and saw these patterns from above did he make the connection: "the patterns the Pitjantjatjara women are making are the patterns of the desert."

## Edge Harmonics

Dennis McCarthy, a mathematician traveling with Mollison, identified the concept as "edge harmonics." Mollison explains:

"Every time we take an area from here and put it there, moving in an even harmonic about that edge, though we haven't altered the area, we have enormously increased the edge. So, if you like, without taking any cows out of the paddock, we can induce an enormous yield at the edge of the paddock."

"Without reducing the oxygen surface of the pond, we can give it as many times more edge as we care to do."

## The Box-Thorn Experiment

Mollison tested edge harmonics on his own land with box-thorn clumps: "Going from a 35-pace diameter clump, I ended up with 286 paces of edge, and I still had the clump practically intact." He then planted the edge and "found that I had also designed a fantastic number of climates. I had cold winds there, and I had hot, salty winds here. I had shaded, dry, cold, salty, hot, and wet areas. I had an enormous amount of potential within that box-thorn clump."

## Designing with Edge

### Ponds
A circular pond has minimal edge for its area. By indenting and elaborating the shoreline, edge increases enormously while water surface area remains the same. More edge means more habitat for fish, frogs, water plants, and birds.

### Gardens
The suburban pattern -- house surrounded by lawn -- has very little edge. By "bringing the whole design forward in front of his house, and it has spilled out onto the council strip" with beans and peas and vines, one example created "a real Eden in this desert of status."

### Fencing
"You can't run a continuous fence out and put your clamps on it. You have to break your fence lines up with little fragments of planting." This principle of edge enhancement applies throughout design.

## Natural Pattern Observation

Mollison urges designers to observe natural patterns directly:

"When you look at the patterns of the desert, or the tundra, or the villi in your intestines, or the surface of your brain, you can see that every time we take an area from here and put it there, moving in an even harmonic about that edge... we have enormously increased the edge."

### What the Site Tells You
"The site itself tells you what happens there. I find it very difficult to work from maps." He dismisses weather station data from miles away: "It is 100 feet from anything, and not near any hills. He is making abstract observations that don't apply to anybody in the district."

### The Humid Landscape Profile
"This is a harmonic, and often an almost endlessly repeated harmonic, and it contains potential." The repeating pattern of ridge, slope, valley, stream represents a harmonic structure that can be used as a template for design.

## Time as a Design Element

"Besides length, breadth, depth and elevation, we have yet another element to consider -- the time element. You must plan for evolution in the design."

"You should work with architects. Some of you here are architects. Interaction between the designer and the architect is a fruitful interaction."

## Every Element Performs Multiple Functions

"For every element we place, we make it work in as many functions as possible. These are the ironclad rules of design." A windbreak might also serve as fire protection, forage for animals, mulch production, and microclimate creation.

"A designer must have answers or he is not a designer."

## The Harmonic of Life

"This horizontal elaboration of edge is what life forms do when that vertical elaboration is not possible. This is true of biochemical harmonic. This is also the case with a biophysical harmonic."

"When one thing is no longer possible, then something else occurs. You will see that an increasing component of this results in a decreasing component of that. Both systems yield, but one yields in another modality."
