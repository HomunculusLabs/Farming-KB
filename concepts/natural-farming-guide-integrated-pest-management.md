# Integrated Pest Management

In permaculture and [[natural-farming]], [[fungal-pest-control]] s, [[companion-planting]], strategic animal integration, and forest garden diversity keeps pests in balance.

## Plant-Based Pest Control Strategies

### Attracting Predators

Plants provide food or shelter to friendly insects that eat pests. Dill, fennel, tansy, carrot, Queen Anne's lace, catnip, and daisy all attract [[parasitic-wasps-biological-pest-control]], bees, and friendly birds. In the Zone 1 garden, dill, fennel, and carrots are specifically left to go to seed to attract parasitic wasps. Yarrow provides ground cover that gives a home to [[hamilton-beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]] has many herbal medicinal uses.

### Sacrificial Plants (Trap Crops)

These plants attract pests so they leave other crops alone. Care must be taken to avoid providing a hotel for pests. Some plants provide a nice home for pests to live in all winter, giving them even more opportunity to destroy crops in summer.

### Pest-Repelling and Pest-Killing Plants

Pyretheum Daisy (Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium) is one of the most important [[jeavons-natural-pest-control]] plants. The flowers prefer dry and somewhat sandy soils. To harvest for use as a [[jadam-natural-pesticide-institute-and-research]], wait for a warm, sunny day when the flowers have been open for a few days. Dry them by hanging upside down or removing the heads and drying in the sun. Store whole in a dark, airtight container. When ready to use, grind into a fine powder and dust or spray (mixed with water) on affected plants. Insects should die almost immediately, without harm to humans.

Neem is a sub-tropical evergreen tree that grows quickly, is tolerant to drought, and prefers well-drained soil. The entire tree has antifungal, antibacterial, sedative, and antiviral properties and is commonly used as a natural pesticide. Extracts have been used to make toothpaste, skin creams, sprays, and many other products.

Fennel and dill also serve as pest control plants while being edible herbs.

### Weed Control Perimeter Bands

Around the outer perimeter of grain fields, grow weed-control plants such as comfrey, lemongrass, or citrus, mulched with sawdust for additional protection.

## Animal Integration for Pest Control

### Birds for Insect Control

Birds that eat larvae and eggs from the bark of trees can be attracted with flowers and herbs. The flowers attract insects, which in turn attract birds. Quail can be released into berry cages to control insects while also providing food (up to six quail per square foot). Quail eat insects and can also live in the greenhouse since they do not eat plants (unlike pigeons, which eat seeds and grain).

### Ducks for Slug and Snail Control

Ducks can be allowed into the garden from fall to spring, where they will totally control the slug and snail population. They stay in the marsh during summer. Geese can also be allowed in to forage on weeds and pests.

### Chickens for Orchard Pests

Once orchard trees are established (3-7 years), chickens at about 100 per acre eat insects and fallen fruit (stopping pests from gathering), fertilize the soil, and scavenge free food. Heritage chicken breeds are more likely to go broody and maintain their own populations.

### Pigs for Cleanup

When fruit falls on the ground, flies and other pests gather. Pigs and birds can clean these up while simultaneously fertilizing as they go.

### Dogs for Predator Control

Foxes, deer, and rabbits can all be controlled with a couple of dogs. If the dog is raised with chickens, it will leave poultry alone and protect them from predators.

## Companion Planting for Pest Deterrence

Companion planting organizes plants into communities (guilds) around a central element based on what grows best together. This reduces root competition, provides shelter, adds nutrients, and deters pests. Companion planting has been used for thousands of years.

The Three Sisters (corn, beans, squash) is the best-known example: corn provides support for beans, squash shades the ground preventing weeds, and together they produce much more food per square foot than when spread out alone.

The apple tree community is more complex. Several rings of plants grow under the canopy:

- At the base: a legume like fava beans
- Groundcover: clover with dandelions and chicory mixed in
- Middle ring: comfrey, artichoke, yarrow, nasturtiums, and dill
- Outer ring: daffodils to stop grass from encroaching

Each plant serves a purpose: attracting bees, providing mulch, fixing nitrogen, and hindering grass cover.

## Coaction Study Method

A systematic approach to finding which plants benefit each other in a community:

1. When Species A and B are put together, they affect each other in one of three ways: positively, negatively, or not at all
2. If Species A benefits but Species B is negatively affected, Species A is a parasite and should not be paired
3. Begin observing each species in a community to tally how it performs relative to neighbors
4. Once studied, arrange plants so each one has a positive interaction with something nearby
5. Remove any species that causes negative effects on its neighbors

Observation is key. Track what plants grow well together over time and what plants have sprung up on their own in close proximity. Success often comes from directly observable characteristics: attracting or repelling deer or birds, attracting or repelling insect species, leaf function (shading sun or mulching ground), and root behavior (fixing nitrogen, loosening soil, providing buffers).

## Forest Garden Strategy for Pest Resilience

Diversity provides inherent pest resistance. When many species grow together in a forest-like pattern, pest outbreaks are naturally limited. If you have a bad year for vegetables, tree fruits, nuts, or other crops may still succeed. This approach yields more total food per area than monoculture, even though individual plants may not produce as much. The key advantage is that forest gardens require very little labor compared to conventional farms, which typically demand 80+ hours per week.
