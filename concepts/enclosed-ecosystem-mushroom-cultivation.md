# Enclosed Ecosystem Mushroom Cultivation

Enclosed ecosystem cultivation is an advanced technique where mushrooms and plants are grown together in sealed transparent containers to create self-sustaining gas exchange systems. These systems model the biological interdependence between fungi and plants, where plants consume CO₂ and produce oxygen while mushrooms consume oxygen and produce CO₂. The concept has applications ranging from classroom education to potential space agriculture systems.

## The Gas Exchange Relationship

Plants and fungi share a complementary gas exchange cycle. Plants photosynthesize, absorbing carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen. Mushrooms respire aerobically, consuming oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide. When properly balanced inside an enclosed chamber, this reciprocal relationship can sustain both organisms without external air exchange. Mushrooms in such systems act as **bioindicators** of gas levels — when oxygen is sufficient, they develop short, thick stems. When oxygen is depleted, stems become elongated and spaghetti-like, signaling an imbalance.

## Building a Classroom-Scale Ecosystem

The basic enclosure uses clear containers to allow light for photosynthesis. Students proportion biomass carefully, since too much or too little of any component destabilizes the system. A small access panel or glove box allows materials to be added or removed without compromising the sealed environment. All anticipated materials should be placed inside at the start to minimize opening the chamber.

## Ethylene Management

A critical challenge in enclosed systems is **ethylene**, a natural plant hormone that accelerates fruit ripening and encourages mold growth, which then produces more ethylene. In a sealed environment, ethylene accumulation becomes toxic to both developing mushrooms and humans. Solutions include:

- Limiting plant [[decomposition]] to reduce ethylene sources
- Using a small duct fan to pull ethylene from the enclosure
- Filtering exhausted air through **[[potassium-permanganate]] crystals**, which oxidize and neutralize ethylene

## College-Level Expansion

More advanced versions of enclosed ecosystems explore:

- **Micropropagation** in very small vessels, with automation for space applications
- Nutrient analysis of various fungal species grown under different light wavelengths
- Solar panel arrays to provide the system's energy needs
- Zero-gravity cultivation chamber design for space travel
- Process design for media treatment, culture expansion, and [[spawn-production]]

## Competitive Experiments

The enclosed ecosystem model lends itself to group competitions where teams receive identical materials and compete to create the most stable, productive system. Teams document daily observations of changes, challenges, and successes. Performance is evaluated by comparing favorable growth of both plants and mushrooms, with the winning system demonstrating the best balance of biomass, gas exchange, and food production.

## Practical Considerations

- Clear containers are mandatory since plants require light
- Temperature must be managed for both plant and fungal optimums
- Humidity control is essential — mushrooms need high humidity while many plants prefer moderate levels
- Even with access panels, some gas exchange is inevitable, which affects long-term results
- The system should be considered experimental rather than truly self-sustaining at small scales
- Water cycling between plant transpiration and fungal needs must be balanced

## Relevance to Space Agriculture

The concept of enclosed fungal-plant ecosystems has direct relevance to space travel, where long-duration missions require food production and air revitalization. Mushrooms provide protein-rich food with minimal inputs, and their role as bioindicators makes them valuable monitoring organisms in sealed habitats where atmospheric composition must be carefully managed.
