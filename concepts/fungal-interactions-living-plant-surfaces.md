# Fungal Interactions with Living Plant Surfaces

## Overview

Fungi colonize living plant surfaces in diverse ways, ranging from obligate biotrophic parasites to saprobic epiphytes. The above-ground portions of plants -- leaves, stems, flowers, and fruits -- provide distinct habitats for specialized fungal communities. These interactions are critical to plant health, ecosystem function, and agricultural productivity.

## Powdery Mildews (Erysiphaceae)

### Biology

The Erysiphaceae are a family of Ascomycetes whose species are obligate parasites on above-ground parts of higher plants. They are nourished by haustoria and develop a characteristic white powdery conidial stage on the host surface.

### Diversity and Distribution

- 19-22 currently accepted genera; ~400 species (majority in genus Erysiphe)
- Hirata (1986) listed 9,838 angiosperm host species across 44 orders, 169 families, and 1,617 genera
- 93% of hosts are dicotyledons; 634 of 662 monocotyledonous hosts are Gramineae
- No powdery mildews occur on gymnosperms, pteridophytes, or lower plants
- Ratio of powdery mildew species to angiosperm species: ~4.5%
- Greatest abundance and damage: California and Israel (warm, rain-free summers, intensive agriculture)
- North-temperate regions have far larger numbers of host species than tropical, subtropical, arctic, or subarctic regions
- Herbaceous hosts (8,250) far exceed tree hosts (2,645), reflecting limitation to leaves and succulent shoots

### Recognition and Collection

- White, dusty conidial state coats leaf and stem surfaces
- Infected tissues may be stunted or chlorotic
- Black, globose perithecia less than 1mm diameter scattered over mycelium
- Immature perithecia yellowish to orange; mature perithecia dark
- Nonostiolate perithecia (cleistothecia) ornamented with hyphal appendages
- Genera delimited by appendage features plus number of asci per perithecium
- Host identification essential (many species are host-specific)
- Perithecia more likely on senescing leaves in older infections

## Sooty Molds

### Biology

Sooty molds are saprobic Ascomycetes belonging to several families of Dothideales. They form black mats on living leaves and stems, particularly abundant in the tropics. They use honeydew secreted by plants or sap-sucking insects (aphids, scale) as nutrient source.

### Characteristics

- Show no host preference
- Colonies may consist of mixed populations of 8+ species
- May form thin hyphal networks, velvety growth, or dark crusts
- Some species produce up to three different asexual forms
- Mycelium composed of dark hyphae up to several millimeters thick
- Conidiogenous cells and various conidia formed within the mycelium
- Metacapnodiaceae produce distinctive moniliform hyphae with rigid smooth-walled cells
- Seuratiaceae produce dark, gelatinous, lobed thalli

### Distribution

- Predominate along continental margins of North and South America, Central America, and Western Hemisphere islands
- Occur in most vegetation types worldwide but prevalent around rain forest margins
- Trunks and branches of Nothofagus forests (New Zealand) can be covered up to 9m height
- Reynolds (1975) recognized two growth forms:
  - **Deciduous**: Thin hyphal layer peeling away in drier months; lowland tropical and southeastern US coastal areas; genera include Limacinula and Trichomerium
  - **Permanent**: Thick cushionlike black hyphal mats on twigs/stems; neotropical highlands and temperate lowlands; genus Limacinia representative

### Collection Methods

- **Colloidal leaf peel technique**: Drop of 2% collodion/parlodion solution spread on leaf colony; dries to transparent film embedding fungus; peeled and placed on slide; collodion removed with acetone
- Alternatively: Water-soluble mucilage glue applied similarly
- Sooty molds on leaves easier to collect and identify than those on tree trunks
- Keys for genera in Luttrell (1973); species descriptions in Hughes (1976) and Batista and Ciferri (1963)

## Black Mildews

### Biology

Black mildews are obligate plant parasites in the order Meliolales (Ascomycota). They produce scattered, dark, superficial, circular colonies on host plant surfaces, especially abundant in the tropics.

### Diversity and Distribution

- More than 1,580 species (Hawksworth et al. 1995); ~1,400 in genus Meliola alone
- Found from Chile to southern US, South Africa to central Europe/Scotland, Japan to Tasmania
- More than 90% of hosts are dicotyledonous angiosperms; also on monocotyledons, gymnosperms, and pteridophytes
- Limited on some large rain forest families (Orchidaceae, Gesneriaceae, Araceae)
- Colonies can be thin or dense/velvety with setae
- Mostly on mature leaves; also on petioles and young twigs; rarely on fruit
- Characterized by very dark, superficial, hyphopodiate hyphae radiating from a central point

## Fly-Speck Fungi

Belong to Microthyriaceae (Microthyriales) of the Ascomycota. Named for dark, flattened, dimidiate-scutate (shield-shaped) ascomata appearing as small dark dots on leaf surfaces.

- Primarily on living leaves; also on young stems and occasionally fruits
- Mostly in tropical and subtropical regions
- Batista (1959) monograph: 447 species in 45 genera
- Largest genus: Micropeltis (100+ species)
- Ascospores uniseptate to multiseptate
- Mycelium usually entirely superficial with haustoria in host epidermal cells

## Foliar Fungi as Bioindicators

The composition of fungal communities on living plant surfaces reflects:

- Air quality (lichens and sensitive foliar fungi respond to pollution)
- Plant health status
- Microclimate conditions
- Insect activity (affecting honeydew availability for sooty molds)
- Seasonal and phenological patterns

## Significance

Fungi on living plant surfaces are important for:

- Plant disease and agricultural losses (powdery mildews)
- Photosynthesis reduction (sooty molds blocking light)
- Nutrient cycling (decomposition of honeydew and plant exudates)
- Bioindication of air quality
- Biodiversity assessment of ecosystems
- Understanding host-parasite coevolution
