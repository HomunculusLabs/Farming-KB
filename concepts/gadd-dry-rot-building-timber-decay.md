# Dry Rot Fungi and Timber Decay in Buildings

## Overview

Dry rot is one of the most destructive forms of [[gadd-fungal-wood-succession|wood decay]] in buildings, caused primarily by two basidiomycete fungi: [[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs|Serpula lacrymans]] in Europe, and [[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs|Meruliporia incrassata]] in North America. These fungi can transform structural timber into crumbling brown cubes, colonizing entire buildings through specialized root-like structures that pipe water from soil to wood. The term "dry rot" is misleading: the fungi require moisture but can transport water indoors through their own anatomy, destroying wood in otherwise dry buildings.

## The Causative Agents

### Serpula lacrymans

The name derives from Latin: *Serpula* meaning serpent, evoking the serpentine taproots; *lacrymans* meaning weeping, referring to fluid droplets on the fungal surface. Mordecai Cooke (1871) wrote that it "is often dripping with moisture, as if weeping in regret for the havoc it has made." Previously known as *Merulius lacrymans*, it has plagued British buildings for centuries and is also found in Europe, Australia, Japan, and parts of India. Its outdoor biology remains mysterious—it has never been found on tree stumps in the British Isles, though it occurs in forests in the Himalayas and Czech Republic.

### Meruliporia incrassata

First described in 1849, this species has caused extensive destruction in California, particularly as agricultural land is converted to housing developments. Buried tree stumps and roots from cleared orchards provide initial inoculum; rhizomorphs then extend through soil and into foundations. The optimum growth temperature is approximately 23 degrees Celsius, and lawn sprinklers, air conditioning, and imported topsoil create ideal conditions for colonization.

## Strands and Rhizomorphs: The Infrastructure of Destruction

Dry rot fungi form two types of root-like structure that enable them to cross non-nutritive barriers:

- **Strands (chords)**: Assemblages of hyphae with a thick "parent" hypha at the core surrounded by thinner hyphae, consolidated by winding tendrils. They develop in response to nutrient exhaustion as exploratory structures that disassemble when food is found. [[gadd-fungal-wood-succession|Serpula]] forms strands but not true rhizomorphs.

- **Rhizomorphs**: More complex structures with a waterproofed surface enclosing a central pipe for water and dissolved nutrient transport. They can grow for many metres, piping water from moist soil bases to dry wood tips. A cut rhizomorph exudes a distinctive mushroomy smell—Scandinavian home inspectors have trained Labrador retrievers to detect dry rot by scent.

These structures enable dry rot to colonize an entire home without forming a single spore. If the rhizomorph supplies sufficient water, hyphae emerge from interior beams, encase surfaces in white mycelium, and use strands to bridge gaps between beams, spanning concrete and other non-nutritive materials.

## The Decay Process

Once established, dry rot fungi digest cellulose in wood cell walls through [[gadd-fungal-nutrition-metabolism|brown rot]] mechanisms, leaving behind dark-pigmented lignin that gives decayed wood its characteristic brown colour. The wood breaks into cubical fragments—"brown cubical rot"—that crumble when touched. Unlike [[gadd-fungal-wood-succession|white rot fungi]] that remove lignin and leave white cellulose, brown rot fungi concentrate lignin as they consume cellulose.

## Fruiting Bodies (Basidiomes)

Advanced infestations produce crust-like basidiomes on beam surfaces, yellow to orange-brown with white margins. As they mature, surfaces develop ridges that extend and fuse into tubes, eventually forming honeycombed or toothed structures that maximize spore-producing surface area. A single basidiome the size of a book can shed billions of spores. In poorly ventilated basements, spores accumulate as a reddish dust covering surfaces. Belgian inspectors documented a single fruiting body covering 6 square metres within a property blanketed by 78 square metres of spore-producing rot.

## Historical Impact

### The Royal Navy

In the 1680s, Samuel Pepys surveyed Royal Navy ships and found planks "perished to powder" with toadstools "as big as my Fists" growing in the holds. The common woodland bracket fungus [[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs|Laetiporus sulphureus]] (sulphur shelf) formed yellow staircases inside rotting hulls. Timber shortages, exacerbated by the dissolution of the monasteries, forced reliance on imported unseasoned wood from Eastern Europe that was often already rotten on arrival. Ship lifespan fell from 25 to 30 years in the seventeenth century to 12 years in the eighteenth, and to "no duration" after the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805. The 110-gun Queen Charlotte deteriorated so rapidly during construction that she required complete rebuilding in 1810.

### Biblical References

The Old Testament book of Leviticus (14:33-53) describes a "fretting leprosy of the house" with "hollow strakes, greenish or reddish" in walls—symptoms more suggestive of dry rot than indoor moulds. The prescribed remedy—inspection, closure, scraping, and ultimately destruction of the house—anticipates modern remediation principles.

## Remediation and Prevention

### Kyanizing (1832)

John Howard Kyan patented a wood preservation method using corrosive sublimate (mercuric chloride). Michael Faraday tested it at the Woolwich "fungus pit" over 5 years and confirmed efficacy, but concerns about toxicity limited adoption.

### Creosote Treatment (1838)

John Bethell's creosote impregnation proved tremendously successful against all wood-decay fungi. Derived from coal tar, creosote contains thousands of compounds including naphthalene and pyrene. The basidiomycete [[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs|Lentinus lepideus]] ("train wrecker") shows unusual tolerance and can attack inadequately treated timber. Creosote was banned in the European Union in 2003; the US EPA banned chromated copper arsenate but has not extended the ban to creosote.

### Prevention Principles

The warmest invitation for dry rot is wood in direct contact with soil. Maintaining a bare concrete gap around building exteriors, ensuring proper drainage, avoiding soil accumulation against foundations, and using adequately seasoned, treated timber are the primary preventive measures. In dry homes, cutting the rhizomorph connection to outdoor soil can eliminate the entire infestation; in wet homes, the fungus can become self-sustaining.

## Modern Context

Dry rot continues to cause billions of dollars in damage annually. Changing climate conditions—particularly increased rainfall—may expand the range of both [[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs|Serpula]] and [[gadd-fungal-ecology-saprotrophs|Meruliporia]]. The steel framing industry views wood-destroying fungi as a marketing opportunity, but the vast existing inventory of timber-framed buildings ensures that dry rot will remain a significant concern for the foreseeable future.
