# Permaculture Site Assessment Checklist
A thorough site assessment is the foundation of any permaculture design. William Ozier Williams presents a systematic approach in his PDC, emphasizing that the designer must analyze the site comprehensively before making any design decisions. This checklist consolidates the key elements to assess.
## Broad Scale Context
Before analyzing the specific site, understand its place in the larger landscape:
- **Hemisphere and continent**
- **Country and region**
- **Bioregion and watershed**
- **County and local jurisdiction**
- **Nearby towns, cities, roads**
- **Nearby hazards**: Trash dumps, industrial sites, sources of pollution
- **Nearby resources**: Forests, quarries, agricultural land, community assets
- **Neighbor relationships**: Existing and potential collaborations or conflicts
## Client Interview
Every design serves a client (even if that is yourself):
- **Permaculture understanding**: How much does the client know?
- **Health needs**: Any particular dietary requirements, physical limitations?
- **Lifestyle goals**: What does daily life in the design look like?
- **Budget and timeline**: Financial constraints and phasing preferences
- **Vision and mission**: What are the client's goals and values?
- **Hands-on involvement**: How much maintenance work will the client do?
- **Occupants**: How many people, pets, livestock?
- **Social patterns**: Entertaining, events, community involvement?
- **Willingness for succession**: Natural progression through ecological stages, or desire for an immediate mature landscape?
## Climate Data
Essential climate information for any design:
- **Solar data**: Solar altitude and azimuth angles for summer and winter solstices
- **Temperature**: Average high and low temperatures; extreme highs and lows; frost dates and growing season length
- **Precipitation**: Average annual rainfall; monthly rainfall distribution; extreme rainfall events
- **Wind**: Direction and speed of prevailing summer winds; direction and speed of prevailing winter winds; storm wind patterns
- **Humidity**: Average high and low humidity levels
- **Biome**: Identify the local biome type
- **Monsoons**: Note seasonal wind and rain reversals if applicable
- **Microclimate data**: Identify any existing microclimates on the site
## Topography and Slope
- **Slope percentage**: Overall and in specific areas
- **Aspect**: Direction slopes face (critical for sun exposure)
- **Keypoint identification**: Where slope changes from convex to concave
- **Ridge and valley patterns**: Landform analysis
- **Elevation changes**: Highest and lowest points on site
- **Contour information**: For [[permaculture-mapping-and-base-maps|mapping]] water flow and earthworks
- **Rock outcrops**: Location of exposed rock
- **Erosion evidence**: Active or historical erosion, gullies, slope failure
## Water
- **Surface water**: Streams, springs, ponds, wetlands, seasonal water flow
- **Surface water flow**: Map the direction and volume of water movement across the site during rain
- **Groundwater**: Water table depth; seasonal variation
- **Flood risk**: Flood plain designation; historical flooding
- **Water quality**: Test if drinking water is planned
- **Drainage**: Areas of poor drainage; seasonal saturation
- **Existing water infrastructure**: Wells, pumps, pipes, irrigation
## Soils
- **Soil type**: Sand, clay, loam, silt -- and variations across the site
- **Soil structure**: Granular, platy, blocky, single grain
- **Soil depth**: Depth to bedrock or hardpan
- **pH**: Acid or alkaline, and by how much
- **Organic matter content**: Percentage of humus
- **Fertility**: Levels of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and trace minerals
- **Infiltration rate**: How quickly water enters the soil (test at multiple locations)
- **Compaction**: Evidence of compaction from foot traffic, machinery, or grazing
- **Erosion status**: Stable, actively eroding, or depositional areas
Take soil samples from different areas of the site. Color-code and number them on the working base map.
## Vegetation
- **Existing plant communities**: What is naturally growing? What does this tell you about the site?
- **Indicator species**: Plants that indicate soil conditions, moisture, and past land use
- **Trees**: Species, health, size, spacing; any worth preserving?
- **Invasive species**: Problem plants that will need management
- **Native vs. introduced**: Proportion of each
- **Forage value**: For livestock, poultry, or bees
- **Timber value**: Existing or potential wood resources
- **Medicinal and edible species**: Any useful plants already present?
## History of the Site
Research the historical patterns of the site:
- **Previous land use**: Agriculture, industry, residential, natural
- **Major climatic events**: Floods, fires, storms
- **Human habitation**: Previous buildings, structures, modifications
- **Chemical use**: History of pesticides, herbicides, fertilizers
- **Plants and crops historically grown**: What has succeeded or failed?
- **Design precedents**: What worked well here in the past?
## Access and Circulation
- **Existing roads and paths**: Condition, width, drainage
- **Vehicle access**: What vehicles need to reach which areas?
- **Pedestrian access**: Daily walking routes
- **Easements and rights of way**: Legal access constraints
- **Future access needs**: Anticipated changes
## Infrastructure
- **Existing buildings**: Condition, orientation, materials
- **Utilities**: Water, electricity, gas, internet
- **Fencing**: Condition, materials, purpose
- **Structures**: Sheds, greenhouses, barns, animal housing
- **Waste systems**: Septic, greywater, composting
## Inventories
Create lists of existing on-site resources:
- Plants and their functions
- Animals (domestic and wild)
- Potential building materials (timber, stone, earth)
- Salvageable materials
- Water sources and storage
- Available labor and skills
- Community resources and networks
## Ecological Design Checklist
Williams recommends evaluating the design against these criteria:
- Use as little energy as possible
- Provide comfortable shelter
- Generate and harness as much energy as possible
- Provide as much clean water as possible
- Provide as much food as possible
- Provide space for social interaction and connection with nature
- Include habitat for other species
- Be easily maintained by inhabitants
- Be designed for maintenance, upgrade, and disposal
- Manage all of its own waste
- Follow the triple bottom line (ecological, social, economic)
