# Forest Gardens

## Overview

A forest garden (also called a [[permaculture-design]] at the garden scale, combining guilds, polycultures, and ecological succession into a unified whole.

The concept draws from millennia of traditional agroforestry practices, particularly the tropical home gardens of Southeast Asia, West Africa, and Latin America, where indigenous peoples have maintained highly productive multistoried food systems beneath forest canopies. Temperate-climate forest gardening is a newer field, with only a few mature examples existing in North America and Europe.

## Why Forest Gardens

Left to its own devices, nearly every landscape on Earth progresses toward forest through ecological succession. Even suburban yards, with their regular irrigation and fertilizer, are constantly trying to become woods — evidenced by the woody seedlings that must be regularly pulled from lawns and garden beds. Forest gardening works with this natural tendency rather than against it.

The rationale is compelling:

- **Trees are the most productive plants per unit area**: An acre of wheat produces 1–2 tons of grain (with annual replanting), while an acre of apple trees yields 7 tons of fruit, and an acre of honey locust produces 15 tons of protein-rich pods — without annual replanting.
- **Three-dimensional productivity**: Forest gardens use volume, not just area. Multiple vertical layers each produce their own yield, dramatically increasing total output per square meter.
- **Low maintenance once established**: Thick vegetation cover reduces water needs, smothers weeds, and renews soil through self-mulching and natural soil building. Mostly perennial plants mean no tilling and minimal seasonal replanting.
- **Maximum wildlife habitat**: The layered structure provides nesting, foraging, and shelter opportunities for birds, insects, amphibians, and small mammals.
- **Soil building**: Trees produce leaf litter that builds humus. Deep roots break up compacted soil and bring minerals to the surface. The fungal networks of forest soils cycle nutrients efficiently.
- **Climate moderation**: Tree canopies reduce temperature extremes, slow wind, and create microclimates favorable to a wider range of species.
- **Erosion control**: Tree roots and perennial ground covers hold soil on slopes and in waterways far more effectively than annual crops.

## The Seven Layers of a Forest Garden

Natural forests structure themselves in vertical layers, each capturing light, water, and nutrients at different heights. A well-designed forest garden mimics this layered structure:

### 1. Canopy Layer (Overstory)
The tallest trees — typically 10–15+ meters. In a home-scale forest garden, this might include standard-sized fruit trees (apple, pear, walnut, chestnut, pecan), timber trees, or nitrogen-fixing trees like black locust. These trees define the overall character of the garden and cast the shade pattern that determines what can grow below.

### 2. Understory Layer (Low Tree Layer)
Smaller trees reaching 3–9 meters — dwarf and semi-dwarf fruit trees (plum, cherry, peach, apricot), persimmon, pawpaw, medlar, mulberry, and ornamental trees that also fix nitrogen (silk tree, golden-chain tree). These fill the space between canopy trees and shrubs.

### 3. Shrub Layer
Woody plants from 1–3 meters — berry bushes (blueberry, currant, gooseberry, raspberry, blackberry), flowering shrubs that attract pollinators (butterfly bush, ceanothus), and fruiting shrubs (elderberry, seaberry, goumi). The shrub layer is often the most productive layer per unit area in terms of food yield.

### 4. Herbaceous Layer
Non-woody perennials — culinary herbs (thyme, oregano, mint, sage), medicinal herbs (comfrey, echinacea, yarrow), [[jerusalem-artichoke]], daikon radish, oca, mashua, and yacon. Deep-rooted species like comfrey and dandelion also occupy this layer, mining minerals from deep soil.

### 7. Vine Layer
Climbing plants that use trees and shrubs as support — hardy kiwi, grapes, passionflower, hops, climbing beans, honeysuckle, and trumpet vine. Vines exploit the vertical space between ground and canopy, producing food and flowers without requiring additional ground area.

## Designing a Forest Garden

### Site Assessment

Forest garden design begins with thorough site observation: sun angles and patterns throughout the year, soil type and depth, water flow and drainage, existing vegetation, prevailing winds, frost pockets, and microclimates created by existing structures or topography.

### Shape and Layout

On a south-facing slope (Northern Hemisphere), the garden forms a natural sun-trap. Trees are placed with the tallest at the back (north) and progressively shorter species toward the front (south), ensuring light reaches all layers. On flat ground, a symmetrical arrangement with tallest trees spaced far apart allows sunlight to penetrate to lower layers.

Key design considerations:

- **Tree spacing**: Large canopy trees should be spaced far enough apart that light reaches shrubs and smaller trees between them. A common approach is to space full-sized trees at 8–10 meter intervals.
- **Access paths**: Winding paths through the forest garden allow access for harvesting and maintenance while creating additional edge habitat.
- **Sunward edges**: Vegetable and flower beds are placed on the sunward side where they receive the most light.
- **Guild integration**: Each major tree is surrounded by its own guild of supporting plants.

### Species Selection

Every plant should serve multiple functions. A nitrogen-fixing shrub might also provide berries, attract pollinators, and produce mulch. An herbaceous perennial might be edible, medicinal, and insectary. Multifunctional species are the hallmark of ecological design — nature's principle that nothing does just one thing.

### Climate Adaptation

Forest gardens vary by climate. Northern gardeners need a more open canopy to allow weak sunlight through to lower layers, while southern gardeners may desire dense spacing for shade. Large properties accommodate full-sized trees; small properties use dwarf varieties and naturally small species.

## Case Study: The Bullock Brothers' Food Forest

The Bullock brothers' several-acre food forest on Orcas Island, Washington, demonstrates temperate forest gardening at scale. Key observations from the site:

- **Year-round harvest**: Every month brings fresh fruit or vegetables — cool-season salad greens in midwinter, berries in late spring, branch-breaking loads of fruit in summer and fall, and medlar fruits in December tasting like cinnamon-spiced pear butter.
- **Abundance**: Twenty students grazing for three weeks in summer on plums, peaches, and berries, with tons of fruit remaining on the trees afterward.
- **Biodiversity recovery**: Birds and animals returned that no one had seen in the area for years.
