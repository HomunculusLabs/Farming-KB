# Myxomycete Sampling, Collection, and Moist Chamber Culturing

Myxomycetes (plasmodial slime molds) present unique challenges for biodiversity surveys. Their fruiting bodies are often minute, transient, and difficult to detect in the field. Many species fruit only briefly and their sporangia may persist for just a few hours. As a result, the moist [[fungal-biodiversity-assessment-methods]]. It consists of a clean container (typically a Petri dish or finger bowl) lined with filter paper or paper towel, moistened with distilled water or a weak antibiotic solution, and inoculated with the collected substrate.

The concept is simple but powerful: by creating a humid, enclosed environment, the moist chamber allows myxomycete plasmodia and spores present on the substrate to develop into observable fruiting bodies under controlled conditions.

Setup is straightforward: a piece of filter paper is placed in the bottom of the container and moistened with distilled water. Substrate samples (bark pieces, wood chips, litter, or dung pellets) are arranged on the moist paper, leaving space between pieces for air circulation and for fruiting bodies to develop. The container is sealed and incubated at room temperature (approximately 20–23°C) under ambient or controlled light conditions.

Incubation times vary by species and substrate. Some myxomycetes fruit within a few days of being placed in the moist chamber, while others may require a month or more. Most researchers examine their moist chambers daily for the first two weeks, then weekly for up to three months. Observation at magnifications of 50–100× using a dissecting microscope is essential, as many fruiting bodies are less than 1 mm in diameter and invisible to the naked eye.

The technique has several advantages beyond merely extending the collection period. It allows the myxomycologist to collect additional substrate samples during field visits that can be stored for later study in the laboratory. Field conditions may not have been suitable for fruiting (too dry or too cold), and the moist chamber compensates for these limitations. The technique is particularly ideal for species that require extended development time.

## Isolation and Culturing

For species-level identification and for maintaining living collections, myxomycetes can be isolated from moist chamber cultures onto agar media. The most important characters for identification are the color and type of plasmodium and the morphology of the fruiting body that develops from it.

When plasmodia are actively feeding on the substrate surface in a moist chamber, a thin slice of the substratum bearing a portion of the plasmodium can be transferred to water agar. The plasmodium is fed sterile, finely ground, uncooked oat flakes to sustain growth and promote eventual fruiting. Fresh spores obtained in this way can be sown on a nutrient-poor agar medium (water agar or half-strength cornmeal agar) in an attempt to complete the life cycle from spore to spore.

More precise isolation begins with crushing a single sporangium under a dissecting microscope. The released spores are spread in a small area on the agar surface of a sterile plastic Petri dish (60 mm diameter × 15 mm height) containing about 8 ml of medium. Some spores are submerged by slashing the agar with a needle; others are left free on the surface. Three drops of a dilute Escherichia coli suspension are added as a food source for the amoebal stage. Culture dishes are stored at 23°C in total darkness.

Different taxonomic groups have different moisture requirements. Trichiaceous and stemonitaceous myxomycetes require free water in the early stages of their life cycle, so the agar surface is flooded with 2 ml of sterile distilled water. Physaraceous species grow best on a dry agar surface, and for those species no free water is added. Physaraceous species are cultured under a 12-hour dark/12-hour light cycle, while other groups are kept in continuous darkness.

After approximately seven days, excess water is pipetted off and pulverized rolled oats are sprinkled over the agar surface. Cultures are returned to the incubator and examined periodically for the appearance of plasmodia and subsequent fruiting bodies. This spore-to-spore technique is essential for obtaining complete life cycle data and for confirming species identifications.

## Microscopic Examination

Many myxomycetes can be identified to species by an experienced myxomycologist using a hand lens or dissecting microscope alone, based on the macroscopic features of the fruiting body. However, most species require examination with a compound microscope at magnifications of 400–1000× to resolve the delicate ornamentation of spores and capillitial threads. A calibrated ocular micrometer is necessary for measuring spore diameters. Phase contrast optics can be particularly helpful for observing spore wall ornamentation without the need for staining.

Internal structures of a sporangium are best observed when no spores are present. Wind-blown sporangia that have already discharged their spores are ideal for preparing mounts, or spores can be removed by gently blowing on the sporangium through a hand-held blower (a flexible rubber tube with a glass pipette tip) while holding the sporangium over a drop of mounting fluid on a glass slide.

Three types of mounts are used:

**Temporary water mounts** are the best choice for routine identification. Sporangia are mounted in a drop of water and a cover slip is dropped into place. Air bubbles trapped under the cover slip produce a useful lens effect that highlights surface markings of spores and capillitial threads.

## Quantitative Sampling Design

While many myxomycete surveys are qualitative (recording presence only), quantitative approaches are increasingly important for comparing diversity across sites, habitats, or time periods. A quantitative moist chamber survey involves counting the number of fruiting bodies of each species that develops from a known amount of substrate.

Standardized [[lichen-biodiversity-sampling-protocols-data-analysis]] have been developed for biodiversity inventories. These typically specify the number of substrate samples per habitat type, the size of each sample, and the duration of moist chamber incubation. A common approach is to collect 20–30 bark samples, 20–30 litter samples, and 10–20 dung samples per site, with each sample of standardized size (e.g., bark pieces approximately 5 × 5 cm).

Recording effort is critical for meaningful comparisons. The number of moist chamber days (number of chambers × number of days incubated) serves as a measure of sampling intensity. Species accumulation curves should be plotted to determine whether sufficient sampling has been conducted to capture the majority of species present at a site.

## Seasonal and Latitudinal Variation

Myxomycete fruiting is strongly seasonal in temperate regions, with peak diversity and abundance occurring during warm, moist periods (late spring through early autumn in the Northern Hemisphere). In tropical regions, where temperature and moisture fluctuations are less pronounced, myxomycetes may fruit year-round, but dry seasons can suppress sporulation.
