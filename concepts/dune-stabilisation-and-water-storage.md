## Dune Stabilisation and Water Storage

Sand dunes are among the most dynamic and challenging landforms in arid environments, yet they also harbour some of the desert's most significant water reserves. Understanding how dunes form, move, and store water is essential for any [[humus]] and dust particles) support quite dense vegetation (50% cover), while others are almost sterile and lack basic plant nutrients.

Any traveller in vegetated deserts will notice that the largest trees—sometimes the only trees—stand in the dune ridges. This is apparent with dunes on harder pavement, dunes on clay, and in coastal dune hollows and deflation hollows in sands. At the edge of large dune complexes, water rushes and sedges appear at base seepage, and even shallow lagoons form that are as much dune-fed as dune-dammed.

It follows that dune on pavement or salted ground is the best site for permanent plantings. The design challenge is how to establish those trees and stabilise the dune.

## Stabilising Isolated Dunes on Pavement

For individual dunes on hard surfaces, a relatively simple approach works after rains:

1. **Plant a fast-growing grain or oat crop**—sorghum, millet, desert legumes such as [[moth-bean]], or yam beans.
2. **Set out hardy [[neem]] tree leaf powder, or magnesite) into a stiff mass.
2. Pass through a mincer with blades removed.
3. Spread onto a vibrating tray with a slight slope where dry powder or dusts are shaken over the extrusion.
4. The "rolls" become round pellets on the shaker tray, which are spread out to dry.

Key advantage: pelleted seed is not eaten by birds or insects. Many pellets will be buried by sand, and if heavy rain occurs, some will germinate.

## Intensive Stabilisation of Severe Blowouts

Severe dune blowouts, particularly on coasts, need more intensive treatment:

1. **Hand planting** of quickset grasses such as *Ammophila* spp.
2. **Pelleted moth bean seed** broadcast.
3. **Brush fences** in 7-metre squares for early stabilisation:
   - Stakes 2 m long, driven 1 m deep.
   - Numerous bushy weaves between stakes.
   - Wind gaps less than 50% to drop sand out.
4. Pits of seed and *[[acacia]] victoriae* may succeed after rain.

## Brush Fence Design and Benefits

Brush fences are fundamental precursors to dune planting:

- Height: 0.9-1 m.
- Spacing: Parallel rows 7 m apart across the main wind direction.
- Posts: Firm, driven every 3-4 m.
- Wind gaps: Less than 50% to cause sand to drop out behind the fence.
- Temperature reduction: 12 degrees C or more measured in the shade of fences.
- Moisture retention: 1.8-3.0% less moisture loss than open areas.

These fences are precursors to planting hardy perennials adapted to dune conditions.

## Chinese Approach: Mat and Basket Planting

In China, rice-straw mats serve as fencing or surface stabilisers. Advanced trees in baskets are planted (basket and all) in the matting shelter. These pioneer trees provide shelter and mulch for later forestry and crop establishment.

## Tree Planting Depth for Dune Survival

As lethal soil temperatures are rarely formed below 15 cm in dunes, careful placement of trees in holes this deep or deeper prevents seedlings from being "cooked" until they can cast their own shade. Holes should be stabilised from collapse using a woven basket or cardboard collar.

Fertiliser placement is critical: it should be thinly placed at planting, as rapid infiltration of water in rains can carry it below root reach. After the tree has established a root web, more fertiliser can be added and will be taken up effectively. All pioneering dune plants must tolerate stem burial until the area is stabilised.

## Natural Stabilisation Mechanisms

Several natural processes contribute to dune stability:

- **Water bonding**: Water creates bonds between sand particles that require much higher wind speeds to move.
- **Salt crusts**: Salt (especially hygroscopic varieties) absorbs atmospheric moisture and cements surfaces.
- **Biological crusts**: [[lichens]], bacteria, fungi, and algal mats form naturally on stable surfaces.
- **Sessile trees**: Species like *Acacia* and *Mesquite* greatly aid the stabilisation process as a travelling barchan stabilises and assumes an oval profile.

These often delicate desert crusts are critical to stability. When hoofed animals, fast vehicles, or agriculture is introduced, wind erosion may quickly follow. Urgent stabilisation can be effected by spraying tar oils, laying pebble beds, or building brush fences.

## Protecting Stabilised Dunes

Once stabilised, dunes must be protected from:

- "Recreation" vehicles (which devastate stabilised surfaces).
- Heavy browsing by livestock.
- Badly aligned roads.
- Sand mining to windward.

Sawdust and mulch in hollows is very beneficial. Mill wastes (bark, sawdust) have helped establish *Acacia sophorae* forest in coastal dunes in Tasmania. All plants in dunes benefit from nitrogenous fertiliser, phosphate, [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]], as old dunes are usually deficient in plant nutrients.

## Dune Swale Management

Side-cast banks (chevron banks) halt flow down dune swales. Hatched areas between banks will support trees in parallel dune series. Swales so chosen must have a slope to enable water flow. As a travelling barchan stabilises, it assumes an oval profile, initiating water infiltration and supporting adapted vegetation.
