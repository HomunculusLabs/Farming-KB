# Fertilizer Selection and Application Rates

## The Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) Recipe

[[steve-solomon]]'s signature contribution to organic gardening is his Complete
Organic Fertilizer formula. Designed to provide balanced mineral nutrition that
most composts cannot, COF is mixed in bulk and broadcast at specific rates. The
basic recipe:

- 4 parts seedmeal (cottonseed, canola, soybean, or linseed meal) - 1 part
agricultural lime OR 1 part dolomitic lime (see soil test guidance) - 1 part
gypsum (only if using dolomitic lime) - 1 part bone meal or 1 part rock
phosphate - 0.5 part kelp meal (for trace minerals)

**Seedmeal selection:** Any oilseed meal works. Cottonseed meal is widely
available and inexpensive in North America. Canola meal is a good alternative
where cottonseed is not available. Soybean meal is excellent but often
expensive. All provide nitrogen in a form that releases steadily.

**Lime versus dolomite:** Use dolomitic lime if a soil test shows magnesium is
deficient. Otherwise, use agricultural lime (calcium carbonate). If using
dolomite, also add gypsum to ensure adequate calcium, since dolomite provides
magnesium as well as calcium.

**Phosphate source:** Bone meal is preferred for its availability and
effectiveness. Rock phosphate is cheaper but much slower to release -- it may
take years to become fully available. For beds being prepared for the first
time, bone meal gives faster results.

**Kelp meal:** Essential for trace minerals including iodine, iron, zinc,
manganese, and dozens of other micronutrients. Solomon considers this the
ingredient that makes COF truly "complete." Basalt rock dust can substitute but
may be less consistently mineral-balanced.

## Application Rates

**Before planting (bed preparation):** Broadcast COF at the rate of 4-6 quarts
(about 4-6 liters) per 100 square feet (10 square meters) and dig it into the
top 6-8 inches (15-20 cm) of soil.

**For hills:** Place a generous handful of COF directly into each planting hill
and mix it in.

**Side-dressing:** For long-season crops (tomatoes, peppers, brassicas),
side-dress with COF at half the initial rate about 6-8 weeks after planting.
Work it into the top inch of soil around the plants, staying several inches from
the stem.

**For clay soils:** Solomon recommends about 1.5 times the usual rate for the
first few years, because clay acts "like a discharged lead-acid battery" that
absorbs nutrients until charged. Do not add extra organic matter to clay; add
extra mineral fertilizer instead.

## Low-Demand Versus High-Demand Vegetables

Solomon classifies vegetables by their nutrient requirements:

**Low-demand:** Leafy greens (lettuce, spinach), radishes, peas, beans (not
soy), turnips, Swiss chard, leeks. These grow adequately in most soils with a
light application of compost or manure. COF is beneficial but not essential.

**Medium-demand:** Carrots, beets, onions, garlic, potatoes, cucumbers, summer
squash, sweet corn. These need more balanced nutrition. Compost alone may
suffice in warm climates where organic matter decomposes quickly, but in cool
maritime climates, COF is usually needed.

**High-demand:** Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, winter squash, melons,
cauliflower, broccoli, cabbage, Brussels sprouts. These crops are the reason COF
was developed. Without complete mineral nutrition, they produce poorly, are more
susceptible to pests and diseases, and are less nutritious.

## Organic Versus Chemical Fertilizers

Solomon takes a pragmatic position: the issue is not whether a fertilizer is
"organic" but whether it provides complete nutrition. Chemical fertilizers
containing NPK alone are insufficient -- they lack the trace minerals and
calcium/magnesium balance that plants need. However, chemical fertilizers that
include trace minerals (labeled "with micronutrients") are effective and
affordable.

He notes that commercial growers, both chemical and organic, routinely push
potassium to high levels for bulk yield. This produces larger but less
nutritious vegetables. The home gardener should aim for mineral completeness,
not maximum yield.

## [[foliar-feeding]]

Solomon discusses foliar feeding (spraying liquid fertilizer on leaves) as a
supplement but not a replacement for soil fertility. The most effective foliar
spray is a dilute solution of fish emulsion or liquid kelp, applied early in the
morning when leaf stomata are open. Foliar feeding provides a quick boost during
critical growth stages but cannot sustain a crop through an entire season.

## Fertigation Rates

When using fertigation (water + dissolved fertilizer delivered through a bucket
or drip system), Solomon recommends:

- Fish emulsion: dilute 1:100 (one part emulsion to 100 parts water).  - Compost
tea: one bucketful of compost per barrel of water, brewed one week.  - COF
solution: dissolve about one cup of COF in 5 gallons of water. This is too
concentrated for most applications; dilute further for sensitive plants.

Fertigation rates by soil type: - Sandy soil (under 10 percent clay): about 2.5
gallons every 10 days per plant.  - Loam soil (10-20 percent clay): about 4
gallons every two weeks.  - Clay soil (over 20 percent clay): about 5 gallons
every three weeks.

## Sacked Manure as Fertilizer

Sacked steer manure from garden centers has little fertilizing value but feeds
soil microbes and improves soil structure. Sacked chicken manure (approximately
NPK 4-3-2) is far better but should not be overused -- it is high in nitrogen
and can burn plants if applied too heavily. Apply chicken manure at about half
the rate of COF.
