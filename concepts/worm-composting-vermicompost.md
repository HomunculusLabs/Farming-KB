# Worm Composting (Vermicomposting)

Vermicomposting uses earthworms to convert organic waste into nutrient-rich castings (worm manure). The primary species used is Eisenia fetida, commonly called the red wiggler or red composting worm. Vermicomposting can be done indoors or outdoors, requires minimal space, and produces one of the highest-quality soil amendments available. Worm castings are rich in plant-available nutrients, beneficial microorganisms, plant growth hormones, and humic acids.

## The Worm: Eisenia fetida

Red wigglers are NOT the common earthworms found in garden soil (Lumbricus terrestris). Key differences:

- Size: 2-4 inches long, thin
- Color: red, with alternating dark and light bands
- Habitat: surface-dwelling (epigeic), lives in top 2-6 inches of organic matter
- Diet: decaying organic matter, not soil
- Temperature range: 55-77F optimal, survives 40-85F
- Reproduction: hermaphroditic, each worm produces 3-7 cocoons per week, each cocoon hatches 1-3 worms in 3 weeks
- Population: doubles every 2-3 months under ideal conditions
- Processing capacity: 1 pound of worms processes approximately 0.5 pounds of food waste per day

Purchase red wigglers from reputable vermiculture suppliers (not bait shops, which may sell different species). Start with at least 500 worms (about 0.5 pounds) for a standard bin.

## Bin Setup

### Plastic Bin (Continuous Flow-Through)

The most common and beginner-friendly setup:

Materials:
- Two opaque plastic storage bins (18-27 gallons), one with lid
- Drill with 1/8 inch and 1/4 inch bits
- Shredded newspaper or cardboard for bedding
- Spray bottle with water

Construction:
1. Drill 20-30 holes (1/8 inch) in the bottom of the inner bin for drainage
2. Drill 30-40 holes (1/4 inch) in the lid for ventilation
3. Place the inner bin inside the outer bin (the outer bin catches leachate)
4. Add 4-6 inches of moist bedding to the inner bin
5. Add worms
6. Cover with damp newspaper and lid

### Wooden Bin

Wooden bins provide better moisture regulation and air circulation:

- Use untreated wood (cedar, pine, or plywood)
- Size: 24x16x12 inches (holds roughly 1,000-2,000 worms)
- Drill ventilation holes in sides and lid
- No drainage holes needed (wood absorbs excess moisture)
- Line with landscape fabric to prevent escapees

### Stackable Tray Systems (Worm Factory)

Commercial multi-tray systems allow continuous harvesting:
- Worms migrate upward through mesh floors toward fresh food
- Lower trays finish and can be harvested without disturbing worms
- Good for processing larger volumes of waste
- More expensive but convenient

## Bedding

Bedding provides the carbon source and habitat structure:

Acceptable bedding materials:
- Shredded newspaper (black-and-white print only, no glossy pages)
- Shredded corrugated cardboard (remove tape and labels)
- Shredded brown paper bags
- Aged leaves
- Coconut coir
- Peat moss (moistened first)
- Straw (chopped)

Preparation:
- Moisten bedding to 70-80% moisture (squeeze hard -- should produce only a few drops)
- Fluff bedding to ensure air pockets
- Fill bin 4-6 inches deep with damp bedding
- Let sit for 24 hours before adding worms (allows chlorine to off-gas from tap water)

## Feeding Do's and Don'ts

### What to Feed
- Fruit and vegetable scraps (chopped small for faster processing)
- Coffee grounds and paper filters
- Tea bags (remove staples)
- Crushed eggshells (provides grit for digestion and calcium)
- Bread, pasta, rice (in moderation)
- Yard waste (soft green materials)
- Shredded paper and cardboard

### What NOT to Feed
- Meat, fish, dairy (attracts pests, creates odor)
- Oils, fats, grease (coats bedding, blocks oxygen)
- Citrus (in excess -- too acidic, use sparingly)
- Onions and garlic (in excess -- worms dislike)
- Spicy foods, heavily salted foods
- Pet waste (cat, dog -- pathogens)
- Glossy paper, coated cardboard
- Sawdust from treated wood

### Feeding Guidelines
- Bury food 2-3 inches under bedding (prevents fruit flies and odor)
- Feed in one area of the bin, rotate locations each feeding
- Start with small amounts -- 1 cup of food per pound of worms per day
- Increase gradually as worm population grows
- If food is not being consumed, reduce feeding
- Overfeeding is the most common beginner mistake

## Maintenance

- Keep bedding moist (70-80%) -- add water or damp bedding as needed
- Maintain temperature 55-77F (keep out of direct sun, freezing conditions)
- Drain leachate from bottom bin weekly
- Add fresh bedding every 2-4 weeks
- Check for and remove any moldy or rotting food
- Keep bin in a dark location (worms are photophobic)

## Harvesting Castings

When the bin is mostly castings (dark, crumbly, uniform material) and little bedding remains (typically every 3-6 months), harvest using one of these methods:

### Light Migration Method
1. Dump bin contents on a tarp in bright light
2. Form into cone-shaped mounds
3. Wait 10-15 minutes -- worms migrate to the bottom of each mound to escape light
4. Scrape off the top layer of castings (worm-free)
5. Repeat until mostly worms remain
6. Return worms to fresh bedding

### Side-to-Side Method
1. Push all bin contents to one side of the bin
2. Add fresh bedding and food to the empty side
3. Over 2-4 weeks, worms migrate to the fresh side
4. Harvest castings from the abandoned side

### Screen Method
1. Build or buy a 1/4 inch mesh screen frame
2. Place finished material on screen over a collection container
3. Shake or rub material through screen
4. Castings fall through; worms and undigested material stay on top

## Worm Tea

Worm tea (properly called "vermicompost tea" or "compost extract") is a liquid amendment made by steeping worm castings in water.

### Passive Leachate vs Active Tea
- Leachate (liquid that drains from the bin) is NOT true worm tea -- it can contain pathogens and is variable in quality. Dilute 1:10 and use within 24 hours.
- Aerated compost tea (ACT) is made by actively brewing castings with aeration:

Aerated Tea Recipe:
1. Fill a 5-gallon bucket with dechlorinated water
2. Add 2 cups of high-quality worm castings
3. Add 1 tablespoon of unsulfured molasses (feeds beneficial microbes)
4. Aerate with an aquarium air pump and air stone for 24-48 hours
5. Use immediately as a soil drench or foliar spray (strain first for sprayers)
6. Apply within 4 hours of stopping aeration

Benefits of aerated worm tea:
- Introduces beneficial microorganisms to soil and foliar surfaces
- Provides soluble nutrients in immediately available form
- Can suppress foliar diseases when used as a spray
- Improves nutrient cycling in soil

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Foul odor | Overfeeding, too wet, anaerobic | Add dry bedding, reduce feeding, improve ventilation |
| Fruit flies | Food exposed on surface | Bury food deeper, add newspaper layer on top |
| Worms escaping | Bin too acidic, too wet, or new worms adjusting | Add eggshells, improve drainage, keep lid on (new worms need 48 hours to settle) |
| Worms dying | Temperature too high/low, toxic materials | Move bin to suitable location, remove offending materials |
| White mold | Normal fungal activity in bin | No action needed (beneficial) |
| Slow processing | Too cold, not enough worms, bedding too dry | Warm the bin, add more worms, moisten bedding |
