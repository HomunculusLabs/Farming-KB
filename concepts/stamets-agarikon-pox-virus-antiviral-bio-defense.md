## Discovery of Antipox Activity

Stamets used stainless steel tree cork borers to remove thin cylinders of tissue
from agarikon conks, taking cultures from the layer just above the spore-
producing hymenial layer. This sampling method minimized impact on the living
mushroom while providing viable tissue for laboratory cloning. The cloned
cultures were then screened through the BioShield program.

Specially prepared extracts from living mycelium showed potent activity against
vaccinia pox and cowpox viruses. Strains of agarikon varied in their potency,
suggesting that [[amanita-preparation-methods]] would not capture the active constituents. Only carefully
prepared extracts from living mycelial cultures demonstrated the antiviral
effect.

## The BioShield Program

The screening was administered jointly through the U.S. National Institutes of
Health (NIH) and the U.S. Army Medical Research Institute of Infectious Diseases
(USAMRIID), a coordinated effort to combat potentially weaponized viruses.
This collaboration placed agarikon research within the formal U.S. biodefense
apparatus, giving it scientific credibility and funding priority.

The broader context is the increasing threat of bioterrorism, particularly from
viruses like smallpox and bacteria like anthrax. Stamets frames protecting fungal
genetic diversity in old-growth forests as a matter of national defense, arguing
that undiscovered mycomedicinal remedies may exist in these endangered ecosystems
that could prove essential for protecting future generations.

## Historical Parallels: Penicillin and Taxol

Stamets draws instructive parallels between agarikon and two previous natural
product drug discoveries. A moldy cantaloupe sent to an army research lab in 1941
by a housewife from Peoria, Illinois, gave rise to the strains of Penicillium
chrysogenum that enabled commercial production of penicillin. This discovery
saved millions of lives and helped win World War II. Similarly, Taxomyces
andreanae, a fungus inhabiting the Pacific yew tree, was found to synthesize
taxol, a potent anticancer drug, earning several patents.

Unlike Penicillium, which enjoys widespread cosmopolitan habits, agarikon is
restricted to a rapidly declining old-growth habitat. This makes conservation
urgently important: once the habitat is gone, the genetic diversity that
produces these antiviral compounds cannot be recovered.

## Three Mechanisms of Mushroom Defense

Stamets articulates three distinct mechanisms by which mushrooms offer protection
from infectious diseases. First, directly as antimicrobial agents through
antibiotics and antivirals produced by their mycelium and fruitbodies. Second,
by activating the host-mediated immune response, boosting an organism's natural
resistance to microbial infection. Third, through mycofiltration using custom-
constructed mycelial mats to reduce infection risk from environmental sources
such as sewage from feedlots and slaughterhouses. The key principle is to match
the specific mushroom species with the specific pathogen for maximum efficacy.

## Conservation Implications

The intersection of agarikon's medicinal potential with old-growth forest
decline creates a compelling conservation argument. Stamets notes that strains
of agarikon living in ancestral forests that have never been logged will likely
prove more potent against pox viruses than what has already been discovered. The
implication is that each destroyed old-growth stand potentially eliminates
unexplored genetic lineages that could yield more powerful antiviral compounds.

What other mycomedicinal remedies await discovery in ancient forests? Stamets
expresses little doubt that many other mushrooms will provide antiviral or
anticancer drugs, provided the forests survive. The survival of future
generations may depend on preserving these fungal genetic resources before
commercial interests eliminate them entirely.
