## The Diet of Non-Discrimination and the Diet of the Sick

Where macrobiotics relies heavily on the yin-yang classification of foods and prescribes specific dietary ratios (the famous 5:1 grain-to-vegetable ratio), Fukuoka explicitly rejected systematic dietary classification. He argued that yin-yang calculations, while useful as a transitional tool, ultimately replaced one form of intellectual discrimination with another. His "diet of non-discrimination" aimed to transcend all such frameworks entirely. The macrobiotic approach, in Fukuoka"s view, was still a "diet of principle" — a step forward from modern eating habits but not the final destination.

## Specific Foods and Dietary Patterns

While Fukuoka discouraged rigid dietary rules, he did offer practical guidance rooted in his observations. He advocated whole grains — particularly brown [[fukuoka-european-distribution-system-critique]]. A person who eats imported food out of season is, in Fukuoka's framework, perpetuating the very agricultural system that degrades both land and health. The solution is not to prescribe a universal diet but to restore the local relationship between farmer, land, and eater — so that the question of what to eat answers itself through proximity and season.

Fukuoka's young disciples on his mountain in Japan embodied this principle: they ate only what grew around them, prepared simply, without nutritional anxiety. He considered this not asceticism but liberation — freedom from the endless cycle of craving, calculation, and consumption that characterizes modern eating.
