# The Diet of Non-Discrimination and the Diet of the Sick

In the concluding section of Chapter 5 in [[fukuoka-natural-way-of-farming-theory-of-natural-farming]], Fukuoka presents his most advanced dietary philosophy -- the "diet of non-discrimination" -- alongside a practical discussion of how sick and healthy people can approach a natural diet.

## The Diet of Non-Discrimination

"This is founded on the view that man does not live through his own efforts but was created and is supported by nature."

"Food is not something that man selects from within nature, it is a gift bestowed upon him from above. Its character as food lies neither exclusively in itself nor in man. A true natural diet becomes possible only when food, the body, and the soul fuse together completely within nature."

"True man with a truly healthy body and mind should be naturally equipped with the ability to take the right food from nature, without discrimination or error. To follow the will of the body and desire freely, to eat when a food is delicious and forbear when it is not, to partake without restraint, without plan or intention, is to enjoy the most subtle and exquisite fare -- an ideal diet."

This is the ultimate goal -- eating without deliberation, without yin-yang calculations, without nutritional science. It is a state of being rather than a dietary program.

## The Diet of Principle as Stepping Stone

Ordinary people must work toward the ideal by first practicing a "diet of principle" -- eating cooked and salted food, consuming all things in moderation, gathering foods of the seasons that grow close at hand. The principles include holism, the inseparability of body from land, and a simple local diet.

"People must realize that a diet of surfeit which relies on foods from far-off lands leads the world astray and invites human ills."

## Why People Cannot Find Natural Food

"Illness begins when man moves away from nature, and the severity with which he is afflicted is proportional to his estrangement."

Many city dwellers now try to obtain natural food, "but even if they succeed, without a natural body and spirit prepared to receive such food, merely consuming it does not constitute a natural diet. Farmers today are simply not producing natural foods."

"To push upon people living in different environments and of different races and temperaments a rigid, standardized natural diet is an impossibility."

## The Contradictory Natural Diet Movements

Fukuoka catalogs the conflicting claims of various "natural diet" movements:
- Some say eat only uncooked food; others say cooking enriches the diet
- Brown rice advocates vs. white rice advocates
- Fresh water is good vs. fresh water is bad
- Salt is invaluable vs. salt causes disease
- Fruits are for monkeys not man vs. fruits are best for longevity
- Raw food advocates vs. physicians warning raw diets are dangerous

"Given the right circumstances, any one of these views is correct, so people end up thoroughly confused by what appear to be so many contradictory claims. Nature is a fluid entity that changes from moment to moment."

## The Food Mandalas as Compass

Fukuoka drew food mandalas (Figs. 5.4 and 5.5) as a "compass by which to set one's course." They can guide either a "centrifugal diet" (moving outward toward greater refinement) or a "centripetal diet" (moving inward toward simplicity). But "once these mandalas have been examined, they may be discarded."

## Saving the Healthy

"Doctors are busy saving sick people, but no one is reaching out to save the healthy. Only nature itself can do so." Fukuoka's provocative claim: "Those very people normally thought of as healthy I would call the seriously ill; saving them is of the greatest importance."

This connects to his broader view that modern civilization itself is a form of sickness. See [[fukuoka-vegetable-disease-insect-resistance]].

## The Trinity of Natural Living

"Natural farming, natural diet, and natural healing are all part of one whole. Without an established natural diet, farmers have no idea what it is they should produce. Yet nothing is clearer than that, in the absence of an established method of natural farming, a true natural diet will never take hold and spread. Both natural diet and natural farming can be achieved only by natural people. This trinity begins and is realized at once. The goal of all three elements is the creation of ideal man."

## The Young People on the Mountain

"The young people living primitively in the orchard huts on the mountain, eating a natural diet and practicing natural farming, stand closest to the ultimate goal of mankind."

## The Warning About Fragmented Development

"People look on complacently, thinking that the world goes on developing in the midst of repeated chaos and confusion. But fragmented development without a goal can lead only to chaotic thinking and, ultimately, destruction of the human race."

"Unless we succeed very soon in clarifying what nature is and what man should and should not do, there will be no turning back."

## Dietary Philosophy in Context of Macrobiotics

Fukuoka's dietary thought shares notable parallels with macrobiotics, particularly the emphasis on local, seasonal food and the integration of dietary practice with a broader philosophical worldview. George Ohsawa, the founder of modern macrobiotics, also promoted brown rice as a staple and advocated eating foods grown in one's immediate region. However, Fukuoka diverged significantly from macrobiotics in important ways.

Where macrobiotics relies heavily on the yin-yang classification of foods and prescribes specific dietary ratios (the famous 5:1 grain-to-vegetable ratio), Fukuoka explicitly rejected systematic dietary classification. He argued that yin-yang calculations, while useful as a transitional tool, ultimately replaced one form of intellectual discrimination with another. His "diet of non-discrimination" aimed to transcend all such frameworks entirely. The macrobiotic approach, in Fukuoka's view, was still a "diet of principle" — a step forward from modern eating habits but not the final destination.

## Specific Foods and Dietary Patterns

While Fukuoka discouraged rigid dietary rules, he did offer practical guidance rooted in his observations. He advocated whole grains — particularly brown rice and barley — as the foundation of diet, supplemented by seasonal vegetables, legumes, and small amounts of fermented foods such as miso, pickles, and soy sauce. He viewed fermentation not merely as a preservation technique but as a form of pre-digestion that makes nutrients more bioavailable, a view shared by many traditional food cultures.

He was skeptical of highly processed foods, refined sugars, and chemical additives, viewing them as products of the same fragmented thinking that produced industrial agriculture. He also cautioned against the fashionable pursuit of "health foods" and supplements, arguing that the compulsion to add specific nutrients to one's diet reflected the same craving-driven mentality he critiqued in [[fukuoka-origin-agriculture-dietary-cravings]]. A person eating simple, local food in a state of gratitude, he suggested, would absorb more nourishment than someone anxiously calculating micronutrient intake.

Fukuoka was not strictly vegetarian. He ate fish and occasionally meat, but in small quantities and with an awareness of the life taken. He opposed both the industrialization of animal husbandry and the dogmatic insistence that any single dietary regime is universally correct. His approach was fundamentally contextual: what constituted natural food depended on where one lived, what the land produced, and what the body instinctively sought.

## Connection Between Diet and Natural Farming Philosophy

The relationship between diet and farming in Fukuoka's thought is reciprocal and inseparable. As he articulated in the trinity of natural living, natural farming depends on knowing what natural food is; natural diet depends on natural farming to produce it; and both depend on natural people to practice them. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle: when people eat food from distant lands, farmers are incentivized to grow export crops rather than local food; when farmers grow for distant markets, local diets degrade; when diets degrade, the demand for natural farming diminishes.

This analysis extends his critique of [[fukuoka-european-distribution-system-critique]]. A person who eats imported food out of season is, in Fukuoka's framework, perpetuating the very agricultural system that degrades both land and health. The solution is not to prescribe a universal diet but to restore the local relationship between farmer, land, and eater — so that the question of what to eat answers itself through proximity and season.

Fukuoka's young disciples on his mountain in Japan embodied this principle: they ate only what grew around them, prepared simply, without nutritional anxiety. He considered this not asceticism but liberation — freedom from the endless cycle of craving, calculation, and consumption that characterizes modern eating. The diet of non-discrimination was, in this sense, the dietary expression of his [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]]: the cultivation of a mind empty enough to receive what nature freely offers.

## The Body as Guide

Central to Fukuoka's dietary philosophy is the idea that the healthy body already knows what it needs. He distinguished between appetite driven by craving (which leads to excess and imbalance) and appetite driven by genuine bodily need (which naturally guides toward appropriate food). The problem, as he saw it, is that modern people have lost the sensitivity to distinguish between these two impulses. Processed foods with added salt, sugar, and flavor enhancers effectively short-circuit the body's natural regulatory mechanisms, making it impossible to trust one's own appetite.

Recovery of this sensitivity requires not dietary restriction but dietary simplification. By eating plain, whole foods for a period, the palate gradually recalibrates and the body's natural signals become discernible again. This process parallels the recovery of ecological sensitivity that Fukuoka advocated in farming: just as the farmer must learn to observe rather than intervene, the eater must learn to listen rather than calculate. Both are aspects of the same return to natural perception.

## Seasonal Eating and Local Adaptation

Fukuoka strongly emphasized eating according to the seasons, arguing that foods naturally available in a given season provide exactly what the body needs at that time of year. Winter roots and stored grains offer dense calories and grounding energy; spring greens provide cleansing and revitalization; summer fruits offer hydration and lightness. This seasonal rhythm, he believed, is not merely cultural tradition but an expression of ecological wisdom that modern food systems have disrupted through year-round availability of every ingredient.

The connection between diet and place was equally important. Fukuoka argued that people are best nourished by foods grown in the same soil and climate they inhabit — a principle he called the "inseparability of body from land." A person eating food grown in distant soils consumes, in a sense, a foreign environment. While the body can adapt to such food, it does so at the cost of its natural equilibrium. This principle aligns with the growing understanding in nutritional ecology that local food systems produce diets better matched to regional micronutrient profiles and environmental conditions.
