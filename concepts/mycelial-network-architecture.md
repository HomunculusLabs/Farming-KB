# Mycelial Network Architecture

Mycelial network architecture refers to the structural and functional
organization of fungal mycelium as it grows through substrates, forming
complex interconnected networks analogous to neural networks, transportation
grids, and distributed computing systems.

## Overview

Fungal mycelium consists of tubular filaments called hyphae that branch and
fuse to form a vast, interconnected network. This architecture is not random;
it follows optimized patterns that balance exploration (finding new resources)
with exploitation (transporting nutrients efficiently). Individual hyphae are
typically 1-10 micrometers in diameter, but networks can span hundreds of
meters in forest ecosystems.

## Structural Components

### Hyphae

Hyphae are the fundamental building blocks of mycelial networks. Each hypha
is a tubular cell enclosed by a rigid cell wall made primarily of chitin.
Hyphae grow at their tips through a process called apical extension, where
vesicles containing cell wall materials fuse with the plasma membrane at the
growing tip. Growth rates vary from 0.1 to several millimeters per hour
depending on species and environmental conditions.

### Septa

Most fungi have septate hyphae divided by cross-walls called septa. These
septa contain central pores that allow cytoplasmic streaming between cells,
enabling the transport of nutrients, organelles, and signaling molecules
throughout the network. Septal pores can be regulated by Woronin bodies or
other structures that plug the pore in response to damage.

### Anastomosis

Anastomosis is the process by which hyphae from the same or different
mycelial networks fuse together. This fusion creates a three-dimensional mesh
with redundant pathways. Anastomosis allows fungi to share resources, create
larger foraging networks, and recover from damage by routing around severed
connections.

## Network Topologies

### Foraging Networks

Species like *Phallus impudicus* and *Coprinopsis cinerea* create foraging
networks optimized for resource discovery. These networks start with dense
growth near the inoculation point, then extend sparse cords outward to explore
the environment. When a resource is found, the network reinforces connections
to it by thickening hyphae into cords or rhizomorphs.

### Rhizomorph Networks

Ectomycorrhizal fungi and wood-decay fungi produce rhizomorphs: densely
packed, rope-like bundles of hyphae. These structures allow long-distance
transport of water and nutrients across meters of soil. Armillaria species
produce melanized rhizomorphs that can survive harsh conditions and bridge
between distant food sources.

## Transport Mechanisms

### Cytoplasmic Streaming

Cytoplasmic streaming is the bulk flow of cytoplasm through hyphae, driven
by pressure gradients and motor proteins moving along the cytoskeleton.
Velocities can reach 10-60 micrometers per second. This mechanism rapidly
distributes nutrients, signaling molecules, and even nuclei throughout the
network.

### Translocation

Fungi actively translocate resources from areas of surplus to areas of need.
Carbohydrates, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water are transported over
centimeters to meters. Translocation follows source-sink dynamics where
resource-rich regions (sources) supply resource-poor regions (sinks). This is
analogous to phloem transport in plants but occurs without dedicated vascular
tissue.

## Optimization and Computation

Mycelial networks demonstrate remarkable problem-solving abilities. In
laboratory experiments, *Physarum polycephalum* (a slime mold with similar
network behavior) and true fungi have been shown to find near-optimal
solutions to shortest-path problems, reconnect severed networks efficiently,
and balance trade-offs between cost and resilience. The network continuously
rewires itself: thickening high-traffic connections and pruning unused ones,
a process called adaptive reinforcement.

## Environmental Sensing

Mycelial networks sense and respond to a wide range of environmental cues
including nutrient gradients, pH, light, temperature, mechanical stress, and
chemical signals from other organisms. These signals are integrated across the
network to coordinate growth decisions. The distributed nature of sensing
means that different parts of the same mycelium can respond differently to
local conditions while maintaining overall coherence.

## Ecological Significance

Mycelial networks are fundamental to ecosystem function. They decompose
organic matter, cycle nutrients, form mycorrhizal associations with over 80%
of land plants, and connect individual plants into common mycorrhizal networks
(wood wide webs). A single mycelial network can connect hundreds of trees,
facilitating nutrient exchange and chemical signaling between different
species.
