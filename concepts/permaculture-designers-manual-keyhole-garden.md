In conventional gardens with parallel paths, paths can take up to 50% of the area. Keyhole beds reduce path space to less than 30% of the ground, dramatically increasing productive growing area. The pattern appears frequently in natural systems as lobular edges that create sheltered microhabitats.

## Physical Design

### Basic Structure

A keyhole bed begins as a circular or roughly circular mound of soil, typically 1.5-3m in diameter, with a wedge-shaped path cutting into the center. The path allows the gardener to reach all plants from a single standing position at the widest part of the wedge.

The keyhole shape resembles a circle with a narrow path (the "keyhole" notch) leading to a small cleared area at the center. This central access point allows the gardener to tend the entire bed without stepping on growing soil.

### Construction Steps

1. Mark out a circle on the ground (1.5-3m diameter)
2. Create a wedge-shaped path cutting into the center from one side
3. Build up the bed with compost, topsoil, and organic matter
4. Shape the bed into a slight dome for drainage
5. Optional: edge with stones, logs, or other materials
6. Plant intensively with companion plantings

## Design Principles

### Path Efficiency

The keyhole bed represents the application of "least-path" design to home gardens. By concentrating access to a single entry point, the design:
- Minimizes walking distance for garden maintenance
- Reduces soil compaction (no need to walk on beds)
- Maximizes the ratio of productive area to path area
- Creates a natural flow pattern for garden work

### Edge Effect

Lobular embayments like keyhole beds are obviously sheltered, spacious habitats for gardens and settlements. The curved edges:
- Create varied microclimates around the bed perimeter
- Provide wind shelter in multiple directions
- Increase the total edge length for planting diversity
- Allow different species to occupy different positions around the curve

### Shelter

Notched or lobular edges, such as those achieved by following hill contours, afford sheltered, wetter, drier, hotter, or more exposed micro-habitats for a variety of species. Keyhole beds create similar micro-environmental variation within a single garden unit.

## Companion Planting Zones

A keyhole bed can be planted using the [[permaculture-mandala-garden-design]], a set of four to eight keyhole beds arranged in a circular pattern with a central feature. The mandala garden combines beauty and efficiency to create a productive growing space with a magical, spiritual quality.

## Applications in Gangamma's Mandala

In Mollison's Gangamma's Mandala design for tropical home gardens, keyhole beds are integral elements. The mandala layout includes:
- Keyhole paths (designated "C" elements) alongside sucked, vice stakes, or gravel paths
- Keyhole wedge beds with paths beside planted narrow beds (designated "D" elements)
- Beds approximately 1.5m across, with all areas accessible without stepping on soil

## Design Variations

Keyhole beds are not limited to circular forms:
- The basic pattern can be rectangular, triangular, or freeform
- Beds can be raised or ground-level
- The keyhole notch can face any direction based on access needs
- In sloping terrain, the keyhole can be terraced
- The concept works at any scale from windowsill to field
