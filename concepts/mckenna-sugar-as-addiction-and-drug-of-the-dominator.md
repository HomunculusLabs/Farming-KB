McKenna draws a deliberate comparison between sugar and hard drugs. As with all stimulants, ingestion of sugar is followed by a brief euphoric rush, which is itself followed by depression and guilt.

Sugar addiction rarely occurs alone as a syndrome. Mixed addictions, particularly sugar combined with caffeine, are far more common and more destructive than either substance alone.

Some addicts use diet pills to control soaring body weight and then take tranquilizers to mitigate the jitteriness caused by the pills. This cascading polydrug pattern mirrors the patterns seen with harder substances.

After alcohol and tobacco, McKenna declares sugar the most damaging addictive substance consumed by human beings. Its uncontrolled use constitutes a major chemical dependence.

## Sugar and the Slave Trade

The distortion of human institutions caused by crack cocaine is nothing compared with what the European desire for sugar accomplished in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

While the modern drug trade involves conditions approaching slave labor, it is not slavery sanctioned by mendacious popes and openly pursued by corrupt but legitimate governments. A further difference is that the modern drug trade is not involved in the wholesale kidnapping and mass murder of populations.

The roots of slavery in Europe reach far back. During Periclean Athens, two-thirds of residents were slaves. Under the Roman Imperium, slaves had no civil rights and their testimony was acceptable only if obtained by torture.

In 1432 Prince Henry the Navigator of Portugal established the first commercial cane sugar plantation in Madeira. More than a thousand men, including debtors, convicts, and unconverted Jews, were taken from Europe to work the sugar operations.

Slavery diminished with the dissolution of the Roman empire, and feudalism replaced it with serfdom. But the sugar economy reversed this progress, re-establishing mass chattel slavery on a scale not seen since antiquity.
