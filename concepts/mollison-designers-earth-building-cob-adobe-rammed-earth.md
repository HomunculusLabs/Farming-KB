# Earth Building - Cob, Adobe, Rammed Earth

Earth building represents humanity's oldest and most widespread
construction tradition. In Permaculture: A Designers Manual, Bill
Mollison presents earthen construction as a cornerstone of sustainable
building, emphasizing that earth is universally available, requires
minimal processing, and produces structures with excellent thermal
performance and virtually zero embodied energy.

## Soil Selection and Testing

The suitability of local soil for earth building depends on the
proportion of clay, silt, sand, and gravel. Different techniques
require different ratios:

- **Cob:** 15-25% clay, 75-85% sand/silt. Must bind when wet but
  resist cracking when dry.
- **Adobe:** 15-30% clay with sand, sometimes stabilized with straw.
- **Rammed earth:** 5-15% clay with well-graded aggregate (sand and
  gravel). Clean, angular aggregate produces the strongest walls.
- **Earth bags:** Wide tolerance; sandy soils work well with cement
  or lime stabilization.

Mollison recommends simple field tests: the jar test for particle size
distribution, the ball test for plasticity (forms a ball without
cracking), the ribbon test for clay content, and the shrinkage test on
dried bricks. If local soil is unsuitable, blending two soil sources
(clay pit and sand pit) achieves the right mix.

## Cob Construction

Cob (from Old English "lump") is a monolithic hand-formed earthen
building technique. A wet mixture of clay-rich subsoil, sand, and
straw fiber is mixed by foot on a tarp and applied in layers (courses)
onto a stone or concrete stem wall. Each course is allowed to partially
dry before the next is added. Walls are typically 500-700mm thick.

Cob walls are sculptural -- builders can shape niches, shelves, curved
walls, and built-in furniture directly into the structure. The
addition of long straw fibers (100-300mm) creates tensile strength
that resists cracking. Mollison notes cob's suitability for [[permaculture-design]]
in that it requires no machinery, no forms, and no cement. Labor is
the primary cost, making cob ideal for owner-builder and community
projects. Cob buildings in Devon, England, have survived 500+ years
with only lime wash maintenance.

## Adobe Construction

Adobe consists of sun-dried earth bricks, typically 300x150x100mm,
molded from a clay-sand-straw mix and cured in the sun for 2-4 weeks.
Adobe bricks are laid with earth mortar in running bond pattern. Walls
are usually 300mm (one brick) or 450mm (one-and-a-half brick) thick.

Adobe suits arid and semi-arid climates where rapid drying is possible.
It is among the most traditional building methods worldwide, from the
American Southwest to the Middle East, Africa, and Latin America.
Mollison connects adobe to traditional ecological knowledge and
emphasizes its revival as a climate-appropriate, low-cost building
technology. Stabilization with 5-10% cement or lime improves water
resistance for damp climates, though pure adobe performs well with
proper roof overhang and raised foundations.

## Rammed Earth

Rammed earth (pise) construction involves compacting damp earth
(with 5-15% clay) between temporary formwork in layers of 100-150mm.
Each layer is tamped to maximum density using manual or pneumatic
rammers. The result is a monolithic wall with visible horizontal
stratification that becomes a distinctive aesthetic feature.

Rammed earth walls are typically 300-450mm thick and achieve
compressive strengths of 2-10 MPa. The addition of 5-10% cement
creates "stabilized rammed earth" with higher strength and water
resistance. Mollison notes rammed earth's thermal mass properties:
the dense walls absorb heat during the day and release it at night,
making it ideal for climates with large diurnal temperature swings.
See [[passive-solar-design]] for integration strategies.

## Earth Bag Construction

Earth bag (superadobe) construction fills polypropylene or natural
fiber bags with damp earth or sand, laying them in courses like
overlapping bricks. Barbed wire between courses provides tensile
bonding. The system was developed by Nader Khalili and adopted widely
in permaculture building.

Earth bags excel for dome structures, retaining walls, and emergency
shelter. They require no foundation in some cases and can use nearly
any soil type. Mollison appreciates earth bag construction for disaster
relief and [[mollison-designers-design-for-disaster-resilience]] applications due to its
speed, low cost, and structural integrity.

## Compressed Earth Blocks (CEBs)

Compressed earth blocks are made by hydraulically pressing a damp
clay-sand mix (sometimes with 5-8% cement) in a mechanical press. CEBs
are uniform, interlocking, and stronger than sun-dried adobe. They can
be produced on-site with small portable presses, making them suitable
for [[village-development-in-permaculture]]. CEB walls typically use thin mortar
joints or dry stacking with surface bonding cement.

## Stabilization Methods

Unstabilized earth buildings require protection from direct rain and
rising damp. Stabilization options include:

- Cement stabilization (5-10%): Increases strength and water
  resistance but adds embodied energy
- Lime stabilization (3-8%): Improves workability and water
  resistance; more breathable than cement
- Bitumen emulsion: Water-resistant surface treatment
- Natural stabilizers: Plant fibers (straw, sisal), animal dung,
  casein, and ox blood (traditional additives)

## Integration with Permaculture Systems

Mollison emphasizes that earth building materials come directly from
the building site, creating zero-transport construction. Earthen walls
decompose back to soil at end of life. The thermal mass of earth
buildings synergizes with [[mollison-designers-passive-solar-heating-building-design]], and earth can be
combined with [[mollison-designers-straw-bale-building-techniques]] for hybrid insulated-mass walls.
Earthen structures serve as [[aquaculture-in-permaculture]] tank walls, root cellars,
greenhouse thermal mass, and [[food-forest-design]] boundary walls in broader
permaculture site designs.
