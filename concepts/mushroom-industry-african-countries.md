# Mushroom Industry in African Countries

The mushroom industry in Sub-Saharan African countries is largely in its infancy
but growing rapidly. Chapter 10 of the MushWorld Handbook provides in-depth
research on the mushroom industries of Kenya, Zimbabwe, and Uganda. Despite
enormous potential driven by abundant agricultural residues, diverse climates,
and growing demand from tourism and urban populations, the industry faces
significant challenges including limited spawn availability, lack of technical
expertise, poor market infrastructure, and widespread misconceptions about
mushroom consumption.

## Kenya

### Current Industry Status

Kenya is a medium-sized East African tropical country of 582,646 sq. km with
a population of 30 million. The mushroom industry produces approximately 500
tons per annum, which is far below estimated potential of over 100,000 tons.
Four major commercial farms account for the bulk of production: Agridutt Ltd.,
Rift Valley Mushrooms, Olive Mushrooms, and Devani and Kanchan Mushrooms.

Button mushroom ([[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]]) dominates at over 95% of production volume.
Oyster [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] is not yet popular, with only four small farms
producing approximately 120kg each per week. Shiitake production is similarly
limited, with some farms producing around 20kg per week.

### Pricing and Market

Mushroom prices in Kenya are very high relative to other vegetables. Agaricus
sells in 250g packs at KES150 (USD2). Shiitake commands KES1,000 (USD13) per
kg. Many poor Kenyans earn less than a dollar daily, making mushrooms
inaccessible to much of the population. Starting a medium-sized mushroom farm
requires approximately KES40 million (USD520,000) including land, construction,
machinery, and staff training abroad.

### Key Constraints

- No domestic spawn manufacturing company exists; farmers must import or
  produce their own with variable quality
- Culture preservation is difficult due to unreliable electricity; vigour
  diminishes after six months of storage
- Existing farmers keep growing procedures highly secretive
- Trained personnel are expensive, as most received training abroad
- The Ministry of Agriculture has no officers specifically assigned to
  mushroom extension services

### Climate Zones for Cultivation

Kenya has three main cultivation zones:
- **Lowlands** (21-34C): Hot, poor in agricultural residues; suitable for
  Pleurotus species
- **Moderate altitude** (18-27C, 800-1,500mm rainfall): Most agricultural
  activity; abundant wheat, rice, maize, and cotton residues
- **Highlands** (14-23C): Cold; suitable for Agaricus bisporus; fewer
  agricultural residues but can source from nearby moderate areas

### Recommendations for Growth

- Establish a local high-quality spawn manufacturer
- Assign dedicated mushroom extension officers within the Ministry of
  Agriculture
- Support women's groups and small farmers with donor funding
- Encourage information exchange between researchers and farmers
- Establish a dedicated mushroom research institution
- Promote mushroom consumption through nutritional education campaigns

## Zimbabwe

### Industry Structure

The Zimbabwe mushroom industry is dominated by small-scale producers and a few
well-established companies. Approximately ten large-scale producers account
for about 300 tons of button mushrooms and 50 tons of [[query-how-to-grow-oyster-mushrooms-at-home]]
annually. Small-scale producers concentrate on oyster mushroom, accounting for
about 60% of total annual production of that species.

Over 60 wild edible species exist in Zimbabwe, found primarily in Miombo
woodlands. The most important wild mushrooms are Termitomyces (termite fungi)
and Cantharellus (chanterelles), which have ready export markets to South
Africa and Italy.

### Climate and Substrate Resources

Zimbabwe's climate varies from subtropical Lowveld (exceeding 35C in summer) to
temperate Eastern Highlands (18C average, 2,500mm+ annual rainfall). The
diversity in soils and climate allows production of various crops with residues
suitable as [[query-what-are-the-best-mushroom-substrates-for-different-species]]. Common substrates include wheat straw, grass,
banana leaves, sawdust, water hyacinth, and groundnut shells.

### Mushroom Prices and Marketing

Wholesale prices range from USD5-7.50/kg for oyster and USD7.50-10/kg for
button. Retail prices reach USD10-15/kg for oyster and USD12.50-17.50/kg for
button. Market information is often kept secret. Primary buyers include fast
food chains, hotels, restaurants, supermarkets, and exporting companies.

### Key Organizations

The Biotechnology Trust of Zimbabwe (BTZ) and the [[holmgren-gigantism-intermediate-technology-and-small-scale]]
Development Group (ITDG) have been instrumental in training rural farmers and
promoting mushroom adoption. BTZ has cultured high-quality spawn at the
University of Zimbabwe, while ITDG focuses on hands-on farmer training.

### Research and Development

University of Zimbabwe research projects include evaluation of mushroom yields
under different crop residues, determination of suitable spawn carriers (wheat
outperformed millet, crushed maize, and sorghum), comparison of biological
efficiencies of different strains, and post-harvest storage studies. Breeding
trials aim to select suitable strains for low rainfall and high temperature
regions.

## Common African Industry Challenges

Across both Kenya and Zimbabwe, shared challenges include:

- Widespread fear of [[european-mushroom-poisoning-history]] among urban populations
- Lack of dedicated mushroom research institutions
- High cost and limited availability of quality spawn
- Poor information exchange between farmers and researchers
- Inadequate extension services and affordable training
- Buyers rather than growers often determine market prices
- Lack of business management knowledge among smallholder growers
- Contamination and pest management without reliance on chemicals
