# Mushroom Industry in African Countries

The mushroom industry in Sub-Saharan African countries is largely in its infancy
but growing rapidly. Chapter 10 of the MushWorld Handbook provides in-depth
research on the mushroom industries of Kenya, Zimbabwe, and Uganda. Despite
enormous potential driven by abundant agricultural residues, diverse climates,
and growing demand from tourism and urban populations, the industry faces
significant challenges including limited spawn availability, lack of technical
expertise, poor market infrastructure, and widespread misconceptions about
mushroom consumption.

## Kenya

### Current Industry Status

Kenya is a medium-sized [[comparison-cavendish-vs-east-african-highland-bananas]] tropical country of 582,646 sq. km with
a population of 30 million. The mushroom industry produces approximately 500
tons per annum, which is far below estimated potential of over 100,000 tons.
Four major commercial farms account for the bulk of production: Agridutt Ltd.,
Rift Valley Mushrooms, Olive Mushrooms, and Devani and Kanchan Mushrooms.

[[growing-gourmet-button-mushroom-agaricus-cultivation]] ([[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]]) dominates at over 95% of production volume.
Oyster [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] is not yet popular, with only four small farms
producing approximately 120kg each per week. Shiitake production is similarly
limited, with some farms producing around 20kg per week.

### Pricing and Market

Mushroom prices in Kenya are very high relative to other vegetables. Agaricus
sells in 250g packs at KES150 (USD2). Shiitake commands KES1,000 (USD13) per
kg. Many poor Kenyans earn less than a dollar daily, making mushrooms
inaccessible to much of the population. Starting a medium-sized mushroom farm
requires approximately KES40 million (USD520,000) including land, construction,
machinery, and staff training abroad.

### Key Constraints

- No domestic spawn manufacturing company exists; farmers must import or
  produce their own with variable quality
- [[biodiversity-fungal-culture-preservation]] is difficult due to unreliable electricity; vigour
  diminishes after six months of storage
- Existing farmers keep growing procedures highly secretive
- Trained personnel are expensive, as most received training abroad
- The Ministry of Agriculture has no officers specifically assigned to
  mushroom extension services

### Climate Zones for Cultivation

Kenya has three main cultivation zones:
- **Lowlands** (21-34C): Hot, poor in agricultural residues; suitable for
  Pleurotus species
- **Moderate altitude** (18-27C, 800-1,500mm rainfall): Most agricultural
  activity; abundant wheat, rice, maize, and cotton residues
- **Highlands** (14-23C): Cold; suitable for [[agaricus-bisporus]]; fewer
  agricultural residues but can source from nearby moderate areas

### Recommendations for Growth

- Establish a local high-quality spawn manufacturer
- Assign dedicated mushroom extension officers within the Ministry of
  Agriculture
- Support women's groups and small farmers with donor funding
- Encourage information exchange between researchers and farmers
- Establish a dedicated [[chaga-mushroom-research]] institution
- Promote mushroom consumption through nutritional education campaigns
