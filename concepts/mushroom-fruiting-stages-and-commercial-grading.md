# Mushroom Fruiting Stages and Commercial Grading

## Overview

The development of a mushroom from invisible [[mycelium]] to harvestable fruiting body follows a well-defined sequence of stages. Understanding these stages is essential for timing harvests to achieve the highest quality product. Commercial mushroom grading is directly tied to the stage of development at which the mushroom is picked.

## The Fruiting Sequence

### 1. Mycelial Maturation

Before fruiting can begin, the mycelium (spawn) must pervade the substratum in all directions, absorbing the necessary water as well as organic and inorganic food materials. During this vegetative phase, a considerable surplus of nutrients accumulates within the mycelial network. This accumulation of food materials is demonstrated by the rapidity with which mushrooms are produced once the fruiting process is initiated.

### 2. Cushion Formation

When the spawn has developed sufficiently throughout the substratum and conditions for fruiting are satisfactory, minute cushion-like areas of growth appear on the large threads of mycelium. These cushions represent the earliest visible stage of reproductive development and are the sites where the fruiting bodies will form.

### 3. Pin Heads

The cushion-like areas soon develop into spherical structures known as pin heads — small, snow-white dots that are the first unmistakable signs of mushroom production. At this stage, the individual mushroom primordia can be identified, though they are extremely small.

### 4. Buttons

The pin heads grow fairly rapidly in size and are soon recognized as "buttons" — young mushrooms that have expanded to a recognizable form but have not yet fully matured. During the button stage, the cap is still tightly closed against the stem, connected by the partial veil.

### 5. Mature Mushrooms

The buttons continue to expand until they reach full maturity as open mushrooms. At maturity, the veil breaks, exposing the gills on the underside of the cap, and the cap expands to its full diameter.

## Timing of Development

Under the best conditions for commercial mushroom production, approximately seven to ten days elapse between the appearance of pin heads and full maturity. This timeline can vary depending on temperature, available nutrients, moisture levels, and the genetic characteristics of the strain being cultivated.

## Commercial Grading Standards

### The Premium: Pre-Veil-Break Harvest

Commercial mushrooms are graded highest if picked just before the veil breaks. At this stage, the mushroom has achieved maximum size while the cap remains closed, the flesh is firm and dense, and the gills are still protected. Mushrooms harvested at this stage have the longest shelf life, the best texture for cooking, and the most attractive appearance for market display.

### Lower Grades

Mushrooms that are allowed to develop past the veil-break stage are graded lower. Once the cap opens, the mushrooms:
- Lose firmness and become more fragile
- Have a shorter shelf life
- Are more susceptible to damage during packing and shipping
- May develop a less desirable texture when cooked
- Show darkening of the gills from pink to brown as spores mature

### Factors Affecting Grade

Several factors beyond harvest timing affect the commercial grade of mushrooms:
- **Size uniformity**: Consistent sizing commands premium prices
- **Color**: Bright white caps are preferred; yellowing or bruising reduces grade
- **Cleanliness**: Freedom from compost particles and soil
- **Freshness**: Mushrooms should be packed and shipped immediately after harvesting
- **Absence of pests or disease**: Any sign of infestation or bacterial spotting disqualifies the product

## Shipping and Market Considerations

### Packaging for Quality

Mushrooms should be packed in small quantities — never more than 10 pounds per basket — because the heat generated by larger quantities causes a "sweating" process. This sweating encourages bacterial and mold development, leading rapidly to loss of flavor and ultimate spoilage. The packaging practices of California fruit growers, where neatness of the package has been shown to be most important, provide a useful model. Mushrooms should be packed in small baskets as one would pack grapes, with attention to aeration and market demands for sorting and selection.

### Shipping Duration

When properly packed and shipped immediately after gathering, mushrooms can withstand 36 to 48 hours of transit during winter months with little danger of deterioration.

### Market Timing and Price

A critical concern for mushroom growers is market timing. The small grower frequently experiences difficulty because beds yield most heavily when outside conditions are favorable — typically during the first continued warm weather of spring — at precisely the time when markets are flooded with product from all producers facing the same conditions. This is a certain indication that growing conditions (particularly temperature control) have not been adequately managed.

The large grower who can control conditions and anticipate this inevitable seasonal flooding of the market will fare better. A well-established high-class trade is not materially injured by seasonal gluts.

## Price Structure

During the period described in this source, the price structure for mushrooms showed considerable variation:
- **Hotel fancy trade**: 40 to 50 cents per pound net
- **Commission men/wholesale**: Less than the hotel trade, averaging below 40 cents
- **Peak production season**: 25 cents or less per pound, sometimes below cost of production
- **Private consumer retail**: 60 to 75 cents per pound

The great future development of the mushroom industry was seen as lying in the direction of retail trade development and direct distribution to consumers, bypassing the wholesale commission system that compressed grower returns.
