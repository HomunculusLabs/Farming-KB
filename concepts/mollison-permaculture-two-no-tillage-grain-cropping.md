# Mollison No-Tillage Grain Cropping and Fukuoka Method

Until the work of Masanobu Fukuoka, there was no satisfactory basis for including grain and legume crops in permaculture. The system outlined in The One-Straw Revolution seems to have solved the problems of no-dig grain cultivation. Fukuoka's methodology, combined with data from P. A. Yeomans and David King, evolved a no-dig and permanent grain-crop system that fits into the [[natural-farm-permaculture-ethics-principles]].

[[permaculture-design]] regarded the Fukuoka system as a breakthrough that brought grain production within the reach of permaculture designers working at any scale, from small homesteads to broadacre farms.

## The Simultaneous Rotation System

The system combines the usual rotation of legume/grain/root crop/pasture/fallow/legume into a single grain/legume mixed crop. There is every reason to do the same for tree-crop systems, including leguminous trees (wattle, black locust, tree lucerne) in any orchard, nut-crop or timber-crop situation.

Any smallholder can, without tractor or machinery, produce a heavy crop of grains and legumes if simultaneous rotation is practised. The method is very well suited to sewage or sullage disposal from holding lagoons, where no poultry manure would be needed.

The principles of continuous mulch with clover plus double-cropping using winter and spring sown grains is what makes it possible to use small areas (400 square meters or less) to support a family of five on grain.

This approach aligns with [[fukuoka-direct-seeded-no-tillage-rice-barley-method]] principles that avoid soil disturbance, preserving soil structure and the biological communities that maintain fertility. It also connects to [[jadam-cover-crop-soil-temperature-management]] that keep the soil covered at all times.

## Paddy Rice Preparation

If paddy rice is to be grown, the area must first be graded or levelled, and a low bund (water-wall) built around the plot, so that 50 mm or so of water can lie on the ground in December. After levelling or preparation in summer, the area has lime or dolomite spread over it, watered in, and made ready for autumn planting.

## The Mulch System

To start the continuous crop system, a complete seed-free mulch cover of straw, seagrass, shredded paper or sawdust is applied at about 900 kg per 1000 square meters (8000 lb/acre). If no mulch is available, seed can be covered as usual by raking in.

The [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-system]] is central to the success of no-tillage grain growing. The mulch suppresses weeds, conserves moisture, moderates soil temperature, and as it breaks down, feeds the soil organisms that maintain fertility. Over time, the mulch layer builds into a rich humus that supports productive crops without any need for synthetic fertilizers.

## Annual Cycle

The system operates across five plots simultaneously, each with a different grain rotation. In April, seed is broadcast below the mulch. A typical mix includes rice with white clover and a winter grain (rye, barley, millet, winter wheat, or oats) on different plots.

In April, a thin layer of chicken manure is broadcast over the area. Use clover at 1 kg per hectare, rye and other grains at 7 to 16 kg per hectare, and rice at 6 to 11 kg per hectare. Seed can be scattered first, then straw-covered, thus protecting it from birds. In the second year, rye and clover are sown into the ripe rice crop.

In May, the previous year's rice is reaped, the crop dried on racks for 2 to 3 weeks, and threshed. All rice straw and husks are returned to the field. Unhusked rice is resown within a month of harvesting.

From June through September, light grazing of the winter crops by sheep or geese assists the stooling of plants and adds manure. When the crop has reached 150 mm or so, about 100 ducks per hectare will reduce pests and add manure.

In November, rye, barley, and other grains are harvested, stacked to dry for 7 to 10 days. The rice is trodden but recovers. When other grains are threshed, all straw and husks are returned to the fields, with each straw type moved onto a different plot.

In December, only rice remains. Summer weeds may sprout; these are weakened by flooding for 7 to 10 days, until the clover is yellow but not dead. From January through March, the field is kept at 50 to 80 percent saturation under rice.

## Long-Term Results

Fukuoka has maintained this no-dig cycle for 25 years, and his soil is improving, with no fertilizer other than chicken and duck manure, no sprays, and no herbicides. In clean-tilled ground the amount of seed needed is 4 to 5 times as much as in the straw-mulch method.

The [[fukuoka-direct-seeded-no-tillage-rice-barley-method]] demonstrates that grain yields comparable to or exceeding conventional agriculture are achievable without soil disturbance, synthetic inputs, or heavy machinery. Over time, soil health actually improves rather than degrading, making the system increasingly productive.

## Grain Species for Permaculture

### Rice (Oryza sativa)

A short-day cereal suited to latitudes to 40 degrees N and S. It is self-pollinating. Good yields may reach 3,000 to 4,000 kg per hectare. Where rice cannot be grown (e.g. very cool areas) other grains may be substituted.

### Rye (Secale cereale)

A long-day plant suited to cool areas; usually winter-grown but some spring types are available. Ripens in about 37 to 71 days. Good yields reach 2,800 kg per hectare.

### Wheat (Triticum aestivum)

A long-day plant for cool areas. Some varieties are grown in Alaska. Both winter and spring wheats exist. Self-pollinating. Good yield is 1,100 kg per hectare.

### Barley (Hordeum vulgare)

A long-day plant for cool areas, sub-tropical to arctic. Spring types mature in 60 to 70 days, winter types in 160 days. Self-pollinated.

The choice of grain species should be matched to the [[mollison-permaculture-two-zone-planning-and-site-layout]]. In cooler regions, rye and oats are most reliable; in warmer areas, rice and millet offer the best yields. Diversity of grain species provides resilience against crop failure.

## Seed Protection

Where sparrows are a problem, the grains are mixed with mud, pressed through wire-mesh and rolled into small balls, or dampened and shaken in a tray of clay dust to form mud-coated pellets. Pellets can also be formed by extruding mud and grain through a domestic mincer, onto a vibrating table of dust or flour.

## Integration with Permaculture Design

The bunds around the paddy-fields should be mown or planted with shrubs or tall perennials to reduce weed invasion. It helps if the area of bunds around the crop is planted to coprosma, comfrey, citrus, mulberry, lemon-grass, tree lucerne, pampas, or other weed-controlling shelter plant. Mulch with sawdust under these borders to prevent weed re-invasion.

Where a paddy is not possible, dry-land rice or other grain species can be used, and spray irrigation replaces summer flooding. In monsoon areas, summer rain should suffice.

### Livestock Integration

The integration of ducks, geese, and sheep into the grain system exemplifies [[pdc-passive-design-principles]]. Each animal performs a useful function: ducks control pests in the paddy, geese graze weeds and stimulate tillering, and sheep add manure while grazing stubble. This multi-functionality is a hallmark of permaculture systems design.

### Soil Building

Over years of no-tillage management with continuous mulch, the soil develops a rich crumb structure teeming with earthworms, fungi, and beneficial bacteria. This connects directly to [[soil-building-permaculture]], where the goal is not merely to avoid damaging the soil but to actively improve it with each cropping cycle.

### Water Management

The water management aspects of the Fukuoka system—bunds, controlled flooding, and moisture retention through mulch—relate to broader Permaculture Water Systems design. Even in non-paddy systems, the principles of capturing, storing, and slowly releasing water apply.

### Green Manures and Cover Crops

White clover serves as a living mulch and nitrogen fixer throughout the system, connecting to [[mollison-designers-cover-crops-and-green-manure-species]] used across permaculture. The clover suppresses weeds, fixes atmospheric nitrogen, and provides a continuous ground cover that protects the soil surface.
