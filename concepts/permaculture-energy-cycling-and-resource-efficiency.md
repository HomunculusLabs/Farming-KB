# [[permaculture-design]] is fundamentally about capturing, storing, and
cycling energy more effectively at every scale — from the household garden to
the bioregion.

## Energy Descent Planning

Holmgren positions energy descent as the central reality of our age. The
tapping of fossil fuels during the industrial era caused the spectacular
explosion in human numbers, technology, and every novel feature of modern
society. The inevitable depletion of these fuels within a few generations will
see a return to the general patterns observable in nature and pre-industrial
societies dependent on [[holmgren-wind-power-and-renewable-energy-debate]]. Permaculture does not attempt to
sustain the unsustainable; it asks how human settlements can function with
progressively less energy while maintaining or improving quality of life.

The contrast between industrial and sustainable culture is stark: industrial
culture runs on non-renewable energy with linear material flows, centralised
organisation, fast movement, and reductionist thinking; sustainable culture runs
on renewable energy with cyclical material flows, distributed networks, slow
movement, and holistic thinking. Permaculture is a whole-hearted adaptation to
the ecological realities of decline, which Holmgren argues are as natural and
creative as those of growth.

## Biological vs. Fossil Energy

A fundamental distinction in Holmgren's framework is between biological energy
(captured daily by photosynthesis and stored in living systems) and fossil
energy (ancient biological storages mined and burned at rates millions of times
faster than they were created). Industrial agriculture substitutes fossil energy
for biological energy and human labour, creating systems that are spectacularly
productive in the short term but degrade their own resource base. Permaculture
designs systems that maximise the capture and use of biological energy while
strategically using remaining fossil energy to build long-term biological
capital — especially trees and soils — that will sustain future generations.

Traditional agriculture was labour-intensive (dependent on human and animal
energy), industrial agriculture is energy-intensive (dependent on fossil fuels),
and permaculture-designed systems are information and design intensive — they
achieve productivity through careful design rather than brute energy input.
This distinction is not merely technical but civilisational: the transition from
fossil energy dependence to biological energy reliance defines the cultural
transformation permaculture seeks to guide.

## Emergy (Embodied Energy)

Holmgren draws extensively on H.T. Odum's concept of **emergy** — written in
capitals to distinguish it from ordinary energy — which means all the available
energy that was used in the work of making a product or service. It is a
contraction of "embodied energy," a more general term covering several
methodologies. Emergy accounting provides the most holistic measure of
environmental cost because it traces every energy input back to its solar
origin, revealing the true energetic cost of goods and services that market
prices completely obscure.

One key application is the **emergy yield ratio**, which compares the emergy of
a resource with the emergy feedback from the economy required to produce it. A
value greater than 1 indicates net gain; over 4 is a high-value source
comparable to resources currently fuelling the economy. Odum's studies show
that annual biomass crops have [[holmgren-biomass-fuels-and-energy-yield-ratios]] barely above 1, wood plantations
yield 1.5 to 4, and 300-year-old rainforest yields 12. The pattern is clear:
the more the work is left to nature, the greater the net yield — but the longer
the time required. This finding challenges the industrial assumption that
intensive management always produces the best return.

Emergy analysis of Costa Rica showed it could sustainably support only 53% of
its population at current consumption levels — compared to 80% suggested by
Ecological Footprint analysis. This demonstrates that emergy accounting
generally provides a deeper and more challenging assessment of sustainability
than simpler methods.
