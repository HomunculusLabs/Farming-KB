# Grafting and [[fruit-tree-propagation]]

Mollison covers grafting extensively in Permaculture: A Designer's Manual as a
critical skill for permaculture practitioners. Grafting allows the combination
of a desired fruiting variety (the scion) with a root system adapted to local
soil and climate conditions (the rootstock), enabling the cultivation of
varieties that would otherwise fail or underperform. It is central to
establishing productive [[food-forest-design-guide]] and orchard
systems.

## Why Graft

Seed-grown fruit trees exhibit enormous genetic variability. A seed from a
good apple may produce a tree with mediocre or inedible fruit. Grafting
preserves the exact genetic characteristics of the parent variety. Additionally,
rootstocks can be selected for specific traits: dwarfing, disease resistance,
tolerance of wet or dry soils, early bearing, or compatibility with the scion
variety.

Grafting also allows a single tree to bear multiple varieties (topworking), a
useful technique for extending harvest seasons, ensuring cross-pollination, or
testing new varieties without planting separate trees.

## Rootstock Selection

Rootstock choice is as important as scion selection. Mollison emphasizes that
the rootstock determines tree size, soil adaptation, disease resistance, and
longevity.

Common fruit tree rootstocks:

**Apple:** Malling (M) and Malling-Merton (MM) series. M9 and M26 produce
dwarf trees (2-3 m), suitable for intensive plantings. MM106 and MM111 produce
semi-dwarf to standard trees (3-5 m), more appropriate for permaculture
systems where larger canopies provide shade, mulch, and habitat.

**Pear:** Quince rootstocks (Quince A, Quince C) produce dwarf trees. Seedling
pear or Pyrus calleryana for full-size trees.

**Stone fruits (peach, plum, cherry):** Various Prunus species rootstocks.
Myrobalan plum is widely compatible and adaptable. St. Julien A for semi-dwarf
plums. Mahaleb and Mazzard for cherries.

**Citrus:** Trifoliate orange (Poncirus trifoliata) for cold tolerance and
dwarfing. Rough lemon and Carrizo citrange for vigorous growth in warm
climates.

Select rootstocks suited to local conditions. In [[permaculture-design]], standard
and semi-dwarf rootstocks are generally preferred because larger trees
contribute more to the overall system -- more leaf fall for mulch, more shade
for understory crops, more biomass for chop-and-drop mulching.

## Compatibility

Grafting is most successful between closely related species. Intra-species
grafts (apple on apple, peach on peach) are generally reliable. Inter-species
grafts within the same genus may work (pear on quince, plum on peach) but
require compatible combinations. Inter-genus grafts rarely succeed.

Signs of incompatibility include: early leaf-out or delayed leaf-out on the
scion relative to the rootstock, swollen or sunken union, premature decline,
and breakage at the graft union.

## Grafting Methods

### Whip-and-Tongue Graft

The standard method for grafting scion and rootstock of similar diameter
(6-13 mm). Both pieces are cut at a matching slant (about 2.5 cm long). A
tongue is cut into each slant by making a downward slice about one-third into
the wood. The two pieces are interlocked so tongues engage, cambium layers
align on at least one side, and the joint is wrapped tightly with grafting tape
or rubber budding strips. This method provides strong mechanical strength and
large cambium contact area. Timing: late winter to early spring, before bud
break.

### Cleft Graft

Used to topwork established trees or when scion and rootstock differ
significantly in diameter. The rootstock is cut square across and split
downward with a clefting tool to a depth of 5-8 cm. Two scion pieces, each
cut to a long wedge (about 5 cm), are inserted into the cleft, one on each
side, with cambium layers aligned. The cleft closes on the scions from natural
pressure. Seal exposed surfaces with grafting wax. Timing: late winter to early
spring.

### Bud Grafting (T-Budding)

The preferred method for summer and early autumn grafting. A single bud is
removed from the scion variety as a shield-shaped piece of bark with a thin
sliver of wood attached. A T-shaped cut is made in the rootstock bark, the
bark is lifted, and the bud shield is inserted beneath it. The bud union is
wrapped with budding rubber or tape, leaving the bud itself exposed. The
following spring, the rootstock is cut back above the bud, forcing the scion
bud to grow. T-budding works well for stone fruits, citrus, and roses.

### Side-Veneer Graft

A slanting cut is made into the side of the rootstock, removing a thin slice
of wood and bark. The scion is cut to a matching wedge and inserted into the
cut. This method does not require cutting back the rootstock top, making it
useful for evergreen species and situations where maintaining the rootstock
leader is desirable. Commonly used for avocado, mango, and other subtropical
species.

## Timing

Grafting success depends on the physiological state of both scion and
rootstock. The general rule: graft when cambium layers are active (slipping
bark) in the rootstock and the scion is dormant.

- **Whip-and-tongue and cleft:** Late winter to early spring (before bud
  break). Collect scion wood during dormancy and store in cool, moist
  conditions (refrigerator in damp newspaper or moss) until use.
- **T-budding:** Mid-summer to early autumn when bark slips freely. Active
  rootstock cambium allows easy bud insertion.
- **Side-veneer:** Spring or late summer depending on species.

Collect scion wood from healthy, disease-free, vigorously growing trees of
known variety. Select 1-year-old wood with plump buds. Cut scions 20-30 cm
long, label immediately, and store properly.

## Aftercare

After grafting, protect the union from drying, physical damage, and excessive
heat. Seal exposed cut surfaces with grafting wax or tree wound compound.
Remove any growth below the graft union that originates from the rootstock.
Once the scion has produced 30-60 cm of new growth, remove or loosen wrapping
to prevent girdling. Staking may be needed to support weak graft unions until
the wood strengthens.
