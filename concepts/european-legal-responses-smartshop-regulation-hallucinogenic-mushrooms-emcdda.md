The legal status of hallucinogenic mushrooms in Europe has been one of the most contentious and evolving aspects of drug policy in the 21st century. A unique feature of mushroom legislation is the confusion created by the fact that [[gartz-conocybe-cyanopus-rare-psychoactive-mushroom]] species grow naturally in the wild across Europe.

### The "Preparation" Loophole
A key legal concept that emerged in several jurisdictions was the distinction between "fresh" and "prepared" mushrooms. In some countries, fresh mushrooms were considered to be in their natural state and therefore not subject to drug controls, while dried, processed, or extracted mushrooms were considered "preparations" containing [[cultural-evolution-and-psychoactive-substances]]. The Dutch policy was based on the principle that fresh mushrooms were a "natural product" and that their sale could be regulated through responsible retail practices rather than prohibition.

The Dutch smartshop system developed a sophisticated self-regulatory framework, including:
- Age verification (minimum age 18)
- Warning labels on packaging, including contraindications for mental illness, pregnancy, and medication use
- Recommendations for safe use (empty stomach, quiet environment, not combining with alcohol or other drugs)
- Training for shop staff on harm reduction

Despite these measures, concerns about increasing reports of adverse incidents — including several high-profile cases involving foreign tourists — led the Dutch government to ban the sale of psilocybin-containing mushrooms in 2007. The ban was part of a broader tightening of drug policy in the Netherlands during this period.

### United Kingdom: Evolving Definitions
The UK's approach to mushroom legislation evolved through several stages, reflecting the difficulty of fitting fungi into existing drug control frameworks:

1. **Initial position**: Under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, the UK initially controlled mushrooms only when they had been "treated or prepared" — meaning dried, cooked, or otherwise processed. Fresh mushrooms were technically legal.

2. **2004 clarification**: The UK government clarified that even packaging of mushrooms constituted a form of "preparation," expanding the scope of prohibition.

3. **2005 comprehensive ban**: The Drugs Act 2005 added "any fungus (of any kind) which contains psilocin or an ester of psilocin" as a Class A controlled substance, removing the "treated or prepared" distinction entirely. This made possession or sale of fresh hallucinogenic mushrooms illegal for the first time.
