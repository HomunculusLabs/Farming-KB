# [[cultivation-of-oyster-mushrooms]] in Korea employs a sophisticated substrate fermentation process borrowed from [[oyster-mushrooms]] with superior texture and appearance compared to bag or bottle cultivation. The fermentation process converts raw agricultural materials into a selectively nutritious medium through the orchestrated activity of successive microbial communities, culminating in the appearance of white actinomycetes that signal substrate readiness.

## Overview of Shelf Cultivation

Shelf cultivation involves filling growing shelves with fermented substrate rather than using individual bags or bottles. This approach offers several advantages: the large substrate mass provides abundant nutrients, eliminates the per-bag labor of filling and handling, and produces mushrooms with characteristics preferred in Korean markets. However, it also carries higher risk because contamination on the shelf can spread rapidly through the entire substrate mass.

## Fermentation Science Fundamentals

Fermentation in [[mushroom-cultivation]] is defined as the conversion of organic matter into unique final products by microorganism enzymes. More specifically, it is the conversion of substrate nutrients into microbial proteins through a succession of microbial populations. The process involves three distinct phases: pre-fermentation, pasteurization, and post-fermentation.

### The Role of Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio

The C:N ratio is the most critical parameter substrate preparation tion. Different mushroom species require different optimal ratios:
- Button mushroom ([[agaricus-bisporus]]): C:N ratio of 17:1, growing naturally on high-nitrogen materials like horse manure
- [[oyster-mushroom]] and shiitake: C:N ratio of 350-500:1 in their natural wood habitat

The C:N ratio affects both the fermentation process and final mushroom yield. Key substrate material compositions include:

| Material | pH | Cellulose | Lignin | Total C | Total N | C:N |
|----------|----|-----------|--------|---------|---------|----|
| Cotton waste | 6.2 | 73% | 6% | 24% | 0.41% | 59:1 |
| Rice straw | 6.7 | 42% | 13% | 46% | 0.63% | 72:1 |
| Wheat straw | 6.9 | 48% | 20% | 47% | 0.48% | 97:1 |
| Corncob | 7.2 | 47% | 25% | 47% | 0.48% | 97:1 |
| Sawdust | 5.5 | 54% | 29% | 49% | 0.10% | 491:1 |

Because the main [[growing-gourmet-substrate-materials-wood-types]] often lack sufficient nitrogen, additives such as rice bran or wheat bran are supplemented. The critical consideration is that fermentation converts nitrogen into ammonia nitrogen, which at excessive levels inhibits [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]]. Research shows that oyster mushroom yield decreases when ammonia concentration exceeds 68 ppm, and also decreases when total nitrogen falls below the optimal amount.

### Nitrogen Supplementation Balance

Studies on oyster mushroom yield with varying nitrogen levels demonstrate a clear optimum:

| Total Nitrogen | Ammonia Nitrogen (ppm) | Yield (kg/m2) |
|----------------|------------------------|---------------|
| 0.98% | 28 | 173.9 |
| 1.08% | 68 | 193.4 |
| 1.48% | 84 | 149.2 |

The optimal nitrogen level of 1.08% with 68 ppm ammonia produced the highest yield. Both insufficient and excessive nitrogen reduced productivity, illustrating the importance of precise supplementation.

## Microbial Ecology of Fermentation

### Natural Microorganism Populations

Substrate materials carry naturally occurring microorganisms that drive fermentation. Cotton waste contains significantly more microorganisms than sawdust:

| Organism Type | Cotton Waste | Hardwood Sawdust | Rice Bran |
|---------------|-------------|-----------------|-----------|
| Mesophiles (30°C) | 75x10^4 | 54x10^4 | 8x10^2 |
| Thermophiles (50°C) | 47x10^4 | 12x10^2 | 12 |

Cotton waste has 390 times more thermophiles than sawdust, and 1.3 times more mesophiles. This abundance of natural microorganisms makes cotton waste a superior base material for fermentation. Materials with more diverse and abundant microorganisms produce more thorough fermentation.

### Succession of Microbial Populations

Fermentation proceeds through a predictable succession of microbial communities:

**Phase 1: Mesophilic growth (20-30°C)**
When water is applied to dry substrate, naturally present mesophilic microorganisms begin propagating. They consume water-soluble carbon sources, utilizing 35% for energy and emitting 65% as heat. Geometric population growth causes substrate temperature to rise progressively from 20°C toward 30°C.

**Phase 2: Transition (30-50°C)**
As temperature exceeds 30°C, mesophiles are suppressed and thermophilic organisms begin to dominate. Both groups continue consuming increasingly complex carbon sources including cellulose, hemicellulose, and eventually lignin. Substrate pile turning at this stage encourages thorough and even fermentation by redistributing materials and reintroducing oxygen.

**Phase 3: Thermophilic peak (50°C)**
At peak temperature, thermophiles actively decompose high-molecular-weight carbon sources. The nutritive substances of the substrate become concentrated in microbial biomass as proteins. This protein-rich microbial biomass becomes the primary nutrient source for mushroom mycelia.

### Why Not Start at 50°C?

Starting fermentation at 50°C rather than allowing natural temperature progression is counterproductive. Thermophiles are relatively few and less diverse in nature compared to mesophiles. A rich succession of diverse microbial populations produces more complete fermentation. Additionally, mesophilic activity is essential because mesophiles serve as nutrient precursors for thermophiles. Research showed that when mesophiles fail to multiply, thermophiles also cannot increase, indicating a trophic dependency between the two groups.

### Actinomycetes as Fermentation Indicators

White actinomycetes appear at the final stage of fermentation, indicating that the substrate has been thoroughly and aerobically fermented. Their presence signals several important conditions:
- The substrate pH has risen above 7, which suppresses green mold growth
- Aerobic fermentation has proceeded properly
- The substrate is ready f mycelial colonization colonization

Actinomycetes grow optimally at pH 8-9 and 45-55°C, making them late-stage participants in the fermentation succession. Thermophilic bacteria peak earlier at pH 7-8 and around 50°C.

## Three-Phase Fermentation Protocol

### Phase 1: Pre-Fermentation (Outdoor)

Substrate materials (typically cotton waste mixed with rice bran and other supplements) are piled outdoors and moistened. The pile undergoes natural heating as microbial activity increases. The pile is turned several times over 5-7 days to ensure even fermentation and adequate oxygen supply. Pre-fermentation is complete when the substrate temperature reaches approximately 50°C and the material shows signs of darkening.

### Phase 2: Pasteurization (65°C for 6-8 hours)

Pasteurization in the context of fermented substrate serves a specific purpose distinct from simple sterilization. At 65°C, mold spores are not killed (they typically require 80°C+). Instead, pasteurization serves to:

1. Soften the substrate materials, making nutrients more accessible
2. Kill mesophilic microorganisms that would otherwise compete with mushroom mycelia at incubation temperatures (20-25°C)
3. Create conditions favorable for post-fermentation thermophiles

The distinction is important: the goal is not to sterilize but to selectively eliminate competitive organisms while preserving beneficial thermophilic populations.

### Phase 3: Post-Fermentation (50-55°C for 3-4 days)

After pasteurization, the substrate is maintained at 50-55°C for 3-4 days. This phase ensures thorough and even fermentation of the entire substrate mass. Any remaining mesophilic organisms are either killed or converted into thermophilic biomass. The key outcome is that post-fermentation leaves the substrate populated only with thermophiles, which cannot grow at mushroom incubation temperatures and therefore cannot compete with mushroom mycelia.

After post-fermentation, the substrate becomes a superior nutritional medium. Nutrients are stored as proteins within living thermophilic microorganisms. Mushroom mycelia, which secrete a greater variety of digestive enzymes than any other microorganism, can digest and absorb these microbial proteins, lipids, minerals, and vitamins.

## Filling, Spawning, and Incubation

After post-fermentation, substrate is cooled overnight to 20-25°C. On commercial farms, filling machines convey substrate onto shelves simultaneously with spawning. Typically 60% of spawn is mixed throughout the substrate mass and 40% is sprinkled on the surface. The substrate is flattened and covered with polythene sheeting.

Incubation proceeds for 17-23 days at 20-22°C initially, gradually increasing to 25°C. However, because actively growing mycelia generate respiration heat, room temperature should be set at 22-23°C despite the theoretical optimum of 25°C for oyster mushroom mycelial growth. Internal substrate temperatures can exceed room temperature by 4-8°C during peak mycelial activity.

## Vinyl Mulching Innovation

A significant innovation in Korean shelf cultivation is the use of black perforated plastic sheeting (vinyl mulch) covering the substrate surface. The mulch has 5-10cm holes every 10-15cm, creating a surface that is 80% mulched and 20% perforated. Fruiting bodies grow only from the perforated openings in clusters. Benefits of vinyl mulching include:

- Reduced labor during harvest
- Prevention of small pin abortion
- Production of quality mushrooms with favorable color and long stipes
- Higher overall yields
- Effective [[cervantes-pest-disease-prevention-overview]], particularly against bacterial brown blotch and fungal diseases
- Reduced waterlogged areas on shelf surfaces

## Decline of Shelf Cultivation in Korea

Despite producing higher quality mushrooms, shelf cultivation has been declining in Korea due to several factors:
- High risk: contamination on a shelf can destroy the entire substrate mass
- Fermentation requires years of experience and skill
- High expenses for large substrate quantities, spawn volumes, and fuel for fermentation
- Bag cultivation is easier and safer, producing acceptable if somewhat lower quality

The fermentation principles remain applicable, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions where fermentation requires less fuel input due to naturally warm conditions. The knowledge may also benefit growers seeking to improve substrate quality through biological processing rather than purely thermal treatment.
