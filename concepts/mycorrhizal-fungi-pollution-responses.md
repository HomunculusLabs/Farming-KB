# Mycorrhizal Fungi Responses to Pollution

Mycorrhizal fungi are among the most sensitive components of forest ecosystems to anthropogenic pollution. Their responses to air pollution, heavy metals, acid deposition, and nutrient enrichment have been extensively documented across European and North American forests, providing critical insights into the health and resilience of belowground fungal communities.

## Decline of Ectomycorrhizal Fungi in Europe

One of the most striking observations in fungal ecology has been the documented decline of ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungal fruiting bodies across Europe, particularly in The Netherlands. Arnolds (1988, 1989a, 1989d, 1991) provided seminal evidence of changes in the macromycete flora, noting reductions in both frequency and distribution of ectomycorrhizal species. A preliminary red data list of macrofungi in The Netherlands highlighted the conservation concern, with ectomycorrhizal species disproportionately affected compared to saprotrophic fungi.

The decline of the iconic chanterelle (*Cantharellus cibarius*) in Dutch forests was investigated by Jansen and Van Dobben (1987), who suggested air pollution as a contributing factor. Termorshuizen and Schaffers (1987, 1989, 1991) correlated carpophore occurrence of ectomycorrhizal fungi in Scots pine stands with stand vitality and air pollution levels, finding that declining forest health was associated with reduced fruiting body production of mycorrhizal species.

## Nitrogen Deposition and Fertilization Effects

Atmospheric nitrogen deposition has emerged as one of the most significant drivers of changes in mycorrhizal fungal communities. Arnebrant (1994) demonstrated that nitrogen amendments reduce the growth of extramatrical ectomycorrhizal mycelium, the critical foraging network that extends from colonized roots into the soil. Similarly, Arnebrant and Söderström (1992) found that different fertilizer treatments altered the ectomycorrhizal colonization potential in two Scots pine forests in Sweden.

Brandrud (1995) conducted experimental nitrogen addition in an oligotrophic spruce forest at Gårdsjön, Sweden, showing significant shifts in the ectomycorrhizal fungal flora. These findings align with broader NITREX (Nitrogen Saturation Experiments) program results synthesized by Gundersen et al. (1998), which demonstrated the profound impact of nitrogen deposition on nitrogen cycling in forests. Rühling and Tyler (1991) further showed that simulated nitrogen deposition to the forest floor altered the macrofungal flora of beech forests.

The mechanism behind nitrogen effects appears to be a shift in the carbon economy of the mycorrhizal symbiosis. Under high nitrogen availability, plants allocate less carbon to their fungal symbionts, reducing the extraradical mycelium and ultimately affecting fungal community composition and fruiting.

## Acid Rain and Ectomycorrhizal Impacts

Acid deposition was one of the earliest recognized anthropogenic stressors on mycorrhizal fungi. Dighton and Skeffington (1987) demonstrated the effects of artificial acid precipitation on mycorrhizas of Scots pine seedlings, finding reduced colonization and altered community structure. Blaschke (1988) observed mycorrhizal infection changes and fine root development alterations in Norway spruce influenced by acid rain under field conditions.

Jansen et al. (1988) compiled comprehensive evidence of ectomycorrhiza and acid rain interactions in a CEC report, and Dighton and Jansen (1991) reviewed the relationship between atmospheric pollutants and ectomycorrhizas, concluding there were more questions than answers at that time. Killham et al. (1983) elucidated some of the mechanisms by which acid rain affects soil microbial activity. The Liphook forest fumigation project (McLeod et al., 1992; Shaw et al., 1992, 1993) provided some of the most detailed field evidence of SO₂ and ozone effects on mycorrhizas of Scots pine and Norway spruce.

Fellner (1988) studied the effects of acid deposition on the ectotrophic stability of mountain forest ecosystems in central Europe (Czechoslovakia), documenting severe impacts on mycorrhizal communities in regions receiving high acid deposition loads.

## Heavy Metal Contamination

Heavy metal pollution from smelters, mining, and industrial activity has profound effects on mycorrhizal fungi. Nordgren et al. (1986) found reduced soil microbial activity, mycelial lengths, and changes in physiological groups of bacteria in a heavy metal polluted area. Rühling et al. (1984) documented fungi in metal-contaminated soil near the Gusum brass mill in Sweden, showing altered community composition.

The interaction between mycorrhizal fungi and heavy metals is complex and species-specific. Bradley et al. (1981, 1982) demonstrated that mycorrhizal infection in *Calluna vulgaris* (heather) provided resistance to heavy metal toxicity, a finding with significant implications for understanding plant-fungal co-evolution in contaminated environments. Colpaert and Van Assche (1992, 1993) studied zinc and cadmium toxicity in ectomycorrhizal *Pinus sylvestris*, showing that the fungal symbiont can moderate metal toxicity to the host.

Denny and Wilkins (1987a, 1987b) investigated zinc tolerance in *Betula* species and the mechanism of ectomycorrhizal amelioration of zinc toxicity. They found that fungal slime played a role in binding zinc and reducing its availability to plant roots (Denny and Ridge, 1995). Hartley et al. (1997a, 1997b, 1999a, 1999b) conducted extensive studies on ectomycorrhizal fungal responses to toxic metals including cadmium, zinc, lead, and antimony, finding evidence for both adaptive tolerance and cross-colonization capabilities.

Bardgett et al. (1994) assessed the impact of pasture contamination by copper, chromium, and arsenic timber preservative on soil microbial properties, finding significant negative effects on microbial biomass and nematode communities.

## Epiphytic Lichens and Air Pollution

Lichens have long served as sensitive bioindicators of air quality. De Wit (1976) studied epiphytic lichens and air pollution in The Netherlands, establishing baseline data. Bates et al. (2001) documented the loss of *Lecanora conizaeoides* and other fluctuations of epiphytes on oak over 21 years of declining SO₂ concentrations, showing some recovery of lichen communities as air quality improved. Brown (1996) reviewed urban, industrial, and agricultural effects on lichens.

## Elevated CO₂ and Mycorrhizal Responses

Rising atmospheric CO₂ concentrations affect mycorrhizal fungi through changes in plant carbon allocation. BassiriRad et al. (2001) reviewed root system adjustments regulating [[plant-nutrient-uptake]] and growth responses to elevated CO₂. Fitter et al. (2000) took a "mycocentric approach" to understanding the impact of elevated CO₂ and global climate change on arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. Rillig and Allen (1999) evaluated the role of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in plant-to-ecosystem responses to elevated atmospheric CO₂, finding evidence of increased hyphal growth and soil aggregation.

Klironomos et al. (1997, 1998) demonstrated soil fungal-arthropod responses to poplar grown under enriched atmospheric CO₂ under field conditions, and showed interspecific differences in the response of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi to *Artemisia tridentata* grown under elevated CO₂. Gorisen and Kuyper (2000) found fungal species-specific responses of ectomycorrhizal Scots pine to elevated CO₂.

## Decomposition and Litter Quality Under Pollution

Pollution affects fungal decomposition processes through multiple pathways. Conn and Dighton (2000) showed that litter quality influences decomposition, ectomycorrhizal community structure, and mycorrhizal root surface acid phosphatase activity. Cotrufo et al. (1998, 2000) investigated increased atmospheric CO₂ effects on litter quality and wood decomposition. Boddy et al. (1996) studied the effects of dry-deposited SO₂ and sulphite on saprotrophic fungi and decomposition of tree leaf litter.

## Conservation and Biogeography

Arnolds (1997) addressed the biogeography and conservation of fungi, highlighting the need for protection of fungal diversity. Watling (1999) reported on the launch of the UK biodiversity action plan for lower plants, including fungi. The recognition that fungal diversity is integral to ecosystem function has grown substantially, with conservation efforts increasingly incorporating belowground fungal communities.

## Mycorrhizal Community Resilience and Recovery

The resilience of mycorrhizal fungal communities following pollution
remediation varies considerably depending on the type and duration of
contaminant exposure. Studies of post-industrial sites reveal that while
some ectomycorrhizal species recolonize disturbed soils within 5-10 years,
full community recovery often requires decades. Early successional
species such as *Laccaria* and *Hebeloma* typically establish first,
followed by more specialized late-stage associates.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) tend to recover more rapidly than
ectomycorrhizal species, partly due to their lower host specificity and
the persistence of spore banks in degraded soils. However, species
richness in recovered communities frequently remains lower than in
reference ecosystems, suggesting that pollution legacies persist in
belowground fungal networks even after aboveground vegetation has been
restored.
