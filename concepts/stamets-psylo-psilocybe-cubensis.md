# [[psilocybe-cubensis]]

**Authority**: (Earle) Singer

**Synonyms**: Stropharia cubensis Earle, Stropharia cyanescens Murr.,
Stropharia caerulescens, Psilocybe cubensis var. caerulescens

**Common Names**: Golden tops, cubies, san isidros, hongos kentesh

## Description

This species, the most majestic of the Psilocybes, is easy to recognize
by its size, golden color, the well-formed membranous annulus, the
blue-staining stem and veil, and the coprophilic habitat. P. cubensis is
the most commonly cultivated psilocybin mushroom in the world and has been
widely cultivated in the United States and Europe since the 1970s.

### Cap (Pileus)

1.5-8 cm broad. Conic-campanulate often with an acute umbo at first,
becoming convex to broadly convex and finally plane in age with or without
an obtuse or acute umbo. Reddish cinnamon brown in young fruiting bodies,
becoming lighter with age to more golden brown, fading to pale yellow or
white near the margin with the umbo or center region remaining darker
cinnamon brown. Surface viscid to smooth when moist but soon dry.
Universal veil leaving spotted remnants on cap but soon becoming smooth
overall. Flesh whitish, soon bruising bluish.

### Gills (Lamellae)

Attachment adnate to adnexed, soon seceding. Close, narrow to slightly
enlarged in the center. Pallid to grayish in young fruiting bodies,
becoming deep purplish gray to nearly black in maturity, often mottled.
Spore print dark purplish brown to violaceous brown.

### Stem (Stipe)

40-150 mm long by 5-15 mm thick. Thickening towards the base in most
specimens. Whitish overall but may discolor to yellowish; bruising bluish
where injured. Surface smooth to striated at the apex, and dry.

### Veil

Partial veil membranous, leaving a well-developed, white, persistent
membranous annulus that often bruises bluish and soon becomes dusted with
purplish brown spores. This prominent annulus is a key identification
feature.

## Microscopy

- **Spores**: Dark purplish brown to violaceous brown in deposit,
  subellipsoid, 11.5-17 by 8-11 um
- **Basidia**: 2- or 3-spored, but usually 4-spored
- **Pleurocystidia**: Nearly pear shaped, sometimes mucronate, 18-30 by
  6-13 um
- **Cheilocystidia**: Fusoid-ventricose with obtuse or subcapitate apex,
  sometimes sublageniform, 17-32 by 6-10 um, with narrow necks 3-5 um
  broad

## Habitat

Scattered to gregarious on dung of bovines (cattle, buffalo), horse, or
elephant dung, in well-manured grounds in the spring, summer, and fall.

## Distribution

Throughout the southeastern United States, Mexico, Cuba, Central America,
northern South America, the subtropical Far East (India, Thailand,
Vietnam, Cambodia), and regions of Australia (Queensland). Likely
imported to the Western Hemisphere with Spanish missionaries and Brahman
cattle from islands off West Africa.

## Season

In the southeastern United States, May and June are the best months for
picking, although they can be found up until January. Largest fruitings
occur in the two months prior to the hottest period of the year.

## Dosage and Potency

Rating: Moderately potent. Reported chemical analyses:

- Heim and Hofmann (1958): 0.50% psilocybin, 0.25% psilocin
- Gartz (1994): 0.63% psilocybin, 0.11% psilocin
- Stijve and de Meijer (1993): Mexican strain 0.15% psilocybin, 0.50%
  psilocin; Amazonian strain 0.15% psilocybin, 0.33% psilocin
- One extraordinary collection: 1.3% psilocybin, 0.45% psilocin

Analyses vary substantially. Bigwood and Beug found a fourfold variation
in cultivated specimens and up to tenfold variation in wild specimens.
Mushrooms grown indoors seem consistently more potent than field-collected
specimens, probably due to nutritional factors and protection from UV
radiation.

Gartz (1989) showed that psilocin levels could be raised from 0.19% to
3.3% with the addition of tryptamine to the substrate, and at least 22%
of psilocybin was derived from the introduced tryptamine.

## Commentary

P. cubensis is thought to have come with the Spaniards during the Cortes
and subsequent missionary expeditions. The epithet "san isidro" is an
indigenous reference to Spanish Catholicism. Although P. cubensis is
widely sold to tourists, the shamans of Oaxaca prefer Psilocybe
caerulescens, P. aztecorum, P. zapotecorum, or P. mexicana.

Strains from the 1970s carried names of their origins: Amazonian, Palenque,
Matias Romero, and Ecuadorian. P. subcubensis is virtually identical,
differing only in smaller spores (9.9-13 by 6.1-7.1 um).

## Similar Species

- **[[psilocybe-subcubensis]]**: Virtually identical, smaller spores
- **[[panaeolus-cyanescens]]**: Similar habitat but black spores, more

- **Psilocybe coprophila**: Smaller, less robust
