# Hyphal Orientation and Anemotropism in Psilocybe cubensis

The wind-directed (anemotropic) growth response of Psilocybe cubensis stipes during early development, proposed to operate through differential evaporative demand across the stipe surface. This represents the primary tropic response in pre-sporulation fruiting bodies, directing stipe elongation toward areas of greater water loss. See also [[tropisms-psilocybe-cubensis-phototropism]], [[tropisms-psilocybe-cubensis-gravitropism]], [[oyster-mushroom-fruiting-abnormalities-environmental-causes]], and [[fungal-water-relations]].

## Anemotropic Growth Response

Badham's wind tunnel experiments demonstrated clear wind-directed growth in P. cubensis based on approximately 100 developing basidiocarps:

- When primordia (Stages 1-2) were placed in the wind tunnel, stipe growth was directed into the airflow
- This response occurred with a 12-hour photoperiod or in complete darkness
- It was present in both fertile and sterile (sporeless) basidiocarps
- Even when basidiocarps were placed with stipes directed away from the wind, they bent back toward it
- When cultures were rotated perpendicular to the wind at 3 rpm, growth was simply upright (wind effect equalized on all sides)
- If upright cultures were placed in the chamber at Stage 2 or later, bending was less pronounced
- The response was robust across all conditions tested, making it the default tropic orientation mechanism
- The anemotropic mechanism operates throughout development but is masked by geotropism once sporulation begins

## Boundary Layer Redistribution Model

Badham proposed that anemotropism operates through redistribution of the boundary layer around the stipe:

- Wind creates a thicker boundary layer on the leeward (downwind) side of the stipe
- A thinner boundary layer on the windward side increases evaporative demand on that surface
- Water vapor is the primary gas affected by this redistribution
- The windward side of the stipe experiences greater drying effect than the leeward side
- Differential drying causes cells on each side to grow at different rates, producing curvature toward the wind
- The stipe effectively "chases" the direction of greatest water loss
- This model does not require a dedicated wind-sensing receptor on the mushroom surface
- Instead, the physical interaction between wind and the cylindrical stipe geometry naturally creates the evaporative gradient

### Evaporative Demand Factors

Four factors control the evaporative demand gradient across the stipe:

1. Wind velocity: increases boundary layer thinning on windward side, intensifying the differential
2. Humidity: higher ambient humidity reduces overall evaporative demand, potentially reducing the tropic response
3. Light: heats the mushroom surface, increasing evaporation; may dominate if strong enough relative to wind
4. Temperature: affects evaporation rate and boundary layer dynamics through its influence on water vapor pressure

These factors interact; the dominant factor at any moment determines the direction and magnitude of the tropic response. Under cultivation conditions where wind is minimal, temperature gradients and light may become the primary drivers of differential growth.

## Cell-Level Response Mechanism

The model proposes that individual stipe cells respond to local evaporative conditions:

- Much cell division has occurred by the time primordia reach a few centimeters in height (Heim and Wasson, 1958)
- Further growth involves acropetal (tip-directed) expansion of existing cells (Plunkett, 1961)
- Some cell division may continue at the upper part of the stipe (Craig, 1977)
- If cells expand independently in response to local water status, differential expansion would naturally produce curvature
- The model does not require a dedicated gravitropic or anemotropic signaling system at the cellular level
- Instead, each cell acts as an independent sensor and effector responding to its local microenvironment
- This distributed mechanism would explain why the response is so robust across different conditions
- The cell walls of the stipe are sufficiently plastic during expansion to allow differential growth rates

## Comparison with Phycomyces

The mechanism may be similar to that of Phycomyces blakesleeanus, a well-studied zygomycete:

- Cohen et al. (1975) demonstrated wind-directed growth (anemotropism) in Phycomyces
- Phycomyces also shows an avoidance response where sporangiophores bend away from objects
- Johnson and Gamow (1971) showed that both wind and water vapor are necessary for these responses
- Russo et al. (1975) suggested ethylene may mediate the autochemotropic avoidance response in Phycomyces
- An alternative explanation is that the mushroom responds directly to the physical force of wind
- Dennison (1961) showed Phycomyces sporangiophore growth responds to gravitational and centrifugal force stimuli
- Both organisms suggest that physical/mechanical stimuli (rather than dedicated chemoreception) may mediate wind responses in fungi
- The Phycomyces literature is more extensive, with identified photoreceptors and signal transduction pathways

## Wind Tunnel Experimental Design

Badham's experiments used a closed-circuit wind tunnel with carefully controlled conditions:

- Wind velocity: 9 +/- 3 cm/sec (measured with TSI hot wire anemometer Model 1650)
- Humidity: 94 +/- 3% RH (measured with wet and dry copper constantan thermocouples)
- Temperature: 24.5 +/- 1.5 C (controlled by temperature proportioner module)
- Light: 0.002 cal/cm2/min from cool white fluorescent at 60cm (12h photoperiod)
- Working area: 43 x 30 x 20 cm with polyester fiber filter to slow wind speed
- Humidity controlled by spin-type humidifier with heater, regulated by relay from thermocouple
- Primordia placed at ~2cm height, harvested after 72 hours at maturity
- Cultures initially grown on autoclaved brown rice, then transferred to pie tins with vermiculite
- Primordia formed under cool white fluorescent light (2685 lux) for 10 days before wind tunnel exposure
- The wind tunnel included an eight-sided air baffle, heat cable, and water bath for temperature stability
- A strip-chart recorder continuously logged temperature and humidity

## Adaptive Significance of Anemotropism

Two adaptive functions for anemotropic growth have been proposed:

1. [[spore-dispersal]] optimization: growing toward airflow positions the cap for maximum spore release when sporulation begins
2. Spacing of cespitose fruiting bodies: directed growth toward evaporative demand could increase spacing between stipes of clustered (cespitose) fruiting bodies, reducing competition for resources and improving individual spore dispersal efficiency

The first function is particularly important for P. cubensis, which often fruits in dense clusters on dung substrates where individual stipes are close together. By growing toward areas of greater airflow, each stipe positions itself to maximize future spore dispersal potential.

## Transition to Geotropism

The anemotropic response is the default tropism until spore production begins:

- At Stage 2, a substance produced in the gills (proposed hormone) appears to override the evaporative demand mechanism
- The mushroom switches to negative geotropic response
- The pileus reorients horizontally for optimal spore release
- This transition occurs at Stage 2 (pileus nearly spherical)
- Sporeless mutants never make this transition, continuing anemotropic growth indefinitely
- When rotated parallel to the wind to eliminate gravity, even Stage 2 mushrooms show horizontal (anemotropic) growth
- This demonstrates that the anemotropic mechanism remains operational but is normally suppressed by the geotropic signal
- The transition point represents a fundamental shift in the hierarchy of tropic stimuli governing stipe growth
- Understanding this transition is important for interpreting growth abnormalities in cultivation
- Stipes that fail to correct to vertical may indicate problems with spore production or gill development
- The anemotropic mechanism is sufficient for initial stipe elongation but inadequate for optimal spore dispersal orientation
- The evolutionary conservation of this two-phase system across multiple mushroom genera suggests strong selective pressure for both efficient stipe extension and precise pileus orientation
- Taber (1973) reviewed morphogenesis in basidiomycetes broadly, noting that tropic responses are integrated with the overall developmental program of fruiting body formation
