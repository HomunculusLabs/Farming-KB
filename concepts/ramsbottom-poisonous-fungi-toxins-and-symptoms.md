John Ramsbottom"s 1945 work "Poisonous Fungi" provides one of the most thorough early classifications of fungal toxins and their clinical effects. He organizes poisonous fungi into distinct categories based on the nature of their toxic substances and the resulting symptom profiles, drawing on both laboratory research and clinical case reports.

## Classification of Poisonous Fungi by Toxin Action

Ramsbottom recognizes that beyond species causing simple gastrointestinal irritation, the truly dangerous fungi fall into three categories based on the mechanism of their toxins:

### Category 1: Cell-Degenerating Toxins (Long Incubation)

Species: Amanita phalloides ([[bloomfield-destroying-angel-amanita-virosa-amatoxin-poisoning-clinical-course]] (Destroying Angel), and the rarer Lepiota helveola.

These contain substances that after a long incubation period bring about the degeneration of the cells of the body. The Amanita species in this group are deadly; L. helveola belongs to this group but is much less dangerous.

### Category 2: Nervous System Paralytics

Species: [[amanita-muscaria-fly-agaric-history-and-pharmacology]]), Amanita pantherina (False Blusher), and Inocybe Patouillardi (Red Staining Inocybe).

These contain substances which paralyse the nervous system, producing distinctive neurological and gastrointestinal symptoms with a shorter onset time than the cell-degenerating group.

### Category 3: Red Blood Cell Destroyers (Haemolytics)

Species: Gyromitra esculenta.

Contains substances which destroy the red blood corpuscles, though subsequent research has shown the action to be more complex than simple haemolysis.

## Detailed Toxin Profiles

### Amanitin (Amanita-toxin)

The primary lethal toxin of Amanita phalloides and its close relatives. Has a complicated and undefined chemical structure. Critically, it resists the action of heat, drying, and digestive juices -- meaning cooking, drying, or normal digestion do not neutralize it. In experimental animals, amanitin produces the majority of the lesions described in human post-mortems. This heat-resistant property is what makes A. phalloides so dangerous: ordinary cooking does not render it safe, and the long incubation period means stomach-pumping measures are insufficient once symptoms begin.

### Phalloidin

A polypeptide isolated from Amanita phalloides. Quickly active but destroyed by heat. While rapidly toxic in isolation, death through eating the fungus is attributed primarily to the heat-resistant amanitin rather than phalloidin.

### Phallin

A glucoside isolated from Amanita phalloides, originally assumed to be the active poison. Attacks the red corpuscles of the blood (haemolysis). Readily destroyed by heat and digestive juices. Since it is heat-labile and the symptoms of A. phalloides poisoning are not those of haemolysis, phallin is now understood not to be the primary lethal agent.
