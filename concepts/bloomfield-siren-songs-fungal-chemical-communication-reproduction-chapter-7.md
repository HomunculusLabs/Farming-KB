# Bloomfield Chapter 7: Siren Songs — Fungal Chemical Communication and the Biology of Reproduction

## Overview

Chapter 7 of [[nicholas-money]]'s *Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard*, titled "Siren Songs," explores the molecular dialogues through which fungi find mates, coordinate development, and orchestrate the formation [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]]. Drawing the title from Homer's *Odyssey*—where sailors were lured to their deaths by the irresistible songs of avian seductresses—Money uses the siren metaphor to illuminate how fungi "sing" to one another in chemical pulses and answer with "a symphony of developmental changes." The chapter moves through three major fungal groups (oomycetes, chytrids, and zygomycetes) before culminating in the complex mating systems of mushroom-forming basidiomycetes, revealing that [[fungal-life-cycles-sexual-reproduction-metamorphosis]] in fungi involves some of the most sophisticated [[plant-hormones-and-chemical-signaling-systems]] in any kingdom of life.

## The Siren Metaphor

Money opens with a striking comparison between the mythological sirens and [[bloomfield-fungal-pheromones-sexual-signaling-antheridiol-oogoniol]]:

> "Bewitched by the sirens' song of Greek mythology, mariners were enticed to their deaths on the jagged rocks of the Straits of Messina... Songs are sung within the human body by molecules rather than tuneful pressure waves, but they have an equally inescapable allure. Consider the behavior of spermatozoa... built to swim up the scented gradient wafting from a ten-thousandfold larger egg. Likewise the fungi. They sing to one another in chemical pulses, and answer with a symphony of developmental changes."

The chapter's central questions are: *Where are you?* and *Who are you?*—the fundamental challenges of mate recognition that every sexually reproducing organism must solve. For fungi, which lack eyes, ears, and the ability to move toward potential partners, these questions must be answered entirely through chemistry.

## Oomycete Water Molds: Steroid Pheromones

### Antheridiol and Oogoniol

The chapter begins with the water mold *Achlya ambisexualis*, an oomycete that produces eggs in sacs called **oogonia** and male structures called **antheridia**. Money describes how these organisms advertise their readiness for intimacy using pheromones called **antheridiol** and **oogoniol**:

- **Antheridiol** is produced by the female strain and stimulates the male to grow antheridial branches toward the oogonia
- **Oogoniol** is produced by the male and signals back to the female
- Remarkably, both compounds are **steroids**—ring-shaped lipids chemically similar to human sex hormones estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone

This structural similarity is extraordinary given the immense evolutionary distance between oomycetes and animals. Both lineages independently converged on steroid chemistry for sexual signaling, suggesting that steroids possess unique properties (lipid solubility, receptor compatibility, chemical stability in water) that make them ideal signaling molecules.

### The Hartog–Trow Rivalry

Money recounts a colorful episode in the [[bloomfield-history-of-mycology-pioneers]]: the bitter professional rivalry between **Marcus Hartog** and **Professor Trow** over the mechanism of oomycete fertilization. Hartog, a student of Anton de Bary ("the founder of modern mycology") at Strasbourg, dismissed Trow's evidence that water mold eggs were fertilized by male antheridia. Hartog compared the claim to "the passage of spiritual mediums like the renowned Mrs. Guppy through brick-walls and closed doors and windows." He published scathing commentaries in the *Annals of Botany*, writing: "I must express my regret that so careful a technique and such powers of observation as the author's should have been overweighted with a priori and obsolete views."

Yet Trow was completely vindicated within years, after he developed a technique called **serial sectioning**—embedding specimens in paraffin, cutting thousandths-of-a-millimeter slices with a microtome, and staining them with gentian violet and eosin to reconstruct three-dimensional cellular anatomy. Trow's method revealed nuclei within intact cells and demonstrated the logical progression of fertilization events that Hartog's crude whole-cell preparations had missed. The episode illustrates how technological innovation (the microtome and improved histological staining) can resolve debates that theoretical argument alone cannot settle.

### The Microscopic Serengeti

Money's description of pond ecology transforms a backyard water feature into a scene of predatory drama. When a caterpillar drowns:

> "The perfume from a drowning caterpillar disperses in the water, swarms of zoospores streak toward the writhing insect. Once the spores strike their target, they glue themselves to the exoskeleton, and bore through its layers of chitin with hyphae that proceed to pulp the creature's tissues. After thorough excavation of the bloated corpse, the nourished mycelium bursts from its surface, converts its hyphal tips into sporangia, and spits the next generation of zoospores into the water."

This passage captures the essential lesson of the chapter: that the same evolutionary principles governing lions and gazelles operate at the microbial scale. Water molds are predators, decomposers, and sexual organisms whose lives are as dramatic as any macroscopic predator's—simply invisible to the naked eye.

### Ecology of Self-Fertility

Money's colleague **Don Thomas** discovered that the ratio of self-fertile hermaphrodites to mate-requiring heterothallic strains in *Achlya* varies with habitat stability. In seasonal ponds that dry in summer and freeze in winter, self-fertile strains outnumber heterothallic ones 10:1. In permanent spring-fed ponds, heterothallic strains dominate. This suggests that self-fertility is an adaptation to environmental instability—when water is ephemeral, the organism cannot afford to wait for a compatible mate and must produce survival capsules (thick-walled oospores) as quickly as possible.

## Chytrid Fungi: Sirenin and Parisin

The chapter then turns to chytrids, the most ancient lineage of fungi, whose gametes are flagellated like animal sperm. Chytrid pheromones differ from oomycete steroids:

- **Sirenin**: A sesquiterpene compound released by the female egg cell
- **Parisin**: The corresponding male pheromone

When sperm cells detect sirenin, their behavior changes abruptly: they stop wandering and begin swimming in a corkscrew path toward the egg. To avoid overstimulation, the sperm cell **inactivates sirenin molecules** as it swims through the pheromonal cloud, allowing it to take frequent directional bearings from the concentration gradient. This chemotactic behavior allows the sperm to home in on the egg with remarkable precision, approaching in "short dashes" until collision.

Money draws an explicit parallel: "The parallel between the behavior of the chytrid and siren-besotted mariners is provocative. Despite occasional signs of gentility, the microbial essence of human behavior is unmistakable."

## Zygomycete Pin Molds: Trisporic Acid and Cooperative Biosynthesis

### The Pin Mold Life Cycle

Zygomycetes (approximately 900 species) are the fungi that grow on bread and other kitchen scraps—first described by **Robert Hooke** in his *Micrographia* of 1665, who found them growing from a sheepskin book cover. Their life cycle is an endless progression of spores → mycelium → spores → mycelium, with [[basidiomycete-mating-systems-sexual-reproduction]] triggered when two compatible strains meet on a common substrate.

### Cooperative Biosynthesis

Zygomycete pheromones are manufactured from **carotenoids** (the class including β-carotene, precursor of vitamin A). Rather than releasing large quantities of pheromone, zygomycetes have evolved an economical scheme called **cooperative biosynthesis**:

1. Each strain releases a minute amount of its own precursor compound
2. The partner strain converts this precursor into the active pheromone **trisporic acid**
3. An individual strain cannot process its own precursor—only its mate possesses the necessary enzyme
4. This mutual dependence ensures that trisporic acid only accumulates when compatible partners are present

In response to trisporic acid, both strains cease asexual sporangium production and instead grow aerial hyphae toward one another. These hyphae perform what Money calls a "cobra-like dance" before fusing and generating a zygospore. The zygospore is a tough, melanized resting structure—similar to water mold oospores—adapted for long-term survival in soil or landfills.

## Basidiomycete Mating: From Hundreds to Thousands of Sexes

The chapter culminates with the most complex fungal mating systems. As Money explains, basidiomycete mycelia "appear as masses of androgynous tubes," but [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] lurks in their nuclei. A single species of ink-cap mushroom can encompass hundreds of different strains, and "in the sense that 'male' and 'female' refer to compatible mating types, there are legions of different sexes of [[singh-basidiomycete-fungi-in-petroleum-hydrocarbon-cleanup]]."

### Somatic Incompatibility and the Balkan Map

When incompatible basidiomycete strains meet and their hyphae fuse, the results are violent:

> "Love is lost in the cytoplasm and the region of mingled juice darkens and decays."

Money describes how this creates visible territorial boundaries in decaying wood—"a map of this tormented region etched in black ink on tree stumps and on the ends of logs." Beech trees serve as battlegrounds where swarms of [[bloomfield-wood-decay-fungi-spalted-timber]] compete. The destruction of a large tree can occupy a century, supporting a rich community of warring mycelia whose boundaries are inscribed in melanin and pigment.

## Key Themes

1. **Convergent evolution**: Steroid pheromones evolved independently in oomycetes and animals, demonstrating the chemical constraints on signaling across vast phylogenetic distances
2. **Economy of signaling**: Different fungal groups have evolved distinct solutions to the problem of mate recognition—some broadcast freely, others use cooperative biosynthesis to prevent wasteful pheromone production
3. **Environmental adaptation**: Self-fertility versus outcrossing strategies are tuned to habitat stability, with unstable environments favoring hermaphroditism
4. **The universality of chemical communication**: From chytrid sirenin to basidiomycete pheromones, the basic logic—release a signal, detect a gradient, move toward the source—is conserved across all fungal lineages and indeed across all kingdoms of life
