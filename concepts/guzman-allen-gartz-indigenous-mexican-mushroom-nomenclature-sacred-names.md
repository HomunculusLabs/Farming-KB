# Indigenous Mexican Mushroom Nomenclature — Sacred Names, Common Names, and Linguistic Diversity

## Overview

The 1997 worldwide distribution survey by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz documents an extraordinary feature of Mexican mycological culture: the sheer diversity of indigenous names for psychoactive and sacred mushrooms. Guzmán (1997) reported more than **two hundred common names** for hallucinogenic fungi in Mexico, many in Indian languages. This linguistic richness reflects not only the cultural importance of these fungi but also the complex relationship between indigenous classification systems, colonial disruption, and modern mycological science. The study of these names provides a window into how different Mesoamerican cultures conceptualized the relationship between fungi, consciousness, and the divine.

## Teonanácatl: The Nahuatl Original

The most famous of all Mexican mushroom names is **teonanácatl**, a Nahuatl word meaning "divine mushroom" or "flesh of the gods" (from *teotl*, god/divine, and *nanácatl*, mushroom). This term was first reported by Bernardino de Sahagún in his *Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva España* (1569–1582), making it the earliest documented indigenous name for a psychoactive mushroom in the Americas.

Sahagún's documentation is historically crucial because the Spanish Inquisition subsequently banned the use of teonanácatl and attempted to eradicate the knowledge surrounding it. The word survives in Sahagún's manuscript only because he was documenting indigenous culture for the purpose of facilitating Christian conversion — his records were meant to help identify and suppress pagan practices, but they inadvertently preserved irreplaceable ethnobotanical knowledge.

The term was later revived by [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]]*.

The use of Catholic saint names for sacred mushrooms represents a form of religious syncretism that allowed indigenous communities to continue their mushroom practices under the cover of Catholic observance. By naming the mushrooms after saints, practitioners could discuss them openly without arousing the suspicion of colonial or ecclesiastical authorities.

## The Mazatec Naming Confusion: «Nise» and the Problem of Homonymy

Guzmán documents a particularly revealing case of nomenclatural confusion among the Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca. The Mazatec word **«nise»** means "little bird" and is used for both *[[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]]* (a psychoactive species) and *[[lycoperdon-mixtecorum]]* and *L. marginatum* as narcotic fungi among the Mixtec. Ott et al. (1975) later investigated these claims in the same locality and identified at least nine species being used indistinctly as narcotic fungi by the Mixtec, including *Vascellum pratense*, *V. curtisii*, *V. intermedium*, *Lycoperdon oblongisporum*, *Rhizopogon* sp., *[[dictyophora-indusiata]]

Among the Chinantec Indians of Oaxaca, the stinkhorn fungus *Dictyophora indusiata* (with its three recognized forms) is used as a special "narcotic" fungus for **divination purposes**. Documented by Heim and Wasson (1958), Wasson (1959a), and Guzmán (1997), this practice is remarkable because *D. indusiata* is not known to contain psilocybin or other classic hallucinogenic compounds. Its use in divination may relate to its dramatic appearance — the tall, net-like indusium that descends from the cap like a bridal veil — which makes it visually striking and symbolically potent.

The Chinantec use of *D. indusiata* demonstrates that the concept of "sacred mushrooms" in indigenous Mexican culture extends beyond the narrow pharmacological category of psilocybin-containing species to encompass any fungus that plays a role in ritual, divination, or healing practices. The indigenous classification is based on cultural function rather than chemical composition.

## The Two Periods of Mexican Mycology

Guzmán (1990a, b) divided the development of Mexican mycology into two periods defined by the study of hallucinogenic fungi: (1) before Wasson, Heim, and Singer's studies (1954–1958), and (2) after those studies. This division reflects the transformative impact that psychedelic mycology had on the broader field. The intense international interest generated by the discovery of active psilocybian species in Mexico attracted specialists who then studied all fungi in the country, not just the psychoactive ones. The sacred mushrooms were, in effect, the gateway through which modern mycology entered Mexico.

## The Sahagún Problem: Colonial Documentation and Its Ambiguities

Bernardino de Sahagún's documentation of teonanácatl, while invaluable, must be read with awareness of its colonial context. Sahagún was a Franciscan friar whose primary purpose was to facilitate the Christianization of the indigenous peoples of New Spain. His extensive interviews with indigenous informants about their religious practices were conducted under the implicit threat that this knowledge would be used to identify and suppress pagan ceremonies.

This raises questions about the completeness and accuracy of Sahagún's mushroom documentation. Did his informants tell him everything? Did they deliberately obscure certain details to protect their practices? The fact that the Spanish Inquisition actively persecuted mushroom users — the same Inquisition that also targeted practitioners of indigenous healing, astronomy, and other traditional knowledge systems — means that the surviving documentation represents only a fraction of what was known.

The gap between Sahagún's 16th-century documentation and Schultes's 20th-century revival of the term represents nearly four centuries of suppression during which much indigenous mushroom knowledge was lost. The names that survive today are the remnants of a much richer nomenclatural system that was partially destroyed by colonial persecution.

## Nahuatl Mushroom Vocabulary Beyond Teonanácatl

While teonanácatl is the best-known Nahuatl mushroom term, it was not the only one. The broader Nahuatl fungal vocabulary included terms for different types of mushrooms, different stages of growth, and different ceremonial contexts. Guzmán's reference to *teotlaquilnanácatl* ("divine fungus") as a distinct term from teonanácatl suggests that the Nahuatl classification system was more nuanced than the single famous word implies.

The existence of multiple Nahuatl terms for sacred mushrooms parallels the situation in other Mesoamerican languages. Each linguistic community had its own vocabulary for describing the fungi they used, reflecting local ecological knowledge, cultural practices, and spiritual beliefs. The pattern of linguistic diversity among mushroom names mirrors the broader pattern of linguistic diversity among Mesoamerican peoples — a region that was, before colonization, one of the most linguistically diverse areas on Earth.

## The Wasson Effect: How One Name Changed Mycology

The involvement of R. Gordon Wasson in the study of Mexican sacred mushrooms beginning in the 1950s had a profound effect on both the popular and scientific understanding of mushroom nomenclature. Wasson's *Life* magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" (1957) introduced teonanácatl to a global audience and inadvertently launched the psychedelic counterculture. The article's enormous readership meant that teonanácatl became the default term for psychoactive mushrooms in the English-speaking world, despite being a specific Nahuatl word tied to a specific cultural context.

This popularization had the effect of flattening the rich nomenclatural diversity documented by Guzmán. In the popular imagination, all magic mushrooms became "teonanácatl," erasing the distinction between *Psilocybe mexicana* (pajaritos), *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]* (san isidros), and *Psilocybe zapotecorum* (derrumbes) — each of which had its own indigenous name, cultural context, and pattern of use.

## Chemical Controversies: When Names Outpace Science

The Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz survey reveals a pervasive problem in ethnomycolgy: the frequent discrepancy between indigenous claims of psychoactivity and the actual chemical composition of the fungi in question. Multiple species reported as "narcotic" or "hallucinogenic" by indigenous communities have subsequently been found to contain no psilocybin or other known hallucinogenic compounds.

*chlorophyllum-molybdites*, for example, was reported by the Yoruba tribe of Africa under the name «a jegba ariwo-orun» ("eat and hear voices from heaven"), strongly suggesting psychoactive use. Yet chemical analysis found no classic hallucinogens, and the species is generally considered poisonous. *Schizophyllum commune* was reported as hallucinogenic in Australia but is a common edible in Mexico and Guatemala. *Dictyophora indusiata* is used for divination by the Chinantec but contains no known psychoactive compounds.

These discrepancies suggest several possible explanations: (1) the effects may be produced by as-yet-unidentified compounds; (2) the fungi may be confused with genuinely psychoactive species that co-occur in the same habitats; (3) the effects may be placebo or contextually mediated within ceremonial settings; or (4) the indigenous classification may be based on visual or ecological similarity to known psychoactive species rather than on verified psychoactivity. The indigenous names, in these cases, encode cultural beliefs and practices that are not fully explained by Western pharmacology.

## Comparative Perspective: Mushroom Nomenclature in Other Cultures

The richness of Mexican mushroom nomenclature can be appreciated by comparison with other ethnomycological traditions. Siberian peoples have extensive vocabularies for *[[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]*, reflecting its central role in their shamanic practices. The Koryak language distinguishes between fresh and dried specimens, different preparation methods, and different ceremonial contexts. In New Guinea, Singer (1958, 1960a) documented the use of *Psilocybe*, *Russula*, and Boletaceous fungi as sacred mushrooms, though with less linguistic elaboration than in Mexico.

The Mexican case is unique in the sheer number of named species, the diversity of indigenous languages represented, and the complex layering of pre-Columbian and colonial-era naming practices. This reflects both the exceptional mycological diversity of Mexico — one of the world's most species-rich countries for fungi — and the particular intensity of mushroom veneration in Mesoamerican culture, which has no exact parallel elsewhere in the world.

## The Yoruba Name and Cross-Cultural Parallels

The Yoruba name for *[[guzman-chlorophyllum-molybdites-toxic-edible-paradox-yoruba-divinatory-use]]* — «a jegba ariwo-orun» ("eat and hear voices from heaven") — deserves special attention as an example of cross-cultural convergence in sacred mushroom nomenclature. The Yoruba description of hearing voices from heaven parallels the claims of telepathy and divine communication associated with psychoactive fungi in cultures [[fungi-around-the-world-species-distribution]]. Whether this reflects genuine psychoactive properties, culturally mediated expectations, or the universal tendency to associate unusual experiences with divine communication, the convergence is striking.

This case also illustrates the global scope of sacred mushroom use. The Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz survey documents sacred fungal practices not only in Mexico and New Guinea but in Africa, Asia, and circumpolar regions. The pattern of fungal veneration appears to be a human universal, expressed in culturally specific forms but sharing common themes of divine communication, cosmological symbolism, and altered consciousness.

## Significance and Threats

The linguistic diversity documented by Guzmán's survey is under increasing threat from multiple directions. As indigenous languages decline and younger generations adopt Spanish as their primary language, the traditional mushroom names are being lost. Each name that disappears takes with it a piece of ethnobotanical knowledge — information about which species were used, how they were prepared, what ceremonies accompanied their use, and what cultural meanings they carried. The documentation of these names by Guzmán and others represents an irreplaceable record of a fading cultural heritage.

The 200+ indigenous mushroom names of Mexico constitute one of the richest bodies of ethnomycological nomenclature in the world, rivaling the fungal vocabularies of any other culture. Their preservation and study is essential not only for ethnobotany but for understanding the full scope of human-fungal relationships across cultures. They remind us that the scientific naming of mushrooms — governed by the International Code of Nomenclature — is only one way of organizing fungal knowledge, and that indigenous classification systems encode information about cultural values, spiritual beliefs, and ecological relationships that no Linnaean binomial can capture.
