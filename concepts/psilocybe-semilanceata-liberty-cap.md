# Psilocybe semilanceata — The Liberty Cap

*[[psilocybe-semilanceata]]*, commonly known as the **liberty cap**, is the most widespread and well-studied psilocybin-containing mushroom in Europe, and likely the most common psychoactive *Psilocybe* species worldwide. It was first confirmed to contain psilocybin in 1963 by a research team including [[albert-hofmann]] and [[roger-heim]], making it the first European mushroom species found to harbor the alkaloid.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

The species has a complex taxonomic history:

- **1799:** First described indirectly by J. Sowerby as *Agaricus glutinosus* Curtis, following E. Brande's famous account of a family's intoxication in London's St. James's Green Park
- **1818:** E. Fries formally described it as *Agaricus semilanceatus* in *Observationes Mycologicae*
- **1870:** Kummer and Quélet classified it within the genus *Psilocybe*

Valid designations in the literature:
- *Psilocybe semilanceata* (Fr.) Kumm.
- *Psilocybe semilanceata* (Fr.) Quélet

The common name "liberty cap" refers to the distinctive conical shape of the cap, which resembles the Phrygian cap worn as a symbol of liberty during the French Revolution. In German, it is called *Spitzkegeliger Kahlkopf* ("pointed conical bald head").

## The 1799 Brande Incident

The earliest well-documented case of *P. semilanceata* intoxication occurred on October 3, 1799, in London. A poverty-stricken family collected mushrooms in St. James's Green Park, prepared them in a tinned iron saucepan, and ate them for breakfast. Symptoms appeared within an hour:

- The 8-year-old son (who had eaten the largest portion) experienced **fits of immoderate laughter** that could not be restrained by threats
- This progressed to vertigo and stupor, with periods of being roused only to relapse
- Pupils dilated to nearly the full circumference of the cornea
- Breathing was quick, pulse variable — sometimes imperceptible, other times too rapid to count
- The 40-year-old father experienced vertigo and reported that **everything appeared black**, which he believed presaged death

Symptoms progressed in wave-like cycles of increasing and fading intensity. After several hours, all disturbances subsided without lasting side effects. Sowerby's 1803 illustration and description of the mushroom responsible — a small, cone-capped species — is fully compatible with modern knowledge of *P. semilanceata*.

## Morphology

### Macroscopic Features

**Cap:** 0.5–4 cm broad, 1–2 cm tall. Uniformly conic to bell-shaped with a pointy or obtuse center forming an almost wart-like protrusion. Initially taller than wide, with margins bent inward. **Hygrophanous** — color changes dramatically with moisture content:

- **Wet:** Dirty olive-brown with translucent striate margins; center ocher or greenish-yellow against smudgy pale yellow, often with greenish stains; margins banded by a dark watery stripe
- **Dry:** Smudgy pale yellow; no stripes or banding evident

The cap surface is bald, thin-fleshed, with an easily separable pellicle that remains gelatinous-sticky for a long time, turning shiny when dry.

**Gills:** Olive brown to blackish purple brown, edges often remaining white. Spacing is quite crowded. Attachment adnate to broadly subdecurrent. Up to 3.5 mm wide.

**Stem:** Very slender, almost uniformly thin and always twisted. 6–12 cm long, 1.25–2 mm thick. Yellowish or whitish, with areas subjected to pressure developing **bluish-green stains**. Silky smooth; cortinate fibrils at roughly the center resemble remnants of a veil — brittle and lined with a white fibrous cord of wool-like texture.

**Flesh:** Cap flesh pale yellow when dry; stem flesh ocher brown, especially toward the base. Odorless (though damp fruiting bodies emit a faint odor reminiscent of radishes or poppies, weaker than that of *P. bohemica*). Flavor mild.

**[[spore-print]]:** Blackish purple brown.

### Microscopic Features

**Spores:** Elongated to elliptical, smooth, large. 12–16 × 6–8 µm.

### Special Characteristics

- **Bluing reaction:** Parts of the cap and lower stem develop blue discoloration, particularly noticeable when wet. Old, wet fruiting bodies may spontaneously develop transparent blue stains across caps. Stem bluing typically doesn't set in until 30–60 minutes after separation from the mycelium. Blue coloration is preserved during drying, though some fading may occur.
- **Fluorescence:** Under quartz lamp (UV) light, *P. semilanceata* specimens turn fluorescent. The responsible substance has not been identified.
- **Blue-staining variety:** M.C. Cooke (c. 1900) noted that a bluing variety (*var. caerulescens*) appeared to be the form responsible for intoxication, and questioned whether the bluing was caused by external factors that altered the mushroom's chemistry.

## Distribution

*P. semilanceata* has been reported from the following European countries: Finland, Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Germany, Switzerland, Austria, The Netherlands, Belgium, France, Russia, Poland, the former Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Scotland, England, Wales, Italy, and Spain.

Beyond Europe, the species is found in North America (particularly the Pacific Northwest), Australia, and Asia. Guzmán (1983) suggests it may be the most common psychoactive *Psilocybe* species worldwide, though the mycofloras of many countries remain undocumented.

In Germany, the species ranges from coastal areas to mountainous regions up to 1,720 m (5,160 ft) above sea level. In the former Czechoslovakia, specimens have been collected from 330 to 1,400 m. The species does not appear to favor any specific altitude.

## Habitat and Ecology

*P. semilanceata* is a **saprotrophic** species that fruits in the following conditions:

- **Season:** Late September through October (fall); occasionally August
- **Habitat:** Wet pastures surrounded by forest areas, acidic soil, grassy terrain alongside trails or around edges of forest lands
- **Substrate:** Soil below older pastures (cow, horse, or sheep); grows near but never directly on dung
- **Associated conditions:** Areas often flanked by creeks or swamp lands that saturate the soil with water; warmth in these wet areas during summer provides optimal mycelial growth
- **Key requirement:** Pastures should have been grazed at least once during the weeks before fruiting season for maximum yield
- **Exclusion:** Does NOT grow where artificial fertilizer has been used
- **Grouping:** Generally found in clusters of 30 or fewer mushrooms

The species does not favor specific areas of marked abundance but can produce huge numbers of fruiting bodies where conditions are excellent. Fruiting bodies from exceptionally favorable locations have been recorded with stems up to 21.5 cm (8.5 inches) tall, though these are exceptional.

## Alkaloid Content

*P. semilanceata* is notable for its consistently **high psilocybin content** compared to other species:

| Origin | Psilocybin (% dry weight) |
|---|---|
| Dubener Marshlands, Eastern Germany | 0.96 |
| Prague, Czech Republic | 1.05 |
| Krásná Lípa, Czech Republic | 0.91 |
| Norway | 0.95 |
| Pacific Northwest, USA | 0.93 |
| The Netherlands | 0.97 |

**Average across all origins: ~1% psilocybin** of dry weight. This means approximately 1 mg of psilocybin per gram of fresh mushroom material (accounting for ~90% water content).

**Baeocystin:** Present in all fruiting bodies at an average of 0.2% dry weight. This is the biochemical precursor of psilocybin (lacking the CH₃ group). Baeocystin is psychoactive in its own right: 4 mg caused mild hallucinations for three hours, and 10 mg was found to be approximately as psychoactive as a similar amount of psilocybin (Leung & Paul, 1967).

**Psilocin:** Only trace amounts, if any, are present.

**Size correlation:** Smaller mushrooms almost always contain more alkaloids per unit weight than larger specimens. This has been confirmed across multiple studies.

**Exceptional specimen:** One sample from Finland was found to contain **2.37% psilocybin** — one of the highest concentrations ever recorded for any mushroom species.

**Chemical stability:** There is no evidence for "chemical races" in *P. semilanceata* — the basic alkaloid profile does not vary dramatically between geographically separated populations, unlike some plant species. Combined analysis of dried mushrooms from multiple countries yielded a consistent ~1% psilocybin regardless of origin.

## Stability and Shelf Life

- A herbarium sample dated **1869** from Finland was still found to contain 0.014% psilocybin
- Another sample from **1843** contained no detectable alkaloids (drying method unknown)
- Temperatures above 50°C cause psilocybin to break down
- Freeze-dried mushrooms have highly porous texture that causes alkaloids to break down quickly at room temperature (20°C); they should be stored at -10°C for analysis
- Among all psilocybin-containing species, *P. semilanceata* has the **slowest psilocybin decay rate**

## Psychoactivity and Dosage

*P. semilanceata* is considered the most potently psychoactive mushroom among European species. Effective doses from self-experiment reports:

- **0.6 g dried, pulverized:** Mild to moderate effects lasting ~4 hours; included child-like wonder, vivid memory recall, sensory enhancement
- **1.3 g dried (30 mushrooms):** Full hallucinatory syndrome with rapid onset (~20 minutes), including heavy flow of tears, visions, "visualization," and periods of dysphoria; effects lasted ~5 hours

## Historical and Cultural Significance

### Medieval European Knowledge

Gartz hypothesizes that early European cultures likely knew about the psychoactive properties of *P. semilanceata* and related species, but this knowledge was lost during the aggressive repression of pre-Christian customs. Evidence includes:

- **[[bwyd-ellylon]]** ("Feast of Fairies"): A Welsh legend describing a poisonous mushroom considered a delicacy by fairies, likely referring to *P. semilanceata* which grows abundantly in the Welsh countryside during fall
- **Bolond gomba** ("fool's mushroom"): Documented by Clusius (1525–1609) in Hungary, used in love potions by wise women; also documented in Slovakia at the same time
- **Witch trials in the Italian Alps:** The Inquisition was unusually active in the Valcamonica, Valtrompia, and Valtellina valleys at ~2,000 m altitude — where nightshade plants ("witching herbs") don't grow, fly agaric is rare, but *P. semilanceata* abounds during fall
- **Ancient rock drawings:** Northern European rock art depicting mushroom themes and zoomorphic entities predates accounts of the Berserkers by over 2,000 years
- **Swedish summer solstice custom:** A poisonous mushroom ("Baran") was ritually cast into bonfires to destroy the powers of evil spirits, suggesting ancient knowledge of psychoactive mushrooms as links to the spirit world

### Mycophobia vs. Mycophilia

The Wassons' concept of mycophilia (mushroom-loving cultures) vs. mycophobia (mushroom-fearing cultures) helps explain why knowledge of psychoactive mushrooms was preserved in Mexico but lost in Europe. In Mexico, mushroom knowledge was integrated into religion and daily life; in Europe, mushrooms were demonized and associated with poison, insanity, and devil worship — a bias that persists in colloquial expressions like the Austrian "He ate those madness-inducing mushrooms."
