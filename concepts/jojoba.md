# Jojoba

Jojoba (Simmondsia chinensis, syn. Simmondsia californica) is a
drought-tolerant evergreen shrub native to the Californian Gulf region
and particularly the Sonoran Desert. The name jojoba (pronounced ho-ho-ba)
comes from the original native American Hohowi. Other common names include
goat nut, pig nut, coffee bush, and deer nut.

## Description

This drought-tolerant evergreen shrub has been recorded growing to a
height of 5 m with a spread of 6 m. The trunks are usually multi-stemmed
and have been recorded up to about 400 mm diameter. The tree lives for
over 100 years, making it an extremely long-lived crop.

## Climate and Soil Requirements

Established plants are capable of withstanding temperatures as low as -9
degrees C without long-term damage, but flower buds and new seed can be
damaged at -2 degrees C. Soil temperature can reach as high as 65 degrees
C with ambient air temperature as high as 48 degrees C.

Within the plant's natural distribution both summer and winter rainfall
areas are experienced, so the plant seems able to cope with either
rainfall pattern. Although the plant will establish in areas as low as
200 mm of rainfall, most production occurs above 400 mm. Where drainage
is good it can grow in areas up to 1300 mm rainfall.

The plant will not tolerate waterlogging. Valley bottoms are usually
avoided because of increased cold and moisture. It seems tolerant of
most soil types but needs reasonable porosity.

## Salt Tolerance

Jojoba is very resistant to saline water. Trials have shown no loss of
production at 7,000 ppm, making it one of the most salt-tolerant crop
plants available. This characteristic makes it suitable for [[salt-tolerant-fodder-species]] systems and rehabilitation of saline lands.

## The Oil

The oil, easily extracted from the nut, is a liquid wax which is very
rare, valuable, and highly prized in industry. It is used for cosmetics
and shampoos but also as a high-grade lubricant for quality machinery.
It is used where previously only sperm whale oil was used, making it an
important sustainable alternative.

The oil cake remaining after extraction and treatment is high in protein
(30%) and considered a good stock feed, adding to the plant's overall
utility in permaculture systems.

## Edible Uses

The edible nuts are eaten raw or roasted. Early settlers used them to
make a coffee substitute. The Coahuila tribe used them to make a drink
by grinding and boiling the nuts, then straining the liquid. The
Mexicans also made a drink by grinding roasted nuts with hard-boiled egg
yolk. The resulting paste was boiled with water, milk, and sugar with
vanilla added to taste. The drink is said to resemble thick chocolate.

## Wildlife and Forage

In its native environment, the plant is browsed by deer, cattle, sheep,
goats, crows, pigeons, jays, and rodents. The plant can be grown as an
unkept hedge, providing shelter and forage for wildlife in permaculture
systems.

## Cultivation Notes

Many people have been disillusioned by promises of instant wealth from
jojoba. This has resulted in a certain apathy toward the plant which has
stopped many people from even trialling a few plants. However, as a
multi-functional permaculture element, jojoba offers significant value
beyond its oil production potential.

The plant is dioecious, requiring both male and female plants for nut
production. It can be grown as an informal hedge or screen, providing
wind protection while also producing a valuable crop. A ratio of
approximately 1 male to 8-10 female plants ensures adequate pollination.

In permaculture systems, jojoba is best placed in zones that receive
full sun and have well-drained soils. Its extreme longevity (over 100
years) means it serves as a permanent element in the landscape design.
The plant's salt tolerance makes it valuable for planting around the
margins of saline areas or where irrigation water has elevated salt
levels.

## Propagation

Jojoba is propagated from seed. Seeds should be planted in well-drained
soil at appropriate depth. Plants establish best when given protection
from grazing animals during the establishment phase. Growth is slow in
the early years but the plant is extremely long-lived once established.

Seed viability can be maintained for several years if stored in cool,
dry conditions. Germination may be improved by light scarification.
Direct seeding is possible in areas with reliable rainfall.

## Historical Context

Native American peoples of the Sonoran Desert have used jojoba for
centuries. The oil was valued for skin and hair care, as a food
supplement, and for medicinal purposes. The Hohowi people gave the
plant its common name. Early European settlers adopted the practice of
using jojoba nuts as a coffee substitute.

## Industry and Economics

The development of jojoba as a commercial crop was driven by the ban on
sperm whale oil imports in the 1970s. Jojoba oil was identified as the
best natural substitute. While commercial monoculture plantings have
had mixed economic results, the plant's integration into diverse
permaculture systems provides multiple yields that improve overall
system resilience and economic return.

## Functions in Permaculture Systems

- Oil production for cosmetics, lubricants, and industrial uses
- Edible nut production for human consumption
- Stock feed from both foliage and oil cake
- Windbreak and hedge plant
- Salt-tolerant crop for marginal lands
- Wildlife habitat and forage
- Drought-tolerant element for arid zone designs
- Long-lived perennial requiring minimal maintenance
