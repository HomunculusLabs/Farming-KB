This page extracts and organizes the core principles as they appear across the 15 pamphlets.

## Energy Principles (Pamphlet I)

### Energy Is Not Created, Only Transferred
"Energy is not created. It is only transferred." This principle underlies all permaculture design: we do not generate energy but move it, store it, and direct it through systems.

### Store Energy Close to Source
Energy should be stored as close as possible to where it is captured. Water stored high on a property uses gravity to serve the house and garden. Biomass stored as standing forest provides building material, mulch, and fuel without transport.

### Every System Has Input Limits
A system can only accept inputs within its capacity. Beyond that threshold, inputs become pollutants. "Pollution is an output of a system for which no input exists."

## Resource Categories (Pamphlet I)

Mollison classifies resources into three types:
1. **Resources unaffected by use**: Information, solar energy, wind patterns. These increase the more they are shared.
2. **Resources that increase with use**: Some biological systems, skills, social networks. The more they are used, the more productive they become.
3. **Resources that decrease with use**: Fossil fuels, topsoil, mineral deposits. These must be carefully stewarded.

## The Yield Definition

"Yield is the sum of useful energy stores." This extends far beyond harvest to include stored water, soil fertility, genetic diversity, built infrastructure, and social capital. True yield measurement must account for all energy stores, not just the crop extracted.

## The Hong Kong Principle

The Hong Kong case study demonstrates that traditional 3x energy return farming became, after modernization, an energy-consuming system returning only 4-6% on energy invested. "Agriculture went from energy productive to energy consuming." This illustrates the principle that technological intensification can reverse net energy yield.

## The Problem Is the Solution

Repeated throughout the course: every problem contains its own solution. Fennel covering 18 acres is not a weed to clear but a licorice crop worth $3,000 per distillation. Pigs invading gardens can be directed as cultivation tools. Fire-prone vegetation becomes mulch for water retention.

## Every Element Performs Multiple Functions

"If you have those rules right, anybody, anywhere, can point to any element you place, and ask, 'Why did you put that there?' and you have answers." Every element must serve multiple purposes: a windbreak also provides fire protection, forage, mulch, and wildlife habitat.

## Every Function Is Supported by Multiple Elements

"No single element should perform a critical function alone." Redundancy increases system resilience. Multiple water sources, multiple food sources, multiple shelter options.

## Relative Location

"Everything is connected to everything else." Place elements so that they serve each other: chickens near the garden for manure and pest control, the kitchen window looking into the greenhouse, the compost pile on the path between house and garden.

## Use Biological Resources

"Biological resources are preferred over fossil resources." A chicken weed patrol replaces herbicides. A living fence replaces wire. Mycorrhizal fungi replace phosphorus fertilizer. This principle drives the preference for perennial polycultures over annual monocultures.

## Small and Intensive

"One gross error that we have all made is to try to occupy too much land, and not really develop any of this land we have." If you develop two acres intensively, "it contains quite a variety of elements, elements that make life worth living." The nuclear approach -- foot by foot care -- outperforms broadscale neglect.

## Accelerate Succession and Evolution

"Design for succession." Don't fight against natural plant succession; work with it. Fukuoka's contribution, recognized by Mollison: "What Fukuoka did was to lift these years and set them on top of each other. He stacked his legumes with his grains, with his ducks, and with his frogs."

## Cooperate with, Not Against, Nature

"The end to which we are working is the reforestation of the Earth and the restoration of its soils to health." Nature is the teacher, not the adversary. "You must learn to observe nature, to recognize how to develop potential uses so that humans may benefit."

## Work With Nature's Patterns

"Imitate the flux" of natural systems. The scarp doesn't retreat in a straight line but "intricately, throwing out ledges, pinching off islands, the desert buttes and towers." Mimicking these natural elaborations creates more edge, more habitat, more productivity.

## Least Change, Most Effect

"Try to make the least change produce the most effect." This is the permaculture Hippocratic oath. The designer who finds existing resources (fennel, clay, wind) and leverages them earns the fee without disrupting the system.

## The Edge Effect

Increase edge to increase productivity. "Without reducing the oxygen surface of the pond, we can give it as many times more edge as we care to do." This principle applies to ponds, gardens, forests, and social systems.

## Zone and Sector Analysis

**Zones** represent frequency of use (Zone 1: daily visit areas around the house; Zone 5: wilderness). **Sectors** represent incoming energies (sun, wind, fire, water, wildlife). Place elements according to both zone and sector needs simultaneously.

## The Super-Client

"In all our design work, we side with that 'super client,' Gaia." The environment itself is the ultimate client. If a human client's desires conflict with ecological health, the designer sides with nature.

## Functional Stacking

"We are laying on functions." Multiple functions occupy the same space in time: rice and ducks in the same paddy, chickens under fruit trees, aquaculture in irrigation ponds, vanilla orchids on palm trunks.

## Recycle at the Highest Level

"We recycle at the highest level possible. Everything can be a resource." Organic waste becomes compost, which becomes food, which becomes humans, who produce waste. Close the loop rather than discarding outputs.

## Source

- Bill Mollison, Permaculture Design Course, 1981, Pamphlets I, II, VIII
