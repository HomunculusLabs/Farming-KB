# Fungi Around the World -- Species Distribution Fungi are among the most widespread and ecologically diverse organisms on Earth, found in virtually every terrestrial and aquatic habitat from polar ice caps to tropical rainforests. Estimated at 2.2 to 3.8 million species globally, only approximately 150,000 have been formally described, meaning the vast majority of fungal diversity remains undocumented. The geographic distribution of fungi is shaped by climate, vegetation type, soil conditions, and co-evolutionary relationships with specific plant hosts. ## Regions Covered ### Amazon The Amazon basin contains extraordinary fungal diversity due to its tropical climate, vast old-growth forests, and high humidity. The region is home to numerous psilocybin-containing species, bioluminescent fungi, and a vast unexplored mycological territory. The sheer volume of fungal biomass in tropical forests is staggering — mycorrhizal networks connect the majority of trees in old-growth tropical forests, creating an underground "wood wide web" of nutrient exchange and chemical signaling. The Amazon's fungal diversity is intimately linked to its plant diversity. Each tree species may host dozens of unique endophytic and mycorrhizal fungal species, meaning deforestation directly destroys unknown fungal taxa. The rate of fungal extinction in tropical regions is estimated to be far higher than the rate of discovery and description. ### Africa African fungal diversity spans from tropical West and Central Africa to the southern temperate zones. Notable features include edible species collected by indigenous peoples for centuries (including termite mushroom cultivation in parts of West and East Africa), bracket fungi of savanna woodlands, and dung- inhabiting species of grassland ecosystems. ### Indonesia and Southeast Asia
Tropical Southeast Asian fungal diversity includes species from rainforests,
volcanic soils, and agricultural landscapes. The region is particularly rich
in both edible species collected for local markets and psychoactive species
used in traditional and recreational contexts. Thailand, Myanmar, Vietnam,
Laos, Cambodia, and Malaysia all have significant fungal diversity in tropical
and monsoon forests.

### India
Indian fungal biodiversity ranges from Himalayan oak forests to tropical
Western Ghats, with distinct fungal assemblages at different elevations. The
country has notable ethnomycological traditions, particularly in northeastern
states and tribal regions, and diverse polypore species associated with its
varied forest types.

### Japan
Japanese mycology is among the most thoroughly studied in Asia, with distinct
species assemblages in temperate and subtropical forests. Matsutake (Tricholoma
matsutake) is culturally and economically significant, commanding premium prices
in domestic and international markets. Japan also has a rich tradition of
[[bloomfield-mushroom-foraging-immigrant-poisonings-paddy-straw-confusion]] and a well-developed mushroom [[mushroom-cultivation-industry-directory]].

## Patterns in Global Fungal Distribution

Several broad patterns emerge from global fungal distribution data:
- **Tropical regions** harbor the highest species diversity but are the least
  thoroughly documented
- **Mycorrhizal fungi** tend to be host-specific, following their plant partners
  geographically
- **Saprotrophic fungi** are more widely distributed, following organic matter
  availability rather than specific hosts
- **Island biogeography** applies to fungi as it does to plants and animals
  — isolated landmasses like Australia and New Zealand have high endemism
- **Human-mediated dispersal** has introduced many fungal species to new
  regions through agriculture, horticulture, and timber trade

## Cosmopolitan vs. Endemic Fungal Species

Not all fungi follow the same [[guzman-global-distribution-patterns-neurotropic-fungi]]. Some species are truly cosmopolitan, found on every continent where suitable substrate exists — common saprotrophs like *[[stamets-cultivator-coprinus-comatus-shaggy-mane-cultivation]]* (shaggy mane) and *Pleurotus ostreatus* (oyster mushroom) fall into this category, aided by wind-dispersed spores that can travel atmospheric currents over vast distances. Others are highly endemic, restricted to single mountain ranges, island chains, or even individual tree species. The Hawaiian Islands, for example, host numerous endemic fungi found nowhere else, having evolved in isolation over millions of years. Understanding which species are cosmopolitan and which are endemic is critical for conservation, as endemic fungi face the same extinction risks as endemic plants and animals but receive far less protection under current biodiversity frameworks. ## Climate Change and Shifting Distributions

Climate change is altering fungal distribution patterns worldwide. As
temperatures rise and precipitation regimes shift, fungal species are migrating
toward higher latitudes and elevations. In the Northern Hemisphere, ectomycorrhizal
fungi historically associated with boreal forests are appearing further north,
while tropical pathogens like *Cryptococcus gattii* have established populations
in temperate regions of the Pacific Northwest. Range expansions of [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]]
specialists into new areas can disrupt established ecosystem dynamics and
introduce novel plant-fungal interactions.

## Conservation of Fungal Diversity

Fungal conservation remains one of the most neglected areas of biodiversity
protection. Few countries include fungi in threatened species legislation, and
most conservation efforts focus on visible flora and fauna. The IUCN Fungal
Conservation Committee has advocated for inclusion of fungi in Red List
assessments, but progress has been slow. Key conservation challenges include:

- **Habitat destruction**: The primary threat to fungal diversity, particularly
  in tropical regions where deforestation eliminates both above-ground and
  below-ground fungal communities
- **Lack of baseline data**: Most fungal species have never been recorded,
  making it impossible to assess population trends or extinction risk
- **Microclimate sensitivity**: Many fungi require precise temperature and
  humidity regimes; even small microclimate changes from [[fungal-species-concept-and-taxonomy]]
  fragmentation can eliminate local populations
- **Collecting pressure**: Popular edible and psychoactive species face
  overharvesting in accessible areas
