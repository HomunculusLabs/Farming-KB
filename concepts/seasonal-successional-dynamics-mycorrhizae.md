# Seasonal and Successional Dynamics of Mycorrhizal Communities

## Overview

Mycorrhizal communities are not static assemblages; they undergo predictable changes on both seasonal and successional timescales. These temporal dynamics profoundly influence nutrient cycling, plant community composition, and ecosystem recovery following disturbance. Understanding these patterns is essential for predicting ecosystem responses to environmental change.

## Seasonal Dynamics of Mycorrhizal Colonization

Mycorrhizal colonization intensity varies across seasons, driven by phenological shifts in host plant demand, soil temperature, moisture availability, and carbohydrate allocation patterns. Seasonal fluctuations in colonization rates directly affect the timing and magnitude of nutrient transfer between fungi and host plants.

### Counterintuitive Seasonal Patterns

One of the most striking examples of seasonal dynamics comes from salt marsh ecosystems, where *Spartina alterniflora* (smooth cordgrass) exhibits **nonmycotrophy during periods of maximal plant nutrient demand**. Rather than increasing mycorrhizal colonization when nutrient uptake needs are highest, *S. alterniflora* reduces or eliminates mycorrhizal associations during peak growing season. This paradoxical pattern may reflect the high nutrient availability in intertidal sediments during growing season flooding, where inorganic nitrogen and phosphorus are abundant enough that the carbon cost of maintaining mycorrhizae outweighs the benefits.

Seasonal colonization patterns in other systems generally show peak arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) colonization during mid-to-late growing season, coinciding with maximum photosynthate production and root exudation. Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) colonization in temperate and boreal forests often peaks in late summer to early autumn, with reduced visible colonization during spring flush and winter dormancy.

### Environmental Drivers of Seasonal Change

- **Soil temperature**: Directly regulates fungal metabolic activity and hyphal growth rates
- **Soil moisture**: Alters hyphal exploration and spore germination; drought typically suppresses colonization
- **Phenology**: Host plant carbon allocation to roots is the primary control on AM colonization intensity
- **Nutrient availability**: High available soil nutrient levels can suppress mycorrhizal dependence seasonally

## Mycorrhizal Succession During Plant Community Development

### Secondary Succession and Mycorrhizal Community Shifts

During secondary succession, the mycorrhizal community undergoes directional changes that parallel shifts in the plant community. These changes are driven by:

1. **Host plant species turnover** — different plant species host distinct mycorrhizal assemblages
2. **Soil development** — organic matter accumulation and nutrient transformation alter the soil environment
3. **Competitive interactions** — early-colonizing fungal species are progressively replaced by later-successional taxa
4. **Microclimate modification** — canopy closure changes light, temperature, and moisture regimes

### General Successional Pattern: AM to ECM Transitions

A well-documented pattern in temperate forest succession is the shift from **arbuscular mycorrhizal dominance** in early successional communities to **ectomycorrhizal dominance** in late-successional forests:

| Successional Stage | Dominant Mycorrhizal Type | Typical Host Plants | Soil Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early (pioneer) | Non-mycorrhizal or AM | Grasses, forbs, herbs | Low organic matter, high disturbance |
| Mid (seral) | Mixed AM and ECM | Shrubs, early-successional trees | Developing organic horizon |
| Late (climax) | Predominantly ECM | *Pinus*, *Quercus*, *Picea* | Thick organic layers, stable conditions |

Early successional plants are often non-mycorrhizal or form only AM associations. Many pioneer species in the Caryophyllales (e.g., *Chenopodiaceae*, *Amaranthaceae*) and Brassicales are characteristically non-mycorrhizal, possibly because they evolved in disturbed, high-nutrient microsites where mycorrhizal benefits are minimal.

As plant communities mature, later successional species increasingly form **ectomycorrhizae**. ECM fungi are generally more effective at mobilizing organic nitrogen and phosphorus from complex substrata — a competitive advantage in the increasingly organic-matter-rich soils of older stands.

## Mycorrhizal Community Changes with Stand Age

### *Pinus* and *Quercus* During Stand Development

Dighton et al. documented systematic changes in mycorrhizal community structure across stand age gradients in *Pinus* (pine) and *Quercus* (oak) forests. Their work demonstrated that mycorrhizal community composition is a reliable indicator of forest developmental stage. Key findings include:

- **Species richness of ECM fungi** tends to increase through mid-succession and may decline slightly in very old stands, following a hump-shaped diversity pattern
- **Morphotype composition** shifts markedly — early-successional stands are dominated by a few generalist ECM species (often *Thelephora*, *Laccaria*, *Pisolithus*), while older stands support more diverse assemblages including *Cortinarius*, *Russula*, and *Lactarius* species
- **Functional diversity** increases with stand age, as the mycorrhizal community encompasses fungi with different exploration strategies (contact, short-distance, medium-distance, and long-distance exploration types)
- In *Pinus* stands, the ratio of vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizae to ectomycorrhizae shifts progressively as the stand ages, reflecting both changes in understory composition and the competitive dynamics among mycorrhizal types
- In *Quercus* forests, ECM morphotype diversity continues to increase for several decades after canopy closure, indicating a long period of mycorrhizal community assembly following initial stand establishment

### Mechanisms Driving Age-Related Changes

The transition in mycorrhizal communities across stand age reflects:

- **Changes in host root architecture** — younger trees have coarser root systems; older trees develop finer, more branched root networks supporting diverse fungal partnerships
- **Accumulation of organic matter** — older stands have thicker fermentation and humus horizons, favoring fungi capable of organic nutrient mobilization
- **Reduced disturbance frequency** — older stands experience less soil perturbation, allowing slow-growing, specialist mycorrhizal taxa to establish
- **Competitive exclusion** — early-successional fungal generalists are outcompeted by specialists adapted to the more complex soil environment of mature stands

## Effects of Fire and Disturbance on Mycorrhizal Communities

Fire is a major disturbance that resets mycorrhizal successional trajectories. The effects of fire on mycorrhizal communities include:

- **Direct mortality** of surface hyphae and fruiting bodies, though resistant propagules (spores, sclerotia) often survive
- **Reduction in mycorrhizal colonization** of surviving roots, persisting for months to years post-fire
- **Shift toward fire-adapted fungal species** — certain ECM fungi (*Rhizopogon*, *Pisolithus*) are stimulated by fire-related cues such as heat, charred wood compounds, or post-fire nutrient pulses
- **Alteration of the soil mycorrhizal inoculum potential**, which influences post-fire plant community recovery

The resilience of mycorrhizal communities to fire varies with fire intensity, frequency, and ecosystem type. Low-intensity surface fires in pine forests may cause relatively transient effects, while stand-replacing crown fires can dramatically reduce mycorrhizal inoculum and slow succession.

## Functional Implications for Ecosystem Processes

### Nutrient Cycling

The transition from AM- to ECM-dominated communities has profound consequences for ecosystem nutrient cycling:

- **AM-dominated systems** favor rapid nutrient turnover, with a predominance of inorganic nutrient cycling pathways
- **ECM-dominated systems** promote organic matter accumulation and slower nutrient cycling, with greater reliance on enzymatic decomposition of complex organic substrates

### Ecosystem Recovery

Mycorrhizae play a critical facilitative role in plant succession and ecosystem recovery:

- **Nurse plant effects** — mycorrhizal networks established by early colonizers can facilitate the establishment of later-successional species through shared hyphal connections
- **Soil stabilization** — mycorrhizal hyphae bind soil particles, reducing erosion on disturbed sites
- **Nutrient capture** — mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective root foraging zone, accelerating nutrient capture during the critical establishment phase of succession

### Carbon Dynamics

Changes in mycorrhizal community composition during succession alter ecosystem carbon dynamics:

- ECM fungi typically produce more recalcitrant necromass than AM fungi, contributing to longer carbon residence times in soils
- The shift to ECM dominance in late succession may represent a positive feedback for soil carbon sequestration

## Summary

The seasonal and successional dynamics of mycorrhizal communities represent a key linkage between plant community dynamics and belowground ecosystem processes. Seasonal patterns in colonization reflect the shifting balance between plant carbon supply and fungal nutrient provision. Successional transitions from AM- to ECM-dominated communities fundamentally alter nutrient cycling pathways, carbon residence times, and soil development trajectories. Fire and other disturbances reset these trajectories, initiating secondary succession in both plant and fungal communities. The work of Dighton et al. on *Pinus* and *Quercus* stand development provides a particularly detailed picture of how mycorrhizal community structure tracks and potentially influences forest ecosystem maturation.
