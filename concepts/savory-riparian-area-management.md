# Riparian Area Management

Riparian areas, the strips of land adjacent to rivers, streams, and wetlands, are among
the most critical and most damaged landscapes in [[savory-brittle-environments]]. They
present unique management challenges because they often lie at the interface between
nonbrittle moisture conditions and very brittle catchments, and the health of the stream
depends entirely on the health of its surrounding watersheds.

## The Riparian Challenge in Brittle Environments

The typical example of riparian complexity occurs along riverine strips in brittle
environments. If rested for prolonged periods, such environments tend to pass on to
very stable woodlands. But this should not fool anyone into believing all riparian
areas need [[savory-rest-as-management-tool]] for all goals. Some riparian areas lack
sufficient subterranean moisture to maintain dense, woody cover.

In addition, even though the area adjacent to the stream or river may be nonbrittle,
it may be surrounded by very brittle catchments. Since the health of the stream is
entirely dependent on its catchments or watersheds, management has to cater for this
larger whole. The serious death of vegetation often seen in riparian areas of brittle
environments results from the failure to manage the broader watershed holistically.

## Causes of Riparian Degradation

Riparian degradation in brittle environments stems from the same fundamental causes as
upland [[savory-desertification-causes]]: the loss of periodic disturbance from large
herding animals and their predators. When domestic livestock replaced wild herds but
were managed at low densities scattered over large areas, the continuous year-round
presence around water points and stream bottoms led to chronic overuse of specific
areas while vast areas of the landscape suffered from partial rest.

The continuous trampling so often seen around gates, water points, and stream crossings
is a product of low-density, set-stock management rather than planned grazing. Under
holistic management, the same areas can receive brief periods of very high
[[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] followed by long recovery, which promotes
vegetation establishment and stream bank stabilization rather than degradation.

## Stream Bank Stabilization Through Animal Impact

Fish management often requires steep, vegetated banks rather than steep eroding ones.
Very high animal impact for very short periods can promote this. Where herds have firmed
up the ground somewhat, stream banks become vegetated and stable. The well-vegetated
river banks produced with cattle trampling and well-planned grazing demonstrate that
livestock, properly managed, can be a powerful tool for riparian restoration.

The key distinction is between prolonged, one-way trailing that threatens to wash out
stock trails to water or down hillsides, and the milling of excited animals for very
short periods. Although it may seem strange that damage caused by trampling can be
cured by trampling, the treatment works because of the vast difference between chronic
low-level impact and brief, intense impact followed by recovery.

## Planning for Riparian Recovery

A common concern for stream banks damaged by years of livestock presence can be
addressed through a combination of a fencing or herding pattern developed especially
for riparian management and the biological planning described in
[[savory-livestock-grazing-planning]]. This requires careful attention to several factors:

Timing of grazing to avoid sensitive periods such as nesting seasons, fish spawning,
and bank saturation from high water. The [[savory-land-monitoring]] procedures become
especially important in riparian areas because changes can be rapid and the
consequences of mismanagement severe.

Stock density must be high enough to achieve desired [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]]
but not so high or prolonged as to cause physical damage to banks during wet periods.
Grazing periods must be short enough to prevent [[savory-overgrazing-vs-overrest]] of
riparian vegetation and [[savory-recovery-and-grazing-periods]] must be long enough for
complete recovery of both upland and stream bank vegetation.

## The Watershed Connection

Riparian management cannot succeed in isolation from watershed management. The water
cycle of the entire catchment determines how much water reaches the stream, how
quickly, and how clean it is. Where [[savory-water-cycle-management]] is poor in the
uplands, flash floods scour stream banks, and base flows decline. Where upland
management promotes effective rainfall penetration and slow release, streams run
clearer and more consistently.

The quantity of edge between different habitats, together with water distribution,
counts for much in riparian planning. A mosaic of habitats with maximum edge between
woodland, grassland, and water provides far more opportunities for
[[savory-wildlife-livestock-integration]] than uniform landscapes. If firebreaks and
control allow hot fire to burn in patches, all the better for increasing habitat
contrast.

## Monitoring Riparian Health

Monitoring the four [[savory-ecosystem-foundation-blocks]] is essential following any
management change in riparian areas. Particular attention should be paid to litter
cover, soil exposure, plant spacings, and age structure of plant populations. Changes
in these indicators give the earliest warning of change in the water cycle through
increased runoff and surface evaporation.

In riparian areas, watch for early signs of erosion on banks, changes in vegetation
composition, and shifts in the balance between woody and herbaceous plants. These
indicators reflect the health of the whole watershed and should trigger replanning
when adverse trends are detected through the [[savory-land-monitoring]] procedures.

## Addressing Common Misconceptions

Many environmental organizations have acquired badly damaged brittle land and slowly
changed their management from a dogmatic hands-off, leave-it-to-Nature approach to
application of the most drastic techniques available. It happens in a predictable
sequence. After years of partial rest, the land responds vigorously and all looks good.
Gradually, however, moribund grasses appear, weedy plants increase, and bare spots open
up.

Inevitably managers conclude that fire should be used. The first unnatural means of
returning the land to Nature leads to another. In brittle environments fire invigorates
mature grasses but threatens all four ecosystem foundation blocks by exposing soil.
Technology often comes next, including seedings, plowings, and check dams. However, the
problem remains insoluble until managers understand the implications of rest in brittle
environments and the role that properly managed livestock can play in riparian
restoration.

## Riparian Areas and [[savory-land-planning-and-grazing-cell-design]]

In long-term land planning, riparian areas deserve special attention in the overlay
mapping process. Separate overlays showing stream corridors, water sources, and
sensitive riparian vegetation help ensure that the final paddock or herding layout
provides adequate protection during critical periods while still allowing the
beneficial effects of periodic animal impact. The goal is to design a system where
livestock can access riparian areas when impact is needed for restoration but can
be excluded during periods when banks are vulnerable.
