# Lichen Symbiosis Mechanisms

Source: Symbiotic Fungi (Varma and Kharkwal, eds.), Chapter 1

## Introduction

[[lichens]] represent a classic mutualistic symbiosis between a fungus (mycobiont) and a
photosynthetic partner (phycobiont), typically an alga or cyanobacterium. The concept
of symbiosis, first defined by de Bary in 1879 as the living together of unlike organisms,
is exemplified by lichen associations. The two organisms jointly form a thallus that is
morphologically and functionally distinct from either partner alone.

## Structural Organization

The fungus most commonly forms the majority of the lichen's bulk, though in filamentous
and gelatinous lichens this may not always be the case. Lichens take the external shape
of the fungal partner and are named based on the fungus.

Fungal taxonomy of lichens:

- Typically members of the Ascomycota (ascolichens)
- Rarely members of the Basidiomycota (basidiolichens)
- Neither group forms monophyletic lineages in their respective phyla
- Several major solely or primarily lichen-forming groups within each phylum

Resource exchange in the symbiosis:

- Fungus derives nutrition (carbohydrates) from the alga
- Alga is protected by the fungal partner
- Bi-directional resource flow creates emergent properties

## Photobiont Partners

The algal partner in lichens may belong to several groups:

- **Blue-green algae (cyanobacteria)**: Anabaena, Nostoc
- **Green algae**: various genera
- **Yellow-green algae**: various genera

When the lichen contains only a blue-green alga, it performs dual functions of
photosynthesis and nitrogen fixation. If a green alga is also present (three-member
association), the blue-green alga becomes relieved of photosynthesis and specializes
in nitrogen fixation exclusively. Evidence: in three-member associations, the
blue-green alga produces more heterocysts (specialized nitrogen-fixing cells).

## Heterocyst Biology

Heterocysts are differentiated cyanobacterial cells that carry out nitrogen fixation
under aerobic conditions. Key features:

- Formed in response to lack of fixed nitrogen (NH4+ or NO3-)
- Contain no functional photosystem II (cannot produce oxygen)
- Retain photosystem I for cyclic photophosphorylation and ATP regeneration
- Provide appropriate conditions for oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase enzyme
- In free-living cyanobacteria: 5-10% of cells become heterocysts
- In symbiotic state: 25-30% of cells become heterocysts

## Physiological Adaptations

Lichens exhibit remarkable adaptations to environmental extremes:

- Extremely slow-growing: approximately 1 mm per year
- Highly tolerant to xeric conditions (desiccation and heat)
- Found growing on tree trunks and rocks in diverse environments
- Very sensitive to atmospheric pollutants
- Rarely seen near human habitations with automobile fumes and factory effluents
- Useful bioindicators of air quality

Vegetative reproduction occurs through soredia and isidia, spreading both symbiotic
partners together and ensuring the integrity of the association during dispersal.

## Comparison with Geosiphon pyriforme

Geosiphon pyriforme, a coenocytic soil fungus forming an endocytobiotic association
with Nostoc punctiforme, provides an instructive contrast with lichen symbiosis:

| Feature | Geosiphon | Lichen |
|---------|-----------|--------|
| Photobiont location | Inside fungal cell (endocytobiotic) | Contact between partners (no endocytosis) |
| Vegetative propagation | Does not exist | Through soredia and isidia |
| Water loss tolerance | Cannot survive | Robust toward dehydration |
| High temperature | Sensitive | Many species resistant |
| Light tolerance | Moderate light only | High irradiance tolerated |

When a Geosiphon hypha contacts free-living Nostoc cells, they are incorporated at the
hyphal tip, which swells and forms a unicellular bladder approximately 1-2 mm in size.
Inside this bladder, cyanobacteria remain physiologically active and dividing. Geosiphon
has been suggested as a model system for studying arbuscular [[mycorrhiza]].

## Cyanobacterial Symbioses Across Plant Groups

Nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterial symbioses parallel lichen associations across the plant
kingdom:

### Bryophytes (Anthoceros, Notothylas)

- Endophyte Nostoc inhabits mucilage cavities on ventral side of thallus
- Produces more heterocysts than in free-living condition
- Anthoceros produces branched hypha-like filaments interspersed with algal colony
- Photosynthate transported to Nostoc; fixed nitrogen transferred in reverse

### Pteridophytes (Azolla)

- Floating fern with Anabaena in ovoid cavities inside leaves
- Heterocyst proportion increases from 5-10% (free-living) to 25-30% (symbiotic)
- Epidermal cells form multicellular hairs with warty outgrowths (transfer cells)
- Grown in rice fields as green manure: supplies 50-75% of crop nitrogen requirement
- Extensively employed in China and Japan for rice cultivation

### Gymnosperms (Cycas)

- Coralloid roots at soil surface infected with Anabaena and Nostoc
- Algae found in midcortical zone as a conspicuous ring
- Roots become negatively geotropic, forming dichotomously branched coralloid masses
- Only cycads form these symbiotic relationships among gymnosperms

### Angiosperms (Gunnera)

- Only Gunnera (Haloragaceae) forms Nostoc symbiosis among angiosperms
- Endophyte situated in glands at petiole attachment points
- Nostoc filaments enter cells intracellularly, producing many heterocysts
- Nostoc is heterotrophic, dependent on host for carbohydrate supply

## Ecological Significance

Lichen symbiosis demonstrates that close, long-term interactions between different
species create novel organismal forms with emergent properties. Key ecological roles:

- Bioindicators of air quality due to pollution sensitivity
- Pioneer organisms in primary succession (colonize bare rock)
- Nitrogen fixation contributes to ecosystem nitrogen inputs
- Important in nutrient-poor and extreme environments
- Model systems for studying fundamental symbiotic mechanisms
