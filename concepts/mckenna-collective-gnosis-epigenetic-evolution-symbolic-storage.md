# Collective Gnosis and Epigenetic Evolution: Symbols, Language, and the End of Biological Evolution

## The Epigenetic Turn in Human Development

In *Food of the Gods*, Terence McKenna advances the argument that humanity has undergone a fundamental transition from 
biological to cultural—or what he terms "epigenetic"—evolution. By "epigenetic," McKenna does not refer to the modern 
molecular biology of gene expression regulation but rather to the broader idea that much of what defines human beings 
exists outside the genome, encoded in culture, language, technology, and social institutions. He writes that humans 
have become "largely epigenetic," meaning that the information necessary for human survival and flourishing is no 
longer carried primarily in DNA but in the vast networks of symbolic communication that constitute human culture. This 
transition marks, in McKenna's view, the effective end of biological evolution as the primary driver of human change. 
Once symbolic systems became sufficiently sophisticated to store and transmit complex information across generations, 
the selective pressures that had shaped hominid evolution for millions of years were largely superseded by cultural 
forces. The genome became a stable platform upon which the far more rapid and flexible processes of [[star-fire-and-sacred-substances]] 
could operate.

## Symbols and Extrasomatic Information Storage

The key mechanism enabling this transition was the development of symbols and symbolic language. McKenna argued that 
symbols represent a fundamentally new kind of information storage—one that exists outside the physical body and can 
persist independently of any individual organism. Unlike genetic information, which is bound to the organism and 
transmitted only through reproduction, symbolic information can be shared, modified, combined, and accumulated at 
rates far exceeding anything possible through biological inheritance. This capacity for extrasomatic storage created 
what McKenna called a "cortex outside the body"—an externalized cognitive infrastructure that augmented and eventually 
surpassed the information-processing capacity of individual human brains. Oral traditions, material culture, written 
records, and eventually electronic databases each represented successive expansions of this external cognitive system, 
each capable of storing more information more reliably than the last.

## Population Size and the Halt of Physical Evolution

McKenna drew on [[aspergillus-fumigatus-population-genetics]] to support his argument that biological evolution effectively ceased with the 
expansion of human populations. In small, isolated populations, genetic drift and selection can produce significant 
phenotypic changes over relatively short time periods. But as human populations grew larger and more interconnected, 
the genetic mixing that accompanies large populations tends to swamp local adaptive changes, preventing the fixation 
of new traits through natural selection. The consequence is that modern humans are biologically very similar to their 
ancestors of fifty thousand years ago. The dramatic changes in human life over that period—from Stone Age foraging to 
digital civilization—are almost entirely cultural rather than biological. The genome has become a conservative force, 
maintaining the basic human blueprint while culture performs the adaptive work that evolution once handled through 
genetic variation and natural selection.

## Libraries, Databases, and the Externalization of Mind

McKenna traced the history of extrasomatic information storage from its origins in oral tradition through the 
invention of writing, the development of libraries, and the creation of electronic information systems. Each 
technology represented a quantum leap in the capacity for external memory, and each reduced dependence on individual 
human minds as primary repositories of knowledge. With writing, information could be preserved with greater accuracy 
and over longer time periods than any oral tradition could sustain. Libraries concentrated this written knowledge in 
accessible repositories, creating institutions of collective memory transcending the limitations of individual 
lifespan. Electronic databases and digital networks made virtually unlimited quantities of information instantaneously 
accessible across global distances. McKenna saw both promise and danger in this progression. Externalization of memory 
freed consciousness for more creative and abstract thought, but it also created dependence on external systems that 
could be controlled, manipulated, or destroyed—raising the specter of civilization depositing its collective 
intelligence in fragile institutional structures.

## Global Informational Simultaneity

One of the most consequential developments in the evolution of extrasomatic storage, according to McKenna, is the 
creation of what he called "global informational simultaneity"—the condition in which information is transmitted and 
received across the planet in real time. Electronic communications media, from telegraph and radio to satellite 
networks and the internet, have created a shared informational environment in which events in one part of the world 
are immediately known and responded to in every other part. This simultaneity has profound implications for human 
consciousness and social organization. McKenna argued that it creates conditions for a genuine planetary culture—a 
shared body of knowledge, values, and experiences that transcends national, ethnic, and linguistic boundaries. At the 
same time, he warned that the homogenizing tendencies of global media could suppress cultural diversity and local 
knowledge systems representing millennia of accumulated wisdom about specific environments and ways of life.

## Collective Gnosis and Universal Truth

Underlying McKenna's analysis is the concept of "collective gnosis"—a shared, intuitive knowledge that transcends 
individual experience and rational analysis. Gnosis, in this sense, is not propositional knowledge but a direct, 
participatory understanding of reality accessible through altered [[mckenna-food-gods-designer-states-of-consciousness]], particularly those induced 
by [[psychedelic-substances-comparison]]. McKenna argued that this collective gnosis underlies the universal human tendency toward 
religious and spiritual experience. The faith that human beings place in universal truths—whether expressed through 
religious doctrine, philosophical system, or scientific theory—reflects an intuitive recognition that reality is 
fundamentally unified and that individual consciousness is a local manifestation of a much larger field of awareness. 
The problem, as McKenna saw it, is that this intuitive recognition has been progressively obscured by the ego-bound 
consciousness of [[dominator-culture-and-partnership-society]], which privileges separation, competition, and instrumental rationality over 
communion, cooperation, and direct experiential knowledge.

## Ideologies as Meaning-Defined Environments

McKenna extended his analysis to the phenomenon of ideology, which he defined as a "meaning-defined environment"—a 
comprehensive system of beliefs and values that shapes perception, behavior, and social organization. Ideologies 
function as symbolic environments that mediate between individual consciousness and the raw data of experience, much 
as Huxley's "reducing valve" mediates between Mind at Large and ordinary awareness. The danger of ideologies is that 
they can become self-sealing systems that resist contradiction and suppress dissent. When widely adopted, they create 
a shared reality that participants experience as simply "the way things are," making it extremely difficult to 
recognize the constructed nature of the beliefs organizing social life. McKenna saw the major ideologies of Western 
civilization—materialism, progress, individualism, technological optimism—as particularly powerful and dangerous 
examples of this phenomenon.

## Julian Jaynes and the Bicameral Mind

McKenna engaged extensively with Julian Jaynes's controversial theory of the bicameral mind, presented in *The Origin 
of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind* (1976). Jaynes proposed that human consciousness as we 
understand it—a unified, introspective self-awareness—emerged relatively recently, perhaps as recently as the second 
millennium BCE. Before this transition, Jaynes argued, human beings experienced their own thoughts as external 
voices—the voices of gods, ancestors, or rulers—which they obeyed without question. McKenna found this historical 
analysis compelling but argued it suffered from a critical omission: the role of hallucinogenic plants. McKenna 
proposed that the "voices of the gods" documented in ancient texts were not mere auditory hallucinations produced by a 
neurologically different brain but rather the genuine voices of plant entities encountered through deliberate 
psychedelic use. The breakdown of the bicameral mind was not simply neurological maturation but a cultural consequence 
of declining availability and use of psychedelic substances in the ancient Near East.

## Shamanism as Intuitive Group Decision-Making

McKenna offered shamanism as an alternative model of social organization—a form of intuitively guided group 
decision-making in which altered states of consciousness serve as a means of accessing information and perspectives 
not available through ordinary rational analysis. In this model, the shaman functions not as a priest or administrator 
but as a cognitive specialist whose trained ability to navigate non-ordinary states of consciousness provides the 
group with access to a wider range of information and possibilities. The shamanic journey, the vision quest, and the 
communal psychedelic ceremony are technologies of collective intelligence that supplement ordinary decision-making 
with insights drawn from the deeper layers of consciousness. This model stands in sharp contrast to the hierarchical, 
bureaucratic forms of decision-making that characterize modern institutions. McKenna argued that the replacement of 
shamanic cognition with bureaucratic rationality represents a profound loss of collective intelligence—a narrowing of 
the informational base upon which human groups make critical decisions about survival, [[mycelial-foraging-resource-allocation]], and social 
organization.

## Toward an Archaic Revival

McKenna concluded his analysis with a call for what he termed an "Archaic Revival"—a deliberate recovery of the 
epigenetic technologies of altered consciousness that characterized earlier human cultures. He argued that the crises 
facing modern civilization—ecological collapse, nuclear threat, social fragmentation—are ultimately crises of 
consciousness stemming from the ego-bound, dominator mentality that replaced the partnership-oriented, ecstatic 
consciousness of shamanic cultures. The recovery of collective gnosis through the responsible use of psychedelic 
substances, combined with the development of new forms of communal organization based on shared intuitive knowledge, 
represents, in McKenna's view, the most promising path toward a sustainable and humane future.
