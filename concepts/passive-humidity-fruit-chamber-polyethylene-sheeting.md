# Passive Humidity Fruit Chamber with Polyethylene Sheeting
A simple and effective approach to mushroom fruiting uses clear plastic bins covered
with polyethylene sheeting to maintain humidity through natural evaporation from the
casing surface. This design avoids the complexity and cost of automated humidification
systems while providing adequate environmental conditions for a wide range of cultivated
species.
The species successfully grown in this system include [[psilocybe-mexicana]],
[[psilocybe-tampanensis]], [[psilocybe-cubensis]], and [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]].

## Design Philosophy

Many cultivation guides describe elaborate fruiting setups incorporating humidifiers,
cool-mist devices, ultrasonic foggers, and automated control systems. While these
systems can produce excellent results, they represent significant additional expense,
complexity, and potential points of failure.
The passive humidity chamber demonstrates that high mushroom yields are achievable with
a far simpler approach that relies on basic physics rather than mechanical intervention.
The fundamental principle is that the evaporation of water from the moist casing soil
surface generates sufficient humidity within an enclosed space to support normal
fruiting.
By containing this evaporated moisture within a sealed or semi-sealed bin, the relative
humidity remains at levels adequate for pin formation and mushroom development without
any mechanical humidification. The system is self-regulating because evaporation rates
naturally adjust based on ambient temperature and humidity conditions.

## Container Construction

The fruiting containers consist of simple clear plastic bins. Clear construction allows
ambient light to reach the developing mushrooms, which is necessary for normal fruiting
body development and directionality. Mushrooms grown in total darkness often produce
malformed fruiting bodies with elongated stems and underdeveloped caps.
Opaque containers can be used if an external light source is provided, but clear bins
require no additional lighting infrastructure beyond normal room illumination. The bins
should be large enough to hold five cased trays comfortably with space between them for
air circulation.
Each tray contains the colonised grain from a single 720 ml jar spread in a layer and
covered with casing soil. The five-tray-per-bin arrangement provides a productive unit
that can be managed as a group throughout the fruiting cycle.

## Polyethylene Covering

The bins are covered with polyethylene sheeting rather than rigid lids. Polyethylene
sheeting is flexible enough to drape over the bin opening and conform to the irregular
surface created by the trays inside. This creates a reasonably airtight seal while
allowing the cultivator to easily lift the sheeting for daily misting and inspection.
Rigid lids, by contrast, create a fixed volume that does not adjust to changes in tray
height or position. The sheeting should be large enough to extend well past the edges
of the bin opening, ensuring that the seal is maintained even when the sheeting is
briefly lifted and replaced during daily maintenance.
Some cultivators use elastic bands or tape to hold the sheeting in place, though the
weight of the sheeting itself combined with the moisture that condenses on its inner
surface is usually sufficient to maintain the seal without additional fastening.
Condensation on the inner surface of the polyethylene sheeting is a normal and
beneficial feature of the system. The condensation indicates that the humidity cycle
is functioning correctly, with water evaporating from the casing, entering the air,
and recondensing on the cooler plastic surface.
This condensation can drip back onto the casing surface, contributing to the overall
moisture cycle within the chamber and reducing the frequency of misting required.

## Stackability and Space Efficiency

A key advantage of this design is stackability. Clear plastic bins of uniform size can
be stacked vertically, making extremely efficient use of limited growing space. A
cultivator with only a small closet or shelf area can maintain multiple bins
simultaneously, each at a different stage of the fruiting cycle.
This vertical organisation allows a significant number of fruiting trays to be managed
in a footprint that would accommodate only a fraction of that number if the bins were
arranged side by side on a surface.
Stacking requires that each bin have a rigid lid or support surface between levels.
The polyethylene sheeting alone cannot support the weight of additional bins. A flat
piece of cardboard, plywood, or a spare bin lid placed on top of the sheeting provides
the necessary support for stacking.
The support should be clean to avoid introducing contamination to the bin below. The
vertical arrangement also creates a natural airflow pattern. Warmer air rises from
lower bins and passes through the upper bins, creating gentle convection currents that
supplement the passive air exchange from the side holes.
This stacked configuration can actually improve air exchange compared to individual
bins placed on a flat surface, as the temperature differential between levels drives
more consistent air movement throughout the growing area.

## Air Exchange Through Side Holes

Air exchange within the fruiting bin is provided by small holes melted into the sides
of the plastic container. These holes allow stale air containing elevated carbon
dioxide levels to exit and fresh air with normal carbon dioxide levels to enter
through passive convection.
The convection is driven by slight temperature differences between the inside and
outside of the bin. The number and size of holes should be balanced to provide
adequate gas exchange without causing the casing surface to dry out too quickly.
The holes can be created by heating a metal rod or nail and pressing it against the
plastic. The resulting melted edges seal the hole perimeter, preventing the plastic
from cracking or fraying over time. This method produces clean, consistent holes
without creating plastic shavings that could fall into the fruiting chamber.
Placement should be distributed around the sides of the bin rather than concentrated
in one area, promoting even air distribution throughout the chamber. The holes can
be covered with mesh material to prevent the entry of fungus gnats and other small
flying insects that commonly infest mushroom growing operations.
Insect pests can introduce contamination, damage developing fruiting bodies, and
create nuisance populations that spread to other areas of the growing space.

## Humidity Dynamics

The system relies entirely on the moisture content of the casing soil for humidity
generation. When cased trays are first placed in the bin, the casing layer is
thoroughly moistened. As the mycelium grows through the casing and prepares to form
primordia, water evaporates from the casing surface.
This evaporated water raises the relative humidity inside the enclosed bin. The daily
misting regimen replenishes moisture lost to evaporation and to the limited air exchange
through the side holes. By maintaining the casing surface at proper moisture levels,
the cultivator indirectly maintains the bin humidity at appropriate levels.
The casing surface functions as a living humidifier, automatically adjusting its output
based on the temperature and airflow conditions within the bin. As temperature rises
and evaporation increases, more water vapour enters the bin air. As temperature falls,
evaporation slows and humidity stabilises.
No sensors, controllers, or mechanical systems are needed to maintain the balance. The
system responds automatically to changing environmental conditions.

## Daily Management Protocol

Successful use of passive humidity fruit chambers depends primarily on consistent daily
attention to the casing surface moisture. The casings should be misted each day and the
casing surface should never be allowed to dry out.
A spray bottle filled with clean water is the only equipment needed for humidity
management. The spray bottle should produce a fine mist rather than a coarse stream to
avoid disturbing the casing surface or the developing mushrooms.
Directly after a flush is picked, watering should be increased because the maturing
mushrooms pull a significant amount of moisture from the casing soil during their
development. The casing may appear noticeably drier after a heavy flush.
Restoring full moisture content at this point supports the initiation of the next flush
and prevents the casing from cracking or pulling away from the tray edges.
It is very difficult to give explicit directions on a watering regime. Cultivators
will have to develop a feeling for it through experience. The casing should glisten
with moisture after misting but should never have standing water on its surface.

## Casing Surface Hygiene

The source emphasises keeping the surface of the casing as clean as possible by
removing dead pinheads, known as aborts, as these can lead to moulds showing up on the
casing surface. Aborts are mushroom primordia that have stopped developing and begun
to decompose.
They appear as small darkened or discoloured pins that fail to grow while their
neighbours mature normally. Regular removal of aborts prevents them from becoming
nutrient sources for competitor organisms. The decaying organic matter of the abort
provides an ideal growth medium for mould spores that may be present in the fruiting
chamber environment.
By removing aborts promptly and cleanly, the cultivator reduces the likelihood of
contamination outbreaks on the casing surface that could spread to adjacent trays
within the same bin.
