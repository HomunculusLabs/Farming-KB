### Method 3: Deep Mulch Garden Bed

Useful in gardens too small to move the tractor daily, or where soil is very poor:

1. Leave the chicken tractor in one place and add about an inch of mulch each day.
2. After about five weeks (the time it takes broilers to grow from chicks to maturity), you will have a thick raised bed ready to plant.
3. Andy Lee warns that leaving the tractor in one place this long may give predators (dogs, skunks, foxes) time to dig under the pen. Lay chicken wire on the ground around the pen and pin it in place to frustrate digging predators.

Both mulch techniques work on slopes as well as level ground. On a slope, the chickens and gravity will leave the mulch thicker on the downhill side, resulting in a level, terraced bed.

## Free-Range Grazing

Chickens can also graze free-range in the garden, where they will glean insects, slugs, and weed seeds. Best practices:
- Wait until garden plants are mature before letting chickens in, as poultry will happily eat tender seedlings
- Allow birds into the garden in the late afternoon -- they won't be there long enough to do damage and will naturally return to their coop or tractor at dusk, sparing a lengthy chicken chase
- Keep an eye on them in case they find a crop they really like, such as berries or tomatoes

## Growing Chicken Forage

A hen needs about 80 pounds of grain per year, which could be grown on about 1,000 square feet. Feeding a small flock of eight hens would require an 80-by-100-foot garden and countless hours of labor -- not realistic for most gardeners. Instead, Hemenway recommends growing multifunctional plants around the yard to supplement the chickens' diets:

- These plants can be integrated into the garden design, supplying habitat, food, nutrients, and all the other needs of the ecological garden, not just chicken feed
- Growing chicken forage creates a closed cycle: as chickens build the soil, the resulting food plants become healthier and more lush, and the chickens in turn grow stronger and more productive
- This approach reduces feed costs and provides valuable vitamins and fresh foods

## Integrating Chickens with Garden Design

Hemenway recommends placing chicken coops and tractors at the Zone 1/Zone 2 boundary for efficiency:
- Toss kitchen scraps to the birds as you head out to the garden
- Give weeds to the chickens on the return trip in exchange for eggs
- Why make three separate trips when one well-placed element can serve multiple functions?

A trick used by other gardeners: connect a chicken coop to a greenhouse. The chickens eat bugs, add CO2 for plant growth, and provide body heat.
