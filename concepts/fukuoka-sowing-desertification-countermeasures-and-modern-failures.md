## Desertification Countermeasures and Modern Failures

In *Sowing Seeds in the Desert*, Masanobu Fukuoka launched a comprehensive
critique of conventional approaches to desertification, arguing that
virtually every major technological intervention pursued by governments and
scientists not only fails to address the root causes but actively worsens the
problem. His analysis spans dams, irrigation schemes, fast-growing tree
plantations, drip irrigation, desalination, and computer-modeled revegetation
plans.

## The Dam Paradigm

When people see no rain and no water in the desert, their first impulse is
to build dams to store river water, followed by waterways and irrigation
canals. Fukuoka cited the Aswan Dam on the Nile, and planned mega-dams on
China's Yellow River and India's Narmada River, as examples of expedient
short-term measures that will become hundred-year mistakes.

The core error is conceptual: the main reason water is disappearing from
rivers is that rain has stopped falling. The first step in countering
desertification is not to redirect river flow but to cause rain to fall
again, which requires revegetation. Trying to revegetate deserts using the
scarce remaining river water is putting the cart before the horse.

## The Narmada River Plan

Fukuoka described India's plan to construct more than two hundred dams along
the Narmada River, the second holiest river after the Ganges. When dammed
waters rise, they submerge forests, destroy livelihoods of river-dwelling
people, and drive millions toward surrounding deserts. This influx puts
greater pressure on scant desert resources, and with the loss of forests, the
deserts grow even larger. He argued India must choose between hydroelectric
power plants and revegetating the desert to bring the earth back to life.
