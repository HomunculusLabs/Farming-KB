## Tribal Pattern Encoding and Mnemonics

Tribal societies have developed sophisticated methods for encoding and transmitting complex information through pattern, song, dance, and visual art — systems that often surpass alphabetical and numerical records in density, memorability, and accessibility.

## Song as Navigation and Data Storage

### Song Navigation
Indigenous peoples worldwide used song as a precise navigational and timing tool. Anne Cameron documented Nootka Indian navigation songs of British Columbia: each destination had its own song — one for China, one for Japan, one for each island. The navigator needed only to know the song to know their position.

On canoe voyages along the Pacific coast from California to the Aleutians, steerswomen used the rhythmic duration of song stanzas to time both current speed and boat speed through water. Current speed was measured between headlands; boat speed was measured against a floating log. Song stanzas functioned as highly accurate, reproducible timers over extended periods — a remarkable precision instrument embedded in culture.

### Song Stanzas as Encyclopedias
Song content served as a record of observations from prior voyages, open to receiving new data. Tribal peoples who could call deer (Paiute wise men), dolphins (Gilbert Islanders), or kangaroos (Tasmanians) possessed profound understanding of interspecific behavioral "pulser" patterns. Eskimos navigated fog by listening to quail dialects specific to certain headlands.

## The Anasazi Spiral Calendar

One of the most remarkable examples of pattern encoding is the [[anasazi-spiral]] petroglyph near the top of a mesa in the desert Southwest USA. This apparently simple spiral form carries profound astronomical and calendrical information:

- **19 intercepts** on its horizontal axis mark the points at which moon-shadows are cast by gnomons onto the spiral
- **Three carefully balanced rock slabs** serve as gnomons, casting moon-shadows or directing vertical daggers of sunlight to specific points
- The spiral records the **18.6-year lunar cycle** (the sway of Earth"s axis), regulating planting and ceremony timing
- It also serves as a **drought and flood prediction calendar** for this same 19-year cycle

This single, simple spiral encodes what would require a small library in our clumsy alphabetical and numerical systems. The knowledge was available to all people living nearby. Only persistent investigation by [[anna-soaer]] (an artist with intuitive observational skills) revealed its significance — scientists had doubted tribal capacity for such long-term pattern encoding.

## Phenomenological Time

Australian Aborigines evolved multiple time concepts, of which our linear clock-based system was the least important in daily life. Far more significant was **phenomenological (phenological) time** — time as given not by clocks but by the life phenomena of flowers, birds, and weather.

Mollison recounts an elderly Pitjatjantjara woman who pointed out a small desert flower coming into bloom and immediately knew that dingoes in distant northern hills were rearing pups, and that it was time for her group to travel to collect them. Thousands of such ecological correlations were known to tribal peoples. Some signals might not occur for 100 or 500 years (such as the flowering of certain bamboos), yet when they did, the appropriate actions and ceremonies were known and linked phenomena identified.

## The Buddhist Mnemonic: Om Mani Padme Hum

The Buddhist chant "Om mani padme hum" (pronounced "Aum ma-ni pay-may hung" by Tibetans and Nepalese) serves as a pattern mnemonic for the nature of reality:

- **Aum (Om)**: The awakening harmonic — the sound of all stillness and all time, recalling the universe itself. The fundamental harmonic.
- **Ma-ni**: The unchanging essence or diamantine core of all phenomena — truth, represented as a diamond, jewel, or thunderbolt. Sometimes depicted as a blue orb or radiant jewel, sometimes as a source of lightning or fire.
- **Pay-may**: "Enfolded in the heart of the lotus" — the visible, everyday unfolding of events, petals, and patterns revealing the essential unchanged core to our understanding. The lotus represents the implicate order of tessellated and annidated events, and the process of unfolding through time to successive revelations. The realization of this core is *nirvana*.
- **Hung**: "It is here, now" — a declaration of belief that also serves as the bridge between chant cycles. All events are present now, forever repeated in their form — the reminder mnemonic of implicate time.

## Dorje: The Primordial Thunderbolt

**Dorje** (or Dorje-chang) is the Tibetan Buddha-figure who holds the dorje (thunderbolt), symbolizing cosmic energy. Represented as a radiant stone, Dorje is the primordial Buddha of Tibet who began the succession of reincarnations. His color is blue (for eternity), and he carries a bell signifying the voiceless wisdom of the inanimate or the sound of the void.

Dorje is an alter ego of Thor of the Norsemen (whose hammer was Mjölnir), Durga of the Hindu, and thunderbolts of other tribal peoples worldwide. This cross-cultural parallel demonstrates the essential oneness of events and beliefs across cultures.

## Universal Pattern Understanding

Tribal chants, arts, and folk decorations across cultures express essentially the same world concept as modern physics and biology — derived from contemplation of natural events. These represent a way of life and philosophy rather than dogma or measurement. They precede and transcend the emphasis on individuals or division of life into disciplines and categories.

In tribal society, wisdom is measured not by years lived but by **degree of revelation**. Those who understand and embody advanced knowledge are the most intuitive and entitled to veneration. Such knowledge is based on pattern understanding, independent of sex or age — one is "aged" by revelation, not time.

## Implications for Design

Pattern, song, and dance offer powerful educational tools that are easy to accurately reproduce. As designers, we should consider reviving meaningful geometries, songs, and dances that encode practical knowledge — reconnecting art with science, society, and functional needs.
