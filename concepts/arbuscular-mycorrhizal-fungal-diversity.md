# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungal Diversity

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi form the most widespread terrestrial mutualism on Earth, associating with the roots of an estimated 80-90% of all plant species. These obligate symbionts in the order Glomales are ancient — fossil evidence and molecular dating place their origin at approximately 400 million years ago. Despite their ecological importance, only 163 species in 7 genera have been described, making them one of the most species-poor yet ecologically dominant fungal groups.

## Biology and Life History

### Obligate Symbiosis

AM fungi cannot be cultured apart from their plant hosts. The plant provides carbon largely via arbuscule-plant cell plasmalemma interfaces, while external fungal hyphae improve phosphorus acquisition. The mycelium is coenocytic, with septa forming only with age or wounding.

### Reproduction

Most evidence indicates AM fungi reproduce clonally via mitospores formed in soil and/or roots. Sporulation commences 4-8 weeks after colonization onset and plateaus at 12-16 weeks in greenhouse pot cultures. Under field conditions, sporulation fluctuates with season and host phenology. New sporulation begins after a threshold level of mycorrhizal biomass is achieved.

### Host Range

Host specificity appears minimal or absent. Over 1,000 isolates of 98 species across all genera can grow and sporulate on a single host plant (Sorghum sudanense). ### Soil Structure

AM fungi produce glomalin — a heat-stable glycoprotein that coats hyphae and spore surfaces and accumulates in soil. Glomalin shows a highly significant correlation with soil aggregate stability and represents a major contribution to soil carbon pools.

### Mycorrhizosphere Effects

The mycorrhizosphere — soil volume influenced by AM hyphae — dramatically affects root- and soil-associated microorganisms. Mycorrhizal roots have altered exudation patterns that influence [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]], and plant-pathogen interactions.

## Sampling and Conservation

AM fungi are found in soil worldwide and have also been detected in aerial epiphytes and vegetative plant debris. The International Culture Collection of Vesicular Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (INVAM) contains almost 1,100 accessions of more than 86 species.
