# Intensive vs Extensive Gardening Water Philosophy

The choice between intensive and extensive gardening is not merely about plant spacing — it is fundamentally about water. [[john-widstoe]]'s 1911 book *Dry Farming* identified four causes of drought, and three of them are directly under the gardener's control: (1) failure to store natural precipitation in the soil, (2) failure to keep the moisture in the soil until needed, (3) sowing too much seed for the available soil moisture. Choosing an extensive approach addresses all three.

## Historical Context

Before 1880, over 90% of all North Americans lived on family farms or in small villages. It would be a fair guess that over 90% of all vegetable gardens were grown without running water. After a few weeks without rain, some would complain about drought, but other gardeners nearby wouldn't be complaining much. The difference was not rainfall — it was gardening method.

## What Intensive Gardening Really Requires

The intensive gardening style (named "biointensive" by John Jeavons) works like this:

1. Beds are loosened to two feet (60 cm) deep so root systems can go down instead of out
2. Plants are spaced very close together in massed plantings on raised beds
3. The high plant density sucks the soil dry rapidly
4. The gardener must water almost daily during the growing season
5. The gardener must make the soil super-fertile to support this intense activity
6. The cost of water will increase with inevitable price increases

## The Yield Illusion

The supposed yield advantage of intensive gardening is largely an illusion:

- **Per square foot**, densely planted crops may produce more initially
- **Per plant**, extensively spaced crops produce far more and of better quality
- **Per gallon of water**, extensive spacing is vastly more efficient
- **Per hour of labor**, extensive spacing wins because harvesting larger, well-spaced plants takes much less time
- The vegetables that respond best to close spacing are leafy greens that produce an ongoing harvest from the same plant. For vegetables that produce a main harvest and then stop, close spacing usually reduces total yield per plant because the plants stop yielding sooner due to overcompetition

## Four Spacing Systems

### Column 1: Intensive (Raised Beds per Jeavons)

Dense spacings for raised beds. Not recommended by the author. Most current garden books and government publications parrot these recommendations without critical evaluation.

**Problems**: Once ultra-crowded plants have formed a crop canopy (fully covered the bed with growing leaves), moisture loss accelerates so rapidly that during sunny weather you must water at least half an inch (1.25 cm) every day or every other day. In sandy soil during hot weather, you may need to apply 1.5 inches of water every other day. This requirement heavily shackles gardeners.

### Column 2: Semi-Intensive (Raised Beds per Solomon)

The author's own spacings, reflecting decades of experience. Most of the garden is grown on 4 × 25 foot (1.25 × 7.5 m) raised beds. These spacings do not form a canopy that completely covers the bed so quickly, so each plant has far more root room. A properly spaced crop needs watering only every few days.

**Advantages**: Vegetables become larger and more succulent because growth has not been slowed by overcompetition. Harvesting and washing takes much less time. The garden goes longer between waterings.

### Column 3: Extensive (Good Rainfall)

Small plants on wide raised beds, larger ones in slightly raised single rows, sprawling ones in hills far apart. Crops like corn or okra are grown on flat ground. Even after crop growth makes it difficult to walk between rows, this garden may go through rainless weeks without significant moisture stress.

**Advantages**: Plants spread sufficient roots to benefit from capillarity. Their ever-expanding root systems discover large amounts of soil water held in storage. Yields per square foot are only slightly lower than Column 2. In dry years some irrigation may be needed, but in most years rainfall is adequate.

**This system has been used by English-speaking North Americans since the Native Americans taught them how to garden**, based on millennia of indigenous experience.

### Column 4: Extensive (Little Rain or Fertigation)

For regions with little rainfall and limited irrigation water, or for gardeners practicing fertigation. Works only if the soil is open to root penetration to considerable depth, weed competition is minimal, and the soil surface is kept loose with a dust mulch.

## Widstoe's Fourth Point: Sowing Too Much

The single most important decision the gardener will make about watering is crop density. When you reduce plant density by spacing more generously, you gain a larger harvest per plant and reduce moisture consumption per unit area.

### The Emergency Harvest Strategy

If drought threatens: harvest every second carrot or beet, no matter what size they are at the moment. This instantly reduces moisture consumption while providing double the capillary moisture to the remaining plants. The end result might not be the loss of half the crop — by preventing moisture stress, you might end up harvesting a lot more.

## The Shift from Intensive to Extensive

The shift from intensive to extensive spacing won't actually be a sacrifice for most people:

- Instead of watering daily and finding it a problematic obligation, people will discover the garden waters itself
- Instead of growing many small, crowded vegetables that take longer to harvest, people will spend less time harvesting larger, more balanced crops
- The vegetables will be more succulent and tender because growth has not been slowed by overcompetition
