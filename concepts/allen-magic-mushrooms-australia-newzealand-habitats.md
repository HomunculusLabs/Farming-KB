## Woodchip Mulch and Deciduous Forests

A distinct ecological guild of psilocybian mushrooms inhabits wood debris, bark mulch, and the woodchips of gardens and parks:

- **[[psilocybe-aucklandii]]** — endemic to New Zealand; found in the Auckland region, likely on woody substrates
- **[[psilocybe-australiana]]** — another wood-inhabiting species found in NSW, Tasmania, and the North Island
- **[[coprophilous-fungi]] on kangaroo droppings — a reminder that substrate associations are not fixed evolutionary traits.

## Seasonal and Climatic Patterns

Fruiting is strongly seasonal and tied to rainfall:

- **Queensland and tropical NSW**: wet-season fruiting (summer-autumn); bumper crops followed the heavy spring rains of 1969
- **Southern Australia and Tasmania**: autumn fruiting (April-June for many species)
- **New Zealand**: autumn peaks; *C. cyanescens* appears in autumn in New Plymouth; *P. semilanceata* fruits in the Otago region during cooler months
- The 500-750mm annual rainfall belt of Queensland, NSW, and northern Victoria provides the most reliable conditions across Australia

## The Surfer Connection

The recreational use of psilocybian mushrooms in Australia has a distinctive cultural geography tied to surfing. Allen traces initial awareness to visiting surfers from New Zealand or the United States (particularly Hawaii) who shared ethnomycological knowledge with local surf communities on the Gold Coast of Queensland. Surfers in both Australia and NZ have been known to ingest mushrooms prior to surfing, and visiting surfers carried knowledge of species and identification between Hawaii, NZ, and Australia. This informal knowledge network preceded scientific documentation and media attention by years.

## New Zealand's Unique Position

New Zealand presents a special case. *Psilocybe cubensis* — the most popular species worldwide — is not known to occur naturally in NZ (though it has been intercepted in the mail). The country's isolation and prolific native flora have supported the evolution of endemic species. Awareness of NZ's native psychoactive mushrooms did not emerge until the early 1980s, when a UK botanist recognized *P. semilanceata* on the Otago peninsula and surfers identified *C. cyanescens* and *P. tasmaniana* in New Plymouth sand dunes.

Media coverage in 1982 ("Magic Mushroom Danger Warning") paradoxically increased popular knowledge by describing effects (inaccurately) while providing precise location information. This triggered "large-scale autumnal pilgrimages" to the New Plymouth area.

There is no evidence that the pre-conquest Maori used psychoactive mushrooms. The Maori are distinctive among indigenous peoples for having used no consciousness-altering drugs of any kind, a conclusion supported by all of NZ's professors of Maori studies.
