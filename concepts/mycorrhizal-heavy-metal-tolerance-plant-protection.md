# Mycorrhizal Heavy Metal Tolerance and Plant Protection

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] protect host plants from the toxic effects of heavy metals
through several complementary mechanisms. Their ability to sequester metals in
fungal tissues, alter rhizosphere chemistry, and modify metal speciation has
important implications for both natural ecosystem resilience and the restoration
of contaminated sites.

Research interest in mycorrhizal heavy metal tolerance has grown substantially
because of its practical application for contaminated site restoration. The
central position of fungi in controlling pollutant metals in terrestrial
ecosystems makes understanding these interactions critical for developing
[[bioremediation-strategies]].

## Mechanisms of Mycorrhizal Metal Protection

Mycorrhizal fungi protect their host plants through multiple strategies operating
simultaneously. Extraradical hyphae encounter heavy metals first in the soil
environment, providing an initial barrier through adsorption, complexation, and
translocation of metals before they reach the plant root.

The fungal sheath (mantle) in ectomycorrhizae acts as a physical and
biochemical filter, binding metal ions and potentially sequestering them within
fungal cell walls or vacuoles. This compartmentalization strategy effectively
detoxifies the root zone while maintaining nutrient uptake function.

Within the [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]], the fungus may either reduce the rate at which
pollutants enter the host plant or, conversely, the presence of pollutants may
reduce the degree to which mycorrhizal fungi can colonize roots under metal
stress.

The presence of pollutant chemicals within the mycelium can lead to changes in
the chemical and physical state of the pollutant, making it more or less toxic
to fungal consumers. Pollutants may be transferred up food webs through grazing
on mycelium or fruiting structures, or released through mycelial death and
decomposition.

## Paxillus involutus and Zinc Protection

The ectomycorrhizal fungus *[[paxillus-involutus]]* has been extensively studied
for its protective effect on birch (*Betula* spp.) grown in zinc-contaminated
soils. In experiments with birch seedlings exposed to 23 mM Zn, mycorrhizal
plants showed dramatically reduced zinc concentrations in their tissues compared
to non-mycorrhizal controls.

The protective effect operates primarily through metal retention in fungal
tissues — zinc concentrations in the mycorrhizal roots were significantly
elevated, while shoot concentrations remained low. The fungal mantle surrounding
the root acts as both a physical and chemical barrier, binding zinc ions through
cell wall carboxyl and phosphate groups before they can cross into the root
cortex.

## Zinc-Cadmium Interactions

Zinc and cadmium interactions in mycorrhizal systems reveal complex competitive
dynamics. Studies have demonstrated that Zn can reduce Cd uptake by host plants,
potentially through two mechanisms operating simultaneously.

First, elements of the same valency or similar ionic radius (Zn²⁺ and Cd²⁺)
may compete between each other for ion transporters across the plasma membrane.
Second, the presence of Zn may induce physiological responses that decrease
sensitivity to both Zn and Cd, such as upregulation of metallothionein
production or enhanced vacuolar sequestration capacity.

Hartley et al. (1997b) suggested there could be some evolution of resistance to
heavy metal toxicity based on prior exposure. However, Denny and Wilkins
(1987a,b) and Colpaert and Van Assche (1992, 1993) found no relationship
between metal resistance and prior exposure in their studies, leaving the
question of evolved vs. inherent tolerance unresolved.

## Ericoid Mycorrhizal Tolerance

The ericoid mycorrhizal associations of heathland species (*Calluna* and
*Vaccinium*) have demonstrated considerable tolerance to heavy metals (Bradley
et al., 1981, 1982; Marrs and Bannister, 1978). These plants naturally occur on
acidic, peaty soils where metals such as aluminum, manganese, and iron are
highly soluble.

It has been suggested that in environments where heavy metals are common, the
plant species may have coevolved with their mycorrhizal fungal endophyte to
develop mutual resistance mechanisms. Ericoid mycorrhizal fungi are particularly
noteworthy because they grow within root cortical cells, placing the fungal
interface directly inside the plant tissue.

## Ectomycorrhizal Tolerance Across Gradients

The ability of ectomycorrhizal species to tolerate heavy metals varies
considerably among species. Denny and Wilkins (1987a,b) surveyed mycorrhizal
colonization along pollution gradients and found that while mycorrhizal
infection frequency decreased with increasing metal contamination, certain
species persisted at levels that would inhibit most [[saprotrophic-fungi]].

Species of *Laccaria*, *Pisolithus*, and *Scleroderma* were among the more
tolerant genera encountered. The mycorrhizal relationship with *Pisolithus
tinctorius* is particularly notable — this ectomycorrhizal fungus is widely used
in forestry and has been investigated for its potential role in reclamation of
metal-contaminated sites.

The survey of aluminum tolerance in *Pisolithus tinctorius* from contaminated
sites (Egerton-Warburton and Griffin, 1995) provided some support for evolved
resistance, though the evidence remains mixed across studies.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Responses to Metals

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi respond differently to heavy metals compared
to ectomycorrhizal species. Leyval et al. (1997) noted that AM colonization of
plant roots can remain high even in heavily polluted spoil soils containing 863
mg kg⁻¹ zinc and 456 mg kg⁻¹ lead.

However, most studies did not correlate mycorrhizal colonization levels with
measures of extractable (bioavailable) heavy metal in soil solution. When
available metal concentrations were measured (Weissenhorn et al., 1995a,b), no
correlation was found between AM root colonization or heavy metal content of
maize plants and the metals available in soil solution.

Because AM fungi cannot be easily grown in pure culture, relatively little
information exists on the direct effects of heavy metals on AM fungal mycelial
growth and physiology.

Using compartmentalized soil systems, Joner and Leyval (1997) demonstrated that
AM fungi enhanced cadmium uptake from soil into the fungal hyphae but reduced
transfer from fungus to plant, retaining Cd in the fungal component and thus
detoxifying the system. Similar findings were reported by Tonín et al. (2001)
for both Cd and Zn in mycorrhizal clover.

Heavy metals (Zn, Cu, Cd, Ni) from contaminated soils can delay AM fungal
invasion of clover roots (Koomen and McGrath, 1990), although Joner and Leyval
(1997) found no reduction of AM hyphal extension into soil at up to 20 mg
extractable Cd kg⁻¹ soil.

## Research Priorities

Critical knowledge gaps remain in understanding mycorrhizal heavy metal
interactions. The signaling systems between fungus and host plant that govern
internal reallocation of elements need elucidation. The behavior of extraradical
hyphae at the soil-fungal interface — where maximal interaction with other soil
organisms occurs — requires further study.

Additionally, the interactions between mycorrhizal extraradical hyphae,
rhizospheric bacterial communities, and the distribution of anaerobic pockets in
soil in relation to mercury methylation processes represent an important frontier
for understanding coupled microbial-metal transformations.
