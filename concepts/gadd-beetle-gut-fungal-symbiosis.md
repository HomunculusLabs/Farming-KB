# Beetle Gut Fungal Symbiosis

The [[fungi-in-the-gut-of-beetles]] (Coleoptera) is a remarkably rich and underexplored
habitat for yeasts and other fungi. Research by Blackwell and colleagues
has revealed diverse [[biodiversity-of-fungi-soil-fungal-communities-agriculture]] [[fungi-in-the-environment-fungi-in-beetle-guts]], from
mushroom-feeding beetles harbouring undescribed yeast species to
wood-boring beetles with specialised xylose-fermenting yeasts in their
hindguts.

## Overview of insect-fungal endosymbiosis

Endosymbionts in arthropods are well established. Rickettsial
endosymbionts can induce sterility and increase speciation rates.
Bacterial gut symbionts provide nutritional contributions and prevent
colonisation by non-indigenous microbes. By contrast, fungal
endosymbionts of insects are less studied, though recent work indicates
insect-yeast interactions are abundant in nature. Fungi in beetle guts
span both true yeasts (Saccharomycotina) and yeast-like organisms from
other ascomycete lineages (Pezizomycotina).

## Mushroom-feeding beetles and yeast diversity

Studies of basidiocarp-feeding beetles have revealed high yeast
diversity. A proposal to isolate gut yeasts from beetles predicted
discovery of more than 50 undescribed species; results far surpassed
this prediction. Molecular analyses including SSU and LSU rDNA sequencing
identified novel clades of Candida species associated with beetle
families including Ciidae, Erotylidae, and Nitidulidae. DNA databases
remain incomplete for many microbial sequences discovered in beetle guts.

## Yeast-beetle specificity and association patterns

Several questions guided investigations of yeast-beetle associations.
Is there specificity between yeasts and their beetle hosts? Have
arthropod-associated yeasts diverged from free-living relatives or are
they repeatedly acquired from the habitat? Do distantly related beetles
using similar basidiomata share similar yeasts? Evidence suggests a
combination of co-evolved specific associations and opportunistic
acquisition from [[brassinosteroid-fungal-fruiting-body-morphology]] habitats.

## Symbiotaphrina and anobiid beetles

Symbiotaphrina species in anobiid beetles (Anobiidae) represent a
well-studied system. These fungi are not true yeasts but pezizomycete
ascomycetes that have secondarily assumed the yeast habit. They play a
role in detoxification of food resources, with the beetle providing
habitat and the fungus contributing hydrolytic enzymes including
detoxifying enzymes that [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]] plant defensive compounds.

## The Pichia stipitis clade and wood-boring beetles

In the passalid beetle Odontotaenius disjunctus, a single yeast taxon
similar to Pichia stipitis was isolated from over 400 individuals. P.
stipitis is a xylose-fermenting and -assimilating yeast, rare attributes
among Saccharomycetes. Xylose forms the backbone of hemicellulose in
plant [[alpha-1-3-glucan-fungal-pathogen-cell-walls]], making this capability significant for wood digestion.
Members of the P. stipitis clade have been isolated from multiple
wood-boring beetle families across different geographic regions.

## Compartmentalised hindgut microbiome

The gut of O. disjunctus features a highly compartmentalised microbial
community. The P. stipitis-like yeast occupies the posterior two-thirds
of the hindgut, attached to the cuticle by branched filamentous holdfast
structures along a longitudinal furrow. The anterior third of the
hindgut has extreme expansion of internal surface area with lobes and
hundreds of spines, covered by morphologically diverse bacteria. Other
organisms include eccrinid trichomycetes, amoeboid protists, and
parabasalids.
