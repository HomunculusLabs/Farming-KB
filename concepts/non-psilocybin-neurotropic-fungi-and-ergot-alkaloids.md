## The Ergot Fungi: Claviceps and Cordyceps

### Claviceps Species

The genus Claviceps represents the most historically significant group of
non-psilocybin neurotropic fungi. Five species are documented:

- **[[ergot-and-lysergic-acid-history]] amide (LSA).
  Ergotism — "St. Anthony's Fire" — caused mass poisonings throughout
  European history when contaminated rye was consumed. The psychedelic
  properties of ergot alkaloids eventually led to the synthesis of LSD
  by Albert Hofmann in 1938.
- **Claviceps nigricans**: A related species with similar alkaloid
  profiles.
- **[[claviceps-paspali]]**: Parasitizes Paspalum grasses.
- **Claviceps rolfesii**: A tropical species with limited study.
- **Claviceps tripsicii**: Associated with Tripsacum grasses.

The ergot alkaloids include water-soluble ergoline derivatives
(ergometrine, lysergic acid amide) and water-insoluble ergopeptines
(ergotamine, ergocristine, ergocryptine). Their effects include
vasoconstriction, uterine stimulation, and psychoactive properties
ranging from hallucinogenic to deliriant depending on the specific
compounds and dosage.

### Cordyceps Species

Two Cordyceps species are documented as neurotropic:

- **[[cordyceps-capitata-ophioglossoides-mexican-ceremonial]]**: Parasitizes Elaphomyces truffle species.
- **Cordyceps ophioglossoides**: Another Elaphomyces parasite.

These species are closely related taxonomically to [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]]
and share some alkaloid characteristics. They are used in Mexico in
special nocturnal ceremonies, sometimes eaten together with Psilocybe
muliercula (called "hombrecitos" — little men — and "mujercitas" —
little women, respectively).

In the center of the ceremony room, a specimen of Elaphomyces (the host
fungus) is placed as a "director" of the ceremony. The Trique Indians of
Alta Mixteca also use Elaphomyces species to heal wounds and to
"rejuvenate the organism." It is important to note that Hofmann found no
indoles in Cordyceps specimens gathered from a Mexican market — the age
of the specimens likely accounts for this negative result, as these
compounds degrade over time.

## Sacred Fungi Without Chemical Studies

### Boletes

Twenty species of boletes from two genera (Boletus with 6 species,
Heimiella with 2 species) and Russula (6 species) are reported as sacred
fungi used by indigenous peoples, particularly in New Guinea. Heim
(1963, 1966, 1978) reported their hallucinogenic use among New Guinea
aborigines. However, Ott (1993) stated that they do not possess any
neurotropic properties, raising questions about whether their effects
are truly chemical or primarily cultural and suggestible.
