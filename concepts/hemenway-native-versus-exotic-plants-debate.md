# Native Versus Exotic Plants Debate in Ecological Gardening

[[toby-hemenway]] tackles one of the most contentious questions in ecological
landscaping: should gardeners plant only natives, or can exotics play a
constructive role? His answer is nuanced and grounded in ecological science
rather than ideology.

## The Case for Native Plant Gardens

Natural gardens consisting almost exclusively of native plants aim to restore
habitat for endangered flora and wildlife. Native plant gardens are important
and have ecological merit, particularly for preserving local gene pools and
providing food sources for co-evolved pollinators and wildlife.

However, Hemenway argues that native-only gardens have a fundamental limitation:
in the United States, all developed land, including cities, suburbs, and rural
towns, covers only about 6 percent of the nation's area. You could fill every
yard and city park with native plants and not even begin to stanch the loss of
native species and habitat.

## The Hidden Cost of Native-Only Gardens

The real environmental damage is done not by cities and suburbs themselves, but
by meeting their needs. The developed 6 percent of land uses between 40 and 70
percent of America's total area to support it, through monocultured farms,
industrial forests, livestock grazing, reservoirs, mines, and other
accoutrements of civilization. Every non-homegrown meal, every trip to the
lumberyard or pharmacy, commissions the conversion of once-native habitat into
industrial use.

A native plant garden, while easier on the environment than a lawn, still means
the owner is causing immense habitat loss elsewhere, out of sight. Every bit of
food, lumber, medicinal herb, or other human product that comes from an urban
yard means one less chunk of land outside the cities needs to be denuded of
natives and developed for human use.

## The Ecological Garden Alternative

By gardening ecologically, designing multifunctional landscapes that provide
food and other goods for ourselves while creating habitat for other species, we
can make our cities truly productive. A yard planted with carefully chosen
exotics, along with natives, will reduce the ecological damage done by the
human occupants far more than a native-plant-only garden.

Taking care of ourselves in our own yards means that factory farms and forests
can shrink. Somewhere a farmer won't have to plow quite so close to a creek,
saving riparian species that would never live in a suburban lot.

## Why Invasive Exotics Succeed

Hemenway reframes the invasive species problem through an ecological lens.
Invasive exotics are almost exclusively pioneer species that need sunlight,
churned-up ground, and often poor soil. They are responding to conditions that
human activity creates: disturbed land, fragmented forests, and edge habitat.

Key points about invasive behavior:
- Invasive exotics crave disturbance and they love edges, two things
  development spawns in huge quantity
- Less-disturbed ecosystems are much more resistant to invasion
- To eradicate invasives, the habitat for them must be changed into a more
  mature, less hospitable landscape
- Simply removing the exotic won't work because the perfect habitat for it
  remains, and nature will rush in with whatever's handy

## Recombinant Ecologies

Permaculture co-originator [[david-holmgren]] calls the rampantly growing blends
of natives and exotics that appear on disturbed land "recombinant ecologies,"
and believes these are nature's effective strategy of assembling available
plants to heal damaged land. Rather than fighting this process, ecological
gardeners can learn from it and work toward more mature ecological phases.

## The Economics of Ecological Choice

Hemenway offers a striking comparison: factory farms and industrial forests,
pesticide-laced, monocropped, and sterilized of everything but a single
species, are far more biologically impoverished than any suburban backyard.
Yet farms and tree plantations are the lands that could truly become wilderness
again. Cities and suburbs are already out of the natural loop, so the goal
should be to make urban land as useful to people as possible, reducing the
pressure on wild lands to provide for human needs.

In Switzerland, 70 percent of all lumber comes from community woodlots. This
model suggests that cities could provide for most human needs, letting
cropland and tree farms return to nature. The ecological garden is the
backyard expression of this philosophy.

## Practical Strategy

Instead of waging endless war against invasives, Hemenway recommends:
- Softening forest margins with low trees and shrubs to shade out
  sun-loving exotics
- Planting edge-loving species like dogwood, cherry, crabapple, and small
  maples at yard and woodlot margins
- Creating conditions that favor mature ecological phases over pioneer ones
- Understanding that "native" is merely a question of temporal and
  geographic perspective
- Avoiding soil disturbance that creates the conditions pioneer invasives
  need to thrive

## The Long View

Wind, animals, sea currents, and continental drift have always dispersed
species into new environments. Eventually, an invasive species, after a
boom-and-bust period, comes into equilibrium with its surroundings. It may
take decades or centuries, but one day the new species becomes "implicated"
into the local ecosystem, developing natural enemies and encountering
unwelcome environments that keep it in check.

The key insight: by creating productive ecological gardens that meet human
needs locally, we reduce the pressure on wild lands far more effectively than
by planting natives-only gardens that still require imported food, materials,
and resources from elsewhere.
