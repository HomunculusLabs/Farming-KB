# Permaculture Chicken Tractors

A chicken tractor is a mobile, floorless enclosure that allows chickens to forage on fresh ground while being protected from predators. In permaculture design, chicken tractors serve multiple functions: soil preparation, pest control, fertilization, weed management, and of course egg and meat production. They embody the principle "each element performs multiple functions."

## Core Functions

1. **Soil preparation** — chickens scratch and till the top few inches of soil, removing vegetation and incorporating organic matter
2. **Fertilization** — manure is deposited directly where it is needed
3. **Pest control** — chickens eat insect larvae, weed seeds, slugs, and other pests
4. **Weed management** — repeated grazing suppresses weed growth before planting
5. **Food production** — eggs, meat, and potentially income
6. **Mowing** — chickens reduce grass height, replacing mechanical mowing

## Design Considerations

### Size and Weight
The tractor must be light enough to move easily but heavy enough to resist wind. A common size for 6-12 hens is 8-10 feet long by 4-6 feet wide. Use lightweight materials (PVC, aluminum, lightweight lumber) for the frame and cover with hardware cloth and a tarp or corrugated roof.

### Height
Provide at least 2-3 feet of headroom for the birds and for the caretaker to access feeders, waterers, and nesting boxes. Taller designs (4+ feet) allow walk-in access but are heavier.

### Floor
No floor — the whole point is ground contact. Wire mesh floors are sometimes used for transport but should be removed during station placement.

### Predator Protection
- Use 1/2-inch hardware cloth (not chicken wire) on all sides and bottom edges
- Bury or stake down bottom edges to prevent digging predators
- Ensure secure latches on doors (raccoons can open simple latches)
- Consider an apron of wire mesh extending outward at ground level
- Provide a covered area for protection from aerial predators during the day
- At night, either move birds to a secure coop or ensure the tractor is predator-proof enough for overnight use

### Mobility
- Skids or runners on the bottom allow dragging by hand
- Wheels can be added but add complexity; axle-mounted designs work well for larger tractors
- Move frequency depends on stocking density and purpose (daily for soil prep, every 1-3 days for general forage)

### Ventilation and Weather
- Open sides for airflow in warm weather
- Removable solid panels or tarps for wind and rain protection
- Shade cloth for hot climates
- Ensure nesting boxes stay dry

## Pasture Rotation Strategy

### Stocking Density
A general guideline is 1 hen per 10-15 square feet inside the tractor, with the tractor moved to fresh ground daily. This gives each patch of ground a recovery period of 30-60 days depending on the rotation circuit.

### Rotation Schedule
- **Daily moves** — best for soil preparation before planting; gives chickens fresh forage and prevents overgrazing
- **Every 2-3 days** — works for maintaining pasture without over-concentrating manure
- **Seasonal rotation** — move tractors through different zones as seasons change

### Avoiding Over-Fertilization
Chicken manure is high in nitrogen. Leaving chickens in one spot too long can burn vegetation and create nutrient runoff. Move frequently and rotate through a large enough area that each spot receives chickens only once per recovery period.

## Garden Integration

Chicken tractors integrate seamlessly with annual and perennial garden systems:

### Pre-Planting Preparation
1. Move the tractor over future garden beds in fall or early spring
2. Chickens remove vegetation, eat weed seeds and pest larvae, and deposit manure
3. After 2-4 weeks of rotation, the bed is cleared, fertilized, and lightly tilled
4. Add a layer of compost and plant directly

### Post-Harvest Cleanup
After a crop is harvested, run chickens over the bed to clean up remaining plant material, eat pest eggs and larvae, and fertilize for the next crop. This is particularly effective after brassica crops (cabbage aphids), squash (squash bugs), and tomatoes (flea beetles).

### Between Rows
In wide-row gardens, narrow tractors can run between rows during the growing season to manage weeds and pests without damaging crops. Ensure the tractor width matches the row spacing.

### Food Forest Integration
Use chicken tractors in [[permaculture-forest-garden-establishment]] areas during the establishment phase. Chickens can clean and fertilize planting spots before trees and shrubs go in. Avoid running tractors directly over newly planted perennials.

## Feed Considerations

Chickens on pasture still need supplemental feed, especially in winter or when forage is sparse. A good forage-based system can reduce commercial feed costs by 20-40%.

### Good Forage Plants
- Clover (white and red) — protein source
- Comfrey — high protein, mineral-rich (cut and drop for chickens)
- Kale, chard, lettuce — greens
- Sunflower seeds — protein and fat
- Daikon radish — greens and root vegetables
- Grains (oats, wheat, barley) — let mature for seed heads

### Supplementation
- Layer feed or mixed grain as base ration
- Oyster shell for calcium (eggshell quality)
- Grit for digestion
- Kitchen scraps (see [[permaculture-food-waste-cycling]])
- Black soldier fly larvae (protein supplement from waste)

## Breed Considerations

- **Heavy dual-purpose breeds** (Rhode Island Red, Plymouth Rock, Sussex) — good foragers, hardy, decent egg layers
- **Lighter breeds** (Leghorn, Australorp) — excellent egg production but may be less cold-hardy
- **Bantams** — work in smaller tractors, good at foraging, but small eggs
- **Heritage breeds** — generally better foragers than commercial hybrids
## Design Integration

This concept integrates effectively within broader permaculture design frameworks, contributing to system resilience and multiple ecosystem functions. When implemented as part of a holistic design, it supports the core permaculture ethics of earth care, people care, and fair share. Consider zone placement, relative location, and energy cycling principles when incorporating into landscape designs.

## Economic Considerations

Cost-benefit analysis should account for both immediate yields and long-term system productivity. Initial investment in infrastructure and establishment is typically offset by reduced maintenance costs and increasing outputs over time. Multiple revenue streams can be developed through value-added products, educational opportunities, and ecosystem services.
