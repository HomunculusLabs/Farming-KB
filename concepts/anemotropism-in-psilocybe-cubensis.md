# Anemotropism in Psilocybe Cubensis

Anemotropism is the growth response of an organism to wind or air currents.
In *Psilocybe cubensis*, anemotropism manifests as directed stipe (stem)
growth into the prevailing airflow during the early stages of fruiting body
development. This response was documented by Edmond R. Badham in a 1982
study published in Mycologia, using a controlled wind tunnel apparatus to
isolate wind as a growth stimulus.

## Discovery and Experimental Setup

Badham's study was conducted using a closed-circuit wind tunnel with a
working area of 43 × 30 × 20 cm. The tunnel provided precise control over
wind velocity (9 ± 3 cm/sec), humidity (94 ± 3% RH), temperature
(24.5 ± 1.5°C), and light (cool white fluorescent, 12-hour photoperiod).
Approximately 100 developing basidiocarps were studied under these
[[four-stage-basidiocarp-development-psilocybe-cubensis]].

## Wind-Directed Stipe Growth

The primary finding was that during Stage 1 (slight pileus swelling) and
Stage 2 (nearly spherical pileus), the mushroom stipe grows directly into
the airflow. When cultures were rotated perpendicular to the wind at 3 RPM,
growth was directed upward instead, indicating that the rotation
equalized the wind stimulus on all sides of the stipe.

This anemotropic response occurred regardless of light direction (incident
from above or below), fertility status, or photoperiod. Even cultures placed
with stipes initially directed away from the wind eventually bent to grow
into the airflow. The response was most pronounced during Stages 1 and 2,
with bending becoming less evident at later developmental stages.

## The Evaporative Demand Hypothesis

Badham proposed that anemotropism in *P. cubensis* operates through a
redistribution of the boundary layer around the stipe. Wind creates a
thinner boundary layer on the windward side and a thicker boundary layer
on the leeward side. This asymmetry affects the local evaporative demand
-- the windward surface experiences greater drying than the leeward surface.

The differential evaporative demand could cause cells on opposite sides of
the stipe to grow at different rates. Cells on the drier windward side may
elongate more rapidly, or cells on the more humid leeward side may elongate
less, producing a curvature that bends the stipe toward the wind. This
mechanism is consistent with the observation that the response depends on
four factors: wind, humidity, light, and temperature -- all of which
affect evaporative demand.

## Comparison with Phycomyces

The zygomycete fungus *Phycomyces blakesleeanus* shows a similar
anemotropic response, known as the avoidance response. Research by Cohen
et al. (1975) and Johnson and Gamow (1971) demonstrated that both wind
and water vapor gradients are necessary for this response in Phycomyces.
Russo et al. (1975) suggested that ethylene may mediate the response.

The mechanism in *P. cubensis* may be analogous, though the basidiomycete
fruiting body is structurally different from the Phycomyces sporangiophore.
Both systems appear to use differential evaporative demand as the sensory
input that drives asymmetric growth, suggesting this mechanism may be
widespread across fungal groups.

## Alternative Explanation: Mechanical Force

An alternative hypothesis is that the mushroom responds directly to the
physical force of the wind. Dennison (1961) demonstrated that Phycomyces
sporangiophores respond to gravitational and centrifugal forces, suggesting
that mechanical stimuli can directly influence fungal growth direction.

If the wind force hypothesis is correct, the bending would be a thigmotropic
response to the mechanical pressure of air currents rather than a response
to the evaporative consequences of wind. The relatively low wind velocity
used in Badham's experiments (9 cm/sec) argues against pure mechanical
force as the sole driver, but both mechanisms may contribute.

## Adaptive Significance

Anemotropism may serve multiple adaptive functions. By growing into the
wind, mushrooms position their caps for optimal spore dispersal -- spores
released from a windward-facing cap are immediately carried away by the
airflow rather than falling back onto the substrate or the parent mycelium.

For cespitose (clustered) fruiting bodies, directed growth toward the
greatest evaporative demand could serve to increase spacing between
individual stipes. This would reduce competition for resources and improve
air circulation around the cluster, benefiting spore production and
dispersal for all members of the cluster.

The response may also help mushrooms emerge from dense vegetation or
substrate by orienting growth toward areas of greater air movement, which
typically correspond to more open spaces where spore dispersal is more
effective. Understanding anemotropism has practical implications for
cultivation, where air circulation patterns in [[pf-tek-mushroom-morphology-and-lifecycle-stages]] yield.

## Two-Phase Growth Model

Badham's research supports a two-phase model of directed growth in
*P. cubensis*. The first phase (Stages 1-2) is dominated by anemotropism,
with the stipe orienting toward the direction of greatest evaporative
demand. The cells of the stipe may respond individually to this stimulus,
producing the observed curvature through differential elongation rates.
