# Cordyceps Neurotropic Fungi in Mexican Indigenous Ceremonies

## Overview

While Psilocybe species dominate discussions of neurotropic fungi in Mexico,
two species of Cordyceps have served as important sacred fungi among
indigenous communities in the Nevado de Toluca region of central Mexico.
Cordyceps capitata (Holmsk.: Fr.) Link and Cordyceps ophioglossoides (Fr.)
Link are used in special nocturnal ceremonies, either alone or in
combination with Psilocybe muliercula. These fungi occupy a unique position
in the ethnomycological landscape of Mesoamerica, bridging the gap between
the well-documented psilocybian mushrooms and the ergot fungi.

## The Species

Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides are both classified within the
Ascomycotina, order Clavicipitales. In the comprehensive checklist of
neurotropic fungi compiled by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998), these two
Cordyceps species are listed alongside five species of Claviceps in the
ergot group of neurotropic fungi. The genus Cordyceps is closely related
taxonomically to [[elaphomyces-granulatus]]** Fr.
- **Elaphomyces muricatus** Fr.
- **Elaphomyces reticulatus** Vitt.

These truffle-like fungi (false truffles, belonging to the
Eurotiomycetes) are placed at the center of the ceremonial space and
function as the "director" of the ritual. This practice reveals a
sophisticated understanding among the indigenous peoples of the
parasitic relationship between Cordyceps and its Elaphomyces host.

## Medicinal Uses of Elaphomyces

Beyond their ceremonial role, Elaphomyces species have documented
medicinal applications among the Trique Indians of the Alta Mixteca
region of Oaxaca, Mexico. These fungi are used:

- As an aid in the treatment of wounds
- For "rejuvenecer el organismo" (rejuvenating the organism)

Trappe et al. (1979) and Guzmán (1994a, 1994b) documented these
practices, demonstrating that the indigenous knowledge of these fungi
extends beyond their ceremonial use to include practical therapeutic
applications.

## Hofmann's Failed Chemical Analysis

Albert Hofmann, the Swiss chemist who first synthesized LSD and
isolated psilocybin, was unable to find any indole compounds in
specimens of Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides. These
specimens had been gathered by R. Gordon Wasson in a popular market
in Mexico as sacred fungi and had been preserved for a long time
prior to analysis. The failure to detect active compounds is almost
certainly attributable to the degradation of alkaloids over time,
consistent with the well-documented volatility and instability of
indole compounds in dried fungal specimens.

## Relationship to Ergot Fungi (Claviceps)

The inclusion of Cordyceps species alongside Claviceps in the
[[lsd-lysergic-acid-diethylamide-experience-guide]]) is well
established. The neurotropic properties of Cordyceps may derive
from similar ergot-like alkaloids, though this has not been
conclusively demonstrated due to the degradation issues noted
above.

## Historical Documentation

The use of Cordyceps and Elaphomyces in Mexican indigenous
ceremonies was first documented by Guzmán (1959) and later expanded
upon in subsequent works (Guzmán 1994a, 1994b, 1997). Wasson
and Wasson (1957) brought broader attention to the sacred
mushrooms of Mexico, though their focus was primarily on the
Psilocybe species. The Cordyceps tradition represents a distinct
and less widely known aspect of Mesoamerican ethnomycology.
