---# Fungal Composting Bioremediation of Contaminated Soils

Composting is one of the most promising reactor systems for hazardous soil
treatment, employing thermophilic microbial activity to degrade organic
pollutants. In the context of [[composting-systems]]
provide favorable conditions for both indigenous and introduced fungi to
degrade a wide range of contaminants including PAHs, chlorophenols,
pesticides, and explosives. Singh's review documents multiple composting
configurations and their effectiveness for fungal-driven remediation of
contaminated soils.

## Composting System Configurations

A wide variety of composting systems are well established, including
circular tanks, in-ground trenches, open bins, rotating drums, windrows,
open piles, and silos. Most composting systems utilize bulking agents,
such as bark chips, straw, and chopped sugar beet, that augment the
porosity and oxygen content in the medium and reduce the moisture levels.
Each configuration offers distinct advantages for different scales of
operation and types of contamination. Windrow systems are particularly
suitable for large-scale soil remediation, while contained systems such
as rotating drums and in-vessel reactors offer better process control
for heterogeneous waste streams.

## Chlorophenol Degradation in Compost

All composting systems showed about 50 to 60% mineralization of
radiolabeled PCP after 30 days (Laine and Jorgensen, 1997). Windrow
systems were employed for pilot-scale composting of 44 mg/kg
chlorophenol-contaminated soils using different inoculants. Nearly 80%
of chlorophenols was eliminated after 2 months. At this stage, highly
contaminated soils of 683 to 1108 mg/kg were added to the composting
windrows. Finally, more than 90% of chlorophenols were eliminated after
3 months. This stepwise approach, beginning with moderately contaminated
soil to establish active [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] before introducing heavily
contaminated material, demonstrates an effective strategy for handling
high pollutant loads.

## PAH Removal in Compost Bioreactors

Composting can reduce the amount of extractable PAHs by stimulating
biodegradation or binding of intermediate compounds to [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]]
soil. A compost bioreactor has been evaluated to enhance the removal of
benzo[a]pyrene in contaminated soils by the white-rot fungus
[[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]] (McFarland et al., 1992). The fungal system
removed about 45% of the initial benzo[a]pyrene over 100 days in
125-ml Erlenmeyer flasks. The influence of compost bioaugmentation on
benzo[a]pyrene removal was also studied using P. chrysosporium
(McFarland and Qiu, 1995). Removal efficiencies were 62.8% and 65.6%
for fungal-amended and unamended compost systems, respectively.

## Bound Residue Formation

In compost bioreactor systems for PAH removal, bound residue formation
is the primary mechanism for the removal of benzo[a]pyrene in active
compost systems. Rather than complete mineralization to carbon dioxide,
PAH molecules or their metabolites become covalently bound to humic
substances in the compost matrix. While this effectively reduces the
bioavailability and toxicity of the contaminants, bound residues may
persist in the environment and could potentially be released through
microbial decomposition of the organic matter over time. Understanding
the long-term stability of bound residues is essential for assessing
the ultimate effectiveness of compost-based remediation.
