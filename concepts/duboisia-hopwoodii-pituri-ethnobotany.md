# Duboisia hopwoodii — Pituri Ethnobotany

Duboisia hopwoodii is a shrub in the nightshade family (Solanaceae) native to the arid interior of Australia. It is the botanical source of Pituri, a psychoactive preparation used by Aboriginal Australians that represents what is likely the longest continuous use of a psychoactive substance in human history — spanning 40,000 to 60,000 years.

## Botanical Description

Duboisia hopwoodii was first described by the German-Australian botanist Ferdinand J. H. von Mueller (1825-1896). The plant is a spiny shrub well adapted to the harsh conditions of the Australian outback, where it grows across vast areas of the central desert regions.

The shrub produces narrow leaves that are harvested, dried, and sometimes fermented for use as Pituri. A related species, Duboisia myoporoides, is also notable for containing large quantities of scopolamine, though D. hopwoodii is the primary species associated with traditional Pituri use.

## Alkaloid Chemistry

Duboisia hopwoodii contains a complex mixture of strongly stimulating but also toxic alkaloids. The principal active compounds include piturine, D-nor-nicotine, and nicotine, with D-nor-nicotine appearing to be the main psychoactive substance.

Additional alkaloids identified in the leaves and stems include myosimin, N-formylnornicotine, cotinin, N-acetylnornicotine, anabasine, anabatin, anatalline, and bipyridyl.

The roots contain a notably different alkaloid profile. The hallucinogenic [[mckenna-witch-ointments-tropane-alkaloids-flying-salves]] hyoscyamine and traces of scopolamine have been discovered in the root tissue, along with nicotine, nornicotine, metanicotine, myosmine, and N-formylnornicotine.

## Pituri Preparation

Pituri refers in the broadest sense to all plants or plant materials with additional ingredients that are used for hedonistic or magical purposes by Australian Aborigines. Generally, the term refers specifically to preparations made from Duboisia hopwoodii.

The leaves are typically mixed with alkaline plant ashes and chewed, similar to chewing tobacco. This alkaline ash addition is critical — it facilitates the release and absorption of the alkaloids through the buccal mucosa.

Various additives are mixed with the dried or fermented leaves: plant ashes, animal hair to hold the material together, plant fibers, yellow ochre, eucalyptus resin, and, more recently, sugar. The effects of the various Pituri preparations differ markedly — some are arousing, others are weak stimulants, some are euphoric, and still others can induce visions.

## Plants Whose Ashes

 Are Added to Pituri

A diverse range of plant species are traditionally used as alkaline ash additives:

- **Proteaceae**: Grevillea striata (Ijinyja)
- **Mimosaceae**: Acacia aneura (Mulga), A. coriacea (Awintha), A. kempeana (Witchitty bush), A. lingulata, A. salicina
- **Caesalpiniaceae**: Cassia spp.
- **Rhamnaceae**: Ventilago viminalis (Atnyira)
- **Myrtaceae**: Eucalyptus microtheca (Angkirra), Eucalyptus spp., Melaleuca sp.

Many of these Acacia and Eucalyptus species are themselves culturally significant to Aboriginal peoples, and their selection as ash additives reflects deep ethnobotanical knowledge.

## Effects and Cultural

 Significance

Pituri removes hunger and thirst and induces intense dreams. The Aborigines use Pituri as a magic substance — entering the dream state (an altered state of consciousness) is an essential concept in Aboriginal cosmology.

In this dream state, all magical processes and acts affect "normal consciousness." There appear to be various types of Pituri for various uses, each linked with particular songs, totems, and appropriate "dream songs" or "songlines." Some songlines are sung specifically as "Pituri-songs."

Pituri carries a deep connection to the place where it grows — there is even a Pituri clan. The plant is said to carry the "dream of the place" where it grows and can instill it into humans. This concept of place-specific spiritual potency is central to Aboriginal relationship with the land.

## Trade Routes

 and Economy

Although Duboisia hopwoodii is widespread in Australia, some areas are better for collection and harvesting than others. The leaves are considered to be filled with the power of the land in which they grow.

Before European contact, a far-reaching trading system existed in the central desert, which gave rise to the so-called Pituri roads and paths. These trade routes carried dried Pituri leaves across vast distances, making Pituri one of the most important commodities in pre-contact Aboriginal economy.

## The Goodenia Substitute

Plants of the genus Goodenia serve as ethnobotanically significant Pituri replacements. When Duboisia hopwoodii is unavailable, Aborigines use Goodenia species as substitutes for the leaves. Goodenia is also valued as a medicinal and nutritional plant in its own right.
