# Cannabis Calcium (Ca)

## Overview

Calcium is a [[plant-secondary-macronutrients-calcium-magnesium-and-sulfur]] classified as **immobile** in cannabis plants. Cannabis requires nearly as much calcium as other macronutrients. Calcium deficiency is somewhat uncommon indoors, but not uncommon in fiber hemp. Because calcium is immobile, deficiency symptoms appear first in the upper, new leaves on top of the plant rather than in older foliage.

## Role in Plant Biology

Calcium is fundamental to cell manufacturing and growth. Unlike most nutrients, calcium does not readily move from one part of the plant to another once it has been deposited in cell walls. It is a structural component of cell walls and is essential for their integrity. Its key functions include:

- Necessary to preserve membrane permeability and cell integrity
- Ensures proper flow of cannabis-nitrogen|nitrogen and sugars
- Stimulates enzymes that help build strong cell and root walls
- Cannabis must have some calcium at the growing tip of each root
- Calcium is transported through the plant via transpiration
- Acts as a secondary messenger in plant signaling pathways
- Helps neutralize organic acids produced during metabolism
- Strengthens cell wall structure, providing resistance to pathogens

Calcium is fundamental to cell manufacturing and growth. Calcium is necessary to preserve membrane permeability and cell integrity, which ensures proper flow of nitrogen and sugars. It stimulates enzymes that help build strong cell and root walls.

## Mobility in Soil

Calcium is **immobile** within the cannabis plant -- once deposited in tissue, it cannot be relocated to new growth. This immobility is why deficiency symptoms always appear first in young leaves and growing tips. In the soil, calcium is moderately mobile and is one of the most abundant cations in most soils. However, it can be leached from sandy soils by heavy rainfall or excessive irrigation. Calcium availability is highest at pH 7.0-8.0 and decreases in acidic conditions. Because calcium is transported through the plant via the transpiration stream, anything that reduces transpiration (high humidity, closed stomata) can induce localized calcium deficiency even when soil levels are adequate.

## Interactions with Other Nutrients

Calcium and magnesium have an important relationship. Extra magnesium in the soil is generally not harmful, but it can inhibit calcium uptake. This is why a balanced approach using dolomite lime (which contains both) is recommended for soil and soilless mixes.

Boron helps with calcium uptake. During flowering, a supplemental foliar dose of calcium may help when growth slows, although both calcium and boron are poor candidates for [[cannabis-foliar-feeding]] because they translocate poorly.

Toxic levels of calcium exacerbate deficiencies of cannabis-potassium|potassium, [[cannabis-magnesium-nutrient]], [[cannabis-manganese-nutrient]], and [[query-how-to-identify-and-fix-cannabis-iron-deficiency]]. The nutrients become unavailable even though they are present in the growing medium. In hydroponics, excess calcium precipitates with [[query-how-to-fix-sulfur-deficiency-in-cannabis]], forming gypsum (calcium sulfate Ca(SO4)2(H2O)) that settles as residue at the bottom of the reservoir.

## Organic Sources

[[organic-fertilizer-sources-guide]] of calcium include:

- Dolomite lime (also provides magnesium)
- Hydrated lime (fast-acting, use sparingly)
- Eggshells (pulverized, slow release)
- Oyster shell flour
- Bone meal
- Gypsum (calcium sulfate, also provides sulfur)

To prevent deficiencies in soil and most soilless mixes, add fine dolomite lime. If growing in a soil or soilless mix with a pH below 7, incorporating one cup of fine (flour) dolomite lime per gallon of medium ensures adequate supplies of calcium and magnesium.

## Deficiency

Frequently, cannabis plants can process more calcium than is available. Calcium also washes out of leaves that are sprayed with water. Growing tips could show signs of calcium deficiency if the humidity is maxed out. At 100 percent humidity, the stomata close, which stops transpiration to protect the plant. The calcium that is transported by transpiration becomes immobile.

Deficiency signs may be difficult to detect at first. They start with subtle symptoms on the youngest growth.

### Symptoms

- Weak stems
- Very dark green foliage
- Exceptionally slow growth
- Young leaves are affected first (immobile nutrient behavior)
- New growing shoots develop yellowish to purple hues
- Growing shoots disfigure before shriveling up and dying
- Bud development is inhibited
- Plants become stunted
- Harvest is diminished

### Deficiency Progression Timeline

1. Slow growth and young leaves turn very dark green
2. New growing shoots discolor
3. New shoots contort, shrivel, and die
4. Bud development slows dramatically

### Treatment

Dissolve one-half teaspoon of hydrated lime per gallon of water. Water deficient plants with calcium-dosed water as long as the symptoms persist. Alternatively, use a complete hydroponic nutrient containing adequate calcium. Keep the pH of the growing medium stable.

To prevent deficiencies in soil and most soilless mixes, add fine dolomite lime or use soluble hydroponic fertilizers containing adequate calcium. If growing in a soil or soilless mix with a pH below 7, incorporating one cup of fine (flour) dolomite lime per gallon of medium ensures adequate supplies of calcium and magnesium.

## Toxicity

Toxicity is difficult to see in the foliage but causes wilting. Toxic levels also exacerbate deficiencies of cannabis-potassium|potassium, [[cannabis-magnesium-nutrient]], [[cannabis-manganese-nutrient]], and [[query-how-to-identify-and-fix-cannabis-iron-deficiency]]. The nutrients become unavailable even though they are present in the growing medium.

### Symptoms

- Wilting foliage
- Exacerbated deficiencies of potassium, magnesium, manganese, and iron
- If excessive soluble calcium is applied early in life, it can stunt growth
- In hydroponics, excess calcium precipitates with [[query-how-to-fix-sulfur-deficiency-in-cannabis]], forming gypsum (calcium sulfate Ca(SO4)2(H2O)) that settles as residue at the bottom of the reservoir

## Common Mistakes

- Using hard water without accounting for calcium already present in the water supply
- Relying on foliar feeding for calcium (it translocates poorly through leaf tissue)
- Not supplementing calcium when growing in pure peat or coco coir, which have naturally low calcium
- Confusing calcium deficiency with humidity stress -- both cause tip burn in new growth
- Adding calcium and sulfur concentrates together in hydroponics without diluting (forms insoluble gypsum)
- Failing to add dolomite lime before planting in soil-based grows
