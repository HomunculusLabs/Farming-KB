## Overview

Commercial mycorrhizal inoculants are available in several formulations for different application scenarios in agriculture, horticulture, and silviculture. Understanding the types of formulations, their proper use, and their limitations is essential for achieving successful mycorrhiza|mycorrhizal establishment. Jeff Lowenfels' "Teaming with Fungi" provides a detailed guide to these products and their application.

Source: [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi]]

***

## Types of Formulations

### 1. Granular/Powder Propagule Mixes

Propagules (a combination of spores and hyphal fragments containing arbuscules) are mixed with granular carrier substances such as sand, peat, clay, or other inert materials.

**Applications:**
- Mixed into soil or growing media
- Applied directly to roots during transplanting
- Rolled onto seeds before planting (seed coating)
- Broadcast onto planting surfaces

**Advantages:** Easy to handle, good shelf life when kept dry, versatile application methods.

### 2. Soil-Based Propagule Mixes

Natural soils containing known mycorrhizal fungi. These can include colonized root fragments and the soil in which they grew.

**Applications:**
- Mixed into planting holes
- Applied as a top dressing
- Used as a component of potting mixes

**DIY option:** Gardeners can make their own soil-fungi mix by collecting root fragments from healthy, colonized plants and mixing with surrounding soil.

### 3. Liquid Formulations

Liquid formulations represent an important innovation that expands the usability of mycorrhizal fungal spores.

**Applications:**
- Drip irrigation systems
- Spray applications to existing plants
- Misting systems in nurseries
- Flood irrigation

**Advantages:** Can reach existing plant root zones without excavation, compatible with automated irrigation systems.

## Choosing the Right Product

### Read the Label

It is vitally important to read the product label carefully:
- Identify the type of fungi included (endo- or ectomycorrhizal)
- Note the specific species included
- Match the fungi to the intended host plants
- Check the stated spore/propagule count
- Note any viability information or expiration dates

### One Size Does NOT Fit All

Different plants require different mycorrhizal fungi:
- Most annual vegetables and herbs need arbuscular (endomycorrhizal) fungi
- Most trees and shrubs need ectomycorrhizal fungi
- Some plants form both types at different life stages
- Some plants (brassicas, beets, spinach, buckwheat) do not form mycorrhizae at all

### Viability Considerations

- Extreme cold generally does not affect propagule viability
- Extreme heat can kill propagules — store in cool conditions
- Moisture can trigger premature germination (reducing viability)
- Viable spores typically last **1-2 years** when stored properly
- Root and hyphal fragments typically last **1 year or more**
- Check packaging for production dates

## Inoculation Methods

### Seed Inoculation
Seeds can be rolled in granular/powder formulations or sprayed with liquid formulas. Spraying seeds with water first helps granular mixes adhere. This is the best way to establish mycorrhizae from the first day after germination.

### Seed Germination Mix Inoculation
All formulations can be mixed into the soil/media in which seeds are germinated. Seed-starting cubes, transplant cubes, and hydroponic growing media can all be inoculated. Mycorrhizal fungi thrive in pH 5.5-7.0, so media must be within this range.

### Bare-Root Inoculation
Bare plant roots can be sprayed, sprinkled with, or dipped into any formulation. Standard advice: roll the roots of transplants in mycorrhizal mixes or sprinkle directly on exposed roots.

### Transplant Inoculation
Both liquid and granular formulations can be applied to plant roots at transplanting time. Spores can also be mixed into transplanting soil.

### Root Inoculation of Established Plants
All formulations can be used to feed roots of existing plants, as long as they contact the roots:
- Liquid formulations applied to the surface
- Granular and soil mixes placed on the surface for shallow-rooted plants in loose soils
- For deeper roots, create channels to the root zone and pack with granular/soil mixes
- The key is root-to-fungi signaling for mycorrhizal formation

### Irrigation System Application
Liquid formulations can be added to drip and irrigation systems, allowing inoculation of established plantings, orchards, and vineyards without disturbing the soil.

***

## Placement: The Critical Factor

Mycorrhizal fungal spores will NOT grow and thrive if they do not come into contact with the proper root exudates. Critical placement guidelines:
- Ensure propagules are in physical contact with plant roots
- Without a signal from the plant, the spore may germinate but will NOT colonize the root
- Mix propagules throughout potting mixes (not just on the surface) for best results
- For containers, the enclosed environment allows higher concentration of propagules

***

## Nursery Considerations

### Container-Grown Plants

- Establishing mycorrhizae in containers can take up to **2 months** (much faster than in agricultural fields)
- Container conditions are more ideal and controllable than field conditions
- Propagules are applied in much greater concentrations in pots
- Container-grown mycorrhizal fungi produce spores more quickly
- Sporulation is triggered by crowding — can begin within a year of inoculation
- Transferring potted plants to the garden: supplement with additional mycorrhizal fungi at the planting location

### Timing

- Apply inoculant as early as possible in the plant's life
- Roll or spray seeds in mycorrhizal formulations before germination for maximum benefit
- Later application still provides benefits but misses early growth advantages
