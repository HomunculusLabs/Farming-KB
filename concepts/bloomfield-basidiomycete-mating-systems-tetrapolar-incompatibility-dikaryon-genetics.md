# Basidiomycete Mating Systems: Tetrapolar Incompatibility, Dikaryon Formation, and the Genetics of Fungal Sex

## Overview

[[singh-basidiomycete-fungi-in-petroleum-hydrocarbon-cleanup]]—the group that includes mushrooms, brackets, rusts, and smuts—have evolved the most complex mating systems in the fungal kingdom. Unlike animals, which typically have two sexes determined by chromosomes, and many plants with their pollen and ovules, basidiomycetes can possess hundreds or even thousands of distinct "sexes" encoded by mating-type genes. These systems govern whether hyphae of different individuals can fuse, share nuclei, and ultimately produce fruiting bodies. The most widespread system, called **tetrapolar incompatibility**, involves two unlinked genetic loci that must both be compatible for a successful sexual union. This remarkable mechanism maximizes outcrossing potential and generates extraordinary [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] within populations. The evolutionary innovation of multiple mating types transforms every forest floor into a vast genetic marketplace where only the most genetically distant partners can successfully reproduce.

## The Mating-Type Locus

In basidiomycetes, sexual compatibility is determined by genes at one or two mating-type loci:

- **Bipolar species** (unifactorial incompatibility): A single mating-type locus with multiple alleles controls compatibility. If two mycelia share the same allele, they are incompatible. This is analogous to a simple "match/mismatch" system. Approximately 25% of basidiomycete species use this system. Many rust fungi and smuts are bipolar.
- **Tetrapolar species** (bifactorial incompatibility): Two unlinked loci, designated A and B, both determine compatibility. Both loci must carry different alleles between partners for a successful mating. The A locus controls nuclear pairing and clamp [[clamp-connection-formation-and-nuclear-migration]]; the B locus governs nuclear migration and dikaryotization. About 65% of basidiomycete species are tetrapolar, including most mushroom-forming species.

The tetrapolar system was first elucidated through the work of **J.B.S. Haldane** and others in the 1930s, using the ink-cap mushroom *[[stamets-cultivator-coprinus-comatus-shaggy-mane-cultivation]]* and related species as model organisms. The discovery that fungi could have thousands of "sexes" was revolutionary in genetics. Haldane himself quipped that the most exciting thing to happen in his lifetime was not the discovery of the structure of DNA but the discovery of the tetrapolar mating system in *[[schizophyllum-commune]]*.

## Molecular Mechanisms

### The A Locus: Nuclear Pairing and Clamp Connections

The A locus encodes two types of homeodomain transcription factors:

- **HD1** and **HD2** proteins, which function as a heterodimer. Each allele produces a distinct HD1 and HD2 protein. When hyphae of different A alleles fuse, the HD1 from one partner heterodimerizes with the HD2 from the other. This active complex activates the genetic program for:
  - Formation of **clamp connections** (hook-shaped lateral branches on hyphal septa)
  - Synchronized nuclear division
  - Maintenance of the **dikaryotic state** (two genetically distinct nuclei per cell)

Clamp connections are visible under the microscope as small curved branches that form at each septum in the dikaryotic mycelium. They are an elegant mechanical solution to the problem of maintaining two nuclei in tandem as the hypha elongates. The process works as follows: before a septum forms, one nucleus migrates into the developing clamp cell; the other nucleus divides in the main hypha; the clamp then curves back and fuses with the subterminal cell, delivering its nucleus. A septum forms at the base of the clamp and at the original division point, ensuring each new cell inherits one nucleus of each mating type.

### The B Locus: Nuclear Migration and Cytoplasmic Fusion

The B locus encodes **pheromone receptors** (similar to G-protein-coupled receptors in animals) and their cognate **pheromones** (lipopeptide signaling molecules). When compatible B alleles meet:

- Pheromones from one strain bind to receptors on the partner's hyphae
- This triggers dissolution of septal pores, allowing **nuclear migration** through the hyphal network
- Cytoplasmic mixing occurs, establishing the dikaryon throughout the colony

If A is compatible but B is not, hyphae may fuse locally and form clamp connections, but nuclei cannot migrate—resulting in a "locked" interaction at the contact point. If B is compatible but A is not, nuclei may migrate but no stable dikaryon forms, and the interaction eventually collapses. Only when both loci are compatible does a robust, self-sustaining dikaryotic mycelium develop.

## The Numbers Game: Thousands of Sexes

In a tetrapolar species with, say, 50 A alleles and 50 B alleles, there are 2,500 possible mating-type combinations. Only about 4% of random encounters between strains would be sexually compatible. In species like *Schizophyllum commune* (the split-gill mushroom), more than 300 A alleles and 100 B alleles have been documented in natural populations—yielding over 30,000 distinct mating types. This staggering diversity ensures that the probability of encountering a compatible mate in nature is extremely low, which:

1. **Maximizes outcrossing**—virtually every successful mating is between unrelated individuals
2. **Promotes genetic recombination**—each dikaryon combines two highly divergent genomes
3. **Prevents [[cervantes-hybrid-vigor-inbreeding-depression]]**—repeated self-fusion is genetically impossible due to the A/B requirement
4. **Creates selective pressure for efficient mate-finding**—favoring organisms with broad dispersal capabilities

## The Balkan Map: Competition in Wood

As [[nicholas-money]] describes in *Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard*, the consequences of these mating systems are visible in the forest:

> "Fungi define the borders in the wood... When these mycelia meet, they fight or fuse. Conflict draws the Balkan map. Where different species and incompatible strains of the same species clash, the wood becomes stained with pigment as the warring mycelia attack their opponent's hyphae and produce thick, melanin-impregnated walls to resist each other's poisons."

Incompatible basidiomycete strains that attempt to fuse undergo **somatic incompatibility** reactions. The mingled cytoplasm darkens and decays, and melanized barricade zones form at the interface. These demarcation lines are visible as dark lines on decaying logs and tree stumps—the "Balkan map" of fungal territoriality. Beech trees in Midwestern forests serve as battlegrounds where swarms of wood decay fungi compete for resources. The destruction of a large tree can take a century or more and supports a rich community of competing mycelia whose boundaries are etched in black pigment.

## Dikaryon Formation: The Bridge Between Fusion and Fruiting

The dikaryotic phase is unique to basidiomycetes (and some ascomycetes). It represents a prolonged state of two genetically distinct nuclei coexisting in each cell, maintained by clamp connections, before eventually fusing (karyogamy) inside the basidium during fruiting. This n+n state differs from:

- **Haploid (n)**: A single nucleus per cell, as in the initial [[growing-gourmet-spore-germination-strain-isolation]] phase
- **Diploid (2n)**: Fused nucleus after karyogamy (brief in basidiomycetes, occurring only inside the basidium)
- **Heterokaryotic (n+n)**: Dikaryon, two unfused nuclei of different mating types

The dikaryon can persist for months or years, colonizing vast substrates, before environmental cues (temperature, moisture, nutrient depletion) trigger the formation of fruiting bodies. Inside each basidium on the gills, the paired nuclei finally fuse, undergo meiosis, and produce four haploid basidiospores—each potentially carrying a novel combination of parental alleles. This brief diploid window is the only point in the basidiomycete life cycle where two genomes physically merge.

## Evolutionary Significance

The tetrapolar mating system offers several evolutionary advantages:

1. **Disease resistance**: High genetic diversity means some individuals will resist any given pathogen or environmental stress
2. **Adaptation**: Novel gene combinations arise with every mating, accelerating adaptation to changing environments
3. **Population stability**: Even small populations maintain high outcrossing potential due to the multiplicity of mating types
4. **Genomic exploration**: The dikaryotic state allows both parental genomes to be expressed simultaneously, creating hybrid phenotypes
5. **Territorial flexibility**: Somatic incompatibility prevents takeover by any single genotype, maintaining community-level diversity

## Vegetative Compatibility and Non-Sexual Fusion

Beyond mating-type recognition, basidiomycetes also possess **vegetative compatibility** (or somatic compatibility) systems that govern whether hyphae of the same species can merge their cytoplasm without forming a sexual dikaryon. These systems involve multiple **vic (vegetative incompatibility) genes** that trigger programmed cell death when incompatible hyphae fuse. This is distinct from mating-type incompatibility:

- **Mating-type incompatibility**: Prevents [[fungal-life-cycles-sexual-reproduction-metamorphosis]] between genetically similar strains, promoting outcrossing
- **Vegetative incompatibility**: Prevents cytoplasmic exchange between non-identical individuals, protecting against the transmission of deleterious cytoplasmic elements such as viral dsRNA (fungal viruses) and senescent mitochondria

The vegetative incompatibility reaction is rapid and dramatic: within minutes of contact between incompatible hyphae, vacuoles collapse, septa are plugged with [[fungal-woronin-bodies-and-septal-pore-organization]] (in ascomycetes) or similar structures, and a zone of dead cells forms at the interface. In wood decay basidiomycetes, this reaction deposits melanin and other pigments, creating the dark zone lines visible on decaying logs.
