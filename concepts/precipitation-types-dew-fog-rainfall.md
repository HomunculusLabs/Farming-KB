# Precipitation — Rainfall, Dew, and Fog

## Overview

Precipitation is the primary water input for any [[permaculture]] system, encompassing two fundamentally different mechanisms: water falling from clouds (rainfall, snow, hail) and water condensed or trapped from air or fogs by cool surfaces (dew, fog drip). Understanding the full spectrum of precipitation is critical because water promises to be the main limiting factor for survival and growth, and the major future expense of food gardens and agriculture.

## Rainfall

### Global Context

World rainfall averages approximately 86 cm (34 inches) per year. While broad categories are useful for general planning:

- **Semi-arid**: 50 cm (20 inches) of rainfall or less
- **Arid/Desert**: 25 cm (10 inches) or less

However, local experience of seasonal or relative aridity can occur in any climate due to long-term cycles and weather effects from periodic fluctuations in jet streams or oceanic currents. Longer periods of increased aridity can also result from deforestation on a broad or local scale.

### Rainfall Data — What Matters

Climatic averages are at best a very general guide. Far more useful data includes:

- **Expected distribution** of rainfall, including extremes such as 100-year flood records
- **Intensity of rains** — determines the sizing of road culverts, dam spillways, and storage capacity
- **Flooding histories** — indicate real limits for placement of plant systems, fences, and buildings

If flood data is omitted from design considerations, life itself can be at risk during intense periods of rain.

### Water as a Limiting Factor

As precipitation rises, available light decreases. At the extremes:

- **Humid/foggy climates**: Light becomes the limiting factor for some plants to ripen or even flower
- **Dry climates** (approaching 50 cm mean rainfall): Evaporation in excess of precipitation becomes the limiting factor, determining arid-land storage strategies

### Rainfall Types by Process

Rainfall is distinguished by the atmospheric processes that cause it:

1. **Orographic Rain**: Air cooling as it rises over mountains or hills. This is the dominant rain-producing mechanism on windward slopes of mountain ranges and is responsible for the dramatic rainfall gradients across mountainous regions.

2. **Cyclonic or Frontal Rain**: The over-riding of cool and warm air masses of the polar circulation. These are the spiral rains that arrive at roughly 10-day intervals in mid-latitude regions and can bring sustained, widespread precipitation.

3. **Convective Rain**: Columns of hot air rising from deserts or oceans into cooler upper atmosphere layers. This produces the intense, localized thunderstorms characteristic of tropical and continental summer climates.

## Dew

### Formation Mechanism

Dew is a common result of clear nights, rapid radiation loss, and a moist air mass over coasts and hills. It occurs more frequently in clear-sky deserts than in cloudy areas, because clear skies allow more rapid radiative cooling. A slight wind speed of 1-5 km/h assists the quantity deposited, while both still air and strong winds reduce dewfall.

### Deposition Patterns

Intensity of dew deposition is greatest 3-100 cm above ground level:

- **Higher deposition** occurs over areas of dry ground (which chill more quickly)
- **Lower deposition** occurs over wet earth (which retains heat and chills less rapidly)

### Dew vs. Guttation and Distillation

Dew must not be confused with moisture found on leaves above warm damp ground on cloudy nights. That moisture is either:

- **Guttation**: Water exuded from the leaves through specialized structures (hydathodes). Guttation water clings to the tips of leaves.
- **Distillation**: Water condensed from rising ground vapour. This represents no net gain to total precipitation.

Dew, by contrast, clings to the whole leaf area and represents actual atmospheric moisture addition.

### Dew in Deserts

Only in deserts is the 4-5 cm (1-2 inches) of dew per year of any significance in precipitation. In desert environments, dew should be regarded as an accessory to trickle irrigation, not a replacement for it.

### Dew Harvesting Techniques

1. **Stone mulch condensation traps**: Building piles of loosely-stacked stones where low night winds cool rock surfaces and dew accumulates to dampen the ground below. In the Negev desert and other dry areas, some plants are associated only with these dew condensers. Each mound of stones may suffice to water one tree.

2. **Large radiation traps**: Structures like those on Lanzarote in the Canary Islands, where cinder-covered pits create cold-air sinks that condense atmospheric moisture. Each pit may grow one grape vine.

3. **Natural vegetation as condensers**: Free-standing shrubs of about 1-2 m (3-6 feet) in height are the most efficient dew collectors. Groups or solid stands of plants do less well, which may help explain the discrete spacing of desert plants — scattered shrubs trap approximately 40% more dew than closed vegetation canopies.

4. **Metallic mesh fences**: Erecting metallic mesh fences about 1 m (3 feet) high as initial condensers, growing shrubs along the fence drip-line, then moving the fence on after plants are established. This technique has been proposed for reforestation of deforested coastal areas in Morocco.

## Fog

### Formation Types

Fog forms where moisture condenses from the air, and is differentiated into three types:

1. **Radiation Ground Fog**: On clear nights, hollows and plateaus cool rapidly and fog forms in much the same pattern as winter frosts. This is the common early-morning fog of valleys and low-lying areas.

2. **Advection Fog**: Cold offshore currents condense the moisture in warm sea airstreams. These are the coastal and offshore fogs that affect many coasts, such as Newfoundland and parts of northwest Europe. Advection fog can persist for days when the atmospheric conditions that create it remain stable.

3. **Upslope or Orographic Fog**: Warm, humid airstreams carried up hill slopes condense as the air cools with altitude. This type is critical for cloud forest ecosystems and mountain agriculture.

### Fog as a Significant Water Source

Unlike dew, fog can provide enormous quantities of moisture. Documented examples include:

- **Table Mountain, South Africa**: 329 cm (128 inches) from fog drip alone
- **Lanai, Hawaii**: 127 cm (50 inches) from fog drip alone

In such fog-prone areas, even field crops may thrive without irrigation. Bare rock and new soil surfaces on sea-facing slopes are colonized with [[lichens]] and mosses, while rainforest develops on richer soils. Much of New Zealand experiences upslope fog precipitation, and dense forests will develop unless burnt or cleared to tussock grasslands.

### Forest Canopy Fog Condensation

The irregular canopy of fog forests is an excellent fog condenser. Even with no visible fog, trees will condense considerable moisture on sea-facing slopes when night winds move in off warm seas over the land and encounter the cool leaf surfaces of forests. This mechanism is one of the reasons why deforestation of coastal mountain slopes can dramatically reduce water availability in downland areas.

### Fog Forest Dynamics

In the very humid air of fog forests, giant trees may accumulate so much moisture that evapotranspiration becomes ineffective if fogs and still air persist. Paradoxically, more large limbs fall in still air than in high winds — still air causes water-saturated limbs to break under their own weight, while high winds tend to snap dry branches rather than living limbs. Almost permanent condensation fogs clothe the tops of high oceanic islands, and hanging mosses and epiphytes develop rapidly there, just as they do at the base of waterfalls, because of the free moisture particles in the air.

## Design Implications

Any strategy that generates, conserves, or stores water is critical to [[permaculture-design]]:

1. **Species selection** should ensure some yield in "drier than usual" conditions
2. **Water storage** must account for both distribution patterns and intensity extremes
3. **Fog and dew harvesting** can supplement water supplies in arid and semi-arid regions
4. **Forest preservation** on windward slopes maintains fog condensation capacity
5. **Site analysis** should include flood histories and 100-year records, not just averages
6. **Microclimate design** can enhance dew and fog capture through appropriate vegetation placement
