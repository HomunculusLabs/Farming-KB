## Overview

In the Negev desert and other dry areas, some plants are associated only with these dew condensers. Each mound of stones may suffice to water one tree.

2. **Large radiation traps**: Structures like those on Lanzarote in the Canary Islands, where cinder-covered pits create cold-air sinks that condense atmospheric moisture. Each pit may grow one grape vine.

3. **Natural vegetation as condensers**: Free-standing shrubs of about 1-2 m (3-6 feet) in height are the most efficient dew collectors. Groups or solid stands of plants do less well, which may help explain the discrete spacing of desert plants — scattered shrubs trap approximately 40% more dew than closed vegetation canopies.

4. **Metallic mesh fences**: Erecting metallic mesh fences about 1 m (3 feet) high as initial condensers, growing shrubs along the fence drip-line, then moving the fence on after plants are established. This technique has been proposed for reforestation of deforested coastal areas in Morocco.

## Fog

### Formation Types

Fog forms where moisture condenses from the air, and is differentiated into three types:

1. **Radiation Ground Fog**: On clear nights, hollows and plateaus cool rapidly and fog forms in much the same pattern as winter frosts. This is the common early-morning fog of valleys and low-lying areas.

2. **Advection Fog**: Cold offshore currents condense the moisture in warm sea airstreams. These are the coastal and offshore fogs that affect many coasts, such as Newfoundland and parts of northwest Europe. Advection fog can persist for days when the atmospheric conditions that create it remain stable.

3. **Upslope or Orographic Fog**: Warm, humid airstreams carried up hill slopes condense as the air cools with altitude. This type is critical for cloud forest ecosystems and mountain agriculture.

### Fog as a Significant Water Source

Unlike dew, fog can provide enormous quantities of moisture. Documented examples include:

- **[[lichens]] and mosses, while rainforest develops on richer soils. Much of New Zealand experiences upslope fog precipitation, and dense forests will develop unless burnt or cleared to tussock grasslands.

### Forest Canopy Fog Condensation

The irregular canopy of fog forests is an excellent fog condenser. Even with no visible fog, trees will condense considerable moisture on sea-facing slopes when night winds move in off warm seas over the land and encounter the cool leaf surfaces of forests. This mechanism is one of the reasons why deforestation of coastal mountain slopes can dramatically reduce water availability in downland areas.

### Fog Forest Dynamics

In the very humid air of fog forests, giant trees may accumulate so much moisture that evapotranspiration becomes ineffective if fogs and still air persist. Paradoxically, more large limbs fall in still air than in high winds — still air causes water-saturated limbs to break under their own weight, while high winds tend to snap dry branches rather than living limbs. Almost permanent condensation fogs clothe the tops of high oceanic islands, and hanging mosses and epiphytes develop rapidly there, just as they do at the base of waterfalls, because of the free moisture particles in the air.

## Design Implications

Any strategy that generates, conserves, or stores water is critical to [[permaculture-design]]:

1. **Species selection** should ensure some yield in "drier than usual" conditions
2. **Water storage** must account for both distribution patterns and intensity extremes
3. **Fog and dew harvesting** can supplement water supplies in arid and semi-arid regions
4. **Forest preservation** on windward slopes maintains fog condensation capacity
5. **Site analysis** should include flood histories and 100-year records, not just averages
6. **Microclimate design** can enhance dew and fog capture through appropriate vegetation placement
