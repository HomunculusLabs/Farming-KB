## Overview

[[humus]] formation is the culmination of a sequential [[decomposition]] process driven by bacteria and fungi working in concert. Understanding the distinct roles each organism group plays—and how to manage their succession—is critical for producing high-quality compost and building long-term soil organic matter. This concept page covers the bacterial-[[fungal-decomposition]] cycle, the nature of humus and [[humic-acid]], the role of molasses in accelerating fungal dominance, and related topics including manure antibiotic degradation and persistent herbicides.

## The Bacteria-Then-Fungi Decomposition Cycle

### Bacteria Start the Process

Bacteria are the first decomposers to colonize organic matter. They consume the **easy-to-use sugars**—simple, rapid-growth food resources including simple carbohydrates, amino acids, and proteins. Bacterial growth is fast, generating heat and rapidly processing the most accessible carbon compounds.

### Fungi Finish the Process

Fungi come along after bacteria and perform the critical work of producing the **complex "waste products"** that become humus, and more specifically, humic acid. Fungi produce enzymes capable of breaking down **lignin and cellulose**—the tough, recalcitrant compounds that bacteria cannot process effectively alone.

### The Full Succession

After fungi have broken down complex materials, bacteria return to finish the job of forming humus. This succession is natural but is often disrupted in conventional composting, which is why humus formation can take so long.

### Key Insight

Most composting operations do not favor fungi from the start. The key to accelerating humus production is to **favor fungal dominance right from the beginning**, since fungi can break down lignocellulosic waste without requiring high temperatures.

## Humus vs. Humic Acid

### Humus (The Broad Category)

Humus is the complete organic matter complex in soil, including:

- **Living organisms:** bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, roots
- **Simple organic matter:** simple sugars, amino acids, proteins, simple carbohydrates, lipids, and thousands of other simple compounds
- **Intermediate complexity:** polysaccharides (multiple simple sugars bound together in increasingly complex, branched forms), amino-sugars, hormones, fats, lipopolysaccharides, fulvic acids
- All of the above branched and bound together in intermediate molecular weight fractions

### Humic Acid (The Most Complex Fraction)

Humic acids represent the highest level of complexity—all of the above components bound together in **very complex tertiary (three-dimensional) binding complexes**. Humic acid formation requires the full complement of soil organisms to be present.

### Organisms Required for True Humic Acid Production

Real humic acid production is not accomplished by fungi alone. It requires:

1. **Fungi** — the primary producers of complex organic compounds
2. **Earthworms** — grind and mix materials, enhancing fungal access
3. **Microarthropods** — fragment organic matter and help maintain fungal dominance over bacterial processes

## Molasses for Accelerating Fungal Dominance

### The Problem with Conventional Composting

Standard composting tends to favor bacteria from the start. Without fungal dominance early in the process, decomposition of woody and lignified materials is slow, and humic acid production is delayed.

### The Molasses Solution

Adding molasses to the compost pile **favors fungi over bacteria** and can dramatically speed up the entire decomposition process. This works because:

- Molasses provides both carbon and some nitrogen.
- The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio in molasses selectively favors fungal growth.
- Fungi, once established, can break down lignocellulosic waste without requiring the high temperatures that bacterial-dominated piles need.

### Application Rate

**500 ml per 4 kg** of dry waste material (approximately 2 pints per 10 pounds) has been demonstrated to work well. This rate also adds trace minerals. Both cane and beet molasses are suitable, and it is often available inexpensively from feed stores or sugar factories.

### What Doesn't Work

Simply adding simple sugars to a wood pile does not improve fungal growth on its own. The specific composition of molasses (including its nitrogen content and trace minerals) is what makes it effective for shifting the microbial balance toward fungi.

## Manure Antibiotics and Chemical Residues

### The Challenge

Manure from treated livestock can contain residues of antibiotics and deworming medications that inhibit the microbial communities needed for composting. The severity of inhibition, from most to least problematic:

1. **Strongylid medicine (de-wormer)** — most problematic
2. **Herbicide residues**
3. **Insecticides and fungicides**
4. **Copper and sulfur**
5. **Antibiotics** — generally easy to handle unless concentrations are overwhelming

### Detection Through Temperature Monitoring

The primary indicator of a contamination problem is **abnormal temperature response** during composting. If temperatures stay low too long after starting a windrow or pile, it signals that microbial activity is being suppressed.

### Remediation Strategies

When contamination is suspected:

1. **Add nitrogen** as [[fish-hydrolysate]] (especially if de-wormer residues are suspected).
2. **Add molasses** as an energy source for the organisms.
3. If temperatures remain low, introduce **specialized microbial inoculants** such as EM (Effective Microorganisms), Biostart, Organica, or Agri-Energy compost starters—these contain bacteria specifically capable of degrading these problematic compounds.
4. **Monitor grass clippings** carefully, as they frequently contain chemical residues.

### Persistent Contaminants

Some contaminants resist biological degradation:

- **Clopyralid** — no known bacteria or fungi routinely found on grass clippings can decompose it. Such organisms may exist but are not common.
- **Chlordane** — while some bacteria and fungi can degrade it, they are not always present in compost starting materials.

## Brix: Measuring Plant Sugar Content

### What Brix Measures

Brix is a measure of sugar content (dissolved solids) in plant sap. Elevated brix readings are often cited as indicators of plant health and nutritional quality.

### Protein Content in Biological Systems

Testing has shown that vegetables, grapes, and strawberries grown with healthy soil food webs contain **3 to 10 times more protein** than conventionally grown plants. This has significant implications for nutritional quality.

### Free Amino Acids and Pest Attraction

If free amino acids accumulate and do not get incorporated into protein synthesis, they attract pests. The question of why amino acids get "stuck" in their free form rather than moving into protein is an important plant physiology question. Excess protein sitting unused can also be harmful to plants—protein must be mobilized into structure and function.

### Limitations of Brix as a Diagnostic

Several factors complicate brix interpretation:

- **Diurnal variation:** Sugar levels fluctuate throughout the day, making it difficult to establish a baseline.
- **Healthy vs. responding:** Elevated brix might indicate the plant is healthy and well-nourished, OR it might indicate the plant is mounting an "immune" response to something sprayed on it.
- **Declining brix:** Could indicate declining health, or could mean sugars are being converted to other materials the plant needs.

## Nitrate and Amino Sugars During Flowering

### Caution with Nitrogen at Critical Growth Stages

Pushing nitrate and amino sugars during flowering or fruit set can cause problems by promoting vegetative growth instead of reproductive growth. While a single compost tea application typically does not add excessive nitrogen, the principle is to **feed the soil organisms, not overwhelm the plant with nutrients**.

### The Plant Controls Its Own Nutrition

When the full complement of organisms is present on leaf surfaces, the plant regulates its own nutrient uptake. If the plant needs foliar nutrients, it pumps sugars onto the leaf surface, which feeds the organisms, elevates CO2, opens stomata, and allows more nutrients in. If the plant does not need nutrients, this cascade does not occur.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Bacteria start decomposition; fungi finish it.** Fungi are essential for lignin and cellulose breakdown.
2. **Humic acid** is the most complex form of soil organic matter, requiring fungi, earthworms, and microarthropods.
3. **Molasses (500 ml per 4 kg waste)** favors fungal dominance and accelerates humus formation.
4. **Manure contaminants** are detected through temperature monitoring and remediated with nitrogen, molasses, and specialized inoculants.
5. **Clopyralid is extremely persistent** — avoid contaminated materials in compost.
6. **Brix is useful but not definitive** as a sole indicator of plant health.
7. **Feed organisms, not plants directly** — let the plant regulate its own nutrient uptake.
