# Plant Vision and Phototropism

Plants possess a form of vision that, while fundamentally different from
human sight, is remarkably sophisticated. They monitor their visible
environment continuously, detecting light direction, intensity, colour, and
duration. [[daniel-chamovitz]], in *What a Plant Knows*, argues that plant
light perception qualifies as genuine sight: just as a person born blind who
gains the ability to discriminate between light, shadow, and colour would
be described as having vision, so too can plants be said to "see" their
environment through specialised photoreceptors.

## Photoreceptors: The Basis of Plant Sight

Human vision relies on photoreceptors in the retina called rods and cones,
each containing light-absorbing chemicals (rhodopsin and photopsins) tuned
to specific wavelengths. Plants operate on the same underlying principle.
They possess their own suite of photoreceptor proteins that absorb
electromagnetic energy across the visible spectrum and beyond, including
ultraviolet and infrared wavelengths that humans cannot perceive. The
critical difference between human and plant vision is not the presence of
photoreceptors but the absence of a central nervous system to process
signals into images. Plants instead translate light information directly
into developmental and behavioural responses.

## Darwin's Phototropism Experiments

Charles Darwin, together with his son Francis, conducted a landmark series
of experiments on plant light sensitivity, published in 1880 in *The Power
of Movement in Plants*. They worked with canary grass (*Phalaris
canariensis*), growing seedlings in total darkness before exposing them to
a dim gas lamp placed twelve feet away, so faint that the experimenters
themselves could barely see. After only three hours, the seedlings had
curved decisively toward the light source. Crucially, the bending always
occurred in the same region, about an inch below the shoot tip.

The Darwins hypothesised that the plant's "eye" was located at the tip,
not at the bending region. They tested five conditions:

- **a.** Untreated control: bent toward light normally.
- **b.** Tip pruned off: no bending occurred.
- **c.** Tip covered with an opaque (lightproof) cap: no bending.
- **d.** Tip covered with a clear glass cap: normal bending.
- **e.** Middle section covered by a lightproof tube: normal bending.

The results were unambiguous. Removing the tip or blocking light from
reaching it eliminated phototropic bending entirely, while shielding the
bending zone itself had no effect. The clear glass cap permitted normal
bending because it still allowed light to strike the tip. The Darwins
concluded that the shoot tip perceives directional light and transmits a
signal downward, instructing the stem to bend. This was the first rigorous
demonstration of rudimentary sight in plants.

## Blue Light and Directional Bending

Julius von Sachs, a contemporary of Darwin's, established in 1864 that blue
light is the primary colour inducing phototropism. Plants are largely
insensitive to other colours when it comes to directional bending. This
blue-light specificity was later traced to dedicated photoreceptor proteins
(phototropins) distinct from those mediating other light responses. The
separation of phototropic bending (driven by blue light) from photoperiodic
flowering control (driven by red and far-red light) reveals that plants
maintain multiple, independent "visual" systems, each tuned to a different
ecological task.

## Photoperiodism: The Maryland Mammoth Tobacco

In 1906, farmers in southern Maryland noticed a new tobacco strain that
grew to extraordinary heights (over four metres) with nearly a hundred
leaves but rarely flowered. Named Maryland Mammoth, it posed an
agricultural puzzle: abundant vegetative growth without reproductive
output meant no seed for future crops.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of
Agriculture solved the mystery. They planted Maryland Mammoth in pots and
subjected one group to artificially shortened days by moving them into a
dark shed each afternoon. The shortened-day group flowered normally,
while plants left in the field continued growing vegetatively through the
long summer days. Maryland Mammoth was a **short-day plant**: it required
long nights (short days) to trigger flowering. This phenomenon, termed
**photoperiodism**, provided the first strong evidence that plants measure
the duration of light they receive.

Subsequent research revealed a broader taxonomy: short-day plants (soybeans,
chrysanthemums) flower when nights exceed a critical length; long-day plants
(irises, barley) flower when nights are sufficiently short. The discovery
also had immediate practical value. Florida farmers realised they could
grow Maryland Mammoth through the mild winter, and the plants would flower
naturally when days shortened in midwinter, avoiding the Maryland frosts
that had previously limited the growing season.

## Two Eyes, Two Functions

A striking finding from the study of photoperiodism is that the plant's
"eye" for measuring day length is anatomically distinct from its "eye" for
phototropic bending. Darwin showed that the tip of the shoot detects
directional light for bending. But for photoperiodic control of flowering,
illuminating any single leaf is sufficient to regulate the entire plant's
reproductive response. If all leaves are removed, leaving only stem and
apex, the plant becomes completely blind to light flashes even under full
illumination. The phytochrome photoreceptors in the leaves receive the
light cue and produce a mobile signal that propagates throughout the plant
to induce flowering.

This dual visual architecture reflects the fundamentally different
information each system extracts from light: directional bending requires
spatial resolution (where is the light coming from?), while photoperiodic
control requires temporal integration (how long has it been dark?).
