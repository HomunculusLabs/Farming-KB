# The Soil Works Itself

The soil lives of its own accord and plows itself. It needs no help from man.
Farmers often talk of "taming the soil" and of a field becoming "mature," but
why is it that trees in mountain forests grow to such magnificent heights
without the benefit of hoe or fertilizer, while the farmer's fields can grow
only puny crops?

## The Wisdom of Forests

Trees seem to grow almost haphazardly in the mountains and forests, but the
cedar grows where it can thrive to its great size, mixed woods rise up where
mixed woods must, and pine trees germinate and grow in places suited for pine
trees. One does not see pines growing at the bottom of a valley or cedar
seedlings taking root on mountain tops. One type of fern grows on infertile
land and another in areas of deep soil. Plants that normally grow along the
water's edge are not found on mountain tops, and terrestrial plants do not
thrive in the water. Although apparently without intent or purpose, these
plants know exactly where they can and should grow.

## Trusting Natural Processes

Natural farming asks us to consider: what if the soil already knows how to
feed plants? What if the complex community of microorganisms, fungi,
earthworms, and other soil organisms already provides everything crops need?
By stepping back and allowing these natural processes to operate, the farmer
can reduce labor, eliminate input costs, and restore the land to health.

This does not mean doing literally nothing. It means working with nature
rather than against it, providing conditions that allow natural soil-building
processes to flourish. A cover of living plants and organic mulch, rather
than bare exposed soil, is the starting point for this restoration.
## Practical Considerations

Successful implementation of The Soil Works Itself requires attention to
several practical factors including environmental conditions,

adaptive management help optimize outcomes across varying
conditions. Integration with other system elements enhances

improve resilience and productivity over time.
