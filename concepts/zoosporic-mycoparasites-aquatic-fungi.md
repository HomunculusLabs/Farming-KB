# Zoosporic Mycoparasites of Aquatic Fungi

Zoosporic mycoparasites are fungi and fungus-like organisms that
parasitize other fungi through zoospore-mediated infection in
aquatic or water-saturated environments. These include members
of the Chytridiomycota, Oomycota (Straminipila), and
Plasmodiophoromycota that have evolved specialized mechanisms
for recognizing, attaching to, and penetrating the hyphae,
sporangia, or zoospores of other aquatic fungi. Unlike the
terrestrial [[zoopagales-dimargaritales-zygomycete-mycoparasites]],
zoosporic mycoparasites exploit water-borne motile stages to
locate and colonize their hosts. They represent a poorly known
but ecologically significant component of aquatic fungal
communities and are most frequently discovered through
baiting experiments rather than routine surveys.

## Diversity and Taxonomic Distribution

Fungicolous organisms in aquatic environments include
representatives of the [[zoosporic-fungi]] in the Oomycota,
Plasmodiophoromycota, and Chytridiomycota. They often
parasitize hosts from the same orders, with intracellular,
often wall-less, thalli of certain Chytridiomycota and
Oomycota commonly growing inside other zoosporic fungi.
Most records of associations between mycoparasites and
aquatic fungi are based on single observations, making
generalizations about their distributions difficult.

Approximately 50 species of biotrophic zygomycetous
mycoparasites are known, along with about 50 parasites of
Glomales, and numerous species in the zoosporic groups.
The true numbers are undoubtedly much higher, as these
organisms are usually encountered only when specially
devised baiting studies target the host fungus rather than
the parasite itself.

## Key Genera and Host Interactions

### Olpidiopsis (Oomycota)

Species of Olpidiopsis are among the best-studied zoosporic
mycoparasites. Olpidiopsis varians parasitizes Achlya species
(Saprolegniaceae), while O. incrassata attacks Saprolegnia
species. The zoospores of the parasite encyst on the hypha
of the host, and the contents of the cyst pass into the
hypha via a penetration tube. Olpidiopsis species require
only between complementary sexual mating types, suggesting
that sexual recognition factors may be involved in host
specificity.

### Rozella (Chytridiomycota)

The genus Rozella, placed in the Blastocladiales, includes
approximately 19 species that are mycoparasitic on other
chytrids and water molds. Held (1981) assigned 25 known
mycoparasitic species of Rozella to five groups based on
morphology and host affinities. These groups consisted of
fungi parasitic on: (1) monocentric chytrids, (2) Blastocladia
and Leptomitales, (3) Monoblepharis and Pythiaceae, (4)
Allomyces and Saprolegniaceae, and (5) miscellaneous hosts.
When Rozella invades a host hypha, it elicits a host reaction
at the papilla with minimal cell disruption. The reaction does
not occur in hyphae that already are infected, so renewed
penetration by the parasite cannot occur.

### Rozellopsis (Chytridiomycota)

Rozellopsis inflata is a particularly interesting generalist
mycoparasite that can attack multiple hosts. It parasitizes
zoospores of the rotifer-capturing Zoophagus insidians and
can also grow on various species of Pythium. The chitinolytic
Mortierella alpina can penetrate and disintegrate the
cellulosic hyphae and oogonia of Saprolegnia species, as
can certain stemphylium-like and acremonium-like parasites.

### Woronina (Plasmodiophoromycota)

Woronina species parasitize Saprolegniaceae. The zoospores
of the parasite encyst on the hypha of the host, and the
contents of the cyst pass into the hypha via a penetration
tube. Several species of Woronina, Octomyxa, and Sorodiscus
represent a group of plasmodiophorid parasites of oomycetes.

### Catenaria (Chytridiomycota)

Catenaria species in the Blastocladiales include both
free-living and parasitic taxa. Catenaria allomycis parasitizes
Allomyces species, with detailed ultrastructural studies of
the host-parasite interface conducted by Sykes and Porter
and by Karling.

## Discovery and Isolation Methods

Zoosporic mycoparasites usually are discovered in specially
devised studies. Baiting with substrata suitable for the host
in combination with samples of water or soil from nature often
also yield the parasites. Fuller and Jaworski (1987) provided
detailed descriptions of methods for observing host-parasite
combinations, including Olpidiopsis varians-Achlya, Woronina
pythii-Pythium, and Rozella allomycis-Allomyces arbuscula.

Karling (1946) discovered numerous zoosporic endoparasites
of rotifers, nematodes, and other microfauna by simply
covering a soil sample with a layer of water and incubating.
This method depends on the natural buildup of populations
of microorganisms followed by parasitism and eventual
population decline, but requires numerous inspections
over long periods.

## Hyperparasitism

Chytrids themselves can act as hyperparasites. Chytridium
parasiticum grows on C. suburceolatum, which itself parasitizes
Rhizidium richmondense. Such chains of parasitism demonstrate
the complex trophic interactions in aquatic fungal communities.
Ingoldian hyphomycetes, whose conidia float in water, can also
be parasitized by aquatic specialists such as Sphaeromyces
coralloides, which parasitizes conidia of Anguillospora crassa
and Trichocladium splendens, and Crucella subtilis (teleomorph
Camptobasidium hydrophilum).
