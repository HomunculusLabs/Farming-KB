# New World Hallucinogens: Overview and Ethnobotanical Context

The New World — North, Central, and South America and the West Indies — harbors an extraordinary diversity of hallucinogenic plants that far exceeds anything documented in the Old World. More than 90 species are employed for their intoxicating principles, compared to fewer than a dozen in the Eastern Hemisphere. This page surveys the broad patterns of New World hallucinogen use and the ethnobotanical questions that surround them.

## Scale and Cultural Significance

- More than 90 species used hallucinogenically, compared to fewer than 12 in the Old World
- Some New World cultures were "practically enslaved by the religious use of hallucinogens"
- Hallucinogens acquired deep, controlling significance in almost every aspect of life in Mexico and South America
- Cultures in North America and the West Indies used fewer hallucinogens, often in secondary roles
- Despite the diversity, no New World hallucinogen has achieved the global significance of Old World cannabis
- No ethnological study of American Indians is complete without an in-depth appreciation of their hallucinogens

## The Old World–New World Disparity

- No evidence that vegetation of one hemisphere is inherently richer in hallucinogenic species
- The reasons for the disparity remain debated among ethnobotanists and anthropologists
- Possible explanations include differential cultural interest in narcotics, differential toxicity profiles, or simply incomplete documentation in the Old World
- The role of hallucinogens in Old World cultural development is only now being investigated in depth
- At every turn, the extent and depth of Old World use are becoming more evident as research progresses
- The disparity may reflect differences in cultural trajectories rather than botanical availability

## The Plant Additive Tradition

- New World cultures demonstrated extraordinary ingenuity in combining hallucinogenic plants
- **Plant additives** (admixtures) are added to preparations to alter, increase, or lengthen narcotic effects
- This practice is becoming increasingly important in modern pharmacological research
- The accomplishments of aboriginal Americans in creating psychoactive mixtures are unparalleled in the Old World

### Ayahuasca Additives

- Ayahuasca/caapi/yajé prepared primarily from Banisteriopsis caapi or B. inebrians bark
- Common additives include:
  - **Psychotria viridis** leaves — contain hallucinogenic tryptamines (DMT)
  - **Banisteriopsis rusbyana** leaves — also contain tryptamines
  - **Brunfelsia** species — hallucinogenic in their own right
  - **Datura** species — tropane alkaloid hallucinogens
- The β-carboline alkaloids in Banisteriopsis act as MAO inhibitors, allowing orally active DMT from additives
- This synergistic combination represents one of the most sophisticated pharmacological discoveries in ethnobotany
- Different indigenous groups use different additive recipes, producing varied experiences from the same base preparation

## Methods of Administration in the New World

- **Eaten fresh or dried:** Peyote, teononacatl (sacred mushrooms), puffballs
- **Juice drunk:** Salvia divinorum leaves crushed and expressed
- **Beverages drunk:** Ayahuasca, San Pedro cactus, jurema wine, iboga root, toloache leaves, morning glory seed preparations
- **Snuffing (insufflation):** Yopo, epena, sébil, rapé dos indios — a distinctly New World custom
- **Smoking:** Originally a New World tobacco custom, later applied to cannabis and other substances; tupa also smoked
- **Rectal administration:** Anadenanthera preparations in some New World Indian cultures
- **Multiple methods:** Virola resin may be licked unchanged, prepared as snuff, made into pellets to eat, or occasionally smoked

## Regional Patterns of Use

### Mesoamerica (Mexico and Central America)

- Mushrooms used as sacraments by multiple indigenous groups (Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Nahua)
- Morning glories (Rivea corymbosa, Ipomoea tricolor) for divination and healing
- Peyote cactus for prophecy, diagnosis, and curing disease
- Datura species for divination and ritualistic curing
- Mixtec puffballs (Lycoperdon mixtecorum and L. marginotum) to hear voices from heaven
- Salvia divinorum for healing and divination among Mazatec people
- Ololiuqui and other morning glory seeds in Aztec and Mazatec traditions
- Culebra borrachera and other local hallucinogens in shamanic practice

### South America

- Ayahuasca complex spanning the Amazon basin (Colombia, Peru, Brazil, Ecuador, Bolivia)
- Virola resin snuffs among Waiká and Witoto peoples of Brazil, Venezuela, and Colombia
- Jurema (Mimosa hostilis) among eastern Brazilian tribes for pre-battle visions
- San Pedro cactus (Echinopsis pachanoi) in Peruvian ceremonial and healing contexts
- Cimora drink used by Peruvian medicine men to assume another person's identity
- Yopo (Anadenanthera peregrina) snuff widespread across the Orinoco basin
- Vilca (Anadenanthera colubrina) seeds in the southern Andes and western Amazon
- Caapi used for initiation rites, dispute resolution, and spirit communication throughout the Amazon

### North America

- Peyote cult spread from Mexico into the southwestern United States and Great Plains
- Native American Church formalized peyote use as a sacramental practice
- Datura used by southwestern and plains tribes in divination and medicine
- Fewer species employed hallucinogenically than in Mesoamerica or South America
- Role of hallucinogens often secondary to other ceremonial elements
- Some evidence of lesser-known hallucinogenic plants among California and Great Basin tribes

## Why New World Identities Remained Unknown

- Despite their vital importance to New World cultures, botanical identities of many hallucinogens remained unknown until recently
- European colonization and suppression of indigenous practices led to catastrophic loss of ethnobotanical knowledge
- Secrecy surrounding ceremonial plants limited access to ethnographic information
- Many preparations involved multiple plant species, complicating botanical identification
- Modern ethnobotanical research continues to uncover species new to the hallucinogen list
- The destruction of pre-Columbian written records (especially Mayan and Aztec codices) eliminated primary sources

## Key Taxonomic Families in New World Hallucinogens

- **Solanaceae:** Datura, Brugmansia (tree daturas) — tropane alkaloids (scopolamine, atropine)
- **Cactaceae:** Peyote (Lophophora williamsii), San Pedro (Echinopsis pachanoi) — mescaline
- **Fabaceae:** Anadenanthera (yopo/vilca), Mimosa (jurema) — tryptamines (DMT, 5-MeO-DMT)
- **Malpighiaceae:** Banisteriopsis (ayahuasca) — β-carboline alkaloids (harmine, harmaline) + tryptamines from additives
- **Rubiaceae:** Psychotria — DMT-containing additive for ayahuasca
- **Convolvulaceae:** Morning glories (Rivea, Ipomoea) — ergoline alkaloids (lysergic acid amide / LSA)
- **Lamiaceae:** Salvia divinorum — salvinorin A (diterpene, unique κ-opioid receptor agonist pharmacology)
- **Asteraceae:** Calea zacatechichi — oneirogenic (dream-enhancing) properties
- **Euphorbiaceae:** Various species used as additives in South American preparations
