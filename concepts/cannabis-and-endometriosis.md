# Cannabis and Endometriosis

**Source:** The Cannabis Health Index, Uwe Blesching, PhD (2017)
**Updated:** 2026-04-19

## Evidence-Based Therapeutic Potential

- **Total Studies Reviewed:** 1
- **CHI Value:** 1

## Overview

Endometriosis is a condition where the tissue that normally lines the uterus (endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most commonly on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, and tissue lining the pelvis. It affects roughly 10% of reproductive-age women and can cause severe pain, infertility, and significantly reduced quality of life. Orthodox treatment includes pain medication, hormone therapy, and surgical removal of endometrial tissue.

Endometriosis is classified into four stages (I–IV) based on the location, extent, and depth of endometrial implants, as well as the presence of adhesions and endometriomas (ovarian cysts). The average delay from symptom onset to diagnosis is 7–10 years, during which many women suffer without effective treatment. Current medical therapies — including hormonal contraceptives, GnRH agonists, and progestins — often produce significant side effects and do not cure the condition, only suppress symptoms during active treatment.

## Cannabis and Endometriosis

One study reviewed cannabinoid effects on endometriosis-related pain. While research is still limited, the endocannabinoid system is known to be involved in pain modulation and inflammatory responses, both of which are central to endometriosis symptoms.

## Mechanisms of Action

The [[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]] is particularly relevant to endometriosis through several biological pathways:

- **CB1 receptor-mediated pain relief:** CB1 receptors are widely distributed in the central and peripheral nervous systems and play a key role in pain signaling. Activation of CB1 receptors reduces nociceptive transmission, providing analgesic effects for the chronic pelvic pain characteristic of endometriosis.

- **CB2 receptor-mediated anti-inflammation:** Endometrial implants outside the uterus trigger a chronic inflammatory response. CB2 receptors on immune cells in the peritoneal cavity modulate the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-8) that drive endometriosis-associated inflammation and pain.

- **Endocannabinoid tone deficit:** Research has shown that women with endometriosis have lower levels of endogenous cannabinoids (anandamide) in peritoneal fluid compared to healthy controls. This endocannabinoid deficiency may contribute to the development and progression of endometriosis, suggesting that supplementation with phytocannabinoids could help restore normal endocannabinoid tone.

- **Angiogenesis inhibition:** Endometriotic lesions require new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis) to establish and grow. Cannabinoids have demonstrated anti-angiogenic properties in multiple tissue models, which may limit the growth and vascularization of endometrial implants.

- **TRPV1 receptor modulation:** The TRPV1 (vanilloid) receptor is involved in pain signaling and is upregulated in endometriosis. CBD and other cannabinoids modulate TRPV1 activity, contributing to pain relief through a non-CB1/CB2 pathway.

## Emerging Research

Since the original review, interest in cannabinoid therapy for endometriosis has grown significantly:

- A 2017 study from the University of Western Australia found that cannabis use among women with endometriosis was associated with significant improvements in pain, sleep, and quality of life.

- Pre-clinical research has demonstrated that CB1 and CB2 receptor agonists reduce the growth of endometrial tissue in animal models of endometriosis.

- FAAH (fatty acid amide hydrolase) inhibitors, which increase endogenous anandamide levels, have shown promise in reducing endometriosis-associated pain in animal models.

## Symptom Management

Cannabis may help manage multiple symptoms experienced by women with endometriosis:

- Chronic pelvic pain (the hallmark symptom)
- Dysmenorrhea (painful menstrual periods)
- Dyspareunia (painful intercourse)
- Fatigue and low energy
- Anxiety and depression related to chronic pain and infertility
- Sleep disturbances

The [[cannabis-chronic-pain-management]] evidence base supports cannabinoid use for chronic pain conditions, which is directly applicable to endometriosis-related pain.

## Dosage and Administration

For endometriosis patients considering cannabis therapy, several factors are relevant:

- [[cannabis-topical-applications]] (pelvic suppositories) may provide localized relief for pelvic pain
- Oral preparations (tinctures, capsules) offer systemic effects for multi-site pain
- Vaporization provides rapid relief for breakthrough pain episodes
- [[cannabis-edibles-guide]] recommendations should be followed for oral products
- Patients should consult [[cannabis-dosing-guidelines]] and monitor for potential [[cannabis-drug-interactions-and-contraindications]] with hormonal therapies

## Strain-Specific Considerations

Given the inflammatory and pain-related nature of endometriosis, both CB1 and CB2 receptor activation may be beneficial. Indica and indica-dominant strains with lower THC:CBD ratios tend to favor CB2 signaling, which is associated with anti-inflammatory effects. CBD has been highlighted across many studies as having significant anti-inflammatory properties without psychotropic effects.

Strains with elevated levels of [[cannabis-minor-cannabinoids]] such as CBG and THCV may provide additional benefits. The [[cannabis-terpene-profile]] of selected strains can contribute to pain relief, with myrcene (sedative, analgesic), beta-caryophyllene (anti-inflammatory, CB2 agonist), and linalool (anxiolytic) being particularly relevant for endometriosis symptom management.

## Complementary Approaches

- **Turmeric (curcumin):** Known anti-inflammatory properties applicable to inflammatory conditions; may reduce endometriosis implant size in animal models
- **Dietary modifications:** Reducing inflammatory foods, increasing omega-3 fatty acids, and limiting red meat and alcohol
- **Pelvic floor physical therapy:** Can help manage pelvic pain and muscle tension associated with endometriosis
- **Acupuncture:** Some evidence supports acupuncture for endometriosis-related pain management
- **N-acetyl cysteine (NAC):** Has demonstrated potential to reduce endometrioma size in clinical studies

## Limitations of Current Research

Clinical evidence for cannabis and endometriosis is extremely limited, with no large-scale randomized controlled trials. Most evidence comes from patient surveys, case reports, and pre-clinical animal studies. The hormonal and immune complexity of endometriosis means that cannabinoid effects may vary depending on disease stage, lesion location, and individual patient factors. Cannabis should not replace conventional endometriosis treatment but may serve as an adjunctive therapy for symptom management.

# Cannabis and Endometriosis Research

**Source:** The Cannabis Health Index (Uwe Blesching, 2017)

**Last Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Overview

Endometriosis is a condition where endometrial tissue (normally lining the uterus) grows outside the uterine cavity, commonly on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, and pelvic lining. Primary symptoms include generalized pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea (painful periods), dyspareunia (painful intercourse), and abnormal bleeding. The only definitive diagnosis is via laparoscopy. The exact cause remains unclear; retrograde menstruation is the leading hypothesis.

## Evidence-Based Summary

**CHI Value: 5 (2 studies reviewed)**

### Key Findings

- **CB1 receptors in endometrial growths (2010, Florida State University):** CB1 receptors are present in nerve cells innervating endometrial growths, and CB1 activation reduces pain sensation. "The endocannabinoid system contributes to mechanisms underlying both the peripheral innervation of the abnormal growths and the pain associated with endometriosis, thereby providing a novel approach for the development of badly-needed new treatments."

- **WIN55,212-2 anti-proliferative effect (2010, Paris, France):** The synthetic cannabinoid WIN55,212-2 abrogated growth of endometriotic tissue and exerted an anti-proliferative effect in mice implanted with endometrial tissue, suggesting a link between the endocannabinoid system and novel treatments.

### Strain-Specific Considerations

WIN55,212-2 binds with higher affinity to CB2 than CB1. Indicas and indica-dominant strains generally present lower THC:CBD ratios, favoring CB2 expression. Both the anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative mechanisms are relevant to endometriosis treatment.

## Pharmacological Mechanisms

The [[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]] plays a significant role in endometriosis through both cb1-receptor and cb2-receptor pathways. CB1 receptors are expressed on nerve fibers innervating endometrial lesions, and their activation modulates pain signaling in the pelvic region. CB2 receptors, found primarily on immune cells, mediate anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative responses.

CBD (cannabidiol) demonstrates particular relevance for endometriosis due to its CB2-preferring affinity and its ability to modulate the immune response without psychoactive effects. THC activates CB1 receptors, contributing to pelvic pain relief, though its psychoactive properties may limit tolerability for some patients.

The endocannabinoid anandamide is involved in uterine function and endometrial cell migration. Dysregulation of endocannabinoid tone may contribute to both the development and progression of endometriotic lesions. Terpenes such as beta-caryophyllene, a selective CB2 agonist found in many cannabis strains, may further enhance anti-inflammatory effects through the [[cannabis-terpene-entourage-effect-profiles]].

Prostaglandin production is elevated in endometriosis, contributing to inflammation and pain. CBD has been shown to inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes and reduce prostaglandin synthesis, providing an additional anti-inflammatory mechanism relevant to endometriosis management.

## Clinical Evidence

The evidence base for cannabis and endometriosis remains limited, with a CHI Value of 5 reflecting only 2 reviewed studies. The Florida State University study (2010) identified CB1 receptors in endometrial growths through immunohistochemical analysis, providing anatomical evidence for cannabinoid involvement. The Paris study (2010) demonstrated anti-proliferative effects using the synthetic cannabinoid WIN55,212-2 in a murine model.

Both studies were preclinical, meaning results have not yet been confirmed in human clinical trials. Sample sizes were small, and the use of synthetic cannabinoids (WIN55,212-2) rather than whole-plant cannabis limits direct clinical applicability. Despite these limitations, the findings are mechanistically compelling and align with broader evidence for cannabinoid involvement in pain modulation and immune regulation.
