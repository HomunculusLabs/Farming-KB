# Cannabis and Endometriosis

**Source:** The [[blesching-cannabis-health-index-autoimmune-conditions]], Uwe Blesching, PhD (2017)
**Updated:** 2026-04-19

## Evidence-Based Therapeutic Potential

- **Total Studies Reviewed:** 1

## Overview

Endometriosis is a condition where the tissue that normally lines the uterus (endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most commonly on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, and tissue lining the pelvis. It affects roughly 10% of reproductive-age women and can cause severe pain, infertility, and significantly reduced quality of life. Orthodox treatment includes pain medication, hormone therapy, and surgical removal of endometrial tissue.

Endometriosis is classified into four stages (I–IV) based on the location, extent, and depth of endometrial implants, as well as the presence of adhesions and endometriomas (ovarian cysts). The average delay from symptom onset to diagnosis is 7–10 years, during which many women suffer without effective treatment. Current medical therapies — including hormonal contraceptives, GnRH agonists, and progestins — often produce significant side effects and do not cure the condition, only suppress symptoms during active treatment.

## Cannabis and Endometriosis

One study reviewed cannabinoid effects on endometriosis-related pain. While research is still limited, the endocannabinoid system is known to be involved in pain modulation and inflammatory responses, both of which are central to endometriosis symptoms.

## Strain-Specific Considerations

Given the inflammatory and pain-related nature of endometriosis, both CB1 and CB2 receptor activation may be beneficial. Indica and indica-dominant strains with lower THC:CBD ratios tend to favor CB2 signaling, which is associated with anti-inflammatory effects. CBD has been highlighted across many studies as having significant anti-inflammatory properties without psychotropic effects.

Strains with elevated levels of [[cannabis-terpene-profile]] of selected strains can contribute to pain relief, with myrcene (sedative, analgesic), beta-caryophyllene (anti-inflammatory, CB2 agonist), and linalool (anxiolytic) being particularly relevant for endometriosis symptom management.

## Complementary Approaches

- **Turmeric (curcumin):** Known anti-inflammatory properties applicable to inflammatory conditions; may reduce endometriosis implant size in animal models
- **Dietary modifications:** Reducing inflammatory foods, increasing omega-3 [[stable-carbon-isotope-signature-fatty-acids-mycorrhizal-carbon-tracking]], and limiting red meat and alcohol
- **Pelvic floor physical therapy:** Can help manage pelvic pain and muscle tension associated with endometriosis
- **Acupuncture:** Some evidence supports acupuncture for endometriosis-related [[cannabis-pain-management-protocols]]
- **N-acetyl cysteine (NAC):** Has demonstrated potential to reduce endometrioma size in clinical studies

## Limitations of Current Research

Clinical evidence for cannabis and endometriosis is extremely limited, with no large-scale randomized controlled trials. Most evidence comes from patient surveys, case reports, and pre-clinical animal studies. The hormonal and immune complexity of endometriosis means that cannabinoid effects may vary depending on disease stage, lesion location, and individual patient factors. Cannabis should not replace conventional endometriosis treatment but may serve as an adjunctive therapy for symptom management.

- [[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]] plays a significant role in endometriosis through both cb1-receptor and cb2-receptor pathways. CB1 receptors are expressed on nerve fibers innervating endometrial lesions, and their activation modulates pain signaling in the pelvic region. CB2 receptors, found primarily on immune cells, mediate anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative responses.

CBD (cannabidiol) demonstrates particular relevance for endometriosis due to its CB2-preferring affinity and its ability to modulate the immune response without psychoactive effects. THC activates CB1 receptors, contributing to pelvic pain relief, though its psychoactive properties may limit tolerability for some patients.

The endocannabinoid anandamide is involved in uterine function and endometrial cell migration. Dysregulation of endocannabinoid tone may contribute to both the development and progression of endometriotic lesions. Terpenes such as beta-caryophyllene, a selective CB2 agonist found in many cannabis strains, may further enhance anti-inflammatory effects through the [[cannabis-terpene-entourage-effect-profiles]].

Prostaglandin production is elevated in endometriosis, contributing to inflammation and pain. CBD has been shown to inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes and reduce prostaglandin synthesis, providing an additional anti-inflammatory mechanism relevant to endometriosis management.

## Clinical Evidence

The evidence base for cannabis and endometriosis remains limited, with a CHI Value of 5 reflecting only 2 reviewed studies. The Florida State University study (2010) identified CB1 receptors in endometrial growths through immunohistochemical analysis, providing anatomical evidence for cannabinoid involvement. The Paris study (2010) demonstrated anti-proliferative effects using the synthetic cannabinoid WIN55,212-2 in a murine model.

Both studies were preclinical, meaning results have not yet been confirmed in human [[mushroom-cancer-clinical-trials-overview]]. Sample sizes were small, and the use of synthetic cannabinoids (WIN55,212-2) rather than whole-plant cannabis limits direct clinical applicability. Despite these limitations, the findings are mechanistically compelling and align with broader evidence for cannabinoid involvement in pain modulation and immune regulation.
