# Intensive Planting and Spacing

[[steve-solomon]]'s analysis of plant spacing systems, from ultra-intensive raised beds to wide-spaced drought gardening. Solomon himself once taught and practiced intensive gardening but came to believe that wider spacing produces better results with far less water and labor.

## Solomon's Evolution

During the 1970s, [[john-jeavons]] was writing about intensive gardening, and Solomon used those methods, wrote books recommending them, and promoted them widely. But by the 1980s, several things became apparent:

- His trial plots with well-separated plants grew just as well as (and sometimes better than) his intensive vegetable garden
- The labor and water requirements of intensive gardening were enormous
- The supposed yield advantage per square foot was largely an illusion
- Intensive gardening makes the gardener a slave to constant watering

## The Four Spacing Systems

Solomon presents four columns of spacing in his plant spacing table:

### Column 1: Intensive Raised Beds (Jeavons method)

Ultra-close spacing on deeply dug raised beds. Solomon does NOT recommend this system. Examples:
- Beets: 4 x 4 inches (100 sq cm per plant)
- Cabbage: 12 x 12 inches
- Lettuce: 8 x 8 inches
- Tomatoes: 18 x 18 inches

**Problems with Column 1**:
- Crop canopy forms quickly and completely covers the bed
- Once canopied, beds need at least half an inch (1.25 cm) of water every day during sunny weather
- Sandy intensive beds may need watering daily in hot weather
- This requirement "shackles" the gardener — you cannot go on holiday during summer
- Plant growth is slowed by overcompetition
- Harvesting takes longer because plants are smaller and more numerous
- The supposed yield-per-square-foot advantage is largely an illusion

### Column 2: Semi-Intensive (Solomon's current method)

This is how Solomon currently gardens most of his plot. Small plants on raised beds, larger ones on raised wide rows. Examples:
- Beets: 4 x 18 inches (465 sq cm per plant — nearly 5x more room than Column 1)
- Cabbage: 12 x 36 inches
- Lettuce: 8 x 24 inches
- Tomatoes: 24 x 36 inches

**Advantages**:
- Plants do not form a canopy quickly — each has far more room
- The crop needs watering only every 2-5 days
- In a good year, rainfall may be adequate without irrigation
- Plants are larger and more succulent because growth isn't slowed by overcompetition
- Harvesting takes much less time
- Root systems can spread to access stored subsoil moisture

### Column 3: Extensive (good rainfall areas)

Small plants on wide raised beds, larger ones in slightly raised single rows, sprawling ones in hills far apart, corn and okra on flat ground.

**Advantages**:
- May go through rainless weeks without significant moisture stress
- Roots greatly benefit from capillarity — they discover soil water "like money in a bank account"
- Yields per square foot only slightly lower than Column 2
- In most years, rainfall alone is adequate
- Hoeing weeds is easy — rows are far apart
- Solomon kept a half-acre weed-free with only one short morning's hoeing each week using this system

**History**: This is the system used by English-speaking North Americans since Native Americans taught European settlers how to garden, based on millennia of their own experience.

### Column 4: Extensive (drought conditions)

For gardeners with little water or fertigation. Everything in hills, very far apart.

**Requirements**:
- Soil must hold moisture to considerable depth
- Weed competition must be minimal
- Soil surface must be kept loose or dust-mulched
- An inch of compost/manure on the surface prevents crust formation

## The Wisdom of Wide Spacing

Solomon argues that the shift from intensive to more spacious gardening:

- Is not a sacrifice but a big gain for most people
- Eliminates daily watering as a constraint
- Produces larger, more succulent vegetables that are faster to harvest
- Allows gardeners to go on summer holidays
- Perennials that produce ongoing harvests (like chard) actually yield more when widely spaced, because when intensively grown they yield well for a short period and then virtually stop — their root systems stunted by overcompetition

## Raised Beds in Solomon's System

Solomon uses slightly raised beds (not the deeply double-dug beds of the intensivists):
- Standard bed size: 4 x 25 feet (120 x 760 cm) = 100 square feet
- Raised about 4-6 inches above the surrounding paths
- Made by raking soil from paths onto the growing area
- Not double-dug — just loosened with a fork and raked level

## The Water-Spacing Connection

The fundamental relationship: closer spacing demands more water. Solomon's four columns are essentially four water-use strategies, from maximum irrigation (Column 1) to almost none (Column 4).
