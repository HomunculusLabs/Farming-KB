### Phosphorus Availability Strategies

- **Manure from seed-eating birds**: Has high phosphate levels and is readily available in many desert regions
- **Mycorrhizal inoculation**: Correct inoculation with phosphate-mobilizing mycorrhiza may be essential for introduced species
- **Small, localized application**: Phosphates must always be applied in small quantities, close to the crop

### Mycorrhizal Benefits

The following plant groups benefit significantly from root associates (mycorrhiza) that enable phosphorus accumulation from surrounding soil:

- **Acacias** and other leguminous trees
- **Eucalypts**
- **Legumes** generally
- **Pines**
- **Casuarinas**
- **Garden crops**

Mycorrhizal networks effectively extend the root system's reach, accessing phosphorus from a much larger soil volume than roots alone can explore.

## Mineral Solubility and pH

Understanding mineral solubility across pH ranges is fundamental to desert soil management:

### Key Mineral Solubility Patterns

- **Iron**: Very soluble at pH 3-3.5 (as limonite, yellow) and at pH 7-8 (as iron oxide, which reddens alkaline deserts)
- **Aluminium**: Very soluble at pH 4-4.5 and pH 9.5-10; relatively insoluble over normal garden ranges
- **Silica**: Slightly but increasingly soluble over pH 0-8, then rapidly becoming more soluble up to pH 10

Iron and manganese (ferromagnesium minerals) are closely associated in soils and show the same spectrum of deficiency with pH changes.

### Soil Amelioration Strategies

- **At higher pH levels**: Simple sulphur addition can restore iron, magnesium, and phosphate availability when these are present but "locked up" (testing as present but unavailable to plants)
- **At low pH (3-4)**: Requires massive calcium input as crushed or burnt lime, or crushed shells, to increase nutrient availability
- **In desert conditions**: Soluble aluminium and silica may be evaporated to form clay minerals allied to illite or montmorillonite soils

### [[desert-temperature-and-soils|Temperature]] Effects on Solubility

Higher temperatures in water greatly increase mineral solubility. This has practical implications for irrigation scheduling—warm irrigation water applied during the heat of day can increase nutrient availability compared to cool morning applications.

### Rock Weathering and Soil Formation

The carbonic acid naturally present in rain (greatly increased as rainwater infiltrates soils) drives mineral weathering:

- **Orthoclase (in granites)**: Carbonic acid removes potash, forming potassium bicarbonate
- **Plagioclase (white granites)**: Calcium and sodium bicarbonates are formed
- **Biotite rocks (biotite, dacite)**: Potassium and magnesium carbonates are formed

## Trace Element Application Methods

Trace elements (zinc, copper) can be applied through multiple methods:

1. **Direct soil application** as sulphates
2. **Foliar sprays** for rapid correction
3. **Infusion into irrigation water** at root level
4. **Slow-release pellets** placed at root level
5. **Bulk soil amendments** such as dolomite

### Dramatic Response Example

The addition of 7 kg/ha each of zinc and copper sulphates to alkaline South Australian soils enables pasture growth (and the carrying capacity of sheep) to **increase by a factor of 40 times**. Similarly, just 50 g/ha of molybdenum in more acid soils enables clover production.

### The Critical Importance of Analysis

**No amount of guesswork can supplant careful and skilled soil analyses.** Plant tissue from healthy garden plants should be analyzed for mineral content, with typical target concentrations including:

| Element | Typical Plant Concentration |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 20,000 ppm |
| Potassium | 10,000 ppm |
| Calcium | 10,000 ppm |
| Magnesium | 3,500 ppm |
