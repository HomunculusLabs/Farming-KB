# Substrate Bag Production for Oyster Mushroom Cultivation

The production of substrate bags is a central operation in the oyster
mushroom cultivation system taught in the FAO training program for disabled
farmers in northeastern Thailand. This step-by-step process transforms raw
agricultural materials into a sterilized growing medium ready for
inoculation with mushroom spawn. The procedures are designed to be
accessible to people with a wide range of physical abilities.

## Raw Materials and Substrate Formulation

The substrate for oyster mushroom bag cultivation is based on sawdust,
supplemented with materials that provide additional nutrients. The standard
formulation includes sawdust as the primary ingredient, supplemented with
rice bran to provide nitrogen and vitamins, and agricultural lime (calcium
carbonate) to adjust the pH. The proportions are critical: too much rice
bran encourages contamination, while too little limits mushroom yield.

The sawdust should be from hardwood species and free from treated or
painted wood. Sawdust from softwoods contains resins and other compounds
that can inhibit mushroom growth. Fresh sawdust should be allowed to weather
for several weeks before use, as very fresh sawdust may contain volatile
compounds toxic to mushroom mycelium.

## Sieving and Mixing

The first physical step in substrate preparation is sieving the sawdust to
remove large particles, bark fragments, and other debris that could create
air pockets or interfere with uniform colonization. After sieving, the dry
ingredients are thoroughly mixed together. This mixing step is critical for
ensuring even distribution of supplements throughout the substrate.

For trainees with mobility impairments, sieving and mixing can be performed
while seated on the floor. The training manual emphasizes that all necessary
tasks can be accomplished with modifications appropriate to each individual's
physical capabilities. The key principle is that there is no single correct
way to perform any task; trainees are encouraged to develop personal methods
that work for their particular situation.

## Moisture Adjustment

Water is added gradually while mixing to achieve a moisture content between
65 and 75 percent. This range is critical: substrate that is too dry will not
support vigorous mycelial growth, while substrate that is too wet creates
anaerobic conditions that favor bacterial contamination over mushroom
mycelium. The correct moisture level can be assessed by squeezing a handful
of substrate firmly; a few drops of water should appear between the fingers
without a stream of water running out.

## Bag Filling and Compaction

The moistened substrate is filled into polypropylene bags. Trainees sit on
the floor and fill bags using a small shovel. Each bag is compacted to
remove large air pockets, either by hitting with an empty bottle or by hand.
A compacting machine may be used where available for commercial-scale
production. Proper compaction is important because large air pockets can
become sites for contamination, while overly dense packing restricts air
exchange needed for mycelial growth.

A plastic ring is placed on the neck of each bag, and the top of the bag is
pulled through the ring and folded down. The bag is secured with a rubber
band. A pointed stick is then inserted through the neck to create a
ventilation hole almost to the bottom of the bag, taking care not to touch
the bottom. Each completed bag should weigh between 800 and 1000 grams.

## Capping and Racking

Plastic caps filled with cotton are prepared to close the bags, providing
a filter that allows gas exchange while excluding contaminant spores. The
capped bags are placed on iron racks, typically nine bags per rack for
commercial pasteurization chambers, and transferred to the pasteurization
area. The racks allow the bags to be handled efficiently and ensure even
exposure to steam during pasteurization.

## Pasteurization Process

Bags are pasteurized in 200-liter drums using a country-style steaming
system. An iron or bamboo screen is placed inside the drum approximately
one inch above four inches of water. Bags are stacked on the screen until
full, typically holding 80 to 100 bags. Everything is covered with double
layers of old rice bags, then plastic sheeting is placed over the rice bags
and closed tightly.

Fire is lit and temperature maintained at 90 to 100 degrees Celsius for
3 to 4 hours from the time steam begins coming out continuously. Temperature
must remain constant, and water must always be present in the drum. After
completing the pasteurization period, the fire is removed and bags are
allowed to cool for approximately 20 minutes before removing the cover.

## Cooling and Inoculation Readiness

After the initial cooling period, the cover bags are removed and the bags
are allowed to cool further to room temperature. This cooling period is
essential because inoculating substrate that is still hot will kill the
mushroom spawn. Only when bags are completely cool are they ready for
inoculation with sorghum seed spawn in a clean working area.

## Accessibility Considerations

Every step of the substrate bag production process has been adapted for
accessibility. Sieving, mixing, filling, and compaction can all be performed
from a seated position. The manual emphasizes that trainees must believe
they can do everything needed, and that specific tasks may need to be done
differently because of certain disabilities. The creative problem-solving
approach, in which trainers and trainees collaborate to find workable
solutions for each individual, is a hallmark of the program.
