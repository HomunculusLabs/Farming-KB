# Sheep Management and Grazing Systems

Sheep are among the most versatile domestic animals in permaculture systems,
providing meat, milk, wool, manure, and vegetation management services. Bill
Mollison included sheep management as part of the broader [[mollison-designers-domestic-animal-integration-design]]
framework, recognising their ability to convert pasture forage into valuable
products while improving pasture health through managed grazing. Sheep are
particularly well-suited to [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-3-farm-forestry]] and [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-4-forage-gathering]] where they can
grze extensive areas with minimal infrastructure.

## Sheep in the Permaculture System

Sheep fill a unique niche in permaculture design. Unlike cattle, which require
fencing that can handle large, strong animals, sheep can be contained with
relatively lightweight [[mollison-designers-woven-fence-hedge-live-barrier]] or [[mollison-designers-electric-fencing-in-permaculture-systems]]. Unlike goats, which
are aggressive browsers that can damage trees, sheep prefer grasses and forbs
and can be grazed under trees without significant damage to woody vegetation
when managed properly. Their small size makes them suitable for smallholdings
where cattle would be impractical.

The [[mollison-designers-wool-insulation-and-natural-textiles]] produced by sheep is a valuable permaculture product with multiple
uses including insulation for [[mollison-designers-straw-bale-building-techniques]], clothing, and mulch material.
Raw wool placed around newly planted [[mollison-designers-tree-planting-techniques-staking-guys]] deters slugs and conserves soil
moisture.

## Breed Selection

Choosing the right breed is essential for successful sheep integration. Mollison
recommended selecting breeds adapted to local climate and forage conditions
rather than importing high-input breeds that require supplementary feeding and
intensive management.

### Meat Breeds

For meat production, hardy breeds such as Dorper, Katahdin, and Wiltshire Horn
shed their fleece naturally, eliminating the need for shearing. These breeds
are well-suited to extensive grazing systems where labour is limited. The
[[mollison-designers-free-range-forage-design-principles]] principles apply directly to meat sheep management.

### Dual-Purpose Breeds

Dual-purpose breeds such as Romney, Coopworth, and Perendale produce both meat
and wool and are adapted to a wide range of climatic conditions. These breeds
are well-suited to [[mollison-designers-cool-humid-climate-design-overview]] and can be integrated into
[[mollison-designers-silvopasture-integrated-tree-livestock]] systems.

### Dairy Breeds

Dairy sheep breeds such as East Friesian and Lacaune produce milk that can be
made into cheese and yoghurt. Sheep dairy requires more intensive management
than meat production but offers higher value per animal. The [[mollison-designers-food-processing-preservation-storage]]
infrastructure of the permaculture settlement should include facilities for
sheep dairy processing.

### Hair Sheep

Hair sheep breeds do not produce wool and are well-adapted to tropical and
subtropical climates. They are resistant to parasites and heat stress, making
them ideal for [[mollison-designers-monsoon-tropical-design-strategies]]. Their manure is valuable for
soil fertility in [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]].

## Rotational Grazing Design

### Principles of Rotational Grazing
Rotational grazing is the foundation of permaculture sheep management. Pasture
is divided into paddocks using temporary fencing, with sheep moved regularly.
This prevents overgrazing, allows plant recovery, distributes manure evenly,
and breaks parasite cycles by moving sheep away from manure before larvae
become infective. Most temperate pasture grasses need 20 to 40 days of rest;
[[mollison-designers-tropical-pasture-grass-species-and-establishment]] species may recover faster in wet seasons.

### Paddock Design

Paddock design follows the [[mollison-designers-slope-analysis-keyline-design]] of the property. Paddock boundaries
should follow contours where possible, with water and shade located in each
paddock to reduce the distance sheep must travel. The [[mollison-designers-access-roads-pathways-and-track-design]]
should allow movement of sheep between paddocks without damaging pasture.

Laneways connecting paddocks should be wide enough for easy sheep movement but
narrow enough to minimise the land area dedicated to non-productive tracks.
The [[mollison-designers-keyline-pattern-plow-technique]] can inform the layout of laneways and paddocks on
sloping land.

### Fencing Systems
[[mollison-designers-electric-fencing-in-permaculture-systems]] is the most cost-effective option for rotational
grazing. Portable electric netting allows rapid paddock subdivision. For
perimeter fencing, [[mollison-designers-woven-fence-hedge-live-barrier]] provide permanent
containment. [[mollison-designers-willow-water-and-natural-rooting-propagation]] hedges can serve as living fences that also
provide fodder and wind protection.

## Integration with Tree Systems

Sheep integrate well with several permaculture tree systems:

### Orchard Grazing

Sheep can be grazed in [[mollison-designers-mixed-orchard-design-species-selection]] after the fruit harvest, cleaning up
fallen fruit that could harbour disease and fertilising the trees with their
manure. Care must be taken to protect young tree trunks from browsing using
tree guards or temporary fencing. Mature trees with trunks above browsing height
can be grazed year-round in some systems.

### [[mollison-designers-silvopasture-integrated-tree-livestock]]

In silvopasture systems, sheep graze between rows of timber or fodder trees.
The [[mollison-designers-legume-shade-tree-pasture-systems]] system combines nitrogen-fixing trees with
sheep grazing for maximum land productivity. Sheep help control understory
vegetation that would otherwise compete with young trees, reducing the need for
manual weed control.

### Shelterbelt Grazing

[[mollison-designers-shelterbelt-economics-for-livestock-and-croplands]] provide wind protection for sheep during cold weather and
shade during hot weather. The shelterbelt design should include species that
provide fodder as well as wind protection, allowing sheep to browse during
periods when pasture is scarce.

## Pasture Improvement

Sheep grazing can be used to improve pasture quality over time. By adjusting
stocking rates and grazing duration, the designer can favour desirable pasture
species and suppress less productive ones. The [[mollison-designers-alley-cropping-and-contour-hedgerow-systems]] system can be
combined with sheep grazing, with sheep fertilising the inter-row pasture while
hedgerow trees provide nitrogen and mulch.

[[mollison-designers-legume-tree-inoculation-and-nitrogen-distribution]] can be fed directly to sheep or laid on the pasture
as mulch. The nitrogen and organic matter from these prunings improve pasture
soil fertility, increasing carrying capacity over time.

## Water, Shelter, and Health
Sheep require clean water at all times. The [[mollison-designers-pond-construction-liner-sealing]] and
[[mollison-designers-hand-pump-and-water-lifting-devices]] systems should serve livestock as well as domestic use.
Shelter from extreme weather is essential. In cool climates, buildings or
[[mollison-designers-shelterbelt-economics-for-livestock-and-croplands]] provide protection; in hot climates, tree shade is primary.
Permaculture sheep management prioritises preventive health through rotational
grazing that breaks parasite cycles. Mixed-species grazing with cattle reduces
parasite loads since parasites are typically host-specific.
