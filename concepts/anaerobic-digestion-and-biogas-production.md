# Anaerobic Digestion and Biogas Production

Anaerobic digestion is the foundational process in biological sewage treatment, converting sewage sludge into methane (biogas) while dramatically reducing pathogens, heavy metals, and organic pollution. This process operates in sealed or shallow pond environments where oxygen is excluded, allowing specialised bacteria to break down organic matter.

## Biogas Composition

Biogas is a mixture of:
- **Methane** (CH₄) — the primary useful fuel component
- **Carbon dioxide** (CO₂)
- **Ammonia gas** (NH₃)
- **Traces** of nitrous sulphide or marsh gas (N₂O)

Biogas is a useful fuel gas for engines or cooking. However, it is also a gaseous component creating the greenhouse effect and should be captured and used, not released to air.

## Digester Design

### Scale and Dimensions

For 1,000 townspeople and associated industries, as little as **270 m² of settling pond 1 m deep** is needed. This can be configured as:
- A long pond 3 m wide × 90 m long
- 3 side-by-side ponds 30 m long × 3 m wide
- A compact digester 3 m deep × 3 m wide × 30 m long

### Sealed and Agitated Digesters

Long narrow ponds can be made totally anaerobic by fitting water seals and a weighted cover of plastic, metal, butyl rubber, or fibreglass. For deeper digester ponds, **artificial agitation is essential** — sludge settles and becomes inactive without it.

Agitation is achieved by pumping biogas back through the sludge to stir it. When sludge is stirred up, it is better broken down to biogas. A critical benefit of sealed and agitated digesters is that no scum forms on the surface, which can slow the breakdown process and cause acid conditions.

## Process Parameters

- **Retention time**: Approximately 20 days
- **Optimal temperature**: 25–30°C (77–86°F)
- **Depth**: Average 1 m (3 feet) for lagoon systems; deeper for compact digesters

Over 20 days at optimal temperature, a very high percentage of total dissolved solids is transferred into methane, with a small proportion passed on as living cells (bacteria or algae).

## Energy Balance

About **1 cubic metre of methane generated removes approximately 2.89 kg of solids**, reducing biological oxygen demand (BOD) proportionally. The methane produced can:
- Run a motor for electricity generation
- Compress gas for cooking or machinery
- Supply heat for the digester process via closed-loop piping
- Compress gas for digester agitation

The motor supplies both heat for the digester process and compressed gas for agitation and energy supply, making the system largely self-sustaining.

## Pathogen and Heavy Metal Removal

In the digester:
- **90–94% of worm eggs** are destroyed
- **Many harmful bacteria** are eliminated
- Useful energy is generated at the treatment location

### Heavy Metal Removal in Anaerobic Ponds

Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) produced combines with soluble heavy metals to produce insoluble sulphides above pH 7. Measured removal rates (Hussainy, Melbourne Metropolitan Water Board):

| Metal | Total Removal | Anaerobic Removal |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|
| Copper | 97% | 78% |
| Cadmium | 70% | 70% (all anaerobic) |
| Zinc | 97% | 83% |
| Nickel | 65% | 47% |
| Lead | 95% | 90% |
| Chromium | 87% | 47% |
| Iron | 85% | 47% |

## Self-Regulating Equilibrium

The sludge becomes its own solution. In an anaerobic shallow pond or deeper agitated pond, the more sludge present, the more active the pond. A self-regulated equilibrium establishes where input balances gas output. **Removing sludge slows or stops the process** — this is a clear case of leaving well alone.

## Sulphur-Loving Bacteria

Specialised bacteria in anaerobic ponds include genera *Thiosporallum*, *Chromatium*, and *Rhodopseudomonas*. These may appear pink and colour the ponds. They use hydrogen sulphide as a hydrogen source for carbon assimilation; their by-product is **elemental sulphur (S)**, which binds to metals present. About 1.8–2.0 mg/l of heavy metals are precipitated as sulphides at 1.0 mg/l of elemental sulphur.

## Seasonal Variations

- **Winter** (10–15°C): More hydrogen sulphide released (8–15 mg/l vs summer's 2–5 mg/l); winds contribute more to oxygen than algae; decomposition slows and sludge builds up
- **Summer** (18–22°C): Residues oxidised to nitrates; oxygen provided more by algae; BOD nearly doubles to 1,034 kg/ha/day vs winter's 495 kg/ha/day; almost twice as much methane is produced

In winter, cooling water from methane-powered engines provides essential heat to digesters via closed-loop pipe.
