# Growing Potatoes

The potato is the most nutritionally productive staple crop except for bare oats, producing many times more actual nutrition per acre than any other staple. Potatoes can contain up to about 11% protein (dry weight), matching the protein content of human breast milk. Understanding how soil fertility, moisture, and variety choice affect potato quality is essential for getting the most from this foundational crop.

## Nutrition vs. Bulk Yield

There is a fundamental tradeoff between bulk yield and nutritional quality:

| Growing Approach | Result |
|-----------------|--------|
| Starchy variety + lots of moisture + excess potassium fertilizer | Larger bulk yield of low-protein, less nutritious spuds |
| Boiling variety + reduced irrigation + mineral-rich, low-potassium soil | Smaller yield of smaller, tastier, more nutritious spuds |

**The key**: If you reduce or avoid irrigating once tuber formation begins, and build soil fertility with considerable mineral nutrients but a rather low level of potassium, you'll get smaller but more nutritious potatoes.

## Climate Considerations

- Potatoes are a cool-season crop — must be planted late enough in spring to emerge after the last frost
- If vines are burned by frost, more will emerge from the seed piece (with some yield loss)
- Potatoes don't like scorchingly hot weather, especially when humid
- In hot summer regions, potatoes are grown as a spring crop and dug early
- Winter potatoes are possible in frostless Florida but suffer from low productivity — the tuber is a savings account, and when the leaves translocate less sugar after midsummer, so does tuber formation decline

## Seed Potato Selection and Preparation

### Chitting (Presprouting)

This is one of the most important steps that home gardeners can do but commercial growers cannot. Farmers must plant unsprouted seed because the sprouts would be knocked off by rough handling. Home gardeners can do much better.

**Method**:
1. About 6 weeks before planting, spread uncut seed potatoes on a tray
2. Place in a brightly lit room that is not too warm (in front of a window with no direct sunlight works)
3. By planting time, potatoes will have turned light green with shoots emerging from many eyes
4. On planting day, cut larger potatoes in chunks, ensuring each chunk has two eyes that are actually sprouting

### Seed Piece Requirements

- Each chunk should weigh at least 2 oz (60 g)
- Must contain at least two eyes
- Chunks cut from larger potatoes must also be at least 2 oz
- Small potatoes (2-3 oz) can be planted whole

### Why Not Save Your Own Seed

Potatoes accumulate virus diseases — over 20 such diseases are transmitted from year to year in saved seed. These don't kill the plant but reduce vitality and lower yield significantly. The more years you plant from your own saved seed, the more viruses the potatoes will contain and the lower yields will become.

**Recommendation**: Always use certified seed. It is not costly compared to the result it provides. If you must save your own, never do it for more than one or two years before starting anew with certified seed.

## Soil Preparation

### The Double-Dig Method

For the highest possible yield:

1. Excavate the row first — remove soil one shovel-blade wide and nearly to shovel depth
2. Set the soil beside the ditch
3. Put COF and/or strong [[compost]] into the ditch
4. Standing in the trench, dig it in as deeply as another full shovel's length
5. This puts fertility about 2 feet deep where the main root system will form

### Simpler Method

1. Spread 4-6 quarts (4-6 liters) of lime-fertilizer per 50 row feet (15 m)
2. Spread a dusting of well-rotted manure or compost in one-foot-wide (30 cm) bands
3. Deeply dig the compost-covered rows (not the spaces between rows)
4. The soil forms a low mound above a zone of loose, humusy soil

### Lime Warning

Some authorities say not to lime potatoes because it causes scab. Results are mixed — if concerned, keep lime out of the COF used for potatoes.

## Planting

### Spacing

- Plant spuds on flat ground in long rows about 36 inches (90 cm) apart on center
- Space seed pieces 8-12 inches (20-30 cm) apart
- **Wider spacing**: More lunkers (huge specimens), slightly lower yield, better ability to handle dry soil
- **Closer spacing**: Moderately sized potatoes, largest possible yield, less ability to handle dry spells

### Planting Depth

- Pull back the soil from a spot in the center of the row, opening a small hole about 4 inches (10 cm) deep
- Gently set the seed in — if there are long shoots, point them up
- Cover it up
- The seed will have been placed only a few inches below the original soil line

## Hilling

### The Farmer vs. Gardener Difference

Farmers must plant deep in loose soil and leave a large ridge to prevent greening. Gardeners can do much better by planting shallowly and hilling up as the plants grow. This provides a loose medium for potatoes to form in.

### Hilling Schedule

1. **First hilling**: When vines have grown about 4 inches (10 cm), hill from both sides of the row, burying the bottom inch (2.5 cm) of the vine
2. **Second hilling**: 5-7 days later, hill up another inch or two. Never cover more than a quarter of the new growth
3. **Continue**: Keep hilling in small increments as weeds emerge between rows
4. **Final result**: By bloom time, you should have an 18-inch-wide (45 cm) mound of loose earth about 10 inches (25 cm) tall

### Benefits of Proper Hilling

- No green potatoes (all tubers stay underground)
- Easier to dig at harvest
- Smoother potatoes
- Weed suppression

## The Potato Growth Cycle

Understanding this cycle explains why timing matters so much:

1. **Leaf growth**: Plant grows leaves; food from leaves makes more leaves
2. **Tuber initiation**: Plant begins forming tubers just below the stems above the seed piece
3. **Peak production**: New tubers form rapidly, growth of new leaves slows
4. **Flowering**: Tuber formation slows; existing leaves pump sugar and nutrients into formed tubers
5. **Senescence**: Flowering ceases, vine dies off, all remaining nutrients translocate into maturing tubers, skins toughen

### Early vs. Late Varieties

| Type | Time to Bloom | Vine Size | Yield | Best Use |
|------|--------------|-----------|-------|----------|
| Early | Short | Short | Lower | Summer eating |
| Late | Long | Long | Higher | Winter storage |

**Key insight**: If the main crop vines die back just before frosts come, their potatoes will have less tendency to resprout prematurely. In long-season climates, delay planting the main crop for a month after earlies are sown.

## Harvest and Storage

### New Potatoes

- As soon as the earliest sowing is in bloom, dig a plant for new potatoes
- They'll grow one size every few days
- Just dig the entire plant whenever you need new potatoes

### Main Harvest

- Wait until vines have browned off, indicating the tubers are mature
- Test by rubbing — skins should be tough and won't rub off easily
