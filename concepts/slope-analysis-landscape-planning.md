## Slope Analysis and Landscape Planning

Slope analysis extends the zone-and-sector framework by incorporating the three-dimensional realities of landform — elevation, gradient, aspect, and orientation — into [[permaculture-design]], these slopes remain firmly in forest cover, contributing to the watershed function without requiring human management.

### Zone C — Lower Slopes and Foothills

The gentle lower slopes and foothill pediments are the **most productive and viable areas for settlement and mixed agriculture**. This is the prime site for human habitation and intensive food production for several reasons:

- **The [[thermosiphon]] effects**: Slope and elevation permit placement of hot air or hot water collectors below their storage tanks, where natural thermal convection circulates fluid without external energy. Even very slight slopes of 1:150 function to collect water and heat if well exploited in design.
- **Water treatment cascades**: Waste water from settlement can flow through a series of treatment ponds and wetlands, each at a slightly lower elevation, purifying water through biological processes before it reaches waterways.
- **Dam placement**: High run-off from upper slopes is led to midslope storage dams at keypoints, then passed with nutrients to lower dams, released as clean water from the site.

Where no natural slope exists, the same effects can be achieved by **raising tanks on towers** for water pressure or **excavating hollows** for heat collectors and solar ponds.

## Sun Angle and Aspect

Beyond slope gradient, the orientation of slope (aspect) and the arc of the sun across seasons critically determine placement:

**Sun angle** describes the arc of the sun during summer and winter months, and determines eave and sill placement of windows, areas of shade, and reflection or absorption angles of surfaces. In every situation — even hot deserts — some part of the system should be left open to the sun for its energy potential.

**Aspect** describes the orientation of the slope relative to the sun. A slope facing the sun receives considerably more direct solar radiation than a slope facing away. The shaded side of hills may delay spring thaw and moderate frost effects in vegetation. In mid-latitudes, designers seek the sunny aspect of slopes to achieve maximum sunlight absorption for settlements and gardens.

## Orientation of Structures

The final act in site planning is orienting all buildings and structures correctly — to face mid-sky, the sun, or the wind systems they reference, or to shelter them from detrimental factors such as cold winter winds or late afternoon sun. When zone placement, sector management, sun angle, slope optimization, and structural orientation are all addressed, the resulting ground design surpasses most conventional planning.

## Settlement at Ecological Junctions

Slope analysis reveals a fundamental principle of permanent settlement: **the most viable human settlements are sited at the junctions of two or more natural ecologies**. Whether on the boundary between foothill forests and plains, the edge of plain and marsh, the transition from land to estuary, or some combination of all these, successful settlements draw from the resources of multiple environments.

Planners who place housing settlements on a plain or plateau may have the "advantage" of simple geometry, but they abandon inhabitants to dependence on a single monocultural landscape. When transport fuels become scarce or expensive, such settlements cannot meet their needs from the surrounding land. The ancient occupied ridgeways of England testify to this commonsense — their present abandonment for industrial suburbs on flatlands reflects a loss of the paleolithic planning wisdom that placed settlements at ecological transitions.

## The Ethics of Slope and Land Use

Mollison argues that no settlement which fails to preserve natural benefits — for example, clearing all forests — can persist. Any design that removes the productive capacity of upper slopes, eliminates forest cover, or converts diverse foothill landscapes to monoculture is, in his terms, "bent on eventual extinction." The slope analysis framework is not merely a technical tool but an ethical imperative: to use land according to its inherent capabilities, preserving the functions that sustain all lower systems.
