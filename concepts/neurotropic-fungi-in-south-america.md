# Neurotropic Fungi in South America

South America harbors significant diversity of
[[neurotropic-fungi-world-checklist]], particularly in
subtropical and tropical forest regions. The continent shares many species with
Mexico and Central America, reflecting ancient biogeographic connections.

## Species Distribution by Country

### Brazil

Brazil has the highest documented neurotropic species count in South America.
Notable species include:

- Multiple endemic Psilocybe: P. brasiliensis, P. paulensis, P. acutipilea,
  P. blattariopsis, P. caeruleoannulata, P. farinacea, P. furtadoana,
  P. microcystidiata, P. pericystis, P. plutonia, P. ramulosa, P. uruguayensis
- [[amanita-muscaria-chemistry-and-pharmacology]] — found in
  pine plantations in tropical regions
- [[copelandia-genus-species-guide]] and C. anomala
- [[gymnopilus-purpuratus]]

Brazil's vast territory, encompassing the Amazon rainforest, Atlantic forest,
cerrado, and subtropical regions, suggests that many more species await
discovery. The country's mycological exploration has been patchy, with most
records concentrated in the southern and southeastern states.

### Colombia

Colombia has 12 reported neurotropic [[psilocybe-subaeruginosa-australia]]
species, several endemic:

- P. colombiana, P. guatapensis, P. antioquensis, P. heliconiae,
  P. subacutipilea, P. pintonii
- P. angustipleurocystidiata (shared with Mexico)
- P. zapotecorum, P. cubensis, P. subcubensis, P. yungensis
- Schultes and Bright (1979) found ancient gold pectorals suggesting
  pre-Columbian ceremonial use of psychoactive fungi

### Chile

Chile represents the southernmost extent of many neurotropic species:

- P. semilanceata — Reaches its southern limit here, also found in Europe and
  North America
- P. fimetaria — Shared with Europe and North America
- P. carbonaria, P. sierrae, P. strictipes, P. liniformans var. americana
- P. zapotecorum, P. lazoi
- gerronema fibula — Disjunct distribution species

### Argentina

- P. zapotecorum, P. wrightii, P. collybioides, P. cubensis
- P. hoogshagenii var. hoogshagenii
- [[amanita-muscaria-chemistry-and-pharmacology]]

### Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, Venezuela

- P. yungensis is widespread across Andean countries
- P. cubensis found throughout tropical lowlands
- P. subcubensis distributed across northern South America
- [[copelandia-genus-species-guide]] common in tropical
  regions
- Venezuela has P. meridensis, P. pseudobullacea

## Biogeographic Significance

South America is considered a likely center of origin for neurotropic Psilocybe
species. The high diversity, combined with the presence of many endemic species,
supports the hypothesis that these fungi may have originated in the southern
hemisphere and subsequently dispersed northward.

Key biogeographic connections:

- South American Psilocybe species show close relationships with those of
  Southeast Asia (New Guinea, Indonesia, Australia), as noted by Horak (1983)
- P. zapotecorum ranges from Mexico through Central America to South America,
  the widest distribution of any neurotropic Psilocybe species
- P. yungensis connects Andean South America with Mexico through cloud forest
  corridors
- gerronema fibula has a disjunct distribution in South
  America, Europe, and Southeast Asia, paralleling patterns seen in other
  fungal taxa

## Habitat Types

Neurotropic fungi in South America occupy diverse habitats:

- Subtropical humid forests (highest diversity)
- Tropical lowland forests (Amazon basin)
- Cloud forests (especially in Andean regions, 1,500–3,000 m elevation)
- Pastureland (P. cubensis on cattle dung in deforested areas)
- Pine plantations (introduced Amanita muscaria in exotic forestry)
- Paramo and high-altitude grasslands (Andean species)

## Traditional and Ceremonial Use

Several South American indigenous cultures have documented or suspected
relationships with neurotropic fungi. The Mazatec-related traditions in southern
Mexico have South American parallels, and pre-Columbian gold artifacts from
Colombia depicting mushroom-like figures suggest ceremonial significance. The
use of psychoactive mushrooms in Amazonian cultures, while less well-documented
than ayahuasca use, likely exists but remains poorly studied by Western
researchers.
