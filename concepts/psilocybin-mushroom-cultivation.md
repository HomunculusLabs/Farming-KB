## Psilocybin Mushroom Cultivation

Techniques for cultivating psilocybin-containing mushrooms, drawn from historical methods, traditional practices, and modern mycological approaches. Based primarily on [[jochen-gartz]]'s comprehensive survey. See also [[mushroom-species-guide]] for species-specific cultivation data.

## History of Cultivation

### Traditional Methods

The indigenous peoples of Mexico cultivated Psilocybe species near their settlements long before European contact. Wasson's 1957 Life magazine article sparked Western scientific interest. In 1958, [[albert-hofmann]] isolated psilocybin at Sandoz Laboratories. R. Heim (Paris) was the first to cultivate Psilocybe species in Europe, working with specimens sent by Wasson from Mexico. Heim successfully cultivated P. mexicana, P. caerulescens, and P. zapotecorum in the laboratory.

### The PF Tek Revolution

The "Psilocybe Fanaticus Technique" (PF Tek) popularized home cultivation in the 1990s. This simple method uses brown rice flour, vermiculite, and half-pint mason jars. The substrate is sterilized, inoculated with spore syringe, and incubated until fully colonized, then fruiting is triggered by introducing light and lowering temperature. This method made cultivation accessible to non-specialists and became the basis for widespread online cultivation communities.

### Gartz's Own Cultivation Work

[[jochen-gartz]] developed innovative techniques including inoculating wood substrates with Psilocybe cyanescens and P. bohemica. He documented successful cultivation of European indoor species using dung-based and wood-based substrates, and experimented with outdoor cultivation methods for temperate species.

## Species-Specific Cultivation

### Psilocybe cubensis

The most commonly cultivated species worldwide due to its fast growth, large size, and relatively simple requirements.

- **Substrate:** Grain spawn (rye, millet, brown rice) followed by bulk substrates (coir, manure, straw)
- **Temperature:** Colonization at 75-80F (24-27C); fruiting at 70-75F (21-24C)
- **Humidity:** 90%+ for fruiting
- **Light:** Ambient light triggers fruiting; no direct sunlight needed
- **Cycle:** 2-3 weeks colonization, 1-2 weeks to first flush
- **Yield:** Typically 50-200g fresh per cake/container
- **Strains:** Numerous named varieties (Golden Teacher, B+, Ecuador, Cambodian, Thai, etc.) show morphological variation but all belong to P. cubensis

### Psilocybe mexicana

- **Substrate:** Wheat straw, grass seed, or dung-based substrates
- **Notable:** Produces sclerotia ("magic truffles") — hard, dense masses of mycelium that store psilocybin. Sclerotia can be harvested without fruiting and are commercially available in the Netherlands.
- **Cultivation difficulty:** More challenging than P. cubensis; longer colonization times

### Psilocybe cyanescens

- **Substrate:** Wood chip beds (alder, birch, Douglas fir); outdoor cultivation preferred
- **Temperature:** Cool-weather species; fruits at 50-65F (10-18C)
- **Season:** Autumn fruiting (October-December in Pacific Northwest)
- **Cultivation difficulty:** Moderate; outdoor beds can produce for years once established
- **Note:** One of the most potent species; contains 0.3-1.5% psilocybin + psilocin dry weight

### Psilocybe azurescens

- **Substrate:** Coastal dune grasses, wood chips, sandy soils
- **Natural range:** Pacific Northwest coast (Oregon, Washington)
- **Cultivation:** Extremely difficult indoors; best grown in outdoor beds mimicking coastal dune conditions
- **Potency:** Among the strongest known; up to 1.8% psilocybin + psilocin

### Panaeolus cyanescens

- **Substrate:** Composted horse or cow dung
- **Temperature:** Subtropical to tropical; 75-85F (24-29C)
- **Cultivation difficulty:** Moderate; requires pasteurized dung substrate
- **Potency:** High; 0.5-2.5% psilocybin + psilocin

### Psilocybe bohemica / P. serbica

- **Substrate:** Wood chips, sawdust (deciduous trees)
- **Temperature:** Cool-weather; similar to P. cyanescens
- **Range:** Central Europe (Czech Republic, Slovakia, Germany)
- **Note:** Documented by Gartz as one of Europe's most significant psychoactive species

## Cultivation Techniques

### Grain Spawn Preparation

1. Soak grain (rye, millet, wheat) for 12-24 hours
2. Load into jars with filtered lids (polyfill or micropore tape)
3. Sterilize in pressure cooker at 15 PSI for 90-120 minutes
4. Inoculate with spore syringe or liquid culture
5. Incubate at 75-80F until fully colonized (shaking at 30% colonization speeds process)

### Bulk Substrate Methods

- **Monotub:** Large plastic container with holes for gas exchange; spawn mixed with coir/vermiculite/manure
- **Spawn bags:** Filter patch bags for larger-scale cultivation
- **Outdoor beds:** Wood chips or dung mixed with spawn in shaded garden areas

### Fruiting Conditions

- **Fresh air exchange (FAE):** Essential for healthy fruiting; CO2 buildup causes elongated, unhealthy mushrooms
- **Humidity:** 90%+ maintained via misting and humidification
- **Light:** 12 hours on/off cycle with indirect light; blue spectrum (6500K) recommended
- **Temperature drop:** Lowering temperature 5-10F from colonization temp triggers fruiting for many species
- **Casing layer:** Non-nutritive layer (peat/vermiculite) on colonized substrate; helps maintain humidity and provide microclimate for pinning

## Psilocybin Content Variation

Potency varies enormously based on genetics, substrate, growing conditions, age at harvest, and storage method. From Gartz's chemical analyses:

- **Intra-species variation:** Can be 3-10x between individual specimens of the same species
- **Substrate effects:** Dung-grown specimens often show different alkaloid profiles than wood-grown
- **Age effects:** Psilocybin content decreases as mushrooms mature; young specimens and pins often have higher concentrations relative to weight
- **Drying effects:** Fresh mushrooms contain primarily psilocybin; drying converts some psilocin (unstable) to psilocybin. Proper drying at low heat (95-110F) preserves maximum potency
- **Storage:** Dried mushrooms lose potency over time; freezer storage in airtight containers with desiccant maximizes longevity

## Sclerotia (Truffles)

Several Psilocybe species form sclerotia — compacted masses of mycelium that serve as nutrient storage organs and contain psychoactive alkaloids:

- **P. mexicana:** Most commonly cultivated for sclerotia; "Philosopher's Stones" or "tampanensis" truffles
- **P. tampanensis:** Originally discovered near Tampa, Florida; produces sclerotia
- **P. atlantis:** Related species also producing sclerotia
- **P. galindoi:** Mexican species cultivated for truffle production

Sclerotia advantages for cultivation: can be harvested without fruiting, longer shelf life, more forgiving of suboptimal conditions. Disadvantages: lower potency per gram compared to dried fruiting bodies of the same species.
