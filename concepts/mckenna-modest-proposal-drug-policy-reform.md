# A Modest Proposal for Drug Policy Reform

In the closing chapters of Food of the Gods, McKenna moves from historical analysis to political prescription. His nine-point proposal for drug policy reform represents his most concrete attempt to translate the archaic revival into legislative action, grounding legalization in democratic principle and practical pharmacology.

## The Fundamentalist Contribution

In the United States, the federal government's zeal to appear to wish to eradicate drugs is directly linked to the degree to which the government has been co-opted by the values of fundamentalist Christianity. We entertain the illusion of constitutional separation of church and state, but the reality is more complex.

When the government prohibited alcohol during Prohibition, when it interfered with reproductive freedom, or with the use of peyote in Native American religious rituals, and when it attempts unreasonably to regulate foods and substances, it is acting as the enforcing arm for right-wing fundamentalist values.

Eventually the right to determine our own food and drug preferences will be seen as a natural consequence of human dignity, as long as it is done in a way that does not limit the rights of others. The signing of the Magna Carta, the abolition of slavery, the enfranchisement of women all swept away ossified structures.

The war on drugs is schizophrenically waged by governments that deplore the drug trade and yet are also the major guarantors and patrons of the international drug cartels. Such an approach is doomed to failure by its own internal contradiction.

## The War on Drugs Was Never Meant to Be Won

The war on drugs was never meant to be won. Instead, it will be prolonged as long as possible in order to allow various intelligence operations to wring the last few hundreds of millions of dollars in illicit profits from the global drug scam. Then defeat will have to be declared.

Defeat will mean that the media will correctly portray the true dimensions of the situation and the real players, and that public revulsion at the culpability and venality of the Establishment's role will force a policy review. The parallel with the Vietnam War is explicit and deliberate.

In cynically manipulating nations and peoples with narcotics and stimulants, modern governments have associated themselves with an ethical disaster comparable to the eighteenth-century rebirth of the slave trade or the recently renounced excesses of Marxism-Leninism.

## The Case for Legalization

The conclusion seems obvious to McKenna: only legalization can lay the basis for a sane drug policy. This opinion has been reached by most disinterested commentators, although the political consequences of advocating legalization have made it slow to be considered.

Arnold Trebach's The Great Drug War marshaled persuasive arguments in favor of a revolution in drug policy. Trebach suggested approaching the subject of drugs more like religion than science, with some form of First Amendment guarantee of freedom to select a personal drug doctrine.

What Trebach does not discuss is the role to be played by hallucinogens in the post-suppression scenario. Psychedelics seem unimportant if the only measure is street sales, but another measure of social importance argues otherwise.

A clue to that other measure is the interest the CIA and military intelligence gave to psychedelics during the sixties through projects such as MK-ULTRA. The widespread belief that the conclusion of these studies was that television was preferable for mass hypnosis should not be taken at face value.

McKenna believes that once drugs are legalized, the fear of vast epidemics of cocaine or heroin addiction will be proven groundless. He also believes there will be increased interest in psychedelics, a possibility of great concern to the Establishment.

## The Nine-Point Proposal

A drug policy respectful of democratic values would aim to educate people to make informed choices based on their own needs and ideals. McKenna's master plan explores nine specific options for comprehensive reform.

First, a 200 percent federal tax on tobacco and alcohol, ending all government subsidies for tobacco production, strengthening packaging warnings, and levying a 20 percent federal sales tax on sugar and sugar substitutes with mandatory nutrition curricula.

Second, all forms of cannabis should be legalized with a 200 percent federal sales tax. Information about THC content and current health conclusions should be printed on all packaging, allowing informed consumer choice.

Third, International Monetary Fund and World Bank lending should be withdrawn from countries that produce hard drugs. Only international inspection and certification of compliance would restore loan eligibility, attacking the financial roots of production.

Fourth, strict gun control must apply to both manufacture and possession. It is the unrestricted availability of firearms that has made violent crime and the drug abuse problem so intertwined, creating a nexus of violence that enforcement alone cannot break.

Fifth, the legality of nature must be recognized, so that all plants are legal to grow and possess. This single provision would fundamentally transform the relationship between humans and the botanical world.

Sixth, psychedelic therapy should be made legal and insurance coverage extended to include it. Seventh, currency and banking regulations need strengthening to prevent large-scale money laundering by criminal cartels.

Eighth, there is an immediate need for massive support for scientific research into all aspects of substance use and abuse and an equally massive commitment to public education, replacing propaganda with honest information.

Ninth, one year after implementation, all drugs still illegal should be decriminalized. The government can sell at cost plus 200 percent, with monies placed in a special fund. Pardons should be given to all offenders whose cases did not involve firearms or felonious assault.

## Psychedelics in the Post-Legalization Scenario

What Trebach does not discuss is the role to be played by hallucinogens in the post-suppression scenario. Psychedelics seem unimportant if the only measure is street sales, but another measure of social importance argues otherwise.

A clue to that other measure is the interest the CIA and military intelligence gave to psychedelics during the sixties through projects such as MK and MK-ULTRA. The widespread belief that the conclusion was that television was preferable for mass hypnosis should not be taken at face value.

McKenna believes that once drugs are legalized, the fear of vast epidemics of cocaine or heroin addiction will be proven groundless. He also believes there will be increased interest in and use of psychedelics, and that this possibility is of great concern to the Establishment.

This new interest in psychedelics should be anticipated and provided for. If use of psychedelics makes it easier to recapture the social attitudes and assumptions of the original partnership cultures, then eventually educational institutions may wish to encourage this awareness.

## Help from Nature and the Archaic Tradition

Help from nature means recognizing that the satisfaction of the religious impulse comes not from ritual, and still less from dogma, but rather from a fundamental kind of experience, the experience of symbiosis with hallucinogenic plants and, through them, symbiosis with the whole of planetary life.

This radical proposal has been anticipated in the work of Arthur Koestler. Nature has let us down, God seems to have left the receiver off the hook, and time is running out. What we expect from pharmacological intervention is not eternal life but the transformation of homo maniacus into homo sapiens.

Koestler concluded that some form of pharmacological intervention will be necessary before we can be at peace with one another. He was apparently unaware of the shamanic tradition or of the richness of the psychedelic experience. Hence he was not aware that managing a global population into balance could involve introducing the experience of an internal horizon of transcendence.

## The Philosophical Foundation

If these proposals seem radical, it is only because we have drifted so far from the ideals that were originally most American. At the foundation of the American theory of social polity is the notion that our inalienable rights include life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.

To pretend that the right to the pursuit of happiness does not include the right to experiment with psychoactive plants and substances is to make an argument that is at best narrow and at worst ignorant and primitive.

The only religions that are anything more than the traditionally sanctioned moral codes are religions of trance, dance ecstasy, and intoxication by hallucinogens. The living fact of the mystery of being is there, and it is an inalienable religious right to approach it on one's own terms.

A civilized society would enshrine that principle in law. This is the ultimate expression of what McKenna calls the archaic revival: not a return to prehistoric conditions but a recovery of the primordial human right to direct experience of the transcendental.

## Legalization and Democratic Expansion

A new global consensus appears to be building. What was previously inchoate and unconscious is now becoming conscious and structured. The collapse of the Marxist alternative to media-dense consumerism has been swift and complete.

For the first time in planetary history, a defined, albeit dimly defined, consensus exists for democratic values. This trend will encounter resistance from various forms of monotheistic religious fundamentalism. It is a phenomenon of expanded consciousness driven by the information explosion.

Democracy is an articulation of the archaic notion of the nomadic egalitarian group. In its purest expression it is thoroughly psychedelic and its triumph seems ultimately certain to McKenna.

The drug problem runs against the tendency toward global expansion of consciousness. A society that sets out to control its citizens' use of drugs sets out on the slippery path to totalitarianism. No amount of police power can solve what is essentially a spiritual and pharmacological problem.

## From the Grasslands to the Stars

McKenna's epilogue ties the drug policy question to the largest possible frame. Human history has been a fifteen-thousand-year dash from the equilibrium of the African cradle to the twentieth-century apotheosis of delusion, devaluation, and mass death.

Now we stand on the brink of star flight, virtual reality technologies, and a revivified shamanism that heralds the abandonment of the monkey body and tribal group that has always been our context. The age of the imagination is dawning.

The shamanic plants and the worlds that they reveal are the worlds from which we imagine that we came long ago, worlds of light and power and beauty that lie behind the eschatological visions of all the world's great religions. We can claim this prodigal legacy only as quickly as we can remake our language and ourselves.

The vision he offers is not a return to some prehistorical golden age but rather a conscious integration of the archaic knowledge of plant hallucinogens into a modern, scientifically informed context. The goal is a society in which the human relationship to psychoactive plants is governed by knowledge, respect, and individual liberty rather than by prohibition, ignorance, and state control.

In such a society, the distinction between recreational and religious use would dissolve, as it has in indigenous cultures for millennia. The plant teachers would be available to all who seek them, within a framework of responsible use and community support.

This is McKenna's modest proposal: not a revolution but an evolution, a return to the relationship with nature that our species has always known.
