## Group 1: Psilocybin and Indole-Containing Fungi

The largest and most chemically characterized group consists of fungi containing psilocybin, psilocin, baeocystin, norbaeocystin, and related indole alkaloids. This group contains the vast majority of known neurotropic species and spans multiple genera across several fungal families. The indole alkaloids in these fungi are tryptamine derivatives biosynthesized via the tryptophan pathway.

### The Bluing Reaction

The blue bruising reaction is one of the most widely recognized field indicators of psilocybin-containing fungi. When the flesh of certain mushrooms is bruised, cut, or otherwise damaged, a distinctive blue to blue-green coloration develops within minutes. This reaction results from the enzymatic oxidation of psilocin and related [[amanita-muscaria]]: The iconic fly agaric, perhaps the most recognizable mushroom in the world. Red cap with white warts, widely distributed across temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere in association with birch and pine.
- Amanita pantherina: The panther cap, similar in appearance but generally more toxic.
- Amanita regalis: The brown fly agaric, a less common species.

Unlike the psilocybin-containing fungi, these Amanita species do not produce a bluing reaction. Their psychoactive effects are mediated through GABAergic rather than serotonergic pathways, reflecting the fundamentally different chemistry of ibotenic acid and muscimol compared to psilocybin.

### Cordyceps

Two species of Cordyceps have been reported to contain neurotropic compounds. These include C. capitata and C. ophioglossoides, both of which have documented use in traditional Mexican ceremonial contexts at highland sites.

## Group 4: Sacred Fungi Without Chemical Confirmation

The fourth group consists of fungi that are used in indigenous or traditional ceremonial contexts but for which chemical analysis has not definitively confirmed the presence of known psychoactive compounds. This group contains 20 species across several genera.

### Composition

- Boletes: 8 species across 2 genera
- Russula: 6 species
- Gasteromycetes: 6 species across 3 genera

These fungi are included in the neurotropic classification based on their documented ethnobotanical use rather than confirmed chemical profiles. Many may contain as-yet-unidentified active compounds, or their effects may be attributable to species misidentification in traditional use contexts. Further chemical investigation is needed.

## Problems with Chemical Studies

The reliability of published chemical analyses of neurotropic fungi has been questioned by multiple researchers. Several factors complicate the accurate determination of alkaloid content in these organisms. See taxonomic confusion neurotropic fungi misidentification for a detailed treatment of these issues.

### Misidentification

Many published chemical studies have been conducted on specimens that were later determined to be misidentified. The morphological similarity between neurotropic species and their non-active lookalikes creates significant risk of error, particularly when chemical analyses are performed by researchers without specialized taxonomic training in mycology.

### Age and Preservation of Specimens

The alkaloid content of neurotropic fungi changes significantly with the age of the specimen and the method of preservation. Active compounds can degrade over time, particularly in dried [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]]. Baeocystin, for example, has been shown to degrade within 20 to 60 days after collection, meaning that studies conducted on older herbarium material may significantly underestimate original alkaloid content in fresh specimens.
