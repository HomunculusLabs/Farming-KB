# Cold Frame Gardening

A cold frame is a simple, bottomless box with a transparent lid that captures solar energy to create a warm, protected microclimate for plants. It is the most basic and affordable season extension structure, providing 10-20°F of frost protection and extending the growing season by 4-8 weeks in both spring and fall. Cold frames are also indispensable for hardening off seedlings started indoors.

## How Cold Frames Work

Cold frames operate on the greenhouse effect:

1. Sunlight passes through the transparent lid and warms the soil and air inside
2. The lid traps the heat, preventing it from escaping
3. The insulated sides and bottom reduce heat loss
4. On cold nights, the captured solar heat radiates back to the plants, providing frost protection

### Temperature Performance

- **On a sunny 40°F day:** Interior temperature can reach 60-80°F
- **On a cloudy 30°F day:** Interior temperature may be only 35-45°F
- **At night:** Interior temperature is typically 5-15°F above outside temperature, depending on insulation, lid quality, and wind
- **With a blanket or row cover inside:** Additional 5-10°F of protection at night

## Design and Dimensions

### Standard Size

- **Width:** 3-4 feet — allows you to reach all plants from the outside without stepping inside
- **Length:** 4-8 feet — matches standard window sash dimensions and provides adequate growing space
- **Front height (south side):** 8-12 inches
- **Back height (north side):** 12-18 inches
- **Slope:** Angled toward the south to maximize solar capture

### Why the Slope?

The lid slopes from the higher back (north) to the lower front (south) to:

- Face the lid perpendicular to the low-angle winter sun (in the Northern Hemisphere)
- Allow rain to run off
- Provide more headroom at the back for taller plants

### Orientation

- **Face south:** The sloped lid should face true south (not magnetic south) for maximum solar gain
- **Slight eastward bias:** Some gardeners orient 10-15 degrees east of south so the cold frame warms earlier in the morning
- **Avoid shading:** Position away from buildings, trees, or fences that cast shadows during the day

## Materials

### Frame (Box)

- **Plywood or OSB:** Cheap and easy to cut. Exterior-grade plywood lasts 5-10 years. Paint or seal for durability.
- **2x6 or 2x8 lumber:** More durable than plywood. Cedar, redwood, or treated lumber for longest life.
- **Concrete blocks or bricks:** Very durable, good thermal mass. Heavy and not portable. Stack without mortar for a simple build.
- **Straw bales:** Arrange bales in a rectangle and place an old window or glass door on top. Very low cost, excellent insulation, but temporary and attracts rodents.
- **Earth bags:** Polypropylene bags filled with earth, stacked to form the walls. Low cost, good thermal mass.

### Lid (Glazing)

- **Old windows (single or double pane):** Free or very cheap from salvage yards, Habitat ReStore, or online marketplaces. Double-pane provides better insulation. Seal any broken panes.
- **Storm doors:** Larger, lighter, and often hinged already. Excellent for wider cold frames.
- **Polycarbonate panels:** Twin-wall or corrugated polycarbonate is lightweight, shatterproof, and has good insulation value. Best new material choice.
- **Acrylic sheet (Plexiglas):** Lightweight and shatterproof but expensive and scratches easily.
- **Polyethylene film:** Cheapest option. Staple to a wooden frame. Lasts 1-2 seasons.

### Hinges and Hardware

- **Hinges:** 2-3 exterior-grade hinges to attach the lid to the back of the frame
- **Prop mechanism:** A notched wooden prop stick, a chain, or a hydraulic arm (gas spring) to hold the lid open at various angles
- **Latch:** To secure the lid closed during high winds or when not in use
- **Automatic vent openers:** Wax-filled cylinders that automatically open the lid when the interior temperature reaches a set point (approximately 65-75°F). Essential if you are away during the day.

## Construction Steps (Wooden Cold Frame with Window Sash)

### 1. Build the Box

1. Cut two side pieces with angled tops (the front edge is 8-12 inches, the back edge is 12-18 inches, creating the slope)
2. Cut a front piece (8-12 inches tall) and a back piece (12-18 inches tall)
3. Assemble with exterior screws — pre-drill to prevent splitting
4. Add corner cleats (2x2 blocks) inside each corner for reinforcement
5. Cut a piece of exterior plywood for the bottom (optional — cold frames can sit directly on soil)
6. Seal or paint the exterior

### 2. Attach the Lid

1. Place the window sash or polycarbonate panel on top of the box, aligned with the back edge
2. Attach 2-3 hinges along the back edge
3. Test the swing — the lid should open smoothly to at least 45 degrees
4. Install a prop mechanism on the inside of the lid and frame

### 3. Position the Cold Frame

1. Choose a level, well-drained site with southern exposure
2. Dig a shallow trench (4-6 inches deep) and set the cold frame into it — this provides insulation from the ground and improves stability
3. Backfill around the outside of the frame and tamp firmly

### 4. Prepare the Interior Soil

1. Fill with a mix of garden soil and compost (6-12 inches deep)
2. Level the surface
3. Optionally, install a layer of hardware cloth on the bottom to exclude burrowing rodents

## Venting and Temperature Management

Ventilation is the most important cold frame management task:

- **Open the lid on sunny days:** Even in winter, a sunny cold frame can reach 80-100°F within an hour. Open the lid before you leave for work.
- **Close the lid by mid-afternoon:** Close the lid 2-3 hours before sunset to trap accumulated heat for the night.
- **Automatic vent openers:** Highly recommended. They open the lid when it gets too hot and close it when it cools down, eliminating the need for manual monitoring.
- **Monitor temperature:** Place a thermometer inside the cold frame. Most cool-season crops grow best at 45-65°F.
- **Extreme cold:** On very cold nights (below 20°F), throw an old blanket, quilt, or row cover over the lid for extra insulation.

## Crops for Cold Frames

### Spring Uses

- **Hardening off seedlings:** Gradually acclimate indoor-started seedlings to outdoor conditions over 7-10 days. Start with the lid partially open for a few hours, increasing exposure each day.
- **Direct sowing:** Sow cold-hardy crops 4-6 weeks before your last frost date: spinach, lettuce, radishes, peas, carrots, beets, Asian greens, kale, Swiss chard
- **Warm-season starts:** Start tomatoes, peppers, and squash 2-3 weeks early in the cold frame (with extra protection on cold nights)

### Fall and Winter Uses

- **Fall harvest extension:** Plant fall crops (lettuce, spinach, radishes, arugula, mache, claytonia) in September-October. Harvest through December or later depending on climate.
- **Overwintering:** Sow hardy crops (spinach, garlic, onion sets, mache) in late fall for very early spring harvest
- **Winter greens:** In mild climates (zone 7+), cold frames can produce greens year-round. In colder climates, add row cover inside for double protection.

### Planting Schedule (Zone 6 Example)

| Month | Action |
|-------|--------|
| February | Sow spinach, lettuce, Asian greens inside cold frame |
| March | Harden off indoor seedlings; direct sow radishes, peas |
| April | Transplant hardened seedlings to the garden; plant warm crops |
| May-August | Remove lid or use cold frame as a raised bed |
| September | Sow fall greens (lettuce, spinach, arugula, radishes) |
| October | Sow overwintering crops (spinach, mache, garlic) |
| November-December | Harvest fall crops; close lid at night |

## Maintenance

- **Clean the lid:** Dirty glazing reduces light transmission by 20-40%. Wash periodically with mild soap and water.
- **Check seals:** Replace weatherstripping around the lid if you notice gaps where heat escapes
- **Replace the covering:** Poly film lasts 1-2 seasons; polycarbonate lasts 10+ years; old windows may need re-puttying
- **Refresh the soil:** Add 1-2 inches of compost each spring before replanting
- **Pest control:** Check for slugs and snails — they love the humid cold frame environment. Set beer traps or hand-pick.
