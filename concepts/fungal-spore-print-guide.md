# Fungal Spore Print Guide

## Overview

A spore print is the pattern of spores deposited when a mushroom cap is placed gill- or pore-side down on a surface. Spore print color is one of the most important macroscopic identification characters for mushrooms, used since the time of Elias Magnus Fries in the early 19th century. This guide covers the technique, spore colors by genus, and how to use spore prints for identification. See [[mushroom-taxonomy-history]] for the historical context of spore-based classification.

## Making a Spore Print

### Materials

- Fresh mushroom specimen (mature but not decayed)
- White paper (half black, half white is ideal)
- Glass, bowl, or jar to cover the mushroom
- Clean, dry workspace

### Step-by-Step Technique

1. Select a mature mushroom with fully developed gills, pores, or teeth. Immature specimens may not release enough spores.
2. Cut the stem off close to the cap using a clean knife. For mushrooms with a ring (annulus), leave enough stem to support the cap.
3. Place the cap gill-side (or pore-side) down on your paper. If using half-white, half-black paper, center the cap on the boundary.
4. Cover with a glass, bowl, or jar to prevent air currents from scattering spores.
5. Leave undisturbed for 2-12 hours. Most mushrooms will produce a visible print in 4-8 hours.
6. Carefully lift the cap straight up. A pattern of deposited spores should be visible on the paper.

### Tips for Success

- Moisture helps: if the mushroom is very dry, place a drop of water on the cap
- Warmth encourages spore release: room temperature (18-22C) works well
- Use white paper for dark spore prints, black paper for light spore prints
- The pattern (radial lines from gills, dots from pores) can also be informative
- For very small mushrooms, use a microscope slide instead of paper
- Some mushrooms (like Cortinarius) drop rusty-colored spores that stain paper — use glass and observe from below

### Safety Note

Making a spore print from an unknown mushroom is safe — you are not consuming it. However, wash your hands afterward and keep the print away from food preparation areas.

## Spore Print Colors by Category

### White Spore Print

Common genera with white spores:
- **Amanita**: Including deadly species (A. phalloides, A. virosa) and the fly agaric (A. muscaria)
- **Lepiota**: Parasol mushrooms; some deadly (L. brunneoincarnata)
- **Tricholoma**: Many species including matsutake (T. matsutake)
- **Pleurotus**: Oyster mushrooms (P. ostreatus)
- **Volvariella**: Paddy straw mushroom (V. volvacea)
- **Marasmius**: Marasmius oreades (fairy ring mushroom)
- **Lycoperdon**: Puffballs (when mature and releasing spores)

### Pink / Salmon Spore Print

Common genera with pink or salmon spores:
- **Pluteus**: Pink spore print helps distinguish from white-spored lookalikes
- **Volvariella**: Has a volva (cup) at base, pink spores
- **Entoloma**: Also has pinkish spores, angular under microscope
- **Rhodocybe**: Pink to salmon spores

Note: Pink spore prints can fade over time, so observe immediately after lifting the cap.

### Brown Spore Print

The largest group of gilled mushrooms:
- **Psilocybe**: Psychoactive mushrooms (P. cubensis, P. semilanceata)
- **Agrocybe**: Field mushrooms, common in disturbed habitats
- **Panaeolus**: Banded mottlegill; some species contain psilocybin
- **Tubaria**: Small brown mushrooms
- **Conocybe**: Small, slender, cone-cap mushrooms
- **Galerina**: Some deadly species containing amatoxins (G. marginata)

### Dark Brown / Chocolate Brown Spore Print

- **Agaricus**: Cultivated button mushroom (A. bisporus), field mushrooms, meadow mushrooms
- **Stropharia**: Garden giant (S. rugosoannulata)
- **Hypholoma**: Sulfur tuft (H. fasciculare)
- **Kuehneromyces**: Brown wood mushrooms

### Black Spore Print

- **Coprinopsis / Coprinellus / Coprinus**: Ink caps, shaggy mane (C. comatus)
- **Panaeolus**: Some species with black spores
- **Psathyrella**: Common in woodchip mulch
- **Lacrymaria**: Weeping widow (L. lacrymabunda)

### Rusty / Cinnamon / Orange-Brown Spore Print

- **Cortinarius**: One of the largest genera; many species, most mycorrhizal
- **Gymnopilus**: Some species contain psilocybin
- **Pholiota**: Scaly caps, often on wood
- **Inocybe**: Many species are toxic (muscarine)
- **Paxillus**: Paxillus involutus (poisonous, causes autoimmune hemolysis)

### Buff / Pale Yellow Spore Print

- **Cantharellus**: Chanterelles
- **Craterellus**: Black trumpet, yellowfoot
- **Hygrophoropsis**: False chanterelle
- **Omphalina**: Some species

### Olive-Green to Olive-Brown Spore Print

- **Tricholomopsis**: Tricholomopsis rutilans
- **Some Stropharia species**: Notably S. aeruginosa

## Using Spore Prints for Identification

Spore print color is a first-level sorting character. It narrows identification to a group of genera but is rarely sufficient for species-level identification. Always combine spore print with:

1. **Habitat**: Wood, ground, grass, dung, specific tree associations
2. **Morphology**: Cap shape, gill attachment, stem features, presence of ring or volva
3. **Microscopic features**: Spore shape and size, presence of cheilocystidia, clamp connections
4. **Chemical tests**: KOH reaction on cap surface, iron salts, guaiac
5. **Bruising/staining**: Blue bruising (Psilocybe, some Boletes), color changes
6. **Odor**: Farinaceous (flour-like), anise-like, mealy, fruity
7. **Taste**: (Caution — spit out and do not swallow unknown species)
8. **DNA barcoding** (see [[fungal-dna-barcoding]]) for definitive identification

## Recording Spore Prints

Document your spore prints for future reference:
- Note the color on white and black backgrounds
- Photograph the print immediately
- Record the date, location, habitat, and associated tree species
- Save the paper print in a labeled envelope or plastic sleeve
- Cross-reference with field guide descriptions
