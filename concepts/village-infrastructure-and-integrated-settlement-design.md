# Village Infrastructure and Integrated Settlement Design

[[permaculture]] village infrastructure encompasses the physical tools, dwelling types, energy systems, and land-use patterns that enable a settlement to function as a self-reliant community. Mollison advocates for the integration of life, work, and recreation—rejecting the modern zoning paradigm that separates these [[plant-micronutrients-and-their-essential-functions]].

## Community Tools and Equipment

A village requires shared tools and equipment for the site at large. Rather than every household owning expensive, infrequently-used equipment, the community can maintain a shared inventory:

### Heavy Equipment
- **Tractor** with slasher (for fire control and land management)
- **Posthole borer** (for fencing)
- **Trailer** (general transport)
- **Chisel plough/soil conditioner** (for land preparation)
- **Chipper** (for biomass, mulch production, and fire control)
- **Tow truck** (for mulch and goods transport)
- **Van** (goods delivery)
- **Back-pack slasher** (for blackberry and brush control)

### Workshop Tools
Essential workshop tools can be made available on lease or timeshare:

- Drill press
- Lathe
- Radial-arm saw
- Welder
- Planer or thicknesser
- Router

Trust B can manage charges for these tools, accounting for replacement cost, wear and tear, fuel or power, and a service charge.

### Village Vehicle Leasing System

A modest tractor-tool-truck hire service, where Trust B leases infrequently-used assets to residents as needed, provides significant savings. Mollison recommends investigating a **total vehicle leasing system** including:

- Fleet purchase and insurance
- Local maintenance
- Bulk fuel supply

## Dwelling Diversity

Villages need a mix of dwelling types to accommodate different household sizes, ages, and needs:

| Type | Ownership | Size | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family homes | Individual or trust | 2–5 bedrooms | Traditional family households |
| Singles quarters | Strata title flats | 1–2 bedrooms | Single adults, some guest accommodation |
| Elderly/hospital quarters | Strata and community trust | Single/double | Aged care and medical needs |
| Terrace housing | Various | Variable | Higher-density living where appropriate |

A diverse housing mix provides far more for varying needs and age differences than the traditional single-family home model. Every village should maintain at least one **empty strata title** for emergencies such as family breakups, or to stage people into permanent housing.

## Energy-Efficient Building Design

Every house built today can approach 100% space heating efficiency through design alone, at no extra construction cost. Key elements include:

- **Solar hot water systems** as routine installations
- **Photovoltaic panels** approaching cost-effectiveness
- **[[passive-solar-design]]** for heating and cooling
- **Good insulation** and thermal mass

Some American towns (notably Davis, California) have enacted ordinances requiring buildings to comply with a 60% self-sufficient space heating requirement, and encourage all non-polluting home-based businesses with bikeway networks throughout the settled area.

## Integrated Settlement Design: Against Modern Zoning

Modern urban planning—designating city sectors as industrial, commercial, residential, or recreational—is, in Mollison's view, **the antithesis of good planning** for transport [[desert-house-water-energy-conservation]]. It bears little relationship to the natural zonation of function and available time around a settlement.

### The Case for Integration

Wherever possible, life, work, and recreation should be integrated in a dwelling. The benefits include:

- Better-informed households
- Less alienated children
- Less isolated adults with more social contact
- Elimination of complex transport systems

### Historical Precedents

Older cities that evolved in energy-conservative environments demonstrate integrated design:

- **Florence and Vienna**: Cultural, crafts, trades, commercial, and domestic functions were integrated
- **Berlin (older blocks)**: Housing over street-level shops on the sunny side; trades and work in accessible interior courtyards (Hofs); market or supply depot close by
- **Istanbul and India**: Trades grouped in streets or market areas where materials, assembly, and sale are all facilitated
- **Pre-1930 villages worldwide**: Almost every settlement that functioned before the fossil fuel era integrated multiple functions

### Benefits of Open Workshop Culture

Young people growing up in environments with accessible tradespeople develop a capacity to use many materials and make whatever they require, as a result of informal everyday association with the "open shops" of small tradespeople where neighbors and family come and go in the workplace.

## Land Allotment Categories

Land not attached to individual dwellings should be allotted according to these categories:

1. **Public buildings and services** — administrative, institutional
2. **Recreation** — trails, walks, sports grounds, parks
3. **Water storages and reserves** — dams, catchments, energy sites
4. **Productive land** — food: livestock, orchard, nursery, greenhouses
5. **Forested areas** — fuels and timbers
6. **Conservation reserves** — wildlife and flora habitat
7. **Easements and utilities** — wayleaves, roads, public utility allotments
8. **Commercial areas** — retail, manufacturing, offices, professional services, common markets

### The Household Microcosm

Family dwellings on 0.2–2 hectare lots can accommodate many of these functions in miniature. Many homes already function as commercial premises, store their own roof water, provide much of their food from gardens, and contain recreational assets. However, a larger site plan allows:

- More convivial access to shared land
- Commercial crop potential at scale
- Significant forests and ponds
- Properly planned access and utility easements

## Public Service Centres

Village-owned public access and service centres provide multiple benefits:

- **Part-time income** for a resident manager
- **Sharply reduced energy needs** compared to individual household provision
- **Wider district resource** availability

Examples include food processing centres, freezer facilities, and laundrette services. These not only reduce per-household energy requirements but also create employment and serve the surrounding district.

## Planning Priority Sequence

Mollison establishes a clear priority order for village planning:

1. **Water** supply and storage first
2. **Energy** systems second
3. **Access** (roads, paths) third
4. **Dwellings and structures** fourth
5. **Land use** indication fifth
6. **Legal, social, and financial systems** discussed last

This sequence reflects the [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]] of starting with foundational elements (water, energy) before proceeding to the [[artillery-fungus-and-built-environment-fungi]] and social organization.

## Village Energy Philosophy

Coupled with domestic energy conservation, modest power units can supply small villages or regions. Mollison advocates a **diverse energy approach** using wind, tide, river, solar, and methane where appropriate. The key criteria for evaluating any energy technology are:

- The process must be non-polluting
- The technology must be socially acceptable and locally benign
- The cost must be affordable and amortize within 10 years
- The technology must be locally producible and maintainable

Most importantly, no matter how efficient a technology may be, if it destroys the basic quality or quantity of soil, water, or clean air, it must be rejected—as this constitutes the **"economics of extinction"**.
