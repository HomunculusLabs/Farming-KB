# Mushroom Pests, Diseases, and Fumigation Protocols (Duggar)

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Benjamin Minge Duggar, *Mushroom Growing* (1915), Chapter X: [[mushroom-enemies]].

## Overview of Mushroom Pests

Duggar divided mushroom pests into two classes: insects and fungi. When conditions are favorable and surroundings are sufficiently sanitary, the grower is not likely to experience great difficulty. However, several common pests are invariably present in untreated compost and may at any time become a serious nuisance or cause of failure. For a modern organic approach to the same topic, see [[cotter-mushroom-pests-diseases]].

## Insect Pests

### Small Flies (Fungus Gnats)

**Species**: Several species, not readily distinguished except by specialists. Referenced in Circular 155, Bureau of Entomology, U.S. Department of Agriculture.

**Life cycle**: Adults deposit eggs at the base of mushroom stems. Larvae (maggots) pass up the stem and ultimately riddle the cap.

**Conditions favoring outbreaks**:
- Temperature decline after bed preparation (when flies multiply rapidly)
- Late summer spawning for early autumn production
- Any period when temperature is high enough for rapid reproduction

**Control**:
- Flies are practically inactive below 55°F — proper [[mushroom-growing-room-environmental-control]] is the primary defense
- If eggs are deposited while compost temperature is still high, flies will emerge but little damage is done because the crop is not yet fruiting
- **Tobacco/nicotine fumigation** — adult flies are readily killed; use amounts commonly recommended for greenhouses
- **Carbon bisulphide** — 2 to 4 pounds per 1,000 cubic feet of space; expensive, considered a last resort
- **Sulphur or hydrocyanic acid fumigation** — use before spawning, not during cropping

### Mites (Tyroglyphus lintneri)

**Description**: Minute arthropods found invariably in manure.

**Damage**: Feed upon both mushrooms and [[mushroom-spawn]]. In large numbers, they may entirely prevent mushroom production or even spawn growth. Considered "one of the undoubted sources of failure when conditions are unfavorable."

**Conditions favoring outbreaks**:
- Much time consumed in compost and bed preparation
- High temperatures during preparation
- Delayed spawning

**Control challenges**:
- Duggar tried many fumigation methods without finding any practical means of eradication
- Even hydrocyanic acid gas at four times normal strength killed only the majority — sufficient survivors remained
- Steam sterilization of manure would kill all insects and fungi but does not simplify the overall problem (see [[mushroom-nutrition-manure-fermentation]] for compost preparation)

**Control recommendations**:
- Carry all compost preparation processes through promptly
- Maintain favorable growing conditions (lower temperatures suppress mite activity)
- Clean the mushroom house thoroughly before starting
- **Meat bait method**: Place scraps of cooked meat on beds, collect after mites gather on them, drop into boiling water, return to bed; repeat as needed

### Springtails

**Description**: Tiny gray-black creatures that hop actively when disturbed, sometimes appearing in colonies so dense that affected areas seem "literally alive."

**Damage**: Attack mushrooms primarily through the gill region or gain entrance through injuries. A "flush" of mushrooms may be completely destroyed in two days.

**Conditions favoring outbreaks**:
- Particularly numerous in damp caves
- Where sanitation rules are grossly violated
- Present in manure but normally controlled by proper treatment

**Control**:
- **Ventilate thoroughly** — reduce excess moisture
- **Dust beds and floors with quick lime**
- **Pyrethrum powder** — serviceable if conditions are not too wet
- **Lysol spray** — 2 to 2.5% solution effective but causes some injury to growing mushrooms

### Sowbugs (Woodlice)

**Description**: Common gray crustaceans of the woods and greenhouse, about half-inch long, elliptical.

**Damage**: Eat into mushrooms like snails or mice; a relatively small number may destroy many pounds of buttons.

**Control**:
- **Hand-picking** — practical for small infestations
- **Paris green bait** — smeared over slices of raw potato distributed about beds; sowbugs eat the poisoned potato
- **Lysol spray** on their favorite haunts

## Fungal Diseases

### Mycogone Disease (La Méle)

**Pathogen**: *Mycogone perniciosa* (provisional name). Possesses two known spore stages. Grows upon both mushrooms and spawn. For help identifying this and other fungal diseases, see [[mushroom-contaminant-identification-key]].

**History**: Known in France for several generations. Extensively studied by Costantin and Dufour (France) and Veihmeyer (United States).

**Types of symptoms**:
1. **Deformed type** — mushrooms somewhat deformed but maintain general form. Cap, stipe, and gills may show enlargements or tubercles. Gills show flaky surface and reduced depth. May be coated with mold-like growth (not always visible in mild form). Grades into the second type through increasingly deformed specimens.

2. **Puffball type** — stem greatly enlarged (irregularly at times), cap poorly or not at all developed. Mushrooms are soft in texture, frequently decay before reaching half the size of a normal button. Giant forms of these abnormal structures also occur.

**Economic impact**: Estimated loss in France in 1897 was about $200,000 annually. Duggar found specimens with mild forms offered for sale on most larger American markets.

**Persistence**: Spores remain alive under unfavorable conditions for more than one year, probably several years under favorable conditions.

**Spread**: Quickly spread by:
- Gathering mushrooms
- Implements used around beds
- Currents of air
- Contact with diseased material

**Control**:
- **Lysol spray** — 2.5% solution on affected beds or areas (recommended by Costantin and Dufour)
- **Sanitary fluids** — dips or sprays used in poultry work are similar in nature and may be employed
- **Complete house fumigation** after the crop ceases bearing — formaldehyde vapor is most effective
- Remove all diseased mushrooms promptly and do not permit them to remain on beds

## Fumigation Protocols

### Formaldehyde Fumigation (Preferred for Routine Disinfection)

**Purpose**: Destruction of Mycogone disease spores and insects between crops.

**Timing**: Shortly before new beds go in. All remains of previous crop must be removed.

**Preparation**:
1. Thoroughly clean and sweep the house
2. Spray with water one or two days in advance to ensure moist conditions
3. Select damp weather if possible
4. Make the house as air-tight as possible (paste paper over cracks)

**Dosage per 1,000 cubic feet**:
- 3 pints commercial formalin (40% formaldehyde)
- 1.5 pounds permanganate of potash

**Procedure**:
- Use tubs or large earthenware containers (houses over 50 feet long need two or more)
- Pour formaldehyde into containers
- Weigh permanganate into paper bags placed beside containers
- Begin farthest from the exit
- Drop permanganate bags into formalin and leave the building quickly
- Vigorous boiling action results — use sufficiently large vessels to prevent overflow

**Post-fumigation**:
- Keep house closed for at least 24 hours
- Open all windows, ventilators, and doors from outside
- Let house air and dry out
- WARNING: Keep lighted lamps away — liquid formalin is non-explosive but the gas in confined state is explosive
