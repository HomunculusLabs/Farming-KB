# Hugelkultur Raised Beds

Hugelkultur is a method of burying old wood in the ground under a garden bed.
It is a very old strategy that patterns the exact processes of the forest and
lends itself very well to land that has been cleared of trees for farming.
Hugelkultur returns massive amounts of organic material to the soil, retains
huge quantities of water for growing plants, and over time breaks down to
leave air pockets that provide necessary oxygen to plant roots. The technique
is a key element of and
design.

## Wood Selection

Certain types of wood may not be suitable. Treated wood, cedar, and black
locust do not rot very well. Woods naturally toxic to plants, like black
walnut, are probably not conducive to growing a garden. You can use fresh
wood, but it will use nitrogen to decompose, robbing it from the soil and
locking it up. Rotten wood will not do this, and if it is very well rotted,
it may actually release nitrogen. Wood that has been laying around for a
couple of years is probably the best material. This includes any kind of
wood material like brush, tree stumps and roots, and general debris.

## Bed Construction

The most difficult part is moving the wood and arranging it into the shape
you want. It is much easier and faster to use a tractor. If you are working
with straight pole logs, a straight bed is easier to make. If you are working
with brush, curved beds work well. The wood does not have to be very high,
but over time the whole thing will settle as air pockets fill with soil and
the organic material breaks down. Because of this, the taller your bed is in
the beginning, the better. The wood can be a couple of feet tall (almost 0.5
meters), and the soil and mulch can be a couple of feet more. Some people
have made beds 6 feet tall (1.8 meters).

## Steps to Building a Hugelkultur Bed

### Step 1: Remove Sod
Remove a strip of sod the size of your bed. This sod will be used as a mulch
layer later.

### Step 2: Lay Down Wood Layer
Lay down your wood layer at least a couple of feet deep. Mix different sizes
and types of wood for the best results.

### Step 3: Add Organic Layers
Sandwich several layers of mulch and soil, including leaves, grass clippings,
compost, old straw, and moldy hay. Take the layer of sod removed in step one,
flip it upside down and lay it down on top. Add another layer of soil over
it. You can use rocks to line the side of the bed so that it does not erode.

### Step 4: Plant a Cover Crop
The first time you use the bed, it is recommended to grow a cover crop such
as clover. Clover fixes nitrogen and makes it available for other plants. If
you plant it in [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]], you will have time to plant [[growing-root-vegetables-guide]]
next: potatoes, carrots, radishes, and similar crops.

## Connection to Forest Ecology

Hugelkultur mimics the natural forest floor, where fallen trees decompose
and feed new growth. This connects directly to principles, as the buried wood creates conditions that
allow later successional plants to thrive. The technique aligns with the

hugelkultur beds become self-sustaining growing systems that improve with

## Shape and Design Variations

The shape of hugelkultur beds can vary widely depending on available
materials and site conditions. Straight beds work well with uniform pole
logs, while brush and branches lend themselves to curved or freeform
shapes. Some builders create keyhole designs for easy access, while others
build long mounds along contour lines to capture runoff water. The beds can
be any length but should be no more than 5 to 6 feet wide so that the
center can be reached from either side without stepping on the bed.

## What to Grow

After the initial cover crop of clover, hugelkultur beds are excellent for
root vegetables like potatoes, carrots, and radishes. The loose, well-
draining soil created by the decomposing wood is ideal for root crops that
need deep, loose soil. In subsequent years, the beds can support a full
range of vegetables, herbs, and even small [[holzer-soft-fruit-bushes-and-berries]]. The moisture-
retaining properties make them particularly valuable in dry climates or
during drought conditions where consistent watering is difficult.
