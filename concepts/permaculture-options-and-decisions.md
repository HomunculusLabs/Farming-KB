# Permaculture Design Options and Decisions

Design as a selection of options and pathways based on decisions is the method that most directly addresses the real-world constraints faced by any landholder. For a specific site and specific occupants, a design is a sequence of options shaped by products, crops, social investment, available skills, processing opportunities, market access, and management capacity. This approach acknowledges that any design has many potential outcomes, and that it is above all the stated aims, lifestyle, and resources of the clients that determine which options are pursued.

## The Nature of Design Options

Every [[permaculture-ethics]] of earth care, people care, and fair share serve as the primary filter through which all options are evaluated. An ethic such as "care of the earth" may lead directly to specific design decisions: erosion control, minimal tillage, and revegetation of steep slopes become firmly indicated for a specific site in light of this guiding principle.

Ethics do not determine every detail, but they establish the boundaries within which design options are considered. Options that violate core ethics are eliminated early, narrowing the field of viable pathways and reducing decision fatigue.

## Cascading Decisions and Nested Options

A key insight of the options approach is that each decision opens a new set of possibilities while closing others. An option, once decided on, indicates further options, priorities, and management decisions. The cascading structure looks like this:

1. **Ethical foundation** → establishes broad direction (e.g., "care of the earth")
2. **First-order decisions** → specific strategies (e.g., "control erosion on steep slopes")
3. **Second-order decisions** → implementation choices (e.g., "swales versus terraces")
4. **Third-order decisions** → species selection, timing, management
5. **Ongoing evolution** → adjustments based on observation and experience

In practical terms, economic considerations intersect at every level. The designer may need to generate short-term or long-term income, which may depend on whether the occupants maintain a part-time non-farm income or commit fully to the land-based enterprise. Each financial decision opens and closes different pathways.

## The Value of Multiple Open Pathways

Following through the options that arise from either decisions or the constraints of site and resources reveals an apparently endless series of pathways. The process itself is inevitable, leading to innovative and practical procedural pathways that agree with the ethical, financial, cultural, and ground constraints of the site and its occupants.

As a bonus, not one or two but several dozen options may remain open at any given time. Mollison considers this a secure position: in an uncertain world, we need all possible doors open. The options approach explicitly preserves flexibility, ensuring that the design does not prematurely commit to pathways that may prove unworkable.

## The Danger of Over-Commitment

While having many options is valuable, it is wise to implement a limited range of options for trial. Taking on too many projects simultaneously incurs stress and work that can overwhelm the occupants and undermine the entire system. The practical advice is to start with a manageable subset of options, observe results, and then expand the design based on actual experience rather than theoretical promise.
