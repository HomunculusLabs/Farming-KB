# Phytochrome Photoreceptor

Phytochrome is a photoreceptor pigment protein found in plants that
enables them to detect red and far-red light. Discovered through the
photoperiodism research of the 1940s and 1950s, phytochrome acts as a
molecular switch that controls numerous developmental processes
including seed germination, [[shade-avoidance-syndrome]], flowering time, and
circadian rhythm entrainment. It is one of the most important sensory
molecules in plant biology and plays a direct role in [[cannabis-flowering-cycles]]
regulation.

## Discovery and the Red/Far-Red Reversibility

The discovery of phytochrome emerged from investigations into
photoperiodism. In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and his colleagues
at the US Department of Agriculture made a breakthrough: red light (~660
nm) applied during the dark period could induce flowering in long-day
plants or suppress flowering in short-day plants. Far-red light (~730
nm) applied immediately afterward could completely reverse this effect.

This red/far-red reversibility operated as a molecular toggle — just
seconds of exposure to either wavelength were sufficient. The sequence
was indefinitely repeatable: red-on, far-red-off, red-on again. By the
time John F. Kennedy was elected president, Warren L. Butler and
colleagues had demonstrated that a single photoreceptor — named
phytochrome, from Greek "phyton" (plant) and "chroma" (color) — was
responsible for both effects.

## Molecular Structure and Photoconversion

Phytochrome is a dimeric protein consisting of two identical subunits.
Each subunit contains a covalently bound chromophore called
phytochromobilin, a linear tetrapyrrole molecule related to heme and
bile pigments. The chromophore absorbs light while the apoprotein
provides the structural framework and downstream signaling.

The protein exists in two photoconvertible forms. **Pr (phytochrome red-
absorbing form)** is the inactive state that absorbs red light maximally
at ~660 nm. Pr is synthesized in the dark and is the default state of
newly produced phytochrome molecules. **Pfr (phytochrome far-red-
absorbing form)** is the biologically active form that absorbs far-red
light maximally at ~730 nm. Pfr is produced when Pr absorbs red light.

When Pr absorbs a photon of red light, the chromophore undergoes a
conformational change — a Z-to-E isomerization around the C15=C16 double
bond — converting the protein to Pfr. When Pfr absorbs far-red light,
the reverse isomerization occurs. This light-driven molecular switch is
one of the most elegant sensory mechanisms in biology.

## Biological Significance in Nature

In natural environments, the red/far-red switching of phytochrome serves
as a highly sensitive environmental detector. During daylight, abundant
red light drives conversion to the active Pfr form. As the sun sets, the
spectral shift toward far-red converts phytochrome to inactive Pr.

This daily cycle serves multiple [[fungal-melanin-biosynthesis-ecological-functions]]. **Daily
timekeeping:** The Pr/Pfr ratio at any moment tells the plant how long
ago it last experienced red light, helping measure the dark period for
photoperiodic flowering control. **[[phytochrome-red-far-red-ratio-shade-detection-plants]]:** Chlorophyll in
overhead leaves absorbs red while transmitting far-red. A shaded plant
experiences reduced red-to-far-red ratio, triggering shade avoidance —
stem elongation and upward leaf movement to escape canopy shade. **Seed
germination:** Many seeds require red light to germinate; far-red
filtered light from deep soil indicates excessive burial depth. **De-
etiolation:** Light-grown seedlings switch from etiolated (pale,
elongated) to normal photomorphogenic development.

## The Phytochrome Gene Family

Arabidopsis has five phytochrome genes (PHYA through PHYE), each with
distinct functions. **Phytochrome A (phyA)** mediates responses to
continuous far-red light and very low fluence rates. It is light-labile,
degrading rapidly in the Pfr form, and is the dominant phytochrome in
dark-grown seedlings. **Phytochrome B (phyB)** is the most abundant
phytochrome in light-grown plants, mediating red/far-red reversible
responses including flowering time, shade avoidance, and seed
germination. PhyB is relatively light-stable and is the critical
phytochrome for photoperiodic flowering. **Phytochromes C, D, and E**
have specialized roles in fine-tuning light responses, with phyE working
alongside phyB in shade avoidance.

## Downstream Signaling Mechanisms

Upon conversion to active Pfr, phytochrome initiates complex signaling
cascades that alter gene expression. Pfr translocates from cytoplasm to
nucleus where it interacts with transcription factors and signaling
proteins. **PIF proteins** (Phytochrome Interacting Factors) are bHLH
transcription factors that promote dark-growth development. Pfr binds
PIF proteins and promotes their degradation, removing repression on
photomorphogenic genes. **COP1/SPA complex** is an E3 ubiquitin ligase
that targets positive regulators of photomorphogenesis for degradation
in darkness. Light-activated phytochrome inhibits COP1, stabilizing
these regulators and promoting light-adapted development.

## Role in Cannabis Flowering

For [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-cannabis-cultivation]], phytochrome B is the most relevant family
member. During vegetative growth under 18/6, long daily light periods
maintain phytochrome predominantly in the active Pfr form, suppressing
flowering genes such as FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT). Switching to 12/12
allows extended dark periods with more complete Pfr-to-Pr reversion.
Reduced Pfr levels relieve FT suppression, allowing the flowering
program to initiate.

This has practical implications: even low-intensity red light during the
dark period — from indicator LEDs, light leaks, or streetlights — can
convert enough Pr to Pfr to disrupt the flowering signal. Far-red light
alone during darkness is less disruptive, leading some researchers to
explore far-red treatments to enhance flowering without disrupting
photoperiodic measurement.
