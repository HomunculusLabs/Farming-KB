# Vegetable Nutritional Decline and Trace Minerals

One of Steve Solomon's most compelling arguments in "Gardening When It
Counts" is that modern vegetables are significantly less nutritious than
those grown by earlier generations. This page covers his analysis of why
nutrition has declined and what gardeners can do about it through
[[solomon-complete-organic-fertilizer-guide]]
and proper soil management.

## The Evidence for Declining Nutrition

Data compiled by the USDA over many decades, and published in Life
Extension Magazine, show that the vitamin and mineral content of
vegetables has steadily decreased. Studies indicate that the nutritional
value of common vegetables has declined by roughly 25 percent or more over
the past several decades. This decline affects all vitamins and minerals,
but is particularly pronounced for trace minerals.

This is not an abstract concern. When Solomon says that modern hybrids
and even open-pollinated varieties are not as nutritious as the old
varieties our grandparents grew, he is not making an anti-hybrid
ideological argument. He is pointing to a measurable decline in the
mineral and vitamin content of our food supply.

## Historical Context: How Varieties Evolved

For thousands of years, each family raised seed for its own unique
varieties, selected over generations to suit the family's needs, tastes,
and local soil. The nutritional quality of each variety was different.
When two varieties were grown on highly fertile soil, one would probably be
more nutritious than the other.

In the era before antibiotics and modern sanitation, children had to run a
gauntlet of childhood diseases. Many died before reaching breeding age.
Some families lost most or all of their children; others rarely lost any.
The family with better vegetable (and cereal) varieties tended to have
children who survived to carry on those varieties. Less-nutritious
varieties tended to disappear. Over thousands of years, vegetable
varieties evolved toward producing good health in those who ate them.

One indicator of possessing a high level of nutrition is good taste;
humans naturally select for flavor. This is why old-fashioned varieties
often taste better -- flavor is a proxy for nutritional completeness.

## The Industrial Era Changes

By about 1870, Europe and the United States had gone through the
industrial revolution. More than 90 percent of people in North America
lived on self-sufficient farms or in small villages where every house had
a half-acre veggie garden. After 1870, increasing numbers of people moved
to cities. Vegetable varieties used for human food were no longer selected
for nutritional quality; the farmer now grew marketable produce, and the
seed trade began selecting for shipping quality, uniformity, and yield
rather than nutrition.

It is generally true that modern hybrids are not as nutritious as
vegetables grown from old-fashioned OP varieties. But it is also true that
most OP varieties developed since 1870 are not as nutritious either. And
much of what passes for "heirloom" OP varieties these days is really just
commercial varieties used before World War II or relatively modern
varieties that have been misnamed by unethical seed growers producing
cheap garden seed.

## The Historical Process of Soil Exhaustion

When English-speaking peoples first settled their lands, they cleared the
forest and burned the duff -- the forest's capital accumulation of
centuries. Abundant harvests followed. But without steady nutrient
addition from falling leaves, with nutrients exported as crops went to
market, and without animal manure from a complete farm ecosystem, soil
fertility decreased. In this industrial era, exhausted land has been
temporarily restored by chemical fertilizers. Were it not for them, most
croplands would be considered worn out. Almost any soil a vegetable
gardener uses these days may be too worn to grow most kinds of vegetables
without amendment. See [[solomon-soil-mineral-depletion]].

## The Trace Mineral Solution

Solomon's [[solomon-complete-organic-fertilizer-guide]] (COF) addresses trace mineral depletion through kelp meal:

- Kelp is gathered from oceans where eroded continental minerals have
  accumulated over millennia.
- Kelp meal provides a broad spectrum of trace elements: iodine, iron,
  zinc, manganese, copper, boron, molybdenum, and more.
- One part kelp meal in the COF recipe supplies these in plant-available
  form.

Other COF ingredients contribute too: rock phosphate and bonemeal provide
calcium, phosphorus, and trace elements; lime provides calcium and
magnesium; seed meals supply the major nutrients (NPK).

## Foliar Feeding with Kelp

Instead of putting dry kelp meal into COF, you can spray liquid kelp on
leaves (foliar feeding). Trace minerals are absorbed directly through the
leaves, bypassing soil chemistry issues. Commercial preparations exist, or
you can make your own. See
[[solomon-liquid-fertilizers-and-foliar-feeding]].

## Calcium, Magnesium, and pH

Vegetables need calcium and magnesium in balance. The lime in COF provides
both. Solomon's approach: forget about pH, focus on balanced nutrition
through COF. Over time, soil pH corrects itself as organic matter increases.
See [[solomon-lime-calcium-ph-management]].

## The Complete Picture

Solomon connects several threads:

1. Old varieties were more nutritious because they were selected over
   centuries for both flavor and health.
2. Modern varieties (both hybrid and OP) have been selected for market
   characteristics, not nutrition.
3. Even old varieties grown in depleted soil will produce less nutritious
   vegetables.
4. Chemical fertilizers restore yield but not the full spectrum of
   minerals.
5. COF with kelp meal provides the broadest possible mineral nutrition.
6. Compost and organic matter maintain soil biological activity that
   makes minerals available to plants.

## What Gardeners Can Do

- Choose varieties known for flavor and nutritional quality, not just
