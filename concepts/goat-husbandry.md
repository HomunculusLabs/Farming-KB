# Goat Husbandry

Goats are among the most versatile and manageable livestock for the homestead.
They provide milk, meat, fiber, and brush-clearing services while requiring
relatively modest inputs compared to cattle. Their curiosity and intelligence
make them both rewarding and occasionally challenging to keep.

## Breed Selection

### Dairy Breeds
- **Nigerian Dwarf** — Compact, high butterfat milk (6-10%), ideal for small
  homesteads. Produce 1-2 quarts daily. Excellent browsers, easy to handle.
- **Nubian** — Large, floppy-eared, high butterfat (4-5%). Loud and vocal.
  Good for cheese-making due to rich milk.
- **Alpine, LaMancha, Oberhasli, Saanen, Toggenburg** — Standard-sized dairy
  breeds producing 1-3 gallons daily with lower butterfat (3-4%).

### Meat Breeds
- **Boer** — Fast-growing, muscular, docile. The standard meat goat.
- **Kiko** — Hardier, parasite-resistant, excellent mothers. Lower maintenance
  than Boers.
- **Spanish** — Resilient, good for brush clearing and crossbreeding.

### Fiber Breeds
- **Angora** — Produces mohair, sheared twice yearly. Requires more grooming
  and shelter.
- **Pygora** — Cross between Pygmy and Angora, smaller, easier to manage.

### Dual-Purpose
- **Kinder** — Nubian x Pygmy cross, good milk and meat on a small frame.

## Housing

Goats detest rain and wind. Dry shelter is non-negotiable.

- Minimum 15-20 sq ft per goat indoors, plus 30+ sq ft outdoor per animal.
- Bed with deep straw or wood shavings; clean weekly, add carbon to compost.
- Ventilation is critical — ammonia buildup causes respiratory illness. Avoid
  drafts at animal level but allow air exchange at the roofline.
- Provide sturdy, level sleeping platforms. Goats prefer to be off the ground.
- Bucks need separate housing during off-season to prevent unwanted breedings
  and buck odor from tainting milk.

## Fencing

Goats are escape artists. Fencing must be both physical and psychological
barrier.

- Woven wire (4x4 inch mesh, 4-5 ft tall) with a strand of electric at the top
  and bottom is the standard homestead setup.
- Electric fencing alone works if trained properly with a strong energizer
  (6+ joules for perimeter, 1+ joule for portable). Goats must be introduced
  to electric fence in a contained area first.
- Corner posts should be wood or steel; line posts can be fiberglass or T-posts.
- Goats will rub, lean, and test every weak point. Inspect regularly.
- See [[livestock-fencing-guide]] for detailed fencing specifications.

## Feeding

Goats are browsers, not grazers. They prefer brush, weeds, leaves, and woody
plants over grass.

### Daily Ration
- Good-quality hay (grass or grass-legume mix) — 2-4% of body weight daily.
- Avoid straight alfalfa for dry does and bucks (too rich, risks urinary
  calculi). Good for milking does and growing kids.
- Loose goat mineral formulated with ammonium chloride (prevents calculi) —
  always available free-choice.
- Fresh water at all times — goats won't drink dirty water.

### Pasture and Browse
- Rotate through paddocks to break parasite cycles. See
  [[pasture-management]].
- Multiflora rose, blackberry, poison ivy, and thistle are all excellent goat
  forage.
- Supplementary grain only for lactating does (1 lb per 3 lbs of milk
  produced) and growing kids.

### Toxic Plants
- Rhododendron, azalea, yew, oleander, cherry (wilted leaves), nightshade,
  and Johnsongrass can be fatal. Fence access carefully.

## Health Care

### Parasite Management
- Internal parasites (especially Haemonchus contortus — barber pole worm) are
  the #1 goat health challenge.
- Use FAMACHA scoring (check inner eyelid color) to identify anemic animals
  needing treatment. Only deworm animals that score 4-5.
- Rotate pastures; avoid overstocking. See [[pasture-management]].
- Manage with copper oxide wire particles (COWP), herbal dewormers (wormwood,
  garlic), and selective breeding for resistance.

### Hoof Trimming
- Trim every 6-8 weeks. Overgrown hooves cause lameness, arthritis, and
  bacterial infection.
- Use sharp hoof shears. Trim to the level of the frog.

### Vaccinations
- CD&T (Clostridium perfringens types C & D + tetanus) — core vaccine.
  Give to does 4 weeks pre-kidding, kids at 6-8 weeks with booster at 10-12
  weeks.
- Consider caseous lymphadenitis (CL) and pneumonia vaccines based on local
  risk.

### Common Issues
- Ketosis in late-pregnancy does — offer propylene glycol or molasses water.
- Mastitis — check udders daily during lactation. Strip and observe milk.
- Scours (diarrhea) in kids — often coccidiosis; treat with sulfa drugs or
  corrid. See [[homestead-poultry-health]] for broader biosecurity principles.

## Breeding

- Does reach puberty at 4-7 months but should not be bred until 75-80% of
  mature weight (usually 8-10 months for standard breeds, 12+ for miniatures).
- Breeding season: August-January (daylength-triggered). Nigerian Dwarfs may
  breed year-round.
- Gestation: ~150 days (5 months). Plan kidding for mild weather.
- Buck-to-doe ratio: 1 buck per 20-30 does for hand-breeding; 1:40-50 for
  pasture breeding.
- Signs of heat: tail flagging, vocalization, swollen vulva, mounting
  behavior. Heat cycle: every 18-21 days, lasts 12-36 hours.

## Milking

- Milk does twice daily (12-hour intervals) or once daily after kids are weaned
  (accept lower production).
- Strip out first few squirts (highest bacterial load) onto the ground.
- Milk into a sanitized bucket or milking machine. Cool milk to 38°F within
  1 hour.
- Filter milk through a milk filter into glass jars. Store at 35-38°F.
- Goat milk naturally homogenizes — cream doesn't separate easily. Use a
  separator for cream and butter.
- Average lactation: 305 days. Dry off the doe 60 days before expected kidding.

## Kidding

- Move doe to a clean kidding pen 1-2 weeks before due date.
- Signs of impending kidding: ligaments around tailhead soften, udder fills,
  discharge, restlessness, pawing.
- Stages of labor: (1) cervical dilation 0-12 hours, (2) active labor with
  pushing 30-60 minutes, (3) placenta delivery within 4 hours.
- Clear membranes from kid's nose immediately. Dip navel in 7% iodine.
- Ensure kid colostrum within first 2 hours — critical for immunity.
- Bottle raise or dam raise depending on management goals. Bottle raising
  produces friendlier, more manageable goats.

## Manure and Byproducts

- Goat manure is "cold" — can go directly on gardens without composting,
  though composting is preferred.
- Average goat produces ~1 ton of manure per year. Rich in nitrogen and
  potassium.
- Goat hides can be tanned for leather. See [[animal-harvest-and-processing]].
- Buck horns (if left intact) and bones are useful for bone broth and
  [[animal-byproducts]].
