## The Forest as a Water Lake

If we imagine the visible (above-ground) forest as water
(and all but about 5-10% of this mass is water), and then
imagine the water contained in soil, humus, and root
material, the forests represent great lakes of actively
managed and actively recycled water. No other storage
system is so beneficial, or results in so much useful
growth.

The amount of soil water is about fifteen times the amount
in channel storage in rivers. This explains why rivers flow
throughout the year even though precipitation is an unusual
event in most areas of the earth. At Paris, it rains for
only 577 hours a year, or about 7% of the time, yet rivers
flow continuously.

## Percolation and Groundwater Recharge

The soil becomes an impediment to water movement, and the
free (interstitial) water can take as long as 1-40 years
to percolate through to streams. This slow movement greatly
alleviates droughts, and also recharges the retention
storages on the way. Thus, it almost seems as though the
purpose of the forests is to give soil time and means to
hold fresh water on land.

At any time, trees may intercept and draw on these
underground reserves for growth, and pump the water again
to air. This continuous cycling means that forests create
more water in streams than the rainfall alone provides, as
condensation from leaf surfaces adds to total precipitation.

## Factors Affecting Soil Water Storage

### Soil Texture
Fine-textured soils with high clay fraction store more
water in retention storage. Coarse sands drain rapidly and
hold less water. The ideal is a loamy soil with balanced
mineral and organic components.
