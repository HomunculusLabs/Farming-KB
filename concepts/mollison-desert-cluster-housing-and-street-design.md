## Traditional Desert House Features

- Cool courtyards in building interiors, narrow and tall to preserve
  shade
- Evaporation strategies from water in tunnels, unglazed pots, tanks,
  fountains, bark mulch, and coke or hessian "wicks" for water
  evaporation
- Narrow east-west streets maximised; broad north-south streets
  minimised to reduce heat gain
- White-painted massive walls (often of mud) as cool surfaces
- Small windows or stone grilles, opening from inner courtyards
- Towers, vanes, and airscoops for ventilation systems
- Cooking outdoors under shade trellis
- Earth sheltered or underground housing of various types
- Vines on walls, over roof areas, gardens, and storehouses
- Roof areas creatively used for drying crops, washing clothes, and
  pigeon lofts

## The Colony of Swallows Nests

Houses can be constructed so that they assist other houses. The
"colony of swallows nests" appearance of many settlements in arid
areas is not a coincidence, as the strategy has independently arisen in
Asia, India, the Middle East, in the Mediterranean, and in the
Americas. Close-clustered dwellings with the long axis of their streets
running east-west and common or close-spaced walls ensures that
neither wind nor heat can easily penetrate the fabric of the settlement.

## Multi-Storey Cooling Dynamics

If all clustered dwellings are sun-facing and of more than one storey
in height, cool air in shaded narrow "wells" is always available in
courtyards and streets. Vents at roof level draw cool air into the
rooms of the dwellings through stack effect ventilation. The bottom
floors and flat roof areas are used for living, while the upper floors
or roof areas serve as bedrooms where night breezes provide comfort.

## Street Orientation Principles

- Dense housing should be closely placed side by side along an
  east-west alignment
- Few, if any, north-south cross streets should be aligned to hot
  winds
- Narrow streets are preferable, ideally overshaded by trees or
  two-to-three-storey buildings
- Multiple storeys are appropriate in restricted sites such as wadi
  banks, niches at the base of cliffs, and small valley sites
- Single storey housing is appropriate on more open sites but then
  needs dense vegetation or trellis on the shade side

## The Village Homes Model

Where cultures are accustomed to more space and privacy, a line of
bungalows or cabins on an east-west axis with dense strips of
vegetation to the shade side can achieve much the same result as
traditional clustering, providing the site itself is correctly
landscaped. Village Homes in Davis, California, designed by Michael
Corbett, is cited by Mollison as an ideal model for such design in
desert and semi-arid climates. See
[[mollison-underground-cave-housing]] for
construction details.

## Practical Considerations

Successful implementation of Desert Cluster Housing and Street Design requires attention to
several practical factors including environmental conditions,
resource availability, and timing. Careful monitoring and
adaptive management help optimize outcomes across varying
conditions. Integration with other system elements enhances
overall effectiveness and creates beneficial synergies that
improve resilience and productivity over time.
