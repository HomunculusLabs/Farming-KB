# Design Report Structure

A professional [[permaculture-design]] report is a comprehensive document that translates [[permaculture-site-analysis]] and client needs into specific, implementable design recommendations. The report serves as the blueprint for transforming a site according to [[permaculture-principles]].

## Overview

An Elfin [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] report includes the design proper, a reference section or bibliography, a resource section, and appendices. Reports for simple designs run 15-20 pages with numerous small pictures and details, but larger designs can be much longer.

## Report Methodology

1. Collect all data: client requirements, maps, local regulations
2. Analyze the client's problems
3. Recognize those [[williams-pdc-additional-permaculture-principles]] that fit this particular situation
4. Apply permaculture principles specifically to the problems involved
5. Design solutions [[allegro-death-and-resurrection-in-the-mushroom-cult]] form of edges, patterning, and increased functional relationships
6. Check everything over to make sure the solution adequately meets all client needs and addresses all problems

## Report Structure

### [[tihkal-introduction-and-methodology]] Site Description
Begin with a brief general description [[allegro-star-of-the-morning-venus-and-the-sacred-mushroom]] site and its placement [[allegro-plants-and-drugs-in-the-ancient-world]] region. Include:
- Orientation of the site
- Existing vegetation
- Existing [[water-management]]
- Soils

Then draw a main map with the site broken into identifiable areas to be treated in detail later.

### Client, Goals, and Resources
- Building resources and constraints
- Energy resources
- Water and sanitation situation
- Site-specific resources and constraints

### Community Context
Description of the surrounding community and how the design relates to it.

### Climate
Detailed climate data for the site and its implications for the design.

### Design Goals
The specific goals established through the client interview process.

### Food, Crops, and Nutrient Cycles
Food production is almost always part of the design. Typically includes:
- Tree crops and unconventional gardening methods
- Solar greenhouses for domestic food production
- Aquaculture, bees, small stock, poultry, and foraging animals
- Soil management
- Disposition of human wastes
- Forage systems development
- Food preservation
- Pest control strategies
- Commercial food production (if applicable)

### Energy
Evaluate all relevant energy options:
- Solar, wind, water power, biomass, and biological energy
- Applications: transportation, space heating/cooling, cooking, hot water, food preservation, equipment operation
- Conservation measures
- Specific proposals for utilizing available energy

### Water
With fresh water availability in drastic decline worldwide, this is paramount:
- Roof catchment systems
- Ponds for gravity-fed storage
- Greywater treatment and reuse
- Contaminated water treatment
- Erosion control and flood management
- Follow the same pattern as energy: resources first, then needs

### Shelter
For existing housing:
- Retrofit for energy efficiency
- Food production integration
- Increased living space quality
- Housing for plants and animals

For new buildings:
- Local material recommendations
- Construction methods from site resources
- Cost and energy savings compared to conventional approaches
- Shelterbelt plantings and shade design

### Hazards and Problems
Anticipate and protect against:
- Weather extremes
- Earthquake, tsunami, tidal wave
- Fire
- Pollution
- Human violence
- Dangerous client activities (e.g., use of toxic substances)

### Special Treatments
Client-specific goals requiring dedicated sections, such as:
- Wetlands management strategies
- Specific crop or livestock systems
- Unique site features

### Economics
- Design implementation costs
- Income development strategies
- Ways to pay for implementation as you go
- Leveraging client skills and site resources

### Staging
Specify the sequence for implementing design recommendations:
- How long each step should take
- Use one aspect of the design to prepare for the next
- Generate resources to fund subsequent phases
- Avoid confusion of implementing everything at once
- Lifestyle changes undertaken in manageable steps

### Appendices
- Species and variety lists
- Bibliography
- Resource people and organizations
- Supplier lists
- Climatic data
- Technical data
- Permaculture Client Design Survey
- Standard designs for common problems

## Standard Designs

You can include standard designs for situations common enough that a general solution is more useful than a fully customized approach. Standard designs can deal with architectural themes, landscaping, and agricultural design. They are a modest income supplement for designers and avoid reinventing the wheel each time a new designer tackles a common problem.

## Plant and Resource Lists

### Plant List
Documented plant list for the design. Be careful to avoid plants illegal in the region or that could become rampant. Initially, build a personal filing system for plants and animals with information about each.

### Resource List
- People in the area who can help the client
- Other clients whose properties can be visited
- Hardware resources, tools, and technologies needed
- Suppliers of plants and products not commonly available
- Establish people as suppliers of materials for future design jobs
