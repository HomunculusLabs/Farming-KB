# Permaculture Species Compendium T–Z

This compendium covers a selection of plant species catalogued in [[bill-mollison]]'s *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, spanning the taxonomic range from **Tamarix** through **Zostera**. These species represent a cross-section of functional roles within permaculture systems — from nitrogen-fixing legumes and multi-purpose trees to water-purifying reeds and staple food crops.

## Multi-Purpose Trees

### Teak (*Tectona grandis*)
Teak is a premier timber tree of tropical permaculture systems. Native to South and Southeast Asia, it produces one of the most durable and weather-resistant woods in the world. In permaculture design, teak is integrated into agroforestry systems where it provides long-term timber yield while its canopy moderates microclimate for understory crops. Teak leaf litter contributes significantly to soil humus formation. However, teak is slow-growing and can be allelopathic, so careful placement relative to food crops is essential. It thrives in humid tropical zones with well-drained soils and is often used in sequential planting designs where initial short-cycle crops give way to maturing timber.

### Cocoa (*Theobroma cacao*)
Cacao is a classic understory tree of humid tropical permaculture. Naturally adapted to grow beneath the canopy of taller trees, cocoa exemplifies multi-strata food forest design. In a well-designed system, cocoa grows beneath shade trees such as Inga, Gliricidia, or banana, producing pods for chocolate while benefiting from the microclimate created by the overstory. Cocoa requires consistent moisture, good drainage, and protection from wind — making it well-suited to valley-bottom planting in humid landscapes. The husks from harvested pods can be returned as mulch, closing nutrient loops within the system.

### Grape (*Vitis vinifera*)
Grapes are versatile perennials suited to temperate permaculture systems. They serve as both a food crop and a functional element in landscape design. Trained on pergolas, grapevines provide summer shade to buildings or outdoor living spaces while allowing winter sun penetration when deciduous. Grapes can be integrated into food forest edges, grown along swale embankments, or used as ground covers on embankments where they help stabilize soil. Their deep root systems can access subsoil moisture and minerals, bringing nutrients to the surface through leaf fall.

## Nitrogen-Fixing Legumes

### Tephrosia (*Tephrosia candida*)
Tephrosia is a fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing shrub native to tropical and subtropical regions. It serves multiple functions in permaculture systems: as a green manure crop, a pioneer species for degraded land restoration, and a source of organic mulch. Tephrosia contains rotenone, a natural insecticide, making its leaves useful as a botanical pest control when applied as a mulch or tea. It establishes readily on poor soils and helps build soil fertility for subsequent planting of more demanding crops. In tropical systems, tephrosia is often intercropped with cereals or used in alley farming systems.

### Clover (*Trifolium* spp.)
Clover species — including white clover (*T. repens*), Kenya white clover (*T. semipilosum*), and red clover (*T. pratense*) — are among the most important living mulch plants in temperate permaculture. White clover is commonly used as a ground cover beneath fruit trees, fixing atmospheric nitrogen (40–130 kg/ha/year) while suppressing weeds and providing grazing for poultry or livestock. Clover's shallow root system helps maintain soil structure without competing aggressively with deeper-rooted tree crops. Its flowers are excellent bee forage, supporting pollinator populations within the system.

### Fenugreek (*Trigonella foenum-graecum*)
Fenugreek is an annual legume valued both as a culinary herb and as a green manure. [[mollison-guinea-pig-integration-in-permaculture-gardens]], it serves as a catch crop, fixing nitrogen during short fallow periods. Its leaves and seeds are rich in nutrients and used extensively in cooking and traditional medicine. Fenugreek grows quickly, making it useful as a nursery crop that can be cut and dropped as mulch before planting main crops. It thrives in well-drained soils and moderate climates.

### Vetch (*Vicia* spp.) and Fava Bean (*Vicia faba*)
Vetch species are vigorous cool-season climbing legumes used extensively as [[cover-crops]]. Hairy vetch (*Vicia villosa*) is one of the best nitrogen-fixing cover crops for temperate climates, capable of fixing over 100 kg/ha of nitrogen. Fava beans (broad beans) serve a dual role as a food crop and a soil builder. Both can be used in no-till systems: the living plant is rolled flat at maturity to create a mulch mat into which subsequent crops are direct-seeded. Vetch is also excellent bee forage, producing abundant nectar in early spring.

### Mung Bean (*Vigna radiata*) and Cowpea (*Vigna sinensis*)
These warm-season legumes are staples of tropical and subtropical permaculture gardens. Mung beans mature quickly (60–90 days), making them ideal for [[query-what-is-succession-planting-and-how-do-i-plan-a-succession-planting-schedule]] intercropping. Cowpeas are extremely drought-tolerant once established and serve as both a food crop and a cover crop. Both fix substantial nitrogen and produce edible biomass that can be fed to livestock or composted. Cowpea leaves are also harvested as a nutritious green vegetable in many traditional systems.

## Soil Stabilization and Remediation

### Vetiver Grass (*Vetiveria zizanoides*)
Vetiver is one of the most important soil conservation plants in permaculture. Its extraordinarily deep root system (reported to reach 3–4 meters) makes it supremely effective at stabilizing slopes, preventing erosion, and trapping sediment. Planted as a dense hedge along contours, vetiver slows runoff, allows water infiltration, and gradually builds terraced earth behind its hedge. Vetiver is also used in [[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]] for wastewater treatment, as its roots can absorb heavy metals and excess nutrients. The roots produce vetiver oil, used in perfumery, providing a potential cash crop alongside its ecological functions.

### Nettle (*Urtica dioica*)
Nettle is often dismissed as a weed but is highly valued in permaculture for its multiple functions. It is a dynamic nutrient accumulator — its deep roots mine minerals (iron, calcium, magnesium, nitrogen) from subsoil layers. Nettle leaves make an exceptional liquid fertilizer (nettle tea) when steeped in water, providing a nitrogen-rich feed for garden crops. Nettle supports over 40 species of insect, including many beneficial predators. Young nettle shoots are edible and highly nutritious, rich in vitamins A and C, iron, and protein. In compost systems, nettle accelerates decomposition through its nitrogen content.

### Gorse (*Ulex europaeus*)
Gorse is a pioneer nitrogen-fixing shrub of poor, acidic soils. While it can become invasive in some contexts, in permaculture it is managed for its beneficial functions: nitrogen fixation, windbreak potential, wildlife habitat, and as a source of biomass for mulch or fuel. Gorse flowers are excellent bee forage, providing early-season nectar. Its thorny branches make effective stock-proof barriers. In succession-based designs, gorse serves as a nurse crop, preparing ground for more productive species that follow.

## Water and Wetland Species

### Reedmace / Cattail (*Typha* spp.)
Cattails are among the most productive and useful wetland plants in permaculture. They purify water by absorbing excess nutrients and heavy metals through their extensive rhizome systems, making them essential components of [[constructed-wetlands-wastewater-treatment]] greywater treatment systems. Nearly every part of the plant is useful: young shoots are edible (tasting like asparagus), pollen is used as flour, rhizomes are starchy and can be processed into food, and the mature stalks provide thatching material or can be woven into baskets. Cattails also provide habitat for waterfowl and amphibians.

### Seagrass (*Zostera* spp.)
Seagrasses are marine angiosperms that form critical underwater meadows in coastal permaculture and restoration contexts. They stabilize seabed sediments, sequester carbon at rates exceeding terrestrial forests (up to 35 times faster per hectare), provide nursery habitat for commercially important fish and shellfish species, and improve water clarity. In permaculture coastal zone management, protecting and restoring seagrass beds is essential for maintaining the productivity and resilience of marine ecosystems.

## Staple Food Crops

### Wheat (*Triticum aestivum*)
Wheat remains one of humanity's most important staple grains. In permaculture systems, wheat is grown as part of diverse polycultures rather than monocultures. It can be integrated into pasture-cropping systems where grain is harvested from among perennial grasses, or grown in rotation with leguminous green manures. The straw is a valuable mulch material and animal bedding that eventually returns organic matter to the soil. [[heritage-and-landrace-crop-varieties-for-small-farmers]] wheat varieties are preferred in permaculture for their adaptability, nutritional quality, and ability to be saved as seed year after year.

### Corn / Maize (*Zea mays*)
Maize is a versatile warm-season cereal used extensively in permaculture systems, particularly in the traditional Three Sisters guild (corn, beans, squash). The tall corn stalks provide a trellis for climbing beans, which fix nitrogen for the corn, while the sprawling squash suppresses weeds and conserves soil moisture. Maize can also be grown in agroforestry systems as an understory crop during the establishment phase of tree plantings. Its stalks provide carbon-rich mulch or animal fodder after harvest.

## Herbs and Smaller Perennials

### Thyme (*Thymus vulgaris*)
Thyme is a low-growing Mediterranean herb valued in permaculture as a ground cover, culinary herb, and beneficial insect attractor. Its aromatic foliage deters certain pests, and its flowers are highly attractive to pollinators and predatory insects. Thyme thrives in well-drained, sunny positions and is an excellent choice for herb spirals, rock gardens, and pathway edges. It is drought-tolerant once established and requires minimal maintenance.

### Violet (*Viola odorata*)
Sweet violet is a shade-tolerant perennial herb suited to the understory layers of food forests. It naturalizes readily in moist, partially shaded positions and provides early spring nectar for emerging pollinators. Violet leaves and flowers are edible, used in salads, teas, and traditional confections. Its spreading habit makes it a useful living mulch beneath taller plants.

### Kangaroo Grass (*Themeda australis*)
A native Australian perennial grass, kangaroo grass is a climax species in temperate grassland ecosystems. In permaculture, it is used in pasture systems and forland rehabilitation. It provides high-quality forage when actively growing, builds soil organic matter through its deep fibrous root system, and supports diverse insect and bird populations. As a warm-season perennial, it pairs well with cool-season grasses to provide year-round ground cover.

### Dandelion (*Taraxacum officinale*)
Often dismissed as a weed, dandelion is a dynamic nutrient accumulator with deep taproots that mine calcium, potassium, and other minerals from subsoil layers. Every part is edible: young leaves in salads, flowers for wine and fritters, and roots as a coffee substitute. Dandelion is one of the earliest blooming flowers in spring, providing critical nectar for bees and other pollinators when few other sources are available. In permaculture design, dandelion is welcomed rather than eradicated.

### Wild Rice (*Zizania lacustris*)
Wild rice is an aquatic grass native to North America, growing in shallow lakes and slow-moving streams. It provides high-protein grain for human consumption while serving critical ecological functions: filtering water, providing habitat for waterfowl and fish, and stabilizing shorelines. In permaculture aquaculture systems, wild rice can be integrated into pond edges and constructed wetlands, combining food production with water purification.

### Ginger (*Zingiber officinale*)
Ginger is a tropical rhizomatous herb widely cultivated in [[nut-tree-cultivation-for-permaculture-food-forests]]. It thrives in the partial shade of taller trees, making it ideal for the understory layer. Ginger requires rich, well-drained soils with consistent moisture. In polyculture systems, it pairs well with turmeric, cardamom, and shade-tolerant food crops. The rhizomes are harvested annually, and the leafy tops can be used as mulch.

## The Permaculture Definition

These species find their purpose within the broader framework that Mollison defined as follows:

> *Permaculture (permanent agriculture) is the conscious design and maintenance of agriculturally productive ecosystems which have the diversity, stability, and resilience of natural ecosystems. It is the harmonious integration of landscape and people providing their food, energy, shelter, and other material and non-material needs in a sustainable way. Without permanent agriculture there is no possibility of a stable social order.*

Each species listed above serves not as an isolated element but as a functional component within designed ecosystems. The selection of species in permaculture is guided by their multiple functions — nitrogen fixation, soil building, pest management, food production, water purification, habitat creation — and their ability to form beneficial connections with other elements in the system.
