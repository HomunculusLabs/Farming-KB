# Cannabis Pest Identification Guide

Early detection is the key to managing cannabis pests. By the time you see obvious damage, populations may already be established. Regular inspection — especially the undersides of leaves — should be part of your routine. This guide covers the most common cannabis pests, identification signs, and treatment approaches.

## General Prevention Strategies

Before addressing specific pests, establish these baseline practices:

- **Quarantine new plants**: Isolate any new clones or plants for 7-14 days before introducing them to your grow space.
- **Inspect regularly**: Check leaf undersides, stems, and soil surface at least twice per week using a loupe or magnifying glass.
- **Maintain environmental control**: Proper temperature, humidity, and airflow reduce pest pressure. Many pests thrive in stagnant, humid conditions.
- **Keep the space clean**: Remove dead plant material, sanitize tools and surfaces between grows.
- **Biological controls**: Beneficial insects and predatory mites prevent outbreaks before they start.

## Spider Mites (Tetranychus urticae)

The most common and destructive cannabis pest. Tiny arachnids (not true insects) that feed on plant sap.

**Identification**:
- Extremely small (0.5mm) — barely visible to the naked eye
- Look like tiny moving dots on leaf undersides
- Fine webbing between leaves and stems (the telltale sign)
- First damage appears as tiny yellow dots (stippling) on leaves
- Severe infestation causes leaves to yellow, bronze, and die
- In heavy infestations, webbing covers entire plants

**Life cycle**: Eggs hatch in 2-3 days; adults reproduce rapidly in warm conditions. A single female can produce hundreds of eggs in her 2-4 week lifespan. Populations can explode in warm, dry conditions (70-80°F, low humidity).

**Treatment**:
- **Mild**: Spray with neem oil (azadirachtin), insecticidal soap, or rosemary oil. Treat every 3-5 days for 2-3 weeks to break the life cycle.
- **Moderate**: Introduce predatory mites (Phytoseiulus persimilis or Amblyseius californicus). These are the most effective biological control.
- **Severe**: If the plant is in late flowering and infestation is heavy, consider early harvest rather than spraying. Discard heavily infested plants.

## Thrips (Order Thysanoptera)

Small, slender insects that rasp leaf surfaces and suck out the cell contents.

**Identification**:
- Tiny (1-2mm), elongated bodies — yellow, brown, or black
- Adults fly when disturbed (look like tiny slivers)
- Silver or bronze streaking on leaf surfaces (feeding damage)
- Tiny dark frass (droppings) on leaves
- Deformed new growth, curled leaves
- Often found in flowers during late flowering — they're attracted to white/pale colors

**Life cycle**: 2-3 weeks from egg to adult. Larvae and adults both feed on the plant.

**Treatment**:
- Sticky traps (blue cards attract thrips specifically)
- Beneficial insects: Orius (minute pirate bugs), Amblyseius cucumeris mites
- Spinosad-based sprays (effective on larvae)
- Neem oil or insecticidal soap for mild infestations

## Fungus Gnats (Bradysia spp.)

Small dark flies whose larvae damage root systems. More common in soil and coco grows than hydroponic systems.

**Identification**:
- Small (2-3mm) dark grey/black flies that fly around the soil surface
- Adults are a nuisance but don't directly damage plants
- Larvae (tiny white/translucent worms in the soil) feed on roots and organic matter
- Symptoms mimic overwatering: wilting, slow growth, yellowing leaves
- Visible larvae in root zone or on the soil surface

**Life cycle**: Larvae develop in moist soil over 2-3 weeks. Adults live 7-10 days and lay eggs in wet medium.

**Treatment**:
- **Cultural**: Allow top inch of soil to dry between waterings. Larvae need moisture to survive.
- **Sticky traps**: Yellow sticky traps placed at soil level capture adults and reduce egg-laying.
- **Biological**: Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis (BTi) drench — sold as Mosquito Bits or Gnatrol. Harmless to plants and beneficial organisms.
- **Beneficial nematodes**: Steinernema feltiae applied to soil prey on fungus gnat larvae.
- **Hydrogen peroxide**: A 1:4 dilution of 3% H2O2 watered into soil kills larvae on contact.

## Aphids (Family Aphididae)

Soft-bodied insects that cluster on new growth and undersides of leaves, sucking plant sap.

**Identification**:
- Small (1-3mm), pear-shaped, soft bodies
- Colors vary: green, black, yellow, brown, or pink
- Often found in dense colonies on new growth tips and leaf undersides
- Leave behind honeydew (sticky residue) which promotes sooty mold growth
- Curled, distorted new growth
- Visible shedding of white exoskeletons during molting

**Life cycle**: Aphids reproduce rapidly — many species give birth to live young (nymphs) without mating. Populations can double every few days in ideal conditions.

**Treatment**:
- **Mild**: Strong spray of water to dislodge them. Insecticidal soap or neem oil.
- **Moderate**: Ladybugs (Hippodamia convergens) are voracious aphid predators. Release 1,000-2,000 per 1,000 sq ft.
- **Severe**: Spinosad or pyrethrin-based sprays. Systemic insecticides (imidacloprid) are effective but should never be used during flowering.

## Whiteflies (Family Aleyrodidae)

Small white flying insects related to aphids and mealybugs.

**Identification**:
- Tiny (1-2mm) white moth-like insects that fly up in clouds when plants are disturbed
- Eggs and nymphs (immature stages) on leaf undersides — look like tiny white scale
- Yellowing leaves, especially lower foliage
- Honeydew and sooty mold on leaf surfaces
- Sticky traps near plants capture many adults

**Life cycle**: Eggs hatch in 5-7 days; nymphs feed on leaf undersides for 2-3 weeks before becoming adults.

**Treatment**:
- Yellow sticky traps for monitoring and population reduction
- Encarsia formosa (parasitic wasp) is highly effective as a biological control
- Neem oil or insecticidal soap sprays targeting leaf undersides
- For severe infestations: Spinosad or pyrethrin sprays

## Root Aphids (Pemphigus, Phorodon)

Similar to above-ground aphids but live on and damage root systems. Particularly insidious because they're hidden below the soil surface.

**Identification**:
- Plants show general decline: wilting, yellowing, stunted growth
- Resembles nutrient deficiency or overwatering
- Upon inspection of roots: small white, yellowish, or brownish aphids clustering on root systems
- Roots may appear swollen or distorted
- Ants may be present (they farm root aphids)
- Often confused with fungus gnat larvae — root aphids are more oval-shaped and attached to roots

**Life cycle**: Reproduce both sexually and asexually. Winged adults can migrate to new plants.

**Treatment**:
- **Difficult to control** — root aphids are among the hardest cannabis pests to eradicate.
- BotaniGard (Beauveria bassiana) soil drench — entomopathogenic fungus that infects and kills aphids.
- Systemic insecticides are most effective but are not safe for consumable flower crops.
- Beneficial nematodes (Steinernema feltiae) can help reduce populations.
- Azadirachtin (neem) soil drenches repeated weekly.
- In severe cases, removing and destroying the entire plant may be necessary to prevent spread.

## Quick Identification Reference

| Pest | Where Found | Key Sign | Treatment Priority |
|------|-----------|----------|-------------------|
| Spider mites | Leaf undersides | Webbing, stippling | High — reproduces fast |
| Thrips | Leaves, flowers | Silver streaks, frass | Moderate |
| Fungus gnats | Soil surface | Flying adults, root damage | Low-moderate |
| Aphids | New growth tips | Colonies, honeydew | Moderate |
| Whiteflies | Leaf undersides | Cloud of white flies | Moderate |
| Root aphids | Root zone | Decline, ants | High — hard to eradicate |
