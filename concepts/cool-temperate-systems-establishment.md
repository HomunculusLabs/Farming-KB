# Cool Temperate Systems Establishment

Establishing productive permaculture systems in cool temperate climates requires specific strategies for dealing with frost, wind, weed competition, and short growing seasons. The approach emphasizes using pioneer and nurse species, protective structures, and intensive small-scale management to build resilient food-producing landscapes.

## Pioneer Species and Succession Management

### Gorse (Ulex europaeus) as a Nurse Plant
In New Zealand, gorse—often considered a problematic invasive weed—can be used as a productive nurse crop for establishing forest systems:

1. **Initial Phase (Years 1-2):** On the 280-hectare Collingwood River property, rampant gorse is rolled down or slashed. Eucalyptus, pines, and acacia are planted into the cleared areas. The "Zone 1" area close to settlement is fully cut and planted with pioneer trees. Tomato cuttings can be planted directly into the slash.

2. **Intermediate Phase (Years 2-3):** Remaining gorse is slashed, and mature rainforest species are slit-seeded into the gorse roots. After 5-6 years, the gorse has rotted completely, and rainforest establishes under acacia canopy (which is in turn felled). Native birds carry in rainforest seed, accelerating natural succession.

3. **Gorse as Living Mulch:** Dick Roberts in Nelson, New Zealand, uses gorse as "rough mulch" to protect fruit and forage trees in steep sheep pasture. Reduction of competing weed species protects the succeeding crop. Tree crops, when thickly planted, eventually shade out the gorse.

### Tree Protection Strategies
In areas with browsing pressure, protective strategies are essential:

- **Fallen Tree Nurseries:** Near desert inselbergs in Central Australia, fallen trees provide protection from feral animals for young trees. Food trees can be established within the branch structure.
- **Thorn Mulch:** In Mahalapye, Botswana, fierce thorny mulch from Acacia tortilis protects newly planted trees from intense browsing pressure in compounds.
- **Blackberry as Protection:** At Kiewa, Victoria, Geoff Wallace observed free-seeded apples thriving in blackberry patches. Cattle demolish the brambles to reach the apples. Planting apples within brambles (a traditional English practice) provides natural protection, and the brambles are eventually trodden down by cattle.

## Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus palmensis)

Tagasaste is one of the most valuable multi-purpose trees for cool temperate systems:

### Frost Protection
Avocado trees in hard frost areas thrive when planted within clumps of tagasaste. The tagasaste canopy provides significant frost protection and wind shelter.

### Forage Production
- **Banks Peninsula, New Zealand:** Planted at 1-meter spacing within rows and 2 meters between rows, tagasaste provides summer forage from coppice and short-period browsing by sheep. Grasses thrive between the rows.
- **Canterbury Plain, New Zealand:** Tagasaste is drilled with turnip for future sheep forage. Seedling trees establish successfully through the brassica crop at Matthew Carpenter's property.

### Mulch Production
Tagasaste yields up to 7 meters of mulch trimmings per annum at Jim and Miriam Tyler's farm, providing abundant organic material for soil building and weed suppression.

## Windbreak Systems

### Willow Coppice
Willow coppice near Christchurch, New Zealand, serves multiple functions:
- Basketry material and medicinals
- "Stickwood" for radiant (mass) heaters
- Active charcoal filters and artist's charcoal
- "Willow water" (fresh chips soaked in water) provides gibberellic acid for promoting root growth in cuttings

### Matsudana Willow
This willow variety provides a strict, columnar windbreak for kiwifruit vine crops on the plains near Nelson, New Zealand. Its narrow form minimizes ground space loss and requires little maintenance to control shape.

### Pampas Grass
Pampas grass serves as both fast shelter for lambing ewes and preferred forage for most livestock. It propagates easily by divisions and combines well with tagasaste strips in shelterbelt design.

## Small-Scale Intensive Growing

### Keyhole Beds
Tomatoes grown in keyhole beds sheltered by Jerusalem artichoke (sunroot) windbreaks succeed on cold and windswept sites at Stanley, Tasmania. The Jerusalem artichokes are grown in old tires for easy establishment.

### Herb Spirals
A herb spiral in the form of a ziggurat provides ample culinary herbs at the kitchen door. The spiral design creates multiple microclimates—drainage and aspect variations suit most cool-area herb species. A spiral 2 meters across and 1 meter high can support dozens of herb varieties in minimal space.

## Small Livestock Integration

### Rabbit Cage Tractors
At Forest, Tasmania, rabbit cage tractors clean clover and grasses from mounded strawberry crops. Rabbits are part of the yield of the weed management system and provide manure for the strawberries.

### Chicken Tractors
At Lake Chelan, Okanogan, USA, chicken tractors manage grasses for small fruit crops. Chickens weed small fruits over the autumn period and provide manure for soil fertility.

### Poultry Forage Systems
- Turkey forage beneath autumn olive (Elaeagnus umbellata) provides abundant berries.
- Siberian pea shrub (Caragana arborescens) and autumn olive are important tree forage legumes for cool and cold climates.
- Lycium ferocissimum hedges carry vines of hops and banana passionfruit while sheltering main potato crops. Their berries are loved by poultry, and the sharp thorny shrub provides ideal shelter (used historically to keep out lions in Africa).

### Chicken-Heated Greenhouses
At Aspen, Colorado, chicken-heated greenhouses combined with solar-heated seedling cloches enable early planting in short-season, high-mountain areas. The body heat from chickens provides supplemental warmth, reducing or eliminating the need for external heating.

## Jean Pain Composting Method

At Tumbarumba, NSW, Neil Dauce and Jason Alexandra reduce forest fire fuels using the Jean Pain method. Wood chips are mulched, composted, and fermented to produce methane. Mechanical removal of forest fuel reduces fire risk and enriches soils, providing an alternative to "cool burns" which impoverish soils and often lead to wildfire.

## Integrated Design Principles

Successful cool temperate establishment follows these principles:

1. **Use pioneer species aggressively:** Gorse, tagasaste, and other vigorous growers create protected microclimates for establishment.
2. **Integrate livestock from the start:** Tractor systems provide fertility, weed control, and additional yields during establishment.
3. **Maximize edge and shelter:** Windbreaks, living fences, and multi-strata plantings create favorable microclimates.
4. **Start small and intensive:** It is better to thoroughly manage a quarter hectare than to scatter plantings over 2 hectares.
5. **Design for succession:** Plan for pioneer species to be replaced by productive climax species over time.
