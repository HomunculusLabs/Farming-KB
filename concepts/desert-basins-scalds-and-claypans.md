# Desert Basins, Scalds, and Claypans

## Overview

The flat areas and depressions of desert landscapes — including tectonic basins, deflection hollows, pans, scalds, and claypans — present unique challenges and opportunities for [[permaculture-design]]. These low-lying features are where water, salts, and silts accumulate, creating distinct micro-environments that can be either rehabilitated or further degraded depending on management approaches.

## Basin Types

### Tectonic Basins
On the large scale, tectonic basins are the most significant flat areas in deserts. These are large, near-circular depressions formed by geological processes. It is in these great depressions that salt lakes and dune seas (draas) form. Smaller depressions gather run-off and develop typical base materials of suspended silts or salts carried by water and left on the pans when water evaporates.

### Deflection (Wind) Hollows
Next in order of scale are fairly large deflection hollows created by wind patterns. These depressions with flat floors accumulate materials blown and washed in from surrounding areas.

### Pans
Depressions with flat floors are called pans. Two main types exist:

- **Claypans**: Clay-based evaporation areas where clay settles out after silty waters pool
- **Salt pans**: Salt-based evaporation areas where salts concentrate

## Clay Management

Clays can be treated in specific ways depending on the design objective:

- **Gypsum**: Decreases clay capacity to infiltrate water (useful for directing run-off)
- **Sodium carbonate or bentonite**: Seals clays where they are to be used to store water
- **Natural montmorillonite or smectite clays**: Swell and form impermeable surfaces after a short period of rain, greatly impeding infiltration

Some small depressions called **gilgais** form as a result of the swelling and shrinking of plastic clays. These are circular in outline and are useful water-capture systems where erosion has left them as hollows. However, once sand-filled, they cease to function as water capture systems.

## Desert Soils

Soils in older deserts usually show some yellow or reddish colour from a complex of iron oxides. Areas may vary in composition from:

- Free sands (typical of dune ridges)
- Heavy, compacted, or cracking clays (notoriously sticky if wet, preventing all vehicle movement)

Clays are most typical of flats and evaporation pans, while softer clay-loams are typical of dune swales and foothills. A typical desert profile shows the interrelationship of these soil types across basin and range topography.

## Scalds

### What Are Scalds?
A scald is a bare clay-pan area where a duplex soil (originally light sandy loams over a sharply-defined transition area with deep clay below) has lost its upper soil layer. The remaining clays are often solonetic (with a high content of sodium ions).

### The Scald Problem
When first wetted in rain, the solonetic clays tend to "melt," effectively sealing the surface of the clay base and preventing infiltration. The wet crust so formed on the surface is almost impermeable — the same effect is used deliberately to seal clays for leading run-off water to tanks.

### Key Difference from Claypans
Scalds are not hollows — they have a flow into and out of the area, unlike claypans which rarely overflow.

### Revegetation Strategy
The revegetation of scalds follows a specific succession:

1. A bank is graded up to **10–15 cm high**, with the cut on the lower side
2. Water backs up a considerable distance — let this be **one-third** of the total area to be treated
3. This allows **two-thirds** of bare soil as run-off
4. Seed pre-sown on this surface will germinate
5. The low bank will eventually "melt" flat
6. The next one-third can then be treated as a successional strategy
7. Over **2–3 years**, a complete vegetative cover can be re-established

**Important caveat**: If banks are made too high or too solid, the plants will drown. Spiral earth-casting and ridges sown at their apex are also successfully used to seed claypans.

### Ecological Importance of Revegetation
Revegetation of scalds is important because, in Australia at least, plague locusts lay their eggs and hatch most successfully in the narrow shaded edges of the scalds. Most scalds are produced by over-grazing, so the desert grazier inflicts plague on whole regions by creating these conditions.

### Active Gully Erosion Treatment
Related to scald management, strategies for healing active gully erosion include:

- **Small gullies**: Can be filled, fenced, and planted
- **Large gullies**: Need multiple strategies including:
  - Diversion of headwaters
  - Swales to hold water on side walls
  - Gabions on gully floor
  - Fencing and tree planting
  - Spreader banks to spread headwaters gently downstream

## Claypans

### Characteristics
Unlike scalds, claypans rarely overflow. They receive silty waters from clay soils, and after pooling for a while, clay settles out and water evaporates. They represent distinct ecological phases depending on water availability.

### Wet Phase Ecology
During the wet phase, tall canegrass (*Eragrostis australasica*) can grow, ducks and waterfowl nest, and as the swamps dry out, marsupials and rodents take refuge. These swamp areas were frequently burnt by Aboriginal peoples in autumn, or as the canegrass dried out, but regenerated in wet periods.

### Seasonal Cycle
Claypans follow a distinct seasonal pattern:

1. **Wet phase**: Water pools, canegrass grows, waterfowl nest
2. **Drying phase**: Marsupials and rodents take refuge as water recedes
3. **Dry phase**: Bare clay surface, potential for managed burning
4. **Regeneration**: Return with rains

This cycle creates important habitat for a variety of desert species and demonstrates how even seemingly barren desert features support ecological function when water is present.

## Basin and Range Topography

The classical basin and range profile in fold mountain series is the typical desert landscape pattern. [[water-conservation]] begins on foothill slopes and forests are generated downhill. Complex foothills provide many sites for water storages and swales. Understanding this topography is essential for effective desert permaculture design, as the basins collect everything that erodes from the ranges above.
