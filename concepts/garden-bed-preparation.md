# Garden Bed Preparation

## Overview

The ideal soil has a good crumbly structure, is rich in [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], drains well enough to prevent waterlogging in heavy rain, and provides nutrients for healthy plant growth. Each soil type (clay, silt, sand, chalk, peat) has specific advantages, disadvantages, and management requirements. A short period of observation and a soil test will establish which mineral predominates.

## Identifying Your Soil Type

Take a handful of soil and rub between finger and thumb:
- **Clay:** Sticky, rolls into a ball that changes shape when pressed
- **Sand:** Coarse and gritty
- **Silt:** Silky smooth
- **Chalk:** Dry, crumbly feel, grayish-white color
- **Peat:** Black and moist

## Clay Soil Management

While clay is difficult initially, sound management can produce excellent results — capable of growing far better crops than sandy soil ever could. The problem: particle spaces are too small for free passage of water and air, so soil settles into a solid, airless mass.

### Digging
- Best in fall: dig when there has been a little rain to soften the hard-baked soil, or when drying out after being soaked, but before it is hard again
- Leave the surface rough and uneven through winter to expose maximum soil surface to the elements
- Work organic material into the upper levels as you dig

### Drainage
- **Flocculation:** Add sufficient lime to bind tiny clay particles into larger crumbs (see [[acid-and-alkaline-soil-management]])
- Dig coarse sand into the soil: ~1-2 bucketfuls per sq yard/meter
- Raise beds above surroundings to improve drainage and help soil warm up

### Organic Matter
- Add plenty of bulky organic matter (compost, manure) to hold particles apart for root and water passage
- After a few years of building organic matter and root systems, clay becomes much easier to work
- Every plant you grow improves the soil for the next generation
- **Critical rule:** Never walk on clay when it is wet or you destroy years of work. Lay boards down if you must cross it.

## Silt Soil Management

The main problem is drainage — silt has the smallest particles of any ground soil (only clay is smaller). Particles pack together when wet, preventing free passage of water and air.

### Digging
- Cultivate only when dry enough not to stick to your boots
- Dig in fall to take advantage of frost and weather breaking the soil down (same mechanism as clay — wet/dry/freeze cycles crack soil into clods)
- Work organic material into the topsoil

### Drainage
- Dig 1-2 bucketfuls of coarse sand per sq yard/meter when adding organic matter
- Raise ornamental beds and grow vegetables on [[comparison-deep-bed-cultivation-vs-square-foot-gardening]] system

### Organic Matter
- Liberal quantities of well-rotted compost or manure hold particles apart
- Keep soil covered with green-manure crop in winter (see [[green-manure-crops-detailed]])

## Sand Soil Management

A very light soil that drains easily and warms up quickly — ideal for early crops. However, sand is "hungry" — nutrients and water drain away as fast as they are applied.

### Digging
- Timing is relatively unimportant — not necessary to leave rough over winter
- Best dug in spring a short while before sowing or planting
- Never walk on it when so wet it sticks to your boots

### Drainage
- Generally too good — the need is to retain water
- Spread organic matter/mulch between plants as often as possible to lower evaporation and improve structure

### Organic Matter
- Add substantial amounts of bulky organic material each year
- Dig into the top few inches or spread on surface (works down quickly due to fast drainage)
- Maintain a cover of vegetation at all times, especially in winter when leaching is most rapid
- Grow green-manure crop in winter, dig in during spring

## Chalk Soil Management

Two big disadvantages: thin, dry, "hungry" (nutrients wash through rapidly), and very alkaline.

### Digging
- Timing is not critical — normally dry enough to work even in winter
- Not necessary to leave rough for frost; dig in spring before sowing
- Keep digging shallow (topsoil is usually thin); consider adding a layer of topsoil

### Drainage
- Generally too good — need is to retain water and nutrients
- Add bulky organic matter that also helps acidify the soil

### Organic Matter
- More than any other soil, keep the surface covered
- Grow green-manure crop in winter; dig in during spring
- During [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]], even sow fast-growing [[comparison-green-manure-guide-vs-mulch-types-and-effects]] between vegetables just to keep soil covered
- Use acid materials as mulch: peat, grass cuttings, compost, manure

## Peat Soil Management

Potentially very fertile and usually easy to work — grow as intensively as you can.

### Digging
- Timing is not critical; not necessary to leave rough in winter

### Drainage
- Moorland and fenland peats are often badly drained — may need drainage system
- Peat soils tend to dry out in hot weather; if allowed to dry completely, they shrink and may be difficult to rewet

### Organic Matter
- Normally unnecessary to add humus-making materials (peat is largely decomposed matter already)
- Low mineral content but excess organic matter
- Likely to need fertilizers for nutrients
- Usually acidic — needs generous lime for fruit and vegetable plots
