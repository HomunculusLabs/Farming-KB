     9|# Phase I Compost Preparation for Agaricus
    10|
    11|Phase I composting is the foundational process in Agaricus
    12|bisporus cultivation, transforming raw organic materials into a
    13|selective, nutritious substrate through controlled microbial
    14|decomposition. The process typically takes 7 to 14 days and
    15|involves pre-wetting raw materials, building the compost pile,
    16|and managing a series of turns to maintain aerobic conditions.
    17|Properly executed Phase I composting produces the chemical and
    18|physical foundation for high-yielding mushroom crops.
    19|
    20|## Raw Material Fundamentals
    21|
    22|Straw provides carbohydrates, the basic foodstuff of mushroom
    23|nutrition. Wheat straw is approximately 36% cellulose, 25%
    24|pentosan, and 16% lignin. Cellulose and pentosan are
    25|carbohydrates that yield simple sugars upon breakdown. Lignin,
    26|a highly resistant material, is converted during composting to a
    27|"nitrogen-rich-lignin-humus-complex" that serves as a protein
    28|source. These structural and chemical properties make straw ideal
    29|for mushroom compost.
    30|
    31|When cereal straw is gathered from horse stables, it is called
    32|"horse manure" though it is actually approximately 90% straw and
    33|10% manure. The quality depends on the proportions of urine and
    34|droppings present. Horse manure is favored because 30-40% of the
    35|droppings are living microorganisms that accelerate composting.
    36|
    37|## Compost Types
    38|
    39|Two fundamental compost types exist:
    40|
    41|- **Horse manure compost**: Uses horse manure as the basic
    42|  starting ingredient. Race track manure is preferred because
    43|  frequent bedding changes produce a lighter material. Boarding
    44|  stable manure is heavier with more urine and droppings.
    45|- **Synthetic compost**: Uses no horse manure. Straw, sometimes
    46|  mixed with hay, is the base ingredient. Because straw is low
    47|  in potassium and phosphorus, chicken manure is the standard
    48|  additive for synthetic composts.
    49|
    50|By themselves, horse manure or straw are insufficient. They must
    51|be fortified with supplements providing protein and carbohydrates
    52|to feed increasing microbial populations.
    53|
    54|## Supplement Groups
    55|
    56|**Group I (High nitrogen)**: Ammonium sulfate (21% N), ammonium
    57|nitrate (26% N), urea (46% N). Max 25 lbs/dry ton for straw
    58|softening in synthetics. Not for horse manure composts.
    59|
    60|**Group III (3-7% N)**: Malt sprouts (4%), brewers' grains (3-5%),
    61|cottonseed meal (6.5%), peanut meal (6.5%), chicken manure (3-6%).
    62|Most widely used due to favorable C:N balance.
    63|
    64|**Group IV (Low N, high carbohydrate)**: Grape pomace, sugar beet
    65|pulp, molasses (0.5% N), cottonseed hulls. Excellent temperature
    66|boosters at 250 lbs per dry ton.
    67|
    68|**Group VII (Minerals)**: Gypsum at 50-100 lbs per dry ton is
    69|essential. It improves structure, increases water holding capacity,
    70|prevents greasy conditions, and supplies calcium.
    71|
    72|## Compost Formulas
    73|
    74|**Formula I (Horse manure based)**: 2,000 lbs wet horse manure
    75|(50% moisture), 30 lbs cottonseed meal (10% moisture), 50 lbs
    76|gypsum. Produces approximately 2,800 lbs compost at 70% moisture.
    77|Resulting nitrogen content: 1.54%.
    78|
    79|**Formula II (Synthetic)**: 2,000 lbs wheat straw (10% moisture),
    80|2,000 lbs chicken manure (20% moisture), 125 lbs gypsum. Produces
    81|approximately 7,000 lbs compost at 71% moisture. Resulting nitrogen
    82|content: 1.62%. This fills approximately 350 sq. ft. of beds at
    83|20 lbs per sq. ft.
    84|
    85|Target nitrogen at initial make-up: 1.5-1.7%. Approximately 40%
    86|of dry matter is reduced during composting, demonstrating the
    87|importance of composting no longer than necessary.
    88|
    89|## Pre-Wetting
    90|
    91|Pre-wetting activates microbes that attack the waxy film on straw.
    92|Methods include dipping in a water tank, spraying with a hose, or
    93|spreading in a 2-3 foot pile with sprinkling. Horse manure needs
    94|approximately 3 days; synthetic composts need 5-12 days.
    95|
    96|## Pile Construction
    97|
    98|Building the pile is called "stacking," "ricking," or "make-up."
    99|Key requirements for the compost pile:
   100|
   101|- Dimensions: 5-6 feet wide by 4-6 feet high, rectangular.
   102|- Sides should be vertical, compressed 3-6 inches from outside.
   103|- Internal section remains less dense than the outer section.
   104|- Pile size should be maximized without creating anaerobic core.
   105|
   106|Air enters from the sides; as oxygen is consumed, warm air rises
   107|through the center in a chimney effect. Airflow depends on pile
   108|size, structure, moisture, and temperature differential.
   109|
   110|## Turning Schedule
   111|
   112|A well-built pile depletes oxygen in 48-96 hours. Turning aerates
   113|the pile, adds water, mixes supplements, and ensures even
   114|decomposition. Monitor with a long-stemmed thermometer; when
   115|center temperature drops, it is time to turn. Outer areas move to
   116|center and vice versa. Gypsum is normally added at the second turn,
   117|as adding it earlier may depress ammonia production.
   118|
   119|## Long Composting Procedure
   120|
   121|For outdoor composting without Phase II rooms:
   122|- Day -10: Break straw bales, water, mix with group 1, 4, or 5
   123|  supplements. Windrow.
   124|- Day 0: Make up pile, 6 feet wide and 4 feet high.
   125|- Day 6: First turn. Move anaerobic center to outside.
   126|- Day 10-12: Second turn. Add gypsum. Distribute actinomycetes.
   127|- Day 13-15: Third turn. Pile height reduced to 24 inches.
   128|- Day 15-17: Fourth turn. Compost should be dark brown with
   129|  actinomycetes throughout. Ammonia gone. Moisture 67-70%, pH
   130|  7.0-7.5. Pasteurize at 135F for four hours.
   131|
   132|## Temperature Zones and Microbial Succession
   133|
   134|Mesophilic bacteria and fungi (below 90F) initiate decomposition.
   135|Thermophilic fungi, actinomycetes, and bacteria dominate at 90-160F.
   136|Above 165F, decomposition is mainly chemical (humification and
   137|caramellization). Studies showed compost from the 120-135F zone
   138|produces the highest yielding crops. Approximately 20-30% of dry
   139|matter is consumed during Phase I, with another 10-15% during
   140|Phase II.
