## Soil Microorganisms and Natural Fertility — One Gram of Soil

One of the most striking empirical claims in [[masanobu-fukuoka]]"s work is
deceptively simple: "One gram of soil on my farm contains about 100
million nitrogen-fixing bacteria and other soil-enriching microbes."
This single observation encapsulates his entire argument about soil
fertility — that nature already possesses the mechanisms for maintaining
productive land, and that human intervention through chemical inputs
disrupts rather than enhances these natural systems. Fukuoka"s natural
farming is, at its core, a practice of nurturing the invisible microbial
economy that sustains all terrestrial life.

## The Microbial Economy of Healthy Soil

Fukuoka's claim of 100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria per gram is
grounded in decades of no-till, no-fertilizer, no-pesticide cultivation
on his farm in Shikoku. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria — including *Rhizobium*
species associated with legume roots and free-living *Azotobacter* and
*Clostridium* species — convert atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) into
biologically available forms that plants can absorb. In a single gram of
healthy soil, these bacteria work alongside fungi, actinomycetes,
protozoa, and countless other organisms in a complex food web that
decomposes organic matter, cycles nutrients, suppresses pathogens, and
maintains soil structure.

The "other soil-enriching microbes" Fukuoka references include
phosphorus-solubilizing bacteria, mycorrhizal fungi that extend plant
root systems and transport minerals, and cellulose-decomposing organisms
that break down plant residues. Together, this community performs all the
functions that [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]] are designed to replace — and does so
more efficiently, more sustainably, and at no cost.

## How Natural Farming Preserves Microbial Life

Fukuoka"s method is designed specifically to protect soil microbial
communities. The key practices that distinguish his approach:

**No tillage.** Plowing destroys soil structure, disrupts fungal
networks, exposes [[anaerobic-organisms-compost]] to lethal oxygen, and buries
aerobic organisms beneath the surface. The soil in natural fields is
worked instead by small animals and roots, maintaining layered microbial
ecosystems. Fukuoka's basic pattern involves continuous no-tillage
direct seeding with straw mulching — a method found by agricultural
testing stations throughout Japan to have no basic problems with soil
health.

**No chemical fertilizers.** Chemical nitrogen suppresses the activity
of nitrogen-fixing bacteria. When plants receive free nitrogen, their
root exudates change in ways that reduce the food supply for microbial
partners. Over time, the soil becomes dependent on external inputs as
native populations decline. Even [[coleman-deep-organic-farming]], with its composting,
can deplete the land from which organic material is taken — creating a
dependency cycle no different in principle from chemical agriculture.

**No pesticides.** Pesticides destroy not only target pests but the vast
majority of beneficial soil organisms. Fukuoka's fields maintained
natural insect balance through biodiversity: spiders, frogs,
dragonflies, and predatory insects kept pest populations in check. The
soil remained alive with decomposers, nitrogen fixers, and nutrient
cyclers that would be annihilated by broad-spectrum chemicals.

## Green Manure as Microbial Host

Green manure plants play a central role as living hosts for soil
microorganisms. Clover, alfalfa, bur clover, and other leguminous ground
covers form [[fungi-in-the-environment-symbiotic-relationships]] with nitrogen-fixing *Rhizobium*
bacteria in their root nodules. These plants are not merely nitrogen
factories; their root systems create habitats for entire microbial
communities through root exudates and leaf litter.

In Fukuoka's orchard, a mixed ground cover of clover fixed nitrogen,
suppressed weeds, prevented erosion, maintained soil moisture, and
provided a continuous food source for soil organisms. When cut and left
as mulch, it fed decomposer organisms and returned nutrients to the soil
surface.

## Straw Mulch as Microbial Food

Straw mulching is the other pillar of Fukuoka's fertility strategy.
After rice harvest, straw is spread over the field rather than removed
or burned. Fungi break down the cellulose, bacteria process simpler
compounds, and earthworms incorporate organic matter into the soil
profile. The mulch moderates temperature, retains moisture, suppresses
weeds, and creates humus that improves soil structure — all without
external input. The straw grown on the field is returned, closing the
nutrient cycle entirely.

## Soil as the Spark That Restores the Deserts

Fukuoka's vision extended beyond his farm to global desertification. His
proposal centered on clay pellets containing microbe-rich soil, mixed
with seeds of green manure trees (black wattle), clover, alfalfa, bur
clover, grain, and vegetable seeds. These pellets could be scattered by
hand over deserts. The clay protects seeds until rain falls; embedded
microorganisms begin building soil communities necessary for a
self-sustaining ecosystem.

Fukuoka felt that "soil containing seeds and these microorganisms could
be the spark that restores the deserts." This is not metaphor. Healthy
soil teeming with microbial life is the foundation upon which all
terrestrial vegetation depends. Restore the soil microbiome, plants
follow. Restore plants, rains return — because rain issues forth from
the ground.

## The Scientific Blind Spot

Fukuoka was sharply critical [[critique-of-scientific-agriculture]]'s failure to
engage with soil microbiology at a systems level. Science rarely looks to
microorganisms for understanding large causal relationships. Botanists
are not to be bothered with microorganisms as these lie outside their
field. Specialists have lost sight of both the starting line and the
finish line. Nature's complexity cannot be dissected into disciplinary
compartments without losing the emergent properties of living soil.
