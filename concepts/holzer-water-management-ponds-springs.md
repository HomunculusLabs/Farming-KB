# Holzer Water Management (Ponds and Springs)

Water management is a foundational principle of [[sepp-holzer-method]]. Holzer's guiding philosophy is to keep water on the land for as long as possible and make maximum use of it exactly where it is found, whether from rain, springs, or surface runoff.

## Core Principle

"Permaculture principles lead us to use water as wisely and carefully as possible. In a permaculture landscape every resource and advantage is made use of exactly where it is." Holzer considers drainage a bad idea, preferring to work with natural wetness rather than against it. If he wants to make money from wetlands, he does not drain them but cultivates plants like orchids or aquatic and marsh plants that prefer damp or wet conditions.

## The Krameterhof Water System

The farm stretches from 1,500m to 1,100m above sea level, with water used for multiple purposes as it descends. Holzer keeps water on his land for as long as possible and makes as much use of it as he can.

### Water Sources

- **Springs**: Used for drinking water and to feed ponds and water gardens
- **Rainwater**: Collected and retained through landscape design
- **Groundwater**: Utilised where available; if absent and no springs exist, rainwater-fed ponds are still possible

### Connected Network

- 60 ponds, water gardens, wetlands, and ditches cover the farm
- Most are connected, allowing water to flow and be used for multiple purposes
- Water travels from upper boundary (1,500m) to valley (1,100m) serving different functions at each level

## Energy from Water

### Hydroelectric Power

- Two small generators using old Pelton wheels, fed from ponds through a penstock
- Height difference of approximately 100m (10 bars) supplies the whole farm with electricity
- Environmentally friendly energy generation
- Cool, oxygen-rich water returns to fish ponds, enabling trout in lower, warmer ponds

### Traditional Water Power

- Two water-wheel mills driven by water wheels
- A hydraulic ram powered by pond water that pumps drinking water without additional energy
- Uses pressure difference: a membrane pulses as drive water flows through, pumping water up through delivery pipe
- Provides up to 15 bars of pressure with a flow rate ratio of 1:10 (10 litres drive water per litre pumped)
- Creates energy systems that also release cool, oxygen-rich water back into fish ponds

## Ecological Benefits of Water Features

Areas of water provide advantages beyond the obvious:

- **Pest regulation**: Wetlands, water gardens, and ponds provide habitat for amphibians, snakes, and other beneficial creatures. The toad (Bufo bufo) is particularly useful against the dreaded Spanish slug (Arion vulgaris).
- **Temperature balancing**: Large water areas help balance temperature fluctuations on adjacent hills by reflecting sunlight and releasing stored heat.
- **Moisture and microclimate**: Evaporation increases soil moisture levels and creates useful [[holzer-climate-zone-microclimate-creation]].
- **Biodiversity**: Innumerable useful creatures find habitat in and around water features.

## Site Assessment for Water Features

When planning ponds or water gardens, Holzer assesses:

1. **Soil conditions**: Determines construction method -- coarse/stony soil requires separation techniques; loam soils seal naturally but need wall stabilisation
2. **Topography**: Critical for pond stability and water flow management. Building the pond properly prevents leaking or sliding.
3. **Existing water**: Natural springs, wet areas, and groundwater are major advantages. Naturally occurring water is a real advantage.
4. **Drainage basins**: Understanding where water naturally collects and flows
5. **Purpose**: Fish/crayfish ponds have different requirements from water gardens for plants or natural swimming pools

## Working with Different Soil Types

### Coarse, Stony Soil (Krameterhof)

- Separate coarse from fine material by shaking into tall mounds with excavator
- Fine material goes to terrace surfaces (fertile soil) or pond bases (watertight)
- Coarse material stabilises walls and banks

### Loam and Clay

- Material does not need separation (except humus layer, which must always be carefully removed, separated, and put back)
- Sealing ponds is easy due to high water retention
- Challenge: stabilising walls of deeper ponds (loam absorbs water quickly, becomes muddy, takes long to dry)
- Cannot be shaken into tall heaps -- will sink and crack under pressure as soil moisture is squeezed out
- Must dry and harden before bearing additional weight
- Separated material must have time to stabilise before further work

## Landscape Design for Water Retention

### Retaining Water

- [[holzer-terraced-landscape-design]] and [[holzer-raised-bed-techniques-hugelkultur]] absorb water over large areas
- Hollows between raised beds store water
- In dry areas, water must never be drained away -- land should be designed to retain it
- In wet areas, drainage prevents acidification of raised beds
- Ditches store surface water and runoff, letting moisture seep into surrounding soil

### Microclimate Effects

- Large water bodies balance temperature fluctuations on adjacent land
- Evaporation increases local moisture levels
- Ponds in front of niches reflect sunlight, creating warm suntraps
- Water gardens with fish and plants that prefer warm conditions profit from stones that absorb and release heat

## Avoiding Drainage

Holzer strongly advises against draining wetlands. Instead, he cultivates plants suited to damp conditions: orchids, aquatic plants, and marsh plants. A [[holzer-aquaculture-pond-construction]] or pond in naturally wet areas requires minimal energy to establish and provides maximum ecological benefit.
