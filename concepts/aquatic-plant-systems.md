# Aquatic Plant Systems and Water Storage

## Overview

Water is the scarcest and most critical resource in most landscapes, yet its management is often neglected in favor of above-ground systems. [[bill-mollison]]'s approach to water in [[permaculture]] treats every water body as a highly productive system, not merely a storage or livestock watering facility. Aquatic plants are described as "a neglected part of aquaculture" that can yield more productive output than animals. The design of ponds, bogs, marshes, and water edges integrates food production, [[amphibian-habitat-creation]], water purification, and [[tree-temperature-effects-and-microclimate-modification]] into a single system.

## Global Water Context

### Water Distribution

- Less than 1% of Earth's water is actively moving (in rivers, soil, atmosphere)
- 75% of all fresh water is unavailable (locked in ice caps)
- World mean average rainfall is 33.8 inches per year
- 77% of atmospheric water falls on oceans, 23% on land
- Of the 23% falling on land, 16 parts transpire or evaporate, leaving 7 parts to run off to the ocean
- The land receives an additional 7 parts of water from horizontal advection — this is where forests intervene

### The Permaculture Approach

Most of the world needs much more surface storage at greater heights than usual, not just in low valleys. Key strategies include:
- Establishing forests to store water in biomass and soil
- Reducing runoff through swales and the Wallace plow
- Using seaweed and seaweed concentrates to improve soil water storage (they work as gel on soil surfaces)
- Designing water storage that guarantees at least two waterings during summer establishment phases for new plantings

## The Zonation of Aquatic Plants

Aquatic plants occupy distinct zones based on water depth and soil saturation, each supporting different species with different productive uses.

### Deep Water (6-15 feet)

Beyond six to nine feet depth, the primary organisms of interest are:
- **Duckweed** (*Lemna* spp.): Floating species useful across climates
- **Algae bloom**: The target production in well-manured ponds. When a white painted disc (Secchi disc) disappears at about 2.5 feet in soft green water, the pond is well-manured. If it blacks out at about one foot, the pond is over-manured. If visible at five feet, the pond needs more manure.

Algae production practically ceases at 12 feet deep. The only reason to maintain 12-15 foot depth is to allow fish to escape low oxygen and extreme temperature conditions.

### Floating and Submerged Plants (3-6 feet)

- **Water lilies**: Best planted by burying the root in a bag of manure inside a tire, which constrains the plant and allows easy harvesting
- **[[chinese-water-chestnut-mollison]]**: Lives as a mud rush at this depth, producing large tubers
- **Indian water chestnut**: A floating chestnut with an anchoring stem — a beautiful sight in India where women float bronze bowls ahead of them, picking chestnuts from the water. Requires careful, slow movement through stems to avoid damage.

Many deeper-rooted species can be planted by wrapping seeds in clay balls with nutrients, weighting with stones, and dropping them into position.

### Shallow Water (0-3 feet)

This is the most productive zone:
- **Wild rice** (*Zizania*): Extremely important for ducks. Seed must be put into mud balls and thrown into ponds, as seeds don't have long air-storage life. Water plants often have fat, squashy seeds that fall, sink, and take root. Wild rice likes still lagoons, slow-flowing rivers, and oxbows, preferring 18 inches to three feet depth with some water movement. It has a three-week dropping period — gather for four to five days, and the rest falls as superb duck food.
- **Taro* (*[[colocasia-esculenta]]*): Grows from coastal Florida to central Canada, in still or slow-flowing water up to three feet deep.
- **Lotus**: Some species have popcorn-like seeds that can be gathered and popped.
- **Duck potato** (*Sagittaria*): At least 80 species with wide climate range. The monocots (also called wapato) grow up past the water surface and bear heavy seed heads. They can be eaten like leeks.
- **Arrowheads**: Important wildfowl food.

### Emergent and Marginal Zone

- **Cattails** (*Typha* spp.): Advice for homeowners is to plant them in tiny ponds in the annual garden and cut off tops to prevent seeding. They produce heavy crops and are good vegetables. They must be kept out of main ponds as they will close off the marginal surface.
- **Rushes and reeds**: Serve varied uses — papyrus grows here, as do plants good for paper and fodder. Reed for mats and thatch.
- **Bamboo**: Won't tolerate sodden conditions but lives on well-drained soil near water, sending feeder roots down to the water table.
- **Bald cypress**: Lives on hummocks or develops hummocks in bog conditions.
- **Fly-catching plants**: Venus fly traps, pitcher plants, and sundews inhabit soaked soils at pond edges, catching tiny insects from the air.

### Wetland Trees

Many swamp trees produce the most durable woods in the world, and some produce the lightest woods. They live within 20 feet of water's edge but never in the water. Many have air cells throughout their stems and root systems for oxygen storage, which is how they manage to live in waterlogged conditions.

## Pond Design and Construction

### Structure

The ideal pond has:
- A sloping or stepped floor for full drainage
- The ability to drain into another pond for species transfer during dry cycles
- Lime applied to the whole base at construction (pond pH is between 6 and 8, much less acidic than garden soil)

### The Dry Cycle

Intensively cultivated ponds should go through periodic dry cycles:
- After a few years as a pond, the land will carry dryland crops for three to four years without further manuring
- The mud and mud surface have fantastic ability to fix nutrients from water
- During the dry cycle: grow heavy feeders in year one, taper off, finish with a modest crop, then roll down and re-flood

### Gley Ponds

Gley is a [[mushroom-compost-fermentation-process]] that seals pond bottoms:
- Once a pond is held in gley, the gley penetrates the soil deeply and perpetuates itself
- It's possible to drain a stable lagoon by punching post holes and inserting gley material
- Ponds made from loose fill material are most prone to obliteration, but well-constructed ponds in hills can last many human lifetimes
- The only ponds that fill quickly are barrier dams in deserts — these can be used as manure sources by cutting out muck

### Silt Traps

Silt traps placed before water enters a pond harvest nutrient-rich sediments and prolong the pond's life.

## Key Organisms in Pond Systems

### Mussels

Freshwater mussels are the superior phosphorus fixer:
- Each mussel pumps approximately 200 gallons of water per day
- They draw all living forms and particles from the water, inject them into the mud floor, and lower phosphorus in the whole system
- They should be harvested only modestly (for chicken grit) to maintain their population
- They don't harm plants

### Freshwater Shrimp

- Excellent harvesters of algae, including diatoms
- A prime step in the food chain from diatoms to fish
- In subtropical regions, they grow large enough for human food
- Harmless to plants — they eat decaying matter and keep stems clean

### Frogs

- Excellent for [[ultra-low-herbal-smoker-greenhouse-pest-control]], particularly slug predation
- Toads (*Bufo* spp.) control slugs and cutworms effectively (they are nocturnal, matching pest activity patterns)
- Bullfrogs are of less value as they feed during daylight when target pests are inactive

### Crawfish and Yabbies

- Crawfish like ponds about three feet deep with brush piles for shelter
- The yabbie is a long burrower (up to 25 feet) that can tunnel through dam walls — select species with restricted burrowing capacity
- Trapping: Beer cans tied to corks or ping pong balls serve as habitat; draw up 20 cans and harvest 20 large crawfish. Ramps and slope traps work faster.
- A Scotch biologist estimated 30 quarter-acre ponds in marine culture could support a family with $20,000-$30,000 annual income (1981 dollars)
- Hundreds of acres of previously non-productive land in Australia are now under crawfish aquaculture

## Mints and Pond Edges

Pond edges are excellent locations for:
- **Mints**: Particularly black peppermint (*Mentha × piperita*). Two or three acres could yield $70,000 through distillation to menthol. Mint is good when dairying with dairy outwash, which produces powerful blooms. However, mint is laterally invasive — confine it with dense bushes on either side.
- **Blueberries**: Pond edges provide ideal [[blueberry-growing-guide]] conditions.

## pH Management

Pond pH runs between 6 and 8 — 100 to 200 times lower in acid than most garden soils. Most pond organisms prefer lime. Lime the entire base when constructing a new pond, then monitor pH regularly. In appropriate climates, watering plants with limey pond water is beneficial.

## The Hay Method

Strewing bales of hay around pond edges (half in, half out) serves multiple purposes:
- Seals ponds by supporting diatom growth
- Diatoms like hay — they drift in the air and colonize the hay
- The resulting diatom-rich water can be dipped and fed to small fish
- Ducks add manure to the system, accelerating decomposition
- Kick more hay in as it rots to maintain the process
