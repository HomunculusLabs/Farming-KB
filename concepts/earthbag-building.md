# Earthbag Building

Earthbag construction uses polypropylene sandbags filled with subsoil to build walls, domes, and vaults. Developed by architect Nader Khalili in the 1990s, this technique is extremely affordable, requires minimal tools, and produces structures that are resistant to earthquakes, floods, hurricanes, and fire. Earthbag building is one of the most accessible natural building methods for homesteaders.

## Core Advantages

- Extremely low cost: Bags cost $0.10-0.50 each; fill material is often free
- Simple tools: Only shovels, tampers, buckets, and a level needed
- Disaster resistant: Flexible walls survive earthquakes; flood-resistant; fireproof
- Fast learning curve: Basic technique can be learned in a weekend workshop
- Uses local materials: Subsoil from your site, bags, barbed wire
- Thermal mass: Thick earth walls provide excellent temperature moderation
- No cement required: Pure earth construction with optional lime stabilization

## Materials

### Bags

- Polypropylene sandbags: Most common, UV-resistant when plastered
- Size: Typically 14x18 inches or 18x30 inches
- Source: Hardware stores, military surplus, disaster supply companies
- Alternatives: Mesh bags, rice bags, custom-sewn tubes

### Fill Material

- Subsoil from your site (preferred — free and local)
- Optimal mix: 25-30% clay content with sand/gravel aggregate
- Test by making a damp ball — it should hold shape when dropped from waist height but shatter when stepped on
- Too much clay: Add sand
- Too much sand: Add clay or use barbed wire for stability
- Lime-stabilized: Add 5-10% lime for water resistance and strength

### Barbed Wire

- Two strands of 4-point barbed wire between every course
- Acts as reinforcement, locking bags together
- Prevents sliding and provides tensile strength
- Standard 4-point barbed wire from any farm supply store

## Foundation

### Rubble Trench Foundation

1. Dig a trench 18-24 inches wide and below frost line (or 12-18 inches in frost-free climates)
2. Fill with crushed rock or gravel to within 6 inches of ground level
3. Add a drain pipe at the bottom if groundwater is a concern
4. Add a few inches of gravel on top as a level bed for the first bags

### Gravel Bag Foundation

In areas with moisture problems or poor soil:
1. Fill the bottom 2-3 courses with gravel-filled bags instead of earth
2. This creates a capillary break that prevents moisture wicking up into earth walls
3. Gravel-filled bags are extremely strong and stable

## Wall Building Process

### Laying Bags

1. Fill bags with damp subsoil — fill to about 80% full (overfilling makes bags too round)
2. Fold the open end under and place the bag with the fold facing down
3. Butt bags tightly against each other — no gaps
4. Use a sheet metal slider under the bag for easy positioning — slide it out after placement
5. Tamp each course solidly with a tamper before laying the next course
6. Bags should compress to about 6 inches tall after tamping
7. Stagger joints like bricks — overlap joints between courses

### Barbed Wire Placement

1. After each course is tamped, lay two strands of barbed wire along the top
2. Keep wire taut — it locks the next course in place
3. The barbs grip both the bags above and below
4. This is the primary reinforcement and earthquake resistance mechanism

### Keeping Walls Plumb

1. Use a plumb bob or level on the inside and outside faces
2. Tap high spots down with the tamper
3. Fill low spots by adding more soil and re-tamping
4. A 2-inch deviation over the full wall height is acceptable for curved walls
5. Use a string line for straight walls

## Dome Building

Earthbag domes are the most dramatic application of this technique. They require no wooden roof structure and create incredibly strong, weatherproof shelter.

### Key Principles

- Start with a circular base (typically 12-20 feet diameter)
- Each course leans slightly inward (about 1-2 inches per course)
- The catenary arch is the ideal shape — a hanging chain forms this curve in reverse
- Windows and doors are built with temporary forms (plywood bucks) that remain embedded
- An arch or lintel is required above every opening
- The top is closed with a keystone bag or a small capstone

### Critical Measurements

- Wall height to diameter ratio should not exceed 1:1 for stability
- Inward lean: approximately 1 inch per course for a 16-foot diameter dome
- Always verify your curve against a catenary template or compass arm

## Plastering

Earthbag walls must be plastered to protect the bags from UV degradation.

### Earthen Plaster (Interior)

- 3 parts sand, 1 part clay, 1 part straw fiber
- Apply in 2-3 coats over wire mesh or directly on bags
- Finish with a fine clay-based finish coat

### Lime Plaster (Exterior)

- 3 parts sand, 1 part lime putty
- More water-resistant than earthen plaster
- Self-healing — small cracks fill as lime carbonates
- Apply in 2-3 coats

### Cement Stucco (Exterior, with caution)

- Can be used but is less breathable than lime plaster
- Must include wire mesh lath for adhesion
- Risk of trapping moisture in walls — use only with adequate drainage

## Strength and Durability

- Compressive strength: 150-300 psi (adequate for single-story structures)
- Earthquake resistance: Flexible walls absorb and dissipate seismic energy
- Fire resistance: Earth is non-combustible — fire rating of 4+ hours
- Flood resistance: Bags can be submerged temporarily without structural failure
- Lifespan: Properly plastered earthbag structures can last 100+ years

## Limitations

- Labor intensive: Filling and placing bags is physically demanding
- Building codes: Rarely accepted without engineering approval
- Roof options: Flat roofs require beams; domes solve this but complicate code approval
- Interior space: Thick walls reduce interior floor area
- Moisture sensitivity: Earth walls must be protected from prolonged moisture exposure
- Bag degradation: Polypropylene eventually degrades — plaster is essential for longevity

## Cost Estimates

- Bags: $0.10-0.50 each (200-400 bags for a small structure)
- Barbed wire: $50-100 per structure
- Fill: Free (on-site subsoil)
- Plaster materials: $100-500
- Total for a 200 sq ft structure: $500-2,000 in materials
