# Solomon's Variety Trial Methodology

[[steve-solomon]] developed his variety trial methodology during his years running
[[territorial-seed-company]] (1979-1986). He argues that honest seed businesses
cannot simply sell whatever varieties "Someone Else" recommends — they must
test numerous varieties themselves and decide what to offer based on actual
performance. This approach produced several key insights that contradict
conventional [[coleman-british-adoption-of-french-intensive-gardening]] wisdom.

## Why Variety Trials Matter

Most gardening advice comes from secondhand sources. A variety recommended in
a book or catalog may perform well in one climate but poorly in another.
Solomon's trials revealed that widely-recommended varieties often underperformed
lesser-known options in his Oregon growing conditions. The only reliable way to
know which varieties work in your specific garden is to test them.

Trial results also exposed problems with the intensive [[chemical-to-biological-gardening-paradigm]].
Solomon found that plants grown with wider spacing in his trial plots
consistently outperformed the same varieties grown intensively — they got
larger, tasted better when not harvested immediately, and often yielded more
per unit area than the intensive gardening gurus claimed.

## Designing a Home Garden Variety Trial

A proper variety trial requires growing multiple varieties of the same crop
side by side under identical conditions. Solomon's approach:

1. **Select 3-5 varieties** of one crop to compare. Include one well-known
   standard variety as a benchmark.

2. **Space plants far enough apart** that each can develop to its full
   potential. This is the opposite of intensive gardening — plants need room
   to show their true characteristics.

3. **Use identical soil preparation** across the entire trial area. Apply the
   same [[complete-organic-fertilizer]] (COF) at the same rate to every plot.

4. **Plant all varieties on the same day** using the same [[coleman-leek-deep-planting-technique]].
   If direct-seeding, sow at the same depth with the same spacing.

5. **Water identically** — either irrigate all equally or grow all without
   irrigation (dry gardening trials are especially revealing).

6. **Harvest and evaluate** on the same schedule. Record yield, flavor,
   disease resistance, days to maturity, and any other relevant traits.

## Key Insights from Solomon's Trials

### Wider Spacing Increases Per-Plant Performance

Trial plots with well-separated plants needed far less irrigation than
intensive beds. The plants also produced larger individual vegetables. While
intensive gardening maximizes total yield per square foot, Solomon argues this
comes at the cost of:

- Higher water consumption
- More fertilizer input
- Greater disease pressure from poor air circulation
- Smaller individual [[growing-vegetables-with-less-fertilizer]] intense flavor
- More demanding management and attention

### Taste and Nutrition Correlate with Spacing

Vegetables given adequate space develop more complex flavors and higher
nutrient density. Solomon attributes this to less competition for water and
minerals, allowing each plant to fully express its genetic potential for
nutrient accumulation.

### Varieties Perform Differently Under Stress

The most revealing trials are those conducted under challenging conditions —
limited water, minimal fertilizer, or pest pressure without intervention.
Varieties that perform well under stress are the ones most valuable for
subsistence and hard-times gardening.

## Trial Data Collection

For each variety tested, record:

- **Days to emergence**: When seedlings first appear
- **Days to maturity**: When first harvestable
- **Total yield**: Weight or count per plant
- **Yield per unit area**: Total yield divided by space used
- **Fruit/vegetable size**: Average and range
- **Flavor rating**: Personal taste assessment on a scale
- **Disease incidence**: Any problems observed and severity
- **Pest damage**: Type and extent of insect or animal damage
- **Storage quality**: How well the harvest keeps
- **Seed saving viability**: Whether the variety breeds true

## Evaluating Trial Results

Solomon emphasizes that yield alone is a poor measure of variety quality. A
variety that yields 20% less but tastes twice as good, stores longer, and
requires less water may be far more valuable for a home gardener. Consider
the full picture:

1. **Growth vigor**: Did the plants grow strongly from the start?
2. **Disease resistance**: Were problems minimal without intervention?
3. **Flavor**: Is the taste noticeably better than alternatives?
4. **Texture**: Is the mouthfeel appealing?
5. **Storage**: Does it keep well without special equipment?
6. **Seed saving**: Can you reliably save seeds from this variety?
7. **Adaptation**: Does it seem well-suited to your specific microclimate?

## Long-Term Trial Programs

For serious gardeners, Solomon recommends running multi-year trials. Weather
varies between years, and a variety that performs brilliantly in one season
may disappoint in another. Three years of data provides a much more reliable
picture than a single season's results.

Keep a garden journal with detailed notes. Over years, patterns emerge that
reveal which varieties are truly reliable performers in your specific
conditions versus those that had one lucky season.

## The Seed Company Perspective

Solomon founded Territorial Seed Company specifically to serve Pacific
Northwest gardeners. His philosophy was that regional seed companies should
trial varieties in the climate they serve, not simply resell whatever the
large national distributors offer. This regional focus is increasingly rare
as seed companies consolidate.

Home gardeners running their own trials are, in effect, becoming their own
regional seed experts — selecting and adapting varieties for their specific
microclimate, soil, and growing practices.
