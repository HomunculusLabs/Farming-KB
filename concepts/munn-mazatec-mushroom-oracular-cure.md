## The Shaman as Speaker

The [[mazatec-shaman-healing-medicine-woman-practices]] presides over the session in an authoritarian context.

Everyone is enjoined to keep silent and listen while the shaman speaks for each person present.

"They pay a sorcerer who eats them [the mushrooms] and tells them what they have taught him" — early Spanish chronicler.

The shaman speaks, chants, and sings the truth — described as "oral poets of their people, doctors of the word."

The Mazatecs say "the mushrooms speak" — if you ask a shaman where imagery comes from: "I didn't say it, the mushrooms did."

Mushrooms liberate not only perceptual spontaneity but linguistic spontaneity.

At times "it is as if one were being told what to say, for the words leap to mind, one after another, of themselves" — similar to surrealist automatic writing but the flow tends to be coherent.

After each phrase, the shaman says "tzo" — "says" — like rhythmic punctuation: "Says, says, says. It is said."

Language is an ecstatic activity of signification.

## The Ceremonial Setting

Participants sit on the earthen floor of huts, close to the fire, breathing [[copal-incense]].

The shaman sits with furrowed brow and marked mouth of speech.

Chanting, clapping hands, rocking to and fro, speaking in the night of chirping crickets.

Mushrooms are blessed in the purifying smoke of copal rising from glowing coals.

Known to ancient Meso-Americans as "Flesh of God," called by her people "Blood of Christ."

The mushrooms are the awakeners of the spirit, the food of the luminous one.

"Flesh of the world. Flesh of language."

## Words as Medicine — The Poesis of Healing

Munn draws on Lévi-Strauss's observation: "The psychoanalyst listens, whereas the shaman speaks."

The shaman provides the sick person with a language for unexpressed and otherwise inexpressible psychic states.

Words themselves are medicine — to enunciate and give meaning to events and situations of existence is life-giving.

The shaman questions the patient and puts answers into their mouth corresponding to the interpretation of their condition.

The transition to verbal expression induces release of the physiological process — reorganization in a favorable direction.

The shaman has a conception of poesis in its original sense as an action.

The purpose of every session is therapeutic catharsis.

The shaman gives medicine to patients (the mushrooms themselves) while simultaneously altering consciousness.

Probably most remedial for psychosomatic complaints and psychological troubles.

Given to the depressed, mushrooms awaken a catharsis of the spirit.

For those with problems, they provide a vision of their existential way.

The shaman repeats therapeutic sessions three times at intervals if the illness is not deemed incurable.

The shaman also works over the sick through massage and suction, their intoxicated condition giving them intense, vibrant energy.

## The Participant Observer Dilemma

Munn's essay illustrates the eternal dilemma of the participant observer.

For hallucinogenic experiences, the dilemma is compounded: two cultural worlds plus "ordinary" and "non-ordinary" realities.

Munn became a convert: "To call such transcendental experiences of light, vision, and speech hallucinatory is to deny that they are revelations of reality."
