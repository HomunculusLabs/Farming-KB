# Matter and Spirit Unity in Food

In the final chapters of *The Natural Way of Farming*, Fukuoka extends his natural
[[cho-natural-farming-philosophy-of-non-interference]] into the realm of diet, arguing that food is not merely physical
nourishment but a meeting point of matter and spirit. Drawing on Buddhist philosophy
and his own observations of nature, he presents a view in which flavor, color, and
nutrition are inseparable from spiritual experience.

## The Buddhist Foundation: Form is Emptiness

Fukuoka grounds his food philosophy in the Buddhist teaching that "form is emptiness
and emptiness is form." In Buddhist terminology, "form" refers to matter and
"emptiness" to spirit. Matter and spirit are one. Matter has many aspects, such as
color, shape, and quality, each of which affects the spirit in many ways. This is the
unity of matter and spirit.

Applied to food, this means that what we eat cannot be separated from how we experience
it. The nutritional scientist who reduces food to calories, proteins, and vitamins
has already lost the essence of food by splitting matter from spirit. The person who
eats with full attention, savoring flavor and color as expressions of nature, experiences
food as the unified whole it truly is.

## The Seven Prismatic Colors of Food

Fukuoka uses the metaphor of light to explain how human judgment fragments the wholeness
of food. The world appears filled with seven prismatic colors, but when combined these
seven colors become white. Viewed with detachment, all things are colorless and white.
But to one distracted, seven moods engender seven colors. Matter is spirit and spirit
is matter; both are one.

The same applies to food. Nature provided man with a vast array of foods. Discerning
what he thought were good and bad qualities, man picked and chose, thinking he had to
create harmonious combinations and blends of color, that he should always partake of
a rich variety. This has been the root of his errors. Human knowledge can never compare
with the greatness of the [[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-order-food-chain-unity]].

## Color as Matter and Spirit

In Buddhist texts, the Chinese character for color represents form or matter itself.
The seven prismatic colors are really one white light, and the apparent diversity of
food is really one nourishment. When we become distracted by categories of food, by
the idea that some foods are superior and others inferior, we fall into the realm of
[[non-discriminating-knowledge-and-critique-of-scientific-reductionism]].

Water undergoes countless transformations but remains always water. Beneath the infinite
variety of creation, all things are essentially one. Although differences may exist
between the seven colors, they are all of equal value. To be distracted by these
differences is to fail to note the matter and spirit underlying them, to be sidetracked
by the inconsequential.

## The Unity of Flavor and Nutrition

Fukuoka argues that the human body should stimulate the human appetite of its own
accord and serve as delicious food. Flavor and nutrition must be one. The Western
approach that separates "eating for health" from "eating for pleasure" is, to
Fukuoka, a fundamental error, a reversal of human thinking.

Mothers who tell their children to eat food they do not like because it is "good"
for them are perpetuating this split. The food that the body naturally craves, eaten
in its season and in its place, is both delicious and nutritious. Only when food has
been alienated from nature through processing, breeding for transport and storage,
and chemical treatment does the split between flavor and nutrition emerge.

## The Nature of Food: Connection with the Soul

We normally think of food only as something needed by the body to live and grow, but
what connection does food have with the human soul? Fukuoka asks this question to
challenge the materialist assumption that food is mere fuel. For animals, it is enough
to eat, play, and sleep. Nothing could be better than if man too were able to live a
life of contentment enjoying nourishing food, health, and tranquil sleep.

What does it mean to enjoy and take pleasure in food? This, along with nourishment
and nutrition, is a question of both matter and spirit. The person who eats wild herbs
without removing their natural astringency, who savors the subtle flavors of sun-dried
[[growing-root-vegetables-guide]] and salt pickles, who appreciates the green tea sipped by the open
hearth, is experiencing food as matter-spirit unity.
