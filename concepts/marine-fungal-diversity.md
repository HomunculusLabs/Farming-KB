# Marine Fungal Diversity

Marine and estuarine fungi represent one of the least explored domains of fungal biodiversity. Despite occupying 75% of Earth's surface, marine habitats remain dramatically under-sampled for mycological diversity.

[[fungal-biodiversity-mangrove]] [[fungal-communities-extreme-environments]] [[fungal-biodiversity]] [[saprotrophic-fungi]]

## Defining Marine Fungi

The field of marine mycology was established only with Johnson and Sparrow's 1961 book on fungi in oceans and estuaries. Marine fungi are not a taxonomic group but are defined ecologically.

Obligate marine fungi grow and sporulate exclusively in marine or estuarine habitats and are permanently or intermittently submerged. Facultative marine fungi normally occupy freshwater or terrestrial milieus but can grow and sporulate in the marine environment. Fungi restricted to immersed parts of estuarine or marine plants are considered terrestrial but halotolerant.

The definition applies equally to members of the Oomycota, a group of nonfungal microbes whose fungus-like morphologies arose through convergent evolution. Oomycotes have diploid mycelial states, form egglike sexual spores (oospores), and have biflagellate swimming zoospores as asexual propagules.

## Taxonomic Diversity

Compared with terrestrial fungi, the number of higher marine fungi is small. To date, 444 species have been described from marine habitats. Marine filamentous Eumycota include ascomycetes, basidiomycetes, and mitosporic fungi. The largest genera include Corollospora, with species growing on buried dead algae or lignocellulose.

No cases of true endemic marine fungi have been recorded, unless host-specific fungi on endemic hosts are considered. Mycophycias apophlaeae, for example, occurs only on Apophlaea species in New Zealand.

## Habitats and Modes of Life

Marine fungi are saprotrophs, symbionts, or parasites on plants or animals. All are microscopic; the largest marine ascomycetes and basidiomycetes are only 4-5 mm in diameter.

Saprobic fungi are important decomposers of cellulose in the form of driftwood, pilings, mangrove roots, and marsh plants. They are also found on washed-up algae, seagrass leaves, and animal products including chitin, keratin, tunicin, and calcium carbonate.

About 40 species of higher marine fungi are parasitic; most belong to the Ascomycotina. All but two known species parasitize algae. Trichomaris is found on crab carapaces, and Cytospora rhizophorae occurs on mangrove proproots. Mycaureola dilseae is the only known filamentous basidiomycete that parasitizes algae.

## Mangrove Ecosystems

Mangroves of subtropical to tropical zones contribute approximately 12 tonnes per hectare per year of litterfall. Most information on marine mycelial oomycotes has been gathered in mangrove ecosystems.

Manglicolous marine fungi grow on permanently or intermittently submerged parts of mangrove trees. The greatest diversity of species is encountered in intertidal habitats including sandy beaches, jetties, salt marshes, and the mangal.

Within as few as 2 hours after leaf fall, mycelial oomycotes begin to occupy mangrove leaves. Within 24-48 hours of submergence, 80-100% of fallen leaves are occupied. Fungal zoospores are released from submerged decaying leaves close to mangrove stands and do not travel more than a few meters.

## Salt Marshes

Salt marshes harbor large numbers of filamentous higher fungi. The main distribution areas of marine fungi are long narrow tracts of the intertidal zone running parallel to coastlines or tidal creeks. Extended salt marshes where Juncus roemerianus may cover thousands of hectares provide vast habitat.

In the salt marshes of the coastal United States, concentrating on the endemic needlerush Juncus roemerianus, more than 100 species of ascomycetes and mitosporic fungi have been found on that host alone, with marine species at the base, halotolerant terrestrial forms at the top, and facultative marine species in between. Almost all were new to science.

Species of cord grass (Spartina species, Poaceae) live in habitats similar to those of needlerush and also harbor large numbers of filamentous higher fungi.

## Deep Sea and Oxygen Limitations

Fungi occur mainly in the intertidal zone where most hosts or substrata are located, but some indigenous species have been collected in the deep sea, with the deepest record at 5,315 m.

Distribution of fungi in marine habitats is limited primarily by availability of dissolved oxygen. Low oxygen levels in water or sediments inhibit or prevent fungal growth. Wood panels submerged off the California coast in a "minimum oxygen zone" with dissolved oxygen content of 0.30 ml/liter showed no fungal settlement, while growth occurred at a site with 1.26 ml/liter.

## Oomycote Diversity

Marine mycelial oomycotic decomposers, principally Halophytophthora species, are primarily decomposers of fallen leaves entering the aquatic environment along the coast. They are a distinct part of the decomposer community of submerged mangrove leaves.

The most prominent contributors of fallen leaves along marine shorelines are mangroves in subtropical to tropical zones. Marine oomycotes are part of the leaf-decomposition system in temperate coastal zones as well, particularly at river mouths and landward edges of saltmarshes.

## Symbiotic Associations

Marine fungi form several types of symbiotic associations. Lichenoid associations have phototrophic partners, usually microscopic cyanobacteria or green algae. True submarine lichens such as Arthopyrenia halodytes and Verrucaria serpuloides represent obligate associations between mycobiont and photobiont.

Mycophycobioses are obligate symbioses between systemic fungi and marine macroalgae in which the alga dominates. Relationships between the intertidal brown alga Ascophyllum nodosum and Mycophycias ascophylli exemplify this category.

Endolithic fungi such as Halographis runica colonize submerged snail shells, worm tubes, and corals.

## Geographic Patterns

Oceans in certain geographic areas have been explored more extensively. Well-studied regions include the British Isles, the Hawaiian Islands, Hong Kong, the Seychelles, the Caribbean, and the South China Sea adjacent to Brunei.

Temperate and tropical organ-decomposing fungi show overlapping distribution patterns. Some species originally thought to have limited distribution (e.g., Halosphaeria bahamensis) now are known to be widespread.

As far as is known, no thorough inventory of marine fungal biodiversity has been carried out in any area, making this one of the most significant frontiers for fungal discovery.

## Sampling Challenges

Isolation methods for marine fungi are similar to those for terrestrial fungi but require seawater-based media and often incubation at marine temperatures. Collecting manglicolous fungi requires wading in mangrove habitats where sediments are very soft.

No thorough inventory of marine fungi has been completed, and the total number of marine species remains unknown but is certainly vastly underestimated.
