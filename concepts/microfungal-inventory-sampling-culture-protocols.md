# Microfungal Inventory Methods — Sampling Strategies, Culture Techniques, and Protocols

## Undertaking a Microfungal Inventory

Microfungal inventories present unique methodological challenges. The resources required for a complete survey of even a small area are enormous, and most microfungal species are too small to be certain of absence even after extensive sampling. As a result, modern practice emphasizes **reproducibility over completeness** (Cannon 1997b). The lack of widely promulgated protocols has historically led to poorly defined surveys that are difficult to compare across studies. Sampling approaches differ significantly depending on whether the goal is a total inventory or a diversity assessment, making standardization crucial.

---

## Sampling Strategies

Sampling design must account for the microscopic scale and extreme diversity of microfungi. Key considerations include:

- **Resource intensity**: Complete surveys demand enormous investment in time, expertise, and materials.
- **Detection limits**: Most species are too small to confirm absence regardless of sampling effort.
- **Reproducibility**: Cannon (1997b) argued that reproducible, well-defined protocols are more valuable than attempts at exhaustive completeness.
- **Protocol standardization**: The absence of widely adopted protocols has produced surveys that cannot be meaningfully compared; standardized methods are essential.
- **Objective alignment**: Sampling design differs for total inventory versus diversity assessment — objectives must be clearly stated and methods matched accordingly.

## Direct Observation

### Complementarity of Methods

Moist chamber and isolation techniques sample **distinct species assemblages** with significant complementarity (Watson et al. 1974; Visser and Parkinson 1975). Polishook et al. (1996) found that ≥79% of species detected by direct observation were also obtained through particle filtration and culturing. Complete surveys therefore require **both** direct observation and culture techniques. Cultures provide additional systematic characters, living cells for genetic analysis, and material for screening technological applications.

### Advantages of Direct Observation

1. **Lower cost** — no agar media or incubators required.
2. **Less airborne contamination** — samples examined in situ without prolonged exposure.
3. **More complete identification** — a higher proportion of taxa produce spores or distinctive structures under moist chamber conditions.

### Disadvantages of Direct Observation

1. Greater difficulty observing many taxa simultaneously.
2. Vegetative structures are often immersed within host tissues, obscuring identification.
3. Greater difficulty recognizing and separating species in mixed communities.
4. Suppression of some taxa by competition among fungi and bacteria within host tissue.
5. Moist chamber conditions are suboptimal for sporulation of certain species.
6. No living cultures are produced for subsequent studies (physiology, genetics, bioprospecting).

### Moist Chamber Protocol

1. Cut areas (circles or squares) of designated size from broad leaves or bark; use measured lengths of petiole, needle, or stem.
2. Make samples as uniform as possible — for leaf tissue, include a consistent proportion of the midrib.
3. Place samples in closed containers on a damp substrate: filter paper, paper towel, moistened sterile [[vermiculite]], or moistened sterile sand.
4. **Heredia (1993)** attached microfungi directly to microscope slides with double-sided adhesive tape for repeated observation without disturbing the specimen.
5. Insecticides or molluscicides may be applied to prevent sample degradation; however, some fungi are sensitive to these chemicals.
6. Inspect containers frequently for emerging sporulating species; correct moisture levels are critical.
7. **Note**: Variation in moisture content may alter the recorded diversity, so moisture must be monitored carefully and kept consistent.

## Culture Techniques

### Bills and Polishook (1994) Method

The most effective culture-based isolation method developed to date:

1. Plant material is mixed with sterile water and pulverized in a sterilized blender.
2. The resulting slurry is washed through mesh filters with distilled water.
3. Particles 100–200 μm in diameter are collected and washed again.
4. The suspension is pipetted onto agar plates of two selective media: **malt agar + cyclosporin A** and **dichloran-rose bengal medium**.
5. New colonies are subcultured onto standard media; sporulation is encouraged on cornmeal agar supplemented with sterilized banana leaves.
6. Originally developed for tropical rain forest litter, the method is equally appropriate for temperate litter, twigs, and wood fragments.

### Factors Influencing Recovered Diversity

- **Particle size**: Particles that are too large allow multiple colonies to overgrow one another; particles that are too small disrupt fungal cells. **Bååth (1988)** recommended a colony-to-particle ratio of 1:1 or less.
- **Fragmentation technique**: Tissues can be cut with a sterile scalpel into equal-size pieces, recording spatial order and referencing fungi to each fragment (Wilson and Carroll 1994; Lodge et al. 1996a). This approach is labor-intensive and unsuitable for well-degraded samples.

### Growth Medium Selection

Nutrient-poor media are generally most effective:

- **Soil extract agar** and **carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) medium** restrict hyphal growth for most species, facilitate colony observation (transparent media), and are inexpensive.
- Rich media promote rapid overgrowth by a few fast-colonizing taxa, suppressing slower-growing species.

### Growth Inhibitors

- **Low temperature incubation** or **chemical inhibitors** (e.g., cyclosporin; Dreyfuss 1986) slow fast-growing species.
- **Cyclosporin-containing media** permit [[spore]] germination but restrict hyphal extension, making colony separation easier.
- These media are not strictly selective but are highly effective for materials with high fungal density.

### Nonsporulating Cultures

Inventories relying heavily on culture methods inevitably produce large numbers of nonsporulating cultures:

- Bills and Polishook (1994) used a wide taxon concept and found that ≥25% of recovered species were once-observed and unidentifiable.
- Growth rate, colony morphology, pigmentation, and hyphal structure can provide acceptable diversity estimates even without sporulation.
- **RAPD analysis** can confirm taxonomic integrity but is expensive.
- Biochemical tests and molecular fingerprinting are valuable but costly relative to traditional morphological classification.
- The lack of comprehensive reference data makes sequencing-based identification impractical for most microfungal groups.
- **Recommendation for large surveys**: maintain a detailed database of character expressions (colony features, growth rates, microscopic morphology) to support consistent operational taxonomic unit (OTU) delimitation.

## Observation and Isolation from Washed Litters

Litter washing techniques, originally developed by Harley and Waid (1955) and Parkinson and Williams (1961), exclude dormant propagules that are not nutritionally associated with the litter substrate. Tokumasu et al. (1997) refined the approach using a detergent wash followed by a distilled water rinse in a mechanical shaker for pine needles in Thailand. After washing, samples can be examined by moist chamber observation or processed through culture techniques.

## Complete Inventories — Two Approaches

### 1. Host-Plant Identity-Based Approach

Samples are organized by host plant species, covering most microfungi except those occurring on unidentifiable rotted wood. This approach maximizes ecological information about host specificity and plant–fungus associations.

### 2. Geographic/Substratum-Based Approach

Transects cross valleys, streams, and rocky outcrops, with samples collected from rotten wood, bark, and other substrata regardless of host identity. This approach captures fungi on degraded or unidentifiable substrates.

**Both approaches are necessary.** A double-pronged protocol must balance the two strategies to prevent duplication while ensuring comprehensive coverage. GIS technology is valuable for mapping rare or targeted host species, and collaboration with trained plant taxonomists is essential for accurate host identification.

## Rapid Assessment Techniques

- Minimizing reliance on experienced fungal systematists is possible through structured protocols.
- **Parataxonomists** (Janzen et al. 1993) — highly trained collectors without formal taxonomic credentials — can collect and sort specimens effectively.
- Untrained staff have proven successful for inventorying spiders, ants, polychaetes, and bryophytes (Oliver and Beattie 1993), suggesting parallels for microfungal work.
- **Industrial screening programs** maximize diversity extraction and defer formal identification to only the most valuable or unusual strains, offering an efficient model for large-scale biodiversity surveys.

## Protocols for Sampling Programs

### Site Selection

Sites may be chosen for practical reasons (accessibility, complementarity with other surveys, environmental threats) or because they are representative of well-defined vegetational or ecological categories. International consortia can coordinate sampling across valued or threatened environments.

### Site Analysis

Comprehensive within-site environmental variation mapping should include:

- **Topographic features**: elevation, aspect, slope.
- **Climatic data**: temperature, precipitation, and insolation records over multiple years.
- **Geologic features**: rock type, soil acidity, composition, and water content.
