# Cycles and Time Niches

Cycles are any recurring events or phenomena. In [[decomposition]], and regeneration differ each time it happens. Thus it is the number of such cycles, great and small, that decide the potential for diversity. We should feel ourselves privileged to be part of such eternal renewal. Just by living we have achieved immortality — as grass, grasshoppers, gulls, geese, and other people. We are of the diversity we experience in every real sense.

The fundamental principle is simple:

> **Every cyclic event increases the opportunity for yield. To increase cycling is to increase yield.**

## The Human Connection to Cycles

People are built up molecule by molecule, cycling through themselves the materials of their environment: its air, soils, foods, minerals, and pathogens. Over time, people create their own local ecology (as do wombats and all sedentary animals); their wastes, exudae, and rejecta eventually create the very soils in which they garden. "Garbage in, garbage out" applies equally to computers and people.

Gardeners are constantly cycling themselves, and by a generational pattern of adjustment become "eco-compatible" with their landscape and climate. We are not the end point of evolution but a step on the way, and part of a whole sequence of cycles.

## Deflection States and Setbacks

Time is a resource which can accumulate in ecosystems. It can be "lost" to an evolving or evolved system by setback — adverse disturbance — just about the same way as we can set back the hands of a clock. Such setbacks are termed **deflection states** by ecologists.

Ecosystems, especially those we are in process of constructing or destroying, are always proceeding to some other state of evolution. Left alone, they may evolve at their own pace to some unknowable endpoint, which was once called a climax state. However, forest climax states are temporary events in the long span of geological time.

Australian studies show that old dune forests lose the battle to mobilize nutrients and begin to show net nutrient loss, aided by rainfall and occasional fire, until they begin to recede to a less vigorous shrubbery system. Most other disturbed forests appear to be building, but if disturbed too often, they never reach the previous vigour, height, or yield.

## Time as Rehabilitative Resource

Time can work as a rehabilitative resource. Active intervention in successions enables us to analyze and supply key nutrients and soil treatments if needed, to assist maximum forest rejuvenation. The designer's role is not to force outcomes but to read the direction of natural succession and assist it.

## The Erosion of Lifetime

A second time concept is that of lifetime — the "quality time" that we have to enjoy, examine, and understand our world. Life-time is very short indeed for those mobile, power-using, bombarded, employed, make-work, and busy humans who make up non-tribal societies, while many tribal peoples still manage to preserve a high quota of celebrations, discussions, contemplations, mutual preening, and creative artwork.

This erosion of the lifetimes of people, exacerbated by the media and messages of the consumer society, is perhaps the most serious effect of that society. As W.B. Yeats wrote:

> *Life is too much with us, late and soon*
> *Getting and gaining, we lay waste our years...*

People so harried that they have "no time for anything else" may find that time has run out to save themselves, their lives, or those of their children.

## Tribal Connection to Place

Tribal peoples are very much aware of, and tied to, their soil and landscapes, so that their mental and physical health depend on these ties being maintained. The rest of us have suffered forcible, historic dislocations from home sites, and many no longer know where home is, although there are new and conscious moves to reinhabit the earth and to identify with a bioregion as "home."

Travel itself causes stress and morbidity. Travellers both carry and acquire pathogens and spread them to other cultures. New settlers bring new species, new timetables, and new concepts. Local systems have to readjust or fail. These processes are analogous to the disturbance of old ecosystems by new ecological or climatic forces. The post-invasion evolution contains part of the old and part of the new system, so is itself a new assembly with new potentials.

## Biocide Damage to Cycles

Deprived systems, like those blasted by biocides, lose most or all opportunity to transcend their prior state. The egg of life is broken, degrades, and assumes a lower potential. When we destroy the cycling organisms — the decomposers, the pollinators, the nutrient recyclers — we sever the very processes that make yield possible.

Cycling of nutrients is continuous in the tropics, but is interrupted wherever drought, cold, or low nutrient status reduces the "base opportunity," just as the killing of fish stocks reduces the yield. Such cycles are slowed or even stopped by climatic factors or by our interference.

## Design Implications

- **Maximize the number of cycles**: Every cycle is a yield opportunity. Design systems with multiple recycling pathways.
- **Integrate animals**: Pigeons, chickens, goats, and other animals are cycling agents that convert waste into fertility.
- **Respect succession**: Work with natural time scales rather than forcing premature outcomes.
- **Preserve lifetime**: Design systems that reduce the busy-work of maintenance and free human time for observation, creativity, and connection to place.
- **Avoid biocides**: Chemical interventions that kill cycling organisms destroy the foundation of long-term productivity.
