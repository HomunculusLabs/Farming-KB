## Community Primary Errors

In *[[permaculture]]: A Designers" Manual*, [[bill-mollison]] identifies a set of recurring
pitfalls that cause [[holmgren-community-design-and-intentional-communities]] to fail or to spend more time mired in
conflict than engaged in productive work. These errors are not merely theoretical —
they are drawn from decades of observed community experiments worldwide. Understanding
them is essential for anyone seeking to design or participate in a sustainable human
settlement.

## The Three Tyrannies

Mollison categorizes the most common governance failures into three "tyrannies" —
structural traps that masquerade as democratic or communal ideals:

### Tyranny of the Dissenter

When a community insists on **group consensus for all decisions**, a single dissenting
voice can paralyze the entire group. While consensus sounds egalitarian, in practice
it grants veto power to the least cooperative individual. Decisions that should take
hours stretch into weeks or months. Energy that could go toward growing food, building
shelter, or restoring land is instead consumed by endless debate.

The dissenter need not even be malicious — a well-meaning member who simply cannot
accept a decision can stall the group indefinitely. Over time, other members lose
patience and leave in frustration, weakening the community"s social fabric. Mollison
advises tiered decision-making: reserve consensus for truly consequential choices and
use delegated authority or majority vote for routine matters.

### Tyranny of the Leader

At the opposite extreme, **leadership by one or a few individuals** concentrates power
in a way that breeds resentment, dependency, and burnout. A charismatic founder may
hold the community together initially, but when that person leaves, falters, or becomes
authoritarian, the community collapses because no one else has been empowered to lead.

This pattern is common in communities that form around a single visionary. Members
become followers rather than participants, and institutional knowledge stays locked in
one person's head. Healthy communities distribute responsibility across roles and
rotate leadership functions so that no single person becomes indispensable.

### Tyranny of Proscription

**Rules about lifestyle** — dictating what members may eat, wear, believe, or do in
their private time — constitute a third form of tyranny. Communities that enforce rigid
behavioral codes drive away diverse talent and suppress the very creativity they need
to adapt and thrive. A community that mandates vegetarianism, specific dress, or particular spiritual
practices narrows its membership and its capacity for innovation.

Mollison emphasizes that ethical guidelines should focus on shared outcomes (care for
the earth, care for people, fair distribution of surplus) rather than prescribing
personal conduct.

## Structural Pitfalls

Beyond governance, several practical errors repeatedly undermine community viability:

- **Large group households** deprive members of essential privacy. People need
  personal space — a room, a small dwelling, or at minimum a retreat — to recharge,
  think independently, and maintain healthy relationships. Overcrowding breeds
  conflict and emotional exhaustion. Mollison recommends small clustered dwellings
  rather than single large communal buildings.

- **Overscheduling of meetings** drains communal energy. When every evening is given
  over to discussion, planning sessions, or process meetings, members have no time
  left for the actual work of the community. Meetings should be minimal,
  well-facilitated, and strictly time-bounded.

- **Neglect of income-producing activities** is perhaps the most fatal error. Idealism
  alone does not pay for tools, repairs, seeds, or healthcare. A community must
  identify viable economic activities — whether through agriculture, craft, education,
  or consulting — and treat them as non-negotiable priorities.

- **Poor financial accounting** leads directly to deferred maintenance. When no one
  tracks where money goes, facilities decay, equipment breaks without replacement,
  and morale collapses. Transparent bookkeeping is not bureaucratic overhead; it is a
  survival skill for any community managing shared resources.

## Voluntary Simplicity

The ethic that Mollison identifies as foundational for successful communities is
**[[voluntary-simplicity]]** — a deliberate choice to reduce material consumption, avoid
waste, and find satisfaction in productive work, relationships, and connection to the
land. This is not austerity imposed from above; it is a shared commitment to modest
consumption that frees the community from the treadmill of earning simply to spend.

Voluntary simplicity is both a personal ethic and a [[design-principle-integration]]. At the community
scale, it means building durable, repairable infrastructure, choosing technologies that
reduce dependence on external inputs, and structuring the local economy around needs.

## Legal and Ethical Foundations

Mollison stresses that viable communities need a **legal and [[ethical-basis-of-alternative-nation]]** built on
three pillars:

1. **Sharing** — resources, knowledge, and labor are pooled for the common good.
2. **Trusteeship** — members hold land and resources in trust for future generations,
   not as private property to be exploited.
3. **Modest consumption** — the community collectively agrees to limit its ecological
   footprint.

These principles provide the legal scaffolding — through trusts, cooperatives, or
[[community-land-trusts]] agreements — that protects the community from internal division and
external appropriation.

## Key Principles

### Users Pay

Those who benefit from a shared resource should bear the cost of maintaining it. The
**"users pay" principle** ensures fairness and prevents free-riding. If a community
tool shed is used by the carpentry guild, that guild funds its upkeep. This principle
aligns incentives and makes costs transparent.

### Proposers Pay Capital

When a member or subgroup proposes a new project (a workshop, a building, an
enterprise), the **"proposers pay capital" principle** requires them to front the
initial investment or risk. This filters out whimsical proposals and ensures that only those with genuine
commitment and a credible plan move forward. It also protects shared funds from
being depleted by untested ideas.

## Commonwork

Mollison advocates for **[[commonwork]]** — a system where members hold **varied roles**
rather than specializing in a single task. A person might split their time between
gardening, teaching, and cooking. This diversity prevents monotony, builds
resilience (knowledge is distributed, not siloed), and keeps members engaged with
the whole system.

Commonwork also insulates the community from economic shocks. If demand for one
product drops, members can shift energy to other activities without losing their
entire income stream.

## Right Livelihood

Drawing on Buddhist ethics, **right livelihood** means earning one's living in ways
that do not harm others or the environment. For permaculture communities, this aligns
naturally with producing food, building with local materials, generating renewable
energy, and offering educational services. Right livelihood is both a moral constraint and a practical advantage — activities
that heal rather than degrade their surroundings tend to be more sustainable over time.
