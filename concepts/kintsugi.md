# Kintsugi

## Overview
Kintsugi is the Japanese practice of repairing broken ceramics with visible seams.
The seams are traditionally made with lacquer mixed or dusted with gold, silver, or other powders.
The word is often translated as golden joinery.
A related term, kintsukuroi, means golden repair.
Kintsugi treats breakage as part of an object's history rather than as a defect to hide.
The repaired vessel remains useful, but it also becomes visually transformed.
Its cracks become lines of memory, care, and craft.
The practice is both a technical restoration method and a cultural metaphor.
It is frequently associated with Japanese aesthetics of impermanence and imperfection.
It also belongs to the practical history of lacquer, tea utensils, and ceramic collecting.
Modern uses range from museum conservation to personal symbolism and contemporary art.

## Materials
Traditional kintsugi uses urushi, a natural lacquer made from the sap of Toxicodendron vernicifluum.
Urushi cures by polymerization in warm, humid conditions.
It is durable, glossy, adhesive, and resistant to water when properly cured.
Raw urushi can irritate skin because it contains urushiol compounds.
Craftspeople handle it carefully and allow repaired objects to cure in a humid cabinet.
Gold powder is the iconic surface material, but silver, brass, tin, or colored powders may be used.

One often repeated story concerns shogun Ashikaga Yoshimasa and a damaged Chinese tea bowl.
According to the story, unsatisfactory metal staple repairs encouraged Japanese artisans to develop a more refined method.
The historical details are difficult to verify, but the tale captures a real cultural shift.
Tea practitioners valued utensils with age, provenance, and subtle irregularity.
Japanese lacquer technology was already highly sophisticated.
Ceramic repair could therefore become an honored craft rather than a mere workaround.
Some tea bowls became more valued after repair because their histories were enriched.
Kintsugi developed in relation to chanoyu, collecting, gift exchange, and connoisseurship.
It also reflects a society in which repair was often more sensible than replacement.
Today the practice is interpreted through both historical scholarship and popular philosophy.

## Types of repair
Crack repair joins pieces along clean fractures.
Chip repair fills missing edge or rim material without reconstructing a large missing area.
Piece method repairs replace missing ceramic with lacquer-based fill material.
Joint-call repair combines fragments from different vessels into one object.
The joint-call approach can create a deliberately hybrid form.
Some repairs use fine lines that respect the original silhouette.
Other repairs emphasize the seam as a new visual feature.
Stitched metal staple repairs are distinct from kintsugi but historically related to ceramic mending.
Museum conservation may use reversible materials rather than traditional  if poorly documented.
Museums must balance appearance, authenticity, stability, and reversibility.
Urushi repair is durable but not always compatible with modern conservation ethics.
Epoxy repairs may yellow or become difficult to remove.
Objects intended for display have different requirements than objects used in tea ceremony.
