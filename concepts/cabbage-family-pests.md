# Cabbage Family Pests

The brassica family (cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts, turnips, radishes, rutabagas, Chinese cabbage) is attacked by a characteristic suite of insects. Understanding their life cycles and implementing targeted controls makes the difference between a successful brassica harvest and a ruined crop.

## Cabbageworms (Pieris rapae and Trichoplusia ni)

### Identification

At least two distinct species (possibly four):
- **Imported cabbageworm**: Larvae of a white butterfly seen fluttering in the garden. Clusters of small, yellowish, bullet-shaped eggs laid on undersides of leaves.
- **Cabbage looper**: Smaller larvae of a night-flying brown moth. Round, greenish-white eggs laid singly on upper leaf surfaces. Larvae hatch quickly and grow rapidly while feeding continuously.

### Damage

Both species can do great damage in a short time, especially during early formation of cabbage heads. Surprisingly, the small larvae of the night flyer are usually more destructive than the large ones of the day flyer.

### Control

- **Handpicking**: In a small garden, handpicking and tossing larvae away from brassica plants can be sufficient
- **[[bacillus-thuringiensis]] (Bt)**: Extremely effective nontoxic pesticide, marketed as Dipel. Lethal only to cabbageworms, cabbage loopers, and close relatives. Can be sprayed the day of harvest. The culture remains active on leaves for about a week
- **Application tips**: Use a quarter teaspoon of cheap dishwashing liquid per quart (liter) as a spreader-sticker. Spray undersides of leaves as much as or more than the tops. Spray every few days after every new brood of worms appears

### The Bt Warning

Growers who become grossly satisfied with Bt may end up with a dead worm appearing in their broccoli or cabbage. Bt is essential to use correctly — spray thoroughly, including leaf undersides.

## Cabbage Root Maggots (Hylemya brassicae)

### The Problem

A major pest in Cascadia (Pacific Northwest) and the United Kingdom. The fly waits until the brassica root system is extensive enough (stem ~1/4-6 mm in diameter) before laying eggs on the soil surface near the plant. Larvae burrow down and feed on roots.

### Susceptibility

Weaker-rooting brassicas are demolished: small-framed cabbage, most cauliflower, all Brussels sprouts (rarely). Chinese cabbage offers no varietal resistance.

### Damage Signs

Plants collapse, die, become stunted, or barely grow. Radishes, turnips, and Chinese cabbage are riddled; rutabagas are usually left alone.

### Control

- **Planting timing**: Sow brassicas mid-May through July in North America to avoid the spring population peak
- **Row covers**: Floating row covers carefully anchored on all sides effectively prevent cabbage root maggots from reaching plants
- **Sawdust collars**: A ring of fresh fine sawdust ~1.5 inches (4 cm) thick, 6-8 inches (15-20 cm) in diameter, not touching the stem, prevents the fly from reaching the soil to lay eggs
- **Parasitic nematodes**: Certain species effectively attack root maggots in the soil. They also control wireworms, onion maggots, carrot weevils, cutworms, and cucumber beetle larvae
- **Hilling**: Careful and persistent hilling of soil around plant stems increases seedling survival by burying the root system deeper

### What Doesn't Work

Wood ashes or lime dustings — once widely recommended — actually attract cabbage flies. They may help in acidic, calcium-deficient soils by boosting brassica growth enough to compensate for increased predation.

## Flea Beetles (Phyllotreta striolata)

### Identification

Tiny, black, hopping insects that chew pinholes in leaves, mainly on cabbage family members.

### When They're a Problem

Only in spring when cool conditions mean plants are not growing rapidly and the garden is mostly bare, forcing beetles to concentrate on a few seedlings. Later feeding is usually of no consequence.

### Control

- **Direct sow 5 seeds for every plant wanted**, thinning only as competition starts to affect growth — gives beetles more to chew on while providing enough survivors
- **Don't sow at the earliest possible moment**
- **[[foliar-feeding]]**: If seedlings are losing more than 10% of leaf area, spray with fish emulsion (the foul smell may confuse beetles)
- **Rotenone/pyrethrum**: If leaf area loss exceeds 20%, spray every few days
- **Husky, well-hardened transplants** normally outgrow flea beetle nibbling

## Aphids (Aphididae family)

### The Threat Level

Small, soft-bodied insects that cluster on leaves and suck sap. A few cause no significant damage. In large numbers they cause leaf curl and stunt plants. They can multiply with amazing rapidity — exploding from nothing to a serious threat in days.

### The Disease Vector Problem

Aphids transmit plant diseases. To stop disease-carrying aphids from infecting plants, you would have to spray something that kills every single one before it enters the garden — and that's not possible. Better to make plants healthy enough to resist most diseases.

### Control

- **Hose spray**: Blast aphids off leaves with a hose and nozzle
- **Insecticidal soap**: Safer's soap is effective and virtually nontoxic to animals and most other insects, though it can burn some plant species (especially spinach). Test a leaf a few days before spraying the whole patch
- **Neem spray**: Long-lasting natural remedy recommended as the final recourse

### The Ant Connection

Aphids sometimes have a close relationship with ants, which "farm" them for their sweet secretion. Aphid control may entail elimination of ant nests.

## Attitude Adjustment

The American Sanitary System suggests food should be "clean" and free of bugs. Consider changing your attitude: a plant showing the occasional insect damage is still vigorous. The critical level where aphid infestation begins to reduce production is when they cover about 5% of the entire leaf area.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.
