## Entheogens in Ancient Cultures

The term *entheogen*, coined in 1979 by a group of scholars including Carl
Ruck, Jeremy Bigwood, Danny Staples, Jonathan Ott, and R. Gordon Wasson, means
"that which generates the divine within." It refers to psychoactive substances
used in religious, shamanic, or spiritual contexts, deliberately avoiding the
loaded cultural associations of terms like "psychedelic" or "hallucinogen."

Terence McKenna"s *Food of the Gods* (1992) presents a sweeping argument that
psychoactive plants and fungi — entheogens — were central to the development of
human religion, culture, and social organization across virtually every
civilization.

## McKenna's Central Thesis

McKenna proposed that the history of human civilization can be understood
through the lens of humanity's relationship with psychoactive substances. He
contrasted what he called the "[[mckenna-dominator-culture-and-suppression-of-ecstasy]]" that emerged
with agriculture, alcohol, and hierarchical social organization.

In McKenna"s framework, the suppression of entheogenic knowledge was not
incidental but central to the establishment of authoritarian power structures.
Psychedelic experiences, by dissolving ego boundaries and fostering a direct
sense of connection to the natural world, were inherently incompatible with
systems of control based on hierarchy, patriarchy, and the subjugation of
nature.

## Key Archaeological and Historical Evidence

### Prehistoric Art

McKenna pointed to the ubiquity of geometric patterns in Paleolithic and
Neolithic rock art — spirals, concentric circles, zigzags, and lattice
patterns — as evidence of entheogenic use. These patterns closely resemble the
form constants identified by Heinrich Klüver in his studies of mescaline-
induced visual phenomena, and are consistent across widely separated cultures
and time periods, suggesting a common experiential origin.

Notable examples include the Tassili n'Ajjer rock paintings in the Sahara
(allegedly depicting mushroom-headed figures), the rock art of the Pecos River
region in Texas, and the geometric motifs of Upper Paleolithic cave art at
sites like Lascaux and Chauvet.
