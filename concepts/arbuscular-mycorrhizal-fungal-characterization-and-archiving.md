# Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungal Characterization and Archiving

## Overview

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi are mutualistic [[endomycorrhizal-fungi]] that form symbiotic associations with the roots of most terrestrial plants. Their characterization involves detailed morphological analysis of spores, mycorrhizal structures, and ecological context. Because these fungi cannot be cultured independently of a host plant, accurate identification relies heavily on [[spore]] morphology and established reference collections. The archiving of specimens and cultures is critical for reproducible taxonomy and long-term research.

## Spore Collection and Trap Cultures

The sporulation capacity of AM fungi in natural settings is presumed to be low, meaning field collections may not capture all species present in a mycorrhizal community. Trap cultures are therefore essential. Setting up a first trap culture from field-collected soil, roots, or both may not induce sporulation in all species. However, a second cycle—reseeded from the first pot after host-plant shoot removal—can double or triple the number of sporulating species. A third cycle can yield even greater species richness, though results vary considerably between sites (Bever et al. 1995; Morton et al. 1995).

Two approaches for obtaining spores in sufficient quantity for accurate description:

1. **Temporal sampling**: Collecting from the same site at different times or seasons, especially after sustained root growth in the plant community.
2. **Trap cultures**: Establishing cultures using field-collected soil, roots, or both, which provides experimental verification that sporulating fungi form [[mycorrhizae]].

## Species Description Procedures

### Obtaining Spores

The first task in species description is obtaining healthy, fresh spores with all pertinent morphological characters present and intact—a rarity in field soils with highly active soil microbiota. Spores showing any evidence of parasitism or degradation should be discarded, as species differences in some genera (such as *Glomus*) are small and misidentification of degraded specimens is likely.

### Slide Preparation

A minimum of 75 spores should be mounted, divided as follows:
- One-third mounted whole
- One-third mounted and broken in [[pvlg]] (polyvinyl-alcohol–lactic acid–glycerol) and water
- One-third mounted and broken in PVLG + Melzer's reagent (1:1 v/v)

Slide preparation guidelines:
- No more than 5–10 spores per coverslip for spores exceeding 200 μm in diameter
- Up to 20–30 spores per coverslip for spores less than 150 μm in diameter
- Whole spore slides examined the same day; broken spore slides stored for at least 72 hours to clear contents and delineate subcellular structures

### Measurement and Documentation

- **Size**: Measured with an ocular micrometer
- **Color**: Reflected color measured by comparison with a color chart at the same illumination, best accomplished with a dual-beam fiber-optic unit
- **Minimum documentation**: At least 75 spores mounted in water and permanent medium (PVLG or PVLG + Melzer's reagent), plus black-and-white or color photographs and/or digital files of important characters from fresh material

### Microscopy Requirements

Most layers of spore cell walls are less than 10 μm thick, and flexible inner wall layers can be less than 1 μm thick. Inner walls may fold on each other, stick together, separate completely, or expand under pressure. Key considerations:

- Standard light microscopes may be adequate with excellent optics
- Differential interference contrast (DIC) systems enhance contrast between layer boundaries, making thin transparent structures visible
- Description of hyphal wall structure and the transition region between hypha and spore is important, as layers of the spore wall vary in their continuation from the subtending hyphal wall

## Challenges in Species Identification

More than 90% of published descriptions are vague or incomplete, including many redescribed taxa. Type specimens are often in poor condition with missing, parasitized, or degraded characters. Some older type specimens were preserved in lactophenol, which causes irreversible loss or degradation of characters and inhibition of histochemical reactions with long-term storage.

### Recommended Identification Protocol

1. Establish as close a match as possible between a specimen and a species description
2. Note all shared characters and any conflicting characters
3. Assign a species name equivocally and prepare permanent vouchers (whole spores preserved in 0.05% sodium azide, broken spores mounted in PVLG alone and PVLG + Melzer's reagent, plus photographs)
4. Contact an experienced taxonomist for verification
5. Seek specimens from living reference cultures of diagnosed species from repositories such as INVAM or BEG for comparison
6. If no match exists, designate the organism as undescribed and define it by an accession number
7. If established in culture, register with INVAM or BEG

## Mycorrhizal Structures as Taxonomic Characters

Mycorrhizal structures differ only at the family level and above, so they are not particularly informative for species description but are important for:

- Providing experimental evidence of membership in Glomales
- Defining variation within suborders and families

### Arbuscules

Arbuscules are the pivotal site of host–fungus communication. Each is composed of fine, dichotomously branched hyphae developing from a trunk hypha following penetration of a host cortical cell wall. A unique interface forms between the arbuscule cell wall and the host plasmalemma.

Staining intensity varies by family (darkest to faintest): Gigasporaceae > Glomaceae > Acaulosporaceae. Archaeosporaceae and Paraglomaceae stain very faintly or not at all.

### Intraradical Hyphae

- **Glomus**: Relatively straight, parallel along the root cortex long axis, often producing "H" branches
- **Acaulospora**: More irregularly branched and looped; thin-walled with variable staining intensity
- **Gigaspora and Scutellospora**: Wide hyphae with inflated/constricted regions producing knobs and projections; dense loops or coils in entry and cortical cells

### Vesicles

Produced only by suborder Glomineae. Thin-walled, saclike intercalary or terminal structures varying in shape by genus:
- Globose to ellipsoidal in *Glomus*
- Irregularly shaped to lobed in many *Acaulospora* and *Entrophospora* species

Vesicles can develop concurrently with arbuscule formation but often increase as arbuscular colonization declines. In some *Glomus* species, vesicles become thick-walled and indistinguishable from extraradical spores.

### Auxiliary Cells

Formed only by suborder Gigasporineae. Thin-walled cells forming as clusters on ends of branching external hyphae:
- Noticeable spiny ornamentations in *Gigaspora*
- Knobby to almost smooth surface in *Scutellospora*

Auxiliary cells often form on germ tubes from spores prior to mycorrhiza formation and decline in abundance as sporulation progresses. Evidence suggests they are not infective propagules.

## Extraradical Hyphae and Spore Formation

Extraradical hyphae vary in form and function:
- **Fine "absorptive" hyphae**: Grow beyond the root zone for nutrient uptake and translocation back to host roots
- **Coarse "runner" hyphae**: Extend along roots and initiate new colonization sites

The extent of external mycelium infectivity varies with taxonomic group. *Glomus* hyphal fragments are highly infective, but *Gigaspora* hyphae show no infectivity—pot cultures of *Gigaspora* lose all infectivity when mycorrhizal roots are present and all spores have died.

Spores are produced asexually, occurring singly on a sporogenous hypha, in aggregates, or within sporocarps. Spores are multinucleate; direct counts range from 2,600 to 3,850 nuclei per spore in *Gigaspora* species (Cooke et al. 1987).

## Microhabitat Descriptions

All [[fungal-hyphae]] are in direct contact with soil, making soil factors critical for:
- Spore germination and growth of infective propagules
- Growth of runner hyphae for mycorrhizal spread
- Growth and architecture of absorptive hyphae for nutrient uptake

Essential environmental data to record:
- Chemical and physical soil properties (especially pH and major cation concentrations)
- Host species identity, including plant community structure and density
- Climatological data (temperature, light, elevation, rainfall, seasonal patterns)
- Microbiological assays for general microbial activity

## Specimen Archiving

### Live Specimens

Viable spores can be extracted, washed 3–5 times, placed on filter paper, and dried slowly. Spores often collapse with desiccation but return to original shape with added water. Both spores and sporocarps may be stored in dry soil at 4°C or room temperature for 1–2 years, though viability differs among species. Fungal propagules in pot cultures can also be stored in liquid nitrogen, but success is highly variable.

Critical requirement: Pot contents must be dried slowly and without disturbance before transfer to cryovials. Propagules exposed to liquid do not survive freezing.

### Preserved Specimens

Whole spores can be stored in 2-ml vials containing:
- **0.05% sodium azide** (fewest morphological changes over time; preferred)
- **5% glutaraldehyde** (greatest morphological changes)
- **3.5% formalin**

All preservatives fail to prevent deterioration of some morphological features. Common preservatives such as FAA (formalin + acetic acid + alcohol) and lactophenol cause major changes in subcellular structure and should be avoided.

## Future Research Needs

Key unanswered questions include:
- Whether the most fecund fungi obtain the greatest resources from hosts and confer the greatest benefit
- The relationship between taxonomic diversity and functional diversity in AM fungal communities
- The role of molecular techniques in detecting individual species within roots and bulk soil
- Identification of indicator taxa sensitive to edaphic and environmental changes for ecosystem health monitoring
