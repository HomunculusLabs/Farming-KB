# Soil Health Indicators

Healthy soil can be assessed through biological, physical, and chemical indicators. While lab tests measure chemistry, the most important indicators of soil health are biological — visible signs of a thriving [[soil-food-web]].

## Biological Indicators

### Earthworms
- **The single most visible indicator of soil health.** If earthworms are present, the soil food web is functioning.
- A healthy garden soil should have 25-30 earthworms per cubic foot
- Earthworms aerate soil, create macropores for water infiltration, and their castings are rich in plant-available nutrients
- Worms appear only when there is organic matter to eat and soil chemistry isn't toxic
- No earthworms = chemical fertilizers, pesticides, or compaction have killed the food web

### Mycorrhizal Fungi
- Look for white/cream thread-like networks in the root zone
- Essential for phosphorus uptake and plant communication
- Destroyed by tillage and high-phosphorus synthetic fertilizers
- See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for details

### Soil Smell (Actinomycetes)
- Healthy soil has a rich, earthy smell (geosmin) produced by actinomycetes
- These filamentous bacteria are indicators of active decomposition
- Bad smells (sour, sulfurous, ammonia) indicate anaerobic conditions or incomplete decomposition

### Protozoa and Nematodes
- Require a microscope but are critical indicators
- Protozoa graze on bacteria, releasing plant-available nitrogen
- Beneficial nematodes indicate balanced predator-prey dynamics
- See [[soil-food-web-advanced]] for the full trophic cascade

## Physical Indicators

### Aggregate Structure
- Healthy soil forms crumbly aggregates (cheese-like structure) when pressed
- Aggregates are held together by fungal hyphae, bacterial glues, and root exudates
- Compacted soil with no structure indicates dead biology

### Water Infiltration
- Healthy soil absorbs water quickly (1+ inches per hour)
- Water should infiltrate, not run off
- Poor infiltration = compaction, lack of macropores, dead food web

### Root Growth Patterns
- Healthy plants develop extensive, branching root systems
- Mycorrhizal associations extend effective root zone 10-100x
- Poor root development often indicates biological deficiency, not nutrient deficiency

### Earthworm Castings on Surface
- Visible castings indicate active worm populations
- Castings contain 5x more N, 7x more P, 11x more K than surrounding soil

## Chemical Indicators

### Organic Matter Percentage
- 3-5% organic matter is the minimum for healthy soil biology
- Each 1% increase in OM increases water-holding capacity by ~20,000 gallons per acre
- OM feeds bacteria and fungi that cycle nutrients

### Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)
- Measures soil's ability to hold and exchange nutrients
- Clay soils: 10-40+ meq/100g; Sandy soils: 1-10 meq/100g
- Higher CEC = more nutrient buffering capacity

### pH Range
- 6.0-7.0 is ideal for most crops
- Extreme pH locks out nutrients regardless of how much is present
- See [[soil-testing]] for proper testing methods

## The Shovelmeter Test

The simplest soil health assessment:

1. **Dig a hole** 6-12 inches deep
2. **Count earthworms** in one cubic foot of soil (target: 25+)
3. **Smell the soil** (earthy = good, sour/bad = anaerobic)
4. **Feel the texture** (crumbly aggregates = good, hard clods = poor)
5. **Check root patterns** (branching = good, stunted = problems)
6. **Look for organic matter** (dark color = high OM)

## Key Rule

> "If it smells good, has worms, and crumbles nicely, your soil biology is working. If not, feed it organic matter and stop killing it with chemicals."
