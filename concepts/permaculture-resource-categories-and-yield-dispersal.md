# Resource Categories and Yield Dispersal in Permaculture

## Overview

Understanding resources — how to classify them, manage them, and maximize their productive use — is central to [[permaculture-design]]. Mollison developed a detailed framework for categorizing resources based on how they respond to use, and a complementary set of strategies for dispersing food yield over time. Together, these frameworks help designers ensure that systems produce continuously, waste nothing, and degrade no resource.

## The Five Categories of Resources

Mollison classified resources into five categories based on their response to use. This classification is not merely academic — it directly informs management decisions about which resources to use freely, which to conserve, and which to avoid entirely.

### Category 1: Resources That Increase by Modest Use

These resources become more abundant or more valuable when used wisely. Examples include:

- **Information**: Sharing knowledge does not diminish it; it grows through application and feedback.
- **Pasture under moderate grazing**: Grazing stimulates root growth and nutrient cycling.
- **Human skills**: Skills improve with practice and use.
- **Soil microbiota**: Activity stimulates reproduction and diversity.
- **Seed stocks**: Properly managed, seed saving increases genetic diversity and local adaptation.

The management implication: use these resources actively and generously.

### Category 2: Resources Unaffected by Use

These resources can be used without diminishing their availability. Examples include:

- **Sunlight**: Capturing solar energy does not reduce the sun's output.
- **Wind**: Wind passing through a turbine continues past it.
- **Gravity**: Water flowing downhill can be used at multiple points along its path.
- **Landscape views**: A scenic outlook is not consumed by being enjoyed.
- **Rainfall on a roof**: Collecting rainwater does not reduce the total rainfall.

The management implication: capture and use these resources at every opportunity — they are truly free.

### Category 3: Resources That Disappear or Degrade if Not Used

These are perhaps the most underappreciated category. Resources that go unused represent waste. Examples include:

- **Annual crops**: If not harvested, they decompose or go to seed and are lost as food.
- **Seasonal water flows**: Water running past a property in winter cannot be used in summer unless stored.
- **Human skills**: Abilities that atrophy without practice.
- **Animal manure**: If not collected and composted, nutrients leach away.
- **Fallen fruit**: If not gathered or fed to animals, it becomes a disease vector.
- **[[green-manure-crops]]**: Must be incorporated at the right time or their benefit is lost.

The management implication: design systems that ensure these resources are captured and used, not wasted through neglect.

### Category 4: Resources Reduced by Use

These resources diminish with every use and must be managed with conservation in mind. Examples include:

- **Soil minerals**: Every harvest exports nutrients that must eventually be replaced.
- **Fossil fuels**: Finite and non-renewable.
- **Old-growth timber**: Centuries to replace, minutes to harvest.
- **Topsoil**: Extremely slow to form, easily eroded.
- **Groundwater from fossil aquifers**: Withdrawn faster than it recharges.

The management implication: minimize use, maximize efficiency, and plan for replacement or substitution with Category 1 or 2 resources.

### Category 5: Resources That Pollute or Destroy Other Resources if Used

These resources cause systemic damage when employed. Their use is antithetical to [[permaculture-ethics]]. Examples include:

- **Persistent poisons**: Pesticides that accumulate in food chains and destroy beneficial organisms.
- **Radioactives**: Nuclear waste that contaminates for millennia.
- **Large areas of concrete and highways**: Seal soil, prevent water infiltration, create heat islands.
- **Sewers from city to sea**: Export nutrients to marine systems where they cause eutrophication.
- **Salt-based fertilizers in excess**: Destroy soil structure and microbial life over time.

The management implication: ban the use of these resources entirely, or accept the system-wide consequences.

## Policy of Resource Management

> A responsible human society bans the use of resources which permanently reduce yields of sustainable resources — pollutants, persistent poisons, radioactives, large areas of concrete and highways, sewers from city to sea.

This policy extends Mollison's ethics into practical governance. It establishes a clear hierarchy: sustainable resources take precedence over extractive ones, and no resource use should compromise the long-term yield of the system.

## The Principle of Disorder

> Any system or organism can accept only that quantity of a resource which can be used productively. Any resource input beyond that point throws the system or organism into disorder; oversupply of a resource is a form of chronic pollution.

This principle has profound implications:

- **Over-fertilization** burns crops and pollutes waterways.
- **Over-irrigation** waterlogs soil and leaches nutrients.
- **Over-feeding** livestock creates metabolic disease and waste.
- **Over-information** (noise, clutter) impairs decision-making.

The boundary between "enough" and "too much" is a critical design parameter. The designer must identify the productive capacity of each component and match resource inputs to that capacity.

## Dispersal of Food Yield Over Time

One of the great challenges in food production is ensuring a continuous supply rather than feast-or-famine cycles. Mollison outlined multiple strategies for spreading yield across the calendar:

### By Variety Selection

- **Early, mid, and late season varieties**: Planting varieties that mature at different times spreads harvest across months.
- **Same variety in different situations**: Planting the same variety in early-ripening (warm, sheltered) and late-ripening (cool, exposed) microsites extends the harvest window.

### By Plant Characteristics

- **Long-yielding varieties**: Cherry tomatoes, indeterminate beans, and chard produce continuously over extended periods.
- **Self-storing species**: Tubers, hard seeds, fuelwood, and rhizomes can be "cropped on demand" — harvested as needed over months.

### By System Design

- **General diversity increase**: When leaf, fruit, seed, and root are all product yields, different harvest times are built into the system.
- **Mixed annuals and perennials**: Annuals peak in summer; perennials provide steady, lower-volume production over the full year.

### By Processing and Storage

- **Preserving**: Fermentation, canning, freezing, and pickling extend availability.
- **Drying**: Dehydrated fruits, herbs, and meats store for months to years.
- **Pitting**: Root cellars and earth pits provide cool, humid storage without energy input.
- **Cool storage**: Simple insulated storage rooms extend the life of many crops.

### By Spatial Strategy

- **Regional trade**: Communities at different latitudes or altitudes can exchange products at different seasons.
- **Altitudinal transects**: In mountainous regions, lower sites produce earlier and higher sites later.

## The Principle of Cyclic Opportunity

> Every cyclic event increases the opportunity for yield. To increase cycling is to increase yield.

[[cycles-in-nature]] are diversion routes away from entropic ends. Life itself cycles nutrients, creating opportunities for yield and thus opportunities for species to occupy time niches. The more cycles a designer can incorporate — nutrient cycles, water cycles, energy cycles, information cycles — the more yield the system produces.

### Types of Niches

1. **Niche in space** ("territory"): nest sites, forage areas, roosting locations.
2. **Niche in time** (cycles of opportunity): seasonal availability, diurnal patterns, successional stages.
3. **Niche in space-time** (schedules): specific locations at specific times — a pond edge in spring, a shaded understory in summer.

Designing for all three niche types maximizes the number of species and functions a system can support.

## The Principle of Order and Disorder

> Order and harmony produce energy for other uses. Disorder consumes energy to no useful end.

Neatness, tidiness, uniformity, and straightness signify an energy-maintained disorder in natural systems. A bare, weeded, perfectly straight row of a single crop is maintained only through constant energy input (weeding, pest control, fertilization). A polycultural [[food-forest]], by contrast, appears messy but produces more total yield with less energy input. The apparent disorder is actually a higher order of functional integration.

## Practical Design Applications

1. **Audit your resources**: Classify every resource on your site into the five categories. This reveals management priorities.
2. **Ban Category 5**: Identify and eliminate resources that pollute or destroy other resources.
3. **Capture Category 3**: Design systems that prevent seasonal or perishable resources from going to waste.
4. **Maximize Category 2**: Harvest sunlight, wind, and gravity at every opportunity.
5. **Invest in Category 1**: Prioritize resources that grow through use — information, skills, soil life.
6. **Conserve Category 4**: Minimize extraction of finite resources and plan for their replacement.
7. **Design for continuous yield**: Use multiple dispersal strategies to ensure year-round food availability.
8. **Increase cycling**: Every additional cycle — nutrient, water, energy — increases total system yield.
9. **Identify the disorder threshold**: Know how much of any resource is productive, and stop before oversupply.

## Key Definitions

- **System yield**: The sum total of surplus energy produced by, stored, conserved, reused, or converted by the design.
- **The role of life in yield**: Living things are the only effective intervening systems to capture resources and produce yield.
- **Limits to yield**: Yield is not a fixed sum — it is the measure of the designer's comprehension, understanding, and ability.
