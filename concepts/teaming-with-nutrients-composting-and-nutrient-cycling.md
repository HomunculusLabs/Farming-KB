## Earthworm Castings

Earthworm castings concentrate nutrients dramatically compared to source soil: 10x more potassium, 5x more nitrogen, 7x more phosphorus, 3x more magnesium, 1.5x more calcium, and 1.4x more humus. Worms ingest organic matter primarily to consume bacteria, protozoa, and fungi, processing the rest into nutrient-rich castings. A single acre with a healthy earthworm population can produce several tons of castings per year.

## Soil Food Web Integration

The soil food web is the engine of nutrient cycling. Bacteria and fungi break down organic matter; protozoa and nematodes consume bacteria, releasing plant-available ammonium; mycorrhizal fungi deliver phosphorus, copper, zinc, and nitrogen to roots; and all contribute to soil structure through their physical activities and metabolic byproducts. ## Compost Tea and Biological Inoculants

Compost tea — a liquid extract produced by steeping finished compost in aerated water — has gained popularity as a method of delivering beneficial microorganisms to soil and plant surfaces. Aerated compost tea (ACT) is brewed with continuous aeration to maintain oxygen levels that support aerobic microbial growth, typically for 24-48 hours. The resulting liquid contains billions of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes per milliliter, along with soluble nutrients and microbial metabolites. When applied as a soil drench, compost tea inoculates the rhizosphere with beneficial organisms that enhance nutrient cycling and suppress pathogens. When applied as a foliar spray, it can colonize leaf surfaces with competitive microorganisms that occupy infection sites and produce antimicrobial compounds. While scientific evidence for compost tea efficacy is mixed and highly dependent on compost quality and brewing conditions, the underlying principle — that inoculating soil and plant surfaces with diverse microbial communities enhances plant health — is well supported by ecological research into the soil food web and plant microbiome.

## Mulching and Surface Nutrient Cycling

Mulching — the application of organic materials (leaves, straw, wood chips, compost) to the soil surface — is one of the simplest and most effective practices for maintaining nutrient cycling in gardens and landscapes. Surface mulches serve multiple functions: they suppress weed growth by blocking light, reduce moisture loss through evaporation, moderate soil temperature fluctuations, and provide a slow-release source of organic matter as they decompose. As mulch breaks down from the bottom up, it creates a gradient of organic matter incorporation into the soil surface, mimicking the natural leaf litter layer of forest ecosystems. Wood chip mulches in particular support fungal decomposition pathways, favoring the growth of saprophytic fungi that contribute to soil aggregation and long-term carbon storage. The "ramial chipped wood" technique — using small-diameter branch wood chips as mulch — has been promoted by researchers in Quebec as particularly effective for building soil organic matter and supporting mycorrhizal networks, because branch wood has a higher proportion of soluble nutrients and cellulose relative to heartwood.

## Sheet Mulching (Lasagna Gardening)

Sheet mulching, also known as lasagna gardening, is a no-till technique that combines composting and mulching into a single process. Layers of cardboard or newspaper (to suppress existing vegetation), followed by alternating layers of green (nitrogen-rich) and brown (carbon-rich) organic materials, are built up directly on the soil surface. Over several months, these layers decompose in place, creating a rich, friable planting bed without the need for digging or tilling. Sheet mulching mimics the natural accumulation of organic matter on forest floors and preserves soil structure, mycorrhizal networks, and microbial communities that would be disrupted by mechanical cultivation. The technique is particularly effective for converting lawn areas to garden beds and for establishing perennial plantings. Its primary limitation is the large volume of organic material required, but for gardeners with access to abundant yard waste, autumn leaves, or municipal green waste, sheet mulching offers a labor-efficient path to nutrient-rich soil.

## Anaerobic Decomposition and Biogas

While aerobic composting is the most common form of managed decomposition, anaerobic processes also play important roles in nutrient cycling. [[biogas-digester-basics]] of organic waste in enclosed systems produces biogas (primarily methane and carbon dioxide) that can be captured and used as fuel, while the remaining digestate serves as a nutrient-rich soil amendment. Biogas systems are widely used in agricultural settings, particularly in Asia, where small-scale digesters process animal manure and crop residues. The digestate retains most of the nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium of the original feedstock in forms that are readily available to plants. While not practical for most home gardeners, anaerobic decomposition illustrates the broader principle that all organic materials eventually cycle back to soil — the question is whether the energy and nutrients they contain are captured and used or lost to the environment.
