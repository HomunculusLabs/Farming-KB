# AACT Fungal Foods — Chitin, Rock Dust, Protozoa, and Beneficial Nematodes

Achieving fungal dominance in [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]] Tea (AACT) requires specific food resources and inoculation strategies that differ markedly from bacterial-dominant brewing. Fungi are essential for perennial crops, trees, shrubs, and ornamentals, and they play a critical role in [[ingham-aact-disease-suppression-through-food-competition]] competitive exclusion and direct antagonism of plant pathogens.

## Why Fungal Dominance Matters

The soil foodweb shifts along a successional gradient from bacterial dominance (early succession, disturbed soils, annual plant communities) [[query-how-does-soil-naturally-transition-from-bacterial-to-fungal-dominance]] (late succession, stable soils, perennial and woody plant communities). Different crops require different fungal-to-bacterial ratios:

- **Vegetables and annuals**: Prefer bacterial-dominated soils (F:B ratio of 1:1 or lower)
- **Row crops and grasses**: Prefer slightly fungal soils (F:B ratio around 1:1)
- **Perennials and shrubs**: Prefer fungal-dominated soils (F:B ratio of 5:1 to 10:1)
- **Trees and conifers**: Require strongly fungal soils (F:B ratio of 10:1 to 100:1)

## Bacterial vs. Fungal Teas

### Bacterial Dominance
Achieved by adding simple sugars (molasses, fruit juice) that bacteria can rapidly metabolize. Bacterial teas are characterized by rapid oxygen consumption early in the brew cycle and a sweet or earthy smell. These teas are ideal for:

- Vegetable starts and transplants
- Lawn and turf restoration
- Annual flower beds
- Soils compacted or recently disturbed

### Fungal Dominance
Achieved by adding complex carbon compounds that bacteria cannot quickly break down, giving fungi a competitive advantage. Fungal teas require:

- Longer pre-activation periods for fungal inoculum
- Careful aeration management (fungi are more sensitive to turbulence)
- Coarser compost containing visible fungal hyphae
- Food resources that select for fungal metabolism

## Fungal Food Resources

### Compost Selection for Fungal Tea
The compost used as the fungal inoculum must itself be fungal-dominated. This requires:

- **Forest litter compost**: Made from deciduous or conifer leaf fall, woody debris, and forest duff
- **Wood chip compost**: Containing significant woody material (C:N ratio above 50:1)
- **[[duggar-mushroom-compost-fermentation-biology]]**: Spent substrate from [[history-of-commercial-mushroom-cultivation]] production, already colonized by fungal mycelium

[[aact-vermicompost-vs-thermal-compost-ingham]] made primarily from manures and green materials tends to be bacterial-dominated and is not suitable as the primary inoculum for fungal tea.

### Rock Dust
Finely ground volcanic or glacial rock dust serves multiple functions in fungal tea:

1. **Physical substrate**: Rock particles provide surfaces for fungal hyphae to attach and grow upon
2. **Mineral nutrition**: Slow-release minerals including silica, calcium, iron, [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]]
3. **pH modification**: Some rock dusts (particularly basalt) can help buffer pH toward neutral
4. **Fungal stimulation**: The mineral matrix provides a structure that fungi can colonize and extract nutrients from

Application rate: **2–4 tablespoons per 5 gallons** of tea. The dust should be finely ground (passing 200-mesh screen) for maximum surface area.

### Chitin and Crab Waste
Chitin, the structural polysaccharide found in crab shells, insect exoskeletons, and fungal cell walls, is one of the most important fungal food resources:

- **Granulated crab waste** provides both chitin and calcium carbonate from the shells
- Chitin stimulates the growth of **chitinolytic fungi** and bacteria that produce chitinase enzymes
- These chitin-decomposing organisms are the same ones that attack fungal pathogens (which also contain chitin in their cell walls)
- This creates a **dual benefit**: feeding beneficial fungi while simultaneously selecting for organisms antagonistic to pathogenic fungi

Application rate: **1–2 tablespoons of granulated crab waste per 5 gallons** of tea

### Humic Acids as Fungal Food
As detailed in the companion ingredient page, humic acids are complex long-chain carbon molecules that serve as the primary soluble fungal food in most AACT recipes. They provide:

- A slow-release carbon source that bacteria cannot monopolize
- Chelating capacity that keeps micronutrients bioavailable for fungal metabolism
- pH conditions (slightly acidic) that favor fungal growth over bacterial growth

### Plant Materials as Fungal Foods
Fresh or dried plant materials can serve as fungal food sources:

- **Oatmeal**: A traditional fungal food that provides complex carbohydrates
- **Wood chips**: Added to compost pre-brew to increase fungal biomass
- **Dry leaf material**: Oak, maple, and other deciduous leaves support diverse fungal communities

These materials should be added to the compost pile (not directly to the tea) to pre-culture fungal biomass before brewing.

## Fungal Preactivation

Fungi grow more slowly than bacteria and require a **preactivation step** before brewing:

1. **Prepare activation medium**: A mixture of compost, fungal foods (humic acid, rock dust), and water at field capacity moisture
2. **Incubate**: Allow the mixture to sit for 24–48 hours at warm temperatures (65–75°F / 18–24°C)
