## Overview

Mycoremediation is an infant science to humans but a highly refined method used by nature for millions of years. The enzymatic versatility of fungi — particularly white rot mushrooms — allows them to dismantle lignin (the most recalcitrant natural polymer), and these same enzymes can break down many synthetic toxins with similar molecular bonds.

## Why Fungi Work for Remediation

### The Lignin Connection
Lignin has hydrogen-carbon bonds similar to those in many pollutants. White rot fungi evolved enzymes (lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, laccases) to break these bonds. The same enzymes can degrade:
- Petroleum hydrocarbons (oil, gasoline, diesel, coal tar)
- Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)
- Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs)
- Dioxins and chlorobenzenes
- Pesticides (DDT, aldrin, chlordane, dieldrin, lindane, atrazine)
- Pentachlorophenol
- Chemical warfare agents (VX, sarin, soman)
- Heavy metals (bioaccumulation and concentration)
- Coliform bacteria and pathogens
- Synthetic dyes and colorants

### Bioaccumulation of Heavy Metals
Mushrooms can concentrate heavy metals from contaminated substrates:
- *Agaricus bisporus* bioaccumulates arsenic, cadmium, copper, lead
- *Boletus edulis* concentrates cesium-137 (studied after Chernobyl)
- Mushrooms at Chernobyl concentrated cesium and cesium-137, some at 10,000 times background levels

## Basic Techniques

### 1. Matching Species to Toxins
The first step is identifying which mushroom species are known to neutralize the target contaminant. Stamets' cross-index charts (in *Mycelium Running*) match mushroom species with specific toxins:

| Species | Key Toxins |
|---|---|
| *Pleurotus ostreatus* (oyster) | Petroleum, PAHs, bacteria, coliforms, E. coli |
| *Trametes versicolor* (turkey tail) | PAHs, PCBs, dioxins, bacteria |
| *Phanerochaete chrysosporium* | PAHs, PCBs, dioxins, chemical warfare agents |
| *Grifola frondosa* (maitake) | PAHs, PCBs, pentachlorophenol, pesticides |
| *Ganoderma lucidum* (reishi) | Heavy metals, bacteria |
| *Stropharia rugoso-annulata* (garden giant) | Coliform bacteria, silt, E. coli |
| *Agaricus brasiliensis* | Heavy metals, bacteria |
| *Lentinula edodes* (shiitake) | PAHs, heavy metals, bacteria |
| *Bjerkandera adusta* | PAHs, dyes |
| *Armillaria ostoyae* | Petroleum, heavy metals |

### 2. Strain Selection and Adaptation
Not all strains within a species perform equally. The process for adapting strains:
1. Collect samples from contaminated sites — native mushrooms are already adapted
2. If no suitable native species exists, import and train strains
3. Train mycelium by introducing small amounts of the target toxin to nutrient agar, incrementally increasing concentration while decreasing other nutrients
4. Select the fastest-growing, most aggressive strains
5. Starving mycelia will adapt their enzymatic pathways to digest toxins as food

### 3. Inoculation Methods
- **Spore oils**: Mix spores into canola oil (100 million spores/liter optimal); apply by cutting through wood or painting onto surfaces
- **Spawn**: Use grain, sawdust, or dowel spawn to inoculate wood chip beds or burlap bags
- **Burlap bag spawn**: Fill burlap sacks with myceliated substrate; stack at contamination sites
- **Wood chip beds**: Layer wood chips with spawn in trenches or berms; cover with soil
- **Cardboard spawn**: Germinate spores on corrugated cardboard; cardboard naturally selects for gourmet mushrooms over contaminants

### 4. Mycofiltration (Water-Based)
Mycofiltration uses mycelium as a membrane for filtering water:
- Install myceliated wood chip beds in swales and drainage paths
- Layer myceliated substrate in filtration chambers
- One gram of soil infused with mycelium contains more than a mile of threadlike cells
- These fine filaments function as a cellular net that catches particles and digests them
- Demonstrated 100-fold reduction in fecal coliforms in field trials at Skookum Inlet, WA

### 5. Soil Remediation
- Layer contaminated soil with myceliated wood chips or straw
- Mycelium grows through the soil, secreting enzymes that break down contaminants
- Multiple treatments may be needed for heavily contaminated sites
- Composting the spent mycelium after remediation further concentrates or destroys toxins

### 6. Petroleum Spill Cleanup
Oyster mushrooms have been shown to fruit from containers filled with crude oil. The process:
1. Contain the spill with berms of myceliated substrate
2. Inoculate contaminated soil/wood with oyster mushroom spawn
3. Mycelium secretes enzymes that break down hydrocarbons
4. Mushrooms fruit, concentrating heavy metals (do NOT eat these mushrooms)
5. Spent substrate is removed and properly disposed of

## Proven Results

### Battelle Memorial Institute Research
- Collaborative research between Stamets and Battelle (approved Dept. of Defense test facility)
- Demonstrated oyster mushrooms can decompose petroleum hydrocarbons
- Customized strains to neutralize chemical warfare agents by training on surrogate neurotoxins
- Found mushroom strains with enzymes for making and breaking phosphorus bonds (critical to nerve gas structure)

### Skookum Inlet Mycofiltration
- *Stropharia rugoso-annulata* beds installed on watershed
- 100-fold drop in fecal coliforms despite doubling livestock
- Also served as fish food source (salmon fed on fly larvae from mushrooms)

### Chernobyl Research
- Mushrooms concentrated cesium-137 at up to 10,000 times background levels
- Demonstrates both the risk (bioaccumulation) and potential (phytoremediation) of fungi in radioactive environments

## Limitations and Cautions

- Mycoremediated mushrooms that have absorbed heavy metals or toxins should NOT be eaten
- Strain variability means results are not always predictable
- Some contaminants require specific enzymes not all species produce
- Complete degradation may require multiple treatments or species combinations
- The science is still developing — field conditions differ from laboratory results
- Regulatory approval may be needed for commercial remediation projects
