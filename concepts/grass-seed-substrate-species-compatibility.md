# Grass Seed Substrate Species Compatibility for Psilocybe and Panaeolus Cultivation

Grass seed serves as a versatile base substrate for cultivating several psychoactive mushroom species.
Different species exhibit varying degrees of compatibility with grass seed, with some producing both
mushrooms and sclerotia on this medium. The method was documented by the cultivator known as "Guid"
and represents a practical approach accessible to home cultivators using readily available materials.

## Species Suitable for Grass Seed Cultivation

The primary species documented as cultivable on sterilised grass seed include four notable taxa,
each with distinct requirements and outcomes on this substrate:

### Psilocybe mexicana

Produces both mushrooms and sclerotia on grass seed.
This species is among the most reliable sclerotia formers when grown on this substrate.
Fruiting typically produces one large flush followed by a smaller second flush.
The mushrooms are smaller than those of P. cubensis but contain psychoactive alkaloids.
Psilocybe mexicana does not benefit from cold shock treatment.
The species is notable for being the original source from which Albert Hofmann first
isolated and named psilocybin and psilocin.

### Psilocybe tampanensis

Also produces both mushrooms and sclerotia on grass seed.
Sclerotia of this species continue to enlarge for up to 4 months after inoculation
when left in the colonised jar without casing.
This species does not benefit from cold shock treatment.
The sclerotia of P. tampanensis are sometimes referred to as "philosopher's stones"
in the underground mycological community.
Like P. mexicana, the jars can simply be stored in a clean, preferably dark location
for sclerotia production without any fruiting chamber setup.

### Psilocybe cubensis

Can be cultivated on grass seed but rye grain is explicitly preferred for this species.
Grass seed remains a workable alternative when rye is unavailable,
though fruiting may be less vigorous.
Some strains of cubensis, particularly the Ecuador variety, are noted as easy fruiting
strains that do not require cold shock.
Harder-to-fruit strains benefit from cold shocking in the refrigerator for one night
before being placed in the fruiting chamber.
P. cubensis on grass seed typically produces 3 flushes, but properly watered casings
may yield 5 or even 6 flushes.

### Panaeolus subbalteatus

Produces mushrooms on grass seed.
This species does not produce sclerotia and does not benefit from cold shock treatment.
It represents a more challenging species to cultivate compared to P. cubensis.
Flush production is more constant across multiple flushes compared to the
single-dominant-flush pattern of P. mexicana.

## Sclerotia-Forming Versus Non-Sclerotia-Forming Species

A critical distinction in grass seed cultivation is between species that form sclerotia
and those that only produce mushrooms. This distinction determines the entire
cultivation workflow and level of complexity required:

**Sclerotia formers** (P. mexicana, P. tampanensis):

- Sclerotia develop within the grain jar itself without requiring casing
- Jars can be stored in a clean, preferably dark location for sclerotia production
- No fruiting chamber needed if only sclerotia are desired, greatly simplifying the process
- Sclerotia also form in the casing layer if the grain is cased for mushroom production
- Harvest window extends to approximately 4 months post-inoculation for maximum sclerotia size
- This method eliminates several contamination-prone steps in the cultivation process

**Non-sclerotia formers** (P. cubensis, P. subbalteatus):

- Require casing and fruiting chamber setup for mushroom production
- Must follow the full colonisation, casing, and fruiting cycle
- Cannot be harvested directly from the jar as mushrooms will not form without a casing layer
- More labour-intensive and expose the culture to additional contamination risk

## Grass Seed Selection and Fungicide Risk

The choice of grass seed matters considerably for cultivation success.
Rye grass seed (Lolium perenne) is the most commonly used variety,
though mixtures of different grass species also work well according to the source documentation.

The primary concern is fungicide treatment:
seed sold with fungicidal coatings will not support fungal growth and must be strictly avoided.
Seed packaging typically indicates whether fungicides have been applied.
Cultivators should carefully read labels and may need to enquire directly with suppliers.

Cost-effective sourcing is important for cultivators working with multiple jars.
Animal feed stores where grass seed is sold as bird feed often offer the best prices,
typically far cheaper than garden centre seed marketed for lawns.

## Water Absorption Variability

Different varieties of grass seed, and even different batches of the same variety,
can differ greatly in their ability to absorb water.
This variability has direct implications for substrate preparation and culture success:

- **Excess water** produces a slimy clump of seed that cannot be shaken,
  leading to anaerobic conditions, poor colonisation, and high contamination risk.
- **Insufficient water** results in substrate that is too dry,
  producing little or no mushrooms or sclerotia.
- **Overnight soaking** before jar filling produces a more homogenous substrate
  and mitigates batch-to-batch variability.

The standard formula for 720 ml jars is 110 grams of grass seed
combined with 180 ml of water, but these ratios may require adjustment
based on the specific seed batch being used.

## Colonisation Timeline and Technique

Colonisation speed varies by species, temperature, and inoculation method.
On grass seed, full colonisation typically requires 5 to 20 days.
Periodic shaking to redistribute colonised kernels dramatically accelerates the process.
When mycelium starts growing in only a few spots, shaking the jars
redistributes colonised kernels throughout the substrate.

## Fruiting Chamber Considerations

The source notes that elaborate fruiting chamber setups with humidifiers
and cool-mist devices are not necessary.
Simple clear plastic bins covered with polyethylene sheeting,
with holes melted in the sides for air exchange, suffice.
Evaporation from the casing surface of five trays placed in one bin
provides adequate humidity.
