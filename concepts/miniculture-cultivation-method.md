# Miniculture Cultivation Method for Psilocybe cubensis

## Overview

Bigwood and Beug (1982) adapted existing grain cultivation techniques into
a "miniculture" system for growing [[psilocybe-cubensis]] under controlled
laboratory conditions. This scaled-down method was designed to produce
multiple sequential flushes from individual cultures suitable for
quantitative alkaloid analysis, enabling the first systematic study of
[[psilocybin-psilocin-variation-across-flushes]] in a single species.

## Historical Development

The miniculture technique was built upon three prior methodological
contributions spanning a decade of mycological research:

1. **San Antonio (1971)**: Developed the original rye-grain substrate
   method for [[agaricus-bisporus]] (the common [[growing-gourmet-button-mushroom-agaricus-cultivation]]) cultivation
   using cased grain spawn in jars. This established the fundamental
   principle that colonized grain could be cased to initiate fruiting
   in a container-based system.

2. **Oss and Oeric (1976)**: Refined and adapted the rye-grain method
   specifically for psilocybin [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]. Their influential
   Psilocybin [[australian-magic-mushroom-common-names]] Grower's Guide documented the technique
   for a wider audience and established rye grain as the standard
   substrate for home [[grass-seed-cultivation-of-psilocybe-mushrooms]] species.

3. **Bigwood and Beug (1982)**: Scaled the method down to half-pint
   jar format for laboratory reproducibility and systematic alkaloid
   sampling across multiple flushes from genetically identical cultures.

## Strain Origin

The strain used in the primary experiment originated from a spore print
collected in the Amazon basin near Pucallpa, Peru. This spore print was
previously documented by Repke, Leslie, and Guaman (1977) in their study
of baeocystin distribution across psilocybin-producing genera. Mycelium
obtained from the spore print was maintained as a stock culture on
various agar media, providing a genetically consistent inoculum source
for all experimental minicultures.

## Substrate Preparation

Each miniculture used a wide-mouth half-pint jar (approximately 250 ml
volume) charged with 10 grams of rye grain and 15 ml of water. The jar
was autoclaved to sterilize the substrate before inoculation. The simple
two-ingredient formula of grain plus water minimized compositional
variables that could affect alkaloid production, making the system
suitable for controlled scientific experiments where reproducibility
was paramount.

## Inoculation and Colonization Protocol

Under sterile conditions, each jar was inoculated with mycelium
transferred from the agar stock culture. A critical step in the
colonization protocol was periodic agitation: every four days for a
28-day colonization period, the jars were shaken manually to distribute
the growing mycelium evenly across the grain surface. This agitation
prevented the mycelium from forming a dense mat at the inoculation
point and instead promoted uniform colonization of the entire grain
bed, which in turn supported more even fruiting.

## Casing Layer Composition and Application

After 28 days of colonization, when the grain was fully colonized, the
jars were opened and the grain was cased with a layer approximately
2 cm deep. The casing mixture consisted of three components in specific
proportions:

- **2 parts peat moss**: Provides organic matter and moisture retention
- **1 part calcium carbonate**: Buffers pH to neutral or slightly
  alkaline conditions favorable for pin formation
- **2 parts perlite and/or vermiculite**: Provides aeration and
  additional moisture retention while maintaining a loose,
  non-compacting structure
