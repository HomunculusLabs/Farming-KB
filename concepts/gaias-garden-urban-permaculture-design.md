### Small Lot Design

On a typical 1/4-acre suburban lot, Hemenway's zone system compresses to fit:
- **Zone 1** may begin only 15 feet from the door and end near the neighbor's fence
- Salad greens, herbs, dwarf fruit trees, patio, lawn, and other often-used items go in Zone 1
- **Zone 2** holds row crops, berries, useful shrubs, a pond, chickens, and a food forest
- **Zone 3** holds larger fruit and nut trees (walnuts, chestnuts, bamboo)
- **Zone 4** is for foraging and firewood, often minimal on a small lot
- A corner of the yard is left wild for Zone 5

### Regulations and Neighbor Relations

Practical urban considerations Hemenway addresses:
- Anti-chicken ordinances exist in many municipalities, though some allow hens (but not roosters)
- Chicken yards, duck pens, and other animal systems may require creative design to comply with local rules
- The biggest obstacles to keeping animals in the city are often the neighbors, who may need gentle education about the animals' benefits
- Supplying neighbors with occasional eggs or produce can quiet concerns
- Muscovy ducks are specifically recommended for urban settings because they are very quiet

## Urban Permaculture Design Strategies

### Zone-and-Sector Planning for Small Lots

Even on a small urban lot, zones and sectors apply:
- **Zones** organize elements by frequency of use and attention needed
- **Sectors** map the energies entering the site (sun, wind, wildlife, views, fire, pollution, crime)
- Fire sectors should always be considered, even in the city -- plant fire-resistant species and leave areas open
- Wildlife sectors exist even in cities -- marauding deer, raccoons, cedar waxwings, or feral cats
- Pollution sectors (noise, smells, power lines) and crime sectors (dark alleys) are particularly relevant to urban design

### The Zone 1 Garden

In small yards, the Zone 1 garden is critical. Hemenway's own experience is instructive:
- For years, their main garden was 100 feet from the house, requiring a "trudge" that made it feel like work
- They built an unobtrusive fence enclosing a semicircle around half the house
- Inside, they sheet mulched heavily and planted a dense blend of useful and ornamental plants
- The difference was dramatic: weeding happens constantly because someone is always there; grass can no longer encroach from all sides; sandwich greens come from a mesclun bed outside the study door

Zone 1 design principles for urban lots:
- Abandon straight-row gardening; curve beds, follow contours
- Create attractive blends of perennials, annuals, salad herbs, shrubs, insect-attracting flowers, and nutrient-accumulating plants
- Integrate food production with beauty -- the garden should look good to neighbors
- Place the chicken coop at the Zone 1/Zone 2 boundary for efficiency

### Maximizing Three Dimensions

Urban lots are limited in area but not in vertical space:
- A garden that uses trees and shrubs in three dimensions offers maximum wildlife habitat, largest possible crop yields, and greatest possibilities for edge and diversity
- Dwarf and naturally small tree species fit together to provide biodiversity even in small yards
- Vines on trellises and fences add production without consuming ground space
- Espalier fruit trees against fences or walls produce food while serving as screens

### Food Forests in Small Spaces

A small urban lot can still support a food forest:
- In a smaller property, dwarf and naturally small species fit together to provide biodiversity
- Northern gardeners need a more open array of trees to allow weaker sunlight to reach ground layers
- Southern gardeners may desire dense spacing for needed shade
- Vegetable and flower beds can be located on the sunward side of the forest garden
- Even a modest fruit tree guild can produce significant food while creating habitat

## Urban Water Strategies

Hemenway's water chapter has special relevance for urban gardeners:
- In a city where water bills in summer can reach alarming levels, water harvesting is especially valuable
- Swales, rain barrels, and greywater systems reduce municipal water dependence
- Most urban soil is low in organic matter and thus holds little water -- building soil organic content is the first step to water conservation
- A city storm drain can serve as overflow for boggy yard water
- Mulching is critical in urban settings to retain moisture and reduce irrigation needs

## Urban Animal Integration

### Chickens

- Place the coop at the Zone 1/Zone 2 boundary
- Use [[gaias-garden-duck-integration]] is especially suitable for urban settings
- Muscovies are very quiet -- ideal for noise-sensitive neighborhoods
- A small pond or water feature provides for ducks' water needs
- Ducks control slugs and insects without scratching up garden beds

### Rabbits

- Rabbits work well in small urban spaces
- Their manure is high in nutrients
- They are voracious vegetarians that control weeds
- A rabbit tractor with a wire bottom prevents burrowing escapes

## Urban Biodiversity

Even in the most impoverished urban landscapes, some wild plants and animals are present and ready to spread into welcoming habitat:
- Most towns have enough vacant lots, neglected corners, parks, and flowery landscaping to nurture a lively community of small wildlife
- Every blossom-decked corner is a reservoir for helpful wildlife
- Removing even one urban yard from the conventional lawn model creates habitat that benefits the broader neighborhood
