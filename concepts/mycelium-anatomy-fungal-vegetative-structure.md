Mycelium constitutes the vegetative body of a fungus, forming the massive network of thread-like filaments (hyphae) that permeates soil, wood, and other substrates. The mycelium is the primary feeding and colonizing structure of fungi, and in many species it is also the longest-lived and most extensive phase of the organism's life cycle. Understanding mycelial anatomy is fundamental to mycology, plant pathology, and applied fungal technologies.

## Hyphal Structure

Individual hyphae are tubular filaments typically 2 to 10 micrometers in diameter, composed of chitin-rich cell walls surrounding a cytoplasm containing nuclei, mitochondria, vacuoles, and various organelles. Hyphae grow from their tips through a process of polarized vesicle secretion, continuously extending the tube while synthesizing new cell wall material at the apex. Growth rates vary enormously among species, from less than 1 millimeter per day in some slow-growing endophytes to over 20 millimeters per day in aggressive saprotrophs and pathogens.

## Septate versus Coenocytic Hyphae

Fungal hyphae fall into two major structural categories. Septate hyphae are divided into discrete cellular compartments by cross-walls called septa. Most basidiomycetes and ascomycetes have septate hyphae, with septa that typically contain a central pore allowing cytoplasmic streaming and even nuclear migration between compartments. Coenocytic (aseptate) hyphae lack regular septa and instead form continuous multinucleate tubes, as seen in the Zygomycota (bread molds and allies). Dolipore septa, found in basidiomycetes, have a more complex barrel-shaped pore structure with parenthesome caps.

## Mycelial Networks and Hyphal Fusion

As mycelium grows, individual hyphae branch and anastomose (fuse) with neighboring hyphae of the same individual, creating an interconnected three-dimensional network. This fusion is regulated by vegetative compatibility (vc) systems that prevent fusion between genetically distinct individuals. The resulting network facilitates efficient resource transport, signal transduction, and coordinated behavior across the entire mycelial body. In some species, the mycelium can transport nutrients and water over distances of meters, enabling the fungus to redistribute resources across heterogeneous environments.

## Specialized Mycelial Structures

### Rhizomorphs

Rhizomorphs are dense, cord-like aggregations of hyphae that resemble roots. They are produced by many wood-decay fungi (notably Armillaria species) and enable long-distance exploration and nutrient transport through soil. Armillaria rhizomorphs can extend for tens of meters and are responsible for the spread of Armillaria root disease in forests. They have a differentiated outer rind of melanized hyphae protecting an inner core of growing hyphae.

### Mycelial Cords

Similar to rhizomorphs but generally smaller and less differentiated, mycelial cords are bundles of hyphae that facilitate resource translocation. They are commonly observed on the surface of decaying wood and in leaf litter, appearing as white or cream-colored strands.

### Sclerotia

Sclerotia are compact, hard masses of melanized hyphae that serve as survival structures. They can persist for years in soil or on wood and germinate when conditions become favorable. Sclerotia are produced by many plant-pathogenic fungi and some edible species (notably the sclerotium-forming king tuber mushroom, Pleurotus tuber-regium).

### Fan-Like Mycelial Mats

Many wood-decay fungi produce visible fan-shaped or sheet-like mycelial mats between bark and wood or within the wood itself. These mats are diagnostic features for identifying the causal agent of wood decay and represent zones of active colonization and enzyme production.

## Extracellular Matrices and Biofilms

Fungal mycelia often secrete extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) that form a slimy matrix surrounding the hyphae. This matrix facilitates adhesion to surfaces, protects against desiccation, concentrates extracellular enzymes near the hyphal surface, and may play roles in inter-hyphal communication. In some contexts, mycelial biofilms contribute to soil aggregation and the formation of stable soil structure.

## The Mycelial Perspective

Mycologist Paul Stamets has popularized the concept of the "mycelial network" as a biological internet, noting that mycelia share resources, transmit chemical signals, and exhibit collective decision-making analogous to neural networks. While the analogy has limits, the capacity of mycelial networks to integrate information about nutrient distribution, environmental conditions, and the presence of competitors across large areas is well documented experimentally.
