# Desert Housing Design Principles

Desert housing must achieve the twin qualities of **summer cooling** and **winter (or night) warmth**. Traditional desert architecture—from the older houses of Iran, Afghanistan, and Rajasthan to the compact pueblos of the American Southwest—demonstrates sophisticated climate-responsive design that modern construction often fails to match.

## Settlement Layout and Orientation

### Clustered Housing

The "colony of swallows' nests" appearance of many arid-area settlements is not coincidental. Close-clustered dwellings with their long axis aligned **east-west** and common or close-spaced walls ensure that neither wind nor heat easily penetrates the settlement fabric. Key principles:

- **Narrow east-west streets maximised**; broad north-south streets minimised
- Multiple storeys create shaded narrow "wells" of cool air in courtyards and streets
- Vents at roof level draw cool air into dwelling rooms
- Bottom floors and flat roof areas used for living; upper floors for bedrooms

Where cultures prefer more space and privacy, a line of bungalows or cabins on an east-west axis with dense vegetation strips on the shade side achieves a similar result. Village Homes in Davis, California, USA is cited as an ideal model.

### Street and Paving Design

- Roads and car parks must be **shaded and narrow**—broad paved (bituminous) boulevards create uncontrollable local heat islands
- In Davis, California, at least **60% of paved areas must be shaded by law**
- Shading of lanes and streets by trellis and lattice creates cool refuges in desert towns
- The white canvas-covered markets of Istanbul are a model for commercial areas
- White-painted roof surfaces reflect heat back to the air above the settlement

## Cooling Strategies

Six primary methods provide cool air for desert dwellings:

### 1. Internal Courtyards
Preferably latticed or shaded overhead, and even more effective when two or more storeys high and naturally shaded by the building. These create shaded microclimates at the heart of the dwelling.

### 2. Fully Enclosed Vine Arbours
With mulched floors and trickle irrigation, these suit single-storey dwellings. Arbors need to be about **30% of total floor area** to provide effective cool air. Hanging ferns or house plants aid cooling, as does a water tank within the space.

### 3. Earth Tunnels
Opened as ditches **20 m long and 1 m deep**, with large pipes, half-round culverts, or evaporative cooling materials in the airflow. Large unglazed pots, pans of wet coke, curtains of coarse fibreglass weave, or beds of coarse bark kept damp provide evaporative cooling. Cool humid air continually falls through these tunnels to house rooms. Even beds of coarse bark kept damp achieve the effect.

### 4. Down-Draughts
Sails, slats, or wind-scoops on roof areas—either fixed or self-steering—force down-flow of constant or prevailing winds. At room outlets, these can be fitted with damp hessian (burlap), coke evaporation beds, or unglazed pots of water for considerable additional cooling and humidification.

### 5. Induced Cross-Ventilation
Achieved by fitting a **black-painted sheet-metal solar chimney** opening from ceilings or roof ridges. As these heat up, they effectively draw air into rooms from any of the above cool-air sources, creating a continuous cool airflow in living areas. The cross-draught from a solar chimney combined with a fully-enclosed vined shadehouse on the shade side can be strong enough to blow out a candle.

### 6. Evaporative Cooling Surfaces
Unglazed pottery, coke mounds, ferns in bark, thick vines, or wet hessian (burlap) screens placed in the through-draught path. Combined with shade, vines, and induced airflow, air can be cooled to **10–15°C below ambient temperature**.

## Heating Strategies

Desert nights and winters can be severely cold. Heating approaches include:

- **Direct solar gain**: Windows allowing sun to strike an edge-insulated concrete slab provide all needed heat
- **Ground insulation**: Trenches cut at or below foundations (0.5–1 m deep) with rigid foam insulation buffer both heat and cold extremes without firewood
- **Trombe walls**: A thick vertical mud brick wall 0.5 m inside a room, faced with a glass window, acts as a heater long after dark
- **Attached glasshouses**: Serve triple purpose—winter heat source, summer ventilation driver, and year-round plant growing space

### Glazing Guidelines by Latitude

| Latitude Range | Sun-Facing Wall Glazing |
|---------------|------------------------|
| 30°–60° N/S | Extensive glazing needed |
| 0°–30° N/S | 20–25% of sun-facing wall |

## The Sun-Side Glasshouse

A glasshouse on the sunward side of a desert dwelling serves four functions:

1. **Winter heat source** — captures and stores solar energy
2. **Summer ventilation driver** — vents hot air to create cross-ventilation through the house
3. **Season extension** — starts spring plants early, ripens late autumn plants, grows winter greens
4. **Food preservation** — dries surplus fruits and vegetables in an enclosed area unavailable to insect pests

## Essentials of the Desert House

A comprehensive checklist for desert house design:

- **No west windows** — west wall painted white and fully shaded, vine-screened, evergreen trees to the west
- **Cold air tunnel or cold air source** as a fully enclosed, bark-mulch shadehouse
- **Positive hot air exhaust** as a solar chimney or small attached glasshouse to the sun side
- **Unimpeded through-ventilation** for cool air movement
- **Either very thick, white, fully-shaded walls** (cool deserts, cool nights) or **very light, screened, or matted walls** (hot days and nights)
- **Evaporative cooling surfaces** in through-draught
- **Cool cellars** for storage, or deep cool internal courtyards with vines and ferns
- **Insulated ceilings** with thick vine mass over roof area

## Vegetation Placement Around Dryland Houses

The low westerly sun adds the most heat to buildings. Vegetation placement follows these rules:

- **West**: Permanent screens of evergreen trees, thick-leaved vines, or turf banks—no windows
- **Shade side**: Enclosed trellis built here for summer living
- **East**: Part-shaded by deciduous trees or vines, small windows acceptable
- **Sun side**: Glazing—100% in cold areas, 25% in warm areas
- **House elongation**: East-west, so winter sun reaches all room floors
- **Eave width on sun side**: Permits winter sun entry, excludes summer sun
- **External blinds** (bamboo, wooden, aluminium) prevent heat entry on hot winter days
- **Deciduous vines** on sun side prevent excessive heating in summer

## Energy and Water Conservation

### Home Energy
- **Solar attic** with water tank, glass cover, and aluminium foil over thick insulation
- **Grid of plastic pipe** in soot-covered sand under glass
- **Simple pipe coil** on a metal roof
- **Solar pond** (4–5 m sides, 1–2 m deep) for groups of 5–10 houses supplies hot water and space heating—covering about 80% of energy needs
- **Photovoltaic cells** for lights and electronics
- **Wastewater to firewood plantation** for cooking fuel

### Water Conservation
- Efficient shower heads; divert shower and handbasin water to flush toilets or garden
- Dry pit toilets (popular in central Australia) work for years without water
- Raise shower and handbasin a few steps above floor level with low-level cistern for gravity-fed toilet flushing
- All roof spouting directed to covered concrete tanks (5,000+ litres is standard in dryland Australia)
- Tanks located on the shade side under trellis provide cool drinking and cooking water
