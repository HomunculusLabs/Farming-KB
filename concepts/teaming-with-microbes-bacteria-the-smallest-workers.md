# Bacteria — The Smallest Workers

Bacteria are the most numerous organisms in soil and among the earth's primary decomposers, second only to [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-the-underground-network]]. A teaspoon of good garden soil contains a billion bacteria. Despite their minuscule size — 250,000 to 500,000 can fit inside the period at the end of a sentence — they are the foundation upon which the [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]] is built.

## Ancient and abundant

Bacteria appeared at least 3 billion years ago, making them the earliest form of life on earth. They are prokaryotes — their DNA is contained in a single chromosome not enclosed in a nucleus. Their size is the main reason gardeners rarely consider them: they are too small to see in detail with standard 1000-power microscopes.

Three basic shapes are found in soil: coccus (spherical or oval), bacillus (rod-shaped), and spiral. All three are represented in garden soils and play roles in decomposition.

Under ideal laboratory conditions, one bacterium can produce 5 billion offspring in 12 hours. If all bacteria reproduced at this rate, it would take only a month to double the mass of our planet. In soil, however, reproduction is limited by natural conditions, predators (chiefly [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]]), moisture availability, and a slower reproductive rate than laboratory counterparts.

## Primary decomposers

Bacteria decompose plant and animal material to obtain nitrogen, carbon compounds, and other nutrients. These nutrients are held immobilized inside the bacteria and released only when bacteria are consumed by predators or die and are decayed. Different kinds of soil bacteria survive on different food sources depending on what is available and where they are located.

Most bacteria do best decomposing young, fresh plant material — the "green" material of composting — which contains lots of simple sugars. These are easier for bacteria to digest than the more complex carbon compounds of "brown" material. Until brown material is broken down into smaller carbon chains, other members of the soil food web more readily digest it than bacteria.

Bacteria break the bonds along organic chains using enzymes, creating smaller chains of simple sugars, fatty acids, and amino acids — the three basic building blocks they need. Enzyme digestion occurs outside the organism before ingestion. Once nutrients are transported through cellular membranes via active transport (molecular pumps that use energy), they are locked up inside the bacterial cell.

## Feeding and the rhizosphere

Root exudates are favorite foods for certain soil bacteria, so huge populations concentrate in the rhizosphere. Bacteria also find nutrition from cells sloughed off during root-tip growth. The rhizosphere is the site where the most nutrient cycling occurs, because that is where plant roots, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes all interact.

Because bacteria are attached to soil particles (via their biofilm), the nutrients they retain remain in the soil near roots rather than washing away as chemical fertilizers do. Bacteria are part of the base of the soil food web food pyramid — without sufficient bacterial populations, the entire web suffers.

## Aerobic vs. anaerobic

Two main groups of bacteria populate soil, and understanding the difference is crucial for gardeners:

### Anaerobic bacteria

Anaerobic bacteria live without oxygen; most cannot survive in its presence. The genus Clostridium invades and destroys the inside soft tissue of decaying matter. By-products of anaerobic decay include hydrogen sulfide (rotten egg smell), butyric acid (vomit smell), ammonia, and vinegar. The notorious Escherichia coli and other gastrointestinal bacteria are facultative anaerobes — they can live in aerobic conditions but prefer anaerobic environments.

Anaerobic conditions foster pathogenic bacteria and kill beneficial aerobic bacteria. These smells should be remembered when composting and gardening: if it smells bad, something is wrong.

### Aerobic bacteria

Aerobic bacteria require air and do not normally produce bad smells. The [[teaming-with-microbes-overview]] (genus Streptomyces) produce enzymes that include volatile chemicals giving soil its clean, fresh, earthy aroma — the smell of "good soil" that every gardener recognizes.

Actinomycetes are different from other soil bacteria: they actually grow filaments almost like fungal hyphae. Scientists believe Streptomyces species use their branching filaments to connect soil particles, making them too big for their natural predators (protozoan ciliates) to engulf. Actinomycetes are particularly adept at decaying cellulose and chitin — two difficult-to-digest carbon compounds found in plant cell walls and fungal cell walls respectively. They are adapted to live in a wider pH range than other bacteria, from acidic to alkaline.

## Biofilms and soil structure

Bacterial slime, or biofilm, is a matrix of sugars, proteins, and DNA that bacteria produce to attach themselves to surfaces. Think of the plaque on your teeth — it enables bacteria to stick, and it is just as strong in soil. This biofilm binds individual soil particles together into aggregates, contributing to soil structure.

### pH buffering

Biofilm is slightly alkaline (pH above 7), which buffers the soil in the rhizosphere and creates conditions favorable for nitrifying bacteria. If there are enough bacteria in an area, the slime they produce keeps the pH above 7, and nitrification can occur.

### Defense and transport

Biofilms save bacteria from desiccation as soil dries. Bacteria often live inside sticky globs of biofilms complete with infrastructure of channels filled with water for transport of nutrients and wastes. Biofilms also defend against antibiotics — bacteria colonies protected by slime are 1000 times more resistant than individual bacteria. Some bacteria even squirt biofilm as a means of propulsion.

## Nutrient retention

Bacteria play a major role in plant nutrition by locking up nutrients that might otherwise leach away. They do so by ingesting nutrients while decomposing organic matter and retaining them in their cellular structures. Since soil bacteria do not travel far and there is ample food in the root zone, nutrients ingested by bacteria are kept near roots.

[[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] consume these bacteria and release excess nitrogen as ammonium (NH4+) in their wastes, deposited right in the rhizosphere where roots can absorb them.

## Nitrogen fixation

Some bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen, converting N2 into forms plants can use. These nitrogen-fixing bacteria usually require a pH of 7 or above for good populations and are particularly important in legume root nodules, where they form visible structures. Without sufficient nitrogen-fixing bacteria, ammonium released by protozoa and nematodes is not converted to nitrate — the preferred nitrogen form for most vegetables and annuals.

## Role in [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-ph-and-biology]]

Bacterial slime raises soil pH, creating alkaline microenvironments. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria generally require pH above 7. In [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]], nitrifying bacteria thrive, converting ammonium to nitrate. This is why vegetables, annuals, and grasses — which prefer nitrates — do best in bacterially dominated soils (Rule #2).

## Bacterial Roles in Plant Disease Suppression

Soil bacteria are among the most important natural agents for suppressing plant diseases, operating through multiple mechanisms that protect crops from pathogenic fungi, bacteria, and nematodes. A healthy, bacterially diverse soil is itself one of the best defenses against crop loss.
### Competition and Antibiosis
Beneficial bacteria outcompete pathogens for space, nutrients, and root exudates in the rhizosphere. When a root surface is already colonized by benign bacteria, pathogenic organisms find fewer attachment sites and less available food. Many bacteria also produce antibiotics that directly inhibit or kill pathogens. Pseudomonas species, for example, secrete compounds such as pyoluteorin, pyrrolnitrin, and 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol that are toxic to soil-borne fungal pathogens like Fusarium and Pythium. Bacillus subtilis produces iturin and surfactin, lipopeptide antibiotics that disrupt the cell membranes of pathogenic fungi.
### Induced Systemic Resistance
Some rhizobacteria trigger a plant's own immune defenses through a phenomenon called induced systemic resistance (ISR). When certain Pseudomonas or Bacillus strains colonize roots, they send biochemical signals (including jasmonic acid and ethylene pathways) that prime the entire plant to respond more rapidly and strongly to subsequent pathogen attacks. Unlike chemical pesticides, ISR does not harm beneficial organisms and can provide broad-spectrum protection.
### Siderophore-Mediated Iron Competition
In iron-limited soils, certain bacteria produce siderophores — high-affinity iron-chelating molecules that scavenge available iron. Since many pathogenic fungi require iron for growth, these siderophores effectively starve pathogens while the bacteria themselves can reabsorb the iron they have sequestered. This mechanism is especially important in the [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-ph-and-biology]] range preferred by many vegetables, where iron availability is naturally limited.
### Lytic Enzymes
Bacteria produce chitinases, glucanases, and proteases that degrade the cell walls of pathogenic fungi. Chitinase-producing Bacillus and Serratia species can break down the chitin in fungal hyphae, directly lysing pathogenic organisms. These enzymes are the same tools that [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-the-underground-network]] and [[teaming-with-microbes-overview]] use in their own competitive interactions.
### Applications in Disease Suppression
The disease-suppressive power of bacteria is the scientific basis for [[compost-tea-aact]] applications. Well-brewed compost tea contains billions of diverse bacteria per milliliter that, when applied to plant surfaces or soil, can colonize leaf and root surfaces and outcompete pathogens. See [[ingham-aact-disease-suppression]] for research on compost tea efficacy, and [[aact-disease-suppression-recipes]] for specific brewing recipes targeting particular pathogens.

## Industrial and Agricultural Applications of Soil Bacteria

The metabolic diversity of soil bacteria has been harnessed for numerous agricultural and industrial purposes, from nitrogen fixation to bioremediation.
### Commercial Inoculants and Biofertilizers
Rhizobium and Bradyrhizobium species are the most widely used commercial bacterial inoculants. Applied as seed coatings or soil drenches, these bacteria form symbiotic nodules on legume roots and fix atmospheric nitrogen at rates of 50-300 kg N/ha per growing season. This reduces or eliminates the need for synthetic nitrogen fertilizer in legume crops. Other commercial inoculants include Azotobacter (free-living nitrogen fixer for cereals), Azospirillum (associative nitrogen fixer for grasses), and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria such as Pseudomonas fluorescens and Bacillus megaterium, which convert insoluble soil phosphates into plant-available forms.
### Biocontrol Products
Several bacterial strains have been developed into commercial biocontrol products. Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) produces crystal proteins toxic to specific insect larvae and has been used as a biological insecticide for decades. See [[cervantes-bacillus-thuringiensis-bt-control]] for Bt application in cannabis pest management. Bacillus subtilis strains (e.g., QST 713, sold as Serenade) are registered as biofungicides and are applied to suppress powdery mildew, Botrytis, and other foliar diseases. Streptomyces lydicus (Actinovate) is marketed for suppression of soil-borne and foliar pathogens.
### Bioremediation
Certain soil bacteria can degrade environmental contaminants including petroleum hydrocarbons, pesticides, and heavy metals. Pseudomonas putida is well-known for its ability to metabolize organic solvents and is used in bioremediation of contaminated soils. These capabilities connect soil bacterial ecology directly to environmental restoration efforts, demonstrating that maintaining diverse bacterial communities has benefits far beyond agriculture.
### Bacteria in the Compost Process
Bacteria drive every stage of the composting process. During the initial mesophilic phase (ambient temperature to 40°C), mesophilic bacteria rapidly multiply and begin decomposing simple sugars and proteins. As their metabolic activity generates heat, thermophilic bacteria take over at temperatures above 45°C, some surviving up to 70°C. These heat-loving bacteria decompose cellulose, hemicellulose, and even some lignin — materials that mesophilic bacteria cannot process. The thermophilic phase is critical for pathogen destruction, as the sustained high temperatures kill weed seeds, human pathogens, and plant disease organisms. As the pile cools, mesophilic bacteria and [[teaming-with-microbes-overview]] return to finish the remaining decomposition. The diversity and succession of bacterial populations throughout composting determines both the speed of the process and the quality of the finished compost. See [[teaming-with-microbes-decomposition-pathways]] for a detailed breakdown of the composting microbial succession.
### Bacterial Quorum Sensing
A relatively recently discovered mechanism called quorum sensing allows bacteria to communicate with each other using chemical signal molecules (autoinducers). When bacterial populations reach a critical density, these signals accumulate and trigger coordinated group behaviors — including biofilm formation, antibiotic production, virulence factor expression, and nutrient acquisition. Quorum sensing means that soil bacteria act not as isolated cells but as coordinated communities capable of collective decision-making. This discovery has transformed our understanding of how biocontrol bacteria operate: Pseudomonas fluorescens, for example, uses quorum sensing to time its production of antifungal compounds to coincide with peak pathogen populations, conserving metabolic resources. Research into quorum sensing disruption (quorum quenching) as a strategy for managing plant diseases is an active area of investigation.

## Bacterial Community Composition

The bacterial community in any given soil is extraordinarily diverse. A single gram of agricultural soil may contain 10,000 or more distinct bacterial species, though the actual number of active species at any moment is lower due to resource limitations and competition. The dominant phyla in most temperate soils are Proteobacteria (especially Alpha- and Betaproteobacteria in the rhizosphere), Acidobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes. Rhizosphere communities differ markedly from bulk soil communities, with Proteobacteria enriched near roots due to the abundance of carbon compounds in root exudates. Understanding this composition matters for gardeners because the relative abundance of beneficial versus opportunistic bacterial groups — and the speed with which beneficial populations colonize root surfaces — directly influences plant health and disease resistance. Compost applications and mulching favor Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes, while no-till practices tend to increase Acidobacteria — a group associated with slower-growing, more oligotrophic (low-nutrient) lifestyles that contribute to long-term soil stability.
