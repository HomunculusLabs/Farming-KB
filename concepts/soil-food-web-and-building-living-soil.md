# Soil Food Web and Building Living Soil

The soil food web is the vast community of organisms living in soil — from bacteria and fungi to earthworms and beetles — that drives nutrient cycling, decomposes organic matter, and builds the fertile, structured soil that plants depend on.

Understanding and supporting this living system is one of the core principles of [[permaculture-design]]. Rather than treating soil as an inert substrate to be fertilized, permaculture approaches soil as a living ecosystem to be fed and nurtured.

## The Scale of Soil Life

Soil is not dirt. It is among the most biologically rich ecosystems on Earth, rivaling tropical rainforests in sheer abundance and diversity of life. Most of this life is invisible to the naked eye, but its collective impact on plant health, water retention, and landscape resilience is enormous.

In a single teaspoon of good pasture soil, you may find roughly a billion bacteria, a million fungi, and ten thousand amoebae. These are not passive residents — they are constantly consuming, excreting, and reshaping the chemical and physical environment around them.

Scale that up to an acre and the numbers become staggering. While the same acre might support a half-ton of aboveground animals — insects, mammals, birds — below ground it harbors two tons of earthworms plus two tons of bacteria, fungi, and other soil animals combined.

That is the equivalent of eight to ten horses worth of animal life operating entirely underground, unseen and largely unappreciated. [[elaine-ingham]], founder of Soil Foodweb Inc, calls these subsurface swarms "microherds" — a fitting name for the grazing, migrating, cycling communities that transform dead matter into living fertility.

Managing these microherds, rather than applying synthetic inputs, is the essence of biological farming. The health of your plants is a direct reflection of the health of the organisms beneath them.

## What Soil Organisms Do

Like most creatures, soil organisms spend their time searching for food, eating, and excreting. In doing so, they move nutrients through the system: food for themselves, food for other soil life, and food for plants.

Their main role from a plant's perspective is to chew up matter that plants cannot eat — fallen leaves, woody debris, rock particles, dead roots — and transform it into palatable, plant-available nutrients.

Soil organisms break down both mineral and organic material. Fungi and bacteria dissolve rock particles through acid secretion, liberating phosphorus, potassium, and trace minerals locked in parent material. Detritivores fragment coarse organic matter into smaller pieces with far more surface area for microbial colonization.

At every trophic level, nutrients are converted from one form to another, gradually approaching the ionic forms that plant roots can absorb. This multi-step conversion is why diverse soil communities are far more effective than any single organism or chemical input.

### Fertility Is Flow, Not Stock

Critically, fertility comes from the briskness and depth of these nutrient flows, not merely the presence of nutrients in the soil. A conventional soil test may show adequate phosphorus, but if no microbial or root-mediated process is mobilizing it, plants will still show deficiency.

Living soil is soil where nutrients are in motion — constantly being cycled, exchanged, and made available through biological activity. This distinction between static nutrient content and dynamic nutrient cycling is fundamental to understanding why biological approaches outperform chemical ones over time.

## The Decomposition Cascade

The fate of a falling leaf illustrates the elegant, multi-stage process by which soil organisms recycle organic matter. This decomposition cascade involves dozens of species working in sequence and in parallel, each preparing the material for the next wave of decomposers.

### Stage One: Moisture and Bacterial Bloom

First, a leaf falls to the ground, initially too dry for most decomposers to colonize. Dew and rain gradually leach out protective polyphenols and other defensive compounds the tree produced while the leaf was alive.

Once sufficiently moistened, bacteria bloom across the leaf surface. They secrete enzymes that tear apart cell wall sugars and soluble compounds. This bacterial bloom is rapid and dense, drawing nitrogen and other nutrients from the surrounding soil to fuel reproduction.

### Stage Two: Fungal Colonization

Next, wind-borne [[fungal-spores]] germinate on the leaf. White hyphae threads knit across the surface and penetrate deeper tissue. Fungi specialize in digesting lignin — the tough, woody molecules that bacteria alone cannot break down — along with other recalcitrant compounds.

Where bacteria work quickly on simple sugars, fungi work slowly and thoroughly on the structural materials that give leaves and wood their rigidity. This fungal digestion is the rate-limiting step in decomposition and the primary source of stable humus formation.

### Stage Three: Invertebrate Processing

Larger invertebrates then join the process. Millipedes, pill bugs, fly larvae, springtails, and earthworms arrive to feed on the partially decomposed material and the rich microbial biomass colonizing it. These are the primary decomposers visible to the human eye, with earthworms being the most ecologically important of all.

An earthworm grabs a fragment of leaf, pulls it into its burrow, and pulverizes it in a muscular gizzard. Symbiotic bacteria in the worm's gut complete the chemical breakdown that mechanical grinding alone cannot achieve.

The worm then excretes "casts" — fertile, organically enriched earth that lines its burrows and dramatically improves the surrounding soil chemistry and structure. Earthworm casts are richer in available nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and microbial life than the surrounding soil.

### The Earthworm's Engineering Impact

Earthworm tunnels loosen, aerate, and fertilize the soil profile. Rain penetrates deeper through these channels, carrying dissolved nutrients further into the root zone. The network of burrows also creates preferential flow paths that reduce surface runoff and erosion.

Collectively, earthworms turn over approximately 25 tons of soil per acre per year. This is the equivalent of generating one inch of new topsoil every ten years — a rate of soil building that no mechanical method can match.

## The Decomposer Hierarchy

Soil organisms form a structured food web with multiple trophic levels, each feeding on the level below and being consumed by the level above. This hierarchy ensures that energy and nutrients captured from organic matter cascade through the system rather than being lost.

### Primary Decomposers

Primary decomposers include invertebrates (earthworms, millipedes, pill bugs), bacteria, algae, fungi, and actinomycetes. These organisms directly consume dead organic matter and mineral substrates, performing the initial breakdown and fragmentation. They are the entry point for dead material entering the soil food web.

### Secondary Decomposers

Secondary decomposers feed on the primary decomposers themselves. Amoebae, nematodes, mold mites, and springtails consume bacteria and fungal biomass, releasing nutrients that were locked in microbial bodies back into the soil solution in plant-available forms.

This predation step is essential — without it, nutrients would remain immobilized inside microbial cells indefinitely. The "microbial loop" of grazing and release is one of the most important mechanisms in terrestrial nutrient cycling.

### Tertiary Decomposers

Tertiary decomposers are the predators of the soil food web. Centipedes, ground beetles, pseudoscorpions, spiders, and ants hunt secondary decomposers, regulating their populations and driving further nutrient cycling through predation and excretion.

These predators keep grazer populations in check, preventing any single group from dominating the system. A healthy soil food web requires all three levels functioning together.

## Humus Formation

As organic material decomposes, its chemical components are broken down at different rates. Sugars and starches go first, providing quick energy for bacteria and other fast-growing microbes. Cellulose and protein decompose more slowly, requiring specialized enzyme systems.

Lignin, chitin, and waxes are the slowest to break down, requiring specialized fungi and significant time — sometimes years. The sequential nature of this breakdown means that different soil organisms dominate at different stages of decomposition, each contributing different byproducts.

### What Humus Is

Humus is the stable, dark, complex residue that remains after decomposition has run its course. It is not a single molecule but a group of complex molecules composed of carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen bonded in resistant configurations that most organisms cannot easily dismantle.

Humus accumulates precisely because the soil food web cannot break it down further under normal conditions. This chemical resistance is what gives humus its longevity in soil — some humus persists for centuries or longer.

### Humus as Nutrient Reservoir

Chemically, humus molecules carry negatively charged oxygen atoms that attract and hold positively charged nutrient ions. These include potassium, calcium, magnesium, ammonium, copper, zinc, and manganese.

This [[cation-exchange-capacity]] makes humus a natural nutrient reservoir. It slowly releases minerals as plant roots demand them through proton exchange, acting as a time-release fertilizer maintained entirely by biological processes.

### Humus and Soil Structure

Physically, humus dramatically improves soil structure. It lightens heavy clay soils, gives body to sandy soils, holds moisture against drought, and allows roots to penetrate deeper. It acts as a gentle, biological form of tilling — opening channels and loosening compaction without the destructive side effects of mechanical cultivation.

### The Positive Feedback Loop

Humus creation sets up a powerful positive feedback loop. Humus allows moisture and organisms to penetrate deeper into the soil profile, which generates more humus at depth, which in turn allows even deeper penetration. Over years and decades, this self-reinforcing process builds genuine topsoil — the foundation of terrestrial productivity.

## [[building-soil]] Structure

Beyond decomposition, soil organisms are the primary architects of soil structure. Bacteria secrete gums, waxes, and gels that bind individual mineral particles together into tiny aggregates called microaggregates. [[fungal-hyphae]] surround and enmesh larger soil crumbs, giving them structural cohesion and resistance to erosion.

Together, these biological glues produce "tilth" — the loose, crumbly, well-aerated structure that gardeners and farmers recognize as healthy soil. Tilth allows water infiltration, root expansion, and gas exchange between soil and atmosphere.

Without the continuous work of soil life, earth either dries and blows away as dust or clumps into hard, root-thwarting clay. Dead soil is not merely less productive — it is structurally unstable and prone to degradation.

## Nutrient Recycling Efficiency

The soil food web achieves a level of nutrient conservation that dwarfs any human-managed system. In one forest study, a hectare contained 365 kilograms of calcium in its biomass and soil. Of that total, only 8 kilograms — roughly 2 percent — were lost annually to runoff.

The remaining 98 percent was recycled back into the system through biological decomposition, root uptake, and mycorrhizal transport. This represents a near-closed nutrient loop maintained entirely by the soil food web and plant-microbe partnerships.

### The Agricultural Contrast

By contrast, conventional agricultural land typically loses 25 to 60 percent of its calcium and other nutrients annually through leaching, erosion, and harvest removal. Nature achieves near-complete recycling efficiency through diverse, multi-layered biological systems, while simplified human systems hemorrhage nutrients and rely on external inputs to compensate.

Those external inputs — synthetic fertilizers, mined minerals, transported compost — are energetically expensive and ecologically disruptive. They address the symptom (nutrient deficiency) while undermining the cause (biological nutrient cycling).

The lesson for permaculture designers is clear. Building living soil is not an optional enhancement — it is the foundation upon which productive, resilient, self-maintaining landscapes are built. Feed the soil food web, and it will feed everything else.
