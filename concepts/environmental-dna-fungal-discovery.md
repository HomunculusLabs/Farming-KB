# Environmental DNA Fungal Discovery

Environmental DNA (eDNA) methods have revolutionized the discovery and
characterization of fungal biodiversity. By extracting and sequencing DNA
directly from environmental samples such as soil, water, air, and plant
surfaces, researchers can detect fungi that cannot be observed directly or
cultured in the laboratory. These approaches have revealed that the previously
known fungal diversity represents only a small fraction of what actually exists
in most ecosystems, fundamentally changing our understanding of [[fungal-ecology]]
and biogeography. The scale of hidden diversity uncovered by eDNA approaches
has been one of the most striking findings in modern mycology, suggesting that
traditional survey methods have captured only the tip of the fungal iceberg.

## Metabarcoding Approaches

The most widely used eDNA method for fungal biodiversity assessment is
metabarcoding, which involves amplifying and sequencing a standardized genetic
locus from mixed community DNA extracts. The nuclear ribosomal internal
transcribed spacer (ITS) region has been adopted as the official DNA barcode
for fungi and is by far the most commonly sequenced marker. Its high
variability among species provides good discriminatory power, while conserved
flanking regions (the 18S and 28S ribosomal RNA genes) allow for relatively
universal primer design across the fungal kingdom. The ITS region is
particularly effective because it evolves rapidly, accumulating mutations that
distinguish even closely related species from one another.

High-throughput sequencing platforms generate millions of sequences per sample,
enabling detection of rare taxa that would be missed by traditional survey
methods. Bioinformatic pipelines process raw sequences through quality
filtering, chimera removal, and clustering into operational taxonomic units
(OTUs) or amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). These units are then compared
against reference databases such as UNITE or GenBank for taxonomic assignment.
However, the quality and completeness of reference databases remains a major
limitation, with a large proportion of environmental sequences remaining
unassigned even to the phylum level, highlighting the vast gap between known
and unknown fungal diversity.

The choice of sequencing platform and primers significantly affects the
composition of detected fungal communities. Different primer pairs vary in
their coverage across fungal groups, with some preferentially amplifying
Ascomycota while others better capture Basidiomycota or early-diverging
lineages such as Chytridiomycota and Zoopagomycota. Researchers must carefully
consider these biases when designing studies and interpreting comparative
results across different investigations. Inter-laboratory standardization
efforts have improved comparability but have not eliminated all sources of
methodological variation that can confound cross-study comparisons.

## Metagenomics and Metatranscriptomics

Beyond metabarcoding, shotgun metagenomics sequences all DNA in a sample
without targeted PCR amplification, providing a broader and less biased view
of fungal community composition and functional potential. Metagenomic approaches
avoid the biases introduced by primer selection and can detect fungi whose
ribosomal regions differ substantially from standard primer binding sites.
They also provide information about the functional genes present in fungal
communities, offering insights into the metabolic capabilities and ecological
roles of uncultured fungi that would otherwise remain inaccessible to study.

Metatranscriptomics, the sequencing of RNA from environmental samples, reveals
which fungal genes are actively expressed in situ. This approach can distinguish
between dormant spores and metabolically active mycelium, identify fungi that
are currently performing specific ecological functions, and reveal temporal
dynamics of fungal activity that DNA-based methods cannot capture. Combining
metagenomic and metatranscriptomic data gives a more complete picture of fungal
community dynamics than either approach alone, linking genetic potential with
actual ecological function in natural environments.

## Airborne and Aquatic Fungal eDNA

Fungal eDNA is not limited to soil and plant surfaces. Air sampling using
filters or impactors captures the vast diversity of fungal spores present
in the atmosphere, providing data on both local spore communities and
long-distance dispersal patterns. Airborne fungal eDNA has practical
applications in allergy monitoring, plant pathology surveillance, and
biosecurity. Aquatic eDNA from freshwater and marine environments has revealed
previously unknown fungal diversity in water bodies, including novel groups of
aquatic fungi that challenge traditional classification systems.

## From Sequence to Species

A persistent challenge in eDNA-based fungal discovery is connecting
environmental sequences to formally described species. Many environmental
sequences do not match any reference sequence in public databases, representing
either undescribed species or known species that have not yet been sequenced
at the target locus. This reference gap is particularly severe for tropical
and Southern Hemisphere fungi, which are underrepresented in barcode libraries
compared to temperate Northern Hemisphere taxa.

Single-cell genomics and metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) offer promising
approaches for recovering genome-level information from uncultured fungi. These
techniques can provide enough genetic data to place environmental sequences
within a phylogenetic framework and potentially link different life stages
(sexual and asexual forms) of the same organism that were previously classified
in separate genera due to their dissimilar morphologies.

## Limitations and Biases

eDNA methods have important limitations that must be considered when
interpreting results. Extracellular DNA from dead organisms can persist in
soil and water for varying periods depending on environmental conditions,
potentially inflating diversity estimates by detecting taxa that are no longer
biologically active. PCR amplification introduces biases based on primer
affinity and template concentration, with some fungal groups amplifying more
efficiently than others. Sample collection, storage, and DNA extraction
protocols can preferentially capture certain types of fungi while missing
others, creating false impressions of community composition.

Despite these limitations, eDNA methods have become indispensable tools for
fungal biodiversity research and continue to drive the discovery of new and
previously unknown fungal lineages at an unprecedented pace, transforming
our understanding of the true scale of fungal diversity on Earth.
