# Direct-Seeded No-Tillage Rice Barley Clover Succession

The direct-seeded, no-tillage, winter grain and rice succession in a clover cover
is the central practical method of Masanobu Fukuoka's [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] system.
This technique produces consistent yields of approximately 22 bushels each of
winter grain and rice per quarter-acre without plowing, [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]],
pesticides, [[cultivator-compost-preparation-phase-methods]], or any form of soil cultivation. The method
relies entirely on natural biological processes and the symbiotic relationships
between rice, winter grain, and clover.

## The Annual Cycle

The farming year begins in autumn with the rice crop still standing in the
field. Before the rice harvest, clover seed and winter grain seed, either barley
or wheat, are broadcast directly over the ripening rice heads. The rice is then
harvested while the young shoots of the winter grain are already emerging
beneath. After harvesting, the rice is left to dry for three days, threshed, and
the uncut straw scattered evenly over the entire field surface. If chicken
droppings are available, these are scattered over the straw at this stage.

Before the New Year, clay pellets containing seed rice are prepared and
broadcast over the straw-covered field. These pellets protect the seed from
birds and rodents while allowing moisture penetration for germination. With the
winter grain growing actively and the rice seed sown, no further work is needed
until the winter grain harvest in late May.

In late May, the farmer notices the clover growing luxuriantly at ground level
and the small rice shoots emerging from the clay pellets. The winter grain is
harvested, dried, and threshed, and its straw likewise scattered uncut over the
field. The field is then flooded for four to five days to weaken the clover and
give the rice shoots an opportunity to break through the dense ground cover.
During June and July, the field is left unirrigated. In August, water is run
through the drainage ditches once every week or ten days until harvest.

## The Role of Clover

Clover serves as the living foundation of this system, performing multiple
functions that in conventional agriculture require separate operations. As a
legume, clover fixes atmospheric nitrogen through its symbiotic relationship
with rhizobium bacteria, eliminating the need for nitrogen fertilizer. Its dense
mat suppresses weed growth through competition for light and space, removing
the need for weeding. The clover cover protects the soil surface from erosion
and maintains soil moisture. When the field is briefly flooded in late spring,
the clover is weakened just enough to allow rice seedlings to establish
themselves, but it recovers and continues growing through the rice season,
providing ongoing soil protection and [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]].

The clover also supports a diverse ecosystem of [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]]. Spiders,
frogs, dragonflies, and other predators thrive in the clover-covered field,
providing natural pest control that eliminates the need for pesticides. Fukuoka
observed that whenever a large outbreak of leafhoppers occurred, spider
populations multiplied in response, always restoring balance without human
intervention.

## No-Tillage Soil Management

The elimination of tillage is perhaps the most counterintuitive aspect of this
method. Conventional wisdom holds that soil must be plowed to aerate it, break
up compaction, incorporate crop residues, and prepare a seedbed. Fukuoka argued
that plowing actually creates the conditions that make plowing appear necessary.
Flooding a field and stirring it with a plow causes the soil to set hard as
plaster, which then must be plowed again the following year. The soil is
killed by this treatment, and its biological activity collapses.

In the no-tillage system, soil structure is maintained and improved by the
natural activity of earthworms, insects, and plant roots. The uncut rice and
barley straw left on the field surface decomposes gradually, adding organic
matter and feeding soil organisms. The clover root system aerates the soil and
contributes to its crumb structure. Over time, the soil becomes increasingly
fertile and well-structured without any human cultivation effort.

Fukuoka reported that his fields had not been plowed for over thirty years,
yet consistently produced above-average yields. One gram of soil from his farm
contained approximately 100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria and other
soil-enriching microbes, a testament to the biological fertility maintained
by this system.

## Rice Straw Mulching

The practice of scattering uncut rice and barley straw over the field surface
replaces compost preparation, one of the most labor-intensive activities in
organic farming. In conventional organic systems, straw must be collected,
composted with manure or other materials over months, and then spread back on
the fields. This process not only requires significant labor but depletes the
land from which the organic material is taken.

In Fukuoka's system, the straw is returned directly to the field where it
grew, creating a closed nutrient cycle. The straw serves as mulch, protecting
the soil from direct sun and rain, suppressing weeds, retaining moisture, and
providing [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] organisms. As it decomposes, it releases
nutrients gradually, feeding the soil ecosystem and the growing crop. This
simple act of returning straw to the field replaces an entire chain of
conventional operations: composting, fertilizing, weeding, and soil
cultivation.

## Clay Pellet Seed Coating

The clay pellet technique was developed by Fukuoka as a method of direct
seeding that eliminates the need for nursery beds and transplanting. Seeds
are coated in a layer of soil and then a layer of clay, forming small pellets
that can be broadcast over the field. The clay protects the seeds from being
eaten by birds and rodents while allowing moisture to penetrate when rainfall
occurs. The seeds remain dormant within the pellets until conditions are
favorable for germination, at which point they sprout and grow.

This technique has applications beyond rice cultivation. Fukuoka proposed
using clay pellets to revegetate deserts by mixing the seeds of green manure
trees such as black wattle, clover, alfalfa, and grain seeds, coating them in
clay, and scattering them by hand over barren land. The pellets would protect
the seeds until rain fell, after which several species would survive and
provide clues about what was suited to the local climate and soil conditions.

## Yields and Labor Efficiency

The labor required for this system is remarkably low. Fukuoka stated that the
work of one or two people was more than sufficient to grow crops on a
quarter-acre. The entire annual cycle reduces essentially to sowing seed and
spreading straw. Despite this minimal labor input, yields consistently
approached 22 bushels of rice and 22 bushels of winter grain per
quarter-acre, with a target of 33 bushels each under optimized conditions.

These yields compare favorably with conventional methods requiring
significantly greater labor, capital, and chemical inputs. The field typically
produced about 250 heads of grain per square yard with an average of 200
grains per head, demonstrating that natural biological processes are fully
capable of maintaining high productivity when allowed to function without
disruption from human intervention.
