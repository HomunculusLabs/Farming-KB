# Controlled Substances Act and Psychedelic Research

**Source:** PiHKAL [[pihkal-cautionary-note-and-legal-context]]; historical context

**Updated:** 2026-04-19

## Overview

The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) of 1970 (Title II of the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act) established the legal framework that governs psychedelic research in the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] to this day. By placing most classical psychedelics in Schedule I, the CSA effectively ended legitimate human research for nearly three decades and created the legal environment described in the [[psychedelic-research-legal-history-united-states]].

## Legislative Background

Several pieces of legislation preceded and informed the CSA:

- The 1938 Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act established the modern FDA regulatory framework

- The 1951 Durham-Humphrey Amendment distinguished prescription from over-the-counter drugs

- The 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendments required proof of efficacy for new drugs

- The 1965 Drug Abuse Control Amendments gave the FDA authority over hallucinogens

These incremental restrictions created the regulatory infrastructure that the CSA would formalize and expand.

## The Five Schedules

The CSA created five schedules classifying drugs by their abuse potential and medical utility:

- **Schedule I**: "No currently accepted medical use," high abuse potential — includes LSD, psilocybin, mescaline, DMT, MDMA (added 1985)

- **Schedule II**: High abuse potential, accepted medical use — includes cocaine, methamphetamine, methylphenidate

- **Schedule III-V**: Progressively lower abuse potential and accepted medical uses

The scheduling decisions were made through a combination of scientific review and political considerations. Critics have noted that the Schedule I placement of psychedelics was driven more by cultural and political factors than by pharmacological evidence, as documented in analyses of [[psychedelic-research-timeline]] shows a dramatic gap in published studies from the early 1970s through the late 1990s.
