# Mahayana and Hinayana Natural Farming

Masanobu Fukuoka adopted Buddhist terminology to describe two distinct approaches to natural farming, which he called "Mahayana" (Great Vehicle) and "Hinayana" (Lesser Vehicle) natural farming. This framework is not a religious statement but a practical philosophical distinction that reflects the degree of human intervention in the farming system.








## Hinayana Natural Farming

Hinayana natural farming represents an intermediate approach that reduces but does not eliminate human intervention. Practitioners of this method may still apply some compost, practice limited pruning, or use selective weeding. The system is more natural than conventional agriculture but still relies on human judgment about what the land needs.

This approach can produce good results and is often where practitioners begin their transition away from conventional methods. Fukuoka did not dismiss Hinayana farming — he recognized it as a necessary stage in the farmer's evolution toward deeper understanding. However, he believed it remained fundamentally limited because the farmer is still trying to improve upon nature rather than trusting natural processes completely.

In practical terms, Hinayana natural farming might include: applying compost made from off-site materials, pruning fruit trees to maintain shape, tilling soil to incorporate amendments, and using natural pest control methods that still require active human management. See [[fukuoka-textdoc-mahayana-hinayana-scientific-farming-ways]] for Fukuoka's detailed comparison.








## Mahayana Natural Farming

Mahayana natural farming is the ultimate expression of Fukuoka's philosophy — a system where the farmer does nothing that nature would not do on its own. No compost is applied because the soil generates its own fertility through the decomposition of plant residues in place. No pruning is performed because trees know their own ideal form. No weeding is done because weeds serve essential ecological functions.

The Mahayana farmer's role is not to manage but to observe and understand. Rather than asking "what should I do for this land?", the Mahayana farmer asks "what is the land already doing that I should not interfere with?" This reversal of perspective is the fundamental shift that distinguishes Mahayana farming from all other agricultural approaches.

Fukuoka described his own farming as Mahayana natural farming. His rice and barley fields produced grain without plowing, fertilizing, weeding, or applying chemicals. His citrus orchard grew fruit without pruning, spraying, or fertilizing. The system worked not because Fukuoka was skilled at management but because he was skilled at non-intervention.








## Buddhist Philosophical Roots

Fukuoka's use of Mahayana and Hinayana terminology draws from the Buddhist distinction between the Greater and Lesser Vehicles of spiritual practice. In Buddhism, the Hinayana path focuses on individual liberation through disciplined practice, while the Mahayana path seeks liberation for all beings through compassion and the recognition of emptiness.

Applied to farming, this distinction becomes: Hinayana farming seeks to grow food through disciplined human effort, while Mahayana farming seeks to allow food to grow through the recognition that nature is already complete and sufficient. The Mahayana farmer does not try to improve nature because doing so implies that nature is deficient — an assumption Fukuoka rejected entirely.








## Practical Implications

The distinction between Mahayana and Hinayana farming has practical consequences for design and management. A Hinayana approach is easier to implement and produces more predictable results in the short term, making it suitable for farmers transitioning from conventional methods. A Mahayana approach requires deep ecological understanding and a willingness to accept unpredictable outcomes, but ultimately produces more resilient and self-sustaining systems.

Fukuoka's own journey, described in [[fukuoka-limits-scientific-method-agriculture]], followed this progression: he began as a conventional agricultural scientist, gradually reduced his interventions, and eventually arrived at Mahayana farming through decades of observation and experimentation. He believed that the journey was necessary — that one cannot simply adopt Mahayana farming without understanding why each intervention is unnecessary.
