# Mahayana and Hinayana Natural Farming

Masanobu Fukuoka classified approaches to agriculture into three distinct
categories drawn from Buddhist philosophy: Mahayana natural farming, Hinayana
natural farming, and scientific farming. This framework provides a philosophical
foundation for understanding why natural farming is not merely an alternative
set of techniques but represents a fundamentally different relationship between
humanity and the natural world.

## The Three Ways of Farming

### Mahayana Natural Farming (Pure Natural Farming)

Mahayana natural farming represents the highest state of agriculture, in which
the human spirit and human life blend completely with the natural order. Man
lives freely as an integral part of the natural world, subsisting on its bounty
without resorting to purposeful effort. This type of farming transcends time and
space and reaches the zenith of understanding and enlightenment.

Fukuoka likened this relationship to an ideal marriage in which partners
realize a perfect life together without asking for, giving, or receiving
anything of each other. Those who achieve this state live as hermits and wise
men. It is the "philosopher's way of farming" that operates in the absolute
world, entirely beyond the realm of human comparison.

In practical terms, Mahayana farming corresponds to ecosystems that function
without any human management at all. The ancient shrine groves of Japanese
villages, where towering trees grew to maturity without nutrition science or
plant ecology, exemplify this condition. Saved from the axe and saw, the trees
grew into large specimens entirely of their own accord.

### Hinayana Natural Farming (Natural and Organic Farming)

Hinayana natural farming arises when man earnestly seeks entry to the realm
of Mahayana farming. Desirous of nature's true blessings, he prepares himself
to receive them. This is the road leading to complete enlightenment, but it
falls short of that perfect state. Fukuoka compared the relationship to a
lover who yearns after his beloved and asks for her hand but has not yet
achieved full union.

This category encompasses what is commonly called organic farming and other
approaches that minimize human intervention while still operating within the
framework of human knowledge. Hinayana farming belongs to the relative world
alongside scientific farming. Both derive from nature as verified through
discriminating knowledge, but Hinayana farming attempts to cast off human
knowledge and devote itself to the pure forces of nature, whereas scientific
farming adds human knowledge on top of natural forces.

### Scientific Farming (Scientific Agriculture)

Scientific farming exists in a state of contradiction: man is estranged from
nature yet longs to return to it. Scientific farming wanders blindly, sometimes
calling upon nature's blessings and sometimes rejecting them in favor of human
knowledge and action. Fukuoka compared this to a lover unable to decide whose
hand to ask in marriage, who imprudently courts multiple parties while
agonizing over indecision.

Scientific farming proceeds by breaking nature apart and studying its
fragments. It collects data on isolated components and attempts to reassemble
them into superior systems. However, because it begins with discriminating
knowledge, it can only ever produce incomplete, fragmented results. The more
it dissects nature, the further it moves from the living whole.

## The Sword-Fighting Analogy

Fukuoka borrowed from the Japanese art of sword fighting to illustrate the
differences among these approaches:

- **Mahayana natural farming** is the unmoving "no-sword" school. It is
  impossible to compare with other approaches because it operates on an
  entirely different plane. The practitioner wins without fighting.

- **Hinayana natural farming** is the "one-sword" school, directed toward the
  center. It seeks to reduce the number of techniques to the minimum, ideally
  to a single perfected approach.

- **Scientific farming** is the "two-sword" school, directed outward. It
  continuously increases the number of techniques, tools, and interventions.

Scientific farming uses all possible means at its disposal, increasing the
number of swords. Natural farming tries to obtain the best possible results
while rendering all means useless, either reducing the number of swords to
one (Hinayana) or doing entirely without (Mahayana).

## Why Scientific Farming Cannot Surpass Nature

The philosophical conviction underlying this framework holds that if man makes
a genuine effort to approach nature, even abandoning all deeds and actions,
nature will take over and perform them for him. No matter how many techniques
scientific farming employs, it cannot exceed nature because:

1. Scientific farming is the sum of finite knowledge extracted from nature.
   Human knowledge is a tiny, closely circumscribed fraction of the
   infinitude of the natural world.

2. The amount of sunlight a field receives is fixed. Rice yield depends on
   photosynthesis per unit area, which depends on available sunlight. No
   degree of human tampering with other conditions can change this upper limit.
   What appears to be high-yielding technology is merely an attempt to
   approach natural yield ceilings or minimize harvest losses.

3. The level of technology needed to fabricate a single persimmon seed or
   leaf artificially is incomparably greater than that required to launch a
   rocket. The world's scientists pooling all resources could not create what
   nature produces effortlessly.

4. An imitation can never outclass the original. Imperfection always lies in
  the shadow of perfection.

## The Centripetal vs. Centrifugal Dynamic

Natural farming moves centripetally toward nature, converging and simplifying.
Scientific farming moves centrifugally away from nature, diverging and
expanding. These paths are forever parallel and never cross. With every two
steps forward science takes, it takes one step back, returning to nature to
drink of its knowledge. Once nourished, it ventures three or four steps away
again, only to return when it runs into problems. The distance between nature
and science grows ever larger.

Fukuoka observed this pattern in the development of sericulture: from
collecting wild cocoons, to indoor rearing with precise climate control, to
a partial return to outdoor rearing when simpler methods were found to work,
and finally to fully industrialized sericulture with artificial feed when
natural silk prices rose. Each swing of the pendulum moves further from
nature than the last.

## Energy Efficiency as the Ultimate Measure

When measured by the efficiency of energy used in production, natural farming
emerges as the most productive method. Natural farming produces 200,000
kilocalories per man-day of labor with zero outside energy inputs. Traditional
farming required ten times this energy expenditure. Small-scale mechanization
doubled it again, and large-scale mechanization doubled it yet again. This
geometric progression has produced the energy-intensive, economically
precarious agriculture of today.

The farmer who mechanizes trades outdoor work in open fields for dreary hours
shut up inside a factory, earning outside income to pay for equipment that
was supposed to make farming easier. The exchange of real agricultural
productivity for apparent productivity measured in monetary terms represents
the fundamental confusion of scientific farming.

## The Philosophy of Mu

Underlying the three-way framework is the Buddhist concept of Mu, or
nothingness. Fukuoka described natural farming as originating in this
philosophy and returning to a "do-nothing" nature. The village philosophy of
old Japan embodied the principle that "philosophy is unnecessary," which
itself constituted the highest philosophy. Farmers of earlier eras were free
of anguish and grief because they lived intuitively, without the need to
intellectually dissect their existence. The modern compulsion to analyze and
improve upon nature has produced not wisdom but confusion, replacing
satisfaction with endless anxiety about yields, pests, and techniques.
