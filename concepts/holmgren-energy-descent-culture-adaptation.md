## Grassroots Approaches

If grassroots approaches to energy descent are to succeed, they must find more efficient ways of communicating and sharing ideas than are currently available. We need to find working relationships with nature that provide foundations for an energy descent culture. Many of the institutional structures we have built will prove highly dysfunctional in the energy descent towards sustainable society.

## Adaptation Requires Flexibility

Actions driven by certainty and belief are less adaptive than those responsive to feedback and observation. Adaptation to energy descent depends on a dynamic engagement with changing conditions, not adherence to fixed blueprints. Nature is always more complex and surprising than our models predict.

Permaculture uses conditions of energy descent to consciously design our pathway. This is the key distinction from survivalism or doom-preparation -- permaculture designs for abundance within constraints, not mere subsistence.

## Culture for Energy Descent

Cultural mechanisms that make ethics central are central in the development of a culture for energy descent. The [[holmgren-household-energy-storages]] and [[bioregional-organization]].

Many of these movements developed in isolation from each other, each addressing a particular symptom of industrial dysfunction. Permaculture provides the integrating framework that connects them into a coherent cultural alternative. The degree to which these examples reflect the design criteria of local scale, low technology, and incremental development is a prediction of their likely persistence in a low-energy future.

## Climate and Biogeography

Despite the enormous variation in characteristics of individual tree species, some general patterns of global climate and biogeography are useful in understanding which systems and species are likely to be most adapted to the local environment. Bill Mollison devoted a whole chapter in Permaculture: A Designers' Manual to explaining broad patterns of climate and microclimate because of the importance of this basic geographic knowledge.

## Deciduous and Evergreen Patterns

One of the most noticeable differences between forests around the world is the predominance of evergreen trees in most climates, except in the temperate zones of the northern hemisphere where deciduous species predominate. The explanation of subtle differences between northern and southern hemisphere temperate climates has major implications for permaculture design of tree crop and orchard systems.

Because of the predominance of ocean in the southern hemisphere, the alternation between summer and winter is less extreme. This milder climate favours the possibility of growth in winter and, in winter-rainfall areas, the leaching of soil nutrients. Thus evergreen species are generally better adapted because they take advantage of more erratic growing conditions.

## Ecosystem Structure and Rainfall

The structure of ecosystems reflects annual rainfall more than sunshine levels. The period of active growth of vegetation also closely follows the seasonality of rainfall. In southern Australia winter-active vegetation predominates, while in northern Australia the reverse is true. Although irrigation allows bypassing these climatic limitations in garden agriculture, large-scale systems need to be designed to reflect and use rainfall where and when it falls.

Efficient use of rainfall is now one of the catchcries of the [[holmgren-ethics-cultural-mechanisms]] movement. Many scientists and farmers now accept that broadacre agriculture requires fundamental redesign to address the failure of annual crops and pastures to use rainfall efficiently.

## Landcare as Vindication

Perhaps the greatest vindication of permaculture, agroforestry and analogue forestry has been the recognition that the lack of perennial and woody plants in Australian agricultural landscapes is the prime cause of salinity and a range of other land degradation symptoms. Annual crops and pastures are simply not efficient at using rainfall. This unused water leaches nutrients and causes acidification, and contributes to a rising saline groundwater.

Systems ecologist Ted Lefroy studied the use of water and nutrients in Western Australian wheat belt agriculture as measures of ecosystem efficiency. His work was one of the starting points for a large-scale multimillion-dollar research and development project called Redesigning Agriculture for Australian Landscapes.
