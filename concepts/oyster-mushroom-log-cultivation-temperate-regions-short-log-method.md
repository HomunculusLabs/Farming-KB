### Sapwood-to-Heartwood Ratio

Since mushrooms feed primarily on sapwood — the lighter, outermost wood layer — the selection of tree trunks should prioritize those with large sapwood areas. Heartwood, the darker inner core, provides minimal nutritional value to the fungus. Logs with greater sapwood proportion will produce mushrooms for more years before becoming exhausted. For best results, favor younger trees or branches with wide sapwood bands and relatively narrow heartwood cores.

## Tree Felling and Log Preparation

### Timing of Felling

Trees should be felled during the dormant season when bark adheres tightly to the wood surface and tree trunks are fully charged with sap and stored nutrients. The dormant period ensures maximum available carbohydrates and minerals for the colonizing mycelium. Felling too late in the dormant season means that buds have already begun to germinate and consume stored reserves, reducing the [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] of the wood.

### Post-Felling Handling

After felling, leave trees uncut in a shaded area for several days before inoculation. This resting period serves two critical functions. First, it allows living wood cells to die — these actively metabolizing cells interfere with mycelial growth and can produce defense compounds that slow colonization. Second, it allows the moisture content to stabilize at the optimal range of 38–42%, which is necessary for smooth transfer of fungal digestive enzymes and dissolved nutrients throughout the wood matrix.

A practical field indicator of proper moisture content: when a coin-thick crack appears in the cross-section of a cut log, the log has reached adequate moisture for inoculation. Avoid using very old, dry logs as insufficient water content significantly retards mycelial growth. Similarly, freshly cut logs with actively living cells should not be inoculated immediately. Cut trees should never contact the ground during storage to prevent contamination from soil-borne organisms.

### Log Dimensions

Ideal log diameter is 15–20 cm for efficient handling and productive colonization. Logs should be cut into 20 cm lengths a few days before the intended inoculation date, allowing the cut surfaces to stabilize.

## Spawn Preparation and Strain Selection

Sawdust spawn is the standard inoculation medium for oyster mushroom log cultivation. It adheres well to log end-grain surfaces and provides a substrate matrix that matches the woody environment the mycelium will colonize. Two strain categories are available:

- **Low-temperature strains** form bunches easily and produce high yields of quality fruitbodies with desirable market characteristics
- **High-temperature strains** flush rapidly with high total yield but produce lower quality mushrooms with less market appeal

Order spawn from a reliable dealer 1–2 months ahead of the intended inoculation date. Specify the desired shipping date to ensure maximum freshness and vitality, as spawn quality degrades with storage time.

## Inoculation Method

### Timing

Begin inoculation when outside daily temperatures range from approximately 5°C (nighttime low) to 20°C (daytime high), typically corresponding to early to mid-spring in temperate regions. While oyster mushroom mycelia survive across a broad range from 5–35°C, they grow optimally at 25–27°C. The spring window provides temperatures warm enough for active growth but cool enough to suppress competitor organisms.

### Procedure

1. **Sort logs by width** to ensure uniform colonization rates across the batch
2. **Prepare inoculum** by mixing 20–50% spawn with a supplement composed of sawdust and rice bran at a 4:1 ratio by volume, adjusted to 60–65% moisture content
3. **Apply inoculum** 5–10 mm thick to each log end, with extra concentration on the margin of cut surfaces where contamination risk is highest
4. **Stack logs** five or six high in sandwich fashion, stacking in the order they were cut to minimize the exposure time of each spawn layer to airborne contaminants
5. **Arrange stacks** in 3–4 rows with walking aisles between them for ventilation and heat dissipation

Excessive supplement increases contamination risk, particularly in warm weather. Start with lower supplement ratios (20%) and increase gradually with experience and favorable conditions.

## Incubation

Cover inoculated logs with plastic sheeting in a shady, moist location. Maintain incubation temperature between 15–20°C with minimal diurnal fluctuation — wide temperature swings significantly increase the risk of fungal infestation by competitor molds. Mycelia require 3–4 days to recover from the physical disturbance of inoculation and resume visible growth. Water the surrounding ground when conditions are dry to maintain ambient humidity without directly wetting the logs. Inspect logs regularly for green mold (Trichoderma spp.) and treat affected spots promptly with [[benlate-fungicide]]. Through summer months, employ shade and ventilation to maintain temperatures near 20°C.

## Burying

By mid-summer, well-colonized logs will adhere firmly to each other through mycelial bonding, indicating the mycelium has fully permeated the wood and the logs are ready for the fruiting stage. Burying is the preferred method over standing because the surrounding sand prevents log drying and maintains stable moisture:

1. Separate each bonded log unit carefully
2. Bury logs vertically in a prepared mound with inoculated sides facing upward
3. Leave approximately 10–20% of total log length above ground level
4. Space individual logs 10–15 cm apart on a 1-meter-wide ridge
5. Direct free water into a 50 cm drainage furrow to prevent waterlogging
6. Cover the entire area with shade cloth or a tent to prevent direct sun exposure and moisture loss
