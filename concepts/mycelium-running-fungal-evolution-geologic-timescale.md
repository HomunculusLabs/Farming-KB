## Ancient Origins of the Fungal Kingdom

Fungi are among the oldest organisms on Earth, with fossil evidence and
molecular clock analyses suggesting their origin dates back approximately one to
two billion years. The earliest undisputed fungal fossils appear in the
Ordovician period, roughly 450 million years ago, though controversial
microfossils from the Precambrian may push the fungal lineage back much further.
Molecular phylogenetics, using ribosomal RNA sequences, indicates that fungi
diverged from animals roughly one billion years ago, making the fungal and
animal kingdoms sister groups.

The earliest fungi were likely aquatic organisms, simple single-celled or
filamentous forms that decomposed [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]] primordial aquatic
ecosystems. As life colonized land beginning in the Ordovician and Silurian
periods, fungi were among the first organisms to make the transition, playing a
critical role in transforming barren rock into soil. Fossil evidence from the
Rhynie Chert in Scotland, dating to approximately 400 million years ago, reveals
diverse fungal symbionts associated with some of the earliest land plants.

The co-evolution of fungi and plants was one of the most important symbiotic
partnerships in Earth history. Fungi provided early land plants with mineral
nutrients scavenged from rock, while plants provided fungi with carbohydrates
produced through photosynthesis. This mutualistic relationship was almost
certainly essential for the successful colonization of land by plants. Without
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] to extract minerals from the thin soils of the early
terrestrial environment, plants may never have established themselves on land.

## The Age of Coal Forests

During the Carboniferous period, approximately 300 to 360 million years ago,
vast [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]] covered much of the supercontinent Pangaea. These forests
were dominated by giant lycopsids, ferns, and early seed plants that grew in
swampy conditions. When these plants died, they fell into oxygen-poor waters
where decomposition was extremely slow, and over millions of years, their
remains were compressed into the coal deposits that powered the Industrial
Revolution.

Stamets and other mycologists have proposed that the Carboniferous coal deposits
exist largely because fungi had not yet evolved the enzymatic machinery to
efficiently [[query-how-do-white-rot-fungi-break-down-lignin]], the tough polymer that gives wood its structural
rigidity. [[brown-rot-and-white-rot-fungi-in-mycoremediation]], which produce lignin-degrading enzymes called
peroxidases and laccases, appear to have diversified significantly toward the
end of the Carboniferous period. Once these fungi evolved the ability to
decompose lignin, the massive accumulation of undecomposed woody material that
had characterized the Carboniferous came to an end.

This hypothesis suggests that the evolution of lignin-degrading enzymes by white
rot fungi was one of the most consequential events in the history of life on
Earth. By enabling the complete decomposition of woody material, these fungi
closed the carbon cycle, preventing the accumulation of organic carbon that had
previously been buried as coal. The end of the great coal-forming period roughly
coincides with the diversification of the Agaricales, the order that includes
most modern mushroom-forming fungi.

## Mass Extinctions and Fungal Proliferation

The end-Permian mass extinction, approximately 252 million years ago, was the
most severe extinction event in Earth history, eliminating an estimated 90 to 95
percent of marine species and 70 percent of terrestrial vertebrate species. In
the aftermath of this catastrophe, fungal fossils show a dramatic proliferation.
A prominent fungal spike appears in the geological record immediately following
the extinction boundary, indicating that fungi were among the primary
beneficiaries of the [[fukuoka-pine-forest-ecosystem-collapse-cascade-matsutake]].

Stamets interprets this fungal spike as evidence that fungi are nature primary
agents of ecological recovery after catastrophic disturbances. When most plant
and animal species were eliminated, the vast quantities of dead organic matter
created ideal conditions for [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]]. The fungi that proliferated
in this post-extinction environment were likely generalist decomposers capable
of breaking down a wide range of organic materials, recycling nutrients and
rebuilding soil for eventual ecosystem recovery.

Similar fungal spikes have been identified at other extinction boundaries,
though none as pronounced as the end-Permian event. The pattern is consistent:
mass extinctions create enormous pulses of dead organic matter, which fungi
decompose and recycle, gradually restoring the conditions necessary for
ecosystem recovery. This role as nature recyclers and ecosystem restorers
establishes fungi as perhaps the most resilient and ecologically important
kingdom of organisms on the planet.
