## Nitrogen as a Plant Nutrient

updated: 2026-04-21

Nitrogen (N) is arguably the most important mineral nutrient for plant growth. Most plants in a garden are about 3 to 4 percent nitrogen by weight — similar to the approximately 3 percent found in the human body, reflecting that we truly are what we eat. Only carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen exist in higher concentrations in plants (and humans).

## Roles in Plant Biology

**Protein building:** Nitrogen is the backbone of [[mycelial-network-nutrient-transport-imaging-gadd]]. These proteins require nitrogen for their structure.

## Mobility and Deficiency Symptoms

Nitrogen remains mobile inside plants, meaning it can be transported to where it is needed most. This mobility is why the first signs of nitrogen deficiency appear in older leaves: the plant robs nitrogen from older cells to sustain new growth. The classic symptom is a yellowing lawn.

## Denitrification Losses

When soil becomes anaerobic, certain microbes use nitrogen compounds as their energy source, converting nitrates back to atmospheric nitrogen gas (N2) or nitrous oxide (N2O). This denitrification represents a significant nitrogen loss for gardeners. Poorly aerated, compacted, or waterlogged soils are particularly susceptible. ## Phosphorus as a Plant Nutrient

updated: 2026-04-21

Phosphorus (P) is one of the three primary macronutrients (alongside nitrogen and potassium) and is critical for energy transfer, genetic coding, and membrane integrity in all plants.

## Roles in Plant Biology

**DNA and RNA:** Phosphorus is a structural component of the nucleotide molecules that store and translate the genetic code — the same role as nitrogen in these molecules.

**Energy currency (ATP):** Phosphorus is the base of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the universal energy molecule. There are two bonds between the three phosphorus atoms in ATP. The bonds contain lots of energy, which is released when they are broken. Plant cells have enzymes that split off phosphorus from ATP molecules and rebind them onto its precursor molecule, adenosine diphosphate (ADP). Breaking these phosphorus bonds produces energy, and making these bonds stores it. Without phosphorus, there is no energy to sustain growth. Stunted growth is the classic sign of [[symbiotic-fungi-mycorrhizal-carbon-trading]] essential for efficient phosphorus uptake, especially in organic systems.
