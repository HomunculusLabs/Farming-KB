# Nitrogen as a Plant Nutrient

updated: 2026-04-21

Nitrogen (N) is arguably the most important mineral nutrient for plant growth. Most plants in a garden are about 3 to 4 percent nitrogen by weight — similar to the approximately 3 percent found in the human body, reflecting that we truly are what we eat. Only carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen exist in higher concentrations in plants (and humans).

## Roles in Plant Biology

**Protein building:** Nitrogen is the backbone of [[photosynthesis-and-chloroplast-function]]. A lack of nitrogen means less green pigment — which is why nitrogen deficiency causes yellowing of leaves.

**Membrane function:** Proteins in cellular membranes act as channels, carriers, and motors necessary for water and [[mycelial-network-nutrient-transport-imaging-gadd]]. These proteins require nitrogen for their structure.

## Mobility and Deficiency Symptoms

Nitrogen remains mobile inside plants, meaning it can be transported to where it is needed most. This mobility is why the first signs of nitrogen deficiency appear in older leaves: the plant robs nitrogen from older cells to sustain new growth. The classic symptom is a yellowing lawn.

## Nitrogen and Soil pH

Nitrogen also has a great influence on the fungal-keratin-decomposition-processes provides over half a billion tons of artificial fertilizers annually, requiring 5 percent of global natural gas production and sustaining more than a third of world food production.

## Biological Nitrogen Fixation

Up to 50 percent of the nitrogen in a garden can come from [[rhizobia-legume-nodule-formation]], soil bacteria forming symbiotic relationships with legumes. Rhizobia provide the enzymes to break triple-bonded nitrogen; the plant provides housing (root nodules) and carbon-based root-exudates|exudates. A specific match is required between Rhizobia species and plant species.

Frankia (actinomycetes) associate with actinorhizal plants including alders, bayberry, dryas, and sea buckthorn. These are pioneer plants that bring plant-available nitrogen into poor soils. See also [[soil-food-web-structure]] for more on bacterial vs. fungal soil dynamics.

## Denitrification Losses

When soil becomes anaerobic, certain microbes use nitrogen compounds as their energy source, converting nitrates back to atmospheric nitrogen gas (N2) or nitrous oxide (N2O). This denitrification represents a significant nitrogen loss for gardeners. Poorly aerated, compacted, or waterlogged soils are particularly susceptible. Maintaining good [[permaculture-plants-nitrogen-fixing-trees]] as cover crops or companion plants
- Applying nitrogen-rich [[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]] (blood meal, fish emulsion, alfalfa meal)
- Maintaining aerobic soil conditions to prevent denitrification losses
- Using [[ozier-pdc-composting-and-soil-building]], where much of it is brought to plants by [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]]. Gardeners can collect nodules from established legumes, open them, and roll seeds in the pink material to inoculate new plantings.

# Phosphorus as a Plant Nutrient

updated: 2026-04-21

Phosphorus (P) is one of the three primary macronutrients (alongside nitrogen and potassium) and is critical for energy transfer, genetic coding, and membrane integrity in all plants.

## Roles in Plant Biology

**DNA and RNA:** Phosphorus is a structural component of the nucleotide molecules that store and translate the genetic code — the same role as nitrogen in these molecules.

**Energy currency (ATP):** Phosphorus is the base of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the universal energy molecule. There are two bonds between the three phosphorus atoms in ATP. The bonds contain lots of energy, which is released when they are broken. Plant cells have enzymes that split off phosphorus from ATP molecules and rebind them onto its precursor molecule, adenosine diphosphate (ADP). Breaking these phosphorus bonds produces energy, and making these bonds stores it. Without phosphorus, there is no energy to sustain growth. Stunted growth is the classic sign of [[query-how-to-fix-phosphorus-deficiency-in-cannabis]].

**Cell membranes:** Phosphorus is a major component of phospholipids, which form cell membranes. It is a key element in the cellular gate-keeping system, absolutely necessary to build and maintain the integrity of every plant cell.

## ATP and Energy Demand

The list of plant cell functions requiring energy is a long one, but at the top is the need for adequate energy to enable plants to utilize the sugars they produce. When supplies of phosphorus are low, plant leaves turn bluish green. This is caused by an accumulation of sugars, which can't be used because of a lack of energy. DNA, RNA, and ATP together define the key role of phosphorus in plants.

## Deficiency Symptoms

When phosphorus supplies are low, leaves turn bluish green. This results from accumulation of sugars that cannot be used without sufficient energy from ATP. Like nitrogen, phosphorus is mobile in plants, so symptoms appear first in older leaves. If deficiency occurs later in the plant's life, blooming and continued root growth are affected — no new cells can form at apical meristems.

## The Phosphorus Cycle

Phosphorus originates from the weathering of apatite rock. It enters soils in two forms:

- **Organic:** from decaying dead plants, manures, and microbes
- **Inorganic:** from weathered rock, adsorbed to clay particle and organic matter surfaces through ionic attraction

The ionic attraction binding inorganic phosphorus to soil particles means it does not move much in soil. In fact, it is so tied up that 1 acre (0.40 hectare) of soil might have only 1 pound (0.45 kg) of phosphorus dissolved in water and available to plants. Eventually, phosphorus makes its way to the ocean and is redeposited as apatite rock — a cycle taking millions of years.

## Global Phosphorus Crisis

Because phosphorus becomes tied up in the soil, tremendous amounts are applied to agricultural fields (and, too often, gardens). Many believe that we have squeezed the phosphorus cycle to the point that the world is going to reach peak production and then quickly run out of phosphorus. More than 22 million tons (20 million tonnes) of phosphorus are applied to fields every year. The Global Phosphorus Research Initiative estimates that mined supplies will be insufficient for crops in only 30 to 40 years. Because 95 percent of remaining mineable phosphorus is found in Morocco, China, South Africa, Jordan, and the United States, this nutrient may become a source of intense military and economic competition — echoing the 19th-century guano wars. See [[symbiotic-fungi-mycorrhizal-carbon-trading]] essential for efficient phosphorus uptake, especially in organic systems.

## Phosphorus and Soil pH

[[query-how-does-phosphorus-availability-change-across-soil-ph-levels]] is particularly sensitive to soil-ph-management|soil pH. When the pH gets below 6, phosphorus in the form HPO4-2 becomes much less available because at lower pH this anion bonds more strongly to the cations of iron and aluminum. It is fixed, meaning it is not usable by plants. On the other side, if the pH gets above 7, too much becomes fixed by reacting with calcium and forming [[bionutrients-lab-calcium-phosphate-calamansi]], rendering phosphorus increasingly unavailable.
