# Nitrogen as a Plant Nutrient

updated: 2026-04-21

Nitrogen (N) is arguably the most important mineral nutrient for plant growth. Most plants in a garden are about 3 to 4 percent nitrogen by weight — similar to the approximately 3 percent found in the human body, reflecting that we truly are what we eat. Only carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen exist in higher concentrations in plants (and humans).

## Roles in Plant Biology

**Protein building:** Nitrogen is the backbone of [[knf-fish-amino-acid]], the structural building blocks of proteins. Without nitrogen, there are no proteins, and without proteins, there are no enzymes. All cellular activities require enzymatic catalysis. Nothing happens on a cellular level without an enzyme being involved.

**Nucleotide synthesis:** Nitrogen is the base element for nucleotide molecules — the building blocks of DNA and RNA, which store and translate the genetic code. Much of a plant's cellular activity is dedicated to ensuring adequate production of nitrogen-based enzymes.

**Chlorophyll production:** Nitrogen is an essential part of the chlorophyll molecule (C55H72MgN4O5). The four nitrogen atoms in chlorophyll are required for [[photosynthesis-and-chloroplast-function]]. A lack of nitrogen means less green pigment — which is why nitrogen deficiency causes yellowing of leaves.

**Membrane function:** Proteins in cellular membranes act as channels, carriers, and motors necessary for water and nutrient transport. These proteins require nitrogen for their structure.

## Mobility and Deficiency Symptoms

Nitrogen remains mobile inside plants, meaning it can be transported to where it is needed most. This mobility is why the first signs of nitrogen deficiency appear in older leaves: the plant robs nitrogen from older cells to sustain new growth. The classic symptom is a yellowing lawn.

## Nitrogen and Soil pH

Nitrogen also has a great influence on the [[soil-testing-methods-interpretation]], which directly affects uptake of all nutrients. When NO3- is taken up, hydroxyl ions (OH-) are released into the rhizosphere, raising pH. When ammonium (NH4+) is taken up, hydrogen ions (H+) are released, lowering pH. This means the form of nitrogen applied has downstream effects on the availability of all other nutrients.

## Atmospheric Nitrogen

The Earth's atmosphere consists of 78 percent nitrogen. However, atmospheric nitrogen (N2) is unavailable to plants because nitrogen atoms form extremely strong triple covalent bonds. Breaking these bonds is one of the most energy-intensive processes in agriculture. Until the early 1900s, only biological means (bacteria and Archaea) could break these bonds. Today the [[fungal-keratin-decomposition-processes]] provides over half a billion tons of artificial fertilizers annually, requiring 5 percent of global natural gas production and sustaining more than a third of world food production.

## Biological Nitrogen Fixation

Up to 50 percent of the nitrogen in a garden can come from [[nitrogen-fixing-trees]] (diazotrophs). The most familiar are [[rhizobia-legume-nodule-formation]], soil bacteria forming symbiotic relationships with legumes. Rhizobia provide the enzymes to break triple-bonded nitrogen; the plant provides housing (root nodules) and carbon-based root-exudates|exudates. A specific match is required between Rhizobia species and plant species.

Frankia (actinomycetes) associate with actinorhizal plants including alders, bayberry, dryas, and sea buckthorn. These are pioneer plants that bring plant-available nitrogen into poor soils. See also [[composting-and-soil-biology]].

## The Nitrogen Cycle

Atmospheric nitrogen (N2) is so tightly bound that it is unavailable for use in most biological systems. A tiny fraction is made available by electrical and snow storms, but specialized microorganisms do the bulk of the work. Some nitrogen-fixing bacteria live in symbiotic relationships with plants in root nodules — Rhizobia on legumes, for example. Others, such as Azotobacter, are free-living soil organisms. Both have the ability to break the triple covalent bond that holds the two nitrogen atoms together. Today, the Haber-Bosch process allows for artificial nitrogen fixation and only about 50 to 60 percent of available nitrogen is produced by biological systems.

Fixed nitrogen in the form of ammonium (NH4+) and nitrate (NO3-) is useful as a nutrient to plants and is taken up and assimilated into various compounds within the plant. When plants die, either a natural death or as a result of being eaten by animals, they decompose. The decay by bacteria and fungi results in ammonification, the production of NH4+. Some of this is taken up by plants, and the rest is converted by nitrifying bacteria to nitrite (NO2-), which is then converted by bacteria and Archaea into NO3-. Some nitrate is absorbed by plants and assimilated, some remains in the soil, and the rest washes out of the soil and is suspended in water.

When soil or water that contains nitrogen becomes anaerobic (lacking oxygen), nitrates are converted back to atmospheric nitrogen, as some anaerobic microbes use nitrogen compounds as their energy source. This denitrification is a big loss for the gardener, but it does complete the nitrogen cycle.

## Nitrogen Forms in Soil

Nitrogen exists in soil in several forms, each with different behaviors and availability:

- **Atmospheric N2:** Triple-bonded and unavailable to most organisms
- **Ammonium (NH4+):** Positively charged cation, held by soil CEC, preferred by perennials, trees, and shrubs in fungal-dominated soils
- **Nitrate (NO3-):** Negatively charged anion, mobile in soil, leaches easily, preferred by annuals and vegetables in bacterial-dominated soils
- **Organic nitrogen:** Contained in proteins, amino acids, and microbial biomass; requires mineralization by soil organisms

The form of nitrogen available matters greatly. Plants in the ground for less than a year generally prefer nitrate form, while perennials, trees, and shrubs prefer ammonium. This preference aligns with soil microbial communities: fungi dominate in acidic conditions producing ammonium, while nitrifying bacteria thrive in more neutral pH producing nitrate. See [[soil-food-web-structure]] for more on bacterial vs. fungal soil dynamics.

## Denitrification Losses

When soil becomes anaerobic, certain microbes use nitrogen compounds as their energy source, converting nitrates back to atmospheric nitrogen gas (N2) or nitrous oxide (N2O). This denitrification represents a significant nitrogen loss for gardeners. Poorly aerated, compacted, or waterlogged soils are particularly susceptible. Maintaining good [[soil-compaction-solutions-ingham]] and drainage is essential for nitrogen conservation. Nitrous oxide is also a potent greenhouse gas — roughly 300 times more warming than CO2 — making denitrification an environmental concern beyond the garden.

## Practical Nitrogen Management

For the organic gardener, nitrogen management focuses on feeding the soil food web rather than directly feeding plants. Key strategies include:

- Growing [[permaculture-plants-nitrogen-fixing-trees]] as cover crops or companion plants
- Applying nitrogen-rich organic amendments (blood meal, fish emulsion, alfalfa meal)
- Maintaining aerobic soil conditions to prevent denitrification losses
- Using [[cervantes-compost-tea-organic-brewing]] to boost microbial nitrogen cycling
- Matching nitrogen form to plant type — bacterial teas for annuals, fungal teas for perennials

The goal is to build a self-sustaining nitrogen cycle where biological fixation and mineralization provide steady supplies, reducing or eliminating the need for external inputs.

## Historical Context

Aside from biologically fixed nitrogen, there are relatively few natural sources of nitrogen. Those that exist are primarily deposits of nitrogen-based minerals and guano, both of which were severely exploited in the 1800s, actually resulting in wars; the rise of the modern German, U.S., and British navies; and the start of modern nation building. Around the start of the twentieth century, the German chemist Fritz Haber figured out how to fix nitrogen, and Carl Bosch scaled up the process industrially. This changed agriculture forever.

## Rhizobia Detail

There must be a specific match between Rhizobia species and plant species — commercial mixtures must be chosen carefully. The amount of oxygen has to be limited for the nitrogenase enzyme to work, and root nodules provide such an environment. There is enough nitrogen for the legume and the bacteria to share. Up to 20 percent excess nitrogen is produced as well, and it moves into the soil and [[composting-and-soil-biology]], where much of it is brought to plants by mycorrhizal fungi. Gardeners can collect nodules from established legumes, open them, and roll seeds in the pink material to inoculate new plantings.

# Phosphorus as a Plant Nutrient

updated: 2026-04-21

Phosphorus (P) is one of the three primary macronutrients (alongside nitrogen and potassium) and is critical for energy transfer, genetic coding, and membrane integrity in all plants.

## Roles in Plant Biology

**DNA and RNA:** Phosphorus is a structural component of the nucleotide molecules that store and translate the genetic code — the same role as nitrogen in these molecules.

**Energy currency (ATP):** Phosphorus is the base of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the universal energy molecule. There are two bonds between the three phosphorus atoms in ATP. The bonds contain lots of energy, which is released when they are broken. Plant cells have enzymes that split off phosphorus from ATP molecules and rebind them onto its precursor molecule, adenosine diphosphate (ADP). Breaking these phosphorus bonds produces energy, and making these bonds stores it. Without phosphorus, there is no energy to sustain growth. Stunted growth is the classic sign of phosphorus deficiency.

**Cell membranes:** Phosphorus is a major component of phospholipids, which form cell membranes. It is a key element in the cellular gate-keeping system, absolutely necessary to build and maintain the integrity of every plant cell.

## ATP and Energy Demand

The list of plant cell functions requiring energy is a long one, but at the top is the need for adequate energy to enable plants to utilize the sugars they produce. When supplies of phosphorus are low, plant leaves turn bluish green. This is caused by an accumulation of sugars, which can't be used because of a lack of energy. DNA, RNA, and ATP together define the key role of phosphorus in plants.

## Deficiency Symptoms

When phosphorus supplies are low, leaves turn bluish green. This results from accumulation of sugars that cannot be used without sufficient energy from ATP. Like nitrogen, phosphorus is mobile in plants, so symptoms appear first in older leaves. If deficiency occurs later in the plant's life, blooming and continued root growth are affected — no new cells can form at apical meristems.

## The Phosphorus Cycle

Phosphorus originates from the weathering of apatite rock. It enters soils in two forms:

- **Organic:** from decaying dead plants, manures, and microbes
- **Inorganic:** from weathered rock, adsorbed to clay particle and organic matter surfaces through ionic attraction

The ionic attraction binding inorganic phosphorus to soil particles means it does not move much in soil. In fact, it is so tied up that 1 acre (0.40 hectare) of soil might have only 1 pound (0.45 kg) of phosphorus dissolved in water and available to plants. Eventually, phosphorus makes its way to the ocean and is redeposited as apatite rock — a cycle taking millions of years.

## Global Phosphorus Crisis

Because phosphorus becomes tied up in the soil, tremendous amounts are applied to agricultural fields (and, too often, gardens). Many believe that we have squeezed the phosphorus cycle to the point that the world is going to reach peak production and then quickly run out of phosphorus. More than 22 million tons (20 million tonnes) of phosphorus are applied to fields every year. The Global Phosphorus Research Initiative estimates that mined supplies will be insufficient for crops in only 30 to 40 years. Because 95 percent of remaining mineable phosphorus is found in Morocco, China, South Africa, Jordan, and the United States, this nutrient may become a source of intense military and economic competition — echoing the 19th-century guano wars. See [[phosphorus-availability]] for more on availability constraints.

## Uptake Requirements

Because phosphorus is so immobile in soil, roots must continuously grow to maintain new contact with it. Additionally, about 95 percent of all plants associate with mycorrhiza|mycorrhizal fungi, which dramatically extend root surface area and provide phosphorus in exchange for carbon from root exudates. This makes [[symbiotic-fungi-mycorrhizal-carbon-trading]] essential for efficient phosphorus uptake, especially in organic systems.

## Phosphorus and Soil pH

Phosphorus availability is particularly sensitive to soil-ph-management|soil pH. When the pH gets below 6, phosphorus in the form HPO4-2 becomes much less available because at lower pH this anion bonds more strongly to the cations of iron and aluminum. It is fixed, meaning it is not usable by plants. On the other side, if the pH gets above 7, too much becomes fixed by reacting with calcium and forming calcium phosphate, rendering phosphorus increasingly unavailable.
