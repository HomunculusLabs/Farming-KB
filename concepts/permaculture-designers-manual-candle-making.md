# permaculture designers manual candle making and Wax Production
# Permaculture Designers Manual - Wax Products from Bees and Plants

updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Candle making and wax production in permaculture are primarily linked to [[permaculture-designers-manual-pig-systems]] and the multiple products of hive management. The Designers Manual references wax and candles as valuable products of bee range management, alongside honey, pollen, propolis, and royal jelly. Wax production also connects to broader themes of local self-reliance, craft production, and integrated resource use.

## Beeswax Production

### Bee Products

Bees produce several valuable and unique products:
- Honey - primary food product and sweetener
- Wax - for candles, polish, and craft
- Pollen - nutritional supplement
- Propolis - medicinal resin
- Royal jelly - bee food with health applications

Bees also carry out the essential service of pollination for a wide variety of food, oil, fruit, and seed crops (mustard, clovers, buckwheat, most smallfruit, apples, and grain legumes).

### Wax Characteristics

Beeswax is a natural wax produced by honey bees (Apis mellifera). It is:
- Produced by worker bees from glands on their abdomen
- Used by bees to build honeycomb cells
- Harvested as a by-product of honey extraction
- A clean-burning, long-lasting fuel for candles
- Edible and used in food processing
- Medicinal and used in salves and polishes

### Propolis

Bees also gather propolis, a hard waxy resinous substance collected from tree buds and bark. Propolis is used by bees to seal gaps in the hive and has antimicrobial properties. It has applications in:
- Natural medicine
- Wood finishing
- Varnish production

## Hive Site Management for Wax Production

### Range Requirements

Bees form a hive or clustered hive site ranging over an area about 2.5km radius. Commercial registered hive sites are thus sited 5km apart in order to restrict disease transmission. Bees prefer to fly 100m or more to forage, and their flight assists in the evaporation of nectar to honey.

### Forage Planning for Wax Production

Bees more efficiently harvest clumped rather than scattered nectar sources, so hedgerows, fields, or clumps of preferred forage species are better than a scatter of the same species in a mosaic of individual plants. Forage planning should consider:
- Species that produce both nectar and pollen
- Succession of flowering species across seasons
- Proximity of forage to hive sites (100m or more preferred)
- Shelter from extremes of cold, wet, wind, and sun

### Hive Site Selection

The hive site itself should be:
- Sheltered from extremes of cold, wet, wind, and sun
- Not atypical of the climate of the range (or bees are tempted out in adverse weather)
- Capable of supporting 100-150 hives depending on range richness

## Candle Making from Beeswax

### Beeswax Candles

Beeswax candles are a traditional craft product with several advantages over paraffin alternatives:
- Clean burning with natural honey scent
- Longer burning time
- Natural negative ions released when burning
- Non-toxic and biodegradable
- Can be made with simple equipment

### Processing Beeswax

After honey extraction, beeswax requires:
1. Melting and filtering to remove debris
2. Moulding into blocks for storage
3. Further processing for specific products (candles, polish, cosmetics)

## Candlenut (Aleurites moluccana)

The Designers Manual references the candlenut tree growing in Hawaii alongside asparagus on sandy soils. The candlenut is a significant plant in traditional Pacific cultures:

### Uses of Candlenut
- The nuts contain high oil content and were traditionally burned as candles
- Oil extracted from nuts used for lighting and cooking
- Wood used for light construction and canoe building
- Ornamental tree in tropical landscapes

### Cultural Significance
The candlenut (kukui in Hawaiian) is the state tree of Hawaii and has been used for centuries as a source of light. The nuts were strung on coconut leaf midribs and burned one at a time, each nut providing approximately 15 minutes of light.

## Wax in Other Contexts

### Leaf Wax

The manual discusses waxy leaf surfaces as adaptations to desert and high-altitude environments. Waxy leaf covers are soft, rough, and hairy, typically found in deserts and at high altitudes. The waxiness often serves as a sun reflector or wind break. Drought-adapted plant species use wax, resin, or other leaf coatings to reduce water loss.

### Wax for Water Catchment

In dryland areas, hill slopes can be sealed with concrete, wax, bitumen, or plastic and used as a "roof" for water catchment. Polynesian "wax paper" and wrapper for baked foods is also referenced.

### Batik Wax-Dye Process

The manual references the batik (wax-dye) process as an example of pattern application in textile arts. Wax is used as a resist in the dyeing process, creating intricate patterns on fabric. This connects wax production to broader cultural and artistic traditions.

### Wax as Fuel

Historically, various forms of wax have been used as fuel. The manual notes that ranged from floating rafts of wax to more sophisticated candle forms, wax has served as an important illumination source across cultures.

## Integration with Permaculture Design

### Multiple Yields from Beekeeping

Candle making represents one of several valuable products from an integrated beekeeping system:
- Primary: Honey production and pollination services
- Secondary: Wax for candles, polish, cosmetics
- Tertiary: Propolis for medicine, pollen for nutrition
- Supporting: Royal jelly, bee venom therapy

### Economic Value

Local candle making from beeswax adds value to the beekeeping enterprise and supports community self-reliance. It provides a craft product that can be sold at farmers' markets and local stores, reducing dependence on imported paraffin candles.

### Seasonal Considerations

Wax production peaks during strong nectar flows when bees are building comb. Hive management for maximum wax production requires:
- Adequate swarm prevention (swarming bees leave with wax resources)
- Supers added at the right time for comb building
- Proper harvesting timing to maximize both honey and wax yields
