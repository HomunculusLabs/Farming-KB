# Fungal Chitosan Biotechnology Applications

## Overview

Chitosan is a linear polysaccharide composed of randomly distributed β-(1→4)-linked D-glucosamine and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units, produced commercially by the deacetylation of chitin — the second most abundant natural polymer after cellulose. While chitosan is most commonly derived from crustacean shell waste (shrimp, crab, krill), fungal chitosan offers several advantages including allergen-free production, consistent quality, and the ability to produce chitosan from specific fungal species cultivated on defined media. The biotechnology of fungal chitosan encompasses production, modification, and diverse applications across agriculture, medicine, environmental science, and industry.

## Fungal Sources of Chitin and Chitosan

### Zygomycetes (Mucoromycota)

Members of the order Mucorales are the richest fungal sources of chitosan, with cell wall chitosan content reaching 20–30% of dry weight:

- **Mucor rouxii (Rhizomucor miehei)**: The most studied fungal chitosan source, producing chitosan with 85–95% deacetylation directly in the cell wall, without requiring additional chemical deacetylation
- **Absidia coerulea**: Produces chitosan with high molecular weight and crystallinity, suitable for biomedical applications

### Soil Amendment

When applied to soil, chitosan:

- Modifies soil microbial communities, generally increasing beneficial bacteria (Pseudomonas, Bacillus) and reducing pathogen populations
- Chelates micronutrients (Fe, Zn, Mn, Cu), improving their availability to plants
- Improves soil aggregate stability through polysaccharide-mediated particle binding
- Stimulates [[psilocybin-shelf-life-stability]] of fruits, vegetables, meat, and seafood
- **Edible films**: Chitosan-based edible films provide moisture barriers and antimicrobial protection
