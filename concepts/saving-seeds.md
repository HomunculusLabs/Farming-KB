# Saving Seeds
[[permaculture-seed-saving-and-selection|Seed saving]] is one of the most empowering homesteading skills. It reduces costs, preserves genetic diversity, and develops plant varieties uniquely adapted to your local conditions. Over generations, saved seeds become more resilient and productive on your specific land.
## Why Save Seeds
- Cost savings: never buy seed for your staple crops again
- Adaptation: plants become better adapted to your soil, climate, and growing methods over generations
- Genetic diversity: preserve heirloom and open-pollinated varieties that may disappear from commercial catalogs
- Self-reliance: independence from seed companies and supply chain disruptions
- Community: share and trade seeds with other local growers
## Open-Pollinated vs Hybrid
Only save seeds from open-pollinated (OP) varieties. Open-pollinated plants reproduce true to type -- the offspring will closely resemble the parent. Hybrid seeds (labeled F1) are crosses between two distinct parent lines. Seeds saved from hybrids will not breed true and may produce inferior or unpredictable offspring. Check seed packets or catalogs -- if it says F1 or hybrid, do not save seed from it.
Heirloom varieties are open-pollinated varieties that have been passed down for generations. All heirlooms are open-pollinated, but not all OP varieties are heirlooms.
## Basic Seed Saving Principles
### Plant Selection
Choose the best plants for seed saving, not the best vegetables. Select for:
- Disease resistance
- Vigor and growth habit
- Flavor and yield
- Adaptation to your conditions
- Seed production (plants that set seed readily)
- Physical characteristics you want to preserve (color, size, shape)
Mark your best plants early in the season and let them go to seed rather than harvesting for food.
### Isolation Distances
Plants of the same species can cross-pollinate, producing offspring that are not true to type. Prevent unwanted crossing through isolation:
- Distance isolation: separate varieties by the required distance
- Time isolation: stagger planting dates so varieties do not flower simultaneously
- Caging: cover plants with mesh bags or screen cages and hand-pollinate
### Isolation Distances by Crop
| Crop | Minimum Isolation Distance |
|---|---|
| Tomatoes | 10-35 feet (mostly self-pollinating) |
| Peppers | 50-500 feet |
| Beans (self-pollinating) | 10-25 feet |
| Peas (self-pollinating) | 10-50 feet |
| Lettuce | 20-50 feet |
| Squash | 0.5-1 mile |
| Corn | 0.5-1 mile (wind-pollinated) |
| Beets/Chard | 0.25-1 mile (biennial) |
| Carrots | 0.25-1 mile (biennial) |
| Onions | 0.25-1 mile (biennial) |
| Brassicas | 0.5-1 mile |
### Population Size
Save seed from enough plants to maintain genetic diversity:
- Self-pollinating crops (tomatoes, beans, peas): minimum 10-20 plants
- Outcrossing crops (squash, corn, brassicas): minimum 50-80 plants
- Biennials (carrots, beets, onions): minimum 80-200 plants
## Seed Processing Methods
### Dry Processing
For crops with seeds in dry pods or husks. Used for beans, peas, corn, lettuce, grains, and most herbs.
1. Allow seed pods to fully dry on the plant (brown and brittle)
2. Harvest the entire seed head or pod
3. Thresh by crushing pods with your hands, feet, or a flail
4. Winnow: pour seed from one container to another in front of a fan to separate lightweight chaff from heavier seeds
5. Further clean with sieves or screens of appropriate mesh size
6. Dry seeds on screens or newspaper in a warm, dry, well-ventilated area for 1-2 weeks
### Wet Processing
For crops with seeds encased in fleshy fruit. Used for tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, squash, melons, and eggplant.
#### Fermentation Method (tomatoes, cucumbers)
1. Squeeze seeds and gel into a jar with a small amount of water
2. Cover loosely and let ferment at room temperature for 2-4 days
3. A mold will form on top -- this is normal and destroys seed-borne diseases
4. Add water, stir, and let seeds settle. Pour off the floating debris and mold.
5. Repeat rinsing until water runs clear and seeds are clean
6. Spread seeds on a screen, paper towel, or plate to dry. Do not dry on paper -- seeds will stick.
7. Dry for 2-3 weeks, stirring daily. Seeds are dry when they snap when bent.
#### Simple Washing Method (peppers, squash, melons)
1. Remove seeds from the fruit
2. Rinse in a fine-mesh strainer under running water
3. Separate seeds from pulp with your fingers
4. Spread on a screen to dry for 2-3 weeks
## Viability Testing
Before planting saved seeds (especially older ones), test germination rate:
1. Place 10 seeds on a damp paper towel
2. Fold the towel and place in a plastic bag
3. Keep in a warm location (70-75F)
4. Check daily for 7-14 days
5. Count sprouted seeds: 8-10 sprouted = excellent; 5-7 = acceptable but sow thicker; under 5 = discard or save more next season
## Seed Storage
Proper storage is critical for maintaining viability:
- Seeds must be completely dry before storage (below 8% moisture for most seeds)
- Store in airtight containers: glass jars with tight lids, metal tins, or moisture-proof bags
- Add a desiccant packet (silica gel) to each container
- Store in a cool, dark, dry location
- Ideal storage temperature: 35-50F
- For long-term storage (5+ years): freeze in airtight containers with desiccant
- Label every container with crop, variety, year saved, and source

## Biennial Crops
Some crops (carrots, beets, onions, cabbage, kale, parsnips) are biennials -- they produce seed in their second year of growth. To save seed:
1. Grow the crop in year one and select the best roots/plants at harvest
2. Store selected roots in a cool, humid location over winter (root cellar, refrigerator in damp sand, or mulched in the ground)
3. Replant in early spring of year two
4. Allow plants to flower and set seed
5. Harvest seed when dry
This requires planning a year in advance and dedicated garden space in year two.
## Seed Libraries and Sharing
Many communities have seed libraries where members can borrow seeds, grow them, save seeds, and return some. This is an excellent way to access new varieties and build local seed sovereignty. Always follow the library's guidelines and return clean, well-labeled seed.
