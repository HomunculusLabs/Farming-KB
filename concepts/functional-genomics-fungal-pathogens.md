# Functional Genomics of Fungal Pathogens

## Overview

Functional genomics encompasses genome-level analytical techniques including expression profiling, high-throughput genetic modification, gene mapping, proteomics, and transcriptional profiling. These technologies are transforming our understanding of fungal pathogens by revealing patterns of protein abundance, networks of gene expression, and the integrated systems underlying pathogenicity. As of 2006, 13 of 41 published complete eukaryotic genomes were from fungal species, reflecting the importance of fungi as model organisms and as pathogens of crops, animals, and humans.

## The Genomics Landscape (circa 2006)

- **Total published genomes**: 355 organisms (26 Archaea, 41 eukaryotic, 207 bacterial).
- **Ongoing projects**: 984 prokaryotic + 590 eukaryotic genome projects.
- **Fungal genomes**: 13 of 41 published eukaryotic genomes — nearly one-third.
- The apparent lag behind bacteria reflects relative genome sizes and complexity, not research effort.

## Functional Genomics Approaches

### Expression Profiling

- **Microarrays**: Measure expression levels of thousands of genes simultaneously.
- **Serial analysis of gene expression (SAGE)**: Quantitative transcript profiling.
- **RNA-seq** (emerging): High-throughput cDNA sequencing.

### High-Throughput Genetic Modification

- **Targeted gene deletion**: Systematic knockouts to determine gene function.
- **RNA interference (RNAi)**: Post-transcriptional gene silencing.
- **Overexpression libraries**: Gain-of-function screens.

### Post-Genomic Technologies

- **Proteomics**: Large-scale protein identification and quantification.
- **Metabolomics**: Comprehensive metabolite profiling.
- **Transcriptional profiling**: Network-level analysis of gene regulation.

### Systems Biology

The ultimate goal: studying organisms as interacting components and integrated systems rather than as isolated genes or pathways.

## Pathogenic Fungi — Classification and Genomic Relevance

Fungal pathogens are classified along a spectrum based on their interaction with host cells:

### Biotrophs

- Obtain nutrients from living host tissue.
- Cause minimal damage to host cells.
- Examples: *[[blumeria-graminis]]* ([[powdery-mildew]]), *Uromyces* spp. (rusts).

### Necrotrophs

- Kill host tissue before or during nutrient acquisition.
- Examples: *[[botrytis-cinerea]]*, *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*.

### Hemibiotrophs

- Initial biotrophic phase followed by necrotrophic phase.
- *Magnaporthe grisea* is classified as a hemibiotroph by some researchers because it does not induce overt disease symptoms until 3 days after infection, and host cells are not damaged during the initial infection period.

### The Significance of Lifestyle Classification

Understanding whether a pathogen is biotrophic, necrotrophic, or hemibiotrophic has profound implications for:

- **Genomic strategy**: Biotrophs may suppress host defence rather than overwhelm it; necrotrophs may deploy toxins and cell-wall-degrading enzymes.
- **Control measures**: Different pathogenic strategies require different intervention approaches.
- **Gene discovery**: Lifestyle-specific gene families can be identified through comparative genomics.

## Key Fungal Pathogen Genomes

Among the 13 completed fungal genomes as of 2006 are several important pathogens:

- ***Saccharomyces cerevisiae***: Baker's yeast — the reference fungal genome and model eukaryote.
- ***Schizosaccharomyces pombe***: Fission yeast — another key model organism.
- ***Magnaporthe grisea***: Rice blast fungus — one of the most important fungal plant pathogens globally.
- ***Ustilago maydis***: Corn smut fungus — model for biotrophic plant-pathogen interactions.
- ***Cryptococcus neoformans***: Human pathogen — encapsulated yeast causing meningitis.
- ***Candida albicans***: Human commensal and pathogen — causes candidiasis.
- ***Aspergillus fumigatus***: Human pathogen — causes aspergillosis, especially in immunocompromised patients.
- ***Aspergillus nidulans***: Model filamentous fungus.
- ***Neurospora crassa***: Model filamentous fungus — extensively studied genetics.

## Comparative Genomics and Gene Function

The availability of multiple fungal genomes enables:

1. **Ortholog identification**: Finding equivalent genes across species.
2. **Gene family expansion/contraction**: Identifying pathogen-specific gene families.
3. **Horizontal gene transfer**: Detecting genes acquired from other organisms.
4. **Evolutionary analysis**: Tracing the evolution of pathogenicity.

### Functional Categories in Fungal Genomes

Analysis of unisequences (unique gene sequences) from fungal genomes reveals functional category distributions:

| Category | Approximate % |
|---|---|
| Unknown protein | ~70% (in early EST studies) |
| Metabolism | Significant proportion |
| Protein destination | ~4% |
| Cell rescue, defence, death and ageing | ~2% |
| Cellular communication/signal transduction | ~2% |
| Transport facilitation | ~2% |
| Intracellular transport | ~2% |
| Cellular biogenesis | ~1% |
| Cellular organization | ~1% |

The high proportion of "unknown" proteins reflects the early stage of fungal genome annotation and the vast number of genes awaiting functional characterization.

## Challenges in Fungal Functional Genomics

- **Genome complexity**: Fungal genomes are larger and more complex than bacterial genomes.
- **Gene redundancy**: Many fungal genes belong to expanded families, complicating knockout analysis.
- **Asexual reproduction**: Some pathogenic fungi lack a known sexual cycle, limiting classical genetic approaches.
- **Essential genes**: Many pathogenicity genes are essential for viability, making knockout lethal.
- **In planta conditions**: Pathogenicity genes are often expressed only during host infection, requiring specialized experimental systems.
