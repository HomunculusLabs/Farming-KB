# London Ridge Mushroom Picking and Market Grades

Falconer's Chapter XX describes in detail the system of growing
mushrooms in ridges outdoors around London, based on his personal
experience working with the London Garden and observations of the
leading market gardeners of the day including Steele, Bagley,
Broadbent, Dancer, Pocock, and Myatt. The picking and grading system
used by these commercial growers was highly organized and remains a
notable example of Victorian market gardening efficiency.

## The London Mushroom Industry

At the time of Falconer's writing, some London gardeners devoted their
whole energy to mushroom-growing alone and made from $2,000 to $5,000 a
year clear profit from one acre of mushrooms grown entirely in ridges
in the open field. There was no other field crop that yielded such a
large profit. They received twenty-four to forty-eight cents a pound for
fresh mushrooms in London, compared to fifty cents to a dollar a pound
in America.

Mr. John F. Barter of Lancfield Street, London, was the king of London
mushroom growers. He grew about 21,000 pounds of mushrooms a year for
the London market. In summer months he employed men for mushroom
bed-making from August to March, then to keep on the same staff, he made
about 10,000 bushels (160,000 bricks weighing 160,000 pounds) of brick
spawn for sale. By the sale of spawn he made just half of his living.

## Ridge Construction and Siting

The site for beds should be a warm, well-sheltered piece of ground,
either in the open field or orchard. Much pains should be exercised to
protect it from cold winds. Although many mushroom ridges were made
under the partial shade of apple and pear trees, Falconer always
preferred making them in open ground. The land should be dry and of a
slightly elevated or sloping nature so that no pools of water can
collect on the surface.

The ground is marked off into strips two feet wide and six feet wide
alternately. The two-foot space is for the mushroom bed, the six-foot
space for the pathway between beds. After the ridges are built, earthed
over and covered with straw, they are almost six feet wide at the base.
Common ridge sizes are two feet wide by two feet high, and two and
one-half feet wide by two and one-half feet high, tapering to six or
eight inches wide at top.

## Heat Management with Iron Bars

Two days after the bed is made up, holes are bored from the top to
nearly the bottom with a small iron bar to let the heat off and prevent
overdrying, spaced about nine inches apart along the center. Old
gardeners who did not use the bar were very particular not to build
ridges before chances of overheating were past. If a bed got overwarm,
they tossed it over, part to each side, exposed it for a day or two to
cool, then remade the bed on the same site.

## The Three-Man Picking Team

In cold weather the mushrooms are gathered at midday. If the weather is
windy and it is possible to postpone gathering for another day, this is
done, as the litter cannot be replaced satisfactorily in windy weather.
The gathering was performed by a highly coordinated three-man team.

One man carefully pulls the straw down from the top of the bed, rolling
it toward him. A second man gathers the mushrooms, pulling them out by
the roots and never cutting them, into baskets. A third man covers up
the bed. In this way the three men go up one side of the ridge and down
the other, and the work is done expeditiously and well without exposing
any part of the bed more than a minute or two at a time.

It is necessary that the uncovering be done by rolling the straw down
from the top of the ridge. If it were rolled up, the covering on the
other side would be sure to slip down a little and break off many small
mushrooms.

## The Three Market Grades

The mushrooms as gathered are sorted into three distinct grades, each
with its own name and market:

- Broilers: the large or wide-spread mushrooms that have opened fully.
- Cups: full-sized mushrooms whose neck frill is merely broken about
  half an inch wide, representing the premium market grade.
- Buttons: small, white mushrooms whose frills are not broken at all,
  the most highly prized and expensive grade.

All three grades are kept strictly separate. The growers who make
mushrooms a specialty assort and pack them in chip baskets, boxes, or
otherwise, as the metropolitan and provincial markets demand. Mr.
Barter wrote that "as to punnetts, we use the same as for strawberries
or peaches; they hold just one pound." It was getting more general to
have little boxes made to hold three to five pounds each, which were
better for packing in larger cases for long journeys.

## Picking Schedule and Yield

The first cutting is a light one. After this the bed is cut twice a
week for three weeks in mild weather, or once a week in inclement
weather. The last two or three pickings are thin and only secured once
a week. Altogether ten or eleven good pickings are gathered from each
bed.

The first beds are made in August, and one or more every month after
till March, just as time, convenience and material permit. Summer beds
are not attempted unless in exceptional cases. The bulk of the beds are
generally put in during September and October. In early fall and spring,
beds yield mushrooms in about six weeks after spawning; in winter they
take eight or nine weeks or more, much depending on the weather.

## Covering the Ridges

After spawning and molding, the beds are covered with litter, that is,
the rankest straw that had been shaken out of the manure, to a depth
of four, six, eight, or ten inches, according to the state of the bed
and weather. If the bed is inclined to be cool or if the weather is
cold, the covering is thickened.

Drenching or long drizzling rains are more injurious to the beds than
cold. To ward them off, old Russia mats and any other sort of cloth or
carpet covering obtainable is laid over the litter on the beds and
weighted down with poles, boards, stones, or anything convenient. This
covering is not disturbed for about four weeks, then on a dry day it
is stripped off and the litter shaken up loosely to dry.

## Salt Watering for Dry Beds

When beds became dry, a handful of salt was dissolved in a large
pailful of water and the beds were freely watered over the straw
covering, but never under it. Falconer never knew of any attempt to
renovate worn-out beds among the London ridge growers. The spent
manure was always in demand for garden crops and lawns on account of
its fineness and freedom from rubbish, and growers who had no further
use for it disposed of it at half the cost price.
