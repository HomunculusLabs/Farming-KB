## Earthworms and Deep Soil Preparation

The double-digging process creates the ideal environment for earthworms. By loosening the soil to a depth of 24 inches, double-digging removes the physical barriers that limit worm activity in compacted soils. The incorporation of compost provides the food source that sustains worm populations. The improved drainage and aeration prevent waterlogging, which can drive worms to the surface or kill them.

Nature, worms, and plant roots continue the work of deep soil preparation after the initial double-dig. Each year, the soil becomes progressively more loose and friable as worm activity and root penetration extend the aerated zone. Jeavons notes that the first year of digging may only reach 15 to 18 inches of effective loosening, but soil depth increases 3 to 6 inches annually as worms and roots work the subsoil.

### Advantages of Vermicomposting
- **Faster processing**: Worms can process organic matter more quickly than a passive compost pile.
- **Year-round production**: Worm bins can operate indoors or in sheltered areas during cold months when outdoor composting slows.
- **Concentrated nutrients**: The resulting castings are more nutrient-dense than standard compost.
- **Space efficiency**: Worm bins require much less space than compost piles.

## The Soil Food Web Connection

Earthworms are a visible and important part of the soil food web, but they work in concert with billions of microscopic organisms. In 1 gram of humus-rich soil there are several billion bacteria, 1 million fungi, 10 to 20 million actinomycetes, and 800,000 algae. The worms process organic matter and create habitat for these microorganisms, which in turn break down nutrients into forms that plants can absorb.

The microbial life provides a "living pulsation" in the soil that preserves its vitality. The microbes tie up essential nutrients in their body tissues as they grow and release them slowly as they die and decompose. They also secrete compounds into the soil that act as "soil glue," improving soil structure. ## Practical Vermicomposting for the Biointensive Gardener

### Worm Species
Red wigglers (Eisenia fetida) and red earthworms (Lumbricus rubellus) are the most commonly used species for vermicomposting. They are surface feeders that thrive in organic-rich environments and reproduce rapidly under favorable conditions. Nightcrawlers (Lumbricus terrestris) are deep-burrowing worms more suited to garden soil than to contained bins.
