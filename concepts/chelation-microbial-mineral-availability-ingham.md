# Chelation: Microbial Mineral Availability

## Overview

Chelation is the biological process by which soil microorganisms bind mineral ions
with organic molecules, neutralizing their electrical charge so that minerals can
cross cell membranes and become available to plants. Without chelation, most
positively charged mineral cations cannot enter root or leaf cells. Dr. Elaine
Ingham emphasizes that chelation is fundamentally a living process — it requires
active biology to produce the molecules that make minerals mobile and
plant-available.

## What Is Chelation

Chelation is a chemical process where an organic molecule (a chelating agent)
binds to a [[alpha-1-3-glucan-fungal-pathogen-cell-walls]]. In order to
speed uptake, cations need to be neutralized, and the typical biological way to
do this is to attach the cation to a protein molecule. This process requires
active, living biology to perform the chelation step.

## How Microorganisms Chelate Minerals

Microbes do much of the work of chelating mineral elements in soil. Bacteria and
fungi need chelated minerals in order to move them into their own cells, so they
perform a significant amount of chelation of charged materials. The microbes

## Why Plants Need Chelated Forms

Plants can only take up minerals that have been neutralized in charge. The plant
has enzymes inside its cells that remove the protein, amino acid, or other
neutralizing elements from the chelated molecule so the mineral can be placed
where needed in cellular machinery. While plants could potentially utilize some
chelated molecules intact, usually the carbon atoms and mineral atoms are needed
in different places, so they are separated. For organic-approved chelated
minerals, the plant has waste-removal mechanisms to deal with the chelating
material once the mineral is released. Synthetic chelators like EDTA, however,
may leave undesirable residues in plant tissue.
