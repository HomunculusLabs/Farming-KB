# Mycorrhizal Root Exudates and Pathogen Interactions

## Overview

Mycorrhizal colonization significantly alters the composition of root
exudates, with implications for interactions with soil-borne pathogens.
Changes in exudate chemistry affect the behavior of pathogen
propagules, including zoospore chemotaxis, spore germination, and
hyphal growth. Research using in vitro bi-compartmental systems and
PCR-DGGE community analysis has revealed complex relationships between
mycorrhizal exudates, pathogen dynamics, and rhizosphere microbial
communities that do not always translate directly from simplified
laboratory assays to more complex soil environments.

## Changes in Exudate Composition

Quantitative and qualitative changes in root exudation after
mycorrhizal colonization have been documented across multiple studies.
Among 27 sugars, amino acids, and organic acids quantified within root
exudates of mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal tomato plants, only proline
and isocitric acid concentrations differed significantly. Proline
concentration in exudates from mature mycorrhizal roots was higher than
from nonmycorrhizal roots and from younger roots. Proline is involved
in plant protection against water and salt stresses and accumulates
following pathogen infection, suggesting it may play a role in the
biocontrol induced by AMF.

## Carotenoid-Derived Compounds in Mycorrhizal Roots

Several carotenoid-derived isoprenoids accumulate specifically within
mycorrhizal roots. Blumenin, mycorradicin, and nicoblumin have been
detected in barley and wheat roots colonized with G. intraradices.
Blumenin accumulation follows a temporal pattern, being low at 2 weeks,
reaching maximum levels at 3-4 weeks, and declining to trace amounts
after 5 weeks. Blumenin applied on barley split-root systems resulted
in systemic suppression of further mycorrhizal colonization,
demonstrating a regulatory role. Some carotenoid-derived compounds
isolated from maize exudates have been shown to inhibit Fusarium
oxysporum, suggesting these molecules may play a dual role in both
symbiosis regulation and pathogen defense.

## Effects on Phytophthora Zoospore Chemotaxis

The chemotactic response of Phytophthora nicotianae zoospores to
mycorrhizal root exudates varies with the developmental stage of the
mycorrhizal association. Exudates from mature mycorrhizal roots attracted
significantly fewer zoospores than water or exudates from
nonmycorrhizal roots. Conversely, exudates from actively growing young
mycorrhizal roots were more attractive than those from nonmycorrhizal
roots. This suggests that young mycorrhizal roots release attractive
molecules, while older mycorrhizal roots produce repulsive compounds
that may deter pathogen zoospore accumulation near the root surface.

## Effects on Pathogen Spore Germination

Mycorrhizal root exudates have shown diverse effects on pathogen spore
germination depending on the pathogen and AMF species combination.
Exudates from strawberry roots colonized by G. etunicatum or G.
monosporum inhibited sporangia production of Phytophthora fragariae.
Microconidia germination of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici was
more than doubled in the presence of root exudates from tomatoes
colonized with G. mosseae. Interestingly, root exudates from twelve
nonhost plant species produced similar stimulatory effects, indicating
that mycorrhization-induced changes in exudates were unrelated to the
pathogen sensitivity of the plant species.

## In Vitro vs. Soil Systems

A critical finding is that exudate-mediated effects observed in vitro
do not always translate to soil systems. While differential zoospore
attraction by mycorrhizal root exudates was clearly demonstrated in
capillary assays, application of mycorrhizal root exudates to soil
did not affect P. nicotianae root infection in tomato. This discrepancy
highlights the complexity of soil environments where multiple factors,
including microbial communities, organic matter interactions, and
physical soil properties, modulate the effects of exudate compounds.

## Mycorrhizal Exudates and Rhizosphere Bacteria

Mycorrhizal root exudates were tested for their ability to modify
rhizosphere bacterial community structure. Using PCR-DGGE analysis of
the 16S ribosomal gene, researchers found that direct AMF root
colonization significantly altered the bacterial community, but
application of mycorrhizal root exudates to nonmycorrhizal plants did
not. This suggests that modification of bacterial community structure in
the mycorrhizosphere is not mediated primarily by changes in root
exudation patterns, but rather by the physical presence of AMF
structures providing surfaces for bacterial colonization and biofilm
formation.

## Role of Glomalin

Glomalin is the only protein detected from AMF structures that is known
to favor soil aggregation. While glomalin has not been directly studied
for its effects on pathogens, its role in soil structure modification
may indirectly influence pathogen dynamics by altering water movement,
soil aeration, and the physical environment where pathogen propagules
survive and disperse. Glomalin's contribution to stable soil aggregates
may create microenvironments less favorable for certain pathogen
activities.

## Flavonoids as Regulatory Signals

The flavonoids acacetin and rhamnetin have been identified in
mycorrhizal root systems and are implicated in the autoregulation of
mycorrhizal colonization. These compounds may also play a role in
pathogen interactions, as flavonoids are well-known defense compounds
in plants. The regulation of mycorrhizal colonization by these
flavonoid-derived signals demonstrates that the molecules governing
symbiosis regulation may simultaneously influence interactions with
soil-borne pathogens within the mycorrhizosphere.
