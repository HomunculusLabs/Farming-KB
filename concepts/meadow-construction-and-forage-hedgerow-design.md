# Meadow Construction and Forage Hedgerow Design

## Overview

Constructing or rehabilitating mixed meadows is one of the most rewarding design enterprises in permaculture. It involves assembling diverse plant groups — grasses, legumes, bulbs, herbaceous forbs, and spike-rooted flatweeds — into self-maintaining perennial systems that produce healthy animals on range while building soil, supporting pollinators, and providing a varied product base. The process requires understanding each plant group's ecological niche, propagation method, and preferred site conditions, as well as the essential role of burrowers, pollinators, and predators in meadow maintenance.

## The Bimodal Growth Curve

Grasslands in cool humid climates exhibit a **bimodal growth curve** with two distinct periods of surplus (spring and early summer) and two periods of deficit:

- **Late summer deficit**: Arises from grass seed shedding, droughting, and drying-off of grasslands
- **Winter deficit**: Slow or no growth, with snow cover or blizzards restricting grazing time

### Bridging the Deficits with Forage Trees

**Green leaf from forage tree plantations** largely overcomes the summer shortage. Important considerations:

- **Evergreen forage trees** may not withstand hard frost if lopped in the last month of autumn — they need mature leaf to carry into frost periods
- Species like **tagasaste** (*Chamaecytisus proliferus*) should not be winter-lopped
- **Willow and poplar**, being deciduous, serve as summer leaf forage, and their wood chips can be fermented to high-value winter concentrates

Summer foliage from evergreen or deciduous forage can be pelleted, made into silage, or pressed as hay if dried after lopping. Tagasaste (unirrigated) produces the equivalent forage weight of irrigated lucerne.

## Contoured Hedgerow Design

For high-value landscapes (well-structured soils at gentle slope), fenced-off browse lots and hedgerow tree forage strips are obvious ways to extend and buffer the pasture system. Permanent electric fencing is cheap and effective for partitioning pastures and protecting new forage hedgerows.

### Planting Stratification

The contoured hedgerow system is designed with three vertical layers:

**Ground level (under-sowing or set planting):**
- Lucerne (alfalfa)
- Comfrey
- Chicory
- Selected dandelion

**Mid-level planting:**
- Tagasaste (*Chamaecytisus proliferus*)
- Caragana (*Caragana arborescens*)
- Coprosma repens
- Pampas grass (*Cortaderia selloana*)

**Tall overstory:**
- Willows (*Salix* spp.)
- Poplars (selected high-value forage cultivars)
- White oak (*Quercus alba*)
- Chestnut (*Castanea* spp.)
- Honey locust (*Gleditsia triacanthos*)
- Known desirable woody browse: hawthorn (*Crataegus* spp.) and rose (*Rosa* spp.)

### Progressive Establishment Timeline

- **Years 1-3**: Hedgerows occupy **10% per annum** of the total area
- **Year 4**: **40% of the total area** would be broad, complex, contoured hedgerow of deep-rooted woody plants and forbs, with evolving tall-tree browse and mast-crop species, some of long-term value as timber
- **Years 4-5**: Timed and observed access to browse permitted for sheep, young stock, and cautious harvesting by cattle
- **Years 6-8**: Longer browse times permitted; in emergencies, species such as willow and poplar can be cut and fed as drought rations

## Tagasaste as a Model Forage Tree

**Tagasaste** (*Chamaecytisus proliferus*) serves as an exemplar of productive forage tree integration:

### Field Drilling
- Given a seed source, tagasaste can be **field-drilled** with a normal seedbox used for other crops
- A mix of tree seed and **daikon radish** or **turnip** is often effective in grass suppression while trees grow

### Seed Collection
- Seed is most cheaply obtained from the soil under mature trees
- A system of **three shaker trays in one frame** separates seed from grass sod and soil — soil is simply shovelled onto the top tray

### Documented Success
Matthew Carpenter, on the cold Canterbury Plain of southern New Zealand, successfully field-drilled tagasaste for summer sheep forage. An unforeseen benefit: grasses between tagasaste strips dry out much later and produce more growth than grasses in open field situations.

### Multiple Yields
- Abundant seeds for chickens
- Honey for bees
- Cut forage: spacing at **1.0-1.5 m** is ideal (stems kept cut at 1 m high)
- Seed and bee production: spacing at **4-5 m** produces better forage

## Plant Groups for Meadow Assembly

### Grasses
Mainly perennial, bunch, or tussock species, with some annuals of great value as food (grains). Examples include ricegrasses, *Panicum* species, and rushes used for thatch and matting.

### Legumes
Grain or pasture legumes including clovers, vetches, lupins, and tares. Bulbous or deep-rooted legumes are used as foods (tubers), and some are twining species.

### Bulbs, Corms, and Tubers
Perennial flowering plants with bulbs, rhizomes, or tuberous root masses; many are dug as food in winter. Species include:
- **Sunroot** (*Helianthus tuberosus*, Jerusalem artichoke)
- **Breadroot** (*Psoralea esculenta*)
- **Crocus**, **camass**, **mariposa** (sego lily)
- Various **orchids**
- The entire **onion** family (*Allium* spp.)

### Herbaceous and Perennial Forbs
Nettles, daisies, and species of the families **Umbelliferae** (fennel, dill) and **Compositae** generally. Flowering plants of meadows including poppies, forget-me-nots, watercress, and buttercups. A few are annuals; many have a wide range of uses.

### Spike-Rooted Flatweeds
Dandelion, thistles, plantains, docks, and chicory — all high-value browse, and some used as salad plants and vegetables. These are characteristic features of mixed pastures, meadows, and grasslands.

## Critical Design Factors

### Mode of Occurrence
Whether species occur as lumps/clumps or are solitary in the system. Some species need to be set out as patches or clumps to persist, while others do well alone. This determines planting layout.

### Method of Propagation
- **Comfrey, narcissus, and sunroot** are naturally propagated vegetatively by being harvested, stored, and forgotten by burrowing animals
- **Flatweeds** such as thistle and plantain seed on burrow spoils, resist close grazing, and therefore occur near burrow mouths or on cropped areas with soil disturbance
- **Disturbance** suits annuals and windblown species

### Preferred Soils and Sites
- **Drainage** (from boggy to free-drained gravels or sands) is one critical factor for meadow species balance
- **Soil moisture reserve** is equally important
- **Acidity/alkalinity** is a second primary factor
- In initial preparation and placement of meadow sites, **variation in these factors can be built in** so that chosen species have a niche or site to occupy
- Most meadow plants prefer free drainage, but **sedges prefer poorly drained areas**
- **Chicory** will colonise compacted ground

## The Animal Associates of Meadows

A meadow is maintained not only by plant growth but equally by its pollinators, browsers, burrowers, and their predators. Many plants only persist if their animal associates are present:

- **Owls** — their regurgitated pellets sprout meadow seed caught in the fur of prey
- **Field mice** — plant seeds and bulbs; their abandoned stores sprout
- **Butterflies** — essential pollinators of meadow flora
- **Bees** — pollinate legumes and flowering forbs
- **Earthworms** — maintain soil structure and aeration

## Ground Preparation for Meadow Recreation

Since a meadow is a perennial or self-seeding system, ground preparation must involve:

1. **Initial earth-shaping** to create drainage and moisture variation
2. **Weed removal** effort to reduce competition during establishment
3. **Seed and plant collection** from catalogues, roadsides, and reference books
4. **Tolerance of burrowers and their predators** — eliminating the underground gardeners eliminates the propagation mechanism for many meadow species

## Revitalisation of Compacted Soils

Any compacted soil or worn-out pasture can be revitalised by **periodic sod-seeding** using a broad-flanged chisel point at **6-10 cm depth** and **0.5-0.6 m spacing**. This technique:
- Cuts the roots of existing grasses
- Provides a seed furrow for more vigorous, deep-rooted species
- Can be done without destroying the existing sward
- Allows gradual introduction of new species into degraded pasture

## The Philosophical Imperative

When we eliminate the bulbs, herbs, mushrooms, copses, hedges, and small animals of the agricultural landscape, we also lose part of the body of the earth and of ourselves. The contemporary agricultural scene, with its policy of "CLEAN, DEAD," is identified as nothing less than dull, anti-intellectual, and sterilising. Thoughtful farmers are increasingly including herbs in their leys — mints, chicory, dandelion, cleavers, daisies, plantain, and vetches — to the great health benefit of their herds.

Today, many meadow seed mixes are commercially available. If the job is done properly, however, the practitioner must also **tolerate the burrowers and their predators** that are integral to meadow ecology. The end result is a very useful and pleasing assembly of plants with a varied product base, producing healthy animals on range.
