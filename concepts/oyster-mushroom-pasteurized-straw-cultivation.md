# Oyster Mushroom Pasteurized Straw Cultivation

Pasteurized straw cultivation is the most accessible and widely practiced [[cultivation-of-oyster-mushrooms]] worldwide. It requires minimal equipment, uses inexpensive and abundant [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] substrates, straw does not require full sterilization. Pasteurization (heating to 160 degrees F / 71 degrees C) selectively kills temperature-sensitive competitor microorganisms while leaving a population of beneficial organisms that provide some biological protection for the [[biological-efficiency]] with P. sajor-caju.

Avoid hay with abundant seed kernels, as grain contamination is common. Straw should be clean, dry, free of mold, and stored properly before use.

## Straw Preparation

### Chopping

Straw must be chopped into 1-4 inch (2.5-10 cm) lengths to increase surface area and improve moisture penetration:

- **Mechanical shredders and chippers**: Fast and efficient for commercial operations
- **Garden chippers**: Suitable for small-scale operations
- **Hand choppers**: Used in India and Nepal for small farms (3-5 cm pieces)

Chopped straw has approximately double the density of loose straw and occupies about half its original volume after wetting.

### Moistening

Target 75% moisture content. Several methods exist:

1. **Short stacking**: Spread chopped straw on a large surface (cement slab or plastic tarp) to a depth no greater than 12 inches. Sprinkle with water over 2-4 days, turning daily to expose dry zones. This is intended for moisture penetration, not composting.

2. **Mechanical wetting**: Large vats fitted with high-pressure water jets and rotating mixing blades can achieve 75% moisture in minutes with sufficient agitation.

3. **Soaking**: Submerge chopped straw in water for 1-2 hours, then drain for 3-4 hours.

The **palm test** provides a simple field check: squeeze a fistful of straw tightly. If just a few drops of water are released, the moisture is correct.

## Pasteurization Methods

### Method 1: Hot Water Bath (Submerged Pasteurization)

The simplest and most common method for small to mid-scale growers:

1. Fill a stainless steel 55-gallon drum with water and heat to 160 degrees F (71 degrees C) using a propane burner
2. Stuff chopped straw into a wire basket
3. Submerge the basket in hot water, holding it down with a weight (brick or similar)
4. Insert a probe thermometer (12+ inches) deep into the straw mass
5. Maintain 160 degrees F for 1-2 hours
6. Remove basket, drain well, and spread straw on clean surfaces to cool rapidly
7. **Important**: After two soakings, discard the water. The discolored "straw tea" becomes toxic to mushroom mycelium after the third soaking. Interestingly, this tea is also toxic to most vegetation and can be used as a natural herbicide.

### Method 2: Phase II Steam Chamber (Commercial)

For operations processing one ton or more per batch:

1. Load moistened straw into insulated room with raised false floor and steam pipes
2. Fill room to no more than 1/8 capacity (7:1 air-to-substrate ratio)
3. Monitor temperature at three points: low (4-6 inches), midway, and high (12-24 inches from top)
4. Inject live steam; outer edges heat first while cold core acts as heat sink
5. When cold core begins heating, reduce steam to prevent thermal runaway
6. Maintain minimum 2 hours above 160 degrees F (71 degrees C); do not exceed 200 degrees F (93 degrees C)
7. Positive-pressure chamber with HEPA-filtered air during cooling
8. Cool to below 105 degrees F (38 degrees C); clean inoculation area with 10% bleach solution

One ton of chopped, soaked wheat straw occupies approximately 250 cubic feet (10 x 10 x 2.5 ft). Five dry tons fills a 1,000 square foot [[duggar-mushroom-growing-room-design]].

### Method 3: Lime Water Soak (Vietnam Method)

1. Prepare limewater: 2 kg slaked lime per 100 kg dry straw, with water just covering the straw
2. Soak straw for 30 minutes
3. Pile on cement floor, cover with plastic or sacking, leave top uncovered
4. Ferment for 7-10 days, turning every 3 days
5. Use as substrate
