### The Brande Account as Cultural Document
The Brande account is remarkable not only as the earliest documented psilocybin intoxication in Europe, but as evidence of how European culture framed mushroom intoxication:
- The symptoms are described entirely in terms of pathology (poisoning, not experience)
- The laughter is described as "immoderate" and uncontrollable
- No attempt is made to understand the subjective experience
- The report frames the incident as a "deleterious effect" of a "poisonous species"
This contrasts sharply with Mesoamerican accounts, where the same type of experience would be described in sacred terms.

## The Mycophobic Framing of Mushroom Effects
### Mental Illness Comparison
In Europe, the symptoms of mushroom intoxication have always been compared to symptoms of mental illness. None of the historical European accounts reflect a distinct appreciation of mushrooms in the tradition of the Mexican Indians ("teonanacatl" = flesh of the Gods). The European accounts focus exclusively on:
- "Semi-schizophrenic reactions"
- States of mental confusion
- "Madness-inducing" effects
- Loss of control and rationality
### The Evolution of Labels
The terminology used to describe psychoactive mushrooms evolved through distinctly negative stages:
1. **"Fool's mushroom"** (Narrenschwamm) -- Appeared during the 1930s
2. **"Mexican mushroom of insanity"** -- Also appeared during the 1930s
3. **"Mexican magic mushrooms"** -- 1950s, after discovery of Central American mushroom cults; more neutral but still distancing (it's "Mexican")
4. **"Hallucinogenic mushroom"** -- Later, relatively neutral
5. **"Intoxicating mushroom"** -- Somewhat derogatory
6. **"Drug mushroom"** -- Essentially meaningless and negative
### The Anti-Drug Hysteria
The frenzy of legislative attempts to control lsd-history|LSD resulted in ever tighter restrictions on the scientific study of not only LSD, but psilocybin as well. Mind-altering substances were no longer thought of in terms of their specific effects and properties, but rather were lumped together into a single group of dangerous chemicals. As anti-drug hysteria continued to intensify:
- Scientific and pharmacological distinctions became all but irrelevant
- Hallucinogens were no longer viewed as different from physically addictive drugs such as heroin
- Proponents of psilocybin research were accused of denying that the alkaloid caused "semi-permanent brain damage"
- This "pseudo-scientific jumble of meaningless jargon" was symptomatic of the escalating controversy
### The "Official Mycophobia"
Gartz identifies the result as an "official mycophobia" -- a misguided, yet entrenched policy that:
- Still prevailed at the time of his writing
- Effectively prevents the scientific investigation of psandoz-pharmaceuticalslications for psilocybin and other alkaloids
- Was established by the anti-drug legislative framework of the mid-1960s
- Continues despite massive research efforts that began when Sandoz Pharmaceuticals distributed psilocybin to qualified scientists
## The Sandoz Distribution Program
A. Hofmann developed the method for synthesis of psilocybin at Sandoz Pharmaceuticals. The company manufactured about 2 kg (ca. 4.4 lbs) of pure psilocybin for scientific research purposes and distributed it to qualified scientists for experimental and psychotherapeutic purposes.
The results of pharmacological testing soon revealed psilocybin as an alkaloid that was "perfectly safe for human subjects under controlled experimental conditions" -- a finding that was completely ignored by the legislative process.
## Childhood Origins of Mycophobia
Gartz offers a personal reflection on the origins of mushroom attitudes:
At about five years old, he was playing in a grassy meadow when a girl pointed to a brown mushroom and earnestly explained that it was inedible and poisonous. While he never forgot this encounter, he grew up to become a devoted mushroom enthusiast. Another childhood memory -- discovering a landfill virtually covered with vast numbers of gilled bluing mushrooms and the sense of awe -- left a different impression.
These early experiences illustrate how unusual mushroom characteristics are most likely responsible for strong impressions formed early in life, which may develop into various attitudes or beliefs later on.
