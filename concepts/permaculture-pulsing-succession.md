# Permaculture Pulsing Model of Ecological Succession

The pulsing model of ecological succession is a framework for understanding how ecosystems cycle through phases of accumulation and release rather than progressing linearly toward a stable climax state. Developed within ecological theory since the 1960s, this model is extensively discussed by david holmgren in [[permaculture-design-principles]] as a fundamental pattern for designing productive and resilient systems in the context of [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]].

## Beyond the Climax Model

Classical ecological theory held that ecosystems progressed through predictable stages of succession toward a stable climax community that would persist indefinitely in the absence of disturbance. This linear model of succession implied that mature ecosystems were the most stable and productive. However, ecological theory since the 1960s has begun to recognise that many ecosystems are evolved to go through periodic disturbance as part of an overall dynamic stability.

The disturbance may be external to the ecosystem, as in the case of bushfire or flood, or internal, as in the case of defoliating insect plagues. When viewed from the organism level, the disturbance may appear as a catastrophe, but for the ecosystem as a whole it is beneficial. The pulse can contribute to efficient use of limited resources and maximise total energy capture.

## The Pattern of Pulse

These pulsing ecosystems typically develop a pattern of long, slow accumulation of biomass during the production phase, followed by a short intense pulse of consumption where total biomass falls rapidly and nutrients are recycled. This is fundamentally different from the old climax concept which showed net growth followed by a flat line of stability.

The pulsing model explains many phenomena that the climax model could not. Why do some forests need fire to regenerate? Why do grazing ecosystems need periodic drought or heavy grazing to maintain their productivity? Why do some wetlands need periodic drying to cycle nutrients effectively? The answer in each case is that the pulse is not a disruption of the system but an integral part of its functioning.

## Applications in Permaculture Design

The pulsing model has direct applications in permaculture design. Rather than designing systems for static stability, permaculture designers can design for beneficial pulsing. A [[permaculture-forest-garden-establishment]] may include elements that are periodically coppiced or harvested, creating pulses of production within a longer cycle of accumulation. A [[permaculture-grazing-and-pasture]] may use high-intensity short-duration grazing followed by long recovery periods, mimicking the natural pulse of herbivore activity.

The concept of the development pulse applies to human systems as well. Holmgren describes a "bike journey" pattern of development where heavy going up the hills is followed by a breezy ride down into the next valley. Some hills prove too steep, and we turn around to roll back down the hill into the last valley. This roller coaster or pulsing pattern of growth can be observed throughout natural and human systems wherever there is the surplus energy to get to the next valley.

## Catastrophism and Landscape Evolution

The pulsing model was already widely accepted in geomorphology before ecologists began questioning the climax model. Called Catastrophism, this theory suggests that many, perhaps most, landforms are primarily the result of very powerful forces acting for relatively short periods of time. The obvious example is the relatively fast formation of mountains by volcanic activity, but more generally mountain-building happens at the edges of the earth's tectonic plates over relatively short periods of geological time.

This convergence between geomorphology and ecology around the pulsing model reinforces its validity as a universal pattern. Whether we look at landscapes, ecosystems, or human societies, the pattern of slow accumulation punctuated by rapid release appears again and again.

## Pulsing in the Context of Energy Descent

In the context of energy descent, the pulsing model takes on special significance. Industrial society has been in an extended accumulation phase, building up enormous stocks of infrastructure, knowledge, and population. The coming energy descent may represent the release phase of this pulse, but it need not be catastrophic. Permaculture design aims to moderate the pulse, spreading the release over a longer period and maintaining essential functions throughout the transition.

The pulsing model also suggests that energy descent itself may be part of a larger cycle. If human societies follow the same pulsing pattern as ecosystems, then the contraction phase will eventually give way to a new accumulation phase, building on the foundations laid during the descent. This long-term perspective can help maintain hope and purpose during the difficult transition period.

## Designing for Pulse

Practical design implications of the pulsing model include designing systems that can absorb periodic disturbance without catastrophic failure, planning for periodic harvest and renewal cycles rather than continuous extraction, maintaining redundancy and diversity so that the system can recover from the release phase, creating buffers and storages that smooth the pulse and maintain function during the transition between phases, and recognising that the release phase, while potentially destructive, is also a time of opportunity and renewal.

## Connection to Other Permaculture Principles

The pulsing model connects directly to [[permaculture-design-principles]] but also informs several other principles. [[catch-and-store-energy-permaculture-principle]] is about the accumulation phase of the pulse. [[permaculture-design-principles]] ensures that the accumulation phase produces useful outputs. [[produce-no-waste-permaculture-principle]] addresses what happens during the release phase. [[permaculture-design-principles]] suggests ways to moderate the pulse.

## Ecosynthesis and Novel Pulsing Systems

The pulsing model is especially relevant to understanding novel ecosystems that arise from human disturbance. In urban and peri-urban landscapes, weedscapes go through their own pulsing cycles as pioneer species colonise disturbed ground, build biomass and soil, and eventually create conditions for more complex communities. The ecological holocaust of the Central Victorian goldrush was the release phase which allowed the piecemeal settlement processes since to lay down the energetic regimes and biological resources for ecosynthesis of new forest systems.

Research on the riparian weedscapes of Hepburn Springs showed how blackberry and gorse-dominated stream corridors were successional stages that would eventually be replaced by deciduous forest of willow, hawthorn, and native blackwood. Understanding the pulsing nature of this succession allowed managers to work with rather than against the natural dynamics, accelerating desirable transitions while accepting the inevitability of periodic disturbance and renewal.

## The Pulse as a Design Metaphor

Beyond its ecological applications, the pulsing model serves as a powerful design metaphor for human projects and organisations. Every successful project goes through phases of intensive creative work followed by periods of consolidation and reflection. Organisations that try to maintain continuous growth without periodic renewal eventually burn out or collapse. The most resilient organisations build renewal cycles into their structure, creating regular opportunities for reassessment, redesign, and reinvestment.

In permaculture education, the pulsing model helps explain why intensive design courses need to be followed by periods of personal implementation and reflection. The course itself is the accumulation phase; the following months and years of practice are where the real learning happens as the pulse of new information is absorbed and integrated into the practitioner's life and work.

## Plants Come Before Animals

A practical implication of understanding successional hierarchy is that plants always come before animals in ecological succession. This is because plants provide the resources to support animals. The slow-moving processes of plants create the food and habitat that animals and humans depend on, even though animals appear more active and dynamic.

This reminds us that we should establish pastures and fodder crops before getting animals. A small rural property with too many livestock, fed from purchased bags and bales, accumulating manure that cannot be absorbed by overgrazed pasture, is an example of the contradiction of succession order. The commercial feedlot illustrates this contradiction on a grand scale: it is financially viable only because of cheap energy, externalised environmental costs, and agricultural subsidies.

## Practical Pulsing in Garden and Farm Design

Understanding the pulsing model allows designers to plan for and manage beneficial disturbance. In a home garden, the annual vegetable bed goes through a clear pulse each season: preparation and planting in spring, rapid growth in summer, harvest in autumn, and rest or cover crop in winter. A [[permaculture-forest-garden-establishment]] pulses on a much longer timescale, with periodic pruning or coppicing creating openings that stimulate new growth.

In farm design, rotational grazing systems explicitly manage the pulse: intensive grazing followed by long recovery. Keyline [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]] and soil building create infrastructure that moderates the pulse of drought and flood, storing water and nutrients during wet periods for use during dry ones. The keyline-design|keyline system is itself an example of designing infrastructure that captures the pulse of rainfall and distributes it more evenly across the landscape.

The skill for designers is to recognise the appropriate timescale and intensity of pulsing for each element and system. Too frequent or intense pulsing prevents accumulation and depletes resources. Too infrequent or mild pulsing allows stagnation and loss of productivity. Finding the right pulse is a matter of careful observation, experimentation, and adaptive management.
