## Ecosynthesis and Novel Pulsing Systems

The pulsing model is especially relevant to understanding novel ecosystems that arise from human disturbance. In urban and peri-urban landscapes, weedscapes go through their own pulsing cycles as pioneer species colonise disturbed ground, build biomass and soil, and eventually create conditions for more complex communities. The ecological holocaust of the Central Victorian goldrush was the release phase which allowed the piecemeal settlement processes since to lay down the energetic regimes and biological resources for ecosynthesis of new forest systems.

Research on the riparian weedscapes of Hepburn Springs showed how blackberry and gorse-dominated stream corridors were successional stages that would eventually be replaced by deciduous forest of willow, hawthorn, and native blackwood. Understanding the pulsing nature of this succession allowed managers to work with rather than against the natural dynamics, accelerating desirable transitions while accepting the inevitability of periodic disturbance and renewal.

## The Pulse as a Design Metaphor

Beyond its ecological applications, the pulsing model serves as a powerful design metaphor for human projects and organisations. Every successful project goes through phases of intensive creative work followed by periods of consolidation and reflection. Organisations that try to maintain continuous growth without periodic renewal eventually burn out or collapse. The most resilient organisations build renewal cycles into their structure, creating regular opportunities for reassessment, redesign, and reinvestment.

In permaculture education, the pulsing model helps explain why intensive design courses need to be followed by periods of personal implementation and reflection. The course itself is the accumulation phase; the following months and years of practice are where the real learning happens as the pulse of new information is absorbed and integrated into the practitioner's life and work.

## Plants Come Before Animals

A practical implication of understanding successional hierarchy is that plants always come before animals in ecological succession. This is because plants provide the resources to support animals. The slow-moving processes of plants create the food and habitat that animals and humans depend on, even though animals appear more active and dynamic.

This reminds us that we should establish pastures and fodder crops before getting animals. A small rural property with too many livestock, fed from purchased bags and bales, accumulating manure that cannot be absorbed by overgrazed pasture, is an example of the contradiction of succession order. The commercial feedlot illustrates this contradiction on a grand scale: it is financially viable only because of cheap energy, externalised environmental costs, and agricultural subsidies.

## Practical Pulsing in Garden and Farm Design

Understanding the pulsing model allows designers to plan for and manage beneficial disturbance. In a home garden, the annual vegetable bed goes through a clear pulse each season: preparation and planting in spring, rapid growth in summer, harvest in autumn, and rest or cover crop in winter. A [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]] and soil building create infrastructure that moderates the pulse of drought and flood, storing water and nutrients during wet periods for use during dry ones. The keyline-design|keyline system is itself an example of designing infrastructure that captures the pulse of rainfall and distributes it more evenly across the landscape.

The skill for designers is to recognise the appropriate timescale and intensity of pulsing for each element and system. Too frequent or intense pulsing prevents accumulation and depletes resources. Too infrequent or mild pulsing allows stagnation and loss of productivity. Finding the right pulse is a matter of careful observation, experimentation, and adaptive management.
