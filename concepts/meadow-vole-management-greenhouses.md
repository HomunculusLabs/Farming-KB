# Meadow Vole Management in Winter Greenhouses

While insect and disease pests have not been a significant concern in winter greenhouse production, one pest has emerged as a serious problem for four-season growers: the [[meadow-vole]] (*Microtus pennsylvanicus*). Unlike typical insect pests that can be managed with biological or organic sprays, voles present a unique set of challenges that require dedicated trapping strategies and persistent year-round management.

## The Vole Problem

Coleman notes that the major pest that has developed in the [[winter-harvest-system]] is not a bug or a microbe but a mammal. The farm is engaged in what he describes as a "battle of wits" with the meadow vole — a contest that requires constant vigilance, creative problem-solving, and an acceptance that total elimination is unlikely.

Meadow voles are small rodents that look like chubby, extra-furry mice. Despite their unassuming appearance, all of their biological qualities make them extraordinarily difficult to control on the small farm:

- **Outdoor habitat**: They live out of doors, making them impossible to exclude from the farm environment entirely
- **Broad diet**: They are vegetarians and seem to like greenhouse crops as much as customers do, feeding on a wide range of vegetable species
- **High metabolic rate**: They need to eat their body weight in plant food every day, meaning each vole consumes a significant amount of crop biomass daily
- **Year-round activity**: They do not hibernate and remain active throughout winter, continuing to feed and breed whenever conditions permit
- **Rapid reproduction**: They can breed at 35 to 40 days old, breed year-round, and produce up to ten litters per year with three to six young each time
- **Tunneling behavior**: They tunnel under the soil and can easily enter greenhouses from below
- **Nest-building with row cover**: They live in underground burrows and like to line their nests with chewed-up shreds of row-cover fabric, destroying valuable frost protection material in the process

Not surprisingly, voles consider greenhouses an idyllic place to spend the winter. The combination of abundant food, moist soil for tunneling, and protection from predators creates ideal vole habitat. Once established inside a greenhouse, a small number of voles can quickly multiply into a population that causes significant crop damage, eating seedlings, gnawing at plant roots from below, and destroying the carefully maintained growing environment.

They are also serious pests for fruit-tree growers, as they girdle the bark of young trees — making them a dual threat on diversified farms that combine vegetable and fruit production. A single vole can girdle multiple young tree seedlings in a single night, destroying years of nursery work.

## Prevention: Imperfect but Necessary

The first response to any pest problem should be prevention rather than cure, and this is certainly the approach Coleman takes with voles. However, the mobile-greenhouse concept that is central to the winter harvest system makes prevention more difficult than it would be in a fixed structure.

Prevention strategies that have been tried include:

- **Deeply buried greenhouse edges**: Mobile-greenhouse designs with more deeply buried edges to prevent voles from burrowing under the structure
- **Blocking ingress points**: Diligently blocking any and all small holes that might allow vole entry into the greenhouse
- **Regular inspection**: Systematic checking of greenhouse perimeters for new holes or tunnel openings, especially after freeze-thaw events

Despite these efforts, prevention alone has proven insufficient. The forces created by winter freezing and thawing cycles can open new holes in the greenhouse perimeter even after all precautions have been taken. The ground heaves and settles with each freeze-thaw cycle, creating gaps that were not there before.

Voles are often already surreptitiously established in an area before the greenhouse is even moved to its winter site, having moved in during the fall when the structure was located elsewhere.

In short, while prevention should always be the starting point, the reality of mobile greenhouse systems means that voles will almost always find a way in. Growers must be prepared to manage an established population rather than relying solely on exclusion. The mobile nature of the houses means that each time they are moved, new vole access points may be created, requiring fresh inspection and blocking efforts at every site.

## Trapping: The Primary Control Method

With prevention imperfect, trapping becomes the primary method of vole control. Coleman relies on snap traps and uses a large number of them. His records show that in one year he trapped over fifty voles in the vicinity of the greenhouses during August and September alone, and a neighbor's cat probably caught almost that many. Even this level of mortality did not seem to make a noticeable dent in the population by winter.

The only effective strategy that has been found is to keep trapping year-round. Seasonal trapping, no matter how intensive, cannot overcome the voles' reproductive capacity. Continuous pressure is necessary to keep populations at manageable levels. This means maintaining traps even during the summer months when voles are less noticeable, preventing the population buildup that would otherwise occur before winter.

### Bait Selection and Bait Avoidance

The usual rodent baits — cheese being the classic example — do not work well for voles. As vegetarians that eat fruit and vegetables, voles are not attracted to the dairy-based or meat-based baits used for rats and mice. The standard snap traps sold for household rodent control are designed with cheese or peanut butter in mind, neither of which holds much appeal for a vole.

Besides the bait mismatch, there is simply too much delicious fresh food available in the greenhouse for standard baits to compete. When a vole can choose between a piece of aged cheese and a tender baby lettuce seedling, the choice is not difficult.

Recommended baits include:

- **Slices of raw fruit**: Apple and similar fruits are attractive to voles and readily available
- **Macadamia-nut butter**: Found to work better than standard peanut butter, likely due to its higher fat content and different flavor profile
- **Carob powder added to nut butter**: This addition was momentarily successful, adding sweetness that attracted voles

Perhaps the most creative bait discovered was **wild-strawberry flavored bubble gum**, which appeared effective at tempting voles in midwinter when fresh fruit was scarce in their diet. Whatever devious ingenuity went into concocting that flavor to tempt small children apparently also worked on voles. This discovery illustrates the kind of creative experimentation that persistent pest management sometimes requires — conventional wisdom may not have all the answers.

### The Bait Avoidance Problem

The most significant discovery about vole trapping is that voles learn to avoid baited traps. Coleman describes the dynamic with humor, imagining the third vole that approaches a baited trap thinking, "Uh-oh, the last time I smelled that, Uncle Harry bought the farm," and shying away.

This bait avoidance behavior means that baits must be changed regularly. No single bait remains effective indefinitely. The voles become conditioned to associate particular scents with danger, and the grower must stay one step ahead by rotating through different baits and introducing novel attractants. Keeping records of which baits work and when they stop working helps build a bait rotation schedule over time.

### Strategic Trap Placement

Based on the key principle described above — that placement matters more than bait — the next strategic development was to move beyond baited trapping altogether. By focusing trap placement on the voles' natural lines of travel, the grower can intercept voles without relying on any attractant at all. This approach eliminates the bait-avoidance problem entirely.

Effective placement locations include:

- **Entrances to burrows**: Voles must enter and exit their underground tunnels, making these natural chokepoints
- **Surface runs**: Voles create visible surface trails when moving between burrows; traps placed across these runs intercept traveling voles
- **Along walls and edges**: Voles prefer to scurry along edges and vertical surfaces; placing traps against greenhouse walls and bed edges exploits this behavior

When successful, these strategically placed traps seemed to catch voles because of accurate placement rather than bait attractiveness. This insight led to the development of unbaited trapping strategies that rely entirely on location. The lesson is clear: understanding vole behavior and movement patterns is more valuable than finding the perfect bait.

### The Homemade Vole Trap

Based on the understanding that placement matters more than bait, Coleman developed a homemade vole trap design that is described as "surefire." While the exact design details are best understood from the original source, the key principle is that the trap is placed directly in the vole's path of travel, where the animal will encounter it during its normal movements regardless of any attraction to bait.

## The Bigger Picture: Integrated Vole Management

Successful vole management requires an integrated approach that combines prevention, trapping, and awareness of vole biology and behavior. No single method is sufficient; the grower must combine multiple strategies and adapt continuously as the voles respond to control efforts.

Key principles include:

1. **Year-round trapping**: Seasonal efforts cannot overcome the vole's reproductive capacity; continuous pressure is essential
2. **Trap rotation and innovation**: When one trapping approach becomes less effective, try something new
3. **Monitoring and record-keeping**: Track trapping numbers by location and season to identify population patterns and evaluate which strategies are working
4. **Habitat management**: Reduce favorable vole habitat around greenhouse perimeters by keeping grass mowed short and removing debris piles, brush, and dense vegetation that could provide cover near the structures
5. **Acceptance of persistence**: Voles will never be fully eliminated; the goal is management, not eradication
6. **Natural predators**: Encourage predators where possible — cats, owls, hawks, and snakes all prey on voles and can contribute to population control. Coleman's neighbor's cat was credited with catching nearly as many voles as the trapping program

## Understanding Vole Biology for Better Control

Effective management starts with understanding the animal's biology. Voles are active day and night, year-round. They do not hibernate and continue to breed even in winter if food is available — which it abundantly is inside a heated or even unheated winter greenhouse.

Their tunnel systems can be extensive, with multiple entrances and connecting passages. A single vole colony may use a network of tunnels covering a significant area. Surface runs connecting burrows are visible as narrow trails in the grass or soil and serve as indicators of vole activity that can guide trap placement.

Learning to read these signs — fresh soil at tunnel entrances, worn pathways through vegetation, chew marks on plant stems — is an essential skill for the vole manager. The more accurately the grower can identify active vole areas, the more effectively traps can be deployed.

Population fluctuations are natural and influenced by food availability, weather, and predation pressure. Vole populations tend to cycle, with periodic irruptions that can overwhelm even well-managed trapping programs. Understanding these cycles helps the grower anticipate heavy pressure years and prepare accordingly.

Coleman acknowledges a strong personal aversion to voles in the winter greenhouse — a sentiment that will resonate with any grower who has seen carefully tended crops destroyed overnight by these persistent rodents. While his efforts are "not yet quite as manic as those of Bill Murray in Caddyshack," he notices a fleeting resemblance on occasion. This passion, while perhaps amusing in the retelling, reflects the real frustration and economic impact that vole damage can cause in a small farming operation.

The vole challenge is a reminder that even the most carefully designed growing systems will encounter pest problems that require ongoing attention and adaptive management. The key is to respond with persistence, creativity, and a willingness to continuously refine the approach based on what works in practice. The grower who treats vole management as a continuous process rather than a one-time solution will have the best chance of keeping damage to acceptable levels.

For growers considering winter greenhouse production, the vole factor should be factored into planning from the start. Building a trapping program into the regular farm routine — rather than treating it as an emergency response — ensures that vole populations never have the opportunity to build to damaging levels.
