# Two-Step Selection Model in Plant Root Microbiomes

## Overview

The **two-step selection model** describes how plant root microbiomes are assembled from the vast pool of soil microorganisms through two sequential filtering processes. This model was notably supported by Winston et al. (2014) in a study of the [[cannabis-sativa]] microbiome (PLOS ONE), which demonstrated that root-associated [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] are shaped first by the soil environment and then by host genotype.

Understanding this model is critical for plant-microbe interaction research, as it explains why endophytic communities differ so dramatically from bulk soil despite being derived from it, and why different plant cultivars harbor distinct microbial assemblages.

## The Two Steps

### Step 1: Rhizosphere Filtering by Rhizodeposits

Bulk soil contains the most diverse microbial communities. As plant roots exude carbon-rich compounds (sugars, amino acids, organic acids, mucilage) — collectively called **rhizodeposits** — they create a concentrated nutrient zone around the root surface known as the **rhizosphere**. This nutrient enrichment acts as the first selective filter:

- Microbes capable of utilizing rhizodeposits proliferate in the rhizosphere
- Communities shift measurably along principal coordinate axes from bulk soil toward endorhiza
- Alpha diversity drops from bulk soil (Chao1 mean = 2010.7) to rhizosphere (Chao1 mean = 1837.2), reflecting this niche filtering

This step is primarily driven by **soil type**, which determines the starting pool of available microorganisms. In unweighted (presence/absence) PCoA analysis, soil type explained **32.06% of variation along PC1**, confirming that the soil environment is the dominant factor determining *which* microbial taxa are present.

### Step 2: Host Genotype-Dependent Endophyte Selection

From the rhizosphere, a subset of microbes colonizes the root interior (the **endorhiza**). This second step involves convergent selection driven by the plant's genotype:

- The plant's immune system, root architecture, and exudate chemistry selectively permit or exclude specific taxa
- Despite originating from the same soil, different cultivars develop distinct endophytic communities
- Alpha diversity drops further to the endorhiza (Chao1 mean = 916.1), roughly half that of bulk soil

In weighted (abundance-aware) PCoA analysis, **Cannabis strain explained 34.51% of variation along PC1**, demonstrating that host genotype primarily controls *how abundant* different community members are, even when the same taxa are present across cultivars.

## Evidence from Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA)

### Unweighted (Presence/Absence) Analysis

- **PC1 (32.06%)**: Driven by soil type — the primary determinant of [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]]
- **PC2 (11.34%)**: Captures the gradient from bulk soil (negative PC2) through rhizosphere (intermediate) to endorhiza (positive PC2)
- Only rhizosphere and endorhiza formed significantly differentiated clusters; bulk soil communities overlapped, suggesting that niche filtering primarily differentiates root-adjacent compartments

### Weighted (Abundance-Incorporated) Analysis

- **PC1 (34.51%)**: Driven by Cannabis strain/cultivar — host genotype controls [[acidifying-pollutants-mycorrhizal-community-structure]]
- **All three sample types** (bulk soil, rhizosphere, endorhiza) formed significantly differentiated clusters
- The contrast between weighted and unweighted results is key: composition (who is present) is set by soil, but abundance (who dominates) is controlled by the plant

## OTU Segregation and Shared Microbe Analysis

- **Unweighted analysis**: No significantly segregating OTUs between cultivars in endorhiza or rhizosphere, indicating that cultivar-specificity operates through relative abundance rather than presence/absence
- **Weighted analysis**: 71 OTUs significantly segregated between cultivars (vs. 657 between soil types), confirming a subtler but real genotype effect
- **Shared OTU analysis** validated that endophytic microbes are **inherited from the surrounding soil** — the endorhiza community is a subset of the rhizosphere, which is a subset of bulk soil

## Cellvibrio as an Indicator of Root Decay

An important secondary finding involved the genus *Cellvibrio* as a marker of root decomposition:

| Condition | *Cellvibrio* Abundance | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Experiment 1 (post-harvest, 8 weeks) | 16.9% | Active root decay |
| Experiment 2 (actively growing) | 0.095% | Healthy root state |

Notably, **cultivar-specific community signatures remained detectable** in Experiment 1's endorhiza samples despite root decay, suggesting that genotype-dependent selection imprints a persistent signal on the root microbiome.

## Key Takeaways

- **Soil determines composition** (unweighted); **plant genotype determines structure** (weighted)
- The two-step model is a progressive filtering process: soil → rhizosphere → endorhiza
- Each step reduces alpha diversity but increases host influence
- Endophytes are recruited from soil rather than being vertically transmitted (in this system)
- Cultivar effects are subtle at the presence/absence level but significant at the abundance level

## Plant Growth Stage and Plant-Soil Feedbacks

The growth stage of the plant profoundly influences its microbiome:

- Actively growing roots produce different exudate profiles than decaying roots
- Plant-soil feedback loops can alter microbial communities over successive growing seasons
- Temporal dynamics (succession) in the root microbiome are not yet well characterized

## Future Research Directions

- **Elucidating cultivar effects on the rhizosphere**: The intermediate zone between soil and root interior remains understudied
- **Cannabinoid effects on [[cannabis-rhizosphere-endorhiza-communities]]**: Cannabis produces unique [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] that may directly shape its endophytic microbiome
- **Time-series sampling**: Longitudinal studies tracking microbiome development across the full plant life cycle
- **Applied microbiome engineering**: Leveraging cultivar-specific microbial associations for plant fitness, disease suppression, and [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]]
- **Cross-species comparison**: Testing the universality of the two-step model across diverse plant families
