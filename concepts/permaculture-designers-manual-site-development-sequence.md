# [[permaculture-design]]ers Manual - Site Development Sequence

updated: 2026-04-18

The sequence of site development is addressed throughout the manual, with explicit procedural stages in Section 12.5 (house and garden development), Section 14.8 (developmental property trusts), and Section 14.9 ([[mollison-village-development-design]]). Mollison consistently argues for a logical order of implementation that respects natural priorities and minimises wasted effort.

## The Fundamental Priority Sequence

### Phase 1: Water

Water is always first. Before any construction or planting, the water system must be designed and at least partially installed:
- Observe existing water flows, springs, and seasonal patterns
- Design and install keyline or swale systems for water distribution
- Establish storage: tanks, dams, ponds
- Plan reticulation: pipes, pumps, gravity-fed distribution
- Design cleanup and recycling: greywater, blackwater, [[nutrient-cycling-in-soil]]

"Any village should be able to catch, store, reticulate, and clean up its own water supply" (Section 14.9).

### Phase 2: Access

Roads, paths, and accessways come second:
- Primary vehicle access from public road
- Internal tracks for management (fire control, stock movement, harvesting)
- Footpaths for daily use
- Position access to work with gravity, not against it
- Roads follow contours where possible to reduce erosion

### Phase 3: Structures

Only after water and access are established:
- Housing and dwellings
- Sheds, workshops, animal housing
- Fencing for stock and crop protection
- Glasshouses and shadehouses

### Phase 4: Planting

Perennial plantings begin after structures and water are in place:
- Windbreaks and shelterbelts first (protect everything that follows)
- Forest and timber trees
- Orchard and tree crops
- Garden beds and vegetable systems
- Pasture and forage systems

### Phase 5: Social, Legal, and Financial Systems

Last in the practical sequence but essential for long-term success:
- Trust formation (Trust A for land, Trust B for trading)
- Community governance structures
- Cooperative enterprises
- Marketing and distribution systems
- Ongoing monitoring and adaptation

## Village Development Procedure (Section 14.9)

Mollison outlines nine procedural stages for village-scale development:

1. Formation of a group or location of a site
2. Arrange site option or purchase terms (options as little as $50/year if price offered is 20-30% of developed value)
3. Obtain "agreement in principle" from local planning authority, establishing road type, number of allotments, required services, and stages of development
4. Do careful sums: price based on roading, water supply, and sewerage
5. Prepare detailed site plan and proposal
6. Advertise to prospective customers and obtain firm commitments
7. Obtain sealed permission for subdivision from local authority
8. Sell to buyers, using a trust fund for road, water, and site preparation
9. When costs are cleared, decide on future projects from profits and skills gained

### Title Allocation Model

For a 100-title village:
- 60% sold at best prices (30% covers all costs)
- 30% gifted to village group for later use
- 10% allotted as labour equity to surveyors, earthmovers, landscapers

Village Trust then allocates gifted titles:
- 20% sold to fund village projects (can be discounted 50% for low-income families)
- 10% reserved for crucial new recruits (medical, computer, energy specialists)

## Finance and Staging

### Development Funding Options

- Developer has all funds for survey and plans
- Developer raises funds from investors
- Potential buyers form a trust account to purchase and develop

### Staged Development

Trusts stage development through a sequence of projects as funds become available:
- Management costs limited to 4-8% of funds
- Remainder devoted to purchase and development
- Costs reduce as trust income grows from mature investments

### Cost Recovery Target

"30% of titles available should cover (in value) all land and development costs, so that surplus titles are available for community access, profits, gifts, labour equity, and new project development."

## The Development Group

### Core Team

"Works best as a small core of 2-4 people, each with special skills such as real estate, design, planning, or law." Surveyors, road-builders, builders, and landscapers are usually locally available. Some contractors will work for equity in the project.

### Handover

The development group should hand over "a site design and user's manual to the directors of Trust A, who can display and circulate the initial design, record changes and modifications, and keep clear the essential land areas for productive use."

### Design as Living Document

"All initial designs are made to be changed. The challenge is to change the design for the better! A design gives a starting point, not an end point."

## Site Types for Development

Mollison identifies seven types of village sites:
- Type 1: City block or suburbs
- Type 2: Adjoining an existing village (probably the easiest to plan)
- Type 3: Within a part-vacant village
- Type 4: Isolated from existing settlement (pioneering approach, needs most intensive planning)
- Type 5: On the site of a pre-existing but now vacant village
- Type 6: New suburban development
- Type 7: Specialised settlements on coasts or near wilderness

## Infrastructure Reservations

The manual lists areas to reserve for future development:
- School, seminar, workshop rooms, computers, library
- Food processing centre, cafe, commercial products
- Workshop (woodwork, metalwork, machine shop, vehicle service)
- Retail shops and nursery
