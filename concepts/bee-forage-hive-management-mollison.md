# Bee Forage and Hive Management (Mollison)

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Bill Mollison, Section 12.11 (pp. 428-430)

## Bee Products

Bees produce several valuable and unique products: honey, wax, pollen, propolis, and royal jelly. See [[permaculture-designers-manual-crop-pollination]] for a wide variety of food, oil, fruit, and seed crops (such as mustard, clovers, buckwheat, most smallfruit, apples, and grain legumes). For basic beekeeping information, see [[bee-forage-plants]] for additional species selection.

## Pollen and Early Forage

As well as nectar producers, bees need pollen to rear replacement workers, especially early in the season. Thus, willows (especially pussywillow), Acacias, pines, and vine crop yielding early pollen are very beneficial on range.

Bees also gather propolis, a hard waxy substance, used to plug wind gaps or repaint hives, and some sources of this are Xanthorrheas, poplars, and pines; very few sources of propolis are needed in the whole range.

## Water for Hives

Clustered hives use very large quantities of water, which is used by the bees to cool the hives. The bees ventilate hives as a "super organism", inhaling and exhaling in about a 1.2 minute rhythm. As hive temperatures are normally high (at about 21 degrees C), so hive insulation in cold weather is important.

In Denmark bees are over-wintered in thatched attic areas, or stored in insulated hive houses in other parts of Europe. Conversely, in areas of hot summers, hives need shade and good ventilation, or honey is lost as energy used in cooling (fanning) by the hive.

Access to water is most safely achieved by providing soaked mats or hessian at pond edges, so that bees cannot drown while drinking, and small ponds or troughs free of dragonflies give less losses than large ponds (where dragonflies are efficient predators).

## Forage Species Selection

Having designed the physical outline of a bee range (which in truth resemble any well-sheltered farm), then local lists of honey plants can be consulted for pathsides, road verges, hedgerow, and forest.

We could handily divide forages into early, mid, and late season for a good spread of yield, and again into classical, bulk, and special honeys and pollens for mixing and specific market.

### Seasonal Forage Categories

**Early Season (Classical):** Rosemary, thyme, catnip, sage.

**Mid Season (Bulk):** Echium fastuosum, white clover, Acacia, blackberry, catmint.

**Late Season (Bulk & Special):** Echium fastuosum, Eucalypt, leatherwood (Eucryphia), mustard, smallfruits.

### Types of Honey

Some classical (highly preferred) honeys are: "heather" honeys (sage, thyme, catmint, rosemary); pine tree or basswood (Tilia spp.) honeys in Europe and the USA; marsh tupelo honeys (subtropics), leatherwood (Eucryphia) honeys of Chile and Tasmania, and citrus honeys.

Bulk honeys suitable for blending derive from clovers, eucalypts, Acacias, field crops such as mustard and buckwheat, fruit crop usually from the family Rosaceae (blackberry, raspberry, apples), and "mixed garden honey".

Special pollens are those collected from plants such as some Acacia and goldenrod, the pollens of which may cause asthma if inhaled by susceptible people. Eaten, these honeys or pollens act to prevent allergic reaction.

## Bee-Pollinated Crops

Given that we have designed a range for bees, then the same area presents a unique opportunity to grow crops, especially seed crops, dependent on bee pollination. Such crops are: kiwifruit, any bramble berry, smallfruit, mustards and Brassicas, clovers, apples or pome fruits, buckwheat, Acacia, stone pine, and so on.

Crops within a mile of hives will outyield crop in bee-deficient areas by a factor of 3-10 times. See also [[grassland-pasture-management-mollison]]
