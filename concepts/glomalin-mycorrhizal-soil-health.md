Discovered by Sara Wright at the USDA [[fukuoka-inductive-deductive-intuitive-reasoning-agricultural-research]] Service
in 1996, glomalin is a sticky, persistent substance that coats fungal
hyphae and binds soil particles together into stable aggregates.

Glomalin concentrations in healthy soils can reach 100 mg per gram of
soil. It accounts for a significant portion of total soil organic
carbon — in some soils, up to 27 percent of all soil carbon. This
makes glomalin one of the most abundant organic molecules in
terrestrial ecosystems worldwide.

The discovery of glomalin revolutionized understanding of how soil
structure forms and is maintained. Previously, soil aggregation was
attributed primarily to polysaccharides and other transient organic
compounds. Glomalin's extreme persistence explains how stable soil
structure can endure for decades.

## Glomalin's Role in Soil Aggregation

Glomalin serves as a primary biological glue in the formation of soil
aggregates — the crumb-like structures that give healthy soil its
favorable physical properties. Glomalin binds clay particles, silt,
sand, [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] into stable aggregates that resist breakdown
from rainfall and cultivation.

Well-aggregated soil has improved water infiltration, better aeration,
enhanced root penetration, and significantly reduced erosion risk.
Aggregates also create microhabitats that protect soil organisms from
predation and environmental stress including drying and temperature
extremes.

Soil aggregation occurs in a hierarchical process driven by biology.
Microaggregates form around bacterial colonies and individual fungal
hyphae. These microaggregates are then bound into larger macroaggregates
by extensive [[teaming-with-microbes-tilling-damage-to-fungal-networks]] and root systems. Glomalin stabilizes
aggregates at both scales.

## Carbon Sequestration Through Mycorrhizal Networks

[[mollison-designers-mycorrhizal-fungi-and-soil-microbiology]] their glomalin production play a major role in
global carbon sequestration. Plants allocate a substantial portion of
their photosynthetically fixed carbon — typically 10 to 20 percent or
more — to mycorrhizal partners. Much of this carbon ends up stored as
glomalin in soil for extended periods.

Unlike labile organic matter that decomposes relatively quickly, glomalin
is highly resistant to microbial breakdown. It persists in soil for 7 to
42 years depending on environmental conditions. This long residence time
makes mycorrhizal-mediated [[fungal-peat-decomposition-and-carbon-storage]] a significant climate tool.

Practices that support mycorrhizal fungi — such as no-till farming,
diverse cover cropping, and reducing phosphorus fertilizer applications
— enhance glomalin accumulation and soil carbon storage. Compost tea
applications that include [[pgpr-mycorrhizal-inoculants-microplant-establishment]] can accelerate this
process in degraded soils.

## Calcium and Fungal Soil Relationships

Calcium plays a crucial role in fungal soil dominance and mycorrhizal
function. In natural ecosystems, calcium is the dominant cation on
fungal-dominated soil exchange sites. Soils with balanced calcium
levels tend to support stronger [[mycorrhizal-networks-and-forest-communication]] better soil
aggregate stability.

High levels of available phosphorus, particularly from synthetic
fertilizer applications, can suppress [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]]. When
phosphorus is readily available in soil solution, plants reduce their
carbohydrate allocation to fungal partners, weakening the symbiosis.

Maintaining appropriate calcium-to-magnesium ratios supports fungal
growth and glomalin production. Many agricultural soils have become
excessively magnesium-dominated through repeated applications of
dolomitic limestone. Selecting calcium sources that avoid adding
excess magnesium helps maintain fungal-friendly soil chemistry.
