# Soft Fruit Bushes and Berries

Soft fruit bushes and berries form a valuable and productive layer in Sepp
Holzer's permaculture systems. From currants and gooseberries to strawberries
and elderberries, these plants provide vitamin-rich food, create habitat for
beneficial insects and birds, and fit naturally into the multi-layered
landscape design of the [[krameterhof-holzer-model-farm]]. They complement
the [[holzer-fruit-forest-establishment]] and [[holzer-wild-fruit-trees-and-hedges]] that form the backbone of his orchard systems.

## The Traditional Kitchen Garden Heritage

Holzer's childhood memories of the family Gachtl (kitchen garden) paint a
vivid picture of the traditional role of soft fruit. The garden was enclosed
with a picket fence and had a variety of fruit bushes. He remembers the
redcurrants, blackcurrants and white currants by the fence and the
strawberries that reached all the way to the wall of the house. A gooseberry
bush and an intensely sweet-smelling double-flowered rose bush grew in the
sunniest part of the garden.

This arrangement was not accidental. The bushes were placed in the sunniest
positions because they are very susceptible to mildew. Damp, which lasts
longer in the shade, encourages mildew. The reasoning behind their placement
demonstrates the careful observation that underpins [[holzer-assessing-the-land]] and all of Holzer's growing practices.

## Currants

Redcurrants, blackcurrants and white currants were traditionally planted
along fences in the kitchen garden. The fence provided support and the
position along the garden boundary gave them good air circulation. Currants
are relatively undemanding once established and produce reliable crops year
after year.

On the [[krameterhof-holzer-model-farm]], the situation with currants is much
the same as with pears and apples: old, locally adapted varieties produce
fruit with higher nutrient content and better flavour than modern commercial
varieties. The quality of old varieties is often far superior, and their
aroma and taste are more intense. This aligns with Holzer's broader
philosophy of [[seed-selection-breeding-holzer]] and variety preservation.

## Gooseberries

The gooseberry bush in the Gachtl was given the sunniest spot, reflecting
its susceptibility to mildew in damp conditions. Gooseberries thrive in well-
drained soil with good air circulation. When planted in the right position,
they require minimal care and produce abundant fruit suitable for jams,
preserves, and desserts.

The integration of gooseberries into [[holzer-vegetable-garden-polycultures]]
is straightforward because they occupy a different vertical layer than most
vegetables and herbs, reducing competition.

## Strawberries

Strawberries were planted right up to the wall of the house, taking
advantage of the heat radiated from the stone wall. This "masonry stove
effect" extends the growing season and improves fruit quality. The wall
absorbs heat during the day and releases it slowly at night, protecting the
strawberries from late frosts and creating warmer soil conditions.

This technique of using walls for heat retention is also applied to
[[holzer-kiwi-and-grape-growing]] and other climbing plants. The principle
of positioning frost-sensitive plants near heat-storing structures is
central to [[holzer-microclimate-creation]].

## Elderberry

Elderberry (Sambucus nigra) serves multiple functions in the permaculture
system. It is an indicator plant for nitrogen-rich soils, provides excellent
bird habitat, and produces fruit used for juices, wines, and preserves. Its
large leaves create shade and its flowers are valuable for bees and other
pollinators.

In the broader landscape, elderberry is one of the good forage plants for
local bird species, along with fly honeysuckle (Lonicera xylosteum),
barberry (Berberis vulgaris), and bramble (Rubus fructicosus). These plants
support the bird populations that play important roles in pest regulation,
connecting to [[sepp-holzer-garden-helpers-and-pest-regulation]].

## Embankment Planting

On terraces, the embankments between the terraces provide relatively dry and
warm conditions, which Holzer always bears in mind when planting crops. He
has had the best results planting fruit bushes and trees on these
embankments. If the right varieties are selected, the fruits and berries
ripen in the autumn after the vegetables and cereals have been harvested.

This use of seasonal crops makes efficient use of the land and avoids the
risk of the crops being damaged. The concept of stacking crops both
vertically and temporally is fundamental to [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]].

## Processing and Value Addition

Fruit trees and bushes fulfil a number of functions within Holzer's
permaculture system. They provide vitamin-rich, healthy food, which can be
processed to create many different products such as jams, preserves, juices,
vinegar, wine, spirits and more. This diverse range of products connects soft
fruit cultivation to the broader theme of farm self-sufficiency and value
addition.

Windfall fruit from all fruit trees makes especially good high-quality feed
for pigs, connecting soft fruit production to [[holzer-pig-keeping-paddock-system]]. The fruit blossoms provide a great number of insects with a rich
source of food, and bees benefit enormously, improving pollination across
the entire system.

## Bilberries and Wild Berries

Bilberries (Vaccinium myrtillus) grow naturally in the alpine pastures
around the [[krameterhof-holzer-model-farm]]. They are one of the wild plant
species that Holzer values as indicators and as food sources. Bilberry leaves
are also used in traditional remedies, brewed in hot water alongside nettles
and French lilac for various purposes.

These wild berries represent the [[holzer-wild-fruit-trees-and-hedges]]
component of the system, providing food without any cultivation effort. They
also support the [[holzer-indicator-plants]] concept, as their presence
indicates specific soil and moisture conditions.

## Integration with Terraced Landscapes

On terraced slopes, soft fruit bushes can be planted at staggered heights
along with shrubs and fruit trees. This multi-layered approach maximises the
productive use of space. The terraces provide different microclimates at
different levels, allowing a wider range of soft fruit species to be grown
than would be possible on a single slope.

The [[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]] create the structural
framework within which soft fruit bushes find their niche, contributing to
the overall diversity and productivity of the system.
