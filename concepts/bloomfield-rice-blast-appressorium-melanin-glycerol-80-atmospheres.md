## Rice Blast Disease
Rice ranks as the second largest crop on Earth and is a staple food for more than
half the world's population. A rice field infected by Magnaporthe looks as if it
has been struck by a bomb, which accounts for the common name "rice blast." The
fungus usually infects the leaves and spreads as an invasive mycelium, consuming
plant tissues and starving the developing grain. Sometimes the neck or panicle is
ravaged, becoming bleached and withered to dry straw—a condition called "neck
blast" that causes severe crop loss.

## The Infection Sequence
Trouble begins when a pear-shaped conidium drifts onto the rice leaf surface.
Impact triggers the release of a sticky globule of mucilage from one end of the
spore that binds the fungus to the waxy cuticle. If the leaf is wet with dew, the
conidium germinates, and a germ tube travels a short distance before adhering at
its tip and swelling into a bulbous structure called the appressorium. This entire
process occurs within hours of initial contact.

## Genome Sequencing and Genetics
Magnaporthe has seven chromosomes containing about 3 times more DNA than the yeast
Saccharomyces and 80 times less than a human. The rice blast genome is being
sequenced to identify distinctive genes whose products orchestrate the intimate
relationship between the fungus and its hosts: genes controlling the timing of
conidium germination and melanin synthesis, genes turned on during epidermal
penetration, and others expressed during feeding on the cytoplasm. Yeast has none
of these specialized pathogenicity genes, making the Magnaporthe genome a valuable
resource for understanding the evolution of plant disease.

## Coffee Rust: A Related Pathogen
Many rusts have edited versions of the life cycle described for Puccinia graminis
(black stem rust of wheat). Coffee rust, caused by Hemileia vastatrix, produces
uredospores, teliospores, and basidiospores, but lacks spermatium and aeciospore-
producing stages. Because the fungus thrives on coffee alone with no alternate host,
prevention of coffee rust is limited to fungicide spraying and resistant crop
varieties. The importance of coffee growing to economies in South and Central
America, Africa, and Asia places considerable urgency upon effective control
measures.
