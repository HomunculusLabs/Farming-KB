# Syncretic Entheogenic Churches

> Based on Ralph Metzner's "Hallucinogenic Drugs in Psychotherapy and Shamanism"

## Definition

Syncretic entheogenic churches are organized religious movements that
incorporate psychoactive plant medicines into communal worship and
ceremony. The term "syncretic" reflects how these traditions blend
indigenous shamanic practices with elements of Christianity, African
spirit traditions, and other religious frameworks.

These movements differ fundamentally from traditional shamanic rituals
in both structure and purpose.

## Distinction from Shamanic Rituals

Traditional shamanism centers on a single practitioner — the shaman —
who serves as healer, diviner, and intermediary with the spirit world.
The shaman's work is primarily diagnostic and therapeutic, aimed at
restoring balance for individuals.

Syncretic entheogenic ceremonies, by contrast, are collective
experiences led by priests or officiants rather than shamans. They
involve large groups of twenty to forty participants, or even hundreds
in major Brazilian hoasca churches.

## Community Bonding and Worship

The primary focus of syncretic ceremonies is community bonding and
celebratory worship, not individual healing or divination. Participants
engage together in singing, prayer, and shared ritual experience.

There is remarkably little discussion of individual visions within
these ceremonies. The entheogenic experience serves as a communal
sacrament rather than a personal diagnostic tool.

By organizing as formal churches, these movements gain both social
cohesion and legal protection — critical advantages that isolated
shamanic practices lack.

## Brazilian Ayahuasca Churches

Three major Brazilian churches use ayahuasca (locally called hoasca)
as their central sacrament: Santo Daime, União do Vegetal (UDV), and
Barquinha. All are legally recognized in Brazil.

### Founding in the 1950s Amazon

Each of these churches was founded in the 1950s by rubber tappers
working in the Amazon basin. Remarkably, each founder independently
reported receiving a vision instructing them to organize a church
around the ayahuasca sacrament.

### União do Vegetal (UDV)

The UDV is the most formal of the three Brazilian ayahuasca churches.
Ceremonies are conducted with participants seated in rows of chairs.
The format includes sermons, structured question-and-answer sessions,
and personal testimonials from members.

The emphasis is on disciplined ritual structure and intellectual
engagement with the entheogenic experience.

### Santo Daime

Santo Daime ceremonies are more ecstatic and participatory. Central to
the ritual is hymn singing, traditionally led by women. Participants
dance and move together as part of the worship experience.

The name "Santo Daime" itself means "Holy Give-Me," reflecting the
devotional and supplicatory nature of the practice.

### Barquinha

Barquinha incorporates elements of Umbanda, an Afro-Brazilian
spirit tradition. This integration brings practices of ancestor contact
and communion with orixás (spiritual entities) into the ayahuasca
ceremony.

Barquinha represents the most spiritually diverse of the three
churches, blending Amazonian, African, and Christian elements.

## Native American Church

The Native American Church (NAC) uses peyote as its central sacrament.
The church is legally incorporated and has historically enjoyed legal
protection for its ceremonial use of peyote in the United States.

NAC ceremonies follow a consistent format: a roadman leads the
ceremony, participants sing peyote songs throughout the night, and
the ritual unfolds within a structured communal setting.

Recent court decisions have eroded some of these legal protections,
creating ongoing challenges for practitioners.

## Bwiti Cult and Iboga

The Bwiti cult, practiced in Gabon and Zaire, centers on the use of
iboga (also called eboga), a powerful psychoactive root bark.

Bwiti practices were originally learned from Pygmy peoples of the
region. The tradition involves a profound death-rebirth initiation
rite in which initiates describe having their "head opened up" and
conversing directly with ancestors.

This ancestor contact is a defining feature of Bwiti practice,
distinguishing it from the primarily Christian-influenced Brazilian
churches.

## Ibogaine and Addiction Treatment

Ibogaine, the principal active alkaloid of iboga, has attracted
significant scientific interest for its potential as an addiction
treatment. Research suggests ibogaine may have specific action on
cocaine receptor sites in the brain.

This has led some researchers to propose ibogaine as a possible
chemical antidote to addiction. More broadly, Metzner suggests that
all consciousness-expanding hallucinogens can counteract the effects
of consciousness-contracting addictive narcotics.

## Health Benefits Research

Long-term studies of Brazilian ayahuasca church members have found
no adverse health effects from regular sacramental use. These
communities actually show reduced incidence of alcoholism and drug
addiction compared to the general population.

The entheogenic sacrament appears to function as a positive force
for both individual wellbeing and community health.

## Cultural Transformation Potential

These syncretic movements have brought entheogenic experiences to
large numbers of diverse people beyond their indigenous origins.
Participants come from varied social, economic, and cultural
backgrounds.

Metzner views these churches as the beginnings of a broader cultural
transformation — one in which consciousness-expanding plant medicines
serve as tools for collective spiritual growth rather than merely
individual therapeutic intervention.

The organized, legal, and communal nature of these churches provides
a model for how entheogenic practice might be responsibly integrated
into modern society.
