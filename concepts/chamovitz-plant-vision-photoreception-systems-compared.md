# Chamovitz Plant Vision — Photoreception Systems Compared

## Overview

In "What a Plant Knows," [[daniel-chamovitz]] builds a compelling case
that plants possess a form of vision by systematically comparing
plant photoreception to human visual systems. Rather than dismissing
plant light sensitivity as a mere chemical reflex, Chamovitz argues
that the ability to detect, differentiate, and respond to different
wavelengths of light constitutes a legitimate sensory modality that
deserves to be called sight.

## The Human Visual System as Baseline

Chamovitz begins by establishing what human vision entails, using
Merriam-Webster's definition: "the physical sense by which light
stimuli received by the eye are interpreted by the brain and
constructed into a representation of the position, shape, brightness,
and usually colour of objects in space."

The human retina contains approximately 125 million rods (sensitive
to all light, enabling night vision) and 6 million cones (color-
sensitive, with three types: red, green, and blue). This gives the
human eye an effective resolution of approximately 130 megapixels
in an area the size of a passport photo.

The key photoreceptor chemicals — rhodopsin in rods and photopsins
in cones — absorb light of different wavelengths:
- Blue light: absorbed by rhodopsin and blue photopsin
- Red light: absorbed by rhodopsin and red photopsin
- Purple light: absorbed by all photopsins except green

Blindness results from defects at any stage: retinal structure,
photoreceptor chemistry, or signal transmission to the brain.

## The Gradualist Argument for Plant Vision

Chamovitz constructs his argument through a thought experiment:
imagine a person born totally blind who gains the ability to
discriminate between light and shadow. This would undeniably be
considered rudimentary sight. Now imagine further gaining the
ability to discern color — the transition from monochrome to
color vision is universally accepted as enhanced sight.

Plants occupy a position on this spectrum that Chamovitz argues
qualifies as vision. They can:

- Detect light vs darkness (day/night discrimination)
- Distinguish between colors (blue, red, far-red)
- Sense light direction (phototropism)
- Measure light duration (photoperiodism)
- Detect light intensity (irradiance sensing)
- Perceive ultraviolet and infrared (extending [[plant-light-discrimination-beyond-human-vision]])

## Plant Photoreceptor Arsenal

Plants possess multiple photoreceptor systems, each specialized
for different aspects [[plant-photoreceptors-and-mechanism-of-light-perception]]:

**Phytochromes**: Detect red and far-red light. These are the
molecular basis for photoperiodism — the ability to measure
night length. Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms:
Pr (absorbs red light, ~660nm) and Pfr (absorbs far-red light,
~730nm). The last color a plant "sees" at dusk is far-red,
signaling it to deactivate. In the morning, red light reactivates
it. This light-activated switch allows plants to measure how long
ago they last saw red light.

**Cryptochromes**: Blue-light receptors involved in circadian
rhythm entrainment, phototropism, and de-etiolation. They are
structurally related to photolyase DNA repair enzymes and
represent an evolutionarily ancient photoreception system.

**Phototropins**: Blue-light receptors specifically responsible
for directional light sensing. These mediate the phototropic
response — bending toward light — that Darwin first documented.

**UV-B receptors**: Detect ultraviolet-B radiation (280-315nm),
triggering protective responses including flavonoid production
and DNA repair pathway activation.

## Plants See Beyond Human Vision

A key argument in Chamovitz's framework is that plants actually
perceive a **broader range of the [[electromagnetic-spectrum-plant-light-perception]]** than
humans:

- **Ultraviolet light** (below 400nm): Plants detect UV that
  humans cannot see, triggering protective pigment production
  and DNA repair mechanisms
- **Far-red light** (700-750nm): Plants use far-red as a signal
  for [[phytochrome-red-far-red-ratio-shade-detection-plants]] and photoperiod measurement, though it
  appears nearly invisible to human eyes
- **Infrared sensing**: Plants detect infrared radiation as heat,
  though this is indirect thermal sensing rather than true
  photoreception

This expanded spectral range means plants monitor aspects of their
light environment that are literally invisible to humans. A plant
"sees" if you've painted your house blue or moved its pot from
one side of the room to another, not because it forms an image,
but because it detects the spectral composition, direction, and
duration of available light.

## Darwin's Demonstration of Plant Sight

Chamovitz highlights Darwin's 1880 [[darwin-phototropism-experiments-plant-vision]] as the
definitive demonstration of plant vision. The Darwins grew canary
grass in total darkness, then exposed it to a gas lamp so dim they
"could not see the seedlings themselves." After three hours, the
plants had curved toward the invisible light.

The critical experiment showed that the "eye" of the plant is at
the shoot tip:
- Untreated seedling: bent toward light
- Tip removed: no bending (blind)
- Tip covered with lightproof cap: no bending (blind)
- Tip covered with clear glass cap: bent toward light (can see)
- Middle covered with lightproof tube: bent toward light (only
  tip needs to see)

This elegantly proved that the tip detects light direction and
sends a signal to the stem to bend accordingly — a functional
visual system.

## Philosophical Implications

Chamovitz's framework challenges the anthropocentric assumption
that vision requires image formation. He argues that the essence
of sight is the **reception and processing of light information**
that guides behavior. By this standard, plants clearly possess
vision, even though their visual experience bears no resemblance
to human pictorial perception.
