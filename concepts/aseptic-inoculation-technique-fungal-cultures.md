# Aseptic Inoculation Technique for Fungal Cultures

Inoculation — the introduction of fungal inoculum into a sterile substrate — is the step where contamination is most likely to be introduced. Mastering aseptic technique is therefore the single most important skill for successful [[mycological cultivation]]. Even perfectly prepared substrate and ideal environmental conditions cannot compensate for poor inoculation hygiene. The difference between consistent success and consistent failure often comes down to discipline at this stage.

## Inoculum Types

Multiple forms of fungal inoculum can be used to start a culture on [[grass-seed substrate]] or other prepared media:

- **Spore syringes**: A suspension of spores in sterile water. The most accessible form for beginners but carries higher genetic variability, as each spore represents a unique genetic individual with potentially different growth characteristics.
- **Mycelium syringes**: Live mycelium in sterile water. Faster colonisation since the organism is already germinated. Shorter shelf life — use promptly.
- **Agar squares**: Small pieces of colonised agar from a petri dish. The highest selectivity since contaminants are visible before transfer. Preferred by experienced cultivators.
- **Liquid culture**: Mycelium grown in nutrient broth. Allows rapid expansion from a small starter. Requires additional equipment.
- **Grain-to-grain transfer**: Colonised grain from an established jar. Extremely efficient but also efficient at spreading undetected contamination. Only use with verified clean jars.

## The Principle of Working Clean

The fundamental principle is minimising the time sterile substrate is exposed to open air. Every second a jar lid is open, airborne contaminants — bacterial spores, fungal spores, mould spores — settle on the substrate. The goal is under 5 seconds of exposure per jar. Speed combined with hygiene is the foundation of success.

## Pre-Inoculation Preparation

### Temperature Check

Jars must be at room temperature before inoculation. Temperatures above approximately 40°C kill spores or mycelium. Wait at least 4-6 hours or overnight after sterilisation. Jars that feel even slightly warm to the touch of the back of the hand are too hot.

### Workspace Preparation

Even without a laminar flow hood, a still-air environment dramatically reduces contamination:

- A cardboard box with an opening acts as a draft shield — wipe interior surfaces with disinfectant
- Clean workspace with 70% isopropyl alcohol or 10% bleach solution
- Wipe all surfaces and tools thoroughly
- Close windows, turn off fans and air conditioning — minimise air movement
- Shower and wear clean clothes; the cultivator's body is a significant contaminant source
- Wear a surgical mask to prevent breath moisture and microbes
- Tie back long hair; wash hands or wear clean gloves

### Tool Sterilisation

**Needles** (for syringes): Flame-sterilise until glowing red-orange. Cool for 15-20 seconds before use. A butane torch or alcohol lamp works well. The needle must cool before contacting substrate to avoid thermal damage.

**Scalpels** (for agar): Flame-sterilise similarly. Cool for 10-15 seconds. Some cultivators cool the blade by touching it briefly to a sterile agar surface before cutting the transfer piece.

### Critical Safety Warning

Alcohol and aerosol disinfectant sprays such as Lysol are **highly flammable**. Flame-sterilising tools near open alcohol containers or freshly sprayed surfaces poses a serious fire risk. Allow all alcohol to evaporate completely before introducing any ignition source. This is repeated in the source documentation and cannot be overstated.

## Inoculation Procedure

1. Ensure jars are at room temperature by touch-testing
2. Set up the still-air box with all tools within arm's reach
3. Flame-sterilise the inoculation tool and allow it to cool
4. Shake the jar to loosen substrate — creating air pockets for inoculum distribution
5. Open the jar lid just a crack — wide enough for the tool but no wider
6. Introduce inoculum targeting multiple sites within the jar
7. Immediately close the lid to minimise exposure time
8. Shake vigorously to distribute inoculum across multiple kernels
9. Loosen the lid again for mycelial respiration during colonisation

## Distribution and Shaking

Shaking after inoculation distributes inoculum across many kernels, each becoming a new growth point. This reduces colonisation time from 30+ days to as few as 5-10 days. For spore syringes, inject at 4 angles around the jar perimeter. Some cultivators perform a second shake a few days later when mycelium is visible at several points.

## Troubleshooting Failed Inoculations

Several common issues can cause inoculation failure:

**No growth after 14 days** typically indicates either dead inoculum (from overheating or age) or contamination that has overwhelmed the introduced species. Inspect carefully before discarding — sometimes growth is occurring in an unobserved part of the jar.

**Growth starts then stalls** may suggest contamination competing with the mycelium, suboptimal temperature, or insufficient moisture in the substrate. Bacterial contamination often produces a distinctive sour smell.

**Uneven growth pattern** (colonised patches with bare areas) usually results from insufficient shaking after inoculation, leaving inoculum concentrated in one area rather than distributed throughout the jar.

## Post-Inoculation Care

- Place jars in a clean, draft-free location away from direct sunlight
- Incubate at 20-25°C for [[Psilocybe]] species
- Monitor regularly for white mycelial growth or contamination signs
- First signs typically appear within 3-7 days (spores) or 1-3 days (mycelium/agar)

## Selecting the Right Inoculum Type

The choice of inoculum depends on the cultivator's experience level and goals:

- **Beginners** should start with spore syringes for accessibility, accepting the trade-off of slower colonisation and genetic variability
- **Intermediate cultivators** benefit from transitioning to mycelium syringes or agar work for improved reliability and speed
- **Advanced cultivators** typically maintain agar cultures and use grain-to-grain transfer for maximum efficiency at scale

Each step up the inoculum hierarchy requires additional equipment, skills, and workspace hygiene but delivers progressively better results in terms of colonisation speed, contamination resistance, and genetic consistency.

## Monitoring Colonisation After Inoculation

After the inoculation procedure is complete, careful monitoring of the jars during the early colonisation period helps identify problems before they become irrecoverable:

- **Day 1-3**: Inspect for any immediate signs of contamination — unusual colours, slime, or gas bubbles forming in the substrate. These indicate the inoculation was not clean enough.
- **Day 3-7**: Look for the first white mycelial growth. For spore syringes, germination may take up to a week. For mycelium or agar inoculation, growth should be visible within 1-3 days.
- **Day 7-14**: Mycelium should be spreading visibly. If growth is visible at only one or two points, a shake at this stage redistributes colonised kernels and significantly speeds up the remainder of the process.
- **Day 14+**: Jars approaching full colonisation will appear almost entirely white. Any remaining uncolonised patches at the bottom or edges should be monitored — if they persist beyond a few more days, contamination may be present in those areas.

Keeping a simple log of inoculation date, inoculum type, and observed growth milestones helps cultivators refine their technique over time and identify patterns in their success and failure rates.

## Contamination Recognition

- **Bacterial contamination**: Sour smell, slimy texture, cloudy liquid, grain discolouration
- **Trichoderma** (green mould): Fast-growing green patches — the most common grain contaminant
- **Aspergillus** (black mould): Dark growth, visible spore clouds when disturbed
- **Cobweb mould**: Grey, wispy spider-web-like growth, faster than mushroom mycelium

Any confirmed contaminated jar should be removed immediately and re-sterilised before opening to prevent spore release into the growing environment.
