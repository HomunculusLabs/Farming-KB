# Log and Stump Cultivation Methods

## Overview

People have been growing mushrooms on logs and stumps for hundreds, possibly
thousands, of years. It is one of the simplest ways to cultivate edible and
medicinal forest mushrooms such as shiitakes, maitakes, and oysters. Some
mushrooms, such as maitakes, are actually easier to grow outdoors under natural
conditions than indoors. This method is generally low-tech and best suited for
home-scale and hobby growing or supplemental income.

Cultivating on stumps is similar to log cultivation, but stumps can take many
years to fruit (once they start, they can fruit for a decade or more). Species
that benefit from stump cultivation include maitakes, chicken of the woods,
reishis, enoki, oysters, and beefsteaks.

## Selecting Wood

### Species Selection

Hardwood species are generally suitable for mushroom cultivation in temperate
climates. Invasive and nonnative tree species should not be overlooked. Different
strains of mushrooms vary in their ability to break down specific wood types, so
consult your spawn supplier about wood-mushroom compatibility.

Evergreens typically do not support mushroom production, with exceptions for
cauliflower (Sparassis crispa), tufted conifer lover (Hypholoma capnoides), and
some chicken of the woods strains. Mushrooms grown on conifers may have a
sap-like flavor and can cause gastric upset.

### Bark Integrity

Ensure logs and stumps have bark intact all the way around. Avoid tree species
that drop their bark when cut, such as elms. Mushrooms form between the inner
and outer layers of bark, using this space as a nursery where primordia can
safely develop. Some mushrooms can lift or split bark with pressure greater than
80 psi.

### Freshness

Logs should have been cut no more than one month ago. Harder woods have a longer
contamination window than softer woods. After cutting, trees remain "alive" for
about a week with natural antifungal defenses still active. Delaying
inoculation for that period allows inhibitory compounds to dissipate.

### Seasonal Timing

In temperate climates, cut logs in winter months after leaves have fallen, when
trees have more sugars available to the mycelium. In tropical climates, focus on
matching wood type with mushroom species rather than seasonal timing.

## Step-by-Step Inoculation

### Step 1: Cutting Logs

Cut logs into manageable lengths of 2 to 4 feet, aiming for a diameter of 6 to
10 inches. Consider the weight you can carry, since you may be picking up logs
frequently for inspection and watering.

### Step 2: Drilling

Drill holes at regular intervals in a diamond pattern, all the way around the
bark-covered surface, at a depth of 1 to 1.5 inches. Space holes 4 to 6 inches
apart. A high-speed drill bit attached to an angle grinder is worth the
investment for large numbers of logs. Closer spacing speeds colonization, but
spacing farther than 6 inches increases the risk of competing fungal spores.

### Step 3: Inoculation

Insert spawn into each hole -- tap with a hammer for plug spawn or tamp with a
dowel for sawdust. Fill until flush with the bark. Brush melted wax over each
spawned hole to seal in moisture and protect the mycelium as it drills outward.
Some growers also wax the log ends; consider waxing only one end to allow
moisture to wick in from the other.

### Step 4: Colonization

Situate logs in a shady area with dappled forest light, near a water source.
Colonization takes six to ten months depending on wood type, temperature, and
spawn spacing. Visit at least once monthly during the first six months. If logs
feel extremely dry and light, soak them overnight (no longer than 24 hours or
mycelium can drown). Alternatively, mist gently over a week to gradually wick
in moisture.

### Step 5: Monitoring Colonization

About six months after inoculation, inspect log ends weekly for white streaks
of mycelium signaling approaching full colonization. At this point, soak logs
overnight and spread them in a "log cabin" formation or lean them against living
trees in a circular pattern.

### Step 6: Fruiting and Harvest

Logs fruit two or three times a year, typically for as many years as they are
inches in diameter, resting a few weeks between flushes. When baby mushrooms
appear as dark brown to black buttons splitting through the bark, mist several
times daily. Keep records of flushing patterns and time watering accordingly.
Once mushrooms are colonized and ready to fruit, they wait for adequate water
to support production.

## Sourcing Wood

If you lack a woodlot, try composting centers, tree removal services, power-line
companies, and arborists. Check with local agricultural experiment stations or
university agricultural departments, many of which maintain mushroom culture
collections.

## Identification of Harvested Mushrooms

Outdoor cultivation means spores from competitive species may land on your
growing substrate. Learn basic identification practices such as taking spore
prints and studying mushroom anatomy. Photograph primordia and send photos to
your spawn supplier for verification. Never identify mushrooms from online
searches alone. Join mushroom clubs through the North American Mycological
Association (www.namyco.org).

## Split Logs

Split logs (firewood) can support mushrooms on bark-covered sides but dry out
more readily. Bury the split side or rest it on the ground to act as a water
wick. All fruiting will take place on the upper surface, which will produce
large numbers of mushrooms.
