# Yage Cross-Cultural Experience: Common Themes in South American Ayahuasca Visions

Michael J. Harner's survey "Common Themes in South American Indian Yage Experiences," published in [[hallucinogens-and-shamanism-harner-anthology]] (1973), represents one of the first systematic attempts to identify cross-cultural regularities in the hallucinatory experiences produced by Banisteriopsis (yage/ayahuasca) among indigenous peoples of the South American tropical forest.

## Method and Sources

Harner drew on ethnographic reports spanning numerous tribes from the western slopes of the Colombian Andes to the eastern foothills of the Bolivian Andes — a vast geographical range. His own fieldwork among the Jivaro (1956-57, 1964) and Conibo-Shipibo (1960-61) provided firsthand data, supplemented by published accounts from Reichel-Dolmatoff, Carneiro, Tessmann, Koch-Gruenberg, and others.

The survey was motivated by Harner's own experience in 1961 among the Conibo, where the effects of ayahuasca were so impressive that they raised fundamental questions about the cross-cultural importance of the hallucinogenic experience in shamanism and religion. He notes that it is difficult to convey to the uninitiated the reality and intensity of the hallucinations.

## Theme 1: Soul Flight

The soul is believed to separate from the physical body and to make a trip, often with the sensation of flight. This is the single most commonly reported experience across all Banisteriopsis-using cultures.

Among the Jivaro, part of the soul leaves the body with the sensation of flying, returning when the drug wears off — an experience referred to as a "trip." The Conibo-Shipibo report the shaman's soul leaving his body in the form of a bird which flies to kill a distant person. The Desana (Tukano) experience "a violent current of air, as if a strong wind were pulling them along" — interpreted as ascent to the Milky Way en route to Paradise.

The Zaparo, Campa, Siona, and Quijos all report versions of aerial journey. A half-breed Peruvian woman told E. H. Snethlage that ayahuasca freed the spirit, which was then "able to travel where it desired."

## Theme 2: Visions of Snakes and Jaguars

The visions most commonly reported for all tribes are of snakes (generally poisonous varieties and the anaconda) and jaguars and other dangerous forest felines. In some cases, these predatory creatures appear to threaten or attack the yage-taker.

Among the Jivaro, the most typical apparitions are pairs of giant anacondas or jaguars which roll over and over through the forest as they fight. The Amahuaca report that the jaguar spirit (yoshi) is the one most often seen during first ayahuasca experiences and "teaches the apprentice drinkers all about yoshi." The Cubeo describe the vision beginning as a room "spinning with red feathers" before one begins to see people "in the bright coloring of the jaguar."

Reichel-Dolmatoff's Tukano informants report "the nightmare of jaguars' jaws or the menace of snakes that approach while he, paralyzed by fear, feels how their cold bodies go coiling around his extremities." The Siona explain that snakes represent the vines of yage itself, and that "he who conquers yage also conquers nature and all the dangers which attack men."

## Theme 3: Contact with Demons and Deities

A sense of experiencing first-hand contact with the supernatural becomes almost routine with the aid of Banisteriopsis. Among the Amahuaca, spirits (yoshi) "drink ayahuasca too, and sing along with the men." They are interrogated about their travels and provide information.

Among missionized groups, Christian elements merge with indigenous supernaturalism. Siona informants described visions of God with a cross, beautiful churches, and the ship of the devils "from which the evil spirits come forth who come to the earth so that people perish." The Desana awaken from trance "convinced of the truth of the religious teachings," having seen Vai-mahse (Master of Game Animals) and the Daughter of the Sun.

Among the Jivaro, the spirits that appear may be the souls of dead shamans, who function as spirit familiars for their living counterparts. The shaman typically describes having acquired his helping spirits in the course of his own drug experiences.

## Theme 4: Clairvoyant Visions of Distant Places

Jivaro shamans, under the influence of ayahuasca, believe they are seeing distant persons and what they are doing. The Conibo-Shipibo travel underground in supernatural boats to see distant cities of the demons, said to be visible "because the sunlight passes through the earth."

Indians of the upper Napo have been known to propose group visualizations: "Let's see cities!" — and have described automobiles, which they had never seen, with sufficient accuracy for observers to recognize specific vehicles. Harner notes that both Jivaro and Conibo-Shipibo Indians who had seen motion pictures told him the ayahuasca experiences were comparable to the viewing of films.

## Theme 5: Divinatory Experience

Ayahuasca is utilized by curing shamans to "see" the shaman responsible for bewitching a sick or dying person, or to see the enactment of recent unsolved crimes, particularly homicide and theft.

Spruce reported in 1908 that Zaparo shamans drink ayahuasca "when called on to adjudicate in a dispute — to discover plans of an enemy — to tell if strangers are coming — to ascertain if wives are unfaithful — in the case of a sick man to tell who has bewitched him." The practice was apparently so widespread and well-established that even rival groups would accept the authority of ayahuasca-induced divination.

## Additional Common Features

Beyond the five major themes, commonly reported experiences include auditory hallucinations, visions of geometric designs, auras, one's own death, and combats between demons or zoomorphic forms. The visions involve very bright colors and the constant changing of shapes as scenes dissolve into one another.

A sense of enormous speed is frequently reported — what the Jivaro describe as the "speed" of the trip, the sense of hurtling through space. Waterfalls, rivers, and seas appear frequently. Among the [[cashinahua-banisteriopsis-use]], the most frequent hallucinations include brightly colored large snakes, jaguars and ocelots, spirits, large trees, lakes filled with anacondas and alligators, villages, traders, and gardens.

## Limits of the Data

Harner acknowledged that all the Banisteriopsis-using peoples surveyed occupy a tropical forest environment and their cultures often share much in content. Given their relative contiguity and environmental and cultural similarities, it is "virtually impossible to isolate the nature of the yage-induced experience from its cultural context" on the basis of ethnographic data alone.

He called for comparative material from non-Amazonian settings — such as the experimental study by [[naranjo-harmaline-yage-experiment]] — to help determine which elements of the experience are pharmacologically determined and which are culturally shaped. The key question remains: to what extent do the hallucinatory experiences produced by Banisteriopsis reflect the content of the users' cultural background, and to what extent do they reflect innate properties of the human mind interacting with the drug?

## Historical Context

Harner's cross-cultural survey of Banisteriopsis experiences was groundbreaking in its systematic approach to comparing visionary content across multiple indigenous cultures. Drawing on ethnographic sources spanning the western Amazon from Colombia to Bolivia, Harner assembled evidence for recurring themes that had previously been noted only anecdotally by individual ethnographers working with specific tribes.

The survey was motivated by Harner's own transformative experience with ayahuasca among the Conibo in 1961, which challenged his anthropological assumptions and led him to reconsider the significance of hallucinogenic plants in shamanism and religion. This personal experience paralleled the transformative encounters documented in [[mazatec-mushrooms-of-language]] and [[naranjo-harmaline-yage-experiment]].

Harner's call for cross-cultural comparative data from non-Amazonian settings was partly answered by Naranjo's experimental harmaline study, which confirmed that many of the visionary themes reported by indigenous South Americans — including soul flight, predatory animal visions, and religious imagery — also occurred in non-indigenous experimental subjects. This convergence of ethnographic and experimental evidence strengthened the case for pharmacologically determined universal features of the hallucinatory experience.

## Modern Relevance

The cross-cultural patterns identified by Harner continue to inform contemporary psychedelic research. The recurring themes of soul flight, predatory animal encounters, and contact with supernatural beings have been replicated in modern studies of ayahuasca experiences among both indigenous and non-indigenous users. The concept of "commons" in visionary experience has implications for understanding the neural correlates of consciousness.

Harner's acknowledgment of the limits of his data — noting the cultural and environmental homogeneity of the Amazonian groups surveyed — remains an important methodological caution. The question he raised about the relative contributions of pharmacology and culture to the content of hallucinatory experience continues to drive research in ethnopharmacology and cognitive anthropology, as discussed in [[shamanic-trance-cross-cultural-patterns]].

The growing global popularity of ayahuasca, including urban healing practices documented in [[urban-ayahuasca-iquitos-healing]], has created new opportunities for cross-cultural comparison. Contemporary ayahuasca drinkers in urban settings often report visions of snakes, jaguars, and spirit beings that closely parallel the indigenous experiences catalogued by Harner, suggesting that these themes may indeed be more pharmacologically than culturally determined.
