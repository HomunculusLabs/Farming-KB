# Free-Range Pig Management

## Overview

Free-range pig management integrates pigs into farm systems as foragers, waste recyclers, and soil cultivators. Pigs on range are healthier, cheaper to feed, and produce meat with less saturated fat than pigs kept in confinement sheds. Their greatest contribution to a permaculture system lies in their ability to consume food wastes, control ground vegetation, and convert diverse forage resources into high-quality protein, all while improving soil health through their rooting and manuring behavior.

## Health and Nutritional Advantages

Range-raised pigs produce meat with less saturated fat compared to confined animals. The soft fats from free-range pigs are healthier for human consumption. While free-range pigs may not always be suitable for bacon without a finishing period of grain-feeding for 2–4 weeks to harden (saturate) the fats, for fresh or frozen pork the naturally produced soft fats are entirely suitable.

Diets high in vegetable oils — from avocados, oil palms, olives, or oily fish — produce softer fats in pigs, which is an important consideration when planning finishing strategies for market.

## Site Selection and Infrastructure

### Ideal Site Characteristics
The ideal pig range is preferably wooded, well-drained, and dry in wet seasons. Mud and wet soils encourage worms and disease, and can cause trouble with suckling piglets. Key infrastructure requirements include:

- **Fencing**: Electric fencing has greatly reduced the historically high cost of pig fencing. This is now the most economical containment method.
- **Farrowing pens**: Each pen supports a sow and litter for nearly 3 months. After resting for 6 months, each pen can be used by two sows per year.
- **Shelter sheds**: Various small or one large shelter shed, preferably thatched or insulated against cold, serve on rearing ranges. In the tropics, dense thickets provide adequate natural shelter.
- **Rubbing posts**: Strong diagonal posts wrapped in hessian create ideal scratching stations. The hessian can be kept dampened with sump oil or vegetable oils containing neem (Azadirachta indica), Persian lilac (Melia azedarach/white cedar), or pyrethrum oil for lice control.

### Wallow and Dust Bath Design
- **Restricted wallows**: Sheltered or dampened sites provide comfort; rested mudwallows planted to cattails (Typha spp.) yield excellent root and shoot foods year-round
- **Duck potato (Sagittaria)**: Planted at pond margins for summer-dry forage
- **Dust baths**: A roof over a soft dry area kills lice; can be supplemented with dried neem tree leaves or pyrethrum flowers for enhanced parasite control

## Nutrition and Forage

### Forage Pasture
Good range pasture consists of legumes (clover, lucerne), comfrey, chicory, and young grasses. Coarse grasses are not palatable to pigs, so grazing beef cattle or horses may help reduce grass dominance, or mowing may be necessary. Pigs consume approximately 11 kg wet weight of green material per day and have larger appetites than confined pigs.

### Supplementary Feeding
Pigs require seed, fruit, or kernels in addition to green forage. Good quality green feed halves the necessity for protein meal and grains. A sow in milk needs:
- 3.5 kg of dry rations when piglets are newborn
- 5.5 kg of dry rations when piglets are 2–3 weeks old

### Waste Integration
Pigs are most economically kept where dairy, orchard, root crop, or meat processing wastes are available. They do well on restaurant or household food scraps, making them ideal waste recyclers in integrated farm systems. This waste-feeding capacity is one of their greatest permaculture values — converting what would be disposal problems into valuable protein.

## Range Establishment

### Ground Preparation
Preparing for free-range pig planting involves:
1. Ripping the ground to break compaction
2. Liming to adjust soil pH
3. Sowing to a good grass-legume mix
4. Pushing comfrey, sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke), and arrowroot pieces into the rip lines
5. Planting trees just outside fences and in corners protected by electric fencing

### Complementary Plantings
Trees and perennial plantings around pig ranges provide:
- Shade and shelter
- Additional forage (fallen fruits, acorns, chestnuts)
- Root crops for winter feed
- Fodder from leaf drop
- Wind protection reducing heat and cold stress

## Integration with Other Systems

Pigs serve as the final element in a layered waste-recycling cascade. In a well-designed permaculture farm:
- Orchard windfalls feed pigs (preventing pest breeding from fallen fruit)
- Dairy waste and processing byproducts supplement pig diet
- Root crop surplus and culls provide winter feed
- Restaurant and household food scraps complete the nutrition picture
- Pig manure in turn fertilizes pastures and orchards

## Comparison with Conventional Systems

The contrast between free-range and confinement pig production mirrors that seen in poultry:

| Factor | Free-Range | Confinement |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| Fat quality | Less saturated, healthier | Higher saturated fat |
| Feed costs | Lower (forage + waste) | Higher (complete commercial ration) |
| Health | Robust, disease-resistant | More vulnerable without medication |
| Infrastructure | Fencing and shelter | Expensive sheds, waste management |
| Soil impact | Beneficial cultivation and manuring | Waste concentration problems |
| Animal welfare | Natural behaviors expressed | Restricted, stress-prone |

## Seasonal Considerations

- **Winter**: In very cold climates, winter shedding may be necessary, though sheltered ranges and good dry beds help mitigate cold stress
- **Hot seasons**: Shade, wallows, and dust baths become critical for thermoregulation
- **Farrowing timing**: Plan farrowing to coincide with favorable seasonal conditions, using the pen rotation system (3 months occupied, 6 months rested) to manage parasite cycles and pasture recovery
