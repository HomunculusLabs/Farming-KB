# Urban Ayahuasca Healing in Iquitos

Marlene Dobkin de Rios's study of ayahuasca healing in the Peruvian Amazon city of Iquitos examines how cultural beliefs, attitudes, and values structure the hallucinatory experience in a context of rapid social change.

## Research Setting: Belen

The study was conducted in **Belen**, a slum of some 12,000 people on the Amazon River in Iquitos. Belen serves as a receiving area for forest migrants seeking work and better schooling, with exceedingly high unemployment, malnutrition, family breakdown, prostitution, and chronic illness.

## The Ayahuasquero's Practice

Mestizo healers (*ayahuasqueros*) gather groups of 5-25 patients in forest clearings at the edge of Amazonian cities:

1. **Healing sessions always occur at night**, beginning about 10:00 PM
2. The healer takes out a communal cup and distributes the ayahuasca potion
3. He recites **orations and whistles** to protect each person who drinks
4. Dosage is varied according to body weight and physical strength; optimal dose found to be **7 mg/kg**
5. **Larger doses** given to persons suffering from psychosomatic illness
6. Healers generally **drink last**
7. The aim is for all to reach a **visionary climax simultaneously**

## The Session

- Patients sit around smoking and chatting for about half an hour waiting for effects
- Vomiting and defecation are common; no effort is made to hide these sounds
- The healer stresses the effectiveness of the **purge** (ayahuasca's emetic effects)
- Healers use **Quechua** in special drug songs; whistling marks many moments
- The healer moves around the circle with a **schacapa** (rattle of dried leaves)
- He blows **cigarette smoke** over sick persons
- For pain, the healer may **suck** the area, producing a spine or thistle believed to have been magically introduced
- Each person receives **counseling and ritual exorcism**
- Sessions last 4-5 hours; patients return home at 2-3 AM

## Dietary Proscriptions

Integral to ayahuasca healing due to belief that the vine possesses a **jealous guardian spirit**:
- No salt, lard, or sweets for at least 24 hours before and after
- **Sexual abstinence** may be demanded before the session

## Beliefs About Illness

In Belen, illness is commonly attributed to:
- **Magical harm** by enemies or evil spirits
- **Susto** (fright/soul loss)
- Psychosomatic origins linked to social dislocation

The healer's diagnosis through ayahuasca visions identifies the supernatural cause and determines the appropriate remedy.

## Cultural Persistence

Remarkably, Dobkin de Rios found that belief systems about ayahuasca among Iquitos mestizo populations were described in "very much the same language" as Karsten's 1923 account of Jivaro use in eastern Ecuador, almost 400 air miles away. This replication despite changes in time and space attests to the persistence of ayahuasca-related beliefs across disparate groups.

## The Anthropologist's Experience

Dobkin de Rios herself took both **100 micrograms of Sandoz LSD** and later **ayahuasca in ritual context** (containing *B. caapi* and *B. rusbyana*). She found that culturally reported visions "seemed difficult to comprehend until a subjective experience clarified the veracity of reports about seeing unidentified persons appear in living color." Her own experience provided an "excellent entry into the world of informants' personal experience."

## Theoretical Contribution

The study demonstrates the importance of **cultural antecedent variables** in structuring hallucinatory experience:
- The **cognitive system** of belief in the drug's efficacy
- **Shared expectations** of community members who report certain visions with great frequency
- The actual **nature and utilization** of the insights and visions obtained

This anthropological approach complements clinical studies that focus on non-cultural antecedent variables by revealing how cultural context shapes the content, interpretation, and therapeutic application of drug-induced experiences.
