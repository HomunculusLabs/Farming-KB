## Fungal Mycelium and the Hidden Feeding Phase

The mushroom that catches the human eye is merely the reproductive organ of a
far larger and more enduring organism. The vast majority of a fungus's life is
spent in an invisible feeding phase, as masses of filamentous cells called
hyphae spread through soil, leaf litter, decomposing wood, and living plant
tissue. This hidden network, collectively called the mycelium, is the true
body of the fungus. Nicholas Money describes the relationship memorably:
stinkhorns and other mushrooms are the tips of mycological icebergs. The
umbilical cord at the bottom of a mushroom's egg connects with a larger
organism that pulses unseen through leaf litter, crawls under the bark of
dying trees, and connects with the roots of healthier ones.

## Hyphal Architecture

Individual hyphae are tubular cells, typically only a few micrometers in
diameter, that grow at their tips. This apical growth is one of the defining
features of the fungal kingdom. As hyphae elongate, they branch repeatedly,
forming an ever-expanding network that can cover enormous areas. A single
[[dighton-fungal-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]],
the world's forests would be buried under mountains of undecomposed woody
debris. The mycelium's ability to secrete powerful enzymes is also what makes
fungi such effective pathogens of plants and animals, as the same digestive
process that breaks down dead wood can also attack living tissue.

## Mycorrhizal Partnerships

One of the most ecologically significant relationships in nature is the
mycorrhizal association between fungi and plant roots. In mycorrhizal
partnerships, fungal hyphae either wrap around (ectomycorrhizae) or penetrate
(arbuscular mycorrhizae) plant root cells, creating an intimate interface for
[[stinkhorn-fungi-and-insect-spore-dispersal]]
and [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]] that may include mycorrhizal connections to living trees.

## Fruiting: From Feeding to Reproduction

The transition from the feeding phase to the reproductive phase is one of the
most dramatic events in fungal biology. A mushroom or other fruiting body is
produced only when the mycelium has accumulated sufficient reserves of food and
absorbed enough water to support the energetically expensive process of
constructing a complex three-dimensional structure. The fruiting body is,
fundamentally, a device for producing and dispersing spores. Money emphasizes
this point repeatedly: mushrooms serve no purpose beyond reproduction. The
massive investment of resources required to build a fruiting body — the
expansion of tissue, the production of pigments, volatile chemicals, and
structural supports — all serves the single goal of getting spores into the
environment where they can germinate and establish new mycelial networks. For
gilled mushrooms, this means supporting massive numbers of spores on a single
stalk, with the fertile tissue folded over gills, ripples, or spines to
maximize spore-producing surface area for minimal investment in tissue.
