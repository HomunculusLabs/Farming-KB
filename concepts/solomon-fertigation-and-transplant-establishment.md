# Solomon's Fertigation and Transplant Establishment Method

Steve Solomon's transplant establishment technique combines liquid
fertilization (fertigation) with careful planting to eliminate transplant
shock and accelerate early growth. This method is central to his low-input,
high-yield gardening approach described in *Gardening When It Counts*.

## The Fertigation Concept

Fertigation is the practice of applying liquid fertilizer through the
watering system. Solomon distinguishes between fertigating seedlings in
flats versus fertigating established garden plants. For transplants,
fertigation serves a specific purpose: creating a nutrient-rich slurry
around the root zone that bridges the gap between potting soil and garden
soil.

Solomon recommends using full-strength liquid fertilizer or compost/manure
tea at one-third concentration for seedling care, applied about every other
watering during sunny conditions. During cloudy spells, plants need less
water and consequently less fertilizer. The sun acts as a natural regulator
— when plants are photosynthetically active and drawing water, they
simultaneously draw in the diluted nutrients.

This approach is fundamentally different from heavy chemical fertigation
used in conventional agriculture. Solomon's method uses modest
concentrations and relies on the plant's own water demand to regulate
nutrient uptake, preventing the accumulation of unused salts in the root
zone.

## The Muddy Slurry Transplant Technique

Solomon's key innovation is the "muddy slurry" method for setting out
transplants. The procedure involves several precise steps:

1. **Prepare the hole**: Dig the hole about twice the volume of the
   seedling's rootball and deep enough that when filled, the soil line
   falls just below the seedling's first two leaves. This ensures proper
   planting depth — too deep and the stem may rot, too shallow and roots
   may be exposed.

2. **Add amendments**: Blend complete organic fertilizer (COF) or compost
   into about a gallon (four liters) of soil removed from the hole.
   Concentrate the amendment in the lower portion of the backfill so that
   most nutrients sit below the seedling's root zone, encouraging downward
   root growth.

3. **Create the slurry**: Mix a bucketful of full-strength liquid
   fertilizer or compost/manure tea. Pour approximately one quart (one
   liter) into the planting hole, pouring gently enough to avoid washing
   soil away but rapidly enough to fill the hole before it soaks in.
   Timing is important — the slurry must still be liquid when the
   rootball is placed.

4. **Set the transplant**: Quickly push loose soil back in around the
   seedling's rootball while the liquid is still present. This creates a
   muddy slurry that settles into every nook and cranny of the rootball.
   Avoid breaking soil off the rootball during handling, as this exposes
   fine root hairs to air and damage.

5. **Result**: A tight connection forms between the roots and surrounding
   soil, eliminating air gaps and ensuring immediate nutrient and moisture
   access.

## Why This Method Works

The slurry technique addresses several problems that commonly plague
transplanted seedlings:

- **Air gaps**: When a rootball from a potting mix meets garden soil,
  textural differences often create air pockets. Roots cannot grow through
  dry air. The liquid slurry eliminates these gaps entirely by filling
  every void with a nutrient-bearing medium.

- **Moisture continuity**: The slurry ensures continuous moisture from the
  surrounding soil into the rootball. Without this bridge, the rootball
  can dry out even when surrounding soil is moist, because the different
  soil textures wick water at different rates.

- **Nutrient availability**: Fertigation places immediately available
  nutrients in the root zone at the moment the plant most needs them —
  during the stress of transplanting. The plant does not need to wait for
  COF or compost to break down; soluble nutrients are present from day
  one.

- **Reduced transplant shock**: Seedlings established this way do not need
  watering any more frequently than established garden plants, and they
  resume rapid growth immediately.

## Seedling Fertigation in Flats

Before transplanting, Solomon recommends fertigating seedlings growing in
flats. The protocol involves diluting any liquid fertilizer to one-third
strength and applying it approximately every other watering. This prevents
overfertilization, which is particularly dangerous for seedlings because
even slight fertilizer toxicity can prevent root growth.

A common mistake is to respond to slow growth by applying more fertilizer,
when the actual problem is fertilizer burn. Seedlings primarily need
fertilizer when the sun is shining and they are actively growing — during
cloudy spells, they need less water and therefore less fertilizer.

## COF-Based Liquid Fertilizer

For gardeners making their own liquid feed, Solomon suggests using complete
organic fertilizer (COF) as the base. COF provides a balanced NPK profile
along with calcium, magnesium, and trace minerals from ingredients
including seedmeal, lime, gypsum, rock phosphate, and kelp meal. When
dissolved or suspended in water, it creates a viable fertigation solution.

Commercial hydroponic fertilizers can also work, provided they include
calcium and magnesium alongside the primary NPK nutrients. However, Solomon
cautions that potting soil made with COF grows seedlings slowly and
steadily, which is actually preferable for producing stocky, resilient
transplants.

## Practical Tips

- Always transplant during cool conditions (early morning or evening) to
  reduce moisture stress while the root-soil connection forms.

- Have the fertigation slurry prepared before removing the seedling from
  its flat to minimize root exposure time.

- Handle rootballs gently to avoid breaking off soil and exposing fine
  roots to desiccation.

- For larger transplants (tomatoes, peppers, eggplant), increase the
  volume of liquid proportionally with the hole size.

- Do not attempt this technique with very cold soil — nutrient
  availability drops sharply in cold soils and the slurry may pool
  rather than absorb.
