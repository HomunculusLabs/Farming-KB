## Overview

Soil disturbance is one of the most significant threats to mycorrhizal fungal populations in agricultural and horticultural settings. Activities such as tilling, plowing, grazing, and construction physically destroy the delicate mycelial networks that connect plants to their fungal partners, reducing crop nutrient uptake and requiring long recovery periods.

Source: [[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]]
- Fall tilling is particularly destructive — damaged hyphae won't be available for cover crops or spring planting

### Plowing

Plowing shares many of tilling's destructive effects but can be more severe due to the greater soil turnover:
- Complete inversion of the soil profile
- Maximum disruption of existing mycelial networks
- Spores distributed throughout the plow depth, many beyond the reach of new roots

### Stock Grazing

Livestock grazing impacts mycorrhizal communities through:
- Physical trampling of soil surface
- Root damage from grazing
- Soil compaction from hooves
- Reduction of carbon supply when above-ground plant material is consumed

### Construction and Development

Construction activities destroy mycorrhizal communities through:
- Complete removal of topsoil (where most fungi reside)
- Soil compaction by heavy machinery
- Alteration of drainage patterns
- Introduction of non-native soils and contaminants

***

## Recovery Timeline

### The Two-Month Gap

If fungal hyphae are damaged or destroyed:
- They will not be available to partner with subsequent plantings
- Reestablishing arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi through natural recovery can take **up to two months**
- This recovery period is particularly problematic in regions with short growing seasons
- During the recovery gap, crops receive reduced nutrient and water uptake

### Reestablishment Challenges

Reestablishing mycorrhizal populations after disturbance is difficult because:
- No host plants are available to provide carbon during fallow periods
- Spores may have been buried too deep for effective root contact
- Soil conditions (temperature, moisture, chemistry) may be suboptimal
- Competing organisms may colonize the disturbed niche first

***

## Minimizing Disturbance

### Conservation Tillage

Reduced tillage or no-till agriculture is one of the most effective practices for preserving mycorrhizal populations:
- Maintains the integrity of the mycelial network
- Keeps spores in the effective root zone
- Preserves soil structure and aggregation
- Reduces erosion

### Strategic Timing

If tilling is necessary:
- Avoid fall tilling when possible — spring tilling gives mycorrhizae more time to recover
- Time tillage to minimize the gap between disturbance and new planting
- Inoculate immediately after disturbance to speed recovery

### Cover Cropping

Maintaining living roots in the soil through cover crops:
- Provides continuous carbon supply to mycorrhizal fungi
- Prevents complete collapse of the mycelial network
- Some cover crops (legumes, grasses) are strongly mycorrhizal
- See crop rotation planning
