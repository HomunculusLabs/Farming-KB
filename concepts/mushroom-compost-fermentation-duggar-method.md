# Mushroom Compost Fermentation Duggar Method

Source: Mushroom Growing (Benjamin Minge Duggar, 1915, Ch. V)

Benjamin Minge Duggar's 1915 treatise provides one of the most detailed early scientific accounts of mushroom compost preparation, establishing principles that remain foundational to modern Agaricus cultivation.

## The Essential Role of Stable Manure

There is no substitute known for stable manure as a compost for commercial mushroom production. The best results are obtained only when manure is hauled fresh from the stables and permitted to undergo a definite type of fermentation. Considerable bedding straw should be present, and good straw from various grains possesses distinct advantages over grasses, perhaps due to resistance to complete fermentation, yielding a highly porous substratum that maintains excellent physical condition in the beds.

Sawdust and shavings are increasingly used as bedding substitutes but do not render the manure unfit, though compost containing them is less ideal. Manure from veterinary hospitals or stables using disinfectants should be avoided. French growers consider manure from grain-fed animals bedded with rye straw the ideal.

## The Fermentation Process

### Principles
The essentials are adequate moisture at all times and sufficient turning (forking over) to effect equal ferment action and uniformity throughout, sometimes to force a temperature decline. The manure contains many soluble organic substances that invite vigorous mould fungi if not fermented. During proper fermentation, common mould fungi do not develop profusely; instead, bacterial and direct chemical action is facilitated. The end result is a stage of decomposition favoring mushroom spawn rather than competing mycelia.

### Procedure
1. **Piling**: Fresh stable manure thrown into piles not more than 4 feet high
2. **Initial watering**: Thoroughly wet throughout
3. **Turning**: Fork over 3-4 times or more, at intervals of 3-5 days
4. **Temperature**: Maintain 140-150 degrees F between the first three turnings
5. **Duration**: Properly fermented in about 3 weeks under ordinary conditions

### Key Warnings
- Small compost piles dry out quickly and "burning" is hard to avoid
- "Burning" = high temperature combined with rapid drying out
- Greatest burning occurs just beneath the surface
- The best fermentation occurs when temperature remains above 125 degrees F for several days
- Manure containing sawdust or shavings requires longer fermentation
- Longest fermentation needed when resin is present in shavings

## Signs of Complete Fermentation

- Practically all objectionable odor has disappeared
- Oleaginous "feel" to the compost
- Pliability of the material
- Uniform brown color of the straw
- Temperature declines to about 120-130 degrees F

## Quantity Requirements

- One full coal carload (~12-14 large wagon loads) suffices for about 3,000 square feet
- One regular two-horse manure wagon load covers about 225 square feet
- Average wagon body holds enough for about 100 square feet
- More straw means greater loss of bulk after composting

## The Quick Method (for home growers)

Where fermentation is not possible for small-scale home consumption:
1. Free fresh manure from long straw
2. Mix thoroughly with one-third light garden loam
3. Moisten if required, let stand 3-4 days
4. Spread in layers of about 2 inches deep, tamping each firmly
5. Build bed to 8 inches deep
6. Observe temperature and follow standard spawning directions

## Substitutes for Compost

Duggar tested decayed leaves, sawdust, and woods mould as alternatives to stable manure. None approached the value of stable manure. Even when equal quantities of manure were combined with sawdust or fermented leaves, mushroom size was reduced and yield lessened. Phosphates and nitrates improved sawdust and leaves but could not bring them to the level of stable manure. Forest species like A. silvicola and A. placomyces might be more amenable to such substrata.
