# [[williams-pdc-rain-water-harvesting]] Reedbeds

Bill Mollison discusses [[constructed-wetland-design]]s and reed beds for wastewater
treatment in Chapter 6 (Water in the Landscape) and Chapter 14 of
Permaculture: A Designers' Manual. He presents reedbeds as an elegant,
low-energy, and biologically based alternative to conventional sewage
systems that turns waste into a productive resource.

## Principles of Constructed Wetlands

Constructed wetlands (reedbeds) treat wastewater by passing it through
a planted substrate where physical, chemical, and biological processes
break down organic matter, nutrients, and pathogens. The treatment relies
on:

- Physical filtration: wastewater percolates through gravel or sand media,
  trapping suspended solids
- Microbial decomposition: bacteria and fungi in the root zone and
  substrate break down organic matter (BOD, COD) through aerobic and
  anaerobic processes
- Plant uptake: wetland plants absorb nitrogen, phosphorus, and other
  nutrients through their root systems, incorporating them into biomass
- Root-zone oxygen transfer: wetland plant roots release oxygen into the
  substrate, supporting aerobic microbial activity in the otherwise
  anoxic root zone
- Pathogen die-off: UV exposure, predation by soil organisms, and
  unfavourable environmental conditions reduce pathogen populations

Mollison emphasises that constructed wetlands are not simply "filtering"
wastewater -- they are living ecosystems that transform waste into plant
biomass, treated water, and soil nutrients.

## Horizontal Flow Reedbeds

Horizontal flow (HF) systems are the simplest and most common type:

- Wastewater enters one end of a shallow (0.3 to 0.6 metre deep),
  level-bottomed bed filled with gravel (10 to 30mm diameter)
- Water flows horizontally through the gravel, maintained below the
  surface to prevent odour and mosquito breeding
- Treatment occurs as water moves slowly through the root zone
- Typical retention time: 2 to 7 days depending on design loading
- The bed surface is planted with reeds and other wetland species
- Treated water exits at the far end through an overflow pipe set at
  the desired water level

Horizontal flow beds are excellent at removing organic matter (BOD) and
suspended solids. They are less efficient at nitrogen removal (which
requires alternating aerobic and anaerobic conditions) and phosphorus
removal (which requires specific substrate chemistry or plant uptake).

## Vertical Flow Reedbeds

Vertical flow (VF) systems offer enhanced treatment through intermittent
dosing:

- Wastewater is distributed across the surface of a bed (0.6 to 1.0
  metre deep) filled with sand or fine gravel
- Water percolates vertically down through the media, draining freely
  at the base
- The bed drains completely between doses, allowing air to refill the
  pore spaces and maintain aerobic conditions
- Dosing is typically intermittent: flood and drain cycles of several
  hours to a day
- The aerobic conditions in VF beds promote nitrification (ammonium to
  nitrate conversion), significantly improving nitrogen removal

VF beds achieve better nitrogen treatment than HF beds but require more
precise dosing and drainage design. Mollison notes that combining HF and
VF beds in series (VF first for nitrification, then HF for denitrification)
provides the most complete treatment.

## Plant Species Selection

Mollison identifies key wetland plant species for reedbed treatment:

- Common reed (Phragmites australis): The most widely used reedbed plant
  worldwide. Deep, extensive root system (rhizomes reach 1 to 2 metres
  deep). Extremely hardy, tolerates a wide range of water depths and
  nutrient levels. Transports oxygen to roots efficiently. Grows tall
  (1.5 to 3 metres), providing excellent habitat and biomass production.
- Cattail / Bulrush (Typha latifolia, Typha domingensis): Vigorous
  grower with dense root mats. Excellent nutrient uptake. Very tolerant
  of fluctuating water levels. Produces large biomass. Can become
  dominant and reduce biodiversity if not managed.
- Soft rush (Juncus effusus): Compact, clump-forming species suitable
  for smaller systems and the edges of larger beds. Good for ornamental
  treatment systems.
- Club rush / Bulrush (Scirpus lacustris, Scirpus validus): Strong
  nutrient uptake, suitable for deeper water zones.
- Iris (Iris pseudacorus): Yellow flag iris provides both treatment
  function and ornamental value. Suitable for the shallower edges of
  treatment beds.
- Water mint (Mentha aquatica): Useful edge plant with aromatic
  properties that may help mask odours.

Mollison recommends planting a diverse mix of species rather than
monocultures, as this provides more robust treatment performance, longer
growing seasons, and habitat value. Local native wetland species should
be prioritised over exotics.

## Sizing Guidelines

Mollison provides approximate sizing for household greywater reedbeds:

- For a household of 4 people producing approximately 300 to 500 litres
  of greywater per day (excluding toilet waste, which should be handled
  by [[composting-toilets]] or separate blackwater treatment):
  - HF reedbed: approximately 2 to 5 m2 per person, or 8 to 20 m2 total
    for a family of 4, at a depth of 0.5 metres
  - VF reedbed: approximately 1 to 2 m2 per person, smaller due to more
    efficient aerobic treatment
- Combined systems (HF + VF): each stage sized for its specific
  treatment function
- Commercial or community systems: sized based on daily flow volume
  and required treatment standard

The sizing depends on wastewater strength, climate (warmer climates
support faster biological treatment), and required effluent quality.

## Maintenance
