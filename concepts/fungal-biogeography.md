# Fungal Biogeography

## Overview

Fungal biogeography is the study of the distribution of fungal species and
communities across space and time. Long overshadowed by research on plant
and animal biogeography, fungal biogeography has undergone a revolution
since the 2010s thanks to molecular methods that can detect fungi directly
from environmental samples without relying on fruiting body observations.

These tools have revealed that fungal [[guzman-global-distribution-patterns-neurotropic-fungi]] are far more
complex and geographically structured than previously assumed.

## The Basidiomycota-Yunnan Hypothesis

An influential body of research suggests that the global center of
diversity for many fungal groups, particularly mushroom-forming
Basidiomycota, lies in Southeast Asia and the broader Yunnan-Guizhou
Plateau region of China.

This region harbors extraordinary numbers of ectomycorrhizal and
saprotrophic basidiomycete species, many of which are endemic. The
hypothesis draws parallels with recognized centers of plant diversity and
reflects the region's complex geological history, topographic
heterogeneity, and climatic stability.

## Latitudinal Diversity Gradients

Like most groups of organisms, [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity-patterns-distribution]] generally increases toward
the tropics. However, the pattern is more nuanced for fungi than for
plants or animals. While overall fungal OTU richness peaks in tropical and
subtropical regions, certain functional groups show contrasting patterns:

- [[query-what-are-ectomycorrhizal-fungi-and-which-plants-do-they-associate-with]] reach peak diversity in temperate and boreal
  forests where they associate with Pinaceae and Fagaceae.
- Some well-known mold species such as [[aspergillus-niger]] and
  Penicillium chrysogenum do appear genuinely cosmopolitan, likely due
  to their association with human environments.

## Dispersal Mechanisms

Fungi employ diverse strategies for geographic dispersal, which directly
shapes their biogeographic patterns:

- Spore-based dispersal: most fungi produce airborne spores that can
  travel hundreds or thousands of kilometers on wind currents. This
  gives many fungal species high dispersal potential.
- Animal-mediated dispersal: fungi associated with insects, mammals,
  and birds can hitch rides across large distances. Truffle-like fungi
  depend entirely on animal vectors for [[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]].
- Water dispersal: aquatic and riparian fungi use water movement to
  spread spores along waterways and coastlines.
- Human-mediated dispersal: global trade has moved many fungal species
  far beyond their natural ranges, sometimes with devastating
  ecological consequences.

## Island Biogeography

[[biodiversity-of-fungi-soil-fungal-communities-agriculture]] on islands follow patterns predicted by island
biogeography theory. Island size and isolation both influence fungal
diversity, with larger and less isolated islands harboring more species.

Oceanic islands frequently host endemic fungal species that have evolved
from colonizing ancestors. Island fungal communities are often dominated
by taxa with efficient long-distance dispersal mechanisms.

Volcanic islands provide natural laboratories for studying fungal
colonization and succession, as new substrates become available for
fungal establishment over known time periods.

## Climate Change Impacts

Shifting climate patterns are altering fungal distributions worldwide.
Warmer temperatures are enabling range expansions of tropical and
subtropical species into temperate zones. Drought stress in Mediterranean
and semi-arid regions is reshaping soil fungal communities.

Changes in plant communities under climate change cascade to affect their
associated mycorrhizal fungi. Permafrost thaw is releasing long-dormant
fungal propagules, with uncertain ecological consequences.

Warming winters are disrupting the fruiting patterns of temperate fungi,
with many species now fruiting earlier in autumn or producing extended
[[azurescens-fruiting-seasons-and-conditions]]. Some spring-fruiting species have shifted to autumn
fruiting entirely in response to changing temperature cues.

## Biogeographic Modeling

Advances in environmental DNA sampling and geographic information systems
have enabled the development of predictive models for fungal distribution.

Species distribution models (SDMs) combine occurrence data from
metabarcoding surveys with environmental variables to predict where
particular fungal taxa should occur. These models are increasingly used
to identify regions of high conservation priority and to forecast how
fungal communities may respond to environmental change.

## Endemism and Conservation

Fungal endemism is now recognized as far more common than previously
thought. Many species show restricted geographic ranges, particularly
ectomycorrhizal fungi associated with specific host trees and fungi
specialized on particular substrates.

Conservation implications are significant:

- Habitat destruction directly eliminates narrow-range fungal species
  that cannot disperse to new areas quickly enough
- Climate change threatens species at the edges of their ranges,
  particularly alpine and boreal fungi with nowhere cooler to migrate
- The IUCN Red List of Threatened Species now includes numerous fungal
  taxa, though fungi remain dramatically underrepresented compared to
  plants and animals
- Protected areas designed for plant and animal conservation may not
  adequately protect fungi with different habitat requirements

## Methods in Fungal Biogeography

Modern fungal biogeography relies on several complementary approaches:

- Metabarcoding of environmental DNA from soil, air, and water samples
  to detect fungal communities without cultivating or observing
  fruiting bodies
- High-throughput sequencing of herbarium specimens and fruiting body
  collections to build historical distribution baselines
- Comparative phylogeography using multi-gene sequencing to reconstruct
  the evolutionary history and migration routes of fungal lineages
- Citizen science platforms like iNaturalist and mushroom observer
  databases that aggregate fungal occurrence records globally
