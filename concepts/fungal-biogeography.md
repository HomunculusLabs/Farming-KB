# Fungal Biogeography

## Overview

Fungal biogeography is the study of the distribution of fungal species
and communities across space and time. Long overshadowed by research on
plant and animal biogeography, fungal biogeography has undergone a
revolution since the 2010s thanks to molecular methods that can detect
fungi directly from environmental samples without relying on fruiting
body observations. These tools have revealed that fungal distribution
patterns are far more complex and geographically structured than
previously assumed.

## The Basidiomycota-Yunnan Hypothesis

An influential body of research suggests that the global center of
diversity for many fungal groups, particularly mushroom-forming
Basidiomycota, lies in Southeast Asia and the broader Yunnan-Guizhou
Plateau region of China. This region harbors extraordinary numbers of
ectomycorrhizal and saprotrophic basidiomycete species, many of which
are endemic. The hypothesis draws parallels with recognized centers of
plant diversity and reflects the region's complex geological history,
topographic heterogeneity, and climatic stability.

## Latitudinal Diversity Gradients

Like most groups of organisms, fungal diversity generally increases
toward the tropics. However, the pattern is more nuanced for fungi than
for plants or animals. While overall fungal OTU richness peaks in
tropical and subtropical regions, certain functional groups show
contrasting patterns:

- **Ectomycorrhizal fungi** reach peak diversity in temperate and boreal
  forests where they associate with Pinaceae and Fagaceae.
- **[[aspergillus-niger]], Penicillium chrysogenum)
do appear genuinely cosmopolitan, likely due to their association with
human environments.

## Island Biogeography

Fungal communities on islands follow patterns predicted by island
biogeography theory. Island size and isolation both influence fungal
diversity, with larger and less isolated islands harboring more
species. Oceanic islands frequently host endemic fungal species that
have evolved from colonizing ancestors. Island fungal communities are
often dominated by taxa with efficient long-distance dispersal
mechanisms.

## Climate Change Impacts

Shifting climate patterns are altering fungal distributions worldwide.
Warmer temperatures are enabling range expansions of tropical and
subtropical species into temperate zones. Drought stress in Mediterranean
and semi-arid regions is reshaping soil fungal communities. Changes in
plant communities under climate change cascade to affect their associated
mycorrhizal fungi. Permafrost thaw is releasing long-dormant fungal
propagules, with uncertain ecological consequences.

## Biogeographic Modeling

Advances in environmental DNA sampling and geographic information systems
have enabled the development of predictive models for fungal
distribution. Species distribution models (SDMs) combine occurrence data
from metabarcoding surveys with environmental variables to predict where
particular fungal taxa should occur. These models are increasingly used
to identify regions of high conservation priority and to forecast how
fungal communities may respond to environmental change.
