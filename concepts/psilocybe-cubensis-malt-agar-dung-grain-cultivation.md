# Psilocybe Cubensis Cultivation on Malt Agar and Dung Grain Substrate

The Gartz, Adam, and Vorbrodt (1990) study employed two distinct cultivation
systems [[synthetic-log-cultivation-methods-guide]] represent established approaches
in [[annulus-loss-morphological-effect-brassinosteroid-treatment]] effects.

## Malt Agar Cultivation

Malt agar is one of the most widely used media for fungal culture in
mycological laboratories. The 6% concentration (6 grams of malt extract per
100 mL of water) used in this study represents a relatively rich medium,
providing ample carbohydrates, amino acids, minerals, and vitamins for
robust [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]].

Malt extract is produced by hydrolyzing barley malt, yielding a complex
mixture of maltose, glucose, dextrins, amino acids, and B vitamins. This
nutritional complexity supports vigorous growth of a wide range of fungal
species, making malt agar a versatile general-purpose medium. The 1.5-2%
agar concentration used for solidification provides a firm surface for
measuring mycelial extension rates.

In the Gartz study, malt agar was used specifically to measure mycelial
growth rate, as the flat transparent surface allows easy visualization and
measurement of colony diameter over time. The ten replicate runs per
treatment condition provided adequate statistical power to detect the
2-3 fold growth rate increase observed with [[suppressed-incomplete-fruit-body-formation-brassinosteroid-treatment]].

## Role of KH2PO4

The addition of 5 g of [[query-how-does-phosphorus-availability-change-across-soil-ph-levels]]. Phosphorus
is an essential macronutrient for fungal growth, required for ATP synthesis,
nucleic acid production, and phospholipid membrane formation.

KH2PO4 also functions as a pH buffer in the moderately acidic range (pH
4.5-5.5 when dissolved at this concentration), which is favorable for most
fungal species. By ensuring adequate phosphorus supply and stable pH, the
researchers eliminated potential nutrient limitations that could confound the
interpretation of brassinosteroid treatment effects.

## Dung/Grain Substrate for Fruiting

The horse dung/rice grain mixture used for fruiting body production reflects
the natural substrate preferences of Psilocybe cubensis. In nature, this
species is a coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungus, commonly found growing
on herbivore dung in tropical and subtropical grasslands.

Horse dung provides a complex organic substrate rich in partially digested
plant material, including cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin fragments, and
[[soil-microbial-biomass-measurement-ecological-significance]]. Rice grain serves as a nutrient-dense supplement,
providing readily available starch and protein that supports rapid
[[mycelial-colonization]]. The combination creates a substrate with both
immediate nutritional availability (from grain) and sustained nutrient
release (from dung).

The dung/grain mixture is prepared by combining the components with water
and typically pasteurizing or sterilizing before inoculation. In the Gartz
study, the substrate was supplemented with KH2PO4 and, in experimental
groups, with 10^-2 ppm brassinosteroid. The water content of the prepared
substrate is critical: too dry limits mycelial growth, while too wet creates
[[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] that favor bacterial contamination.

## Fruiting Induction and Flush Patterns

Psilocybe cubensis typically fruits in multiple flushes (harvests) from the
same substrate. In the Gartz study, five flushes were monitored during the
fruiting period. The flush pattern reflects the cyclical nature of fruiting
body production: a period of active fruiting followed by a rest period
during which the mycelium accumulates resources for the next flush.

In control cultures, the first flush appeared within 4-5 weeks of
inoculation. With brassinosteroid treatment, this was accelerated to 3-3.5
weeks. The first flush typically produces the largest and most numerous
fruit bodies, with subsequent flushes yielding progressively fewer and
smaller mushrooms as substrate nutrients are depleted.
