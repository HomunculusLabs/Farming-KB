# Pesticides and the Destruction of Ecological Chains

Masanobu Fukuoka provided a detailed account of how pesticide application in Japanese [[query-how-do-parasitoid-wasps-control-pest-populations]] in check.

## The First Application: Death of the Spiders

Fukuoka described the effects of pesticide application on a typical Japanese rice field.

Before the first spraying, the field teems with life — tens of thousands of young spiders inhabit each square yard of land.

These spiders are the primary natural predators of rice leafhoppers, the very pest the pesticide is intended to control.

The first application of pesticide does not kill the hordes of rice leafhoppers.

But the spiders vanish — every one of them.

The swarms of fireflies that rise from the stands of grass disappear at once.

In a single pass, the pesticide has eliminated the field's most effective [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]] kill off pollinating insects such as bees and gadflies.

Without these pollinators, the trees cannot reproduce naturally.

Humans must then collect pollen themselves and artificially pollinate each blossom — a labor-intensive process that replaces a free ecological service with expensive human labor.

Man cannot possibly replace all the myriad activities of plants, animals, and microorganisms in nature, Fukuoka argued.

Yet he goes out of his way to block their activities through pesticide application, then studies each lost function carefully and attempts to find substitutes.

This pattern — destroy a natural service, then invent an artificial replacement — is the central absurdity of pesticide-dependent agriculture.

## Why Pesticides Fail to Reduce Crop Damage

Fukuoka noted that hardly any agricultural scientists had investigated why the amount of insect damage in sprayed fields remains largely the same as in unsprayed fields.

Pesticides appear effective because they kill visible pests, but they simultaneously destroy the predators and parasites that would have controlled those pests in subsequent generations.
