## Chitin in Fungal Cell Walls

Chitin is a core structural component of fungal cell walls, where it is interlinked to
beta-1,3-glucans via a beta(1-4) linkage (Fontaine et al., 2000). The chitin-glucan
complex forms the fibrillar skeleton of the cell wall, providing rigidity and protection.
Because much of the nitrogen in fungi is contained within chitin, measurements of chitin
content have been used to estimate [[fungal-stable-isotope-ecology]] and nutrient flow pathways.

## Fungal Chitinase Systems

Fungi produce chitinases (EC 3.2.1.14) that hydrolyze the beta-1,4-glycosidic bonds in
chitin. These enzymes include endochitinases, which cleave internal bonds randomly, and
exochitinases including N-acetylglucosaminidases (beta-N-acetylglucosaminidase), which
release N-acetylglucosamine monomers from the non-reducing end. The enzyme
N-acetylglucosaminase (also called chitobiase) further processes oligosaccharide products.

Chitinase genes are induced by chitin-containing substrates and are regulated by complex
signaling pathways. The production of chitinases by soil fungi is an important component
of their [[fungal-competition-antagonism-soil]] applications.

In fungal-insect interactions, the production of chitin-degrading enzymes by
entomopathogenic fungi facilitates penetration of insect cuticles, which are composed
largely of chitin and protein. [[fungal-communities-extreme-environments]], chitinase-producing fungi may play disproportionately important roles in
nitrogen cycling because chitin represents one of the more readily degradable organic
nitrogen sources available.

# Fungal Succession on Carrion

The decomposition of animal carcasses (carrion) by fungi represents a specialized decomposition pathway that is ecologically distinct from the decomposition of plant litter and wood. Fungal communities on carrion undergo characteristic successional patterns driven by the changing chemical composition of the decomposing substrate, with different fungal functional groups dominating at different stages of decay. The study of fungal succession on carrion provides insight into fungal ecology, forensic science, and nutrient cycling in terrestrial ecosystems.

## Stages of Carrion Decomposition

Carrion decomposition proceeds through a well-characterized sequence of stages: fresh, bloat, active decay, advanced decay, and dry remains. Each stage presents a distinct chemical and physical environment that selects for different microbial decomposer communities. The fungal succession on carrion parallels these stages, with the composition of the fungal community shifting as the substrate changes.

During the fresh stage, the carrion is relatively intact and rich in readily available nutrients including simple sugars, amino acids, and other water-soluble compounds. Fungal colonization during this early stage is limited by competition with bacteria, which proliferate rapidly on the nutrient-rich substrate. The fungi that do colonize during this stage are typically opportunistic species with rapid growth rates.

The bloat stage is characterized by the accumulation of gases produced by anaerobic bacterial metabolism within the body cavity. The chemical environment becomes more complex as proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates begin to break down. Fungal colonization increases as the substrate becomes more heterogeneous and as bacterial activity begins to create conditions more favorable for fungal growth.

## Early Colonizers

The initial fungal colonizers of carrion are typically species of Mucor, Rhizopus, and other zygomycetes that can grow rapidly on nutrient-rich substrates. These fungi are often present as spores in the air and soil and can quickly colonize the carrion surface when conditions permit. Their rapid growth allows them to establish on the substrate before slower-growing but more competitively effective species arrive.

Yeasts, including species of Candida and Cryptococcus, are also common early colonizers. These unicellular fungi can exploit the soluble nutrient pool in the carrion fluids and may be transported to the carrion by insects that visit the carcass. The yeast communities on carrion can be diverse and may include species that are also associated with the insect vectors that transport them.

In addition to true carrion, fungi are also found in association with dead animal bodies including feces, as noted by Dighton (2003), who identified clear successions of mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi across a range of dead organic materials including animal remains.

## Mid-Successional Species

As decomposition progresses and the readily available nutrients are depleted, the fungal community shifts toward species with greater enzymatic capabilities. Ascomycete fungi, including species of Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Cladosporium, become more prominent during the active and advanced decay stages. These fungi are capable of degrading more complex substrates, including proteins, chitin, and keratin.

Keratinophilic fungi, which can degrade the structural protein keratin found in hair, feathers, skin, and nails, become increasingly important as the soft tissues decompose and the keratinous structures are exposed. Species of Chrysosporium, Microsporum, and related genera contribute to the degradation of these recalcitrant materials.

## Late-Successional Species

In the final stages of carrion decomposition, when the remaining substrate consists primarily of bone, keratinous structures, and highly decomposed organic matter, the fungal community is dominated by keratinophilic and cellulolytic species. These fungi continue the slow process of breaking down the remaining recalcitrant materials.

The fungal community on dry remains may include species that are more typical of soil and litter environments, reflecting the increasing similarity between the decomposed carrion and the surrounding soil organic matter. The boundary between carrion decomposition and soil organic matter turnover becomes increasingly blurred as decomposition approaches completion.

## Interactions with Insects

Insect activity plays a crucial role in fungal succession on carrion. Insects, particularly flies (Diptera) and beetles (Coleoptera), serve as vectors for fungal spore dispersal to the carrion. Many fungal species associated with carrion are also associated with the insects that colonize carcasses, and the insect and fungal successions on carrion are closely linked.

Fungi on carrion also interact with insects through trophic relationships. Fungal mycelium and spores serve as food for carrion-feeding insects and their larvae. Conversely, some fungi are pathogens of carrion insects, regulating insect populations on the decomposing substrate. These interactions create complex food web dynamics on carrion that influence the rate and trajectory of decomposition.
