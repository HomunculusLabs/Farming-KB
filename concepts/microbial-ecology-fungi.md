# Microbial Ecology of Fungi

Fungi occupy a central position in nearly all terrestrial and many aquatic
ecosystems. As principal decomposers, pathogens, and mutualistic symbionts,
they channel carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus through food webs and shape the
composition of microbial and plant communities. Their ecological roles are far
more diverse than those of any other group of eukaryotic microorganisms.

## Trophic Strategies

Fungi exploit three broad nutritional modes. **Saprotrophs** decompose dead
organic matter, secreting extracellular enzymes that break down lignin,
cellulose, chitin, and keratin. White-rot basidiomycetes are virtually the only
organisms capable of mineralizing lignin, making them indispensable to global
carbon cycling. Brown-rot fungi depolymerize cellulose while leaving lignin
partially modified as a brown residue.

**Biotrophs** obtain nutrients from living hosts. Obligate biotrophs, including
many rust and powdery mildew fungi, maintain living host tissue and cannot be
cultured axenically. Necrotrophs kill host cells before colonizing them, often
via phytotoxin production. Hemibiotrophs begin as biotrophs before switching to
a necrotrophic phase.

**Mutualists** engage in reciprocal exchanges with partner organisms.
Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi associate with forest trees, trading soil minerals
for plant-derived carbohydrates. Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, belonging
to Glomeromycota, form intracellular structures within root cortical cells and
associate with over 70% of land plant species. Endophytic fungi inhabit plant
tissues without causing disease and can confer drought tolerance, herbivore
resistance, and improved growth.

## Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling

Fungal decomposition follows recognizable succession patterns on coarse woody
debris. Soft-rot ascomycetes colonize fresh wood first, followed by
white-rot and brown-rot basidiomycetes as moisture content decreases. The
choice between white-rot and brown-rot pathways has major consequences for soil
carbon storage: white-rot fungi release more CO2 but leave less recalcitrant
residue, while brown-rot fungi generate polyphenolic residues that contribute
to humus formation.

In leaf litter, fungal hyphal networks fragment and chemically alter organic
matter, making it accessible to bacterial decomposers and soil fauna. This
"priming effect" accelerates overall decomposition rates and is particularly
important in boreal and temperate forests where leaf litter accumulates
seasonally.

## Fungal-Interorganism Interactions

Fungi interact with bacteria through physical contact, metabolic exchange, and
signaling molecules. The mycosphere, the zone of soil influenced by hyphae,
harbors distinct bacterial communities enriched in Burkholderia,
Pseudomonas, and Streptomyces species. Some of these bacteria produce
antifungal compounds, creating a competitive arena that shapes both fungal and
bacterial community composition.

Fungal-bacterial consortia degrade recalcitrant pollutants including
polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and explosive compounds more efficiently than
either group alone. Mycelial networks serve as highways for bacterial dispersal
and as platforms for biofilm formation.

Fungi also engage in combat with other fungi through mycoparasitism,
interference competition via antifungal metabolites, and resource
preemption. Trichoderma species are aggressive mycoparasites used as
biocontrol agents against plant-pathogenic fungi.

## Environmental Gradients and Community Assembly

Fungal community composition responds strongly to pH, moisture, temperature,
and substrate quality. Soil pH is often the single strongest predictor of
fungal community structure at continental scales. Ectomycorrhizal fungi
dominate in acidic forest soils, while arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and
saprotrophic ascomycetes are more prevalent in neutral-to-alkaline
grassland soils.

Global climate change is shifting fungal distributions. Warming temperatures
expand the range of thermotolerant species and accelerate decomposition rates
in cold biomes, potentially releasing stored soil carbon. Altered precipitation
patterns affect the seasonal dynamics of fungal fruiting and spore dispersal.

## Methods in Fungal Ecology

High-throughput sequencing of ITS (Internal Transcribed Spacer) regions has
revolutionized fungal community ecology, enabling detection of species that
cannot be cultured. Metatranscriptomics reveals which fungal genes are actively
expressed in situ, linking community composition to ecosystem function.
Stable isotope probing tracks the flow of carbon and nitrogen from substrates
into specific fungal taxa, illuminating their trophic roles.
