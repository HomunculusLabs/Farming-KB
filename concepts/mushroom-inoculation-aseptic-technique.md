All tools and surfaces should be cleaned and disinfected before beginning.
The work area should be as clean as possible, with drafts minimized. A
simple [[cardboard-box-inoculation-hood-draft-prevention-mycology]] hood is sufficient to prevent drafts and reduce
airborne contamination during the procedure.

## Flame Sterilization

When using syringes, the needle must be flame-sterilized before each
inoculation. The needle is heated in a flame until it glows red, then
allowed to cool for several seconds before use. A common technique is to
flame the needle and then draw a small amount of sterile air or water
through it to cool it before inserting it into the substrate jar.

When using agar squares, the scalpel used to cut and transfer the agar
pieces must be similarly flame-sterilized between each transfer. The
scalpel is heated until the blade glows red, allowed to cool, then used
to cut a small section from the colonized agar plate.

## Critical Safety Warning

The source document emphasizes a critical safety concern: alcohol and spray
Lysol are highly flammable. These disinfectants are commonly used to sanitize
work surfaces and the air in cultivation spaces, but they must never be used
near an open flame. Cultivators should complete all spraying and allow the
disinfectant to fully dissipate before flame-sterilizing needles or scalpels.
The combination of aerosolized alcohol and flame creates an immediate fire
and explosion hazard.

## Inoculation Procedure

The jar lid is opened just a crack -- not fully removed. The inoculant is
introduced quickly through the small opening. Minimizing the time the jar
is open reduces exposure to airborne contaminants. After inoculation, the
lid is closed and the jar is shaken vigorously to distribute the inoculant
throughout the substrate.

After shaking, the lid is loosened again. This is essential for gas exchange
during colonization. Mycelium requires oxygen and produces carbon dioxide.
A tight lid would create [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] that inhibit growth and
may promote bacterial contamination.

## Draft Prevention

Even a simple hood made from a cardboard box significantly reduces
contamination rates by preventing air currents from carrying spores of
competing molds and bacteria into the open jar. The hood does not need to
be elaborate or expensive -- any enclosure that blocks drafts is beneficial.

More sophisticated options include still air boxes (plastic containers with
arm holes) and laminar flow hoods (HEPA-filtered laminar airflow workstations).
These provide progressively higher levels of protection but are not strictly
necessary for successful cultivation, particularly when working with spore
or [[comparison-agar-vs-liquid-culture]] syringes that can be injected through a self-healing
injection port.

## Post-Inoculation Handling

After inoculation and shaking, jars are placed in a clean, draft-free
location for colonization. The incubation temperature affects colonization
speed significantly, with warmer temperatures (up to approximately 25°C)
producing faster growth. Temperatures above 27°C should be avoided as they
favor thermotolerant contaminants over the desired fungus.

Intermediate shaking during colonization dramatically speeds up the process.
When mycelium is visible growing in only a few spots within the jar,
shaking redistributes the colonized kernels throughout the substrate,
creating dozens of new growth points simultaneously. This technique can
reduce total colonization time by 50% or more.
