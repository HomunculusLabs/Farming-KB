Strigolactones are a class of carotenoid-derived terpenoid lactones
that function as both endogenous plant hormones and exogenous signaling
compounds in the rhizosphere. First identified as germination stimulants
for root parasitic plants of the genera Striga and Orobanche,
strigolactones were later recognized as the long-sought branching
inhibition signal that suppresses axillary bud outgrowth. They also
serve as critical [[hyphal-branching-regulation-nutrient-sensing-gadd]] factors for [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]]
(AM) fungi, mediating the establishment of one of the most ancient
and widespread plant-fungal symbioses.

## Biosynthesis

Strigolactone biosynthesis originates from all-trans-beta-carotene,
a C40 carotenoid produced in plastids. The pathway begins with
sequential isomerization by beta-carotene isomerase (D27) from all-
trans-beta-carotene to 9-cis-beta-carotene. This intermediate is
cleaved by carotenoid cleavage dioxygenase 7 (CCD7) and CCD8 to
produce carlactone, the central precursor of all strigolactones.

Carlactone is exported from plastids to the cytoplasm where it
undergoes oxidation by the cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP711A (MAX1 in
Arabidopsis). This oxidative conversion produces the diverse array of
strigolactone structures observed across the plant kingdom. The
structural diversity arises from modifications including hydroxylation,
oxidation, and further ring formation, generating compounds such as
5-deoxystrigol, orobanchol, strigol, and sorgolactone that vary
among plant species.

## Hormonal Function: Shoot Branching

The endogenous hormonal role of strigolactones centers on the
regulation of shoot architecture through suppression of axillary bud
outgrowth. In the absence of strigolactones, axillary buds grow
unchecked, producing highly branched phenotypes. Mutants deficient in
strigolactone biosynthesis (max1, max3, max4 in Arabidopsis; dad1,
dad2, dad3 in rice; rms1, rms2, rms5 in pea) exhibit excessive
branching, confirming the hormone's role as a negative regulator of
branching.

The strigolactone signaling pathway involves perception by the alpha/
beta-hydrolase receptor D14 (DWARF14), which undergoes an
irreversible conformational change upon strigolactone binding. This
activated receptor complex interacts with the F-box protein MAX2 to
form an SCF ubiquitin ligase complex that targets downstream
transcriptional repressors of the SMXL family (SUPPRESSOR OF MAX2
1-LIKE) for proteasomal degradation. Removal of SMXL repressors
derepresses genes that inhibit bud outgrowth.

Strigolactones interact antagonistically with auxin, which is both
upstream and downstream of strigolactone signaling. Auxin transported
basipetally in the stem promotes strigolactone biosynthesis in
roots and stem, while strigolactones inhibit auxin transport
canalization at bud sites. This feedback loop creates a self-
reinforcing system that maintains apical dominance and regulates
branching in response to environmental conditions.

## Symbiotic Function: Mycorrhizal Signaling

In the rhizosphere, strigolactones exuded by plant roots serve as
hyphal branching factors for [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]. At
extremely low concentrations (picomolar range), strigolactones
trigger extensive branching of fungal hyphae, increasing the
probability of contact with host roots and facilitating colonization.
This signaling function is ancestral, predating the hormonal role
in shoot branching, and is conserved across most land plant lineages.

The hyphal branching response is mediated by fungal receptor proteins
that perceive strigolactones and activate downstream calcium
signaling and mitochondrial activity in the fungus. The specificity
of this signaling is broad, with AM fungi generally responding to
strigolactones from diverse plant species, reflecting the ancient
and nonspecific nature of this symbiotic relationship. Under low
phosphate conditions, plants increase strigolactone biosynthesis
and exudation, enhancing mycorrhizal colonization and improving
[[comparison-phosphorus-solubilizing-microorganisms-vs-mycorrhizal-phosphorus-acquisition]].

## Role in Parasitic Plant Germination

The same strigolactones that signal beneficial mycorrhizal fungi
are exploited by root-parasitic plants of the Orobanchaceae family
as germination cues. Striga (witchweed) and Orobanche (broomrape)
species have evolved extremely sensitive strigolactone receptors
that detect trace quantities exuded by host roots. Upon perception,
parasitic seeds break dormancy and initiate germination, after
which the radicle attaches to the host root and forms a haustorial
connection to extract nutrients and water.

This parasitic exploitation represents one of the most significant
agricultural problems in sub-Saharan Africa and the Mediterranean
basin. Striga species cause estimated annual losses of over 7 billion
dollars in cereal production across Africa. The evolutionary arms
race between host plants and parasites has driven diversification of
strigolactone structures, with some plant species producing modified
strigolactones that are less stimulatory to parasites while
retaining mycorrhizal signaling activity.

## Regulation by Environmental Conditions

Strigolactone biosynthesis and signaling are tightly regulated by
[[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]], particularly phosphate and nitrogen. Low
phosphate conditions strongly upregulate strigolactone production,
redirecting resources from shoot growth to root exploration and
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-symbiosis]]. This response involves the transcriptional
regulator PHR1 (PHOSPHATE STARVATION RESPONSE 1), which activates
expression of strigolactone biosynthetic genes under phosphate
limitation.

Light quality and quantity also modulate strigolactone levels.
Shade conditions that reduce the red-to-far-red light ratio suppress
strigolactone biosynthesis, releasing axillary buds from inhibition
and promoting branching as an adaptive response to compete for light.
Drought stress increases strigolactone production, which may serve
dual roles in reducing water loss through reduced leaf area and
promoting root growth for water acquisition.
