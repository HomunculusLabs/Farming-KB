# Discovery of Psilocybin Mushrooms by Western Science

The scientific discovery of psilocybin-containing mushrooms is a story spanning nearly 500 years, from the first Spanish chronicles of Aztec "flesh of the gods" ceremonies to [[albert-hofmann]]'s isolation of [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] in a Swiss laboratory in 1958. It involves some of the most important figures in ethnobotany, chemistry, and psychiatry.

## The Spanish Chronicles (16th Century)

The first European accounts of [[powell-mayan-civilisation-and-psilocybin-mushroom-use]] come from Spanish missionaries and conquistadors who witnessed Aztec ceremonies:

- **Bernardino de Sahagún** (c. 1547–1569) described *teonanácatl* ("flesh of the gods" or "divine flesh") in his *Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva España*, recording that indigenous people consumed mushrooms in night-long ceremonies to commune with the gods and divine the future

- **Francisco Hernández** (1577), the royal physician to Philip II, documented the mushrooms and noted their intoxicating properties

- The Spanish Inquisition ultimately suppressed mushroom use, driving the ceremonies underground among indigenous communities in remote highland areas

For nearly 400 years after the conquest, teonanácatl was assumed to be a metaphor or a confused reference to peyote or other substances. The actual mushrooms were largely forgotten by Western science.

## Blas Pablo Reko and the Mexican Ethnobotanical Revival (1930s)

**Blas Pablo Reko**, an Austrian-Mexican anthropologist, was the first modern scholar to take the Spanish chronicles seriously. Reko:

- Argued that teonanácatl was a genuine psychoactive mushroom, not a metaphor
- Located indigenous communities still using visionary mushrooms in the Sierra Mazateca highlands of Oaxaca
- Identified two key plants: **piule seeds** (*Ipomoea violacea*, morning glory containing LSD-related indoles) and **teonanácatl** ([[psilocybin-mushrooms]])
- In 1937, sent samples to **Henry Wassen**, curator of the ethnographical museum in Gothenburg, Sweden — the first psilocybin mushroom specimens to reach scientific attention

## Richard Evans Schultes (1938–1939)

**[[richard-evans-schultes]]**, a young Harvard ethnobotanist, received Reko's forwarded samples and became the key figure in identifying teonanácatl:

- Published (with [[weston-la-barre]] of Yale) evidence that teonanácatl was a genuine psychoactive mushroom
- Traveled to Huatla de Jimenez in the Sierra Mazateca to collect specimens
- Forwarded collections to Harvard for identification
- His research was interrupted by WWII; he was sent to the Amazon for rubber research by the OSS (precursor to the CIA)
- Later became the "father of modern ethnobotany," spending decades documenting Amazonian psychoactive plants

## The First Velada (1938)

In 1938, **Jean Basset Johnson** (an anthropology student) and others became the **first non-indigenous people to attend a nightlong mushroom ceremony (velada)**. This was in the same Sierra Mazateca region where, decades later, R. Gordon Wasson and Albert Hofmann would have their own transformative experiences.

## Wasson and the Life Magazine Article (1957)

**R. Gordon Wasson**, the banker-turned-ethnomycologist who had previously studied *Amanita muscaria* in Siberia, learned of the [[stamets-psi-mazatec-mushroom-ceremonies-ethnobotany]] and traveled to Mexico:

- In 1955, Wasson and his wife [[valentina-pavlovna-wasson]] became the **first outsiders to participate in a velada** led by the Mazatec curandera **María Sabina**
- Wasson's account was published in **Life magazine on May 13, 1957** — "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" — bringing psilocybin mushrooms to worldwide attention for the first time
- The Life article, read by millions, triggered a wave of psychedelic tourism to Mexico
- Wasson's visit to María Sabina ultimately had devastating consequences for the Mazatec community, as it attracted unwanted attention from authorities and tourists

## Albert Hofmann and the Isolation of Psilocybin (1958)

**Albert Hofmann**, the Swiss chemist who had discovered LSD in 1943, received dried mushroom specimens from Wasson:

- Hofmann's team at Sandoz Laboratories in Basel identified and isolated the active compounds:
  - **Psilocybin** (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) — the stable prodrug
  - **Psilocin** (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) — the active metabolite, dephosphorylated in the body
- Both are **tryptamine derivatives** closely related to DMT and serotonin
- The isolation was completed in **1958** and published in *Experientia*
- Sandoz marketed psilocybin as **Indocybin** for psychiatric research

## María Sabina

**María Sabina** (c. 1894–1985), the Mazatec curandera (healer/shaman), became the most famous figure associated with psilocybin mushrooms:

- She was a *sabia* (wise woman) who conducted veladas for [[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]]
- Her chants (*cantos*) during ceremonies were considered the mechanism through which the mushroom's wisdom was expressed
- After Wasson's Life article, her village was flooded with counterculture seekers (including Bob Dylan, John Lennon, and Timothy Leary)
- The Mexican authorities eventually arrested and harassed her
- She reportedly regretted ever having shared the mushrooms with outsiders: "From the moment the foreigners arrived, the 'holy children' [mushrooms] lost their purity. They lost their force; they ruined them."

## Timothy Leary and Harvard (1960–1963)

**Timothy Leary**, a Harvard psychology professor, read Wasson's Life article and traveled to Mexico in 1960:

- Had a transformative experience with psilocybin mushrooms in Cuernavaca
- Returned to Harvard and founded the **[[leary-harvard-psilocybin-project]]** with Richard Alpert (Ram Dass)
- Conducted experiments including the **Concord Prison Experiment** (psilocybin given to inmates to reduce recidivism) and the **Good Friday Experiment** (psilocybin given to theology students in a chapel)
- Was fired from Harvard in 1963 for distributing psilocybin to students
- Became the most prominent public advocate for psychedelic drugs

## The Legacy

The discovery of psilocybin mushrooms by Western science is significant because:

1. It confirmed the **reality of the Spanish chronicles** — teonanácatl was a genuine psychoactive mushroom, not a metaphor
2. It established a **direct link between ancient religious practices and modern pharmacology**
3. It launched the modern **psychedelic research paradigm**, which continues today with clinical trials for depression, anxiety, and addiction
4. It raised profound **ethical questions** about cultural appropriation, intellectual property of indigenous knowledge, and the consequences of exposing traditional practices to global attention
5. It demonstrated that **psychoactive compounds could be precisely characterized chemically** while their subjective effects remained scientifically intractable — a tension that persists in psychedelic research
