## Anemotropism in Psilocybe Cubensis

Anemotropism — wind-directed growth — is a principal tropic response governing stipe
elongation in *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]* prior to sporulation. First documented systematically
by Badham (1982) using controlled wind-tunnel experiments, this response operates
alongside and then yields to negative geotropism as the basidiocarp matures. The
interplay between these two tropisms ensures that the mushroom positions its pileus
for effective [[spore]] release into moving air.

## Overview

Tropisms are growth responses in which an organism orients itself relative to an
environmental stimulus. *Psilocybe cubensis*, a coprophilous agaric, exhibits a
two-phase developmental program: an early anemotropic phase during stipe elongation,
followed by a negative geotropic phase once spore production begins. This dual
strategy was studied in wind-tunnel experiments where laminar airflow could be
precisely controlled and resulting directional changes in growth measured.

## Stage 1–2 Growth: Anemotropic Response

During initial basidiocarp development (stages 1–2), the stipe elongates directly
**into** the prevailing airflow. This anemotropic response is remarkably robust:

- It occurs in both **light and darkness**, indicating no photoreceptive mediation.
- It is observed in both **fertile and sterile** (sporeless) basidiocarps,
  confirming that spore production is not required for the wind response.
- The response operates **regardless of initial stipe orientation** — whether the
  stipe begins pointing up, down, or horizontally, elongation curves to align
  with the wind direction.

When a culture is rotated 90° so wind becomes perpendicular to the original growth
axis, the stipe redirects upward rather than into lateral airflow, suggesting the
anemotropic response integrates with an inherent vertical growth tendency.

## Stage 2+ Growth: Negative Geotropic Response

At the onset of spore formation (stage 2+), a second tropism emerges: **negative
geotropism**. The pileus reorients upward, away from the wind direction, and opens
with the lamellae (gills) facing downward. This transition is conditional on two
factors:

1. **Fertility**: Sterile, sporeless strains do *not* exhibit the negative geotropic
   turn. Only basidiocarps actively producing spores redirect their caps upward.
2. **Light**: The geotropic response is absent in continuous darkness. Light is
   required for both spore formation and the geotropic sensitivity that reorients
   the pileus.

The transition from wind-directed to gravity-directed growth marks a developmental
switch tied to reproductive maturation and environmental cues.

## Mechanism of Anemotropism

The physical mechanism underlying anemotropic bending in *P. cubensis* likely
involves **differential evaporative stress** across the stipe surface:

- A boundary layer of still air surrounds the stipe. On the **leeward side**
  (sheltered from wind), this boundary layer is thicker, reducing evaporative
  water loss.
- On the **windward side** (facing wind), the boundary layer is thinner or
  disrupted, increasing evaporative demand and causing cells to lose water more
  rapidly.
- The resulting **differential in cell turgor and growth rate** causes the stipe
  to bend toward the wind — the faster-growing, more hydrated leeward side
  elongates relative to the drier windward side.

This model parallels anemotropism in the zygomycete *Phycomyces blakesleeanus*,
where water vapor redistribution across the sporangiophore drives bending toward
airflow. In *P. cubensis*, the same boundary-layer asymmetry principle applies to
the multicellular stipe.

## Hormonal Regulation of the Tropic Switch

A key question is how the mushroom transitions from anemotropic to geotropic growth
at sporulation. Badham (1982) proposed that a **substance produced in the gills**
may act as a hormonal signal modifying the tropic response:

- As spores develop on gill surfaces, this hypothetical substance accumulates and
  diffuses into stipe tissue.
- The substance suppresses or overrides anemotropic sensitivity, shifting the
  dominant tropic response to gravity.
- **Sporeless mutants**, which lack functional gill tissue, never undergo geotropic
  reorientation — they remain locked in the anemotropic growth mode.

The identity of this gill-derived factor remains unknown, but analogous hormonal
tropic regulators have been proposed in other basidiomycetes.

## Role of Light

Light plays a multifaceted role in tropic behavior:

- **Basidiocarp initiation**: Light is required for initial fruiting body formation.
- **Spore formation**: Continuous darkness prevents spore production, blocking the
  transition to negative geotropism.
- **Geotropic sensitivity**: The geotropic response is suppressed without light,
  suggesting photoreception modulates the gravisensing apparatus.
- **Evaporative effects**: Light may influence tropism indirectly by heating the
  stipe surface, increasing evaporation and potentially amplifying the differential
  drying that drives anemotropic bending.

## Adaptive Significance

The dual tropic strategy suits *P. cubensis*'s niche as a **coprophagous and
lignicolous species** inhabiting open, exposed substrates where wind is constant:

- **Anemotropism** directs the elongating stipe into airflow, positioning the
  developing cap in a zone of maximum air exchange, enhancing evaporative cooling
  and gas exchange during rapid growth.
- **Negative geotropism** ensures the mature cap opens with lamellae facing
  downward — the optimal orientation for **basidiospore discharge and dispersal**
  by gravity and wind. Spores fall clear of the stipe and are entrained in air.
- The **partial veil** may shield the unexpanded stipe from excessive wind and
  light before the final cap readjustment.

For species in **unstable habitats** where substrates may be disturbed or buried,
rapid wind-directed growth followed by gravity-correct cap orientation maximizes
the window for successful spore release.

## Comparison with Other Fungal Tropism Studies

The anemotropic response in *P. cubensis* shares mechanistic features with tropisms
studied in other fungi:

| Organism | Tropism | Mechanism | Similarity to *P. cubensis* |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Phycomyces blakesleeanus* | Anemotropism | Boundary-layer asymmetry, differential evaporation | Nearly identical physical mechanism |
| *Pilobolus* spp. | Phototropism | Lens-like vesicle orients toward light | Light requirement shared; different stimulus |
| *Coprinopsis* spp. | Gravitropism | Statolith-like amyloplasts in stipe cells | Geotropic cap reorientation analogous |
| *[[flammulina-velutipes]]* | Gravitropism | Auxin-like growth regulators | Hormonal tropic switching concept shared |

The study of anemotropism in *P. cubensis* contributes to broader understanding of
how fungi integrate multiple environmental signals — wind, gravity, light, and
hydration — into a coordinated developmental program optimizing reproductive fitness.
