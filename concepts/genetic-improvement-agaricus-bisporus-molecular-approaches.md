# Genetic Improvement of [[agaricus-bisporus]]

## Molecular Approaches in Breeding the Cultivated Button Mushroom

Despite over three centuries of cultivation, strain improvement in *Agaricus
bisporus* has lagged behind other crops. The primary obstacle is the species'
unusual secondary homothallic sexuality: most basidia produce only two spores,
each containing two compatible nuclei, making the isolation of self-sterile
homokaryons extremely difficult. Unlike most basidiomycetes, *A. bisporus*
lacks clamp connections, so fertile heterokaryons and sterile homokaryons are
morphologically indistinguishable. These constraints historically limited
breeding to selection from spontaneous mutations. Modern molecular tools have
begun to overcome these barriers.

## Sexuality and Breeding Challenges

*A. bisporus* is classified as secondary homothallic. About 70% of spores are
binucleate and fertile (heterokaryotic), while only about 20% of basidia bear
three or four spores with single uninucleate spores that are self-sterile
homokaryons. The rare tetrasporic variety *A. bisporus* var. *burnettii*,
discovered in the Sonoran Desert of California, has 85% tetrasporic basidia,
greatly simplifying homokaryon isolation for breeding programs. The Agaricus
Resource Programme (ARP), founded by Rick Kerrigan in 1988, maintains over 206
strains identified using isozyme markers, providing a valuable genetic resource.

## Selection Methods

Three selection procedures have been used. Tissue culture from stem or cap is
the oldest method but has shown limited promise for strain improvement in *A.
bisporus*. Multispore culture (mass germination of spore prints) was used for
most commercial strains prior to hybrids but generates limited genetic
variability. Single-spore isolation is the most acceptable method: about 70% of
spores are fertile and can be screened for superior traits. However, its role
is considered short-term, with limited long-term genetic gains expected.

## Hybridization Programs

After the sexual cycle was elucidated by Miller (1971) and Elliott (1972),
hybridization became possible. It involves mating compatible self-sterile
homokaryotic lines through anastomosis. The first commercially successful hybrid
spawn was released in the Netherlands in 1981 (strains U1 and U3), combining
desirable qualities of off-white and pure white types. These hybrids are highly
productive with better size and have dominated commercial markets since. Other
notable hybrids include Amycel 208 (USA) and NCH-102 (India).

The breeding cycle involves: selecting parent fruit bodies with desired traits,
single-spore isolation by serial dilution and microscopic transfer, testing for
homokaryotic nature, crossing homokaryons in various combinations, identifying
hybrids, and grow-out testing for expression of desired characteristics.

## Molecular Markers for Breeding

### Isozyme (Allozyme) Markers

The first genetic test available to breeders was isozyme analysis. Mycelial
extracts are run on electrophoretic gels and stained with enzyme-specific
reagents. Homokaryons and heterokaryons show characteristic banding patterns,
and hybrids are recognized by novel combinations. More than a dozen isozyme
markers have been identified in *A. bisporus*.

### RFLP Markers

Restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis detects heritable
differences in DNA fragment lengths generated by restriction endonuclease
digestion. DNA fragments are separated by gel electrophoresis, transferred to
nitrocellulose membranes, and visualized with labeled DNA probes. RFLPs measure
genetic differences at the DNA level and have been used for both homokaryon
identification and hybrid confirmation. Limitations include high recurring costs
and requiring large quantities of pure DNA (5-10 micrograms).

### RAPD Markers

Randomly amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD) analysis amplifies random DNA
segments via PCR. RAPD requires smaller DNA quantities, is faster, and handles
multiple samples simultaneously, making it more user-friendly than RFLP.
However, RAPD is considered less robust: RFLP results are more consistent and
reproducible across laboratories. Both RAPD and RFLP are used to create DNA
fingerprints for patent applications, protecting proprietary strains.

### Auxotrophic and Resistance Markers

Auxotrophic mutants (with additional nutritional requirements) can identify
hybrids through complementation on minimal media, but are difficult to obtain
due to the multikaryotic nature masking single-gene mutations. Fungicide-
resistant mutants produced by UV mutagenesis allow hybrid identification by
dual-resistance selection on media containing two fungicides.

## Genetic Engineering

Genetic engineering (transformation) permits precise transfer of specific genes
within and between species. For *A. bisporus*, the primary vector is
*Agrobacterium tumefaciens*-mediated gene transfer, following the discovery in
1995 that this bacterium operates in yeast fungi. Gill tissue from mushrooms
with intact veils is cut into 2-5 mm pieces, vacuum-infiltrated with
*Agrobacterium* carrying an antibiotic (hygromycin)-resistant gene, and placed
on hygromycin-amended medium. Transformed cells grow into visible culture within
several weeks.

Alternative methods include electroporation and the ballistic gun system
(bombarding tissue with DNA-coated tungsten particles). Although transformation
has been successful in *Coprinus* and *Schizophyllum*, significant success in
*A. bisporus* remains limited. Genomic libraries have been constructed, and
desirable clonable genes from *A. bisporus* and other sources have been
identified for future incorporation. Three main target areas are: virus
resistance, improved shelf life, and improved compost utilization.

## Mutation Breeding and Protoplast Technology

Mutagenesis has created valuable variability: the original white strain of *A.
bisporus* arose from a spontaneous mutation in a cream population in 1927.
However, no comparable success to penicillin yield improvement in *Penicillium*
has been achieved through mutation cycles. Mutagenesis may have value primarily
for producing markers for hybrid identification.

Protoplasts (cells with digested cell walls) serve two purposes. First, when a
heterokaryon is protoplasted, some regenerated colonies possess single nuclei,
yielding homokaryons. Second, protoplasts allow DNA introduction without the
cell wall barrier, facilitating genetic engineering. Protoplast fusion using
polyethylene glycol and CaCl2, or electrofusion, enables creation of somatic
hybrids, though no significant improvement has yet been achieved in *A.
bisporus* through this method.

## Future Directions

The Amycel/Spawnmate biotechnological group has pioneered systematic breeding
through molecular techniques, specific wild traits, and backcrossing strategies
analogous to plant breeding. Future commercial spawns will increasingly use
genetic engineering, with novel strains protected through patents. The
dominance of off-white hybrid strains is expected to end as new technologies
enable exploitation of the full genetic potential of *A. bisporus*.
