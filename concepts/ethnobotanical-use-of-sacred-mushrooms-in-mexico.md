# Ethnobotanical Use of Sacred Mushrooms in Mexico

## Overview

The indigenous peoples of Mexico have used psychoactive mushrooms in
sacred and healing ceremonies for centuries, possibly millennia. The
ethnobotanical tradition surrounding these fungi is among the richest
and most complex of any psychoactive plant or fungus tradition in the
world, encompassing more than 200 documented common names and spanning
numerous ethnic groups across southern Mexico.

## The Name Teonanácatl

The most historically significant indigenous name for sacred mushrooms
is *teonanácatl*, meaning "divine mushroom" or "flesh of the gods" in
Nahuatl. This term was first recorded by the Spanish friar Bernardino
de Sahagún between 1569 and 1582 in his monumental *Historia General
de las Cosas de Nueva España*. Sahagún documented the use of
teonanácatl in Aztec ceremonies, providing the earliest European
account of [[powell-mayan-civilisation-and-psilocybin-mushroom-use]]. A related term, *teotlaquilnanácatl*
("divine fungus"), appears in other colonial-era sources.

## Wasson's Rediscovery

The modern rediscovery of Mexican mushroom ceremonies is credited to R.
Gordon Wasson, a vice president of J.P. Morgan and amateur mycologist.
In the 1950s, Wasson and his wife [[valentina-pavlovna-wasson]] traveled to
Mexico and participated in a nocturnal mushroom ceremony led by the
Mazatec curandera María Sabina in Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca. Wasson's
1957 *Life* magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" brought
global attention to these practices and launched the modern era of
psychedelic research.

## Indigenous Groups and Ceremonial Use

Multiple indigenous groups in southern Mexico maintain traditions of
ceremonial mushroom use, though practices vary between cultures:

### Mazatec
The Mazatec people of Oaxaca are the most well-known practitioners of
mushroom ceremonies. Their *veladas* (night vigils) are healing rituals
conducted by curanderos and curanderas who consume the mushrooms to
diagnose illness, locate lost objects, and communicate with the divine.
The Mazatec use several distinctive names for the mushrooms, including
*shi thó*, meaning "that which eruptions (of stars) come from."

### Chinantec, Mixtec, and Trique
Neighboring indigenous groups including the Chinantec, Mixtec, and
Trique peoples also maintain mushroom traditions, though these are less
extensively documented than Mazatec practices. Each group uses its own
distinct common names reflecting local cosmological frameworks.

## Common Names by Species

Guzmán (1997) documented more than 200 common names for psychoactive
mushrooms across Mexico's indigenous cultures. Several names are
strongly associated with particular species:

- *P. cubensis* — called *san isidros*, after Saint Isidore the
  Farmer, reflecting syncretism between indigenous and Catholic
  traditions.
- *P. mexicana* — called *pajaritos* ("little birds"), a poetic name
  evoking the light, fluttering sensation of the mushroom experience.
- *P. zapotecorum* — called *derrumbes* ("landslides"), referring
  to the destabilizing perceptual effects of the mushrooms.

Other notable names include *apipiltzin* ("kid" or "child") and
*atkad* ("mayor" or "elder"), suggesting the mushrooms were sometimes
personified in ceremonial contexts.

## Non-Psilocybe Species in Ceremonial Contexts

Indigenous Mexican mushroom traditions extend beyond *Psilocybe* to
include several other fungal genera used in ceremonial or medicinal
contexts:

### Cordyceps and Elaphomyces
At the Nevado de Toluca volcano, indigenous communities have
traditionally used species of *Cordyceps* and *Elaphomyces* (truffle-
like fungi) in ceremonies alongside or as substitutes for psilocybin
mushrooms. These fungi may have mild psychoactive properties or serve
symbolic functions.

### Ganoderma lobatum
In Chignahuapan, Puebla, a church was reportedly constructed using
timber colonized by *Ganoderma lobatum*, reflecting the cultural
significance of bracket fungi in the region.

### Puffballs
Various puffball species are known by the name "fallen stars" in some
[[sacred-mushroom-use-mexico-teonanacatl-indigenous-traditions]], linking their appearance to celestial events.

### Dictyophora indusiata
The striking stinkhorn fungus *Dictyophora indusiata* (the "veiled
lady mushroom" or "long net stinkhorn") has been used for divination
purposes in some Mexican indigenous communities.

## Five Historical Periods of Use

The history of sacred mushroom use in Mexico can be divided into
approximately five periods:

1. **Pre-Columbian** — Deep indigenous traditions of mushroom use in
   [[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]], documented archaeologically through mushroom
   stone artifacts and codices.
2. **Colonial (16th–19th centuries)** — Spanish suppression of
   indigenous practices drove mushroom ceremonies underground while
   friars like Sahagún documented them.
3. **Early Scientific (20th century)** — Initial ethnobotanical
   documentation by scholars including Schultes, Reko, and Singer.
4. **Wasson Era (1950s)** — Wasson's publicity brought global
   attention and sparked academic and scientific interest.
5. **Recreational Degeneration (1960s onward)** — The influx of
   Western counterculture seekers disrupted traditional practices,
   commercialized the ceremonies, and contributed to the deterioration
   of authentic indigenous mushroom traditions.

## Degeneration of Traditional Practices

The period following Wasson's publications saw a rapid deterioration
of traditional mushroom practices. The arrival of hippies and drug
tourists in Oaxaca during the 1960s transformed what had been sacred,
closely guarded healing rituals into commercialized experiences. María
Sabina herself expressed regret over her role in this cultural loss,
noting that the mushrooms had lost their power after being shared
with outsiders. The commodification of [[psilocybin-chemistry-stability-indigenous-knowledge]] continues
to be a significant ethical concern in ethnobotanical research.
