# Mushroom Formation Without Meristems: Hyphal Construction and Inflation

## Overview

Unlike plants and animals, which develop through the division of specialized cell
groups called meristems and stem cells, mushrooms form through a fundamentally
different process. [[natfarm-earthbag-construction-method]] is unique to fungi — nothing else in the biological
world develops in quite the same fashion — and it has profound implications for
how mycologists study mushroom development, tissue differentiation, and
regeneration.

## The Problem of Studying Fungal Development

In many organisms, developmental biology has been extraordinarily productive.
The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, for example, develops from a fertilized egg
into an adult with exactly 959 somatic cells. Because this number is small and
predictable, researchers have documented every cell division, creating a complete
anatomical blueprint that reveals the developmental origin of every cell type.
This has been tremendously useful for understanding organ formation, tissue
maturation, and nervous system development.

The same investigative approach has not been productive for mycologists. Unlike a
worm or a plant, in which numerous types of tissue are built from distinct kinds of
cells, fruiting bodies consist of nothing but tangled masses of identical cells.
There is no simple cell lineage to trace, no predictable sequence of divisions
that converts a [[hplc-analysis-psilocybin-psilocin-mushroom-tissue]] appears like
pavement made up of roughly spherical cells that flatten wherever they make contact.
An apple or a potato looks very similar under the microscope. But this resemblance
is merely superficial. Apples and potatoes grow by repeated division of specialized
groups of cells called meristems. Meristems situated at different positions within
a plant throw off cells that form the tissues of leaves, stems, roots, flowers, and
fruits. Each meristem produces a specific type of tissue, and the overall
architecture of the plant emerges from the coordinated activity of these
specialized growth centers.

Fungi do not have meristems. Instead, mushrooms are produced by the elongation and
branching of innumerable hyphae — the microscopic, threadlike filaments that
constitute the fungal body. The reason that the tissues of apples and mushrooms
look so similar under the microscope is that it is nearly impossible to cut along
a single hypha for any significant distance before passing through the other side
of the thread. The cross-sectional view inevitably shows circles and ellipses
rather than the elongated cylinders that the hyphae actually are.
