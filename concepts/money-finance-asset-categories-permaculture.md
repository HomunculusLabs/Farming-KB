## Money, Finance, and Asset Categories in Permaculture

[[bill-mollison]]'s treatment of money and finance in [[permaculture]] is fundamentally a critique of how modern societies confuse financial abstraction with real wealth, paired with a practical framework for understanding and organizing assets within bioregional economies. His analysis moves from the anthropological origins of exchange systems through the pathology of fiat currency to a categorized model of asset types that can guide community [[homestead-financial-planning]].

## From Social to Fiscal Accounting

In small, unified groups such as tribes, what is achieved by financial systems elsewhere is accomplished through **exchanges, gifts, obligations, and feasts**. In these societies, social accounting replaces fiscal accounting — everybody "owes" the others, and the web of mutual obligation creates both economic circulation and social cohesion. Many smaller villages operate similarly, using barter and informal exchange for most transactions while maintaining a modest financial component only for travel and external trade. Symbolic wealth such as cowrie shells may serve as trade intermediaries.

Only in highly mobile societies does abstract money begin to replace **fair dealing, objective value, and shared hospitality**. The intrinsically valueless tokens of currency — cheap strips of paper or lumps of metal — come to represent real goods and services, but the representation is always partial and prone to distortion. Even within fiscal societies, Mollison notes, barter remains highly developed; even multinational firms engage in formal barter, and local barter centers are evolving to distribute surplus goods for real or imagined needs.

## The Fading Faith in Fiscal Systems

Mollison observes that faith in the fiscal system — which he calls "an essential delusion if money is to maintain any barter value" — is fading as nation states and giant corporations fail to meet their debts, repudiating obligations or entering voluntary liquidation. In every case, the cost falls back on ordinary people. Large banks not only lose depositors' money but make depositors pay for the loss. Large companies receive public subsidies that "would make millionaires of paupers." The fiscal system, in Mollison's view, systematically transfers wealth from the productive to the parasitic.

Fiscal societies give a false impression of security that collapses roughly every 40 years when inflation — driven by greed — renders currency valueless. But Mollison extends this analysis to its ecological conclusion: the final "inflation" is the collapse of the environmental system itself. No amount of gold or diamonds can soften the blows that nature delivers, and in the final accounting, "a cabbage can be worth a king's castle (or more) if it saves your life." For the preceding 40 years, money has been made through the destruction of real wealth — soils and forests — and the debts are now being called in by nature herself.

## Money as Transport, Not Resource

Money itself is not a resource and has no intrinsic value or use. It is, Mollison argues, analogous to water in a landscape: it is an **agent of transport**, the shaper and mover of trade. The critical metric is not the total amount of money in a community but the number of uses or duties to which money can be diverted, and the number of cycles of use. Leakage from the community must be prevented and recycling made the rule — just as water retention and cycling are fundamental to [[holzer-permaculture-landscape-design]].

All money ultimately arises from the wealth of the natural world: plants, clean water, clear air, and stored energy. The accumulation of unused wealth, or wealth that does not lead to the proliferation of life, is a pollution comparable to any unused resource. "Manure and money have much in common," Mollison observes — both are only valuable when circulating through living systems.

## The Five Asset Categories

Mollison adapts and extends a framework from Turnbull (1975) to identify five categories of assets, each with distinct implications for community wealth and sustainability:

### 1. Degenerative Assets

Those assets that **decay, rust, or wear out**: buildings, roads, cars, furnishings, and appliances. Too many degenerative assets in any region will impoverish it in the long term because they require continuous expenditure on maintenance and replacement without generating new wealth. A society that invests primarily in degenerative assets is consuming its capital rather than building it.

### 2. Generative Assets

The **tools of society** — those things which manufacture or process raw materials into useful products: huskers, grinders, blenders, lathes, furnaces, and similar equipment. These do wear out but can be used to repair each other in workshops. All groups need some processing and repair tools; a wise farmer hires out or shares such tools rather than owning everything individually. Generative assets convert raw materials into higher-value products but still require degenerative inputs over time.

### 3. Procreative Assets

The **trees, wildlife, fish, invertebrates, mammals, and domestic livestock** of a region. People who maximize a procreative asset base can support the use of some tools and modest degenerative assets. Procreative assets are self-renewing — they literally create new wealth through biological reproduction and growth. People who maximize degenerative assets instead of procreative ones eventually fail to organize upkeep and repair, hence the prevalence of ruined castles and stately homes.

### 4. Informational Assets

**Information, education, and applied intelligence** — these make the best use of all other asset categories, decide balances in the asset base, assess future trends, and foresee needs and changes. Seeds have high information content (genetic instructions for growth), as do books, databases, and skilled practitioners. Informational assets are unique in that they can be shared without being diminished; a seed given away still reproduces, and knowledge taught to another still resides with the teacher.

### 5. Conservative Assets

**Insulation, dams, money recycling systems, good storage areas, and strategic forests** that guard against erosion or [[desertification]]. These conserve resources for future use and are essential to sustainable systems. Conservative assets do not generate new wealth but prevent the loss of existing wealth — they are the community's insurance against crisis and depletion.

## The Law of Return: Users Pay

Mollison articulates a powerful financial principle rooted in ecological law: **"everything must pay."** More specifically, in community enterprises, "proposers pay capital" and "users pay costs." In natural systems, this is the law of return — one cannot use soils, crops, or forests without costing total upkeep and replacement without impoverishing the commonwealth. The "users pay" principle should apply at every level of community except for hardship or welfare services.

This principle extends to tools and equipment: every major tool needs to be costed for running costs, repairs, replacement, and interest on capital. Tools on hire should completely repay their cost through piecework or hourly rates over a period realistically estimated at half their working life. This ensures that equipment investment is self-sustaining and that unused tools are expensive only for those who chose to acquire them — a lesson in modesty and responsibility.

## Ethical Financial Institutions

Mollison identifies the following types of financial institutions available to communities: credit unions, credit cooperatives, trusts and foundations, savings and loan associations, insurance agencies, finance companies and lending organizations, commercial banks, investment brokers, limited liability companies, trading companies, and cooperatives.

For any such institution to serve a community rather than exploit it, Mollison insists on two "foundation stones":

1. **An ethic** — expressed as a published, legally binding, and publicly known charter that guides all institutional decisions.
2. **Restriction to appropriate resource development and trade** — ensuring the institution operates within ecological and social bounds.

Without ethics or restrictions, any financial institution becomes "a danger and a weakness in a community." With sound ethics and resource usage restrictions, a financial institution prevents leakage of wealth and erosion of basic resources, becoming an asset to community that builds wealth for re-investment.

Critically, Mollison argues that ethical banking systems must **belong to and be governed by the community they serve**, and therefore not be open to distant or centralized control. Community ownership ensures that financial decisions reflect local needs and values rather than external profit motives.

## Regional Wealth Strategy

Mollison's framework implies a clear priority for regional financial planning: expenditure on procreative, informational, and conservative assets (categories 3-5) conserves and creates wealth, while over-investment in degenerative assets (category 1) consumes it. A community with abundant procreative assets can afford some degenerative ones, but a society that spends only on categories 1 and 2 will first pollute, then extinguish, its resource base.

Communities must also distinguish between **locally conserved resources** (the basis of regional wealth, such as soil) and **exportable surplus** (goods or services that can be traded for scarce external resources). Financial institutions should prevent leakage of essential resources while facilitating the export of true surplus — but never at the cost of irreplaceable resources like soil or humus.

## Design Considerations

Effective implementation requires careful site analysis including
sun patterns, water flow, existing vegetation, and soil type.
Zone planning places frequently-accessed elements closer to
the home. Observing natural patterns informs better design.
## Regional Adaptations

Principles must be adapted to local climate, altitude, and
growing season length. Mediterranean, tropical, temperate, and
arid regions each require different species selections, water
management strategies, and seasonal timing approaches.
## Economic Aspects

Cost-benefit analysis should account for long-term yields,
reduced input costs over time, and ecosystem services provided.
Initial establishment costs are offset by decreasing maintenance
as the system matures and self-regulates.

## Water Management

Water harvesting, storage, and distribution form critical
infrastructure in any land management system. Swales, ponds,
and keyline design capture and retain rainfall. Greywater
recycling reduces demand on freshwater sources.

## Soil Building Strategies

Building soil organic matter through composting, mulching, cover
cropping, and reduced tillage improves structure, water retention,
and nutrient availability. Biological soil management prioritizes
feeding soil organisms over direct plant fertilization.
## Implementation Notes

Practical implementation benefits from starting small and scaling
based on observed results. Document conditions, inputs, and
outcomes systematically. Connect with experienced practitioners
in your bioregion for locally-adapted knowledge and support.
