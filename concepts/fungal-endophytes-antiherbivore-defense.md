## Overview

[[fungal-endophytes]] are fungi that grow within plant tissues for a period and then egress to sporulate, generally causing no apparent damage to their host. They represent a model system demonstrating how fungi can have important cascading effects upward through entire communities by virtue of their interactions with grazing animals. The most extensively studied endophyte-plant interactions involve members of the Clavicepitales and grasses, where endophyte presence in leaves reduces insect herbivory, grazing by ungulates, and oviposition by insects.

## Definition and Classification

Clay (1990) defines fungal endophytes as those that grow within a plant for a period and then egress to sporulate. In general, these fungi cause no apparent damage to their host plant. Clay (1997) states that fungal endophytes are a model system showing how fungi can have important effects upward through the whole community by virtue of their interactions with grazing animals.

The relationship between endophytes and host plants exists along a continuum between herbivory defense and pathogenicity, which strongly influences the fitness of the host plant. Clay (1990) described this continuum with six possible fitness relationships:

- **Pathogenic**: Endophyte provides no defense against herbivory
- **Conditionally mutualistic**: Endophyte gives some defense against herbivory, or is pathogenic in absence of herbivory
- **Unconditionally mutualistic**: Herbivore is detrimental, or mutualism is strongest when herbivory is present

Where A = uninfected plant with no herbivory, B = endophyte infection with no herbivory, and C = endophyte infection with herbivory, the six orderings (A.B.C through C.B.A) describe a full spectrum from pathogenicity to strong mutualism.

## Antiherbivore Effects in Grasses

### Reduction of Insect Herbivory

The presence of [[endophytic-fungi]] in grass leaves has been documented to reduce the incidence of insect herbivory across a wide range of herbivore species and host plants (Table 5.11 in source). Documented effects include:

- **Avoidance**: Rhopalosiphum padi on Festuca
- **Reduced survival and development rate**: Spodoptera frugiperda on Cenchrus, and S. eridania on multiple genera (Cyperus, Danthonia, Festuca, Glyceria, Lolium, Panicum, Paspalum, Stipa, Tridens)
- **Reduced feeding and oviposition**: Sphenophorus parvulus and Blissus leucopterus on Lolium and Festuca
- **Reduced survival**: Acheta domesticus on Lolium, Agrotis segetum on Dactylus, Tribolium castaneum on Festuca and Lolium
- **Reduced feeding and oviposition**: Crambus spp. and Listronotus bonariensis on Lolium

### Reduction of Ungulate Grazing

Endophyte-infected grasses also reduce grazing by larger herbivores. The mechanisms include altered nutrient content of the host plant and production of secondary metabolites that act as deterrents.

## Alkaloid Production and Defense Mechanisms

### Secondary Metabolites as Deterrents

Endophytic fungi alter both the nutrient content of the host plant and the production of secondary metabolites that act as deterrents to animals. Species of the genus Baccharis, for example, produce high concentrations of macrocyclic tricothecenes that are toxic to cattle (Jarvis et al., 1987).

Richardson (2000) discusses the importance of alkaloid production by endophytic fungi as the causal agent of herbivore avoidance. These alkaloids are secondary metabolites of the fungi and are considered to have evolved as an adaptation to confer competitive advantage to the endophyte-infected host plant.

### Environmental Stability of Defense

The degree of protection afforded to a host plant by an endophyte does not appear to change under changing environmental conditions. Marks and Lincoln (1996) studied the influence of elevated carbon dioxide on the rate of grazing of grasses by the fall armyworm Spodoptera frugiperda. They observed an increase in grazing intensity with elevated CO2, but the proportion of plant consumed by the insects was similar to that of plants grown in ambient conditions. This suggests the endophyte-mediated defense is robust across environmental gradients.

## Transmission Strategies

### Vertical Transmission

In grasses, the close association between plant and endophyte has evolved to the point where the two partners are inseparable. Examples include the fungi Acremonium lolii and A. coenophialum, associated with ryegrass and tall fescue, respectively. Wilson (1993) suggests that the improved fitness from alkaloid production by the endophyte has led to vertical transmission of the endophyte from plant to plant via the seed. In this way, all new plants start life with the endophyte and its advantages.

### Horizontal Transmission

In contrast, Wilson suggests that endophytes of many other plants, such as the fungus Discula quercina of oak, are transmitted horizontally via rain splash from tree to tree. The chances of a plant becoming colonized by the appropriate fungus depend upon environmental conditions, resulting in a mosaic of both colonized and uncolonized plants. The resulting community depends upon the competitive and fitness advantages conferred on the plant by the endophytic fungi.

## Effects on Vegetative Growth and Reproduction

In addition to reducing herbivore impacts, fungal endophytes in the Clavicepitales alter vegetative growth and reproduction of the host plant. Clay (1990) documented these effects across multiple endophyte genera:

| Endophyte Genus | Host Species | Vegetative Growth | Reproduction | Herbivore Resistance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acremonium | 100 | Increase | No effect | Yes |
| Atkinsoniella | 4 | Increase | Suppressed | Yes |
| Balansia | 100 | Increase and decrease | Suppressed | Yes |
| Epichloe | 100 | Increase | Suppressed | Yes |
| Myriogenospora | 20 | Decrease | Suppressed | Yes |

The trade-off between increased growth and suppressed reproduction is notable — endophyte-infected plants may grow more vigorously but allocate fewer resources to seed production, or vice versa.

## Seed Protection and Fitness Enhancement

### Seed Colonization by Endophytes

Endophytes infect seeds where the pericarp is missing or damaged, acting as an antagonist against pathogenic fungi. Fitness of the host plant is enhanced by endophyte presence in seeds. Clay (1990) cites examples where grass seeds were rendered ineffective in germination due to insect damage in the absence of endophytic fungi. Where the endophyte was present, germination levels were normal.

### Implications for Measuring Plant Success

The degree of endophyte development within the host plant and proportion of seeds infected can be an important aspect of actual fecundity and competitive ability. This may be overlooked if plant success is measured only in terms of seed production and not seed germination potential.

## Evolutionary Dynamics

Clay (1997) showed that endophyte-infected plants outperformed uninfected plants in the presence of herbivory and suggested that the evolutionary trajectory of increasing endophyte infection of plants is associated with the level of herbivory. Lane et al. (2000) demonstrated that infection of grasses with asexual Neotyphodium endophyte exerts natural selection in favor of the host plant, increasing its fitness in competition with other plant species due to antiherbivore and plant-growth-promoting factors.

## Potential for Biological Pest Control

The use of fungal endophytes for pest management is emerging as a new science. Prestidge and Ball (1997) cite evidence of the adverse effects of Acremonium-infected tall fescue on:

- 12 species of beetle
- 2 species of flies
- 16 species of Hemiptera and Homoptera
- 8 species of lepidopteran larvae

This broad-spectrum effectiveness suggests endophytes could serve as important biological control agents of agricultural pests, reducing reliance on chemical pesticides.

## Physiological Effects Beyond Defense

Richardson (2000) postulated that alkaloid production may have additional properties influencing plant fitness beyond herbivore defense. He suggested that in other fungal-plant interactions, secondary metabolites can influence host plant physiology. The presence of endophytic fungi in host plants increases the concentration of apoplastic carbohydrates in leaves, allowing a greater supply of energy to be made available to the fungus. These fungi are also capable of altering the nitrogen balance within the host plant in favor of making more nitrogen available to the fungus.

This evidence supports the idea that mutualistic associations (mycorrhizae and endophytes) may exist in the middle of a continuum of fungal physiologies, ranging from saprotrophy at one end to pathogenicity at the other.
