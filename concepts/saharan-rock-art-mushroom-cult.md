# Saharan Rock Art and the Oldest Known Mushroom Cult

The Tassili Plateau in southern Algeria preserves some of the oldest evidence for human use of psychoactive mushrooms anywhere in the world. Rock paintings dating back 7,000 to 10,000 years depict anthropomorphic figures holding mushrooms, engaged in ecstatic dance, and wearing mushroom-shaped headdresses -- providing compelling evidence for what may be the oldest known mushroom cult in human history.

## The Tassili Discovery

### The Researchers

During the late 1980s, Italian mycologist G. Samorini and Terence McKenna, working independently, found evidence for the oldest known mushroom cult in Africa. Their discoveries were described as "not just sensational, but most surprising as well," though as Gartz notes, it should not come as a surprise that the oldest traces of human contact with mushrooms were found on the African continent -- the cradle of humanity.

### Dating and Context

The rock drawings date from as far back as 10,000 B.C. up to the present. Those from the Stone Age (7,000-5,000 B.C.) are described as typical of the so-called "round head phase." They include pictures of:

- Pasture animals (cattle, sheep, goats)
- Evergreen and deciduous trees
- Mythical beings with anthropomorphic and zoomorphic attributes
- Deities with masks and horns holding mushrooms in their hands
- Mushrooms shown attached directly to body parts
- Images of anthropomorphic beings with mushroom-like heads

### The Green Sahara

Between 9,000 and 7,000 years ago, the area between Tassili (southern Algeria), Acacus (Libya), and Ennedi (Chad) was populated by human beings who created magnificent rock drawings. This was a time when the Sahara was still a "blooming garden" -- a fertile landscape with abundant vegetation, water, and wildlife. No one then suspected that erosion and desolation processes, starting around 3,500 B.C., would turn the area into a desert hostile to human life.

## Key Rock Art Images

### The Mushroom Dance (Tin-Tazarift)

Among the most striking renditions at Tin-Tazarift, Tassili District (Algeria) is a picture titled "Anthropomorphic Beings Engaged in Mushroom Dance." This figure includes:

- Masked anthropomorphic beings engaged in ecstatic dancing
- Several dashed lines connecting the mushroom with the center of the head
- The lines represent "a flow of energy, maybe even the mushrooms' influence on the human soul"
- The picture is clearly indicative of psychotropic mushroom use

This depiction is remarkable because it shows that as early as 9,000-7,000 years ago, the head was apparently considered the seat of consciousness. By contrast, four or five millennia later during the European classical era, the brain's role was merely thought to be similar to that of a kind of cooler.

### Mushroom Shapes

The rock drawings consistently show two kinds of mushroom shapes:

1. **Liberty cap type:** Caps drawn with an acute umbo on top, resembling [[psilocybe-semilanceata]]
2. **Large mushroom type:** Larger mushrooms with a habitus much like that of the [[amanita-muscaria-fly-agaric-history-and-pharmacology]] or Stropharia species

### Color Symbolism

Despite their age, the rock drawings' colors have retained brilliant hues. Mushrooms were drawn in:

- **White**
- **Several shades of ochre**
- **Blue** (rare, but possibly a representation of the bluing phenomenon characteristic of psychoactive Psilocybe and Panaeolus species)

The blue color representations are particularly significant, as the bluing reaction is a unique identifying feature of psilocybin-containing mushrooms.

### Fish Motif

Other rock drawings depict mushrooms as being mythologically linked with fish. This connection may relate to the aquatic habitats where some psychoactive species grow, or to symbolic associations between water, fertility, and psychoactive experience.

## Potential Mushroom Species

The psychoactive mushrooms that could have grown in the Sahara 7,000-10,000 years ago include:

- **Psilocybe cubensis relatives** -- Dung-inhabiting species that would have thrived near pastoral animals
- **Panaeolus cyanescens relatives** -- Another dung-inhabiting species
- **Psilocybe semilanceata** -- A nitrophilic species that grows in grasslands enriched by animal waste
- **Psilocybe cyanescens relatives** -- Species growing on raw compost and plant debris

These mushrooms could have grown on several substrates available in the ancient Sahara:

- Fallen twigs and raw compost
- Ground litter from evergreen and deciduous trees
- Dung left behind by pasture animals (cattle, sheep, goats)

## Modern Evidence: African Psychoactive Mushrooms

### Algeria

On October 24, 1912, R. Maire first collected several specimens of bluing, dark-spored mushrooms growing on raw compost underneath cedar trees at Chrea Pass near Blida, south of Algiers. He collected additional specimens every year until 1926, publishing his findings in 1928 as Hypholoma cyanescens. Later classifications placed this as Psilocybe mairei, which Krieglsteiner considered identical to [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]].

### Morocco

G. Malencon classified similar specimens from the Central Atlas Mountains as belonging to the same species as Maire's Algerian collections.

### Kenya

In 1945, E.R. Cullinan and D. Henry described 22 cases of intoxication in Nairobi, where a yellow Stropharia species was mistaken for a culinary mushroom. The symptoms started one hour after ingestion, peaked within three hours, and persisted for 24-48 hours. Symptoms included:

- Emotional imbalance
- Fits of mirthful and irresponsible laughter alternating with depressive moods
- Nightmarish closed-eye imagery
- Patients remained conscious with rational speech throughout

### South Africa

In January 1994, Gartz and M. Smith discovered a bluing Psilocybe species in Natal Province -- the first psychotropic Psilocybe species ever found in the area. The species was generally whitish, had no annular ring, was comparable in size to Psilocybe cubensis, but did not grow directly on dung. It was published as Psilocybe natalensis.

### Panaeolus Species

Ola'h's monograph mentions two strongly psychoactive African Panaeolus species:

- Panaeolus africanus Ola'h
- Panaeolus tropicales Ola'h

## The Significance

The Tassili rock art represents the earliest known visual evidence for the ritual use of psychoactive mushrooms. The sophistication of the imagery -- showing specific mushroom shapes, depicting the "flow of energy" from mushroom to head, and portraying communal ecstatic dance -- suggests a well-developed cultic practice rather than casual or accidental use.

This finding supports the hypothesis that psychoactive mushroom use may be as old as human consciousness itself, originating in Africa alongside the emergence of modern humans and spreading with human migration across all continents.
