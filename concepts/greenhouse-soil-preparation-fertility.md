## Greenhouse Soil Preparation and Fertility

## Overview

Successful winter harvest greenhouse production depends fundamentally on creating and maintaining exceptionally high-quality soil. Unlike commercial greenhouse operations that rely on hydroponics and artificial media, the [[eliot-coleman]] approach emphasizes building a living, mineral-rich soil connected to the earth — essentially a potting-soil-quality growing medium that remains part of the larger soil ecosystem. This page covers the complete cycle of greenhouse soil management: initial preparation, ongoing fertility maintenance, specialized tools and techniques, and the compost supply system that sustains year-round production.

## Creating Anthropogenic Soil from Poor Land

The foundation of this soil system began with some of the most challenging raw material imaginable. The original farmland in coastal Maine was covered in spruce and fir forest, with a sandy acid podzol soil at pH 4.3 and glacial stones scattered throughout. A podzol is a soil scientist's term for the heavily leached, nutrient-poor soils typical of coniferous forest regions — characterized by a dark organic surface layer overlying a bleached, silica-rich horizon with little available fertility.

Through decades of effort, enough stones were removed and sufficient fertility was created to transform approximately one and one-half acres of this material into productive market-garden loam. When asked about soil type, Coleman describes it as "anthropogenic" — soil made by human beings. This concept of intentionally creating soil from unpromising raw material draws direct kinship with the historical Parisian maraîchers described by Kropotkin, who similarly "made the soil themselves." The lesson is clear: poor land is not a permanent barrier to intensive production, provided the grower is willing to invest in soil building as a foundational, long-term practice.

## Initial Greenhouse Soil Preparation

When serious greenhouse growing began, the goal was to create the most ideal soil possible — a medium approaching potting-soil quality but retaining the critical advantages of being connected to the living earth. These advantages include dependable moisture supply from deeper soil layers, access for earthworms and other soil organisms, increased rooting depth, and the biological buffering capacity that comes from being part of a larger soil ecosystem.

The preparation process starts with spreading a 2-inch layer of peat moss over the existing soil surface, along with sufficient ground limestone to neutralize the peat's natural acidity. This is then tilled in to full tiller-tine depth. Peat moss contributes relatively little immediately available plant food, but it provides abundant slow-to-decompose fibrous organic matter that dramatically improves soil structure. Good structure — meaning optimal aeration and biological activity — is the most critical quality for greenhouse soil, where the semi-artificial growing environment makes soil health even more important than in open fields.

The limestone serves the dual purpose of raising the pH toward the neutral range preferred by most vegetable crops and supplying calcium, which is essential for cell wall integrity and disease resistance. The exact amount of limestone needed depends on the starting soil pH and the buffering capacity of the native soil, so a soil test is valuable before application.

Once this structural foundation is established, all subsequent amendments — compost, rock powders, dried seaweed, alfalfa meal — can function at full effectiveness. The peat moss acts as a physical matrix that holds these inputs in the root zone and makes them accessible to soil biology. The distinction from hydroponic or container-based systems is critical: this enriched soil remains connected to the subsoil, allowing crops to draw on deeper moisture reserves and interact with a far more diverse soil ecosystem than any artificial growing medium can provide.

## Maintaining Fertility with Rock Powders and Seaweeds

### Basic Mineral Amendments

The initial mineral foundation mirrors the standard organic farming program: limestone for pH adjustment and calcium, phosphate rock for phosphorus, and greensand for potassium and trace minerals. These are discussed extensively in *The New Organic Grower* and provide the baseline fertility upon which all other inputs build.

### Deeper Organic Farming

For truly consistent, year-in, year-out results, going beyond these basics is essential. The approach Coleman calls "deeper organic farming" involves paying close attention to specific soil mineral deficiencies identified through soil tests, careful observation, and controlled trials. The goal is to optimize every possible factor affecting crop quality in order to produce the most nutritious and pest-resistant food possible. This is not merely about meeting minimum fertility thresholds — it is about actively pursuing nutritional excellence through precise mineral management.

### Boron as a Key Supplement

For sandy acid podzol soils, boron has proven to be a critical supplemental mineral. Extensive reading, soil testing, and field trials all confirmed that additional boron addresses a specific deficiency in this soil type. Boron is essential for cell wall formation, pollen viability, and calcium uptake — deficiencies can manifest as hollow stems in brassicas, poor fruit set, and general crop weakness. The identification of boron as the limiting micronutrient for this particular soil illustrates the importance of moving beyond generic organic amendment programs toward site-specific mineral management.

## Alfalfa Meal as Standard Fertilizer

Alfalfa meal serves as the standard between-crop fertilizer in the greenhouse rotation. Its appeal rests on two key qualities. First, it works effectively as a balanced, moderate-nitrogen amendment suitable for a wide range of crops. Second, it is an amendment that could theoretically be produced on-farm if necessary, supporting the principle of farm self-sufficiency. Alfalfa meal provides nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and trace minerals along with triacontanol, a natural growth stimulant found in alfalfa leaf wax.

### The Case for Low-Test Amendments

In most cases and for most crops, a "low test" amendment like alfalfa meal is preferable to high-analysis fertilizers such as dried blood. The greenhouse environment makes it easy to push crops too hard with excessive nitrogen, leading to overly lush, pest-susceptible growth and potential nutrient imbalances. Moderate, slow-release amendments like alfalfa meal provide steady, balanced nutrition that supports healthy rather than forced growth. This principle of restraint in fertilization is central to the overall philosophy of sustainable greenhouse production.

### Additional Nitrogen for Heavy-Feeding Crops

Certain crops place exceptional demands on soil nitrogen reserves. Spinach and claytonia, both popular winter greenhouse crops, are notably heavy feeders that can significantly deplete soil nitrogen over a growing season. For crops following either of these in the rotation, supplemental nitrogen beyond the standard alfalfa meal application is necessary to restore depleted soil reserves.

Historically, cottonseed meal served this purpose. However, the widespread adoption of genetically modified cotton made cottonseed meal an undesirable input for organic growers concerned about GMO contamination. The replacement is a locally available fertilizer produced from dried crab waste, which provides the needed nitrogen boost from a sustainable, regional source. This substitution illustrates the broader principle of adapting fertility programs to reflect changing agricultural realities and local resource availability.

## The Tilther: Electric Shallow Tillage

### Design and Purpose

Greenhouse beds require frequent replanting — often just one or two beds at a time — making full-size rotary tillers impractical. The ten-horsepower walk-behind tiller used outdoors is too large for confined greenhouse work, and its combustion engine produces unacceptable fumes in an enclosed space. The human-powered three-tooth cultivator initially used for the task was so unpopular it earned the nickname "the human rototiller."

The Tilther was designed specifically to solve this problem. It is an electric tiller engineered to work only the top 2 inches of soil. This shallow working depth was chosen deliberately based on weed science research showing that weed seeds rarely germinate from deeper than 2 inches below the soil surface. By cultivating only this shallow zone, the Tilther incorporates amendments and prepares seedbeds without bringing dormant weed seeds up into the germination zone. This preserves soil structure at depth and dramatically reduces weed pressure compared to deeper tillage methods.

### Mechanical Design

The Tilther uses a side-mounted gearbox arrangement modeled on tractor tillers, which eliminates the untilled center strip that rear-gearbox designs create. Power comes from a cordless drill that sits on top of the housing, driving a shaft connected by a chain to the tine shaft below. A rope around the drill's trigger is pulled from the handlebars for control. The result is a simple, quiet, fume-free tool ideally suited to the greenhouse environment where air quality and noise matter.

The cordless drill power source also means the Tilther shares batteries with other common farm tools, reducing the need for specialized charging equipment and making it a practical addition to any small-scale operation.

## The Broadfork for Soil Aeration

Before any mechanical cultivation with the Tilther, the soil is aerated using a broadfork. This hand tool consists of two 4-foot-long handles set upright at either end of a 30-inch-wide metal crossbar, with seven soil-penetrating tines — each 10 inches long — attached to the crossbar.

The broadfork technique involves pressing the tines into the soil with one foot, then pulling back on the handles just enough to lift and slightly loosen the soil at depth. The tines are then withdrawn, the operator steps back 6 inches, and the process repeats down the length of the bed. This action opens channels for air and water penetration, relieves compaction, and loosens the subsoil without inverting the soil profile or bringing weed seeds to the surface. The broadfork reaches well below the Tilther's 2-inch working zone, addressing aeration needs in the lower soil profile while the Tilther handles surface-level amendment incorporation.

The combination of broadfork aeration followed by Tilther cultivation represents a complete two-pass soil preparation system: deep biological aeration paired with shallow mechanical amendment mixing. This sequence addresses both the subsurface physical conditions that roots need and the surface-zone fertility that seedlings depend on.

## Compost Application Technique

### Surface Application Philosophy

Years of experience have demonstrated that greenhouse compost applications are most effective when applied to the soil surface and mixed only shallowly — into the top 1/2 inch at most. This approach works exceptionally well, likely because it mimics natural soil profiles where the highest concentration of organic matter is concentrated at the surface. Keeping compost at the surface produces outstanding seed germination rates and supports the biological activity that is most concentrated in the upper soil horizon.

The surface compost is incorporated more deeply at the end of each crop cycle when the beds are prepared for replanting, creating a natural cycle of shallow surface feeding followed by deeper incorporation as each crop rotation completes.

### Bed Finishing Tools

After compost is applied and amendments are incorporated with the Tilther, the bed surface is prepared for seeding. A 30-inch-wide Austrian hay rake is used to smooth the bed and mix compost shallowly into the surface. A wooden-tooth grading rake from a hardware store is an acceptable substitute. The Austrian hay rake is preferred because its design allows for more precise control of the mixing depth, ensuring compost stays in that critical top half-inch zone.

The bed is then firmed lightly with a single pass of a 30-inch-wide mesh-surfaced roller. This firming provides the consistent surface texture needed for the toothed wheels of a "pinpoint" seeder to operate effectively, ensuring accurate seed placement at uniform depth.

## The Compost Supply System

### Compost Production

Compost is produced from mowed forage crops supplemented with vegetable wastes, clay (as described in *The New Organic Grower*), seaweed gathered from the nearby Maine coast, and manure from farm livestock. Each ingredient contributes specific qualities: forage crops provide carbon and bulk, vegetable wastes add nitrogen and diverse microbial populations, clay improves the compost's ability to hold and exchange nutrients, seaweed supplies trace minerals and growth hormones, and manure accelerates the decomposition process.

The material is turned twice during the summer before use by loading it continuously into a small manure spreader that chops, aerates, and throws it out to form a windrow as the spreader advances. This mechanical turning process produces a thoroughly decomposed, crumbly compost that serves as the foundation of greenhouse fertility.

### Winter Compost Storage

Good compost is a key ingredient for soil fertility whether in the greenhouse or the open field, but access to finished compost during the winter months presents a logistical challenge in cold climates. Maintaining a year-round compost supply is essential for continuous winter greenhouse replanting. Each fall, a temporary plastic-covered A-frame structure is erected over one of the compost windrows. The plastic is secured using wiggle-wire channels over each end and sandbags along the bottom edges. This structure serves three critical functions: it keeps rain and snow off the compost, prevents nutrient leaching from winter precipitation, and provides enough insulation to limit freezing to the surface layer only.

During the coldest months — typically December through February — an additional layer of plastic is draped directly over the windrow inside the A-frame for extra protection against hard freezing. This double-plastic system ensures that usable compost remains accessible even during the harshest Maine winters. In milder winter climates, a simpler approach suffices: placing the windrow in a sunny site and covering it directly with a sheet of plastic secured at the edges with sandbags or rocks will keep compost thawed and workable throughout the season.

## Key Principles Summary

1. **Soil can be made, not just found** — poor land is not a permanent limitation
2. **Connect to the earth** — greenhouse soil should be living soil, not isolated media
3. **Structure first** — peat moss and organic matter create the physical foundation
4. **Site-specific minerals** — go beyond generic organic programs to address actual deficiencies
5. **Moderate amendments** — low-test fertilizers like alfalfa meal are generally preferable
6. **Shallow cultivation** — working only the top 2 inches suppresses weeds and preserves structure
7. **Surface composting** — keeping compost near the surface mimics natural systems
8. **Year-round supply** — plan compost production and protection for winter availability
