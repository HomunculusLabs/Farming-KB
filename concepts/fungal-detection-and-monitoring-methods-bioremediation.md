Two primary categories of assays are used: immunological and molecular methods. ## Immunological Assays Immunological methods detect specific fungal antigens or proteins using antibody-based technologies. These approaches are valuable for identifying and quantifying target fungi in complex environmental samples. ### Principles Immunological assays exploit the specificity of antibody-antigen interactions: - **Polyclonal antibodies**: Raised against whole-cell preparations or crude extracts; recognize multiple epitopes - **Monoclonal antibodies**: Highly specific to single epitopes; more reproducible - **Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)**: Quantitative detection using enzyme-conjugated antibodies - **Immunofluorescence**: Microscopic detection using fluorescently labeled antibodies ### Applications in Mycoremediation - Monitoring survival and spread of inoculated white-rot-fungi-effluent-decolorization in field applications ### Advantages - Relatively rapid turnaround time - Can be designed for field-portable formats - Quantitative capabilities with proper calibration - Cost-effective for routine monitoring ### Limitations - Cross-reactivity with non-target organisms possible - Antibody production requires purified antigens - May not distinguish between viable and non-viable cells - Environmental matrix interference (soil humic acids, etc.) ## Molecular Assays Molecular methods detect fungal DNA or RNA, providing highly specific identification and quantification capabilities. ### PCR-Based Methods - **Species-specific PCR**: Uses primers targeting unique DNA sequences for identification - **Quantitative PCR (qPCR)**: Real-time amplification enabling quantification of fungal biomass - **Nested PCR**: Two-stage amplification for increased sensitivity in low-biomass samples - **Multiplex PCR**: Simultaneous detection of multiple fungal species - **RT-PCR**: Detects RNA (expressed genes), indicating [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] ### DNA-Based [[rhizosphere-fungal-community-analysis-rrna-rdna]] - **Denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE)**: Community fingerprinting - **Terminal restriction fragment length polymorphism (TRFLP)**: Community structure analysis - **Clone libraries**: Identification of community members through sequencing - **Metagenomics**: Comprehensive community characterization ### Genetic Markers for Fungal Detection - **Internal transcribed spacer (ITS)** regions: Primary barcode for [[pcr-methods-fungal-identification-monitoring]] - **18S rRNA genes**: Broad phylogenetic marker - **Functional genes**: Laccase, MnP, LiP genes indicate degradative potential - **Species-specific markers**: Designed for individual target organisms ### Applications in Mycoremediation - Verifying establishment of inoculated fungi at treatment sites - Monitoring changes in fungal community structure during remediation - Quantifying expression of [[fungal-biomonitoring]] approaches using indicator species can complement [[guzman-allen-gartz-conflicting-chemical-analyses-neurotropic-fungi]] for brown-rot-fungi-biodegradation or peroxidase electrodes for phenol detection
- Enzyme electrodes for monitoring pollutant degradation

### Whole-Cell Biosensors
- Living fungal cells coupled to electrochemical or optical transducers
- Detect toxicity or specific pollutant concentrations

### Affinity-Based Biosensors (Immunoassays)
- Antibody-coated surfaces for specific fungal antigen detection
- Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) for label-free detection

## Microscopy and Cultural Methods

Traditional approaches remain valuable complements to molecular
techniques for fungal detection in bioremediation contexts:
- **Direct microscopic examination**: Visual identification of
  fungal hyphae and spores in soil or water samples using light
  or fluorescence microscopy after staining with calcofluor white
  or fluorescent brightener dyes
- **Plate counting and selective media**: Culturing environmental
  samples on nutrient agar with antibiotics to suppress bacterial
  growth, enabling isolation and enumeration of viable fungal
  propagules from contaminated sites
- **Most probable number (MPN) assays**: Statistical estimation of
  fungal population density using serial dilution cultures, useful
  for quantifying degradative capacity in heterogeneous samples
- **Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)**: High-resolution imaging
  of fungal colonization on substrate surfaces, revealing hyphal
  penetration patterns and enzyme secretion zones

## Enzyme Activity Assays

Measuring enzyme production provides a functional indicator of
fungal bioremediation activity rather than simply detecting presence:
- **Laccase assays**: Monitor phenol oxidase activity using ABTS
  or syringaldazine substrates to track white-rot fungal lignin
  degradation capacity in treated soils
- **Manganese peroxidase (MnP) assays**: Quantify MnP activity
  using phenol red or vanillylacetone oxidation as proxy measures
  for ligninolytic activity at contaminated field sites
- **Lignin peroxidase (LiP) assays**: Track veratryl alcohol
  oxidation rates to assess the degradation potential of inoculated
  white-rot fungi for recalcitrant pollutant breakdown
- **Cellulase and xylanase assays**: Measure hydrolytic enzyme
  activity as an indicator of general fungal metabolic activity
  and organic matter decomposition in composting systems

## Stable Isotope Probing (SIP)

Stable isotope probing links fungal identity to function by
tracking the assimilation of isotopically labeled substrates:
- Fungi actively degrading labeled pollutants (e.g., ^13C-PAHs)
  incorporate the isotope into their biomass DNA and RNA
- Labeled nucleic acids are separated by density gradient
  centrifugation and identified through molecular methods
- This approach definitively identifies which fungal taxa are
  participating in contaminant degradation rather than merely
  being present in the environment
- SIP combined with metagenomics provides community-level
  functional profiling of active degradative fungal populations

## Field Application Considerations

Implementing fungal detection protocols at contaminated field
sites presents unique challenges beyond laboratory conditions:
- Soil heterogeneity requires multiple sample points and replicate
  extractions to achieve representative detection of inoculated
  fungi across treatment zones
- Humic substances in soil interfere with both PCR amplification
  and antibody-based detection, necessitating sample cleanup steps
- Seasonal variation in temperature and moisture affects fungal
  activity levels and therefore detection sensitivity throughout
- Sampling depth matters because fungal colonization patterns
  differ between surface and subsurface contaminated zones
- Long-term monitoring requires standardized protocols to enable
  meaningful comparison of fungal population data across time
