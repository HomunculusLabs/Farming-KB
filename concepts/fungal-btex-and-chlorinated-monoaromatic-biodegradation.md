# Fungal BTEX and Chlorinated Monoaromatic Biodegradation

Fungi degrade monoaromatic compounds including BTEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and m-, o-, p-xylenes) and chlorinated monoaromatics through both intracellular and extracellular enzyme systems. Understanding these pathways is critical for bioremediation of contaminated groundwater and soil.

## Sources of Contamination

Monomeric aromatic compounds enter the environment from:

- Petroleum and fuel oil underground storage tank leakages (BTEX in soil and groundwater)

- Solvent-based paint, lacquer, and varnish manufacture

- Manufactured gas plant activities

- Pulp and paper mill chlorine-based bleaching (chlorophenols, chlorocatechols, chloroveratroles, chloroguaiacols)

- Herbicide manufacturing (chlorobenzenes as intermediates)

- Wood-preserving operations (pentachlorophenol, 2,4,6-trichlorophenol)

- Incineration of organic materials in the presence of chloride

## General Aerobic Biodegradation Features

All fungal biodegradative systems described are aerobic and proceed in two stages:

1. **Preparation of the benzenoid nucleus for ring cleavage**: Introduction of hydroxyl substituents if not already present in the required number and arrangement. At least two hydroxyl substituents (ortho or para to each other) are necessary for ring fission. Side chains may need removal or conversion to hydroxyl groups.

2. **Ring fission and further degradation**: Cleavage of the prepared ring structure and degradation to intermediates of central metabolism.

Hydroxylation reactions are catalyzed by monooxygenases and enzymatic components of fungal ligninolytic systems.

## Fungal Transformation Pathways

### Ligninolytic Enzyme Systems

White-rot fungi employ extracellular ligninolytic enzymes for nonspecific oxidation of aromatic compounds. These systems generate highly reactive radicals that attack aromatic rings without requiring prior modification. The broad substrate specificity of ligninolytic systems is advantageous for mixed-contaminant sites.

### Cytochrome P450 Systems

Non-ligninolytic fungi use intracellular cytochrome P450 monoxygenases for initial ring oxidation, similar to mammalian liver metabolism. These systems introduce hydroxyl groups via arene oxide intermediates.

### Monooxygenase-Mediated Alkyl Side Chain Degradation

Substrate interactions of toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylenes indicate their degradation at the alkyl side chain by the monooxygenase enzyme. During the metabolism of toluene by Cladophialophora sp. strain T1, hydroxylation of toluene to benzyl alcohol and subsequent transformation to benzoic acid occurred prior to hydroxylation and cleavage of the aromatic ring.

## Key Degrading Organisms and Performance

### Cladophialophora sp. Strain T1

This soil fungus does not metabolize benzene, but alkylated benzenes such as toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylenes are degraded by a combination of assimilation and cometabolism. Degradation of para-xylene does not occur in complex BTEX mixtures, but 60% depletes when combined with toluene. 1H-NMR metabolic profiles revealed the formation of phthalates as end products of ortho- and meta-xylenes.

### White-Rot Fungi

Three white-rot fungi (Phanerochaete chrysosporium, Pleurotus ostreatus, and Coriolus versicolor) demonstrated the ability to degrade oil in contaminated soil. Total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPHs) were reduced considerably after 4 months under nitrogen-rich conditions. P. chrysosporium degraded all BTEX components either individually or in a composite mixture.

### Additional Notable Organisms

- Cunninghamella elegans: Extensively studied for aromatic hydrocarbon ring hydroxylation

- Exophiala jeanselmei: Demonstrated toluene degradation capability

- Cladosporium sphaerospermum: Toluene and ethylbenzene degradation

- Trichosporon veenhuisii: Alkane-assimilating yeast able to assimilate aromatic compounds

- Various Bjerkandera, Trametes, and Pleurotus species

## Non-Ligninolytic Degradation of BTEX

P. chrysosporium degraded BTEX under non-ligninolytic conditions in malt extract medium in which extracellular lignin peroxidase (LiP) and manganese-dependent peroxidase (MnP) are not produced. In vitro studies by wild or mutant types also confirmed that LiP and MnP are not involved in BTEX degradation. This indicates that cytochrome P450 or other intracellular enzyme systems drive BTEX degradation.

Uniform ring-labeled [14C]benzene and [14C]toluene underwent greater mineralization to 14CO2. Compared to natural soil microflora, pure fungal cultures reduced hydrocarbon residues by 26 to 35% in 90 days.

## Hydrocarbon Metabolism by Yeasts and Fungi

Filamentous fungi do not exhibit preferential degradation for a particular chain length, unlike bacteria. Hyphal structures and increases in surface area allow better penetration of the hydrocarbons and hydrocarbon-impregnated soil aggregates. Many fungal species are xero- and osmotolerant. Certain fungi contain extracellular enzymes that may assist in the initial degradation of hydrocarbons.

## Biosensor Applications

Enzymes catalyzing key steps in catabolic pathways can be used in biosensor design for environmental pollutant detection. Laccase-based biosensors from Trametes versicolor and other fungi offer sensitive detection of phenolic compounds at micromolar concentrations.

## Practical Considerations

- Fungal mycelial networks provide advantages in accessing sorbed contaminants in soil matrices

- BTEX degradation by fungi typically requires co-metabolism with other carbon sources

- The broad substrate specificity of ligninolytic systems is advantageous for mixed-contaminant sites

- Bioavailability limits degradation rates in aged contaminated soils

- Fungi tolerate lower pH conditions than bacteria, expanding the applicable treatment range
