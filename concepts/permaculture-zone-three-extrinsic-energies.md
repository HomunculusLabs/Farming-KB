## Zone Three: The Farming Zone

**Zone three** is the main farming area in [[permaculture-design]], positioned beyond the intensively managed zones one and two. This zone is characterized by systems that require only **infrequent management** and periodic attention rather than daily care. It is the broad-scale production layer of the [[permaculture]] homestead.

### Key Features of Zone Three

- **Unpruned fruit and nut production** — standard orchards and nut groves left to develop natural form
- **Seedling propagation areas** — nurseries for trees and plants destined for wider planting
- **Self-harvesting systems** — designs where food can be gathered on periodic visits without ongoing tending
- **Wide-spaced plantings** — larger trees with understory grazing, functioning as integrated polycultures
- **Occasional mulching, pruning, and harvesting** — management tasks measured in weeks or months, not days

Zone three is where you transition from garden to farm. It is still domesticated but operates at a lower intensity, relying on natural systems to carry much of the maintenance burden. The designer's role shifts from active management to **observation and strategic intervention** — visiting weekly or monthly rather than daily.

## Semi-Domesticated Animals in Zone Three

Zone three is ideal for **grazing animals** that can coexist with tree crops. Short-grazing animals are particularly valuable because they maintain pasture beneath orchard trees without damaging bark or roots.

- **Sheep** — classic short grazers that fertilize as they move through orchards
- **Wallabies and wombats** — native short grazers (in Australian contexts) that maintain lawns under trees
- **Geese** — effective grass managers in orchard understories

The key principle is selecting animals whose grazing behavior **complements** rather than competes with the plant systems above them.

## Wild Corridors and Animal Integration

One of the most powerful design [[yield-theory-and-strategies-in-permaculture-design]] is **bringing wild corridors to the house** — designing wildlife pathways that pass close to the living areas, making wild animals a deliberate design element rather than a problem to be excluded.

### The Marsh-to-Lake Design

A classic example: designing a **marsh system that grades into a lake**, where swans maintain open water and wombats graze the surrounding lawns. The swans keep aquatic vegetation in check, the wombats keep grass short, and the system produces food and habitat simultaneously. This creates what [[bill-mollison]] called **"swan-wombat lawns"** — a self-maintaining productive landscape.

### The Vegetarian Community Disaster

Mollison recounts a cautionary tale of a vegetarian community that planted unfenced gardens directly within a wildlife corridor. When native animals naturally browsed the crops, the community responded by **poisoning the animals** — a catastrophic failure of design that turned a wildlife asset into a conflict.

### The Permaculture Alternative

The correct approach is the opposite:

1. **Guard the garden** — use fencing to protect intensive food production areas
2. **Use corridors** — designate specific wildlife pathways through and around the property
3. **Let animals live** — design for coexistence rather than eradication

### Dogs in the System

Dogs can serve as **guard animals** for poultry and livestock, but Mollison warns against keeping **too many dogs**. Excessive dogs become a destructive force — harassing wildlife, compacting soil, and consuming resources disproportionate to their function. One or two well-trained dogs serve the system; a pack undermines it. Dogs, like all elements, must be placed in a **functional relationship** to the whole system — their energy and behavior should serve a defined purpose.

## Extrinsic Energies in Design

**Extrinsic energies** are the external forces that act on a site — wind, cold air masses, sun, fire, [[albedo-absorption-and-heat-transfer]]. These forces are free, constant, and predictable. Understanding and working with these energies is fundamental to [[permaculture-site-design-and-zones]].

### Wind Energy

Wind is not merely a problem to be blocked — it is an **energy resource** that can be harnessed:

- **Cold wind can generate heat** — a vertical axis windmill connected to a kettle or friction heater can convert cold wind energy into usable heat
- **Hot desert winds can cool** — a "wind dodger" design uses wind passing over water wicks to create [[bloomfield-mushroom-evaporative-cooling-and-thermal-regulation]], turning a hot wind into a cooling breeze
- **Wind sectors** — mapping where winds come from allows designers to place windbreaks, turbines, and cooling structures precisely

### Defining Energy Sectors

Every site has identifiable sectors for incoming energies:

- **Wind sectors** — prevailing and storm wind directions
- **Fire sectors** — directions from which [[wildfire]] threat is greatest
- **Cold sectors** — directions of cold air drainage and winter winds
- **Sun sectors** — solar access paths across the sky throughout the year

These sectors become the **framework for placement** of every element on the site. Mapping them accurately requires seasonal observation — spending a full year on site noting wind direction, fire history, frost pockets, and sun angles before committing to final placement decisions.

### The Wind-View Conflict

A common design dilemma: the best views often face the prevailing wind. Mollison's solution is elegant — **travel to zone three for the view**. Position the house for shelter [[aquaculture-design-principles-and-energy-efficiency]], and create a pleasant destination in zone three (a sheltered seat, a small pavilion) where you walk to enjoy the panorama. This avoids the energy cost of heating or cooling a house oriented purely for views.

### View Design for People

For those who cannot easily travel — **children, the elderly, and the sick** — views matter at the house. Design bird-attracting shrubs and flowering plants directly outside windows so these people can observe wildlife from indoors. This brings the wild corridor experience to the most vulnerable members of the household.

## Fire-Immune Landscape Elements

Designing for fire resilience means incorporating elements that do not carry fire:

- **Mud crops** — wetland plants and rice paddies that cannot burn
- **Mulched gardens** — heavily mulched areas with high moisture content
- **Roads and tracks** — bare earth barriers that act as firebreaks
- **Short grazers** — animals that reduce fuel load by keeping grass low
- **Low-litter plants** — species that drop minimal combustible material

These elements form a mosaic across the landscape, creating **fire-buffered zones** that slow or stop wildfire spread. The goal is not to eliminate fire from the landscape — fire is a natural process — but to ensure that the **structures, crops, and animals** you value are protected within a network of fire-resistant elements.

## Three-Dimensional Design

Permaculture design is never flat. It operates in **three dimensions**:

- **Elevation** — thermal layers (cold air sinks, warm air rises), frost pockets, hilltop exposure
- **Sun angle** — varying throughout the day and year, creating microclimates on slopes
- **Slope and water flow** — gravity moves water and cold air downhill; earthworks capture both
- **Air flow** — cold air drainage patterns, wind acceleration through gaps, thermal updrafts

Reading the three-dimensional landscape tells you where to place every element for maximum efficiency. A designer who ignores elevation, slope, or air flow will place elements in the wrong position and spend years compensating for that error through costly earthworks or energy inputs.

## The Time Element in Design

Design is not a snapshot — it is a **process planned over time**. A permaculture system evolves through phases:

1. **Establishment** — soil building, water systems, pioneer species
2. **Development** — maturing trees, increasing yields, animal integration
3. **Maturity** — stable polycultures, self-regulating systems, full production
4. **Evolution** — ongoing adaptation to changing conditions and needs

Setting priorities means understanding **what must come first** and what can wait. A poorly phased installation wastes resources and creates dependencies that are difficult to reverse. Thoughtful sequencing allows each phase to support the next.

## Energy Priority Sequence

Mollison defines a clear order for installing systems on a site:

1. **Energy-productive systems first** — elements that generate or harvest energy (solar, wind, water collection, food forests)
2. **Energy-conserving systems second** — elements that reduce energy loss (insulation, windbreaks, mulching, thermal mass)
3. **Energy-consuming systems last** — elements that require energy input (processing facilities, heated structures, mechanical systems)

This sequence ensures that the **energy foundation** of the site is established before energy demands are created, making the entire system more resilient and self-sufficient. A site that produces and conserves energy before consuming it can sustain itself indefinitely; one built in the reverse order will always depend on external inputs.
