# Fungi in Grassland Ecosystems

Grassland ecosystems, including prairies, savannas, steppes,
and pastures, support distinctive fungal communities that play
critical roles in nutrient cycling, plant health, and soil
structure. Although grasslands are sometimes overshadowed by
forests in mycological research, they harbor diverse assemblages
of saprotrophic, mycorrhizal, and pathogenic fungi that are
essential to ecosystem function.

## Grassland Soil Characteristics and Fungal Habitats

Grassland soils are enriched by organic accretion throughout
the soil profile, especially from grass roots that are annual,
finely divided, and contribute a biomass that far exceeds
above-ground biomass. This root-dominated organic input creates
a unique substrate environment for fungal decomposers and
symbionts. In mesic or wet grasslands and in most shrublands
and heathlands, the superficial debris is underlain by an
amorphous, aromatic layer of organic material (humus) that
rests on mineral soil impregnated with humus.

The Wisconsin survey program provides some of the most
comprehensive data on grassland fungal diversity. Prairie
sites yielded more than 111 taxa from 5,700 isolates across
25 stands. Prairies interspersed with southern hardwood
forests and savannas are prevalent communities in many
temperate regions. In deserts, desert-grasslands, and some
forest communities, the above-ground debris often consists of
relatively intact organic matter (litter) only, creating
different conditions for fungal colonization.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi in Grasslands

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi are particularly important
in grassland ecosystems. The majority of grass species form
arbuscular mycorrhizal associations, and these symbioses are
critical for phosphorus uptake and plant community dynamics.
AM fungal diversity in grasslands can be substantial, with
species of *Glomus*, *Gigaspora*, *Scutellospora*, and
*Acaulospora* commonly encountered.

Grassland AM fungal communities are influenced by plant
species composition, soil disturbance, and management
practices such as grazing and fire. The ratio of
host-endemic AM fungal species to generalists likely differs
between grasslands and forests, reflecting differences in
plant community structure and root architecture.

## Saprotrophic Fungi and Decomposition

Saprotrophic fungi contribute approximately 78 to 90% of
the total decomposer biomass in grassland soils. Basidiomycetes
are prominent decomposers of grass litter, although they have
historically been described as "the missing link in soil
mycology" because culture-based methods severely
underrepresent them. Common saprotrophic genera in grassland
soils include species from the Eurotiales, Sordariales,
Onygenales, and numerous mitosporic fungi.

The fungal succession on grass litter follows predictable
patterns, with different functional groups dominating at
different stages of decomposition. In grasslands, nine
species of *Fusarium* may occur together, and correlations
between fungal community composition and soil properties
are especially pronounced and predictable.

## Endophytes of Grasses

Grasses harbor specialized endophytic fungi, particularly
species of *Neotyphodium* (anamorphs of *Balansiae*,
Clavicipitaceae), which colonize leaf, culm, and root tissues
of cool-season grasses extensively. These systemic endophytes
are transmitted in host seeds and can produce alkaloids that
protect the grass against insect herbivory and grazing mammals.

The distinction between clavicipitaceous grass endophytes
and nonsystemic fungal endophytes of grasses is important.
Systemic endophytes function essentially as a single organism
with their host, whereas nonsystemic endophytes represent
more generalized and diverse fungal assemblages. The
ecological roles of these different endophyte types in
grassland ecosystems remain an active area of investigation.

## Leaf Area and Sampling Considerations

Leaf area indices in grasslands range from 4.0 in savanna
and shrubland to much higher values in more productive
systems. The scale of sampling required to adequately
characterize grassland fungal diversity is considerable.
Sampling protocols must account for the spatial heterogeneity
of grassland plant communities and the seasonal dynamics of
fungal fruiting and sporulation.

## Disturbance and Management Effects

Human activities including cultivation, grazing, burning, and
fertilization significantly alter grassland fungal communities.
Conversion of native prairie to agricultural use dramatically
reduces fungal diversity and shifts community composition.
Understanding these effects is essential for conservation of
grassland fungal biodiversity and for sustainable land
management practices that maintain the ecosystem services
provided by grassland fungi.
