## Ayahuasca Divination — Seeing Crime, Witchcraft, and Disease Causation

## Overview

Across South American indigenous cultures, ayahuasca (*Banisteriopsis*) is used extensively for divination—the ability to "see" hidden causes of events, particularly crimes, illnesses, and acts of witchcraft. This concept page examines the divinatory use of ayahuasca as documented by anthropologists among the Jivaro, Candoshi, Tschamikuro, Zaparo, Noanama, and Embera peoples, and explores the psychological and cultural mechanisms that make hallucinogen-assisted divination effective.

## Divination of Witchcraft

### The Jivaro

Among the Jivaro of eastern Ecuador, ayahuasca is the primary tool for identifying which shaman has bewitched a sick or dying patient. The curing shaman drinks ayahuasca and "sees" the responsible sorcerer. "Generally, he can recognize who it is, unless it is a shaman who lives far away, or in another tribe."

This limitation—recognizing only shamans who are geographically or socially proximate—suggests that the "vision" of the bewitching shaman is not purely hallucinatory but draws on the healer"s existing knowledge of the social landscape. The ayahuasca experience appears to facilitate a kind of intuitive synthesis of available information, rather than providing genuinely supernatural knowledge.

### The Candoshi (Murato and Shapra)

Tessmann (1930) reports that among the Candoshi, the caapi drink is used "for better vision, i.e., in order to discover the cause of death and then to recognize the perpetrator." The same pattern holds: ayahuasca reveals both the cause and the agent of harmful events.

### The Tschamikuro

Among the Tschamikuro, "the caapi drink serves to allow one better vision while curing." The focus on curing contexts suggests that divination is primarily a diagnostic tool—it is employed when something has gone wrong and the cause is unknown.

### The Zaparo

For the Zaparo, Tessmann notes the use of the caapi drink "to allow for better diagnosing." The consistent terminology—"better vision," "better diagnosing"—across these cultures suggests that ayahuasca is understood as an enhancer of an existing perceptual capacity, not as a source of entirely new knowledge.

## The Noanama and Embera

Reichel-Dolmatoff (1960) reports the use among the Noanama and Embera of the Choco region of both *Banisteriopsis* and *Datura* "to produce hallucinations, generally with divining as the purpose." The use of two different hallucinogenic plants for the same purpose supports Harner"s argument that the divinatory function is not plant-specific but reflects a general human use of altered states for gaining information.

## Jivaro Shamanic Vision Drawings

Harner reproduces drawings made by a Jivaro shaman that provide visual documentation of diagnostic [[ayahuasca-visions]]:

### Coiled Snake in the Abdomen

One drawing shows a coiled snake as seen in a patient's abdomen during a diagnostic session. The snake represents the cause of illness—a spiritual entity that has taken up residence in the patient's body. This is a literal visualization of the concept of "soul loss" or "spiritual intrusion" that underlies much of indigenous medical theory.

### Golden Spheres and the Butterfly Demon

Another drawing shows golden spheres revolving about the shaman, with a giant butterfly demon to his left. The swirling field of spheres represents the "spirit of natemd"—the visionary energy that surrounds the shaman during the diagnostic trance. The butterfly demon represents a threatening spiritual presence that must be confronted and neutralized.

### The "Trip" Drawing

Perhaps the most remarkable drawing depicts a "trip"—the shaman"s visionary journey. Forked lines represent two different routes the shaman took to two white men's towns (indicated by crosses). One trail is "beautifully decorated with beads" (shown as pendant circles). This drawing literally maps the visionary experience as a journey through geographic and spiritual space.

## Mechanisms of Divinatory Accuracy

### Intuitive Pattern Recognition

The apparent accuracy of ayahuasca-assisted divination may be explained by the drug"s capacity to enhance intuitive pattern recognition. By altering consciousness in specific ways—reducing anxiety, increasing cognitive flexibility, enhancing visual imagery—ayahuasca may allow the healer to perceive patterns and connections that are available but not normally accessible to conscious awareness.

This is analogous to the "tip of the tongue" phenomenon, in which information that is known but not currently retrievable becomes accessible under certain conditions. Ayahuasca may create the conditions under which information about social relationships, behavioral patterns, and environmental cues can be synthesized into a diagnostic picture.

### Social Knowledge Integration

In small-scale societies where everyone knows everyone else, a great deal of information about social tensions, conflicts, and suspicious activities is available but diffuse. The shaman, as a central figure in the community, is exposed to a wide range of social information. Ayahuasca may facilitate the integration of this scattered information into coherent narratives about causation.

### The Placebo and Nocebo Effects

The knowledge that the shaman has "seen" the cause of illness can have powerful therapeutic effects for both patient and community. If the shaman identifies a sorcerer, the community"s attention is directed toward resolving the social conflict that may underlie the illness. If the shaman identifies a natural cause, the patient is relieved of the anxiety of supernatural persecution.

## The Role of Visual Evidence

The Jivaro drawings are significant because they provide "visual evidence" of the shaman"s diagnostic work. In oral cultures without writing, drawings serve as a form of documentation—proof that the shaman actually saw what he claims to have seen. The drawings also function as teaching tools, showing apprentices what kinds of visions to expect and how to interpret them.

## Crime Detection

Harner notes that ayahuasca is used not only for medical divination but for detecting crimes, particularly homicide and theft. The shaman drinks ayahuasca and "sees" the enactment of recent unsolved crimes, identifying the perpetrator and reconstructing the event. This forensic use of hallucinogens parallels the diagnostic use—the same visionary capacity that reveals the cause of illness can also reveal the perpetrator of a crime.

In societies without formal police or judicial systems, the shaman"s divinatory capacity serves as a mechanism of social control. The knowledge that a shaman can "see" who committed a crime creates a deterrent effect, even for those who do not believe in the shaman's supernatural powers.
