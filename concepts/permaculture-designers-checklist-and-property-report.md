# Permaculture Designer's Checklist and Property Design Report

The transition from understanding [[permaculture-principles]] to applying them on real properties requires a structured approach. A designer's checklist ensures that critical factors are not overlooked, while a property design report provides a systematic format for documenting site analysis, design decisions, and implementation plans. This concept presents both the practical checklist for site design and the recommended format for producing professional property design reports, grounded in the philosophy that design works best when it is collaborative, educational, and responsive to the people who will live with the results.

## Designer's Checklist

### Climate and Shelter
- **Design solid housing** to cope with winter cold
- **Shelter the house** with earthbanks, walls, or dense crescentic plantings on poleward (windward) sides
- **Drain house and barn areas** well for muddy thaw periods in cold climates

### Slope and Frost
- **Choose midslope ([[thermal-belt]]) locations** for house and garden sites
- Be aware of **frost extent on slopes**—cold air drains downslope and pools in valleys, while midslope positions often escape the worst frost

### Fire Protection
- Where **summer-dry regimes** dominate, design the house and inner zones for fire protection
- Provide **radiation shields** (stone walls, earthbanks, thick hedgerows)
- **Develop swales** in fire-prone areas to hold moisture and create green firebreaks

### Plant and Animal Systems
- Use **plant/animal guilds** in orchards
- Balance guilds to **reduce or eliminate the need for biocides**
- **Use minimal or no-tillage systems** in crop areas, maintaining or providing soil [[humus]] and soil structure
- Use **hedgerows for windbreak** around fields and settlements

### Cold Climate Considerations
- **Beware [[avalanche]]** in snowy climates
- Do not develop ski runs in fragile slope forests or tundra
- **Beware acid rain effects** on uplands above villages
- **Hedgerows and swales** will harvest snowmelt effectively
- **Bury all pipes below frost level** in soils
- Allow for **ice depth on water storages**
- Do not destroy peats or dwarf trees on permafrosts; **resod bare areas**

### Grazing and Grassland Management
- Do **very careful fencing and grazing rotations** in grasslands
- Try to include or develop a **mix of grazing species** in savannahs
- **Encourage native game species** on all farms
- Study text for data on free-range development for bees, chickens, pigs, and other livestock

### Winter Preparation
- **Plant to create winter stores** of grain, roots, and fodder for barn animals
- **Integrate the house and barn** in severe winter climates for efficiency and animal care
- Livestock products are important winter foods

### Building Health
- **Beware the ill effects** of hyperinsulation, radon, and carbon monoxide in buildings
- Arrange for **solid-mass heaters** and **good ventilation**
- Balance energy efficiency with indoor air quality

### Productive Land Use
- **Maximize forestry**, social income, free-range animals, and special products
- Consider accommodation, teaching, and other social income streams alongside production

## Property Design Report Format

When called upon to locate, design, or supervise work on projects and properties, a written report following this format ensures thoroughness and professionalism:

### 1. Identification
Give clear addresses of:
- **Designer** and **client**
- **Location** (with map) of the property
The purpose is to clearly identify place and people involved.

### 2. Scope of Work
State mutually agreed-upon instructions for the job. This section:
- Sets the **limits of the report**—what is included and what is excluded
- Establishes whether any **discretionary money** is available for maps, travel, soil analyses, aerial photos, water analysis, or minor equipment
- Prevents scope creep and misunderstanding between designer and client

### 3. General Property Description
Describe the property in general terms, including:
- **Climate**: rainfall patterns, temperature ranges, growing seasons
- **Winds**: prevailing directions, seasonal variations, extreme events
- **Soil erosion**: existing degradation, stability assessment
- **Fencing**: current state, adequacy for planned use
- **Access**: roads, tracks, easements
- **Fire risk**: vegetation type, history, slope exposure
- **Water sources**: existing supply, potential for water harvest
- **Slope and aspect**: topographic analysis, solar exposure
- **Energy supply**: existing infrastructure, renewable potential
- **Market potential**: proximity to markets, transport options
- **Human resources and skills**: available labor, expertise
- **Capital available** for development
- **Wildlife and potential pest species**: existing fauna, likely conflicts

All physical factors should be clearly marked on maps.

### 4. Land Use Particularization
Assign land use on the basis of soils, slopes, potential species, and activities. This section has two key dimensions:

#### a. Areas
Treat similar areas (marked on a map) alike. Examples:
- "Steep eroding slopes for forestry"
- "Swampy lowland suited to chinampa"
- "Gentle north-facing slope for orchard and market garden"
- "Ridgetop for wind generator and communication"

#### b. Themes
Themes cover cross-cutting design concerns:
- **Access**: roading, pathways, vehicle circulation
- **Fencing**: livestock management, wildlife exclusion
- **Soil treatment**: amendment strategies, erosion control
- **Crop or forage systems**: specific plant assemblies
- **Water sources and control**: dams, swales, irrigation
- **Potential for farmlink, commonwork, trust management**: social and economic structures
- **Village development**: if applicable, settlement patterns

Point out unique existing resources: gravels, clean water, young forests, heritage trees, geological features.

### 5. Appendices
Attach supporting materials:
- **Maps**: topographic, soil, vegetation, zoning
- **Drawings and plans**: layout concepts, detailed designs
- **Plant lists**: species selections with quantities and placement
- **Resource lists**: addresses of people, services, suppliers
- **Photocopies of relevant studies**: published research supporting design decisions
- **Photos taken during site assessment**: baseline documentation

## Design Philosophy

### Working with People, Not Over Them
The report format and design approach are grounded in several philosophical principles:

- **Never impose designs**—an externally imposed design is a form of insult, an implicit assumption of superiority on the part of the planner
- **Work with and educate owner-designers and community groups**, making them part of the design process
- **Proceed in stages**, working alongside the people who asked for the design
- **Incorporate the ideas of the people on the ground**—they will only try to make it work if many of their own ideas are included
- A designer cannot do a worse job of settlement and agricultural planning than what is commonly seen about us, but the willingness of the people on the ground to make it work is the ultimate determinant of success

### Best Practices for Designer-Client Relationships
- Keep property reports **informal** and accessible
- Offer **free enquiry** on details
- Try to **revisit at various stages** of implementation
- Prefer to **train people to self-design** rather than create permanent dependence
- Encourage people to **visit other properties** and **join support networks**
- Supply a **one-year subscription to a [[permaculture]] journal** as part of the service

### The Three Courtesies of Design Practice
1. **Never knock on closed doors**—go only where you are invited
2. **Always stay on the ground**—do not instigate grand projects that nobody really wants, then expect people to accept them. Rather, define needs as stated or requested and work towards supplying these
3. **Always pay your way**—save people more than you cost them
