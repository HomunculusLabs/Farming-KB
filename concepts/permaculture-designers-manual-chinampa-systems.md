# Chinampa Systems
# Permaculture Designers Manual - Channel, Canal, and Chinampa Culture

updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

The chinampa is probably the most efficient culture configuration for natural feeding of fish and water plants in integrated aquaculture-agriculture systems. Originally developed in the Valley of Mexico over 2000 years ago, chinampas were the main source of food for the inhabitants of the entire valley, producing as many as seven different crops in a year, two of which were maize. Mollison extended the concept to include any system of parallel canals and banks developed for growing fish and marginal plants.

Chinampa systems represent one of the most productive polycultures yet devised, combining the best of both soil and water culture in a single integrated system. They are a prime example of [[mollison-designers-edge-effect-design]] maximization applied to food production.

## Historical Chinampas

The original chinampas of the Valley of Mexico date back more than 2000 years. The Aztecs at Lake Tenochtitlan developed what is possibly the most productive system of polyculture yet devised, with chinampa crop, waterfowl, reedbed, and fish culture combined.

### Construction Methods

The traditional construction involved:
- Rafts of water vegetation cut from the canal surface
- Towed to mounded banks and built up in layers
- Covered with rich mud scooped from the canal bottom
- Properly maintained, chinampas remained fertile for centuries without fallow periods

## Modern Chinampa Design

Mollison's extended definition includes any system in which a sequence of canals and banks in approximate parallels are developed for growing fish and marginal plants. The system requires:
- A body of standing water (lake or swamp), OR
- A humid landscape with a clay base retaining water year-round, OR
- A water table close to the land surface

With these conditions, it is possible to create a cross-section harmonic of land and water whose uses are bounded only by climate, imagination, and the harvest capacity of the designer.

## Design Specifications

### Channel Dimensions

- Channels of 0.5-2.0m deep are widely used in fish culture
- Channels are the only economic way to develop "ponds" on slopes of more than 8 degrees unless water terraces are developed
- Channels maximize edge effects, and natural foods are substantially more available in channel culture

### Land-to-Water Ratio

- Normal ratio of channel to dryland culture: approximately 1:1 to 1:3
- Reversed ratio (more water): herbivorous fish, plankton eaters, and crayfish become self-foraging
- Islands typically never more than 30 feet (10m) wide
- Canals between 100-300 feet (30-100m) long

### Creating Chinampa Swamps

Chinampa swamps can be cheaply created with:
- A few compacted retaining walls
- Water levels regulated to back up over chinampa systems
- The ratio adjusted based on desired production balance

## Species Integration

### Fish Species
- Crayfish and grass carp in channels 2m wide
- Ranging over a swamp strip of 5-10m wide
- Rich forage supply plus land edges
- The terrace or swamp can be drained and harvested in 3-4 weeks
- Vegetation of terrace and shelf acts as fish food and manure

### Yields

With the right selection of species (ducks, mussels, weed-eating fish, crayfish, or eels), chinampa systems give yields in excess of 1000 kg/ha of water surface, as many food organisms and plants are intimately available to the canal fish.

### Integrated Species Examples

From the Designers Manual, chinampa systems in deltaic regions of Thailand grow:
- Fish and truck crop
- Ducks and fruit trees
- Water plants (kangkong, watercress, water chestnuts)
- Reed bed species (Typha, Phragmites)
- Mussels and other molluscs
- Eels in deeper channels

## Strategic Benefits

### Containment of Rampant Species

One strategic benefit of chinampa systems is that useful but potentially rampant species can be water-isolated from other land systems. Species that can be controlled through water isolation include:
- Runner bamboo
- Vine blackberry
- Hops
- Horseradish

Small moated islands have the same facility, and waterfowl can nest or rest on these without interference from foxes and feral cats.

### Slope Applications

On slopes, Huet (1964) reports good yields from trout canals in Switzerland (fed or unfed) at 40-60% greater than broad-pond culture. For hill canals:
- A reliable water intake is essential to permanence
- Clay soils are essential to water retention
- The vegetation of the edges, shallows, and margins is a critical factor in nutrient supply

## Drainage and Harvest

The terrace or swamp can be drained periodically for:
- Harvesting fish and crayfish
- Cropping vegetation
- Dredging accumulated muck (which should be used as compost or mulch, or to grow seedlings)

This periodic drainage and re-flooding cycle mimics natural flood cycles and maintains system productivity.

## Integration with Other Systems

Chinampas integrate naturally with:
- [[permaculture-designers-manual-ponds-and-dams]] as extensions
- [[permaculture-designers-manual-wetland-treatment]] as polishing ponds
- [[permaculture-designers-manual-charcoal-production]] from aquatic vegetation
- [[permaculture-designers-manual-irrigation-methods]] from canal water
- Duck and poultry foraging systems
- Reed bed construction for multiple products

## Hummock Systems

Hummock or chinampa systems allow self-irrigated crop, waterfowl, and fish culture combined. The chinampa system of Mexico is one of the antique systems, as are the terraced padi systems of Asia and the deltaic cultures of major river systems.

## Yields Outside the Pond

The total yield of chinampa systems includes not just fish flesh, but upstream, incorporated, and downstream yields:
- Industrial fish food pellets fed to ducks or pigs, whose manures fertilize ponds
- Fertiliser on land crop, fed to pigs and ducks, manures used for fish
- Permanent low-fertiliser crops for pigs (banana, papaya, acorns)
- Alcohol recovery and biogas digestion of green feed, tubers, or starchy food
- Side cycles to worms, notonectids, or Daphnia for better utilization

The flow-down system becomes: tree forage > animal protein > manures > alcohol > biogas > water crop (plants) > forage-fish, with a yield at every step.
