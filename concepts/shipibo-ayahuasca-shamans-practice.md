# Shipibo Ayahuasca Shamans and Practice

## Overview

The Shipibo-Conibo people of the Peruvian Amazon are among the most renowned ayahuasca practitioners in South America. Their elaborate visual art, complex icaro (sacred song) tradition, and deep integration of ayahuasca into daily life have made them the primary cultural group associated with [[ayahuasca-visions]], applied to textiles, pottery, body painting, and architecture
- **Musical tradition**: Complex vocal and instrumental music, particularly the icaros used in ayahuasca ceremonies
- **Healing practices**: A sophisticated system of plant-based medicine centered on ayahuasca and dietas (plant diet retreats)
- **Language**: The Shipibo language (Panoan family) remains widely spoken, though Spanish is also common

### Contact and Religious Change
Christian missionaries — particularly Pentecostal and Adventist groups, with some Catholic presence — have made significant inroads into Shipibo communities. Many Shipibo have converted to Christianity, publicly disavowing shamanism. However, Stuart observed that some converts privately continue to consume ayahuasca and practice native religious traditions, suggesting a syncretic adaptation rather than wholesale abandonment.

## The Role of the Ayahuasquero

### Traditional Functions
In traditional Shipibo culture, the ayahuasquero (onanya) serves multiple roles:

1. **Healer**: Diagnosing and treating illness through ayahuasca visions and plant remedies
2. **Spiritual guide**: Leading community members through transformative experiences
3. **Ceremonial leader**: Conducting rituals for the community's well-being
4. **Botanical knowledge keeper**: Maintaining extensive knowledge of medicinal plants and their applications
5. **Community mediator**: Resolving disputes and maintaining social harmony

### Training and Lineage
Shipibo ayahuasqueros typically train through:

- **Apprenticeship** with an established practitioner, often a family member
- **Dietas**: Extended periods (weeks to months) of dietary restriction combined with specific plant medicines to develop relationships with plant spirits
- **Visionary learning**: Receiving songs (icaros), healing techniques, and diagnostic abilities directly through ayahuasca experiences

## Icaros: The Sacred Songs

### Function and Belief
Icaros are the most distinctive element of Shipibo ayahuasca practice. They are melodic songs believed to:

- Call and direct healing spirits
- Structure the ceremonial space and protect participants
- Modulate the intensity and quality of the ayahuasca experience
- Diagnose illness by the shaman "seeing" the cause in visions
- Communicate with plant spirits and natural forces

### Musical Characteristics
Icaros vary widely among practitioners but share common features:

- **Melodic structure**: Often based on descending or ascending pentatonic patterns
- **Vocal quality**: Ranging from whispered invocations to full-throated calls
- **Duration**: Can extend for hours during a single ceremony
- **Personal nature**: Each shaman develops their own repertoire, though some icaros are passed down through lineages
- **Language**: May be sung in Shipibo, Spanish, or a mixture

Stuart heard icaros being sung from the home of a shaman called "E" in San Francisco during private family ceremonies, noting their haunting quality in the darkness of the Amazon night.

## San Francisco: Centro Ceremonial del Ayahuasca

### Community Profile
The Shipibo town of San Francisco, accessible by taxi from Yarina, has positioned itself as a ceremonial center. The town entrance features a large sign proclaiming it the "Centro Ceremonial del Ayahuasca." Stuart observed that:

- Almost every Shipibo family in San Francisco includes at least one ayahuasquero
- Many practitioners are willing to host ceremonies for both Peruvians and foreigners
- The community maintains traditional practices alongside its commercial activities

### Practitioner Profiles from Fieldwork

#### "B" (Yarina)
An elderly shaman who hosted nightly ceremonies attracting up to two dozen visitors. Stuart lived in the ceremonial hut in B's backyard for a week. Despite B's son giving workshops in the United States, Stuart found B to be:

- A frequent liar
- A shoplifter
- Someone who regularly asked for money or gifts at every opportunity
- Not a reliable indicator of shamanic quality, despite the family's professional operation

#### "E" (San Francisco)
Widely regarded as an honorable man in the community. Stuart heard E singing icaros during private family ceremonies at night. E charged approximately $10 per tourist ceremony. His reputation for integrity and his private spiritual practice suggest a practitioner who maintains traditional values alongside commercial activity.

#### B's Nephew
An enterprising young shaman earning a couple hundred dollars per month from tourists while neighbors couldn't afford a 30-cent taxi ride. He charged $30 for a tourist ceremony versus approximately $2 for a Peruvian — a 15x price differential that illustrates the economic dimension of ayahuasca tourism.

#### "A" (Unspecified Location)
A practitioner whose invitations for rituals Stuart declined after A swindled him on the price of a tobacco pipe — illustrating the personal risks of navigating unfamiliar social terrain as a foreigner.

## Ethics of Ayahuasca Tourism

### Authenticity Concerns
Stuart's observations highlight several tensions:

- **Commercial vs. traditional**: Commercial ceremonies tend to be shallower than family/community rituals
- **Economic exploitation**: Tourists pay 5–15x more than locals for equivalent ceremonies
- **Quality variability**: There is no certification or quality control — finding a "good" ayahuasquero is largely a matter of chance
- **Cultural impact**: The economic incentives of tourism may distort traditional practices

### Finding a Reputable Practitioner
Stuart notes that "almost every shaman claims to be the only person in all of Amazonia who knows how to properly brew the magic potion." For prospective tourists, he suggests:

- Spending extended time getting to know a practitioner before committing to ceremonies
- Seeking referrals from trusted sources rather than responding to advertising
- Understanding that character and integrity are at least as important as technical skill
- Being prepared for the possibility of exploitation

### Alternatives to Tourism
Stuart suggests that those interested primarily in the ayahuasca experience — rather than cultural immersion — could consider home brewing with ethnobotanical supplier ingredients, using an experienced friend as a sitter. This "do-it-yourself approach" could potentially be more cost-effective and personally meaningful than traveling to South America.
