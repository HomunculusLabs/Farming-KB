## Plant Stacking (Multi-Tier Agriculture)

Williams' description of traditional arid agriculture illustrates the principle: "The palm tree rises above every field... in its shade grows the olive tree. Under the olive, the fig grows, and under the fig, the pomegranate and vine, then the grain and vegetables."

This is not a crowded forest but a designed system where each species casts shade *for* rather than competing with others. The key: "It is fortunate indeed that plants cannot read!"

### Strategies for Intensive Stacking
1. Prevent water loss through high shade, hedge systems, and trenched walls that reduce wind evaporation
2. Introduce drought-resistant species (cacti) as windbreak hedges
3. Use vertical, buried sheet-plastic moisture barriers between garden and dry adjacent soil

### The Core Principle
"An environment can in this way be made to accommodate many species without competition between them." (Watt)

A well-occupied system resists invasion by rampant forms (blackbirds, blackberries). Initial diversity plus lack of disturbance preserves diversity-stability. "I cannot stress too much the importance of keeping a small area fully occupied with plants, as a strategy to reduce work."

### Edge Effect
The interface between two ecosystems represents a third, more complex system combining both. Gross photosynthetic production is highest at interfaces (estuaries, coral reefs). "A landscape with complex edge is interesting and beautiful; it can be considered the basis of the art of landscape design."

## Plant-Plant Interactions

Plants interact in ways most gardening books ignore:

### Support Relationships
- Act as trellis (grape on mulberry, fig, boxthorn)
- Screen and shade (coffee under palms)
- Provide nutrients (comfrey leaves for potato sets)
- Cross-fertilize (different plum and nut varieties)
- Obligate relationships (fungi under oaks and pines)
- Provide graft material (apple, pear, nut species)

### Plant Defense Guilds (Atsatt and O'Dowd, 1976)

**Category 1: Breeding grounds for pest predators (insectaries)**
- Phacelia in orchards reduces Prospatella pest
- Sorghum or lucerne interplanted with cotton
- Umbellifera (dill, fennel) with brassicas
- Strawberries near peach orchards
- Wild blackberry near grapes

**Category 2: Physical/chemical pest deterrence**
- Ranunculus protects grasses from cattle (lactone irritates mucous tissue)
- Trifolium fragiferum protects white clover from hares
- Brassicas grown with tomatoes are less attacked by pests (olfactory interference or predator concentration effect)
- Poisonous weeds can deter feral stock (rhododendron and azalea shoots kill feral goats)

**Category 3: Disease relationships**
- Some plants (Berberis) carry diseases of others (rust on grain) — either contra-indicated or used deliberately to suppress pest plants

### Nutrient Cycling
- Leucaena, lucerne, and most legumes provide essential elements
- Deep-rooted perennials probe below leached upper soil levels
- All material cycles via wind, water, dust, and animal agencies
- Some plants act as "catch-nets" for rare and essential elements

### Synergistic Design
"Any system which pretends to be designed must take account of the synergistic nature of plants and animals, or to put it more simply, place plants and animals in the correct array in order to obtain a third benefit or interaction."
