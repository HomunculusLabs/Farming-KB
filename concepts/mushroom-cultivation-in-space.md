# Mushroom Cultivation in Space

Cultivating mushrooms in space presents unique challenges and extraordinary opportunities. As humanity looks toward extended space missions, lunar bases, and eventual Martian colonization, mushrooms emerge as an ideal candidate for space agriculture due to their minimal light requirements, rapid growth, ability to grow on waste substrates, and high protein content. Space-based mushroom cultivation represents an extreme form of permaculture — a closed system where everything must be recycled and fed back into the cycle.

## Why Mushrooms for Space?

Mushrooms offer several advantages over traditional crops for space cultivation:

- **Low light requirements**: Mushrooms need only a few pulses of light every few minutes, not continuous illumination, to develop fruiting bodies. Pulse timers can save over 90 percent of the energy needed compared to continuous light.
- **Rapid propagation**: Mushrooms propagate by division and can expand significantly in just a few weeks, unlike plants that require full growth cycles from seed.
- **Minimal space**: Mycelium can be cultivated in compact containers and substrates.
- **High protein content**: Mushrooms provide essential protein for astronaut nutrition.
- **Waste substrate flexibility**: Mushrooms can grow on many by-products and waste materials that would otherwise be discarded.
- **No soil required**: Mushrooms colonize and consume their substrate rather than taking root in soil.

## Challenges of Space Cultivation

### Gas Exchange

Half of Earth's oxygen comes from phytoplankton and algae in oceans and surface waters, not land plants. In a space vessel, oxygen must be generated artificially. Flat-panel cultivation units using phytoplankton and algae are being researched at the **Institute of Space Systems in Stuttgart, Germany**, with the goal of creating systems that support human life while scrubbing carbon dioxide from mushroom cultivation chambers.

### Ethylene Accumulation

Plants and many fungi produce **ethylene**, a plant hormone known for ripening fruit. In the confined air of a spacecraft, ethylene accumulation can become problematic. This trace gas must be removed or filtered from the air.

**Solution**: Filters of **potassium permanganate** can capture ethylene by oxidizing it into water and carbon dioxide. Potassium permanganate is nonflammable and safe for use in space with basic precautions (gloves and safety glasses). The additional carbon dioxide produced by this oxidation must then be offset by additional plant or algal culture.

### Spore Management

Allowing mushrooms to fully mature into a sporulating state in space is strongly discouraged. Airborne spores can clog air filtration systems and degrade air quality in enclosed environments. Strategies to mitigate spore risk include:

- Cultivating mushrooms in chambers with lower light and higher CO₂ levels to produce long, stringy mushrooms with little or no cap — and minimal spore production
- Harvesting mushrooms young, as buttons rather than full-size fruiting bodies
- Breeding or discovering mushroom strains with very low spore loads that still produce great yields
- Designing cultivation systems that integrate seamlessly with life support and filtration systems

### Radiation Protection

On planetary surfaces like Mars, high levels of radiation would damage dividing fungal cells, causing mutations that could kill mycelia or prevent mating and fruiting body formation. Growing chambers would need to be extremely well-insulated to protect fungi from radiation while still allowing diffuse light to enter.

## The Algae-Mushroom Symbiosis

Algae and mushrooms are an excellent complementary pairing for space life support systems:

### Nutritional Completeness

One cup of dried algae contains approximately:
- 375 calories
- 57 grams of protein
- 19–20 percent of the recommended daily allowance for all nutrients and minerals combined

Combined with vitamin D supplements and protein-rich mushrooms, this pairing approaches a complete diet. This is far more practical than aquaculture, which would require copious water and involves difficult breeding cycles.

### Waste Cycling

Excess algal biomass can serve as growing substrate for mushrooms. In space, the growing medium can be pasteurized by briefly exposing it to the external space environment — the extreme heat or cold would kill molds and bacteria instantly, just as autoclaving would on Earth. The sterilized biomass would then be returned to the pressurized cabin for inoculation.

### Fuel Production

Some algae species, when cultivated in anaerobic reactors, produce hydrogen that can be separated for hydrogen fuel. Mushrooms offer a parallel application: **false morels** (*Gyromitra* spp.) produce gyromitrin, a water-soluble hydrazine that hydrolyzes into monomethylhydrazine — a component of some rocket fuels. The high-protein false morel is edible once the hydrazine is removed, making it both a food and fuel source in one organism.

## Energy-Efficient Cultivation

LED lights in the blue-green spectrum require minimal energy and can be powered by solar arrays. Beyond basic energy savings, cultivating mushrooms under specific wavelengths of light can mediate the development of various nutritional and bioactive constituents to address nutrient deficiencies and maintain astronaut health.

These experiments have direct applications for Earth-based mushroom farming as well. Flooding fruiting rooms with continuous artificial light is expensive and wasteful; pulse lighting and wavelength-specific LED systems developed for space could significantly reduce energy costs for commercial cultivators.

## Cultivation on Mars

The Martian atmosphere is primarily carbon dioxide, making initial mushroom cultivation there dependent on first establishing sufficient algal or plant growth to generate oxygen. Several additional considerations apply:

- **Temperature extremes**: Mars at noon near equatorial regions reaches approximately 70°F (21°C) but drops to nearly −225°F (−143°C) near the poles. Temperature differentials would strongly influence the siting of cultivation operations.
- **Ethylene management**: Potassium permanganate filters would be needed, with additional plant or algal culture to offset the CO₂ produced.
- **Substrate recycling**: Every effort should be made to recycle growing substrates and create soil for successive generations of plant cultivation.
- **Radiation shielding**: Extremely well-insulated growing chambers would be required.
- **Insulation design**: Chambers must protect fungi from radiation while allowing diffuse light entry.

## Mycelial "Space Cakes"

A revolutionary concept for space food production is the **mycelial space cake** — eating the colonized mycelium and substrate directly rather than fruiting mushrooms. This approach offers multiple advantages for space environments:

### Benefits Over Fruiting

- **Less space**: No need for fruiting chambers with controlled humidity and air exchange
- **Lower oxygen consumption**: Mycelium requires less oxygen than developing fruiting bodies
- **Faster production**: Mycelium is ready to eat in about one week versus weeks for fruiting
- **No light required**: Mycelial growth does not depend on light exposure
- **No spore production**: Eliminates the air quality risk from sporulation

### How Space Cakes Work

By the time mycelium fully colonizes a growing medium and thickens, it has converted much of the substrate biomass to mushroom protein while breaking down lignin and other structural plant cell wall components — making the material significantly more digestible than the raw substrate.

To make a space cake:
1. Create liquid mushroom spawn (agar spawn or grain slurries)
2. Pour directly into prepared growing substrate (beans, grains, rice)
3. Allow the substrate to be supercolonized by mycelium
4. Cook fresh or dehydrate and compress for long-term storage

Fresh cakes can be sliced and baked to reproduce the flavor and texture of the original mushroom species. Dehydrated cakes rehydrate within minutes in warm water.

### Strain Selection

Choose mushrooms with high mycelial binding strength and rapid growth to hold the cake together and outcompete other organisms:
- **Shiitake** (*Lentinula edodes*)
- **Oyster mushrooms** (*Pleurotus* spp.)
- **Reishi** (*Ganoderma* spp.)

Importantly, use nonsporulating cultures to avoid airborne contamination in enclosed environments — unlike traditional tempeh starter culture (*Rhizopus oligosporus*), which sporulates prolifically.

### Light-Enhanced Nutrition

Growing mushrooms under specific light wavelengths could improve the nutritional biochemistry of the myceliated cakes. Most mushrooms are capable of synthesizing additional vitamin D under UV light exposure, and manipulating light conditions can optimize other nutritional compounds.

### Dual-Use as Starter Cultures

Mycelial cakes can serve as starter cultures for further expansion. The cakes themselves are compressed mycelium that can be pulverized in water to make slurries capable of substantial inoculation and expansion — creating a self-perpetuating food production system.

### Earth Applications

While developed with space in mind, mycelial space cakes may prove to be a superfood right here on Earth. They can be manufactured on Earth and dehydrated for long-term storage, used as emergency food supplies, or sold as novel mushroom-based food products.

## Broader Significance

Whether or not mushroom cultivation on Mars becomes a reality, the engineering and methods developed for space cultivation have inherent value. This research reinforces environmental responsibility, since one of the core challenges of growing anything in space — maintaining balance in a closed system — mirrors the challenge we face on Earth. The constraints of space force innovations in efficiency, recycling, and sustainability that benefit terrestrial agriculture and cultivation practices.
