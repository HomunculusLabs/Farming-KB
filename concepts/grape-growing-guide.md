## Overview
Grapes are long-lived perennial vines that can produce fruit for 50+ years with proper care. They are versatile — grown for fresh eating (table grapes), winemaking, juice, raisins, and ornamental landscaping. This guide covers vine training systems, pruning methods, variety selection, disease management, and harvest.
## Vine Training Systems
Training establishes the permanent structure of the grapevine — the framework on which fruit-producing canes or spurs grow each year.
### Kniffen Systems (4-Arm Kniffen)
- Most common home garden training system
- Vine trained to a single trunk with two levels of horizontal cordons (arms)
- Lower wire at 3 feet, upper wire at 5-6 feet
- Four fruiting canes are retained each year, one per arm
- Simple to understand and maintain
- Works well for American and hybrid varieties
- Posts set 8-10 feet apart with wires stretched between them
### High Cordon (Bilateral Cordon)
- Trunk extends to a single wire at 5-6 feet
- Two permanent cordons (arms) extend along the wire in each direction
- Spurs (short stubs with 2-3 buds) are retained along each cordon
- Standard system for many Concord-type grapes
- Easy to maintain, good for vigorous varieties
- Harvest is at comfortable height
### Low Cordon
- Similar to high cordon but wire at 3-3.5 feet
- Better in windy sites (lower center of gravity)
- Fruit hangs close to ground — increased disease risk in humid climates
- Good for French-American hybrids
### Vertical Shoot Positioning (VSP)
- Used extensively in commercial vineyards, especially for wine grapes
- Trunk with permanent cordon on a wire at 3-4 feet
- Shoots are trained upward through movable catch wires
- Creates a vertical curtain of foliage
- Excellent light exposure and air circulation
- Requires more maintenance but produces premium fruit quality
- Best for Vitis vinifera and quality wine production
### Geneva Double Curtain (GDC)
- Two parallel wires at 5-6 feet, separated by 4 feet
- Two cordons per vine, one on each wire
- Doubles the fruiting area per vine
- For very vigorous varieties with high production potential
- Requires strong end posts and support
### Choosing a Training System
- **Home garden (few vines)**: 4-Arm Kniffen or high cordon — simple and effective
- **Wine production (quality focus)**: VSP — maximizes fruit quality
- **High vigor varieties**: GDC — handles excess growth
- **Small space/ornamental**: Fan training against a wall or arbor
## Pruning
Proper pruning is the single most important factor in grape quality and productivity. Grapes fruit on one-year-old canes (last season's growth). Without annual pruning, vines become overgrown, shaded, and produce poor-quality fruit.
### Spur Pruning
- Short stubs (spurs) of 2-4 buds are retained along permanent cordons
- Each spur produces 1-2 fruiting canes
- Simpler and faster than cane pruning
- Used for most American and hybrid varieties
- Spur spacing: 4-6 inches apart along the cordon
- Remove all other growth back to the cordon
- Replace old spurs (3+ years) by selecting a new shoot from near the cordon base
### Cane Pruning
- Entire one-year-old canes (8-15 buds) are retained and tied to wire
- Used for varieties that produce best fruit at cane base
- Typically retain 2-4 canes per vine plus renewal spurs
### When to Prune
- Late winter before bud swell (February-March); summer pruning for water sprouts and suckers
## Varieties
### Table Grapes (Fresh Eating)
- **Concord**: Classic American grape, slipskin, vigorous, disease-resistant
- **Thompson Seedless (Sultanina)**: Green, sweet, seedless; needs heat
- **Reliance**: Seedless, pink-red, very cold-hardy (zone 4)
- **Canadice**: Seedless, red, good flavor
- **Himrod**: Seedless, golden, excellent flavor
- **Mars**: Seedless, blue, disease-resistant
- **Interlaken**: Seedless, green, excellent for home gardens
- **Jupiter**: Seedless, large, muscat flavor
### Wine Grapes
- **Cabernet Sauvignon**: Full-bodied red; needs long warm season
- **Merlot**: Medium-bodied red; widely adaptable
- **Pinot Noir**: Thin-skinned red; needs cool climate, disease-prone
- **Chardonnay**: White; widely adaptable
- **Riesling**: White; needs cool climate for best quality
- **Norton (Cynthiana)**: Native American red wine grape; disease-resistant, excellent wine
- **Marquette**: Cold-hardy hybrid (zone 3); excellent wine quality
- **Frontenac**: Cold-hardy hybrid (zone 3); robust red wine
### Juice Grapes
- **Concord**: Standard for purple grape juice
- **Niagara**: White juice grape, vigorous
- **Fredonia**: Labrusca-type, cold-hardy
### Disease-Resistant Varieties
- For organic and low-spray growing: Marquette, Frontenac, Brianna, La Crescent, Traminette
- Many newer University of Minnesota releases combine cold hardiness with disease resistance
## Disease Management
Grapes are susceptible to several fungal diseases, especially in humid climates. Prevention is key.
### Powdery Mildew
- White powdery coating on leaves, shoots, and fruit
- Most common gneem disease worldwide
- Prevention: good air circulation, proper pruning, resistant varieties
- Organic controls: sulfur sprays (do not use above 85F), potassium bicarbonate, [[neem]] oil
- Remove and destroy infected plant material
### Downy Mildew
- Oily yellow spots on upper leaf surface; white fuzzy growth underneath
- Favored by warm, wet conditions
- Organic controls: copper-based sprays (Bordeaux mix), phosphorus acid products
- Avoid overhead watering; ensure good drainage
### Black Rot
- Small reddish-brown spots on berries that expand and shrivel into black mummies
- Very common in eastern North America
- Sanitation critical: remove mummified berries from the vine and ground
- Organic controls: copper sprays, sulfur, lime-sulfur
- Resistant varieties available
### Botrytis Bunch Rot
- Gray mold on berries, especially in wet conditions near harvest
- More problematic on tight-clustered varieties
- Improve air circulation, thin clusters, remove leaves around fruit
- Can be beneficial in dry conditions (noble rot in wine grapes)
### Pierce's Disease
- Bacterial disease fatal to European (vinifera) grapes
- Spread by sharpshooter insects
- Primarily a problem in the southeastern US and California
- No cure; plant resistant varieties in affected areas
- American and Muscadine grapes are resistant
### Integrated Management
1. Choose disease-resistant varieties suited to your climate
2. Prune annually for good air circulation
3. Remove fallen leaves and mummified fruit in autumn
4. Apply preventive sprays in humid climates; avoid overhead irrigation
## Harvesting
### When to Harvest
- Taste is the best indicator — sample berries regularly as they approach ripeness
- Table grapes: 16-22% sugar (Brix), good flavor balance
- Wine grapes: specific Brix depends on variety and wine style
- Color change alone is not sufficient — some varieties color up before they are sweet
- Seeds turn brown at full ripeness (seeded varieties)
- Stems at the cluster base turn from green to brown
### How to Harvest
- Cut clusters from the vine with shears or a knife
- Handle gently; harvest in morning after dew dries
- Pick during dry weather; discard damaged berries
### Yield Expectations
- Table grapes: 15-30 lbs/vine; Wine grapes: 8-15 lbs/vine
- Significant production by year 3-4; full production by year 5-6
