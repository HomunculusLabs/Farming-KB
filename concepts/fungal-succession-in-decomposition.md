# Fungal Succession in Decomposition

When a tree falls or organic matter is deposited on soil, a predictable sequence of fungal species colonizes the substrate over time. This process — fungal succession — determines how quickly nutrients are released, what type of decomposition dominates, and what organisms follow. Understanding succession is key to managing compost, forest health, and soil building.

## Stages of Wood Decomposition

### Stage 1: Primary Colonizers (Weeks to Months)
- **Pioneer fungi**: Fast-growing, opportunistic species
- Species: Trichoderma, Mucor, Rhizopus, Penicillium
- Characteristics: Rapid spore germination, broad substrate range, produce antibiotics to suppress competitors
- Role: Initial breakdown of easily available sugars, starches, and simple compounds
- Represent the "sugar rush" phase — simple carbohydrates consumed first

### Stage 2: Secondary Decomposers (Months to Years)
- **White-rot fungi**: The dominant wood decomposers
- Species: Phanerochaete chrysosporium, Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus ostreatus, Fomes fomentarius
- Characteristics: Produce ligninolytic enzymes (laccase, lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase)
- Role: Break down lignin and cellulose simultaneously — full wood decomposition
- Create characteristic white, bleached appearance in wood
- Most efficient decomposers of hardwood

### Stage 3: Tertiary Decomposers (Years to Decades)
- **Brown-rot fungi**: Specialize in cellulose decomposition
- Species: Serpula lacrymans, Gloeophyllum trabeum, Piptoporus betulinus (birch polypore)
- Characteristics: Produce Fenton reaction (H2O2 + Fe2+) to depolymerize cellulose
- Role: Remove cellulose and hemicellulose, leave lignin behind
- Create characteristic brown, cubical cracking in wood
- Most important decomposers of conifer wood
- Create humus-rich residue

### Stage 4: Late Succession (Decades)
- **Soil fungi**: After wood is sufficiently decomposed
- Species: Mycorrhizal fungi, soil saprotrophs
- Role: Final processing of remaining organic matter into humus
- Build soil organic matter and stable carbon pools
- Support plant growth in the enriched soil

## Factors Affecting Succession

### Wood Type
- **Hardwood** (oak, maple, beech): Favored by white-rot fungi, decomposes in 10-30 years
- **Softwood** (pine, spruce, fir): Favored by brown-rot fungi, decomposes in 20-50 years
- **Heartwood vs sapwood**: Sapwood decomposes first (higher nutrient content, less decay resistance)

### Environmental Conditions
- **Moisture**: Decomposition requires 20-60% wood moisture content
- **Temperature**: Optimal 20-30°C; slows dramatically below 5°C
- **Oxygen**: Aerobic fungi dominate; waterlogging selects different communities
- **pH**: Most decomposers prefer slightly acidic conditions (pH 4-6)

### Forest Type
- **Temperate broadleaf**: Diverse fungal communities, rapid decomposition
- **Boreal conifer**: Brown-rot dominated, slower decomposition
- **Tropical**: Fastest decomposition, diverse white-rot communities

## Nutrient Release Pattern

- Early stages: N and P are immobilized (fungi incorporate nutrients into biomass)
- Peak immobilization: When fungal biomass is maximal
- Net mineralization: After fungal biomass begins to decline, nutrients are released
- Complete cycle: 10-50 years for full nutrient release from a fallen tree

## Management Applications

### Forestry
- Leave fallen logs and snags (standing dead trees) for fungal habitat
- Coarse woody debris is critical for forest biodiversity
- Dead wood supports 30-40% of forest fungal species

### Composting
- [[compost-building-guide]] — the composting process mirrors fungal succession
- Hot phase: thermophilic fungi dominate
- Curing phase: mesophilic decomposers and mycorrhizal fungi recolonize
- Finished compost has a diverse fungal community

### Mushroom Cultivation
- Understanding succession helps select the right mushroom for the right substrate
- Shiitake: late-stage decomposer of hardwood logs
- Oyster: early-to-mid stage decomposer of many substrates
- Wine cap: decomposes wood chips in garden mulch
