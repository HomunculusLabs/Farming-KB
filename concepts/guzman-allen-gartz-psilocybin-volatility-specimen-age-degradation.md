# Psilocybin Volatility and Specimen Age Degradation

The active compounds in neurotropic fungi, primarily psilocybin and
psilocin, are not stable over time. Guzmán, Allen and Gartz documented
several lines of evidence showing that these alkaloids degrade
progressively as specimens age, whether in dried storage, herbarium
preservation, or during the natural senescence of fruiting bodies.
This degradation has important implications for chemical analysis,
ethnobotanical practice, and the reliability of negative results in
analytical studies.

## Experimental Evidence for Age-Related Decline

Repke et al. (1977a) demonstrated that the presence of baeocystin
varies according to the age of the studied materials. In Psilocybe
baeocystis and P. cyanescens, they found no trace of indole compounds
in specimens that had been analyzed 20 to 60 days after collection.
This finding suggests that the window for accurate chemical detection
may be quite narrow, and that delayed analysis can produce false
negative results. Ohenoja et al. (1987) provided dramatic quantitative
evidence of this decline in herbarium specimens of P. semilanceata.
They detected psilocybin concentrations of 0.014%, 0.67%, and 0.84%
dry weight in specimens collected in 1869, 1954, and 1976
respectively. This represents a roughly sixty-fold difference between
the oldest and newest specimens, showing a clear inverse relationship
between specimen age and detectable alkaloid content.

## Volatility of Psilocybin and Psilocin

Guzmán concluded from both experimental and observational evidence
that psilocybin and psilocin are volatile compounds. This volatility
was dramatically illustrated by a personal experience described in
the paper. While conducting fieldwork in Oaxaca, Mexico in 1958,
Guzmán experienced colored hallucinations despite having consumed no
fungi. He was trying to sleep in a small closed room of an Indian
home that was filled with a large collection of fresh or semi-fermented
neurotropic specimens. The mushrooms, a mixture of Psilocybe
caerulescens, P. cordispora, P. cubensis, P. mexicana, and P.
zapotecorum, had been gathered with local Indians for commercial
purposes and stored in sacks. The air of the room was heavy with a
strong acrid aroma from the fungi. His prolonged exposure to this
atmosphere produced vivid hallucinations that stopped immediately
when he left the room to breathe fresh air. This incident suggests
that the active compounds can become airborne under conditions of
high concentration and poor ventilation.

## Indigenous Knowledge of Alkaloid Stability

The Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca possess traditional knowledge that
aligns precisely with the scientific findings on alkaloid
degradation. They say that old dried specimens kept for more than
one year are not good to use, and they throw them out. This
traditional practice effectively prevents the use of degraded
material that would produce weak or absent psychoactive effects.
Guzmán confirmed this empirically when he observed that normal doses
of hallucinogenic fungi, specifically P. mexicana and P. caerulescens,
were only slightly neurotropic or entirely inactive in people who
consumed them because the fungi had been kept dried for almost a
year. The convergence of indigenous knowledge and laboratory analysis
provides strong evidence that alkaloid degradation is a real and
significant phenomenon.

## Implications for Chemical Studies

The volatility and degradation of psilocybin have direct consequences
for the interpretation of chemical studies on neurotropic fungi. A
negative result, that is the failure to detect psilocybin or related
compounds, cannot be taken as definitive evidence that a species lacks
neurotropic properties. If specimens were old, poorly preserved, or
analyzed long after collection, the alkaloids may have degraded below
the threshold of detection. This is particularly relevant for studies
on herbarium material, where specimens may be decades old. The
experience with Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides is
illustrative: Hofmann, in the work of Heim and Wasson (1958), found
no indole compounds in specimens that Wasson had gathered in a
popular market in Mexico and preserved for a long time. These
Cordyceps species are considered very important by the Indians of
the Nevado de Toluca region and are used in nocturnal ceremonies,
suggesting they may indeed contain active compounds when fresh.

## Cordyceps Ceremonial Use and Preservation

The Cordyceps species discussed in the paper are used in Mexico in
special nocturnal ceremonies where they are eaten either alone or
together with Psilocybe muliercula. In the center of the ceremony
room, a specimen of Elaphomyces, the host genus of Cordyceps, is
placed as a director of the ceremony. Elaphomyces species such as
E. granulatus, E. muricatus, and E. reticulatus are also used by
the Trique Indians of Alta Mixteca in Oaxaca as an aid in healing
wounds and to rejuvenate the organism. The fact that these fungi are
collected from markets and stored before analysis, rather than being
studied fresh in the field, likely explains the negative chemical
results obtained by researchers working with material long removed
from its natural state.

## Relevance to Modern Analytical Practice

These observations highlight the importance of analyzing neurotropic
fungi as soon as possible after collection, using fresh or properly
flash-frozen material. Modern analytical techniques such as liquid
chromatography-mass spectrometry can detect compounds at much lower
concentrations than the methods available to earlier researchers, but
they cannot compensate for alkaloids that have genuinely degraded
or volatilized from specimens. The Guzmán, Allen and Gartz discussion
of this topic remains relevant because it establishes a framework
for evaluating the reliability of both positive and negative chemical
reports in the mycological literature.
