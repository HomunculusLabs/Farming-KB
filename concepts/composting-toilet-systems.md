# Composting Toilet Systems

Composting toilets treat human waste on-site through aerobic decomposition,
converting it into a safe, nutrient-rich soil amendment. They eliminate the
need for conventional septic systems, reduce water consumption, and are a
key component of off-grid sanitation infrastructure. This guide covers
system types, design, maintenance, and legal considerations.

## Why Composting Toilets

- **Water conservation** — eliminates 20-30% of indoor water use (toilet
  flushing)
- **Off-grid viability** — no water, septic, or sewer connection required
- **Nutrient recovery** — returns nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium to
  the soil cycle
- **Reduced infrastructure** — no septic field, leach lines, or connection
  to municipal systems
- **Cost savings** — eliminates septic installation ($5,000-15,000) and
  maintenance

## System Types

### Self-Contained Units

The toilet and composting chamber are a single unit installed directly in
the bathroom. Waste is processed inside the unit.

**Design:**
- Electric models use heaters, fans, and agitators to accelerate composting
- Non-electric models rely on gravity, ventilation, and manual bulking
- Capacity: 1-4 people for continuous use; larger households need more
  units or a centralized system

**Advantages:**
- Easy installation — no below-floor plumbing
- Portable — can be moved if needed
- Good for occasional use (cabins, guest houses)

**Disadvantages:**
- Small capacity — requires frequent emptying
- Electric models consume 100-200 watts
- Limited processing capacity for full-time residential use
- Visual and odor issues if not well managed

**Popular models:** Nature's Head, Separett Villa, Sun-Mar Compact,
Olander

### Centralized / Remote Systems

The toilet chute connects to a larger composting tank located in a basement
or outside. More processing capacity than self-contained units.

**Design:**
- Toilet fixture (typically urine-diverting) sits above a chute that drops
  waste to a central composting chamber
- Larger composting volume allows longer processing time and higher capacity
- May include rotating drums, rakes, or automated agitation
- Ventilation pipe runs from the chamber to the roof

**Advantages:**
- Higher capacity (suitable for full-time families of 4-6)
- Less frequent maintenance than self-contained units
- Better composting due to larger volume and longer retention time
- Toilet room looks more conventional

**Disadvantages:**
- Requires space below the toilet (basement or crawlspace)
- More complex installation
- Higher cost ($3,000-10,000+)
- Some models require electricity

**Popular models:** Sun-Mar Centrex, Clivus Multrum, Envirolet, Phoenix

### Urine-Diverting Dry Toilets (UDDT)

Separates urine from feces at the point of collection. Each stream is
processed separately, dramatically improving composting efficiency.

**How it works:**
- The toilet bowl has two compartments: urine drains forward to a collection
  pipe or container; feces drop into a composting chamber behind
- Bulking material (sawdust, peat moss, coconut coir) is added after each
  feces deposit to cover and provide carbon
- Urine is either diverted to a leach field, diluted for direct soil
  application (1:5-1:10 with water), or collected in a tank
- Feces decompose aerobically in the composting chamber over 6-12 months

**Advantages:**
- Faster, cleaner composting — no excess liquid in the compost pile
- Minimal odor when managed properly
- Urine (diluted) is an excellent nitrogen fertilizer
- Low cost — DIY versions can be built for $100-500
- No electricity required

**Disadvantages:**
- Users must sit to urinate (or use a separate urinal)
- Requires regular addition of bulking material
- Two waste streams to manage
- Education required for proper use by all household members and guests

## DIY Composting Toilet

### Simple Two-Bucket System
The most basic and affordable composting toilet:

**Materials:**
- Two 5-gallon buckets with lids (one for urine, one for feces)
- Plywood or MDF box to enclose the buckets with a toilet seat on top
- Sawdust, peat moss, or coconut coir as cover material

**Use:**
1. Urinate in the urine bucket (or separate container)
2. Defecate in the solids bucket
3. Add 1-2 cups of sawdust after each use
4. When the solids bucket is full, empty into an outdoor compost bin
5. Let the outdoor compost cure for 12-18 months before garden use
6. Dilute urine 1:10 and apply directly to non-food plants

### Advanced Moldering Toilet
A permanent installation with a larger composting chamber:

1. Build a sloped chamber below the bathroom floor (4-8 feet long)
2. Install a toilet seat over the chamber opening
3. Wire mesh at the bottom allows liquid to drain to a leach field
4. Add bulking material regularly; compost slowly moves down the slope
5. Harvest finished compost from the lower end after 1-2 years

## Maintenance

### Self-Contained Units
- Add bulking material (peat moss mix) as needed per manufacturer instructions
- Rotate or agitate compost every 2-3 days (electric models)
- Empty the finishing drawer when full (every 4-6 weeks for 2-person use)
- Clean ventilation fan and filter annually
- Check for plumbing blockages in urine line

### Centralized Systems
- Add bulking material weekly
- Rotate drum or rake compost monthly
- Empty finished compost annually (or per manufacturer schedule)
- Inspect and clean ventilation system annually
- Check liquid overflow and leach field function

### Compost Safety
- **Retention time**: minimum 12 months, preferably 18-24 months
- **Temperature**: thermophilic composting (above 122F / 50C) for at least
  3 days ensures pathogen kill
- **Testing**: finished compost should test negative for fecal coliforms
  before use
- **Application**: use on ornamental plants, fruit trees, and non-food crops;
  avoid root vegetables if any doubt about pathogen kill

## Legal Considerations

- **Regulation varies widely** by state, county, and municipality
- **Some areas** allow composting toilets as the primary sanitation system
- **Others** require a conventional septic or require the composting toilet
  to be "approved" by NSF International (NSF/ANSI 41 standard)
- **Permits** may be required — check with your local health department
- **Graywater** — if the toilet is part of a larger waterless system, you
  may need graywater permits; see [[rainwater-harvesting-systems]] for
  water system design
- **National standards**: NSF/ANSI 41 is the US standard for composting
  toilets; look for certified models
