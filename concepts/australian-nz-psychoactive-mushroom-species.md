# Australian and New Zealand Psychoactive Mushroom Species

Australia and New Zealand host a diverse assemblage of psilocybin-containing mushrooms, including both endemic species and species introduced through European colonization. John W. Allen's field guide documents more than a dozen confirmed and suspected psychoactive species across the two countries, distributed across a range of habitats from tropical cattle pastures to temperate native forests. This overview summarizes the species diversity, distribution patterns, and ecological relationships of Australasian psychoactive fungi. See [[cattle-dispersal-of-psychoactive-fungi]] for the introduction history and [[hallucinogenic-mushrooms-european-trends]] for comparison with European patterns.

### Species of Uncertain Status

- **Panaeolina foenisecii** ("haymaker's mushroom") — Common lawn mushroom; confirmed NOT hallucinogenic by Guzman, Stijve, Watling, and Young
- **Panaeolus antillarum** — Resembles Copelandia; does NOT contain psilocybine despite some literature claims
- **Psilocybe collybioides** — Listed by Southcott (1974); identification later found to be inexact by Guzman and Watling
- **Psilocybe kumaenorum** — Known only from New Guinea but may occur in Australia's Gold Coast region

### Unidentified Species

Dr. Karl L.R. Jansen reports at least 5 unidentified psychoactive species from New Zealand. An unidentified bluing Psilocybe species with affinities to the North American *Psilocybe caerulipes* has also been reported from New Zealand.

## Geographic Patterns

### Australia

The highest diversity of psychoactive species occurs in:
- **New South Wales** — Most species-rich state with 8+ confirmed species
- **Queensland** — Tropical species dominate (P. cubensis, C. cyanescens)
- **Tasmania** — Temperate species diversity (P. subaeruginosa, P. tasmaniana, P. australiana)
- **South Australia** — P. subaeruginosa and C. cyanescens

### New Zealand

- **North Island** — C. cyanescens, P. aucklandii, P. australiana, P. eucalypta, P. tasmaniana, P. subaeruginosa
- **South Island** — P. makarorae (endemic), P. semilanceata, P. subaeruginosa

Notable absence: *Psilocybe cubensis* is NOT confirmed from New Zealand despite its abundance in Australia and apparently suitable conditions.

## Habitat Categories

1. **Dung-inhabiting** — P. cubensis, P. subcubensis, C. cyanescens, P. subbalteatus. These species were likely introduced with cattle.
2. **Manured soil** — P. semilanceata. Grows in soil enriched by ruminant dung but not directly on dung.
3. **Wood-inhabiting** — P. subaeruginosa, P. australiana, P. eucalypta, P. makarorae, P. aucklandii. Found on woody debris, forest litter, bark mulch.
4. **Dung/wood debris mixture** — P. tasmaniana. Grows on debris intermixed with dung including kangaroo dung.
5. **Wood (large)** — G. purpuratus, G. junonius. Large wood-inhabiting species.

## Potency Comparison

Bioassays reportedly indicate that the Australian/New Zealand wood-inhabiting species (P. australiana, P. eucalypta, P. tasmaniana, P. makarorae) have potency comparable to *Psilocybe cyanescens* of the Pacific Northwest — one of the most potent psilocybin mushrooms known. Wild-collected *P. cubensis* from Australia is notably less potent than cultivated specimens, requiring larger doses.

## Public Awareness History

Awareness of psychoactive mushrooms in Australia and NZ emerged through:
1. Visiting surfers from NZ, Hawaii, or the US providing ethnomycological information to locals (McCarthy, 1971)
2. Media reports in the 1960s-70s describing accidental and deliberate intoxications
3. R. Gordon Wasson's 1957 Life Magazine article on Mexican mushroom ceremonies
4. Word-of-mouth communication within drug-using subcultures
5. The World Wide Web and Internet from the 1990s onward

## Common Names

- "Gold tops" / "Gold caps" — P. cubensis and sometimes C. cyanescens
- "Blue meanies" — C. cyanescens (referring to both potency and blue bruising)
- "Magic mushrooms" — Generic term for all psilocybin species
- "Liberty cap" — P. semilanceata
- "Psilos" — P. semilanceata (European term)

# Psilocybin Mushroom Law and Legal History in Australia

The legal history of psilocybin-containing mushrooms in Australia spans from the late 1960s through to the present day, beginning with early possession charges and evolving into comprehensive state-level scheduling. [[insect-allies]] documented this history extensively, revealing how Australian authorities responded to the emergence of recreational mushroom use through legislation, enforcement, and public awareness campaigns.

## Early Use and First Prosecutions (1969-1971)

The non-traditional use of psychoactive mushrooms increased rapidly in Australia between 1969 and 1975. Heavy rains in the spring of 1969 produced bumper crops of [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]], which were consumed in large quantities by members of Australia's counter culture. Users described the effects as being similar to LSD but more natural, eating them raw, on toast, or in soup.

Government authorities noted that popularity diminished after many regular users began experiencing extreme depression and lethargy, with some reporting they had "lost their will to live." This resulted in a number of "freakouts" that attracted law enforcement attention.

On Friday, July 11, 1969, four young men aged 20-22 from New South Wales were each fined $200 on charges of possessing psilocybin. The complaint was registered by the manager of Sippy Downs station near Nambour, about sixty miles north of Brisbane, who reported that the men had gained illegal entry onto his private property. Police found a box of mushrooms in their parked van. The magistrate allowed them two weeks to pay or serve one month imprisonment.

By March 1971, an export market had been established by dealers who made [[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]] available to users in Sydney and other cities throughout Australia. Tasmanian authorities became concerned that widespread collection in their state would attract people from the mainland. Dr. Malcomb Hall stated in 1973 that "exportation of fungi from Tasmania to the mainland is highly likely, as knowledge of suitable species becomes more widespread."

## State-Level Legislation

While each Australian state enacted legislation controlling psychoactive substances and their analogues, only Queensland declared a specific mushroom — [[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]] — as a prohibited "plant" under the Queensland Health Act of 1937-1971. Intoxications from this species were reported as early as 1958.

Dr. Malcolm C. Hall of the Canberra Commonwealth Police Force noted that psilocybin and psilocin were listed as Schedule 3 drugs in the Tasmanian Dangerous Drugs Order of 1965. Both chemicals were later designated as Schedule 1 drugs by the International Convention on Psychotropic Substances of 1971.

## Arrest Statistics and Enforcement Patterns

Dr. Hall reported that 60 people in Australia were charged with offenses involving possession or sales of psilocybin mushrooms during 1972. However, charges against several individuals were later withdrawn when no psychoactive compounds were found in the confiscated specimens. One year later, Hall stated that approximately 74 persons were charged in 1972. By 1973, only 27 persons were charged with psilocybin offenses, suggesting a decline in enforcement or use.

## The 1980 Government Report

In 1980, a special government report on drugs documented growing concern about mushroom abuse. A Queensland school teacher told the commission that because LSD was difficult to obtain and expensive, people were picking hallucinogenic mushrooms around Samford, Ferny Grove, Pinkenba, Dayboro, and Beenleigh.

A senior Queensland Police Officer confirmed that "gold top" mushrooms ([[psilocybe-cubensis-profile]]) were plentiful in the Gold Coast area, particularly in the Currumbin and Tallebudgera areas during the wet season, though no evidence suggested commercial cultivation.

Captain B.C. Mundy of the Salvation Army in Darwin reported that "gold tops" and "blue meanie" mushrooms grew freely in the tropical climate in animal manure and manured gardens. One notable case involved a resident of the Red Shield Hostel in Darwin who was accused of housebreaking but found not responsible after boiling "blue meanies" from the hostel garden, which had recently been treated with fowl manure. This was reportedly the first case of [[panaeolus-cyanescens-bali-omelette]] (blue meanies) fruiting from poultry manure rather than ruminant dung.

## Cultivation Concerns

While no evidence in the literature indicated that cultivation of hallucinogenic mushrooms was common among Australian users, Allen noted that mushroom growing kits from the United States were sold through the mail to Australian citizens on a monthly basis. The clandestine nature of home cultivation — in basements, garages, attics, or closets — made detection difficult compared to outdoor Cannabis cultivation.
