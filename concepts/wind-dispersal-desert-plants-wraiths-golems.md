# Wind-Dispersal Desert Plants: Wraiths and Golems

Whole plants, seed-heads, leaves, dried manurial pellets, seed pods, and dust blow with the wind across desert plains, becoming trapped in depressions, pits, swales, and against fences and tree-lines. A distinctive category of desert plants depends entirely on wind for dispersal, appearing as either substantial **rolling balls** (golems) or dry, light, airy panicles (wraiths).

## Wind-Trapping Infrastructure

Brush fences can be buried by windblown drift and need to be vertically extended when sand is unstable. These natural trapping mechanisms create microsites for seed germination and plant establishment in otherwise hostile environments. Swales, pits, and fence lines all function as passive seed catchers.

## Golem Species (Rolling Dispersers)

### Black Roly-Poly (*Bassia quinquecuspis*)
- A **short-lived perennial shrub** that breaks off at the base and rolls across the landscape
- Distributes spiny seeds as it travels
- Considered a "nuisance" to grazers but occurs thickly **only where overgrazing occurs**
- Functions as a **defending pioneer** that protects a wide range of soils
- Under its canopy, more palatable or useful species can grow
- Mollison notes that what is truly "noxious" is the state of mind which ignores its useful ecological function

### Copperburrs (*Bassia spp.*) and Salt Bushes
- Attempt to **repair scalds** (areas where topsoil is completely gone)
- Most have thorny seeds that form dense mats
- Seeds penetrate rubber sandals and clothing, making them unpopular with pastoralists
- Despite human inconvenience, they perform critical soil-stabilisation and reclamation work

## Wraith Species (Airy Panicle Dispersers)

### Tumbleweed (*Amaranthus albus*)
- A more effete invader, a stiff wraith preferring **towns and settled areas**
- Rarely abundant in truly wild desert
- A related species, *A. viridis*, is cooked as a vegetable and found in arid-area towns as a "weed"

### Mustard (*Sisymbrium officinale*)
- Has some forage and medicinal value
- Usually found only in cultivated (wheat) areas
- Spreads dry seed stems as a wraith during dust-storms

## Ecological Significance

These wind-dispersed species are among the **first colonisers** of severely degraded land. Their ecological roles include:

- **Soil stabilisation**: Preventing further erosion on bare, scalded, or disturbed ground
- **Seed trapping**: Creating microenvironments where other species' seeds can accumulate and germinate
- **Moisture retention**: Their presence shades soil surfaces and reduces evaporation
- **Organic matter contribution**: As plants die and decompose in place, they add organic matter to depleted soils
- **Nurse plant function**: Providing shelter for the establishment of more palatable or productive species beneath their canopies

## The Pioneer Paradox

Many of these species are officially classified as "weeds" or "noxious" by agricultural authorities, yet they are **symptoms of land degradation rather than causes**. Black roly-poly, for example, only becomes dominant where overgrazing has eliminated perennial grasses. Its presence signals that the land is attempting to heal itself.

Effective land management recognises these species as **allies in restoration** rather than enemies to be eradicated. Once perennial grasses and woody vegetation are re-established through proper grazing management, the pioneer species naturally diminish as conditions improve and competition increases.

## Arid-Area Grasses and Forbs

A very careful selection of perennial and annual grasses can greatly increase the number and condition of animals on range. Good grasses exist for every situation, from sand seas to gilgais (natural swales developed from pockets of expanding clays). Their uses range from human food to poultry and waterfowl forage, thatch, green forage, and hay.
