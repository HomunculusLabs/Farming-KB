# Mycorrhizal Carbon Cycle

## Overview

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the vast majority of terrestrial plant species, creating a critical conduit for carbon flow between plants and soil. Plants allocate a substantial portion of their photosynthetically fixed carbon — estimated at 10–30% of total net primary production — to mycorrhizal fungal partners belowground. This carbon flux through [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]] represents one of the largest and least understood pathways in the global carbon cycle, with profound implications for soil carbon storage, ecosystem productivity, and climate regulation.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF)

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (phylum Glomeromycota) associate with approximately 70–80% of all terrestrial plant species, including most agricultural crops, grasses, and many tropical trees. AMF form intracellular structures (arbuscules and vesicles) within root cortical cells, creating intimate interfaces for bidirectional nutrient exchange. The extraradical mycelium of AMF extends centimeters to meters from the root surface into the surrounding soil, exploring a soil volume far beyond the root's own depletion zone.

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (EMF)

Ectomycorrhizal fungi (primarily Basidiomycota and Ascomycota) associate mainly with woody plants — particularly trees in the families Pinaceae, Fagaceae, Betulaceae, and Myrtaceae. EMF form a characteristic Hartig net of hyphae between root cortical cells and a dense fungal mantle covering the root surface. EMF are dominant in boreal and temperate forest ecosystems, where they can account for the majority of belowground microbial biomass.

### Ericoid and Other Mycorrhizae

[[comparison-orchid-mycorrhizae-vs-ericoid-mycorrhizae]] associate with plants in the order Ericales (heaths, heathers, blueberries) growing in acidic, nutrient-poor soils. Orchid mycorrhizae are essential for orchid seed germination and early growth. Each type has distinct carbon dynamics, but all share the fundamental pattern of plant-to-fungus carbon allocation in exchange for soil-derived nutrients.

## Carbon Allocation to Mycorrhizal Fungi

### The Carbon Sink

Mycorrhizal fungi constitute a massive belowground carbon sink. Under field conditions, the fungal biomass in mycorrhizal networks can equal or exceed the biomass of fine roots. In temperate and boreal forests, EMF biomass alone may account for hundreds of grams of carbon per square meter. This biomass represents carbon that has been removed from the atmosphere by photosynthesis but is not immediately returned through respiration.

### Regulation of Carbon Flow

Carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi is not a fixed proportion of photosynthate but is dynamically regulated by both plant and fungal partners. Plants increase carbon allocation to mycorrhizae under conditions of nutrient limitation (particularly phosphorus), low light, or drought stress. Fungi can influence allocation patterns through signaling molecules that effectively communicate [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]] and fungal demand to the host plant.

### Carbon Forms Transferred

The primary form of carbon transferred from plant to fungus is sucrose, which is converted to trehalose, mannitol, glycogen, or lipids within the fungal tissue. AMF store significant carbon as triacylglycerols (neutral lipids) in both intraradical and extraradical hyphae and in vesicles. EMF allocate substantial carbon to the production of [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]], organic acids, and hydrophobic compounds that facilitate [[ectomycorrhizal-mineral-weathering-phosphate-mobilization-gadd]] and soil exploration.

## Pathways of Mycorrhizal Carbon in Soil

### Fungal Respiration

A significant portion of plant-derived carbon allocated to mycorrhizal fungi is rapidly respired back to the atmosphere as CO₂. Maintenance respiration (supporting existing biomass) and growth respiration (producing new biomass) together account for roughly 50–70% of the carbon received by the fungal partner. This respiration flux is a major component of total soil respiration, particularly in forest ecosystems.

### Fungal Necromass and Soil Organic Matter

When mycorrhizal hyphae die and decompose, their biomass contributes to soil organic matter (SOM) pools. Mycorrhizal necromass is enriched in compounds that are relatively resistant to decomposition, including chitin (in cell walls), glomalin-related soil proteins (from AMF), and melanized cell-wall residues (particularly from EMF). These compounds contribute to the formation of stable soil organic carbon pools with turnover times ranging from years to centuries.

### Carbon Transfer to the Wider Soil Food Web

Mycorrhizal fungi are embedded in complex soil food webs. Their hyphae are grazed by collembola, mites, nematodes, and other microfauna, transferring mycorrhizal-derived carbon to higher trophic levels. Fungal exudates (organic acids, sugars, amino acids) stimulate bacterial activity in the mycorrhizosphere, supporting secondary carbon flows. Viruses and bacteria that infect mycorrhizal fungi also divert carbon through alternative pathways.

### Common Mycorrhizal Networks

Mycorrhizal fungi frequently form "common mycorrhizal networks" (CMNs) — shared hyphal connections linking multiple individual plants of the same or different species. Through CMNs, carbon can flow from one plant to another, though the [[lichen-biology-guide]] of this inter-plant carbon transfer remains debated. Evidence suggests that CMNs may facilitate carbon movement from dominant canopy trees to shaded understory seedlings, from nutrient-rich to nutrient-poor microsites, and potentially from dying plants to their neighbors. The carbon dynamics within CMNs add a layer of complexity to understanding mycorrhizal contributions to the carbon cycle.

### Seasonal and Phenological Dynamics

Mycorrhizal carbon flux is not constant throughout the year. In temperate and boreal forests, carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi peaks during periods of active root growth — typically spring and early summer — and declines during dormancy. Seasonal patterns of hyphal production and turnover create pulses of carbon input to soil that may not coincide with peaks in decomposer activity, creating temporal uncoupling that favors carbon stabilization.

## The Mycorrhizal Carbon Pump

Recent research has identified a "mycorrhizal carbon pump" analogous to the well-known biological pump in marine ecosystems. In this model, mycorrhizal fungi actively move carbon from the rhizosphere — where decomposition rates are high due to abundant labile substrates — into deeper soil horizons and into more recalcitrant molecular forms. This downward and molecular-level translocation effectively separates labile carbon from decomposer organisms, increasing the mean residence time of plant-derived carbon in soil.

### Depth Translocation

Ectomycorrhizal hyphae in particular extend into mineral soil horizons well below the organic-rich surface layers. Carbon deposited at depth through hyphal turnover, exudation, and [[staycare-fungal-organic-acid-production-metal-leaching]] enters soil environments with lower microbial activity and different physicochemical conditions that favor carbon stabilization through organo-mineral associations.

### Molecular Stabilization

The chitin, melanin, and proteinaceous compounds produced by mycorrhizal fungi form strong associations with soil [[fungal-community-profiling-rock-mineral-surfaces-gadd]] (iron and aluminum oxides, phyllosilicate clays). These organo-mineral associations protect organic molecules from enzymatic attack, dramatically slowing decomposition. The formation of stable aggregates around mycorrhizal hyphae and roots further physically protects occluded carbon.

## Priming Effects

Mycorrhizal activity can either accelerate or decelerate the decomposition of existing soil organic matter — a phenomenon known as the "priming effect." When mycorrhizal fungi release organic acids and enzymes to mine nutrients from SOM, they can simultaneously destabilize older organic matter pools, increasing CO₂ release (positive priming). Conversely, the stabilization of new carbon inputs through the mechanisms described above can effectively protect existing SOM (negative priming). The net effect appears to be context-dependent, varying with nutrient availability, fungal type, and soil properties.

## Implications for Climate Change

### Carbon Sequestration Potential

The mycorrhizal carbon pump has significant implications for climate change mitigation. If mycorrhizal fungi contribute disproportionately to stable soil carbon formation, then management practices that support mycorrhizal abundance and diversity — [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]], diverse crop rotations, minimal fertilizer inputs — could enhance [[mycorrhizal-fungi-soil-carbon-sequestration-by-type]]. However, the magnitude and stability of mycorrhizal-derived carbon pools remain active areas of research.

### Feedback Loops

Climate-driven changes in atmospheric CO₂ concentration, temperature, and precipitation patterns affect both plant productivity (altering carbon supply to mycorrhizae) and fungal physiology (altering carbon demand and processing). Shifts in [[mycorrhizal-effects-on-plant-community-composition]] — for example, boreal forest expansion with warming — change the dominant mycorrhizal type (from EMF to AMF or vice versa), fundamentally altering belowground carbon cycling pathways. Understanding these feedbacks is critical for predicting future carbon-climate interactions.
