# Holzer Pig Keeping and Paddock System

Pigs play a central role in the integrated farming system at the Krameterhof. Sepp Holzer raises old and endangered pig breeds in a paddock system that keeps all animals outside year-round. The pigs serve multiple functions: soil preparation, pest control, fertilization, and ultimately meat production. Their integration into the broader farm ecosystem exemplifies the [[holzer-alpine-farming-techniques]] of every element performing multiple functions.

## Old Pig Breeds

Holzer deliberately chooses heritage breeds that are well-adapted to outdoor living and resistant to disease. By raising endangered breeds, he also contributes to the conservation of genetic diversity in livestock.

**Mangalitza**, known as 'woolly pigs', have a thick layer of fat and produce exceptionally high-quality meat. Their thick, curly 'wool' protects them against cold in the harsh alpine winters of the Krameterhof, making them ideally suited to the climate without the need for heated shelter.

**Swabian-Hall Swine** are large-framed with distinctive black and white colouring. They are incredibly good-natured, extremely hardy, and produce very high quality meat. This breed is now endangered, and Holzer's keeping of them contributes to the preservation of an important genetic lineage.

**Duroc** pigs have a reddish coat and medium to large frames with an arched back. They are distinguished by their calmness and docility, and show strong resistance to stress. These temperament traits make them easier to manage in a free-range paddock system.

**Turopoije**, from the river Sava in Croatia, are excellent swimmers and well suited to pastures with water features. This breed is critically endangered, and Holzer's breeding program helps maintain the remaining genetic population.

## Pigs as Helpers

Pigs make pleasant and helpful workers in many respects. The soil can be greatly improved by their rooting and digging activity. When they search for food, they plough through the top layers of earth, loosening and aerating the soil. This natural tillage is particularly valuable on the Krameterhof, where much of the terrain is rough and stony, making it impossible to plough with conventional machinery.

It is easy to precisely direct the pigs by scattering loose feed in appropriate places, effectively using them as living ploughs that can be targeted to specific areas needing soil preparation. An agricultural research project conducted on the Krameterhof showed that plant diversity in areas grazed by pigs doubled compared to ungrazed control areas, demonstrating the positive ecological impact of their soil disturbance.

Pigs also make a large contribution to the regulation of snails and other pests. Movable pens can be used to place the pigs exactly where pest control is needed. Insects like cockchafers and chafers are controlled by the pigs' enthusiastic rooting activity, which disrupts their lifecycle by exposing grubs in the soil to predation by birds and other animals.

## The Paddock System

On the Krameterhof, all of the land is managed as part of a paddock system. This means that all livestock are kept outside the whole year round. Animals that have their natural needs fulfilled remain healthy and happy, grow well, and provide good offspring. The paddock system mimics the natural grazing patterns of wild animals, preventing overgrazing while ensuring regular soil disturbance and fertilization.

Pigs do not have sweat glands, so they have to rely on bodies of water and wallowing to regulate their body temperature in hot weather. When they wallow, the pigs cover themselves in a layer of mud which protects light-skinned breeds from sunburn and helps control external parasites. Access to water for wallowing is therefore an essential design consideration in the paddock system, connecting to [[sepp-holzer-permaculture-and-rebel-farming]] approach.

## Fencing and Management

The paddock system requires robust fencing to contain the pigs, which are strong and inquisitive animals. Holzer uses electric fencing and permanent fencing combinations to create paddocks of appropriate size. Regular rotation between paddocks prevents overgrazing and allows vegetation to recover. The rotation schedule varies with season, stocking density, and the condition of the vegetation in each paddock.

## Cyclical Nature

Pigs serve as a good example of the cyclical nature of [[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]]. The cycle works as follows: the soil is prepared and fertilised by the pigs' rooting and manure; plants grow lush and healthily in the enriched soil; windfall fruit, roots, and forage crops serve as feed; snails and unwanted insects are eaten as part of the pigs' natural foraging behaviour; and ultimately there is high-quality bacon from humanely kept animals that have lived their natural lives outdoors.

This integration of livestock into crop production eliminates the need for synthetic fertilizers, mechanical tillage, and chemical pest control. The pigs perform all of these functions while producing a valuable food product. Nothing is wasted, and every output of one element becomes the input for another.

## Integration with the Broader Farm System

The paddock system is not an isolated enterprise but integrated into the broader Krameterhof farm. Pig manure fertilizes orchards and crop fields. Pigs clear and prepare land for new plantings. Their rooting activity incorporates organic matter into the soil, reducing the need for composting or mulching. The connection between the pig system and [[holzer-raised-bed-design-principles]] is direct: land that has been prepared by pigs can be immediately planted without further soil work.

## Breeding Considerations

Holzer's approach to breeding emphasizes natural selection and minimal intervention. Breeding animals are chosen for hardiness, temperament, and mothering ability rather than for maximum growth rate alone. Piglets are born outdoors and raised by their mothers in the paddock system, which produces robust animals well-adapted to the farm environment.

The avoidance of indoor farrowing crates and concentrated feeds aligns with [[holzer-livestock-philosophy-pig-breeds]] of working with nature rather than against it. Breeding sows have access to shelter but are not confined, and they construct natural nests from available materials when farrowing.

## Winter Management

The Krameterhof's alpine location means pigs must cope with cold winters, deep snow, and frozen ground. Holzer's heritage breeds are specifically selected for their ability to thrive in these conditions. The Mangalitza's thick woolly coat provides excellent insulation, while other breeds develop dense winter coats. Shelter is provided through simple, open-fronted structures that protect from wind and precipitation while allowing free movement. Deep bedding of straw or hay provides additional insulation and allows pigs to root and nest naturally.

Access to water for wallowing is replaced by access to snow in winter, which pigs will eat for hydration. Feed requirements increase during cold months as pigs burn more calories maintaining body temperature. Holzer supplements forage with stored hay, root vegetables, and grain during the winter months when pasture is unavailable.

## Disease Prevention

Holzer's outdoor system has a natural advantage in disease prevention compared to intensive indoor operations. The constant access to fresh air, sunlight, and space reduces the transmission of respiratory and skin diseases that plague confinement operations. The diverse diet, including fresh forage, roots, and soil organisms, supports a robust immune system. The paddock rotation system breaks parasite lifecycles by moving pigs away from contaminated ground before pathogens can build to problematic levels.

## Economic Considerations

While the paddock system requires more land per animal than confinement operations, Holzer argues that the economic equation favours outdoor production when all costs are accounted for. There are no veterinary bills for the routine health problems of confinement systems, no costs for heating, ventilation, or waste disposal infrastructure, and no need for purchased antibiotics. The manure is distributed directly onto productive land rather than becoming a waste disposal problem. The end product — high-quality meat from humanely raised, genetically valuable animals — commands premium prices in local and specialty markets.

## Paddock Rotation Schedules

Holzer does not prescribe fixed rotation intervals, as the optimal timing depends on stocking density, season, vegetation recovery rate, and the specific objective for each paddock. However, the general principle is that pigs should remain in any single paddock only long enough to achieve the desired soil disturbance without destroying the vegetation completely. In practice during the active growing season, this typically means moving pigs every 1-4 weeks, depending on paddock size and the number of animals.

A common pattern involves a series of 8-12 paddocks arranged to allow each area a minimum recovery period of 6-8 weeks before pigs return. During this recovery window, disturbed vegetation regenerates from roots and seed bank, manure breaks down and integrates into the soil, and parasite lifecycles are interrupted. The rotation is adjusted dynamically — areas with heavy rooting damage are given longer recovery, while robust paddocks with rapid regrowth may be returned to sooner.

For land preparation purposes, the rotation schedule is reversed: pigs are concentrated on the target area for a longer period — typically 4-8 weeks — to achieve thorough soil cultivation, then moved to the next area. The heavily disturbed paddock is then left to rest and can be directly planted without any further tillage, as the pigs have effectively performed all necessary soil preparation.

## Root Harvesting Behavior and Soil Aeration

Pigs are omnivorous foragers with a powerful rooting instinct driven by their highly sensitive snout. When they root through soil in search of roots, tubers, grubs, and other subsurface food, they perform several beneficial soil functions simultaneously. The physical disruption of compacted soil layers improves water infiltration and root penetration for subsequent plantings. Aeration from rooting increases oxygen availability to soil microorganisms, accelerating the decomposition of organic matter and the mineralization of nutrients.

The rooting behavior also incorporates surface organic matter — leaf litter, crop residues, and weed biomass — into the soil profile, where it becomes accessible to decomposer organisms. This natural inversion of the soil surface is far less destructive than mechanical ploughing because it is selective rather than uniform: pigs concentrate their rooting where food rewards are highest, leaving areas with less desirable vegetation relatively undisturbed. This selective disturbance creates a heterogeneous soil surface that supports greater plant diversity than uniformly tilled land.

Research conducted at the Krameterhof confirmed that pig-rooted areas showed doubled plant diversity compared to ungrazed controls. The disturbed patches provide germination sites for seeds that require exposed mineral soil, while undisturbed patches preserve existing vegetation. This mosaic pattern mimics the natural disturbance regimes created by wild boar in forest ecosystems.

## Integration with Crop Planting Cycles

The pig paddock system is directly synchronized with the crop planting calendar on the Krameterhof. In early spring, pigs are moved into areas designated for [[holzer-raised-bed-design-principles]] or field crop establishment. Their rooting activity during the 4-8 weeks before planting breaks up compacted soil, incorporates residual organic matter, and deposits manure that provides a slow-release fertility source. Once the pigs are rotated out, the prepared land is immediately planted — often with root crops, potatoes, or pumpkin that benefit from the loosened soil profile.

For orchard establishment, pigs are run through future fruit tree planting areas in the autumn before spring planting. Their deep rooting opens soil profiles to the depth needed for tree root establishment, and their manure provides the fertility that young trees need during their critical establishment years. This preparation replaces the heavy machinery and imported compost that conventional orchard establishment requires.

A particularly elegant integration involves intercropping within pig paddocks. Holzer sows peas, beans, or sweetcorn in areas where he wants pigs to concentrate their rooting activity. The pigs are attracted to these crops and focus their disturbance accordingly, allowing the farmer to precisely direct soil preparation to specific zones without fencing.

## Infrastructure Requirements

The paddock system requires specific infrastructure that differs significantly from conventional pig housing. Robust fencing is the primary requirement — pigs are strong, intelligent, and persistent in their efforts to explore beyond boundaries. Holzer uses a combination of permanent fencing (wooden or metal posts with wire) for paddock boundaries and portable electric fencing for subdividing paddocks and directing pig movement. Electric fencing must be powerful enough to deliver a convincing shock through the pig's tough skin and layer of fat.

Water access is essential year-round. In summer, pigs need wallowing areas deep enough for full body immersion — Holzer designs these as natural ponds connected to his broader [[sepp-holzer-permaculture-and-rebel-farming]] system. In winter, access to running water or snow is necessary for hydration. Water infrastructure must be pig-proof, as pigs will destroy inadequately protected pipes, troughs, and fittings.

Shelter structures on the Krameterhof are simple, open-fronted buildings that protect from wind and precipitation without enclosing the animals. These are oriented to block prevailing winter winds while allowing sun exposure. Deep bedding of straw or hay provides floor insulation and allows natural nesting behavior. Feed storage structures must be secure against pig access — pigs will readily break into inadequately protected stores.

## Stocking Density and Carrying Capacity

Determining appropriate stocking density is critical for the paddock system's success. Too many animals per unit area leads to overgrazing, soil compaction, and parasite buildup, while too few animals fails to achieve adequate soil disturbance and nutrient cycling. Holzer recommends approximately 0.5-1 hectare per sow with litter on productive alpine pasture, though this varies with terrain quality, vegetation type, and the season. Steeper, rockier terrain requires lower stocking densities because vegetation recovers more slowly and the pigs' rooting exposes more mineral soil.

Carrying capacity is not fixed but varies with the season. Spring and summer support higher stocking densities due to rapid pasture growth, while autumn and winter require reducing animal numbers or providing supplementary feed. Holzer manages this seasonal variation by adjusting paddock sizes — combining paddocks in summer to provide adequate area, and concentrating animals in smaller, more sheltered paddocks in winter. This flexible approach to stocking density is fundamentally different from conventional systems that maintain fixed animal numbers regardless of seasonal conditions.
