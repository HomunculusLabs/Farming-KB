### City Stable Manure
Available around New York in any quantity at reasonable rates. Market
gardeners hauled vegetables to market and brought back manure. Agents
specialized in supplying selected pure horse manure in quantities from
500 lb bales to unlimited loads. The 350-500 lb bales were convenient
for small growers, containing only clean horse manure.

### Cow Manure
Falconer tried it repeatedly without finding any advantage over plain
horse manure. His chief objection was that it was a breeding place for
pernicious bugs, grubs, and earthworms. However, cow manure was a
requisite ingredient in spawn manufacture.

### Substitutes and Supplements
- **German peat moss**: Used as bedding; great absorbent retaining urine.
- **Sawdust stable manure**: Fermented well and produced good mushrooms,
  but Falconer preferred any other manure if available.
- **Tree leaves**: Oak leaves were best; coniferous leaves useless. Leaf
  beds should be 20-30 inches deep (vs. 9-15 inches for manure).
- **Spent hops**: Usable as a warming base layer under horse manure, but
  never alone or near the surface where spawn must travel through.

## Preparation of the Manure (Chapter VIII)

### General Process
1. Accumulate manure in a pile (not too large), straw and all, preventing
   violent heating or "fire-fanging."
2. If heating too fast, turn over loosely, sprinkle with water, spread out,
   and when cooled, tread firmly and pile again.
3. When enough has accumulated, turn it over, shaking loosely and mixing.
4. Throw aside dry strawy parts, white "burnt" manure, and all extraneous
   matter (sticks, stones, old tins, bones, leather, rags).
5. Retain all wet straw -- it contained valuable urine.
6. If too dry, sprinkle freely with water. If too wet, spread thin to dry.
7. Throw into a compact oblong pile 3-4 feet high, tread down.
8. Leave until fermentation starts briskly (2-3 days in fall, 6-10 in
   winter).
9. Turn again, shaking thoroughly, keeping outside material inside and
   vice versa. Tread down firmly. This compaction reduced the number of
   required turnings.
10. Continue turnings until the manure has lost its tendency to heat
    violently and its hot rank smell is gone (usually about 3 weeks).

### Critical Principle
At every turning, ammonia was wasted -- "the most potent food of the
mushroom." The aim was to get along with as few turnings as possible,
while never allowing any part to burn.

### Proper Condition
Squeeze a handful tight: it should be unctuous enough to hold together
but dry enough that no water could be squeezed out. "Far better fail on
the side of dryness than on that of wetness."

- Beds that never exceeded 100-110 degrees still produced excellent crops.

### Reviving Cold Beds
If a bed would not rise above 75-80 degrees: break it up, add fresh
horse droppings, mix thoroughly, remake. Or sprinkle with barnyard urine,
fork surface 2-3 inches, compact lightly, and cover with straw until
heat rises. Saltpeter or nitrate of soda (1 oz per 3 gallons) encouraged
mycelium spread after spawning.

## Loam for the Beds (Chapter XII)

### Best Loam
Rich, fresh, mellow soil from the top spit of a pasture field, broken up
with spade or fork, used rough (never sifted) for [[casing-layer-principles]]. Ordinary garden
soil was used most frequently with satisfactory results. Roadside dirt,
well-rotted for a few months, was also good. Sandy soil was inferior.
Peat soil failed to give good returns. Heavy clayey loam was excellent
if kept equably moist, but cracked when dry, breaking mycelium threads.
