c4 and [[comparison-c3-c4-and-cam-photosynthesis-pathways]] are evolutionary adaptations that concentrate
CO₂ around Rubisco, suppressing photorespiration and improving
efficiency under conditions that disadvantage standard C3 photosynthesis.
C4 plants achieve this through spatial separation — initial CO₂ fixation
in mesophyll cells, Calvin cycle in bundle sheath cells — while CAM
plants separate the same processes temporally, fixing CO₂ at night and
running the Calvin cycle by day. Together they represent remarkable
convergent evolution in plant biology.

## C4 Photosynthesis Mechanism

C4 plants feature Kranz anatomy (German for "wreath"): vascular bundles
surrounded by thick-walled bundle sheath cells enclosed by an outer ring
of mesophyll cells. CO₂ enters through stomata and is fixed by PEP
carboxylase (PEPC) in mesophyll cells, which has higher CO₂ affinity
than Rubisco and no oxygenase activity. PEPC combines CO₂ (as HCO₃⁻)
with phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to form oxaloacetate (OAA), a 4-carbon
acid — hence "C4 photosynthesis." OAA is converted to malate or
aspartate and transported to bundle sheath cells via plasmodesmata,
where decarboxylation releases concentrated CO₂ around Rubisco for the
Calvin cycle. The resulting pyruvate returns to mesophyll cells and is
regenerated to PEP by pyruvate phosphate dikinase (PPDK) using ATP.
This pump raises bundle sheath CO₂ to 10-60× ambient, effectively
suppressing photorespiration. The pathway costs 5 ATP per CO₂ versus
3 in C3.

## Three C4 Biochemical Subtypes

Three decarboxylation pathways define C4 subtypes. In NADP-ME species
(maize, sugarcane, sorghum), malate is decarboxylated in bundle sheath
chloroplasts by NADP-malic enzyme; these have reduced PSII in bundle
sheath cells, creating a low-O₂ environment. In NAD-ME species (millet,
amaranth), aspartate is converted to malate and decarboxylated in
bundle sheath mitochondria by NAD-malic enzyme. In PCK species
(Guinea grass), aspartate is decarboxylated by PEP carboxykinase in
both chloroplasts and mitochondria. Some species use mixed or flexible
subtypes. PCK is often considered a secondary subtype operating
alongside NADP-ME or NAD-ME pathways.

## Evolution of C4 Photosynthesis

C4 has evolved over 60 independent times in angiosperms across 18-19
families including Poaceae, Amaranthaceae, Chenopodiaceae, Euphorbiaceae,
and Cyperaceae — among the most spectacular convergent evolution known.
The pathway proceeds through C3-C4 intermediates: species like
Moricandia and Neurachne concentrate photorespiratory CO₂ by confining
glycine decarboxylase to bundle sheath mitochondria. The genus Flaveria
spans the full C3-to-C4 spectrum. C4 first appeared 30-35 million years
ago during the Oligocene when declining atmospheric CO₂ (from ~1000 to
~200-300 ppm) created strong selection pressure. C4 grasslands expanded
dramatically 7-5 million years ago in the late Miocene.

## Major C4 Crops and Ecology

Major C4 crops include maize (~1.2 billion tonnes/year), sugarcane
(~1.8 billion tonnes, ~80% of global sugar), sorghum (5th most
important cereal, drought-tolerant), and pearl millet (critical Sahel
and Indian subcontinent staple). C4 plants excel at high temperatures
(optimal 30-40°C) and high light, achieving 40-80 μmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹
versus 20-30 in C3, with 2-3× the water-use efficiency and improved
nitrogen use efficiency (less Rubisco protein needed). However, the
higher ATP cost makes them less competitive in shade or cool
environments; at temperatures below 15-20°C, C3 quantum yields exceed
C4. Only ~3% of plant species use C4 photosynthesis.

## CAM Photosynthesis Mechanism

Crassulacean Acid Metabolism separates CO₂ fixation temporally using
the same key enzymes as C4 — PEP carboxylase for initial fixation and
Rubisco for the Calvin cycle. Four phases operate (Osmond, 1978).
Phase I (night): stomata open, PEPC fixes CO₂ into malate stored in
large vacuoles at concentrations causing nocturnal acidification (pH
drops from ~6.5 to ~3.0). Phase II (early morning): transitional
period with some direct C3 fixation as light increases. Phase III
(midday): stomata close, malate is released from vacuoles and
decarboxylated by NAD-ME, NADP-ME, or PCK, releasing CO₂ for Rubisco
while minimizing transpirational water loss. Phase IV (late afternoon):
stomata may briefly reopen for additional CO₂ uptake. This temporal
separation gives CAM plants 10-100× the water-use efficiency of C3
plants, but lower maximum daily carbon gain (5-15 μmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹).
