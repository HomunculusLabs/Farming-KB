# Permaculture Built Environment Design

Updated: 2026-18

Permaculture built environment design applies ecological design principles to buildings and settlements, treating the built environment as an integrated component of the broader ecosystem rather than an isolated technical system.

## Passive Solar Design

Passive solar design is the foundation of permaculture building. Buildings are oriented, shaped, and constructed to capture, store, and distribute solar energy without mechanical systems:

- Orientation: In the southern hemisphere, living spaces face north for maximum winter sun. In the northern hemisphere, they face south.
- Glazing: Glass areas on the sun-facing side capture solar gain in winter. Eaves are designed to exclude summer sun while admitting winter sun.
- Thermal mass: Dense materials (concrete, stone, earth, water) absorb solar heat during the day and release it slowly at night, stabilizing temperature.
- Insulation: Walls, floors, and ceilings are insulated to reduce heat loss in winter and heat gain in summer.
- Ventilation: Natural cross-ventilation and stack-effect ventilation reduce cooling loads without mechanical systems.

The result is a building that maintains comfortable temperatures with minimal or no mechanical heating and cooling, dramatically reducing energy demand.

## Bioregional Architecture

Building design must respond to local climate, materials, and culture rather than importing standardized solutions:

- Cold climates: Compact designs with high thermal mass, earth-sheltered elements, minimal glazing on cold sides. Northern European ecological building has returned to traditional materials -- rough-sawn local timber and sod roofs -- driven partly by sick building syndrome concerns.
- Humid tropics: Lightweight structures on poles for airflow, deep overhanging roofs for rain and sun protection, materials resistant to termites and mold.
- Temperate regions: Moderate thermal mass, good insulation, adjustable shading, rainwater integration.
- Arid regions: Thick earth walls for thermal mass, small windows, courtyard layouts, evaporative cooling.
- Continental climates: Subterranean or earth-sheltered designs for thermal stability.

## Materials

Permaculture building prioritizes materials that are:

- Locally sourced: Reducing transport energy and supporting local economies
- Low embodied energy: Requiring less energy to produce than alternatives (timber vs steel, earth vs concrete)
- Non-toxic: Not off-gassing chemicals that harm indoor air quality
- Durable: Lasting decades or centuries without replacement
- Recyclable: Can be returned to biological or technical cycles at end of life
- Beautiful: Supporting psychological wellbeing as well as function

Natural building materials include timber (especially locally grown, naturally durable species), straw bale, rammed earth, cob, light earth, bamboo, stone, and natural insulation materials (wool, hemp, cellulose).

## Integration with Food Systems

Permaculture buildings are integrated with food production:

- Greenhouses attached to the sun-facing wall for food production and space heating
- Rainwater tanks integrated into building design for garden irrigation
- Greywater systems recycling household water through biological treatment to garden irrigation
- Chicken coops and animal housing integrated with garden zones
- Root cellars and food storage spaces built into building foundations
- Herb gardens and kitchen gardens immediately adjacent to kitchen doors
- Trellises and espalier fruit trees on building walls

## Retrofitting Existing Buildings

Most permaculture building work involves retrofitting existing structures rather than new construction:

- Adding insulation to walls, ceilings, and floors
- Installing double-glazing or secondary glazing on windows
- Adding thermal mass where it is lacking
- Sealing against drafts while maintaining ventilation
- Installing rainwater harvesting systems
- Adding solar hot water and photovoltaic systems
- Creating outdoor living spaces that extend the usable area seasonally
- Converting garages, sheds, and underutilized spaces to productive uses

The principle of progressive retrofit -- making incremental improvements over time rather than demolishing and rebuilding -- is both more practical and more energy-efficient.

## Energy Systems Integration

Buildings in permaculture design are integrated with energy systems:

- Solar hot water systems on sun-facing roofs
- Photovoltaic panels with battery storage
- Wood heating using sustainably harvested fuel from the property
- Rocket stoves and masonry heaters for efficient combustion
- Biogas digesters for converting organic waste to cooking gas

## Waste Systems

Permaculture buildings incorporate waste management:

- Composting toilets that convert human waste to safe soil amendment
- Greywater treatment through constructed wetlands or mulch basins
- Kitchen waste composting systems integrated with garden zones
- Recycling infrastructure built into kitchen and utility design

## Water Integration

Water is managed as an integral part of building design:

- Roof catchment systems sized to rainfall patterns and household demand
- First-flush diverters to ensure water quality
- Storage tanks positioned for gravity feed to garden and house
- Overflow systems directing excess to garden swales or infiltration zones
- Greywater treatment and reuse systems reducing fresh water demand
