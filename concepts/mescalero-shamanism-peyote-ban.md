## Mescalero Apache Shamanism and Peyote Prohibition

## Overview

The Mescalero Apache of southern New Mexico represent a historically significant case study in the relationship between shamanism, hallucinogenic plant use, and social control. Unlike many other indigenous North American groups who incorporated peyote (*Lophophora williamsii*) into enduring religious practices, the Mescalero Apache explicitly abandoned and subsequently prohibited peyote use in shamanistic rituals. This prohibition offers insight into how psychoactive substances can destabilize social structures and why communities may reject otherwise culturally significant tools.

## Shaman vs. Pseudoshaman Personality Profiles

Psychological research conducted by Boyer, Klopfer, and colleagues among the Mescalero Apache distinguished sharply between genuine shamans and pseudoshamans based on Rorschach testing and clinical observation:

- **Shamans** displayed creative characteristics, a high degree of reality testing potential, and the capacity to regress in the service of the ego. They were found to be less hysterical than their culture-mates and approached normality more closely when viewed heteroculturally within Devereux"s framework.
- **Pseudoshamans**, by contrast, had impoverished personalities, variable reality testing, and were generally scorned by the community. They were employed only by the most suggestible individuals.

Shamans were able to use imposture convincingly whereas pseudoshamans could not. Both genuine shamans and pseudoshamans were considered by Devereux to be neurotic or psychotic relative to a hypothetical psychological normal, but Boyer clarified that they were not autocultural deviants — they fulfilled normative roles within Apache society.

## Why Peyote Was Banned

### The Social Dynamics of Peyote Camps

The introduction of peyote into Mescalero Apache shamanistic practice created a fundamentally destabilizing social situation. Peyote camps involved the **simultaneous assumption of authority by more than one practitioner**, each vying for supremacy of power and status. This was a foreign element in Apache shamanistic procedures, which traditionally centered on a single practitioner's relationship with the supernatural.

### Aggression and Drug-Induced Regression

Apache child-rearing practices engendered considerable hostility. Aggression was institutionally directed toward outsiders, witches, ghosts, and cultural bogies in an effort to repress hostile impulses originally aimed at familial and societal members. This repression was never fully successful.

Under the influence of peyote:
- The unstable repression of hostile impulses toward parent and sibling surrogates became overt
- Drug-induced regression caused aggression to be released in its earlier, childish form, directed at those closest to the individual
- Bloodshed and feuds occurred during peyote ceremonies
- The Apache "wisely banned the peyote camps"

### Projection of Evil onto Peyote

The Mescalero ascribed the quality of **evil** to peyote power — a basic deviation from the broader Apache conceptualization of power (*biyáti* or *bik'égó*), which was normally understood as having no intrinsic moral properties. This ascription of evil served to:
- Deny the presence of intragroup hostility
- Project murderous wishes onto the external agent (peyote)
- Legitimize the prohibition by framing it as protection from an inherently dangerous force

Notably, only peyote and owl power were considered evil in themselves. In both cases, murderous wishes were projected onto the power in question.

## Dual Fears Surrounding Peyote

The Mescalero, Chiricahuas, and Lipans articulated two explicit reasons for fearing peyote:

1. **Evil power**: Peyote was believed to have an intrinsic evil power that would drive users to commit evil acts. This represents a projection of intrapersonal asocial tendencies onto the plant.

2. **Hallucinations**: Peyote was feared for causing visual aberrations and strange qualities of movement — essentially a loss of accurate perception and judgment. This may represent a displacement of fear of losing control over destructive urges onto fear of losing perceptual accuracy.

## Exceptions to the Prohibition

### Illicit Shamanic Use

Two powerful shamans — Black Eyes and Ancient One — continued using peyote in illicit practices despite the ban. Both were considered very powerful and were feared by most Apaches. Ancient One was said to have killed many individuals through apparently supernatural means. They likely continued peyote use to:
- Demonstrate contempt for fellow Mescaleros
- Command greater recompense and respect for performing illegitimate rituals
- Exploit the social transgression for material gain

### Pseudoshaman Use

Two pseudoshamans also used peyote in their rituals, despite being generally scorned. They confided while intoxicated that they doubted their own claims of power and consciously sought to deceive others. Their peyote use appeared to be an attempt to raise their own esteem and potentially become genuinely powerful by exploiting hallucinogenic effects.

## Peyote vs. Alcohol: A Comparative Puzzle

A notable paradox exists in Mescalero culture: alcohol use was socially permissible despite producing effects parallel to peyote (hallucinosis, violent actions, blurred perception), while peyote was strictly proscribed. The key difference lies in the **ceremonial context**:

- **Peyote** was incorporated into the shamanistic ritual complex, where multiple practitioners competed for supernatural authority, fostering interpersonal conflict
- **Alcohol** was never culturally acceptable in ceremonial contexts; aggression during drinking parties was channeled outside the personally mediated world of the supernatural

## Contemporary Relevance

Boyer raised prescient questions about future Apache involvement with hallucinogens as their use became commonplace among adolescents and young adults throughout the United States. The key questions remain:
- Would the ban extend to other hallucinatory agents?
- Might familiarity with some hallucinogens pave the way for redefining peyote, especially given the diminished commitment of present-day Apaches to the shamanistic supernatural belief system?

These questions highlight the dynamic nature of cultural attitudes toward psychoactive substances and the potential for rapid sociocultural change in drug prohibition norms.
