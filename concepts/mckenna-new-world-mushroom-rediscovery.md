# The Rediscovery of the New World Mushroom

In *Food of the Gods*, Terence McKenna traces the chain of events by which psychoactive mushrooms — suppressed in Europe for millennia and surviving only in remote Mesoamerican communities — were rediscovered by Western science in the [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]]. This rediscovery, culminating in the identification of psilocybin by Albert Hofmann in 1958, set the stage for [[powell-paradigm-shifting-and-the-psychedelic-revolution]] of the 1960s. McKenna frames this history as the latest chapter in humanity's long relationship with what he calls the "mushroom cult."

## Teonanacatl: The Flesh of the Gods

The Spanish conquistadors and missionaries who arrived in Mesoamerica in the sixteenth century encountered indigenous peoples who consumed psychoactive mushrooms in [[entheogenic-folk-religious-ceremonies]]. The Aztecs called these mushrooms *teonanacatl* — literally "flesh of the gods" — and their use was deeply embedded in the religious and cultural life of the region. The Spanish, committed to the eradication of indigenous practices they associated with devil worship, suppressed the mushroom ceremonies with particular brutality. The Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún documented the ceremonies in detail while simultaneously participating in their suppression.

For nearly four centuries, teonanacatl remained a mystery to European scholarship. Some scholars speculated that the Aztec accounts referred to peyote or to morning glory seeds rather than mushrooms. The mycologist William Safford argued in the early twentieth century that no such mushrooms existed and that teonanacatl was merely a figment of colonial misunderstanding. The mushroom cult was believed to have been completely extinguished by colonial persecution — a belief that would prove spectacularly wrong.

## Blas Pablo Reko and Richard Evans Schultes

The modern rediscovery began with the work of the Mexican anthropologist Blas Pablo Reko, who collected specimens of psychoactive mushrooms from indigenous communities in the 1930s. Reko obtained his mushroom sample from Roberto Weitlander, a European engineer working in Mexico. In 1938, a small group including Weitlander's daughter and anthropologist Jean Basset Johnson became the first non-indigenous people to attend a nightlong mushroom ceremony, or *velada*.

Reko forwarded his samples to Harvard, where they came to the attention of the young ethnobotanist Richard Evans Schultes. Schultes had been a medical student until he encountered Klaeber's work on mescaline. He believed Reko's mushroom might be the mysterious teonanacatl described by the Spanish chroniclers, and in 1939 published "The Identification of Teonanacatl, a Narcotic Basidiomycete of the Aztecs," correctly solving an enigma that had puzzled Mesoamerican scholars for centuries.

The following year, Schultes accompanied Reko to the village of Huatla de Jimenez in the Sierra Mazatecan highlands, where specimens of psychoactive mushrooms were collected and forwarded to Harvard. But larger forces intervened — as the world slipped into war, ethnobotanical research slowed to a stop. Reko retired, and Schultes accepted an assignment to the Amazon Basin to study rubber extraction for the U.S. government's wartime Office of Strategic Services.

Schultes's wartime expedition to the Amazon proved to be one of the most productive ethnobotanical expeditions in history. Over the course of twelve years in the Colombian Amazon, he documented hundreds of plant uses by indigenous peoples, collected thousands of botanical specimens, and laid the groundwork for the modern discipline of ethnobotany. His work on curare, ayahuasca, and other psychoactive plants expanded the map of human-plant relationships far beyond anything known before. Though the mushroom question would be taken up by others after the war, Schultes's identification of teonanacatl remained the foundational achievement.

## Albert Hofmann and the Invention of LSD

Even as ethnobotanical research stalled, a fundamental breakthrough occurred in Switzerland. In 1938, Albert Hofmann was engaged in pharmaceutical research at Sandoz Laboratories in Basel, working with vasoconstricting substances derived from ergot. He synthesized d-lysergic acid diethylamide tartrate — LSD-25 — but the compound was cataloged and placed into storage, where it remained for five years.

On April 16, 1943, Hofmann absorbed an unknown dose of LSD through his skin and experienced its extraordinary effects. His account of that day — the remarkable restlessness, the dreamlike condition, the "uninterrupted stream of fantastic pictures, extraordinary shapes with intense, kaleidoscopic play of colors" — became one of the most famous narratives in the history of pharmacology. The discovery of a megahallucinogen active in the microgram range opened an entirely new chapter in humanity's relationship with psychoactive substances.

## Wasson and the Life Magazine Revelation

In 1957, Valentina and Gordon Wasson published their now-famous article in *Life* magazine announcing the discovery of the psilocybin mushroom complex to the general public. A New York investment banker by profession, Wasson was well acquainted with the establishment and turned to his friend Henry Luce, publisher of *Life*, for a public forum. The article's tone — fair, detailed, and scientifically minded — contrasted sharply with the hysteria and distortion that would later characterize media coverage of psychedelics.

Wasson's journey to the Mazatec village of Huatla de Jimenez, where he became one of the first outsiders to participate in a velada under the guidance of the curandera Maria Sabina, was the culmination of years of research into the role of mushrooms in human culture. His earlier work on the identity of the Vedic Soma — proposing that it was the fly agaric mushroom, *[[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]* — had established him as the leading figure in ethnomycology.

The Wassons had first become interested in mushrooms through their cross-cultural study of mycophagy — the practice of eating mushrooms. They observed that societies could be broadly divided into "mycophilic" cultures that prized mushrooms and "mycophobic" cultures that regarded them with fear and disgust. Their research suggested this division had deep historical roots, potentially connected to the suppression of mushroom cults in antiquity. The *Life* article, titled "Seeking the Magic Mushroom," reached millions of readers and introduced the concept of sacred psychoactive mushrooms to mainstream Western consciousness for the first time.

## The Isolation of Psilocybin

The chemical identification of the active compounds was completed by Albert Hofmann, who made his second major contribution to psychedelic pharmacology by chemically isolating psilocybin and determining its structure in 1958. In the short space of a dozen years, from 1947 to 1960, the major indole hallucinogens — LSD, psilocybin, and DMT — were characterized, purified, and investigated.

Hofmann's identification of psilocybin confirmed that the Mazatec mushrooms belonged primarily to the genus *Psilocybe*, with [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] as the principal active compounds. The discovery also revealed a striking pharmacological kinship between these mushrooms and LSD — both were tryptamine derivatives acting on [[psilocybin-pharmacology-and-serotonin-receptors]] in the brain, a finding that deepened the biological understanding of how psychoactive plants interact with human consciousness.

Steven Szara's synthesis of DMT in 1956 added another piece to the puzzle. DMT remains the most powerful and short-acting of the known hallucinogens; when smoked, intoxication peaks in about two minutes and abates over ten. Szara's discovery that DMT, normally inactive orally, becomes highly psychoactive when combined with MAO inhibitors paralleled the indigenous Amazonian discovery of ayahuasca — a combination of DMT-containing plants with harmine-rich *Banisteriopsis caapi* vine — by thousands of years.

## LSD and the Psychedelic Sixties

McKenna argues that the sudden introduction of a powerful deconditioning agent like LSD into Western society had effects comparable to the original impact of [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-emerging-trend-europe]] on early human consciousness. LSD is unique among drugs in the power of its dose range — detectable at fifty micrograms, ten thousand doses could theoretically be obtained from a single gram. It is odorless, colorless, and mixable in liquids; hundreds of doses could be concealed under a postage stamp. Prison walls and national borders were no barrier.

The sudden availability of a substance that dissolved cultural conditioning and forced reform of community values created what McKenna calls a "mass defection from community values, especially values based on a dominator hierarchy accustomed to suppressing consciousness and awareness." The political and cultural upheavals of the 1960s were, in his reading, an inevitable consequence of introducing a deconditioning agent into a society built on hierarchy and control.

McKenna notes the bitter irony that the very substance which might have helped [[eleusinian-mysteries-psychoactive-fungi-western-civilization]] confront its own cultural blind spots was simultaneously co-opted by institutions of control. The CIA's MK-Ultra program experimented with LSD as a tool for interrogation and mind control, while the military explored its potential as a chemical weapon. The suppression that followed — the scheduling of LSD, psilocybin, and other psychedelics as Schedule I substances in 1970 — was, in McKenna's analysis, a replay of the suppression of the mushroom cult in sixteenth-century Mesoamerica.

## Legacy

The chain of rediscovery — from Reko's first specimens through Schultes's taxonomic identification, Wasson's popular revelation, and Hofmann's chemical isolation — transformed the relationship between Western civilization and psychoactive mushrooms. The knowledge that had been suppressed for four hundred years was not merely recovered but amplified: psilocybin was now a known chemical compound, reproducible in the laboratory, and its effects could be studied with the tools of modern pharmacology.

McKenna viewed this rediscovery as having profound implications beyond chemistry. The fact that a mushroom ritual survived four centuries of systematic suppression suggested to him that the mushroom-human relationship was not merely cultural but archetypal — rooted in something deeper than social practice. The Mazatec curanderas who preserved the velada tradition had maintained, in his view, a direct line to a form of consciousness that Western civilization had lost when it rejected the mushroom cult of antiquity.

## Wasson's Soma Hypothesis and Ethnomycology

Before his Mexican expedition, Gordon Wasson had spent decades investigating the identity of the Vedic Soma — the sacred plant deified in the Rig Veda, whose ritual preparation was central to ancient Indo-Iranian religion. Wasson proposed that Soma was the fly agaric mushroom, *Amanita muscaria*, a hypothesis that generated intense debate among Indologists, mycologists, and historians of religion. His 1968 book *Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality* marshaled linguistic, ethnographic, and pharmacological evidence for this identification, arguing that the mushroom's psychoactive properties — including its capacity to produce visionary experiences, altered perception of scale, and sensations of flying — matched the Vedic descriptions of Soma's effects.

While Wasson's Soma hypothesis remains controversial and is not accepted by most mainstream Indologists, his work established ethnomycology as a serious academic discipline. The broader methodological lesson — that psychoactive plants and fungi may have played central roles in the development of human religion and culture — has influenced fields from archaeology to comparative religion. Wasson's interdisciplinary approach, combining botanical knowledge with linguistic analysis, historical research, and direct ethnographic experience, set a standard for the study of psychoactive substances in cultural context that continues to inform scholarship today.

## Schultes's Legacy and the Future of Ethnobotany

Richard Evans Schultes's contributions extended far beyond the identification of teonanacatl. His twelve years in the Colombian Amazon (1941-1953) produced one of the most comprehensive ethnobotanical surveys ever conducted. He documented the preparation and use of ayahuasca (Banisteriopsis caapi combined with DMT-containing plants), curare (used as an arrow poison and later adapted as a muscle relaxant in surgery), and dozens of other psychoactive and medicinal plants used by indigenous peoples. His Harvard students, including Wade Davis and Michael Balick, continued this tradition of field-based ethnobotanical research, helping to preserve [[psilocybin-chemistry-stability-indigenous-knowledge]] while discovering compounds of pharmaceutical value.

Schultes always emphasized that ethnobotanical knowledge is held by indigenous peoples as a cultural trust, not as raw material for commercial exploitation. He advocated for intellectual property protections for traditional knowledge and warned against biopiracy — the appropriation of indigenous plant knowledge by corporations without compensation. This ethical dimension of ethnobotany, which Schultes helped establish, has become increasingly important as pharmaceutical companies, agricultural firms, and biotechnology companies seek to exploit traditional knowledge of plant and fungal properties.

## Maria Sabina and the Consequences of Publicity

The Mazatec curandera María Sabina (1894-1985) played a pivotal role in the rediscovery story. Gordon Wasson's 1955 velada under her guidance in Huatla de Jiménez was the event that brought the mushroom ceremony to Western attention. Sabina's chants, sung in a mixture of Mazatec and Spanish during the all-night ceremony, were recorded and later published, becoming an important document of indigenous spiritual practice. However, the aftermath of Wasson's visit proved devastating for Sabina and her community. The influx of foreign seekers — estimated at hundreds per year by the late 1960s — commercialized and desacralized a practice that had been sacred for centuries. Sabina herself expressed regret about her role in revealing the ceremony, telling a Mexican interviewer that "from the moment the foreigners arrived to search for God, the 'saint children' lost their purity." Her village became a destination for psychedelic tourism, and the Mexican authorities eventually intervened to restrict foreign access.

## The Mazatec Mushroom Species

The mushrooms used in Mazatec ceremonies belong primarily to several species within the genus *Psilocybe*, though other genera are occasionally employed. *Psilocybe mexicana*, the species from which Hofmann first isolated psilocybin, grows in grassy fields and along trails at elevations of 1,000-1,800 meters. *Psilocybe caerulescens* and *Psilocybe zapotecorum* are also traditionally used. The Mazatec distinguish between species by their local names and perceived qualities, selecting particular mushrooms for specific purposes — healing, divination, or spiritual guidance. This sophisticated ethnobotanical classification system reflects centuries of empirical observation that in some cases anticipates modern chemical taxonomy.

## The Stoned Ape Hypothesis and Deep Time

McKenna's interest in the rediscovery extended beyond history into deep evolutionary time. His "stoned ape" hypothesis, articulated in *Food of the Gods*, proposed that the consumption of psychoactive mushrooms by early hominids in the African savanna played a catalytic role in the emergence of human consciousness, language, and religion. The hypothesis suggests that *Psilocybe cubensis*, growing naturally on dung of ungulate herds, was incorporated into the hominid diet, and that low doses enhanced visual acuity (useful for hunting), moderate doses increased sexual arousal and social bonding, and high doses dissolved ego boundaries and catalyzed the experience of the transcendent. While speculative and not widely accepted by anthropologists or evolutionary biologists, the hypothesis highlights McKenna's conviction that the mushroom-human relationship extends far deeper than historical time into the very emergence of our species.

The consequences of this rediscovery continue to unfold. The psychedelic renaissance of the twenty-first century — with clinical trials of psilocybin for depression, PTSD, and end-of-life anxiety — represents what McKenna might have called the latest attempt to regain paradise, this time through the tools of science rather than the rituals of shamanism.
