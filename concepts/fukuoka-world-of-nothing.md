# Fukuoka World of Nothing

## Overview

In the closing philosophical chapters of "The Road Back to Nature," Fukuoka describes his own experience of the "world of nothing" -- a state of being he first tasted after quitting his post at Yokohama Customs. He contrasts this authentic experience with the false paths people take in seeking God through knowledge, ritual, and intellectual pursuit.

## The Taste of Nothing

"I never knew such complete ease and happiness. I lived in a world of great joy. Ever since, I have merely lived my life while recalling those days. I found no joy in anything else." No matter what Fukuoka did, the happiest moments were those when he had nothing at all on his mind. To this day, everything he has done in many places has been mere diversions.

In Tokyo, he has been treated to Japan's best buckwheat noodles, but to him the coarse fare in his orchard huts is plenty. If he works up an appetite on his hill, then buckwheat grown on that same hill suits him just fine.

## The Richest Life

The discovery that the world of nothing has everything is central to Fukuoka's philosophy:

- One needs nothing; it is enough to be able to eat, work, and sleep

- Why watch all sorts of famous movies when it is enough to open the window and look out at the green world outside

- Without learning any instruments or knowing the musical scale, one can enjoy the bird calls and the croaking of the frogs -- although noisy perhaps, these mix in their own way to form an orchestra

- By renouncing absolutely everything and breaking away from the world of man, one can discover that the world with nothing at all has everything and enables one to lead the richest life possible

"How very simple it is -- that the world with nothing has everything. A place with nothing at all has everything." This insight connects directly to the [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]] that underpins all of Fukuoka's thought.

## The Farmer from Yamagata

Fukuoka tells of farmers from Yamagata Prefecture who arrived while he was sitting in one of his hilltop huts and said, "Why this is the poorest place we've seen, both spiritually and materially." Fukuoka felt like replying: "Doesn't the highest joy and peace reside in a mountain hut that appears materially and spiritually destitute?" But he said nothing. "One cannot know this until one has experienced it for himself."

This encounter illustrates the fundamental paradox of Fukuoka's teaching: the richest life appears poorest to those still caught in the world of material acquisition and intellectual striving.

## Renunciation as the Only Path

"There is no way other than for that person to notice the error himself." Fukuoka has never assumed the role of leader and rallied people on with "Let's go over there," "Let's return to nature." He cannot pull anyone along or say "Head west" or "Head east." But he can say something to the effect of "Go to a place beyond that."

The problem is that even when people say they have understood, they then say "I'm going to do this and that." "Whether you go east, west, south, or north, whatever the direction in which you head, all ways are blocked." The only way forward is to stop trying to go anywhere -- to arrive at the world of nothing.

## The Tragedy of In-between People

Fukuoka describes visitors who come to his mountain orchard for a year or two, see the lights of Matsuyama glittering off in the distance, and think "Oh, I'd like to go into town, stop by a coffee shop and buy myself a cup of coffee again." They might as well be in town. And yet when they return to Tokyo, within a few days they are prepared again to return to the mountain.

Dogen said of these people: "No matter where they are, they are nowhere." That is the tragedy of man. Given the choice of living in the middle of Tokyo and living somewhere in the country, one is better off in the country, because when one is close to nature, one is also close to God.

## The Farmer Near to God

"This is why I say that the farmer is near to God." By "near," it means he is where it is easiest to find the bluebird of happiness. God is right there behind the farmer, but when one looks back, He recedes off into infinitude. Yet God cannot be perceived even if He is right in front of you, because when the mind is empty, God is there, and when the mind becomes active, God hides.

## Critique of Religious Practice

Fukuoka questions why people who say they understand God still build elaborate temples, perform sutra readings, and erect graves: "If someone could cross over to the world of the dead and back himself, he would soon realize whether all this ceremony and devotion is necessary or not." Since no one has any real idea where we go after death, there should be nothing we can do.

He connects this to the cult of guardian deities: "The role of guardian spirits of religion is to release humanity from such spells of the world. I believe that the ultimate goal of religion is to transcend time and space, to transcend the world of the here and the hereafter."

Once he sent off a Buddhist priest with the words, "Go and live the life of a farmer in the mountains behind your temple." And he told a Zen priest from the famous temple Eiheiji: "There's no need to sit forever in meditation. You may try to achieve a state of perfect serenity through meditation, but you're doing fantastically if you're able to free your mind entirely even for a short space of one or two minutes."

## Against Esoteric Buddhism

Fukuoka is particularly critical of esoteric Buddhism, which he calls "a heretic road that diverts from the Great Way of Buddhism": "Esoteric Buddhism is like sorcery, but involves divination through a type of meditation. It reminds me of occultism and is something I have no patience for. The Great Ways of Buddhism and Christianity do not lie in such teachings as these. They are a broad-minded, generous world."

He praises Kobo Daishi's early work Sankyo Shiki as sufficient -- everything is said in that one thin volume. The esoteric elements were probably added later.

## Knowledge Destroys God, Nature, and Man

The closing warning: "Knowledge destroys God, nature, and man." The more people discuss and argue over God and war, the more peace eludes them. "He who lives by the sword shall die by the sword" -- this is an eternal truth.
