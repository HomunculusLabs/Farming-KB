# Permaculture: Care for Earth — Biodiversity, Stewardship, and Setting Limits

This page explores the deep ethical dimensions of "Care for the Earth" beyond simple planetary stewardship, including biodiversity ethics, land stewardship, the relationship between indigenous and Western land ethics, and the practical and philosophical challenges of setting limits to consumption and reproduction.

## Stewardship and Bioregionalism

### Beyond Planetary Stewardship

[[wendell-berry]]'s critique of planetary stewardship as arrogant disconnection from nature: "The question which must be addressed is not how to care for the planet, but how to care for each of the planet's millions of human and natural neighbourhoods, each of its millions of small pieces and parcels of land, each one of which is in some precious and exciting way different from all the others."

### The Stewardship Question

The stewardship concept demands: **Will the resource be in better shape after my stewardship?** This challenges the ethical validity of individual land ownership. In a low-energy future, control of land and natural resources will again become the primary focus for ethics, politics, and culture.

### Land Ethics

- Indigenous land rights and agrarian land reform challenge prevailing Western ethics about land
- The ethic of earth stewardship provides moral imperative for vesting land control in collective structures
- Efforts over the last century show this is not an easy task
- [[permaculture]] demands that we make all reasonable efforts to increase, and even transform, the biological capacity of soil for future generations

## Biodiversity Ethics

### Intrinsic Value

Care for the Earth encompasses caring for all diverse lifeforms — not dependent on their current usefulness to us — accepting them as valid parts of the living earth with intrinsic value.

### The Limits of Our Ability

Our ability to directly care for all species is as limited as our ability to care for the whole planet. Permaculture is a pragmatic philosophy that takes ecological limits as foundations. The traditional saying "live and let live" encapsulates a modest notion of avoiding harm where possible.

### Holmgren's Interpretation of Caring for Other Lifeforms

1. Accept all lifeforms or species as intrinsically valuable, no matter how inconvenient
2. Reduce total environmental impact as the best way to care for all living things, without needing to understand or control every individual impact
3. When harming and killing other living things (which is inevitable), always do so in a conscious and respectful way — not to use what we kill is the greatest disrespect

### Indigenous vs. Western Approaches to Pests

**Western scientific approach:** [[fukuoka-critique-genetic-engineering]] to make foxes sterile to save native animals — extends Enlightenment individual rights to individual animals.

**Aboriginal approach:** Introduced animals (donkeys, rabbits) are seen as *mwerranye* (belonging to the land), even though introduced by white people. Mass extermination programs are seen as immoral waste and disrespectful to individual and collective animal life.

This contrast provides an important perspective: [[traditional-indigenous-use-sacred-fungi]] views value individual and collective animal life even in pest management.

## Care for People in Depth

### Human-Centered Environmental Philosophy

Care for People makes permaculture unashamedly human-centered, placing human needs and aspirations as central concern. At the most local level this means accepting [[permaculture-self-regulation-energy-hierarchy-personal-responsibility]] for our situation.

### The Expanding Circles

Care for People starts with the self and expands in widening circles: family → neighbors → local community → wider communities. This follows the pattern of almost all traditional ethical systems.

### The Self-Reliance Paradox

"Look after yourself first" is not an invitation to greed but a challenge to grow up through self-reliance and personal responsibility:

- Our comfort is based on the "rape of planetary wealth," depriving others of local resources
- As we reduce dependence on the global economy and replace it with household and local economies, we reduce demand that drives inequities
- To be able to contribute to wider good, one must be healthy and secure

### Non-Material Well-Being

One of the best ways to apply Care for People is to focus on non-material values:

- Enjoy a sunset rather than watching a movie
- Walk for health rather than consuming medicine
- Play with a child rather than buying them a toy

The Genuine Progress Indicator has been declining since 1978 in the US despite huge rises in per capita consumption. Much new wealth offsets [[adverse-effects-and-harm-reduction-psilocybin]] of over-consumption: remedial healthcare, pollution control, crime control, litigation.

## Setting Limits: Abundance and Scarcity

### Abundance

A sense of abundance emerges when experiencing nature's gifts, sharpest within a context of constraint. The first strawberry of the season is special because nature doesn't provide them year-round. Luxuries lose their power when they become everyday realities.

The experience of abundance encourages distributing surplus beyond our circle of responsibility. The sense of abundance is lost through addictive indulgence to excess and waste — only possible through power over nature and people.

### Scarcity as Manufactured Reality

Except in extreme famine and natural disasters, **scarcity is a culturally mediated reality** — largely created by industrial economics and power rather than actual physical limits. This manufactured scarcity encourages unrestrained consumption and reproduction.

Recognition of limits comes from mature understanding, not from experiencing scarcity.

### What Is Enough?

Setting limits requires considering:
- The needs and wants that drive material gain
- The capacity of earth and people to provide those needs
- Tools like the Ecological Footprint to audit personal demand

### Population Ethics

Costa Rica studies: ecological footprint analysis suggested the country could sustainably support 80% of its 1987 population at current consumption levels; [[emergy-analysis]] suggested only 53%.

Key findings on population:
- Birth rates drop rapidly when: women are economically secure, they control their own reproduction, infant mortality is low
- Almost every child born in rich countries accelerates consumption
- Adoption and fostering provide opportunities to satisfy instincts without adding population
- Learning to see all children as our heirs is a great challenge for male culture worldwide

## Redistributing Surplus

### Traditional Forms

In most indigenous and peasant societies:
- Nature-spirit traditions required gifts, work, or devotion to maintain the living world
- Planting long-lived [[pdc-trees-and-forests]] redistributed surplus for future generations
- Land management to [[query-how-does-rock-dust-improve-soil-fertility]] was a form of stewardship

### Modern Forms

- Overseas aid and development projects
- Social service clubs and philanthropic trusts
- Voluntary and community work
- Arts and culture (no strong marketplace value)
- Permaculture and Landcare movements

### The Problem of Motive

Social and environmental good works can be:
- Atonement for collective sins rather than providing what people/nature really need
- Disguised self-interest creating dependency
- How to give with true altruism is an ethical question of great magnitude and complexity

### Surprising Finding

Many traditional and materially poor societies redistribute relatively more of their material wealth than affluent modern ones.

## Key Takeaways

1. Care for the Earth is most concretely expressed through stewardship of specific places, not abstract planetary management
2. Indigenous approaches to biodiversity ethics offer valuable alternatives to Western individual-rights frameworks
3. "Look after yourself first" means self-reliance and personal responsibility, not selfishness
4. Non-material well-being often increases while material consumption decreases
5. Scarcity is mostly manufactured by economic systems, not caused by physical limits
6. The experience of abundance (not scarcity) motivates sharing surplus
7. Traditional societies often redistribute more wealth than affluent modern ones
8. Conscious, respectful use of what we kill is the greatest respect for other lifeforms
