**Ideal candidates**: Pumpkins, potatoes, dry beans/peas, onions, parsnips, turnips, apples, pears, tomatoes, most root vegetables.

### Setup Requirements

- Leave dirt on root vegetables (protects from decay)
- Use plastic buckets or enamel cans (rodent/decay-proof)
- Store fruits away from vegetables — apples produce ethylene gas that causes sprouting and ripening in nearby produce
- Pack roots in damp sawdust, sand, or moss
- Keep potatoes in complete darkness (light makes them green and poisonous)
- Monitor temperature daily with inside/outside thermometers
- Open door in cold weather, close in very cold or hot weather
- Maintain 60-75% humidity (pans of water, damp sawdust, sprinkle floor)
- Remove spoiled food immediately
- Check stored produce weekly for signs of rot
- Different crops need different humidity levels — organize storage accordingly

### Storage Durations

| Food | Duration | Method |
|------|----------|--------|
| Apples (Winesap, Granny Smith, etc.) | 4 months | Small crates stacked 2-3 high, ripest on top |
| Cabbage | 3 months | Pick before frost, remove roots/outer leaves, upside down in loose layer |
| Carrots | 4 months | Crate lined with leaves, stacked upright |
| Chestnuts | 6 months | Soak 2 days, remove floaters, dry 1 day, store in sand bucket |
| Root vegetables | Varies | Layer in waterproof container with sand/sawdust, not touching |
| Squash | 3-8 months | Wipe with vegetable oil, wrap loosely in newspaper |
| Tomatoes | 4 months | Pull entire plant in early autumn, wrap each tomato, hang upside down |

## The Clamp

A clamp is a traditional outdoor storage alternative to a cellar — a pit 8-20 inches deep lined with wire mesh, clay, or brick:

1. Dig a trench in well-drained soil
2. Heap vegetables in a conical pile
3. Layer dry material (straw, leaves) between vegetable layers
4. Leave a ventilation hole or tunnel in the center filled with twigs
5. Cover with straw, then a layer of soil
6. Top with a wooden board, plastic sheet, and heavy rocks

**Best for**: Potatoes, carrots, beets, parsnips. The clamp must have good drainage, ventilation holes at top, be accessible for periodic harvesting, and be protected from freezing temperatures.

## Leaving Vegetables in the Ground

Some root vegetables can overwinter in place with mulch protection. Carrots, parsnips, leeks, and Brussels sprouts actually improve in flavor after frost. Mulch heavily with straw or leaves and harvest as needed throughout winter.

| Vegetable | Protection |
|-----------|-----------|
| Parsnip, salsify, turnip | Cover with straw |
| Jerusalem artichoke | Cover with straw |
| Beet root | Leave until very cold |
| Brussels sprouts, carrot, radish | Cover with straw + plastic sheet |
| Leek | Cover with straw + plastic |
| Cabbage | 8x8 inch trench east-west, lay cabbages stem south, cover with straw |
| Lettuce | 16x16 inch trench, heads not touching, cover with straw |
| Endive | Cut leaves, cover with 8 inches dirt, eat shoots in early spring |

## Solar and Electric Drying

For foods that don't store well live: sun drying (hot dry climates), electric dehydrator (low energy), or reflector-based solar drying. See [[faires-food-fermentation-methods]] store for months without energy
- **Smoking**: Low-temperature smoking preserves meat and fish without refrigeration

## Planning Your Storage System

A well-designed no-energy storage system combines multiple methods:

1. **Root cellar** for bulk storage of hardy vegetables through winter
2. **Clamps** for overflow or when cellar space is limited
3. **In-ground storage** for crops that improve with frost
4. **Drying** for fruits, herbs, and vegetables that don't store well fresh
5. **Fermentation** for vegetables like cabbage, cucumbers, and daikon

Together these methods can store a full year's harvest with zero ongoing energy costs. See [[natfarm-food-drying-dehydration]]
