# Natural Farming Four Principles

Natural farming, as developed by Japanese farmer and philosopher Masanobu Fukuoka, is founded on four core principles that distinguish it from both conventional scientific agriculture and organic farming. These principles are not merely techniques but express a fundamental philosophy of non-intervention with nature. Fukuoka demonstrated over fifty years on his farm in Ehime Prefecture, Japan, that crops can be grown productively without plowing, chemical fertilizers, weeding, or pesticides.

## Overview

The four principles of natural farming are:

1. **No cultivation** (no tillage)
2. **No fertilizer**
3. **No weeding**
4. **No pesticides**

These emerged from Fukuoka's realization that nature already possesses the complete capacity to grow plants without human intervention. His method produces approximately 22 bushels (1,300 pounds) of winter grain and 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre with only the labor of one or two people, compared to the heavy labor and chemical inputs required by conventional methods. In terms of energy efficiency, natural farming produces 130 pounds of rice (200,000 kilocalories of energy) per man-day of labor, roughly 100 times the daily caloric intake of a farmer on a natural diet.

## 1. No Cultivation

### The Problem with Plowing

Plowing is widely regarded as essential to farming, yet Fukuoka argued it actively damages soil health. The conventional reasoning holds that plowing loosens soil, increases air porosity, and encourages nitrification. In practice, however, plowing breaks soil into progressively smaller particles with more regular arrangement and smaller interstitial spaces, producing a harder, denser soil over time. The process is analogous to kneading bread dough: the more the soil is worked, the more compact it becomes.

In paddy fields, farmers traditionally tilled five to seven times per growing season. When herbicides became available after World War II and farmers reduced plowing frequency, their yields improved, demonstrating that intertillage had been effective only as a weeding process, not as a means of loosening soil.

### Nature Tills the Soil

The soil is a living system that works itself. Grass roots penetrate 12 to 15 inches or more into the earth, far deeper than the 4 to 8 inches typically plowed by farmers. As roots grow, air and water penetrate alongside them. When roots die and decompose, microorganisms proliferate, increasing humus content and softening the soil. Earthworms appear where humus accumulates, and moles begin burrowing through the improved soil.

In mountain forests, trees grow to magnificent heights without hoe or fertilizer because the grasses, trees, earthworms, and moles have performed the work of plow horses and oxen, completely rearranging and renewing the soil. The natural aggregation of soil particles into larger structures creates porosity and fertility that no mechanical tillage can replicate.

### How Plowing Creates Dependence

Plowing, fertilizing, and weeding are interdependent problems. Applying chemical fertilizers encourages shallow root structures that extend along the topsoil. Fertilizer application and weeding halt the normal aggregation and enrichment of topsoil. Clearing land by pulling up trees and bushes robs deeper soil layers of humus, stopping the active proliferation of soil microbes. These very actions are what make repeated plowing appear necessary, creating a cycle of dependence on human intervention.

## 2. No Fertilizer

### Crops Depend on Soil, Not Fertilizer

Fukuoka conducted experiments with fruit trees, rice, and winter grain to determine whether crops could be cultivated without fertilizers. He demonstrated that by taking full advantage of nature's inherent powers, yields equal to or better than those obtained with heavy fertilization are achievable. Mountain forests produce approximately two tons of new growth per quarter-acre per year without any fertilizer, suggesting that fruit trees could similarly produce two to four tons of fruit annually under natural conditions.

### The Illusion of Fertilizer Efficacy

The scientific reasoning that establishes fertilizer as necessary works backwards: scientists analyze plants, identify nutrients, apply them as fertilizer, observe growth, and conclude fertilizer causes growth. But if one starts from the view that a tree grows of its own accord, nutrient uptake becomes an effect of growth rather than its cause. A tree has a shape perfectly adapted to its environment and grows in total harmony with natural cycles.

### Documented Harm of Fertilizers

Fukuoka identified numerous problems with fertilizer use:

- **Temporary gains masking long-term weakening**: Fertilizers speed crop growth temporarily but inevitably weaken the crops, similar to rapid acceleration by hormones.
- **Reduced disease and pest resistance**: Plants weakened by fertilizers have lowered resistance to diseases and pests.
- **Ineffectiveness under field conditions**: Approximately 30% of the nitrogenous component of ammonium sulfate applied to paddy fields is denitrified by microorganisms and escapes into the atmosphere. Phosphate fertilizers penetrate only about two inches into the soil surface.
- **Soil acidification**: More than 70% of ammonium sulfate, super-phosphate, and potassium sulfate is concentrated sulfuric acid. Japan alone dumps approximately 1.8 million tons of sulfuric acid onto farmlands annually in fertilizer form.
- **Trace element deficiency**: Heavy reliance on chemical fertilizers has led to deficiencies in zinc, manganese, boron, iodine, and other trace elements essential to crops.
- **Complex antagonistic interactions**: Too much potassium blocks iodine uptake and reduces boron absorption. Higher nitrogen and phosphate levels cause zinc and boron deficiency while reducing manganese deficiency.

### The No-Compost Principle

While organic compost is closer to nature than chemical fertilizers, Fukuoka argued that the composting of organic materials is fundamentally unnecessary. All the trouble taken to prepare compost (frequent turning, stimulating aerobic bacteria, adding water, nitrogenous fertilizers, lime, and manure) accelerates decomposition by at most 10 to 20%. Applying straw directly to the field half a year before crops are sown achieves outstanding results without this labor.

## 3. No Weeding

### Are Weeds Really Harmful?

Fukuoka challenged the fundamental assumption that weeds are harmful to crops. In nature, plants live and thrive together in peaceful coexistence. Bushes grow at the base of large trees, grasses spread among shrubs, and mosses flourish beneath grasses. Rather than cutthroat competition for nutrients, this is a world of mutual benefit. The moment a farmer began caring for crops, he regarded other plants with disgust as weeds and has striven to remove them ever since.

In natural farming, where plants grow without relying on applied fertilizers, surrounding weeds pose no problem. The concept of "weed utility" recognizes that all plants arose for a reason and contribute to the development and enrichment of the biosphere.

### How Weeds Enrich the Soil

Grass roots penetrate deep into the earth, loosening soil. When roots die, they add humus and allow soil microbes to proliferate. Rainwater percolates through the improved soil, supporting earthworms and moles. Without grasses growing over the ground surface, rainwater washes away several tons to over a hundred tons of topsoil per year even on gentle slopes. In twenty to thirty years, topsoil would wash entirely away without vegetative cover.

### Practical Weed Management

Rather than pulling weeds, Fukuoka advocates replacing them with beneficial ground covers. In rice and barley cultivation, he seeds clover together with the grain crops, creating a living mulch that suppresses unwanted weeds while fixing nitrogen and building soil. In orchards, he used ladino clover as the primary ground cover, which eliminates annual weeds in one year and biennials in two years, creating a solid clover field within two to three years.

## 4. No Pesticides

### Disease and Pests as Natural Balance

Fukuoka argued that a thousand plant diseases exist in nature, yet in truth there are none. Insect pests and crop diseases are self-defense measures taken by nature to restore balance when the natural order has been disturbed. They are divine warnings that something has gone wrong with the health of the crop or the ecosystem.

In his fields, spiders, frogs, locusts, and dragonflies maintained natural balance. Whenever a large outbreak of leafhoppers occurred, spider populations multiplied in response without fail. After thirty to forty years of farming without pesticides, he observed that provided the farmer grows healthy crops, there is never any need for pesticides.

### The Cascade of Harm from Pesticides

Applying pesticides to rice fields kills tens of thousands of young spiders per square yard and eliminates swarms of fireflies. The second application kills chalcid wasps (important natural predators) along with dragonfly larvae, tadpoles, and loaches. The blanket application of pesticides destroys the entire ecological community that naturally suppresses pest outbreaks.

Fukuoka also documented how eliminating one pest often leads to increases in others. The complete elimination of yellow rice borers in Kochi Prefecture through post-season cultivation did not increase yields because other pests and diseases filled the ecological vacuum. Pests and diseases sometimes offset each other; a decline in insect infestation can open the door to rice blast disease or sclerotium rot.

### No Truly Non-Polluting Pesticides

Fukuoka argued that no truly non-polluting pesticides exist. Even so-called targeted pesticides that inhibit enzymes specific to certain insects affect animal, plant, and microbial cells that are fundamentally similar. A pesticidal effect is referred to as phytotoxicity in plants and pollution in animals and humans. The distinctions made between "safe" and "dangerous" pesticides are merely word games based on minor differences in susceptibility.

## Philosophical Foundation

These four principles arise from what Fukuoka called the philosophy of "Mu" (nothingness). Natural farming is a centrifugal, convergent approach that seeks non-action and a return to nature, in contrast to scientific farming, which is centripetal and divergent, expanding outward through increasing complexity and inputs.

Fukuoka stated: "All begins by relinquishing human knowledge." The principles are not dogmas to be followed rigidly but expressions of a deeper understanding that nature is already complete and self-sufficient. The farmer's role is not to grow crops but to assist nature, which alone has the power to produce something from nothing.
