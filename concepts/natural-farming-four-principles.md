# Four Principles of Natural Farming

Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming system is built upon four foundational principles that, when practiced together, create a self-sustaining agricultural ecosystem. These principles emerged from decades of observation and experimentation on his family farm in Shikoku, Japan, and represent a radical departure from both conventional and organic agriculture.







## First Principle: No Cultivation

The first principle rejects all plowing, tilling, and soil disturbance. Fukuoka observed that the earth cultivates itself naturally through the activity of plant roots, soil organisms, and weather. Tilling disrupts soil structure, destroys mycorrhizal networks, and exposes organic matter to rapid oxidation. In his rice-barley system, seeds are directly broadcast onto untilled fields, with the previous crop's straw serving as a protective mulch layer. See [[no-till-rice-barley-succession]] for the complete methodology.

The no-cultivation principle extends to orchard management as well. Fukuoka's citrus orchard thrived for decades without any soil turning, relying instead on ground cover plants and natural decomposition to maintain soil fertility and structure. This approach preserves the complex soil ecosystem that conventional tillage destroys.







## Second Principle: No Chemical Fertilizer or Prepared Compost

Fukuoka argued that plants grown with chemical fertilizers or even prepared compost become dependent on external inputs, losing their natural vigor and disease resistance. Instead, he maintained soil fertility through the return of all plant residues to the field — straw, chaff, and weed material are left to decompose in place.

In practice, this means the straw from the rice harvest is spread evenly across the field after harvest, and barley is sown directly into this straw mulch. The decomposing straw provides all the nutrients the next crop needs, while also suppressing weeds and maintaining soil moisture. Clover growing between crops fixes atmospheric nitrogen, completing the nutrient cycle without any imported materials. See [[fukuoka-textdoc-straw-mulching-rice-barley-cropping]] for details on the straw mulching technique.







## Third Principle: No Weeding by Tillage or Herbicides

Rather than fighting weeds through cultivation or chemical sprays, Fukuoka incorporated weeds into his farming system as beneficial ground cover. In his rice paddies, white clover and winter weeds were managed rather than eliminated — they fixed nitrogen, prevented erosion, and provided habitat for beneficial insects.

The key insight is that weeds and crops can coexist productively. Fukuoka found that a diverse weed population actually supports healthier crop growth by maintaining soil biology, providing insect habitat, and cycling nutrients. When weed pressure becomes excessive, it indicates an imbalance in the system that should be addressed through design changes rather than eradication.







## Fourth Principle: No Dependence on Chemicals

The fourth principle eliminates all pesticides, herbicides, and synthetic chemicals from the farming system. Fukuoka demonstrated that a balanced natural ecosystem maintains its own pest control through the complex web of predator-prey relationships. His rice paddies supported populations of spiders, frogs, dragonflies, and other natural predators that kept pest populations in check without any intervention.

This principle extends to the philosophical level: Fukuoka believed that human knowledge of ecological relationships is inherently incomplete, and that attempts to manage nature through chemical intervention always produce unintended consequences. The healthier approach is to design farming systems that work with natural processes rather than against them. See [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] for how these principles relate to broader design frameworks.







## Integration of the Four Principles

The four principles work synergistically — they are not independent practices but facets of a unified philosophy. No-till preserves soil structure, which supports the microbial communities that decompose straw mulch, which eliminates the need for fertilizer, which allows natural plant vigor to resist pests, which eliminates the need for chemicals. Removing any one principle weakens the entire system.

Fukuoka's rice-barley dual cultivation, described in [[fukuoka-natural-rice-barley-cultivation]], demonstrates all four principles working together: the field is never tilled, no fertilizer is applied beyond returned straw, weeds are managed through timing rather than eradication, and no chemicals are used for pest control. Despite — or because of — this minimalist approach, Fukuoka's yields matched or exceeded conventional farms in his region.
