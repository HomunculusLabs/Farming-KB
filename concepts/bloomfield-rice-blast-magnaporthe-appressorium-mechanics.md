## Rice Blast as an Agricultural Crisis

A rice field infected by Magnaporthe appears as if it has been struck by a bomb,
accounting for the common name blast. The fungus usually infects rice leaves and spreads
as an invasive mycelium, consuming plant tissues and starving the developing grain.

and withers to dry straw. Because grains normally ripen on the panicle, crop loss due

## Melanin and the Pressure Chamber

Once the appressorium adheres to the leaf by secreting an O-ring of glue, it deposits
a layer of melanin pigment against the inner surface of its cell wall. Most cell walls
are leaky structures allowing small molecules to pass freely, but a melanized wall
turns the cell into an impregnable pressure vessel. The glycerol generated inside the
cell draws water inward by osmosis, and because the melanin wall prevents glycerol
from escaping, immense hydrostatic pressure builds. Pressure inside an appressorium
can exceed 80 atmospheres, making Magnaporthe arguably the most powerful pathogen on
the planet in mechanical terms.

## Mechanical Force Compared

The pressure exerted by the Magnaporthe appressorium against the rice leaf exceeds the
pressure of a 400-pound dancer engaged in a brief pirouette, concentrated on the area
of a stiletto heel. Richard Howard of DuPont, responsible for much groundbreaking
research on appressorial function, discovered that these structures can puncture
Kevlar, the material used in bulletproof vests. This extraordinary force is generated
by a single [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] into the substance of the leaf. The
penetration hypha is narrower than the appressorium itself and passes through a
narrow pore in the cell wall at the base of the dome. Once inside the leaf, the
fungus establishes itself as a feeding mycelium that spreads between plant cells,
using haustoria to extract nutrients while keeping the host cells alive.
