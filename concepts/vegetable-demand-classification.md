# Vegetable Demand Classification

## Purpose

Steve Solomon's three-tier classification system allows gardeners to
match their soil fertility program to specific crop requirements. Rather
than applying a uniform fertility level across the entire garden,
gardeners allocate their limited compost, manure, and fertilizer
resources where they produce the greatest return (see [[wide-spacing-dry-gardening-low-irrigation-solomon]]).

This framework is central to Solomon's low-input gardening philosophy
and is detailed across several related pages: [[solomon-vegetable-demand-classification-low-medium-high]]
provides the complete tier breakdown with fertility requirements, while
[[solomon-vegetable-demand-classification-soil-fertility]] explains the
gradient approach to soil improvement.

## Low-Demand Vegetables

These crops [[solomon-vegetable-demand-classification-low-medium-high]] produce adequately with minimal soil improvement — as little
as a 1/4 inch (6mm) layer of compost or manure. They are the best
choices for new gardens, poor soils, or gardeners with limited access to
[[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]] (see [[solomon-green-manure-decomposition-and-carbon-nitrogen-timing]]).

### Examples
- Most legumes (beans, peas)
- Root crops adapted to poorer soils (certain potatoes, radishes, turnips, carrots, parsnips)
- Some leafy greens (kale, chard in moderate soils, certain lettuces)
- Certain herbs and alliums (garlic, onions depending on variety)
- Jerusalem artichokes

### Fertility Requirements
- Compost/manure: 1/4 inch layer
- Chicken manure: 1/4 inch layer
- Lime: 50 lb per 1,000 sq ft
- COF: 4-6 quarts per 100 sq ft

### Strategy
Low-demand vegetables are ideal for areas of the garden that received
manure or compost the previous year. Residual fertility from prior
amendments will still be present in the soil, often sufficient for
these undemanding crops. [[solomon-starting-new-garden-sod-removal-tillage-techniques]] recommends starting with low-demand crops
in newly broken ground.

## Medium-Demand Vegetables

The majority of common [[companion-cultivation-of-mushrooms-with-garden-vegetables]] fall into this category. They
require moderate soil fertility for acceptable production — roughly a
1/2 inch (12mm) layer of compost or manure plus COF.

### Examples
- Tomatoes, peppers (sweet and hot), eggplant
- Brassicas (cabbage, broccoli, smaller cauliflower types)
- Onions, garlic, leeks
- Summer squash, cucumbers, zucchini
- Corn, spinach, Swiss chard, kale

### Fertility Requirements
- Compost/manure: 1/4 inch layer of steer manure plus 1/4 inch chicken manure
- Lime: 50 lb per 1,000 sq ft
- COF: 4-6 quarts per 100 sq ft

### Strategy
Most garden vegetables fall into this tier. COF at the recommended rate
will usually adequately feed medium-demand vegetables. [[solomon-complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe]] provides the full recipe.
If using coprameal as the basis of the COF mixture, err on the side of
generous application since coprameal is slightly weaker in NPK than
other seed meals.

## High-Demand Vegetables

High-demand vegetables are heavy feeders that require the most fertile
soil and the most intensive fertilization. Without adequate nutrition,
these crops will underperform significantly. See [[solomon-difficult-vegetables-celery-cauliflower-high-demand-crops]]
for detailed profiles of the most demanding crops.

### Examples
- Heavy feeders: Large-fruited tomatoes, corn (for maximum production), large-headed cabbage
- Cole crops: Brussels sprouts, large cauliflower heads
- Cucurbits: Pumpkins, winter squash, melons, watermelons
- Root crops: Large potatoes, sweet potatoes
- Alliums: Large bulb onions (exhibition-sized)
- Celery (extremely demanding — see [[solomon-difficult-vegetables-celery-cauliflower-high-demand-crops]])

### Fertility Requirements
- Compost/manure: 1/2 inch finished compost plus 1/4 inch chicken manure
- Lime: 50 lb per 1,000 sq ft
- COF: 4-6 quarts per 100 sq ft, with regular side-dressing

### Strategy
For maximum production, side-dress every 3-4 weeks during active growth.
Sprinkle fertilizer in a band alongside the row, just beyond the leaf drip line.
If a seedbed doesn't respond within a few weeks after side-dressing, that
signals a deeper soil problem that additional fertilizer cannot fix —
the issue may be soil structure, drainage, pH imbalance, or [[cervantes-micronutrient-deficiency-chelation]].

## The Soil Improving Gradient

Solomon advocates a gradient approach to soil fertility rather than an
all-or-nothing system. Each step up the gradient gives better results,
allowing gardeners to work within their means (see [[solomon-vegetable-demand-classification-soil-fertility]]):

**Step 1 (minimum):** No soil improvers — only low-demand crops are feasible.
**Step 2:** Quarter-inch layer of composted manure — enables low and medium-demand vegetables.
**Step 3 (better):** One-inch layer of steer manure or half-inch finished compost plus COF.
**Step 4 (best):** Generous compost/manure application plus COF.
