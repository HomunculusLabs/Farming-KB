# Permaculture Cold Climate Strategies

Permaculture cold climate strategies are design approaches for productive living in regions with long winters, short growing seasons, frost risk, and low temperatures. [[permaculture-design]] addresses cold climates in a dedicated chapter of Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, providing specific techniques for extending growing seasons and reducing heating costs.

Cold climate permaculture requires more intensive design intervention than temperate or tropical systems. The shorter growing season means that every design decision has greater impact on total annual yield. [[permaculture-climate-strategies]] provides the broader framework, while [[cold-climate-gardening]] covers specific gardening techniques.

## House Design for Cold Climates

In cold areas, earthbanks plus shelterbelt combined with a sun-facing aspect is a critical design strategy for house placement. [[permaculture-thermal-belt-siting]] places houses in the midslope zone where cold air drains away.

Shelterbelt including thick vine trellis around a house can effect a 20-30% saving in heating fuels in moderate to severe winters. Wind chill factors can remove 60% of heat from glazed areas and hot water collectors alone.

The [[energy-efficient-house-design]] approach integrates passive solar heating, thermal mass, superinsulation, and airtight construction to minimize or eliminate heating costs.

Earth-sheltered housing with solar hot water, turf roof, and slab floor combines passive solar gain with thermal mass storage. Cooling is achieved through transpiration and shade.

## Frost Protection and Season Extension

Frost is a primary limiting factor in cold climates. The [[permaculture-frost-protection-strategies]] framework provides detailed techniques including thermal belt siting, windbreak design, water body placement, and structural protection.

Greenhouses, cold frames, and cloches extend the growing season by protecting plants from frost. A chicken-heated greenhouse combines animal heat with solar gain to create a productive growing space through cold months.

The [[permaculture-building-design]] approach integrates season extension structures into the overall site plan rather than treating them as separate additions.

## Windbreak and Shelterbelt Design

Windbreak design is especially critical in cold climates. Blizzards will kill livestock and newborn lambs, and even hardy animals can lose 30% of body weight in three days of blizzard.

Shelterbelt effects on house design are significant: wind chill removes heat from buildings and exposed skin. A well-designed shelterbelt saves 20-30% of heating costs.

Sheep weight in unsheltered fields is 15% less than in sheltered areas. Cattle fed winter rations on exposed sites eat 16% less food. Wind chill causes 20% of all lamb losses in Australia.

For croplands, a matrix of shelterbelt species 10-16 meters in height and 33-66 meters apart affords wind protection. Citrus fruit damage drops from 50% in unsheltered areas to 18.5% in shelterbelt systems.

The [[windbreak-and-shelterbelt-design]] page provides detailed guidance on species selection, placement, and establishment.

## Water Management in Cold Climates

Water management in cold climates must account for freezing. Swales and earthworks should be designed to handle freeze-thaw cycles without structural damage.

Snow management becomes an important design consideration. Juniper in high country grows better in areas of snow drift. Swales at drift-prone places enable more snow melt for root moisture in spring and summer.

Snowdrift across highways is more effectively blocked by hedgerows of hardy Caragana and Eleagnus, estimated to be 50% cheaper than fences and outlasting them.

The [[permaculture-dam-design]] approach must consider ice expansion, frost heaving, and reduced water availability during frozen periods.

## Animal Systems for Cold Climates

Animal housing in cold climates must provide protection from wind and extreme cold while maintaining adequate ventilation. Joint accommodation units where animals and humans share heated spaces are traditional in cold regions.

In the [[permaculture-zone-planning]] system, animal housing in cold climates is placed in Zone 1 or integrated with the house (Zone 0) because winter forces all people and animals indoors.

The [[mollison-animal-integration-in-permaculture]] approach adapts animal systems for cold conditions by selecting hardy breeds, designing appropriate shelter, and managing winter feed supplies.

Fences in cold climate pastures must allow easy downwind escape to woodlots, sheltered valleys, or lower elevations. Fenced corners can trap herds escaping blizzards, causing smothering.

## Energy Systems for Cold Climates

Heating is the primary energy demand in cold climates. [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] prioritizes reducing heating demand through passive design before supplying it through active systems.

Wood from sustainably managed [[mollison-woodlot-timber-durability-and-hedgerow-barriers]] provides renewable heating fuel. Passive solar design captures and stores solar heat in thermal mass for nighttime release.

Solar flat plate collectors provide hot water, while solar ponds can provide space heating through thermosiphon circulation. The [[solar-water-heating]] approach supplements passive design.

## Soil Strategies for Cold Climates

Cold soils slow biological activity and plant growth. Raised beds warm faster in spring, extending the growing season. Dark mulches absorb more solar radiation and transfer heat to the soil.

Cover crops protect soil from erosion during winter and add organic matter when incorporated in spring. The [[soil-building-permaculture]] approach maintains soil biological activity through winter using mulches and compost.

The [[permaculture-crop-rotation]] system in cold climates emphasizes early-maturing varieties, season extension techniques, and storage crops for winter consumption.

## Community Design for Cold Climates

Cold climate communities benefit from compact settlement patterns that reduce transportation energy and enable shared heating infrastructure. The [[permaculture-community-design]] approach addresses these scale considerations.
