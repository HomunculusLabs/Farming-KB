# Composting Methods and Techniques

Composting is one of the most fundamental practices in natural farming, turning organic waste into nutrient-rich soil amendment. In a well-managed permaculture system, food scraps alone represent about 30% of household garbage that can be diverted from landfills back into the garden cycle. All waste should be sorted at the source in the household.

## Kitchen Composting

Keep two buckets under the kitchen sink and add all food scraps except meat and dairy. Orange peels, banana peels, and eggshells should be crushed and cut up to break down faster. Small paper scraps can also be added. Once a bucket is full, dump it in the compost heap. Commercial black plastic bins work well in urban environments because their dark color absorbs heat, speeding decomposition.

For a [[composting-organic-garden]] toilet setup, a much larger bin is needed. If placing compost in a greenhouse as thermal mass, it should be at least 5 feet (1.5 meters) in diameter. Wooden bins outside should be at least 5 feet squared.

Every material not used as mulch should go in the pile: food scraps, composting toilet solids, leaves, garden waste, and manure. Seaweed and organic material from commercial garden services can be collected (verify no chemical treatment). Mulching items like leaves, straw, pinecones, bark, and sawdust are kept separate. The pile must be sheltered from rain unless in a very dry climate.

## The Composting Process

Once collected, material begins heating up within 24 hours as microorganisms feast on the materials, raising the temperature. It should rise over 120 degrees F (49 degrees C). During this time, leave the pile alone and keep adding material on top. When the temperature drops, stir the pile to mix in oxygen. Insects will move in, or worms can be introduced.

## Carbon-to-Nitrogen Balance

Woody materials ("brown" materials) should make up about half the bulk: paper, leaves, dry grass, and straw, which are high in carbon. The other half is "green" materials high in nitrogen: manure and garden waste. This is not an exact science, and experience will guide adjustments. If the pile is too wet, add more brown materials. If too dry, add more green. Chopping materials before adding helps them break down faster. The pile should be about as moist as a damp sponge.

Common reasons piles fail to heat up:

- Pile is smaller than 3 feet square
- Pile is too wet or too dry
- Imbalance of brown and green materials

## Vermicomposting (Worm Composting)

Raising worms is no more difficult than composting and is especially valuable for urban dwellers. It can even be done in an apartment. The worms need a covered bin with holes in the sides and bottom, optionally lined with mesh fabric, set on a drainage tray. The bin is watered like a houseplant.

Steps for setting up a worm bin:

1. Fill the bin with bedding material: black soil, shredded newspapers, or a mixture of both
2. Obtain red wigglers (redworms), not common garden worms. About 0.5 pound (500 regular sized worms) per cubic foot
3. Bury kitchen scraps in the bedding after each meal. Follow the same rules as compost: no meat or dairy. Garlic and potato peelings are also a bad idea. Add a steady diet of crushed eggshells sprinkled on top
4. Worms eat their weight in kitchen scraps per day
5. They do not like cold temperatures. Keep outside in summer but bring inside if nights get chilly
6. Every couple of months, open the bin in bright light. Worms dive down to escape the light. After ten minutes, scrape off the top layer of worm castings (valuable soil amendment). When you see worms, wait another ten minutes and repeat. Continue until all castings are harvested, then refill with fresh bedding

## Composting Toilets and Humanure

The average toilet uses 3.5 gallons per flush, totaling 3,832 gallons per year. A waterless separating toilet is the recommended design: solids go into a compostable bag in a bucket, liquids diverted to graywater separately. Solids are carried out every few weeks for outdoor decomposition, which takes about one year.

The best DIY approach is a humanure toilet using a 5-gallon bucket fitted with a comfortable toilet seat in a wooden box with hinged lid. Keep sawdust next to the toilet and add a thin layer after each use. Sawdust must come from untreated lumber only (pressure-treated wood contains toxic chemicals).

The outdoor compost pile needs a concrete base covered in a large layer of soil with leaves and grass on top. The concrete prevents leaching. Key management principles:

- Empty the bucket frequently from the house
- Keep the pile covered with dry material at all times
- Make sure the compost pile gets hot (above 98.6 degrees F / 37 degrees C)
- Build the pile on a concrete slab
- Put it inside the greenhouse or build nice wooden walls around it
- Do not turn the pile (unlike regular compost)
- Keep the lid closed to keep flies away

## Biomass Compost Water Heating

Compost reaches 140 degrees F (60 degrees C), which can be harnessed passively as a heat exchanger. Place the compost heap inside a building you want to heat and run plastic piping full of water through it. An advanced version uses two rings of chicken wire: the inner ring is filled with compost and organic material, a coil of plastic pipe is wrapped around it, and a second ring is placed at least 4 feet outside. More compost fills the space between the rings. The pipe carries water that is heated by the decomposition process. This is commonly used in greenhouses.

## Compost Classification Guide

Materials can be directed to three uses: compost, mulch, or animal feed.

Suitable for compost: banana peels, ashes, citrus peels, coffee grounds, corn cobs, dust, eggshells, hair, lint, manure, nutshells, rhubarb stems, seafood shells, sod (placed upside down), tea leaves, toadstools, soil, weeds, and wood chips.

Suitable for mulch: cardboard, pine needles, grass clippings, hay, straw, leaves, newspaper, sawdust (untreated only), and fabric.

Suitable for animals: dairy products, diseased plants, fish, meat, and bones.

Important notes: corncobs and peels should be chopped first; crush seafood shells; rhubarb leaves are poisonous so ensure they are fully composted; use untreated sawdust only.

## Soil Teas for Quick Nutrient Delivery

A liquefied tea adds nutrients to the soil quickly when plants fall victim to bad weather or attacks. It is particularly useful for tomatoes and peppers.

Steps for making soil tea:

1. Mix one part manure to three parts water
2. Leave to ferment for at least two weeks in a container with a loosely fitting lid
3. Add 10-15 parts more water; it should look like weak tea
4. As you use it, keep adding more manure and water for continuous supply
5. Extras can be added: comfrey (adds potassium), seaweed, or kitchen compost

## Compost in the Permaculture System

In the ideal Zone 1 garden, when you harvest a plant, as much is eaten as possible and the rest is composted. In winter, a green manure crop is grown and turned over to return nutrients. Volunteer tomatoes and cucumbers from the compost heap are replanted in empty spots. Organic compost contains trace elements and bacteria that plants need, making it superior to synthetic fertilizers. Encouraging worms and other beneficial creatures in the soil provides the best cultivation, as they do their own composting and mulching.

## Sheet Mulch Composting for New Beds

Sheet mulching serves as a slow composting method that creates garden beds in place. By layering cardboard, organic waste, straw, and compost, materials decompose over months into rich soil. This combines composting and bed preparation into one process. The method eliminates the need for separate compost bins and garden beds, as the compost becomes the bed directly.

## The Carbon Cycle and Compost

Composting is the practical application of the carbon cycle. Plants absorb carbon from the air during growth. When plant material decomposes, that carbon returns to the soil as humus, improving soil structure, water retention, and nutrient availability. Without this return of organic matter, soils become depleted and lifeless. In conventional agriculture, organic matter is removed from the land and not returned, leading to soil degradation. Composting closes this loop by returning carbon and nutrients to the soil in a form that feeds both plants and soil organisms.

## Compost for Greenhouse Thermal Mass

Placing the compost pile inside the greenhouse serves a dual purpose. As the pile decomposes and generates heat (reaching 140 degrees F / 60 degrees C), it provides passive heating for the greenhouse during cold months. The compost can also be piped with plastic tubing to create a hydronic heating system, circulating warm water through the greenhouse. In summer, the finished compost is available for garden use without needing to transport it from an outdoor pile.

## Troubleshooting Compost Problems

Bad smells indicate the pile is too wet or lacks oxygen. Add brown materials and turn the pile. If the pile attracts flies, ensure food scraps are buried under brown material. If the pile does not seem to be decomposing, it may be too small (minimum 3 feet square), too dry, or lacking nitrogen-rich green materials. In cold climates, insulate the pile with straw bales on the outside to maintain heat through winter.
