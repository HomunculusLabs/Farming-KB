# Psychedelic Plants and Civilization

The central thesis of Terence McKenna's *Food of the Gods* is that Psychedelic Plants and Civilization are not peripheral curiosities in human history but are foundational to the emergence of civilization, consciousness, and culture itself. McKenna argues that human history can be read as a series of relationships with plants—relationships made and broken—and that the suppression of [[hallucinogenic-plants-of-the-world]] is the defining wound of Western civilization.

## Plant-Human Coevolution

McKenna proposed that the relationship between humans and psychoactive plants is best understood as a form of [[mycorrhizal-plant-signaling]]. Drawing on examples from nature—clown fish and anemones, leafcutter ants and their cultivated fungi, mitochondria joining with cells—he argued that plants and humans have coevolved in relationships of mutual benefit over millions of years.

The key insight is that psychoactive compounds in plants may function as "exopheromones"—chemical messengers that act across species lines. Where plant hallucinogens occur, cultures are "quickly introduced to ever more novel information, sensory input, and behavior and thus are bootstrapped to higher and higher states of self-reflection." This is the Transcendent-Other: the encounter with nature as alive and intelligent, "Nature without her cheerfully reassuring mask of ordinary space, time, and causality."

McKenna suggested that the notion of nature as an organism whose interconnected components communicate through chemical signals is only beginning to be studied. "Nature, far from being endless warfare among the species, is an endless dance of diplomacy. And diplomacy is largely a matter of language."

## The Four-Part Structure

McKenna organizes human history into four phases defined by plant relationships:

### I. Paradise

The era of partnership-society, characterized by the use of [[african-mushroom-cult-history]] mushrooms in a Goddess-worshiping, egalitarian culture. This period produced the Tassili rock art of the Sahara, the Natufian culture of Palestine, and Qatal-Huyuk in central Anatolia—McKenna's candidate for the original Eden and "the Neolithic and eastern culmination" of partnership culture.

Human consciousness, language, and religion emerged from the symbiotic relationship between hominids, cattle, and Stropharia cubensis mushrooms. The mushroom was "the umbilicus to the feminine mind of the planet." The Genesis story of Eden, in McKenna's reading, is the allegorical account of humanity's expulsion from this mushroom-based partnership paradise—Eve's fruit of the Tree of Knowledge being the psilocybin-containing mushroom, and the angel with flashing sword guarding Eden symbolizing the unforgiving harshness of desert drought.

### II. Paradise Lost

The transition from partnership to dominator-culture, driven by Indo-European invasions (the "Kurgan Waves"), the rise of monotheism, and the replacement of hallucinogenic plants with alcohol. The great mystery religions—the [[eleusinian-mysteries-history-and-structure]], the Dionysian rites, the Soma cult—were gradually suppressed and forgotten. The phonetic-alphabet reinforced linear, dominator thinking over the gestalt pictographic awareness of earlier cultures. "The dark night of the planetary soul that we call Western civilization began."

### III. Hell

The era of addictive substances controlled by dominator institutions. This phase includes:
- **Sugar** and the slave economy it created, driving mercantile empires defended by the greatest military powers
- **Alcohol** and its role in suppressing the feminine and maintaining patriarchal power—"alcohol helps nerve a man for battle, helps nerve men and women for love, and keeps an authentic perspective on self and world forever at bay"
- **Tobacco** and opium as tools of colonial exploitation and social control
- **Synthetic drugs**—heroin, cocaine, and television (which McKenna called "the dominator drug par excellence")

McKenna argued that the distinction between legal and illegal drugs has nothing to do with relative harm and everything to do with which drugs support dominator values (alcohol, tobacco, caffeine, television) versus which drugs dissolve them ([[cannabis-addiction-recovery]], psychedelics). Cannabis is "anathema to the dominator culture because it deconditions or decouples users from accepted values."

### IV. Paradise Regained?

The modern rediscovery of psychedelic plants and compounds—from [[ayahuasca-brewing-guide]] in the Amazon to the accidental invention of [[lsd-compound-profile]] by albert hofmann in 1943, from the characterization of [[african-mushroom-cult-history]] in 1958 to the synthesis of [[5-meo-dmt-compound-profile]] by Steven Szara in 1956. McKenna saw the psychedelic movement of the 1960s as a brief eruption of Archaic partnership values into dominator culture—quickly suppressed through the drug war, CIA operations, and the promotion of cocaine and television as alternatives, but potentially revolutionary.

## Plants as Agents of Evolution

McKenna's most radical claim is that psychoactive plants are not merely cultural ornaments but are "biochemical agents whose ultimate impact is not on the direct experience of the individual but on the genetic constitution of the species." The inclusion of [[african-mushroom-cult-history]] in the early human diet produced increased visual acuity, increased reproductive success, and stimulation of protolinguistic brain functions—all logical consequences that could account for the otherwise inexplicable tripling of human brain size.

McKenna argued that the most powerful mutagens in the natural environment occur in molds and fungi, and that "mushrooms and cereal grains infected by molds may have had a major influence on animal species, including primates, evolving in the grasslands."

## The Abundance Problem

McKenna noted a striking geographical pattern: the New World tropics are phenomenally rich in hallucinogenic plants, while the Old World tropics—especially Africa—support almost none. He suggested this is because "the longer an environment has been exposed to human beings, the fewer its native hallucinogens." Plants that catalyze consciousness may have been consumed to extinction in their original habitats, or the human-plant symbiosis they supported may have been disrupted by dominator culture's expansion.

## The Diet as Destiny

McKenna's broader argument extends beyond hallucinogens to encompass all psychoactive-plants and their role in shaping cultural styles:

- **Alcohol** produces ego reinforcement, violence, and the suppression of the feminine—perfectly suited to dominator culture
- **Sugar** creates addiction and dependency, driving mercantile empires and slavery
- **Coffee and tea** reinforce the values of industrial workplace culture
- **Cannabis** promotes boundary-dissolution, feminine values, and intuitive thinking—making it anathema to dominator hierarchy
- **Psychedelic mushrooms** dissolve cultural conditioning entirely, making them the ultimate threat to established power

Each plant relationship "will tend to accentuate some concerns and diminish others," creating what McKenna called "meaning-defined environments" that are invisible yet determine what people think about themselves and reality. "Ideologies can be thought of as meaning-defined environments. They are invisible, yet they surround us and determine for us, though we may never realize it, what we should think about ourselves and reality."

## The Fundamental Claim

McKenna concluded that "there is no solution to the 'drug problem,' or to the problem of environmental destruction or the problem of nuclear weapons stockpiles, until and unless our self-image as a species is reconnected to the earth." The [[mckenna-archaic-revival]] he advocated begins with this reconnection—recognizing that the enhanced capacity for cognitive experience made possible by plant hallucinogens "is as basic a part of our humanness as is our sexuality."

## The Path Forward

McKenna argued that the lessons contained in humanity's ancient and formative relationship with psychoactive plants "can be raised into consciousness, integrated into social policy, and used to create a more caring, meaningful world, or they can be denied just as discussion of human sexuality was repressed until the work of Freud and others brought it into the light."

The question of "how quickly we develop into a mature community able to address these issues lies entirely with us."

## Merged: Stoned Ape Hypothesis
The stoned ape hypothesis is Terence McKenna's theory that [[african-mushroom-cult-history]]-containing mushrooms catalyzed the rapid evolution of hominid brain size, language, and consciousness. First presented in *Food of the Gods* (1992), it remains one of the most provocative—and controversial—claims in psychedelics-and-human-evolution. McKenna called Stropharia cubensis "the Ur plant, our umbilicus to the feminine mind of the planet, which, when its cult, the Paleolithic cult of the Great Horned Goddess, was intact, conveyed to us such knowledge that we were able to live in a dynamic equilibrium with nature, with each other, and within ourselves." The hypothesis frames the human-mushroom relationship as a form of [[mycorrhizal-plant-signaling]]. The stoned ape hypothesis underpins McKenna's broader concept of the [[mckenna-archaic-revival]].
