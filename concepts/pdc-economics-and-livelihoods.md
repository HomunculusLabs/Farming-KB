# Permaculture Design Course -- Economics and Livelihoods

## Right Livelihood

Living sustainably is more than architecture and infrastructure -- it is also about lifestyle choices and personal habits. One of the most important lifestyle choices is how we earn our livelihood. The true definition of livelihood means "securing the necessities of life." While money is the modern means of acquiring necessities, there are many more ways to acquire them. With Permaculture Design, one can create systems that directly generate the necessities of life.

## Five Economic Input Streams

A Permaculture economist understands there are five economic input streams:

### 1. Market Economy
Goods and services traded for legal currency. The typical economy we all think of -- having a paying job or other form of gaining legal tender.

### 2. Barter Economy
Trade -- give me that and I will give you this. Often tit-for-tat equal exchange, though what constitutes "equal" is subjective. One person's junk is another person's treasure.

### 3. Gift Economy
An act of taking something you have in abundance and giving it to someone else. Typically, one never asks for a thing -- things are simply given. The gift economy is of a wholly different ethical nature than barter.

### 4. Locally Produced Natural Resources
If you can produce food, water, medicines, and building materials through high (or low) yield Permaculture systems, you directly gain the necessities of life without any exchange mechanism.

### 5. Skill Shares
People getting together to share skills with others -- teaching and passing on skillful knowledge. With skills, one can acquire the necessities of life.

## The Integration Principle

Permaculture Principle #5 (Integrate Rather Than Segregate) applies to livelihood: a person living right livelihood attempts to integrate all five forms of economic input streams. You should not eschew any of them, BUT you should not be solely dependent on any one of them. Permaculture is about harnessing multiple input streams -- these five economies are all input streams. Utilize each as much as possible without being dependent on any one.

## Yield from the System

A Permaculture Design should have systems that yield resources for livelihood. The idea is not to pull from the system, but to have the system yield at a sustainable rate. The hierarchy of goals:

1. Ensure the health of the Earth
2. Take care of personal needs
3. Generate abundance
4. Create stores for lean times
5. Share abundance with others through the gift economy

## Reducing Dependence on Money

The more of your immediate needs your system can provide, the less money you need to survive:
- Grow as much food as you can
- Harvest rainwater
- Create local infrastructures that support your needs
- Develop skills that let you produce what you need
- Trade and share within your community

## The Triple Bottom Line in Practice

For any livelihood endeavor to be sustainable, it must be positive in all three bottom lines:

- **Financial** -- the economic viability of the endeavor
- **Social** -- positive impact on people and community
- **Environmental** -- ecological health maintained or improved

The manager must balance all three, ensuring they are roughly equally positive for the greatest sustainable longevity.

## Permaculture as a Livelihood

Permaculture Design itself can be a livelihood:
- Design consulting services for clients
- Teaching and PDC instruction
- Installing and maintaining Permaculture systems
- Creating value-added products from system yields
- Writing, speaking, and community organizing

## Commercial Interfaces in Community Design

In community design, the "commercial interface" is where community members can do business with the public. This should be on the edge of the community layer, connected to both the public and community layers. Examples include studios, classrooms, galleries, storefronts, business offices, and tourist centers.
