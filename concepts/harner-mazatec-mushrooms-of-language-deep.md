# [[mazatec-mushroom-culture-and-velada]]s of Language

The Mazatec Indians of the Sierra Mazateca in northeastern Oaxaca,
Mexico, have developed one of the world's most sophisticated traditions
of [[australian-nz-psychoactive-mushroom-species]] use. Henry Munn's essay "The Mushrooms of
Language" provides an unparalleled account of how [[psilocybin-mushroom-cultivation-basics]]s
function not merely as hallucinogens but as catalysts for inspired
speech, transforming the shaman into an oral poet and oracle whose words
heal and reveal truth.

## The Mushrooms as Medicine

The Mazatec Indians eat the mushrooms only at night in absolute
darkness. It is their belief that eating them in daylight would cause
madness. The depths of night are recognized as most conducive to
visionary insights into the mysteries of existence. Usually several
family members eat the mushrooms together: fathers, mothers, children,
uncles, and aunts all participate. The mushrooms themselves are eaten
in pairs, a couple representing the dual principle of procreation and
creation.

For the Mazatecs, the mushrooms were called the Flesh of God and are
now called the Blood of Christ, reflecting a syncretic blending of
pre-Columbian and Christian cosmology. Through their miraculous
mountains of light and rain, the Indians say that Christ once walked
(it is a transformation of the Quetzalcoatl legend) and from where
his blood, the essence of his life, fell, the holy mushrooms grew.
These are the awakeners of the spirit, the food of the luminous one.

## The Phenomenology of Inspired Speech

The Mazatecs say that the mushrooms speak. If you ask a shaman where
his imagery comes from, he is likely to reply: "I didn't say it, the
mushrooms did." No mushroom speaks; only man speaks, but he who eats
these mushrooms, if he is a man of language, becomes endowed with an
inspired capacity to speak. The shamans are the speakers who chant and
sing the truth, the oral poets of their people, the doctors of the
word.

Intoxicated by the mushrooms, the fluency, the ease, the aptness of
expression one becomes capable of are remarkable. At times it is as if
one were being told what to say, for words leap to mind one after
another without having to be searched for, a phenomenon similar to the
automatic dictation of the surrealists except that the flow of
consciousness tends to be coherent: a rational enunciation of meanings.
As the shaman chants, at the end of each phrase he says tzo ("says"),
like rhythmic punctuation: "Says, says, says. It is said. I say."

## Words as Medicine

For the Mazatecs, the psychedelic experience is inseparably associated
with the cure of illness. The idea of malady encompasses not only
physical illness but mental troubles and ethical problems. The shaman
actually gives medicine to his patients by administering mushrooms
physiologically while simultaneously altering their consciousness. The
treatment is both chemical and spiritual.

The shaman's most important function is to speak for the sick one. The
Mazatec shamans eat the mushrooms that liberate the fountains of
language so their words will arrive and be heard in the spirit world.
The shaman has a conception of poesis in its original sense as an
action: words themselves are medicine. To enunciate and give meaning
to the events and situations of existence is life-giving in itself.

Claude Levi-Strauss observed: "The psychoanalyst listens, whereas the
shaman speaks." The shaman provides the sick woman with a language
by means of which unexpressed and otherwise inexpressible psychic states
can be immediately expressed. The transition to verbal expression,
making it possible to undergo a real experience that would otherwise be
chaotic and inexpressible in an ordered and intelligible form, induces
the release of physiological processes and reorganizes them in a
favorable direction.

## The Shamaness as Oral Poet

The shamaness sits on the earthen floor of the hut, breathing the
incense of copal, holding mushrooms in the purifying smoke. After the
enlightenment and enlivenment, out of the silence she begins to speak,
to chant, to pray, to sing. Her discourse is not a stereotyped rite
with prescribed words but a spontaneous utterance covering everything
from the ancient to the modern, addressing poverty, illness, fertility,
and the challenges of cultural change.

She speaks of her communal world where "we" is more frequent than "I."
She addresses the economic condition of poverty: "Why are we poor? Why
are we humble in this town of Huautla?" She pleads for wealth and well-
being for all children, invoking a moral universe where goodness,
vitality, reason, transcendence, and joy are paths to follow.

## Language and the Brain

Munn proposes that the chemical constituents of the mushrooms have
some connection with the linguistic centers of the brain. The common
characteristic of mushroom experiences across different individuals
and societies would be discourse itself. It is paradoxical that the
rediscovery of these chemicals related their effects to madness and
called them drugs, when shamans spoke of them as medicines and said
they put one into communication with the spirit.

The medicine woman defends the mushrooms against those who dismiss
them: "They are doing nothing but talk, those who say that these
matters are matters of the past. They are doing nothing but talk, the
people who call them crazy mushrooms." They claim knowledge of what
they have no experience of; their contentions are nonsense compared
to the revelations the mushrooms provide.

## Cultural Transition

The Mazatec world is being transformed by modern Mexico. The children
speak two languages, Mazatec and Spanish, and live between two times:
the timeless, cyclical time of the People of the Deer and the time
of progress and development. Roads have been blasted through the
mountains, connecting the community with the society beyond. The
shamaness recognizes this transition, praying that her children learn
to read and write, to speak the language of the new world, while
retaining the wisdom of the old. The mushrooms serve as a bridge
between these worlds, a means of finding language adequate to the
crisis of cultural transformation.
