# The Tree Model and Pattern Matrices

The tree model is [[permaculture-design]].

## The Tree as Universal Model

The patterns and forms of a tree are found throughout nature and in evolved structures. An explosion, erosion sequence, idea, germination, or rupture at an edge between two systems or media may generate the tree form in time and space. Specific phenomena that share tree-form morphology include:

- Fetus and placenta
- Vertebrae and skeletal structures
- Vortices and whirlpools
- Mushrooms and trees
- Internal organs
- Volcanic and explosion phenomena
- Erosion patterns of waves, rivers, and glaciers
- Communication networks
- Industrial location patterns
- Migration routes
- Genealogical trees
- Possibly the universe itself

The tree form portrays the movement of energy and particles in time and space, making it a general teaching model for geography, ecology, and evolution.

## Anatomy of the Core Model

The general core model spirals out of a plane (P) that separates two media (M1 and M2). In the case of a real tree, this plane is the earth's surface separating atmosphere from soil.

### Key Structural Elements

**Origin (O)**: The point of germination or initiation — where the initial rupture of the surface between media occurs. This is where the event begins.

**Growth Phases (T1–T6)**: The expression of energy potential through time. Every event has an origin, a phase of growth, decay, and dissolution into other events of like or unlike form.

**Streamlines (S1–S9)**: Curvilinear paths that spiral through the origin. In a real tree, these correspond to phloem (storage cells carrying sugars downward) and xylem (sap-bearing cells carrying water upward). Traffic through streamlines moves in both directions — each margin both collects and distributes materials from different media.

**Plane (P)**: The interface between media (e.g., earth surface). At this zone, the xylem and phloem interweave or cross over as they spiral in and out of the media — external to stems, internal to roots for xylem, and the reverse for phloem.

**Media (M1, M2)**: The two environments on either side of the plane (e.g., atmosphere above, soil below). The tree trades both ways with elements of both media, with active water and gaseous exchange.

## The Vortex Nature of the Model

This deceptively simple "apple core" or tree shape, spiralling out of the plane, is a slow-moving vortex — the same form seen in tornadoes and whirlpools. The pattern is three-dimensional and must be thought of as extending into and out of a plane.

The stem forces open an ever-expanding flow through the plane between the media, allowing more material to pass through as time accumulates. The event expands the initial rupture of the surface, permitting greater flow.

## Cross-Section Projections

A crosscut of the model reveals several important pattern forms:

### Target Pattern (Dendrochronology)
The basis of tree-ring dating. A target pattern of expanding growth records annual cycles and reveals past occurrences of drought, seasonal changes, atmospheric composition, fire, and wind. This is the time-recording aspect of the model.

### Spiral Patterns
Screw palms (*Pandanus* spp.) develop ascending stem spirals reminiscent of fan turbine blades. Sunflowers create open seed spirals in two directions, common in many whorled plants. These spirals arise from the same fundamental growth dynamics.

### Yin-Yang and Infinity Symbols
Simple or multiple pathways through the model describe yin-yang, swastika, infinity, and mandala symbols — ancient pattern recognitions of the same fundamental flow dynamics.

### Torus Forms
A torus of contained forces evolves with the energies of the pattern, like the doughnut of smoke encircling the pillar of an atomic explosion. This emerges when circular flow patterns close upon themselves.

## Matrices: Tessellation of Core Patterns

### The Matrix Concept
A set of intersecting sine waves developed over a regular square or hexagonal matrix will generate a surface composed of the core model shapes. Whether the sine waves are static or flowing, the core model maintains its shape — flow does not necessarily deform the pattern.

Such pattern matrices demonstrate that the core models **tessellate** (from Latin *tesserae*, meaning tiles) to create whole surfaces. If landscapes are composed of such models, they must be able to tessellate.

### Natural Matrix Examples
- **Desert convection cells**: Roughly hexagonal air cells 1–5 km across, with whirlwinds forming at their junctions
- **Forest tree spacing**: Trees in forests exhibit basic matrix spacing patterns
- **Glacial landscapes**: Whole series of tessellated pattern forms
- **River headwaters**: Regular branching patterns following matrix rules
- **Solar surface events**: Phenomena on the sun's surface follow matrix organization

### Creating Matrices
A matrix can be created by either method:
1. Developing intersecting wave forms over a regular grid
2. Adding samples of the core pattern as tiles to a floor

This reveals that Euclidean concepts of points and lines underlie the curvilinear natural forms. Even irregular models tessellate, centered on nets or regular grids.

## Event Evolution

Every pattern event — whether abstract or palpable — shares a common lifecycle:

1. **Origin**: Initial point in space-time
2. **Growth**: Expression of energy potential through defined phases
3. **Maturity**: Maximum flow and interaction with media
4. **Decay**: Reduction in energy flow
5. **Dissolution**: Breakdown into other events of like or unlike form

This lifecycle framework applies equally to trees, volcanic eruptions, human settlements, ideas, and civilizations. Understanding where a system sits in this lifecycle informs appropriate design interventions.

## Discrete Event Scales

In nature, events are ordered in discrete units. There are smaller and larger orders, and when like forms are arranged by their orders, clusters of measures appear at certain sizes, volumes, lengths, or other dimensions. This scaling principle is observable in:
- River branch hierarchies (Strahler stream orders)
- Social caste systems
- Settlement size distributions (rank-size rule)
- Body size distributions within taxonomic groups
- Dune field patterns
- Planetary and galactic distributions

## Design Applications

The tree model and matrix understanding provide designers with:
- A vocabulary for describing and analyzing landscape patterns
- Predictive capacity for how patterns will develop over time
- Understanding of flow paths that can be enhanced or modified by design
- Recognition of tessellation principles for composing whole-system designs from pattern units
- Insight into the multi-scaled nature of natural systems, from cellular to planetary
