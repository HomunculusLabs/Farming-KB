# Trichoderma Prevention and Treatment

Trichoderma, known as Forest Green Mold or simply Green Mold, is one of
the most common and damaging contaminants in mushroom cultivation.
Classified under Fungi Imperfecti in the order Moniliales and family
Moniliaceae, its name derives from the Greek "trichos" (hairy) and
"derma" (skin). In
[[stamets-contamination-identification-and-control]], Trichoderma ranks among the most frequently encountered
molds alongside Penicillium and Aspergillus.

## Habitat and Occurrence

Trichoderma is very common on compost, casing soil, and to a lesser
degree on grain and agar. Many species grow on wood or woody tissue and
are abundant in peat. It frequently grows on the wooden trays holding
compost. The mold often parasitizes mushrooms under cultivation and can
inhibit or reduce fruitings. It is primarily airborne when contaminating
agar or grain cultures, and introduced through peat or humus on casing
soils. Trichoderma is also spread during harvesting, bed cleaning, or
watering. Species in this genus generally prefer acidic conditions in the
pH 4-5.5 range.

## Macroscopic Appearance

Trichoderma appears as a cottony mold growing in circular colonies on
casing soil or compost. It is grayish and diffuse at first, rapidly
growing, and soon becomes forest green from spore production. On malt
agar, colonies are aerial and cottony with brilliant forest green
mycelium, whereas Penicillium appears appressed, granular, and blue
green. Some species are whitish or yellowish, but most encountered in
mushroom culture are greenish. Parasitized mushrooms develop dry brownish
blotches or sunken lesions on the cap or stem, often enveloped by fine
downy mildew that may become greenish.

## Microscopic Characteristics

Conidiophores are clear and profusely branched, bearing small bunches of
ovoid greenish pigmented, smooth spores. Uniquely shaped sporogenous
cells roughly resembling bowling pins are arranged as triads. Unlike
Aspergillus and Penicillium, conidia are in loose clusters or scattered
individually rather than linear chains. The most distinctive feature is
that conidia are encased in a mucus-like substance, making spores
sticky. Spores measure 3-5 x 3-4 microns.

## Prevention Strategies

Preventing Trichoderma outbreaks requires attention to multiple factors
in the cultivation process:

1. Use clean casing materials lacking undecomposed woody tissue
2. Avoid casings incorporating potting soils
3. Maintain proper environmental parameters to avoid stagnant air and
   high CO2 levels
4. Ensure adequate
   [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]]
5. Practice careful picking and dispose of dead and diseased mushrooms
6. Control [[stamets-cultivator-humidity-management-fogging-systems]]
   to avoid prolonged high moisture conditions
7. Ensure proper
   [[stamets-cultivator-pasteurization-vs-sterilization-methods]]
   of casing materials

## Treatment Methods

When Trichoderma is detected, several treatment approaches are available.
Since Trichoderma thrives in acidic habitats, raising the pH of
surrounding soil inhibits further growth. The simplest method is covering
the infecting colony with salt, sodium hypochlorite, or sodium
bicarbonate (baking soda) or a solution thereof. Recognizing and treating
the fungus in its earliest stages, before spore production, greatly
reduces risk of satellite colonies spreading throughout the growing room.

Mushrooms afflicted with Trichoderma should be carefully isolated. All
items in contact (tools, workers, etc.) should be resanitized per
[[stamets-contamination-sources-laboratory-hygiene]]
protocols. Steam pasteurization at 160 degrees F for one hour effectively
kills spores.

## Environmental Management

Trichoderma is encouraged by improperly adjusted environmental parameters.
Excessively high and prolonged humidity combined with stagnant air and
high CO2 levels tip the ecological balance of the casing soil micro-ecology
in favor of this contaminant. Once populations bloom, the mold quickly
infects newly formed primordia and developing fruitbodies, causing
deformities. It is also prone to excessively wet casings applied to
sterile grain spawn. Increasing air circulation to 3-5 exchanges per hour
while maintaining 85-92% humidity can arrest further spread.

## Associated Problems

Trichoderma spores serve as food for red pigmy mites, creating a vector
for further contamination spread. Because spores are sticky, they attach
to anything coming in contact with them. Trichoderma is also alleged to
secrete toxins that inhibit mushroom primordia formation and growth.
The mold grows readily on discarded mushroom debris, particularly stem
butts, making thorough bed cleaning between crops essential.

## History and Notes

Trichoderma is not known to be pathogenic to humans. One interesting
industrial application utilizes Trichoderma, Penicillium, and
Cladosporium to precipitate precious metals such as gold and platinum
from solutions. In cased grain culture, Trichoderma is the most
frequently encountered contaminant on the casing layer, typically
originating from peat-based materials. Upon casing, spores harbored in
peat infect exposed grain kernels and sporulate, creating a platform for
further contamination that spreads through the casing layer until it
breaks through to the surface.
