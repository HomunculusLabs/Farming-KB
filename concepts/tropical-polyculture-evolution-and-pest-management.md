# Tropical Polyculture Evolution and Pest Management

Tropical polycultures represent the most species-rich and productive agricultural systems known, capable of supporting 200-400 cultivated species in a complex village situation. Their evolution from grassland or compacted soil to multi-strata food forest follows a systematic process, while integrated pest management replaces chemical inputs with biological relationships.

## Evolving a Polyculture from Scratch

When starting a permaculture on grasslands or compacted soils, the establishment process follows a careful sequence:

### Phase 1: Earthworks and Water Storage
The very first step is to thoroughly plan the site, then rip, swale, pit, or dam every area to be planted. This ensures maximum wet-season soil water storage to carry over to dry periods. The process should commence at the highest point of the property and around the house or village site.

### Phase 2: Initial Co-planting
On loosened soils and in mulched swales, a diverse mix is co-planted with one plant every 1-1.5 meters:

| Species Category | Spacing |
|---|---|
| Acacia (nitrogen-fixing trees) | 3 × 3 meters |
| Banana | 2 × 2 meters |
| Fruit trees | 5 × 8 meters |
| Palms | 10 × 10 meters |
| Smaller species (gap fillers) | 1-1.5 meters |

The co-planting mix includes tree legumes, fruits, bananas, papayas, arrowroot (Canna), cassava, sweet potato, and comfrey.

### Phase 3: Ground Cover Seeding
All larger planting holes should be seeded with a diverse non-grass mix including:
- Nasturtium, Dolichos, Haifa clover, broad bean (fava)
- Buckwheat, Umbelliferae (dill, fennel)
- Lupin, vetch (hairy or woolly), dun peas
- Chilies, pigeon pea
- Any useful non-grass mix suitable to climate and landscape

The aim is to completely carpet and overshade the ground within the first 18-20 months of growth.

### Phase 4: Mulch Application
Dense plantings should be grass or hay mulched using monsoon grasses, swamp grasses, and later the tops of arrowroot, comfrey, banana, and Acacia green crop.

### Phase 5: Successional Planting
As the canopy develops:
- Shade-loving species such as coffee and dry taro are placed in open spots
- Paths for access and openings for annual crops are established
- Bee plants are placed on edges
- Flowers and fire-resistant "wet" ground covers (comfrey, Tradescantia, Impatiens, succulents) are added
- Fruit trees are kept free of grass and mulched by cutting crowded Acacia and banana

## Species Richness and Zonation

The number of productive, managed, and effectively-harvested species in a polyculture is determined by seven factors:

1. **Labour:** The number of people responsible for managing one or two crops or animal species
2. **Zoning:** Proximity of the complex to village or settlement
3. **Fiscal economics:** The cost-benefit balance of increasing inputs to optimum levels
4. **Plant guilds:** The need for effective ecological assemblies
5. **Processing capacity:** Whether marketing and processing can cope with complex products
6. **Total area controlled:** Larger areas demand increasing simplicity
7. **System maturity:** Older systems provide more niches; younger systems provide more regular product

## Species Inventory for Complex Village Systems

In a complex village polyculture, the following usage classes are dominant:

| Category | Species Count |
|---|---|
| Basic food species | 70-90 |
| Mulch and fodder provision | 10-30 |
| Medicinals and biocides | 20-50 |
| Structural and craft | 5-20 |
| Culinary herbs | 20-30 |
| Beverages and export specialties | 10-15 |
| Fuelwood and coppice | 12-30 |
| Special/sacred uses | 8-15 |
| **Total cultivated species** | **155-280** |
| Plus wild-gathered species | 40-80 |
| **Total species utilised** | **195-360** |

## The Fiscal vs. Nutritional Optimum

A critical insight for polyculture design is that two different optima exist:

- **Fiscal optimum:** Peaks at approximately 6-8 species in a system, where market economics favors simplicity and scale
- **Nutritional-total yield optimum:** Peaks at 50-100 species, well-distributed over all seasons

These two factors (extensive-intensive, fiscal-nutritional) must be defined by the grower for their needs and profoundly affect design. The sensible approach is zonation: species richness close to settlement, concentration on fewer high-value species as the system extends outward. Gardens serve as trial areas for outer zones.

## Village Complex Elements

A complete humid tropical village complex includes:

- Well-arranged housing around a compound
- Processing and storage areas, threshing floors, meeting houses
- Cooperative or retail store, bulk fuel depots, firewood and mulch depots
- Well, piped water, tanks, or dam integrated for clean water
- Plant nursery serving gardens and forest
- 0.25-1.0 hectare home garden and orchard per house
- Border ranges for domestic species (chickens, guinea pigs, ducks, rabbits, small pigs, pigeons)
- Fish ponds where water is plentiful, with some animals housed above
- Fuelwood plantation integrated with windbreak
- Stockyard and sheds for cattle and pigs at commercial level
- Tree polyculture zonation
- Special facilities: boat-building areas, net drying racks, wharf, freezer, solar pond, power house, craft areas

## Pest and Disease Management

### Botanical Insecticides
Powerful biocides found in plants are harmless or short-term and totally biodegradable:

- **Chrysanthemum spp:** Source of pyrethrins
- **Derris spp:** Source of rotenone—controls aquatic organisms and insects
- **Neem tree (Melia/Azadirachta indica):** Neem oil and leaves deter pests in stored foods, used for centuries in India

A few of these plants in home gardens and small clumps in crops provide a ready source of insect control.

### Integrated Mosquito Control
Broadscale mosquito control combines multiple agents:
- Fats or oils (e.g., lecithin) as surface films
- Neem oil as poison
- Bacillus thuringiensis as infective agent
- Small fish and notonectids (backswimmers) as biological predators in open water

### Vertebrate Pest Managers
Multiple animal species contribute to pest management across different strata:

| Stratum | Species | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Ground | Chickens, pigs, cattle, tortoises | Fallen fruit, larval insects |
| Canopy | Birds, frogs | Canopy infestations |
| Ground level | Tiliqua lizards, ducks | Snails, slugs |
| Pasture | Birds, small mammals, marsupials | Pasture grubs |
| Mulch layer | Tropical land crabs | Larval insects in mulch |
| Grass control | Guinea pigs | Kikuyu grass around trees |

### Nematode Management
In tropical tree crop monocultures, soil fungi and nematodes become persistent pests. Two botanical approaches:

- **Marigolds (Tagetes):** Reduce or eliminate nematode populations
- **Crotolaria:** A leguminous green crop that traps nematodes in its root mycelia

Mulch and green manures (soil humus) buffer the effects of these and fungal pests by hosting fungal predators.

## Livestock in Tropical Polyculture

### Primary Functions
The main function of animal species in the tropics is to recycle plant wastes and help control rampant ground cover growth. Domestic species include pigs, chickens, geese, guinea pigs, rabbits, pigeons, and milking goats, cattle, or buffalo.

### Species-Specific Roles
- **Chickens:** Control rhinoceros beetle larvae in palm groves; weed control in pineapple, ginger, and taro
- **Guinea pigs:** Free trees from grass competition and provide manures
- **Geese:** Control pond-edge grasses
- **Wallaby or small grazers:** Keep forest clearings and paths open (encouraged by feeding-out bran in target areas)
- **Pythons:** Rat and mouse control
- **Owls:** The best rodent predator
- **Bees:** Pollination services
- **Pigs:** Ideal scavengers in tree crops below palms and fruit

### Predator Management
Where foxes or pythons threaten poultry, special housing and pigeons in safe elevated roosts may be necessary. Electric fences in tropical areas are often nullified by rank growth of coarse grasses. Fence lines must be planted with dense perennial ground cover of low herbaceous plants to exclude grasses, or overshaded by tree canopies.

## Planning the Whole Site

### For Established Polycultures
Re-survey with attention to:
- Main access and harvesting ways
- Earth-shaping for rainwater harvest and specific crops
- Sufficiency of mulch
- Best water and irrigation strategies
- Better village planning
- Improved processing for market

### For New Sites
Priority steps:
1. Assess market, future prices, processing potential, labour, legal systems, and self-reliance needs
2. Analyse soils and necessary nutrients
3. Plan ground layout, windbreak, access, and water
4. Plan and carry out essential earthworks
5. Establish nursery with selected varietal forms
6. Commence broadscale placements with or after windbreak and nurse crop
7. Continue with constant assessment, consultation, feedback, and innovative trials—fill niches as they evolve

## Key Insight

Complex polycultures work best at small scale with close human attention. The depopulated, dehumanized wasteland of modern agriculture is unable to cope with any but the most basic intercrop systems, sacrificing yield, quality, stability, and ultimately people. The garden remains the essential testing ground where we learn the value of successful extension without sacrificing large amounts of energy and capital.
