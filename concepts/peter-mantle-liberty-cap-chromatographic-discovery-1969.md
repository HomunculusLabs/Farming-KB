# Peter Mantle and the Liberty Cap Chromatographic Discovery (1969)

## The British Biochemist Who Confirmed Psilocybin in the Liberty Cap

The story of how the hallucinogenic properties of the Liberty Cap
(Psilocybe semilanceata) were confirmed in Britain is a tale of missed
connections, academic caution, and the gulf between laboratory science
and popular culture. At its centre is Peter Mantle, a young biochemist
at London's Imperial College, whose careful chromatographic work
confirmed what European scientists had already suspected but failed to
communicate effectively.

## The Heim-Hofmann-Tscherter Chromatographic Survey

Flushed with their Mexican discoveries, [[roger-heim-scientific-study-psilocybin]] and albert hofmann (along with their lab assistant Hans Tscherter) wondered whether any European species might also prove psychoactive. Chromatographic techniques allowed them to test for the presence of psilocybin in a variety of species without having to eat the mushrooms personally. Of all the species tested, only the Liberty Cap, Psilocybe semilanceata, turned up trumps. Rather satisfyingly, it proved to be the closest economical relative to the Mexican species.

## A Portentous Finding Ignored

Heim and Hofmann's finding was published in 1963 in the voluminous,
multidisciplinary French journal Comptes Rendus Hebdomadaires des
Seances de l'Academie des Sciences. The journal's formidable obscurity
meant that the discovery went largely unnoticed by both the scientific
community and the general public. Had such a portentous line appeared in
an American journal, it would, almost certainly, have triggered an
immediate response, especially if the research had been conducted in a
city that was, like London, in the grip of psychedelia.

## Mantle's Independent Confirmation at Imperial College

The finding was independently replicated in 1969 at London's Imperial
College by Peter Mantle, a young biochemist. His paper concluded that
ingestion of about 3g of air-dry sporophores of Psilocybe semilanceata
might be expected to produce psychoactive effects. This was a careful,
scientifically precise statement that the common British mushroom, found
in pastures across the British Isles, was indeed hallucinogenic.

## The Academic-Underground Gulf in Britain

Mantle's interest in psilocybin was purely biochemical; he was not a
hippy, nor did he ever try the mushrooms himself. He obtained specimens
from the experimental station at Kew in Hertfordshire, and undertook the
assay alone. The final sentence of the paper was mind-expanding only in
the sense that it was intended to further scientific knowledge, while
the journal in which it appeared, Transactions of the British
Mycological Society, was obscure enough to ensure that no one stumbled
across it accidentally.

A greater gulf between academia and the underground existed in Britain
than in the States, at least within this subject area. The channels of
communication between academic biochemistry and the countercultural
underground were almost non-existent in 1960s Britain.

## The Broader Context of British Mycological Discovery

The discovery of psilocybin in the Liberty Cap occurred within a broader
tradition of British mycological investigation stretching back to the
eighteenth century. The species had been described as Agaricus glutinosus
in earlier taxonomies, and later reclassified. The Swedish mycologist
Elias Magnus Fries had given the species its Latin epithet 'semilanceata'
(naked head, half-lance shaped) in the nineteenth century. The species
grows saprophytically upon the dead root cells of certain grasses, not
as a dung-lover as is sometimes assumed.

## Robert Graves's Accusation of Negligence

As [[robert-graves-british-mushroom-connection]] later
complained, both the European and British scientific establishments
could perhaps be excused for overlooking the main findings, but the
accusation that they were negligent still holds. The information was
available for anyone who knew where to look, but the gulf between
academic publication and popular awareness meant that the knowledge
remained locked away.

Graves had been proselytising about magic mushrooms since the late
1950s, in high-brow books and essays, and had placed wild claims about
the hallucinogenic properties of British fungi into the revised editions
of The White Goddess and The Greek Myths. If academic scientists had
been paying attention to this wider discourse, the discovery might have
reached a broader audience much sooner.

## Delayed Impact on the British Underground

It would be several more years before knowledge of the Liberty Cap's
psychoactive properties filtered through to the British underground. The
process of discovery and rediscovery illustrates how scientific
information about psychoactive plants often languishes in specialist
journals, inaccessible to the wider public, until someone bridges the
gap between academic knowledge and popular awareness.

The eventual spread of knowledge about the Liberty Cap in Britain would
owe more to folk knowledge, word of mouth, and the [[london-underground-and-british-magic-mushroom-discovery]] than to any formal scientific publication.

## The Chemistry Confirmed

Mantle's chromatographic work confirmed what Heim and Hofmann had found:
that the Liberty Cap contained a high and predictable concentration of
psilocybin, averaging about 1 per cent, along with significant amounts
(0.36 per cent) of another psychoactive alkaloid, baeocystin
(4-phosphoryloxy-N-methyltryptamine). This stability of concentration
was crucial, as without it dosage would be impossible to gauge and the
mushroom would probably not have been adopted as a psychoactive drug.
