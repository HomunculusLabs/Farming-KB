## Known Species

### East Africa (Kenya)

- **Psilocybe cubensis** — Reported from Kenya, though early reports by Cullinan
  et al. (1945), Charters (1957, 1958) and Vendcourt & Trump (1969) are
  considered confusing and may represent misidentifications or introductions
- **P. aquamarina** — Reported by Pegler (1977) from Kenya; closely related to
  P. cubensis but with distinctive coloration
- **Panaeolus** species documented from dung-inhabiting habitats

### South Africa

- **Psilocybe natalensis** — The only confirmed neurotropic Psilocybe from
  South Africa (Gartz et al., 1995). Named after Natal province. This species
  is of particular interest due to its apparently restricted range and the
  possibility that it represents an endemic African lineage

### North Africa (Morocco, Algeria)

- **Psilocybe mairei** — Known from northern Africa (Morocco and Algeria). Also
  reported from Czechoslovakia in Europe (Malencon & Bertault, 1970; Semerdzieva
  & Nerud, 1973), suggesting a disjunct distribution across the Mediterranean

### Tanzania

- **Amanita muscaria** — Found in pine plantations, as in other tropical regions
  where it has been introduced with exotic forestry

### Other Records

- **Amanita muscaria** — Present in exotic pine plantations in several African
  countries, always associated with introduced forestry
- **Claviceps purpurea** and other Claviceps species — Ergot fungi present on
  cereal grasses, particularly in temperate highland regions
- **Panaeolus** species — Likely widespread on dung but poorly documented
- **Copelandia** species — Probably present in tropical regions but surveys
  lacking

## The Psilocybe cubensis Puzzle

P. cubensis is very common in Mexico, Central America, and South America, where
it grows on cow dung. However, it does not grow in Europe. Guzman (1983) proposed
that P. cubensis was introduced to the Americas through the slave trade during
Spanish colonial times (16th–17th centuries), as cattle were also introduced by
the Spanish. The absence of P. cubensis from Europe despite cattle being native
there is notable. Its presence in Africa (if confirmed from Kenya) would support
an African origin hypothesis.

This biogeographic question has significant implications for understanding the
natural distribution of psilocybin-producing fungi and the role of human
activity in dispersing them across continents.

## Knowledge Gaps

Africa represents the largest gap in global knowledge of neurotropic fungi:

- Vast areas of West, Central, and East Africa have never been surveyed for
  psychoactive mushroom species
- The continent's high general biodiversity suggests many undiscovered species
  of neurotropic fungi likely exist
- Samorini (pers. comm. to Guzman) suggested possible traditional ceremonial
  use of neurotropic fungi by some African tribes, but this remains
  undocumented
- No records exist from most countries south of the Sahara except South Africa
  and Kenya
- Tropical African forests (Congo Basin, West African rainforests) may harbor
  significant undocumented diversity
- The relationship between African and South American neurotropic fungi has
  not been systematically studied despite the biogeographic connections through
  the Atlantic
