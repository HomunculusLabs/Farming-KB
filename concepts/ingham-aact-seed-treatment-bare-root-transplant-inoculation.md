Ingham's field guide provides comprehensive protocols for using compost tea as a
seed treatment, bare root dip, and transplant inoculant across multiple crop types
including orchards, vineyards, vegetables, and flowers. These methods leverage the
[[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] in compost tea to establish beneficial biology at the
critical moment of planting, when roots and seeds are most vulnerable to pathogens
and environmental stress.

## Seed Treatment Protocol

Fungicide-free seed should be rolled in compost tea containing VAM (vesicular-
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]]) spores before planting in spring. The tea coats the seed
with [[ingham-aact-beauveria-fruit-fly-beneficial-bacteria-inoculant]] and fungi that protect against soil-borne pathogens during
germination, while the VAM spores initiate mycorrhizal colonization as soon as the
seedling root emerges. This early colonization window is critical because
[[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] are most easily established in young root systems before
the root cortex becomes fully suberized.

If seeds have been pre-treated with fungicide (as many commercial seeds are), the
fungicide coating must be removed or bypassed. Ingham recommends placing 5 gallons
of compost tea per acre in the planting row directly under the seed, so that as
the seed germinates and the root emerges, it immediately encounters the beneficial
biology in the tea zone. This in-row application method ensures contact even when
the seed itself cannot be directly coated.

## Bare Root Planting Protocol

For new bare root plantings, Ingham recommends creating a slurry mixture of compost
and worm castings with additional nutrients (if recommended by SFI testing) combined
with compost tea. The bare roots are dipped into this slurry to thoroughly coat the
root system before planting. This accomplishes several things simultaneously: the
compost and worm castings provide [[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] slow-release nutrients, the tea
inoculates the root surface with beneficial bacteria and fungi, and the physical
coating protects the exposed roots from desiccation during handling and planting.

After planting, the hole is filled with native soil and watered in with compost tea
to establish the [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] throughout the root zone. The key principle is
to avoid sharp demarcations between the amended planting hole and the surrounding
native soil, which can create water and root-growth barriers. Ingham recommends
gradually mixing the compost and worm castings into the native soil away from the
root plug.

## Transplant Inoculation

For all transplanting operations, Ingham recommends a standard amendment ratio of
30-50% compost and/or worm castings mixed with site soil. This applies equally to
trees, vines, vegetable seedlings, and ornamental transplants. The compost provides
both the microbial inoculant and the organic substrate for the biology to colonize
and persist. After planting, all transplants should be watered in with compost tea
to jump-start the soil foodweb in the root zone.

## VAM Establishment Through Tea

The vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi are a critical component of the
transplant inoculation strategy. VAM fungi extend the effective root zone of plants
by forming symbiotic associations with root cells, improving phosphorus uptake,
water absorption, and disease resistance. Compost tea, when brewed from
fungal-dominated compost, contains VAM spores and hyphal fragments that can
initiate colonization of new plant roots.

Ingham specifically recommends dipping bare vine roots in tea with VAM spores and
[[gibberellins-plant-growth-hormones]] as an excellent method for establishing [[teaming-with-microbes-mycorrhizal-associations]] at
planting. The growth hormones in the tea stimulate root initiation and extension,
creating more colonization sites for VAM fungi. For orchard trees, the same approach
applies: the bare roots are dipped before planting, and the planting hole is
watered in with compost tea to ensure thorough inoculation.

## Post-Transplant Care

After transplanting, the care program continues with compost tea applications
according to the specific crop schedule. For orchards and vineyards, this means
monthly applications during [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]. For vegetable row crops, foliar
sprays every 10-14 days during the growing season starting at two weeks before bud
break are recommended. If any pesticides are used during the establishment period,
compost tea should be applied three days after each pesticide application to
restore the beneficial biology damaged by the chemical treatment.

## Cover Crop Integration

Ingham integrates cover crops with the transplant inoculation strategy. For
orchards, she recommends planting thyme or marjoram as understory crops between
tree rows. These perennial, ground-hugging plants provide living root exudates that
feed the soil foodweb between the trees, maintaining the beneficial biology
established by the compost tea program. Citrus orchards can use wintergreen thyme.
These cover crops can be mowed before harvest or allowed to flower and seed to full
maturity, which supports pollinators and produces seed for natural reseeding.

Between rows in orchards, grasses can be planted in strips with a nitrogen fixer
in the middle. These also support the soil foodweb and can be managed by mowing or
allowed to complete their life cycle. The combination of compost tea inoculation at
planting and living cover crops between rows creates a continuous biological
support system for the transplanted crop throughout the year.

## Nutrient Cycling During Establishment

The first and possibly second year after transplanting may require additional
nutrient inputs while the soil foodweb [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]] system is becoming
fully established. Ingham recommends adding these nutrients through the compost
tea itself or with the compost and worm casting amendment. If [[hamilton-nutrient-deficiencies-and-treatment]]
appear during the establishment period, petiole analysis should guide targeted
additions to the compost tea. The organisms in the tea significantly increase the
plant's ability to take up applied nutrients faster than they would be available
from raw fertilizer applications alone.

## Disease and Pest Management During Establishment

When the extension service issues disease or pest warnings during the critical
establishment period, Ingham recommends applying compost tea weekly with an
additional registered biocontrol agent added to the tea if available. This
intensified application continues until the alert stops, then returns to monthly.
If a disease outbreak occurs despite these preventive measures, compost tea should
be applied immediately every three to five days until the disease subsides.

For pest management, beneficial bacteria species in compost tea can repel
whiteflies, aphids, and leafhoppers from foliage. Getting an inoculum of specific
beneficial bacteria species into the tea has reduced insect feeding in field
observations. The SFI Beneficial Organism Assay can assess the presence and
enumerate about 20 different beneficial species, helping growers identify gaps in
their tea biology that could be addressed with targeted inoculant additions.

## Water Conservation Benefits

A notable benefit of the compost tea transplant program is progressive water
reduction. As the soil microbiology rebuilds soil health, water usage typically
decreases by 30% in the first year and up to 50-60% by the second and third years.
This occurs because improved soil aggregation increases water-holding capacity and
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]] extend the effective root zone well beyond the physical root
mass. Growers should proactively reduce irrigation applications when using compost
tea, and after 4-5 years when trees are fully established, applications can
decrease to seasonal only.
