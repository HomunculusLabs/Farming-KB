# Neotropical Psilocybe Species (Guzman, Allen & Gartz)

The neotropical region -- spanning Mexico, Central America, the Caribbean, and
South America -- harbors the greatest diversity of neurotropic fungi on Earth.
Guzman, Allen and Gartz (1998) documented this region as the probable center of
origin for the genus Psilocybe and the heartland of indigenous sacramental
mushroom use.

## Mexico: The World's Richest Psilocybe Flora

Mexico leads the world with 76 total neurotropic fungal species, including 44
Psilocybe taxa representing 39% of the global total. The country's remarkable
diversity reflects its position as a biogeographical bridge between North and
South American mycobiotas.

Key Mexican Psilocybe species documented in the paper include:

- P. mexicana -- the type species for the famous "teonanacatl" tradition, used
  by the Mazatec and other Oaxacan peoples. Belongs to Section Mexicanae.
- P. caerulescens var. caerulescens -- known locally as "derrumbes" (landslides),
  one of the most important ceremonial species. Also occurs as var. ombrophila.
- P. zapotecorum -- named for the Zapotec people, widespread in southern Mexico
  and extending throughout South America. Belongs to Section Zapotecorum.
- P. aztecorum var. aztecorum -- grows at subalpine and alpine elevations up to
  4,000 m in Mexico's high mountains. Belongs to Section Aztecorum.
- P. hoogshagenii var. hoogshagenii and var. convexa (syn. P. semperviva)
- P. cubensis -- known locally as "san isidros," extremely common on cow dung
- P. muliercula (syn. P. wassonii) -- called "hombrecitos" (little men) by the
  Trique Indians
- P. galindoi, P. barrerae, P. banderillensis, P. herrerae, P. sanctorum
- P. laurae, P. armandii, P. singerii, P. wassoniorum, P. weldenii
- P. cordispora, P. subcubensis, P. subtropicalis, P. yungensis (southern
  species that reached Mexico via Alnus-associated mesophytic forests)

Non-Psilocybe neurotropic fungi in Mexico include Conocybe siligineoides (a
sacred mushroom collected only once by Wasson in 1955 and never re-found),
Copelandia cyanescens, C. mexicana, C. tropicalis, Dictyophora indusiata (used
by the Chinantec for divination), Cordyceps capitata and C. ophioglossoides (used
in nocturnal ceremonies with Elaphomyces as a "director"), and several puffball
species used in Mixtec traditions.

## Central America and the Caribbean

Central America has a more limited documented mycoflora but shares many species
with Mexico:

- P. cubensis is widespread, recorded from Belize, Costa Rica, Guatemala, and
  throughout the region
- P. cordispora occurs in Belize and Costa Rica
- Copelandia cyanescens is documented in Belize and Trinidad
- P. dumontii is known from Costa Rica
- Caribbean islands including Puerto Rico have endemic species such as
  P. portoricensis and P. guilartensis

The Caribbean distribution includes the Bahamas, Bermuda, Cuba, Dominican
Republic, Haiti, Jamaica, Puerto Rico, and Trinidad, with Psilocybe cubensis
being the most widely recorded species across these islands.

## South America: High Diversity, Poorly Explored

Despite relatively limited mycological exploration, South America shows
impressive neurotropic fungal diversity:

**Colombia** is particularly rich with 12 documented neurotropic Psilocybe
species: P. angustipleurocystidiata, P. antioquensis, P. columbiana, P. cubensis,
P. guatapensis, P. heliconiae, P. hoogshagenii, P. pintonii, P. subacutipilea,
P. subcubensis, P. yungensis, and P. zapotecorum. Ancient gold pectorals
suggesting mushroom worship have been found in Colombia (Schultes & Bright,
1979).

**Brazil** contributes numerous species: P. acutipilea, P. brasiliensis (endemic),
P. caeruleoannulata, P. caerulescens, P. cubensis, P. farinacea, P. furtadoana,
P. paulensis (endemic), P. plutonia, P. ramulosa, P. uruguayensis, P. zapotecorum,
and others. Gerronema fibula, Gymnopilus spectabilis, and various Panaeolus
species are also present. Amanita muscaria occurs in pine plantations.

**Other South American countries** with documented neurotropic fungi:

- Chile: P. semilanceata, P. carbonaria, P. fimetaria, P. sierrae, P. strictipes,
  P. zapotecorum, Gymnopilus purpuratus
- Argentina: P. collybioides, P. cubensis, P. hoogshagenii, P. wrightii,
  P. zapotecorum
- Bolivia: P. cubensis, P. mammilata, P. subcubensis, P. yungensis
- Peru: P. cubensis, P. zapotecorum
- Venezuela: P. caerulescens, P. meridensis, P. plutonia, P. subcubensis
- Ecuador: P. subcubensis, P. yungensis
- Uruguay: P. caeruleoannulata, P. uruguayensis

## Habitat and Ecological Preferences

The majority of neotropical neurotropic Psilocybe species grow in:

- **Mesophytic (cloud) forests** -- the single most important habitat type,
  especially in Mexico where 34 of 42 species are associated with these forests
- **Tropical and subtropical humid forests** -- particularly for widespread
  species like P. cubensis and Copelandia cyanescens
- **Subtropical deciduous forests** -- along the eastern coast of Mexico and into
  Central America
- **Dung and disturbed habitats** -- P. cubensis, Panaeolus, and Copelandia
  species are coprophilous, commonly growing on cattle dung
- **Alpine and subalpine grasslands** -- P. aztecorum at extreme elevations in
  Mexico's volcanic mountains

Psilocybe zapotecorum deserves special note for its extraordinarily wide range,
occurring from Mexico through Central America and across much of South America
(Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Chile, Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil), making it one of
the most broadly distributed neotropical neurotropic species.

## The African Question

Africa remains a major gap in neurotropic fungal knowledge. Only 6-8
neurotropic Psilocybe species are documented from the entire continent despite
its enormous biodiversity. P. cubensis occurs in Kenya, P. natalensis is known
only from South Africa, and P. mairei occurs in North Africa (Morocco, Algeria)
and Czechoslovakia. The authors suggest possible unexplored indigenous use of
neurotropic fungi in Africa.

## Biogeographical Significance

The neotropical region's dominance in Psilocybe diversity supports the hypothesis
that the genus originated in the southern hemisphere and dispersed northward. The
close taxonomic relationships between neotropical species (Mexican and South
American) and species from Southeast Asia and New Zealand further suggest
ancient distribution patterns that predate the breakup of Gondwana or reflect
trans-Pacific dispersal events.
