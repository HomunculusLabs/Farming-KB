# Jarrold Straw Chopping, Soaking, and Draining Substrate Preparation

**Jarrold [[oyster-straw-substrate-preparation]]** is a method for processing cereal straw into a suitable growing medium for [[mushroom-cultivation]], described by D.H. Jarrold in *Indoor [[duggar-mushroom-growing-history-and-methods]] Technique for the Boil-A-Bag*. The procedure converts raw straw into a hydrated, nutrient-supplemented substrate through a sequence of chopping, soaking with [[straw-substrate-preparation-marmite-yeast-extract-soak]], draining, and supplementation with [[brown-rice-flour-substrate]]. It is designed to be accessible to amateur growers using household equipment.

## Overview

Straw is a carbon-rich, lignocellulosic material that serves as a base substrate for many [[saprotrophic-fungi]]. Raw straw is too dry, too coarse, and nutritionally imbalanced for direct inoculation. Jarrold's method addresses all three deficiencies in a straightforward, equipment-light workflow suitable for small-scale indoor cultivation using the [[boil-a-bag-mushroom-cultivation-technique]].

## Chopping

The first stage is reducing straw to lengths of **3–10 cm**. Shorter pieces increase the surface area available for [[mycelium|fungal hyphae]] to colonise, improve packing density inside grow bags, and allow more uniform hydration during soaking. Overly long straw creates air pockets that impede colonisation and can harbour contamination.

Jarrold notes that pre-cut straw of suitable length is commercially available from pet stores, sold as bedding for small animals. This eliminates the need for growers to chop straw themselves with secateurs or a garden shredder. When sourcing pre-cut straw, it should be clean, free of mould, and ideally from organic or pesticide-free sources.

Growers who prefer to prepare their own straw should use clean, sharp tools to avoid crushing or fraying the ends of the pieces. Crushed straw compacts more tightly and can restrict air movement within the substrate. A simple method is to bundle the straw and cut it with hedge shears or a machete on a clean cutting board.

## Soaking

Soaking hydrates the straw and introduces supplementary nutrients. The procedure is as follows:

1. Place the chopped straw in a **waterproof container** large enough to hold the batch.
2. Dissolve a **heaped tablespoon of Marmite or other yeast extract** in a small quantity of boiling water.
3. Pour the dissolved yeast extract over the straw.
4. Add **6 litres of additional boiling water** to the container.
5. Place a weight on top of the straw to keep it fully submerged.
6. Allow to soak for **1 hour**, then check hydration; extend to **2 hours total** if necessary.

The use of boiling water serves a dual purpose: it rapidly hydrates the straw and provides a degree of pasteurisation, reducing the load of competing organisms present on the straw surface. The hot-water soak is not a full sterilisation but is sufficient for the low-tech, fast-colonising approach described by Jarrold.

### Role of Marmite and Yeast Extract

Marmite and similar yeast extract products are concentrated sources of [[b-vitamins]] and trace minerals. Jarrold specifies that adding yeast extract to the soak water **quickens spore germination time**, giving inoculated fungi a competitive advantage over slower-colonising contaminants. The B vitamins (particularly thiamine) are essential co-factors for fungal metabolism, and their presence in the substrate can reduce the lag phase before visible colonisation begins.

Other yeast extract products, such as Vegemite, nutritional yeast, or brewing yeast extracts, can serve as substitutes for Marmite. The active components are consistent across these products: water-soluble B-complex vitamins, amino acids, and mineral salts derived from the autolysis of *[[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]]* cells. Growers should avoid flavoured or sweetened variants that may introduce unwanted additives.

## Draining

After soaking, the straw is **turned out into a bathtub** and left to drain for **1 hour**. Proper drainage is critical because excess water in the substrate creates [[lowenfels-aerobic-anaerobic-soil-conditions|anaerobic microsites]] within the packed bag. These oxygen-depleted zones are ideal breeding grounds for anaerobic bacteria and wet-spot contaminants such as *[[bacillus-subtilis]]* species and various slime moulds, which produce foul odours and can stall or kill mycelial growth.

The bathtub drain allows gravity to pull free water away from the straw mass. Jarrold emphasises that the straw should drain **thoroughly** — a brief drain is insufficient and is a common source of failure for beginners.

## Moisture Adjustment

Following drainage, the straw is **spread evenly in a tub** for a final moisture check. Jarrold instructs growers to **squeeze handfuls of straw by hand**: if water streams freely from the squeezed handful, the straw is too wet and must be worked further or allowed additional drain time.

The target moisture content for straw-based [[kh2po4-phosphate-supplementation-mushroom-substrates]] is broadly accepted to be in the range of **60–75% by weight**, depending on species and cultivation method. Jarrold's squeeze test is a practical field approximation of this target:

- **Too wet**: water drips or streams freely; substrate compacts, excludes air, and promotes bacterial contamination.
- **Too dry**: straw springs back when squeezed with no visible moisture; colonisation slows significantly as the mycelium cannot access sufficient water for [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]].
- **Correct**: a few drops of water escape when firmly squeezed; the straw clumps briefly then falls apart.

Achieving proper moisture content is one of the most important steps in the entire preparation process. Errors here are difficult to correct after the substrate has been bagged and inoculated.

## Supplementation

Once the straw is at the correct moisture level, [[brown-rice-flour-substrate]] is sprinkled evenly over the surface and **thoroughly mixed in by hand**. Jarrold recommends **3–4 handfuls for a batch yielding 6 grow bags**.

### Why Brown Rice Flour?

Cereal straw is predominantly composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin — all carbon-rich structural polymers. While fungi can metabolise these materials, their growth rate is limited by [[plant-available-nitrogen-ammonium-nitrate]]. Brown rice flour provides a readily accessible source of nitrogen, additional B vitamins, and complex carbohydrates that supplement the straw's nutritional profile. This accelerated nutrition allows the mycelium to colonise the substrate faster, reducing the window of vulnerability to contamination.

Brown rice flour is preferred over white rice flour because the bran layer retained in brown rice contains higher concentrations of lipids, minerals, and vitamins. It is also inexpensive and widely available.

Supplementation should be performed immediately after moisture adjustment, as the wet straw provides ideal conditions for mixing. The grower should use clean, dry hands or disposable gloves and turn the straw repeatedly from the bottom of the tub to ensure even distribution. Clumps of dry flour that remain undistributed can become focal points for contamination.

## Food-Safe Handling Considerations

Jarrold's method employs household items (bathtub, kitchen containers) for substrate preparation. Growers should observe the following precautions:

- **Clean all containers and surfaces** with hot, soapy water or a dilute bleach solution before use.
- **Rinse thoroughly** after cleaning to remove residual detergent or bleach, which can inhibit mycelial growth.
