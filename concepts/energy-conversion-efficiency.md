# Energy Conversion Efficiency

## Overview

Understanding the conversion efficiency of various energy devices and systems is essential for making informed decisions about [[appropriate-technology]] in [[permaculture]] [[integrated-land-management-and-village-design]]. Mollison compiled comparative efficiency data from multiple sources, covering both primary conversions (e.g., gas to electricity) and secondary conversions (e.g., waste heat to high-grade gas). This data helps designers select technologies that maximize useful energy output while minimizing waste and [[fertilizer-pollution-environmental-impact]].

## Solar Energy Systems

### Photovoltaic Cells

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest (%) | Average (%) | Highest (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Amorphous silica solar cells | Sunlight → electricity | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| Crystalline photovoltaic cells | Sunlight → electricity | — | 13.5 | — |
| [[gallium-arsenide]] photovoltaic cells | Sunlight → electricity | — | — | 75 |
| Modern photovoltaic solar cells | Sunlight → electricity | — | 25 | 29 |

**Amorphous silica cells** are cheap to produce in quantity but have very low efficiency (2–5%). If gallium arsenide cells were affordable, all home energy could be electrical from a 100 m² roof area.

### Solar Thermal

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest (%) | Average (%) | Highest (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solar flat plate collectors | Sunlight → hot water | 10 | 30 | 80 |
| Concentrating solar collectors | Sunlight → water heat | — | 75 | 80 |
| Solar ponds for electricity | Sunlight → electricity | — | 15 | — |
| Solar ponds for heat | [[albedo-and-solar-radiation]] → water heat | — | 89 | 90 |
| Solar attic | Sunlight → heated air | — | 78 | 90 |
| Unglazed fast-flow collector | Solar radiation → water heat | — | — | 70 |

Solar flat plate collectors show enormous variation in performance depending on conditions (10–80%). Concentrating solar collectors and solar ponds used for heat achieve the highest solar thermal efficiencies at 75–90%.

### Stirling Engines

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest (%) | Average (%) | Highest (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stirling hot air engines | Solar → mechanical energy | — | 20 | — |

Stirling engines convert solar [[phase-change-materials-thermal-energy-storage]] to mechanical energy at moderate efficiency but are valued for their durability and simplicity.

## Biomass and Wood Energy

### Direct Combustion

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest (%) | Average (%) | Highest (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Open fires | Wood → room heat | — | 15 | — |
| Kenyan bell-bottom cookstove | Wood → cooking heat | — | 30 | — |
| Well-made slow combustion stove | Wood → hot water, cooking, space heat | — | 81 | — |
| Thermal mass heater | Heat stored → heat given out | — | 92 | 93 |

Open fires are identified as very wasteful of wood (only ~15% efficiency). Well-designed slow combustion stoves reach 81% but may pollute air. Thermal mass heaters are the most efficient wood-burning option at 92–93%.

### Biogas and Methane

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest (%) | Average (%) | Highest (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cow dung burnt as fuel | Dung → water heat | — | 7 | — |
| Cow dung converted to methane | Heat rating → gas BTU | — | 60 | 65 |
| Biogas conversion | Heat rating → gas BTU | — | 92 | 93 |

Burning cow dung directly as fuel is extremely inefficient (7%) and reduces available fertilizer compared with [[anaerobic-digestion-biogas-production]]. Converting dung to methane yields 60–65% efficiency while preserving the fertilizer residue. Full biogas conversion reaches 92–93%.

### Wood-to-Electricity

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest (%) | Average (%) | Highest (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood burnt to generate power | Wood → electricity | — | 12 | — |

Direct wood-to-electricity conversion is relatively inefficient at ~12%.

## Water Power (Hydro)

### Water Wheels

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest (%) | Average (%) | Highest (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Undershot straight-paddle wheels | Water flow → shaft HP | — | 18 | 20 |
| Undershot Poncelot (curved vane) wheels | Stream flow → shaft HP | — | 30 | 35 |
| Breast water wheels (curved vanes) | Water power → shaft HP | — | 75 | 80 |
| Well-designed overshot water wheels | Stream flow → shaft HP | — | 90 | 92 |

Overshot water wheels are the most efficient traditional water wheel design at 90–92%, followed by breast wheels at 75–80%. Undershot designs range from 18–35%.

### Turbines

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest (%) | Average (%) | Highest (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Propelior turbines (hydraulic) | Stream flow → shaft HP | — | 80 | 86 |
| Rotary hydraulic turbines | Water at head → shaft HP | — | 81 | — |
| [[pelton-wheel]] coupled to air compressor | Falling water → compressed air | — | 90 | 92 |
| Good Pelton or Turgo impulse wheels | Falling water → shaft HP | — | — | +100 |

Pelton and Turgo impulse wheels are the most efficient water-to-mechanical converters, potentially exceeding 100% effective utilization in optimized installations.

## Fossil Fuel Engines

### Diesel Engines

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest (%) | Average (%) | Highest (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typical diesel engine | Fuel → horsepower | — | 35 | — |
| Turbo-charged diesel engine | Fuel → horsepower | — | 40 | — |
| Adiabatic (cool) diesel | Fuel → horsepower | — | — | 50 |
| Diesel co-generation | Diesel fuel → heat (60%) + electricity (30%) | — | 90 | 93 |

Diesel co-generation, where waste heat is used constructively, achieves 90–93% overall efficiency — dramatically higher than diesel engines used for electricity alone. This makes co-generation the most efficient use of diesel fuel.

## Specialized and Amplified Systems

### Heat Pumps and Compressed Air

| Technology | Conversion | Amplified Efficiency |
|---|---|---|
| Heat pumps | Low-grade heat → useful space heat or cooling | 350% |
| Compressed air heaters | Expanding cool compressed air before engine use | 550–600% |

Heat pumps achieve 350% efficiency (factor of 3.5–7× gain) by extracting low-grade heat for useful purposes. Compressed air heaters applied to air lines achieve 550–600% efficiency compared with using fuel directly in engines.
