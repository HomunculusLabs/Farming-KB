# Sericulture and the Entanglement of Natural and Scientific Farming

Fukuoka used the history of sericulture as a detailed case study of his
central thesis: [[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]], as it involved selecting and
managing the insects, but the methods remained close to natural conditions.

## The Spiral of Artificial Intensification

People eventually replaced wild species with artificially bred varieties. They
noticed that silkworms thrive on mulberry leaves and that young larvae grow
more rapidly when the leaves are fed finely chopped. At this point it became
easier to raise them indoors, so they built shelves for growing large numbers
of worms inside. Special feeding tools and cocoon production equipment were
devised, and growers became very concerned about optimum temperature and
humidity.

The labor demands were extreme. Farmers had to rise very early, shoulder a
large basket, and walk to the mulberry grove to pick leaves one at a time.
Leaves were carefully wiped free of dew with dry cloths, chopped into strips
with a large knife, and scattered over the silkworms on tens and hundreds of
feeding shelves. Room temperature and ventilation had to be maintained day and
night using heaters and by opening and closing doors.

The silkworms improved by artificial breeding were weak and susceptible to
disease. It was not uncommon for worms that had finally grown to full size to
be suddenly wiped out by disease. During silk spinning from the cocoons, all
family members pitched in, rarely getting any sleep. If a late frost killed
the young mulberry leaves, farmers usually had no choice but to throw away
the entire lot of silkworms.

## The Brief Return Toward Natural Methods

Given such labor-intensive methods, growers began seeking less strenuous
techniques starting fifteen to twenty years before Fukuoka wrote. Sericulture
methods that approached natural farming spread widely. These consisted of
throwing branches of mulberry leaves onto the silkworms rather than picking
and chopping individual leaves.

Once growers learned that such a crude method works for young silkworms as
well as fully grown larvae, the next thought was to raise them outdoors in a
small shed, under the eaves, or in a sort of hotbed. On trying this out,
growers found that silkworms are really quite hardy and never had to be raised
under constant [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]].
