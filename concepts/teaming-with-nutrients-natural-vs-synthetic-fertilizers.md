# Natural vs Synthetic Fertilizers

updated: 2026-04-21

The choice between natural (organic) and synthetic fertilizers has profound consequences for [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] health, [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]], and long-term garden productivity. While synthetic fertilizers can supply nutrients in ionic form that plants absorb identically to naturally-derived ions, the broader ecological effects are dramatically different.

## Why Natural Fertilizers Are Superior

**Soil food web support:** Natural fertilizers feed the [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]], which creates soil structure. Bacterial slime and mycorrhiza|fungal hyphae bind soil particles together, creating pore spaces that serve as reservoirs for air, water, and habitat for smaller organisms. Tunnels and burrows from soil fauna further increase aeration and water-holding capacity. Synthetic fertilizers generally do not build or sustain soils.

**Carbon deposition:** mycorrhiza|Mycorrhizal fungi deposit carbon via glomalin coatings on soil particles. Synthetic fertilizers reduce mycorrhizal populations, eliminating this carbon input.

**Plant protection:** Mycorrhizal sheaths contain chitin (a nitrogen polymer) that serves as both a nitrogen source and a defense against parasitic nematodes. Some mycorrhizal fungi also produce antifungal chemicals. Synthetic fertilizers kill or repel these protective organisms.

**Root exudate relationships:** Plants and soil microbes have co-evolved relationships over 420 million years. About 60 percent of carbon from [[photosynthesis-and-chloroplast-function]] is used to synthesize root exudates that influence pH, microbial diversity, nutrient uptake, plant defense, and soil structure. Synthetic fertilizers disrupt these relationships.

**Cation exchange capacity:** Organic fertilizers add organic matter that increases the [[teaming-with-nutrients-composting-and-nutrient-cycling]] of soil, improving its ability to hold and exchange nutrient ions. Synthetic fertilizers do not add bulk organic matter.

## Negative Effects of Synthetic Fertilizers

- **Nutrient tie-up:** Can chemically bind essential nutrients, making them unavailable
- **pH disruption:** Quickly change soil pH, affecting uptake of all nutrients
- **Osmotic stress:** High salt concentrations from synthetic fertilizers can pull water out of root cells
- **Soil biota harm:** Kill or repel bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and arthropods; macro- and micro-arthropods and worms shun areas with synthetic fertilizers
- **Mycorrhizal suppression:** High nitrogen and phosphorus levels prevent formation of mycorrhizal and nitrogen-fixing symbiotic relationships
- **Nutrient pollution:** Highly soluble anions (especially nitrate and phosphate) leach easily, causing water pollution

## The Gulf of Mexico Dead Zone

Runoff from thirty-one U.S. states and two Canadian provinces enters the Mississippi River and flows to the Gulf of Mexico. Excessive nitrogen and phosphorus from synthetic fertilizers cause massive algal blooms. When the algae die and sink, their decomposition depletes oxygen in the lower saltwater layer (hypoxia), killing fish and other organisms. In the spring, warm freshwater runoff creates a barrier layer atop the Gulf that cuts off the saltier water below from oxygen in the atmosphere. The cooler temperatures of winter disrupt the barrier layer, but the cycle starts again each spring. Studies show gardeners use three times more synthetic nitrogen per acre than farmers.

## Energy and Environmental Footprint

Making chemical fertilizers is an extremely energy-intensive activity. These products also have to travel great distances to get to market, which adds to their environmental footprint. Many synthetics are anions because farmers need to get their plants fed quickly and need fertilizers that are instantly soluble in water. These anions are easily leached out of the soil and cause serious pollution as they run off into water bodies. In addition, it is often impossible to determine exactly what is in commercial synthetic fertilizers. In the United States, each state has its own labeling laws, and many allow the use of fillers without having to identify their source.

## Slow-Release Advantage

The strongest argument against natural fertilizers — that they release nutrients slowly — is actually their greatest strength. Most natural fertilizers are slow-release, providing nutrients over weeks or months as the soil food web mineralizes them. This prevents the boom-and-bust cycles of synthetic fertilization and reduces leaching losses. Once established, a healthy soil food web releases nutrients continuously from organic matter reserves. (Although the reverse is also true: synthetics are available too quickly and not for long periods.)

## Completeness

Natural fertilizers supply many more nutrients simultaneously than synthetic N-P-K formulations. They include [[plant-micronutrients-and-their-essential-functions]], trace minerals, cofactors for enzymatic reactions, and organic bulk that feeds the entire soil ecosystem. Natural fertilizers contain their nutrients in bulk, so their use also increases the [[teaming-with-nutrients-composting-and-nutrient-cycling]] (CEC) of soil. Applying organic fertilizer is like constructing a condominium for the organisms that fix nitrogen and mineralize and cycle plant nutrients, ultimately making them available in the very same ionic form as do chemical fertilizers.

## The Soil Structure Argument

The single most important difference between natural and synthetic fertilizers is their effect on soil structure. When synthetic fertilizers kill or repel soil organisms, the aggregates, pore spaces, and water-holding capacity those organisms create begin to deteriorate. Bacterial slime and fungal hyphae initially stick soil particles together; earthworm tunnels and root channels further improve porosity. Dead organisms contribute carbon that supports living ones. Without this biological infrastructure, soil becomes compacted, poorly drained, and increasingly anaerobic.

In anaerobic conditions, carbon dioxide from root and microbial respiration cannot escape. It reacts with water to form carbonic acid, lowering pH and triggering a cascade of negative effects: nitrogen fixation diminishes, nutrient availability changes, and plant roots suffer. Organic fertilizers prevent this by maintaining the pore structure that allows gas exchange.

## Humus and Long-Term Soil Health

When organic fertilizers are applied, natural substances are broken down by soil microbes into organic compounds. Eventually, humus — the end product of composting — is created. Humus is what good soil is all about, and it cannot develop without organic matter and microbes. Humus provides:

- Long-term nutrient reservoir with slow-release properties
- High cation exchange capacity for holding nutrient ions
- Improved water retention in sandy soils
- Better drainage in clay soils
- Buffering capacity against pH changes
- Habitat and food for beneficial soil organisms

## The GMO Consideration

Some natural fertilizers raise GMO concerns. Cottonseed meal may contain pesticide residues and comes from a heavily GMO crop. Soybean meal is one of the biggest GMO crops, and inserting bacterial genes into plant genomes may require special microbes for decomposition. Corn gluten meal may also originate from GMO plants. For gardeners maintaining strictly organic systems, choosing non-GMO sources or alternatives like alfalfa meal, blood meal, or fish-based fertilizers may be preferable.

## Sandy Soils

If you have sandy soils, using natural fertilizers is the only way to hold in water and may be the only way to really provide adequate nutrients to plants. The organic matter in natural fertilizers helps create soil structure in which the carbon dioxide from microbial activity is not trapped. This structure also ensures that proper moisture content, which is critical for nutrient uptake, can be much more easily maintained.

## Nutrient Release Rates Compared

A key practical difference between natural and synthetic fertilizers lies in their nutrient release profiles:

- **Synthetic nitrogen (urea, ammonium nitrate)**: Releases 50-80% of its nitrogen within the first 2-4 weeks after application. The remaining nitrogen leaches or volatilizes relatively quickly. This rapid release creates a flush of available nitrogen followed by a sharp decline, which can stress plants and feed weeds that capitalize on the brief nutrient surplus
- **Compost**: Releases nitrogen slowly over 6-12 months as the soil food web mineralizes organic matter. Only about 5-10% of total nitrogen in finished compost becomes plant-available in the first growing season. The remainder continues feeding plants through subsequent seasons as a slow-release reservoir
- **Blood meal**: A faster organic nitrogen source, with approximately 70% of nitrogen becoming available within 2-4 weeks as microbial decomposition proceeds. This makes it useful for nitrogen-hungry crops but still slower than synthetic alternatives
- **Cottonseed or soybean meal**: Intermediate release, with nitrogen availability peaking at 4-8 weeks after application. The microbial community gradually decomposes the protein-rich meal, providing a sustained nutrient supply through the middle of the growing season
- **Rock phosphate**: Releases phosphorus over 3-5 years as soil acidity slowly dissolves the mineral matrix. This extremely slow release contrasts sharply with synthetic triple superphosphate, which is immediately soluble but also immediately subject to fixation (binding with soil minerals into unavailable forms)
- **Greensand and granite dust**: Release potassium and trace minerals over multiple years. These are the slowest of all natural fertilizer sources, serving as long-term mineral reservoirs rather than quick nutrient fixes

## Soil Biology Impacts

The effects of fertilizers on soil biology go beyond simple nutrient supply. Synthetic and natural fertilizers create fundamentally different soil ecosystems:

- **Bacterial biomass**: Natural fertilizers increase total bacterial biomass by 30-60% compared to unfertilized soil, as diverse organic substrates support diverse bacterial communities. Synthetic fertilizers increase only certain bacterial groups (nitrifiers) while suppressing others, reducing overall diversity
- **Mycorrhizal colonization**: Plants fertilized with natural sources typically show 40-70% mycorrhizal root colonization. The same species fertilized with synthetic phosphorus at recommended rates show less than 10% colonization, as the plant perceives no need for fungal assistance when phosphate is abundant
- **Earthworm populations**: Soils receiving regular organic amendments support 5-10 times more earthworm biomass than synthetically fertilized soils. Earthworms avoid high-salt synthetic fertilizer zones, depriving the soil of their mixing, aeration, and casting activities
- **Protozoan and nematode diversity**: Natural fertilizers support diverse communities of bacterial-feeding protozoa and nematodes that cycle nutrients. Synthetic fertilizers simplify these communities, reducing the efficiency of the mineralization loop that converts organic nitrogen into plant-available forms

## Cost Comparison

The economics of fertilizer choice depend on whether costs are calculated per application or over the long term:

- **Per-application cost**: Synthetic fertilizers are typically cheaper per unit of N-P-K than natural alternatives. A 50-lb bag of 10-10-10 synthetic fertilizer (costing approximately $15-20) provides the same macronutrient quantity as $40-60 worth of organic equivalents
- **Long-term soil productivity**: When factoring in the cost of replacing soil organic matter, improving drainage, and addressing compaction caused by synthetic use, natural fertilizers become more economical over a 5-10 year horizon. Healthy soils requiring less irrigation, fewer pest controls, and less amendments offset the higher per-unit nutrient cost
- **Hidden costs of synthetics**: These include groundwater contamination (which affects well water quality), downstream nutrient pollution, the energy cost of Haber-Bosch nitrogen production (1-2% of global natural gas consumption), and the degradation of soil carbon stocks that would otherwise sequester atmospheric CO2
