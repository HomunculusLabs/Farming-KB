## The Four Grand Divisions

Palmer's classification was based on what a novice observer sees when inverting a mushroom and examining the underside of the cap:

1. **Agaricini** — thin plates (gills) radiating from a central point like wheel spokes
2. **Polyporei/Boleti** — a spongy substance of fine tubes (pores) beneath the cap
3. **Hydnei** — small points or spines instead of gills or pores
4. **Puff-balls (Lycoperdons)** — globular fungi with enclosed spores, no external spore-bearing surface

## Puff-balls (Lycoperdons)

### Safety Profile
Palmer considered puff-balls the **safest mushrooms for beginners**. He stated: "I have for years eaten every species of white puff-balls I could find, and never saw a hurtful kind." No poisonous fungus resembles a young white puff-ball.

### Identification
- Globular shape, smooth skin
- Pure white inside when young and edible
- If a knife leaves a yellow stain, the specimen is too old
- The giant puff-ball (Lycoperdon giganteum) is the largest species
- Smaller varieties are equally safe when white throughout
- They change rapidly after gathering and should only be eaten in their freshest state

### Medicinal Notes
Old puff-balls produce an anesthetic smoke when burnt and have been used to stupefy bees and even as a surgical anesthetic. The young ones have a smell "somewhat like valerian." The puff-ball contains an anesthetic substance about which little was known in Palmer's time.

### Dark-Fleshed Species
Puff-balls dark inside when young (Scleroderma) are not considered edible. They are coarse and uninviting, though no authority brands them as specifically noxious.

## Boleti

### Safety Profile
Palmer believed Boleti were "probably free from danger to human life" and noted that "there is no recorded case of death from toadstool poisoning that has been traced to a boletus." The Italians made extensive harvests of Boleti in American forests, using the same varieties they ate at home.

### Distinguishing Edible from Inedible
The critical distinction is between Boleti (ground-growing, with distinct stems and easily separable pores) and their "twin brothers," the Polyporei (mostly wood-growing with abortive stems):

**Edible indicators:**
- Pores are brown, yellowish, or greenish
- Top is russet or any shade of brown
- Flesh remains white when cut
- Modest, subdued coloring

**Warning signs:**
- Brilliant coloring (red or yellow tops)
- Tubes/pores that are red at the orifice
- Bitter taste
- Slimy texture
- Color change to red or blue when bruised or cut (though this alone does not indicate poison unless accompanied by bitterness)

### Notable Edible Species
- [[hydnum-repandum]] (spreading hydnum) is the most common edible species

### Characteristics of Hydnum repandum
- Usually yellow, sometimes reddish
- Always firm-fleshed
- Stem deformed, indistinct, or eccentric
- One side of the cap is frequently much higher than the opposite side
- The tooth-like spines are sufficient for identification

## Agaricini (Gill-bearing Fungi)

### Safety Profile
Palmer regarded the Agaricini as "the least safe for the amateur" despite containing the common mushroom. The family is too large and diverse for safe generalization. Each species must be individually identified and tested.

### The Common Mushroom (Agaricus campestris and Agaricus arvensis)

The meadow mushroom and horse-mushroom are so similar that "cooks and gardeners do not recognize any distinction between the two." Key features:

- Starts as a button resembling a puff-ball
- A veil or web appears beneath the cap, soon rupturing to expose gills
- Gill color progresses from pink through purple to black
- Ring around the stem (may disappear in old specimens)
- Cap color ranges from pure white to deep brown
- Peculiar taste and smell that are distinctive once learned
- Takes about 10 days to mature from pin-head button to full size

### The Maned Mushroom (Coprinus comatus)
- Turtle-egg shape when fit for food, brown smooth top with white mane or fringe
- Hollow stem resembling macaroni when cut, movable ring around the stem
- Gills turn black, then the entire mushroom deliquesces into ink
- Has "no poisonous counterpart" but must never be eaten when stale or with black gills
- Berkeley and Curtis considered it poisonous twenty years before Palmer's time

### The Elm-Tree Mushroom (Agaricus ulmarius)
- Grows only in autumn on elm trees, thick solid stem, firm white flesh
- Light yellow top, at first smooth then spotted and cracked
- Often reappears yearly in the same spot
- Called "tree-fish" for its fresh, tonic smell resembling broiled fish

### The Fairy-Ring Champignon (Marasmius oreades)
- Small, leathery toadstool growing in rich, grassy lawns, cream colored with musk flavor
- Can be kept indefinitely by drying; expands in water
- Often confused with other species even by warned gatherers

### The Honey-Colored Mushroom (Agaricus melleus)
- Very acrid when raw but harmless when cooked; heat destroys the acrid principle
- Despite Paulet's report of a dog dying from it, Palmer considered it a "favorite article of diet"

## Russulae and Lactarii

### Safety Profile
The Russula family is "quite a safe one for beginners" because the noxious principle is always detectable by taste. The closely related Lactarii (milk mushrooms) share this property. Palmer wrote that in years of experience he "never discovered" dangerous members of either family.

### Identification
- Very brittle flesh — gills and whole fungus snap suddenly when bent (unlike the pliable common mushroom)
- Gills break into small pieces rather than matting down
- Lactarii exude an albuminous milk containing resin when broken; when mild, they are edible; when acrid, salt and water often neutralizes the hot principle

### The Taste Test
Palmer's rule: "If they are perfectly dry, taste a small piece. If it is not an edible russula, it will be likely to bite and sting the tongue, while all the esculent varieties of the genus are tasteless, mild, or with the flavor of chestnuts."

### Notable Species
- Russula alutacea — bright red top, buff-colored gills, stout short stem; edible but easily confused with [[russula-emetica]] and Russula rosacea (both acrid)
- Palmer always tasted a small piece of stem from each specimen while washing, even with familiar species
