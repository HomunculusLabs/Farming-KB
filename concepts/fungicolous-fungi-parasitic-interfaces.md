# Fungicolous Fungi Parasitic Interfaces and Ecology Source: Biodiversity of Fungi (Hyde et al., Ch. 17) Fungicolous fungi are fungi that grow on other fungi, encompassing a spectrum of interactions from commensalism through parasitism to necrotrophy. This diverse guild plays important but often overlooked roles in fungal community structure. ## Mycoparasite-Host Interface Types Jeffries and Young (1994) distinguished five principal types of mycoparasite-host interfaces: ### Necrotrophic Interfaces (host cytoplasm degenerates) 1. **Contact necrotrophic**: Neither hyphae nor haustoria penetrate the host; it is damaged by hyphal interference. Examples: Arthrobotrys superba and A. oligospora (nematophagous fungi functioning as contact mycoparasites); Tilletiopsis species attacking powdery mildew Sphaerotheca fuliginea. 2. **Invasive necrotrophic**: Parasite hyphae penetrate cell wall and enter host cell with considerable growth within. Examples: Talaromyces flavus, Schizophyllum commune, Trichoderma species, and many others. ### Biotrophic Interfaces (host cytoplasm remains healthy) 3. **Haustorial**: A short haustorial branch from the parasite penetrates the host hypha. Examples: Mycoparasites among the Zygomycota and Tremellales; Sporidesmium sclerotivorum parasitizing Sclerotinia sclerotiorum. 4. **Fusion**: Micropores develop in walls of host and parasite hyphae in contact, allowing cytoplasmic contact. Examples: Gonatobotrys simplex, Hansfordia parasitica, Melanospora zamiae. Tetragoniomyces uliginosus on Rhizoctonia solani and Syzygospora pallida on Phanerodontia produce micropores in their haustoria. 5. **Intracellular**: The complete thallus of the mycoparasite enters a host hypha. Examples: Many species of Chytrids and some Oomycetes growing inside other fungi. ## Defense Reactions Host fungi exhibit defense reactions including callosity formation (wall thickenings) at penetration points, lipid droplets, and invaginations of the host plasmalemma. These were first described by Swart (1975) and have been documented when Coprinus heptemerus encounters Ascobolus crenulatus (Ikediugwu 1976a), or Phlebiopsis gigantea encounters Heterobasidion annosum (Ikediugwu 1976b). ## Host Range and Specificity Most mycoparasites are unspecialized and can infect a wide range of host fungi across diverse systematic groups. Some can also be entomogenous or nematophagous. More specific associations exist, however. Lichenicolous fungi are generally specialized and rarely grow on nonlichenized host fungi. Several fungicolous fungi are hyperparasites of other fungicolous fungi. ## Physiological Mechanisms - **Necrotrophic mycoparasites** release toxins and lytic enzymes (particularly chitinase) into the environment. - **Biotrophic types** have not been demonstrated to produce these. - **Antibiotic metabolites**: Production is medium-dependent. Some efficient mycoparasites (Acremonium strictum, Clonostachys rosea, Trichothecium roseum, Sistotrema brinkmannii) overgrow other fungi without being inhibited. - **Volatile metabolites**: Trichoderma species exhibit various modes of interspecific interaction mediated by volatile and nonvolatile metabolites. - **Gene transfer**: Horizontal transfer of genetic material from host to parasite has been demonstrated in the mucoralean association between Absidia glauca and Parasitella parasitica (Kellner et al. 1993; Wöstemeyer et al. 1995), with the prototrophic parasite compensating for artificially induced deficiencies in the host. ## Evolution of Mycoparasitism A semi-biotrophic mode (biotrophic organisms later becoming necrotrophic or saprotrophic) may be more primitive than holobiotrophy and saprotrophy (Cooke and Whipps 1980). Obligate parasitism may be a fundamental attribute of primitive groups, from which saprotrophic subgroups have arisen repeatedly. Specific associations between parasites and hosts are products of long coevolutionary processes likely as old as the fungi involved. ## Taxonomic Distribution The fungicolous habit is widespread throughout the fungi but particularly common in certain groups. Chytridiomycota, Oomycota, and Hyphochytridiomycota all contain fungicolous species. The Tremellales are notably rich in fungicolous taxa, many with unusual morphologies that may have resulted from coevolution with their hosts. ## Detection Methods - Water mounts with cotton blue or lactic acid for microscopic observation - Phloxine staining to differentiate living from dead cells - Immunolabeled dyes and fluorescence methods - Infrared photomicrography for detecting enzymatic activity sites - Dual culture assays on cellophane disks for screening biocontrol potential ## See Also - [[fungicolous-fungi]]

## Trichoderma as a Model Mycoparasite

Trichoderma species are the most commercially important mycoparasites
and have been extensively studied as biocontrol agents against plant
pathogenic fungi. T. harzianum and T. viride are widely used in
agriculture to suppress soil-borne pathogens including Rhizoctonia,
Pythium, and Fusarium species. Their mycoparasitic activity involves
a multi-step process: chemotropic growth toward the host, recognition
of host surface molecules, coiling around host hyphae, penetration
via enzyme secretion (chitinases, glucanases, proteases), and
subsequent colonization. Genome sequencing of T. virens and
T. atroviride has revealed large expansions of mycoparasitism-related
gene families including secondary metabolite clusters and hydrolytic
enzyme genes, reflecting their evolutionary specialization.

## Hyperparasitism and Trophic Cascades

Hyperparasitism -- fungi parasitizing other parasitic fungi -- creates
complex trophic cascades within fungal communities. Ampelomyces
quisqualis parasitizes powdery mildew fungi and is used commercially
as a biocontrol agent. Cladosporium cladosporioides can parasitize
both rust fungi and other fungicolous species, occupying multiple
trophic levels. These hyperparasitic relationships can suppress
plant disease outbreaks in natural systems, where the population of
a primary pathogen is regulated not just by host plant resistance
but by a web of secondary and tertiary fungal parasites. Understanding
these cascading interactions is essential for predicting the outcomes
of biocontrol introductions and for managing fungal disease in
agricultural and forest ecosystems. In some cases, hyperparasites
have been shown to drive coevolutionary arms races that increase
pathogen genetic diversity over ecological time scales.

## Fungicolous Fungi in Cultivation Contexts

In mushroom cultivation, fungicolous fungi represent a significant
source of contamination and crop loss. Trichoderma species are the
most common contaminants of mushroom compost and spawned substrate,
often outcompeting the cultivated species. Cobweb mold (Cladobotryum
dendroides) parasitizes the fruiting bodies of Agaricus bisporus
and Pleurotus species, reducing yield and quality. Verticillium
fungicola causes dry bubble disease on button mushrooms. Managing
these contaminants requires strict hygiene, proper pasteurization,
and sometimes specific biological controls. Paradoxically, some
fungicolous fungi that cause problems in cultivation are the same
species valued for biocontrol in plant pathology, highlighting the
context-dependent nature of these interactions.

## Molecular Tools for Studying Mycoparasitism

Modern molecular methods have transformed the study of fungicolous
interactions. Metatranscriptomic analysis of dual cultures reveals
the gene expression changes that occur during mycoparasitic attack,
identifying which hydrolytic enzymes and secondary metabolites are
upregulated during different phases of the interaction. GFP-tagged
strains allow real-time microscopic observation of hyphal
penetration and host response. CRISPR-based gene knockouts in
Trichoderma have identified specific chitinase genes essential for
mycoparasitic efficacy against different host species. These tools
are enabling the development of engineered biocontrol strains with
enhanced activity against target pathogens while minimizing
non-target effects on beneficial soil fungi. Comparative genomics
across mycoparasitic lineages is now revealing convergent evolution
of parasitism genes, suggesting that similar molecular solutions
have evolved independently in distantly related fungal groups.
