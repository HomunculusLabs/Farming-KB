# Fungicolous Fungi Parasitic Interfaces and Ecology

Source: Biodiversity of Fungi (Hyde et al., Ch. 17)

Fungicolous fungi are fungi that grow on other fungi, encompassing a spectrum of interactions from commensalism through parasitism to necrotrophy. This diverse guild plays important but often overlooked roles in fungal community structure.

## Mycoparasite-Host Interface Types

Jeffries and Young (1994) distinguished five principal types of mycoparasite-host interfaces:

### Necrotrophic Interfaces (host cytoplasm degenerates)

1. **Contact necrotrophic**: Neither hyphae nor haustoria penetrate the host; it is damaged by hyphal interference. Examples: Arthrobotrys superba and A. oligospora (nematophagous fungi functioning as contact mycoparasites); Tilletiopsis species attacking powdery mildew Sphaerotheca fuliginea.

2. **Invasive necrotrophic**: Parasite hyphae penetrate cell wall and enter host cell with considerable growth within. Examples: Talaromyces flavus, Schizophyllum commune, Trichoderma species, and many others.

### Biotrophic Interfaces (host cytoplasm remains healthy)

3. **Haustorial**: A short haustorial branch from the parasite penetrates the host hypha. Examples: Mycoparasites among the Zygomycota and Tremellales; Sporidesmium sclerotivorum parasitizing Sclerotinia sclerotiorum.

4. **Fusion**: Micropores develop in walls of host and parasite hyphae in contact, allowing cytoplasmic contact. Examples: Gonatobotrys simplex, Hansfordia parasitica, Melanospora zamiae. Tetragoniomyces uliginosus on Rhizoctonia solani and Syzygospora pallida on Phanerodontia produce micropores in their haustoria.

5. **Intracellular**: The complete thallus of the mycoparasite enters a host hypha. Examples: Many species of Chytrids and some Oomycetes growing inside other fungi.

## Defense Reactions

Host fungi exhibit defense reactions including callosity formation (wall thickenings) at penetration points, lipid droplets, and invaginations of the host plasmalemma. These were first described by Swart (1975) and have been documented when Coprinus heptemerus encounters Ascobolus crenulatus (Ikediugwu 1976a), or Phlebiopsis gigantea encounters Heterobasidion annosum (Ikediugwu 1976b).

## Host Range and Specificity

Most mycoparasites are unspecialized and can infect a wide range of host fungi across diverse systematic groups. Some can also be entomogenous or nematophagous. More specific associations exist, however. Lichenicolous fungi are generally specialized and rarely grow on nonlichenized host fungi. Several fungicolous fungi are hyperparasites of other fungicolous fungi.

## Physiological Mechanisms

- **Necrotrophic mycoparasites** release toxins and lytic enzymes (particularly chitinase) into the environment.
- **Biotrophic types** have not been demonstrated to produce these.
- **Antibiotic metabolites**: Production is medium-dependent. Some efficient mycoparasites (Acremonium strictum, Clonostachys rosea, Trichothecium roseum, Sistotrema brinkmannii) overgrow other fungi without being inhibited.
- **Volatile metabolites**: Trichoderma species exhibit various modes of interspecific interaction mediated by volatile and nonvolatile metabolites.
- **Gene transfer**: Horizontal transfer of genetic material from host to parasite has been demonstrated in the mucoralean association between Absidia glauca and Parasitella parasitica (Kellner et al. 1993; Wöstemeyer et al. 1995), with the prototrophic parasite compensating for artificially induced deficiencies in the host.

## Evolution of Mycoparasitism

A semi-biotrophic mode (biotrophic organisms later becoming necrotrophic or saprotrophic) may be more primitive than holobiotrophy and saprotrophy (Cooke and Whipps 1980). Obligate parasitism may be a fundamental attribute of primitive groups, from which saprotrophic subgroups have arisen repeatedly. Specific associations between parasites and hosts are products of long coevolutionary processes likely as old as the fungi involved.

## Taxonomic Distribution

The fungicolous habit is widespread throughout the fungi but particularly common in certain groups. Chytridiomycota, Oomycota, and Hyphochytridiomycota all contain fungicolous species. The Tremellales are notably rich in fungicolous taxa, many with unusual morphologies that may have resulted from coevolution with their hosts.

## Detection Methods

- Water mounts with cotton blue or lactic acid for microscopic observation
- Phloxine staining to differentiate living from dead cells
- Immunolabeled dyes and fluorescence methods
- Infrared photomicrography for detecting enzymatic activity sites
- Dual culture assays on cellophane disks for screening biocontrol potential

## Practical Applications

The principles outlined on this page connect directly to hands-on work in gardens,
farms, and homesteads. Integrating these concepts with complementary techniques from
related disciplines can significantly improve outcomes. Start with small-scale
implementation and scale up as understanding deepens through direct observation.

## Research Significance

The study of these specialized fungal groups contributes to our broader
understanding of fungal diversity, ecology, and evolution. Many species in
these groups have unique adaptations that make them valuable model organisms
for scientific research. Their ecological roles, while often overlooked, are
essential to the functioning of the ecosystems they inhabit. Continued
documentation and study of these fungi is important for advancing both
basic mycology and applied fields such as biocontrol and bioremediation.

## Conservation Status

Like many fungal groups, these organisms face threats from habitat destruction,
pollution, and environmental change. Their often-specific habitat requirements
make them particularly vulnerable to ecosystem disruption. Conservation efforts
should include protection of habitat types that support diverse fungal
communities, as well as documentation of fungal biodiversity through
systematic surveying. Many species in these groups remain undescribed or
poorly known, making inventory work especially valuable.

## Field Identification

Identifying these fungi in the field requires attention to substrate,
microhabitat, morphological features, and sometimes microscopic characters.
Many species are small and easily overlooked, requiring careful searching
and examination. Photography and collection of voucher specimens support
accurate identification and contribute to biodiversity records. Working
with experienced mycologists and consulting specialist literature greatly
improves identification accuracy for these often-challenging groups.
