# Deduction from Nature in Permaculture Design

Design by [[deduction-from-nature]] is the practice of adopting lessons learned from natural systems and applying them consciously to designed landscapes. The impetus for [[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s remarkable voyage to [[natural-farming]] was the sight of healthy rice plants growing and yielding in untended, uncultivated road verges. If rice can thrive naturally, he reasoned, why do we labor to cultivate the soil? He eventually achieved high-yielding rice production without cultivation, fertilizers, biocides, or machinery — entirely by learning from and imitating natural processes.

This approach rests on a fundamental premise: nature already contains the solutions to most design challenges. The designer's task is to observe carefully, understand the principles at work, and then apply those principles using species and assemblies that serve human needs while maintaining ecological function.

## Structure: Imitating Natural System Architecture

The first level of learning from nature is **structural imitation**. If the natural systems of a region include palms, vines, large evergreen trees, an edge of herbaceous perennials, a groundcover of bulbs or tubers, and a rich bird fauna, then a designed system can reconstruct or imitate that structure using:
- Some native species for pioneers, bird forage, and vine supports
- Additional species of greater direct use to the settlement — food, craft, medicine, and fuel plants

After studying the natural placement of woody legumes or windbreaks in local ecosystems, the designer can imitate these patterns. It is often possible to improve on local species by finding out-of-region or exotic species even better suited to specific roles than those of an impoverished or degraded native flora. The key is to select species offering a wider range of uses while maintaining the structural integrity of the natural model.

## Process: Understanding Natural Dynamics

Beyond structure, the most critical area of study is **process** — how natural systems actually function over time. Where does water run? How does it absorb? Why do trees grow in special sites in deserts? Can these processes be constructed or harnessed for human benefit?

Some natural processes are energized by animals, wind, water, pioneer trees, forbs, or fire. Since every design is a continuous process, the designer should above all try to create useful **self-generating systems** — systems that propagate, maintain, and improve themselves with minimal human intervention.

### Self-Propagation by Water
On Lake Chelan in Washington state, walnuts self-generate from seed rolling downhill in the valleys of intermittent streams. Similar systems work for palms in the tropics, candle-nut (Aleurites) in Hawaii, and asparagus along sandy irrigation channels. The designer can set up headwater plantations and allow self-propagation downstream — for willows, Russian olive, water plants including taro in unstable floodwater lowlands — saving enormous labor.

### Self-Propagation by Animals
Birds spread useful forage plants such as elderberries, Coprosma, Lycium, autumn olive, pioneer trees and herbs, and preferred grains like Chenopodium species. By placing a few of these plants and allowing free-ranging pigeons or pheasants, they plant more. Dogs and foxes spread loquats; bears spread small fruits; cattle spread hard seeds like honey locusts. Burrowers and hoarders such as gophers carry bulbs and root cuttings into prairie. Jays, squirrels, choughs, and currawongs spread oaks by burying acorns.

### Pioneer Species and Succession
In grasslands and old pastures, pioneer species such as tobacco bush, pine, or Acacia provide roosting sites for birds, initiating soil change that allows clumps or coppices of forest to form. The designer can use the same techniques with allied species of more direct use. Many native peoples evolve scattered forest nuclei based on pioneer trees, termite mounds, and compost heaps. Perches for birds to drop pioneer seeds can establish plant nuclei in degraded lands.

### Biological Pest Control
Nest holes attract owls to control rodents, purple martins to reduce mosquitoes, and woodpeckers to control codling moth (woodpeckers alone reduce codling moth by 40-60%). Nurse plants allow insect predators to overwinter, feed, or shelter within gardens. Small ponds provide habitat for frogs. Rock piles shelter lizards. If these aids to pest control are desired, the designer must provide habitat for them.

### Using Succession Against Invasive Species
Nature imposes successions and limits on every species. Once the rules are understood, succession can be used to limit or exclude problem species. Soft vines smother prickly shrubs. Browsed or cut out, they allow trees to permanently shade out the shrub or rot its seeds in mulch. Kikuyu grass is blocked from spreading by low hedges of comfrey, lemongrass, arrowroot (Canna spp.), or nasturtiums. Rampant species like Lantana can be smothered by vines such as chayote (Sechium edulis) and succeeded with palm-legume forests.

### Design by Analogy
If thistles grow around a rabbit warren, then disturbing the soil, supplying urine and manure, and sowing seed may produce globe artichokes. Penning goats or sheep on a place, then shutting them out and planting, was the thinking behind chicken or pig "tractors" for removing stubborn weeds like nut-grass, Convolvulus, onion-weed, and twitch before planting a new succession. Fences or pits can trap wind-blown debris — dried leaves, manures, seagrasses — for garden use.

## Landscape: Reading and Creating Niches

Gullies, ridgetops, natural shade, the sides of multi-storey buildings, and exposed sunny sites all demonstrate different opportunities, just as various velocities and grades of streams or rock-falls present specific niches. Every such special site can serve a purpose — as an aid to food storage, food dehydration, an energy source, or a site for a special animal or plant.

Over time, as groves of trees grow, earthbanks are raised, houses are built, or caves are excavated, new opportunities are created. It is in the creation of **microclimates** that natural diversity and richness increase most dramatically. Every clump of trees invites new species to establish, every shaded area provides refuge from heat, and every stone pile creates a moist, shaded soil site. The designer can plan these evolutions and plant to take advantage of them using data derived from close observation of natural systems.

## Philosophy: The Deeper Value of Learning from Nature

Life in a well-designed system is not merely survival. First by designing well, and then observing system evolution, the practitioner gains contemplative and celebratory time. In celebration, the myths and skills important to future generations can be incorporated. In contemplation, more refined, profound, or subtle insights into good procedures emerge.

To become a designer or gardener is to directly benefit nature or society, and one inevitably generates natural ethics and philosophies. To become a good designer is to be in search of an understanding of nature, and to be content with the search itself — to design by natural example, becoming aware, taking notes, sitting a long time in one place, watching the wind behave and the trees respond, thrusting a hand into the soil to feel for moisture, and becoming sensitive to the processes and sights about you.

The huge information store that is nature is itself a primary reason for its preservation. No equivalent education system operates without cost or bureaucratic involvement. These are the very best lessons to adopt — in microcosm and macrocosm, we can learn from the world, and some lessons are so obvious that we could pinch ourselves for failing to notice them.

## The Practical Synthesis

All the strategies derived from observing natural processes can be used consciously in design to achieve a great reduction in work and energy inputs. The methodology is design by analogy: selecting analogous or botanically allied species for trials, observing how nature solves problems, and adapting those solutions to serve human needs within an ecologically sound framework. This approach — learning from nature and applying those lessons — represents perhaps the most powerful and enduring design methodology available to the [[permaculture]] practitioner.
