# Mollison: Bioregional Organisation and Resource Index

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14, Section 14.5)

## Definition of a Bioregion

A bioregional association is an association of the residents of a natural and identifiable region. This region is sometimes defined by a watershed, sometimes by remnant or existing tribal or language boundaries, at times by town boundaries, suburban streets, or districts, and at times by some combination of the above factors. The acid test of a bioregion is that it is recognised as such by its inhabitants.

There is an obvious conflict between the need to live in a region in a responsible way (bioregional centrality) and the need to integrate with other people in other places (global outreach). We need not only to "think globally and act locally" but to "act and think globally and locally."

## Scale and Organisation

Ideally, the region can be limited to that occupied by 7,000 to 40,000 people. Of these, perhaps only a hundred will be initially interested in any regional association, and even fewer will be active in it. The work of the bioregional group is to assess natural, technical, service, and financial resources of the region, and to identify areas where leakage of resources (water, soil, money, talent) leave the region.

The regional office needs 4 to 6 people to act as consultants and coordinators, with others on call when needed. All associations can use the office for registration, address, phone, and newsletter services, paying a fee for usage.

The regional office can serve as:
- Land access centre.
- Leasehold and title register.
- Community self-funding scheme centre.
- Trust and society collection point.
- Contact centre to other regions.
- Trade or coordination centre.

## The Bioregional Resource Index

Mollison and Maxine Cole compiled a Resource Index for Bioregions for the Northern Rivers Bioregional Association of New South Wales, Australia. The primary categories are:

### Category A: Food and Food Support Systems

Criteria: Native and economic species, organic and biocide-free, products of good nutritional value.

Subcategories include:
- A1: Plant resources (nurseries, seed exchanges, native species reserves, botanical gardens, research institutes, demonstration farms, skilled botanists and horticulturists, publications, contractors, produce).
- A2: Animal resources (breeders, hatcheries, species collections, demonstration farms, skilled farriers and vets, produce).
- A3: Pest management (insectaries, biological controls, safe chemicals, IPM information).
- A4: Food processing (equipment suppliers, processing centres, preservation information, yeasts and bacterial ferment materials).
- A5: Markets and outlets (local markets, delivery services, export, urban-rural co-ops, roadside sales, annual barter fair).
- A6: Support functions (residue testing, soil and water analysis, hydrological services, fencing, natural fertilisers, farm machinery, glasshouse supplies, lime quarries, trace mineral sources, geological resources).

### Category B: Shelter and Buildings

Criteria: Energy-efficient house design and non-toxic materials only.

- B1: Construction materials (timber, stone, gravel, earth, plumbing, roofing, bricks, tiles, paints, furniture, tools, libraries, housing surveys, sources of toxins).
- B2: Energy systems (energy-efficient appliances, hot water systems, space heating, power generation, appropriate technology groups, low-energy designers, publications, reliable contractors).
- B3: Wastes and recycling (sewage and greywater, compost, solid wastes, recycling-based occupations).

### Category C: Livelihoods and Support Systems

Criteria: Right livelihood, durable and well-made items.

- C1: Community finance (barter, small business loans, community banking, land access, trusts, legal services).
- C2: Livelihood support (small business centres, skills resource bank, self-employment, training).
- C3: Crafts and manufacturing (clothing, footwear, basketry, pottery, steelwork, woodwork, engines, glasswork, paper recycling, catering, draughting, soaps).

### Category D: Information, Media, Communications, Research

Criteria: Essential community information.

- Regional radio and CB, ham radio.
- Regional news and newspapers.
- Audio-visual services, photography, television, film.
- Business communications (fax, telex, modem, computer, journals, libraries).
- Computer services and training.
- Libraries and data collections.
- Maps.
- Bioregional groups and contacts, local and overseas.
- Standard documents and data sheets.

### Category E: Community and Security

House and livestock security, house sitting, neighbourhood watch, cattle watch, flood cleanup, bush rescue, communication systems, emergency communications.

### Category F: Social Life

Introductory services, think tanks, expeditions, work groups.

### Category G: Health Services

Medical services, nutrition services, ecological midwifery, morbidity data (accidents, infectious diseases, addictions, genetic defects, nutritional problems).

### Category H: Future Trends and Potential Threats

Sea level rises, greenhouse effect, water pollution, biocides, radioactives, financial recession.

### Category I: Transport

Barge and sea systems, draught animal systems, group delivery, innovations, local fuels, transport routes, air and ultralight craft.

### Category M: Appendices

Bioregional map, geological map, plant system map, soils map, sources and references, regions and parishes, land titles, access and roads, reserves and easements, rivers and water supplies.

## Key Insight: Jobs Vacant

If essential services are listed, deficiencies noted, and leaks of capital detected, then there is immediately obvious a category of "jobs vacant." If there is a modest investment or funding organisation set up, then capital to train and equip people to fill these gaps is also available. When basic needs are supplied locally, research and skills will reveal work in producing excess for trade, which can be information and education to other regions.

## The Secret of Success

The secret of bioregional success is assembling sufficient commonsense people in one area. If land titles in a region are bought out and occupied by any group sharing an ethical philosophy, the shops, markets, processing centres, equipment, and support services for the new economy become worthwhile and available.

## The Life Census

Every bioregion should monitor tree cover, wildlife, seaweed beds, bird colonies, species counts, and productive cultivated land at regular intervals. If these have increased in yield and maintained in species, the area maintains health. A "life census" needs to be compiled every 2 to 5 years.

Every region needs to act as a curator and refuge for some critical life elements of allied regions, so that absolute loss of species is unlikely short of global catastrophe. In some land trusts, this biological-environmental accounting sets the basis for the "economic rent," and in the event of a degenerative trend, even the basis for continuing in occupation.

## Merged: Mollison: Money, Finance, and Asset Categories
Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 14, Section 14.11) Money is in itself not a resource; it represents (or should represent) a resource which lies "somewhere else." Money, in a sane society, must therefore be tied or fixed in value relative to a useful real asset; this is the very basis of fair trade in large societies. All money arises from the wealth of the natural world (plants, clean water, clear air, stored energy).
