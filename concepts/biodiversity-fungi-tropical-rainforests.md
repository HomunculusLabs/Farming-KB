# Fungal Biodiversity in Tropical Rainforests

Tropical rainforests represent the most species-rich terrestrial ecosystems on Earth, and their fungal diversity is correspondingly vast but poorly documented. Current estimates suggest that at least 74,000 to 120,000 fungal species have been formally named, with total global fungal diversity estimated at 1.5 million species or more — meaning at most only about 5% of fungal species on Earth are known. A substantial proportion of the undiscovered fungi are thought to inhabit tropical ecosystems.

## Scale of Diversity

Evidence from studies on plants in the tropics, combined with critical molecular investigations, suggests that even the 1.5 million figure may be too low. No single site in the world has been comprehensively surveyed for fungi. In temperate areas, approximately six times as many fungi as native plants can be expected to occur at a site, with 2,500 to 3,000 species found in about 200 hectares when a variety of specialists study the site for more than 25 years. Tropical rainforests likely support considerably higher ratios of fungal to plant species diversity.

## Patterns in Tropical Forests

Meta-data analyses of plot-based studies across North America, Europe, China, and Costa Rica have demonstrated that habitat type and tree diversity play a larger role in explaining macrofungal diversity differences between studies than sampling effort. Tropical forests, with their high plant diversity and complex vegetation structure, support particularly rich fungal communities. Macrofungi fruiting in tropical forests show distinct seasonal and successional patterns, with species turnover related to changes in substrate quality and microclimate conditions.

## Litter and Soil Fungi

Tropical rainforest leaf litter supports diverse fungal assemblages. Studies in the Ivory Coast identified 129–165 species from undisturbed tropical forest plots, while research in Puerto Rico recovered 338 species from wet forest litter using particle-washing techniques. Studies from Panama documented approximately 500 species from leaf litter of just six tree species. The litter-to-soil transition in humid tropical forests creates a particularly rich zone for fungal activity, though the relative contributions of litter fungi versus soil fungi can be difficult to discriminate.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi in the Tropics

The diversity of ectomycorrhizal fungi in tropical forests is notable, though their distribution is patchy and linked to the presence of specific ectomycorrhizal host trees. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are broadly distributed across tropical to boreal AM trees and shrubs. Mycorrhizal sporocarp production can be increased through removal of leaf litter and debris, which acts as a moisture sink for maintenance of mycorrhizal fungi in seasonally dry tropical forests.

## Endophytes and Other Cryptic Fungi

Tropical plants harbor rich endophytic fungal communities. Studies of tropical palms and broad-leaved trees have yielded numerous new species. Endophytes in tropical forests are ubiquitous in phanerogams, and intensive surveys of tropical hosts invariably yield new species. The ratio of endemic endophyte species to host species may be greater than 1 in temperate plants, though whether this holds in tropical forests remains to be fully investigated.

## Challenges and Sampling

Sampling fungal biodiversity in tropical rainforests faces particular challenges: many fungi produce fruit bodies only at intervals of many years, and those structures may persist for only a few hours. Other fungi live inside plants or arthropods. Many can be found only by examining surfaces microscopically or by isolating and culturing until they sporulate. Standardized sampling protocols using fixed-size plots, transects, and complementary methods for different fungal guilds are essential for meaningful comparison across tropical sites.

## Conservation Concerns

Tropical deforestation and habitat fragmentation pose severe threats to undocumented fungal diversity. Many species may be lost before they are ever described. Comprehensive fungal inventories of tropical rainforest sites are urgently needed to establish baseline data against which future changes can be measured.

## Sampling Challenges and Protocols

Sampling fungal biodiversity in tropical rainforests faces particular challenges. Many fungi produce fruit bodies only at intervals of many years, and those structures may persist for only a few hours or days. Other fungi live inside plants or arthropods. Many can be found only by examining surfaces microscopically or by isolating and culturing until they sporulate.

Standardized sampling protocols are essential for meaningful comparison across tropical sites. These include:
- Fixed-size plots for macrofungal surveys
- Transect methods for assessing fruiting patterns
- Complementary methods for different fungal guilds (litter fungi, soil fungi, endophytes, ectomycorrhizal fungi, wood-inhabiting fungi)
- Particle-washing techniques for recovering microfungi from leaf litter
- Moist chamber incubation for inducing sporulation
- Selective isolation media targeting specific fungal groups

## Tropical Deforestation and Fungal Extinction

Tropical deforestation and habitat fragmentation pose severe threats to undocumented fungal diversity. Many species may be lost before they are ever described. The relationship between plant diversity and fungal diversity in tropical forests means that loss of plant species through deforestation cascades into loss of associated fungal species — endophytes, mycorrhizal partners, litter decomposers, and specialized pathogens.

Comprehensive fungal inventories of tropical rainforest sites are urgently needed to establish baseline data against which future changes can be measured. Meta-data analyses of plot-based studies across North America, Europe, China, and Costa Rica have demonstrated that habitat type and tree diversity play a larger role in explaining macrofungal diversity differences between studies than sampling effort.

## Macrofungal Fruiting Patterns

Macrofungi fruiting in tropical forests show distinct seasonal and successional patterns. Species turnover is related to changes in substrate quality and microclimate conditions. The continuously warm and humid conditions in tropical rainforests allow rapid fungal colonization and decomposition, with shorter leaf litter residence times compared to temperate systems.

## The Endophyte Reservoir

Tropical plants harbor rich endophytic fungal communities. Studies of tropical palms and broad-leaved trees have yielded numerous new species. Endophytes in tropical forests are ubiquitous in phanerogams, and intensive surveys of tropical hosts invariably yield new species. The ratio of endemic endophyte species to host species may be greater than 1 in temperate plants, though whether this holds in tropical forests remains to be fully investigated. If endophyte-to-host ratios in the tropics approach or exceed temperate ratios, the number of undiscovered endophyte species alone could account for hundreds of thousands of unnamed fungi.

## Insect-Fungal Symbioses in the Tropics

Insect-fungal symbioses are responsible for approximately 20% of Neotropical photosynthate turnover. The attine ant-fungus association, termite-fungal gardens, and ambrosia beetle-fungus relationships are all particularly diverse and well-developed in tropical ecosystems. These symbioses represent some of the most complex mutualistic interactions in nature and are critical to tropical forest ecosystem functioning.

## Key Researchers and Pioneers

Important contributors to tropical mycology include:
- E.J.H. Corner, whose extensive surveys of Malaysian tropical fungi in the 1930s-1950s established foundational taxonomic work
- R.W.G. Dennis, who studied neotropical fungi in Trinidad and Venezuela
- J. Furtado, who surveyed microfungi in Malaysia
- G.F. Bills and J.D. Polishook, who developed modern quantitative methods for tropical litter mycology
