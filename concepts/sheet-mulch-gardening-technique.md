## Sheet Mulch Gardening Technique

[[sheet-mulching]] is the foundational technique for creating productive garden beds without digging, tilling, or weeding. It converts compacted, damaged, or weed-infested ground into rich, living soil by layering organic materials over existing ground. Mollison describes it as saving "a great deal of labour, and a great deal of water, while dispensing with material that normally goes into landfill."

## What Sheet Mulch Suppresses

The system suppresses all weeds including the most persistent:
- Ivy
- Onion and spear twitch
- Kikuyu and buffalo grass
- Docks, dandelions, oxalis
- Onion-weed
- Even blackberries

## The Complete Layering Process

### Preparation
Before starting, plant any large trees or shrubs from the nursery as usual. Do not dig, level, or weed the area. Start very close to the house, preferably from a foundation or path that is itself weed-free.

### Base Layer: Amendments
Sprinkle the area with:
- A handful of [[dolomite]]
- A handful of chicken manure or blood and bone (adds nitrogen to start carbon reduction)

### Sheet Layer: Weed Barrier
Tile and overlap the area with sheet mulch material:
- Cardboard
- Wallboard
- Newspaper
- Old carpet, underfelt
- Old mattresses or clothing
- Rotted palings or thin wood

If you have non-noxious kitchen waste (tea leaves, peelings, leaves, small food scraps), scatter these first for worms. If you have weed-seedy hay, bury it below the overlapped material.

Cover the area **completely**, leaving no holes for weeds to poke through. If a valuable tree or shrub is in the way, tear paper half across and pull it around the stem, then add another piece at right angles. Leave only valuable plants (dandelions, clover, useful small plants) with leaves poking out.

### First Organic Layer (75 mm)
Choose one or more of:
- Horse-stable straw
- Poultry manure in sawdust
- Seagrass or seaweed
- Leaf mould or raked leaves

All are manurial, contain essential elements, and hold water well.

### Top Dry Layer (150 mm)
Choose one or more of:
- Pine needles
- [[casuarina]] needles
- Rice husks
- Nut shells
- Seagrass (Zostera)
- Leaf mould or raked leaves
- Cocoa beans
- Dry straw (not hay)
- Bark, chips, or sawdust

### Final Depth
Always put at least 225 mm of cover over the paper/cardboard. 300 mm is better. 375 mm is too much. Less than 225 mm is of no use.

"Do a small area very well, not a large area thinly or sloppily." It takes about 20 minutes to cover 10 m².

## Planting into Sheet Mulch

### Large Seeds and Tubers
Burrow down a small hole to the base of the loose top mulch. Punch or slit a hole in the paper/carpet with an old axe or knife. Place a double handful of earth in the hole. Push in seed or tuber. Pull mulch back over.

### Seedlings
Same hole-punching process, but hold leaves softly in one hand and bring mulch up to the base of the plant.

### Small Seeds (Radish, Carrot)
Pull back the mulch in a row. Lay down a line of sand. Sow small seeds. Cover with a narrow board for a few days until seeds sprout (or sprout them on damp paper first). Remove board and draw mulch up as tops grow.

## Planting Plan Example

- **Near the path**: Chamomile and thyme
- **Behind them**: Larger herbs (marjoram, sage, [[comfrey]])
- **Behind that**: Potatoes and tubers
- **Outer border**: Small fruits and fruit trees
- **Fill holes with**: Strawberries, garlic cloves, onion plants, potatoes, or other useful plants at random

## First-Year Limitations

- **Root crops don't do well** in the first year (soil below still compacted; too much manure causes forking). Plant most root crops in the second year.
- **More watering needed** initially, as the rotted [[fungal-hyphae]] and plant layer at the base develop slowly.
- **Newly planted seedlings** need water initially, as in normal gardening.

## Ongoing Maintenance

### Watering
Water only when plants wilt. Feel down in the mulch; if damp at base, it doesn't need water. In one drought summer, an Anderson family garden survived all summer with one watering at Christmas.

### Weeding
Some strong weeds may push through. Carry damp newspaper and a bucket of sawdust. Push the weed down in the mulch, put damp paper on its head, cover with sawdust. All eventually die out. Another approach: dig up dock roots, bury kitchen scraps there, re-mulch.

### Repeat Planting
No need to rotate plants or "rest the ground." Potatoes are simply placed on top of old mulch and re-mulched. No room needed for hoeing or digging, so plants may be stacked more closely in mixed beds rather than strict rows. By frequent random replanting, the garden assumes "the healthy appearance of a mixed herbal pasture."

### Mulch Maintenance
- Add top mulch to keep levels up (chips, bark, pine needles, hay mix)
- Scatter a little lime or blood and bone
- For permanent beds, no more is needed
- Annuals need occasional fresh mulch after harvest
- Tuck all food wastes from kitchen under the mulch
- "Worms are so active that the leaves and peelings disappear overnight"
- Never bury sawdust or chips; put on top where atmospheric nitrogen breaks down wood
- Keep mulch loose; don't let it mat
- Mix lawn clippings or sawdust with stiff dry material like chips or pine needles

## Tree Performance

"Trees make quite phenomenal growth in this system, and bear several years earlier than in clean-tilled ground." In a few years, larger trees and shrubs become self-mulching, herbs hold their own, and only annuals need annual attention.

## Living Barriers

Ground invaders (kikuyu, couch, buffalo grass) constantly attack mulched areas. Plant a living barrier of immune species:
- Lemon-grass
- Pampas
- Comfrey
- Bamboo
- Coprosma
- Other vigorous, shady, or mat-rooted useful plants

Mulch the barrier well with cardboard, sawdust, or straw. The same approach can confine useful rampant species: blackberries in forest openings, cumbungi at pond edges surrounded by ti-tree, mint confined by shady dense bushes.

## Biological Benefits

- Mulch preserves soil organisms and steady temperature/moisture conditions
- These encourage organisms hostile to Phytophthora fungi (dieback, fire-blight, cinnamon fungus)
- Well-mulched gardens are less affected by disease than logged, roaded, and burnt forests
- Potatoes grown in mulch are often disease-free and "blight-resistant"

## The Social Dimension

"By using all organic wastes productively, you make the grade from consumer to producer, and the very nature of your garbage pail alters to harmless materials." Chris Stoltz of Ballarat extended mulch onto his nature strip and "became a lesson in productivity and an inspiration to his neighbours. The mind boggles at the end result of mass urban mulching."

## Keeping Annuals Perennial

Several techniques keep annuals "turning over" without resowing:
- **Leeks**: Leave a few to run to seed; many small bulbs form around the base; plant out like onion sets
- **Perennial alliums**: European chives (coarse and fine), asiatic garlic chives, shallots, potato onions (25 per one planted), Welsh onions, tree onion top bulbils, garlic cloves in the strawberry patch
- **Broad beans**: Leave large pods to dry and hay-mulch in late summer; they resprout in autumn
- **Corn**: Good summer interplant
