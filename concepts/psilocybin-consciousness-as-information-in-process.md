# Psilocybin Consciousness as Information in Process

In "The Psilocybin Solution," Simon G. Powell develops a comprehensive theory that consciousness is a form of information, specifically a pattern of information embodied within the flowing electrochemical state of the brain. This informational model of mind is derived from examining the effects of [[psilocybin-mushroom-identification]] and other [[entheogenic-ceremony-preparation]] agents upon the brain's neuronal architecture.

## The Mind/Body Problem Revisited

Powell begins with the classic philosophical mind/body problem articulated by Descartes in the 17th century. Descartes concluded that there were two sorts of universal substance, mind and matter, interacting in some mysterious ghostlike way. This dualistic "ghost in the machine" view has troubled philosophers and scientists for centuries. Scientists dislike talk of incorporeal entities somehow interacting with matter, which explains why most psychologists ignored consciousness until recently.

Powell proposes that we should abandon the Cartesian dualist position and instead embrace "informational monism," a view in which the reality process consists of one substance only: information. He argues that the nature of the [[turner-psilocybin-mushroom-experience-guide]] strongly supports this unified framework.

## Consciousness as Neuronal Information

Powell's central thesis is that mindstuff resolves itself as being informational stuff. Consciousness, whatever it is, is the "stuff" which mediates all science and all human experience. When chemically induced changes occur in global states of neuronal firing, these changes are equatable with changes in consciousness. Since global states of neuronal firing must be global states of information, we can conclude that [[mckenna-consciousness-expansion-and-drug-war]] is a form of information.

The evidence for this comes from multiple directions. [[psilocybin-mushroom-identification]], being a substance closely resembling the neurotransmitter Serotonin Receptor Psychopharmacology, alters the electrochemical dynamics of the brain. The resultant visionary experience demonstrates that consciousness is mutable and dependent upon the precise informational state of neuronal systems. Change the chemistry, change the neuronal firing patterns, change the informational state, and consciousness changes accordingly.

## The Phenomenology of Information

Under the influence of [[psilocybin-mushroom-identification]], Powell suggests that one is able to penetrate deeper into the informational content of objects. He compares this to looking up a word in a dictionary and noting all of its meanings, thus coming to understand the word in its fullest sense. Normally we might perceive only a fraction of an object's true semantic content, but in the entheogenic state more becomes accessible.

Powell argues that all objects, whether organic or inorganic, possess intrinsic meaning or sets of relations to other objects. They possess informational content linked to a network of relations. Real seeing involves the perception of what an object signifies: its role, function, and relations within a vast network of objects. Psychedelic perception involves accessing latent information normally occluded by the ordinary information processing constraints of the brain.

## The Self and the Other

Powell proposes that during entheogenic experience, consciousness appears to split into two informational entities: the self (ego) and the Other. The Other represents an organised source of intentional information that communicates with the individual whose neuronal system is infused with [[entheogenic-ceremony-preparation]] alkaloids.

The Other can be understood as a dissociated or higher-level informational source acting as one component in a dialogue process. Through entheogens, the individual psyche manages to open itself to the realm of the Other, facilitating a dialogue of thought in which radical knowledge is received. This Other aligns with what Aldous Huxley Doors Of Perception called "Mind at Large" and what [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]] called the "Gaian Mind."

## The Language of Shamanic Contemplation

The revelatory thoughts and ideas that erupt into consciousness during entheogenic ecstasy appear to be a manifestation of the Other, representing the holistic patterning of neuronal-mediated information. This is often experienced as a kind of internal dialogue with a wise being. Profound thoughts take on a rapidly flowing life of their own, generating further thoughts and insights.

Powell notes that this inner psychedelic language involving complex thoughts and ideation works far more efficiently than the language system of the spoken word. Everyday language appears sluggish and cumbersome in contrast to the language of [[shamanic-trance-cross-cultural-patterns]], which moves at a profoundly different pace. The sheer fluency and dramatic insightfulness of shamanic contemplation explains its emotional impact and ineffability.

## The Information Paradigm

Powell's model yields a sweeping reconceptualization of reality. If consciousness is information in process, and if Matter can also be described in informational terms, then everything, whether atoms, molecules, muscles, or thoughts, consists of essentially the same kind of substance: particular forms of information. This informational paradigm dissolves the mind/body distinction entirely.

The implications extend beyond philosophy into practical understanding. Organic visionary ecstasy, once tasted, is not forgotten. The difficulty lies in integrating the new view of reality with the old, merging them so that the insights gained through [[psychedelic-integration-guide]] become permanent features of one's worldview.

## Information Theory and Psychedelic States

The application of Claude Shannon's information theory to the study of consciousness represents one of the most promising frameworks for understanding how psychedelic compounds like [[psilocybin-mushroom-identification]] alter the informational dynamics of the brain. Shannon's foundational 1948 paper, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," established that information could be quantified in terms of the reduction of uncertainty — a concept measured in binary digits, or "bits." While Shannon's theory was originally developed for telecommunications engineering, its implications for neuroscience and the study of consciousness have proven profound.

In the context of normal waking consciousness, the brain can be understood as an information processing system that must balance two competing demands: the integration of diverse sensory inputs into a coherent model of reality, and the segregation of distinct informational streams for specialised processing. This balance is maintained by a hierarchical organisation in which higher-level cortical areas generate predictions about incoming sensory data, while lower-level areas process the raw data itself. The brain's default informational architecture is thus one of controlled efficiency: it admits only a fraction of the available information, discarding redundancy and privileging information relevant to immediate survival needs.

Psychedelic states appear to fundamentally disrupt this hierarchical organisation. Neuroimaging research, particularly the work of Robin Carhart-Harris and colleagues at Imperial College London, has demonstrated that psilocybin reduces the functional connectivity between the default mode network (DMN) — a set of interconnected brain regions associated with self-referential thinking, autobiographical memory, and the maintenance of the ego — and other brain networks. This reduction in hierarchical constraint leads to what Carhart-Harris has called "entropic brain" states, in which the brain's informational dynamics become more disordered, more flexible, and more sensitive to a wider range of inputs.

From an information-theoretic perspective, this can be understood as an increase in the brain's Shannon entropy — a measure of the uncertainty or unpredictability of its informational states. Under psilocybin, the brain explores a vastly expanded repertoire of possible informational configurations, many of which are inaccessible under normal conditions. This expansion of the brain's informational "state space" correlates with the phenomenological richness of the psychedelic experience: the enhanced perception of colour, pattern, and meaning; the dissolution of habitual thought patterns; and the emergence of novel associations between previously unrelated concepts.

The concept of mutual information — the amount of information that one variable carries about another — is also relevant. In normal consciousness, mutual information between brain regions is optimised for efficient communication along established pathways. Under psilocybin, mutual information becomes more widely distributed across brain regions that do not normally communicate directly, leading to the phenomenon of synaesthesia and to the experience of meaningful connections between seemingly unrelated phenomena. This informational perspective aligns with Powell's thesis that psychedelics make more information available to consciousness, but grounds it in the rigorous mathematical framework of information theory.

## The Predictive Processing Framework

The predictive processing framework (also known as predictive coding) has emerged as one of the most influential theories in cognitive neuroscience, and it offers a particularly elegant account of how psychedelic compounds produce their characteristic effects on consciousness. First developed by Karl Friston and colleagues, and elaborated by researchers such as Andy Clark and Jakob Hohwy, predictive processing holds that the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine: rather than passively receiving sensory input and constructing perceptions from it, the brain actively generates predictions about what it expects to perceive, and updates these predictions based on the discrepancy between expected and actual sensory data.

In this framework, the brain is organised hierarchically. Higher-level cortical areas generate top-down predictions about the likely causes of sensory input, while lower-level areas generate bottom-up "prediction errors" — signals indicating the degree of mismatch between prediction and reality. The brain's goal is to minimise prediction error, either by updating its predictions to better match incoming data or by acting on the world to make incoming data better match its predictions. This process of prediction error minimisation is what the brain is doing constantly, and it is what we experience as normal, stable perception.

Psychedelics disrupt this process by altering the relative weighting of top-down predictions and bottom-up sensory signals. According to the "REBUS" model (Relaxed Beliefs Under Psychedelics) proposed by Carhart-Harris and Friston in 2019, compounds like psilocybin and [[mdma-reference]] reduce the precision — the confidence or weight — of higher-level predictions, allowing bottom-up sensory signals and lower-level priors to exert greater influence on conscious experience. In essence, the brain's "filtering" or "editing" function is relaxed, allowing a flood of normally suppressed information to reach conscious awareness.

This model elegantly explains many characteristic features of the psychedelic experience. The enhancement of visual perception — the intensified colours, patterns, and textures reported by users — can be understood as a consequence of reduced top-down prediction, allowing raw sensory data to be experienced with less interpretive filtering. The dissolution of the ego, or "ego death," can be understood as the consequence of reduced precision in the default mode network's predictions about the self, allowing the rigid boundary between self and world to become more permeable. The experience of synaesthesia — seeing sounds or hearing colours — can be understood as the consequence of reduced precision in the brain's predictions about which sensory modalities should remain segregated.

The predictive processing framework also provides a neurobiological account of the therapeutic potential of psychedelics. Under normal conditions, maladaptive mental states such as depression and addiction are maintained by rigid, high-precision prior beliefs — deeply ingrained patterns of thought and behaviour that resist change. By temporarily relaxing the precision of these priors, psychedelics create a window of cognitive flexibility in which new, healthier patterns can be established. This is consistent with clinical findings that a single psychedelic session can produce lasting therapeutic change, particularly when combined with [[psychedelic-integration-guide]] practices.

The predictive processing framework thus provides a rigorous, mechanistic account of psychedelic experience that is broadly compatible with Powell's informational model while grounding it in contemporary computational neuroscience. Where Powell speaks of consciousness as "information in process," predictive processing specifies exactly how that processing is organised and how it is disrupted by entheogenic compounds.

## The Entropic Brain Hypothesis in Detail

Robin Carhart-Harris's "entropic brain" hypothesis, first proposed in 2014 and refined in subsequent publications, provides one of the most comprehensive neuroscientific accounts of how psychedelic states relate to normal consciousness from an informational perspective. The hypothesis draws on the concept of entropy from statistical thermodynamics — a measure of the number of available configurations of a system — and applies it to the patterns of functional connectivity in the brain.

Under normal waking conditions, the brain operates in a state of relatively low entropy: its activity is highly organised, with well-defined functional networks processing information in specialised, efficient ways. The default mode network, the salience network, and the executive control network maintain a dynamic balance that supports coherent self-awareness, goal-directed behaviour, and a stable model of reality. This low-entropy state is adaptive for survival — it enables rapid, efficient decision-making — but it comes at a cost: it filters out a vast amount of potentially relevant information.

Under the influence of psilocybin, the brain transitions to a higher-entropy state. Functional connectivity becomes less constrained by the usual network boundaries, and brain regions that normally operate independently begin to communicate more freely. The default mode network, which normally acts as a hub for self-referential processing, loses its integrative dominance, leading to the dissolution of the ego and the emergence of a more fluid, less bounded sense of self. Carhart-Harris and colleagues have used functional MRI to demonstrate these changes quantitatively, showing that psilocybin increases the "signal diversity" of brain activity — a measure closely related to Shannon entropy — across multiple frequency bands.

Crucially, the entropic brain hypothesis proposes that this high-entropy state, while destabilising, is not simply a loss of order. Rather, it represents a transition to a more exploratory mode of brain function in which a wider range of informational configurations becomes accessible. The brain, in this view, temporarily sacrifices the efficiency of its usual low-entropy operating mode in exchange for the creative potential of a more flexible, less constrained informational architecture. This trade-off between efficiency and flexibility mirrors the fundamental tension in information theory between compression (which reduces information for efficiency) and expressiveness (which preserves information for richness).

## Clinical Implications of the Information Paradigm

The informational perspective on psychedelic consciousness has significant implications for understanding the therapeutic mechanisms of compounds like [[psilocybin-mushroom-identification]]. If the therapeutic benefit of psychedelics derives from their capacity to increase the informational richness and flexibility of brain function — to temporarily dissolve rigid, maladaptive patterns of information processing — then the optimal therapeutic approach is one that supports and integrates this increased informational capacity rather than merely managing symptoms.

This understanding supports the growing emphasis on [[psychedelic-integration-guide]] in contemporary therapeutic practice. The acute psychedelic experience is understood not as an end in itself but as a catalyst for informational reorganisation — a window of heightened neural plasticity during which new patterns of thought, emotion, and behaviour can be established. The integration process, which typically involves therapy sessions, journaling, meditation, and lifestyle changes, is the means by which the informational innovations of the psychedelic state are consolidated into enduring changes in the brain's informational architecture.

The informational paradigm also suggests that the therapeutic value of psychedelics may extend beyond the treatment of specific pathologies. By enhancing the brain's capacity for informational processing — by making more information available to consciousness and facilitating novel connections between previously segregated informational streams — psychedelics may support enhanced creativity, problem-solving, and emotional intelligence even in healthy individuals. This "enhancement" paradigm, while more controversial than the clinical therapeutic model, is consistent with Powell's broader argument that entheogenic compounds reveal capacities of consciousness that are ordinarily suppressed.

## Critiques and Limitations of the Information Paradigm

While the informational model of consciousness offers a powerful framework for understanding psychedelic experience, it is not without its limitations and critics. Some philosophers of mind have argued that equating consciousness with information risks a category error — that information, as a mathematical abstraction, cannot account for the qualitative, subjective character of conscious experience (what philosophers call "qualia"). The "hard problem of consciousness," articulated by David Chalmers, asks why and how physical processes should give rise to subjective experience at all. Powell's informational monism, while elegant, does not obviously solve this problem; it simply relocates it from the domain of matter to the domain of information.

Neuroscientists have also pointed out that the concept of "information" in the brain is far more complex than Shannon's original mathematical formalism suggests. Biological information is not merely a matter of signal transmission; it involves complex processes of encoding, storage, retrieval, and interpretation that are mediated by the physical and chemical properties of neurons and synapses. The reduction of consciousness to "information in process" may, if applied too reductively, obscure the specific material substrate — the wet, electrochemical reality of the brain — without which informational patterns cannot exist.

Furthermore, the informational paradigm faces the challenge of defining what counts as "information" in the context of psychedelic experience. If consciousness is information, then the visions, insights, and emotional states produced by psilocybin must be understood as informational patterns. But are all informational patterns equally "real"? The sense of encountering an autonomous "Other," for example, is a powerful and frequently reported feature of the psychedelic experience — but from an informational perspective, it might be understood as a pattern generated by the brain's own informational dynamics rather than a genuine encounter with an external source of information. The tension between these two interpretations — the internal and the external — remains one of the deepest unresolved questions in psychedelic phenomenology.

## Information, Meaning, and the Explanatory Gap

The relationship between information and meaning represents a particularly challenging aspect of the informational paradigm. Shannon's information theory is deliberately silent on the question of meaning: it measures the quantity of information transmitted without reference to what that information signifies. Yet the psychedelic experience is overwhelmingly characterised by the emergence of meaning — the sudden perception of significance in objects, patterns, and experiences that ordinarily seem devoid of it. A simple geometric pattern seen under the influence of psilocybin may be experienced as profoundly meaningful, as revealing deep truths about the nature of reality. How does an informational model account for this qualitative dimension of experience?

Powell's response is to argue that meaning itself is informational — that the semantic content of an object is constituted by its relations to other objects within a vast informational network. On this view, the psychedelic experience does not add meaning to the world but reveals meaning that is already present, hidden by the ordinary filtering mechanisms of consciousness. This is an elegant philosophical move, but it raises further questions. If meaning is relational information, then any change in the perceiver's informational state will necessarily change the meanings they perceive. The "meaning" revealed by psilocybin would then be relative to the altered informational state of the psychedelic brain, rather than an absolute feature of the world.

This tension between the subjective and objective dimensions of informational meaning is not unique to psychedelic experience — it pervades the philosophy of mind and epistemology more generally — but it is thrown into particularly sharp relief by the intensity and conviction with which psychedelic users report their experiences of meaning. The challenge for the informational paradigm is to develop a framework that can accommodate both the genuine phenomenological force of these experiences and the epistemological caution that scientific inquiry demands.
