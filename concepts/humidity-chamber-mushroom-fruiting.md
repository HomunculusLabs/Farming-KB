# Humidity Chamber Design for Mushroom Fruiting

Once [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]] has fully colonized a substrate, it must be exposed to conditions that trigger fruiting — the formation of mushroom fruiting bodies. The primary [[fungal-fruiting-body-formation-environmental-triggers]] are high humidity (85-95% relative humidity), fresh air exchange, indirect light, and a slight drop in temperature. The humidity chamber (also called a [[comparison-laminar-flow-hood-vs-fruiting-chamber]] or grow chamber) is the enclosure that provides these conditions.

## Why Humidity Matters

Mushroom fruiting bodies are composed of approximately 90% water. During fruiting, the mycelium transfers large volumes of water from the substrate to the developing mushrooms. If the ambient humidity is too low, the mushrooms lose water faster than the mycelium can supply it, causing them to dry out, crack, or abort entirely. High humidity reduces transpiration rates and allows the mushrooms to develop properly.

The optimal relative humidity for fruiting varies by species but generally falls within the 85-95% range. [[cultivation-of-oyster-mushrooms]] tolerate lower humidity (80-85%) better than most species, while species like Shiitake may require sustained humidity above 90% for proper fruiting.

## Basic Humidity Chamber: Fish Tank Method

The simplest humidity chamber described by Jarrold uses a standard glass fish tank (approximately 4 feet × 2 feet):

### Humidity Maintenance

A layer of soaked hydroponic clay pellets (hydro pellets / LECA — Lightweight Expanded Clay Aggregate) is placed at the bottom of the tank. The pellets are soaked in water and provide a large surface area for evaporation, maintaining high humidity inside the enclosed space. The tank is then covered with a layer of plastic sheeting or cling film, sealed around the edges to trap moisture and prevent insects from entering.

### Fresh Air Exchange

While high humidity is essential, the developing mushrooms also require fresh air to supply oxygen and remove carbon dioxide produced by mycelial respiration. Without adequate air exchange, CO2 accumulates and mushrooms develop abnormally — long, stringy stems with small or absent caps (a condition called "leggy" growth).

The fish tank method requires manual fresh air exchange: twice daily, the cultivator removes the plastic cover briefly and fans fresh air into the chamber with a piece of cardboard or similar object. This brief exchange is sufficient to replenish oxygen while maintaining overall humidity.

### Monitoring

Check the chamber at least twice daily for:

- Signs of mushroom primordia (tiny pin-like formations, called "pins") emerging from the substrate
- Proper humidity (condensation on the tank walls indicates adequate moisture)
- Signs of contamination (unusual colors or odors)
- Mushrooms ready for harvest (caps opening, veil tearing)

## Self-Humidifying Chamber with Air Pump

A more automated approach uses an aquarium air pump to continuously circulate humid air:

### Construction

1. Start with a sealed container (a plastic storage box or modified fish tank works well)
2. Install a small hole near the top of the tank for the air supply tube
3. Feed aquarium tubing through the hole to a water container at the bottom of the chamber
4. Attach an air stone (airstone/air brick) to the tubing inside the water container
5. Connect the other end to a small aquarium air pump outside the chamber
6. Optionally add angled drip lids made from 2mm plastic sheet on the inside to distribute condensation evenly and prevent water droplets from falling directly onto the mushrooms

### How It Works

The air pump pushes air through the tubing and air stone, creating bubbles in the water. These bubbles burst at the surface, releasing tiny water droplets into the air and significantly increasing the humidity inside the chamber. The continuous air movement also provides some fresh air exchange, reducing the need for manual fanning.

### Materials Available From

- Hardware stores — plastic sheeting, tubing, containers
- Car accessory shops — plastic nuts, bolts, and small connectors for constructing the frame and fittings
- Pet/aquarium stores — air pumps, air stones, tubing, hydro pellets

## Warm Humid Air Delivery Chamber

For growers [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]] or those cultivating during winter, a warm humid air delivery system can be constructed:

### Design

- A sealed container serves as both the humidity reservoir and delivery system
- The container is partially filled with several inches of water
- A water heater with a timer thermostat maintains the water temperature
- One large duct serves as the main warm humid air input
- Multiple smaller ducts around the container serve as distribution outlets
- A fan pushes the warm humid air through the system

### Setup Considerations

- The water heater timer requires calibration — it may take several hours to find the right setting that maintains the desired [[greg-green-temperature-and-humidity-control]] without overheating
- The warm air helps maintain the slight temperature drop (typically 3-5°C below colonization temperature) that many species need as a [[cold-shock-fruiting-trigger-species-specificity-psilocybe-panaeolus]]
- Ensure all connections are sealed to prevent air leaks that would compromise humidity

## Transitioning from Colonization to Fruiting

When the substrate is fully colonized (visible as a solid white mass of mycelium throughout the bag), it is time to introduce [[fruiting-conditions]]:

1. Remove the filter can from the top of the bag
2. Cut the bag down the sides, removing as much plastic as needed to expose the colonized substrate
3. Leave at least 2 cm of the bag bottom intact so the substrate block can stand upright in the humidity chamber
