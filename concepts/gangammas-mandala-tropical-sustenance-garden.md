# Gangamma's Mandala and Tropical Sustenance Garden

## Overview

Gangamma's Mandala is a permaculture-based tropical garden system designed to provide
complete sustenance for a family of five. Named after a permaculture graduate from
Karnataka, India, the system synthesizes principles from the East-West Institute
(Hawaii) and the Samaka gardens of the Philippines. It uses permaculture "least-path"
layout principles to minimize labor while maximizing yields across food, fuel,
medicine, and water management. Though designed for tropical and subtropical climates,
the system is adaptable to temperate regions.

## Interplant Strategies for Tropical Gardens

### Phosphate Fixation and Mycelial Associates

Select plant genera form associations with mycorrhizal fungi that fix and mobilize
phosphorus in tropical soils:

- **Banksia** — Proteaceae family member with proteoid root clusters
- **Casuarina** — Nitrogen-fixing through actinorhizal associations; also aids
  phosphorus cycling via fungal partners
- **Gigasperma** — Fungal associate that enhances soil phosphorus availability
- **Pultenea** — Leguminous shrub supporting mycelial phosphate networks

These plants serve as biological nutrient pumps, accessing otherwise unavailable
phosphorus in highly weathered tropical soils and cycling it through leaf fall and
root exudates to companion plants.

### Sugar and Carbohydrate Cycling

Soil biology in tropical gardens thrives on carbohydrate inputs. Multiple pathways
feed soil microorganisms:

- **Sugar-pumping plants** — Species that exude excess carbohydrates from roots,
  directly feeding soil microbial communities
- **Insect exudates** — Leaf-sucking insects and scale insects exude sugary honeydew
  from stems, which drips to soil and activates bacterial and fungal activity
- **Molasses and cane/sorghum juice** — Dilute applications of molasses or freshly
  pressed cane or sorghum sugar juices activate soil fauna, stimulating decomposition
  and nutrient cycling

### Biological Pest Control

[[integrated-pest-management]] in tropical gardens relies on companion plants with
natural insecticidal or repellent properties:

- **Marigolds (*Tagetes* spp.)** — Root exudates suppress nematodes; above-ground
  foliage repels certain insect pests
- **Neem tree (*Azadirachta indica*)** — Leaves and berries contain azadirachtin,
  a potent natural insecticide; neem planted to overhang ponds controls mosquito
  larvae and water flies
- **Pyrethrum daisy (*Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium*)** — Flowers yield pyrethrins
  for effective soil pest control without harming beneficial organisms

## Savannah Grass Management

Tough savannah grasses present a challenge in tropical garden establishment. They
require either heavy mulching to suppress growth and build soil, or tree canopy
overshading to reduce light and outcompete the grasses. Mulch-tree species should be
established as a priority to create the shaded conditions that favor garden plants
over aggressive grass species.

## Garden Bed Design

### Physical Structure

- **Mounded beds** — Essential for wet season drainage; raised surfaces prevent
  waterlogging during heavy tropical rains
- **Mulch and mulch-tree species** — Continuous mulching builds topsoil over time,
  while dedicated mulch-producing trees supply material sustainably
- **Log edges** — Logs placed along garden bed edges define beds, retain mulch,
  slow runoff, and create habitat for beneficial organisms as they decompose

## Garden Design Principles

A complete tropical sustenance garden must address multiple simultaneous needs:

1. **Full nutrition** — Provide complete dietary requirements for an average family
2. **Water conservation** — Capture, store, and recycle all available water
3. **Cultural appropriateness** — Include species preferred in local food traditions
4. **Climate resilience** — Design for drought, flood, storm, and temperature stress
5. **Protein sources** — Integrate livestock (rabbits, guinea pigs, poultry, small
  pigs) alongside grain and legume crops
6. **Water routing** — Plan water flow through the garden for passive irrigation
7. **Staple crops** — Ensure reliable calorie sources (root crops, grains, legumes)
8. **Fresh produce** — Continuous supply of vegetables and fruits year-round
9. **Support species** — Fuels, medicinals, and flowers for pollinator attraction

## Total Garden Design Elements

The integrated design encompasses five core elements:

- **House structure** — Sized and oriented for family needs and climate
- **Garden zones** — Arranged by frequency of use and plant requirements
- **Hedgerow** — Windbreaks, privacy screens, wildlife corridors, and forage
- **Livestock** — Integrated with garden waste cycling and protein production
- **Path access** — Efficient movement routes minimizing steps between zones

## House Room Design

Tropical house design follows permaculture principles of thermal comfort and local
resource use:

- **Family-scaled rooms** — Adjusted to family size, avoiding wasted space
- **Floor** — Slab or raised pise (rammed earth) construction
- **Insulation** — Edge-insulated for temperature stability
- **Ventilation** — Cross-ventilation design with vertical sashes and shutters for
  passive cooling
- **Outdoor living** — Extended living areas under trellis for shaded outdoor use
- **Materials** — Locally sourced and appropriate to climate

## Garden Function and Waste Integration

The tropical garden functions as a closed-loop system:

- **Water acceptance** — All household water (greywater) and wastes flow into the
  garden system
- **Mulch production** — Garden prunings and crop residues cycle back as mulch
- **Fodder** — Provides mulch and fodder for small livestock (rabbits, guinea pigs,
  poultry, small pig)
- **Accessibility** — Well-designed for easy daily harvesting and maintenance
- **Natural aesthetics** — Designed to appear natural rather than regimented
- **Dry toilet** — Manure directed under trees; hedge plants like *Nicotiana*
  (tobacco) and *Leucaena* serve as soft "toilet paper" alternatives

## Appropriate Technology Accessories

Off-grid and low-energy accessories complement the garden system:

- **Hot water collector** — Solar thermal for household hot water
- **Oil lamps** — Low-energy lighting from locally produced oils
- **Photovoltaic array** — Electricity for essential loads
- **Solar oven** — Cooking without fuelwood
- **Solar food drier** — Preservation of surplus harvest

## Gangamma's Mandala — Step 1: The Central Pit

The first construction step establishes the biological heart of the 100m² garden:

### Site Preparation

1. **Mark the center** of the 100m² garden area
2. **Describe a 2-meter circle** at the center point
3. **Excavate to a dish shape** — 0.6 to 1 meter deep from the hollow (center) to
   the rim (edge), creating a shallow depression

### Layering and Filling

4. **Cover the excavation** with wet paper, cardboard, or banana leaves to suppress
   any remaining vegetation and create a moisture-retaining base
5. **Fill with rough mulch** — Short logs, twigs, hay, rice husks, and sawdust,
   building organic volume that will decompose over time
6. **Add amendments** — Manure, wood ash, lime, and dolomite to balance pH and
   supply calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus

### Stone Ring and Planting

7. **Bank stones** around the outside rim for structural stability and thermal mass
8. **Plant the rim** with:
   - 4–5 papaya trees
   - 4 or more dwarf bananas
   - 8–10 sweet potato plants (trailing over the rim)
   - Yams and taro planted inside the rim (in the mulch-filled depression)
9. **Later additions** — Beans climbing the papaya and banana stalks for vertical
   nitrogen fixation and additional protein harvest

### Functional Platform

10. **Place a grid or wooden platform** over the mulch-filled central pit, creating
    an outside shower and wash-up area. Water draining through the platform directly
    irrigates and feeds the decomposing mulch below, completing a water-nutrient
    cycle with zero waste.

## Key Design Philosophy

Gangamma's Mandala embodies the permaculture principle of "least path" — every
element serves multiple functions, and the garden is laid out so that daily tasks
(harvesting, watering, feeding animals, washing) require minimal movement. The
central pit acts simultaneously as a composting system, irrigation sink, shower
drain, and intensive food production zone, demonstrating how integration of function
creates resilient, low-maintenance tropical food systems.
