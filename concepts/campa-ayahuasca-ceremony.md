# Campa Ayahuasca Ceremony

The Campa (also Asháninka) are an Arawakan-speaking people of the Peruvian Montaña whose kamdrampi (ayahuasca) ceremony represents an intriguing case where shamanistic performance blends into priestly ritual. Their ayahuasca use was documented by Gerald Weiss in [[hallucinogens-and-shamanism-harner-anthology]].

## Preparation

The Campa call their ayahuasca preparation kamalampi, a word meaning "to vomit" that reflects its extremely bitter and sometimes emetic qualities. It is prepared by boiling fragments of ayahuasca vine (also called kamdrampi) which the Campa find growing wild and transplant to the vicinity of their settlements, combined with leaves from an uncultivated tree bearing the Campa name horéva (Psychotria viridis).

The preparation follows a straightforward process: vine segments and leaves are combined in a pot with water and heated. Unlike some other Amazonian groups, the Campa preparation involves no elaborate ritual in the gathering or cooking of the ingredients.

## The Ceremony

At nightfall, participants convene, arranging themselves sitting or lying on mats in the open clearing of the settlement or under a house roof, with women separated from men in traditional Campa fashion. The shaman serves as the center of attention, with the vessel containing the kamdrampi beside him.

Using a small gourd bowl, the shaman drinks first, then gives each participant a drink. This procedure repeats at intervals until the supply is consumed. About half an hour later, the drug begins to take effect, and the shaman begins to sing. He sings one song after another as long as he remains under the influence, and the séance may last until dawn.

## The Shaman's Singing

A distinctive quality characterizes the singing of a Campa shaman under the influence of kamdrampi — an eerie, distant quality of voice. His jaw may quiver; he may cause his clothing to vibrate. What is understood to be happening is that the good spirits have come to visit the group: they come in human form, festively attired; they sing and dance before the assembled mortals, but only the shaman perceives them clearly.

When the shaman sings, he is understood to be repeating what he hears the spirits sing — merely singing along with them. At no time is he possessed by a spirit, since Campa culture does not include a belief in spirit-possession.

The songs mainly extol the excellence and bounty of the good spirits. One song marks the appearance of the hawk Kodkiti in human form, speaking of tobacco brought from River's Beginning. Another marks the appearance of hummingbird spirits, described as "they come running" and "group without blemish."

## Soul Flight

Even while singing, the shaman's soul may go on a flight to some distant place, returning later. Some shamans move from the sight of the rest of the group during the ceremony and pretend to disappear bodily on such a flight, only to return. The soul-flight is an optional concomitant, a personal experience that does not intrude upon the actual performance of the ceremony.

## Ambivalence Between Shamanism and Priesthood

The entire atmosphere of the ceremony is one of decorum without frenzy, even though the shaman is in a drugged trance. The ceremony, following a definite if simple format, presents the appearance of a group of people reverently making contact with the good spirits under the leadership of a religious practitioner.

This embodies a certain ambiguity: the very same acts are simultaneously acts of worship. The shaman, as leader of the group, reverently makes contact with the good spirits and praises them in song. The effect is that of an optical illusion — the same behavior looks like a séance one moment and like worship the next.

In one part of Campa territory, a local variation accentuates the element of worship: men take turns singing so the shaman remains director but is no longer the only virtuoso. Men and women separately and together dance and sing in praise of the good spirits. Here the arrow of communication is unambiguously from mortals to immortals rather than the reverse, and it takes the form of adoration.

## Significance for Shamanic Studies

Weiss proposed that the Campa ceremony reveals a behavioral link between generalized shamans and specialized priests. The total range of variation of shamanistic phenomena may include a special variant whose features are ambivalent in such a way that a slight shift in how participants interpret what they are doing could transform an essentially shamanistic séance into a priestly ritual.

If this is the case, the Campa kamdrampi ceremony may represent the transitional form that permitted the historical emergence of priesthood from shamanism — a question of fundamental importance for understanding the development of religious institutions.

## Historical Context

The Campa (Asháninka) are one of the largest indigenous groups in the Peruvian Amazon, with a population numbering in the tens of thousands. Their ayahuasca tradition likely extends back centuries, sharing roots with the broader Arawakan-speaking peoples of the region. Gerald Weiss's documentation in the mid-twentieth century captured a practice at a moment of transition, as missionary activity and encroaching colonization were beginning to reshape Campa religious life.

The ambivalence between shamanism and priesthood that Weiss identified has important implications for understanding the evolution of religious institutions worldwide. Many scholars of comparative religion have proposed that priesthood emerged from shamanism through a process of ritualization and institutionalization, but few ethnographic cases demonstrate this transition as clearly as the Campa kamdrampi ceremony.

The Campa's use of ayahuasca shares botanical and pharmacological foundations with neighboring groups documented in [[cashinahua-banisteriopsis-use]] and [[yage-cross-cultural-experience]], yet the Campa ceremony's distinctive emphasis on choral praise of spirits marks it as a unique cultural variant with significant theoretical implications.

## Modern Relevance

The Asháninka continue to practice ayahuasca ceremonies today, though the tradition has been impacted by decades of political violence, displacement, and cultural change. The Shining Path conflict of the 1980s and 1990s devastated Asháninka communities, and many traditional practices were disrupted or lost.

Weiss's analysis of the shamanism-priesthood continuum remains relevant to contemporary debates in religious studies and anthropology. The question of how religious authority transitions from charismatic, individual revelation to institutional, priestly function is central to understanding religious change in both indigenous and global contexts.

The Campa ceremony also offers insights relevant to the modern therapeutic use of ayahuasca, as described in [[urban-ayahuasca-iquitos-healing]]. The element of communal praise and worship — as opposed to the more diagnostic, problem-solving orientation of urban mestizo ayahuasca healing — represents an alternative therapeutic model centered on devotional practice rather than clinical intervention.
