# Campa (Asháninka) Ayahuasca Ceremony

The [[campa]] (Asháninka) [[ayahuasca-ceremony]] is a communal nighttime ritual
practiced in the Peruvian Amazon, centered on the consumption of
**kamdrampi**—a psychoactive brew prepared from the ayahuasca vine
(*[[banisteriopsis-caapi]]*) and the leaves of horéva (*[[psychotria-viridis]]*).
The ceremony represents one of the most ethnographically significant
ayahuasca traditions in the Amazon, not only for its detailed ritual
structure and spirit-interaction dynamics, but for the scholarly hypothesis
it has generated concerning the behavioral transition from shamanism to
priesthood. The Campa are an Arawakan-speaking people of the montaña
region of eastern Peru, and their kamdrampi ritual has been extensively
documented as a paradigmatic example of Amazonian shamanic practice.

## Kamdrampi: Preparation and Psychoactive Properties

The term "kamdrampi" translates literally as "to vomit," a name that
reflects the brew's extremely bitter taste and frequently emetic qualities.
The preparation involves boiling sections of the ayahuasca vine—locally
also referred to as kamdrampi—alongside the leaves of horéva (*Psychotria
viridis*), a plant known to contain the potent short-acting tryptamine
N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT). The ayahuasca vine provides [[harmala-alkaloids]]
(harmine and harmaline) that function as monoamine oxidase inhibitors
(MAOIs), rendering the orally active DMT in the *Psychotria* leaves
psychoavailable. This botanical combination is characteristic of ayahuasca
preparations across the western Amazon and constitutes one of the most
well-documented examples of indigenous psychopharmacological knowledge.

The admixture of *Psychotria viridis* is particularly significant. While
ayahuasca vine alone produces a distinctive but comparatively mild
intoxication characterized by visual phenomena and nausea, the addition of
DMT-containing leaves dramatically intensifies the visionary and auditory
hallucinatory dimensions of the experience. For the Campa, this intensified
experience is precisely what enables the shaman to perceive the spirit world
with sufficient clarity to fulfill his ceremonial role. The resulting brew
is consumed in quantity by the shaman and distributed to all participants,
establishing a shared altered state of consciousness that forms the
experiential foundation of the ritual.

## Ceremony Structure

The ceremony takes place at nightfall, convening participants in the
settlement clearing or beneath the shelter of a house roof. Men and women
are seated or reclined on mats in segregated groups, with the shaman
positioned as the focal point of the gathering. Using a small gourd bowl,
the shaman first drinks the kamdrampi and then administers it to each
participant in turn. This distribution procedure is repeated at intervals
until the entire supply of the brew has been consumed.

The communal and participatory nature of the ingestion is significant. All
present consume the brew, not merely the shaman, though the shaman's role
as the central religious specialist remains undisputed. The repeated rounds
of consumption ensure that participants remain within the psychoactive state
throughout the extended ceremony.

Approximately thirty minutes after ingestion, the psychoactive effects begin
to manifest. At this point the shaman commences his singing, continuing one
song after another for as long as the intoxication persists. The séance may
extend until dawn, constituting a marathon of sustained ceremonial
engagement that occupies the full span of the nocturnal period. The duration
of the ceremony thus corresponds to the pharmacological duration of the
ayahuasca experience, with the ritual structure adapting to and being
shaped by the properties of the psychoactive substance itself.

## Shamanic Singing and Vocal Phenomenology

A distinctive and consistently reported feature of the Campa ceremony is the
remarkable vocal quality exhibited by the shaman while under the influence
of kamdrampi. The singing is described as possessing an eerie, distant
timbre that is qualitatively unlike ordinary vocal production. Physical
manifestations may accompany this vocal phenomenon: the shaman's jaw may
quiver visibly, and he may cause his clothing to vibrate through the
intensity of his performance. This vocal transformation is understood by the
Campa as evidence that the good spirits have arrived and are singing through
or alongside the shaman, a perceptual interpretation grounded in the
cultural framework rather than treated as a mere somatic side effect of the
intoxicant.

The transformation of the shaman's voice serves an important social and
ritual function. It provides audible evidence to the assembled participants
that the supernatural dimension of the ceremony is active, even though the
spirits themselves remain invisible to all but the shaman. The singing thus
bridges the gap between the shaman's private visionary experience and the
group's collective participation in the ritual event.

## Spirit Visitation

Central to the Campa kamdrampi ceremony is the visitation of good spirits,
who are understood to appear in human form, festively attired, singing and
dancing before the assembled participants. Crucially, only the shaman
perceives the spirits clearly; the lay participants rely on the shaman's
mediation to confirm and interpret the spirit presence. When the shaman
sings, he is understood to be repeating what he hears the spirits sing—he
is singing along with them, not composing original material.

The Campa framework explicitly denies that the shaman is possessed by the
spirits; Campa culture does not include a belief in spirit-possession. This
distinction is ethnographically important, as it separates the Campa
tradition from those Amazonian and Andean practices in which the shaman
serves as a temporary vessel for a disembodied entity. The shaman remains
himself throughout, functioning as a perceiver and repeater of spirit
activity rather than a vessel. The spirits come as visitors, not as
occupants, and the relationship between shaman and spirit is one of
perception and accompaniment rather than fusion or takeover.

## Song Content

The repertoire of ceremonial songs is primarily devotional in character,
extolling the excellence and bounty of the good spirits. Specific songs mark
the appearance of named spirit beings. One such song celebrates the arrival
of the hawk **Kodkiti** in human form, identifying this spirit as a figure
of particular importance within the Campa spirit pantheon. Kodkiti's
appearance in anthropomorphized form is greeted with special song, and the
hawk spirit's attributes—keen vision, aerial mobility, predatory
power—are presumably transmuted into qualities of supernatural beneficence
within the ceremonial context.

Another song announces the appearance of hummingbird spirits, who also
manifest in anthropomorphized form. The hummingbird, with its iridescent
plumage and rapid, hovering flight, occupies a distinctive place in
Amazonian symbolism, often associated with beauty, vitality, and the
threshold between visible and invisible worlds. The song texts thus function
as both announcements of spirit presence and acts of praise directed toward
the visiting entities.
