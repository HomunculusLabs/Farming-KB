# Rhodopsin to Phytochrome - Parallels Between Human and Plant Photoreception

## Overview

Daniel Chamovitz, in What a Plant Knows, draws a provocative parallel between human vision
and plant photoreception. While plants do not form images, they possess sophisticated
light-detection systems that share fundamental mechanistic principles with the human visual
apparatus. The comparison between rhodopsin and photopsins in human eyes and phytochrome in
plants reveals a deep structural homology in how living organisms convert electromagnetic
radiation into biochemical signals.

## Human Vision: Rods, Cones, and the Retina

Human vision depends on photoreceptor cells in the retina, which contains approximately
125 million rods and 6 million cones in an area the size of a passport photo. This density
is equivalent to a 130-megapixel digital camera, far exceeding common consumer cameras at
8 megapixels and even the highest-resolution outdoor LED displays at approximately 10,000
LEDs per square meter.

Two types of photoreceptors serve different functions:

- **Rods** are more sensitive to light and enable vision in low-light conditions but
  cannot detect color.
- **Cones** respond to different colors of light and come in three types, sensitive to
  red, green, and blue wavelengths respectively.

The critical chemicals enabling light detection are:
- **Rhodopsin** in rods, which absorbs all visible wavelengths
- **Photopsins** in cones, with blue, green, and red variants that absorb specific
  wavelength ranges

Each photoreceptor absorbs light of specific wavelengths. Blue light is absorbed by
rhodopsin and the blue photopsin; red light by rhodopsin and the red photopsin. The pattern
of activation across all receptors is processed by the brain into a coherent visual image.

## Plant Vision: The Tip Sees, the Stem Responds

Charles Darwin and his son Francis demonstrated in 1880 that the "eyes" of a plant are
located at the tip of the seedling, while the bending response occurs in the midsection.
Their classic experiment with canary grass (Phalaris canariensis) showed that:

1. An untreated seedling bends toward lateral light
2. A seedling with its tip removed cannot bend toward light
3. A seedling with a lightproof cap on its tip cannot bend toward light
4. A seedling with a clear glass cap on its tip continues to bend toward light
5. A seedling with a lightproof tube around its midsection still bends toward light

Julius von Sachs, a contemporary of Darwin, had previously established in 1864 that blue
light is the primary color inducing phototropism, while plants are generally insensitive to
other colors for this purpose.

## Structural Parallels Between Human and Plant Photoreception

Chamovitz highlights several fundamental parallels:

1. **Protein-based light absorption**: Both human photoreceptors (rhodopsin, photopsins)
   and plant photoreceptors (phytochrome, cryptochromes) are proteins that absorb
   specific wavelengths of electromagnetic radiation.

2. **Signal transduction**: In both systems, light absorption triggers a biochemical
   cascade that is transmitted from the site of perception to the site of response. In
   humans, the signal travels from retina to visual cortex; in plants, from tip to
   stem.

3. **Wavelength specificity**: Just as human cones distinguish between red, green, and
   blue light, plants use different photoreceptors for different purposes. Blue light
   drives phototropism (bending toward light), while red and far-red light regulate
   photoperiodism (measuring day length for flowering).

4. **Color discrimination**: Plants demonstrably differentiate between colors. They use
   blue light to determine light direction and red light to measure the length of
   continuous darkness. This dual photoreceptor system is functionally analogous to
   the rod/cone system in human vision.

## Defining Vision Beyond Image Formation

Chamovitz employs a thought experiment to challenge anthropocentric definitions of vision.
If a person born totally blind were given first the ability to discriminate between light
and shadow (distinguishing night from day, inside from outside), and then the ability to
discern color, these capacities would unquestionably be considered "vision." By this
standard, plants possess rudimentary but genuine sight: they can detect light direction,
intensity, duration, color, and even the ratio of red to far-red light.

The electromagnetic spectrum relevant to both human and plant vision occupies a narrow
band between 0.0000004 and 0.0000007 meters, with blue light at the shorter wavelengths
and red at the longer. Both humans and plants have evolved proteins precisely tuned to
absorb energy within this range.

## Extended Plant Photoreception Beyond the Visible Spectrum

Plants extend their light detection beyond the visible range in ways humans cannot. Plants
perceive:
- Ultraviolet light (which causes sunburn in humans)
- Infrared radiation (which we experience as heat)
- Far-red light (wavelengths slightly longer than red, barely visible at dusk)
- Extremely low light levels, from candlelight to full sun

This broader spectral sensitivity means that in some respects, plant "vision" encompasses
more of the electromagnetic spectrum than human vision, even if it lacks the image-forming
capacity that depends on high receptor density and neural processing.

## Implications for Understanding Plant Intelligence

The structural parallels between human and plant photoreception undermine the assumption
that plants are passive organisms without sensory capabilities. Both systems represent
evolutionary solutions to the problem of extracting information from electromagnetic
radiation, and both employ protein-based molecular machinery to convert light energy into
biological signals that guide behavior. The difference is one of complexity and
processing, not of kind.
