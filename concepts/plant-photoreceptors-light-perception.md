# Plant Photoreceptors and Light Perception

## Overview

Plants possess sophisticated light-sensing systems that allow them to perceive
light color, direction, intensity, and duration with a breadth exceeding human
visual capabilities. Plants detect ultraviolet light that causes sunburn, infrared
light that produces warmth, and discriminate between red and far-red wavelengths
using multiple distinct [[plant-photoreceptor-evolution-from-photolyases]] classes. While fundamentally different
from animal vision, these systems constitute a legitimate form of plant
perception.

## The Electromagnetic Spectrum

Light is electromagnetic radiation within a narrow band of the spectrum:

- **Radio waves**: Very long (~0.5 mile for AM)
- **Infrared**: Longer than visible, perceived as heat
- **Visible light**: 400-700 nm (0.0000004 to 0.0000007 m)
- **Ultraviolet**: Shorter than visible, causes sunburn
- **X-rays**: Extremely short, one trillion times shorter than radio waves

Within visible light, blue has the shortest wavelength (~400 nm) and red the
longest (~700 nm), with green, yellow, and orange in between. This wavelength
ordering explains the consistent color pattern of rainbows.

## Human Vision as Comparison

The human retina contains ~125 million rods and 6 million cones in an area the
size of a passport photo — equivalent to a 130-megapixel camera. Rods detect
all visible wavelengths and enable night vision in shades of gray, containing
the protein rhodopsin. Cones come in three varieties (red, green, blue)
containing photopsins, enabling color vision. The brain processes signals
from all photoreceptors into a coherent image.

## Can Plants "See"?

Merriam-Webster defines sight as light stimuli received by the eye and
interpreted by the brain. While plants lack eyes and brains, Chamovitz argues
plant light perception constitutes a form of vision: they detect light across a
wider spectral range than humans, discriminate between colors, detect direction,
intensity, and duration, and respond with coordinated physiological changes. A
person gaining the ability to discriminate light from shadow, then color, would
unambiguously be described as gaining sight. By this standard, plants possess
a form of vision.

## Classes of Plant Photoreceptors

### Phytochromes (Red/Far-Red Sensors)

[[phytochrome-photoperiodism]] detects red (660 nm) and far-red (730 nm) light. It exists in
two interconvertible forms: Pr (inactive, absorbs red) and Pfr (active, absorbs
far-red). Phytochrome regulates [[photoperiodism-in-plants]], [[cannabis-seed-germination-methods]], shade
avoidance, and de-etiolation. Pfr slowly reverts to Pr in darkness, providing
the molecular basis for night-length measurement. Plants possess multiple
phytochrome genes (phyA through phyE in Arabidopsis), each with different roles.

### Cryptochromes (Blue/UV-A Sensors)

[[cryptochrome-and-blue-light-photoreception]] absorbs [[chamovitz-julius-von-sachs-blue-light-phototropism]] (320-500 nm) and UV-A. It regulates
circadian clock entrainment, flowering time, photomorphogenesis, stomatal
opening, and stem elongation inhibition. Structurally related to DNA photolyase
enzymes, cryptochromes use flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) as chromophore and
are found across all kingdoms of life, including humans where they regulate
circadian rhythms.

### Phototropins (Directional Blue Light Sensors)

Phototropin mediates [[phototropism]] — directional growth toward light. It
regulates phototropic bending, chloroplast relocation for optimal light capture,
stomatal opening, and leaf positioning. Phototropins contain two light-sensing
domains (LOV1 and LOV2) that undergo conformational changes upon blue light
absorption, activating a kinase domain initiating the directional growth signal
cascade.

### UVR8 (UV-B Sensor)

UVR8 detects UV-B radiation (280-315 nm), triggering production of
UV-absorbing flavonoids, DNA repair enzyme activation, and protective gene
expression. It is unique among photoreceptors in using tryptophan residues as
its chromophore rather than a separate cofactor molecule.

## What Plants Perceive

The combination of photoreceptor systems gives plants comprehensive light
perception:

- **Direction**: Detected by phototropins, enabling shoots to grow toward light
- **Intensity**: Monitored across multiple receptor types, regulating growth rate
  and shade avoidance
- **Spectral quality**: Distinguished through combined phytochrome, cryptochrome,
  and phototropin responses
- **Duration**: Measured via phytochrome-mediated [[photoperiodism-in-plants]] for
  flowering regulation
- **UV radiation**: Detected by cryptochromes and UVR8, triggering protection
- **Infrared/far-red**: Sensed by phytochrome, indicating shade and time of day

## Evolution

Plant photoreceptors predate animal eyes evolutionarily. The shared use of
flavin-based chromophores in plant and animal cryptochromes suggests a common
ancestral light-sensing protein that diverged across lineages — convergent
evolution toward light detection.

The evolution of multiple photoreceptor classes allowed plants to extract
maximum information from their light environment. A single photoreceptor type
can detect presence or absence of light, but multiple receptors operating at
different wavelengths allow plants to construct a detailed picture of their
surroundings: not just "is it light or dark?" but "what color is the light?",
"where is it coming from?", "how long has it been dark?", and "are other plants
shading me?"

## Photoreceptor Signaling Mechanisms

All plant photoreceptors function through similar general principles: light
absorption by a chromophore causes a conformational change in the receptor
protein, which then initiates a signaling cascade ultimately leading to changes
in gene expression. However, the specific mechanisms differ between classes:

**Phytochrome signaling**: Pfr translocates to the nucleus where it interacts
with transcription factors called PIFs (phytochrome-interacting factors). In
the dark, PIFs promote growth; light-activated phytochrome binds and
inactivates PIFs, shifting the plant from dark to light-adapted growth.

**Cryptochrome signaling**: Blue light causes conformational changes allowing
cryptochrome to interact with COP1 (constitutive photomorphogenic 1). In
darkness, COP1 targets photomorphogenesis transcription factors for degradation.
Light-activated cryptochrome inhibits COP1, allowing these factors to
accumulate and promote light-adapted development.

**Phototropin signaling**: Blue light absorption by the LOV domains activates
the kinase domain, which phosphorylates downstream targets including auxin
transporters. This asymmetric auxin redistribution causes differential cell
elongation on shaded versus illuminated sides of the stem, producing the
bending response characteristic of [[phototropism]].
