# Harry Roberts and the Green Gulch Zen Center Tribute

Harry Roberts (1906-1986) was a Native American elder of the Yurok tribe and a
central figure in one of the most emotionally charged passages in Masanobu
Fukuoka's *Sowing Seeds in the Desert*. His relationship with Fukuoka
exemplifies the cross-cultural spiritual bond that natural farming can create
between people who share a deep reverence for the living earth.

## Background and Role at Green Gulch Farm

Roberts served as a guide and farming helper at the [[fukuoka-green-gulch-zen-center-desert-reclamation]]
outside San Francisco. The farm is surrounded by bare, savanna-like hills,
though a nearby national park contains a redwood forest, suggesting the area
was once heavily forested. Roberts helped the Zen Center students with their
farming operations and served as a bridge between Native American ecological
wisdom and the Asian-influenced Zen agricultural practices at the center.

Born in 1906, Roberts spent his childhood summers with the Yurok people on the
Klamath River in Northern California. He was trained for twenty years to be the
"high man" of his tribe, the spiritual leader entrusted with carrying on the
lineage and traditions of his people. His life was remarkably diverse: he
worked as a blacksmith, cook, cowboy, horticulturist, naturalist, and spiritual
adviser. In the 1950s, he planted the first Metasequoia (dawn redwood) trees
in California, specimens that can now be found in San Francisco's Golden Gate
Park, the UC Berkeley Botanical Garden, and parks throughout Northern
California.

## The Muir Woods Encounter

During Fukuoka's first visit to the United States in 1979, Roberts guided him
through the redwoods at Muir Woods. They walked among trees 200 to 250 feet
tall, observing the sword ferns and huckleberries growing over a large area.
Fukuoka noted that the ecosystem of mixed trees and understory plants closely
resembled the virgin forests of Japan. The two men concurred that this
observation provided a vital clue for [[fukuoka-mountain-soil-restoration]].

Fukuoka explained to Roberts that redwood trees grow quickly to large size but
have shallow roots, making them prone to falling unless supported by the root
systems of neighboring trees. This vulnerability had worsened as topsoil eroded
throughout the redwoods' natural range. In contrast, the Yaku Island and Yanase
Cryptomeria (Japanese cedars), which are related to redwoods, send down deep
roots capable of penetrating the subsoil. Fukuoka promised to send Roberts
seeds of those deep-rooted varieties.

The exchange of gifts and respect was mutual. Fukuoka called Roberts "the
guardian deity of the American forests" and "a giant both in body and spirit."
Roberts replied: "You are small in stature, but to me you are a giant of the
Orient." After returning to Japan, Fukuoka sent a handful of Cryptomeria
seeds, and Roberts reciprocated with a cup carved from redwood wood.

## The Deathbed Seed Planting

When Fukuoka returned to Green Gulch seven years later during his 1986 visit,
Roberts had passed away. The Zen Center students greeted Fukuoka by showing him
a photograph of Roberts on his deathbed. The great elder had raised himself
into a sitting position and was planting seeds in a seedling flat, even as he
lay dying. His final words to his disciples were: "These seeds are Fukuoka's
spirit. Sow them carefully, and when the seedlings have developed, plant them
in those three valleys over there." Shortly after giving these instructions, he
died.

The students then took Fukuoka to see where the seedlings had been planted.
Many Cryptomeria saplings were already growing, some six feet tall, each
protected by iron stakes and wire fencing against deer and wild animals. The
effort required by these young people to protect and nurture the trees was
immense. Across the valley, Roberts's grave was marked by crudely piled stones
covering about twenty square feet, a resting place Fukuoka found reminiscent
of grave sites he had seen in the desert of [[fukuoka-somalia-desert-greening-mission]].

## Fukuoka's Emotional Response

The encounter with Roberts's memory produced one of the most powerful emotional
moments in all of Fukuoka's writings. He described standing rooted to the spot,
heedless of tears pouring down his cheeks, thinking that Roberts might be the
only person he would ever meet who truly understood him, who would "live with
me and die with me." Fukuoka noted it was only the second time he had cried in
fifty years. The first was during his 1979 visit, when speaking at a summer
camp at French Meadows in the Sierra Nevada, recalling the spring of his
twenty-fifth year and his spiritual awakening. Though the circumstances were
different, Fukuoka felt they were "the same tears."

He reflected that Roberts was no longer present in body or spirit in any world,
and it was precisely because he knew Roberts's spirit was not drifting in some
other world that he was able to cry. He sensed the tears flowed from a place
that transcended life and death, and that he had been "bathed in tears of
ecstasy."

## Significance for Natural Farming Philosophy

The Roberts narrative illustrates several core themes of Fukuoka's
[[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]]:

- The spiritual dimension of [[fukuoka-spiritual-philosophy-farming]] that
  transcends cultural boundaries
- The power of seed-saving and tree-planting as acts of faith connecting
  generations
- The [[fukuoka-natural-farming-spiritual-light]]
  ecological worldviews
- The concept that [[fukuoka-touching-the-great-spirit]] those who
  serve the land without expectation of return

Roberts's final act of planting seeds on his deathbed stands as a powerful
symbol of the natural farmer's devotion: the work of [[fukuoka-desert-revegetation-natural-farming]] continues
beyond individual life, carried forward by seeds, soil, and community.
