## Keyline Design Water Management

## Overview

Keyline design is a land management system developed by Australian farmer P.A. Yeomans in the 1950s. It uses the natural topography of a landscape to optimize water distribution, soil development, and fertility across broad acreage. Unlike conventional contour plowing, keyline cultivation spreads water from wet valleys to dry ridges, creating uniform soil moisture and reducing erosion.

## Core Concepts

### Keypoint Identification

The **keypoint** is the point in a valley (primary watercourse) where the slope transitions from convex (steepening) to concave (flattening). At this point, water naturally slows and begins to spread laterally. This is the most important reference point in keyline design because it marks where water can be most effectively captured and redirected across the landscape.

To find the keypoint, walk up a valley from the bottom. The point where the ground stops getting steeper and starts flattening is the keypoint. This typically sits roughly 60-70% of the way up from the valley floor to the ridge crest, though it varies by terrain.

## Benefits

- Dramatically improved water infiltration and retention in soil
- Reduced erosion by slowing and spreading surface water
- Deep soil aeration without plow pan formation
- More uniform soil moisture across valleys and ridges
- Gravity-fed irrigation without pumping infrastructure
- Drought resilience through increased soil water-holding capacity
- Improved pasture and crop growth on previously dry ridges

## Limitations

- Requires sufficient slope to identify a clear keypoint
- Less applicable on flat terrain or very steep, dissected landscapes
- Initial earthworks investment can be significant
- Keyline plows require suitable tractors and operator skill
- Full benefits develop over 3-5 years as soil structure improves
