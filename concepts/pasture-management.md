# Pasture Management

Pasture management is the foundation of any grass-based livestock system.
Properly managed pastures produce more forage per acre, build soil health,
sequester carbon, reduce feed costs, and break parasite life cycles. The
principles of [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]] and adaptive multi-paddock management can
transform marginal land into productive, resilient pasture.

## Rotational Grazing

### The Principle
Rotational grazing divides pasture into smaller paddocks and moves animals
through them on a schedule, allowing each paddock a rest period for regrowth.
This mimics natural grazing patterns of wild herbivores and stimulates both
plant and soil health.

### Benefits
- Increased forage production (30-200% compared to continuous grazing).
- Improved forage quality — plants are grazed at optimal stage.
- More uniform manure distribution ([[natural-fertilizer-sources-and-application]]).
- Reduced parasite load — larvae die during rest periods before animals return.
- Deeper root systems — plants grow deeper when grazed and rested properly.
- Improved water infiltration — root channels [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] increase
  soil porosity.
- Carbon sequestration — grazing stimulates root exudates that feed soil
  microbes, building [[soil-organic-carbon-fractions-turnover-dynamics]].

### Implementation
- Start simple: divide existing pasture into 4-8 paddocks with portable
  fencing. See [[livestock-fencing-guide]].
- Move animals when the pasture is grazed to the target residual height.
- Rest each paddock until plants have fully recovered.
- Adjust stock density (animals per acre) based on forage availability and
  season.

### Grazing Management Rules
- **Graze half, leave half:** Remove no more than 50% of above-ground plant
  material per grazing event. This maintains root reserves for rapid regrowth.
- **Recovery period:** Grass needs 15-60 days to recover depending on
  species, temperature, and moisture. Faster in spring, slower in summer.
- **Stock density:** Higher stock density for shorter duration is better than
  lower density for longer. This ensures uniform grazing and reduces selective
  grazing.
- **Avoid re-grazing:** Do not return animals to a paddock until regrowth
  reaches 8-12 inches (for cool-season grasses). Re-grazing too soon depletes
  root reserves and weakens plants.

## Paddock Design

### Layout Principles
- Paddock shape: rectangular is most efficient. Avoid narrow, irregular shapes.
- Orient long axis perpendicular to prevailing slope for water infiltration.
- Place water sources so every paddock has access, or design lanes to a
  central water point.
- Shade is critical in summer — design paddocks to include trees or
  portable shade structures.
- Lane width: 12-16 feet minimum for cattle, 8-10 feet for sheep/goats.

### Water Infrastructure
- Provide water in every paddock when possible (reduces energy waste from
  walking to central water).
- Options: buried pipe with frost-proof hydrants, above-ground poly pipe
  with quick couplers, or portable water tanks.
- Flow rate: 5-10 gallons per minute for cattle paddocks, 2-5 for sheep/goats.
- Plan for 1-2 gallons per 100 lbs of animal body weight per day in cool
  weather, 2-3 gallons in hot weather.

### Shade
- Cattle and sheep need shade when temperatures exceed 80°F.
- Natural shade (trees) is ideal. Plant shade trees in paddock interiors.
- Portable shade structures: canvas tarps on metal frames, cattle shades,
  or simple A-frame structures.
- Allow 20-30 sq ft of shade per head of cattle, 5-10 sq ft per sheep.

### Lane Systems
- Permanent lanes connect paddocks to handling facilities and water.
- Fence lanes with permanent fencing (woven wire or high-tensile). See
  [[livestock-fencing-guide]].
- Width: 12-20 feet for cattle, 8-12 feet for sheep/goats.
- Gate placement: allow movement in all directions without backtracking.

## Forage Species

### Cool-Season Grasses (Grow in Spring/Fall)
- **Orchardgrass:** Persistent, palatable, good for grazing. Tolerates shade.
- **Kentucky Bluegrass:** Dense sod, persistent, good for horse pastures.
  Lower productivity than other grasses.
- **Perennial Ryegrass:** Highly palatable, fast establishment, but
  short-lived in many climates.
- **Tall Fescue:** Very hardy, deep-rooted, drought-tolerant. Endophyte-
  infected types cause fescue toxicosis in livestock — use novel endophyte
  varieties.
- **Timothy:** High quality, good for hay. Less persistent under grazing.
- **Smooth Bromegrass:** Aggressive, sod-forming. Good [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]].

### Warm-Season Grasses (Grow in Summer)
- **Bermudagrass:** Highly productive in hot climates. Requires good
  drainage.
- **Switchgrass:** Tall, deep-rooted, good for summer grazing and wildlife.
- **Eastern Gamagrass:** Tall, high quality, good for summer.
- **Big Bluestem:** Native prairie grass, deep-rooted, drought-tolerant.
- **Indiangrass:** Native, high quality, good summer production.

### Legumes (Nitrogen Fixation + High Protein)
- **White Clover:** Low-growing, persistent, high protein. Excellent in
  grazing mixes. Fixes 50-100 lbs N/acre/year.
- **Red Clover:** Biennial, taller, good quality. Fixes 70-150 lbs N/acre.
- **Alfalfa:** Deep-rooted, very high protein and productivity. Requires
  well-drained soil and careful grazing management (bloat risk). See
  [[hay-and-forage-management]].
- **Birdsfoot Trefoil:** Non-bloating legume, tolerant of wet and acid soils.
- **Crown Vetch:** Erosion control, not ideal for grazing (low palatability).

### Forb Species
- **Chicory:** Deep taproot, drought-tolerant, high protein, anthelmintic
  properties (reduces internal parasites in sheep and goats).
- **Plantain (broadleaf):** Palatable, drought-tolerant, high minerals.

### Mix Design
- A good pasture mix contains 2-3 grasses and 1-2 legumes.
- Example cool-season mix: orchardgrass (8 lbs/ac) + perennial ryegrass
  (4 lbs/ac) + white clover (2 lbs/ac).
- Include chicory and plantain for summer productivity and parasite control.

## Rest Periods

### Determining Rest Duration
- Depends on: plant growth rate, temperature, moisture, species, and grazing
  severity.
- General guidelines for cool-season pastures:
  - Spring (rapid growth): 14-21 days
  - Early summer: 21-30 days
  - Mid-summer (drought): 30-60+ days
  - Fall: 30-45 days
- Rest until plants have 3-4 fully expanded leaves before re-grazing.

### Stockpiling
- Allow pasture to grow ungrazed in late summer/fall for winter grazing.
- Tall fescue and orchardgrass stockpile well. Strip-graze with temporary
  fencing to minimize waste. Can reduce winter hay feeding 30-60 days.
