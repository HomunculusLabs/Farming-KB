# Phytoremediation Combined with Mycoremediation

## Overview

Phytoremediation uses plants to remove, degrade, or contain environmental contaminants. When combined with mycoremediation through mycorrhizal fungal associations, the synergistic partnership can significantly enhance remediation outcomes. Mycorrhizal fungi — symbiotic associations between soil fungi and plant roots — are ubiquitous in natural environments and play critical roles in nutrient transport and plant stress protection. The rhizosphere zone around plant roots is the active seat of metabolic substrates responsible for fungal growth and survival in soil ecosystems.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

Seven types of mycorrhizal associations have been classified: arbuscular (VAM), ectomycorrhiza (ECM), ectendomycorrhiza, ericoid (ERM), arbutoid, orchid, and monotrophic. The vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhiza (VAM) is the most ancient type, colonizing about two-thirds of plant species and scavenging for phosphate. Over 150 VAM taxa belong to the Order Glomales. The second most common type is the ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi, with more than 6000 species from 148 genera known worldwide. ECM fungi are more specialized in nutrient capture and are considered extremophiles due to their occurrence in habitats with extreme temperature, pH, salt, and metal concentrations.

## Mycorrhizal Functions in Remediation

The fungal mycelium associated with host plants enhances the volume of rhizosphere soil. In temperate forest ecosystems, mycorrhizal mycelium accounts for up to 45% of total soil biomass. Mycelial lengths of up to 2000 m/m root have been recorded, with surface areas up to 47-fold greater than uncolonized roots. Ectomycorrhizal fungi mobilize essential plant nutrients from rock by excreting organic acids, affecting nutrient cycling in forests. These extensive mycelial networks increase the efficiency of soil exploration and capture of nutrients and contaminants.

## Metal Tolerance and Uptake

ECM, ERM, and VAM fungi can increase plant tolerance to heavy metals at toxic concentrations by accumulating metals in extramatrical hyphae and extrahyphal slime. This immobilizes metals near roots and decreases translocation to shoots. Key species include [[mycoremediation-heavy-metals-detailed]], which increases resistance of Pinus massoniana to aluminum toxicity, and Suillus bovinus, which protects Pinus sylvestris from heavy metals. VAM fungi decrease concentrations of Cd, Mn, and Zn in leaves of host plants. Inoculation of grasses with Glomus mosseae or G. intraradices removes 26-72% of 137Cs and 24-89% of 90Sr from contaminated soil.

## Mechanisms of Metal Response

Metal response mechanisms fall into two categories: avoidance and sequestration. Avoidance reduces metal concentration through precipitation, [[mycoremediation-biosorption-mechanisms]], uptake, or efflux. Sequestration involves intracellular chelation through polyphosphate granules, metallothioneins, phytochelatins, and organic acids in vacuoles. X-ray microanalysis has located high Zn concentrations in extramatrical hyphae cell walls and extrahyphal polysaccharide slime. The fungal sheath can prevent metals from reaching the root surface, with its permeability playing an important role in determining fungus-mediated host metal tolerance.

## Petroleum Hydrocarbon Degradation

ECM fungi can survive in petroleum-contaminated soils and utilize crude oil as a nutrient. VAM fungi (Glomus aggregatum, G. mosseae) occur in both petroleum-polluted and nonpolluted soils, though colonization is reduced under pollution stress. ECM fungi can establish degradative consortia in the root zone, forming parenchymatous patches on contaminated soil that support hydrocarbon-degrading bacterial biofilms. When inoculated with Pseudomonas fluorescens containing a toluene-degrading plasmid, these rhizosphere associations degrade meta-toluate.

## Lignin and Phenolic Degradation

A limited number of ECM and ERM fungi degrade lignin and soluble phenolics. In pure cultures, five ECM species metabolize 14C-labeled plant lignin at rates lower than white-rot fungi. ERM fungi are more effective than ECM fungi at lignin degradation, with Hymenoscyphus ericae showing well-developed tannin-degrading abilities. The facultative ECM fungus Paxillus involutus degrades lignin more readily than obligate ECM species such as Suillus bovinus.

## PAH and Explosive Degradation

ECM fungi degrade [[mycoremediation-of-petroleum-hydrocarbons]] and [[mycoremediation-explosive-contaminants]] through extracellular enzymatic processes. ERM and ECM fungi produce phenol-oxidizing enzymes including tyrosinase, catechol oxidase, and laccase. Recent data support identification of lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase genes in a broad taxonomic range of ECM fungi, though these enzymes may be induced only under certain conditions. The ECM fungus Pisolithus tinctorius mineralized 8.4% of [14C]anthracene, while Suillus variegatus mineralized 6.3%.

## Glomalin as a Biostabilizer

Glomalin, an insoluble glycoprotein produced by VAM fungi, can sequester potentially toxic elements including Cu, Cd, Pb, and Mn. Glomalin extracted from polluted soils contained these metals, and glomalin from Gigaspora rosea hyphae sequestered up to 28 mg Cu/g in vitro. Glomalin can act as a biostabilizer for remediation of polluted soils, providing a long-term immobilization mechanism.

## Degradative Enzyme Production

ECM fungi produce a variety of enzymes for degradation of complex organic compounds in soil. Hydrolytic enzymes (proteinases, peptidases, amylase, gelatinase, lipase, urease) mobilize nitrogen from organic matter. Mat-forming ectomycorrhizal fungi show elevated levels of cellulase, phosphatase, peroxidase, and proteinase compared to nonmat types. VAM fungi produce xylanases, mannases, and other glycanase complexes that may aid partial degradation of hazardous compounds. Cellulolytic enzyme activities have also been detected in ECM fungi.

Both ECM and ERM fungi produce phenol-oxidizing enzymes: tyrosinase, catechol oxidase, ascorbate oxidase, and laccase. Exolaccase production by Thelephora terrestris catalyzes oxidation of phenols to phenoxy radicals, which can transform PAHs. Remarkable extracellular oxidase activities have been detected in species of Lactarius and Russula. Suillus granulatus produces intracellular tyrosinase, laccase, and peroxidase activities. These enzyme releases contribute to humification and detoxification processes in soil.

Several studies indicate partial mineralization of lignin by both ERM and ECM fungi, suggestive of peroxidative enzyme production. Chambers et al. (1999) demonstrated MnP activity in Tylospora fibrillosa with a gene equivalent to a known isozyme from Phanerochaete chrysosporium. Genes of lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase have been identified in a broad taxonomic range of ECM fungi, though expression appears induced only under certain conditions.

## Radionuclide Transport

Fungi play an important role in uptake, mobilization, and translocation of radionuclides in forest ecosystems and are responsible for long-term retention of radiocesium in organic soil horizons. Transfer factors quantify radionuclide availability from soil to fungal fruiting bodies. About 22% of 137Cs bound by fungal mycelia is estimated in the top 5 cm of coniferous forest soil. Horizontal transport of radiocesium occurs in Scandinavian forests through local enrichment by fungal clusters. Plants inoculated with specific mycorrhizal fungi show increased ability to remove 137Cs and 90Sr from contaminated soils, with grasses inoculated with Glomus mosseae or G. intraradices removing 26-72% of 137Cs and 24-89% of 90Sr.

## Molecular Biology and Genetics

The molecular mechanisms of mycorrhizal symbiosis establishment are not fully understood. Root cell elicitors regulate fungal gene expression for symbiosis, including genes responsible for Hartig net and hyphal mantle development. Stable genetic transformation and expression of gene markers have been reported in transgenic Pisolithus tinctorius. A symbiosis-regulated gene (PF6.2) has been cloned from Laccaria bicolor, expressed within 6 hours of interaction with Pinus resinosa. Metallothionein-encoding genes have been identified in Gigaspora margarita and Tuber borchii. However, no studies have been conducted on the genetics of ECM fungal adaptation to contaminated soils in the field.

## Challenges and Future Directions

Key challenges include limited knowledge of ECM and VAM contribution to petroleum hydrocarbon degradation, tolerance limits, and the role of nutrients. No correlation exists between in vitro tolerance of ECM fungi and their expression of increased metal tolerance in host plants. Combining in vitro, in vivo, and field studies is important for evaluating metal tolerance. Additional studies are needed on mycorrhiza hyperaccumulators as protection mechanisms against heavy metals. The protective effect of mycorrhiza against plant Zn uptake correlates with changes in Zn solubility due to soil solution pH changes or immobilization in extraradical mycelium.

## Comparison of Mycorrhizal and White-Rot Fungi

Mycorrhizal and [[mycoremediation-white-rot-fungi-lignin-degradation-enzymes]] differ fundamentally in their bioremediation approaches. White-rot fungi are free-living saprophytes that produce potent extracellular ligninolytic enzymes capable of degrading a wide range of recalcitrant pollutants independently. Mycorrhizal fungi are obligate or facultative symbionts dependent on host plants for carbon, limiting their independent application but enabling rhizosphere-mediated remediation. White-rot fungi generally show higher degradation rates for PAHs, dyes, and explosives, while mycorrhizal fungi excel at metal immobilization, nutrient cycling, and supporting plant survival in contaminated environments. The two groups can complement each other: mycorrhizal fungi establish plant cover and immobilize metals while white-rot fungi or their enzymes degrade organic contaminants.

## Multi-Process Phytoremediation Systems

Multi-process phytoremediation systems combining volatilization, photooxidation, microbial remediation, and phytoremediation have proven approximately twice as effective as phytoremediation alone for removal of 16 priority PAHs. These systems leverage the rhizosphere effect, where plant exudates stimulate microbial activity and fungal colonization. The rhizospheres of nine plant species showed enhanced PAH removal compared to unvegetated controls. Pilot-scale constructed wetlands treating PAH-contaminated water identified 40 fungal species from 24 genera, with 33 species showing efficient fluoranthene degradation.

## Methods for Studying Mycorrhizal Fungi

Multiple methods exist for studying mycorrhizal fungi in bioremediation contexts. Isolation of spores from field soil employs sieving and decanting, flotation-adhesion, airstream fractionation, and centrifugation techniques. Quantification of VAM colonization uses microscopy with gridline intersect methods. Fatty acid profiles (phospholipid fatty acid, PLFA) can estimate infective VAM propagule biomass, with 16:1omega5c as a marker fatty acid. Molecular methods including PCR-RFLP, competitive PCR, and real-time PCR enable species identification and biomass quantification. The detection threshold of competitive PCR for Hebeloma cylindrosporum is 0.03 pg of genomic DNA. Taxon-specific oligonucleotide probes have been used to identify fungal components of ectomycorrhizas.
