## The Structural Comparison

McKenna explicitly noted the structural parallel:

- **Serotonin**: 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT)
- **Psilocybin**: 4-phosphoraloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine
- **DMT**: N,N-dimethyltryptamine

All three compounds share the indole ethylamine backbone characteristic of tryptamines. Serotonin has a hydroxyl group at the 5-position of the indole ring, while psilocybin has a phosphoryloxy group at the 4-position and two methyl groups on the terminal amine. DMT is the simplest of the three, consisting of the tryptamine backbone with only the N,N-dimethyl substitution.

McKenna"s observation that serotonin is "the major neurotransmitter in the human brain, found in all life and most concentrated in humans" frames the structural similarity as evidence for a deep evolutionary relationship between these compounds and human neurochemistry. The implication is that tryptamine hallucinogens are not foreign substances imposing artificial effects on the brain, but rather variants of a molecular theme that is fundamental to neural function.

## The Pharmacokinetic Argument: Onset Speed as Evidence

McKenna used the pharmacokinetics of DMT to support his endogenous familiarity argument. He noted that DMT"s onset is extremely rapid — approximately 15 seconds when smoked — and the experience lasts only about 5 minutes. This pharmacokinetic profile contrasts sharply with LSD, which McKenna noted "is retained in the body for some time."

The rapid onset and short duration, McKenna argued, indicate that "the brain is absolutely at home with this compound." His reasoning was that a molecule the brain had never encountered would likely require more complex metabolic processing, resulting in slower onset and longer duration. The fact that DMT acts within seconds suggests that the brain has pre-existing machinery — receptors, metabolic pathways, signal transduction cascades — optimized for handling this molecular structure.

### Modern Pharmacological Context

From a modern pharmacological perspective, McKenna"s observation about rapid onset is consistent with DMT's high affinity for the 5-HT2A receptor and its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier efficiently due to its relatively simple structure and lack of polar groups. The short duration reflects rapid metabolism by monoamine oxidase (MAO), which is why DMT is not orally active without an MAO inhibitor (as in ayahuasca).

However, McKenna's interpretation that rapid onset implies endogenous familiarity is more speculative. Rapid onset primarily reflects pharmacokinetic properties (lipophilicity, receptor affinity, metabolic stability) rather than prior evolutionary exposure. Many exogenous drugs with no endogenous counterparts also show rapid onset (e.g., nicotine, certain anesthetics). Nevertheless, the argument that the brain's serotonergic system is the natural target of tryptamine hallucinogens is pharmacologically sound.

## The Contrast with LSD

McKenna drew a sharp distinction between the tryptamine family (DMT, psilocybin, ayahuasca) and other hallucinogens, particularly LSD. He stated that "psilocybin and DMT invoke the Logos, although DMT is more intense and more brief in its action," and that "they work directly on the language centers, so that an important aspect of the experience is the interior dialogue."

This emphasis on language centers and the Logos was central to McKenna"s argument that tryptamines are categorically different from other hallucinogens. LSD, despite being a partial tryptamine analog (it contains an indole ring fused to a bicyclic structure), does not produce the same translinguistic phenomena according to McKenna's phenomenological reports. He noted that while he had observed similarities between LSD experiences and Mahayana Buddhist iconography, "what amazed me was the total absence of the motifs of DMT. It is not there; it is not there in any tradition familiar to me." This absence from both personal experience and historical artistic traditions suggested to McKenna that the DMT experience represents something genuinely novel and previously inaccessible to human consciousness.
