# Grass Seed Substrate Cultivation of Psilocybe Mushrooms and Sclerotia

Grass seed is an effective substrate for cultivating several species of Psilocybe and related genera, supporting both mushroom fruitbody production and sclerotia formation. This method, documented in the Exotic Mushroom Cultivation Guide, provides a straightforward approach using widely available materials and standard equipment.

## Suitable Species

Grass seed substrate is suitable for cultivating:

- [[Psilocybe mexicana]] — mushrooms and sclerotia
- [[Psilocybe tampanensis]] — mushrooms and sclerotia
- [[Psilocybe cubensis]] — mushrooms only (rye grain is preferred for this species)
- [[Panaeolus subbalteatus]] — mushrooms

## Substrate Preparation

### Ingredients

The standard formula for 720 ml jars:

| Ingredient | Quantity |
|-----------|----------|
| Grass seed (Lolium perenne or mixed species) | 110 grams |
| Water | 180 ml |

### Seed Selection

The most commonly sold form is rye grass seed (Lolium perenne), but mixtures of different grass species also work well. The critical requirement is that the seed must **not be treated with fungicides**. Treated seed should be labeled as such on the packaging. Inexpensive seed can often be sourced from animal feed stores, where it is sold as bird feed.

### Moisture Calibration

Different varieties of grass seed and even batches of the same variety can differ greatly in their ability to absorb water. This is one of the most important variables in the entire process:

- **Too much water**: Results in a slimy clump of seed that cannot be shaken
- **Too little water**: Produces substrate that is too dry, yielding little or no mushrooms or sclerotia

An alternative approach is to **soak the grass seed overnight** in water before filling jars. This produces a more homogenous substrate by allowing even water absorption.

### Jar Preparation

After water and seed are filled into jars, the lids are placed on but **not screwed tight** — they must remain loose. A double layer of aluminium foil is crumpled over the lid and top part of the jar. This prevents moisture from entering during sterilisation while allowing pressure equalization.

## Sterilisation

Jars must be sterilised in a **pressure cooker or autoclave** — a normal pot will not suffice. Sterilise for **one hour** following the pressure cooker's directions. Larger jars (1.5 litre) and spawn bags require **two full hours**.

### Post-Sterilisation Handling

1. After the cooker is no longer under pressure, remove jars
2. **Shake jars** to mix wet and dry kernels (always check for cracks first)
3. Allow jars to cool in a clean place with lids still loose to prevent vacuum formation
4. Inoculations can proceed once jars reach room temperature

## Inoculation

The critical principle is to **work clean**. Multiple inoculation methods are acceptable:

- Spore syringes
- Mycelium syringes
- Agar squares
- Other inoculant types

### Aseptic Technique

- Always flame the needle before using syringes
- Always flame the scalpel when using agar squares
- **Warning**: Alcohol and spray Lysol are highly flammable — never flame near these
- Even a simple hood made from a cardboard box helps prevent drafts and contamination
- Do not expose sterilised grain to air longer than absolutely necessary
- Open jar lids just a crack and work swiftly

After inoculation, close the lids, shake the jars to distribute the inoculant, then loosen the lids again so the mycelium can breathe.

## Colonisation

Place inoculated jars in a clean, draft-free location at **room temperature (20°C) or slightly higher**. When mycelium starts growing in only a few spots, shake the jars to redistribute colonised kernels — this dramatically speeds colonisation. Depending on temperature, species, and inoculation method, complete colonisation takes **5 to 20 days**.

### Metabolite Warning

If jars are incubated too long or at too high a temperature, the mycelium will excrete **yellowish metabolites**. This is a warning sign — the jars should be cased as soon as possible when this occurs.

## Casing (For Mushroom Production)

When the grass seed is fully colonised, it must be cased to initiate fruiting.

### Casing Procedure

1. Shake colonised grass seed loose and pour into a 1-litre disposable plastic tray
2. Break up any lumps with the clean rim of the jar
3. Level the grain surface evenly
4. Cover with a thin layer (1.5-2.0 cm) of casing soil
5. Keep the casing surface even but rough (small valleys and hills)
6. Cover with tin foil and store at 20-25°C
7. When mycelium grows through the casing, expose to air and light to start fruiting

### Casing Soil Recipe

| Ingredient | Parts |
|-----------|-------|
| Peat | 10 |
| Vermiculite | 5 |
| Limestone (Marl) | 2 |

Mix ingredients in dry form, add water while stirring. The goal is maximum moisture without creating mud. Sterilise the casing soil for one hour in the pressure cooker before use. The authors recommend sterilisation over pasteurisation based on their experience — untreated or pasteurised casing soils gave consistently poor results.

## Fruiting

Simple clear plastic bins covered with polyethylene sheeting are sufficient. Five cased trays per bin, with evaporation from the casing surface providing adequate humidity. Holes melted in the bin sides provide air exchange (cover with mesh to exclude flies).

### Cold Shocking

Harder-to-fruit strains benefit from **cold shocking** — placing them in the fridge for one night before putting in fruiting bins. Easy fruiting strains (e.g., Ecuador cubensis) do not require this. [[Psilocybe mexicana]], [[Psilocybe tampanensis]], and [[Panaeolus subbalteatus]] do not benefit from cold shocking.

### Flush Management

- First pinheads appear 6-15 days after placing casings in bins
- Mushrooms mature in 5-7 days
- Expect 3 flushes normally, but up to 5-6 with proper watering
- [[Psilocybe mexicana]] typically produces one big flush and a small second flush
- Increase watering directly after picking a flush
- Remove dead pinheads (aborts) promptly to prevent mould

## Sclerotia Production

[[Psilocybe tampanensis]] and [[Psilocybe mexicana]] produce **sclerotia** while still colonising in the jar — no casing is needed for sclerotia production. Simply leave fully colonised jars in a clean, preferably dark location. In the experience of the authors, sclerotia of P. tampanensis continue to enlarge until **4 months after inoculation**. Sclerotia of these species also form in the casing layer of cased trays.
