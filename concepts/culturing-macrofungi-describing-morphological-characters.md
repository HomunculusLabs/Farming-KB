# Culturing Macrofungi and Describing Morphological Characters

Successful isolation and culture of macrofungi, combined with careful morphological documentation, are fundamental practices in fungal taxonomy, biodiversity studies, and phytopathology. This page covers standard techniques for obtaining pure cultures from spores, tissue, and substratum, as well as protocols for describing macromorphological and micromorphological characters.

## Culturing from Basidiospores and Ascospores

The most common approach to culturing macrofungi is germinating spores directly on an agar surface.

### Spore Print Method

The [[spore-print]] technique exploits active spore discharge to deposit spores onto agar in a controlled pattern. Suspend the ascoma or hymenial surface near the edge of the Petri plate lid, attaching with petroleum jelly, small agar chips, or sterile water drops. Tilt the plate at a steep angle with the hymenium at the upper end—this produces a **cascading spore print** with high spore density at the upper end, decreasing toward the lower end, enabling both polyspore and single-spore isolates from the same print. Remove the source specimen as soon as spores are detected.

**Alternative single-spore isolation:** Rotate a horizontal Petri dish lid periodically (30 sec to 10 min intervals) for distantly spaced isolates. Identify individual spores under a dissecting microscope with an adjustable mirror (spores appear bright against a dark background), then transfer well-separated germinated spores to individual plates.

### Timing and Environmental Considerations

- **8–12 hours** is usually sufficient for spore print observation, though less time may be needed for dark-spored species. Prints are viewed by turning the plate upside down and focusing through the agar.
- **Tropical fungi:** The hymenium or ascomata must be removed within **12–24 hours** to avoid contamination by fast-growing molds such as *Fusarium* and *[[trichoderma]]*.
- **Temperate and boreal fungi:** Spore discharge may require **several days to a week**.

### Barometric Pressure and Temperature

Barometric pressure changes from elevation shifts of several hundred meters can cause spore drop failure (Dennis 1970); a field spore-printing setup may be needed if the lab is at a very different elevation. **Tropical [[basidiomycetes]]** refrigerated >1 hour fail to drop spores and cultures die at 5–10°C, though this cold effect is **not** observed in temperate basidiomycetes or tropical ascomycetes.

### Spore Viability

Spore longevity varies dramatically among taxa:

- ***Armillaria*:** Air-dried spores viable for less than a few days.
- ***Marasmius*:** Viable for only ~2 hours (D. Desjardin).
- **Tropical rain forest fungi (many):** Lose viability after 1–2 hours; best to drop directly onto agar.
- **Morels (*Morchella*):** Spore prints on aluminum foil kept frozen for >1 year without viability loss (T. Volk).
- Field-collected spore prints are commonly made on sterile **60 mm Petri plates** and later transferred to culture tubes or agar slants.

### Streaking Method

An alternative to the print method is to transfer a drop of spore suspension in sterile water onto agar and streak it using a small piece of agar or a flamed inoculating loop/rod. This distributes spores across the surface for subsequent isolation.

## Ascospore-Specific Methods

Obtaining cultures from ascomycetes requires attention to the condition of the ascomata and the mechanics of ascospore discharge.

### Ascospore Discharge and Contamination Avoidance

Use **mature, moist, and turgid ascomata** (fresh or rehydrated); air-dried Xylariaceae should be soaked in water for **30 min to 2 hours** before discharge. To reduce hyphomycete contamination, invert the Petri dish and place ascomata on the lid below the agar, allowing **upward spore discharge**. Perithecia may need elevation with clay or a small lid. Ensure **ostioles are not clogged**; pick off spore deposits with a sterile needle and streak on agar with a water drop.
- If ascomata fail to discharge entirely, extract ascus contents using a sterile scalpel, flamed needle, dental tool, or insect pin, and streak the material on agar.
- Single spores are obtainable, but **germination within the ascus is common**.

### Tissue Culture Caution

Culturing from the ascomal wall or internal stromatal tissue of small ascomycetes is **not recommended**, as contaminants are frequently isolated instead of the target fungus. The only notable exception is *Hypoxylon*, which can sometimes be cultured from *Xylaria* stromatal tissue—though there remains a significant risk of isolating a contaminant rather than the target species.

## Isolating Tissue from Basidiomes

Tissue isolation is a reliable method for obtaining pure cultures, particularly for basidiomycetes.

### Standard Technique

1. Split the basidiome down the middle.
2. Remove a small tissue piece from the **upper stipe** or **pileus** using a sterile scalpel and forceps.
3. **Tearing** (rather than cutting) the tissue reduces both surface contamination and tissue damage.

### Tough and Reflexed Species

For reflexed or ungulate species with tough basidiomes, cut partway through the basidiome and tear tissue from the extraction area. Place the tissue on **test tube slants** rather than plates.

### Resupinate Basidiomycetes

Thin resupinate basidiomycetes can be isolated using the **"hyphal bridge" technique**: place the specimen on its substratum in a moist chamber; aerial hyphae or mycelial strands that bridge air gaps toward paper toweling or the chamber lid are then transferred to agar.

### Rhizomorphs and Cordons

Fasten rhizomorphs or cordons (vegetative structures) to the lid of an agar plate. Clean hyphae typically grow from broken ends within **1–2 days**; transfer hyphal tips to fresh agar.

### Difficult Species

For particularly difficult species, transfer larger hyphal masses on agar chips with the hyphal side facing up. **Invert the plate** to reduce contamination from airborne spores during incubation.

## Direct Isolation from Substratum

For fungi that inhabit wood but fruit infrequently, direct isolation from the substratum is essential.

### Sampling

- Place wood chips or cores directly onto agar media.
- **Record the sample position relative to disease symptoms**, as many pathogens can only be isolated from the advancing disease front.

### Increment Boring

A flame-sterilized increment borer is used to extract wood cores:

1. Clean the surface with **70% ethanol**, **10% sodium hypochlorite**, or a flame-sterilized knife.
2. Place cores in a sterile container (plastic drinking straws work well).
3. Friable wood samples should be wrapped in aluminum foil.
4. For internal distribution studies, core from sections at recorded positions along the sample.

### Surface Sterilization

- **Chips and cores:** Dip in 95% ethanol and flame, or wash under a strong stream of distilled water.
- **Large wood or root surfaces:** Submerge in **0.5% sodium hypochlorite** for 10–15 minutes.

### Identification of Wood-Inhabiting Cultures

Cultures of polypores and other wood-inhabiting fungi are identified using the standard keys of **Nobles (1965)** and **Stalpers (1978)**.

## Describing Macromorphological and Micromorphological Characters

Accurate morphological description is critical for identification, especially in the Agaricales where macromorphological characters (color, shape, size, odor) are the most diagnostic features.

### Fresh Documentation

- For fleshy species, many characters are **lost on drying**; documentation must be completed **before drying**.
- Nonputrescent wood-inhabiting fungi tend to retain features when preserved.

### Systematic Description Protocol

1. Assign workers to **specific taxonomic groups** to ensure consistency in descriptions.
2. Begin with the **pileus**, then proceed downward through the **lamellae or tubes**, **stipe**, and **veils**.
3. Record **context data** including color, color changes upon exposure, consistency, and presence of latex.
4. Include **all available developmental stages** (buttons, immature, mature, senescent).

### Lighting and Color Accuracy

Good lighting is essential for accurate color perception:

- Natural incandescent and standard fluorescent lighting **distort colors**, particularly in the red-to-purple spectrum.
- **Vita-Lite fluorescent tubes** or **Ott-Light True Color Lamps** provide near-natural illumination and are recommended.
- Always use **color standards** (e.g., Munsell or Ridgway color charts) alongside specimens for reference.

### Standard Equipment

- Pencil or pen with **permanent ink**
- Pocket knife with a **sharp, thin blade**
- **Single-edge razor blades** for sectioning
- **Hand lens** (10–20×) or dissecting microscope
- **Color standards** for consistent color documentation

**Preprinted or computerized forms** ensure that all diagnostic features are systematically recorded, reducing the risk of omitting important characters and facilitating comparison across specimens and taxa.
