# [[eu-hallucinogenic-mushroom-legislation-timeline]] [[psychedelic-plants-legal-status]] in Europe

## International Framework

[[psilocin]] and [[psilocybin]] are controlled under the 1971 UN Convention on
Psychotropic Substances and listed in Schedule I under the name
psilocybine and psilocine (or psilotsin) respectively. All EU Member
States control them accordingly. However, although many plant-based drugs
have been self-administered for thousands of years, the imposition of
criminal sanctions is mostly a product of the twentieth century.

## The Legal Challenge of Fungi

Legal controls do not always apply to the same mushrooms. Denmark
controls "Psilocybe semilanceata, Psilocybe cubensis or other fungi/
spores containing psilocin or psilocybin," whereas Italy lists "Fungi of
the genus Strofaria, Conocybe and Psilocybe." Other countries simply
list "Fungi containing psilocin or an ester of psilocin" (e.g. the UK).

### How to control wild fungi?

Some countries have had legal difficulties not wanting to unjustly
criminalise people who may own land where mushrooms grow wild. Solutions
included:

- **"Treated or prepared" standard**: Irish and UK legislation
  initially controlled mushrooms only if "treated or prepared,"
  indicating intent of use
- **Dutch Supreme Court ruling**: mushrooms were under control when
  "dried or processed" but not fresh
- **UK packaging argument**: In 2004, the UK argued that even packaging
  was a form of "preparation"

### Is it a plant?

The Italian, Greek, Cypriot, and Lithuanian laws have a catch-all term
prohibiting cultivation of plants from which narcotic substances can be
extracted. Since a mushroom is a fungus and not strictly a "plant,"
Germany amended its law in 2005, changing the definition from "plant" to
"organism" to clarify that mushrooms are included.

## Timeline of Legislative Changes (2001-2006)

Six EU countries tightened legislation since 2001, coinciding with
concerns about increasing prevalence:

### Denmark (July 2001)
Added "Mushrooms or spores of Psilocybe semilanceata, Psilocybe
cubensis or other fungi/spores containing psilocin or psilocybin,
including grown, dried or processed in any other manner" as a
controlled substance.

### Netherlands (November 2002)
The Opium Act specifically prohibits psilocin, psilocybin, and
"preparations" containing these substances. The Supreme Court clarified
that the Act applies to mushrooms that have been "prepared" by being
dried, mashed, or processed in food, but not to fresh mushrooms.

### Germany (March 2005)
Changed the definition of controlled substance from "plant" to
"organism" to clarify that mushrooms are included.

### Estonia (May 2005)
Clarified that cultivation of hallucinogenic mushrooms is an offence.

### United Kingdom (July 2005)
Added "Fungus (of any kind) which contains psilocin or an ester of
psilocin" as a controlled substance, no longer controlled only "if
treated or prepared." This was a Class A drug classification.

### Ireland (February 2006)
Criminalised possession or sale of "fungus of any kind or description,
which contains psilocin or an ester of psilocin," exempt if growing
uncultivated. No longer controlled only "if treated or prepared." The
change was initiated when mushroom sales were increasingly commonplace
and after a young man died after consuming psychoactive mushrooms.

## Impact of Legislation

New legislation appears to have had an immediate impact on availability.
The UK Mixmag survey conducted in 2005, shortly after stricter controls,
found 67% of respondents reporting mushrooms were less available than
previously. Internet sites in the UK stopped selling mushrooms, and
UK-based retailers switched to legal alternatives such as *Amanita
muscaria*.

Reports in the UK suggest that legislation had an impact on the
availability of mushrooms and the overall volume of internet sales.
Since reclassification as a Class A drug, seizures by law enforcement
have been recorded, including one case involving mushrooms worth GBP
6,000 (EUR 8,700) seized in Glasgow in 2005.

## Legal Loopholes and Confusion

The fact that hallucinogenic mushrooms containing an internationally
controlled substance grow in the wild has created confusion regarding
legal controls. In countries where legal controls are exercised, they
are not always applied to the same mushrooms. This confusion has
provided loopholes for mushroom retailers and presented obstacles to
controlling supply.

The Czech Republic reported an example where a Prague shop sold
hallucinogenic mushrooms under the label of "aromatic and decorative
goods" with a warning leaflet telling customers not to ingest them,
thus evading national legislation.

## Drug Law Offences

Very few data are available on drug law offences:
- Czech Republic: Police National Drug Squad reported 9 offences
  related to hallucinogenic mushrooms in 2003 and 2004
- Greece: 37 offences (2003) and 20 offences (2004) related to
  hallucinogenic mushrooms
