# Psilocybin Mushroom Potency Species Comparison and Dosage Guidelines

## Overview

The EMCDDA 2006 thematic paper compiled potency data and dosage information for hallucinogenic mushrooms, drawing on clinical research (particularly Hasler et al., 2004) and user community resources (Erowid, Shroomery). This data reveals significant variation in psychoactive compound concentration across species, making standardized dosing inherently challenging and highlighting the importance of species identification in both clinical and recreational contexts.

## Potency Variation by Species

### High-Potency Species

The EMCDDA identified the following species as having the highest psilocybin/psilocin concentrations:

- **Psilocybe semilanceata** (liberty cap) — Considered the most potent commonly available species, with reported psilocybin content up to **1%** by dry weight. This species is the most widely distributed psychoactive mushroom in Europe and the primary target of wild foraging activities
- **Psilocybe azurescens** — Reported to have even higher potency than *P. semilanceata* in some analyses, though it is not naturally distributed in Europe (native to the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] [[huckleberries-and-wild-berries-of-north-america]])
- **Psilocybe bohemica** — A European species reported to have high potency, found in Czech Republic and surrounding regions

### Moderate-Potency Species

- **Psilocybe cubensis** — The most commonly cultivated and commercially sold species worldwide, with [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] content up to **0.6%** by dry weight. This species dominates the smartshop market due to ease of cultivation rather than potency. The EMCDDA notes that the research of Jochen Gartz has shown *P. cubensis* can achieve a baseline psilocybin content of 1% under optimal conditions (brown rice substrate, young harvest), potentially matching *P. semilanceata* potency
- **Panaeolus cyanescens** (Copelandia) — Marketed as "Hawaiian" in smartshops, with potency generally considered higher than typical *P. cubensis*

### Potency Variables

The EMCDDA identified four primary [[factors-affecting-mycoremediation-efficiency]] mushroom potency:

1. **Species or variety** — The genetic determinant of maximum potential potency
2. **Origin/geography** — Environmental conditions of the growing location affect alkaloid production
3. **Growing conditions** — Substrate, temperature, humidity, and light exposure during cultivation
4. **Age/maturity** — The single most controllable factor affecting actual potency of harvested specimens

## Age-Related Potency Decline

### Scientific Basis

The EMCDDA report, drawing on Paul Stamets' research, confirmed that **small immature specimens are significantly more potent than larger mature specimens**. This is a critical finding that has practical implications for both harm reduction (avoiding excessive doses from small specimens) and quality optimization (harvesting at peak potency).

### The Abortion Phenomenon

Over half of the small primordia that initially form on a colonized substrate will **abort** — cease growing, becoming convoluted and deformed. PF (Psilocybe Fanaticus) reported that these abortive mushrooms are "primo in magic alkaloids," being more potent than the mature specimens that develop normally. This observation aligns with the general principle of age-related potency decline: the earlier in the development cycle a specimen is harvested, the higher its alkaloid concentration per unit weight.

### Maturation and Potency Loss

As a mushroom matures through its fruiting cycle, several changes occur that reduce potency:

1. **Cap expansion** — The cap spreads from conical to flat/upturned ("like an inverted umbrella"), increasing physical size while alkaloid content per gram decreases
2. **Veil rupture** — When the partial veil under the cap breaks, sporulation begins and the mushroom enters its reproductive rather than psychoactive-optimized phase
3. **Sporulation** — Energy is diverted from alkaloid production to spore production
4. **Color darkening** — Caps and stems develop dark bluish colors, indicating oxidation of psilocybin
5. **Spore deposition** — Purple spore deposits on stems indicate full reproductive maturity

## Dosage Guidelines

### Psilocybin Dose Range

The EMCDDA cited Hasler et al. (2004) for the psychoactive dose range of psilocybin:

- **Effective dose**: **45 µg/kg to 315 µg/kg** body weight of psilocybin
- For a 70 kg adult, this translates to approximately **3.15 mg to 22.05 mg** of pure psilocybin

### Mushroom Weight Equivalents

The report provided the following dosage ranges in terms of dried mushroom weight:

- **Standard recreational dose**: **1 to 3.5–5 grams** of dried mushrooms
- **Fresh mushroom equivalent**: approximately **10 times higher** (10–50 grams), reflecting the approximately 90% water content of fresh mushrooms

### Species-Specific Dosing (Stamets Scale)

The EMCDDA included Paul Stamets' dosage guidelines using *P. semilanceata* as the baseline species:

| Dose (dried) | Effects |
|---|---|
| 1–2 grams | Moderate effects, equivalent to 10–20 mg psilocybin. Lasts 4–5 hours. Recommended for first-time users |
| 2 grams | First colorful geometric patterns, noticeable changes in auditory perception |
| 3 grams | Visual "waves" — air appears liquid with tidal movements distorting scenery |
| 4 grams | More intense experience lasting 4–5 hours |
| 5 grams | Nearly overwhelming, spanning 6 hours. Not recommended above 7 grams even for experienced users |

### Individual Sensitivity Variation

Stamets reported two notable cases of atypical sensitivity:

- A mycologist who experienced effects at only **1–2 grams** equivalent to 3–4 times the normal dose (hypersensitivity)
- A woman who felt **no effects whatsoever** at doses exceeding **5 grams** (hyposensitivity)

These cases demonstrate the wide inter-individual variation in response, which complicates universal dosing recommendations.

## Routes of Administration

### Oral Ingestion (Primary Route)

The standard route is oral ingestion. Mushrooms are consumed:
- **Raw** — Chewed and held in the mouth (potentially faster onset due to sublingual absorption)
- **Dried** — Eaten directly or chopped into smaller pieces
- **Brewed as tea** — Stewed into tea, which may reduce nausea but potentially alters onset and duration

The EMCDDA notes that mushrooms generally do not taste good, and users recommend chopping them into smaller pieces to facilitate consumption. Eating them mixed or cooked into food may cause nausea and vomiting.

### Smoking (Unverified Route)

User reports on the internet suggest that dried mushrooms are also smoked, said to induce faster but milder effects. The EMCDDA noted that it is not clear whether smoking psilocybin/psilocin-containing mushrooms can produce psychoactive effects, as the alkaloids may degrade at combustion temperatures.

### Intravenous Injection (Dangerous Route)

A report on the Shroomery website referred to literature reports about mushroom extract being **intravenously injected**, which results in more severe acute effects. This route is extremely dangerous and carries risks of infection, embolism, and unpredictable dosing.

## Onset and Duration

- **Onset**: 10 to 60 minutes, depending on ingestion method, physical condition, food intake, and user experience
- **Duration**: Usually 2 to 6 hours, described as a "trip"
- **After-effects**: Additional 2–6 hours, including difficulties sleeping

Short-term tolerance develops rapidly to both physical and psychological effects, meaning that dosage escalation may occur within a single session if users redose, though with diminishing returns.

## Physiological Effects Profile

The EMCDDA documented the following physiological effects:

**Common**: Dizziness, nausea, weakness, muscle aching, shivering, abdominal pain, pupil dilation (mydriasis), mild-to-moderate increased heart rate (tachycardia) and breathing (tachypnea), elevated blood pressure. Body temperature generally remains normal.

**Less common but reported**: Severe stomach pain, persistent vomiting, diarrhea. Over 25% of recent users (Mixmag 2005) experienced nausea or vomiting.

**Cardiovascular risk**: The tendency for temporarily increased blood pressure may be a risk factor for users with cardiovascular conditions, especially untreated hypertension (Hasler et al., 2004).
