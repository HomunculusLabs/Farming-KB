In this chapter from "The Road Back to Nature," Fukuoka argues that children possess a natural capacity for farming that adults have lost through accumulated scientific knowledge. He advocates for thinking as little as possible and allowing spontaneous wisdom to develop.

This chapter connects directly to [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy]] by demonstrating that the principles of natural farming are not learned but remembered. Children, unburdened by the false certainties of agricultural science, naturally understand what adults must struggle to relearn.

## Spontaneous Knowledge

Fukuoka prefers to think as little as possible. When challenged that "you can't practice natural farming if you don't know clover from sorrel," he responds:

- This is spontaneous knowledge that existed before scientific knowledge

- One does not need to learn knowledge from science; it is enough to learn (imitate) nature

- All plants grow and exist for a reason — teaching children petty microcosmic scientific knowledge causes them to lose sight of the true reason for each plant's existence

The concept of spontaneous knowledge parallels the Zen Buddhist idea of "beginner's mind" (shoshin) — a state of openness and lack of preconception that allows direct perception of reality. Fukuoka believed that this state was not merely a spiritual ideal but a practical necessity for anyone who wishes to farm in harmony with nature.

## The Problem with Botanical Knowledge

- Knowing that clover is useful as green manure while sorrel is not, or that there are many varieties of clover, is the same type of thinking as believing one must know the many faces of God to attain Buddhahood

- Children do not need to be told "this here is wood sorrel, it looks like clover but it's not"

- Teach a child that clover is green manure and pearlwort is medicinal, and the child loses sight of the plant's true reason for existence

- If children are allowed to play freely in a world that transcends science, they will develop natural methods of farming by themselves

Fukuoka's comparison to Buddhist theology is telling. Just as reducing God to a catalogue of attributes misses the essence of the divine, reducing a plant to its utility for human purposes misses the essence of the living being. Each plant exists for its own sake within the web of life — a principle central to [[fukuoka-what-is-nature]].

This critique of reductionist knowledge connects to Fukuoka's broader argument in [[fukuoka-learning-about-nature-distances-one-from-nature]]: the more we study nature analytically, the further we move from actually understanding it.

## Natural Farming Is Always Incomplete

- Even Fukuoka's own method is "only the very beginning"

- The goal is a "macro" form of natural farming where nothing does not serve its purpose

- Wood sorrel may sometimes get in the way on a small farm, so replace it with clover — but in a grander system, anything at all would be allowed

This remarkable humility distinguishes Fukuoka from nearly every other agricultural reformer. Where others present perfected systems, Fukuoka presented an open-ended process. His natural farming was not a final destination but a direction of movement — toward ever-greater integration with nature's own patterns.

This openness is reflected in his refusal to establish schools or methods, a theme explored in [[fukuoka-naming-problem-of-natural-farming]]. Any codified system would already be a departure from the spontaneity that natural farming requires.

## Children on the Farm

Fukuoka describes groups of kindergarteners visiting his farm, running about and playing freely. He observes that young people grow and search for food in the midst of nature as they find it, while adults cannot practice natural farming without distinguishing between clover and weeds. Weak, spiritless youths thrown into the hills to fend for themselves manage quite well — they know how to live.

The contrast between children and adults mirrors Fukuoka's broader contrast between natural and scientific farming. Adults, trained in agricultural science, cannot see a field without categorizing every plant as "crop" or "weed." Children simply see a living landscape in which they participate naturally. This is the vision that underlies [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-philosophy]].

Fukuoka's observation about youths surviving in the hills is not casual — it reflects his deep belief that human beings possess an innate capacity to live in harmony with nature. Civilization has buried this capacity under layers of intellectual knowledge, but it can be recovered through direct experience of the natural world.

## Science Only Necessary While Farming Is Immature

Fukuoka's book "The Natural Way of Farming" was an attempt to explain in macro scientific terms that one can practice natural farming without micro farming knowledge. Scientific explanations serve only while natural farming remains confined within the realm of scientific agriculture.

This paradox — writing a book to argue that books are unnecessary — is one Fukuoka embraced fully. He understood that most people would need some intellectual bridge before they could take the leap into direct, non-conceptual engagement with nature. His writings serve as a raft to cross a river, to be abandoned once the crossing is complete.

## The Educational Implications

Fukuoka's views on children and farming have profound implications for education more broadly. If scientific knowledge distances us from nature, then the standard model of agricultural education — teaching botany, soil chemistry, entomology — may be precisely the wrong approach. Instead, Fukuoka suggests that children should be given direct experience of growing food in natural conditions, allowing understanding to emerge from practice rather than preceding it.

This philosophy aligns with aspects of [[natural-farming-food-philosophy]]: just as natural food is eaten in its whole, unprocessed state, natural farming knowledge is absorbed through whole, unanalyzed experience.

## Children vs. Scientific Experts

Fukuoka often noted the irony that agricultural scientists, with all their years of training, could not achieve what untrained children could accomplish intuitively on his farm. The scientist approaches the field with a checklist of variables to measure and control. The child approaches the field with curiosity and openness. The child's approach, Fukuoka argued, is closer to the truth.

This observation connects to [[fukuoka-what-is-nature]]: the child who has not yet learned to classify and categorize the natural world is actually closer to perceiving nature as it truly is. Every label applied — "weed," "crop," "pest," "beneficial insect" — is a simplification that obscures the actual complexity of the living system.

## Merged: Purposelessness of Life and the Innocence of Children

# Purposelessness of Life and the Innocence of Children

In the philosophical dialogues of [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-impact]], Fukuoka engages young visitors in discussions about the meaning of existence, the burden of purpose-seeking, and the natural wisdom of children. These passages represent some of his most direct and accessible philosophical statements.

## The Youth's Existential Crisis

A young visitor asks: "Why was I born into this world? To go to nursery school?" This question leads Fukuoka to reflect on how modern society channels human energy into increasingly abstract pursuits of meaning.

"Right up through college people study diligently to learn why they were born. Scholars and philosophers, even if they ruin their lives in the attempt, say, they will be satisfied to understand this one thing."

## No Purpose, No Problem

Fukuoka's answer is startling in its simplicity: "Originally, human beings had no purpose. Now, dreaming up some purpose or other, they struggle away trying to find the meaning of life. It is a one-man wrestling match."

"There is no purpose one has to think about, or go out in search of. You would do well to ask the children whether or not a life without purpose is meaningless."

This is not nihilism but a liberation. The search for purpose is itself the source of suffering. Children live fully without needing a reason.

## The Beginning of Sorrows

"From the time they enter nursery school, people's sorrows begin. The human being was a happy creature, but he created a hard world and now struggles trying to break out of it."

This mirrors Fukuoka's broader pattern: human knowledge and deliberate action create problems that then require further knowledge and action to solve, in an endless spiral. See [[fukuoka-errors-human-intellect]].

## Life, Death, and Nature's Joy

"In nature, there is life and death, and nature is joyful. In human society, there is life and death, and people live in sorrow."

The difference is not in the facts of existence but in human consciousness. Nature experiences the cycle of life and death as a harmonious whole; humans, trapped in the intellect, experience it as suffering.

## The Four-Year-Old's Question

"The other morning I heard a four-year-old girl ask her mother, 'Why was I born into this world? To go to nursery school?' Naturally, her mother could not honestly say, 'Yes, that's right, so off you go.' And yet, you could say that people these days are born to go to nursery school."

The child's question exposes the absurdity of the modern trajectory: birth, education, work, death -- a sequence that has lost its connection to any deeper meaning.
