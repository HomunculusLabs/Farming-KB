In this chapter from "The Road Back to Nature," Fukuoka argues that children possess a natural capacity for farming that adults have lost through accumulated scientific knowledge. He advocates for thinking as little as possible and allowing spontaneous wisdom to develop.

This chapter connects directly to [[fukuoka-what-is-nature]].

This critique of reductionist knowledge connects to Fukuoka's broader argument in [[fukuoka-naming-problem-of-natural-farming]]. Any codified system would already be a departure from the spontaneity that natural farming requires.

## Children on the Farm

Fukuoka describes groups of kindergarteners visiting his farm, running about and playing freely. He observes that young people grow and search for food in the midst of nature as they find it, while adults cannot practice natural farming without distinguishing between clover and weeds. Weak, spiritless youths thrown into the hills to fend for themselves manage quite well — they know how to live.

The contrast between children and adults mirrors Fukuoka's broader contrast between natural and scientific farming. Adults, trained in agricultural science, cannot see a field without categorizing every plant as "crop" or "weed." Children simply see a living landscape in which they participate naturally. This is the vision that underlies [[natural-farming-food-philosophy]]: just as natural food is eaten in its whole, unprocessed state, natural farming knowledge is absorbed through whole, unanalyzed experience.

## Children vs. Scientific Experts

Fukuoka often noted the irony that agricultural scientists, with all their years of training, could not achieve what untrained children could accomplish intuitively on his farm. The scientist approaches the field with a checklist of variables to measure and control. The child approaches the field with curiosity and openness. The child's approach, Fukuoka argued, is closer to the truth.

## Life, Death, and Nature's Joy

"In nature, there is life and death, and nature is joyful. In human society, there is life and death, and people live in sorrow."

The difference is not in the facts of existence but in human consciousness. Nature experiences the cycle of life and death as a harmonious whole; humans, trapped in the intellect, experience it as suffering.

## The Four-Year-Old's Question

"The other morning I heard a four-year-old girl ask her mother, 'Why was I born into this world? To go to nursery school?' Naturally, her mother could not honestly say, 'Yes, that's right, so off you go.' And yet, you could say that people these days are born to go to nursery school."

The child's question exposes the absurdity of the modern trajectory: birth, education, work, death -- a sequence that has lost its connection to any deeper meaning.
