# Fungal Rock Weathering and Mineral Cycling

## Definition and Scope

Bioweathering is the erosion, decay and decomposition of rocks and minerals mediated by living organisms. Fungi are among the most important microbial agents of bioweathering. Although bacteria have historically dominated geomicrobiological studies due to their metabolic versatility, fungi can dissolve minerals and mobilize metals at higher pH values and over a wider redox range more efficiently than bacteria. Fungi are well suited as weathering agents because they can be highly resistant to extreme environmental conditions (metal toxicity, UV radiation, desiccation), adopt various growth and metabolic strategies, exude protons and metal-complexing metabolites, and form mutualistic symbiotic associations with plants, algae and cyanobacteria.

## Fungal Habitats on Rocks

Fungal communities have been components of terrestrial environments since the Ordovician period (480-460 MYBP). The filamentous growth habit provides high surface-area-to-mass ratio, [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]] (weddelite and whewellite) is commonly associated with various fungal ecophysiological groups. Chitin, the major component of fungal cell walls, is a substrate on which calcite readily nucleates. Fungi precipitate secondary calcite, whewellite and glushkinskite.

### Oxalates

Fungi produce metal oxalates with Ca, Cd, Co, Cu, Mn, Sr, Zn, Ni and Pb. Calcium oxalate acts as a reservoir for calcium and influences phosphate availability. Toxic metal oxalate formation provides a tolerance mechanism for environments with high metal concentrations. Beauveria caledonica transforms cadmium, copper, lead and zinc minerals into oxalates.

### Reductive and Oxidative Precipitation

Fungi precipitate reduced forms of metals/metalloids (elemental Ag, Se, Te). Fungi can oxidize manganese and iron from siderite and rhodochrosite, precipitating them as oxides. Desert varnish, an oxidized metal layer on rocks in arid regions, may involve fungal activity.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi in Mineral Transformations

Mycorrhizal fungi are involved in proton- and ligand-promoted metal mobilization, biosorption, accumulation, and extracellular precipitation of toxic metal oxalates. Ectomycorrhizal hyphae excrete organic acids (oxalic, citric, succinic, formic, malic) at micro- to millimolar concentrations, associated with micropores in weatherable soil minerals. Piloderma extracts K and Mg from biotite, microcline and chlorite. Paxillus involutus and Suillus granulatus release elements from apatite and wood ash. Zinc phosphate dissolution and host plant protection depend on the phosphorus status of the environment and the zinc tolerance of the fungal strain.

## Environmental Significance

Fungal mineral transformations affect contaminated land treatment, [[dighton-fungal-roles-in-nutrient-cycling]], metal and carbon cycling. Diverse communities have been found in the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica, extreme hot and cold deserts, and rock substrates buried kilometres below the Earth's surface. Traditional culture-based methods capture only about 5% of environmental fungi at best. DNA-based characterization techniques are increasingly important, using PCR amplification of SSU (18S) or ITS rDNA regions. The ITS region provides higher species resolution than 18S.
