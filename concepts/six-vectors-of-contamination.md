# Six Vectors of Contamination in Mushroom Cultivation

Cultivating mushroom mycelium in a laboratory is tantamount to not cultivating contaminants. Diagnosing the source of contamination, and the vector or pathway through which contaminants travel, is the key to successful [[comparison-seed-saving-techniques-vs-tissue-culture-cloning]]. Over years of practice, six distinct and separate vectors of contamination have been identified. If a contaminant arises in the laboratory, the cultivator should systematically examine each vector category as being the possible cause. Through a process of elimination, the distressed cultivator can determine the vector through which contaminants spread. Once discovered, the vector can be closed and the threat eliminated. If one vector is open, a multitude of contaminants pass through it, often confounding the diagnoses of inexperienced cultivators.

## The Overriding Coefficients

Two factors affect every vector: the number of contaminants and the exposure time. The more of each, the worse the infestation. These coefficients interact multiplicatively -- even a small number of contaminants with prolonged exposure can cause total crop failure.

## Vector 1: The Cultivator

The human body teems with populations of microorganisms. Diverse species of fungi (including yeast), bacteria, and viruses call your body their home. When healthy, these populations achieve an equilibrium. When ill, one or more groups proliferate out of control. Hence, unhealthy people should not be in a sterile laboratory, lest their disease organisms escape and proliferate to dangerous proportions.

**Primary transmission routes:** Touch, breath, and flaking of skin. Most frequently, contaminants are spread into the sterile laboratory via touch or breath. The flaking of skin is a direct cause of contamination.

**Mitigation strategies:**
- Wear gloves to minimize skin-borne contaminants (wash every 20-30 minutes with antibacterial soap)
- Disinfect hands with 80% isopropyl alcohol immediately before inoculations and every few minutes throughout procedures
- Shower and wear freshly laundered clothes before entering the lab
- Unhealthy individuals should not enter sterile areas
- Minimize movement and talking during critical inoculation steps

## Vector 2: The Air

Air can be described as a sea of microorganisms hosting invisible contaminants that directly contaminate sterilized media once exposed. Many particulates remain suspended. When a person walks into the laboratory, they bring in contaminants and their movement disturbs contaminant-laden dust on the floor, re-releasing it.

**Positive pressure solution:** Always maintain at least three doors prior to entry into the sterile laboratory from outside. Each room has fewer airborne particulates the nearer they are to the lab. Positive-pressurize the laboratory with micron-filtered air so the airstream naturally directs against entering personnel.

**Still-air alternative (for those without micron filters):**
1. Spray aerosol disinfectant (dilute isopropanol or bleach) high in the lab
2. Walk backwards as you retreat, spraying behind you
3. As the mist descends over 1-2 minutes, airborne particulates are trapped and carried to the floor
4. Re-enter and begin work
5. Never mix disinfectants (especially bleach and ammonia)
6. Appropriate precautions are strongly recommended -- this method can damage lungs

**UV lights** interfere with DNA replication but shadowed areas remain fully protected. They should be electrically linked to the lab door so they turn off on entry. UV exposure is health-threatening (skin cancer, corneal damage).

**Water misting for growing rooms:** Spray down each growing room twice daily, from ceiling to center floor. Each wash-down of 1,000 sq ft takes about 15 minutes. Rain is nature's best method of cleansing air.

**Diagnostic patterns:** Airborne contamination enters petri dishes when the lid is opened (during pouring or inoculation) or during incubation through gaps. It spreads evenly across media or forms along the inside periphery. Taping petri dish tops and bottoms with Parafilm or stretchable plastic wrap prevents spore entry from barometric pressure fluctuations.

## Vector 3: The Media

Often the medium upon which a culture is grown becomes the contamination source. Insufficient sterilization is usually the cause.

**Sterilization guidelines:**
- Malt extract or potato dextrose agar: minimum 40 minutes at 15 psi (not the commonly cited 15-20 minutes, which is far too brief for endospore-forming bacteria in additives)
- Soil extracts: soak 24 hours, then sterilize extracted water for 1+ hour (soil extracts are resplendent with contaminants)
- Small batches of sawdust: 2 hours at 15 psi
- Large autoclave loads of [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]]: 4+ hours at 19 psi (Badham 1988 found 4 hours at 19 psi equivalent to 14 hours at atmospheric pasteurization)
- [[gottlieb-psilocybin-cultivation-bulk-substrate-preparation]]: absolute sterilization is impractical; goal is reducing contaminants below threshold within the 2-week colonization window

**Key principle:** Increasing the spawn rate accelerates colonization, narrowing the window of opportunity for competitors. Should colonization not be complete in two weeks, contaminant development elsewhere is not unusual.

## Vector 4: The Tools

Scalpels, loops, syringes, and other implements carry contaminants between transfers. Every tool must be flame-sterilized between uses. The scalpel cools instantly upon contact with agar, so sterilization does not slow the process. The pressure cooker exterior should be thoroughly wiped clean before opening in the clean room to prevent hand-transfer of contaminants from its surface.

## Vector 5: The Inoculum

The culture or spawn being transferred may itself harbor hidden contamination. This is critical for Grain Masters (G1), which expand by a factor of 100-1000x -- any contamination is exponentially multiplied, not by a factor of 10, but by a factor of thousands.

A single kernel of grain contaminated with Penicillium, surrounded by thousands of clean kernels impregnated with pure mushroom mycelium, makes the entire container useless. Molds have advantages over mushrooms in that within 2-4 days, every spore can produce hundreds of conidiophores bearing dozens more spores, while mushroom mycelium typically extends as a linear progression.

**Safeguards:**
- Inspect all cultures carefully before use for sectoring, abnormal colors, or uneven growth
- Use cultures no more than two weeks old
- Maintain a margin of uncolonized agar along the dish periphery to detect recently landed contaminants
- Eliminate any culture showing the slightest abnormality

## Vector 6: Mobile Contamination Units (MCUs)

Insects (ants, flies), mites, and other mobile organisms transport contamination on their bodies and in their digestive tracts. Mites are particularly devastating -- nearly invisible, they can destroy entire cultures. Nematodes in substrate can also introduce bacterial contamination.

**Note on [[query-how-to-grow-oyster-mushrooms-at-home]]:** Thorn and Barron (1984) first noted that Pleurotus ostreatus exudes a metabolite toxic to nematodes. As the nematode lies stunned, mycelium invades through its orifices. This explains why nematodes have never been reported as a pathogen in [[gourmet-medicinal-mushroom-oyster-cultivation-methods]], while they are economically devastating in [[growing-gourmet-button-mushroom-agaricus-cultivation]] (Agaricus brunnescens) cultivation.

## The Blank Control Method

The most powerful diagnostic tool: leave uninoculated samples ("blanks") at every step of cultivation. Observe for two weeks. Under ideal conditions, no contamination should occur no matter how long uninoculated media is stored. If blanks contaminate within 48-72 hours, media was insufficiently sterilized. If contamination appears near the end of two weeks, it is likely endemic to the laboratory environment.
