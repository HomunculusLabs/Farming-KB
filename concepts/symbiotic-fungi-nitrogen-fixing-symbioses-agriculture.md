# Nitrogen-Fixing Symbioses and Their Agricultural Applications

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Symbiotic Fungi (Varma and Kharkwal, eds.), Chapter 1: Symbiosis - The Art of Living

## Overview

Nitrogen fixation is one of the most important symbiotic processes for agriculture. Biological nitrogen fixation contributes approximately 50-75% of the nitrogen requirement of crops in many agricultural systems. Symbiotic relationships in which both species benefit are mutualistic. One of the most important examples of mutualism in the overall economy of the biosphere is the symbiotic relationship between certain nitrogen-fixing bacteria and their legume hosts.

## Rhizobium-Legume Symbiosis

### The Symbiotic Process

Rhizobium has two types of strains:
- **Fast growers**: Include R. leguminosarum, Bacillus radicicola, etc.
- **Slow growers**: Include R. japonicum, R. lupinii, etc.

Rhizobia (collective name for representatives of gram-negative genera Azorhizobium, Bradyrhizobium, Mesorhizobium, Allorhizobium, Rhizobium, Sinorhizobium) are found in the soil of crop fields where bacteria and plant specifically affect each other's growth.

### Nodule Formation Process

The establishment of the Rhizobium-legume symbiosis involves several well-defined stages:

#### Presymbiosis Stage

A diverse group of rhizobia may inhabit the rhizosphere of a specific legume, but only one or a limited number of species will interact with the legume. Each species of legume excretes a spectrum of flavonoids, stachydrines, and aldenic acids into the rhizosphere, making the biochemical environment unique for each type of legume. This unique concentration gradient and spectrum of flavonoids attracts the appropriate rhizobial species.

Some root exudates are also used as nutrients by rhizobia -- for example, homoserine released by pea plants is the preferred nutrient (carbon and nitrogen source) for R. leguminosarum biovar vicieae.

#### Root Hair Attachment

Recognition factors between the host root and Rhizobium are proteins and sugars (glycoproteins, also known as lectins). Lectins present on the host root surface recognize polysaccharide receptors on compatible Rhizobium. When polysaccharides bind with a lectin molecule, this facilitates attachment.

As the bacteria attach to the cell wall of the root hair, morphological and physiological changes occur, including root hair curling ("Shepard's crook") with rhizobia attached to the inside surface of the curled root hair wall.

#### Infection Thread Formation

The area on the plant cell wall where bacteria are attached begins to grow inwards (invaginates), forming a growing tube (infection thread) made of plant cell wall material and membrane with the rhizobia inside. The infection thread extends from the root hair cell toward the underlying cortical cells.

#### Nodule Formation and Function

Cortical cells divide and differentiate into a nodule primordium. The infection thread delivers rhizobia into nodule cells via endocytosis. Each rhizobial cell becomes enclosed in host plant cell membrane to form a symbiosome -- a quasi-organelle specialized for symbiotic nitrogen fixation.

The bacteroides produce the enzyme nitrogenase, which converts atmospheric N2 to NH3. Leghemoglobin (red pigment produced in the nodule) maintains the low oxygen concentration necessary for nitrogenase function while allowing sufficient oxygen for bacterial respiration.

### Types of Nodules

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Determinate | No meristem, limited growth, spherical | Soybean, French bean, mungbean |
| Indeterminate | Active meristem, continuous growth, elongated/branched | Pea, clover |

## Agricultural Significance of Rhizobium Inoculation

### Yield Enhancement

Inoculation of wheat cultivar CDC Teal grown in field soil with Azorhizobium caulinodans (a diazotroph isolated from the legume Sesbania rostrata) caused increases in grain yield and total biomass of 34% and 49% respectively. Inoculated plants produced more tillers and had larger leaf area than uninoculated plants. This demonstrates the potential for exploiting nitrogen-fixing bacteria beyond traditional legume hosts.

### Legume Crop Production

Leguminous crops can fix nitrogen through the symbiotic process with Rhizobium species. This biological nitrogen fixation:
- Reduces dependence on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers
- Improves soil nitrogen status for subsequent crops
- Is more energy-efficient than industrial nitrogen fixation (Haber-Bosch process)
- Reduces greenhouse gas emissions associated with fertilizer production

### Integration with Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

The interaction between rhizobia and AM fungi has received considerable attention because of the relatively high phosphorus demand of nitrogen fixation. The two symbioses typically act synergistically, resulting in greater nitrogen and phosphorus content in combination than when each is applied alone.

Legumes are typically coarse-rooted and therefore inefficient at extracting phosphorus from soil. AM fungi associated with legumes provide the essential link for adequate phosphorus nutrition, leading to enhanced nitrogenase activity that promotes root and mycorrhizal growth.

Biological nitrogen fixation by Rhizobium in legume hosts can be enhanced through co-infection with AMF.

## Frankia-Actinorhizal Symbiosis

### Non-Leguminous Nitrogen Fixation

Actinomycetes of the genus Frankia form nitrogen-fixing root nodules on at least eight families of non-leguminous woody plants (e.g., Casuarina, Alnus, Myrica, Hippophae, Ceanothus). Characteristics:

- Frankia forms branching filaments with globular vesicles at their ends (sites of nitrogen fixation)
- Nitrogen fixation is oxygen-sensitive and requires molybdenum and cobalt
- Some nodules can be as large as baseballs (Alnus, Ceanothus) or soccer balls (Casuarina)
- Nodules are long-lived compared to rhizobium nodules of legumes
- Host-symbiont specificity is broad

### Agricultural and Restoration Applications

While not of direct economic significance for crop plants (none of the major crop plants is infected with Frankia), Frankia symbiosis is important for:
- **Soil improvement**: Alder and Casuarina fix nitrogen and improve soil fertility on degraded sites
- **Reforestation**: Nitrogen-fixing trees establish on nitrogen-poor sites
- **Erosion control**: Casuarina and Hippophae are used in erosion control plantings
- **Agroforestry**: Nitrogen-fixing trees in alley cropping and windbreak systems

## Azolla-Anabaena Symbiosis

### The Fern-Cyanobacteria Partnership

Azolla, a floating fern, forms a symbiotic relationship with the blue-green alga Anabaena that fixes atmospheric nitrogen. This association:
- Is important for rice production in some regions ("a hungry world")
- Provides an easily maintainable system for laboratory exercises
- Produces heterocysts at 25-30% frequency (vs 5-10% in free-living condition)
- Transfers fixed nitrogen from Anabaena to Azolla

### Rice Paddy Applications

Azolla has been used as a green manure in rice paddies in Asia for centuries. When incorporated into flooded rice fields:
- Fixes atmospheric nitrogen for rice nutrition
- Reduces weed growth by covering the water surface
- Provides organic matter when decomposed
- Can contribute significant nitrogen (up to 100+ kg N/ha per season)

## Lichen Nitrogen Fixation

### Cyanobacterial Lichens

Lichens containing blue-green algae (Anabaena, Nostoc) as partners perform dual functions of photosynthesis and nitrogen fixation. In three-member associations containing both blue-green and green algae, the blue-green alga is relieved of photosynthesis and produces more heterocysts for exclusive nitrogen fixation.

### Soil and Ecosystem Contributions

Lichens contribute to:
- Nitrogen inputs in primary succession and nutrient-poor habitats
- Soil formation through weathering of rock surfaces
- Organic matter accumulation in early successional communities
- Nitrogen fixation in arctic, alpine, and desert ecosystems

However, lichens are very sensitive to atmospheric pollutants and are rarely seen near human habitations with automobile fumes and factory effluents.
