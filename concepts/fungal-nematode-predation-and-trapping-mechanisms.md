# Fungal–Nematode Predation and Trapping Mechanisms

nematophagous fungi are a diverse group of fungi that capture, kill, and digest
nematodes. They occupy a critical position in the soil food web and have attracted
intense interest as biocontrol agents against plant-parasitic nematode species that
cause billions of dollars in crop losses annually. Based on the framework outlined
by Boddy & Jones (in Gadd et al., Chapter 6), these fungi employ three broad
predatory strategies: adhesive trapping, constricting rings, and endoparasitism,
alongside toxin-mediated immobilization.

## Adhesive Trapping Structures

Adhesive traps are the most common predatory organs among nematode-trapping fungi.
Fungi deploy several morphologically distinct adhesive structures, each suited to
different soil microhabitats:

- **Adhesive knobs**: Single spherical cells on short stalks that secrete a sticky
  coating. When a nematode brushes against the knob, adhesion is instantaneous and
  the fungus penetrates the cuticle within hours. *Monacrosporium* spp. are classic
  examples.

- **Adhesive networks**: A three-dimensional mesh of hyphae coated with adhesive,
  produced by *[[dionaea-muscipula]]*) is striking: both use
rapid, touch-triggered movements to capture motile prey, yet they achieve this through
entirely different biophysical mechanisms—osmotic swelling in fungi versus
turgor-driven snap-buckling in plants.

## Endoparasitism

Endoparasitic fungi take a fundamentally different approach: they produce **ingestible
spores** (conidia or zoospores) that nematodes consume while feeding. Inside the
gut, spores germinate and the fungus proliferates through the host's body cavity,
eventually killing it and emerging to produce new infective propagules.

Species in the genera *Catenaria* and *Harposporium* exemplify this strategy. Some
endoparasites produce adhesive spores that stick to the nematode exterior before
penetrating the cuticle, blurring the boundary between endoparasitism and adhesive
trapping. The life cycle is tightly synchronized with nematode activity: spore
production peaks when nematode populations are high, ensuring efficient transmission.

This strategy mirrors insect **parasitoid wasps**, which lay eggs inside hosts with
the developing larvae consuming the host from within. Both endoparasitic fungi
and parasitoid wasps are lethal to hosts and rely on precise molecular recognition.
