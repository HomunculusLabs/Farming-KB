# Cannabis and Asthma — Bronchodilation via THC and Cannabinoids

## Overview

The Cannabis Health Index (Blesching, 2015) reviews evidence from seven clinical studies examining the bronchodilatory effects of THC and cannabis on asthma patients. The combined CHI (Cannabis Health Index) value of **24** reflects substantial evidence that cannabinoids can produce significant and sustained airway relaxation, though the route of administration remains a critical consideration for this respiratory condition.

Asthma is characterized by chronic airway constriction, inflammation, and hyperresponsiveness. The paradox of using inhaled plant material to treat a respiratory disease is acknowledged, but the pharmacological data on THC's bronchodilatory properties are compelling and span four decades of research.

## Asthma Pathophysiology

### Standard Understanding

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways involving:

- **Bronchoconstriction** — smooth muscle spasm in the upper airways narrows breathing passages.
- **Airway hyperinflation** — trapped air causes overexpansion of lung tissue.
- **Inflammation** — chronic immune activation produces swelling and mucus.
- **Hyperresponsiveness** — airways overreact to triggers including dust, mold, exercise, emotional stress, [[hemenway-cold-air-drainage-and-frost-management]], and pollutants.

### Orthodox Treatment Limitations

Standard treatments include bronchodilators (albuterol, salbutamol), inhaled corticosteroids, and beta-2 adrenergic agonists. Key limitations noted by Blesching:

- Regular inhaler use may contribute to **worsening asthma over time**.
- Pharmaceutical bronchodilators often have **shorter duration** of effect compared to THC.
- No pharmacological cure exists; treatment focuses on symptom management.
- Children and elderly are most vulnerable and may be least tolerant of side effects.

## Clinical Evidence for THC Bronchodilation

### Study 1: Tashkin et al. (1974) — Double-Blind Crossover

The foundational study enrolled ten patients with stable asthma in a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover design comparing:

- Smoked cannabis
- Oral THC (15 mg)
- Placebo
- Isoproterenol (standard bronchodilator)

**Results:** While isoproterenol produced a more pronounced initial effect, both smoked cannabis and oral THC caused **significant bronchodilation lasting at least 2 hours** — longer than the pharmaceutical comparator.

### Study 2: Tashkin et al. (1975) — Chemical and Exercise-Induced Bronchospasm

Eight stable asthmatics received smoked cannabis (2% THC) under two challenge conditions:

1. Chemically induced bronchospasm
2. Exercise-induced bronchospasm

**Results:** Smoked cannabis produced **prompt correction of bronchospasms and associated hyperinflation**, with efficacy similar to isoproterenol in both challenge models.

### Study 3: Williams et al. (1976) — THC vs. Salbutamol

A double-blind, placebo-controlled study of ten asthma patients compared THC with salbutamol:

**Results:** Both THC and salbutamol significantly improved ventilatory function. Salbutamol achieved maximal bronchodilation more rapidly, but **at 1 hour both drugs were equally effective**. The researcher noted that THC's mode of action **differs from sympathomimetic drugs** (which stimulate heart rate and blood pressure), suggesting THC could serve as a suitable adjuvant without cardiovascular side effects.

### Study 4: Tashkin et al. (1977) — Aerosolized THC

Eleven healthy subjects and five asthma patients received aerosolized THC in a randomized, double-blind trial:

**Results:** Aerosolized THC was less pronounced than isoproterenol at 5 minutes but **significantly better at 1–3 hours**. However, the study also identified a critical caveat — THC caused **moderate to severe bronchoconstriction with cough and chest discomfort in 2 of 5 asthmatic subjects**. The local irritating effect of THC on airway mucosa may make aerosolized delivery unsuitable for some patients.

### Study 5: Tashkin et al. (1978) — Dose-Response with Aerosolized THC

Asthmatic patients received aerosolized THC at steady-state dosages of 50–200 mcg:

**Results:** Treatment increased peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) and forced expiratory volume, confirming bronchodilatory efficacy at controlled doses. The dose-response relationship helped establish therapeutic ranges.

## Mechanism of Action

### Distinct from Sympathomimetic Drugs

A key finding across multiple studies is that THC's bronchodilatory mechanism **differs from that of beta-2 adrenergic agonists**:

- **Beta-2 agonists** (albuterol, salbutamol) stimulate the sympathetic nervous system, producing rapid bronchodilation but with cardiovascular side effects (increased heart rate, blood pressure).
- **THC** appears to act through cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and/or CB2) expressed on airway smooth muscle, producing bronchodilation **without the same cardiovascular stimulation**.

### Endocannabinoid System in the Lungs

The lungs express both CB1 and CB2 cannabinoid receptors:

- **CB1 receptors** on bronchial smooth muscle may mediate the direct bronchodilatory effect.
- **CB2 receptors** on immune cells in lung tissue may contribute to the anti-inflammatory component.
- The endogenous cannabinoid anandamide has been shown to have **biphasic effects** on airway constriction — low doses cause bronchoconstriction while higher doses produce bronchodilation, suggesting complex receptor pharmacology.

### Anti-Inflammatory Contribution

Beyond direct smooth muscle relaxation, cannabinoids may help address the underlying inflammation of asthma through:

- Reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokine release.
- Modulation of immune cell activity in lung tissue via CB2 receptors.
- Potential reduction of airway hyperresponsiveness over time.

## Route of Administration Considerations

The paradox of treating asthma with inhaled combusted material is significant:

| Route | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| **Smoked** | Rapid onset, effective bronchodilation | Combustion byproducts, potential airway irritation |
| **Vaporized** | Rapid onset, avoids combustion | Limited clinical data, device variability |
| **Aerosolized THC** | Controlled dosing, direct lung delivery | Local irritation in some patients (cough, discomfort) |
| **Oral THC** | No airway irritation | Slower onset (30–60 min), less predictable dosing |

### The Irritation Caveat

The 1977 aerosolized THC study showed THC can be a **local airway irritant**, causing bronchoconstriction in some patients. This means not all asthma patients will tolerate cannabinoid inhalation, and careful titration is essential.

## Mind-Body Medicine Perspective

Blesching connects asthma to emotional and psychological factors:

- Emotional distress is a recognized **asthma trigger**.
- The relationship between anxiety and bronchospasm creates a **feedback loop** — anxiety worsens breathing, difficulty breathing worsens anxiety.
- Cannabis may interrupt this cycle through both pharmacological bronchodilation and **anxiolytic effects**.
- The relaxing psychoactive properties of THC may complement its direct bronchodilatory action, addressing both the physical and emotional components of an asthma response.
