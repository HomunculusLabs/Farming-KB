# Palmer on Mushroom Anatomy and Structure

## Palmer's Approach to Description

Julius Auboineau Palmer's "About Mushrooms" (1894) provides detailed
descriptions of fungal anatomy from the perspective of a dedicated
amateur mycologist writing in the late [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]]. Palmer
deliberately avoided excessive scientific terminology while still
providing generic names to enable identification. His anatomical
descriptions emphasize practical recognition features rather than
microscopic detail.

## The Four Grand Divisions

Palmer proposed a practical classification based on the nature of the
spore-bearing surface. He recognized four fundamental divisions of
fleshy fungi, each distinguishable by simple observation of the
underside of the cap:

1. Gills (plates running like wheel spokes)
2. Pores (sponge-like substance)
3. Teeth (small spine-like projections)
4. Enclosed spores (as in puffballs)

## Gill-Bearing Fungi (Agaricini)

The largest family of mushrooms bears thin plates set on their edges,
running inward to a common center like the spokes of a wheel. These
plates are called the gills, and the stem corresponds to the hub.
This is the family of Agaricini or Agarics. Palmer regarded this as
the least safe for the amateur, despite it containing the common
meadow mushroom.

In the common mushroom, the gills are pliable and the flesh can be
bent without breaking. The gills start as a faint shade of pink,
deepening through purple into black as the mushroom ages. When pink,
the ring around the stem is quite perfect. The gills are free from the
stem, meaning they do not attach to it, which is one reason the stem
leaves the cap so easily when pulled.

The veil or web is an important anatomical feature. In young
specimens, a membrane (the [[partial-veil-tropism-protection-before-spore-release]]) extends from the stem to the
cap margin, protecting the developing gills. As the mushroom expands,
this veil ruptures, often leaving a ring or annulus on the stem. In
older specimens, no trace of ring or veil may remain.

## Pore-Bearing Fungi (Boleti)

Where gills would be, the pore-bearing mushroom has a substance
resembling fine sponge. Generically this is a polyporus. When the
stem is distinctly defined, of ordinary length, and the pores or
tubes are easily separable from each other, it is a boletus. Palmer
distinguished edible boleti from their polyporei relatives by noting
that the latter grow mostly on wood with abortive stems, while edible
boleti grow from the ground with distinct stems and easily separable
tubes.

The color of the pores is diagnostic: brown, yellowish, or greenish
pores with a russet or brown cap and white flesh indicate an edible
or harmless variety. Brilliant-colored, red or yellow pores, or flesh
that turns blue when bruised, suggest a dangerous specimen. Red pores
at the orifice are considered doubtless poisonous.

## Teeth-Bearing Fungi (Hydnei)

Instead of gills or pores, some fungi bear small points or spines on
the underside of the cap. These belong to the teeth-bearing fungi,
generically the Hydnei. The spreading hydnum ([[amanita-caesarea]], which has yellow gills)
- White, tasteless, nearly scentless flesh in young specimens
- A stem free from the gills, surrounded near the top with a ring
  or apron
- A bulbous stem base covered with the sheath or volva

The spore deposit of Amanitas, when a specimen is laid on blue paper
for a few hours, shows a delicate white deposit resembling an artist's
shading. The cap colors are highly variable: red, orange, green,
yellow, and white with intermediate shades.

## Spawn and Subterranean Growth

Palmer described the hidden [[mycelium]] or "spawn" as a white, vine-like
substance running through the soil, dividing and sub-dividing like
veins on the back of the hand. Just as a grape-vine cutting will
grow and bear fruit, a portion of spawn transplanted will continue
to ramify and produce mushrooms. The whole earth beneath one's feet
is alive with this vegetation, with distinct varieties as recognizable
as hop-vines from woodbine.

## Growth Rate

Contrary to popular belief, Palmer observed the common mushroom takes
about ten days to mature from a pin-head button to a disk three or
four inches in diameter. It forms for days below the soil; a heavy
dew or shower straightens the stem and expands the cap, causing it
to break the earth overnight.

## Brittleness as a Diagnostic Feature

Palmer drew particular attention to the brittleness of certain
families. The milk-mushrooms and Russulae are very brittle: the gills
do not mat down like those of the horse-mushroom or elm-tree mushroom,
but break into small pieces while the whole fungus snaps suddenly on
being bent. This contrasts with the pliable, flexible gills of the
common mushroom, whose flesh can be bent without breaking.
