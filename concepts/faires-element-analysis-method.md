# Element Analysis Method

The element analysis is the core design methodology in Nicole Faires' approach to permaculture. Every element on a property -- whether a chicken, a rock pile, a herb garden, or a fruit tree -- is analyzed using a simple card system that identifies its inputs, characteristics, and outputs. The goal is to match the output of one element to the input of another, creating closed loops where nothing is wasted.

This method is part of the broader [[permaculture-design-process]] and connects to [[permaculture-design]]. It should be performed after initial [[permaculture-site-analysis]] and before placing elements into zones via [[faires-zone-and-sector-planning]].

## Step 1: Site Inventory

Before analyzing individual elements, take a thorough inventory of everything already present on the land. Walk the property in all seasons and at different times of day. Record observations including:

- **Temperature changes**: Frost pockets, heat zones, microclimates
- **Effort between places**: Distance and difficulty of daily tasks
- **Plant observations**: Prickly plant locations, tree species and conditions, early-fruiting plants, poisonous plants
- **Animal signs**: Insect groupings, wildlife tracks, bird activity
- **Water patterns**: Water movement, groundwater indicators, damp or boggy ground, springs
- **Weather evidence**: Fire signs, wind-shaped trees, sun and shadow patterns
- **Physical features**: Erosion areas, dead wood concentrations, slopes, cliffs, rocks
- **Soil conditions**: Erosion patterns, damp spots, compaction

See [[permaculture-site-assessment-checklist]] for a comprehensive inventory template.

## Step 2: Categorize Resources

Sort all discovered resources into three categories, as described in [[edge-effect-in-permaculture]]:

**Life resources**: Plants, animals, insects, fungi, soil organisms

**Energy resources**: Wind patterns, wood lots, water flow, gas potential, solar exposure, geothermal potential

**Social resources**: Teaching opportunities, recreation potential, gathering spaces. Also include off-land resources: markets, sawmills, schools, community networks, neighbors with complementary skills

## Step 3: Analyze Each Element

For each element on the property, document three aspects:

**Inputs** -- What does this element need to function?
- Food, water, shelter, maintenance, materials
- Energy sources and resources required
- Environmental conditions (temperature, light, soil type)

**Characteristics** -- What are its behaviors and properties?
- Size, habits, temperature needs, relationships with other elements
- Positive and negative behaviors
- Seasonal patterns and life cycle stages
- Special attributes (nitrogen-fixing, pest-repelling, shade-producing)

**Outputs** -- What does this element produce?
- Yield (food, materials, products)
- Waste (manure, heat, excess) -- in permaculture, "waste" is just an unused output
- Byproducts and services (shade, pest control, pollination, windbreak)

## Step 4: Create Element Cards

Write each element on a 3x5 index card with the three categories clearly labeled. This tactile, low-tech approach makes it easy to rearrange connections physically on a table. Examples:

### Example: Rock Pile
- **Inputs**: Sunlight, human labor to position
- **Characteristics**: Dark slate absorbs heat during day, releases at night; provides habitat for insects and reptiles; durable and permanent
- **Outputs**: Passive solar heat (microclimate warming), structural support for terracing, windbreak, insect habitat for pest-control species

### Example: Herb Garden
- **Inputs**: Sun, water, mulch, compost, human attention for harvesting
- **Characteristics**: Perennial herbs; attracts pollinators and predatory insects; some species repel pests; aromatic; requires well-drained soil
- **Outputs**: Medicinal herbs, cooking herbs, bee forage, pest control services, mulch material when pruned

### Example: Chickens
- **Inputs**: Food (grain, kitchen scraps), water, grit, calcium, shelter, protection from predators
- **Characteristics**: Barred Rock breed, dual-purpose (eggs and meat); flock social structure; daily egg laying; scratch and peck behavior; need secure coop at night
- **Outputs**: Eggs, meat, manure (high-nitrogen fertilizer), feathers, pest control (eat insects and weed seeds), garden tilling (scratching)

See [[chicken-husbandry-in-permaculture]] and [[chicken-integration-permaculture-guide]] for deeper chicken system design.

## Step 5: Match Outputs to Inputs

Once all cards are created, the design process becomes a matching game. This is where the real design insight emerges:

1. Look at each element's output list
2. Find another element whose input list matches that output
3. Place those elements close together physically
4. Repeat until as many connections as possible are made

**Practical examples**:
- Bees need nectar food; herbs provide it -- plant herb garden near beehives
- Chickens need daily food and attention; place coop near the house (Zone 1)
- Chickens produce manure; place near the compost pile so manure goes directly in
- Woodlot provides forage (insects, seeds) for chickens; pen can open into the trees
- Rock pile absorbs heat; place near frost-sensitive plants for microclimate protection
- Herb garden attracts pollinators; place near fruit trees that need pollination

## Step 6: Draw Flowchart of Connections

Create a visual diagram showing all the connections between elements. Arrows flow from outputs to inputs. This [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]] reveals:

- Which elements are highly connected (central hubs)
- Which outputs are unused (potential pollution or waste)
- Which inputs are unmet (work you must provide yourself)
- Opportunities for new connections by adding missing elements

The more connections between elements, the more self-sustaining the system becomes. Every unmatched output represents pollution or waste; every unmet input represents work you must do yourself. This directly applies the principle of [[permaculture-design-principles]].

## Step 7: Place Elements in Zones

Once the inventory and connection analysis are complete, place elements in zones based on the zone map and zone definitions. See [[faires-zone-and-sector-planning]] for zone details.

- Elements needing daily attention go in Zone 0 or Zone 1 (near the house)
- Elements needing weekly attention go in Zone 2
- Elements needing monthly attention go in Zone 3
- Elements needing only seasonal attention go in Zone 4
- Wild elements belong in Zone 5

## Coaction Study

For plant communities, a coaction study determines which species work well together. When Species A and Species B are placed together, they affect each other positively, negatively, or not at all. The goal is to arrange plants so every species receives at least one positive interaction from its neighbors. This is the biological basis of [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]].

This methodology applies the permaculture principle that "every element should serve many functions" (see [[permitted-vs-forced-functions]]) by ensuring that outputs of one element become inputs for another, creating self-reliant systems that require minimal external inputs.
