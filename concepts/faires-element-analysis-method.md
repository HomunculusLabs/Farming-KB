## Step 2: Categorize Resources

Sort all discovered resources into three categories, as described in [[chicken-husbandry-in-permaculture]] and [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]] reveals:

- Which elements are highly connected (central hubs)
- Which outputs are unused (potential pollution or waste)
- Which inputs are unmet (work you must provide yourself)
- Opportunities for new connections by adding missing elements

The more connections between elements, the more self-sustaining the system becomes. Every unmatched output represents pollution or waste; every unmet input represents work you must do yourself. This directly applies the principle of [[faires-zone-and-sector-planning]] for zone details.

- Elements needing daily attention go in Zone 0 or Zone 1 (near the house)
- Elements needing weekly attention go in Zone 2
- Elements needing monthly attention go in Zone 3
- Elements needing only seasonal attention go in Zone 4
- Wild elements belong in Zone 5

## Coaction Study

For plant communities, a coaction study determines which species work well together. When Species A and Species B are placed together, they affect each other positively, negatively, or not at all. The goal is to arrange plants so every species receives at least one positive interaction from its neighbors. This is the biological basis of [[permitted-vs-forced-functions]]) by ensuring that outputs of one element become inputs for another, creating self-reliant systems that require minimal external inputs.
