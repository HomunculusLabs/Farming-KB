# Water Treatment and Filtration Methods

Water treatment in [[permaculture-design]] draws on both traditional techniques and biological processes to purify water for domestic, agricultural, and ecological use. Bill Mollison outlines a comprehensive suite of methods ranging from simple physical filtration to complex multi-stage pond systems, emphasizing low-energy, biologically integrated approaches wherever possible.

## Common Water Treatment Methods

The foundational treatments for improving water quality include:

- **Aeration** — Introducing air into water to release dissolved gases (e.g., hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide) and oxidize dissolved iron and manganese. Aeration can be achieved through cascades, fountains, or simply allowing water to flow over rocks and through the air.
- **Settling (Sedimentation)** — Allowing suspended particles to settle by gravity in still or slow-moving water. Detention time, basin depth, and the presence of baffles all influence settling efficiency.
- **Skimming and Sieving** — Removing floating debris, oils, and large particulates from the water surface or by passing water through screens or sieves of decreasing mesh size.
- **Filtration** — Passing water through porous media (sand, charcoal, gravel) to physically strain out suspended solids, pathogens, and some dissolved contaminants.
- **Coagulation and Flocculation** — Adding coagulants (alum, ferric chloride, or natural alternatives like seed extracts) to neutralize particle charges, causing them to clump into larger flocs that settle more readily.
- **Biological Removal** — Using living organisms (bacteria, plants, mussels) to metabolize or sequester contaminants such as nutrients, pathogens, and organic matter.
- **pH Adjustment** — Modifying water pH using lime (to raise) or organic acids (to lower) to optimize conditions for subsequent treatment stages or to precipitate dissolved metals.

## Sand Filtration

Sand filtration is one of the oldest and most reliable water purification methods. In the permaculture context, slow sand filters are preferred for their simplicity and effectiveness.

### Design and Construction

- A typical slow sand filter consists of a bed of fine sand approximately **1.2 meters (4 feet) deep** contained within a waterproof basin.
- Water is applied at the surface and percolates slowly downward under gravity — the "slow drip" approach — passing through a biological layer (schmutzdecke) that forms on top of the sand.
- The schmutzdecke is a living film of bacteria, algae, and protozoa that provides significant biological treatment in addition to physical straining.
- Supporting gravel layers beneath the sand collect filtered water and direct it to an underdrain system.

### Maintenance

- Over time, the schmutzdecke thickens and flow rates decline. When the rate drops below acceptable levels, the top 1–2 cm of sand is scraped off and replaced.
- Full sand replacement is needed periodically (every few years depending on load and water quality).
- Slow sand filters require no chemicals, minimal energy, and can be built from local materials — making them ideal for rural and off-grid settings.

## Activated Charcoal Filtration

Activated charcoal (carbon) removes organic compounds, chlorine, some heavy metals, and unpleasant tastes and odors through adsorption. In permaculture systems, charcoal can be produced from a variety of locally available materials:

- **Animal bones** — Bone char is particularly effective at removing fluoride and heavy metals.
- **Willow wood** — Willow charcoal provides good adsorption capacity and can be sourced from coppiced willow plantings.
- **Coconut husks** — Coconut shell activated carbon has a highly porous structure with excellent adsorption properties.

The charcoal must be "activated" by heating in a low-oxygen environment (pyrolysis) to create a vast internal pore structure. Activated carbon filters can be configured as simple bucket filters or as more elaborate column systems. When combined with silicon dioxide columns, they form effective multi-stage treatment trains for removing a broad range of contaminants.

## Trickle Filters and Biological Towers

Trickle filters (also called trickling filters) pass water over a media bed colonized by aerobic bacteria and biofilm organisms:

- Water is distributed over the top of a porous media (gravel, slag, plastic media, or rock) and trickles downward.
- Air circulates through the open spaces, supplying oxygen to the microbial community.
- Bacteria metabolize organic matter and convert ammonia to nitrite and then nitrate (nitrification).

**Trickle towers** are taller, more concentrated versions optimized specifically for ammonia removal in aquaculture and wastewater systems. They provide high surface area for bacterial colonization and excellent gas exchange.

## Freshwater Mussels as Biological Filters

Freshwater mussels are exceptional natural water purifiers:

- A single mussel can filter **25–50 liters of water per day**, removing suspended algae, bacteria, and fine particulates.
- Mussels sequester nutrients and heavy metals into their shells and tissues.
- They require clean, oxygenated water with stable flow — making them best suited for protected channels or dedicated treatment reaches rather than raw sewage.
- In permaculture design, mussel beds can serve a dual purpose as water purifiers and protein harvest systems.

## Pond Stabilization and Aquatic Plant Systems

Constructed ponds and wetlands use aquatic plants to stabilize water quality, reduce nutrient loads, and prevent erosion.

### Key Aquatic Plants

- **Water lilies (Nymphaea spp.)** — Shade the water surface, reducing algae growth; their root systems provide substrate for beneficial bacteria.
- **Azolla** — A small floating fern that fixes atmospheric nitrogen and rapidly absorbs phosphorus and heavy metals from water.
- **Water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes)** — Extremely fast-growing; excellent at removing nutrients, heavy metals, and organic pollutants. Must be managed carefully as it can become invasive.
- **Watercress (Nasturtium officinale)** — Absorbs nitrates and some dyes from water; also serves as an edible crop, providing a productive harvest from treatment systems.

### Bank Stabilizers

Plants that stabilize pond banks and prevent erosion while contributing to water treatment include:

- **Juncus (rushes)** — Dense root mats bind soil and filter runoff.
- **Scirpus (bulrushes)** — Deep-rooted emergent plants that trap sediments and provide wildlife habitat.
- **Grasses** — Various species provide quick ground cover and slow sheet erosion along pond margins.

## Chemical and Supplemental Treatments

Several mineral-based treatments complement biological systems:

- **Liming** — Adding agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) reduces turbidity by causing colloidal particles to flocculate and settle. Lime also raises pH, precipitates dissolved metals, and can reduce phosphorus concentrations.
- **Carbon for nitrogen removal** — Activated carbon columns or carbon-amended beds adsorb nitrogenous compounds and organic contaminants.
- **Lime for phosphorus and sludge removal** — In sewage treatment, lime addition precipitates phosphorus as calcium phosphate and conditions sludge for easier handling and dewatering.

## Sewage Treatment Using Natural Processes

Mollison strongly advocates for [[natural-sewage-treatment-systems]] over conventional mechanical plants, highlighting their lower energy requirements, lower cost, and ecological benefits.

### The Werribee Sewage Lagoon System (Case Study)

The Werribee treatment facility near Melbourne, Australia, is one of the world's largest examples of land-based sewage treatment:

- **Scale**: 1,500 hectares of treatment ponds serving approximately **2.7 million people**.
- **Design**: A series of ponds arranged in a cascading treatment train.
- **Pond types in series**:
  1. **Anaerobic ponds** — Deep, unaerated basins where anaerobic bacteria break down organic solids. These produce biogas (methane) and reduce BOD significantly.
  2. **Facultative ponds** — Intermediate-depth ponds with both aerobic surface zones and anaerobic bottom zones. Algae at the surface produce oxygen; bacteria in the lower zones continue decomposition.
  3. **Aerobic ponds** — Shallow basins fully oxygenated by algal photosynthesis and surface aeration, providing final polishing of the effluent.

### Pond Sizing Considerations

Sizing sewage treatment ponds depends on several key factors:

- **Population equivalent (PE)** — The organic load expressed as equivalent number of people.
- **Organic loading rate** — Typically 20–40 kg BOD per hectare per day for anaerobic ponds, with lower rates for facultative and aerobic stages.
- **Retention time** — Anaerobic ponds: 3–5 days; facultative ponds: 20–40 days; aerobic ponds: 5–10 days. Longer retention improves treatment quality.
- **Climate** — Warmer climates accelerate biological activity and allow smaller pond footprints; cold climates require larger ponds or longer retention times.
- **Depth** — Anaerobic: 2–5m; facultative: 1.2–2.5m; aerobic: 0.5–1.5m. Depth controls the oxygen environment and algal productivity.

The Werribee system demonstrates that appropriately designed pond systems can handle urban-scale sewage loads entirely through natural biological processes, producing treated effluent suitable for irrigation while also supporting grazing land and wetland habitat.

## Integrated Treatment Design

In permaculture practice, these methods are rarely used in isolation. Effective water treatment systems combine multiple approaches in series:

- **Pre-treatment stage**: Skimming and settling remove large debris and heavy sediments before they reach finer filtration systems.
- **Primary treatment**: Anaerobic ponds or settling basins reduce organic load significantly.
- **Secondary treatment**: Facultative ponds, trickle filters, or constructed wetlands with aquatic plants remove dissolved nutrients and remaining organics.
- **Polishing stage**: Sand filters, activated carbon columns, or aerobic ponds produce final effluent clean enough for irrigation, aquaculture, or safe discharge.

This multi-barrier approach ensures redundancy — if one stage underperforms, downstream stages compensate. It also mirrors natural water purification processes, making the system more resilient and self-regulating than conventional mechanical treatment plants.

## Key Principles

- Use the lowest-energy approach that achieves the required water quality.
- Favor biological and passive systems over chemical and mechanical ones.
- Design for resilience — multiple treatment stages provide redundancy.
- Harvest yields where possible (mussels, watercress, grazing from treated land).
- Size systems generously; undersized systems fail under peak loads.
