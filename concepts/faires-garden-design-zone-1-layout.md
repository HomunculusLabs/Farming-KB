# Faires Garden Design and Zone 1 Layout
Zone 1 is the most intensively managed area — closest to the house and kitchen door.
Everything here needs frequent attention and harvesting. The design prioritizes
convenience, efficiency, and maximum yield from minimal space.
## Zone 1 Components
### Herb Spiral
Located as close to the kitchen door as possible for quick access while cooking.
A spiral mound of earth:
- **Top:** Thyme, rosemary, sage (drought-tolerant, full sun)
- **Shady areas:** Mint, cilantro, parsley, chives
- **Very bottom:** Tiny pond lined with plastic for water chestnut or watercress
- **Sprinkler** at the top waters the whole thing
### Salad Bed
Narrow bed for easy reach from the path. More herbs, salad greens, chives,
shallots. Greens grow quickly when trimmed; keep soil mulched throughout the year.
### Path Vegetables
Both sides of pathways around the house are planted with vegetables harvested
throughout summer. Transplanted from the greenhouse: Swiss chard, Brussels
sprouts, onions, celery, broccoli, kale, mustard, spinach, peppers, zucchini,
fennel. Most can be picked as needed. If used up, have seedlings ready to
transplant. At season's end, leave some to go to seed for next year.
### Main Garden Beds
**Narrow beds** (frequent picking needed):
- Beans, tomatoes, carrots, peas, zucchini, eggplant, chamomile, cumin
- Easy access from path
**Wide beds** (harvest once, mature all summer):
- Corn, melons, onions, potatoes, beets, leeks, turnips
- Planted close together, mulch themselves
- Less maintenance; can add extra wide beds in Zone 2 as cash crops
### Hedges
At the edge of Zone 1 and around gardens/outbuildings. Functions:
- Keep out animals, wind, and weeds
- Create mulch material
- Provide animal food
- Fix nitrogen
- Produce human food
**Planting:** Mulch soil heavily with cardboard, straw, or sawdust. While waiting
for hedges to grow, use fast-growing temporary barriers: comfrey, bamboo,
Jerusalem artichoke, Siberian pea shrub (band 4 ft wide outside hedge bed).
For small garden barriers: perennial herbs like rosemary.
### Wind Barriers
If strong winds and hedges aren't grown yet, build instant barriers with tires
(also thermal mass absorbing heat). Prepare ground first with newspaper and
mulch. Stack tires, fill with earth/compost/hay/scraps. Plant a wind-tolerant
species on top.
### Shade Trees
Deciduous trees provide summer shade and allow winter heat through. Position
based on sector analysis of sun angles.
## Design Principles
1. **Curves over straight lines** — more edge, more growing space
2. **Paths first** — lay out paths, plant into remaining areas
3. **Sheet mulch** — don't dig; lay down cardboard, mulch, compost, plant directly
4. **Vertical growing** — trellises, arbors, hanging baskets maximize space
5. **Edge planting** — plant productive species along every edge and boundary
6. **Water access** — ensure every bed is reachable with a hose or watering can
7. **Kitchen proximity** — most-used plants closest to the door

## Composting in Zone 1
Every Zone 1 layout should include at least one composting station positioned for
convenient daily access from the kitchen. A three-bin system allows continuous
processing: one bin receiving fresh scraps, one actively decomposing, and one
curing for garden use. Kitchen scraps mixed with carbon-rich browns (dry leaves,
straw, shredded paper) decompose in 3-6 months during warm seasons. Compost tea,
brewed from finished compost, provides a liquid nutrient boost for heavy-feeding
crops like tomatoes and brassicas. The compost area should be screened by a hedge
or trellis to maintain visual appeal, as it is frequently visited.
## Perennial Vegetables for Zone 1
Zone 1 is ideal for planting perennial vegetables that benefit from frequent
attention and harvesting. Asparagus, rhubarb, globe artichokes, and sea kale are
planted once and produce for decades with proper care. Perennial onions (Egyptian
walking onions, potato onions) and perennial leeks provide alliums without annual
replanting. Sorrel, Good King Henry, and perennial kales supply early spring
greens before annual crops are ready. Walking through the garden daily makes it
easy to spot pest problems or water stress on these long-lived plants before they
become serious.
## Companion Planting Strategies
Companion planting reduces pest pressure and increases yields in the compact space
of Zone 1. Basil planted among tomatoes repels tomato hornworms and improves
flavor. Marigolds deter nematodes when interplanted with root crops. Nasturtiums
serve as a trap crop for aphids while their edible flowers and leaves add to
salads. Beans fix nitrogen that benefits neighboring heavy feeders like corn and
squash in a three sisters arrangement. Alliums (garlic, chives, onions) planted
around the perimeter of beds deter a range of insect pests. Aromatic herbs like
rosemary, sage, and thyme confuse pest insects searching for host plants by
masking crop scents.
## Water Harvesting at the Doorstep
Zone 1 is the logical place to capture and use water close to where it is needed
most. Rain barrels connected to downspouts provide irrigation water with no
pumping required. A small swale or rain garden along the edge of Zone 1 captures
runoff from paths and the house roof, filtering it through soil and plant roots
before it reaches drainage systems. Greywater from the kitchen sink can be
directed to a mulch basin planted with thirsty plants like bananas, comfrey, or
willow. Every drop of water harvested in Zone 1 reduces the need to carry water
from distant tanks or pumps.
## Integrated Pest Management
Zone 1's intensive planting requires vigilant pest management that works with
natural systems rather than against them. Attracting beneficial insects is the
first line of defense: umbelliferous flowers (dill, fennel, cilantro in bloom),
alyssum, and yarrow provide nectar and pollen for parasitic wasps, hoverflies,
and ladybugs. Ponds and water features support frogs and dragonflies that control
mosquito populations. Hand-picking large pests like caterpillars and snails is
practical at this small scale. Physical barriers — row covers, netting over
fruit, copper tape around raised beds — prevent damage without chemicals. Healthy
soil produces healthy plants that are naturally more resistant to pest and disease
pressure, reinforcing the importance of [[sepp-holzer-composting-and-humus-building]]
and mulching.
## Seasonal Planning for Zone 1
Effective Zone 1 design accounts for seasonal changes in sun angle, temperature,
and rainfall. Winter sun paths are lower, so deciduous shade trees and vines
allow light to reach beds that would be shaded in summer. Cold frames positioned
along south-facing walls extend the growing season for salads and herbs through
frost. A planting calendar specific to the local climate ensures continuous
harvest: early spring peas followed by summer beans, autumn brassicas, and
overwintering garlic. Succession planting — sowing small batches of lettuce,
radishes, and beans every two weeks — prevents gluts and ensures a steady supply.
Seed starting stations with grow lights or a small greenhouse in Zone 1 allow
transplants to be ready exactly when garden space opens up.
