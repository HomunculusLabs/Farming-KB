# Earth-Shaping and Mulch Application in the Tropics

## Overview

Earth-shaping (earthworks) in tropical environments serves dual purposes: managing the extreme water flows characteristic of heavy tropical rainfall, and creating optimal growing conditions for crops in soils that are often nutrient-poor and prone to degradation. Combined with comprehensive mulch strategies, earthworks form the physical foundation of productive tropical permaculture systems.

## Earth-Shaping for Different Slopes and Conditions

### Gentle Slopes (2–8°) in Wet-Dry Tropics

On gently sloping land, a series of large **contour banks or swales** has an excellent soil preservation effect. This system works best when combined with:

- **Gradual terrace development** — swales evolve into terraces over time as organic matter accumulates
- **Wet-season water retention** — swales capture and hold seasonal rainfall
- **Mulch-providing hedgerows** — planted on the swale banks to supply continuous mulch material

Between the main hedges, additional mulch hedgerows and borders can be developed in crop, or the terraces can be flooded seasonally for irrigated crops such as rice.

### Very Flat Sites (Less Than 4°)

On nearly level ground, two contrasting strategies apply depending on rainfall and drainage:

**For very wet areas (high rainfall, poor drainage):**
- **Raised mounds or ridges** drain crops by elevating root zones above the water table
- Essential for cassava, yams, and cucurbits in areas where drainage is problematic and rainfall is intense

**For drier areas (rainfall less than 76 cm / 30 inches):**
- **Pits** impound water for absorption, retaining both mulch and moisture
- Pits function similarly to desert water-harvesting techniques
- Useful in savannah areas during dry seasons

### Additional Earth-Shaping Techniques for Wet-Dry Tropics

- **Chisel plow and sod-seeding** — minimum tillage techniques that preserve soil structure
- **Low bunds for water retention** — small earthen banks across slopes
- **Soakage pits** — capture runoff for slow infiltration
- **Keyline techniques** — flood control using the keyline pattern of plowing and water distribution
- **Swales across slopes** — impound runoff for absorption into the soil profile

## The Swale-to-Ridge Succession System

A sophisticated design approach uses a graded series of swales progressing from valley to ridge:

1. **Wet zone swales** (lowest) — broad, water-retaining
2. **Windbreak swale** (or terrace mound in humid areas)
3. **Production swale** — planted with hardy trees (mango, olive, etc.)
4. **Ridge windbreak swale** — or interswale zone
5. **Road access** — with runoff directed to swales 4 and 6
6. **Onward production swales** — wind-tender trees (avocado, citrus)
7. **Interswale areas** — deep mulch and forage crops (*Dolichos*, pigeon pea, *Acacia leucarena*, lupin, coprosma, lucerne, tree lucerne)

This graduated system manages water from the wettest low zones through productive middle zones to the drier ridges, with each level supporting appropriate species.

## Mulch Application in Practice

### Initial Establishment

When first establishing home gardens, mulch should be applied thickly — 20–25 cm (8–10 inches) deep. This heavy initial application:

- Suppresses existing grasses and weeds
- Begins [[building-soil]] organic matter immediately
- Protects soil from rain splash erosion
- Reduces nutrient leaching
- Maintains soil moisture

### Ongoing Mulch Sources

Once the initial garden is established, mulch can be derived from:
- **Green herbage** from within the garden
- **Border plantings and windbreaks** — lemongrass, comfrey, and other barrier species
- **Tree legumes** — *Gliricidia*, *Acacia*, *Prosopis*, and other hedgerow species
- **Pioneer tree crops** — bananas, bamboo, and palms provide continuous mulch
- **Aquatic weeds** — collected from bunds, ponds, and waterways

### Coconut Husks and Fronds

Coconut husks serve multiple purposes:
- **Mulch for valued crops** such as vanilla orchids
- **High potash value** when shredded with palm fronds
- **Activated charcoal** — burn and steam to create filter charcoal
- **Solid fuel** — useful where other fuel is scarce
- **One drawback:** They hold small sections of water that can breed mosquitoes

Shredded bark and broken shells are ideal mulches for ginger, turmeric, and vines.

### Plastic Sheet Mulch

Plastic sheet mulch has good effects on row crops:
- Prevents rain splash and nutrient leaching
- Condenses groundwater at night
- Suppresses weed growth

However, it is not recommended for remote village situations because:
- It does not add humus to soils
- It does not improve [[cation-exchange-capacity]]
- It may release unwanted chemicals to the soil
- It represents an external input that cannot be produced on-site

### Fire Control and Mulch Management

In grassland areas that have replaced fallen forests, fire is a constant threat. Mulch management for fire control includes:

- **Raking under bamboo and clump canes** to remove accumulated dry material
- **Routing leaf mulch through animal bedding** or poultry strawyards before applying to gardens
- **Using branches of legumes and forage trees** on their path to the garden
- **Strip-mowing and mulching** while trees re-establish in rampant grasslands
- **Timing slashing carefully** — seed-free mulch (second-cut grass) is essential for garden use; seed-head mulch should not be placed where it might become a nuisance

## Building Productive Systems in Poor Tropical Soils

Growing in exhausted or poor tropical soils is possible but requires:

1. **Hard work** in the early rehabilitation phase
2. **Seed** for pioneer and crop species
3. **Essential fertiliser resources** for initial establishment
4. **A strategy of starting small and expanding at the periphery** — dense planting of nucleus areas plus mulch

The recommended pioneer assemblage includes:
- **Quick-growing tree Acacias** — for nitrogen fixation and wind protection
- **Bananas** — rapid biomass production and mulch generation
- **Legumes** such as lablab — nitrogen fixation and ground cover
- **Deep-rooting comfrey** — nutrient accumulation from subsoil
- **Bamboo and palms** — continuous mulch production and structural material

This pioneer assemblage provides continuous mulch for gardens, main crops, fruit trees, and valued plants as the system expands outward from its nucleus.

## Universal Principle

No crop or tree suited to a specific tropical locality has been found that does not grow, produce, and thrive in mulch. Similarly, no widespread pest has been found to grossly affect total polyculture yield. Ginger, taro, beans, bananas, palms, fruit trees, flowers, yams, sweet potato, melons, and many other crops have been trialled successfully in thick mulches of straw, fronds, nut husks, cardboard, and sawdust. A thick mulch almost totally eradicates kikuyu grass and other persistent grasses, making it the single most important tool for tropical garden establishment.
