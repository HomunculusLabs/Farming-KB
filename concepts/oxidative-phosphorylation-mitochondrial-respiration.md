Oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is the metabolic pathway in which cells produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through the coupled action of electron transport and ATP synthesis. It is the final stage of cellular respiration, occurring at the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) in eukaryotes and the cytoplasmic membrane in prokaryotes. OXPHOS is responsible for approximately 90% of cellular ATP under aerobic conditions, making it the dominant energy-producing pathway in most multicellular organisms. The pathway couples two processes: electron transfer through the electron transport chain (ETC), which oxidizes NADH and FADH₂ while reducing molecular oxygen to water, and ATP synthesis by ATP synthase, which harnesses the resulting proton electrochemical gradient. Peter Mitchell proposed the chemiosmotic coupling mechanism in 1961, earning the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

## Electron Transport Chain Complexes

### Complex I — NADH:Ubiquinone Oxidoreductase (EC 1.6.5.3)

Complex I oxidizes NADH to NAD⁺ and transfers electrons to ubiquinone (CoQ10), pumping 4 protons from matrix to intermembrane space per electron pair. The mammalian complex contains 44 subunits (7 mtDNA-encoded, 37 nuclear-encoded) with a mass of ~970 kDa in an L-shaped structure. Cofactors include FMN and 8–9 iron-sulfur clusters. Electron flow: NADH → FMN → Fe-S clusters → ubiquinone. Complex I is the largest ETC complex, and mutations in its subunits are the most common cause of mitochondrial disease.

### Complex II — Succinate Dehydrogenase (EC 1.3.5.1)

Complex II is the only ETC complex that does not pump protons. It oxidizes succinate to fumarate (TCA cycle) and transfers electrons via FAD and three iron-sulfur clusters ([2Fe-2S], [4Fe-4S], [3Fe-4S]) to ubiquinone. With 4 subunits (SDHA–D) and ~140 kDa mass, it is the smallest ETC complex, serving a dual role as both a TCA cycle enzyme and an ETC component.

### Complex III — Cytochrome bc₁ Complex (EC 1.10.2.2)

Complex III transfers electrons from ubiquinol to cytochrome c via the Q cycle, pumping 4 protons per electron pair (2 from QH₂ oxidation + 2 translocated). The mammalian complex has 11 subunits (1 mtDNA-encoded: cytochrome b) and ~248 kDa mass. Cofactors include two cytochrome b hemes (bL, bH), cytochrome c₁ heme, and the Rieske [2Fe-2S] protein. The Q cycle doubles proton yield by recycling a ubiquinone semiquinone intermediate within the complex.

### Complex IV — Cytochrome c Oxidase (EC 7.1.1.9)

Complex IV is the terminal oxidase, transferring electrons from cytochrome c to O₂, reducing it to H₂O. It pumps 2 protons and consumes 2 matrix protons per electron pair. The mammalian complex has 13 subunits (3 mtDNA-encoded: COX1–3) and ~204 kDa mass. Cofactors include heme a, heme a₃, and copper centers (Cu_A, Cu_B) forming a binuclear center. Cyanide and carbon monoxide bind this center with extreme affinity, explaining their acute toxicity.

### Complex V — ATP Synthase (F₁F₀-ATPase, EC 7.1.2.2)

ATP synthase harnesses the proton gradient to synthesize ATP from ADP + Pi. The mammalian enzyme has 16 subunits (2 mtDNA-encoded: ATP6, ATP8) and ~600 kDa mass. The F₀ sector (membrane-embedded) contains the c-ring (8 subunits in mammals) and a subunit; the F₁ sector (α₃β₃γδε) contains catalytic sites. Proton flow through the c-ring drives rotation of the central stalk (γε), inducing conformational changes in F₁ β subunits that synthesize ATP. The c-ring stoichiometry varies across species (8 mammals, 10 yeast, 14 some bacteria), directly affecting H⁺/ATP ratio.

## Electron Flow and Mobile Carriers

NADH enters at Complex I; FADH₂ enters at Complex II (from succinate), ETF-ubiquinone oxidoreductase (from β-oxidation), or glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase. Both pathways converge at ubiquinone (CoQ10), a lipid-soluble mobile carrier diffusing within the IMM. Electrons then flow: Complex III → cytochrome c (12 kDa water-soluble hemoprotein in the intermembrane space) → Complex IV → O₂.

## Proton Motive Force and ATP Yield

The proton motive force (PMF) has two components: Δψ (~150–180 mV, inside negative) and ΔpH (~0.5–1.0 unit, matrix alkaline). Total proton translocation: 10 H⁺/NADH (4+4+2) and 6 H⁺/FADH₂ (0+4+2). With an 8-subunit c-ring, ~3–4 H⁺ are needed per ATP, yielding P/O ratios of ~2.5 ATP/NADH and ~1.5 ATP/FADH₂. Complete glucose oxidation yields ~30–32 ATP. Cytosolic NADH yield varies by shuttle: malate-aspartate yields ~2.5 ATP/NADH, glycerol-3-phosphate yields ~1.5 ATP/NADH.

## Chemiosmotic Theory and Binding Change Mechanism

Paul Boyer's binding change mechanism (1997 Nobel Prize, shared with John Walker) describes ATP synthase: the three F₁ β subunits cycle through Open (releases ATP), Loose (binds ADP + Pi), and Tight (catalyzes ATP) conformations with each 120° γ rotation. Each 360° rotation produces 3 ATP. Rotational catalysis was directly visualized by Yoshida and Kinosita (1997) using fluorescent actin filaments attached to the γ subunit.

## Reactive Oxygen Species

The ETC is the major cellular superoxide (O₂•⁻) source. Complex I produces it on the matrix side when the ubiquinone site is fully reduced (high Δψ). Complex III produces it at the Qo site, released to both sides. Superoxide dismutase (SOD1/SOD2) converts it to H₂O₂, which can form hydroxyl radical via Fenton chemistry. ROS contribute to aging (mitochondrial free radical theory, Harman 1956), neurodegeneration, cardiovascular disease, and cancer, but low-level ROS also serve as signaling molecules (redox signaling, HIF-1α stabilization, immune defense).

## Uncoupling and Regulation

ADP availability governs respiratory control: high ADP → State 3 (active); low ADP → State 4 (resting). Uncoupling proteins dissipate the PMF without ATP synthesis: UCP1 (thermogenin) in brown fat generates heat, activated by fatty acids, inhibited by GDP; UCP2 regulates ROS; UCP3 modulates fatty acid metabolism. Chemical uncouplers (2,4-DNP, FCCP) collapse the PMF by proton shuttling. DNP was used as a weight-loss drug but caused fatal hyperthermia.

## ETC Inhibitors

Rotenone and piericidin A inhibit Complex I; malonate inhibits Complex II; antimycin A (Qi site) and myxothiazol (Qo site) inhibit Complex III; cyanide, carbon monoxide, and azide inhibit Complex IV; oligomycin blocks Complex V F₀. These inhibitors have been essential for elucidating ETC function.
Rotenone is used as a pesticide and has been linked to Parkinson's-like
neurodegeneration in animal models. Antimycin A-induced superoxide production
at Complex III is a commonly used experimental model for oxidative stress.
Oligomycin is widely used to distinguish between mitochondrial and
non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption in respirometry experiments.

## Mitochondrial Disorders

OXPHOS defects affect ~1 in 5,000 people. Key disorders: Leigh syndrome (most common pediatric OXPHOS disorder, usually Complex I, brainstem/basal ganglia lesions, lactic acidosis), MELAS (m.3243A>G, stroke-like episodes, seizures), MERRF (m.8344A>G, myoclonic epilepsy, ataxia), LHON (Complex I, acute bilateral vision loss in young males), NARP (ATP6 mutation m.8993T>G/C, neuropathy, ataxia). Maternal mtDNA inheritance produces complex pedigree patterns.
Heteroplasmy — the coexistence of wild-type and mutant mtDNA within a cell —
is a unique feature of mitochondrial genetics. The phenotypic threshold depends
on the proportion of mutant mtDNA, and this ratio can vary between tissues
and change over time, contributing to the variable expressivity and delayed
onset characteristic of many mitochondrial disorders. MELAS, MERRF, and LHON
all exhibit heteroplasmy-dependent penetrance.

## Comparative Biology

Bacterial ETCs are simpler (14 subunits in E. coli Complex I vs. 44 mammals) with branched chains using alternative donors (H₂, Fe²⁺) and acceptors (NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, Fe³⁺, CO₂). Bacterial c-rings vary (10 in E. coli, 14 in Bacillus). Mitochondria evolved from an α-proteobacterial endosymbiont ~1.5–2 billion years ago. Anaerobic alternatives include fermentation (2 ATP/glucose), anaerobic respiration (NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, CO₂ acceptors), and photophosphorylation (light-driven PMF, analogous to OXPHOS).

## Supercomplex Organization

ETC complexes do not exist as isolated entities but assemble into higher-order
structures called respiratory supercomplexes or respirasomes. The most studied
is the I+III₂+IV supercomplex (respirasome), in which Complex I, a Complex
III dimer, and Complex IV are physically associated. These supercomplexes are
stabilized by cardiolipin, a mitochondria-specific phospholipid, and may
enhance electron transfer efficiency by substrate channeling between complexes.
Supercomplex composition varies with tissue type, metabolic state, and
pathological conditions. Disruption of supercomplex organization is observed in
Barth syndrome (tafazzin deficiency affecting cardiolipin remodeling) and
various neurodegenerative diseases.

## Mitochondrial DNA and Biogenesis

Mitochondria contain their own genome (mtDNA), a 16.6 kb circular DNA molecule
encoding 13 ETC subunits (7 in Complex I, 1 in Complex III, 3 in Complex IV,
2 in Complex V), 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs. All 13 proteins are hydrophobic
membrane components. The remaining ~80 nuclear-encoded ETC subunits are
imported from the cytosol. Mitochondrial biogenesis is regulated by PGC-1α
(peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha), the
master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis that coactivates NRF1 and NRF2
transcription factors, which in turn activate expression of nuclear-encoded
ETC subunits and mtDNA replication machinery (POLG, TWNK).

## Metabolic Integration

OXPHOS is tightly integrated with other metabolic pathways. The TCA cycle
provides NADH and FADH₂; fatty acid β-oxidation generates acetyl-CoA and
FADH₂; amino acid catabolism feeds into the TCA cycle at multiple points.
The malate-aspartate shuttle and glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle transfer reducing
equivalents from cytosolic NADH into mitochondria. The ATP/ADP translocase
(ANT) exchanges matrix ATP for cytosolic ADP, while the phosphate carrier
imports Pi in symport with H⁺. These transporters are essential for maintaining
the adenine nucleotide pool across the IMM.
