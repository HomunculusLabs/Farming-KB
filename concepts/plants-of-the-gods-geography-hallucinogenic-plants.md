# Geography of Hallucinogenic Plant Use

## Overview

*Plants of the Gods* by Schultes, Hofmann, and Ratsch provides a
comprehensive geographical survey of hallucinogenic plant use across
the globe. The distribution is strikingly uneven, with the New World
dominating in both number of species and depth of cultural
integration, while the Old World has fewer species but includes some
of the most ancient and widespread psychoactive plants.

## Africa

Despite its size and extremely varied vegetation, Africa appears poor
in hallucinogenic plants. The most famous is [[iboga-and-the-bwiti-cult-initiation]], a root of the Dogbane family
(Apocynaceae) employed in Gabon and parts of the Congo in the Bwiti
cult. The Bushmen of Botswana slice the bulb of Kwashi (Amaryllidaceae)
and rub it over scarifications on the head, allowing active principles
to enter the bloodstream. Kanna (Sceletium) was used by the Hottentots
of South Africa, inducing gaiety, laughter, and visions. In scattered
regions, relatives of Thorn Apple and Henbane were used for
intoxicating properties.

## Eurasia

Eurasia is home to many plants employed for hallucinatory effects. The
most significant is [[plants-of-the-gods-cannabis-ancient-cultures]], today the most widespread of all
narcotics. The most spectacular Eurasiatic hallucinogen is the
[[plants-of-the-gods-amanita-muscaria]], consumed by
scattered tribesmen in Siberia and possibly the sacred Soma of ancient
India. Datura was employed over wide areas of Asia. In Southeast Asia
and Papua New Guinea, sundry poorly understood hallucinogens are used.
Nutmeg may once have been taken in India and Indonesia for narcotic
effects.

## Europe

The heyday of hallucinogen use in Europe occurred in ancient times,
when they were used almost exclusively in witchcraft and divination.
The major plants involved — Thorn Apple, [[plants-of-the-gods-nightshade-hexing-herbs]] — belong to the Nightshade family. The fungus
ergot-fungus|Ergot, a parasite on rye, frequently poisoned entire
regions, causing hundreds to suffer hallucinations, permanent insanity,
gangrene, or death in the plague known as St. Anthony's Fire. Ergot was
never purposefully used as a hallucinogen in medieval Europe, but
suggestions link it to the Eleusinian Mysteries of ancient Greece.

## North America

North America (north of Mexico) is quite poor in hallucinogens. Various
Datura species were employed rather widely, but most intensely in the
Southwest. The Red Bean or Mescal Bean (Sophora secundiflora) was the
basis of a vision-seeking ceremony in Texas. In northern Canada,
Indians chewed the roots of Sweet Flag (Acorus calamus) for
hallucinogenic effects.

## Mexico

Mexico represents without doubt the world's richest area in diversity
and use of hallucinogens in aboriginal societies. The Peyote cactus
is the most important sacred hallucinogen, though other cactus species
serve as minor hallucinogens. Of almost equal importance are mushrooms,
known to the Aztecs as Teonanacatl ("divine flesh"), with at least
twenty-four species employed today. Ololiuqui (Morning Glory seeds)
represents another hallucinogen of great Aztec importance. Secondary
hallucinogens include Toloache (Datura), Mescal Bean, Pipiltzintzintli,
diviner's sage (Salvia divinorum), Piule, Sinicuichi, and many others.

## South America

South America ranks second to Mexico in number and magico-religious
significance of hallucinogens. The Andean cultures had half a dozen
species of [[plants-of-the-gods-brugmansia-angels-trumpet]] (Borrachero, Floripondio, Toe). In Peru
and Bolivia, the San Pedro cactus is the basis of cimora, used in
vision-seeking ceremonies. In the Orinoco and Amazon, powerful snuffs
called Yopo and [[plants-of-the-gods-virola-epena-snuff]] are made from Anadenanthera and Virola
trees. The [[plants-of-the-gods-virola-epena-snuff]] drink represents perhaps the most
complex pharmacological preparation known, combining MAO inhibitors
with DMT-containing plants.

## The New World Dominance

The book notes that notwithstanding the greater age of cultures and
widespread use of hallucinogens in the Eastern Hemisphere, the number
of species so used is greater in the Western Hemisphere. Anthropologists
have explained this disparity on cultural grounds, though there does not
appear to be a significant difference between hemispheres in the total
number of plants possessing hallucinogenic principles.

## West Indies

The Caribbean islands had some hallucinogenic species, primarily the
snuff known as Cohoba. The early indigenous populations used Cohoba
mainly, and it is believed that this custom was imported by Indians
invading the Caribbean Islands from the Orinoco regions of South
America. This represents the northernmost extension of the South
American snuff-taking complex centered on Anadenanthera and Virola.

## Patterns of Use

The book identifies several cross-cultural patterns in the geography
of hallucinogenic plant use. In almost all instances, in both the Old
and New World, use is restricted to adult males, though striking
exceptions exist: among the Koryak of Siberia, Amanita may be used
by both sexes; in southern Mexico, sacred mushrooms are taken by
both men and women (the shaman is usually a woman); and in Africa,
Iboga may be taken by any adult. The exclusion of women from some
practices may relate to the abortifacient potential of many
hallucinogenic plants.
