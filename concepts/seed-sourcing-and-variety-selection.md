- The term "heirloom" generally refers to varieties that have been in circulation for at least 50 years and are often associated with particular regions or cultural traditions.

**Hybrid (F1) Varieties:**

- Created by crossing two genetically distinct inbred parent lines.
- Exhibit "hybrid vigor" (heterosis), often producing more uniform, vigorous, and higher-yielding plants than either parent.
- Seeds saved from hybrid plants will not breed true; subsequent generations segregate unpredictably.
- Hybrids are often bred for specific commercial traits: uniform ripening, shipping tolerance, shelf life, and disease resistance.
- Some hybrids may be less flavorful or nutritious than heirloom counterparts, as commercial breeding has prioritized yield and appearance over eating quality.
- Hybrids must be repurchased each season, creating ongoing seed costs and supply chain dependence.

**Solomon's recommendation:**

For the low-input gardener, Solomon generally recommends heirloom and open-pollinated varieties because they allow seed saving and have been selected under conditions more similar to home garden growing. However, he acknowledges that some hybrids offer genuine advantages, particularly in disease resistance, and recommends careful selection rather than dogmatic avoidance.

**Types of seed suppliers:**

- **Large commercial seed companies:** Offer wide selections and competitive prices but may focus on commercially-oriented varieties.
- **Regional seed companies:** Often carry varieties specifically adapted to local growing conditions.
- **Specialty heirloom seed companies:** Focus on rare, historic, and open-pollinated varieties.
