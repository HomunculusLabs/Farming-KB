## What a Plant Smells: Volatile Organic Compound Detection

Plants are exquisitely sensitive to volatile [[bloomfield-siren-songs-fungal-chemical-communication-reproduction-chapter-7]]. The plant effectively "calls for
help" using volatile signals, requiring perception of both the initial
herbivore damage (through mechanoreception and detection of herbivore-specific
compounds in the oral secretions) and the subsequent emission of specific
wasp-attractant compounds.

Fruit ripening provides another example of plant chemical communication.
Ripening fruits release ethylene gas, a gaseous plant hormone that triggers
ripening in nearby fruits. This positive feedback loop explains why placing a
ripe banana next to unripe avocados accelerates their ripening — the banana's
ethylene production is detected by the avocados, which respond by accelerating
their own ripening process. Commercial fruit shippers exploit this phenomenon,
using ethylene gas to synchronize ripening in shipments of climacteric fruits.

## What a Plant Feels: Mechanoreception in Plants

Plants are highly responsive to mechanical stimulation. The most dramatic
example is the Venus flytrap (*Dionaea muscipula*), whose trap leaves snap
shut in approximately 100 milliseconds when trigger hairs on the inner surface
are touched twice within about 20 seconds. The two-trigger-hair requirement
within a specific time window prevents false closures triggered by raindrops
or wind-blown debris. This rapid response is mediated by changes in cell
[[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] driven by rapid ion fluxes across cell membranes, not by
muscle-like contractile proteins — a fundamentally plant-specific mechanism.

The sensitive plant (*Mimosa pudica*) exhibits thigmonasty — rapid leaf
folding when touched — through a similar turgor-based mechanism in specialized
structures called pulvini at the base of each leaflet and leaf. Less
dramatically but more universally, most plants exhibit thigmomorphogenesis:
altered growth patterns in response to chronic mechanical stimulation such as
wind, rain, or physical contact. Plants grown in windy environments develop
shorter, thicker stems with smaller leaves and more extensive root systems
compared to those grown in still air. This response, mediated by calcium
signaling cascades and touch-responsive gene expression, explains why
greenhouse-grown seedlings must be gradually hardened off before outdoor
planting — they lack the structural reinforcements that wind stimulation would
have produced during their early growth.

Roots are particularly mechanosensitive, using touch to navigate around
obstacles in the soil. Root tips detect physical barriers and redirect growth,
a process involving rapid calcium waves, changes in reactive oxygen species
production, and alterations in auxin distribution that modify the pattern of
cell expansion in the root elongation zone.
