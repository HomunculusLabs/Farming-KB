## Overview

Mycorrhizal mushrooms form mutually beneficial relationships with the roots of trees and other plants, creating vast underground networks that connect individual trees and even different species across entire forest ecosystems. [[mycopesticides-stamets]]' "Mycelium Running" documents the remarkable biology of these partnerships and their implications for forest ecology and [[mycoforestry-principles-stamets]].

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

### Ectomycorrhizal Mushrooms

Ectomycorrhizal fungi form exterior sheaths around the roots of partner plants:
- The mycelium grows beyond the plant's roots, dramatically extending the absorption zone
- Surface area of [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] may be **10 to 100 times greater** than the surface area of leaves in a forest
- The mycelium brings distant nutrients and moisture to the host plant
- The plant provides sugars (mostly hexoses) that the fungi convert to mannitols, arabitols, and erythritols
- Major ectomycorrhizal mushrooms include matsutake, porcini ([[boletus-edulis-entity]]), chanterelles, and truffles

### Endomycorrhizal (Arbuscular) Associations

Endomycorrhizal fungi invade the interior root cells of host plants:
- Formerly called vesicular arbuscular mycorrhizae (VAM)
- Form arbuscules (tree-like branching structures) inside root cells for nutrient exchange
- Associate with grasses and many other plants in addition to trees
- Western red cedar (Thuja plicata) is an important tree host for arbuscular mycorrhizae

## The Simard Experiment: Interspecies Nutrient Transfer

One of the most significant experiments in forest ecology demonstrated that mycorrhizal fungi can transport nutrients between different tree species. The study (Simard et al., 1997) involved three tree species:

1. **Douglas fir** (Pseudotsuga menziesii) — ectomycorrhizal
2. **Paper birch** (Betula papyrifera) — ectomycorrhizal (same mycorrhizal species as Douglas fir)
3. **Western red cedar** (Thuja plicata) — endomycorrhizal (VAM)

### Method and Results

Researchers covered the Douglas fir to simulate deep shade, reducing its ability to photosynthesize sugars. In response:
- The mycorrhizae channeled sugars from the root zone of the birch to the root zone of the fir
- More than **9% of the net carbon** compounds transferred to the fir originated from the birch's roots
- The cedar received only a small fraction of the transferred nutrients
- The amount of sugar transferred was directly proportional to the amount of shading

### Significance

This experiment demonstrated that:
- A common mycelial net can unite multiple species of trees
- Mycorrhizal fungi can keep diverse species of trees fed, particularly younger trees struggling for sunlight
- The mycelium acts as a nutrient "budgeting" system, multidirectionally allocating resources
- A single mycorrhizal mushroom nutritionally supported both a conifer and a deciduous tree
- The mycelium "guards the forest's overall health"

This explains how saplings survive in the shadows of elder trees that block essential light.

## The Candystick Plant and Matsutake

A remarkable example of the complexity of mycorrhizal partnerships involves the candystick plant (Allotropa virguta):
- The candystick is a non-chlorophyll-producing plant — it cannot photosynthesize
- It gains virtually all its sugars from the matsutake (Tricholoma magnivelare) mycelium
- The matsutake in turn associates with western hemlock and/or Sitka spruce
- This creates a three-way partnership where the tree feeds the fungus, and the fungus feeds both the tree and the non-photosynthetic plant

One mycologist speculates that the spot fruitings of matsutake on a slope of Oregon's Mount Hood may be from a vast interconnected mycelial colony extending over thousands of acres.

## Forest Biomass

### Mycelial Contribution

The scale of mycelial biomass in forests is enormous:
- A large portion — **one-tenth to one-fifth** — of the total biomass in the topsoil of a healthy Douglas fir forest may be mycelium
- Even more biomass exists if endomycorrhizae and ectomycorrhizae thriving in the canopy are included
- Stamets concludes: "I doubt a forest can be defined without its fungi"

### Bigleaf Maple Aerial Roots

Research by Cobb et al. (2001) showed that bigleaf maples (Acer macrophyllum) in Pacific Northwest rain forests:
- Project vinelike aerial roots that ascend to the canopy
- The biomass of these aerial roots is similar to the biomass of subterranean roots
- A complex habitat has evolved on these aerial roots, including mosses and licorice ferns
- The roots are teamed with mycorrhizae, creating a "biosphere high above the forest floor"

## Matsutake: Ecology and Challenges

### Cultural and Economic Importance

Matsutake (Tricholoma magnivelare in North America, T. matsutake in Japan) are among the most valued wild mushrooms:
- In Japan, matsutake can sell for hundreds of dollars per pound
- North American matsutake harvesting supports significant commercial activity
- The mushroom has deep cultural significance in Japanese cuisine and tradition

### Ecological Requirements

Matsutake are mycorrhizal with specific tree species:
- In North America: primarily western hemlock and Sitka spruce
- In Japan: primarily red pine (Pinus densiflora)
- They require old-growth or mature forest conditions
- They are sensitive to forest disturbance and management practices

### Cultivation Challenges

Growing mycorrhizal mushrooms has proved extremely challenging due to complex interdependencies:
- Once mycelium has been established, decades may pass before a single mushroom forms
- Nuances of climate, soil chemistry, and predominant microflora limit success
- The challenge is to "tilt the balance so that a species of our choice can take up residence"
- Species native to a region are more likely than imported species to adapt successfully

## Truffle Cultivation

### European Truffle Orchards

The renowned Perigord black truffle (Tuber melanosporum) is mycorrhizal, growing in association with filberts and oak trees:
- Fetches up to **$500 per pound**
- European truffle orchards in France, Spain, and Italy are well established
- Commercial companies market truffle-inoculated trees

### American Truffle Efforts

Many American growers have tried to cultivate European truffles:
- Dozens of growers have planted truffle-inoculated trees on American oaks or filberts
- Calcareous soils in Texas, Washington, and Oregon have been suggested as ideal sites
- Only a few successes — one from North Carolina and one from Northern California — have produced European truffles after more than a decade of effort
- One company reported waiting **20 years** until the first truffles could be harvested

### Why Truffles Fail Outside Europe

The primary obstacle is competition from native mycorrhizal fungi:
- Native species of mycorrhizae quickly outcompete foreign European truffles
