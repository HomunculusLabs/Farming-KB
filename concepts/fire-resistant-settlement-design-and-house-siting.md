## Fire-Resistant Settlement Design and House Siting

The placement and design of settlements in fire-prone landscapes is one of the most critical decisions a land designer can make. Poor siting can transform a [[wildfire]] from a manageable event into a catastrophe, while intelligent design can mean the difference between survival and total loss. This concept covers the principles of safe house siting, radiation shielding, fuel reduction zones, and practical strategies drawn from both design theory and real-world fire survival.

## House Siting Principles

### Sites of Maximum Danger
Houses are at greatest risk from upslope fire. The most dangerous locations include:
- **Sharp ridgetops** where fire funnels over the crest
- **Hill saddles** with diverging ridgelines creating a wind (fire) funnel effect
- **Driveways lined with inflammable trees** (eucalypts) or grasses (pampas grass), which create blowtorch-like funnel effects toward the house

### Sites of Maximum Safety
The safest house sites follow a clear hierarchy:
1. **Damp valley mouths** where moist air and natural water reduce fire intensity
2. **Well-tended built-up areas** where fuel reduction is maintained
3. **Farms with flood-flow or Keyline irrigation** fitted to sheet water over slopes
4. **Irrigated areas** where constant moisture prevents fuel drying
5. **Peninsulas in dams and lakes** surrounded by water on multiple sides
6. **Downslope plateaus** where the house can be nested into the hillside

### The Plateau House
A house nested into a shelf on a hillside offers multiple advantages:
- Protection from radiant heat on the exposed face
- No open underfloor area (the site is excavated rather than raised on stilts)
- The ability to add a rimwall, pond, or earthbank at the plateau edge as additional protection
- Easy incorporation of a cave or dugout refuge behind the shelter of the house itself

### Fire Refuges
Every house in a fire-prone area should incorporate a dedicated fire refuge:
- Fully earthed over with a wet blanket door
- A dogleg or curved entrance to escape direct radiation
- A small permanent water tank (approximately 270 liters) incorporated within
- A supply of old blankets and a bucket or two of water

These refuges provide absolute "fire insurance" for people caught at home—typically women and young children who may not be able to evacuate quickly.

## The 30-Meter Fuel Reduction Zone

Around every house and building site, it is essential to reduce forest and grass fuel to a distance of at least 30 meters (100 feet). This does not require tree removal but rather strategic species selection:

### Recommended Fire-Safe Trees
- **Coprosma** species
- **Deciduous fruit trees**
- **Figs**
- **Willows**
- **Poplars**

### Species to Avoid Near Houses
- Olives
- Pines
- Eucalypts

### Recommended Ground Cover and Underplanting
- **Lily clumps**: [[agapanthus]], spring bulbs, arum, Canna
- **"Summer-green" ground covers**: [[comfrey]], iceplants, [[tradescantia]], Impatiens
- **Short grass sward**

These plantings reduce both flame height and radiant heat effects, creating a survivable zone around structures.

## Radiation Shields

Radiant heat—not direct flame contact—is the primary killer of both plants and animals in wildfire. Most human fire casualties are not initially burnt but are either smothered by toxic smoke and fumes (from burning furniture or plastics) or killed by radiation alone.

### Types of Radiation Shields
- Stone walls (white-painted brick is ideal)
- Thick tree trunks
- Hollows or caves
- Hedgerows
- Car bodies
- The house itself

### The Fire Shadow Effect
A fire shadow extends to approximately 4–5 times the height and width of the solid radiation shield. This means:
- Shields of trees or walls should extend well past the house they are protecting
- A tall stone wall creates a much larger safe zone than a low hedge
- Multiple shields in sequence create compound protection

### Building Materials and Radiation
- **White paint** on houses reduces radiation absorption
- **Wooden panelling** transmits relatively little heat
- **Stone, brick, and mud** can convey heat indoors unless of sufficient thickness to absorb and dissipate it
- **Fire-proof insulation** (mineral wool, seagrass, sawdust, feathers, wool) keeps interiors cool and assists fire control
- **Thatch and shingle roofs** must be replaced by tile or metal cladding in fire-prone areas

## The Sacrificial Hedge: A Proven Strategy

A documented survival strategy from the 1967 Hobart [[firestorm]] demonstrates the effectiveness of a sacrificial fire barrier:

### What Was Done
- All lower limbs, loose bark, twigs, fallen leaves, dry brush, dry grass, and dead stumps were removed annually from around the house
- Removed material was chopped and stoneweighted into hollows and swales, or burnt as cooking fuel
- Tall tree stems were retained, providing wind protection; they regenerated after the fire
- A downslope line of willows and fern-leaved (not hard-leaved) Acacia was planted as a sacrificial hedge

### How It Worked
- The sacrificial hedge was fire-killed but rejuvenated from the roots when cut down
- It dampened out the ground fire
- Willow leaves did not combust but shrivelled and released steamy ash
- The house was blistered and zinc roof coating flaked, but close-fitted boards were unburnt
- White-painted walls and fitted screens provided additional protection
- Ample bucket water was stored both indoors and outdoors for spot fires

### The Outcome
- In that street alone, 70 houses burnt to the ground
- In the area, 1,100 houses were destroyed and 90 people killed
- The house survived with minimal damage (sore eyes and short beards were the only injuries)

## Fuel Reduction Methods

### Safe Methods
1. **Grazing off** excess vegetation with livestock
2. **Slashing** tall grasses and brush
3. **Composting in swales** to convert fuel to soil-building material
4. **Using as firewood** for heating and cooking
5. **Replacing tinder** with sappy green plants

### Unsafe Methods
"Cool burning" is identified as the most unsafe fuel reduction method, as it is difficult to control and often causes the very fires it was meant to prevent.

## The Ida and Jean Pain System

The Pain system (documented in *Another Kind of Garden*, 1982) represents a comprehensive, broadscale approach to fire reduction:
1. **Chip all dry forest fuels** mechanically
2. **Compost** the chipped material or use it for **biogas production**
3. The biogas **fuels the chipping and carting operations**, making the system self-sustaining
4. **Compost and sludge** are then used to grow gardens, improve soils, and further reduce litter

This system is recommended as essential for any bioregion where forests are to be preserved and eventually made fireproof.

## Design Recommendations for Developers

In scattered suburban developments in fire-prone areas, the following should be compulsory:
- Houses built to fire specifications with large roof tanks and ponds
- Fire-damping dams able to operate by radio control to sheet water over slopes on a Keyline principle
- The goal: restrict fire to remote dry-ridge forests and lightning strikes

## Personal Survival During Wildfire

For individuals caught in wildfire:
- Cover the body with **wool or cotton** clothing
- Shelter behind a tree, car, house, or in a trench
- If possible, cape the whole body in a **wet blanket**
- Wait until flames have passed, then move cautiously onto burnt ground
