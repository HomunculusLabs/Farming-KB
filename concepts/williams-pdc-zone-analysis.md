# Permaculture Zone Analysis

[[query-what-are-permaculture-zones-and-how-do-i-use-them]] are a graphic analytical tool used to understand and organize
the relative intensity of human impact across a site, guiding the placement of
elements based on how frequently they need human attention. The zone system was
formalized by Bill Mollison in the Permaculture: A Designers' Manual and remains one of the foundational tools in any
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] process.

## What Are Permaculture Zones

A Permaculture zone is not to be confused with zoning used in modern urban
planning, such as residential or commercial zoning. In Permaculture Design,
zones are a graphic tool drawn on paper that help one understand the impacts on
a site and aid in the design process. Being a graphic tool means zones are
something you draw on paper; they do not really exist out on the ground per se.

Permaculture zones are ranked from 0 to 5, with each rank denoting the amount
of human impact on an area. Zones are usually used in planning gardens and
crops, but they have many more implications than just gardening. Understanding
zones is important to designing the relative location of elements in a
Permaculture Design.

## Common Misconceptions

When being described for students and beginners zones are often represented as
concentric circles. However, this is just for demonstration and explanation
purposes only. In reality zones will almost never be concentric circles. They
can be any shape in a design but will usually be big oblong, or globular areas.

Zones are a rough estimate of impacted areas, not the literal truth. While zones
will be represented by lines on a piece of paper, in reality they will not have
sharp lines of delineation. Be very careful when making zones in an urban
environment. Many neophyte designers believe that every zone analysis has to have
all five zones, but that is not true. In dense urban environments your site may
only have a Zone 0 and a Zone 1.

## The Six Zones

**Zone 0** is the zone for permanent built structures, usually the house or
other buildings. This zone is considered to be permanently impacted because the
building will never move, and so is often considered ecologically inert. However
with systems such as [[query-how-do-green-roofs-and-living-walls-benefit-urban-environments]] and living walls some of the ecological activity
can be remediated by a skillful designer.

**Zone 1** is the area impacted by humans on a daily basis, directly around the
Zone 0 and around any other place where humans come and go. Designers will
usually put gardens in Zone 1, anticipating that humans will be coming and going
to the garden to pick things and tend to it.

**Zone 2** is for systems that require or will attract human impact only a few
times a week at most. The systems should be fairly self supporting, requiring
only occasional human intervention. Examples include beehives, compost piles,
food producing bushes, or pump houses for water systems.

**Zone 3** is impacted roughly monthly or seasonally. Zone 3 might be a good
place for orchards, crops that will be seasonally harvested, ceremonial sweat
lodges, or similar elements that need periodic attention.

**Zone 4** is where human impact is present but minimal. It may have light
impact such as bird watching, camping, hiking, or wildcrafting, and some
sustainable [[mycelium-running-forestry-practices-and-mushrooms]] such as thinning and coppicing. Zone 4 has no
human built structures or systems.

**Zone 5** is true wilderness, with no structures, no roads, no human-impacted
forests. For the most part you cannot have wilderness within urbanized areas.
Even a large untamed patch of woods surrounded by urban development would count
as Zone 4, not Zone 5.

## Relationship to Sector Analysis

Zones work in tandem with [[zone-and-sector-analysis|sector analysis]], which
maps the energies and influences arriving at a site from outside -- sun paths,
prevailing winds, water flow, fire risk, noise, and views. Where zones organize
elements by intensity of human use, sectors organize them by external natural
forces. A well-designed system places elements where their zone requirements and
sector exposures align optimally.

## Zones as Analytical and Design Tools

The designer has two options: place appropriate systems within the existing
zones, or decide that the site may need some serious zoning overhaul and
redesign the zones. You should create an overlay layer on your base map for the
zone analysis. The zone analysis is then used in conjunction with the sector
analysis, soil data, and other site information to determine the optimal
placement of all design elements.

## Zones in Different Contexts

The zone system adapts to different scales and contexts. A rural homestead might
use all six zones across a large property, while an apartment dweller may only
have Zone 0. The key is not forcing every site into all six zones but honestly
assessing the actual patterns of human use and impact that exist on the site.

## Integration with Permaculture Principles

The zone concept embodies the permaculture principle of "relative location" --
placing elements so that they support one another through proximity. Elements
that need frequent attention belong close to the center of human activity.
Elements that are largely self-managing can be placed further away. This
principle, when combined with thoughtful
[[gaias-garden-many-gardens-garden-design-process|garden design]], creates
efficient systems that minimize wasted energy and maximize productive output.
