# Permaculture Zone Analysis

Permaculture zones are a graphic analytical tool used to
understand and organize the relative intensity of human
impact across a site, guiding the placement of elements
based on how frequently they need human attention.

## What Are Permaculture Zones

A Permaculture zone is not to be confused with zoning used
in modern urban planning, such as residential or commercial
zoning. In Permaculture Design, zones are a graphic tool
drawn on paper that help one understand the impacts on a
site and aid in the design process. Being a graphic tool
means zones are something you draw on paper; they do not
really exist out on the ground per se. By drawing zones
onto a base map or design it helps the designer to
understand the site.

Permaculture zones are ranked from 0 to 5, with each rank
denoting the amount of human impact on an area. Zones are
usually used in planning gardens and crops, but they have
many more implications than just gardening. Understanding
zones is important to designing the relative location of
elements in a Permaculture Design.

## Common Misconceptions

When being described for students and beginners zones are
often represented as concentric circles. However, this is
just for demonstration and explanation purposes only. In
reality zones will almost never be concentric circles. They
can be any shape in a design but will usually be big
oblong, or globular areas. Zones are a rough estimate of
impacted areas, not the literal truth. While zones will be
represented by lines on a piece of paper, in reality they
will not have sharp lines of delineation.

Be very careful when making zones in an urban environment.
Many neophyte designers believe that every zone analysis
has to have all five zones, but that is not true. In dense
urban environments your site may only have a Zone 0 and a
Zone 1. Perhaps it may have a Zone 2 and 3, but rarely
will an urban site have a Zone 4 and it will never have a
Zone 5, true wilderness in an urban area.

## The Six Zones

Zone 0 is the zone for permanent built structures, usually
the house or other buildings. This zone is considered to be
permanently impacted because the building will never move,
and so is often considered ecologically inert. However with
systems such as green roofs and living walls some of the
ecological activity can be remediated by a skillful
designer.

Zone 1 is the area impacted by humans on a daily basis,
directly around the Zone 0 and around any other place where
humans come and go. Designers will usually put gardens in
Zone 1, anticipating that humans will be coming and going
to the garden to pick things and tend to it. Other features
may include a cob oven, yard space for playing or parking.

Zone 2 is for systems that require or will attract human
impact only a few times a week at most. The systems should
be fairly self supporting, requiring only occasional human
intervention. Examples include beehives, compost piles,
food producing bushes, or pump houses for water systems.

Zone 3 is impacted roughly monthly or seasonally. Zone 3
might be a good place for orchards, crops that will be
seasonally harvested, ceremonial sweat lodges, or similar
elements that need periodic attention.

Zone 4 is where human impact is present but minimal. It
may have light impact such as bird watching, camping,
hiking, or wildcrafting, and some sustainable forestry
practices such as thinning and coppicing. Zone 4 has no
human built structures or systems. This is where humans
interact with the natural world as is.

Zone 5 is true wilderness, with no structures, no roads,
no human-impacted forests. For the most part you cannot
have wilderness within urbanized areas. Even a large
untamed patch of woods surrounded on all sides by urban
development would count as Zone 4, not Zone 5.

## Using Zones as a Design Tool

Now that you understand the zones you can see they can be
used as both an analytical tool and a design tool. You can
analyze an existing area and determine the human impact and
what type of zones exist across the site. The designer then
has two options: place appropriate systems within the
existing zones, or decide that the site may need some
serious zoning overhaul and redesign the zones.

You should create an overlay layer on your base map for the
zone analysis. The zone analysis is then used in conjunction
with the sector analysis, soil data, and other site
information to determine the optimal placement of all
design elements. Zones help ensure that elements needing
frequent attention are placed where they will receive it,
while those requiring minimal intervention are placed at
the periphery of human activity.

## Zones in Different Contexts

The zone system adapts to different scales and contexts. A
rural homestead might use all six zones across a large
property, while an apartment dweller may only have Zone 0.
The key is not forcing every site into all six zones but
honestly assessing the actual patterns of human use and
impact that exist on the site and designing accordingly.

Remember that zones are a tool for the designer, not a
rigid prescription. They should serve your design process,
not constrain it.
