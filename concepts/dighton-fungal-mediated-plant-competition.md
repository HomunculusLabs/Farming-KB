# Fungal-Mediated Plant Competition

## Overview

Mycorrhizal fungi mediate plant-plant interactions and are key determinants of plant [[edaphic-determinants-cannabis-microbiome-community-structure]]. By differentially enhancing nutrient acquisition, altering competitive fitness, and modifying host plant physiology, mycorrhizae can shift the outcome of interspecific and intraspecific competition, ultimately shaping plant [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]].

## The Mycorrhizal Response Continuum

Francis and Read (1995) demonstrated that plant families vary continuously in their response to AM infection, from strongly positive to strongly negative:

### Positive (Mutualism)
Asteraceae, Ericaceae, Fabaceae, Liliaceae, Pinaceae, Plantaginaceae, Ranunculaceae

### Neutral (Commensalism/Neutralism)
Burmanniaceae, Gentianaceae, Gramineae, Monotroaceae, Orchidaceae, Triuridaceae

### Negative (Antagonism)
Boraginaceae, Brassicaceae, Caryophyllaceae, Chenopodiaceae, Polygonaceae, Resedaceae, Scrophulariaceae

This variation in plant response invokes differences in **competitive fitness** among plant groups and thus determines plant community structure under any given set of environmental conditions.

## Mycorrhizae as Drivers of Plant Community Structure

### Diversity Effects

van der Heijden et al. (1998) showed in calcareous grassland mesocosms that:
- **Low AM diversity**: Asymmetric benefits among plant species caused large shifts in [[leake-mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration-plant-community-composition]] as AM species changed
- **High AM diversity**: More even beneficial effects across the plant community; community structure less likely to shift
- At high AM diversity, each plant species has a greater chance of associating with an efficient mycorrhizal partner

### Fungicide Experiments

Gange et al. (1993) applied fungicide to grassland communities:
- Reduced total root colonization by AM fungi
- Reduced plant species diversity
- Differential effects on different AM species likely amplified community impacts

## Competition Between Specific Plant Species

### Holcus vs. Dactylis

Watkinson and Freckleton (1997) modeled interactions between the grasses *Holcus lanatus* and *[[dactylis-glomerata]]* with and without mycorrhizal infection:
- [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] slightly altered the competition/density response surface
- *Holcus* always dominated over *Dactylis* regardless of mycorrhizal status
- Mycorrhizal benefit was compensated for by changes in both intra- and interspecific competition strengths

### Pine vs. Grass (Niche Separation)

Pedersen et al. (1999) examined competition between slash pine (*Pinus elliottii*, ECM-associated) and native grass (*Panicum chamaelonche*, AM-associated):
- Pine inoculated with *[[pisolithus-arhizus]]* took up more P when competing with nonmycorrhizal grass than with another pine
- **Pine is more competitive at higher nutrient concentrations; grass is more competitive at lower concentrations**
- This suggests niche separation based on [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]] and mycorrhizal type

## Simpson's Paradox and Community-Level Responses

Allison and Goldberg (2002) explored whether community-level responses to mycorrhizae could be predicted from individual species responses:

### Hypothesis 1 (Rejected): Uniform Response
All plant species respond similarly -- reduced AM colonization with increasing P supply → general reduction in [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] at the ecosystem level.

### Hypothesis 2 (Supported): Variable Response
Each plant species responds differently to AM colonization × P supply interactions. The net community response is zero because positive and negative responses cancel out.

If Hypothesis 2 operates in natural communities, varied species-specific responses to both mycorrhizal colonization and environmental variables would lead to changes in community structure as conditions change. The magnitude of mycorrhizal influence is proportional to the relative fitness enhancement provided to each species.

## Soil Feedback Mechanisms

### Plant-Soil Community Feedback

Bever et al. (1997) developed a model of feedback between soil communities and plant [[fukuoka-textdoc-microclimate-mite-scale-population-dynamics]]:
- Growth of four plant species was enhanced or inhibited by soils where the same or different species had previously grown
- Changes in soil organisms, nutrients, or plant-antagonistic chemicals create positive or negative feedback
- These feedbacks affect the growth of subsequently planted species

### Nutrient Heterogeneity Effects

Farley and Fitter (1999) showed that root proliferation of seven co-occurring woodland species responded differently to localized nutrient-rich patches. This differential response:
- Was not affected by mycorrhizal status
- Led to improved competition by species producing more root biomass in response to patches
- Created small-scale spatial heterogeneity in competitive outcomes
