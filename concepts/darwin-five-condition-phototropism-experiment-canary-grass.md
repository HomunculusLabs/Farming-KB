Charles Darwin and his son Francis conducted one of the most elegant experiments
in the history of plant biology, demonstrating that the "eyes" of a plant reside
at the tip of the seedling while the bending response occurs further down the stem.
Their experimental design with canary grass (Phalaris canariensis) remains a
masterclass in hypothesis-driven research.

## Background: The Mystery of Phototropism

Darwin was fascinated by the effects of light on plant growth. In his final book,
The Power of Movement in Plants (1880), he wrote: "There are extremely few plants,
of which some part does not bend towards lateral light." The universal observation
that plants bend toward light sources had been known since antiquity, but the
mechanism remained entirely unknown. Darwin hypothesized that [[phototropism]] was not
related to photosynthesis but rather to some inherent light-sensing ability of the
plant.

Julius von Sachs had established by 1864 that blue light is the primary color that
induces phototropism, while plants are generally insensitive to other colors for
directional bending. This suggested the existence of a specialized photoreceptor,
but no one knew where in the plant this receptor was located or how the signal
traveled from the site of perception to the site of response.

## The Five Experimental Conditions

The Darwins grew canary grass in a totally dark room for several days to
eliminate any pre-existing directional light responses. They then placed a very
small gas lamp twelve feet (3.5 metres) from the pots, kept so dim that they
"could not see the seedlings themselves, nor see a pencil line on paper." After
only three hours, the plants had curved toward the light. The bending consistently
occurred about an inch (two centimetres) below the tip.

They then tested five different conditions to localize the light-sensing function:

1. **Untreated control**: The first seedling was left intact, confirming that
   the experimental conditions were conducive to phototropism. This plant bent
   normally toward the light source.

2. **Tip removed**: The second seedling had its tip pruned off. Without the tip,
   the plant went "blind" and did not bend toward the light, demonstrating that
   the tip was essential for directional light perception.

3. **Tip covered with opaque cap**: The third seedling had its tip covered with
   a lightproof cap. Despite the rest of the plant being fully exposed to light,
   this seedling also failed to bend, confirming that it was the tip that needed
   to perceive the light, not the bending region itself.

4. **Tip covered with transparent glass cap**: The fourth seedling had its tip
   covered with a clear glass cap. This seedling bent normally toward the light,
   proving that it was not the physical presence of the cap but rather the
   exclusion of light from the tip that abolished the phototropic response.

5. **Middle section covered with opaque tube**: The fifth seedling had its middle
   section (the region that normally bends) covered by a lightproof tube. Despite
   the bending region being in darkness, this seedling bent normally toward the
   light, proving that the bending region does not need to perceive light directly.

## Interpretation and Significance

The logic of this experiment is a textbook example of elegant scientific reasoning.
Conditions 2 and 3 demonstrated that the tip is necessary for phototropism.
Condition 4 demonstrated that the tip must perceive light (not merely be physically
present). Condition 5 demonstrated that the bending region need not perceive light
at all, proving that a signal travels from tip to bending zone.

Taken together, these five conditions established the existence of a mobile signal
that originates at the shoot tip in response to light perception and travels
downward to cause asymmetric growth in the stem. This mobile signal was later
identified as auxin ([[indole]]-3-acetic acid), the first plant hormone discovered.
Auxin accumulates on the shaded side of the stem, promoting cell elongation on
that side and causing the plant to bend toward the light.

## Darwin's Broader Botanical Legacy

It is not widely known that for the twenty years following the publication of On
the Origin of Species (1859), Charles Darwin conducted extensive botanical research.
While his work on evolution by natural selection is universally celebrated, his
plant physiology experiments were equally rigorous and groundbreaking. The
phototropism experiments with Francis were among the last major works Darwin
published, appearing when he was seventy-one years old.

The discovery of phototropism's mechanism established several principles that
remain central to plant biology: that plants possess specialized sensory organs
(analogous to animal eyes), that information can be transmitted over distances
within the plant body, and that plant responses to environmental stimuli are
mediated by specific chemical signals rather than by diffuse physiological changes.

## Modern Understanding and Canary Grass as a Model

Canary grass (Phalaris canariensis) remains a useful model organism for studying
phototropism. Its rapid growth, consistent phototropic response, and well-
characterized anatomy make it ideal for classroom demonstrations and research. The
molecular basis of phototropism is now understood to involve phototropins (blue
light photoreceptors) that activate a signaling cascade leading to asymmetric auxin
distribution via the PIN-FORMED (PIN) auxin efflux carrier proteins.

## Implications for Understanding Plant Intelligence

Darwin's phototropism experiment challenges the common assumption that plants are
passive organisms. The separation of perception (at the tip) from response (at the
stem) implies a sophisticated internal communication system. Plants do not simply
grow toward light because light causes local cell expansion; they actively sense
their environment at a specific location, integrate that information, and transmit
a targeted signal to the appropriate effector region. This is fundamentally the
same logic that underlies animal nervous systems, albeit operating on different
timescales and through different molecular mechanisms.

The experiment also raises the question of what constitutes an "eye." If the
criterion for vision is the ability to detect light direction and respond
appropriately, then the shoot tip of a plant qualifies. While plants lack the
image-forming capacity of animal eyes, they possess a directional light-sensing
capability that is exquisitely sensitive. Darwin's seedlings responded to a light
source so dim that the experimenters themselves could not see it, suggesting that
plant "vision" may in some respects exceed human visual sensitivity.

## Connection to Auxin Discovery

The mobile signal hypothesized by Darwin was identified decades later as auxin,
the first plant hormone to be discovered. Auxin is synthesized primarily in the
shoot apex and young leaves, then transported basipetally (downward) through the
plant. In the context of phototropism, blue light perceived by phototropins at the
shoot tip causes auxin to be redistributed to the shaded side of the stem. The
resulting higher auxin concentration on the shaded side promotes cell elongation
through acid growth: auxin activates proton pumps that lower cell wall pH,
activating expansin proteins that loosen the cell wall and allow turgor-driven
expansion. The differential elongation between the illuminated and shaded sides
produces the characteristic bend toward light.

## Agricultural Relevance

Understanding phototropism has practical implications for crop management. In
dense plantings, phototropic responses influence canopy architecture and light
interception patterns. In greenhouse cultivation, uniform lighting can be used to
promote straighter, more even growth. In cannabis cultivation, light placement and
intensity gradients influence branching patterns and overall plant form, making
phototropic understanding relevant to optimizing grow room design.
