# Deep-Organic vs. Shallow-Organic Farming

Eliot Coleman draws a critical distinction between two fundamentally
different approaches to organic agriculture in "The [[coleman-claytonia-winter-harvest-cultivation]]
Handbook." This distinction — between "deep-organic" and "shallow-
organic" farming — is not about certification standards or input lists
but about underlying worldviews that produce radically different
farming systems, different food quality, and different relationships
between farmers and the agricultural industry.

## The Deep-Organic Approach

Deep-organic farmers use freely available natural soil foods from
deep-rooting legumes, [[jeavons-green-manures-cover-crops]], and composts to correct the
**causes** of an infertile soil by establishing a vigorous soil life.
They acknowledge that the underlying cause of pest problems — insects
and diseases — is plant stress.

Deep-organic farmers know they can avoid pest problems by managing:
- Soil tilth and structure
- Nutrient balance
- [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] content
- Water drainage
- Air flow
- Crop rotations
- Varietal selection

In doing so, they free themselves from the need to purchase fertilizers
and pest-control products from the industrial supply network. The goal
of deep-organic farming is to grow the most nutritious food possible
and to respect the primacy of a healthy planet.

## The Shallow-Organic Approach

Shallow-organic farmers, after rejecting agricultural chemicals, look
for quick-fix inputs. Trapped in a belief that the natural world is
inadequate, they end up mimicking the patterns of chemical agriculture.
They use bagged or bottled [[hamilton-organic-fertilizers-and-plant-nutrition]] to supply nutrients that
temporarily treat the **symptoms** of an infertile soil.

They treat the symptoms of plant stress — [[vegetable-insect-and-disease-control]] problems —
by arming themselves with the latest natural organic weapons. In doing
so, shallow-organic farmers continue to deliver themselves into the
control of an industrial supply network that is only too happy to sell
them expensive symptom treatments.

The goal of shallow-organic farming is merely to follow approved
guidelines and respect the primacy of international commerce.

## A Difference in Life Views

The difference between these approaches is, at its core, a difference
in life views. Coleman states this plainly:

The **shallow view** regards the natural world as consisting of mostly
inadequate, usually malevolent systems that must be battled or modified.

The **deep-organic view** understands that the natural world consists
of elegant, impeccably designed, smooth-functioning systems that must
be studied and nurtured.

This is not merely a philosophical distinction — it has practical
consequences that determine every aspect of how a farm operates, what
inputs it purchases, what relationship it has with suppliers, and what
quality of food it produces.

## The Industrial Establishment's Response

The industrial agricultural establishment sees deep-organic farming as
a threat to the status quo since it is not an easy system for outsiders
to quantify, to control, or to profit from. Deep-organic farmers
create their own fertility, manage their own pest control, and operate
independently of the supply chains that sustain conventional and
shallow-organic agriculture alike.

Shallow-organic farming, by contrast, is an acceptable variation of
chemical agribusiness. It is an easy system for the industry to
quantify, control, and profit from in the same ways it has done with
[[knf-vs-chemical-farming-comparison]]. Shallow-organic farming sustains the dependence of
farmers on middlemen and fertilizer suppliers.

This is why the agricultural industry has largely embraced organic
certification — it creates a premium market while preserving the
supply-chain relationships that profit middlemen. The farmer becomes a
consumer of approved organic inputs rather than an independent producer
who creates fertility from natural systems.

## Unknown Benefits of Living Soil

The deep-organic pioneers learned that farming in partnership with the
natural processes of soil organisms makes allowance for the unknowns.
The living systems of a truly fertile soil contain all sorts of
yet-to-be-discovered benefits for plants — and consequently for the
livestock and humans who consume them.

These are benefits scientists do not even know how to test for because
they are unaware of their mechanisms, yet deep-organic farmers are
conscious of them every day in the improved vigor of their crops. This
practical experience of farmers is unacceptable to scientists who
disparagingly call it mere "anecdotal evidence."

Coleman notes that good farmers contend scientists are passing judgment
on things they know nothing about. The demand for replicated studies
cannot capture the systemic benefits of living soil nurtured over decades.

## In Defense of Instinct

Coleman argues that it is difficult for organic farmers to defend
their ideas scientifically where so little scientific data has been
collected. However, the passion is there because the farmer's instincts
are so powerfully sure that differences exist. The farmer who has
worked living soil for decades develops an intuitive understanding that
no laboratory study can replicate.

This does not mean deep-organic farming rejects science. Rather, it
insists that science must study the right questions — whole systems
rather than isolated variables, long-term effects rather than single-
season trials, and biological complexity rather than chemical
simplicity.

## Practical Implications for the Small Farm

For the small farmer, the deep-organic approach offers economic
independence. By creating fertility on-farm through compost, green
manures, and crop rotation, the farmer eliminates the need for
purchased inputs that erode profit margins. By managing pests through
plant health rather than purchased products, the farmer avoids both
the cost and the cycle of dependency that comes with symptom treatment.

The winter harvest system at Four Season Farm is itself an expression
of deep-organic philosophy. The unheated greenhouses, the succession
planting, the surface composting — all reflect a partnership with
natural processes rather than an attempt to override them with
technology and purchased inputs.
