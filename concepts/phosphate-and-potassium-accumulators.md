# Phosphate and Potassium Accumulator Plants

Phosphorus and potassium are two essential plant nutrients that are
notoriously difficult to liberate from their mineral forms in soil.
While these elements may be present in adequate total quantities, they
are often locked in compounds that plants cannot readily absorb. Dynamic
accumulator plants that specialize in accessing and concentrating these
minerals provide a valuable biological tool for building soil fertility
without relying on imported fertilizers.

## The Challenge of [[phosphorus-availability]]

Phosphorus is one of the three primary macronutrients required by plants,
along with nitrogen and potassium. However, phosphorus has a strong
tendency to bind with soil minerals, particularly in acidic or
alkaline soils, forming insoluble compounds that are unavailable to
plant roots. This means that even soils testing high in total
phosphorus may have very low levels of plant-available phosphorus. The
challenge for gardeners is to convert this locked-up phosphorus into
forms that crops can use.

## Phosphate-Accumulating Plants

Several plant species are particularly effective at dissolving,
absorbing, and cycling phosphorus. Three stand out as especially
important: legumes (particularly alfalfa), buckwheat, and mustards.

### Legumes as Phosphorus Accumulators

Legumes accumulate phosphorus and help release it from the soil. When
alfalfa is left in the field as a cover crop or green manure, there is
a measurable and gradual improvement in the soil's supply of available
phosphorus. Using alfalfa or any of the legume family as a cover crop
or green manure is an effective way to enhance the cycling of
phosphorus within a garden or farm landscape.

### Buckwheat

Buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum) is an excellent summer soil builder
with a fibrous and somewhat shallow root system. The large quantities
of organic fiber deposited in the soil by buckwheat roots help to
loosen clay soils and improve the moisture and nutrient retention of
sandy soils. Buckwheat is a fast-growing summer annual that can be
planted in succession throughout the warm season, making it versatile
for filling gaps between main crops.

### Mustards

Mustards (Brassica species) are effective phosphorus accumulators
particularly suited to the cooler part of the growing season. They have
deeper roots and a more taproot-like structure compared to buckwheat,
making them useful for improving the drainage and tilth of deeper clay
soils. Mustards can be planted in early spring or fall, providing
phosphorus cycling during periods when other cover crops may be less
productive.

## Root Exudation: A Key Mechanism

All three of these phosphate accumulators share an important
characteristic beyond simple uptake. Research by W. F. Loehwing ("Root
Interactions of Plants," The Botanical Review, 1977) demonstrated that
these plants not only dissolve and absorb mineralized forms of
phosphorus, but actually exhibit "subsequent excretion out of the roots
of appreciable amounts of the phosphorus absorbed." This means that
phosphorus accumulators release some of the phosphorus they have
absorbed back into the soil through their roots, making it available
to neighboring plants even before the accumulator is incorporated as
green manure.

This root exudation mechanism is significant because it means
phosphate-accumulating plants can benefit companion crops during the
growing season, not just after being tilled in. The phosphorus is
released in a plant-available form directly into the root zone, where
surrounding plants can access it.

## Potassium Accumulators and the Bracken Fern

Potassium (potash) is as difficult to liberate from its mineral state
in the soil as phosphorus. Dynamic accumulators that concentrate
potassium provide a natural source of this essential nutrient. The
bracken fern (Pteridium aquifolium) is a particularly notable potassium
accumulator with a long history of practical use.

### Historical Use of Bracken Fern

In early 1900s England, bracken fern was harvested commercially as a
source of potash for washing laundry. The process involved collecting
green fern fronds during summer, drying them, and burning them to
produce ash. The ash was then moistened and moulded into balls for use
as a cleaning agent. Many early detergents were potassium-based. The
industry was eventually killed by the cheapness of soda, as documented
in H. C. Long's "The Story of the Bracken Fern."

### Bracken Fern in Gardening

Alan Chadwick, the father of biodynamic French intensive gardening,
recognized the value of bracken ferns and had his students collect
green bracken fronds each spring for composting, particularly for root
crops. Green bracken ferns average an impressive 25 percent potash and
can contain as much as 55 percent, making them one of the most
potassium-rich plant materials available. This transforms what many
consider a weed into a significant garden asset.

## Recycling Potassium Through Composting

Once a potassium accumulator has concentrated potash in its tissues,
the nutrient can be recycled through composting, mulching, or direct
incorporation as green manure. Composting is generally the preferred
method because the decomposition process converts the potassium into
stable, plant-available forms while also building soil organic matter
and improving soil structure.

## Practical Integration

To make effective use of phosphate and potassium accumulators in your
garden, consider planting buckwheat during warm summer months, mustards
during cool seasons, and including legumes like alfalfa in your
rotation plan. These three plant families deserve important roles in
the design and maintenance of any edible landscape focused on
nutrient self-sufficiency.
