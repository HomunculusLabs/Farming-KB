## Jivaro Shamanic Apprenticeship

## Overview

Among the [[jivaro]] (Shuar) people of the Upper Amazon, shamanic power is fundamentally rooted in the acquisition and mastery of *[[tsentsak]]* — magical darts that serve as the primary instruments of both sorcery and healing. The apprenticeship process by which a novice becomes a practicing shaman is a rigorous, multi-year undertaking involving the transmission of tsentsak from an established practitioner, prolonged periods of sexual abstinence, the ingestion of psychoactive substances, and the cultivation of relationships with spirit helpers. The ethnographic record, particularly as documented by [[michael-harner]] in *[[hallucinogens-and-shamanism]]*, reveals a system in which the boundary between curing and bewitching is razor-thin, hinging almost entirely on the novice"s capacity for self-discipline during the critical early months of training.

## Transmission of Tsentsak

The apprenticeship begins with a formal act of transmission from an experienced shaman to a novice. The donor shaman, who already possesses a store of magical darts within his body, cuts a portion of his *tsentsak* off with a machete and gives it to the novice to swallow. Upon ingestion, the recipient experiences acute pain in his stomach and is confined to his bed for approximately ten days. During this convalescent period, the novice drinks *natemd* (ayahuasca, *[[banisteriopsis-caapi]]*) every evening, a practice that initiates him into the visionary world through which shamanic power is accessed and exercised.

The Jivaro conceptualize the stomach as a permanent repository for magical darts. Tsentsak can be kept indefinitely within the body and regurgitated at will when needed. To reinforce the transfer and augment its efficacy, the donor shaman periodically blows on and rubs the entire body of the novice throughout this period — a ritualized act of empowerment that parallels practices observed in many other Amazonian shamanic traditions.

## The Critical First Month and the Forking Path

At the end of the first month of the apprenticeship, a *tsentsak* emerges spontaneously from the novice's mouth. This event constitutes a pivotal moment in the shaman's career, as the newly acquired magical dart produces in its owner an overwhelming desire to bewitch — to use the dart against a living target. The novice now faces a defining choice:

- **If he succumbs to the impulse** and casts his tsentsak against another person, he irrevocably becomes a *bewitching shaman* (*wawek* or sorcerer).
- **If he exercises self-control** and reswallows the first tsentsak, suppressing the urge to harm, he becomes a *curing shaman* (*paju* or healer).

This binary division is not merely a matter of personal volition, however. The character of the donor shaman exerts a powerful influence on the outcome. If the shaman who provided the tsentsak was himself primarily a bewitcher, the magical darts carry with them an inherent desire to kill, and the novice will be "strongly inclined to adopt their attitude." One Jivaro informant characterized the homicidal urge experienced by bewitching shamans as arriving "with a strength and frequency similar to that of hunger" — a physiological metaphor that underscores how deeply the aggressive impulse is felt to be embedded in the tsentsak themselves, not merely in the psychology of their owner.

## Sexual Abstinence and the Accumulation of Power

Sexual abstinence is a non-negotiable requirement of the apprenticeship and is understood as directly proportional to the shaman"s growing supernatural efficacy:

- **Three months** of abstinence is the minimum required for the novice to become any kind of shaman at all. Failure to maintain this discipline, as some do, results in the loss of shamanic potential entirely.
- **Five months** of abstinence confers the practical power to kill (for a bewitcher) or to cure (for a curer).
- **One full year** of abstinence is considered necessary to become a genuinely effective practitioner in either capacity.

During this extended period of celibacy, the new shaman undertakes the essential work of building his arsenal. He collects a wide variety of insects, plants, and other small objects, which he now has the power to convert into additional tsentsak by swallowing them. Almost any object small enough to be ingested can become a magical dart, including living insects and worms. Different types of tsentsak are associated with different kinds and severities of illness. The greater the variety of tsentsak a shaman possesses within his body, the greater his overall shamanic ability — a principle that encourages continuous collecting throughout a practitioner's career.

## The Dual Nature of Tsentsak

A central and philosophically sophisticated feature of Jivaro shamanic ontology is the doctrine that each tsentsak possesses both a *natural* and a *supernatural* aspect. These two modes of being are not simultaneously accessible; rather, they are revealed under different perceptual conditions:

- **Natural aspect:** Without the influence of natemd, a tsentsak appears as an ordinary material object — a thorn, a bone fragment, an insect, or a stone. This is the form visible to non-shamans and to shamans in ordinary waking consciousness.
- **Supernatural aspect:** Under the influence of natemd, the same object is revealed in its "true" form — as a demonic spirit helper with a distinct name and a terrifying or powerful appearance, such as a giant butterfly, a jaguar, or a monkey. In this aspect, the tsentsak is not a passive object but an active agent who assists the shaman in his tasks.

This dual-aspect ontology means that the shaman operates simultaneously in two registers of reality: the mundane world of material objects and the visionary world of spirit beings, bridged and mediated by the hallucinogenic experience.

## The Mechanics of Bewitching

Bewitching is always directed against a specific, known individual — typically a neighbor or fellow tribesman — and is most commonly undertaken to avenge a perceived offense against the shaman"s family or friends. The process unfolds as follows:

1. The shaman ingests *natemd* to activate his visionary capacity.
2. He secretly approaches the house of his intended victim, positioning himself just out of sight in the forest.
3. He drinks green tobacco juice, which enables him to regurgitate a tsentsak.
4. He hurls the magical dart at the victim as he emerges from his house.

If the tsentsak is sufficiently powerful and thrown with adequate force, it passes entirely through the victim's body, causing death within a period ranging from a few days to several weeks. More commonly, however, the dart lodges within the body, producing illness that may be treatable — or fatal, depending on the skill of the curing shaman who is eventually consulted.

A distinctive feature of Jivaro sorcery is that the victim is given no indication that he is under supernatural attack. The bewitcher actively conceals his actions, since awareness of the bewitching would prompt the victim to seek the protective services of a curing shaman before the tsentsak could take full effect. This element of secrecy and stealth differentiates Jivaro witchcraft from the more overtly confrontational sorcery practices documented in some other Amazonian societies.

## Spirit Helpers in Sorcery

Bewitching shamans can enlist two principal categories of spirit helpers to augment their attacks:

### Pasuk

The *pasuk* is a specialized spirit helper of paramount importance. Each shaman possesses only one pasuk, and each pasuk serves only a single shaman — an exclusive, dyadic bond that makes the pasuk the chief among a shaman's spirit allies. After the bewitcher has departed, the pasuk remains near the victim in the guise of an insect or forest animal. Its function is to serve as a reserve weapon: if a curing shaman succeeds in extracting the original tsentsak, the pasuk shoots its own magical objects into the victim, renewing the assault.

The pasuk is armored for supernatural combat. When perceived by a curing shaman under the influence of natemd, the pasuk appears in human form and size but is "covered with iron except for its eyes." This metallic armor renders it nearly invulnerable; the curing shaman can destroy it only by shooting a tsentsak directly into its eyes — the sole unprotected point.

### Wakani Birds

*Wakani* (meaning "soul" or "spirit") birds are a more generally available class of supernatural servant. Unlike the pasuk, they are not exclusive to any single practitioner but can be called upon by any shaman who knows how to summon them. The shaman blows on the wakani birds and dispatches them to the victim"s house, where they circle the target repeatedly. This spectral harassment is believed to produce fever and insanity, with death following shortly. Wakani birds thus represent a form of indirect, psychological attack complementary to the direct physical assault of the tsentsak.

## Anamuk and Supernatural Animals

In addition to projecting tsentsak directly into victims, shamans can employ their magical darts to create supernatural animals that carry out attacks. A shaman possessing a pointed armadillo bone tsentsak, for example, can shoot this object into a river while the intended victim is crossing on a balsa raft or canoe. Beneath the water, the bone manifests in its supernatural aspect as an anaconda, which rises and capsizes the craft, drowning the victim. This technique illustrates the versatility of tsentsak as weapons that operate across the natural-supernatural boundary.

## Supernatural Animal Attacks (Anamuk)

Beyond direct projection of tsentsak into victims, shamans can employ their magical darts to create supernatural animals that carry out attacks through environmental means. This technique, known as *anamuk*, illustrates the versatility of tsentsak as weapons that operate across the natural-supernatural boundary:

- A pointed armadillo bone tsentsak, shot into a river while the victim is crossing on a balsa raft or canoe, manifests beneath the water as an anaconda, which rises and capsizes the craft, drowning the victim.
- A tooth from a killed snake can similarly be deployed as a tsentsak that manifests as a poisonous serpent to bite the victim.
- Jaguars and pumas can also be created through the same mechanism to hunt and kill targeted individuals.

In each case, the material object (bone, tooth) serves as a seed that, when empowered by the shaman's intent and activated by the supernatural aspect of the tsentsak, grows into a full predatory entity. The attack therefore appears to the community as a natural accident or animal encounter, further concealing the shaman's involvement.

## The Five-Year Power Test

Approximately five years after receiving his initial tsentsak, a bewitching shaman must undergo a critical evaluation of his continued efficacy. This test involves the attempted bewitching of a tree. Under the influence of natemd, the shaman attempts to project a tsentsak through the tree at the junction of its two main branches. If his strength and aim remain adequate, the tree appears to split at the precise moment the tsentsak enters it — though this splitting is visible only to an observer who is also under the influence of the hallucinogen.

Failure in this test carries serious consequences. A shaman who cannot split the tree knows he is no longer capable of killing a human victim. He is immediately placed in a position of extreme vulnerability, as other shamans may perceive his weakness and target him. The weakened shaman must therefore urgently seek out a stronger practitioner from whom to purchase a new supply of tsentsak. Until he can afford this purchase, he drinks large quantities of natemd, tobacco juice, and piripiri daily to maintain what defenses he has, while resting on his bed at home to conserve his strength. Crucially, he attempts to conceal his weakened condition from potential enemies — a dangerous deception, since any bewitcher who discovers his incapacity can attack with virtual impunity.

## Complementarity of Bewitching and Curing

The relationship between bewitching and curing shamans is fundamentally complementary. Curing is the necessary counterforce to sorcery, and the curing shaman's diagnostic procedure mirrors the bewitcher's modus operandi. When called to treat a patient, the curing shaman first determines whether the illness is attributable to witchcraft by drinking natemd, tobacco juice, and *piripiri* (a psychogenic Cyperus species) in the late afternoon and early evening. These substances enable him to see into the patient's body "as though it were glass."

If a tsentsak is found lodged within the body, the curing shaman assesses whether it can be extracted. The prognosis depends on two variables:

1. **The force of the throw:** A tsentsak shot with great force may pass entirely through the victim, leaving nothing for the curer to extract. Such cases are invariably fatal.
2. **The character of the dart itself:** Different tsentsak produce different degrees of illness, and some are more resistant to extraction than others.

A tsentsak that remains embedded within the body can theoretically be removed through sucking — setting the stage for a direct supernatural confrontation between the curing shaman and the bewitcher"s pasuk. The curer must also contend with the possibility that the bewitcher has stationed a wakani bird near the patient's dwelling, requiring the additional step of tracking and eliminating this spirit sentinel before the cure can be completed.

## Pharmacological Basis of the Apprenticeship

The entire apprenticeship process is mediated by psychoactive substances, each playing a distinct role in the development and maintenance of shamanic power:

- **Natemd** (*Banisteriopsis caapi*, ayahuasca): The primary hallucinogen, described by Harner as "the chemical door to the otherwise invisible world of the Jivaro shaman." It enables the perception of the supernatural aspect of tsentsak, reveals the identity of bewitchers, and facilitates all diagnostic and combative shamanic operations.
- **Green tobacco juice** (*Nicotiana* spp.): Used to enable the regurgitation of tsentsak for both offensive (bewitching) and defensive (sucking) purposes. Though not truly hallucinogenic, tobacco juice produces a narcotized state believed necessary to keep one's tsentsak alert and ready to repel enemy darts.
- **Piripiri** (*Cyperus* spp.): An additional psychoactive preparation consumed alongside natemd and tobacco during curing sessions to enhance diagnostic vision.

The apprentice's daily consumption of these substances during the training period serves not merely to induce visionary states but to establish and maintain the physiological and perceptual conditions under which the supernatural world becomes accessible and navigable. The shaman who ceases to drink these preparations risks losing contact with his spirit helpers and becoming vulnerable to attack.

## Social and Economic Dimensions

Shamanic power among the Jivaro operates within a distinctly economic framework. Tsentsak are bought and sold — the initial transfer from donor to novice is a commercial transaction, not a gift. As shamans age and their power wanes through the expenditure of magical darts in curing or combat, they must repeatedly purchase new supplies from stronger practitioners. The most powerful shamans are therefore those with the resources to continually replenish their arsenals.

This economic dimension creates a perpetual hierarchy of shamanic power and a cycle of dependency. A shaman weakened by the loss of his tsentsak — whether through combat, the natural decay of power over time, or having them forcibly extracted by a stronger practitioner via a rainbow bridge — must find a new supplier. The inability to pay for fresh tsentsak can be fatal, as it leaves the shaman defenseless against both bewitching attacks and the ordinary illnesses that shamans, like all Jivaro, are subject to.
