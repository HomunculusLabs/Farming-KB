# Sterilization Techniques for Mushroom Cultivation

Sterilization is the single most critical step in mushroom cultivation.
Without it, competing organisms overrun substrates before the intended
mycelium can colonize. Every successful grow begins with proper
sterilization.

## Why Sterilization Matters

Mushroom substrates are rich in nutrients: carbohydrates, proteins,
minerals, and moisture. This makes them ideal environments not just for
mushroom mycelium but for bacteria, yeasts, and molds. When you
inoculate a substrate with mushroom spores or liquid culture, you are
introducing a relatively small amount of fungal material into a vast
nutrient resource. If competitors are already present, they will
outcompete the mushroom mycelium every time.

## Pressure Sterilization

### Autoclaving

Autoclaves are the gold standard for substrate sterilization. They use
pressurized steam at 121 degrees Celsius (250 degrees Fahrenheit) at
15 PSI to destroy all microbial life, including resistant bacterial
endospores. Standard sterilization times range from 60 to 120 minutes
depending on substrate volume and density.

For grain substrates, 90 minutes at 15 PSI is the minimum
recommendation. Larger bags or denser substrates like supplemented
sawdust blocks require 2 hours or more. The key principle is that
heat penetration takes time; the center of a substrate mass lags behind
the surface temperature significantly.

### Pressure Cookers

Home cultivators typically use pressure canners rather than laboratory
autoclaves. The All-American pressure canner is the most popular choice
because it has no rubber gasket to degrade and can hold multiple quart
jars. Standard stovetop pressure cookers with rubber gaskets work but
require more maintenance. Electric pressure cookers like the Instant Pot
are not recommended because they cannot reliably reach and maintain 15
PSI.

## Steam Sterilization

For cultivators without a pressure vessel, atmospheric steam
sterilization is a viable alternative for certain substrates. The
method involves placing substrate in a sealed container and steaming it
for 6 to 24 hours. This works well for unsupplemented substrates like
plain straw or unpasteurized hardwood sawdust.

The extended time compensates for the lower temperature. Pasteurization
(60 to 80 degrees Celsius) kills most competitors while preserving
beneficial thermophilic organisms that can help defend against
contamination. Full sterilization at atmospheric pressure is difficult
to achieve; steam sterilization occupies a middle ground.

## Chemical Sterilization

### Hydrogen Peroxide

Hydrogen peroxide at 3 percent concentration can be used to sterilize
substrates without heat. This method, developed by R.R. Wayne, involves
soaking the substrate in peroxide solution before inoculation. The
advantage is that mushroom mycelium produces catalase, an enzyme that
breaks down hydrogen peroxide, while most contaminants do not. This
selective advantage allows the mushroom mycelium to thrive while
competitors are suppressed.

### Bleach

Diluted bleach solutions (10 percent bleach, 90 percent water) can
surface-sterilize mushroom tissue for cloning. A 30-second dip in this
solution is sufficient to kill surface contaminants on a wild mushroom
specimen before transferring inner tissue to agar.

### Isopropyl Alcohol

Isopropyl alcohol at 70 percent concentration is the standard for
surface sterilization of tools, work surfaces, and container exteriors.
It is important to use 70 percent rather than 91 percent or 99 percent
because the water content is necessary for protein denaturation in
microbial cells. Higher concentrations evaporate too quickly and are
actually less effective.

## Dry Heat Sterilization

Oven sterilization at 160 to 170 degrees Celsius for 2 to 3 hours can
sterilize glassware, metal tools, and dry powders. This method is not
suitable for moist substrates because the moisture would evaporate. It
is primarily used for preparing empty jars, scalpel blades, and other
tools that must be completely dry.

## Filtration

Liquid culture and syringes are sterilized by filtration rather than
heat. A 0.22 micron syringe filter removes bacteria and fungal spores
from nutrient solutions without exposing them to damaging temperatures.
This is essential for heat-sensitive formulations and for producing
clean liquid inoculant.

## Common Mistakes

Overloading the pressure vessel is the most frequent error. Packing too
many jars into a pressure cooker prevents adequate steam circulation
and extends heat penetration time beyond safe limits. Each jar should
have space around it for steam to flow freely.

Skipping the cool-down period causes another common failure. Opening a
pressure vessel while it is still pressurized creates a violent steam
release that can disturb jar lids and compromise sterility. Always wait
for the pressure to return to zero naturally.

## Verification

The simplest verification method is the biological indicator test.
Place a small vial of bacterial endospores (Geobacillus
stearothermophilus) inside the substrate load. After sterilization,
incubate the vial. If the spores grow, sterilization failed. For
routine work, simply observing the substrate for signs of contamination
in the days following sterilization provides adequate feedback.
