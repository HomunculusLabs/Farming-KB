# Incremental Design in Permaculture

Incremental design is the process of evolving a permaculture system through successive small adaptations and improvements to working designs, rather than attempting to create a complete and final system from scratch. Drawing on Kevin Lynch's concept of "design by incremental adaptation of already-existing designs," this approach recognizes that the most successful and durable designs in human history have emerged through centuries of gradual refinement.

## The Basis of Incremental Design

Almost all engineering design is based on small changes to existing designs until some ultimate limit in efficiency or performance is reached. The whole process can take centuries, and the end result can be mass-produced if necessary. In permaculture, this principle applies equally to houses, roads, garden beds, water systems, and plant assemblies.

Kevin Lynch (1982), in his book *Site Planning*, writes of site designing by incremental adaptation of already-existing designs: "design by following physical systems that have been shown to work." He maintains that the best site planning of the past is a result of this process, and that it works very well unless some external and important condition—such as market forces or land ownership changes—disrupts it.

## What Incremental Design Does Well

Known effective design units and specifications—whether of roads, culverts, houses, garden beds, or technologies—have been subjected to long tests and have evolved from trials (or prototypes) to working and reliable standards. Even if "old-fashioned" (like overshot water wheels), they may yet represent a simplicity and efficiency hard to beat without a considerable increase in expense and complexity.

Incremental design is therefore:

- **Proven** — Based on designs that have been tested over time
- **Simple** — Avoids unnecessary complexity
- **Efficient** — Builds on what already works
- **Low-cost** — Uses readily available materials and methods

## What Incremental Design Cannot Do

While incremental adaptation is the most successful way to proceed after selective placement and energy conservation have been addressed, it has significant limitations:

1. **Complex priority systems** — It cannot satisfy the demands of a complex system that has to satisfy a complex set of priorities (such as a private home and garden serving food production, recreation, aesthetics, and wildlife habitat simultaneously).

2. **Changing futures** — It does not cope well with rapidly changing conditions, new information, and evolving sets of values.

3. **Self-reliance and self-governance** — It may not address the need for communities to become self-reliant and self-governing, which requires more radical redesign than incremental adjustment allows.

## The Context: Whole System Design

Incremental design is one component within a broader [[zone-and-sector-analysis]]** for spatial planning
- **Slope, aspect, and elevation analysis** for landscape placement
- **Guild design** for species assemblies
- **Pattern understanding** for broad-scale planning
- **Ethical assessment** for determining what should and should not be done

The danger of relying solely on incremental design is that fine-tuning a successful design for a specific climate and purpose can be "totally inappropriate if transferred out of culture, climate, or if applied to a different purpose."

## Feedback in System Establishment

Incremental design is the basis of feedback in systems undergoing establishment. During this phase, the designer makes additions or changes to houses or plant systems based on observation of what is working and what is not. This continual adaptation is not a substitute for good initial design—it is a refinement process that follows sound placement and energy conservation principles.

## Social Design Considerations

The limitations of incremental design become most apparent in social and settlement planning. Mollison warns that neglecting the social effects of settlement size and the needful local functions related to settlement size may lead to "designing for human misery, the under-servicing of needs, and even for such sociopathologies as riot and crime."

Such designs may be economic in cash terms, efficient for one use only, and totally inhuman—yet they are built every day. Mollison cites the "Bruce effect": rats subjected to breathing the same airstream as their fellows experienced severe instability, physiological stress, and consequent pathologies akin to crowding stress. This effect may apply equally well to people, yet people are crammed into 16-40 storey office buildings with no opening windows and only a single airstream.

Site designing needs "not a specialist approach, but rather a multi-disciplinary and bio-social approach that takes into account the effects the environment has on its intended occupants."

## Permaculture vs. Conventional Design

Mollison provides a comparative framework contrasting [[permaculture-design]] stimulus with conventional design:

| Dimension | Permaculture | Conventional Design |
|---|---|---|
| **Stimulated by** | Perceived social problem | Drive to erect monuments or make money |
| **Subject to** | Values of energy conservation, self-reliance, harmonious human occupancy | Economic (cash) considerations and desire for profit |
| **Mediated by** | Consideration of long-term biosocial factors | Purely functional values for short-term cost and material factors |
| **Achieved by** | Research and consultation with clients, or assisting people to gain education in design | External funding, little consultation, little client/people education |
| **Refined by** | Allowing space, finance, and feedback to adjust activity | Selling off and not taking responsibility for results |
| **Leading to** | A dynamic and healthy area inhabited by people with power and understanding | A dislocated population, powerless to effect change easily |
| **Result** | The stability of dynamic local adjustment | The instability of perceived individual powerlessness |

## The Role of Computer-Aided Design

Mollison suggests that assembling the diverse and effective knowledge of both parts and whole-order design into a computer search or game-playing programme could advance the whole design process. Such programmes could deal with fussy detail (plant list specifications, home construction details), leaving the designer to focus on "imponderables about the processes observed on the land, the likely trends of future societies, future needs, and a measure of human satisfaction."

## Summary

Incremental design is a powerful but incomplete methodology. It excels at refining proven systems but fails at addressing complex, multi-priority challenges and rapidly changing conditions. In permaculture, it serves as the ongoing refinement process that follows sound initial design, ethical assessment, and energy-efficient placement. The key is to combine incremental adaptation with broader strategic thinking to create systems that are both proven and adaptable.
