## Hyphal Structure

The hypha is a microscopic javelin-shaped cell containing a plasma
membrane, a rigid cell wall of chitin and glucans, cytoplasm with
organelles, and vesicles that stream toward the tip delivering enzymes
and wall materials. Hyphae extend exclusively at their tips through **tip
growth**: vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane at the apex, adding new
surface and releasing wall-building enzymes. The extreme polarization of
growth to the tip is one of the most [[bloomfield-fungal-biology-and-hyphal-growth]].

## The Turgor Debate

### Classical View

The classical model held that osmotic water influx pressurizes the
cytoplasm, the wall yields preferentially at the tip (where it is thinnest
and most plastic), and pressure drives irreversible wall extension like
inflating a balloon. Plasmolysis experiments (removing turgor) supported
this by halting growth.

## Osmotic Mechanism

Hyphae maintain pressurized state through active ion transport:

- **Proton export**: A transport protein exports hydrogen ions across the
  plasma membrane, creating an electrical gradient
- **Solute accumulation**: Carrier proteins exploit this gradient to
  import sugars and amino acids via symport (co-transport with returning
  protons)
- **Water follows**: Accumulated solutes create an osmotic gradient
  drawing water into the cytoplasm

The resulting hydrostatic pressure is contained by the rigid wall. Car
tires are inflated to ~2 atmospheres; many fungi maintain 3-5, with some
reaching 10 atmospheres. Any wall breach causes explosive decompression
— exactly what happens when stinkhorn eggs hatch.

## Invasive Growth

Turgor pressure becomes critical when hyphae encounter solid barriers:

1. **Enzymatic softening**: Hyphae secrete cellulases, ligninases, and
   other enzymes to weaken the substrate
2. **Physical penetration**: Turgor forces the tip through weakened
   material
3. **Substrate colonization**: Once inside, the hypha branches
   extensively to exploit the food source
