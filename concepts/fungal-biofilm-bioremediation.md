# Fungal Biofilms in Bioremediation

Fungal biofilms are structured communities of fungal cells embedded in a self-produced extracellular polymeric matrix, adhering to surfaces or interfaces. In [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]], fungal biofilms play important roles in attached-growth [[mycoremediation-bioreactor-design]], in the colonization of contaminated solid substrates, and in the natural attenuation of pollutants in soil and aquatic environments. The biofilm mode of growth offers several advantages for environmental applications compared to planktonic (free-floating) growth.

## Biofilm Formation by Filamentous Fungi

Filamentous fungi show several distinct morphologies in submerged cultures that are relevant to biofilm formation. Mycelia can be freely dispersed or become agglomerated in the medium. Loose hyphal aggregates are referred to as clumps, while denser, often spherical aggregates are termed pellets. Hyphae can range from linear filaments to branched structures, and clumps and pellets vary in size, density, and surface structure.

Filamentous fungi can colonize moist substrates such as wood and breach the water-air interface to grow into the air. Schizophyllum commune reduces the surface tension of water before escaping the aqueous phase to form aerial hyphae or fruiting bodies. This surface tension reduction results from the secretion of large amounts of hydrophobin, which forms a stable amphipathic protein film at the water-air interface.

## Attached-Growth Reactor Systems

Mycoreactors based on attached growth include trickling filters, rotating biological contactors (RBCs), upflow fixed-film reactors, and fluidized-bed reactors. These systems all rely on the formation of fungal biofilms on solid support surfaces. Several types of cell immobilization can be used in biofilm systems: attachment of the microorganism to the surface of a carrier, colonization of a porous carrier by organisms, immobilization in hydrogel, and microbial pellets or granulated sludges.

In these reactor configurations, the fungal biofilm provides a large surface area for contact between the [[fungal-enzyme-activity-nutrient-availability]] and the pollutants being treated. The biofilm matrix can also adsorb pollutants, concentrating them near the fungal cells and enhancing degradation efficiency.

## Rotating Biological Contactors

The rotating biological contactor (RBC) is commonly used as a wastewater treatment process and can be adapted for [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]] applications. In an RBC, fungal biofilms grow on rotating disks that are partially submerged in the wastewater. As the disks rotate, the biofilm is alternately exposed to the wastewater and the atmosphere, providing both contact with pollutants and aeration.

RBC systems offer good oxygen transfer and mixing characteristics. The rotating action helps maintain a thin, active biofilm by shearing off excess biomass. Fungi growing on RBC media can produce [[ligninolytic-enzyme-systems-white-rot-fungi]] that degrade recalcitrant organic compounds in the wastewater.

## Trickling Filters

Trickling filters are among the oldest biofilm reactor systems used in wastewater treatment. Wastewater is distributed over a bed of solid media on which fungal biofilms develop. As the wastewater trickles through the bed, pollutants are degraded by the biofilm organisms. Air circulates through the void spaces in the media, providing oxygen for aerobic degradation.

Celite is used as a carrier for fungi in trickling filter applications, but clogging can occur as a result of excessive growth and fluffy biofilm formation. This is controlled by phosphate limitation and other strategies to maintain an appropriate biofilm thickness. The selection of appropriate media with adequate surface area and void volume is critical for trickling filter performance.

## Fluidized-Bed Reactors

Fluidized-bed reactors suspend solid carrier particles in an upflowing liquid stream. Fungal biofilms develop on the surfaces of these particles, which are kept in constant motion by the fluid flow. This configuration provides excellent mass transfer characteristics and prevents the clogging problems associated with packed-bed systems.

In three-phase fluidized and airlift suspension reactors, solids are suspended by the combined action of liquid and gas (air) flow. These reactors are used for aerobic wastewater treatment and for the cultivation of immobilized and pellet-forming microorganisms. Pellet-forming fungi are cultivated in aerated fluidized-bed or suspended airlift loop reactors.

## Immobilized Fungal Systems

Immobilization of fungal biomass enhances biofilm formation and stability in bioreactor systems. Fungi are used in immobilized form in various reactors to facilitate rapid biodegradation. Alginate-entrapped fungal cells have been used for wastewater treatment, providing slightly better performance than free cells. The immobilization matrix protects the fungal cells from shear forces and toxic shocks while maintaining close contact with the wastewater.

The use of immobilized fungi has several advantages, including prolonged retention of fungal cells or enzymes with minimal washout even at high dilution rates. However, most reactor systems suffer limitations in maintaining a consistent high level of enzyme activity and steady-state continuous enzyme production over prolonged periods. The development of suitable bioreactor systems and immobilization technology remains an active research area.

## Membrane Bioreactors

Membrane bioreactors designed for cell immobilization are now employed in the detoxification of industrial phenolic wastewater. A novel polysulfone capillary membrane bioreactor system with immobilized fungal biofilm of Neurospora crassa has been used to degrade phenol and para-cresol. The capillary bioreactor operated in a dead-end configuration and showed high percentage removal of both compounds.

Laccase production was sustained at a level of 10 U/ml of permeate over 30 to 40 days, representing the first report on continuous production of laccase by N. crassa grown in immobilized systems. The biodegradative capacity of the bioreactor was maintained continuously to sustain removal efficiency for 4 months, demonstrating the long-term stability potential of immobilized fungal biofilm systems.

## Packed-Bed Reactors

The packed-bed reactor system has been employed for the degradation of pentachlorophenol (PCP) by wild-type strains of Phanerochaete chrysosporium. Among five biocarriers tested, polyurethane foam produced the highest biomass. The Biostage carrier produced the highest lignin peroxidase (LiP) activity of 22.6 U/ml on day 11, with an average LiP activity of 12 U/ml and an average manganese peroxidase (MnP) activity of 4.9 U/ml through 7 to 21 days.

Steady-state continuous degradation of PCP over an extended period reached a degradation ratio exceeding 80%. Polyurethane foam and polyethylene were also used as carriers for the immobilization of P. chrysosporium in static and agitated cultures for 4-chlorophenol degradation. These carriers allowed total 4-chlorophenol disappearance within 4 to 6 days for initial batches.

## Biofilm Monitoring

Video imaging is used to measure biofilm thickness in situ. Flocculation, granulation, and biofilm dynamics are studied using various analytical techniques. The knowledge-based control systems used in biotechnological processes can be applied to biofilm reactor monitoring and control, including process data classification, qualitative process state identification, and supervisory process control.

## Multistage Biofilm Processes

Multistage biofilm processes combining fungal and bacterial treatment stages have been developed for complex wastewaters. In pharmaceutical wastewater treatment, a multistage biofilm process achieved 90% COD and 95% TOC elimination with better removal of toxicity. The pH must be adjusted to 4 in the fungal stages and then raised to 7 for subsequent treatment stages, demonstrating the need for careful process integration.

## Circulating Floating-Bed Reactor

An innovative process involving a gas-lift mobile bed and a circulating floating-bed reactor (CFBR) has been proposed for wastewater treatment. The three-phase CFBR bioreactor does not require complex devices, resulting in easier effluent and airflow distribution, no primary settling, and no backwashing. This bioreactor guarantees excellent synergy between hydrodynamic and biological performance, making it suitable for fungal biofilm applications.

## Challenges and Future Directions

Controlling biofilm thickness and preventing excessive growth are ongoing challenges in fungal biofilm reactor operation. The development of new carrier materials with optimized surface properties for fungal attachment is an active area of research. The combination of fungal biofilm processes with other treatment technologies, including membrane bioreactors and advanced oxidation processes, offers potential for more efficient and robust treatment systems.
