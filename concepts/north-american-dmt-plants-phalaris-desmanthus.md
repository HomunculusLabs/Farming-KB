## Overview

Several common North American plants contain N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), a potent Schedule I hallucinogenic tryptamine. The most notable among these are species of Phalaris (reed canary grass) and Desmanthus (prairie mimosa), both of which are widely distributed across the United States and are frequently encountered in agricultural and natural settings without any recognition of their psychoactive properties.

These plants represent a unique category in the landscape of psychoactive botanicals: they are not cultivated for drug purposes but grow wild or are planted for entirely unrelated reasons such as forage, erosion control, or ornamental use. Their DMT content was discovered through phytochemical investigation rather than ethnobotanical tradition.

## Phalaris Species

Phalaris arundinacea (reed canary grass) and Phalaris aquatica (harding grass) are the two most commonly discussed DMT-containing Phalaris species. Both are tall perennial grasses that grow in moist to wet habitats including stream banks, marshes, and ditches.

Reed canary grass is native to North America and Eurasia and has become widely naturalized. It is commonly planted as a forage crop for livestock and for erosion control along waterways. Despite its agricultural utility, the grass has become invasive in many wetland ecosystems, forming dense monocultures that exclude native vegetation.

The DMT content of Phalaris species varies considerably between individual plants, populations, and growth stages. Alkaloid concentration is influenced by genetic factors, environmental conditions, and plant age. Some strains have been specifically bred for low alkaloid content to reduce toxicity to grazing livestock.

## DMT Biosynthesis in Phalaris

Research by Baxter and Slaytor (1972) demonstrated the biosynthesis and turnover of DMT and 5-methoxy-DMT (5-MeO-DMT) in Phalaris tuberosa. These studies showed that tryptamine serves as the precursor, with methylation by N-methyltransferase enzymes producing first N-methyltryptamine and then DMT.

Further work by Mack, Mulvena, and Slaytor (1988) developed a mathematical model for DMT production in Phalaris aquatica seedlings, showing that biosynthetic rates vary with developmental stage. Young seedlings may have different alkaloid profiles than mature plants.

The genetic inheritance of indole alkaloids in Phalaris arundinacea was studied by Barker and Hovin (1974), who found that alkaloid concentration is a heritable trait. This means that selective breeding can effectively reduce or increase DMT content, which has practical implications for both forage safety and the potential for clandestine cultivation.

## Desmanthus Species

Desmanthus illinoensis (prairie bundleflower or Illinois mimosa) is a perennial legume native to the central and southern United States. It grows in prairies, open woodlands, and along roadsides, and is sometimes used in revegetation projects and as a nitrogen-fixing component of pasture mixtures.

Thompson, Nicollier, and Pope (1987) identified indolealkylamines in Desmanthus illinoensis, confirming the presence of DMT and related tryptamine compounds. The roots and bark of this plant contain the highest concentrations, though the total alkaloid content is generally lower than that of Phalaris species.

Unlike Phalaris, Desmanthus has received relatively little attention in the phytochemical literature, and comprehensive profiling of its alkaloid constituents across different populations and growth conditions remains incomplete.

## Harmala Alkaloid Co-Occurrence

A significant complication with extracting DMT from Phalaris species is the co-occurrence of beta-carboline alkaloids, particularly harmane and norharmane. These compounds are monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) that can potentiate the effects of DMT if consumed together, as they inhibit the enzymatic breakdown of DMT in the gastrointestinal tract.

This combination of DMT plus MAOI is pharmacologically analogous to ayahuasca, the Amazonian psychoactive brew that combines DMT-containing plants with Banisteriopsis caapi, which contains harmine and harmaline. However, the specific beta-carboline profile of Phalaris differs from that of B. caapi, and the ratio of DMT to MAOI compounds is not optimized for oral activity.

The presence of beta-carbolines in Phalaris means that crude extracts could potentially be orally active, unlike pure DMT which is inactive orally without an MAOI. This has implications for both toxicity to grazing animals and the potential for misuse.

The specific beta-carboline profile varies between Phalaris accessions, with some populations containing predominantly harmane and others containing higher proportions of norharmane or related compounds. This chemical diversity adds complexity to understanding the pharmacological effects of ingesting Phalaris material.

## Toxicity to Livestock

The alkaloid content of Phalaris species has long been recognized as a cause of "Phalaris staggers," a neurological disorder affecting cattle, sheep, and other livestock that graze on high-alkaloid strains. Symptoms include tremors, incoordination, convulsions, and in severe cases, death.

The syndrome was initially attributed primarily to the tryptamine alkaloids, but subsequent research has implicated a range of compounds including DMT, 5-MeO-DMT, and various beta-carboline derivatives. Different clinical syndromes may result from acute versus chronic exposure and from different alkaloid profiles.

Low-alkaloid cultivars of Phalaris arundinacea have been developed specifically to address this toxicity problem. These cultivars are identified by screening and selective breeding, providing safer forage options for livestock producers in regions where reed canary grass is a dominant forage species. The development process involves both chemical analysis of individual plants and field evaluation of animal performance on different accessions.

In addition to acute neurological effects, chronic consumption of Phalaris with high alkaloid content may lead to progressive weight loss, reduced reproductive performance, and increased susceptibility to other diseases in affected livestock herds.

## Legal Status and Recognition

DMT is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under the United States Controlled Substances Act. However, the plants that contain DMT are not specifically scheduled, creating a legal gray area regarding their possession, cultivation, and distribution.

This gap exists because the Controlled Substances Act schedules specific chemicals rather than the plants that contain them. While extraction of DMT from these plants would constitute manufacture of a Schedule I substance, the plants themselves can be legally purchased, grown, and possessed in most jurisdictions.

The Halpern (2004) review notes that this situation could change if awareness of these plants' psychoactive properties leads to increased abuse. The relative obscurity of these DMT-containing plants among the general public has so far prevented significant regulatory attention, unlike the more visible cases of peyote and psilocybin mushrooms.

## Ecological Distribution

Phalaris arundinacea is found in all 50 U.S. states except for Hawaii, making it one of the most widely distributed DMT-containing organisms on the continent. It thrives in riparian zones, wet meadows, and disturbed areas, and its aggressive rhizomatous growth habit allows it to colonize new areas rapidly.

Desmanthus illinoensis has a more restricted range, primarily found in the central United States from the Great Plains eastward to the Appalachian region and southward into Texas and northern Mexico.
