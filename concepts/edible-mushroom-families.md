# Edible Mushroom Families

> A survey of the major families of edible fungi as classified and tested by
> Julius A. Palmer Jr. (1829–1900), the 19th-century Boston mycologist whose
> empirical methods helped establish practical foraging knowledge in America.

## Palmer's Empirical Testing Methodology

Before classifying any species as edible, Palmer subjected it to a rigorous
progressive testing protocol. His method was deliberately conservative:

1. **Initial trial** — Cut a small piece of the fresh cap and eat it first
   thing in the morning on an empty stomach, when any reaction would be most
   detectable.
2. **Daily escalation** — Increase the dose each succeeding day, building
   gradually from a small fragment to a full specimen.
3. **Observation** — Monitor for any adverse symptoms throughout the testing
   period. If discomfort, nausea, or other ill effects appear at any stage,
   the trial is abandoned and the species recorded as inedible.
4. **Final verdict** — Only after consuming an entire specimen without
   incident is the species declared safe.

Palmer reported that once a species passed this full protocol, he never once
reversed his verdict. A mushroom found edible by this method remained edible —
a principle he held as fundamental to mycological safety.

## The Puff-Ball Family (Lycoperdaceae)

Puff-balls are among the most recognizable and safest of all edible fungi. They
appear as rounded white masses on lawns, pastures, and forest floors, typically
after rains. Palmer considered them the ideal starting point for novice
foragers.

The critical identification rule is straightforward: when a puff-ball is cut
open, the interior flesh must be **perfectly white** throughout. Any
discoloration — yellow, purple, or darkening toward the center — indicates the
specimen is past its edible prime or belongs to a different genus.

Palmer made the strong assertion that among true puff-balls in the white
immature stage, there are **no poisonous varieties**. This makes the family
unique among large fungal groups in its uniform safety. The young white stage
is excellent when sliced and fried like eggplant or breaded and cooked.

## The Boleti Family (Boletaceae)

The [[boleti]] are large, fleshy toadstools immediately distinguished from gilled
mushrooms by the underside of the cap. Instead of blade-like gills, boleti bear
a porous, sponge-like surface composed of tiny tubes. This single feature makes
them easy to separate from the gilled agarics, where most poisonous species are
found.

Palmer noted that Italian immigrants and other European foragers had long
harvested boleti with confidence, and American woods were rich with edible
species. The family as a whole was considered among the safest for foraging,
with the general rule that most boleti are esculent. Those with bright red or
orange pore surfaces were treated with caution, but the majority — including
species that turned vivid blue or red when cut — were wholesome and delicious.

The boleti were central to Palmer's refutation of the color-change myth, as
many excellent edible species in this family undergo dramatic color changes
upon exposure to air.
## The Russula Family

Russulas are characterized by their **brittle** texture — the flesh and gills
break cleanly and crisply, rather than bending or tearing fibrously. They
produce no juice when broken, and their gills are of equal length (not
intermixed with shorter gills as in many agarics).

Palmer drew an important inference from the behavior of forest animals.
Squirrels, tortoises, and other creatures were observed eating russulas
selectively, apparently choosing on the basis of taste. Palmer concluded from
this natural evidence, combined with his own systematic tests, that all
pleasant-tasting russulas were edible. The rule for this family is elegantly
simple: taste a small piece raw. If it is mild and agreeable, the species is
safe. If it is acrid or bitter, it should be avoided.

This family illustrates Palmer's broader principle that taste, when applied
with discipline and caution, is a reliable guide to edibility.

## The Lactarii (Milk Mushrooms, Lactarius)

The Lactarii are closely related to russulas and share their brittle texture,
but are distinguished by the milky juice they exude when the flesh is cut or
broken. This juice varies in character from species to species — some produce a
watery, mild latex, while others yield a thick, acrid fluid that burns the
tongue.

Palmer's rule for this family mirrors his approach to russulas. When the
exuded milk is **naturally mild**, the mushroom is edible and often excellent.
When the milk is acrid or peppery, the raw mushroom is unpalatable, but Palmer
found that many such species could be rendered edible through a simple
salt-and-water treatment — soaking or boiling in brine to extract the acrid
principle. This observation underscored his broader thesis that many mushrooms
rejected as poisonous were merely unprepared.

## The Clavaria Family (Coral Mushrooms)

Clavarias are among the most distinctive and visually striking fungi. They grow
as clusters of branching, club-shaped structures arising from a common base,
closely resembling marine coral or tiny antlers. Their colors range from white
to pale yellow, pink, and orange.

Palmer held clavarias in exceptionally high regard as food. He described them
as **delicate**, comparing them favorably to both cauliflower and vermicelli
— a remarkable endorsement from a man who tested hundreds of species. They are
best gathered young and firm, as their intricate structure makes them
susceptible to rapid insect infestation. Palmer advised cutting below the
branching tops and gathering clean specimens, then cooking them promptly in
soups or stews where their delicate texture could be appreciated.

## The Color-Change Myth

One of the most persistent misconceptions in [[mycology]], according to Palmer, is
the belief that a mushroom which changes color when cut or bruised must be
poisonous. The *Boletus cyaneescens* turns a striking blue upon being cut —
yet Palmer classified it as thoroughly edible, noting that European foragers
had long prized it. Other boleti turned blood-red or deep brown when exposed
to air, all without toxic effect. Conversely, the deadly *Amanita* —
responsible for the vast majority of fatal mushroom poisonings — remains the
purest white throughout every stage of handling. Palmer used this contrast to
illustrate that surface appearances and simple color reactions are unreliable
indicators of safety or danger.

## The Elm Mushroom of Boston Common

Among Palmer's most celebrated foraging exploits was his discovery and harvest
of a large elm mushroom on Boston Common. The elm tree (*[[ulmus-americana]]*) is a
notable host for edible fungi, and Palmer found impressive specimens growing on
the ancient elms of the Common — a public park in the heart of Boston. His
ability to identify and prepare this [[cotter-urban-mushroom-cultivation]] demonstrated that edible
fungi could be found even in the most public spaces, given proper knowledge.

## Summary of Palmer's Family-Level Guidance

| Family | Key Feature | Edibility Rule |
|--------|------------|----------------|
| Puff-balls (Lycoperdaceae) | Round, white mass | White inside = safe; no poisonous varieties |
| Boleti (Boletaceae) | Spongy pore surface under cap | Most species edible; color change is normal |
| Russulas (Russulaceae) | Brittle, no juice, equal gills | Pleasant taste = edible |
| Lactarii (Lactarius) | Brittle, exudes milky juice | Mild milk = edible; acrid may be treated |
| Clavarias (Clavariaceae) | Coral-like branching clusters | Excellent food; gather young and cook promptly |
