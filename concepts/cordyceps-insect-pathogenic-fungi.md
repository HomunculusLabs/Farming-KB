# Cordyceps and Insect-Pathogenic Fungi

## Overview

*Cordyceps* is a genus of [[ascomycota-phylogeny-major-classes-lineages|ascomycete]] fungi that parasitize
insects and other arthropods, producing some of the most extraordinary fruiting
bodies in the fungal kingdom. The genus exemplifies a sophisticated arms race
between fungi and insects that has probably been waged for hundreds of millions
of years.

These fungi are capable of piercing the chitinous exoskeletons of insects,
manipulating their behavior, and ultimately killing them to broadcast infectious
spores from the corpse. They belong to the Ascomycota, the largest grouping of
fungi, which encompasses more than 32,000 species occupying every ecosystem on
the planet.

Ascomycetes are defined by the production of a unique kind of spore called the
**ascospore**, which is formed inside a fluid-filled purse called the ascus. As
cytoplasm within this cell is reorganized, embryonic spores are delineated by
a pair of encircling membranes. Water diffuses into the ascus by osmosis, and
the structure becomes pressurized. When the tip of the ascus opens, often in an
explosive fashion, the spores are shot into the air.

## The Infection Process

*Cordyceps* begins its attack when spores land on or near an insect host. The
fungus penetrates the chitinous exoskeleton using enzymatic action and
establishes itself within the host's body cavity. Once inside, the symptoms of
disease become apparent:

- Infected ants engage in hopeless grooming behavior and move with an erratic
  gait, their legs twitching uncontrollably
- Some insects exhibit epileptic convulsions, climbing plant stems and oozing
  mucus from their mouthparts
- In their death throes, insects grip the top of stalks with their legs and
  clamp down with their can-opener mouthparts before expiring
- Sometimes the bodies of dead insects are scavenged by other ant species,
  leaving only the infected head secured to the stalk by its powerful jaws

Other insects show the reverse behavior following infection, moving down a tree
trunk or burrowing into wood. But for the pathogen, the outcome never varies: a
few days after death, its hyphae burst through joints in the insect's skeleton
and sprout the elongated fruiting body.

### Summit Disease

The climbing behavior exhibited by infected insects is termed **summit disease**.
By scaling vegetation, colonial insects remove themselves from their nests,
potentially limiting the opportunity for an epidemic among their kin. This
self-removal may have evolved as a protective behavior for sterile workers whose
genetic legacy depends entirely upon the health of the queen and her suitors.

Alternatively, the fungus may exert direct control over the insect's behavior
by manipulating its brain chemistry. Although purely hypothetical, the
neurological symptoms exhibited by infected insects make this a very provocative
idea. By inducing the summit response in its host, the fungus would place itself
in an ideal position for wind dispersal of its spores, provided that the insects
are not picked off by birds.

## Fruiting Body Development

The upper portion of mature *Cordyceps* ascocarps is covered with pimples
marking the openings of tiny flask-shaped chambers called **perithecia**, in
which the asci develop. Each perithecium holds many asci, and each ascus
contains eight ascospores.

When each ascus matures, it elongates, pushes its tip through the opening in
the center of the pimple, perforates at its apex, and fires its clutch of
thread-shaped spores, one at a time, before retracting into the perithecium.
A few minutes later, the next ascus protrudes from the pimple and discharges
its spores.

### The Segmented Spore Missile

Each spore spans half a millimeter in length and is built from 100 or more
segments that fragment in the air or when they hit something. This murderous
projectile is the ultimate fungal weapon, a missile with numerous warheads.

Because the number of warheads or spore segments is multiplied by the number of
ascospores, and then by the number of asci per perithecium, and the number of
perithecia, a single fruiting body can dispense millions of infectious particles.

This staggering disease-causing potential reflects the intensity of the arms
race between *Cordyceps* and insects. The evolution of monstrous species like
*Cordyceps gunnii* has involved the progressive elongation of the fruiting body
stalk so the pathogen can escape from the deepest graves of its victims. Conflict
at the biochemical level has probably been equally fierce, with the proliferation
of toxic and antitoxic cocktails of ever-increasing strength.

## Species Diversity

*Cordyceps* species have been found parasitizing a wide range of hosts:

- Cicadas and tarantula spiders in Thai rain forests
- Ants and termites across tropical and temperate regions
- Root-feeding caterpillars (as with *Cordyceps gunnii* in Australia)
- Wood-boring beetles and other insects in temperate forests

The Australian *Cordyceps gunnii* produces an ascocarp as fat as a garden hose
and longer than a stick of celery. It infects a root-feeding caterpillar and
then elongates until its tip emerges into the air through the opening to the
insect's burrow.

Some termites infected by recently discovered *Cordyceps* species die in pairs,
harnessed to one another's jaws by a weft of fungal mycelium. Perhaps one
termite is infected by the other when it attempts to groom its diseased kin.

## Chinese Medicine and Traditional Use

*Cordyceps* has been used in Chinese medicine for hundreds of years. Its
fruiting bodies are called **dong chong xia cao**, meaning "winter insect,
summer grass," evoking the apparent conversion of caterpillars into roots from
which the ascocarps extend into the air.

The colonized insect is sold in a dried form that resembles a twig. Usually,
dong chong xia cao is powdered and made into a tea or mixed with other
ingredients and swallowed as a tablet. Marketed claims include body building
assistance, fatigue reduction, improved lung and kidney function, and increased
male sexual potency.

*Cordyceps sinensis* from the Himalayas is among the most expensive biological
commodities in the world, with high-quality specimens fetching thousands of
dollars per kilogram. Interest also exists in its efficacy as a
performance-enhancing drug for marathon runners.

## Related Pathogens: Claviceps (Ergot)

The ergot fungus *Claviceps purpurea*, a relative of *Cordyceps*, is a
pathogen of rye whose toxins cause blood vessels to constrict so powerfully
that hands and feet become gangrenous. An eighteenth-century English report
referred to "a singular calamity, which suddenly happened to a poor family in
this parish, of which six persons lost their feet by a mortification not to be
accounted for."

The vasoconstricting poison **ergotamine** is preserved during baking, and most
poisoning cases have resulted from contaminated rye bread. Ergot also synthesizes
isoergine (lysergic acid amide), a less potent relative of LSD, causing
terrifying hallucinations in addition to gangrene.

Linnda Caporael proposed in the 1970s that the Salem witch trials of 1692 may
have been triggered by ergot poisoning. The spring and summer of 1691 were
unusually warm and wet, providing perfect conditions for *Claviceps*. Symptoms
began in December, immediately after threshing of Salem's grain harvest, and
disappeared abruptly following the drought of 1692.
