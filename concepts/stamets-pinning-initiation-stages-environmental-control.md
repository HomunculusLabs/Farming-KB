# Pinning Initiation Stages and Environmental Control

## Overview

The change from the vegetative state of mycelial growth to the generative state
of primordia formation is called pinning, pin setting, pinhead initiation, or
fructification. Primordia are knots of mycelium that precede development into
small mushrooms. All species require a set of environmental conditions for
pinning that are quite different from the conditions for mycelial growth.

In nature, primordia formation is primarily influenced by seasonal changes.
In temperate climates most mushrooms fruit during the cool, wet fall, while in
tropical and subtropical climates they fruit during the rainy season.

## Basic Pinning Strategy

Mushrooms fruit indoors in response to much the same conditions that trigger
fruiting in the wild. Several environmental factors work in combination to
provide an ideal environment. Most cultivated mushrooms fruit at lower
temperatures than the optimum for mycelial growth. Usually a drop in temperature
is accompanied by an increase in humidity. Mushrooms form only when there is a
coincidence of all required factors.

The key environmental factors for pinning are water, humidity, temperature,
fresh air, CO2 levels, and light. The modification of any one beyond fruiting
requirements can inhibit or stop the process.

## Stage I: Preparation

Following casing application, the casing is conditioned for even mycelial
growth. Once established, the grower manages these requirements:

1. The casing layer is at optimum moisture capacity.
2. The casing layer surface is rough and porous.
3. The relative humidity of the growing room air is 95%.
4. The substrate is incubated in total darkness.

Water in the casing moves by capillary action to the surface where it is drawn
into the air by evaporation. This constant movement slowly depletes the casing
of moisture needed to protect pinhead development. Therefore, in conjunction
with optimum casing moisture, the relative humidity must be held at 95%.

The casing surface should be rough and open with minute mountains and valleys,
providing more surface area for pinhead formation, a humid microclimate, and
allowing diffusion of metabolic gases.

## Stage II: Environmental Transition

[[pinhead-initiation-techniques]] should begin when the mycelium reaches the
valleys of the casing surface. Within this one to two day period:

1. Substrate and air temperatures are lowered to the fruiting range.
2. The humidity is maintained at the 95% level.
3. Carbon dioxide content is reduced by introducing fresh air.
4. The room is lighted on a 12 hour on/off cycle.

The temperature "shock" is accomplished by ventilation with a large volume of
cool fresh air, lowering the room temperature below the optimum for spawn
running. For [[agaricus-brunnescens]], this means dropping air temperature from
70 degrees F to 64 degrees F. Within 48 hours, the substrate temperature should
fall to fruiting temperatures, effectively slowing vegetative growth.

The exact timing for initiation varies with the strain. Some cultivators
initiate when 20% of valleys show mycelial growth while others wait until 90%
are run through. Normally within 12-48 hours from when mycelium is first
visible in the valleys, the initiation sequence is started.

## The Role of CO2 Reduction

Since Agaricus brunnescens does not pin properly above 2000 ppm CO2, lowering
this level is critical. Not until CO2 is removed will pinheads form. If CO2
remains high, the mycelium covers the casing surface entirely, a condition
called overlay that makes the casing impervious to water and produces few
pinheads. Overlay can also occur if the casing is too dry, humidity too low,
or air temperature too high.

## Stage III: Primordia Formation (Knotting)

Once substrate temperatures have been lowered and CO2 levels reduced, primordia
will begin to form. The grower should maintain:

1. A constant fresh air supply to remove metabolic gases, with CO2 below 1000
   ppm.
2. A constant temperature within the fruiting range.
3. A relative humidity of 95%.
4. A 12 hour on/off light cycle.

Pinhead initials form in the humid valleys of the casing layer and are visible
as small knots of mycelium. Within five days these knots enlarge into small
mounds or buttons that soon differentiate into mushrooms.

Oversupply of fresh air can lead to high evaporation rates and excessive
drying. The humidity should never fall below 90%. Some growers knock down the
mycelium with forceful watering on the first day of initiation, but once
pinning has begun, any forceful watering will kill developing pins.

## Stage IV: Pinhead Development

After pinheads have grown to pea size (3-5 mm), their further development is
primarily dependent on air temperature and relative humidity:

1. Air temperature held constant within the fruiting range.
2. Relative humidity lowered to 85-92%.
3. Constant fresh air supply with CO2 below 2000 ppm.
4. A 12 hour on/off light cycle.

Lowering humidity to 85-92% increases the evaporation rate, an essential
requirement for pinhead maturation. If humidity remains too high, development
will be retarded. The easiest way to reduce humidity is to raise air temperature
by 1-2 degrees F or to increase air movement.

Under no circumstances should pockets of stagnant air be allowed to form.
Evaporation is negligible in stagnant air pockets, which are also breeding
grounds for contaminants.

## Relationship Between Pinning and Yield

The importance of primordia formation cannot be over-emphasized. Key
relationships include:

- During the primordia formation period, pinheads for the first and second
  flush are being generated. Because 60-75% of total yield is harvested from
  the first two flushes, these few days are the most critical.

- The greater the number of pins set, the higher the yield, provided
  sufficient nutrients are available. More pinheads mean smaller mushrooms.

- The substrate will only support a certain number of primordia per flush.
  Under normal circumstances, some pinheads may abort from insufficient
  nutrients or late formation.

- Pins that form early delay growth of neighboring primordia. Removing these
  "volunteers" before they develop is advantageous to the main flush.
