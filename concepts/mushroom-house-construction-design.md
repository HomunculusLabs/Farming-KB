# Mushroom House Construction and Design

## Overview

Successful commercial [[mushroom-cultivation]] requires structures that provide precise control over temperature, moisture, and ventilation. While caves and cellars offer naturally stable conditions, purpose-built mushroom houses allow greater control and convenience. The design and construction of these structures has evolved considerably, informed by centuries of European experience and adapted to American conditions.

## Site Selection and Excavation

### Drainage as the Primary Concern

Perfect drainage is a factor of the first importance in [[mushroom-house-construction]] design. This requirement is often complicated by the fact that for convenience in heating and for more uniform temperature during warmer seasons, it is frequently desirable to construct the house over an excavation that may be several feet below the natural ground surface. While this semi-basement arrangement improves thermal performance, it demands exceptional attention to drainage engineering.

### Loading and Unloading Access

Regardless of the construction type, arrangements must be made for the utmost convenience in unloading compost materials and for cleaning out old beds after cropping. The logistics of moving tons of manure-based compost in and spent beds out directly impacts operational efficiency and labor costs.

## Construction Types

### Simple Structures

For temporary or small-scale use, a mushroom house may consist of a very simple boarded shed or unused barn. A heater is necessary since winter is the important season for mushroom work. Many commercial mushroom houses that are not particularly elaborate have proven quite profitable, demonstrating that elaborate construction is not a prerequisite for success.

### Permanent Commercial Houses

As a permanent investment, the mushroom house should be built with due consideration for convenience, durability, and economy. No one going into the work on an extensive scale should build before examining a successful operation.

## Wall Construction

### Double Walls

For some time it has been the custom in building the better class of mushroom houses to make the walls double, packing fresh shavings between the inner and outer walls for insulation. This construction method addresses the fundamental challenge that mushroom houses always maintain a moist atmosphere, and the contact of moisture-laden air with cold exterior walls produces constant precipitation of moisture or "sweating."

### Premium Materials

More expensive houses may be constructed of hollow tile or concrete. Hollow tile has much to recommend it where the outlay is justified, as it provides excellent durability and resistance to the perpetually moist interior environment. Concrete construction offers similar advantages with greater structural versatility.

## Roof Design

A sufficiently sloping roof provided with an air space (essentially a double-roof or ventilated roof construction) corrects the moisture condensation problem to a large extent. The air space between the inner ceiling and outer roof surface prevents warm, moist interior air from contacting cold outer surfaces, thereby reducing condensation and dripping onto the beds below.

## Ventilation Systems

Arrangement should always be made for ventilation. It is usually advisable to place ventilators in the roof, the main point being that the desired air exchange is accomplished with less inconvenience from direct drafts than would occur with wall-mounted vents. Roof ventilators allow warm, moist, CO2-rich air to rise and exit naturally, while fresh air enters through lower openings, creating a gentle circulation pattern that does not subject the mushroom beds to drying drafts.

## Bed Support Systems

### Tiered Bed Construction

The greatest economy in mushroom house construction is attained by building houses high enough to accommodate beds in tiers of from two to five levels. This vertical stacking maximizes the productive area per unit of floor space and per unit of building cost.

### Spacing Requirements

- **Between tiers**: Approximately 2.25 to 2.5 feet of free space is necessary between beds in tiers, since each bed is at least 8 to 10 inches deep and somewhat more than 3 feet in total height must be allowed for each bed level to permit working access.
- **Bed width**: Wall beds should seldom be more than 3 feet across (accessible from one side only). Beds approachable from either side may be up to 6 feet across.
- **Aisles**: At least 3 feet of aisle width is usually sufficient, with one or more wider aisles where houses are very large.


### Structural Supports

The upright supports for beds in tiers of four or five should be scantling not less than 2x6 inches, placed about 4 feet apart. Experienced commercial growers have adopted large gas piping for the supports, which offers great permanency and is perfect from a sanitary standpoint — the smooth metal surfaces resist contamination and are easily cleaned.



## Cave and Cellar Bed Preparation

When beds are to be prepared in caves, cellars, or merely on the floors of buildings, the matter of preparing for the beds is a very simple one. When every foot of available space is to be used for commercial production, and especially in purpose-built houses, the construction of tier supports requires particular attention. Adequate structural support is essential — underestimating the weight of compost (which can be extremely heavy when moist) is a common and costly mistake.



## American vs. European Practice

In the United States, production has been confined to special houses, caves, and cellars. Near Philadelphia, Chicago, Boston, and other production centers, mushrooms are grown wholly in special mushroom houses, with the major part of the American market supply produced this way. A few commercial growers in Minnesota, Ohio, Indiana, New York, and Missouri have secured underground quarries, caves, or storage cellars of considerable extent. In England, mushrooms are grown in specially constructed houses, cellars, caves, and sometimes in the open air with protection.



## Heating

Some form of heating is essential for winter mushroom production. The specific heating method — steam, hot water, forced air, or direct combustion — depends on local conditions, fuel availability, and the scale of operations. The key requirement is maintaining the temperature range favorable for mushroom fruiting (generally 55-60 degrees F) during cold weather while providing adequate fresh air exchange through the ventilation system.
