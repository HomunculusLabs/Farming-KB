# Mushroom Identification Dangers and Tips

Jochen Gartz dedicates an entire chapter of "Magic Mushrooms Around the
World" to the dangers of erroneous mushroom identification, opening with a
sobering account of a fatal poisoning. On December 16, 1981, three teenagers
hunting for psychoactive mushrooms on Whidbey Island, Washington, collected
specimens of a Galerina species under the mistaken belief that they were a
Psilocybe species. All three fell ill after eating the mushrooms but did not
seek medical help for two days, afraid of prosecution for using psilocybin.
Two recovered after treatment; the third, a sixteen-year-old girl, died on
December 24. This tragedy underscores the potentially lethal consequences
of careless identification.

## The LBM Problem: Little Brown Mushrooms

The greatest identification challenge lies in the vast category of "LBMs"
-- little brown mushrooms. These small, nondescript brown species are
extremely difficult to differentiate from one another, even with modern
mycological keys. Both psychoactive Psilocybe species and deadly poisonous
Galerina species fall into this category, and they can grow side by side in
the same habitat. Gartz himself once discovered a grassy area where
Psilocybe stuntzii specimens were so entangled with a Galerina species that
individual fruiting bodies could be identified only by spore analysis.

Galerina species contain the same deadly amatoxins found in the death cap
(Amanita phalloides) and its relatives. These toxins are insidious: no
symptoms appear for approximately twelve hours, during which the toxins can
cause major irreversible internal damage. A common North American species,
Galerina autumnalis, grows on wood debris in parks and forests -- precisely
the same habitat favored by several Psilocybe species. The Galerina species,
however, does not turn blue when bruised.

Another dangerous genus is Pholiotina (also known as Conocybe), which
includes North American species containing amatoxins. At least one European
Galerina species (Galerina marginata) contains the same toxins found in
the death cap, growing on decaying wood with about one-third the amanitine
content of Amanita phalloides. Incredibly, older mushroom books list this
species as edible.

## The Bluing Reaction: An Unreliable Guide

The bluing reaction -- the characteristic blue or blue-green
discoloration that develops when psilocybin-containing mushrooms are
bruised or cut -- is the most widely known field marker for identifying
psychoactive species. However, Gartz emphasizes its serious limitations.

Not all species that contain psilocybin turn blue. [[psilocybe-semilanceata]], Conocybe cyanopus, and Inocybe aeruginascens develop only slight stem stains after relatively long pressure. Conversely, many non-psychoactive mushrooms can also turn blue -- including the brown and white varieties of the commercial champignon mushroom, which change color when exposed to metol solution.

Metol (p-methylaminophenol) testing, long propagated in North American
field guides as a specific test for Psilocybe species, is essentially
worthless. The reagent merely reacts with laccase enzymes found in many
mushroom species and cannot confirm the presence of psilocybin. Melzer's
Reagent, similarly promoted as a Psilocybe identification tool, turned out
to be just as nonspecific.

Only spontaneous bluing reactions or those caused by injuries provide
reliable clues, and even then, the bluing phenomenon reveals nothing about
the type or quantity of specific indole compounds present.

## Easily Identifiable vs. Problematic Species

[[psilocybe-semilanceata]] is one species that, at
prime locations, can be easily identified without microscopic tests, thanks
to its uniquely distinctive conical shape, hygrophanous cap, and crooked
stem. The situation is entirely different for Psilocybe species that thrive
on wood debris, where confusion with deadly Galerina species is a real and
constant danger.

Gartz notes a striking paradox he calls "mycophile or mycophobe": some
individuals develop expert knowledge of a single psychoactive species --
they can identify [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] with drill
and accuracy -- yet cannot identify common table mushrooms or differentiate
Agaricus species (champignons) from other mushrooms growing on the same
pasture. Such selective expertise is dangerous because it encourages
confidence that may not extend to the specific identification challenges
that matter most for safety.

## Practical Identification Tips

Based on Gartz's extensive field experience, several practical guidelines
emerge for safe mushroom identification:

**Habitat knowledge is essential.** Knowing what species typically grow in
a given environment -- pasture, wood debris, bark mulch, dung -- is a
fundamental first step in identification. Panaeolus species in particular
tend to invade commercial cultivation areas, fruiting before the
cultivated species and creating opportunities for misidentification.

**Spore prints provide important but limited information.** A spore print
is made by placing the cap on black or white paper (depending on expected
spore color) for several hours under a glass vessel. However, spores from
different genera may share the same color, and there is a high degree of
similarity among spores from different [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]]
species.

**Ecological data must be part of every identification.** The time of
year, associated plant species, substrate type, and weather conditions all
provide crucial contextual information that helps narrow the range of
possible identifications.

**Expert consultation is irreplaceable.** Gartz emphasizes that competence
in mushroom identification is a skill developed through extensive fieldwork,
consultation with established mycological experts, and careful analysis of
samples. Relying on a single field guide or a brief encounter with one
specimen is insufficient for safe identification.

**The "chewing test" is not a reliable safety measure.** Failure to
carefully chew mushrooms before swallowing means that only a fraction of
available alkaloids are extracted and absorbed. While this might reduce
the effects of a psychoactive species, it provides no protection against
deadly amatoxins, which are lethal at extremely small doses regardless of
chewing.

## The Role of Legal Fear in Delayed Treatment

Gartz identifies a particularly dangerous factor in mushroom poisoning
cases: the fear of legal consequences. The Whidbey Island teenagers waited
two days to seek medical help because they were afraid of being prosecuted
for psilocybin possession. This delay likely contributed to the fatal
outcome. In general, stomach pumping is both too drastic and often
unnecessary for psilocybin intoxication, but it can be lifesaving in cases
of amatoxin poisoning -- if administered in time. The overlap of habitats
between psychoactive and deadly species means that anyone consuming wild
mushrooms identified in the field is always at some risk of having made an
error.
