# Night Interruption Photoperiodism and Commercial Floral Manipulation

One of the most practically significant discoveries in plant photobiology is that
plants measure not the length of the day but the length of the continuous dark period.
This insight, emerging from the [[garner-allard-photoperiodism-experiment-methodology|Garner-Allard]]
experiments on Maryland Mammoth tobacco, led to the development of night interruption
(night break) techniques that now form the backbone of commercial floriculture
scheduling worldwide. Chamovitz's account in "What a Plant Knows" provides an accessible
explanation of how this discovery transformed greenhouse production and enabled precise
control over crop flowering timelines.

## The Critical Discovery: Plants Measure Darkness, Not Light

Around the time of World War II, researchers discovered that they could manipulate
flowering time simply by briefly interrupting the dark period. When a short-day plant
like soybean was exposed to just a few minutes of light in the middle of a long night,
it failed to flower — as if the night had never happened. Conversely, when a long-day
plant like iris was given a brief flash of light in the middle of a short night, it
flowered as if it had experienced a long day. This proved conclusively that plants
measure the length of continuous darkness, not the length of daylight, reversing the
assumption implicit in the term "short-day plant."

This discovery had immediate commercial implications. It meant that flowering could be
controlled with minimal energy input — a few minutes of lighting during the night
rather than extending daylight by hours. The [[photoperiodism-plants-measure-night-length]]
finding is the foundation upon which all modern [[photoperiodism-flowering-plants|photoperiod]]
management in controlled-environment agriculture is built, and it applies equally to
food crops (like [[cannabis-photoperiodism-and-light-cycles|Cannabis]]) and ornamental
flowers.

## Spectral Specificity: Only Red Light Interrupts the Night

A further critical finding was that not just any light could disrupt the dark period
measurement. Only red light (approximately 660 nm) was effective at resetting the
plant's night-length counter. Blue or green flashes during the night had no effect on
flowering time. This spectral specificity pointed directly to the involvement of a
specific photoreceptor — later identified as [[phytochrome-photoreceptor|phytochrome]] —
rather than a general metabolic response to light energy or photosynthetic activity.

The red-light specificity also means that commercial night-break lighting can use
narrow-spectrum LEDs that target only the phytochrome absorption peak, minimizing
energy waste on wavelengths that have no photoperiodic effect. Modern greenhouse
operations routinely use red LED arrays for night interruption, consuming a fraction
of the energy that broad-spectrum incandescent or fluorescent lighting would require,
while achieving more reliable flowering control because the red wavelength precisely
targets the phytochrome switching mechanism.

## The Far-Red Reversal and the Light-Activated Switch

The most elegant discovery in photoperiodism research came when Harry Borthwick and
colleagues at the USDA — the same laboratory where [[maryland-mammoth-tobacco-photoperiodism|Maryland Mammoth]] was first studied — found that far-red light (approximately 730 nm, wavelengths
slightly longer than bright red) could completely reverse the effect of a red light
pulse. If irises were given red light in the middle of the night, they flowered; if
far-red light followed immediately, they did not flower as if they had never seen the
red pulse. If red light followed the far-red, flowering was induced again. This
red/far-red reversibility could be cycled indefinitely with just seconds of exposure to
each wavelength, functioning as a molecular light-activated switch.

Chamovitz frames this as a form of plant memory: the plant "remembers the last colour
it saw." On a more philosophical level, this bistable switching mechanism means that
the plant's developmental trajectory can be set or reset by momentary light cues, with
the final state determined by the most recent photon absorbed by phytochrome. This
[[chamovitz-red-far-red-phytochrome-switch-and-plant-memory|red-far-red memory]]
system is one of the most remarkable examples of molecular information storage in
biology, predating modern concepts of binary switches and flip-flop circuits by decades.

## Chrysanthemum Production: The Commercial Application

Chamovitz uses the chrysanthemum as the canonical example of commercial photoperiod
manipulation. Chrysanthemums are short-day plants that naturally flower in autumn as
days shorten. However, Mother's Day (a major market for chrysanthemums in North America)
falls in spring, when days are lengthening — precisely the wrong photoperiod for a
short-day plant. Without intervention, chrysanthemums cannot meet this market window.

Greenhouse growers solve this problem through night interruption: throughout autumn
and winter, lights are turned on for a few minutes each night, preventing the
chrysanthemums from perceiving short days and keeping them in vegetative growth.
Approximately two weeks before the target harvest date, the night-break lighting is
stopped, and the plants perceive the naturally shortening (or short) nights and begin
flowering in unison. This technique allows precise scheduling of flowering to meet
market demands, regardless of the natural photoperiod at any given time of year.

## Mechanism: Phytochrome's Two Stable Forms

Warren L. Butler and colleagues demonstrated that a single molecular photoreceptor —
[[phytochrome-discovery-butler-single-receptor-model|phytochrome]] — mediates both
the red and far-red responses. Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms:
Pr (absorbing red light, peak ~660 nm) and Pfr (absorbing far-red light, peak
~730 nm). Red light converts Pr to Pfr (the "active" form that promotes flowering
in long-day plants and inhibits it in short-day plants), while far-red light converts
Pfr back to Pr. In natural conditions, the last light of the day is enriched in far-red
(due to atmospheric scattering), creating the Pfr→Pr conversion that "turns off" the
plant's day-perception, while morning light is enriched in red, creating the Pr→Pfr
conversion that "turns on" day-perception.

## Distributed Perception: Leaves as the Photoperiodic Eye

Unlike [[phototropism-and-darwins-light-experiments|phototropism]], where the
light-sensing tip sends a directional signal to the responding stem, photoperiodic
perception is distributed across the leaves. Illuminating any single leaf with red
light in the middle of the night is sufficient to regulate flowering in the entire
plant, while a plant with all leaves removed cannot perceive the light cue at all.
This leaf-based perception initiates the production of a mobile floral signal — later
identified as FT (FLOWERING LOCUS T) protein in Arabidopsis — that travels through
the phloem to the shoot apical meristem and triggers the floral transition.
