# Fungal Biochemical Weathering Mechanisms

Fungi are among the most powerful biological agents of [[ectomycorrhizal-mineral-weathering-phosphate-mobilization-gadd]] on
Earth. Gadd and colleagues identify four distinct biochemical mechanisms by which
fungi dissolve minerals: acidolysis, complexolysis, redoxolysis, and
protonolysis. These mechanisms often operate simultaneously and synergistically,
enabling fungi to access nutrients locked in rock and mineral matrices.

## Why Fungi Excel at Mineral Weathering

Fungi possess several advantages over bacteria for [[dighton-fungal-rock-weathering-mineral-dissolution]]:

1. **Hyphal penetration**: Fungal hyphae can physically invade rock crevices,
   microfractures, and mineral grain boundaries, bringing their biochemical
   arsenal into direct contact with mineral surfaces that bacteria cannot reach.

2. **Wider pH range**: Fungi dissolve minerals effectively at both acidic and
   near-neutral pH values, whereas bacterial mineral dissolution is largely
   confined to acidic conditions.

3. **Broader redox capacity**: Fungal metabolism spans a wider range of
   oxidation-reduction conditions, enabling them to attack minerals through both
   oxidative and reductive chemistry.

4. **Colony-scale effects**: A single fungal colony can create localized
   microenvironments with dramatically different chemistry from the surrounding
   bulk soil — pH gradients, redox gradients, and organic acid concentration
   gradients that collectively enhance weathering.

5. **[[dighton-fungal-extracellular-enzyme-systems-decomposition]]**: Fungi secrete a diverse array of
   [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]] and organic acids that can attack mineral structures
   without requiring direct cellular contact.

## Acidolysis

Acidolysis is the dissolution of minerals through the action of organic acids
produced by fungal metabolism. Fungi excrete low-molecular-weight organic acids
including oxalic acid, citric acid, gluconic acid, malic acid, and fumaric acid.
These acids contribute protons (H⁺) that displace cations from mineral
structures, destabilizing the crystal lattice.

The general acidolysis reaction for a silicate mineral can be represented as:

    Mineral-M + nH⁺ → Mineral-(M-n) + Mⁿ⁺

Where M represents a metal cation (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺, Fe²⁺/³⁺) bound in
the mineral structure. Each proton displaces one cationic charge.

Oxalic acid is particularly important in fungal weathering because it is
produced in large quantities by many fungal species and can attack a wide
range of minerals. Oxalic acid is unique among fungal metabolites because it
can participate in both acidolysis (proton donation) and complexolysis (anion
complexation) simultaneously.

## Complexolysis

Complexolysis is mineral dissolution through the formation of soluble complexes
between metal cations and organic acid anions. When a fungal organic acid
dissociates, its anion (e.g., oxalate²⁻, citrate³⁻) can bind to metal cations
released from mineral surfaces, forming soluble chelate complexes. This
removes cations from solution, driving further dissolution according to Le
Chatelier's principle.

For oxalic acid, the complexolysis reaction takes the form:

    M²⁺ (mineral) + C₂O₄²⁻ → [M(C₂O₄)] (soluble complex)

The stability constants of these metal-organic complexes vary. Citrate forms
particularly strong complexes with iron and aluminum, making it effective
against iron- and aluminum-bearing minerals. Oxalate forms strong complexes
with calcium, making it highly effective against limestone, marble, and other
calcium-bearing rocks.

The combination of acidolysis and complexolysis makes oxalic acid an
exceptionally powerful weathering agent. Each oxalic acid molecule can
simultaneously donate two protons (acidolysis) and provide an oxalate anion
that complexes the released cation (complexolysis), effectively pulling the
dissolution reaction in both directions.

## Redoxolysis

Redoxolysis is mineral dissolution through oxidation-reduction reactions
mediated by fungal metabolism. Changing element oxidation states destabilizes
mineral structures.
