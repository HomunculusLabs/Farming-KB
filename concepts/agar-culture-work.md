# Agar Culture Work

Agar culture work is the practice of growing mushroom mycelium on a solid,
nutrient-rich gel medium in Petri dishes or similar containers. It is the
cornerstone of advanced mushroom cultivation, enabling the cultivator to isolate
clean strains from contaminated material, select for desirable genetic traits,
maintain long-term culture libraries, and propagate mycelium indefinitely
without genetic degradation. Working with agar elevates cultivation from a
reliance on commercial spore syringes to true tissue culture.

## What Is Agar

Agar is a gelatinous polysaccharide derived from red algae. In mycology, it
serves as a solid support matrix that holds water and nutrients in a stable,
accessible form. When combined with nutrients and poured into shallow dishes,
agar solidifies into a firm surface on which mycelium can grow visibly. The
advantage of agar over liquid or grain media is that individual colonies can be
observed and physically separated from contaminants.

Nutrient agar for mushroom cultivation typically contains agar powder, water,
and a carbohydrate source. Malt extract agar (MEA), made with malt extract powder
at 10 to 20 grams per liter, is the most widely used formulation. Potato dextrose
agar (PDA) is another common choice. Both provide sugars and minerals that
support robust mycelial growth. Additional supplements such as yeast extract or
peptone can be added for faster growth or for recovering stressed cultures.

## Preparing Agar Plates

Agar is prepared by dissolving the dry ingredients in water, bringing the
mixture to a boil to fully hydrate the agar, and then sterilizing the solution.
A standard batch uses 20 grams of agar powder and 20 grams of malt extract per
liter of water. The solution is sterilized in a pressure canner at 15 PSI for
30 to 45 minutes.

After sterilization, the agar solution is allowed to cool to approximately 120
to 130 degrees Fahrenheit, at which point it is still liquid but not hot enough
to generate excessive condensation in the dishes. Pouring is done inside a still
air box or in front of a laminar flow hood. Each standard 90 mm Petri dish
receives roughly 20 to 25 mL of agar, enough to form a layer 3 to 5 mm deep.
Deeper agar provides more moisture reserves but takes longer to cool and is more
prone to condensation issues.

After pouring, the lids are placed on the dishes and the agar is allowed to
solidify undisturbed for 30 to 60 minutes. Plates should then be stored upside
down in a clean container or bag at room temperature until use. Condensation on
the lids is normal and will resolve as the plates equilibrate. Plates with
excessive condensation can be dried by leaving the lids slightly cracked inside
the sterile workspace for a few hours.

## Inoculation Techniques

Several methods are used to transfer mycelium onto agar plates. Spore inoculation
involves streaking a spore solution across the agar surface, which germinates
into individual colonies that can be selected and isolated. This is the starting
point for most cultivators working from wild specimens or purchased spores.

Tissue culture involves taking a small piece of inner mushroom tissue from a
fresh fruiting body and placing it on the agar surface. The tissue is already
the genetic product of a single mycelial individual, so tissue culture captures
the exact genetics of the parent mushroom, unlike spore germination which
produces new genetic combinations.

Transfer from an existing agar plate involves cutting a small wedge of colonized
agar, typically 5 to 10 mm square, and placing it cut-side down on a fresh
plate. This is the standard method for expanding a clean culture, cleaning up a
contaminated plate by transferring healthy mycelium away from the contaminant,
and maintaining a culture over successive generations.

## Isolation and Strain Selection

When mycelium is grown from spores on agar, multiple distinct colonies appear,
each representing a unique genetic individual. The cultivator can observe the
growth characteristics of each colony and select the one that displays the most
vigorous rhizomorphic growth, fastest colonization speed, and strongest
resistance to contamination. This process of selection is how superior strains
are developed.

Rhizomorphic mycelium, characterized by thick, rope-like strands that radiate
outward, is generally preferred over tomentose mycelium, which appears as a
flat, fuzzy mat. Rhizomorphic growth is associated with faster colonization and
more aggressive fruiting. However, some species naturally display tomentose
growth even in their most vigorous strains.

## Contamination Identification

Agar plates make contamination visible in a way that grain jars do not. Bacterial
contamination appears as wet, glossy, or slimy patches, often with a milky or
iridescent sheen. Mold contamination appears as fuzzy growth in colors other
than white, most commonly green, black, pink, or yellow. Yeast contamination
appears as small, round, raised colonies that may be white or cream-colored.

When contamination is found on a plate, the standard response is to transfer a
clean wedge of mycelium from the leading edge of growth, as far from the
contaminant as possible, onto a fresh plate. This may need to be repeated over
several successive transfers to achieve a completely clean culture.

## Long-Term Storage

Agar cultures can be stored in a refrigerator at 35 to 38 degrees Fahrenheit for
3 to 6 months before the agar dries out or the mycelium degrades. For longer
storage, cultures can be transferred to slant tubes, which have a smaller
surface area and thus lose moisture more slowly. For storage of a year or more,
cultures can be frozen in glycerol solution or preserved on sterilized distilled
water, techniques that effectively put the mycelium into suspended animation.
