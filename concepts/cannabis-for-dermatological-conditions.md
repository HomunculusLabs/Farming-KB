# Cannabis for Dermatological Conditions

## Overview

The skin possesses its own functional endocannabinoid system, with CB1 and CB2 receptors present on keratinocytes, sebocytes, hair follicles, and immune cells throughout the dermis. The Cannabis Health Index (Blesching) describes how topical and systemic cannabis applications can address dermatological conditions including psoriasis, eczema, acne, and general skin inflammation. The endocannabinoid system of the skin in health and disease offers novel perspectives and therapeutic opportunities for conditions that are often resistant to conventional single-molecule treatments.

## The Cutaneous Endocannabinoid System

The [[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]] in skin is remarkably comprehensive. Biro et al. (2009) described the endocannabinoid system of the skin in health and disease as offering novel perspectives and therapeutic opportunities. Key features include:

- **CB1 receptors** on nerve endings, hair follicles, and sebaceous glands regulate pain sensation, itching, and sebaceous oil production
- **CB2 receptors** concentrated on skin immune cells (mast cells, Langerhans cells) regulate inflammation and immune response
- **Keratinocytes** produce their own endocannabinoids (anandamide and 2-AG)
- The skin ECS regulates cell growth, differentiation, and apoptosis -- fundamental processes in skin health and disease
- Disruption of the cutaneous ECS is implicated in psoriasis, eczema, acne, itching, and hair growth disorders
- The cutaneous ECS is so comprehensive that neuro-dermatologists have written: "Topical cannabinoid agonists represent a new, promising class of anti-inflammatory agents"

## Conditions

### Psoriasis

Psoriasis involves hyperproliferation of keratinocytes and chronic inflammation mediated by TNF-alpha and other pro-inflammatory cytokines. Cannabinoids (especially CBD) inhibit keratinocyte proliferation and promote normal cell differentiation. CB2 receptor activation reduces the inflammatory cytokine cascade in psoriatic plaques. Topical cannabis preparations applied directly to plaques can reduce scaling, redness, and itching. THC and CBD work synergistically: THC addresses itching and pain while CBD targets inflammation and cell proliferation. CBD has been shown to decrease bone resorption by inhibiting RANK/RANKL expression and pro-inflammatory cytokines -- a mechanism also relevant to psoriatic joint involvement. The [[cannabis-minor-cannabinoids]] detail specific cannabinoid ratios most effective for inflammatory skin conditions.

### Eczema (Atopic Dermatitis)

Cannabis reduces the inflammatory response and itching that characterize eczema. CBD has been shown to reduce mast cell degranulation, decreasing histamine release. The Blesching source references research linking specific attitudes and emotions to dermatological conditions, supporting the psychosomatic component of eczema flares. Topical applications provide localized relief without systemic effects. Patients report reduced need for topical corticosteroids, which carry risks of skin thinning with long-term use. Stander et al. (2006) documented topical cannabinoid efficacy for pruritus, providing clinical evidence for this application.

### Acne

Sebaceous gland activity is regulated by CB1 and CB2 receptors. CBD has been shown to normalize sebaceous gland lipid production (sebum), addressing the excess sebum that drives acne formation. Yosipovitch et al. (2007) demonstrated the connection between psychological stress, sebum production, and acne vulgaris in adolescents -- suggesting that cannabis's combined sebum-regulating and stress-reducing effects may be particularly relevant. Anti-inflammatory effects reduce the redness and swelling of acne lesions. Antimicrobial properties of certain cannabis terpenes (pinene, limonene) may help with bacterial colonization. THC may increase sebum production via CB1 -- CBD-dominant topicals are preferred for acne.

### Itching (Pruritus)

Pruritus is one of the most distressing dermatological symptoms and a major feature of eczema, psoriasis, and kidney disease. The cutaneous ECS modulates itch through CB1 receptors on sensory nerve fibers in the skin. Activation of CB1 on these nerve endings reduces the transmission of itch signals to the spinal cord. Topical cannabinoids have shown promise for intractable pruritus that has not responded to conventional antihistamines. Stander, Reinhardt, and Luger (2006) documented the efficacy of topical cannabinoid agonists for pruritus treatment.

### Seborrheic Dermatitis

Seborrheic dermatitis involves inflammation of sebaceous gland-rich areas. Given that CB1 and CB2 receptors regulate sebaceous gland function and inflammation, topical cannabinoids may help modulate the excessive sebum production and inflammatory response characteristic of this condition.

### Skin Cancer Prevention

Research from Casanova et al. (2003) demonstrated that activation of cannabinoid receptors inhibits skin tumor growth and angiogenesis in vivo. The skin ECS's role in regulating cell proliferation and apoptosis suggests a protective function against malignant transformation. While clinical applications remain pre-clinical, this research supports the broader anti-cancer potential of cannabinoids documented in [[cannabis-cancer-symptom-management]].

## Hair Growth Disorders

The cutaneous ECS also regulates hair follicle function. CB1 receptor activation has been implicated in hair follicle regression (catagen phase), suggesting that CB1 antagonists or CB2-predominant preparations might support hair growth. Conversely, unwanted hair growth (hirsutism) may be modulated through CB1 activation. Biro et al. (2009) identified the endocannabinoid system as a key regulator of hair follicle biology, opening therapeutic possibilities for both hair loss and hirsutism.

## Wound Healing and Skin Repair

Cannabis topicals have antioxidant properties that protect against environmental damage. The anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects of cannabinoids support wound healing by reducing excessive inflammation that can impede tissue repair. CB2 activation in skin immune cells modulates the inflammatory phase of wound healing, potentially promoting faster resolution. Terpenes in full-spectrum preparations (bisabolol, linalool, beta-caryophyllene) contribute their own wound-healing and skin-soothing properties. Once CB2 is activated, biological changes take place that strengthen the immune system, reduce severe inflammations and swelling, stimulate deep wound healing, and produce analgesic effects.

A 2009 Worcester meta-analysis reviewing all classes of cannabinoids confirmed that cannabinoids and non-cannabinoid parts of the plant effectively reduce pain from inflammation associated with post-surgery wounds, supporting the use of cannabis topicals in wound care.

## Anti-Inflammatory Mechanisms in Skin

The skin's inflammatory response involves mast cell degranulation, histamine release, cytokine cascades, and leukocyte infiltration. Cannabinoids address this multi-step process through several mechanisms: CB2 activation on mast cells reduces degranulation and histamine release; CBD inhibits NF-kB and reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine production; and terpenes like beta-caryophyllene provide additional anti-inflammatory activity through CB2 agonism. This multi-target approach makes cannabis uniquely suited for inflammatory skin conditions that resist single-molecule treatments. See [[cannabis-for-inflammatory-conditions]] for shared anti-inflammatory pathways.

## Topical Application

Topicals act locally without producing psychoactive effects -- even THC-based topicals do not enter the bloodstream in significant amounts. This is because the cannabinoid molecules are too large to penetrate through the skin layers into systemic circulation. Balms, salves, creams, and oils are common delivery methods. Topical creams are used to deliver medicine to specific and isolated problem areas, with absorption rates similar to ingestion but less systemic and more local. See [[cannabis-topical-applications]] for formulation guidance. Consistent application (2-3 times daily) is recommended for chronic conditions. Patch testing before widespread use is advised. Full-spectrum preparations are preferred for the entourage effect.

## Clinical Evidence for Topical Preparations

While large-scale randomized controlled trials for topical cannabinoids remain limited, the clinical evidence is growing. Patient-reported outcomes consistently show improvements in pain, itching, and skin appearance across multiple dermatological conditions. The Biro et al. (2009) review in *Trends in Pharmacological Sciences* established the scientific foundation for targeting the cutaneous ECS. Stander et al. (2006) provided early clinical evidence for topical cannabinoid agonists in treating pruritus. Commercially available topical CBD products have proliferated rapidly, though quality and consistency vary significantly. Patients are advised to seek third-party lab-tested products with verified cannabinoid content.

## Complementary Skin Support

Turmeric, myrrh, coconut oil, and (E)-beta-caryophyllene-containing spices (black pepper, clove, cinnamon, rosemary) all demonstrate skin-supportive properties that may complement cannabinoid therapy. Aloe vera and calendula are traditional topical supports that pair well with cannabis preparations.
