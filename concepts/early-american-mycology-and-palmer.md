# Early American Mycology and Julius Palmer

## The State of American Mycology in the Late 19th Century

[[julius-auboineau-palmer]] Jr. was one of the earliest American mycologists to seriously study the edibility and toxicity of mushrooms. His work, published primarily in the 1870s and 1880s, represented some of the first original contributions to [[palmer-mushroom-charts-american-mycological-education]] literature. In his introduction, Palmer notes that his book was only the second ever printed in America on the subject of fungi edibility. The first was his own earlier work, published through the enterprise of Messrs. [[louis-prang]] & Co., the well-known lithographers.

The field was so neglected that Palmer worked largely in isolation. He writes that "save for the use of text-books this study was prosecuted by me without sympathy or companionship." His long days in the woods were accompanied only by "the moan of the wind through the lofty pine or the rustle of oak-leaves above which the little toadstool showed its modest head." This isolation meant that conclusions had to be verified through direct [[palmer-mushroom-cooking-and-personal-experience]] rather than peer review.

## Three Critical Fungi Problems

When Palmer began his studies, no hint of his later conclusions existed in any writer. Three fundamental problems defined the state of knowledge:

1. **Amanitine absorption**: The deadly toxins of *Amanita* species were absorbed by the human system in ways not yet understood. The standard purgative and emetic treatments were not only useless but actually aggravating in cases of [[amanitine-poisoning-and-absorption-mechanisms]].

2. **Emetic Russula acridity**: The acrid, peppery taste of many *Russula* species caused digestive disturbance, leading to their blanket classification as "poisonous" — a designation Palmer considered misleading since many were merely unpalatable rather than toxic.

3. **Decomposition and fermentation**: Common edible mushrooms in a state of decomposition or fermentation caused illness, leading to confusion about which species were genuinely poisonous versus which were simply spoiled.

## Palmer's Mentors and Supporters

Palmer acknowledges several key figures in his work. A close friend who served as chemist to the Havemeyer sugar refineries provided weekly encouragement for over ten years. Palmer credits this friend's "clear, thorough, and educated mind" with supplying insights his own "more superficial nature lacked." The medical profession received such significant acknowledgment that Palmer dedicated the work to them.

He also credits the press for welcoming his communications and allowing reprints, as well as public officials who granted him access to State, city, and hospital grounds for collecting fungi — areas otherwise closed to the public.

## The Boletus Subtomentosus Case

One of Palmer's most instructive experiences involved the *[[boletus-subtomentosus]]* (the olive-red Boletus of Mr. Peck's catalogue). He had eaten "some bushels" of this mushroom from the same location, but specimens plucked after heavy rain or dew exposure caused illness in three nearly simultaneous cases.

Palmer identified the cause as chemical change: the mushroom has a marked acid reaction under litmus paper, and when heavily charged with water, its spongy substance enters a state of "acidulous fermentation." During this state, the mushroom exhibits brilliant blue coloration when pressed, cut, or broken. Mr. Casamajor confirmed the blue matter was soluble but could not explain why it appeared upon exposure to air.

The lesson, according to Palmer: "Not that the mushroom is unfit for food, but that none but young specimens with substance too solid to admit of fermentation should ever be eaten."

## Berkeley's Alkali Theory

Palmer initially appeared to endorse the idea of M.J. Berkeley that mushrooms could become poisonous from an excess of alkali. However, he later carefully reconsidered and rejected this theory in an article contributed to the *Boston Medical and Surgical Journal*, as noted on page sixty-five of his published volume.

## The Taste-Test Theory

Palmer found that the theory of using taste as a guide for rejecting non-esculent mushrooms was only applicable to certain families — specifically the Russule, Boleti, Clavarie, and doubtless others. It was "utterly worthless, even illusive, when applied to the deadly Amanite." The deadly *Amanita* species could taste pleasant while containing lethal toxins.

It was not until after 1877 that Palmer's attention was directed to the Amanita as possessing properties fundamentally different from the Lactarii or Boleti. Even the best authorities, with one exception (a hint in M.C. Cooke's popular manual), spoke of these families in similar terms regarding their effects on humans.

## The Truffle Question in America

Palmer devoted considerable attention to the question of whether truffles (*Tuber* species) were indigenous to America. The truffle differs from all other mushrooms in that both the spawn and the fruiting body remain below the soil surface. He proposed an interesting experiment: importing from France the swine and dogs already trained to detect truffles, and releasing them in American oak plantations to determine whether the fungus grew natively.

Isolated cases of truffle specimens had been reported found in America, but Palmer could not determine the reliability of these reports. Those interested in learning more were directed to the *Revue des Deux Mondes* for April 1st and 15th, 1875, for a thorough review of the truffle's nature and cultivation.

## Palmer's Original Classification Key

Perhaps Palmer's most lasting contribution was his entirely original classification key for determining the edibility of large, fleshy fungi. This key organized mushrooms into series based on observable characteristics — the nature of the spore-bearing surface (gills, pores, teeth, or enclosed), color changes upon bruising, presence of milk or juice, taste reactions, and other macroscopic features. This practical key was designed specifically for those "commencing the study of these plants with a view to their properties, esculent or poisonous."

## Errors and Corrections

Palmer candidly acknowledged errors in his earlier work. The most notable was his condemnation of *[[coprinus-micaceus]]* as non-esculent — he later recognized it as perfectly safe and important due to its repeated crops. He attributed the original error to contamination in a stew that contained non-deliquescent mushrooms from the sub-genera *Naucoria* and *Stropharia*.

## Legacy

Palmer's work represents a crucial transitional period in American mycology — moving from reliance on European authorities and folk wisdom toward systematic, experience-based study of native fungi. His insistence on direct observation, his rejection of oversimplified identification rules, and his original classification scheme laid groundwork for the more rigorous American mycology that would follow through the work of Charles H. Peck and others.
