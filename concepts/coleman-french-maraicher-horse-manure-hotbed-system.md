# French Maraîcher Horse Manure Hotbed System

## Overview

The French maraîcher (market gardener) system of nineteenth-century Paris represents one of the most productive and sophisticated [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]] methods ever developed. Central to this system was the manure hotbed — a technique using the heat generated by decomposing horse manure to warm growing beds and produce fresh vegetables throughout winter. Eliot Coleman documents this system extensively in The Winter Harvest Handbook as historical inspiration for modern unheated winter growing. The hotbed system allowed Parisian growers to defy the cold and produce fresh salads in January and early cucumbers and melons in May and June.

## Historical Context

The system reached its peak between 1850 and 1900 in Paris, where [[coleman-french-maraicher-parisian-intensive-market-gardening]] gardens covered approximately 600 hectares (about 1,500 acres) — roughly 6% of the land within the city limits. These gardens made Paris self-sufficient in fresh vegetables year-round and even exported produce to England. The system was powered by the manure of an estimated 100,000+ horses that provided the city's transportation, creating a remarkable example of urban [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]].

The cultural roots of the system trace back even further, to the royal potager (vegetable garden) at Versailles under head gardener La Quintinie in the 1670s and 1680s, who developed early forms of [[coleman-protected-cultivation]] using glass frames.

## The Hotbed Principle

A manure hotbed works by harnessing the exothermic (heat-producing) process of microbial decomposition:

1. Fresh horse manure mixed with straw is piled in a pit or frame
2. Mesophilic and then thermophilic bacteria colonize the manure, breaking down organic matter
3. The [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] of these microorganisms generates heat, with internal temperatures reaching 100-160°F (38-71°C)
4. This heat radiates upward into a growing bed placed on top of the manure
5. Glass frames (cloches or sashes) cover the bed, trapping solar heat and creating a warm microclimate
6. The combined heat from below (manure) and above (solar gain through glass) allows plant growth during cold months

## Construction and Design

### The Manure Base

The foundation of every hotbed was the manure pile itself:

- **Manure source**: Fresh horse manure mixed with straw bedding from city stables, delivered daily by wagons returning from vegetable deliveries
- **Preparation**: The manure was stacked in a compact heap 2-3 feet deep and allowed to begin heating. When the initial heating rush subsided (typically 3-7 days), it was turned and packed into the hotbed frame
- **Volume requirements**: Coleman notes that the system used 100 to 400 tons of horse manure per acre, depending on the number of hotbeds in operation
- **Manure in walkways**: Decomposing manure was also filled into the walkways between glass-covered frames, adding supplementary heat to the entire growing area

### The Frame

- **Material**: Traditionally wooden frames holding single-pane glass lights (sashes)
- **Size**: Typical frames were 3 × 6 feet, with the glass sloping toward the south to maximize [[savory-energy-flow-and-solar-capture]]
- **Insulation**: On cold nights, one-inch-thick rye straw mats were rolled out over the glass for extra insulation. Some operations had thousands of these mats
- **Ventilation**: Each frame had to be individually vented on sunny days using small notched sticks to prop up one edge. Operations with 3,000+ cloches required enormous daily labor

### The Growing Bed

- **Soil**: Rich, dark compost made from previously spent hotbed manure, mixed with garden soil
- **Depth**: 4-6 inches of growing medium on top of the heated manure base
- **[[cervantes-reservoir-temperature-management]]**: By regulating the depth of the manure, the timing of loading, and the ventilation of the glass, growers could maintain growing-bed temperatures of 50-65°F (10-18°C) even when outdoor temperatures dropped well below freezing

## The Manure-to-Compost Cycle

One of the system's most elegant features was its circular material flow:

1. Fresh horse manure delivered from city stables
2. Manure used as heating fuel in hotbeds for 6-8 weeks
3. Once heat production declined, the spent manure (now partially composted) was shoveled out
4. This crumbly compost was used as soil amendment on outdoor beds
5. The cycle repeated continuously throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]

Coleman observed that "compost made from a mixture of horse manure and straw is the best of all for [[fukuoka-natural-vegetable-growing]]," a conclusion the French maraîchers strongly shared. Many growers considered composted horse manure the only suitable manure for vegetable production.

## Intensive Cropping on Hotbeds

The French growers maximized production through remarkable intercropping sequences:

- **Example sequence**: An early spring hotbed would be broadcast-sown with radish and carrot seed, then interplanted with lettuce transplants at the same time
- **Harvest timing**: Radishes harvested first, freeing space for carrots growing between the lettuces
- **Sequential planting**: Once lettuces were harvested, young cauliflower transplants were set out among the carrots
- **Continuous production**: After carrot harvest, cauliflowers had the frame to themselves until ground preparation for the next crops

This level of intensive year-round production yielded at least four and usually up to eight harvests per year from the same piece of ground.

## Tools and Labor

The system required specialized tools and enormous human labor:

- **Hottes**: Woven willow basket backpacks with extensions arching over the worker's head. Manure was loaded into these and deposited by bending forward and letting it tumble over the head onto the beds. Narrow 10-inch paths made wheelbarrow use impossible
- **Straw mats**: Rye straw mats, 1 inch thick, used for overnight insulation of glass frames. Some growers had thousands of mats and dedicated laborers just for rolling them on and off each day
- **Bell jars (cloches)**: 18-inch-diameter glass bell jars used for crops not in hotbeds. Each cloche had to be individually vented and could be covered with straw mats during cold periods
- **Labor**: The system required constant daily attention — venting, covering, uncovering, watering, transplanting, harvesting. It was skilled, demanding work

## Decline of the System

The French maraîcher system declined in the early [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]] due to converging forces:

- **Rise of the automobile**: Horses disappeared from cities, eliminating the manure supply that powered the hotbeds
