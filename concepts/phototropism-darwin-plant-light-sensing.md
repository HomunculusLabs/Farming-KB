## Phototropism and Darwin's Plant Light Experiments

## Overview

toward lateral light. This universal behaviour begged a fundamental question: which part
of the plant actually "sees" the light, and how does the plant translate that perception
into coordinated movement?

## The Canary Grass Experiments

To answer this question, the two Darwins designed a remarkably simple experiment using
canary grass (*Phalaris canariensis*). They grew seedlings in a completely dark room
for several days to eliminate any pre-existing light cues or directional biases. They
then lit a very small gas lamp approximately twelve feet (3.5 metres) away — so dim
that the experimenters themselves could not see the seedlings or read a pencil line on
paper. Yet after only three hours of exposure to this minimal light source, the
seedlings had clearly curved toward it. Crucially, the curving did not occur at the tip
but about an inch (two centimetres) below it, in the midsection. The part detecting
light was not the same part that bent. Something in the tip was sensing light direction
and sending a signal downward, instructing the midsection to elongate differentially.

## The Five Seedling Scenarios

To rigorously test the hypothesis that the tip was the plant's "eye," the Darwins
designed a classic experiment with five different treatment groups:

**Seedling A — Untreated control.** Left completely intact, this seedling bent toward
the light, confirming the setup was conducive to phototropism and providing a baseline
for all other treatments.

**Seedling B — Tip removed.** The tip was pruned off before light exposure. This plant
did not bend at all. Without its tip, the seedling was effectively blind, even though
the midsection that normally performs the bending was intact.

**Seedling E — Midsection covered with a lightproof tube.** The bending zone was
wrapped in an opaque tube while the tip remained fully exposed. This seedling bent
normally, proving the midsection does not need to perceive light directly.
