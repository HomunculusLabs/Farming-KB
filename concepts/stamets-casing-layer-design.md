Casing is the practice of covering the substrate surface with a layer of moist material having specific structural characteristics. It encourages fruiting and enhances yield potential in many, but not all, cultivated mushroom species.

## Functions of the Casing Layer

1. **Protect from drying**: Mycelium is extremely sensitive to dry air. The casing protects the exposed cropping surface. Moisture loss from substrate is also reduced.

2. **Provide humid microclimate**: The casing provides a moist environment where mycelium develops an extensive, healthy network -- a platform supporting primordia formation and growth.

3. **Water reservoir**: Mushroom enlargement strongly depends on available water. The casing supplies sufficient moisture for successive flushes, especially important during heavy flushes when mushrooms compete for water.

4. **Support beneficial microorganisms**: Select groups of microorganisms (especially Pseudomonas putida) result in increased primordia formation, earlier cropping, and higher yields. A sterilized casing lacks these beneficial microorganisms and has little resistance to contaminants.

## Properties of a Good Casing

### Water Retention
Must absorb and release substantial quantities of water, sustaining both vegetative growth and successive generations of fruitbodies.

### Structure
Surface must be porous and open despite repeated waterings. Small moist cavities protect developing primordia and allow metabolic gas diffusion. If the surface becomes closed, gases build up and inhibit primordia formation.

### Microflora
High levels of Pseudomonas putida bacteria increase primordia formation. Dense/deep casing layers yield more because they slow gas diffusion, building up CO2 prior to primordia formation. These same bacteria give the casing natural resistance to competitors.

### Nutritive Value
Casing should have LOW nutritional value. A nutritive casing supports a broader range of competitor molds. Wood fragments and undecomposed plant matter are prime sites for mold growth and should be screened out.

### pH
Agaricus brunnescens prefers pH 7.0-7.5. Even at pH 7.5 when first applied, it gradually falls to nearly 6.0 by end of cropping due to acids secreted by mycelium. Buffer with limestone flour to counter this acidification.

### Hygienic Quality
Must be free of pests, pathogens, and extraneous debris. Nematodes and insect larvae must not be present.

## Casing Materials

### Soil Components
- **Sand**: Large particles, large air spaces, well aerated, heavy, holds little water
- **Clay**: Minute particles bound in aggregations, few air pockets, structurally "closed", holds water well
- **Loam**: Mixture of sand and clay with high humus content

Agaricus growers found clay/loam to be the best type. Problems: contamination risk from fungi and nematodes, and loss of structure after repeated waterings. Reduce contamination by pasteurizing (steamed 2 hours at 160F) or baking (oven 2 hours at 160F).

### Peat-Based Casings
Practically eliminated the use of soil in mushroom culture:
- Peat is highly decomposed plant matter, pH 3.5-4.5
- Acidic condition precludes many contaminants -- considered fairly "clean"
- Rarely requires pasteurization
- Must add calcium buffering agent (limestone) to raise pH
- Coarse fibrous peat preferred (holds structure better than fine peat)
- Liming causes peat particle aggregation, giving structure similar to clay/loam

### Buffering Agents
1. **Chalk** (used extensively in Europe): soft texture, holds water well, chunks from dust to 1 inch improve structure, long-lasting buffering
2. **Limestone flour** (most widely used in US): 97% CaCO3, less than 2% magnesium
3. **Limestone grit** (no. 9): excellent structural additive, low buffering ability
4. **Dolomitic limestone**: rarely used due to high magnesium (depresses mycelial growth)
5. **Marl**: soft lime from dry lake bottoms, clay + CaCO3, good water holding
6. **Oyster shell**: CaCO3, similar to limestone grit but should not be sole buffering agent (low solubility)

## Casing Formulas

### Formula 1
- Coarse peat: 4 parts
- Limestone flour: 1 part
- Limestone grit: 1/2 part
- Water: approximately 2-2.25 parts

### Formula 2
- Coarse peat: 2 parts
- Chalk or Marl: 1 part
- Water: approximately 1-1.25 parts

Optional: add 1/2 to 1 part coarse vermiculite (pre-soaked to saturation) to improve water retention, especially on thinly laid substrates.

## Application

### Moisture
- Target: 90% of saturation level (2-4% below saturation)
- Peat-based casings: balanced to 70-75% moisture content
- Method: remove 10-20% of dry mix volume, saturate remaining 80-90%, add back the dry material

### Depth
- **General rule**: the more mushrooms expected per sq. ft., the deeper the casing
- Minimum on bulk substrates: 1 inch absolute minimum
- 6-8 inch deep substrates: cased 1.25-1.5 inches
- Substrates deeper than 8 inches: cased 1.5-2 inches
- Sterilized grain (shallow layers): 3/4 to 1 inch
- Dutch research showed 2-inch casing supported more microorganisms and produced more mushrooms than 1-inch

### Evenness
- Apply evenly on a level substrate surface
- Use "depth rings" (PVC pipe cut to casing depth) as guides for large areas
- Surface should remain rough and porous with small "mountains and valleys" -- key to optimum pinhead formation

## Casing Colonization

Environmental conditions same as during spawn running:
- Substrate temperature in optimum range for mycelial growth
- Relative humidity: 90-100%
- Fresh air kept to minimum (only to offset overheating)
- CO2 build-up is beneficial
- If fresh air cannot be controlled, place plastic sheet over casing to prevent moisture loss

After casing, substrate temperatures surge upward due to hampered gas diffusion. This is a positive sign of mycelial vitality if room temperature can be controlled.
