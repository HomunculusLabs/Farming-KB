## Fukuoka Do-Nothing Farming and Spiritual Life

Book V of *The One-Straw Revolution* explores the spiritual dimension of Fukuoka"s philosophy -- the paradox of advocating "do-nothing" farming while recognising the hard physical work it actually requires, and his [[natural-farming-philosophy]] in miniature -- a community living close to nature, growing [[fukuoka-night-heron-revelation-origin-story]] cried sharply and flew away. In that instant: "All my doubts and the gloomy mist of my confusion vanished. Everything I had held in firm conviction was swept away with the wind. "In this world there is nothing at all.'"

The next day he resigned from his job, wandered Japan for a time, and eventually returned to his father"s farm to put his realization into practice. This experience, which he calls understanding "nothing," became the foundation of everything that followed.

## Discriminating vs. Non-Discriminating Knowledge

"People study because they think they do not understand, but studying is not going to help one to understand. They study hard only to find out in the end that people cannot know anything."

Fukuoka argues that what people call "knowing" is merely superficial familiarity:
- The astronomer knows the names of stars but not the stars themselves
- The botanist knows classifications but not the living plant
- The artist knows aesthetics but not nature itself
- "The more involved they become with the activity of the intellect, the more they set themselves apart"

**The paradox**: All one must do to know nature is to realise that one does not really know anything. When discriminating knowledge is abandoned, non-discriminating knowledge arises of itself. "There is no other way than through the destruction of the ego, casting aside the thought that humans exist apart from heaven and earth."

He illustrates this with a story about children: "People think they understand things because they become familiar with them. This is only superficial knowledge." A person can analyse a butterfly as far as they like, but they cannot make a butterfly.

## Who Is the Fool?

Fukuoka tells the story of children choosing their leader: not the clever one who tricks others out of snacks, but the one "who laughs happily by the side of the road." He uses Aesop"s fable of the frogs: when they asked God for a king, first they got a log (good), then they asked for a greater king and got a crane that ate them all.

His prescription for leadership: "A dumb log" -- someone like daruma-san, the weighted toy that always sits back up when pushed over. "Knowing that you should keep your hands and feet folded, he scowls silently at the people who want to stick theirs out."

The title of the chapter says it all: "Foolishness Comes Out Looking Smart." Fukuoka argues that the clever person is "clever in the wrong way, someone who would oppress others," while the one who goes about their own business, eats and sleeps well, lives in the most satisfactory manner. "There is no one so great as the one who does not try to accomplish anything."

## Against Development

"Why do you have to develop? If economic growth rises from 5% to 10%, is happiness going to double? What"s wrong with a growth rate of 0%? Isn't this a rather stable kind of economics? Could there be anything better than living simply and taking it easy?"

Fukuoka"s critique: "The more people do, the more society develops, and the more problems arise. The increasing desolation of nature, the exhaustion of resources, the uneasiness and disintegration of the human spirit, all have been brought about by humanity"s trying to accomplish something."

He connects this directly to farming: "These thirty years have taught me that farmers would have been better off doing almost nothing at all." The farmer became too busy when people began to investigate the world and decided it would be "good" to do this or that. All of Fukuoka"s research went in the opposite direction: "How about not doing this? How about not doing that?"

## On Life and Death

Using the cycle of rice as metaphor: planted in spring, the seed sends out shoots, reaped in autumn it appears to die, but the ritual repeats year after year. "You could say that the rice we are cutting now lives continuously."

The same cycle occurs within the human body -- tens of thousands of cells die and are born each day, blood replaced monthly. If this continuous cycle can be experienced and savoured each day, nothing more is necessary. But people cling to life as already experienced, creating fear of death, and forget they are living on the earth here and now.

"In nature, there is life and death, and nature is joyful. In human society, there is life and death, and people live in sorrow." The difference is that people divide experience with the discriminating mind, seeing life and death as opposites rather than aspects of a single continuous process.

## Drifting Clouds and the Illusion of Science

Fukuoka uses the image of a simple scene -- washing citrus boxes by the river, red sumac leaves against blue sky -- to show how the scientific mind fragments experience. One path leads to philosophy and religion (looking deeply into the one who asks "What is nature?"). The other path divides the scene into water, current, waves, wind, clouds -- each becoming an object of investigation spreading endlessly in all directions. "This is the path of science."

He concludes: "In trying to gain a clear understanding of what it is that fills him with wonder, a man heads into a world of endless questioning." The world used to be simple; from the time people undertook to explain one drop of dew scientifically, "they trapped themselves in the endless hell of the intellect."

## The Village Without War and Peace

Fukuoka extends his philosophy to questions of conflict and peace. When a snake seizes a frog and a boy throws a rock at the snake, three young people offer three interpretations: nature as competition (strong consume weak), nature as cooperation (mutual benefit), and nature as balance (strong take only what they need). Fukuoka denies all three: "The world itself never asks whether it is based upon a principle of competition or of cooperation."

He argues that the concepts of self and other, strong and weak, attack and defence, are all products of the discriminating mind. "The act of defence is already an attack. Weapons for self-defence always give a pretext to those who instigate wars." The path to peace: "Sharpening the sickle instead of the sword."
