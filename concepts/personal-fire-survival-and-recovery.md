## Personal Fire Survival and Recovery

Surviving [[wildfire]] requires both physical preparation and psychological readiness. Most fire casualties are not caused by direct flame contact but by radiant heat, toxic smoke inhalation, and asphyxiation. Understanding survival mechanics and preparing accordingly can mean the difference between life and death.

## Immediate Physical Survival

### Protective Actions During Fire Front Passage
The primary survival strategy during a wildfire front is to shelter from radiant heat:

1. **Cover the body** with wool or cotton clothing — synthetic fabrics melt and burn
2. **Shelter behind** a solid object: tree, car, house wall, or in a trench
3. **Ideally, cape the whole body in a wet blanket** for maximum protection
4. **Wait until flames have passed completely** before moving
5. **Move cautiously onto already-burnt ground** — this is the safest terrain

### Critical Warning: Water Hazards During Firestorms
In [[firestorm]] conditions, oxygen is periodically exhausted and the fire briefly goes out. During these moments, people lose consciousness from asphyxiation. For this reason:

- **Never take refuge in small water tanks, dams, or small rivers** — unconscious people will drown
- The **sea** and **large rivers** (approximately 100 m or more across) are safe to run to
- Beware of fainting from lack of oxygen even near large water bodies
- The skin itself needs oxygen; prolonged oxygen-depleted environments are dangerous even near water

### Hydration
Drink large quantities of water to prevent dehydration. Ensure children and stock animals also receive water to replace lost body fluids. Dehydration significantly reduces survival chances.

## Pre-Fire Preparation Checklist

Preparation well before fire season is the most effective survival strategy. The following actions should be completed as part of annual fire readiness:

### Water Preparation
- Fill **fish ponds** and metal buckets with water
- **Fill roof gutters** (or plug and flood them — plugs should be chained to gutters near downpipes)
- **Fill baths and sinks** with water for emergency use during fire passage
- Ensure **ample stored water** both indoors and outdoors for fighting spot fires

### Building Preparation
- **Remove doormats** (inflammable)
- **Remove flammable materials** from around the house exterior
- **Hose down the garden** thoroughly before fire arrives
- Fit **fine metal mesh screens** on all windows, vents, and openings
- **White-paint exterior walls** to reduce radiation absorption

### Clothing and Personal Items
- Prepare **wool or cotton garments** to wear during the fire (synthetics are dangerous)
- Take in **outside clothing** that could catch embers
- Prepare **blankets** (preferably soaked) for body covering
- Prepare **important papers** for quick pickup via the rear door

### Stock and Animals
- **Tie up or pen stock animals** in safe refuge areas
- **Muster stock** into close-grazed paddocks or near settlement
- Ensure **chickens are in solid housing** (they survive well; their scratching creates bare fire-safe zones around sheds)
- **Part-bury chicken sheds** in earth banks for complete fire protection

## Post-Fire Immediate Response

### For Individuals and Families
After the fire front has passed:
- **Move cautiously** — burnt ground may still be hot and unstable
- **Extinguish spot fires** around the property using stored water and buckets
- **Check for smouldering materials** in roof spaces, gutters, and underfloor areas
- Stay alert for **re-ignition** from wind-blown embers

### Community Emergency Response
Civil authorities and emergency services should implement:

#### Radio Communications
- Maintain **constant fire reports** on fire direction and speed
- Broadcast **open escape routes** and road conditions
- Establish **family location centres** for reuniting separated family members
- Announce **refuge area locations** and availability
- Provide **continuous instructions** to householders and travelers

#### Ground Teams
- Well-equipped teams **preceded by a bulldozer** should clear roads immediately after the fire front passes
- Teams should **extinguish spot fires** in unoccupied houses
- Teams must remain at the **fire periphery** — it is suicidal to advance into the path of wildfire

#### Security
- **Police or disciplined volunteers** should roster guards to prevent looting
- Security continues until the area is re-occupied and services reconnected
- Guards should be positioned at the fire periphery, not within the active fire zone

## Psychological Effects and Recovery

### Expected Psychological Impacts
Fire survivors typically experience a range of psychological effects that can persist for months or years:
- **Sense of isolation** and disorientation from the destruction of familiar landscapes
- **Noise trauma** from the extreme sounds of firestorm (described as "deafening")
- **Confusion** during and immediately after the event
- **Survivor guilt**: "Why should I have survived when people around me have died?"
- **Desolation** from losses of property, animals, landscapes, and community members

### Building Psychological Resilience
Recovery is significantly enhanced by:
- **Having realistic expectations** about fire risk and behavior beforehand
- **Having planned moves** and practiced evacuation procedures
- **Having assisted others** before, during, and after the fire — knowing you did your best greatly helps recovery
- **Being prepared for looters** and the social disruption that follows disaster
- Understanding that **survivor guilt is normal** and shared among all survivors

This psychological preparation applies to all catastrophes, not just fire.

## Rebuilding After Fire

A critical principle for post-fire rebuilding: **do not rebuild "just the same" as before**. Most people instinctively rebuild identically, reproducing the same vulnerabilities. Instead:

- Build to **survive the next disaster**
- Incorporate **fire-resistant [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]]** from the outset
- Improve **siting** based on lessons learned about fire behavior on the property
- Install **permanent fire refuges** as part of the new structure
- Create **better fuel management zones** than existed before

## Lessons from Historical Firestorms

The 1967 Hobart firestorm provides a compelling case study in survival through preparation:
- **1,100 houses burnt** in the area
- **70 houses burnt** on a single street
- **90 people killed**
- One family survived in a dense eucalypt forest through rigorous prior preparation:
  - All lower limbs removed annually
  - All loose bark, twigs, fallen leaves, dry brush removed
  - Dry grass and dead stumps cleared
  - Material chopped and stoneweighted into hollows and swales
  - Tall tree stems retained (saved house from fire wind and regenerated after fire)
  - Downslope line of willows and [[acacia]] planted as sacrificial fire barrier
  - All walls white-painted, screens fitted
  - Ample bucket water stored indoors and out
- Result: House was blistered and zinc roof coating flaked, but **close-fitted boards were unburnt**
- Apart from sore eyes and short beards and hair, **little damage resulted**

## The Designer's Responsibility

Every [[permaculture]] designer working in fire-prone areas carries the responsibility to:
- Educate clients and communities about fire risk and survival
- Design settlements that minimize fire damage and mortality
- Create systems that allow people to survive "caught at home" scenarios
- Plan for the inevitable recurrence of fire on 8-30 [[query-why-do-periodical-cicadas-emerge-in-13-and-17-year-cycles]]
- Ensure that rebuilding after fire incorporates improved fire-resistant design
- Accept that in every large wildfire in settled areas, people will be lost or badly injured, property destroyed, and psychological effects will persist — but good design dramatically reduces these losses
