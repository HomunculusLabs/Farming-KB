Bone salve is a traditional farm remedy that Sepp Holzer learned from the "bone salve burning man" (Beinsalbenbrennermandl) who visited farms in late autumn. The salve has multiple uses: treating wounded livestock, deterring bark-stripping animals, protecting fruit trees from rodents, and repelling biting insects.

## Traditional Bone Salve Recipe

### Materials
- Bones from cattle and pigs (saved throughout the year)
- Two 10-litre cast iron pots
- A wire grate
- Clay and wet earth for sealing
- Wood for fire

### Preparation
1. Save bones throughout the year in a specially prepared chest
2. The chest should have a ventilation grate for air circulation and protection from mice
3. The bones should be smoked (because most meat was smoked for storage)
4. In late autumn, crush the bones so they fit into the cast iron pots

### Setup
1. Place crushed bones in one of the two 10-litre pots
2. Put a wire grate on top (to keep bones in)
3. Empty a mug of water (approximately 250ml / quarter litre) into the second pot
4. Bury the second pot in wet, mossy soil at a distance from the house, flush with the ground, upright
5. Place the first pot (with bones) upside down with the grate facing down, on top of the second pot
6. Seal the space around the two pots with clay and wet earth

### Firing
1. Lay wood over the covered pots and start a fire
2. The fire must be carefully regulated -- not too much or too little heat
3. The correct temperature drains fat from the bones without burning it
4. Keep the clay seal intact and wet throughout the process
5. If the seal is damaged, sparks could reach the steaming oil inside and cause an explosion

### Result
- Bottom pot: contains a glutinous brown mass (the bone salve)
- Top pot: contains only light grey flakes of used-up bone
- The salve has a pungent smell similar to mineral naphtha or wood tar

### Uses of the Bone Salve
- Treating wounded livestock (castration wounds, etc.)
- Watered-down form spread on draft animals with a rag to protect from flies and horseflies during haymaking and harvest
- Deterrent against bark stripping in forest cultures
- Protection for fruit trees from rodent gnawing
- Provides excellent protection for many years

### Modern Sourcing
- Obtain bones from a slaughterhouse
- Place on a grill and smoke them
- The rest of the burnt bones can be used in the garden as fertiliser

## Bark Protection Salve (using bone salve as base)

For protecting fruit trees from browsing and bark stripping, mix the bone salve with:

- Linseed oil (from flax seeds, available in wholefood shops)
- Fresh cow dung
- Slaked lime
- Very fine quartz sand

Mix until paintable consistency. See [[holzer-deer-browsing-protection]] for full details on application.

## Calendula Salve (Simple Alternative)

A simpler salve that can be made by anyone:

### Materials
- Whole calendula plants (Calendula officinalis) including stems, leaves, and flowers
- Lard (from butcher) or vegetable oil (e.g., olive oil)
- Beeswax (if using vegetable oil)

### Recipe
1. Cut two heaped double handfuls of calendula finely
2. Heat roughly half a litre of lard
3. Carefully fry the calendula whilst keeping it moving
4. Cover and leave to stand for one day
5. Lightly warm, filter through a cloth, pour into container

If using vegetable oil instead of lard:
- Add a thickening agent: 200-250g beeswax per litre of oil
- Melt the wax first, then mix into the filtered calendula oil
- More wax = stiffer salve; less wax = creamier consistency

### Uses
- Treats all kinds of injuries
- Wound healing
- Skin conditions

## Drawing Salve

Another traditional farm remedy made from:
- Tree resin (liquid larch pitch)
- Various medicinal herbs

Mixed into a poultice. The effect can be so intense that the poultice must be removed. It treats inflammation and festering wounds very quickly.
