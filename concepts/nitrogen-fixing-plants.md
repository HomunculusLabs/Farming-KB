# Nitrogen Fixing Plants

Nitrogen fixing plants are a critical group of soil-building species in
permaculture. These plants harbor bacteria or fungi among their roots
that extract nitrogen from the air and convert it to plant-available
form, providing a continuous supply of this essential nutrient to the
garden ecosystem.

## How Nitrogen Fixation Works

Nitrogen fixers form symbiotic relationships with microorganisms living
in nodules on their roots. In legumes (the pea family), Rhizobium
bacteria perform this role. In other families such as Elaeagnaceae
(goumi, Russian olive) and Ceanothus, actinorhizal fungi serve the same
function. These microbes convert atmospheric nitrogen (N2), which plants
cannot use directly, into ammonium (NH4+), a form that plants can absorb
through their roots.

The process benefits not only the host plant but its neighbors as well.
When nitrogen fixers shed leaves, drop twig litter, or are cut down for
mulch, the nitrogen stored in their tissues becomes available to
surrounding plants through decomposition. Living nitrogen fixers also
exude some nitrogen into the soil through their roots, feeding nearby
plants in real time.

## Debate: Live vs. Dead Release

Some people believe that nitrogen fixers must die to release their
nitrogen, but both research and practical experience show that live
N-fixers are at least as growth-boosting as dead ones. While the largest
pulse of nitrogen comes when a nitrogen fixer is cut down and its
tissues decompose, the ongoing root exudation from living plants
provides a steady, continuous supply.

## Key Nitrogen Fixing Species

Nitrogen fixers come in all sizes, from ground covers to tall trees:

### Ground Covers
- White clover (Trifolium repens)
- Other clovers (Trifolium spp.)

### Shrubs
- Goumi, Autumn olive, Russian olive (Elaeagnus spp.)
- Siberian pea shrub (Caragana arborescens)
- Sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides)
- Wild lilac, buckbrush (Ceanothus spp.)
- Bush clover (Lespedeza thunbergii)
- Bladder senna (Colutea arborescens)
- Scotch broom (Cytisus scoparius)
- Spanish broom (Spartium junceum)
- Hackberry (Celtis occidentalis)
- Elderberry (Sambucus nigra)

### Small Trees
- Golden-chain tree (Laburnum anagyroides)
- Black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia)
- Silk tree, mimosa (Albizia julibrissin)
- Mesquite (Prosopis spp.)
- Acacia (Acacia spp.)

### Large Trees
- Alder (Alnus spp.)
- Casuarina (Casuarina spp.)

## Role in Permaculture

Nitrogen fixers play multiple roles in the ecological garden. Their
rapid growth makes them ideal [[nurse-plants]], protecting young fruit
and nut trees while pumping nitrogen into the soil. They serve as
[[hemenway-mulch-makers-and-plant-functional-roles]] when slashed, providing nutrient-rich material for
sheet mulching. Their flowers attract [[beneficial-insects-guide]] and
pollinators. Many provide edible fruit, as with goumi, sea buckthorn,
and autumn olive.

In the [[zone-system]], nitrogen fixers are placed throughout all zones.
In Zones 1 and 2, clover may serve as a living [[gaias-garden-sheet-mulching-guide]] beneath fruit
trees. In Zones 3 and 4, larger nitrogen-fixing trees like black locust
and alder build soil for food forests and timber plantings.

## Management

Nitrogen fixers are fast-growing and can become dominant if not managed.
Ecological gardeners typically slash them periodically for mulch, coppice
them for fuel wood, or allow taller desired species to eventually
outshade them. In a "two plants in every hole" strategy, a nitrogen
fixer is planted alongside every fruit tree and cut back as needed.

## Legumes vs. Actinorhizal Plants

The largest group of nitrogen fixers is the legume family (Fabaceae),
which includes peas, beans, clovers, lupines, wisteria, black locust,
mesquite, and acacia. Legumes associate with Rhizobium bacteria, which
are abundant in most soils but can be inoculated for better results.

A second important group uses actinorhizal fungi instead of bacteria.
This group includes Elaeagnus species (goumi, autumn olive, Russian
olive), Ceanothus (wild lilac), Casuarina, alder, and mountain
mahogany. Actinorhizal plants are especially valuable in permaculture
because many are also drought-tolerant, wildlife-friendly, and produce
edible fruit.

## Integrating Nitrogen Fixers into the Garden

When designing plantings, include at least one nitrogen fixer in every
[[mollison-guild-analysis-and-species-interaction]] or planting cluster. In a fruit tree guild, a small nitrogen-
fixing shrub like goumi or Siberian pea shrub planted nearby will
continuously feed the soil. In annual beds, a cover crop of clover or
vetch between seasons adds nitrogen. In food forests, alder or black
locust planted among productive trees provides long-term fertility.

The key principle is that nitrogen fixers should be present throughout
the landscape, not isolated in one area. Their benefit is greatest when
they are interplanted with heavy-feeding species that can use the
nitrogen they provide.

## Avoiding Potential Problems

Some nitrogen fixers can become invasive if not managed. Scotch broom
and Spanish broom are aggressive colonizers in some regions. Russian
olive and autumn olive can spread into wild areas. Always check local
invasiveness before planting. Hybrid varieties (such as hybrid broom)
are often less invasive than their parent species.

Tree of heaven (Ailanthus altissima) is a nitrogen-fixing pioneer that
tolerates pollution but is highly invasive and should generally be
avoided in favor of better-behaved alternatives.
