# AACT Nitrogen Forms and Microbial Nutrient Cycling

## Overview

Elaine Ingham's field guide devotes significant attention to the different forms of nitrogen in compost tea and soil systems, and how these forms interact with the soil food web. Understanding nitrogen speciation is critical because the form of nitrogen present directly affects plant health, microbial community dynamics, and disease suppression capacity. Ingham emphasizes that the goal of AACT is not to supply nitrogen directly to plants but to establish the biological systems that cycle nitrogen naturally.

## Forms of Nitrogen in AACT and Soil

### Nitrogen as Nitrate

Nitrate (NO₃⁻) is the most readily available form for plant uptake, but Ingham warns it can be harmful in excess:

- Plants take up nitrate directly without biological mediation
- Excess nitrate makes plants susceptible to disease and pest pressure
- High nitrate concentrations suppress beneficial fungal communities
- Nitrate-heavy systems select for bacterial-dominated soil biology
- Systems dependent on nitrate fertilizers have lost biological nutrient cycling

Ingham's position is that nitrate should not be the primary nitrogen source. A system dependent on nitrate input has lost its biological cycling capacity.

### Nitrogen in Protein Form

Protein-based nitrogen supports the soil food web:

- Proteins and amino acids are food sources for bacteria and fungi
- Well-fed bacteria and fungi grow, reproduce, and become slow-release nitrogen
- Protozoa and nematodes consume bacteria and fungi, releasing plant-available nitrogen
- Creates a self-regulating system where nitrogen release matches plant demand via root exudates

This natural nitrogen cycle existed before synthetic fertilizers disrupted it. Protein form feeds the soil food web, and the food web feeds the plant.

### Nitrogen as Dormant Bacteria and Fungi

Much of the nitrogen in healthy soil is stored in living and dormant microbial biomass:

- Bacterial and fungal cells contain nitrogen in their protein structures
- Dormant organisms represent a significant stable nitrogen reservoir
- When conditions improve, organisms wake up and resume nutrient cycling
- This dormant biomass nitrogen is stable and won't leach like nitrate

A well-made AACT contains active and dormant organisms that activate once applied with adequate food resources.

## The Nutrient Cycling Mechanism

### Bacterial Nutrient Cycling

- Bacteria consume simple sugars, proteins, and carbohydrates
- Their biomass has a low carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (3:1 to 10:1)
- Bacterial-feeding nematodes and protozoa graze and excrete ammonium (NH₄⁺)
- This grazing releases nitrogen in plant-available form

### Fungal Nutrient Cycling

- Fungi break down lignin, cellulose, and recalcitrant compounds
- Higher C:N ratio (15:1 to 30:1) means longer-lasting nutrient reservoirs
- Fungal-feeding nematodes release nitrogen through grazing
- Mycorrhizal fungi directly exchange nutrients with plant roots

### Protozoan and Nematode Grazing

Higher trophic levels are where cycling becomes plant-relevant:

- Flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates consume bacteria
- Predators have lower C:N ratios than their prey
- Excess nitrogen consumed is excreted as plant-available ammonium
- Plants access organic nitrogen through this biological mediation, not direct decomposition

## Nitrogen in Compost Tea Brewing

### During Brewing

- Protein-based nitrogen sources (fish hydrolysate, kelp) feed diverse communities
- Simple sugars (molasses) selectively feed bacteria
- Complex carbohydrate sources (humic acids) support fungal growth
- Excessive nitrogen drives the system anaerobic through rapid growth

### Avoiding Anaerobic Conditions

- Too much nitrogen food causes explosive bacterial growth
- Rapid growth depletes dissolved oxygen faster than aeration replaces it
- Anaerobic bacteria produce toxic metabolites harmful to plants
- Add food gradually and monitor dissolved oxygen

## Plant-Microbe Signaling

Plants regulate nitrogen supply through root exudates:

- Plants exude compounds that feed bacteria and protozoa when nitrogen is needed
- The microbial community responds by increasing cycling activity
- Nitrogen is released in the root zone exactly when the plant needs it
- This signaling is disrupted by high-nitrate conditions
- Restoring biological cycling means restoring this communication pathway

## Key Principles

1. Feed the soil food web, not the plant directly
2. Protein-based nitrogen supports biology; nitrate bypasses it
3. Dormant microbial biomass is a stable nitrogen reservoir
4. Nutrient cycling requires all trophic levels: bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes
5. Anaerobic conditions from excess nitrogen are a primary AACT failure cause
6. Chemical nitrogen tests are inadequate for biological nitrogen assessment

## Correcting Nitrogen Imbalances

When nitrogen cycling is impaired, the solution is biological:

1. If bacterial cycling is insufficient, add bacterial foods and inocula
2. If fungal cycling is insufficient, add fungal foods and inocula
3. If protozoa are absent, add compost or vermicompost containing them
4. If nematodes are absent, add compost with diverse nematode communities
5. Avoid nitrate fertilizer, which further disrupts biological cycling

## Assessing Nitrogen Status

Ingham recommends soil food web assays over chemical nitrogen tests:

- Chemical tests measure nitrate and ammonium but miss biological nitrogen pools
- Total and active bacterial biomass indicate bacterial cycling capacity
- Total and active fungal biomass indicate fungal cycling capacity
- Protozoa and nematode numbers indicate whether the grazing cycle is functional
- A comprehensive soil food web test reveals whether nitrogen is being cycled properly

## The Role of Mycorrhizal Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi play a special role in nitrogen access:

- They extend the effective root zone far beyond physical roots
- They access nitrogen forms that roots cannot directly absorb
- They exchange nitrogen and phosphorus for plant carbon
- Their networks connect multiple plants, redistributing nutrients
- Mycorrhizal assessment is a critical step in Ingham's twelve-step protocol
