# Grain Spawn Production in The Mushroom Cultivator

Source: The Mushroom Cultivator (Stamets & Chilton, 1983), Chapter III
Updated: 2026-04-18

## Overview

Mushroom spawn is the inoculum used to seed prepared substrates, consisting of a carrier material fully colonized by mushroom mycelium. In 1932, Dr. James Sinden patented the process of using cereal grain as the mycelial carrier, which remains the basis for most modern spawn production. The distinct advantage of grain spawn is the increased number of inoculation sites -- each individual kernel becomes a point from which mycelium can spread. A liter of rye grain spawn containing approximately 25,000 kernels represents a vast improvement over older [[mushroom-inoculation-methods-spore-syringe-mycelium-syringe-agar-squares-comparison]].

## Suitable Cereal Grains

- **Rye** (Secale cereale): Most common choice. Availability, low cost, and ability to separate into individual kernels all recommend its use. ~30 kernels/gram, 15% moisture content (commercial).
- **Wheat**: Works equally well as rye for spawn making and fruitbody production.
- **Millet**: Higher number of inoculation points per gram but more difficult to formulate. Amycel successfully uses millet as primary spawn medium.
- **Sorghum/Milo**: Spherical kernels work relatively well; used for years by Stoller Spawn Company.
- **Wheat/Rye Grass Seed**: Many more kernels per gram (415-450/gram) but tendency to clump. Widely used for sclerotia [[sclerotia-formation-in-psilocybe-species]] species.
- **Brown Rice**: Tends to clump due to sticky outer coat.

## Spawn Container Selection

- **Narrow-mouth jars** preferred over wide-mouth: less chance of contamination from airborne spores due to smaller openings
- Narrow-mouthed jars more suited to synthetic filter discs
- Regular-mouthed half-gallon mason jars considered ideal by the authors
- Filter discs in conjunction with one-piece screw top lids having a 3/8-1/2 inch diameter hole
- Jars are temporary housing for incubating mycelium before laying out in trays or inoculating bulk substrates

## Sterilization

A single gram of commercial rye contains an estimated 50,000-100,000 bacteria, more than 200,000 actinomycetes, 12,000 fungi, and many yeasts. Key sterilization guidelines:

- **Liquids**: 25 minutes at 15 psi (250°F)
- **Solids (rye grain)**: Minimum 1 full hour at 15 psi
- Higher pressure reduces time (e.g., 30 minutes at 30 psi equals 60 minutes at 15 psi)
- Always inoculate jars as soon as they cool to room temperature -- leaving uninoculated jars sitting overnight is not recommended
- Clean the outside of the pressure cooker before bringing it into the sterile inoculating room

## Spawn Formulas (Cereal Rye)

### Quart Jars
- 1 cup rye grain (240 ml)
- 2/3 to 3/4 cup water (170-200 ml)

### Half-Gallon Jars
- 3 cups rye grain (600 ml)
- 1-3/4 cups water (400-460 ml)

The optimum moisture content for grain spawn is 49-54%. These formulas fill jars to nearly 2/3 capacity after autoclaving.

**Optional Additions:**
- Chalk (CaCO3) and gypsum (CaSO4) at 1-3 parts per 100 parts grain (dry weight)
- Ratio of chalk to gypsum: 1:4
- Necessary for Agaricus brunnescens; optional for most other species
- Add 10% more water when using calcium buffers

## Agar-to-Grain Inoculation

1. Select a vigorously growing culture covering no more than 3/4 of the agar surface
2. Flame sterilize a scalpel and cut triangular wedges of mycelium-covered agar
3. Transfer wedges to awaiting jars, exposing grain for minimal time
4. One petri dish (2/3 covered with mycelium) inoculates 6-8 quart jars
5. **Double wedge technique**: Cut a single wedge in half and insert both pieces -- grows out far faster
6. Loosen lids prior to inoculation for speed
7. After transfer, tightly secure lids and shake each jar thoroughly
8. Incubate at appropriate temperature; grain should be fully colonized in 7-10 days

## Grain-to-Grain Transfer

The most efficient method for large-scale spawn production:

1. **Inspect masters**: Look for abnormalities -- heavy growth, slimy kernels, unusual colors, sour odors
2. **Shake masters**: Break grain into individual kernels against a cleaned car tire or padded surface
3. **Wait 12-24 hours**: Set masters aside to verify recovery; jars not recovering may be contaminated with bacteria
4. **Re-inspect**: Confirm renewed [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]]
5. **Transfer**: Each master jar inoculates 10-12 quart jars or 7-9 half-gallon jars
6. **Incubate**: At species-appropriate temperature for one week

**Exponential Expansion:**
- G-1 (first inoculation from agar): 10 jars
- G-2 (grain-to-grain from G-1): 100 jars
- G-3 (from G-2): 1,000 jars
- Transfers beyond third generation are not recommended
- If contamination exceeds 10% at G-2, consider it terminal

## Liquid Inoculation Techniques

A highly effective technique uses fragmented mycelium suspended in sterile water, injected into grain jars. Mycelial fragments are evenly distributed, each becoming a point of inoculation. This technique gives faster colonization than agar wedge transfers.

## Alternative Spawn Media

Some species do not grow well on grain:
- **Lentinus edodes** and **Flammulina velutipes** grow on sawdust/bran media
- **Perlite/bran** spawn: Perlite is sterilized while providing structural medium; bran provides nutrition

## Incubation of Spawn

Key incubation principles:
- Temperature must be appropriate for the species being cultivated
- The best temperature for vegetative (spawn) growth is typically several degrees higher than for fruiting
- Agaricus brunnescens takes up to 2.5 weeks to colonize grain
- [[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]] colonizes in 7-10 days
- Slower species benefit from shaking on Day 5 and Day 9
- Semi-sterile environment preferred for incubation
