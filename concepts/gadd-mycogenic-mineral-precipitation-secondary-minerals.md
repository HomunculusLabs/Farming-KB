# Mycogenic Mineral Precipitation and Secondary Mineral Formation

## Overview

Fungi do not merely weather and dissolve rocks; they also actively precipitate
secondary minerals from solution through mycogenic mineral formation. This
capacity represents the constructive counterpart to fungal weathering,
contributing to soil development, element cycling, and [[staycare-phytochelatins-and-intracellular-metal-detoxification-in-fungi]].
Fungi precipitate carbonates, oxalates, phosphates, sulfides, and oxide
minerals through diverse metabolic pathways with profound implications for
soil chemistry, environmental remediation, and geological interpretation.

## Carbonate Precipitation

### Calcite Formation in Limestone and Calcareous Soils

Many fungi precipitate calcium carbonate, particularly calcite, as a
consequence of [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]]. In limestone and calcareous environments,
fungal dissolution of calcium carbonate by organic acids is often accompanied
by reprecipitation of secondary calcite in different crystallographic forms.
This dissolution-reprecipitation cycle alters porosity, permeability, and
mechanical properties of rock or soil. Calcite precipitation is driven by
local pH increases from ammonia production during nitrate assimilation or
amino acid deamination. The fungal cell wall and extracellular matrix provide
nucleation sites, and biogenic calcite often exhibits distinct morphologies
compared to inorganic precipitates. Fungal calcite precipitation occurs on
building stone, in cave speleothems, and in soil carbonate horizons.

## Calcium Oxalate Biomineralization

### Calcium Oxalate as a Calcium Reservoir

Calcium oxalate is the most widespread ecologically significant mycogenic
mineral. Fungi precipitate two crystalline forms: the dihydrate weddellite
(CaC₂O₄·2H₂O) and the monohydrate whewellite (CaC₂O₄·H₂O). These form on
hyphal surfaces, within the extracellular matrix, and sometimes
intracellularly. Precipitation sequesters excess calcium that might interfere
with cellular metabolism. Upon fungal death, calcium oxalate decomposes and
oxalotrophic bacteria transform it back to calcium carbonate, completing a
biogeochemical calcium cycle. This reservoir function critically affects
phosphate availability: by removing calcium as calcium oxalate, fungi reduce
formation of insoluble [[bionutrients-lab-calcium-phosphate-calamansi]], increasing bioavailable phosphate
for plant and microbial uptake.

### Toxic Metal Oxalates as Tolerance Mechanisms

Fungi exploit oxalate biomineralization for detoxification by precipitating
insoluble oxalate salts of toxic heavy metals. Beauveria caledonica
extensively studied for precipitating metal oxalates of cadmium, copper, zinc,
and lead. Upon toxic metal exposure, abundant [[fungal-oxalate-production-metal-transformation]] chelates
metal cations and precipitates crystalline deposits on and around the mycelium.
The resulting crystals are substantially less bioavailable than free ions,
reducing the toxic burden on fungal cells. In metal-contaminated soils,
mycogenic metal oxalate formation immobilizes heavy metals and reduces
leaching into groundwater, with specific crystalline forms varying by metal.

### Crystalline Forms and Stability

Weddellite (dihydrate) forms tetragonal bipyramidal crystals, is relatively
unstable, and tends to dehydrate to whewellite over time. Whewellite
(monohydrate) forms monoclinic crystals that are thermodynamically stable and
persist longer in soils. Relative proportions depend on calcium concentration,
oxalate production rate, pH, and presence of other ions. Many soil fungi
produce both forms, and interconversion represents a dynamic aspect of the
fungal calcium cycle with implications for long-term soil mineralogy.

## Role of Chitin as Nucleation Substrate

The fungal cell wall provides surfaces for heterogeneous nucleation. Chitin,
the principal structural polysaccharide, possesses reactive amino and hydroxyl
groups that bind metal cations and template crystal nucleation. The spatial
arrangement of chitin fibrils influences orientation and morphology of
precipitated crystals. Mineral precipitation concentrates at specific cell
wall sites where chitin is exposed or wall architecture creates favorable
nucleation conditions. This directed biomineralization distinguishes fungal
mineral formation from abiotic precipitation and produces distinctive
morphologies in mycogenic minerals.

## Metal Immobilization via Biosorption

Fungi immobilize metals through biosorption onto cell wall surfaces and
extracellular polysaccharides. Cell walls contain carboxyl, phosphate, amine,
thiol, and hydroxyl groups binding metals through electrostatic attraction,
ion exchange, and complexation. Dead fungal biomass retains significant
biosorption capacity, exploited in bioremediation technologies. Extracellular
polysaccharides extend biosorption surfaces beyond the cell wall, creating
diffuse metal immobilization zones around colonies. In soils, this capacity
influences metal bioavailability, mobility, and distribution.

## Reductive Precipitation of Elemental Metals

Some fungi reduce soluble metal ions to elemental forms. Fungal reduction of
silver ions produces elemental silver nanoparticles with materials science
applications. Similar reductive precipitation occurs for selenium and
tellurium, converting soluble selenite and tellurite to insoluble elemental
forms. Transformations are mediated by intracellular and extracellular
reductases including nitrate reduction and [[blesching-cannabis-oxidative-stress-and-antioxidant-defense]] enzymes.
Resulting precipitates are typically amorphous or nanocrystalline, deposited
on cell surfaces, in the extracellular matrix, or intracellularly. This
capacity enables biorecovery of precious metals and bioremediation of
metal-contaminated environments.

## Desert Varnish and Oxidation Patinas

Fungi contribute to desert varnish formation, the dark manganese- and iron-
rich patina on exposed rock surfaces in arid environments. While desert
varnish has mixed biological and abiotic origins, fungal hyphae create
localized oxidizing microenvironments promoting manganese and iron oxide
precipitation that accumulates over centuries to millennia. Manganese oxidation
patinas occur in humid environments too, where fungal and bacterial
communities collaborate in oxidative metal deposition. Iron oxidation similarly
produces distinctive oxide coatings altering surface chemistry. These patinas
influence weathering rates and surface albedo, creating feedback loops between
biological activity and mineral surface transformation.

## Ecological and Environmental Significance

Mycogenic mineral precipitation connects biological metabolism with geological
processes. In soils, fungal mineral formation develops structure, retains
nutrients, and sequesters carbon in stable mineral-organic associations. In
contaminated environments, fungal metal immobilization through oxalate
precipitation, biosorption, and reductive precipitation provides natural
contaminant attenuation. Understanding these processes is essential for soil
[[coleman-compost-and-organic-fertility-management]], polluted site remediation, and interpreting the
mineralogical record of environments where fungi have left distinctive
biomineral signatures.
