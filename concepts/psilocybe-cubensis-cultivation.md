# Psilocybe cubensis Cultivation

Psilocybe cubensis is the most commonly cultivated [[psilocybin-mushroom]] species
due to its relatively fast growth cycle, moderate environmental requirements,
and tolerance of a range of cultivation conditions. Cultivation methods range
from simple amateur techniques to sophisticated commercial operations. The
fundamental principles involve preparing a nutritious substrate, inoculating it
with mushroom mycelium, maintaining appropriate environmental conditions for
colonization and fruiting, and harvesting the mature mushrooms.

## Mycelium and Spores

Cultivation begins with either spores (sexual propagation) or live mycelial
culture (clonal propagation). Spores are collected from mature mushroom caps by
depositing a spore print on sterile paper or foil. Spore syringes, created by
suspending spores in sterile water, are the most common starting material for
beginner cultivators. Spore syringes are legal to possess and distribute in
many jurisdictions because they contain no controlled substances.

Liquid culture (LC) involves growing mycelium in a nutrient broth, typically
containing sugars, starches, or honey. LC allows rapid expansion of mycelial
biomass and consistent genetics from a single isolate. Agar plates provide a
medium for isolating specific strains, testing for contamination, and
performing tissue culture from mushroom tissue samples. A single tissue sample
from a desirable mushroom can produce a genetically identical culture.

## Sterile Technique

Contamination by bacteria, molds (particularly Trichoderma, Aspergillus, and
Penicillium species), and competing fungi is the primary challenge in mushroom
cultivation. Sterile technique involves working in a still-air environment,
ideally a laminar flow hood, and sterilizing all tools, containers, and
substrates before inoculation. Flame sterilization of inoculation loops and
needles, use of seventy percent isopropyl alcohol for surface disinfection,
and careful hand washing are standard practices.

Autoclaving (pressure sterilization at fifteen psi for sixty to ninety minutes)
is the gold standard for substrate sterilization. For cultivators without an
autoclave, pressure canners achieve similar results. Bulk substrates that are
difficult to sterilize can be pasteurized by submersion in hot water at 160 to
180 degrees Fahrenheit for one to two hours, which kills most contaminants
while preserving beneficial thermophilic microorganisms.

## Grain Spawn Preparation

Grain spawn is the inoculated substrate used to transfer mycelium to bulk
growing substrates. Common grains include rye, wheat, millet, and brown rice.
Grains are hydrated (soaked or simmered), drained, loaded into jars or bags,
and sterilized. After cooling, they are inoculated with spore syringes, liquid
culture, or agar wedges.

Colonization of grain spawn typically takes two to four weeks at room
temperature (70 to 78 degrees Fahrenheit). During this phase, jars or bags are
stored in the dark with gas exchange provided by filtered lids or injection
ports. Full colonization is indicated by the grain being completely enveloped
in white mycelium. Shaking jars at fifty percent colonization distributes the
mycelium and accelerates the process. Spawn should show no signs of
contamination (discolored patches, slimy texture, or off odors) before use.

## Bulk Substrate Preparation

Bulk substrates provide the nutritional and structural medium for fruiting.
Common substrates for Psilocybe cubensis include coco coir, vermiculite, and
composted manure (the CVG or CV tek), straw, and hardwood sawdust. Coco coir
is widely favored for its availability, consistency, and resistance to
contamination. The standard CVG recipe combines coco coir, vermiculite, and
gypsum (calcium sulfate) at a ratio of approximately eight parts coir, two
parts vermiculite, and one part gypsum by volume.

The substrate is hydrated to field capacity (moisture level where a handful
squeezed firmly yields a few drops of water without dripping) and pasteurized
or sterilized. After cooling, it is inoculated with grain spawn at a ratio of
one part spawn to two to four parts substrate by volume. Higher spawn ratios
colonize faster and resist contamination better but are more resource-intensive.

## Monotub Cultivation

The monotub is the most popular fruiting chamber for home cultivators. It
consists of a large plastic storage bin (typically fifty to eighty quarts)
modified with holes for fresh air exchange (FAE) and packed with inoculated
bulk substrate. Holes are covered with micropore tape or polyfill to allow
gas exchange while filtering airborne contaminants.

During colonization, the tub is sealed or minimally vented and kept in the
dark at 75 to 80 degrees Fahrenheit. Once fully colonized (typically ten to
fourteen days), a thin casing layer of pasteurized coco coir or peat-vermiculite
may be applied, though Psilocybe cubensis fruits readily without casing.
Fruiting is initiated by introducing fresh air exchange, indirect light on a
twelve-hour cycle, and a slight temperature drop (68 to 74 degrees Fahrenheit).

## Environmental Parameters

Humidity is critical during fruiting. Relative humidity should be maintained
at ninety to ninety-five percent. In monotubs, moisture retained by the
substrate and condensation on the walls typically provides adequate humidity
without external humidification. Misting the walls and substrate surface
replenishes moisture lost to evaporation and mushroom transpiration.

Fresh air exchange supplies oxygen for mycelial respiration and removes carbon
dioxide. High CO2 levels promote vegetative mycelial growth, while reduced CO2
triggers fruiting. Adequate FAE also reduces the risk of bacterial blotch and
other moisture-related diseases. Light, while not required for vegetative
growth, serves as a pinning trigger and directs mushroom growth upward.
Indirect ambient light or a low-wattage LED on a twelve-hour cycle is
sufficient. Direct sunlight or intense artificial light can overheat the
growing environment.

## Fruiting Stages

Pinning (primordia formation) occurs when environmental conditions signal the
mycelium to produce reproductive structures. Pins appear as tiny white bumps
on the substrate surface that develop into miniature mushroom heads with stems.
The pinning stage is the most sensitive period; fluctuations in humidity,
temperature, or CO2 can cause pins to abort.

After pinning, mushrooms enter the fruitbody expansion phase, growing rapidly
over three to seven days. Psilocybe cubensis mushrooms are typically harvested
when the veil connecting the cap to the stem begins to tear. Harvesting at
this stage maximizes potency per dry weight, as psilocybin content begins to
decrease as the cap fully opens and spores are released. Mature mushrooms
are harvested by twisting gently at the base or cutting with a sterile blade.

## Harvesting and Drying

Fresh mushrooms contain approximately ninety percent water and must be dried
for long-term storage. Dehydrators set to 95 to 115 degrees Fahrenheit dry
mushrooms in twelve to twenty-four hours. Desiccant chambers using silica gel
or DampRid provide an alternative drying method. Properly dried mushrooms are
cracker-dry (snap when bent) and contain five to ten percent residual moisture.

Dried mushrooms should be stored in airtight containers with desiccant packs
in a cool, dark location. Under optimal conditions, potency is maintained for
one to two years, though gradual degradation occurs over time. Vacuum sealing
and refrigeration or freezing can extend shelf life. The primary degradation
pathway is oxidation of psilocin; psilocybin is more stable but slowly
converts to psilocin and then to inactive blue oxidation products.
