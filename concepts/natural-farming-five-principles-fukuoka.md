# Natural Farming: The Five Principles of Masanobu Fukuoka

## Overview

Masanobu Fukuoka (1913–2008) was a Japanese farmer and philosopher who developed
a method [[microdosing-theory-and-practice]] of Green Philosophy* articulates
a vision of agriculture that works in harmony with nature rather than against it.

Fukuoka practiced his method for over fifty years on his farm in Ehime Prefecture,
Japan, consistently achieving yields comparable to or exceeding those of
conventional [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]] have only been considered indispensable for the last fifty
years or so. While the ancient practice of using manure and compost can speed
crop growth, it also depletes the land from which the organic material is taken.
In Fukuoka's view, soil enriches itself year in and year out without any human
intervention. One gram of soil on his farm contains approximately 100 million
nitrogen-fixing bacteria and other soil-enriching microbes.

Rather than importing fertility, Fukuoka returned all crop residues to the
field. Rice straw scattered uncut over the entire field, combined with the
natural nitrogen-fixing activity of clover, maintained soil fertility
indefinitely. Chicken droppings were occasionally scattered when available, but
no prepared compost was ever used.

### 3. No Pesticides

Fukuoka maintained that although a thousand diseases attack plants in fields and
forests, nature strikes a balance — there was never any need for pesticides. Man
grew confused when he identified these diseases as insect damage and created with
his own hands the need for labor and toil.

In Fukuoka's fields, spiders and frogs, locusts and dragonflies all coexisted.
Whenever a large outbreak of leafhoppers occurred, spider populations multiplied
in response. As long as natural enemies were present, a [[hamilton-beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]] alongside pests, create
pollution problems, and leave crops dependent on continued chemical
intervention.

### 4. No Weeding

Nature does not arbitrarily call one plant a weed and try to eradicate it. In
Fukuoka's system, clover served as a living mulch that suppressed unwanted
vegetation while simultaneously fixing nitrogen. The clover cover was managed
through timing: the field was flooded briefly to weaken the clover and give rice
shoots a chance to break through, but the clover always recovered and continued
providing ground cover.

### 5. No Pruning

A fruit tree grows best in its natural habitat, Fukuoka argued. The branches do
not tangle, sunlight falls on every leaf, and the tree bears fully each year,
not only in alternate years. Pruning, like the other interventions, creates the
conditions that appear to make it necessary.

## The Do-Nothing Method

Fukuoka's rice-barley succession system is remarkably simple. He broadcast clover
and winter grain (barley or wheat) over the ripening heads of rice before the
fall harvest. After harvesting the rice while treading on the young shoots of
winter grain, he scattered all rice straw uncut over the entire field. Clay
pellets containing seed rice were then scattered over the straw before the New
Year.

With the winter grain growing and rice seed sown, there was nothing left to do
until the harvesting of the winter grain. In late May, the clover grew
luxuriantly and small rice shoots emerged from the clay pellets. After
harvesting the winter grain, the straw was scattered again, and the field was
flooded briefly to weaken the clover and give rice a head start.

The labor of one or two people was more than enough to grow crops on a
quarter-acre. After thirty years of eliminating unnecessary practices, Fukuoka
reduced his labor to essentially just sowing seed and spreading straw.

## Energy Efficiency

Natural farming produces approximately 130 pounds of rice (200,000 kilocalories)
per man-day of labor, without the input of any outside materials. This is about
100 times the daily caloric intake of a farmer. Traditional farming with horses
and oxen required ten times as much energy input. Small-scale mechanization
doubled this again, and large-scale mechanization doubled it yet another time.

By this measure, natural farming is the most energy-efficient method of food
production known. Modern agriculture's apparent productivity gains are illusory
when measured against the energy inputs required — they merely attempt to
artificially restore full productivity after man has hamstrung nature.
