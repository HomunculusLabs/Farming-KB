# Soil Erosion Control

## Overview

Soil erosion is the detachment and transport of soil particles by water, wind, or gravity. It is the leading cause of soil degradation globally, with topsoil forming at rates of 0.5-1.0 ton per acre per year while erosion on conventionally farmed land can exceed 5-10 tons per acre per year. Erosion control is fundamental to [[pollinator-conservation|conservation]], [[water-management]], and sustainable land stewardship on any homestead.

## Types of Erosion

### Water Erosion
Water is the primary erosive force in most environments:

- **Sheet erosion**: Uniform removal of thin layers of topsoil across a broad surface; often goes unnoticed until significant soil loss has occurred. The most insidious form of erosion because it is subtle and gradual.

- **Rill erosion**: Concentrated flow creates small channels (rills) on the soil surface. Rills are small enough to be erased by normal tillage but indicate that sheet erosion has progressed to a more damaging stage.

- **Gully erosion**: Concentrated flow in larger channels that cannot be smoothed by tillage. Gullies are permanent landscape features that expand during each rainfall event. They form when rills are left unchecked and flow concentrates.

- **Stream bank erosion**: Flowing water undercuts and removes soil from stream banks and channel margins. Accelerated by removal of riparian vegetation, increased runoff, and channelization. See [[riparian-zone-restoration]] for bank stabilization methods.

- **Splash erosion**: Raindrop impact detaches soil particles; the initial step in the water erosion process. A single raindrop can splash soil particles 2 feet vertically and 5 feet laterally. Bare soil is most vulnerable; any ground cover dramatically reduces splash erosion.

### Wind Erosion
Wind erosion dominates in arid and semi-arid regions:

- **Suspension**: Fine particles (silt, clay) are lifted into the air and carried long distances; these particles represent the most fertile portion of topsoil
- **Saltation**: Medium-sized particles (fine sand) bounce along the surface, dislodging other particles; accounts for 50-75% of total wind erosion
- **Surface creep**: Large particles (coarse sand, gravel) are pushed or rolled along the ground by wind or the impact of saltating particles

Wind erosion is most severe when soil is bare, dry, loose, and finely structured—conditions common on tilled fields, overgrazed pastures, and construction sites.

### Gravity-Driven Erosion
- **Landslides**: Mass movement of soil and rock on steep slopes when soil becomes saturated
- **Slumps**: Rotational failures where a block of soil slides along a curved failure surface
- **Creep**: Very slow downhill movement of soil due to freeze-thaw cycles, wetting-drying, and gravity
- **Tunnel erosion**: Water moving through cracks and root channels creates underground tunnels that eventually collapse

## Factors Affecting Erosion

### The Universal Soil Loss Equation (USLE)
A = R x K x LS x C x P

- **R (Rainfall erosivity)**: Energy of rainfall events; higher in tropical and high-rainfall areas
- **K (Soil erodibility)**: Inherent susceptibility of soil to erosion; sandy and silty soils are most erodible
- **LS (Slope length and steepness)**: Longer and steeper slopes increase erosion exponentially
- **C (Cover and management)**: Vegetative cover and management practices; the factor most easily modified by landowners
- **P (Support practices)**: Structural measures like terraces, contour farming, and stripcropping

### Key Principles
1. Any exposed soil is vulnerable to erosion
2. Erosion increases exponentially with slope steepness and length
3. Vegetation cover is the single most effective erosion control measure
4. Soil organic matter and aggregation dramatically reduce erodibility
5. Concentrated water flow is far more erosive than sheet flow

## Vegetative Control Measures

### Cover Crops
Living plant cover protects soil from raindrop impact and binds soil with roots:

- **Winter cover crops**: Cereal rye, winter wheat, crimson clover, hairy vetch protect soil through winter
- **Summer cover crops**: Buckwheat, cowpeas, sorghum-sudangrass cover soil between cash crops
- **Permanent cover**: Maintain permanent vegetation on steep slopes, waterways, and critical areas
- See [[cover-cropping-guide|cover crop]] for species selection and management details

### Permanent Vegetation
- **Grassed waterways**: Plant grasses in natural drainage channels to convey water without erosion; use sod-forming species like tall fescue, smooth bromegrass, or switchgrass
- **Critical area plantings**: Steep slopes, gullies, and highly erodible areas should be planted to permanent vegetation; native prairie grasses and forbs are ideal; see [[prairie-ecology]]
- **Buffer strips**: Strips of permanent vegetation along waterways, field edges, and property boundaries filter runoff and trap sediment

### Mulching
Mulch protects soil surface from raindrop impact and reduces runoff velocity:

- **Organic mulch**: Straw, hay, wood chips, leaves, and compost; 2-4 inches minimum depth
- **Straw mulch**: Excellent for newly seeded areas; 2-3 tons per acre (60-90 bales)
- **Wood chips**: Long-lasting; ideal for perennial plantings and pathways
- **Hydromulch**: Wood fiber mulch mixed with seed and tackifier; applied by hydroseeding equipment
- **Gravel/rock mulch**: Permanent mulch for non-vegetated areas; effective for wind erosion control

## Terracing and Earthworks

### Terraces
Terraces break long slopes into shorter segments, reducing runoff velocity:

- **Broad-base terraces**: Gentle, wide terraces suitable for cropland; built with farm equipment
- **Bench terraces**: Stepped terraces that create level planting surfaces; common in hillside agriculture worldwide
- **Narrow-base terraces**: Steeper terraces with grassed backslopes; suitable for pasture and orchard
- **Contour farming**: Planting rows on the contour (across the slope rather than up and down) reduces erosion by 25-50% compared to up-and-down slope farming

### Swales
Swales are shallow channels on contour that slow, spread, and sink runoff:

- Intercept runoff before it concentrates and erodes
- Allow water to infiltrate into the soil, recharging groundwater
- Can be planted with grasses, shrubs, or trees
- See [[swale-design-construction|swales]] for detailed design and construction guidance

### Diversion Structures
- **Diversions**: Channels that divert water away from vulnerable areas to safe discharge points
- **Water and sediment control basins**: Structures that capture runoff and allow sediment to settle
- **Grade stabilization structures**: Drop structures that control water flow down steep slopes

## Geotextiles and Structural Materials

### Erosion Control Blankets
Rolls of natural or synthetic material that protect soil and hold seed in place:

- **Straw blankets**: Short-term (3-12 months); biodegradable; suitable for low-slope seeding
- **Coconut (coir) blankets**: Medium-term (2-5 years); excellent for slopes up to 2:1; holds moisture
- **Jute blankets**: Medium-term (1-2 years); biodegradable; good for moderate slopes
- **Excelsior (wood fiber) blankets**: Medium-term; holds seed well; conforms to uneven surfaces
- **Synthetic blankets**: Long-term (3-5 years); polypropylene netting with straw or coconut fill

### Geotextiles
Woven or non-woven fabrics used for soil stabilization:

- **Woven geotextiles**: High strength; used under riprap, retaining walls, and road subgrades
- **Non-woven geotextiles**: Filter fabric; allows water passage while retaining soil particles
- **Coir logs**: Cylindrical rolls of coconut fiber; used for stream bank and shoreline stabilization
- **Turf reinforcement mats**: Three-dimensional synthetic mats; provide permanent reinforcement for grassed areas

### Rock and Riprap
- **Riprap**: Angular stone placed on slopes to resist water erosion; sized based on flow velocity
- **Gabions**: Wire cages filled with rock; used for retaining walls, channel linings, and bank protection
- **Check dams**: Small rock or log dams in swales and drainage channels that slow flow and trap sediment

## Monitoring and Assessment

### Simple Erosion Monitoring
- **Visual inspection**: Walk the property after heavy rains; look for rills, gullies, sediment deposition, and bare areas
- **Stake measurements**: Drive stakes into the ground and measure soil loss relative to the stake top over time
- **Photo monitoring**: Photograph fixed points at the same time each year to document changes
- **Sediment fences**: Install silt fence below disturbed areas to measure and capture sediment loss
- **Turbidity monitoring**: Measure water clarity in runoff to estimate sediment loads

### Erosion Hazard Assessment
Consider these factors when assessing erosion risk:

- Slope steepness: >3% requires management; >15% requires intensive measures
- Slope length: Long uninterrupted slopes concentrate runoff
- Soil type: Sandy and silty soils are most erodible; clay soils resist detachment but produce hard-to-manage runoff
- Cover condition: Bare soil erodes at 100x the rate of well-vegetated soil
- Drainage patterns: Concentrated flow paths require structural protection
