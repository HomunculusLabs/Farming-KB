# Herbarium Specimens and Fungal Collections

Herbarium specimens and live fungal cultures are critical resources
for systematists, ecologists, and conservation biologists. These
collections document species distribution patterns, fruiting
phenologies, host associations, and species composition data for
specific sites. Collections that originated more than 100 years ago
provide irreplaceable historical baselines against which to measure
contemporary changes in [[fungal-biodiversity]].

## Importance of Fungal Collections

Material housed in herbaria serves multiple functions. It provides
the comparative material essential for identification of newly
collected specimens, for defining and understanding morphological
variation within particular species, and for arriving at taxonomic
decisions. Herbarium collections also provide materials for
chemical and molecular analyses. They contain the historical
information necessary to examine changes in species compositions
and abundances, fruiting patterns, and host associations caused by
natural events and human activities.

## Preparation of Fungal Specimens

Most fleshy fungi can be dried under a constant flow of warm air
(35-45 degrees C) to prevent decomposition. In areas without
electricity, shelves or screens are placed over a heat source such
as a light bulb. The goal is rapid desiccation to preserve
morphological features and prevent overgrowth by molds or
bacteria. Soft specimens that collapse during drying can be
supported by herbarium paper inserts. Field notes should record
fresh characters including color, odor, taste, and texture that
may be lost during drying.

## Packaging and Storage

Fungal specimens are stored in packets or boxes rather than mounted
on herbarium sheets. A packet is cut from sturdy herbarium paper
slightly longer than the specimen. Packets are mounted on
herbarium sheets by gluing, with one packet per sheet to facilitate
handling. Collections are stored in herbarium cabinets, preferably
of archival quality. Archival boxes are used to store packets and
labels and prevent deterioration. Collections from a single site
or project should be placed in the proper location in the herbarium
to serve as cross-references.

## Label Information

Essential label information includes: collector and collector
number; scientific name of the organism; taxonomic authority;
locality information including geographic coordinates; habitat and
substrate; date of collection; and herbarium name and specimen
number. Additional useful information includes host species,
associated organisms, color descriptions, odor, and abundance.
Labels should be prepared using archival-quality materials. Data
should also be recorded in an automated format for database
management.

## Disinfestation and Maintenance

Herbarium collections are vulnerable to insect pests. Disinfestation
can be achieved through freezing (-20 degrees C for several days)
or fumigation, depending on institutional policy. Specimens should
never be mounted directly on herbarium sheets without packet
protection. Regular monitoring and maintenance of collections
ensures their long-term preservation.

## Filing Systems

Herbaria file their specimens according to several different
systems. Most use a taxonomic arrangement, filing by family,
genus, and species within major groups. Geographic filing by
country, state, or county may also be used. Cross-referencing
between taxonomic and geographic filing systems facilitates
retrieval. Duplicate specimens should be distributed to multiple
herbaria to safeguard against loss.

## Type Specimens

Type specimens are the nomenclatural standards upon which species
names are based. Holotypes, isotypes, syntypes, and lectotypes
each have specific nomenclatural significance. Carlo Vittadini's
collections at the University of Torino, housed since the early
19th century, comprise a seminal collection of type specimens for
truffles and other [[sequestrate-fungi]]. Proper curation and
accessibility of type specimens is essential for taxonomic
stability.

## Culture Collections

Live fungal cultures complement herbarium specimens by preserving
the living organism. Some fungi grow readily in culture, while
others cannot be maintained on artificial media. Cultures are
preserved by lyophilization, storage in liquid nitrogen, or
periodic transfer. Major fungal culture collections include the
American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and numerous
institutional collections. The Kenneth B. Raper Cellular Slime
Mold Culture Collection at Ohio University is a specialized
resource.

## Digital Resources

Electronic databases are increasingly important for managing
and disseminating collection data. Online specimen databases,
digital images, and interactive identification keys enhance
accessibility. The Mycological Resources website provides
comprehensive links to fungal databases and identification
tools. Integration of collection data with geographic
information systems enables biogeographic analyses.
