## Growing Celery and Celeriac

Celery and celeriac require deep sandy [[loam-soil]] to grow to perfection. Soils containing more than about 25% clay will not grow great celery; clay soils (more than half clay) are out of the question. However, an herbal form called "Chinese" celery or "cutting" celery is far better adapted to poor situations — it's a short-stalked type with a stronger flavor.

## Soil Requirements

- **Deep sandy loam** is ideal
- Sandy loams hold quite enough moisture for excellent results
- Soils with more than 25% clay: results will suffer
- Clay soils (50%+): not viable for standard celery
- The bed must be worked deeply because celery makes a weak taproot that needs to extend down about 4 feet (120 cm)
- Wild celery grows in marshes — because it has little need to forage for moisture, its root might not extend much more than 1 foot (30 cm)

## Double-Digging

Celery is a crop for which you should double-dig. The process:

1. Dig a trench one shovel's blade deep
2. Temporarily place the soil removed from the trench to the side
3. Put a 2-inch-deep (5 cm) layer of well-finished compost in the bottom of the ditch, plus a full dose of complete fertilizer
4. Use a hose to briefly direct water over the soil removed from the trench so when you return it, the amendment will be mixed into the topsoil
5. If you encounter clay at depth, remove it and fill the ditch with topsoil
6. Blend the clay into your next compost heap

This creates a zone of super-fertile loose soil about 12 inches (30 cm) wide and 24-30 inches (50-75 cm) deep.

## Starting Seedlings

### Why a Nursery Bed

Celery grows extremely slowly — it can take 10 weeks or more to reach transplanting size. If you direct-seed a large patch, you may waste a lot of viable garden space while waiting.

### Nursery Setup

1. Make the nursery as fertile as the growing row will be (no need to double-dig it)
2. Most gardeners need only about 8 feet (240 cm) of nursery row — this produces 24-36 husky seedlings
3. Rake smooth, sprinkle fine seed thinly in narrow bands 1/2 inch (1.25 cm) wide
4. Bands should be about 12 inches (30 cm) apart
5. Cover with fine soil or fine compost about 1/4 inch (6 mm) thick
6. Pat down firmly enough to restore capillarity

### Timing

- In maritime, short-season, and moderate climates: start when earliest apple trees are blooming
- Where summers are steamy: consider celery an autumn/winter crop, sow after heat of summer breaks
- Celery often dies in extremely hot weather
- Celery seed germinates slowly — commonly taking 11-14 days to emerge
- In late-summer sowing, you may need to erect shade structures and water frequently

### Thinning

Once seedlings are up, thin progressively to 2-3 inches (5-7 cm) apart. Keep the nursery moist.

### Root Pruning (Optional)

Some gardeners prune the roots once or twice by lifting and cutting back the bottom portion when seedlings reach over 4 inches tall. This makes seedlings develop stronger roots that better withstand transplanting shock.

## Transplanting

1. Dig up seedlings and transplant into the prepared row
2. Take care to lift with soil and roots intact
3. Do as little damage to the root structure as possible
4. Press earth firmly around them after setting in
5. Consider growing an early crop (like peas) on the row that will receive the celery transplants first
6. If growth slows after transplanting, side-dress with COF or [[chicken-manure]] compost, or fertigate/foliar feed

## Direct-Seeding Alternative

Where water is short, or if you're mainly growing your garden on rainfall, direct-seed small clusters in their final positions and thin progressively to a single plant. Transplanting destroys the taproot and converts the plant into more of a surface feeder — direct-seeded crops endure rainless spells better.

## Pests and Diseases

Insects rarely trouble this crop when growing healthily. Disease prevention involves:
- Rotation
- Not crowding the bed
- Ensuring plants continue growing rapidly (determined by soil conditions, fertility, and moisture)

## Varieties

- Quality seed companies offer quality market varieties — Utah and Ventura are well-known
- Be cautious about betting on heirlooms — much breeding has gone into modern commercial varieties for bolting resistance and disease resistance
- Be especially wary of cheap celeriac seed — it will not come true to type
- For home seed savers, herbal/cutting celery types are much closer to wild celery and more tolerant of less-than-ideal conditions

## Harvest and Storage

- Where celery can withstand winter frosts, snap off individual stalks as needed
- Pull celeriac plants as wanted while the others continue growing
- Where freezing cold weather arrives, celery and celeriac should be large enough to harvest by end of summer
- **Celeriac is eminently cellarable** — and so is celery
- Keep whole plants intact and store close to freezing — they can overwinter
- Celery can also overwinter in its growing bed if hilled with earth and the soil is kept from freezing solid

## Saving Seed

- Celery is a biennial — plants that act as annuals, going to seed quickly, are highly undesirable
- Do not save seed from first-year bolters
- The flowering and maturation process is like that for carrots, but celery makes thousands of small seed-bearing flowers instead of only a few per plant
- You should not try to maintain a fancy celery or celeriac variety unless you can manage at least 25 plants in the gene pool
- Grow one of the herbal types for easier seed saving — they're much more tolerant of less-than-ideal conditions
