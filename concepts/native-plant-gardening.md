# Native Plant Gardening

Native plant gardening uses species that evolved naturally in a specific region, supporting local ecosystems, pollinators, and wildlife while reducing maintenance demands. It is a cornerstone of [[permaculture-design|permaculture]] design and ecological design.

## Why Grow Native Plants

- Adapted to local climate, rainfall, and soil conditions without supplemental irrigation or fertilizers
- Provide food and habitat for native pollinators (bees, butterflies, moths), birds, and beneficial insects
- Deep root systems improve soil structure, prevent erosion, and increase water infiltration
- Require fewer inputs — lower water, no chemical pesticides, minimal fertilizer
- Support the local food web: native insects can only digest native plants, which in turn feed birds

## Regional Plant Considerations

Selecting natives depends on your ecoregion, not just USDA hardiness zone. Key factors:

- **Precipitation patterns**: Wet vs dry regions demand different species
- **Soil type**: Clay, sand, loam, rocky — match plants to existing soil
- **Sun exposure**: Full sun, part shade, deep shade niches
- **Microclimate**: Frost pockets, heat islands, wind exposure

Consult local native plant societies, university extension offices, and regional floras for species lists appropriate to your area.

## Pollinator Gardens with Natives

Native pollinator gardens focus on providing continuous bloom from early spring through late fall:

- **Spring bloomers**: Wild columbine (Aquilegia canadensis), wild geranium, pussy willow
- **Summer bloomers**: Coneflower (Echinacea spp.), bee balm (Monarda), milkweed (Asclepias), black-eyed Susan
- **Fall bloomers**: Asters, goldenrod (Solidago), Joe-Pye weed

Include host plants for butterfly larvae (milkweed for monarchs, pawpaw for zebra swallowtail). Leave some bare soil for ground-nesting bees. Avoid double-flowered cultivars — they often lack accessible pollen and nectar.

## Rain Gardens

Rain gardens are shallow, planted depressions designed to absorb stormwater runoff from roofs, driveways, and lawns. They combine [[water-management]] with native planting:

- Position at least 10 feet from building foundations
- Size: typically 5-10% of the drainage area
- Use native plants tolerant of both wet and dry conditions (switchgrass, blue flag iris, turtlehead, cardinal flower)
- Amend with compost to improve infiltration in heavy soils
- Layer with gravel or sand at the bottom for drainage

Rain gardens filter pollutants, reduce flooding, and recharge groundwater while creating wildlife habitat.

## Design Principles

- **Layer the landscape**: canopy trees, understory shrubs, herbaceous perennials, ground covers
- **Plant in drifts and groups** rather than singles — more natural appearance and easier for pollinators to locate
- **Mimic natural plant communities**: prairie, woodland edge, wet meadow, savanna
- **Leave seed heads and dried stalks** through winter for bird food and insect overwintering habitat
- **Reduce lawn area** incrementally by expanding native planting beds

## Maintenance

- Water deeply during the first growing season to establish root systems
- Mulch with 2-3 inches of shredded bark or leaf litter (avoid dyed mulch)
- Weed aggressively in years 1-2 until natives fill in and outcompete invasives
- Cut back perennials in early spring (not fall) to leave winter structure
- Divide every 3-5 years if plants become crowded
- Monitor for invasive species encroachment and remove promptly

## Native vs "Nativar" Debate

Nativars are cultivated varieties of native plants bred for specific traits (color, size, disease resistance). Research shows some nativars retain ecological value while others lose pollinator appeal. Straight species generally provide the most reliable ecological benefit. When choosing nativars, prioritize those that maintain the original flower shape and color, as these traits signal pollinators.

## Starting a Native Garden

1. Remove existing turf (sheet mulching or sod cutting)
2. Assess site conditions (sun, soil, moisture)
3. Select species matching your ecoregion and site
4. Plant in fall (preferred) or early spring
5. Water and weed during establishment
6. Be patient — native perennials often "sleep, creep, leap" over three years
