# Low Island and Coral Cay Strategies

Low islands, coral sand cays, and atolls present some of the most challenging environments for human settlement and food production. Lying mostly within 28 degrees of the equator, these fragile landforms face hurricanes, limited freshwater, poor soils, and nutritional constraints. Careful [[casuarina-equisetifolia]]*, sea grape (*Coccolobus unifera*), coastal shrubs (*Scaevola*, *Tournefortia*, *Pemphis*), mangroves, and *Barringtonia* trees.

### Water Supply

The sole natural freshwater sources on low islands are:
1. **Biological storages** — Baobabs and coconuts (about 12 coconut trees provide one person's drinking water for a year)
2. **The water lens** — Fresh rainwater trapped in sand below the caliche, floating on seawater

As rain falls, it quickly absorbs in surface soils and percolates through the caliche to the water lens, which itself leaks slowly to sea between or below tide levels. With the beach berm only 2–4 m above sea level and the inner atoll about 2–3 m, the delicacy of the freshwater situation is obvious.

### Nutritional Risks

Island diets tend to be high in carbohydrates and oxalic acids, creating risks of poor nutrition. Design must prioritize dietary diversity through expanded vegetable and fruit production.

## Preventing Water Lens Pollution

The freshwater lens is the most critical resource on any atoll. A polluting source at the centre of the island can affect the entire water lens, all plants, and all people. Protective zoning is essential:

**Keep to the periphery:** Settlement, toilet areas, processing, livestock, and even gardens should be located on the periphery of the island. The interior should be devoted to dense natural stands of food and native trees, kept free of pesticides, industrial pollutants, and fuels.

### Increasing Potable Water Supply

Three methods for increasing freshwater availability:

1. **Roof run-off to tanks** — Reinforced cement-coral-sand mix tanks, above or below ground, widely used in construction on all but remote atolls
2. **Deep mulches and water-retentive plants** — Coconut, banana, arrowroot, papaya, legumes, and similar plants provide leaf and trunk materials for garden mulch, dramatically reducing irrigation needs
3. **Leaf ferment seals (gleys)** — Gently-sloped coral pits lined with 20 cm of mashed or shredded banana, papaya, and soft green leaf material, covered with plastic until fermenting (4–5 days), then pumped full of water from the freshwater lens. Such ponds can also take roof run-off

**Health note:** Surface ponds are often a source of cross-infection in children. It is sensible to swim in the lagoon and drink tank water. Seawater serves for many toilet uses.

## Planting Trees on Coral Cays

### Tree Planting Technique

To plant trees on coral cays:

1. Clear a patch of topsoil and break open the caliche
2. Make a pit about 40 x 40 cm and 60–80 cm deep
3. Create a humus pile of domestic and plant wastes, plus sulphur and mineral trace elements
4. Plant a coconut or other seedling tree

**Root behavior:** Tree roots spread out above the caliche layer, while feeder roots penetrate below the caliche to the water lens, usually 0.75–1.0 m down and 1.0–2.0 m deep as a saturated sand layer. Trees are thus well anchored and easily obtain water.

### Phosphates

Usually, guano from seabirds provides sufficient phosphates if colonies of terns, gulls, boobies, frigate birds, and shearwaters are protected or encouraged. Failing natural sources, domestic pigeons, quail, pheasant, geese, ducks, and chickens can be kept as such predators as feral cats and foxes are usually absent on islands.

## Pit Gardens on Atolls

### The Pit Garden System

The best approach to atoll gardening is the pit garden — an excavation of about 8 m x 15 m, sloped for stability to about 2 m deep, often damp or wet at the base.

**Construction:**
- Sloped sides are stabilised as steps with coconut logs or caliche
- Spoil is banked around the rim
- Mulch is thickly applied to the base and behind the stepped terraces
- When mulch is rotted, a range of plants are grown

**Planting sequence from base to crown:**

| Level | Height | Plants |
|-------|--------|--------|
| Base (damp, mulch) | Ground level | Watercress, parsley, chives, brassicas, taro, kangkong, salad greens |
| First Terrace | 18–25 cm above base | Tomatoes, peppers, taro, sweet corn, beans, peas, taller crops |
| Second Terrace | 25–60 cm above base | Banana, papaya, sweet potato, cassava (all provide mulch) |
| Third (Highest) Terrace | Top | Cassava, sweet potato, banana, dry-tolerant vegetables, mulch trees (*Leucaena*, *Glyricidia*, *[[tipuana-tipu]]*, *[[moringa-oleifera]]*), local legume shrubs, palms for frond mulch and high shade, vines (passionfruit, four-winged bean) |

### Surface Gardens

Under light palm-legume canopies, boxes of palm logs will hold thick mulch and household waste for surface gardens. These can be planted with potato, yam, sweet potato, and normal vegetable crops. The thicker the mulch, the less watering needed.

### Atoll Garden Essentials

The four essentials for good atoll gardens are:
1. **Log boxes** — Containing mulch and soil mixtures above the caliche
2. **Pits** — Deep excavations reaching moisture and allowing terraced planting
3. **Thick mulch** — Conserving moisture and building soil in alkaline sands
4. **High shade canopy** — Palms and tall trees providing microclimate moderation

### Food Staples

Atoll food systems rely on:
- **Coconut** as the primary tree crop
- **Root crops** (taro, cassava, sweet potato, yam) as calorie staples
- **Fruits** (banana, papaya, breadfruit) for vitamins and diversity
- **Salad vegetables** from pit gardens
- **Fish and shellfish** from lagoons and coasts

## Avoiding Hurricane Damage

### Coastal Shelterbelt Design

Sandy coasts of atolls and cays need a sequence of perennial shelterbelts to hold the shoreline against hurricanes:

1. **Beach level** — *Convolvulus* (*Ipomoea pes-caprae*) and beach pea
2. **Beach berm** — Dense shrubbery of vines, *Tournefortia*, *Scaevola*, and in sheltered bays, mangroves
3. **Inland** — 5–6 tree deep layer of coconut palm, *Casuarina*, *Coccolobus*, *Barringtonia*, and other hardy beach trees

### Airstrip and Reef Entry Safety

When creating access infrastructure on atolls:

- **Reef entries** should be cut on the slant through the reef at the east or west quarters (the most sheltered sectors). Gaps should be just wide enough (6–10 m) to admit a vessel or barge. Careless gap placement can allow waves or winds to destroy the island
- **Airstrips** should be aligned about 20 degrees off prevailing winds. Both ends and sides should be of tall palms and trees, especially coastal borders, so light planes drop in using rudders to straighten up below tree crown level. Airstrips carelessly made have destroyed whole islands when hurricane winds have cut them in two

## Design Priorities

The five key design inputs for low island systems are:

1. Extend vegetable and fruit crop production
2. Extend water storage and conserve water
3. Conserve natural vegetation and unique birds, reptiles, and lagoon or reef fauna
4. Use shallow marine waters for aquaculture and pond fish
5. Develop energy resources locally
