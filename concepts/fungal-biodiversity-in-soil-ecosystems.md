### Mycorrhizal Symbionts
Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic associations with plant roots, exchanging soil nutrients (especially phosphorus) for plant-derived carbon. They are found in 80-90% of all plant species and dramatically extend the effective root surface area. Ectomycorrhizal fungi associate primarily with trees in temperate and boreal forests, while arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi associate with most herbaceous plants and some trees.

### Pathogens
Soil-borne fungal pathogens cause diseases in plants and animals. Understanding pathogen populations and dynamics is critical for agriculture and forestry.

### Endophytes
Fungi living within plant tissues without causing disease. They can enhance plant stress tolerance, deter herbivores, and produce bioactive compounds.

## Methods for Studying Fungal Biodiversity

### Cultivation-Based Methods
Traditional agar plating is the oldest approach but has significant limitations:
- Only a small fraction (1-5%) of soil fungi can be cultured
- Mycorrhizal fungi are reluctant to grow on agar plates
- Growth on plates may not reflect in situ community structure or diversity
- Fast-growing species outcompete slower ones on plates

## Assessing Soil Fungal Diversity: Methodological Approaches

The choice of assessment method significantly affects estimates of fungal diversity, and each technique captures different aspects of the fungal community:

- **Plate count methods:** Serial dilution plating on selective media (Rose Bengal agar, potato dextrose agar with antibiotics) provides colony-forming unit (CFU) counts and allows isolation of culturable species. While useful for isolating specific fungi for applied work, plate counts capture less than 5% of total fungal species and are biased toward fast-growing sporulating species. Microscopy of soil washings provides complementary data on hyphal length and spore counts but cannot resolve species-level diversity.
- **Phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis:** PLFA profiles quantify total fungal biomass and provide a community-level fingerprint. The 18:2ω6,9 fatty acid marker is used as an indicator of fungal biomass. PLFA is relatively fast and inexpensive but offers limited taxonomic resolution and cannot identify specific species.
- **Molecular fingerprinting methods:** Denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE) and terminal restriction fragment length polymorphism (TRFLP) generate community profiles based on amplified ITS regions. These methods are useful for comparing communities across treatments or time points but do not provide species-level identification and have limited resolution for complex communities.
- **Clone library sequencing:** Constructing clone libraries from amplified ITS or 18S rDNA allows species-level identification through Sanger sequencing. This approach provides good taxonomic resolution but is labor-intensive and typically captures only the most abundant taxa (hundreds rather than thousands of sequences).
- **High-throughput sequencing and metagenomics:** Next-generation sequencing platforms (Illumina MiSeq, Ion Torrent) generate millions of ITS amplicon sequences per sample, revealing the full depth of fungal community diversity. Shotgun metagenomic approaches capture both taxonomic and functional gene information without PCR bias. These methods have revealed that soil fungal diversity is orders of magnitude higher than previously estimated, with typical forest soils containing 2,000-5,000 fungal OTUs per sample. However, challenges remain in bioinformatic processing, database completeness, and standardization of protocols across studies.

## Fungal Diversity and Soil Health Indicators

Soil fungal diversity correlates with multiple indicators of soil health and ecosystem function:

- **Soil aggregation and structure:** Fungal hyphae and glomalin (produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi) are major contributors to soil aggregate stability.
