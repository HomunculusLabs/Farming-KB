# Fungal Interactions with Humans and Environmental Change

As human populations expand and industrial activities intensify, the effects of anthropogenic environmental change on fungal communities and the ecosystem processes they mediate have become a critical area of mycological research. The interactions between human-induced pollutants and fungi—while rarely making headlines in the popular press—represent fundamental disruptions to nutrient cycling, plant health, and ecosystem stability.

## The Evolution of Acid Rain Research

The story of acid rain research in mycology illustrates how [[fukuoka-fallacies-scientific-understanding]] evolves. Following the [[mckenna-caffeine-coffee-and-industrial-revolution]] in Europe, industrially-related changes in ecosystems became apparent. The emergence of a dark form of the peppered moth (*[[biston-betularia]]*) adapted to soot-coated bark was one early observation. More relevant to mycology, the dramatic decline of lichens near industrial centers provided one of the first [[biological-indicators-of-soil]] [[lichen-bioindicators-atmospheric-pollution]].

The identification of declining forest health in central Europe during the 1970s—the "Waldsterben" (forest dieback) effect—focused attention on acid rain as the culprit. Sulfuric acid, dissolved in rain droplets, was partly a by-product of energy production using high-sulfur coal. Initially, plant physiologists documented damage to cuticular waxes and reduced photosynthetic capacity of tree canopies. It was only in the late 1970s that soil ecologists connected acid rain to root damage and mycorrhizal disruption.

Key researchers including Ulrich et al. (1979), Hütterman (1982), and Blaschke et al. (1985) demonstrated that acid rain was affecting both root growth and the mycorrhizal status of trees in Bavarian forests. Führer (1990) cautioned, however, that forest decline was exacerbated by natural stressors including soil nutrient depletion, harvest management practices, and drought.

## The Pollutant Landscape

Modern understanding recognizes multiple interacting [[acidifying-pollutants-mycorrhizae]]:

- **Sulfur dioxide (SO₂)**: Released from fossil fuel combustion; combines with water to form sulfuric acid
- **Ozone (O₃)**: Tropospheric (ground-level) ozone damages cuticular waxes and photosynthetic capacity
- **Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)**: Products of fossil fuel combustion that contribute to nitrogen deposition
- **Nitrogen deposition**: Both oxidized and reduced forms of N from atmospheric sources

Research evolved from studying individual pollutants to recognizing that none operate in isolation. The reality is some combination of all acidifying pollutants influencing ecosystem processes, making it difficult to tease apart the relative contribution of each. This is especially challenging because different pollutants may have contrasting effects—simultaneously damaging plant structure while reducing or increasing nutrient availability in soil.

## The Dighton and Jansen Two-Directional Impact Model

Dighton and Jansen (1991) proposed an influential model explaining how acidifying pollutants reduce mycorrhizal associations through two independent mechanisms:

### Pathway 1: Above-Ground Photosynthesis Reduction
Pollutant-induced reduction in the photosynthetic capacity of the tree canopy reduces the allocation of photosynthate to roots and their mycorrhizae. This reduced energy supply has two consequences:
- Overall mycorrhizal colonization of roots decreases
- Selection favors fungal species that can survive on low carbohydrate supplies, shifting community composition toward less demanding taxa

### Pathway 2: Below-Ground Soil Toxicity
Acidifying pollutants reduce soil pH, making toxic metals (aluminum, manganese, magnesium) more soluble and plant-available. This increased toxicity leads to:
- Reduced root growth and root dieback
- Reduced mycorrhizal fungal growth
- Decreased root colonization by mycorrhizae

Both pathways converge on the same outcome: reduced mycorrhizal function and altered community structure, which compromises the tree's ability to acquire nutrients and resist root pathogens.

## Aluminum Toxicity to Fungal Hyphae

Aluminum toxicity represents one of the most direct mechanisms by which acidification damages fungal communities. Research has shown significant variation among fungal species in their sensitivity:

- **Cenococcum**, **Pisolithus**, and **Thelephora**: Growth suppressed at 146 mM aluminum (Thompson and Medve, 1984)
- **Suillus**: No growth reduction below 1000 mM concentration, demonstrating remarkable tolerance

The ratios of Al to other nutrients—Al:Ca, Al:Mg, and Al:PO₄—are important determinants of fungal growth (Jongbloed and Borst Pauwels, 1988, 1989; Kottke and Oberwinkler, 1990). Interestingly, improved growth of various ectomycorrhizal species at low Al:PO₄ ratios, even at high aluminum concentrations, was explained by the absorption and immobilization of aluminum into polyphosphate droplets within fungal hyphae.

## Field Fumigation Experiments

Field-scale experiments have provided crucial evidence. In a fumigation experiment where SO₂ and O₃ were released over plots containing Scots pine, Sitka spruce, and Norway spruce (McLeod et al., 1992), multivariate analysis revealed that SO₂ fumigation caused a reduction in *[[paxillus-involutus]]* mycorrhizae on Scots pine. Paradoxically, *Paxillus involutus* fruit bodies were more abundant in high-SO₂ plots—the converse of root colonization patterns, highlighting the complex relationship between root colonization and fruiting.

## Ozone Effects on Arbuscular Mycorrhizae

Ozone exposure altered the nature (not the degree) of arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization in sugar maple (*Acer saccharum*). Duckmanton and Widden (1994) observed reduced arbuscules but increased vesicles and hyphal coils within the root cortex. They interpreted this as an energy-conserving response: vesicles and hyphal coils are less energy-demanding to produce than arbuscules, and vesicles serve as storage structures, suggesting a stress response to reduced photosynthesis.

## Nitrogen Deposition: Fertilizer and Toxin

Nitrogen deposition represents a uniquely complex pollutant because nitrogen is simultaneously a plant nutrient and an ecological disruptor:

- In N-limited ecosystems, additional nitrogen acts as a fertilizer, increasing plant growth
- In N-saturated conditions, excess nitrate leaches into watercourses, harming aquatic organisms
- At extreme saturation levels, increased N₂O and CH₄ production contributes to greenhouse gas emissions

Plants can acquire atmospheric nitrogen through foliar uptake (2–8% of total N demand for spruce trees), which may alter root demand for N and consequently the physiology of roots and their ectomycorrhizae. This foliar N acquisition pathway adds complexity to understanding below-ground fungal responses to atmospheric nitrogen deposition.

## Macrofungal Fruit Bodies as Biological Indicators

Changes in the composition and abundance of macrofungal fruit bodies, particularly ectomycorrhizal Basidiomycotina, have been adopted as biological indicators for terrestrial forested ecosystems in Europe (Fellner, 1988; Colpaert and Van Tichelen, 1996). In healthy forest ecosystems, fruit bodies of mycorrhizal fungi typically form 45–50% of all fruit bodies found. In polluted stands, this drops to approximately 10%. However, researchers have cautioned that fruit body appearance may bear little relation to actual mycorrhizal abundance on root systems, adding complexity to interpreting monitoring data.

## Emerging Research Directions

Dighton and Jansen (1991) identified two critical gaps in the research: understanding changes in ectomycorrhizal community structure (now being addressed through molecular analysis) and understanding the functional consequences of community change for ecosystem processes. The diversity-function debate in mycorrhizal ecology is still in its infancy, though early evidence suggests that mycorrhizal diversity per se may be a better determinant of plant nutrient status than specific species composition.
