# Nutrient Agar Media for Fungal Cultivation

The preparation of solid nutrient media is a foundational skill in [[mycology]]. Agar-based media provide a sterile, controllable surface on which fungal [[mycelium]] can be grown, isolated, and stored. Two standard media — [[potato-dextrose-agar]] (PDA) and [[malt-extract-agar]] (MEA) — have been the workhorses of both amateur and professional mycology since the mid-twentieth century.

## What Is Agar?

Agar is a pectin-like polysaccharide extracted from certain species of red algae (Rhodophyta), primarily *Gelidium* and *Gracilaria*. When dissolved in boiling water (approximately 1.5–2% w/v) and allowed to cool, agar solidifies to a firm, gelatinous consistency that remains stable at temperatures up to about 85–90°C. This property makes it ideal as a solid support medium for microbial and fungal cultures — it provides structural integrity without contributing significant nutrition.

Agar is available from virtually any scientific supply company and is also stocked by many health food stores and Asian grocery stores as a dietary supplement. The *Magic Mushroom Growers Guide* (Oss & Oeric, 1976) notes that agar can be stored indefinitely in sealed containers.

## Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA)

PDA is one of the most widely used general-purpose media for cultivating higher fungi. It is inexpensive, easy to prepare from common household ingredients, and supports vigorous mycelial growth for most basidiomycetes.

### Recipe (1 liter)

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Potatoes (peeled) | 250 g |
| Agar | 15 g |
| Dextrose (glucose) | 10 g |
| Nutritional yeast or yeast extract | 1.5 g |

### Preparation

1. Peel and shred the potatoes
2. Rinse the shredded potatoes under cold tap water for 30 seconds (to remove excess starch)
3. Combine potatoes with 1 liter of water and boil gently for 30 minutes
4. Filter the resulting potato broth through muslin or cheesecloth; discard the potato solids
5. Add agar, dextrose, and yeast to the hot potato broth while stirring gently
6. Boil gently for an additional 10 minutes, or until the solution is completely clear
7. Take care not to allow the solution to boil over
8. Add distilled water if necessary to return the total volume to 1 liter
9. Pour the hot solution into petri dishes, baby food jars, or slant culture tubes

### Notes

- Fill petri dishes or jars to a depth of approximately ¼ inch
- Fill slant tubes about one-third full, then angle them while cooling to create a slanted surface with maximum area for growth
- Premixed PDA is available commercially and requires only dissolution in boiling distilled water — typically 15–20 g per liter

## Malt Extract Agar (MEA)

MEA provides a richer nutrient profile than PDA, with a higher concentration of sugars, amino acids, and growth factors derived from malt. It is particularly recommended for maintaining vigorous mycelial cultures and for use in environments where fungal growth may be stressed (e.g., high altitude, low humidity).

### Recipe (1 liter)

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Malt or malt extract (powder or syrup) | 20 g |
| Corn-steep liquor (optional) | 15 g |
| Agar | 20 g |
| Potassium phosphate dibasic (K₂HPO₄) | 0.1 g |
| Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) | 0.1 g |

### Preparation

1. Add all dry ingredients to 1 liter of gently boiling water
2. Stir until completely dissolved and the solution is clear
3. Pour into receptacles as described for PDA

### Corn-Steep Liquor

Corn-steep liquor is an optional but beneficial ingredient that adds B vitamins and amino acids. It can be prepared at home:

1. Boil dried corn in water
2. Let the broth stand for several days
3. Sterilize in a mason jar in a pressure cooker
4. Refrigerate until use

Liquid malt extract sold in grocery store syrup sections is also suitable as a substitute or supplement.

## Supplementation

Both PDA and MEA benefit from the addition of 1.5 g of yeast extract or nutritional yeast per liter. This provides an additional source of proteins and B vitamins that promote more vigorous mycelial growth.

## Why Use Both Media Alternately?

The *Magic Mushroom Growers Guide* recommends preparing and alternating between PDA and MEA when building stock inocula. The rationale is biological:

- Different media present different nutrient profiles and physical properties
- The fungus is forced to activate different metabolic pathways when switched between media
- This prevents **senescence** — a decline in mycelial vigor that can occur when an organism is propagated indefinitely on a single medium
- Alternating media exercises different parts of the fungal genome, maintaining genetic and metabolic flexibility

Senescence is a real concern in long-term mycelial culture. Strains that have been subcultured on the same medium for many generations can lose their fruiting ability or show reduced growth rates. Media rotation is one of the simplest and most effective countermeasures.

## Sterilization

### Equipment

A standard home pressure cooker (the authors recommend the All-American 941) can be used if a laboratory autoclave is unavailable. The key requirements are:

- Ability to reach 15–20 psi
- Capacity to hold multiple culture vessels
- A venting stopcock

### Procedure

1. Place approximately 1 liter of tap water in the bottom of the pressure cooker
2. Arrange the filled receptacles carefully — a small enameled tray is useful for stacking
3. **Important:** If using pre-sterilized plastic petri dishes, pour the medium *after* sterilization — do not autoclave plastic plates
4. Leave lids on baby food jars and culture tubes loose (not screwed tight) to allow pressure equalization
5. Seal the pressure cooker lid but leave the stopcock open
6. Heat on high until a vigorous head of steam vents from the stopcock
7. **Critical:** Close the stopcock only after a strong head of steam has been established — premature closure causes pressure to rise without adequate steam, resulting in dry (and less effective) heat sterilization
8. Allow pressure to build to 15–20 psi, then reduce heat to maintain this level
9. Sterilize for 45–60 minutes at pressure (the standard 15-minute recommendation is often insufficient for complete sterilization of solid media)
10. Turn off heat and allow the cooker to cool to room temperature before opening the stopcock — sudden pressure release will cause the medium to boil over and contaminate the receptacles

### Storage

Any leftover medium can be sealed in its flask and stored indefinitely in the refrigerator. When needed, it can be reliquefied over heat and reused.

## Storage of Prepared Plates

Properly sterilized and cooled plates can be stored at room temperature for several weeks if kept sealed. For longer storage, refrigeration extends shelf life to several months. Plates should always be inspected for contamination (fuzzy growth, discoloration) before use.

## Inoculation Environment

Inoculation of sterile media should be performed in the cleanest possible environment:

- **Inoculation hood:** A homemade or commercial [[laminar-flow-hood]]. A simple box made of wood with clear plastic on one side, all joints sealed with silicone caulk, is effective
- **Surface preparation:** Spray all surfaces with Lysol aerosol, a 25% chlorine bleach solution, or both
- **Personal preparation:** Wash hands and arms thoroughly with soap and water, then wipe with alcohol. Wear thin latex gloves sprayed with Lysol (talcum powder inside makes them easier to put on)
- **Clothing:** Short-sleeved or sleeveless shirts to avoid introducing contaminants from fabric

If a hood is not available, inoculation can be performed in still air — a room without drafts, pre-sprayed with disinfectant — though contamination rates will be higher.
