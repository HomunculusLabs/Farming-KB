# Mycorrhizal Fungi: Colonization, Nutrient Transport, and Soil Building

## Overview

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the roots of over 90% of terrestrial
plant species. These associations are among the most important ecological relationships
on Earth, yet they are routinely destroyed by conventional agricultural practices. Dr.
Elaine Ingham's research demonstrates that mycorrhizal colonization is not merely
beneficial — it is essential for healthy plant nutrition, disease resistance, and
long-term soil structure.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF)
Also called VAM (vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizae), these are the most common type,
associating with most crop plants, vegetables, grasses, and many perennials. AMF
penetrate root cortical cells, forming arbuscules (tree-like structures) where
nutrient exchange occurs, and vesicles (storage organs) for carbon reserves. They
do not form visible fruiting bodies — their lifecycle occurs within or on roots.

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (ECM)
These associate primarily with trees, especially conifers and oaks. They form a sheath
around root tips without penetrating cell walls. ECM fungi produce visible mushrooms
and are critical in forest ecosystems, essential for orchard and forestry management.

### Ericoid and Other Types
Ericoid mycorrhizae associate with Ericaceae family plants (blueberries, cranberries,
rhododendrons). Other specialized types exist for orchids. Each type is adapted to
specific soil conditions and plant requirements.

## The Nutrient Exchange Mechanism

The mycorrhizal symbiosis is fundamentally an exchange: the plant provides carbon
(sugars produced through photosynthesis) to the fungus, and the fungus provides
mineral nutrients and water to the plant. This exchange is mediated through the
arbuscule interface in AMF associations.

### Phosphorus Acquisition
Phosphorus is relatively immobile in soil, forming insoluble compounds that roots
cannot easily access. Mycorrhizal hyphae extend far beyond the root zone, accessing
phosphorus that would otherwise be unavailable. The fungus absorbs phosphorus,
transports it through its hyphal network, and releases it at the arbuscule in
plant-available form. AMF can increase phosphorus uptake by 60-200%.

### Nitrogen and Micronutrients
Mycorrhizal fungi also transport nitrogen, zinc, copper, sulfur, and other micronutrients.
Their extensive hyphal networks effectively increase the root absorption surface area
by 100-1000 times. In nitrogen-limited soils, mycorrhizal plants consistently outperform
non-mycorrhizal plants because the fungal network can access nitrogen from organic matter
decomposition that root hairs alone cannot reach.

### Water Transport
During drought conditions, mycorrhizal hyphae continue to extract water from soil
micropores that roots cannot access. Mycorrhizal plants show significantly improved
drought tolerance, often reducing irrigation requirements by 20-40%.

## Colonization Assessment and Targets

Ingham's soil foodweb testing assesses mycorrhizal colonization by examining stained
root samples under a compound microscope. The percentage of root length colonized is
the standard metric:

- **0-15% colonization:** Severely deficient. Plants are essentially operating without
  mycorrhizal support. Direct inoculation with mycorrhizal spores placed next to
  roots or seeds is required. This is the intervention threshold.
- **15-40% colonization:** Moderate. The symbiosis is established but not optimal.
  Adding humic acid and fungal foods can stimulate further colonization without
  additional inoculation.
- **40-70% colonization:** Good. Most agricultural crops perform well at this level.
- **70-100% colonization:** Excellent. Typical of undisturbed natural ecosystems,
  perennial pastures, and mature orchards.

## Practices That Destroy Mycorrhizae

Mycorrhizal fungi are fragile organisms that are easily disrupted:

- **Tillage** severs hyphal networks and physically destroys the connection between
  fungal mycelium and roots. A single deep tilling can reduce colonization by 50-80%.
- **High-phosphorus fertilizers** suppress mycorrhizal colonization because the plant
  no longer "needs" the fungal partner for phosphorus. The plant cuts off carbon supply
  to the fungus, and the association collapses.
- **Fungicides** kill mycorrhizal fungi along with pathogenic fungi. Even systemic
  fungicides applied to foliage can be transported to roots and affect mycorrhizae.
- **Fallow periods without host plants** cause mycorrhizal networks to die back
  because they depend on live root exudates for carbon.
- **Soil compaction** reduces oxygen and physically restricts hyphal growth.

## Restoring Mycorrhizal Colonization

Ingham's protocol for restoring mycorrhizal fungi follows these steps:

1. **Stop destructive practices** — eliminate tillage, reduce phosphorus fertilizer,
   avoid fungicides, and maintain living root coverage year-round.
2. **Inoculate with mycorrhizal spores** if colonization is below 15%. Commercial
   inoculants containing AMF spores are placed directly in the planting hole or seed
  furrow so that spores contact emerging roots immediately.
3. **Feed fungi** with humic acid, kelp, and fish hydrolysate to promote hyphal
   growth without stimulating bacterial dominance.
4. **Apply AACT** made from fungal-dominated compost to supply the full suite of
  beneficial organisms that support mycorrhizal function, including bacterial
  competitors that suppress mycorrhizal pathogens.

## Glomalin: The Soil Glue

One of the most important contributions of mycorrhizal fungi to soil health is the
production of glomalin — a glycoprotein excreted by AMF hyphae. Glomalin acts as
a biological glue that binds soil microaggregates into stable macroaggregates, creating
the crumbly soil structure essential for water infiltration, root penetration, and
aeration.

Glomalin is remarkably persistent, with a turnover time of 7-42 years. It contributes
significantly to soil organic carbon pools. Soils under long-term mycorrhizal management
accumulate substantial glomalin reserves, improving soil structure beyond what physical
amendments alone can achieve.

## Disease Protection

Mycorrhizal colonization provides significant disease protection:

- **Physical barrier:** Fungal sheath blocks pathogen entry points on roots.
- **Chemical defense:** AMF produce antifungal and antibacterial compounds that suppress
  rhizosphere pathogens.
- **Systemic resistance:** Colonization triggers plant immune responses that protect
  foliage against foliar pathogens.
- **Nutrient competition:** Efficient nutrient acquisition reduces plant stress and
  opportunistic infection susceptibility.
