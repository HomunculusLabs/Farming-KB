# Agaricus Species Guide
The genus Agaricus contains some of the most commercially important and widely consumed mushrooms in the world, including the common button mushroom and its mature forms (cremini and portobello). The genus also includes excellent wild-foraged species and a few toxic members that foragers must learn to distinguish.
## Overview
Agaricus is a large genus of mushroom-forming fungi with a worldwide distribution. Most species are saprotrophic, decomposing organic matter in grasslands, forests, and compost. The genus includes the single most cultivated mushroom species on Earth (A. bisporus) and several prized wild edibles. Identification within the genus relies heavily on spore print color, bruising reactions, and habitat.
## Agaricus bisporus (Button, Cremini, Portobello)
### Overview
The most cultivated mushroom species globally, accounting for the majority of worldwide mushroom production. Native to Europe and North America, it was first cultivated in France in the 1700s and has since become a staple food worldwide.
### The Three Forms
What appears to be three different mushrooms are actually the same species at different stages of maturity:
- Button mushroom: immature, cap closed, white; mild flavor
- Cremini (baby bella): immature, cap slightly open, light brown; earthier flavor
- Portobello: fully mature, cap fully expanded, dark brown; rich, meaty flavor and texture
### Cultivation
A. bisporus cultivation is the most industrialized mushroom production system in the world:
- Substrate: composted straw, horse manure, and/or chicken manure with gypsum
- Two-phase composting: Phase 1 (outdoor, thermophilic composting for 7-14 days) and Phase 2 (pasteurization and conditioning at 140 F for 7-10 days)
- Spawn: grain spawn (rye or millet); commercial spawn is highly selected
- Casing layer: peat moss and limestone applied over the colonized compost to trigger fruiting
- Grow rooms: climate-controlled rooms with precise temperature, humidity, and CO2 management
- Temperature: 55-62 F for fruiting
- Yield: approximately 1 lb per square foot of growing surface per flush
- 3 flushes typical, with first flush being largest
- Total cycle: approximately 12-16 weeks from spawning to final harvest
### White vs Brown Varieties
- White strain: most common commercially; accounts for majority of world production
- Brown strain (cremini/portobello): increasingly popular; richer flavor, firmer texture
- Off-white strains: intermediate color and flavor
## Agaricus campestris (Meadow Mushroom / Field Mushroom)
### Overview
The wild ancestor of the cultivated button mushroom, A. campestris is a common find in pastures, meadows, and grasslands across Europe and North America. It is often considered superior in flavor to the cultivated A. bisporus.
### Identification
- Cap: 2-4 inches, white to cream, smooth, becoming brownish with age
- Gills: pink when young, progressing to chocolate brown as spores mature
- Stem: short, thick, white, with a thin ring (skirt)
- Flesh: white, firm, mild and pleasant odor (not chemical)
- Spore print: dark chocolate brown
- Habitat: open grasslands, pastures, meadows, lawns; often in fairy rings
- Season: late summer through fall (after rain)
### Foraging
- Found in open, grassy areas (not in forests)
- Often grows in rings (fairy rings) in pastures
- Check that the area has not been treated with herbicides or pesticides
- Pink gills are the key identification feature for young specimens
- As gills darken to chocolate brown, the mushroom is fully mature
### Look-alikes and Safety
- Amanita species: some have white gills and are deadly; A. campestris has pink-to-brown gills, not white gills
- Agaricus xanthodermus (Yellow Stainer): turns bright chrome yellow when the base is cut; has a chemical/phenol odor; causes GI distress
- Key safety check: cut the stem base and watch for yellow staining; if it stains bright yellow and smells like ink or chemicals, discard
- Always verify chocolate brown spore print
### Culinary Use
- Superior flavor compared to cultivated button mushroom
- Excellent sauteed, in soups, sauces, omelets, and stuffings
- Fresh shelf life: 3-5 days refrigerated
- Dries well for winter use
## Agaricus arvensis (Horse Mushroom)
### Overview
A large, robust wild Agaricus species found in pastures and grasslands. One of the finest wild edible mushrooms in Europe, prized for its size and almond-like aroma.
### Identification
- Cap: 4-10 inches, white to cream, smooth, can become scaly
- Gills: pink when young, dark chocolate brown when mature
- Stem: thick, solid, white with a prominent double ring
- Flesh: white, firm
- Odor: distinctive pleasant almond or anise scent (critical ID feature)
- Spore print: dark chocolate brown
- Habitat: pastures, meadows, grassy areas; often near horses or stables
- Season: late summer through fall
- Bruising reaction: cap may stain slightly yellow when bruised, but NOT the intense chrome yellow of A. xanthodermus
### Distinguishing from A. xanthodermus
- A. arvensis has a pleasant almond odor (not chemical/phenol)
- A. arvensis stains pale yellow, not intense chrome yellow
- A. arvensis is significantly larger
- A. arvensis has a double ring; A. xanthodermus has a single ring
- When in doubt, do not eat — the consequences of misidentification with A. xanthodermus include severe GI distress
### Culinary Use
- One of the best-flavored Agaricus species
- Excellent almond aroma that carries into cooked dishes
- Cap flesh is thick and meaty
- Use in any dish calling for mushrooms; pairs well with cream, butter, and herbs
## Agaricus silvicola (Wood Mushroom)
### Overview
A woodland-dwelling Agaricus species found in forests and wooded areas across Europe and North America. Less commonly collected than the meadow species but equally edible when correctly identified.
### Identification
- Cap: 3-6 inches, white to cream, smooth
- Gills: pink when young, dark brown when mature
- Stem: slender, white, with a thin ring
- Flesh: white
- Odor: almond or anise (similar to A. arvensis)
- Spore print: dark chocolate brown
- Habitat: conifer and mixed forests, often along trails and forest edges
- Season: late summer through fall
- Bruising: may stain slightly yellow (not intense chrome yellow)
### Key Difference from A. arvensis
- A. silvicola grows in forests, not open grasslands
- Generally smaller and more slender
- Less robust and meaty
### Culinary Use
- Good edible, though less meaty than A. arvensis
- Almond aroma is pleasant
- Use in any mushroom dish
## Identification Key for Common Agaricus Species
### Step 1: Habitat
- Open grassland/pasture: A. campestris or A. arvensis
- Forest/woodland: A. silvicola
- Cultivated/compost: A. bisporus
### Step 2: Odor
- Pleasant almond/anise: A. arvensis or A. silvicola
- Mild, mushroomy: A. campestris or A. bisporus
- Chemical/phenol/ink: A. xanthodermus — TOXIC, discard
### Step 3: Bruising Reaction
- Cut the stem base and wait 30-60 seconds
- Chrome yellow staining + chemical odor: A. xanthodermus — discard
- Pale yellow or no staining: likely A. arvensis, A. silvicola, or A. campestris
- No staining + chemical odor: still possibly A. xanthodermus — discard
### Step 4: Spore Print
- Dark chocolate brown: Agaricus genus confirmed
- White or other colors: NOT Agaricus — reconsider identification entirely
### Step 5: Size and Features
- Very large (4-10 inch cap) + double ring + almond odor: A. arvensis
- Medium (2-4 inch cap) + thin ring + mild odor: A. campestris
- Medium + forest habitat + almond odor: A. silvicola
- Cultivated/compost origin: A. bisporus
## Foraging Safety Rules
1. Always take a spore print (dark chocolate brown for all Agaricus)
2. Cut the stem base and check for yellow staining
3. Smell the mushroom (chemical/phenol = toxic)
4. Never eat any mushroom with white gills that you cannot positively identify
5. Learn A. xanthodermus thoroughly before collecting any wild Agaricus
6. When in doubt, throw it out
