# Mycoremediation of Explosives

Energetic compounds including TNT (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene), RDX (hexahydro-1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazine), and nitroglycerin contaminate military sites and manufacturing facilities worldwide. White-rot fungi degrade these nitroaromatic and nitramine compounds through ligninolytic and non-ligninolytic enzymatic pathways.

## TNT Degradation

TNT is the most studied explosive for fungal degradation. Phanerochaete chrysosporium serves as the primary model organism:
- LiP catalyzes initial oxidative attack on the TNT aromatic ring
- Laccase mediates coupling of TNT metabolites to humic substances
- MnP contributes to TNT oxidation through Mn(III)-mediated reactions

The primary fungal degradation pathway differs from bacterial routes. Fungi catalyze oxidation of the aromatic ring rather than the reductive dechlorination predominant in bacteria. Initial products include amino-dinitrotoluene derivatives and hydroxylated intermediates.

One important discovery: a TNT metabolite formed during P. chrysosporium degradation can inhibit lignin peroxidases, creating a self-limiting effect on the degradation process (Bumpus and Tatarko, 1994).

## RDX and HMX Degradation

RDX (hexahydro-1,3,5-trinitro-1,3,5-triazine) and HMX (octahydro-1,3,5,7-tetranitro-1,3,5,7-tetrazocine) are cyclic nitramine explosives:
- White-rot fungi degrade RDX through direct enzymatic attack
- P. chrysosporium removes both TNT and RDX to allowable limits from mixed waste streams
- Denitrifying fungi (Fusarium solani) show RDX transformation capacity
- Fungal interactions with RDX include both degradation and tolerance mechanisms

## Nitroglycerin Degradation

Nitroglycerin (glyceryl trinitrate) is degraded by several fungal species:
- Geotrichum candidum bioconverts glyceryl trinitrate to mononitrates
- P. chrysosporium achieves regioselective bioconversion to glyceryl 2-mononitrate
- Phosphatase and esterase enzymes catalyze nitrate ester hydrolysis

## Coupling to Humic Substances

A significant mechanism in explosive remediation is enzymatic coupling of TNT metabolites to soil organic matter:
- Laccases catalyze oxidative coupling of amino-dinitrotoluene derivatives to guaiacol and humic acid models
- Coupled products are generally less toxic and less bioavailable
- The irreversible nature of coupling is debated -- some studies suggest compost-bound residues may be less tightly associated than previously thought
- Whether coupling represents true detoxification or merely contaminant immobilization remains controversial

## Composting Approaches

Thermophilic composting has been applied to explosives-contaminated soil:
- Fungal and bacterial consortia in composting degrade TNT under thermophilic conditions
- First production-level bioremediation of explosives-contaminated soil in the US used composting
- Full-scale anaerobic bioremediation of TNT-contaminated soil demonstrated (Funk et al., 1995)
- Two-stage bioremediation (anaerobic followed by aerobic) improved treatment outcomes

## Full-Scale Applications

Sublette et al. (1992) demonstrated the first full-scale fungal treatment of explosives:
- P. chrysosporium immobilized on rotating biological contactor discs
- Treated "pink water" (TNT manufacturing waste stream)
- Effectively removed TNT and RDX to allowable discharge limits
- Demonstrated viability of engineered fungal systems for continuous treatment

## Limitations and Future Directions

- Self-inhibition by degradation products limits complete mineralization
- Irreversibility of humic binding needs further investigation
- Identification of fungal genes responsible for explosive degradation is ongoing
- Hybrid organisms combining fungal and bacterial degradation pathways may improve efficiency
- Development of more efficient organisms for field-scale schemes is needed

## Specific Fungal Species for Explosive Remediation

Several white-rot fungal species beyond Phanerochaete chrysosporium have demonstrated significant explosive degradation capabilities. Irpex lacteus has shown particularly effective TNT mineralization in soil systems, with some studies reporting complete removal of TNT from contaminated soil within 30 days under controlled conditions. Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster mushroom) degrades TNT through both ligninolytic and non-ligninolytic pathways and has the practical advantage of being easy to cultivate at scale. Trametes versicolor produces laccase enzymes at high levels and has been used in bioreactor systems for continuous TNT treatment. Bjerkandera adusta produces manganese peroxidase and versatile peroxidase, giving it a broad substrate range that includes both TNT and RDX.

## Molecular Mechanisms of TNT Degradation

The enzymatic degradation of TNT by white-rot fungi involves multiple pathways operating simultaneously. Lignin peroxidase (LiP) initiates oxidation of the aromatic ring through one-electron oxidation reactions, producing TNT radical cations that undergo subsequent transformations. Manganese peroxidase (MnP) generates Mn(III) chelates that act as diffusible oxidants capable of attacking TNT molecules at locations distant from the fungal hyphae. Laccase, a multicopper oxidase, catalyzes the oxidation of amino-dinitrotoluene metabolites, facilitating their polymerization and coupling to soil humic substances. Beyond these ligninolytic enzymes, non-ligninolytic mechanisms including cytochrome P450 monooxygenases and nitroreductases contribute to TNT transformation. The redundancy of these pathways is significant because it means that inhibiting one pathway does not necessarily prevent degradation.

## Phytoremediation Synergies with Mycoremediation

Combining fungal remediation with plant-based approaches can enhance overall treatment effectiveness. Certain plants, including poplar, parrot feather, and various aquatic species, can uptake and metabolize TNT and its breakdown products. When mycorrhizal fungi colonize the roots of these plants, the combined system can access explosives from both the soil solution and the soil matrix. The plant provides carbohydrates to fuel fungal metabolism, while the fungi extend the effective treatment zone beyond the root rhizosphere. This synergistic approach has shown particular promise for treating mixed contamination where explosives co-occur with heavy metals or petroleum hydrocarbons.

## Environmental Factors Affecting Treatment

The effectiveness of mycoremediation for explosives is strongly influenced by environmental conditions. Temperature affects both fungal growth rates and enzyme activity, with most white-rot fungi showing optimal activity between 25-30°C. Soil moisture must be maintained at levels that support fungal growth without creating anaerobic conditions that favor bacterial reductive pathways. pH affects enzyme stability and activity; most ligninolytic enzymes function best under slightly acidic conditions (pH 4-5). The presence of alternative carbon sources can either enhance or inhibit explosive degradation depending on the fungal species and the type of carbon source available. Nitrogen availability is particularly important because many explosive compounds are nitrogen-rich, and excess nitrogen can suppress the production of ligninolytic enzymes through nitrogen catabolite repression.

## Scale-Up Challenges and Field Applications

Translating laboratory-scale mycoremediation results to field conditions presents several challenges. Maintaining optimal moisture, temperature, and aeration conditions in large-scale soil treatment systems is more difficult than in controlled laboratory experiments. Competition from native soil microorganisms can reduce the effectiveness of introduced fungal strains. The heterogeneity of field contamination, where explosives concentrations can vary by orders of magnitude across a single site, complicates treatment design. Despite these challenges, several successful field applications have been documented. Inoculation of compost windrows with white-rot fungi has been used to treat TNT-contaminated soil at military installations, achieving treatment goals within 20-40 weeks of active composting. Mycofiltration systems using fungal mats have been deployed to treat explosives-contaminated groundwater at several sites.

## Enzyme Engineering Approaches

Recent advances in molecular biology have opened new possibilities for enhancing the explosive degradation capabilities of white-rot fungi. Genetic engineering approaches include overexpressing ligninolytic enzymes in fungal strains, introducing bacterial explosive degradation genes into fungal hosts, and creating enzyme cocktails with enhanced activity against specific explosive compounds. Directed evolution techniques, where enzymes are subjected to iterative rounds of mutation and selection for improved activity, have produced lignin peroxidase and laccase variants with significantly enhanced TNT degradation rates. While regulatory and public acceptance challenges limit the deployment of genetically modified organisms in field remediation, these approaches provide valuable tools for understanding degradation mechanisms and developing more effective treatment strategies.

## Cost-Benefit Analysis

Mycoremediation of explosives offers significant cost advantages compared to conventional remediation technologies. Excavation and incineration of contaminated soil, the most common conventional approach, typically costs $200-800 per cubic meter of treated soil. Thermal desorption processes cost $150-500 per cubic meter. In contrast, fungal composting approaches typically cost $50-150 per cubic meter, and in situ treatment using fungal inoculation can cost as little as $10-50 per cubic meter. These cost advantages are particularly significant for large military sites where contamination may extend over hundreds of hectares and to depths of several meters. Additionally, mycoremediation is less energy-intensive, produces fewer greenhouse gas emissions, and restores soil biological function rather than sterilizing it, providing long-term ecological benefits beyond contaminant removal.

## Phytoremediation Synergies

Mycoremediation of explosives can be enhanced through combination with phytoremediation, the use of plants to clean contaminated environments. Certain plant species can take up explosive compounds from soil through their roots, and the rhizosphere -- the zone of soil immediately surrounding plant roots -- provides an enriched environment for both plant-associated bacteria and mycorrhizal fungi. Research has shown that combining white-rot fungi with appropriate plant species can accelerate TNT degradation rates beyond what either approach achieves alone. The plants provide root exudates that stimulate fungal activity, while the fungi degrade explosive compounds in the rhizosphere, reducing the toxic effects on plant roots and allowing more vigorous plant growth. This synergistic approach is particularly promising for the revegetation of contaminated sites where establishing a plant cover is a secondary remediation objective.

## Military Application and Regulatory Framework

The United States Department of Defense has funded significant research into mycoremediation of explosives, recognizing the potential for cost-effective remediation of the thousands of military training ranges and munitions production facilities contaminated with explosive residues. The Strategic Environmental Research and Development Program (SERDP) and the Environmental Security Technology Certification Program (ESTCP) have supported field demonstrations of fungal remediation technologies at multiple military installations. Regulatory approval for mycoremediation as a cleanup technology requires demonstration that degradation products are less toxic than the parent compounds and that the treatment achieves applicable cleanup standards. As regulatory frameworks for biological remediation technologies mature, mycoremediation is increasingly recognized as a viable alternative to conventional approaches.

## Comparative Remediation Technologies

Mycoremediation exists alongside several other biological and physicochemical remediation technologies for explosive-contaminated soil. Phytoremediation uses plants to take up, sequester, or metabolize explosive compounds. Biostimulation adds nutrients and oxygen to stimulate the activity of native explosive-degrading bacteria. Bioaugmentation introduces specific bacterial strains with documented explosive degradation capabilities. Compared to these approaches, mycoremediation offers the advantage of fungal ligninolytic enzymes that can degrade a wider range of recalcitrant compounds than most bacterial systems. The optimal remediation strategy often combines multiple approaches: for example, soil composting with white-rot fungi followed by phytoremediation with native plant species provides both initial contaminant reduction and long-term ecological restoration. The choice of technology depends on site-specific factors including contaminant concentration, soil type, groundwater depth, timeline, and budget.
