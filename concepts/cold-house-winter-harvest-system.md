# Cold House Winter Harvest System

The cold house winter harvest system, developed by Eliot Coleman at Four Season Farm in
Harborside, Maine (USDA Zone 5), demonstrates that fresh vegetables can be produced
year-round in cold climates using unheated greenhouses combined with simple, low-cost
technologies. The system is built on three foundational components that work in synergy:
cold-hardy vegetables, [[coleman-succession-planting]], and [[coleman-protected-cultivation]] through double-
[[coleman-double-layer-climate-modification-temperature-data]]. (see [[coleman-double-cover-microclimate-protection]]).

## The Three Components

### Cold-Hardy Vegetables

The first component is the selection of appropriate plant species. Cold-hardy vegetables
are those that tolerate and even thrive in cold temperatures. Many of these species
naturally grow in mild winter climates without any protection at all. The cold house
system takes these naturally cold-tolerant species and extends their growing range into
much harsher climates by providing minimal but effective shelter. (see [[query-what-are-predatory-mites-and-how-can-i-use-them-for-pest-control]]).

Coleman has identified approximately thirty vegetable species suitable for cold house
production. The most reliable and productive include spinach, mache (corn salad),
claytonia (miner's lettuce), carrots, leeks, scallions, tatsoi, mizuna, pak choi,
radicchio, endive, escarole, kale, chard, arugula, parsley, radishes, turnips, and
beet greens. These crops share several characteristics: they tolerate repeated freezing
and thawing, they have lower light requirements than warm-season crops, and their
eating quality actually improves in cool temperatures. Sugars accumulate in response
to cold, making winter-harvested vegetables sweeter than their summer counterparts.

Notably, the cold house system does not attempt to grow warm-season crops (tomatoes,
peppers, cucumbers, eggplants) through the winter. Instead, it focuses on leafy greens
and root crops that naturally belong to the cool season. This is a fundamentally
different approach from heated greenhouse production, which attempts to maintain summer
conditions year-round at great energy cost.

### Succession Planting

The second component is careful succession planting. In the cold house system, sowing
dates are critical and must be calculated in reverse from the goal of having crops at
or near maturity before the shortest days of winter. The key insight is that plant
growth slows dramatically as day length decreases below ten hours per day. Once past
mid-November at Coleman's latitude (approximately 44°N), most crops essentially stop
growing and enter a state of semi-hibernation. The harvest during the deepest winter
months comes from cutting existing plants (cut-and-come-again harvesting) rather than
from new growth.

Coleman begins planting winter crops on August 1, which he calls the "second spring."
Planting continues through September and October, with the latest sowings intended to
reach harvestable size just as the day length begins increasing again in February.
Each crop has specific ideal sowing dates that must be determined through local
experimentation — the dates that work in coastal Maine will differ from those in the
Midwest, the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], or the South.

The succession approach means that greenhouses are never left empty. Within twenty-four
hours of harvesting a crop, the bed is cleared, the soil is prepared, and the next
crop is planted. Careful records are maintained to ensure varied crop rotation and to
refine sowing dates from year to year.

### Protected Cultivation (Double-Layer System)

The third and most innovative component is the double-layer protection system. The
outer layer is a standard unheated hoop house (gothic-arch, pipe-frame, plastic-
covered). Coleman's commercial houses are 30 feet wide and 96 feet long, aligned on
an east-west axis. The inner layer is floating row cover material (spunbonded
polyester) supported by flat-topped wire wickets at approximately 12 inches above the
soil surface, spaced every 4 feet along the length of 30-inch-wide growing beds.

This double-layer system creates a "twice-tempered" climate. When the outdoor
temperature drops to -15°F (-26°C), the air temperature inside the greenhouse drops
to approximately 28-32°F (-2 to 0°C), and the temperature under the inner row cover
layer drops only to 15-18°F (-10 to -8°C). This temperature modification is sufficient
to protect cold-hardy vegetables from freeze damage while keeping the soil from
freezing solid.

The inner layer also increases relative humidity, which provides additional freeze
protection and reduces moisture stress on the plants. The soil itself acts as a thermal
mass, absorbing heat during sunny days and releasing it slowly at night. No artificial
heating, [[coleman-soil-heat-mass-passive-thermal-storage]] systems, or insulating materials are required.
