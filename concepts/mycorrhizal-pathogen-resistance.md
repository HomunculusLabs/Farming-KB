### The Hartig Net

In both ectomycorrhizal and some endomycorrhizal associations, the Hartig net — an intricate network of fungal hyphae that penetrates between root cortical cells — provides an additional layer of physical protection. By occupying the intercellular spaces, the Hartig net leaves no room for pathogenic fungi or bacteria to establish themselves within the root cortex.

## Biochemical Defense

Beyond physical barriers, mycorrhizal fungi deploy an arsenal of chemical defenses that actively suppress or destroy pathogenic organisms.

### Antibiotics and Biocides

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi create metabolites that include biocides, antibiotics, and pest-specific chemicals capable of attacking and destroying harmful organisms. These compounds are produced constitutively and can be released into the rhizosphere, creating a zone of inhibition around colonized roots. The specific chemistry of these metabolites varies among fungal species, but the effect is consistent: pathogens that approach mycorrhizal roots encounter a hostile chemical environment.

### Nutrient Competition

Mycorrhizal fungi compete with pathogenic organisms for the limited amounts of nutrients available in the soil. By efficiently scavenging and sequestering nutrients, mycorrhizae deprive potential pathogens of the resources they need to establish infections. This competitive exclusion is particularly effective against opportunistic pathogens that require nutrient-rich conditions to proliferate.

### Mycorrhizospheric Organisms

Mycorrhizal fungi support communities of mycorrhizospheric organisms — bacteria and other microorganisms that live in association with the mycelial network. Many of these organisms provide their own protective metabolites, including additional antibiotics and enzymes that degrade pathogen cell walls. The mycorrhizosphere thus functions as a cooperative defense ecosystem, where multiple species contribute to plant protection. The role of [[mycorrhizal-plant-signaling]] that occurs through these networks represents a paradigm-shifting understanding of plant communication.

### Allelopathic Signals

Mycelial networks transmit not only defensive warning signals but also allelopathic compounds — toxins that can suppress the growth of neighboring competing plants. This dual function means the network simultaneously protects its host community from external threats and manages internal competitive dynamics.

## Priming of Plant Immune Responses

Mycorrhizal colonization does not merely provide external protection — it fundamentally alters the plant's own immune system, making it more responsive and effective.

### Systemic Defense Activation

When mycorrhizal plants are damaged, they can heal faster by forming wound barriers and clogging stomata — the tiny pores in plant leaves through which gases and water vapor pass. This accelerated wound response suggests that mycorrhizal colonization primes the plant's systemic defense pathways, enabling faster and more effective responses to injury.

### Hormonal Mediation

The formation of mycorrhizae increases the production of several plant hormones that are directly involved in defense signaling. Jasmonic acid and salicylic acid, both of which help plants defend themselves against pathogens and insects, are produced in greater quantities in mycorrhizal plants. These hormones are key regulators of systemic acquired resistance (SAR) and induced systemic resistance (ISR), two major pathways by which plants activate broad-spectrum immune defenses.

### Stronger Vascular Systems

Mycorrhizal plants develop stronger vascular systems, which not only improve nutrient transport but also make it more difficult for vascular wilt pathogens — such as Verticillium — to colonize and block water-conducting tissues. A robust vascular system is inherently more resistant to the kind of internal disruption that vascular pathogens cause.

## Nematode Resistance

Specific attention has been given to the interaction between mycorrhizal fungi and parasitic nematodes, with hundreds of studies pairing specific crop plants with specific fungi.

The defense mechanisms against nematodes operate on multiple levels:

- Chemical deterrents: Arbuscular mycorrhizae produce chemicals that reduce nematode reproduction, feeding, and attraction to plant roots.

- Physical exclusion: The fungal mantle surrounding roots creates a physical barrier that nematodes must penetrate to reach root tissues.

- Root architecture: The shorter, thicker, more densely branched roots of mycorrhizal plants are less susceptible to nematode damage.

- Vigor: Colonized plants are stronger and healthier overall, enabling them to tolerate or resist nematode feeding damage more effectively.

Studies confirm that soybeans, tomatoes, oats, onions, and cotton are all less susceptible to nematodes when arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi are present and active in the soil. The common burrowing nematode, among other species, shows significantly reduced impact on mycorrhizal plants.

## Practical Applications

The pathogen resistance conferred by mycorrhizal fungi has direct applications in agriculture and horticulture:

- Crop protection: Inoculation with appropriate mycorrhizal species can reduce dependence on chemical nematicides and fungicides, lowering production costs and environmental impact.

- Disease-suppressive soils: Managing soils to support robust mycorrhizal communities contributes to the development of disease-suppressive soils that naturally limit pathogen populations.

- Hydroponic production: The protective benefits extend to hydroponic systems, where inoculated roots resist common pathogens including Rhizoctonia, Fusarium, Pythium, and Phytophthora.

- Integrated pest management: Mycorrhizal fungi can serve as a component of IPM strategies, working alongside other biological control agents such as Trichoderma species.

- Root rot management: Colonized plants show increased resistance to major root rot pathogens, reducing losses in susceptible crops.
