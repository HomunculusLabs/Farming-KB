# Keypoint Irrigation and Saddle Dams

## Overview

Keypoint irrigation and saddle dams represent the upper tier of [[permaculture]] water
strategy. Where valley dams collect water at the base of slopes, keypoint systems
and saddle dams operate at the highest points of a property, turning ridgelines and
saddles into gravity-fed water distribution hubs. These high storages unlock
irrigation, hydro-electric power, fire protection, and wildlife support across the
entire landscape below.

## Flag Irrigation from Keypoint Dams

The flag irrigation system is a remarkably efficient gravity-fed watering method
that allows a single person to irrigate hundreds of acres. A keypoint dam is
constructed at the keypoint of a valley — the point where the convex slope of the
ridge flattens into the concave slope of the valley floor. From this high position,
water is released through gated pipes into a system of contour ditches that follow
the land's natural contours across the slope.

Flags (simple markers or adjustable gates) are placed at intervals along these
contour ditches. The operator moves from flag to flag, opening each gate to release
water downslope. Because water flows by gravity through the ditch system, the
operator simply walks the line, opening and closing gates in sequence. This method
requires no pumps, no fuel, and minimal physical labor — one person with a flag
system can water very large areas that would otherwise require mechanical equipment
and significant energy inputs.

## Ditch-as-Lake Construction

Mollison advocates a technique where irrigation ditches are constructed with the
profile of small lakes — wide, shallow, and capable of holding substantial volumes
of water rather than mere channels. These "ditch-as-lake" systems serve dual
purposes: they transport water across the landscape while simultaneously providing
surface storage and evaporation reduction through their greater depth. A large-scale
ditch-lake system becomes a chain of interconnected small ponds threading across a
slope, each section holding water for slow release into garden or agricultural zones.

## Saddle Dams: Highest Water Storages

A saddle dam is built in a saddle — the low point between two hilltops along a
ridgeline. These are the highest water storages possible on a property, often
sitting hundreds of feet above the valley floor. Because they occupy natural
saddles, the earthworks are relatively small: the saddle provides most of the
containment, and only the low point needs to be dammed. Saddle dams capture
rainfall runoff from both sides of the ridge and store it at the highest available
elevation, maximizing gravitational potential for all downstream uses.

## Hydro-Electric and Mechanical Power

A 400-foot fall from a high saddle dam to a lower collection point generates
substantial hydraulic head for power generation. Even modest flows at this head can
drive pelton wheels or turbines for hydro-electric generation. Mollison describes
staging pressure systems where water is dropped in stages, with each stage
extracting mechanical or electrical energy before the water continues downward.
This cascading approach extracts maximum useful energy from the stored water before
it reaches the lowest point for irrigation or domestic use. A high saddle dam can
provide reliable off-grid power, especially combined with wind or solar for dry-
season backup.

## Fire Protection from High Storage

High-elevation water storages are invaluable for fire protection. A saddle dam or
keypoint tank positioned above structures provides gravity-fed water pressure for
sprinkler systems without any pumps or external power. Mollison recommends
installing fire protection sprinklers fed directly from the high storage, with
piping running downslope to vulnerable zones. During a fire event, these systems
operate even if power is lost, because gravity provides all the pressure needed.
The water from a saddle dam at height can protect homesteads, animal shelters, and
forest edges from advancing [[wildfire]].

## Wildlife Enrichment

High water storages dramatically increase wildlife habitat value across the
property. Mollison emphasizes that seed-eating birds — critical for natural
reforestation and pest control — must have access to water within 24 hours of
eating dry seeds, or they cannot digest them properly. Without high-elevation water
sources, birds that feed in upper forest zones must descend to valley dams, wasting
energy and reducing foraging range. Saddle dams distributed across the upper
landscape allow birds and other wildlife to remain in productive foraging zones
while meeting their water needs, increasing seed dispersal and biodiversity.

## Contour Dams on Knolls

Contour dams are shallow dams built on the contour lines of knolls — small hills
rising from the landscape. Because they are sited on convex upper slopes, they are
naturally shallow with large surface areas relative to their volume, making them
extremely cheap to construct. Minimal earthworks yield substantial evaporative
surface and habitat area. They serve as wildlife watering points, micro-climate
moderators, and emergency irrigation reserves. Multiple small contour dams across
a property create a distributed water network that reduces reliance on any single
large dam.

## Spillway Design

Proper spillway design is critical to dam safety. Mollison's key principle: the
spillway must contour away from the dam wall, never over or adjacent to it. Water
overflowing a dam must be directed along the contour, away from the earthworks,
where it grades out gradually into sheet flow across the landscape. This prevents
concentrated erosive flow from undermining the dam structure. The spillway should
be wide, vegetated, and armoured with stone or grass to disperse energy. A well-
designed spillway turns a potentially destructive overflow event into gentle
distributed irrigation across the slopes below.

## Water Priority Systems

When designing a property-wide water system, Mollison establishes a clear priority
hierarchy:

1. **Domestic supply** — drinking water, household use, and fire protection receive
   the first and highest-quality allocation, sourced from the cleanest storages.
2. **Intensive garden** — vegetable gardens, orchards, and nursery systems receive
   the next priority, as they produce the highest value per unit of water applied.
3. **Extensive agriculture** — broadacre crops, pasture, and livestock grazing use
   remaining water through larger-scale methods like flag irrigation or flood flow.

This hierarchy ensures critical uses are protected even in drought. High storages
feed the top priorities by gravity, while lower dams supply extensive agriculture.

## Target Water Coverage

Mollison suggests that 13-15% of the landscape should ideally be under water in
some form — dams, swales, contour channels, marshes, and irrigated garden surfaces.
This target provides sufficient storage for year-round supply, adequate wildlife
habitat, fire protection coverage, and microclimate moderation. Achieving this
through a combination of high saddle dams, keypoint dams, valley dams, contour
dams, and [[swale]] networks creates a resilient, self-watering landscape that minimizes
external irrigation dependency and maximizes ecological function.

## Design Principles Summary

- Always store water at the highest possible elevation for maximum gravitational
  utility across all downstream functions.
- Use saddle dams on ridgelines as the primary high-elevation storage.
- Design spillways to contour away from dam walls and grade to sheet flow.
- Prioritize water allocation: domestic first, intensive garden second, extensive
  agriculture third.
- Distribute water storages across the landscape to support wildlife movement and
  reduce single-point-of-failure risk.
- Aim for 13-15% water coverage across the total property area.
