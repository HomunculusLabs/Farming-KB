## Psychedelic 1960s Counterculture and Mushrooms

The 1960s counterculture represented a fundamental [[ceremonial-shamanic-use-psychoactive-mushrooms]]. What had been an obscure
anthropological curiosity became, within a decade, one of the defining symbols of a
generation's rebellion against mainstream values.

## From Wasson to the Masses

The [[wasson-mexico-expeditions]] of the 1950s set the
stage, but it was [Wasson's 1957 Life magazine article "Seeking
the Magic Mushroom" that ignited the fuse. Read by millions, the article introduced
the concept of psychoactive mushrooms to a vast Western audience for the first time.

Key figures of the emerging psychedelic movement, including [[timothy-leary]] at Harvard, experimented with psychedelics as a direct result. Leary
transferred his focus from LSD to mushrooms after reading Wasson's account, helping
to establish psilocybin as a central element of the emerging psychedelic
consciousness.

## The Pilgrimage to Huautla

The trickle of visitors to [Maria Sabina's village of Huautla de Jimenez in the early 1960s became a flood, until
Oaxaca was awash with hippies following the magic mushroom trail. The trip became
fashionable, and with rock stars including Pete Townshend, John Lennon, and Bob
Dylan rumoured to have made the pilgrimage, Huautla was perceived as an essential
countercultural destination.

This celebrity association, whether real or imagined, lent the mushroom experience
an aura of authenticity and spiritual depth that was enormously attractive to the
counterculture.

## Clash of Cultures

The arrival of hippie culture in indigenous Oaxaca created profound cultural
friction. For hippies, the mushrooms were "psychedelic," which meant they were bound
up with notions of authenticity, freedom, individualism, bohemianism and rebellion.
Used to seeing psychedelics as drugs rather than deities, as commodities rather than
conscious entities, many visitors expected to buy and consume mushrooms on demand,
irrespective of local sensibilities.

They did not want to be bound to the curandero-led velada, or to the archaic mores
of an animist peasant culture. One American hippy visitor was recorded as saying:
"Look, man. You can go for that curandero stuff if you want, but you don't need
all that Indian baggage."

This attitude contrasted sharply with the Mazatec understanding of mushrooms as
sacred entities, beings with whom healers could form beneficial relationships and
who spoke through the curanderos in improvised, poetic chants believed to possess
healing power.
