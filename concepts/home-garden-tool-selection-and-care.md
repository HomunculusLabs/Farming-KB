## Home Garden Tool Selection and Care

## Overview

[[dryland-vegetable-gardening]] requires the right tools, properly maintained. Unlike power gardening approaches that rely on rototillers and mechanized equipment, the low-input garden depends on well-chosen hand tools that can perform efficiently with minimal financial investment. The philosophy is that a few quality hand tools, kept sharp and in good repair, will outperform a shed full of cheap, poorly maintained equipment. This approach is particularly relevant for gardeners working with limited budgets, small plots, or in situations where fuel and electricity may be unreliable.

## Essential Hand Tools for the Low-Input Garden

Solomon identifies several tools as essential for the serious vegetable gardener:

**The Garden Fork (Digging Fork):**

- The primary soil-working tool for the low-input gardener.
- Superior to a spade for initial soil breaking and loosening because it aerates rather than compacts.
- Four tines of square or rectangular cross-section, approximately 11-12 inches long.
- Best quality forks have tines that are individually forged and set, not stamped from a single piece.
- A good fork allows the gardener to work clay soils without creating the hard clods that spade-work produces.
- The fork is used for turning soil, incorporating amendments, lifting root crops, and breaking compacted ground.

**The Garden Spade (Digging Spade):**

- Used for specific tasks where a fork is less suitable, such as digging trenches, edging beds, and slicing through roots.
- A flat blade with a slight curve or lift at the bottom edge.
- The spade should be pushed into the ground with the foot, not swung like a shovel.
- D-handled spades offer better control than long-handled variants for precise work.
- Stainless steel blades resist soil adhesion but are more expensive; carbon steel is adequate with proper care.

**The Garden Hoe:**

- Perhaps the most important weeding tool in the low-input garden.
- Solomon distinguishes between several types:
  - The draw hoe (traditional pattern): Pulled toward the gardener for weeding and hilling.
  - The scuffle hoe (stirrup hoe or oscillating hoe): Pushed and pulled with a slicing action; excellent for small weeds in loose soil.
  - The collinear hoe (developed by [[coleman-row-cover-fabric-management]] and hoops:** For [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

**Cleaning and rust prevention:**

- Clean tools after each use to remove soil and plant material.
