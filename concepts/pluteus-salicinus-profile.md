# Pluteus salicinus

Pluteus salicinus is a little-known wood-inhabiting psychoactive mushroom species
belonging to the Pluteaceae family. It represents one of the less commonly
encountered European species containing psilocybin and is notable for growing on
dead wood rather than in pastures or grassland like many other psychoactive species.

## Taxonomy and Distribution

Within the Pluteaceae family, approximately 45 European species of the genus
Pluteus exist, some of which produce psilocybin. Historically, the Pluteus species
were classified as belonging to the Amanitaceae family (alongside
[[amanita-genus-morphology]] species), but were later reclassified into
their own family based on distinctive morphological and genetic characteristics.

Pluteus salicinus has been identified in several European countries and grows on
dead wood, particularly willow (Salix), from which its species name derives.
The species has also been reported in North America, suggesting a wide
distribution across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The Pluteaceae family also includes [[amanita-genus-morphology]] species,
which are mycorrhizal, but Pluteus itself is saprophytic, obtaining nutrients
from decaying wood — a fundamental ecological difference that separates the two
genera despite their taxonomic proximity.

## Morphology

Pluteus salicinus displays several distinguishing features:

- **Cap:** 1.5–4 cm broad, convex to plano-convex, often with a low umbo.
  Surface smooth to slightly wrinkled, grayish-brown to dark brown, sometimes
  with a bluish or bluish-green tint that develops particularly at the center
  or base. Some specimens are entirely white (albino forms), though these
  albino fruiting bodies have stems that still display the characteristic blue
  staining at the base
- **Gills:** Free from the stem (a characteristic of the Pluteaceae family),
  turning pink as spores mature — unlike the dark spore prints of
  [[psilocybe-genus-diversity-and-taxonomy]] and
  [[panaeolus-subbalteatus-profile]] species
- **Stem:** 3–6 cm long, 2–5 mm thick, cylindrical, white to pale gray,
  sometimes with blue-green staining at the base. No ring or volva present
- **Flesh:** Thin, white to grayish, no distinctive odor or taste
- **Spore print:** Pink to salmon-colored, which is a key distinguishing feature
  from the purple-brown to black spore prints of most other psychoactive genera
- **Spores:** Smooth, elliptical, 6–8 × 5–6 µm, pinkish in mass

## Microscopic Features

- **Spores:** Smooth, ellipsoid to broadly ellipsoid, 6–8 × 5–6 µm, thin-walled,
  pinkish in mass deposit
- **Cheilocystidia:** Numerous, ventricose to cylindrical with a narrow neck,
  sometimes capitate, a key diagnostic feature for the genus Pluteus
- **Pleurocystidia:** Similar to cheilocystidia but less numerous
- **Pileipellis:** Cutis of cylindrical hyphae with embedded pigment

## Chemistry

Chemical analysis of Pluteus salicinus has confirmed the presence of psilocybin,
[[query-what-is-the-difference-between-psilocybin-and-psilocin]], and
[[amanita-muscaria-chemistry-and-pharmacology]], placing it firmly
among the psychoactive mushroom species.

The presence of urea is notable from a chemo-taxonomical perspective. Among the
species discussed in Gartz's work, urea is present only in
[[panaeolus-subbalteatus-profile]] and Pluteus species. Urea was
detected incidentally during thin-layer chromatography testing intended to
discover indole compounds. While its levels are trivial in terms of psychoactivity,
urea serves as a potential marker for distinguishing these genera.

## Habitat and Ecology

Unlike the pasture and grassland species such as
[[psilocybe-semilanceata]] or the dung-inhabiting
[[panaeolus-subbalteatus-profile]], Pluteus salicinus
is a wood-inhabiting species. It grows saprophytically on decaying hardwood,
particularly willow (Salix species), but also on other fallen timber including
elm, ash, and beech.

This habitat preference places it in a similar ecological niche to
[[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]], which also colonizes wood
debris, though the two species are not closely related taxonomically. Fruiting
occurs from late summer through autumn.

## Field Identification Challenges

Pluteus salicinus is easily overlooked in the field for several reasons:

1. Its small size compared to more conspicuous mushroom species
2. Its growth on dead wood, often in shaded, damp environments
3. The similarity of its general appearance to many non-psychoactive Pluteus
   species that also grow on wood
4. The variability in its coloration, ranging from typical brownish to entirely
   white albino forms
5. The blue staining, when present, can be subtle and easily missed

The pink spore print is the most reliable macroscopic characteristic for placing
a specimen in the genus Pluteus, but this does not distinguish psychoactive from
non-psychoactive species within the genus.

## Cultural and Research Significance

Pluteus salicinus is among the least studied of the European psychoactive mushroom
species. No well-documented cases of intentional use or self-experimentation have
been reported in the literature. Its significance lies primarily in expanding our
understanding of the taxonomic diversity of psilocybin-producing fungi.

The discovery of psilocybin in yet another unrelated genus reinforces the
understanding that psilocybin biosynthesis has evolved independently multiple
times across the fungal kingdom, rather than being a marker of close evolutionary
relationship.
