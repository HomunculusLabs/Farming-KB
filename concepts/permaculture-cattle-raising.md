# Permaculture Cattle Raising

Cows can be integrated into [[permaculture-design]] systems for dairy, meat, and manure, grazing in orchards and pastures while improving soil fertility.

## Role in the System

Cows can graze in the [[permaculture-zone-planning]] orchard after at least 7 years if [[permaculture-sheep-raising]] have done well without harming trees. They should not clear vegetation but simply graze to control grass. They need fresh, clean water at all times and a weather-sheltered salt block.

### Pasture

Best dairy cow pasture includes lush clover and alfalfa with grasses and herbs like lavender, mustard, rosemary, and sage. Winter feed: tender green hay cut just after blooming, 2-3 pounds (0.9-1.3 kg) per 100 pounds (45 kg) body weight. Each cow needs about two tons of hay over the winter. Don't give more than she can eat between milkings or she won't eat old stuff.

### Supplemental Feeding

After calving, supplement with grains to increase milk production, increasing slowly and tapering near end of lactation. Maximum: 1 pound (0.5 kg) grain per 3 pounds (1.4 kg) milk. With quality grass and 0.5 pounds grain she gives about 90% of capacity. Cornstalks, washed sliced root vegetables, and whole sunflower heads make great supplements. Grains should be chopped or ground, mixing different types together. Quality alfalfa pasture or hay may eliminate need for supplements.

If you have no fenced pasture, stake a milk cow with a large bucket of water and move her three times daily.

## Breeds

Hundreds of breeds divided into beef and dairy. Dairy cows produce more milk and are gentler. Dual-purpose breeds like Shorthorn and Brown Swiss are most versatile for small farmers -- young bulls used for beef, steers for oxen.

If inexperienced, buy a 4-5 year old cow (more experienced in calving) rather than a heifer. Best dairy breeds: Ayrshire, Brown Swiss, Guernsey, Holstein, Jersey, Red Poll, Devon.

## Handling

Cattle are red-green color blind with nearly 360-degree vision and startle easily. Their lives are based on habit -- be patient, quiet, gentle, and utilize their herd instinct (if one does something, they all follow). Use food as a reward and they will come when called.

Safety warnings:

- Cows with calves are very protective

- Cows in labor can be violent

- Never turn your back on a bull

- Never let kids around a bull

- If a bull threatens you, eat it

- Don't breed a bull younger than 18 months

### Common Problems

- Downed cow: help reposition legs on a level surface; if injured, feed and water until movable

- Scours (severe calf diarrhea): give Kaopectate (pectin and kaolin clay); prevention is clean birthing area and colostrum

- Heel fly: eggs laid in heel, larvae bore through skin; prevent by reducing fly population with frogs or lizards

## Breeding

A cow can have a calf every year and provide milk almost the entire year, until 10-16 years old. Don't breed before one year old, but be careful -- a three-month-old can conceive. Breed her, then dry her up 2-3 months before calving. Gestation: 285 days (9.5 months). She goes into heat 30-60 days after calving; heat lasts 18-24 hours. Signs: restless, stamping feet, tail twitching, vulva red and swollen, other cows try to mount her. If breeding mid-May, calves arrive early March.

## Milking

Keep her milked at least once daily to prevent mastitis. Expect at least a gallon per milking. First 4 days after birth is colostrum for the calf. A family needs about a gallon per day; a couple of extra cows can produce a small profit. Care and milking is the same as for [[permaculture-goat-raising]].

Weaning: when the calf shows interest in other foods, use a creep feeder (structure with tempting food only calves can enter). Wean by 4-5 months. During summer they need at least an acre of good pasture. In winter a weaned calf eats about 2 pounds hay per 100 pounds body weight daily. Three pounds of cornstalks and cobs equals one pound of hay.

## Pasture Management for Cattle

Cattle are powerful tools for pasture improvement when managed through [[permaculture-grazing-and-pasture]] systems. Their large hooves break soil crust, incorporate organic matter, and press seeds into contact with soil -- a natural form of aeration that reduces the need for mechanical intervention.

Rotational grazing principles for cattle:

- Divide pasture into 8-12 paddocks minimum; move cattle every 1-3 days during peak growth

- Rest each paddock for 30-90 days depending on season and rainfall

- Stock at 1-2 animal units per acre on good pasture; less on poor soil

- Graze tall grass down to 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) to maintain root reserves

- Avoid overgrazing -- if grass is grazed below 3 inches, recovery time doubles

Integrating cattle with silvopasture-systems|silvopasture provides shade, reduces heat stress, and diversifies income through timber or fruit production. Cattle manure deposited on pasture returns approximately 80% of consumed nutrients to the soil surface, building organic matter over time.

## Health and Preventive Care

A proactive herd health program prevents most common cattle diseases and reduces the need for costly interventions.

Core vaccination schedule:

- BRD (bovine respiratory disease) complex: vaccinate calves at branding and weaning

- Leptospirosis: annual booster for breeding cows

- Clostridial diseases (7-way or 8-way): standard for all cattle

- IBR and BVD: viral respiratory and reproductive diseases; critical for breeding herds

Hoof care is essential -- trim hooves at least once per year, more often in wet climates. Foot rot, caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum, is common in muddy conditions and treated with zinc sulfate foot baths. Proper drainage in high-traffic areas around waterers and feeders prevents many hoof problems.

Parasite control:

- Deworm cattle based on fecal egg counts rather than calendar schedules

- Use pour-on or oral dewormers strategically to avoid drug resistance

- Copper oxide wire particles can help control internal parasites in grazing cattle

- Young cattle are most susceptible; mature cattle develop partial immunity

## Butter and Cheese Making

Processing milk on-farm transforms a perishable product into stable, value-added goods that align with [[produce-no-waste-permaculture-principle]].

Butter making basics:

- Let fresh cream rise to the top (natural separation takes 12-24 hours at 50-60°F / 10-15°C)

- Skim cream and agitate (churn) until butter grains form, separating from buttermilk

- Wash butter in cold water to remove residual buttermilk (extends shelf life)

- Salt to taste (unsalted butter spoils faster); 1 teaspoon salt per pound of butter

- One gallon of whole milk yields roughly 1-2 cups of cream, producing about 0.5 pounds of butter

Simple farm cheese (chevre-style from cow milk):

- Warm milk to 86°F (30°C), add mesophilic culture and let ripen 1 hour

- Add rennet, stir gently, and let set until clean break forms (30-60 minutes)

- Cut curd into half-inch cubes, heat slowly to 102°F (39°C), hold 30 minutes

- Drain curds in cheesecloth, hang for 4-6 hours

- Add salt and herbs to taste; stores 1-2 weeks refrigerated
