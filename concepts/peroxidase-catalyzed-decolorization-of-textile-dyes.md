# Peroxidase-Catalyzed Decolorization of Textile Dyes

The extracellular lignin-degrading enzymes of white-rot fungi -- primarily lignin peroxidase (LiP) and [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]] have demonstrated broad-spectrum dye decolorization. Key performance data:

- Azure B: 84% color removal in 20 minutes by crude LiP

- Bromophenol Blue: 93% removal in 15 minutes at 0.1 U LiP concentration

- Methyl Orange: 85% removal

- Methyl Green: 86% removal

- Remazol Brilliant Blue R: 77% removal (10-80 uM)

- Congo Red: 54% removal (with 2 mM veratryl alcohol)

The addition of veratryl alcohol (VA) as a mediator dramatically enhances decolorization: Reactive Red 22 removal increased from 15% to 96% with VA addition. LiP from P. chrysosporium catalyzed degradation of Methylene Blue via N-demethylation oxidation, producing Azure B and Azure A derivatives.

### LiP Catalytic Mechanism

LiP oxidizes sulfonated azo dyes, generating sulfophenyl hydroperoxides. The catalytic cycle involves compound I (resting peroxidase + H2O2), which oxidizes the substrate to compound II (releasing a substrate radical), and compound II returns to the resting state.

The substrate radical then undergoes spontaneous reactions leading to dye degradation. LiP has a higher redox potential than most peroxidases and appears to oxidize a greater range of chemicals. The pH optimum for LiP activity is approximately 2.3-2.5, although the enzyme is unstable at this pH during long-term use. Ollikka et al. (1993) found that pH optima for decolorization varied according to the dye being treated and also varied between isoenzymes.

## Manganese Peroxidase (MnP) in Dye Decolorization

MnP isozymes from Bjerkandera adusta and Pleurotus eryngii decolorize azo dyes and phthalocyanine complexes through Mn(II)-independent reactions. Oxidation of Reactive Blue 15 accounted for 60% of sulfophthalimides by purified MnP from B. adusta, with a proposed oxidative fission mechanism.

MnP from P. chrysosporium was responsible for effective Orange II and Poly R-478 decolorization. Decolorization depends not only on MnP activity but also on cofactors: [[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]] decolorized Remazol Brilliant Blue R (RBBR) both with and without veratryl alcohol. VA stabilized and improved the rate of RBBR decolorization. Laccase from Pycnoporus cinnabarinus has been developed for decolorization of a range of industrial dyes.

McCarthy et al. (1999) developed optimal conditions for laccase from P. cinnabarinus, noting that pH optima varied depending on the substrate being converted. The laccase of P. cinnabarinus has different optima for guaiacol, syringaldizine, and the artificial laccase substrate ABTS.

## Color Shift Phenomena

During decolorization, color in the blue region (550-650 nm) is often easily removed, but with some fungi a residual red coloration (ca. 490-520 nm) remains. With Reactive Black 5, nearly 100% reduction in A(465) may be achieved but only 80% reduction in A(520), with the color changing from dark blue/black to light red.

New chromophores can arise through spontaneous or enzyme-catalyzed coupling of degradation products. Laccase and MnP can catalyze coupling of chemicals to give new chromophores. Consequently, it is essential to note changes across the whole visible spectrum and not restrict observations to one wavelength.

## Practical Considerations

- Crude enzyme preparations from Trametes versicolor and Bjerkandera adusta decolorize Reactive Blue 38 and Reactive Violet 5

- The efficiency of enzymatic decolorization is often lower than whole-culture treatment, suggesting other enzymes contribute during initial degradation steps

- Peroxidases are not yet commercially available at scale (unlike some laccases), limiting practical application

- Different enzyme systems may attack one type of dye molecule but fail against others

- A 1:10 dye-to-H2O2 ratio showed nearly 80% Methylene Blue decolorization, suggesting [[mycoremediation-of-textile-dyes]]
