# Land Requirements Per Diet Type and Carrying Capacity

## Overview

Masanobu Fukuoka presents a striking analysis of how dietary
choices determine the amount of land needed to support human
life. By calculating the land area required per person
depending on whether they eat grains, potatoes, milk, pork,
or beef, Fukuoka demonstrates that modern meat-centered diets
fundamentally limit the earth's carrying capacity.

## The Land Area Calculations

Fukuoka provides specific figures for the land required to
support one human being under different dietary regimes:

- **Grain diet:** 200 square yards per person
- **Potato diet:** 600 square yards per person
- **Milk diet:** 1,500 square yards per person
- **Pork diet:** 4,000 square yards per person
- **Beef diet:** 10,000 square yards per person

The ratios are dramatic: a person subsisting entirely on beef
requires fifty times the land area of someone living on grains,
nearly seven times that of someone on a milk diet, and two and
a half times that of someone on pork.

## Earth's Carrying Capacity by Diet

Translated to global terms, these figures reveal the stark
implications of dietary choice:

- **Beef diet:** Current world population represents the
  approximate limit
- **Pork diet:** Earth could support three times the current
  population
- **Milk diet:** Earth could support eight times the current
  population
- **Potato diet:** Earth could support twenty times the current
  population
- **Grain diet:** Earth could support sixty times the current
  population

If the entire human population were dependent on a diet of
just beef, humanity would have already reached its limits of
growth. Fukuoka points to the United States and Europe as
clear evidence that beef impoverishes the soil and denudes
the earth.

## Energy Efficiency of Livestock Production

The energy ratios of animal product conversion:

- **Broilers (chicken meat):** 50 percent energy efficiency
- **Pork:** 20 percent energy efficiency
- **Milk:** 15 percent energy efficiency
- **Beef:** 8 percent energy efficiency

Raising beef cattle cuts the food energy recoverable from
land tenfold. People who eat beef consume ten times as much
energy as people on a diet of rice. The livestock industry,
which raises cattle in indoor stalls with feed grain shipped
from the United States, has helped deplete American soil.

## The Japanese Bantam Comparison

Fukuoka illustrates the principle of natural caloric
efficiency with the Japanese Bantam chicken. Left to roam
freely, the Bantam lays just one small egg every other day.
But a breeding pair will hatch chicks and within a year
grow to a flock of ten or twenty birds that together lay
many times as many eggs each day as the best White Leghorn.

The Bantams are very efficient calorie producers because
they feed themselves and lay eggs on their own, literally
producing something from nothing. Moreover, as long as the
number of birds remains appropriate for the space available,
raising chickens in this way does not harm the land.

## Genetically Improved Breeds Are Counterproductive

Genetically-upgraded White Leghorns raised in cages lay one
large egg a day. But in order for the chickens to lay so
many eggs, they must be given feed grain having twice the
caloric value of the eggs produced. Such artificial methods
are basically counterproductive. Instead of increasing
calories, they actually cut the number of calories in half.

## What Should Be Raised

Native breeds and varieties such as Jersey cattle, which are
thought to be of lower productivity, actually have a higher
feed efficiency and do not lead to depletion of the land.
Being closer to nature, the wild boar and the black Berkshire
pig are in fact more economical than the supposedly superior
white Yorkshire breed. Profits aside, it would be better to
raise small goats than dairy cattle.

## The Ideal of Natural Livestock Integration

Fukuoka's vision features bees making the rounds of clover
and vegetable blossoms beneath trees laden heavy with fruit.
Semiwild chickens and rabbits frolic with dogs in fields of
growing wheat. Great numbers of ducks and mallards play in
the rice paddy. At the foot of the hills, black pigs and
boars grow fat on worms and crayfish, and goats peer out
from the thickets and trees.

An environment comfortable for small animals is also an
ideal setting for humanity.

## Historical Context: India and Large Animals

People believed that using horses and oxen would lighten the
labor of humans, but contrary to expectations, reliance on
these large animals has been to our disadvantage. Farmers
would have been better off using pigs and goats. It takes
over two acres of pasture to feed just one horse or cow.
This much land could feed fifty or even a hundred people.

India's farmers are poor today because they raised large
numbers of cows and elephants which ate up all the grass,
and dried and burned the droppings as fuel. Such practices
have depleted soil fertility and reduced the productivity
of the land.

## The Fundamental Principle

The way to enrich humanity's diet is easy. It does not entail
mass growing or gathering. But it does require humanity to
relinquish human knowledge and action, and to allow nature to
restore its natural bounty. If poultry and livestock are to
truly benefit humanity, they must be capable of feeding and
fending for themselves under the open sky.
