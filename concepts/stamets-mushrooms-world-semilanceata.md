# Psilocybe semilanceata — Liberty Caps

Psilocybe semilanceata (Fries) Kummer, commonly known as the liberty cap, is
one of the most widely distributed and potent psilocybin-containing mushrooms.
It is the quintessential temperate grassland Psilocybe, found across much of
the Northern Hemisphere and beyond.

## Macroscopic Description

Cap 0.5-2.5 cm broad. Conic to obtusely conic to conic-campanulate to
campanulate with an acute umbo — the distinctive "nipple" that makes this
species one of the easiest for amateurs to identify. Margin translucent-striate,
incurved and sometimes undulated in young fruiting bodies, often darkened by
spores. Color variable, extremely hygrophanous. Usually dark chestnut brown
when moist, soon drying to a light tan or yellow, occasionally with an olive
tint. Surface viscid when moist from a separable gelatinous pellicle.

Gills mostly adnexed, close to crowded, narrow. Pallid at first, rapidly
becoming brownish and finally purplish brown with edges remaining pallid.

Stem 40-100 mm long by 0.75-2 (3) mm thick. Slender, equal, flexuous, and
pliant. Pallid to more brownish towards the base, where the attached mycelium
may become bluish tinged, especially during drying. Partial veil thinly
cortinate, rapidly deteriorating, leaving an obscure evanescent annular zone
of fibrils usually darkened by spores. Often this zone is entirely absent.

## Microscopic Features

Spores dark purplish brown in deposit, ellipsoid, 12-14 by 7-8 microns.
Basidia 4-spored. Pleurocystidia few to absent. Cheilocystidia 18-35 by
4.5-8 microns, lageniform with an extended and flexuous neck, often forked.

## Habitat and Distribution

Scattered to gregarious in the fall in pastures, fields, lawns, or other grassy
areas, especially rich grasslands grazed by sheep and cows. Especially abundant
about clumps of sedge grass in the damper parts of fields. Found west of the
Cascades from Northern California to British Columbia in the fall to early
winter, and to a lesser degree in spring along coastal Oregon and Washington.

Reported in grassland habitats throughout Europe (France, Holland, Italy,
Norway, Switzerland), South Africa, Chile, northern India, Australia, and
Tasmania. First reported from New Zealand in 1993, with specimens from
high-altitude grasslands of the South Island (Johnston and Buchanan 1996).

## Potency

Moderately active to extremely potent. In the Pacific Northwest, this is one
of the most common active Psilocybes. Gartz (1993) reported an average of 1%
psilocybin, with a range of .2-2.37% — the highest psilocybin content yet
reported for any species at the time. Cultivated fruitbodies yielded maxima of
1.12% psilocybin, no psilocin, and .21% baeocystin. Stijve and Kuyper (1985)
found a very high concentration of 1.7% in one specimen.

The high psilocybin and low psilocin accounts for its excellent storage life.
Christiansen et al. (1981) found variation of .17-1.96% psilocybin in dried
specimens, with the youngest specimens being most potent on a dry-weight basis.
Psilocybin content was not adversely affected by the drying process.

## The Bluing Anomaly

Since P. semilanceata is low in psilocin (the primary co-indicator of
bluing), it rarely bruises bluish. In this species, the strength of the bluing
reaction is NOT an indication of activity — unlike the majority of potent
species which are comparatively higher in psilocin. This is a critical
identification caveat.

## Varieties and Confusion

Several distinct varieties can be encountered. The archetypal variety is easy
to recognize by its conic to campanulate cap with a sharp umbo. One unusually
large form with a pronounced incurved margin and contorted bell-shaped cap
occasionally surfaces late in the season in the Pacific Northwest.

The most dangerous form resembles a Galerina — a denuded, nearly sporeless,
orange form of P. semilanceata. With sparse spore production, the color of the
overlying translucent flesh shows a yellowish orange instead of normal purplish
brown. Woodland Galerinas can coexist in the same habitat, especially in
recently converted pastures.

Psilocybe strictipes is a similar species but lacks the distinctive umbo. P.
semilanceata is to temperate grasslands what [[stamets-mushrooms-world-mexico-central-america]] is to subtropical grasslands.

## Ecological Relationship

An interesting ecological study by Keay and Brown (1990) illustrates the close
relationship of P. semilanceata to the rhizomes of grasses. Canary (Phalaris)
grasses, known for their high content of serotonin precursors, may have a
potentiating effect on psilocybin and/or psilocin production (Gartz 1989).
