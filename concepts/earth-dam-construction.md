## Landscape Zoning Framework

The landscape can be conceptualised as a set of four interrelated zones that guide dam placement:

- **Catchment zones** — where water is collected via rainfall, runoff, and groundwater. Understanding catchment size, gradient, and vegetation cover is critical for estimating water yield.
- **Storage zones** — where water is held in dams, tanks, and swales. Dams are the primary storage mechanism for larger volumes.
- **Usage zones** — where stored water is applied to gardens, orchards, pastures, or domestic supply. Gravity flow from elevated storage is highly desirable.
- **Revitalisation zones** — downstream areas where released water rehydrates soils and restores ecological function.

This cyclical model ensures dams are integrated components of a holistic [[water-management]] strategy.

## Site Selection Criteria

Proper dam placement is arguably more important than construction technique itself. Storage dams must be carefully located with respect to several key factors:

### Soil Type

- Core out a **sample pit** at the proposed dam site to assess clay fractions in the subsoil.
- Clay content above **50%** is required for a naturally waterproof earth barrier.
- In suspect soils where adequate clay is not present on-site, the entire dam core can be constructed from **carted clay** imported from elsewhere.

### Grade Behind the Wall

- Lower slopes behind the dam wall provide the **greatest storage [[mollison-designers-soil-water-storage-and-field-capacity|capacity]]** for a given wall height.
- Steeper sites may store less water and require taller, more expensive walls.
- Aim for a broad, gently sloping catchment area behind the wall to maximise volume.

### Downstream Safety

- Assess all structures, houses, roads, and infrastructure **downstream** of the proposed dam.
- A dam failure must not endanger life or property. Select sites where the worst-case flood path has minimal risk.
- Consider the consequences of overtopping, piping, or catastrophic breach.

### Height Above Use Points

- Position dams **above** the areas where water will be used so that gravity can deliver water without pumping.
- This reduces energy inputs and increases system resilience.
- The higher the dam relative to use points, the greater the operational flexibility.

### Available Catchment or Diversion

- Evaluate the contributing catchment area and average rainfall to ensure reliable filling.
- Where natural catchment is insufficient, consider **diversion channels** or swales that direct additional runoff into the dam.
- A dam that never fills is a wasted investment; one that regularly overflows may need a spillway upgrade.

## Soil Requirements and Compaction

The waterproof integrity of an earth dam depends heavily on the soil used and the quality of compaction.

### Clay Content

- Tamped earth with **clay fractions exceeding 50%** forms an effective waterproof barrier.
- This material is suitable for dam walls up to **3.6 m (12 feet)** in height.
- Typical depths for small permaculture earth dams range from **4.5–6 m (15–20 feet)**.

### Moisture Conditions During Construction

- Earth should be **neither so dry that it crumbles nor so wet that it slumps**.
- The optimal moisture content allows the soil to compact densely without air pockets or deformation.
- This "optimum moisture content" is typically assessed by field testing — a handful of soil squeezed should form a ball that breaks cleanly rather than crumbling or smearing.

### Compaction Technique

- Slopes leading to the crest should be **concave** in profile, which aids stability and drainage.
- **Every 25 cm of lift**, a machine (or hand tamper for small dams) should ride along and thoroughly tamp down the earth.
- Well-compacted dams exhibit **shrinkage of less than 1%**, indicating minimal settlement over time.
- Exclusion of foreign material is critical: **boulders, logs, grass clumps, and topsoil** must all be removed from the dam core to prevent weak zones, organic decomposition, and seepage paths.

## Wall Slopes and Dimensions

The geometry of the dam wall is governed by the need for structural stability against both the hydrostatic pressure of stored water and the weight of the earth itself.

### Slope Ratios

- **Inner slope (upstream, waterside):** 3:1 (horizontal to vertical). The gentler inner slope accommodates water-level fluctuations and reduces the risk of slumping when the reservoir is drawn down.
- **Outer slope (downstream, dry side):** 2:1 to 2.5:1. The steeper outer slope reflects the fact that it is not subject to water saturation and can therefore be slightly steeper while remaining stable.

### Freeboard

- A **freeboard of 0.9 m (3 feet)** above the maximum expected water level is required.
- Freeboard prevents overtopping from wave action, unexpected inflows, or minor miscalculations in spillway capacity.

### Key Cutting

A **key** (or key trench) is a critical structural element cut into the foundation beneath the dam wall.

- The key is needed on **all walls 1.8 m (6 feet) or more** in height.
- It should be **0.6–0.9 m (2–3 feet) deep** and extend the full length of the dam wall.
- The key serves two essential functions:
  - **Prevents shear failure** by locking the dam wall into the underlying substrate.
  - **Cuts off base seepage** by extending the seepage path beneath the wall, forcing water to travel a longer distance before it can emerge on the downstream side.
- The key trench is backfilled with compacted clay, creating a continuous impermeable barrier from the dam core down into stable ground.

### Core Construction in Suspect Soils

- Where native soils lack sufficient clay, the **entire core** of the dam can be constructed from imported clay.
- The core is the central impermeable zone of the wall; surrounding it with coarser, more stable material provides structural support while the clay core prevents water passage.
- This approach allows dam construction in a wider range of geological settings.

## Gabion Dams

Gabion dams are a specialised form of water management structure particularly suited to **dryland environments** where traditional earth dams may be impractical due to low rainfall, dispersive soils, or high evaporation rates.

### Construction

- Gabions are **permeable barriers** made from wire mesh baskets filled with rocks.
- They are placed across eroding valleys or drainage lines to slow water flow.
- For typical farm-scale construction, gabion dams range from **0.5–2.5 feet** in height, though larger installations are possible.

### Purpose and Function

- The primary purpose is **not to store free surface water** but to create conditions for **silt deposition** and **water spreading**.
- During flood conditions, water ponding behind the gabion slows sufficiently for suspended **silt loads to deposit** in a flat area upstream.
- Over time, these deposited silts form **absorption beds** that hold moisture in the landscape, supporting revegetation and reducing downstream erosion.
- The permeability of gabions allows water to pass through at a controlled rate, preventing the structure from being overtopped and destroyed during large flood events.

### Advantages in Drylands

- Low cost and simple construction using locally available rock.
- Self-draining — no risk of catastrophic failure from water pressure buildup.
- Gradually build soil and organic matter upstream through sediment trapping.
- Create micro-environments for pioneer vegetation establishment.
- The base opening in gabion structures allows **silt scour**, keeping the dam itself free of siltation while trapping sediments in the flat deposition zone behind.

## Integration with Permaculture Design

Earth dams should never be designed in isolation. They are one component of a comprehensive water harvesting and distribution system. Key integration principles include:

- **Position dams at the highest practical point** in the landscape to maximise gravitational distribution potential.
- **Use spillways** that direct overflow into productive systems such as swales, chinampas, or infiltration zones rather than wasting water.
- **Plant the dam embankment** with deep-rooted, non-invasive vegetation to stabilise slopes and reduce erosion.
- **Design for multiple functions** — a dam may provide irrigation water, fire protection, habitat, aquaculture, and microclimate moderation simultaneously.
- **Consider seasonal patterns** — ensure the dam fills during wet seasons and retains sufficient water through dry periods to support planned usage.
