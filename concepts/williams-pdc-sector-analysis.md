# Sector Analysis in Permaculture Design

Permaculture sectors are an analytical tool to help
understand the flow of forces across a site, using a
circular diagram centered on the home to map energies
like sun, wind, fire, and noise that shape the design.

## Understanding Sectors

A sector analysis is presented as a circle over your site,
with the circle centered on your Zone 0, or the center of
your lowest zone. Pie slices out of that circle represent
forces that flow across the site. For example if easterly
winds blow in cold air you would draw a wedge that points
to the east and mark it as the cold easterly winds sector.

There is no exhaustive laundry list of what type of sectors
your analysis should include; that is up to the designer to
determine what forces exist that affect the design. Sectors
represent external energies and influences that cross the
site boundary and interact with the design.

## Common Sectors to Analyze

Fire hazard is worth noting on every design, identifying
which direction a wild or other fire would most likely come
from. The fire sector combines topography analysis with
prevailing wind patterns to determine the most likely fire
approach direction.

Summer wind sector typically captures the predominant wind
direction during summer. Some simple climactic research
should tell you this. The wedge should probably be about 80
degrees wide on the circle.

Winter wind sector captures the typically different
predominant wind direction during winter time. The wedge
should also be about 80 degrees wide on the circle.

Noise sectors account for large noise generators near your
site such as roads, neighbors, or commercial areas. Create
a noise sector for each source, with angular width at your
discretion.

Summer sun sector denotes the solar azimuth during the
summer solstice. The actual angles of the wedge should
match the actual angles of the solar azimuth.

Winter sun sector denotes the solar azimuth during the
winter solstice. This wedge should also reflect the actual
angles of the solar azimuth.

Undesirable views and desirable views should be noted as
sectors so the design can block or preserve sight lines
as appropriate.

## Drawing the Sector Diagram

While sector diagrams can sometimes be frustrating because
information visually overlaps, a recommended approach is to
start with a large circle centered on Zone 0. Then make a
number of concentric rings equal to the total number of
sectors you plan on drawing. Starting from the mid point
draw the angle lines out to the appropriate concentric ring
and label it.

It is typical to put the summer sun sector on the outermost
ring, the winter sun second, the prevailing winds third,
and then the rest in whatever order suits your fancy. You
can either color in the outermost arc of each sector, or
color in the whole wedge. If you choose to color the whole
wedge it is still suggested to first color the arcs then go
back to fill in the wedges to avoid overlapping colors.

## Using Sectors in Design

Before beginning your sector analysis the designer should
sit down and make a list of factors to be analyzed. Once
you have the sector analysis complete you can look at it and
let it tell you where various elements should go. Sectors
inform placement decisions to either block a force across
your site, allow that force access, or catch and harness
that force.

For example, placing a windbreak along the winter wind
sector protects structures from cold winds. Positioning
a greenhouse to capture the winter sun sector maximizes
passive solar gain. Locating animal housing away from the
noise sector keeps livestock calm. The sector analysis
becomes a guide for intelligent element placement.

## Sectors Combined with Zones

The sector analysis is always used in combination with the
zone analysis. Zones tell you where to place things based
on human use frequency; sectors tell you how to orient and
position things based on natural forces. Together they
provide a comprehensive framework for element placement
that accounts for both human needs and natural energies.

## Fire Sector Analysis in Detail

To determine a fire sector requires combining the
topography of the surrounding land with prevailing wind
patterns. Fire travels fastest uphill and with the wind.
By analyzing both slope and wind direction the designer
can identify the most likely fire sectors. This information
is critical for placing structures, choosing building
materials on the fire-exposed side, and planning fuel
reduction zones around critical infrastructure.

## Observation Before Analysis

As with all permaculture analysis, the sector analysis
begins with observation. Spend time on the site at
different times of day and different seasons. Feel where
the wind comes from, notice where the sun hits and where
shade falls. Listen for noise sources. Look at what is
visible from different vantage points. The more time you
spend observing the actual forces on your site, the more
accurate and useful your sector diagram will be.
