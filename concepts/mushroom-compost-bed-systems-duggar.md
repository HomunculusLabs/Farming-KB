# Mushroom Compost and Bed Systems — Ridge vs Flat Beds, Casing, and Substitutes (Duggar)

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Benjamin Minge Duggar, *Mushroom Growing* (1915), Chapters on composting, bed preparation, and nutrition.

## Stable Manure — The Irreplaceable Substrate

Duggar conducted extensive experiments on compost substitutes and concluded decisively that stable manure (horse manure with straw bedding) is the superior substrate for mushroom cultivation. No alternative tested could match its performance.

### Compost Substitute Experiments
Duggar tested multiple alternatives to stable manure:

**Sawdust** — Decisively inferior to stable manure. Even with added nutrients, sawdust-based composts produced poor mycelial growth and low yields.

**Decaying leaves** — Also inferior, despite containing some organic matter. The physical structure and nutrient profile were inadequate.

**Woods mould (forest floor material)** — Failed to support vigorous mushroom growth, despite containing fungal associations in its natural state.

**Phosphates and nitrates** — Adding chemical fertilizers to the substitute materials improved growth somewhat but not enough to be competitive with stable manure.

### Why Stable Manure Works
The unique value of stable manure lies in:
- Balanced carbon-to-nitrogen ratio from the mixture of straw and manure
- Physical structure that provides both aeration and moisture retention
- Biological complexity — the resident microflora contribute to fermentation
- The straw component provides structural support for mycelial growth
- Nutrient availability matches mushroom requirements after fermentation

### Forest Agaricus Species on Alternate Substrates
Duggar proposed that forest-dwelling Agaricus species (A. silvicola and A. placomyces) might be successfully cultivated on non-manure substrates, since they naturally grow on forest floor material. This remained a proposal rather than a confirmed result, but it anticipated the modern cultivation of various mushroom species on diverse substrates.

## The Biology of Fermentation

### Fermentation Is Biological, Not Chemical
Duggar's Chapter VI on nutrition established a critical principle: the fermentation of manure before use in mushroom beds is a biological requirement, not merely a chemical transformation.

**Key experiment**: In sterile culture, fresh (unfermented) manure supported faster mycelial growth than fermented manure. This would seem to contradict the need for fermentation. However:

In natural (non-sterile) conditions, fresh manure contains abundant soluble organic compounds that fuel rapid bacterial growth. These bacteria outcompete mushroom mycelium for resources and space. Fermentation removes these competitive substrates through microbial activity, leaving a partially decomposed, stabilized compost that favors mushroom growth over bacterial competitors.

### Practical Implications
- Do not skip fermentation to save time — it will likely result in bacterial contamination
- Over-fermentation can reduce available nutrients
- The goal is a stable, partially decomposed compost with reduced bacterial load
- Temperature management during fermentation is critical

## Ridge vs Flat Bed Systems

### Flat Beds
Flat beds are constructed level across their width, with optional side boards:
- **With side boards**: Duggar preferred sided beds because they encourage heavier mushrooms and better moisture retention
- **Depth**: 8-10 inches after compression
- **Advantages**: Simpler construction, more uniform growing surface, heavier mushrooms
- **Disadvantages**: May have drainage issues if not properly constructed

### Ridge Beds
Ridge beds are constructed in a triangular cross-section:
- **Base width**: 20-24 inches
- **Top width**: approximately 6 inches
- **Height**: 12-14 inches from base to peak
- **Shape**: Triangular ridge, like a miniature roof

**Advantages of ridge beds**:
- Better sanitation — the peaked shape sheds water and reduces pooling
- Improved air circulation around the bed surface
- Reduced disease pressure due to better drainage
- Traditional in European mushroom growing

**Disadvantages**:
- Lighter mushrooms in first and last harvests (compared to flat beds)
- More complex to construct
- Less efficient use of floor space
- Harder to maintain uniform casing depth

### Species-Specific Bed Requirements
Different Agaricus species have different bed preferences:
- *A. campestris* — adapts well to both flat and ridge beds
- *A. fabaceus* — may prefer specific conditions
- *A. arvensis* — typically larger-growing, benefits from deeper beds
- *A. silvicola* — forest species, may require different substrate altogether

## Casing Soil

Casing soil is the layer of material (typically loam or clay-loam) applied on top of the spawned compost. Duggar emphasized its importance:

### Functions of Casing
- Provides a humid microclimate at the bed surface
- Triggers the transition from vegetative (mycelial) growth to reproductive (fruiting) growth
- Protects the compost from drying out
- Provides physical support for developing mushrooms
- Moderates temperature fluctuations at the bed surface

### Ideal Casing Material
- **Loam** — the traditional and still preferred material
- Must be free of organic matter that could support competing fungi
- Should have good water-holding capacity without becoming waterlogged
- pH should be neutral to slightly alkaline
- Must be sterilized or pasteurized before use to kill weed fungi and pests

## Sanitation and Disease Prevention

### Never Reuse Old Compost
Duggar's strongest sanitation recommendation: never reuse old compost or spent soil. Reusing old material increases disease and insect pressure many times over. Old compost is, however, excellent for:
- Flower garden amendment
- Lawn top-dressing
- General garden soil improvement

### Cleaning Between Crops
- **Lime-wash (stone lime)** — applied to walls, floors, and bed frames between crops
- **Copper sulphate spray** — alternative or supplement to lime-wash
- Both sanitize surfaces without leaving harmful residues that could affect the next crop

### Fumigation
- **Hydrocyanic acid gas** — extremely effective but highly dangerous; requires careful handling
- **Sulphur fumes** — effective against both insects and fungi; 2 ounces powdered sulphur per 1,000 cubic feet of space
- **Formaldehyde vapor** — most practical for routine disinfection; 3 pints commercial formalin (40% formaldehyde) plus 1.5 pounds permanganate of potash per 1,000 cubic feet; keep house closed 24 hours
