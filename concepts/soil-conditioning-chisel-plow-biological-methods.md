Soil conditioning is the process of opening compacted soil to
restore its forest-like absorption capacity. In [[permaculture]], the
primary aim is to store water in the soil rather than allowing it
to run off. The Wallace soil conditioner (Yeomans plow), a chisel
plow with narrow shanks, is the primary mechanical tool, though
biological alternatives exist for every situation.

## The Chisel Plow Method

The Wallace plow or similar chisel implement is drawn through the
soil at intervals, creating deep slots without turning the earth.
The shanks penetrate to a depth of about nine inches, producing
ribbed patterns across the landscape. Water encounters these ribbed
systems and is stored in the soil rather than running off the
surface.

Seeds wash into the little crevices and germinate along the ridges.
Roots follow these channels right down into the soil, then branch
off themselves, creating further pathways for water penetration at
even greater depth. The process restores soil to its forest
absorption capacity.

## Results of Soil Conditioning

The results are dramatic. First, a fantastic amount of water is
stored within the landscape. Second, soil temperature in winter may
be as much as 25 degrees Fahrenheit above surrounding soils. Wet
soil is an enormous heat mass, and the air spaces in conditioned
soil amplify this effect. Conditioned soils commonly average 19
degrees Fahrenheit above surrounding temperatures.

This temperature differential sharply decreases frost. A field that
has been soil conditioned often remains unfrosted while surrounding
fields freeze, because the difference is typically 15 to 19 degrees.
Growing seasons are extended at both ends. Trees make faster growth:
olives that might bear in 17 or 18 years will normally bear within
three years in conditioned soil.

It pays to wait two or three years for conditioning to take full
effect before planting trees. Even with this delay, you are further
ahead than planting first in compacted soils.

## Maintenance Cycle

In strong soil under normal grazing, conditioning needs to be
repeated only every three or four years. On football fields with
heavy compaction, treatment every two or three years suffices. In
orchards, reconditioning is rarely needed because tree roots and
root channels maintain deep soil structure.

## The Hard Pan Problem

In some soils, hard pan forms mainly from superphosphate
application combined with high evaporation. Rain carries
superphosphate down to certain depths; then summer moisture
evaporates and an insoluble tri-calcium phosphate forms as a
concrete block about 15 inches down. Superphosphate should never
be applied to tropical calcium soils. Phosphate rock is acceptable
on calcareous soils but not superphosphate.

## Biological Alternatives to Mechanical Conditioning

For clients who cannot afford a soil conditioner, several biological
methods achieve similar results:

Daikon radish: Large radishes are broadcast and slashed. They spike
the soil to about two feet. Being biennial, they rot in place and
never need pulling. If the area is too steep for the plow, daikon
radish accomplishes the work biologically.

Pioneer tree species: Western red cedar and similar trees act as
soil spikes, starting the process of opening compacted ground. They
create root channels that follow the same principle as chisel
slots.

Compost holes: In small areas, holes are dug, a handful of compost
is added with a radish seed, and vegetables are planted where logs
rot beneath the ground. This spot treatment works in difficult
situations.

## Conditioning and Irrigation Synergy

When an irrigation drain runs across conditioned soil, it encounters
the ribbed systems that slow and store water. This creates a
powerful synergy between mechanical water distribution and natural
soil storage. Even on slopes as gentle as three inches of fall per
quarter mile, this system works perfectly. Below-surface ponds can
be created that do not perch on top of the ground at all.

## Nutrient Capture in Biological Net Systems

A key concept in conditioned landscapes is the biological net for
nutrient capture. Water that looks perfectly clear may carry a heavy
weight of dissolved solids eroded from hillside. The idea is to
catch these nutrients in biological systems without high technology.

Permaculture salt tolerant in ponds, algae, mussels, and snails absorb calcium
and fix dissolved solids. These can be returned to the land as
duck manure, fish, and wild rice. The ideal is to start with clean
high dams, gradually enrich the water with manurial nutrients,
then pass it through wetland plant systems. Water released from
the property is clean.

This entire biological water treatment system can operate within a
vertical drop of six feet, going from zone to zone. Even a space
the size of a room can accommodate a complete sequence: rice patch,
algae-eating fish, mussel pond with watercress, then clean water
release.

## Key Principles Summary

The overarching principle is to treat the landscape as a living
sponge. Every technique described serves one goal: keeping water
on the land longer. Whether through mechanical slots, biological
root channels, or biological nutrient capture, the result is
increased fertility, extended growing seasons, and reduced erosion.
Conditioned soil is the foundation upon which all other permaculture
systems depend.
