# Fungal Partnerships with Wildlife

Fungi form intricate mutualistic partnerships with a wide range of animals, from insects to mammals. These relationships represent some of the most ancient and sophisticated symbioses in nature, with some fungal-animal partnerships dating back more than 50 million years. Understanding these partnerships provides models for how humans might live in greater ecological harmony with fungal communities.

## Insect-Fungal Mutualisms

### Leafcutter Ants and Fungal Agriculture

Attine ants, including the leafcutter ants of Central and South America, maintain one of the most complex mutualistic relationships in the natural world. These ants cultivate Lepiota mycelium as a substrate for a benign bacterium that produces antibiotics against destructive microfungal parasites of the genus Escovopsis.

The ants actively farm the fungus by collecting fresh vegetation, chewing it into a pulp, and using it as a growth medium for their fungal gardens. They also feed Lepiota mycelium directly to their larvae. This tripartite partnership — ant, fungus, and antibiotic-producing bacterium — has allowed attine ant lineages to survive for more than 50 million years and establish massive colonies numbering in the millions.

Studies at Oregon State University (Currie et al. 2003) confirmed that the bacterium associated with the ant-fungal gardens specifically produces compounds targeting Escovopsis, demonstrating a co-evolved chemical defense system of remarkable specificity.

### Termites and Cultivated Mushrooms

The mushroom genus Termitomyces includes several species intimately associated with terrestrial termite colonies, particularly in tropical Africa and Asia. Termites construct their elaborate nest structures from organic matter and cultivate Termitomyces mycelium within the nest matrix.

When termite colonies are abandoned or die, the mycelium-rich nest material sprouts Termitomyces mushrooms, including the highly prized Termitomyces robustus, a choice edible mushroom collected for food throughout central Africa. The primordium of these mushrooms begins several feet underground, deep within the termite nest, extending upward as a long "taproot" before forming a mushroom on the surface.

How termites first began cultivating this mushroom has puzzled mycologists for decades. The relationship appears to be obligate — the termites depend on the fungus for food and nest structural integrity, while the fungus depends on the termites for substrate preparation and spore dispersal.

### Beetles and Polypore Mushrooms

Many species of mushrooms use insects as vehicles for spore dispersal. The birch polypore (Piptoporus betulinus) produces an attractive scent that draws beetles, which then burrow into the mushroom's flesh, piercing the spore-rich underlayer to feed on the soft internal tissue. In doing so, the beetles become thoroughly covered with spores.

When a beetle travels to another tree and burrows into the bark to lay eggs, the tree is inoculated with birch polypore spores. The emerging mycelium provides food for the beetle's developing larvae while softening the wood. This softened, mycelium-colonized wood then attracts woodpeckers searching for beetle grubs. When the woodpeckers leave, they carry spores to new locations. An entire ecosystem spirals outward from a single beetle-inoculated tree.

## Snails as Fungus Farmers

The marsh periwinkle (Littoraria irrorata), a seaside snail, practices a form of fungal farming. The snail damages and defecates on cordgrass (Spartina alternifolia), creating conditions for a particular fungus to grow. Days later, the snail returns to consume both the fungus and the now-fungus-covered grass.

Research by Silliman and Newell (2003) demonstrated that grasses without the snail-enabled fungus grew 50 percent faster but were less appealing and nutritious for the snails, while fungus-covered plants were more palatable. This snail-fungus relationship affects the broader marsh environment by influencing grass growth patterns.

## Mammalian Fungal Relationships

### Bears as Spore Dispersers

Bears play an important but historically underappreciated role in forest fungal ecology. Black bears scratch trees to mark their territories, creating wounds in the bark that serve as entry points for polypore mushroom spores. Mycelium and mushrooms subsequently sprout from these scratch marks, attracting more beetles and insects, and initiating cascading ecological cycles.

More broadly, bears contribute to forest nutrient cycling by fishing salmon and trout from streams and depositing the carcasses on stream banks. The fish remains replenish the forest with essential sea salts and nitrogen-rich nutrients. The spawning fish themselves feed upon grubs growing in fly-infested mushrooms washed into streams by heavy rains. These interconnected cycles — linking bears, salmon, mushrooms, flies, and forest trees — sustain biodiversity across the ecosystem.

Historically, timber companies commissioned the wide-scale killing of bears in the Pacific Northwest during the 1940s through 1960s, based on the mistaken belief that bear-scratched trees represented timber damage. It is now understood that bears and other animals are essential contributors to the health of old-growth forest ecosystems.

### Deer and Squirrel Interactions

Deer and squirrels interact with fungal communities both as spore dispersers and as disturbers of mycorrhizal networks. Their foraging activities can disrupt mushroom crops, as documented in chanterelle cultivation experiments where grazing animals disturbed planted seedlings. However, their movement through forest habitats also serves to distribute fungal spores across the landscape.

## Endophytes as Mutualistic Partners

Endophytes are primarily benevolent, non-mycorrhizal fungi that partner with many plants, from grasses to trees. Their mycelia thread between plant cell walls without entering them, enhancing the plant's growth and nutrient absorption while staving off parasites. Many endophytic fungi provide their host plants with chemical defenses against herbivorous insects and grazing animals, representing another form of the protective fungal partnerships seen in the insect world.

## Lessons for Human-Fungal Partnerships

Shulgin noted that the mutualism between ants, mushrooms, and bacteria provides a useful model for how humans can live in closer harmony with their environment. Both ants and people benefit from the guardianship of mycelium — by partnering with fungi, many organisms, including humans, can resist disease.

## Historical Context

The recognition of mutualistic partnerships between fungi and animals has evolved significantly over the past century. Early mycological research focused primarily on fungi as pathogens or decomposers, and the beneficial relationships between fungi and animals received comparatively little attention until the mid-twentieth century, when the importance of mycorrhizal associations for plant nutrition began to be widely appreciated.

The study of fungal-animal mutualisms expanded dramatically with the discovery of the importance of fungal endophytes in plant-herbivore interactions, the documentation of leaf-cutter ant fungus agriculture, and the recognition that gut fungi play essential roles in the nutrition of ruminants and other herbivorous animals.

## Modern Significance

Contemporary research on fungal-animal partnerships has revealed an astonishing diversity of mutualistic relationships. These include the cultivation of fungal gardens by leaf-cutter ants and termites, the role of fungal endophytes in defending plants against herbivory, the importance of mycorrhizal networks for forest regeneration, and the emerging recognition that fungal communities in the animal gut are essential for health and nutrition.

These partnerships have significant implications for conservation, agriculture, and ecosystem management. Understanding fungal-animal mutualisms is increasingly recognized as essential for maintaining healthy ecosystems and for developing sustainable approaches to land management and food production.
