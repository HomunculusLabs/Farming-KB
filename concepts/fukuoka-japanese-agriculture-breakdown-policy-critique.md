# The Breakdown of Japanese Agriculture

[[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s second chapter presents a sweeping indictment of the forces that
dismantled Japan's [[jadam-nutrient-cycling-traditional-farming-mineral-science]] culture and replaced it with an industrialized,
policy-driven system that impoverishes both the land and the farmer. His analysis weaves
[[amanita-muscaria-cultural-history]], economics, and philosophy to argue that modern agriculture represents
not progress but a catastrophic fall from a truthful way of living.

## Life in the Farming Villages of the Past

Fukuoka begins with a portrait of pre-modern Japanese farming life that is at once
unromantic and deeply reverent. Japanese peasants were materially poor, yet proud,
self-supporting, and independent. They lived noble lives in the great outdoors, close
to what Fukuoka calls God — nature incarnate, experienced directly through daily work.
Working their fields from sunrise to sunset, each day was "as wide and infinite as the
universe." The farming way of life violated nothing and was itself not violated; it
existed in harmony with the natural order.

Central to this way of life was the **philosophy of Mu** — the radical idea that
"philosophy is unnecessary." This was not anti-intellectualism but a deep intuitive
wisdom that transcended the need for formal thought. Farmers possessed an innate
understanding of life and death that freed them from anguish and grief. They did not
need doctrines because their daily engagement with soil, weather, plants, and animals
provided a complete education in the cycles of existence. This Mu philosophy gave
farmers an enduring strength rooted in direct experience rather than abstract reasoning.

## The Disappearance of Village Philosophy

Over roughly twenty years preceding Fukuoka's writing, this ancient culture was
systematically erased by mechanization and modernization. Chain saws replaced hand saws
in the mountains. Mechanical plows and tractors replaced manual labor in the fields.
Vinyl-covered greenhouses turned productive land into something resembling factories.
Automatic spraying machines removed the farmer's hand from the land entirely.
Mechanization and systematization destroyed the human touch that had defined village
life for centuries.

The cultural losses were equally devastating. Singing voices in the fields were
replaced by the drone of television sets. Fireside discussions — the communal
storytelling and knowledge-sharing that bound generations together — vanished. The
disappearance of the traditional sunken hearth (*irori*) extinguished the last embers
of ancient farming [[fukuoka-textdoc-sunken-hearth-extinction-village-culture]]. That hearth had been the physical and spiritual
center of the home; without it, the rhythms of family life shifted irreversibly.
Fukuoka's verdict is stark: "We have fallen from a true way of life to one that is
false."

## Post-WWII Transformation

The demographic and economic shifts were enormous. Japan's farming and fishing
population plummeted from roughly 50% of the total population to under 20%. This was
not a natural migration but a deliberate appropriation: farmers' labor was effectively
commandeered to build urban civilization. Their production, surplus, and land subsidized
Japan's rapid industrial rise.

The arrival of the **motorized transport-tiller** marked the major [[fukuoka-textdoc-post-season-cultivation-kochi-turning-point]].
Horse-drawn plows gave way to tillers, which gave way to full-sized tractors. Motorized
sprayers were eventually abandoned in favor of helicopter spraying — removing the
farmer from the field almost entirely. Traditional farming with draft animals and
organic inputs was replaced by heavy [[fukuoka-textdoc-basic-agriculture-law-japan-food-self-sufficiency-crisis]]**, which
established systematic controls over farmers and "passed the reins" to the financial
community. Under this law, the public was encouraged to view farmland as better used
for industry and housing than for growing food. Farmers came to be seen as "selfish
monopolizers of land" whose attachment to their fields stood in the way of progress.

The results were devastating. Japan's food self-sufficiency dropped below 30%. Farmers
lost both their land and their freedom to choose crops, as production quotas replaced
local knowledge. Fukuoka describes the policy environment as containing literal "plans
to wipe out small farms and proposals for a euthanasia of farming." Behind every
modernization program, he detects "thinly-disguised contempt for the farmer."

## The Reality of Rice Pricing

Fukuoka's analysis of rice pricing reveals an economic architecture designed to extract
farmer wealth. The farmer does not set the price of rice; rather, the price is
calculated to support equipment manufacturers and the broader industrial supply chain.
The rice price must be high enough to justify new farm implements, fuel, and chemical
inputs — ensuring the farmer's earnings flow outward to industrial suppliers.

Distribution costs in Japan were roughly 5 times those of the United States and 2 times
those of West Germany. In 1979, American rice cost approximately $0.50 per pound — the
same as economy rice in Japan. Reports of importing rice at one-quarter to one-third of
local prices struck Fukuoka as "incredible." Meanwhile, federal assistance per farmer
in the United States was twice that of Japan, and in France it was three times greater.

## US Agriculture: A Cautionary Comparison

During a 1979 visit to the United States, Fukuoka observed a system that confirmed his
worst fears. [[night-break-photoperiodism-manipulation-commercial-agriculture]] with
large-scale monoculture farming," he warns, is inherently fragile.

## Warnings on Genetic Engineering

Fukuoka extends his critique to genetic engineering, which he sees as the logical and
terrifying endpoint of humanity's separation from nature. A scientist studying molecules
and atoms, he argues, loses sight of the original leaf. Genetic engineering gives man
"a sorcerer's staff" — power without wisdom.

He foresaw interspecific recombination producing organisms that violate natural
boundaries: gigantic rice, trees crossed with bamboo, tomatoes growing on trees. He
anticipated nitrogen-fixing tomatoes from legume gene transfer. He warned of hybrid
creatures — bee-flies, butterfly-dragonflies — where beneficial insect and pest become
indistinguishable.

The same logic, he feared, would extend into human life: test-tube babies, "superior
humans" bred in culture media, and meat-like products from crossing soybean genes with
cow and pig genes. His final image is haunting: "The farmer may find himself assisting
the scientist as a laborer in a tightly sealed factory."

## The Future Question

Fukuoka offers no easy optimism. The future [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] depends on the broad
acceptance of the Mu philosophy and a fundamental reversal of society's value system —
a shift from the arrogance of human intellect to the humility of working with nature.

Even **[[coleman-deep-organic-farming]]** — often celebrated as a progressive alternative — is in his
view only a temporary stopgap that will eventually be assimilated by scientific
agriculture. Organic methods still operate within the framework of human control; they
substitute natural inputs for chemical ones without challenging the assumption that
nature must be directed. Even self-sufficient agriculture may not turn Japanese thinking
around, so deeply has the industrial mindset penetrated the culture.

His closing assessment is sobering: "The current situation is almost beyond hope." Yet
the very act of articulating this diagnosis — naming what was lost, identifying the
forces responsible, and refusing to accept inevitability — preserves the possibility
that a return to truthful farming, and a truthful way of life, remains conceivable.
