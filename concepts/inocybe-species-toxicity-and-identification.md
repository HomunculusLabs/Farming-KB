Inocybe is a genus of agaric fungi characterised by radiating fibrils or
scales on the cap surface, dull brown spores, and generally small
stature. Most species are brownish and inconspicuous, easily overlooked
by casual collectors. [[john-ramsbottom]], in his 1945 monograph *Poisonous
Fungi*, emphasised that several Inocybe species contain muscarine-type
toxins and should be regarded as dangerous. While none are known to be
edible, only a handful have been definitively linked to poisoning
incidents.
## Muscarine-Type Poisoning in Inocybe
The toxic species of Inocybe produce symptoms similar to those of
[[amanita-muscaria]], though the active principle differs. Ramsbottom
classified Inocybe poisoning as nervous-system paralysis type, along
with Amanita muscaria, Amanita pantherina, and Clitocybe species. The
symptoms typically appear one to three hours after ingestion and include
giddiness, profuse sweating, vomiting, salivation, lacrimation, and in
severe cases, respiratory depression.
Unlike the deadly Amanita phalloides group, which has a long incubation
period of ten to twelve hours before symptoms manifest, Inocybe
poisoning acts relatively quickly. The rapid onset means that stomach-
emptying measures may still be effective if administered promptly.
Atropine was the standard 1940s treatment for muscarine-type poisoning,
as it counteracts the parasympathomimetic effects of the toxin.
## Inocybe Patouillardi — Red Staining Inocybe
Inocybe Patouillardi is one of the more distinctive species in the genus
because of its striking colour changes. When young, the entire fungus is
creamy white. As it matures, it develops a pinky-yellow hue before
becoming more or less completely vermilion or brownish-red. Most
notably, the flesh immediately stains red on handling, a trait that
earned it the common name "Red Staining Inocybe."
The cap is fleshy, conical to campanulate when young with an inrolled
margin, covered with silky fibrils. As it expands, it flattens while
retaining an acute central boss, and the margin may become lobed and
torn. The gills are almost free, crowded, initially whitish-pink,
turning rusty brown or olivaceous with a white floccose edge that
becomes blotched with red. The stem is stout, solid, slightly swollen at
the base, and mealy at the apex. The flesh is firm and white, reddening
slightly, particularly in the stem.
### Habitat and Distribution
Inocybe Patouillardi occurs in summer in deciduous woods, especially
under beech trees. It is one of several Inocybe species that redden on
contact, and distinguishing between them requires microscopic
examination of spore and cystidia characters. This taxonomic difficulty
is significant because the edibility or toxicity of closely related
species may differ.
### Documented Poisoning Cases
Ramsbottom recorded that Inocybe Patouillardi has caused occasional
deaths. A fatality occurred in Surrey in 1937. Other individuals who
consumed the fungus "in a more reasonable manner" experienced giddiness
followed by profuse sweating and vomiting. In 1925, some soldiers ate
what was probably this species and suffered similar symptoms, though not
all were affected equally. The variable response between individuals
mirrors the pattern seen with many fungal toxins, where factors such as
amount consumed, individual sensitivity, and preparation method
influence severity.
## Inocybe Geophylla — Earthy Inocybe
Inocybe geophylla is a smaller, more widely distributed species. The
silky fibrillose cap is conical with an inrolled margin, later spreading
with a central boss. Its colour is highly variable: white, yellowish,
fawn, reddish, lilac, or blue, often with the centre a different shade.
The gills are crowded, usually free, whitish initially and finally pale
ochraceous. The stem is whitish, solid, firm, satiny, with a mealy apex
and slightly thickened base. The flesh is white with an earthy smell and
insipid taste.
The lilac-coloured specimens are recognised as variety lilacina. An
interesting feature noted by Ramsbottom is that the lilac colour is lost
after a day or so in darkness but is regained with exposure to light,
suggesting a photoresponsive pigment system. Inocybe geophylla occurs in
woods from late summer to winter, making it one of the later-fruiting
toxic species.
### Toxicity Status
Inocybe geophylla is often described as poisonous in field guides, but
Ramsbottom noted that "there is no definite information about it." This
cautious stance reflects the broader problem in mycotoxicology where
species are labelled poisonous based on their genus membership rather
than confirmed poisoning cases. While precaution is warranted, the
actual danger posed by I. geophylla remains uncertain.
## Identification Challenges
Several features make Inocybe species particularly dangerous for
foragers. First, their small size and brownish coloration mean they are
easily confused with each other and with small edible species. Second,
the key diagnostic characters (spore morphology, cystidia shape,
presence of pleurocystidia) require microscopic examination that is
impractical in the field. Third, several species share the trait of red-
staining on bruising, but this characteristic alone cannot distinguish
the toxic species from each other.
Ramsbottom's observation that "it is very difficult to distinguish
between them except on microscopic characters" remains relevant today.
Modern DNA barcoding has improved species delimitation, but field
identification of Inocybe remains challenging even for experienced
mycologists. The safest approach is to avoid consuming any Inocybe
species.
## Comparison with Other Muscarine-Containing Genera
Muscarine-type poisoning is not unique to Inocybe. Clitocybe species,
particularly Clitocybe dealbata (the fool's funnel), also contain
significant muscarine concentrations. The genus Clitocybe shares with
Inocybe the general trait of being brown-spored and easily overlooked.
The key distinction is that Clitocybe species typically have decurrent
gills, while Inocybe species have gills that are more or less free.
Amanita muscaria, despite its fame as the "fly agaric," actually
contains relatively little muscarine. Its primary psychoactive compounds
are ibotenic acid and muscimol, which produce CNS excitation rather than
the parasympathomimetic effects of true muscarine poisoning. The
clinical presentation of Inocybe poisoning is therefore closer to
Clitocybe poisoning than to Amanita muscaria intoxication, despite the
historical grouping of all three under "muscarine-type" poisoning.
## Historical Context and Ramsbottom's Contribution
Ramsbottom's 1945 treatment of Inocybe was among the first accessible
accounts of these species for a general audience. His emphasis on the
difficulty of field identification and his documentation of actual
poisoning cases provided a foundation for later mycotoxicological work.
The specific cases he recorded, the 1937 Surrey fatality and the 1925
soldiers' incident, remain cited in moderntoxicological literature.
His observation that Inocybe geophylla lacks confirmed poisoning
records, despite being "often said to be poisonous," illustrates the gap
between field guide caution and scientific evidence. This gap persists
today, where many species are listed as toxic based on genus-level
assumptions rather than verified case reports.
