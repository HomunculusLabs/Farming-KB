## Sporulation-Linked Geotropism in *Psilocybe cubensis*

## Overview

Badham (1982) discovered a critical link between spore production and the expression of [[spore-formation-geotropic-response-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]].

## The Two-Phase Tropic Model

The development of *P. cubensis* basidiocarps follows a two-phase directed growth pattern:

### Phase 1: Anemotropism (Stages 1-2)

During early development, the stipe grows toward the direction of greatest [[anemotropism-evaporative-demand-fungi]], which typically means growing **into the wind**. This phase:

- Occurs in both fertile (spore-producing) and sterile (sporeless) basidiocarps
- Happens in both light and darkness
- Is driven by differential evaporative demand across the stipe surface
- Dominates until the pileus reaches near-spherical expansion (Stage 2)

### Phase 2: Negative Geotropism (Stage 3+)

At maturity, when spore production begins, a second tropism emerges:

- The pileus turns **upward** from whatever direction it was growing
- The cap opens with lamellae facing **downward**
- This reorientation occurs regardless of light direction (from above or below)
- The stipe curves so the pileus achieves a horizontal orientation

## Key Evidence: The Sporeless Mutant Experiment

The most compelling evidence for the sporulation-geotropism link comes from Badham's study of a sterile mutant strain of *P. cubensis*:

| Condition | Fertile Strain | Sterile (Sporeless) Mutant |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------------|
| Phase 1 anemotropism | Present — grows into wind | Present — grows into wind |
| Phase 2 negative geotropism | Present — pileus turns up | **Absent** — no recurvature |
| Spore production | Yes | No |
| Light requirement for Phase 2 | Some light needed | N/A (no Phase 2) |

The sporeless mutant grows normally through Phase 1, curving into the wind just like the fertile strain. However, when Stage 2 is reached and the fertile strain undergoes the dramatic pileus reorientation, the sporeless mutant continues growing in its original direction without any upward correction. This demonstrates that spore production is a prerequisite for the geotropic switch.

## The Gill Hormone Hypothesis

### Background

The existence of a mushroom "growth hormone" has been postulated by several researchers:

- **Gruen (1963)** — Proposed endogenous [[brassinosteroid-cross-kingdom-growth-regulation-plants-fungi]] in carpophores of *[[agaricus-bisporus]]*
- **Hagimoto (1963)** — Linked a [[mushroom-growth-hormone-decapitation-gill-diffusate-evidence-basidiomycetes]] to geotropic response in *[[agaricus-bisporus-cultivation-biology]]*
- **Hagimoto and Konishi (1960)** — Showed that agar diffusates from the gills can partially replace the influence of the pileus

### Decapitation Experiments

Surgical removal of the pileus (decapitation) abolishes [[fungal-tropic-responses]] in developing mushrooms. The effect can be partially restored by applying agar diffusates collected from the gill (lamellar) surface. This strongly suggests that the gills produce a diffusible substance — likely a hormone or growth regulator — that influences stipe growth direction.

### Proposed Mechanism for Sporulation-Linked Geotropism

Based on Badham's findings, the proposed sequence is:

1. Gills mature and begin producing spores during Stage 2-3
2. The gills simultaneously produce (or release) a tropic hormone
3. This hormone modifies the stipe's response to environmental stimuli
4. The mushroom switches from responding primarily to evaporative demand (anemotropism) to responding primarily to gravity (negative geotropism)
5. The stipe curves upward, positioning the pileus horizontally with lamellae facing down — the optimal orientation for spore release and dispersal

### Relationship to Light

Light plays a complex role in this system:

- **Light is necessary** for the negative geotropic response to occur
- However, this light requirement may be **indirect** — light is needed for spore production itself
- In the absence of light, few or no spores are formed, and consequently no geotropic switching occurs
- When basidiocarps are rotated horizontally under constant illumination (equalizing gravity's directional effect), growth and pileus expansion are both horizontal
- If the light requirement is only for spore production rather than for the geotropic mechanism itself, then small quantities of light that trigger sporulation could activate the entire cascade

## Hormone Identity: Unresolved Questions

The chemical identity of the gill-produced tropic hormone remains unknown. Several candidates have been investigated:

- **Indole acetic acid (IAA)** — Found in *Agaricus bisporus* fruitbodies (Konishi and Hagimoto, 1961), but no evidence suggests it regulates [[adaptive-significance-mushroom-tropisms-spore-dispersal]]
- **Gibberellin-like substances** — Detected in basidiomycete sporophores (Pegg, 1973), but functional role unclear
- **Ethylene** — Implicated in autochemotropism of *Phycomyces* (Russo et al., 1977), a related fungus that shows similar tropic responses
