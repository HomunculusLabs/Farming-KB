# Canker Fungi and Gall-Forming Fungi on Plants

## Overview

Cankers and galls represent two major categories of plant disease symptoms caused by fungi and fungus-like organisms. Cankers are sunken necrotic lesions on stems or branches resulting from localized death of the cambium, while galls are abnormal growths of host tissue induced by infection. Both have significant impacts on plant health, forest composition, and ecosystem dynamics.

## Canker Fungi

A **canker** is a sunken necrotic lesion on a stem or branch caused by localized death of the cambium. Cankers on the main stem may result in death of the tree by expanding to the point of girdling. They are also the point at which wood-decay fungi enter the heartwood of trees. The size and shape of cankers vary with environmental conditions (temperature, aspect), species of fungus, host response, host size and age, and time of infection.

Many types of cankers are caused by **facultatively parasitic ascomycetes** or their anamorphs, which become established at the site of a wound or other host stress. Cankers are also caused by obligate parasites such as rusts.

### Woody Stem Perennial Cankers

Perennial or **target cankers** are generally circular to lens-shaped with sunken centers. These cankers persist for years and slowly expand at the same rate as the radial growth of the tree. They are surrounded by concentric ridges of host tissues formed during each growing season in response to the infection, producing a pattern reminiscent of a target. The alternating ridges and depressions record the history of the interaction between host and pathogen — each ridge represents a growing season in which the host partially contained the infection, while each depression represents renewed fungal expansion.

**Associated fungi:**
- Pyrenomycetes such as *Nectria* and related Hypocreales (Lohman and Watson 1943; Samuels et al. 1990)
- *Ceratocystis* and related Ophiostomatales (Wingfield et al. 1993), especially on broadleaf trees

The Hypocreales include some of the most commonly encountered canker fungi on hardwood trees in temperate regions. *Nectria* species are frequently associated with target cankers on a wide range of deciduous hosts, and their brightly colored perithecia (red, orange, or pink) are often visible on the canker surface. The Ophiostomatales include important wilt pathogens such as *Ophiostoma novo-ulmi*, the causal agent of Dutch elm disease, which creates a different type of vascular canker.

### Woody Stem Diffuse Cankers

**Diffuse cankers** expand more rapidly than a host tree grows radially, including little or no callus. Infected tissues are often discolored, and the margin between healthy and diseased tissues may be raised slightly. Fruiting bodies are often found within a few centimeters of this margin. Rapid growth can kill trees in a few years. Diffuse cankers are often seen in shade-stressed understory trees.

**Associated fungi:**
- Members of the 98 genera of Diaporthales (e.g., *Valsa*, *Diaporthe*, *Gnomonia*, *Cryptosporella*, *Cryphonectria*, *Endothia*)
- Diatrypales genera such as *Eutypa* and *Leucostoma*
- Leotiales genera including *Cenangium*, *Tympanis*, and *Encoelia*
- *Lachnellula* species (on conifers)
- *Hypoxylon* species (Xylariaceae)

### Twig and Shoot Blights

Twig and shoot blights contain little or no callus, develop rapidly, and persist for only one season. They may be detected by discolored or slightly sunken tissues that take on a pimpled or roughened appearance from fruiting bodies. Blights frequently occur on young stems or twigs predisposed by abiotic factors such as frost, drought, sun scalding, or wounding.

**Associated fungi:**
- *Melanconis* (Melanconiaceae, Diaporthales)
- Pycnidial mitosporic fungi such as *Sclerophoma*, *Cytospora*, *Phoma*, *Gelatinosporium*, and *Diplodia*
- Anthracnose fungi (e.g., *Colletotrichum*)

### Seasonal Observation

Cankers are observed best on deciduous trees in temperate regions during **late winter and early spring** when leaves are absent. Fruiting bodies generally are present at this time. Bark of diffuse cankers is typically discolored and sunken with a raised ridge around the leading edge. Excessive pitch production is often associated with cankers on conifers.

## Gall-Forming Fungi

Several groups of fungi induce gall formation on their host plants through mechanisms including hypertrophy (cell enlargement) and hyperplasia (cell proliferation). The resulting galls can range from microscopic to conspicuous, and from benign to severely damaging to the host.

### Rust-Induced Galls

Certain rust fungi are associated with galls on woody hosts:
- *Endocronartium harknessii* on lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*) — produces woody galls on branches and stems that can be quite large and persistent
- *Gymnosporangium* species on juniper — produces gall-like structures during part of their life cycle, completing the cycle on rosaceous hosts such as hawthorn or apple

### *Apiosporina morbosa*

This fungus causes galls on *Prunus* species (plums, cherries) in temperate climates, producing rough, black, cylindrical gall formations on branches known as "black knot." The galls can girdle and kill branches, reducing fruit production and overall tree health.

### *Cyttaria*

A parasitic discomycete known only from galled branches and smaller twigs of 12 south-temperate species of *Nothofagus* (southern beech). Korf (1983) recognized three groups based on geographic position, host range, and other features. The fruiting bodies are striking, bright orange, golf-ball-sized structures that are edible and were traditionally consumed by indigenous peoples in Patagonia.

### *Synchytrium* (Chytridiales)

A large genus of obligately biotrophic microfungi associated with galls on algae, mosses, ferns, and flowering plants. The roughly 120 species occur worldwide in tropical, temperate, and arctic regions: 106 from North America, 24 from South America, 5 from Central America, 8 from Australia and New Zealand, 16 from Africa, 56 from Europe, and 88 from Asia (Karling 1964).

More than 1,350 plant species in over 800 genera and 165 families have been reported as hosts. Some species occur on only one host genus, others are limited to a single family, and one species (*S. macrosporum*) has been shown to infect more than 1,300 host species under greenhouse conditions.

Galls range from scattered and inconspicuous to large and clustered or compound, sometimes brightly colored. They may form by enlargement of a single infected cell or by proliferation of surrounding cells. The infected host organ may become noticeably hypertrophied and deformed. Two species, *S. fragariae* and *S. endobioticum*, infect roots and underground stems. Species infecting algae cause enlargement and elongation of the infected cell. Identification is based primarily on host determination; voucher specimens should consist of dried plant material including galls containing resting sporangia. Species cannot be grown in vitro.

### *Phomopsis* and Other Gall-Inducing Fungi

*Phomopsis* species cause galls on a number of woody temperate angiosperms. *Nectriella pironii* and *Sphaeropsis tumefaciens* cause galls on many woody and herbaceous plants in the subtropics (Sinclair et al. 1987).

## Plasmodiophoromycetes

The **Plasmodiophoromycetes** (Protoctista) comprises 15 genera of obligate endoparasites of plants, algae, aquatic protoctists, and fungi that frequently evoke gall-like symptoms. These organisms are found in soil and fresh water, and their distributions follow those of their hosts. They are true endoparasites that cannot be grown in culture away from their hosts.

### Symptoms

Macroscopic above-ground symptoms include stunting, wilting, chlorosis, and discoloration of leaves of infected plants. Underground symptoms can be striking and include galls, lesions, cankers, warts, and dry rot. Species of *Ligniera* and *Polymyxa*, found in roots and root hairs, do not cause host cells to enlarge and are therefore difficult to detect on underground parts, although above-ground symptoms may be present.

### Biology

They are characterized by multinucleate, unwalled protoplasts that develop into sporangia or cystosori within the host. Sporangia produce motile cells called zoospores, whereas cystosori produce aggregates of thick-walled cells (cysts). Cysts are liberated into the soil with the death and breakdown of host tissue. After a resting period, a cyst germinates to produce a single zoospore.

### Genera and Identification

The genera are distinguished by the organization of the cystosori in the host cells (Dylewski 1990):

- *Plasmodiophora* — cysts irregularly arranged and free within host cell; includes the economically important clubroot pathogen *P. brassicae*
- *Sorosphaera* — cysts arranged in hollow spheres
- *Spongospora* — cysts in loosely arranged, spongelike clusters
- *Woronina* — cysts arranged in a single layer
- *Sorodiscus* — cysts arranged in bilayered disks
- *Octomyxa* — cysts united in groups of eight
- *Tetramyxa* — cysts united in groups of two or four
- *Membranosorus* — cysts of irregular shape and size in a single layer conforming to the host cell

Identification requires microscopic examination of infected cells (Karling 1968). Voucher specimens should include permanent slides of infected host cells containing the cysts.
