# Garden Tools — Selection and Sharpening

## Overview

[[steve-solomon]] argues that manual gardening is neither exhausting nor difficult — provided you have quality tools and, critically, keep them properly sharpened. The single most common reason new gardeners find digging and hoeing impossibly hard is that their tools are blunt. A sharp shovel cuts into soil with ease; a blunt one turns every dig into a battle.

## The Essential Three Tools

To handle a garden up to a quarter acre (1,000 square meters), Solomon says you need only three essential tools:

1. **A [[combination-shovel]]** (his preferred all-around digging tool)
2. **A bow rake** (for leveling, smoothing, and cultivating)
3. **A hoe** (for weeding and cultivating between rows)

Plus one maintenance tool:

4. **A 10-inch (25 cm) mill bastard file** for sharpening the shovel and hoe

With these four items, a reasonably fit person can maintain a substantial vegetable garden without any power equipment.

## Shovel vs. Spade vs. Combination Shovel

### Shovel

Shovels are designed to **move loose material** — piles of sand, soil, [[compost]], or gravel. They have:
- Broad, deeply curved blades that hold a lot of material (like a soup spoon)
- Rolled-over tops for pressing with the foot
- Handles designed for lifting and tossing material

Shovels are excellent for filling wheelbarrows, moving piles, and transporting loose material over distances.

### Spade

Spades are designed to **cut into and lift compacted earth**. They have:
- Flatter, narrower blades than shovels
- Shorter handles (usually D-shaped)
- Designed to be pressed vertically into the ground with the foot
- More effective for breaking new ground and turning soil

Spades come in different sizes for different statures:
- Regular spades: for average-height adults
- Short spades: for people under five feet (1.5 m) tall or for children

### Combination Shovel (Recommended)

The combination shovel merges the functions of both. Its blade is:
- Narrow enough to press into compacted earth without excessive effort (like a spade)
- Curved enough to lift and move soil effectively (like a shovel)

Solomon's strong preference is for the combination shovel because it handles both cutting into soil and moving it. The one task it's less suited for is moving loose material over considerable distances, where a dedicated shovel's broader blade is more efficient.

### Spading Fork

Spading forks are designed for naturally loose (sandy) soils. They work effectively only on light ground:
- Push into sandy soil easily
- Lift soil by picking it up between the tines
- **Not suitable for heavy ground**: the tines bend when used with enough force to work clay or compacted soil

**Short-handled spading forks**: Solomon considers these "an instrument of torture." They require the gardener to bend over repeatedly while working, which is exhausting and hard on the back. For anyone of average height or taller, a long-handled tool is far preferable.

## Choosing Quality Tools

### What to Look For

When selecting shovels, spades, and hoes, Solomon recommends:

1. **Brand names used by tradespeople** — professionals can't afford tools that break
2. **Solid handle attachment** — loose handles mean a short tool life
3. **Light and well-balanced, yet strong** — heavy tools are fatiguing
4. **Rolled-over blade top** (on shovels and spades) — allows pressing with your foot without pain
5. **Good steel quality** — test by attempting to sharpen; if the file cuts rapidly with little pressure, the metal is too soft

### Where to Buy

- **Landscaper's or nursery supply houses** — cater to professionals who use tools all day
- **Specialty hardware stores** — better selection than discount retailers
- **Secondhand sources** — take a file to thrift stores and test before buying
- **Mail-order catalogs** — some offer high-quality tools at reasonable prices

### What to Avoid

- **Discount store tools** — invariably made of inferior steel that dulls quickly and breaks under stress
- **Short-handled tools** (unless you are very short or buying for a child) — bending over while digging is exhausting
- **Novelty or gimmick designs** — stick with proven, traditional patterns

## Tool Steel Quality

### The Hardness Trade-off

Making tool steel involves inherent compromise:

| Steel Quality | Hardness | Edge Retention | Brittleness | Durability |
|--------------|----------|---------------|-------------|------------|
| Very hard (razors) | Very hard | Excellent | Very brittle | Chips easily |
| Medium (garden tools) | Moderate | Good | Moderate | Balanced |
| Soft (cheap tools) | Soft | Poor | Tough | Bends, won't hold edge |

Garden implements encounter rocks, roots, and bending stress. They need an intermediate grade of good-quality steel — hard enough to hold an edge but tough enough not to snap when twisted or struck against buried obstacles.

### Testing Steel Quality

The best way to assess tool steel quality:

1. **Try to sharpen it**: If a file or sharpening stone cuts the metal rapidly with little pressure, the steel is too soft
2. **Check how long it stays sharp**: A quality shovel will dig all summer without needing resharpening (unless it hits many rocks)
3. **Look for rust patterns**: Surface rust doesn't indicate quality, but deeply pitted rust may suggest the steel is wearing thin
4. **Inspect blade thickness**: If a used shovel's blade is visibly thin from years of sharpening, its useful life is ending

## Sharpening the Shovel

### Step-by-Step Process

1. **Secure the shovel**: Place it on the edge of a porch or on the ground, with one foot on the handle behind where it sockets into the blade. Alternatively, have someone hold it for you.

2. **Set the angle**: Hold the file so the angle between the blade and the file is about **15°**. Maintain this angle firmly throughout — do not let the file change angle during strokes.

3. **File from center outward**: If the cutting edge is curved, always start strokes at the center of the blade and work out toward one side. Alternate: a few strokes center-to-left, then a few strokes center-to-right.

4. **Apply firm, controlled pressure**: Press down firmly but not so hard that you lose control of the angle.

5. **Continue until a bevel forms**: After several hundred strokes (it takes time the first time), you'll have produced a chisel-like bevel. The shovel should be sharp enough to nearly cut your finger on.

6. **Remove the wire edge**: During filing, a thin "wire" (burr) forms on the back side of the blade. Flip the shovel over and, holding the file at about 5°, make a few light strokes to remove it. Don't create a matching bevel on the back — just remove the wire.

### Bevel Consistency

For a truly sharp edge, the bevel must have:
- No variations in angle along its length
- The exact same angle of bevel across the entire cutting edge
- A smooth, continuous surface

It is not necessary to make the bevel go all the way to the sides of the blade — most of the cutting effort happens within about four inches (10 cm) of the center.

## Sharpening the Hoe

Hoes should be ground to a **20° angle** (slightly less acute than a shovel's 15°). The process is otherwise similar to sharpening a shovel, but the slightly stouter angle helps the hoe withstand the lateral stress of chopping through weed stems and soil.

## File Maintenance

- **Mill bastard files** (10-inch / 25 cm) are the standard choice for garden tool sharpening
- When a file seems to cut more slowly than it used to, it's time for a new one — files wear out
- **Keep a light coat of oil** on files that will sit unused for months to prevent rust
- Files are inexpensive — don't struggle with a worn one
