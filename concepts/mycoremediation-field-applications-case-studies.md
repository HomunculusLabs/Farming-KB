# Field Applications and Case Studies of Mycoremediation

## Overview

While much mycoremediation research has been conducted at laboratory and bench scale, field applications and pilot-scale studies demonstrate the practical potential and limitations of fungal-based remediation. Field deployments must address competition from native microorganisms, environmental variability, scale-up challenges, and regulatory requirements. This page summarizes documented field applications and case studies from the Singh (2006) source text across multiple contaminant types.

## Treatment Technology Landscape

Table 4.1 from the source summarizes applicable treatment technologies for petroleum hydrocarbon removal. Bioremediation is applicable to both soil and groundwater in both in situ and ex situ configurations. Mycorrhizas are applicable to soil in ex situ treatment, while white-rot fungi are applicable to soil in ex situ treatment. Engineered in situ bioremediation has received wide acclaim because it requires little energy, preserves soil structure, and can detoxify contaminants. Complete mineralization to innocuous end products is preferred over partial degradation to metabolites with unknown fate and effects. Natural attenuation has become a popular cost-effective remedy, as has [[comparison-phytoremediation-vs-mycoremediation-detail]].

## Petroleum Hydrocarbon Field Applications

### Oil Spill Response
Crude oil spills are global problems causing severe environmental damage. Fungi and bacteria in soil catalyze bioremediation processes, with the long-term environmental consequences in agricultural and forest areas being less severe than in coastal ecosystems. Key field-relevant findings include:

- Cladosporium resinae degrades 20-40% of petroleum when added to inoculum and is ubiquitously distributed in oil-contaminated environments.
- Population of oil-utilizing fungi increased from 60% to 82% in soil during oil decomposition studies, indicating enzymatic adaptation to hydrocarbons.
- Application of waste oil to soil promotes growth of Graphium and Paecilomyces spp.
- Terrestrial fungi including Acremonium, Graphium, Fusarium, Penicillium, Paecilomyces, and Trichoderma are capable of degrading crude oil hydrocarbons in soil.
- Fungal cultures inoculated on ceramic support media degraded vapor-phase volatile organic compounds (VOCs) as sole carbon and energy sources.

### PAH-Contaminated Soil
Multiple field-scale and pilot studies demonstrate PAH remediation:

- Pleurotus ostreatus removed benzo[a]pyrene up to 30%, 47%, and 94% after 1, 2, and 4 months, respectively, in creosote-contaminated soil. Total PAHs were lowered to 96% after 4 months.
- Phanerochaete sordida achieved 75% removal of 3- and 4-ring PAHs from creosote-contaminated soil (4017 ug/g) in 8 weeks of soil inoculation.
- P. sordida on wood chips removed 85-95% of three-ring PAHs and 24-72% of four-ring PAHs in 60 days.
- Compost bioreactors with P. chrysosporium removed 45% of initial benzo[a]pyrene. Compost bioaugmentation achieved 62.8% removal efficiency.
- Pilot-scale constructed wetlands employed to treat PAH-contaminated water identified 40 fungal species from 24 genera, with 33 species showing efficient fluoranthene degradation.
- On-site treatability and case studies on PAH degradation have been conducted at multiple locations.

### Multi-Process Phytoremediation
A multi-process phytoremediation system combining volatilization, photooxidation, microbial remediation, and phytoremediation proved approximately twice as effective as phytoremediation alone for removal of 16 priority PAHs. The rhizospheres of nine plant species showed enhanced removal compared to unvegetated controls.

## Pesticide and Herbicide Field Studies

- Composting systems showed 50-60% mineralization of [UL-14C]PCP during pilot-scale composting of chlorophenol-contaminated soil.
- White-rot fungi amendment to compost windrows achieved >90% chlorophenol removal.
- Trichoderma harzianum thrives in various rhizospheres as a biocontroller and contributes to [[mycoremediation-pesticide-herbicide-contamination]].
- Laccase immobilized on modified PVDF microfiltration membranes has been applied for pesticide removal from water.

Pesticide degradation by fungi is particularly important for organochlorine and organophosphate compounds that persist in soil for years. The ligninolytic enzyme systems of white-rot fungi can dechlorinate and oxidize these molecules through non-specific radical reactions, converting them into less toxic, more water-soluble metabolites that are subsequently mineralized by soil bacteria. Field-scale applications have included amendment of contaminated agricultural soils with lignocellulosic substrates (straw, sawdust, wood chips) inoculated with Trametes or Pleurotus species, where the added organic matter serves dual purposes: supporting fungal enzyme production and stimulating overall soil microbial activity.

## Heavy Metal Field Applications

- Aspergillus niger biomass produces weak organic acids (mostly citric acid) for leaching heavy metals from contaminated soils, achieving remediation to Quebec Provincial Standards for heavy metals within 20-25 days at approximately $5.00 per cubic meter. The process is applicable for both in situ and ex situ mycoremediation.
- Three grass species inoculated with Glomus mosseae or G. intraradices removed 26-72% of 137Cs and 24-89% of 90Sr from contaminated soil, offering a feasible, low-cost strategy for radionuclide remediation.
- Inoculation with Pisolithus tinctorius at Pinus massoniana roots increases resistance to aluminum stress in acidic, metal-contaminated soils.
- Mine spoils serve as target sites for studying mycorrhizal fungal applications in metal remediation.

The advantage of fungal-based heavy metal approaches is that, unlike chemical leaching, they operate at ambient temperatures and pressures and do not require harsh reagents. However, metals are not degraded but rather mobilized or bioaccumulated — treatment endpoints must specify where the mobilized metals end up. Collection systems for leachate, or harvest of metal-laden fungal biomass, are essential components of a complete remediation design. This distinction between degradation (applicable to organic pollutants) and mobilization/sequestration (applicable to metals) is fundamental to mycoremediation practice.

## Dye and Textile Wastewater Applications

- Case studies on dye removal from textile wastewater have been conducted using white-rot fungi in pilot-scale systems.
- Industrial effluent from nitrated stilbene-sulfonic acid manufacturing (containing high sodium chloride and sulfate) was successfully decolorized by white-rot fungi, demonstrating tolerance to high salt concentrations relevant to chemical industry wastes.
- Repeated-batch mode using Funalia trogii pellets maintained decolorization efficiency over multiple cycles within 24 hours per cycle.

The textile industry generates enormous volumes of colored wastewater, with an estimated 10-15% of synthetic dyes used in dyeing processes being released directly into effluent streams. These dyes are often recalcitrant to conventional biological treatment due to their complex aromatic structures and synthetic origin. White-rot fungi are uniquely suited to address this challenge because their lignin-modifying enzymes (laccase, manganese peroxidase, lignin peroxidase) are non-specific oxidants capable of breaking down the same types of aromatic bonds found in synthetic dye molecules. Field-scale bioreactors using immobilized fungal biomass on supports such as polyurethane foam, coconut coir, or wood chips have demonstrated sustained decolorization over periods of weeks to months, offering a viable alternative to expensive physical-chemical treatment methods such as activated carbon adsorption or advanced oxidation processes.

## Assessment Methods for Field Applications

Field assessment methods include:

- **Respirometric tests**: Measurement of total CO2 production provides knowledge on potential hydrocarbon degradation. The Biometer flask test assesses mineralization rates from small soil samples. Radiorespiratory methods using n-[1-14C]hexadecane and [9-14C]phenanthrene verify mineralization of aliphatic and aromatic compounds.
- **Fungal enumeration**: Total heterotrophic and specific hydrocarbon-degrading fungal counts provide understanding of soil biological conditions and acclimatization of indigenous populations.
- **Enzyme assays**: FDA hydrolyzing and laccase activities in soil correlate with degradation rates.
- **Molecular methods**: PCR amplification of 16S rRNA gene sequences detects and characterizes microbial populations in treatment bioreactors.

Combining multiple assessment methods provides the most reliable picture of treatment progress. Chemical analysis of contaminant concentrations (the primary metric for regulatory compliance) should be supplemented with biological indicators such as enzyme activity and microbial community shifts, which can signal treatment efficacy before contaminant concentration changes become statistically significant. This multi-parameter approach is especially important in heterogeneous field soils where contaminant distribution is uneven and sampling variability can mask treatment effects.

## Challenges in Field Deployment

Key challenges for field-scale mycoremediation include: (1) White-rot fungi may remain metabolically inactive in highly contaminated field soils. (2) Autochthonous microflora sometimes show greater degradation potential than inoculated fungi. (3) Maintaining optimal conditions (moisture, nutrients, aeration) over large areas is logistically challenging. (4) Competition from native microorganisms limits colonization by introduced fungi. (5) Regulatory approval processes for deliberate fungal introduction into the environment. (6) Monitoring and verification of treatment effectiveness at field scale. (7) Cost-effectiveness compared to established physical and chemical treatment methods.

## Economic and Regulatory Considerations

The economics of field-scale mycoremediation are highly site-specific. Treatment costs typically range from $50 to $300 per cubic meter of contaminated soil, compared to $200 to $1,500 for excavation and landfill disposal. However, mycoremediation timelines are longer — treatment may require 6 months to 2 years to achieve target cleanup levels, depending on contaminant type, concentration, and environmental conditions. Biopile and landfarming configurations that combine fungal inoculation with mechanical aeration and nutrient amendment tend to offer the best balance of speed and cost.

Regulatory frameworks for mycoremediation vary significantly by jurisdiction. In the United States, the EPA has accepted bioremediation as a documented remedy under CERCLA and state voluntary cleanup programs, but deliberate introduction of non-native fungal species may require additional permitting under the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA) or state biological control regulations. The use of native or commercially available saprobic fungi such as Pleurotus ostreatus generally faces fewer regulatory hurdles than genetically modified organisms or exotic species. Site closure criteria based on chemical concentration thresholds remain the standard, with mycoremediation projects typically requiring periodic soil sampling to demonstrate progress toward target levels.

## Emerging Contaminants and New Applications

Beyond the well-documented applications above, mycoremediation research is expanding into emerging contaminant classes:

- **Pharmaceutical residues** including antibiotics, hormones, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) have been targeted by Trametes versicolor in lab and pilot-scale bioreactors. Laccase and manganese peroxidase enzymes from white-rot fungi can degrade ibuprofen, diclofenac, and carbamazepine, compounds that persist through conventional wastewater treatment.
- **Endocrine-disrupting compounds** (EDCs) such as bisphenol A (BPA) and nonylphenol are susceptible to enzymatic degradation by Pleurotus species. Field-scale constructed wetlands inoculated with ligninolytic fungi have shown promise for treating municipal wastewater containing these micropollutants.
- **Explosives and munitions** constituents including TNT and RDX have been degraded by Phanerochaete chrysosporium in soil bioremediation trials at military training ranges, where conventional excavation is impractical due to the scale of contamination.
- **Microplastics** degradation by fungi is an emerging research area. Certain terrestrial fungi colonize polyethylene and polypropylene surfaces, and enzymatic oxidation by ligninolytic systems has demonstrated weight loss in plastic films under controlled conditions, though field applications remain years away.

## Scaling and Implementation Considerations

Translating bench-scale mycoremediation results to field deployment involves several practical considerations:

- **Inoculum production** at commercial scale requires reliable spawn or liquid culture methods. Grain spawn and sawdust spawn production techniques from [[cotter-oyster-mushroom-cultivation]] are directly applicable, though specific fungal species may require optimized media formulations.
- **Site assessment** should include contaminant profiling, soil characterization (texture, organic matter, pH, moisture), native microbiome analysis, and hydrological mapping. Pre-treatment bioassays using the species intended for deployment help predict field performance.
- **Monitoring protocols** must establish baseline contaminant concentrations and track degradation over time. The respirometric and molecular methods described above provide complementary data, with chemical analysis (GC-MS, HPLC) serving as the definitive measure of contaminant reduction.
- Integration with existing technologies such as biopiles, windrow composting, and constructed wetlands can enhance treatment. Mycoremediation works synergistically with [[comparison-phytoremediation-vs-mycoremediation-detail]] when fungal inoculation targets the rhizosphere zone, where root exudates support sustained microbial activity.

A critical but often overlooked factor in successful field deployment is the timing of inoculation relative to environmental conditions. Fungal inoculation during warm, moist seasons (spring or early autumn in temperate climates) maximizes establishment success, while inoculation during hot, dry summer months or freezing winter conditions leads to poor survival. Pre-inoculation of substrates (spawn or inoculated wood chips) in controlled environments before field deployment can circumvent this timing constraint by allowing fungal biomass to establish before exposure to field conditions.
