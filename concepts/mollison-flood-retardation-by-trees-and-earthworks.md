# Flood Retardation by Trees and Earthworks

Mollison documents the powerful role of trees and earthworks in reducing
flood peaks, moderating stream flow, and preventing the catastrophic
consequences of rapid runoff from cleared landscapes. This page covers
the hydrological functions of forests and the design of structures for
flood management.

## Trees and Flood Discharge

Woodlands may increase available runoff by extending the period of
surface flow. The rush of water from bare hillsides is exchanged for
the slower delivery from the matted carpet of the woodland. The maximum
floods of rivers are reduced, and the lowest summer flow increased. The
planting of suitable trees along ridges and a little way down the slope
facing the rain-bearing and damp winds produces the maximum of desired
effects in proportion to the area occupied.

A narrow belt of woodland along the foot of a slope will arrest soil
brought down by rains from the hillside. The encouragement of dense
vegetation along the bottom of a narrow valley may check the rate of
flood discharge to a useful extent. Suitable tree and bush growths in
swampy areas and around their margins will increase their effect in
checking flood discharge, and may prevent these areas from contributing
large quantities of silt to streams during heavy rains.

## Forest Loss and Flood Consequences

When forests are cleared, several hydrological changes occur:

- Rain that once infiltrated through forest soils now runs rapidly off
  bare slopes.
- Flood waters are largely lost to downstream systems rather than
  being stored in soil.
- The maximum floods of rivers increase dramatically.
- The clearing of forests will result in flood and drought cycles.
- Following deforestation, up to 30% more silt per annum flows into
  water storages, reducing their useful life.

## Key Principles

1. Forests reduce flood peaks and increase low flow -- clearing forests
  causes flood-drought cycles.
2. A narrow belt of trees can produce disproportionate flood reduction
  relative to area occupied.
3. Check dams and gabions convert destructive flood energy into
  beneficial sediment deposition.
4. Swales on contour convert runoff to infiltration, reducing downstream
  flood peaks.
5. Always design for 100-year flood events -- omitting flood data can
  cost lives.
6. Treat sediment as a resource: capture it where it does good rather
  than letting it damage downstream systems.
