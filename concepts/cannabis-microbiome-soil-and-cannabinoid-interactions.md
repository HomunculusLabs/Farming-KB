# Cannabis Microbiome: Soil and Cannabinoid Interactions

> Based on findings from Winston et al. (2014), this page details how soil type,
> [[cannabinoid-microbiome-correlation-confounded-edaphic-factors]], and cannabinoid chemistry shape the cannabis-associated
> microbiome across rhizosphere, bulk soil, and endorhiza compartments.

## Overview

Winston et al. (2014) conducted pooled experiments to disentangle the relative
influences of **soil type**, **sample type** (compartment), and **cannabis
strain** on microbiome composition. All three factors were highly significant
in structuring [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]], but they operated through distinct
mechanisms and at different magnitudes.

## Soil Type as the Dominant Driver of OTU Composition

Soil type exerted the **strongest influence** over operational taxonomic unit
(OTU) differences in the [[cannabis-microbiome]]:

- **690 weighted OTU differences** were attributable to soil type.
- **657 unweighted OTU differences** were attributable to soil type.

This dominance held across both abundance-weighted and presence/absence metrics,
meaning soil type affects *which taxa are present* and *how abundant they are*.
The two soil types examined (MB and OC) hosted meaningfully different microbial
communities even before accounting for plant effects.

### Strain vs. Sample Type Effects

| Factor | Weighted OTU Differences | Unweighted OTU Differences | Interpretation |
|--------|-------------------------|----------------------------|----------------|
| Soil type | 690 (strongest) | 657 (strongest) | Affects both presence/absence and abundance |
| Strain | Larger than sample type | No significant differences | Structures **abundances** only, not presence/absence |
| Sample type | Moderate | Present | Shapes both dimensions |

Strain effects were **abundance-driven** — a given strain did not recruit or
exclude entirely different taxa, but shifted relative abundances of the shared
community. [[cannabis-cultivar-specificity-endorhiza-microbiome]] operates through modulation rather than
deterministic selection at the OTU membership level.

## The Two-Tier Selection Model: Cross-Soil OTU Sharing

A critical experiment supporting the **two-tier model** of root microbiome
assembly involved growing the same strain (White Widow) in two different soils
and comparing [[cannabis-rhizosphere-endorhiza-communities]]:

- Endorhiza communities shared **more OTUs with their own soil** than with the
  other soil where the same strain was grown.
- Even with genotype held constant, the local soil microbiome — not the strain —
  determines the primary pool of available endorhiza colonizers.

This provides direct evidence for hierarchical assembly:

1. **Tier 1 (Soil):** Determines which taxa are available for colonization —
   the dominant filter.
2. **Tier 2 (Strain):** Modulates abundances within the available pool.

## Beta Diversity: Compartmental Differentiation

- **Rhizosphere and bulk soil** microbiomes were **more similar to each other**
  than either was to the endorhiza (beta distances significantly lower).
- The **endorhiza** is the most differentiated compartment.

This gradient reflects increasing selective pressure from the plant host as
proximity to the root interior increases: bulk soil is shaped by abiotic factors;
the rhizosphere adds exudate effects; the endorhiza adds immune selection and
physical entry barriers.

## Cannabinoid Concentration and Endorhiza Community Structure

A striking finding was the **significant correlation** between cannabinoid
concentrations and [[endorhiza-microbiome]] structure:

- **Mantel test:** r = 0.863, p = 0.001

However, this result is **confounded by soil effects on THC production** — soil
type independently influences both THC levels and microbiome composition. Without
controlled experiments decoupling soil chemistry from plant chemistry, the
directionality remains ambiguous. Despite the confound, the correlation is
biologically plausible: [[root-exudates]] (including cannabinoids) could serve as
carbon sources or signaling molecules shaping the endorhiza community.

## Sphingomonas wittichii: A Strain-Associated Specialist

**Sphingomonas wittichii** was identified as strain-associated, particularly
prevalent in the **Maui Wowie** cultivar:

- Can metabolize **phenazine-1-carboxylic acid**, an antibiotic compound
  produced by certain soil bacteria (e.g., *Pseudomonas* spp.).
- This metabolic capability may confer competitive advantage in the rhizosphere
  where phenazine-producing organisms are common.
- Implicated in **soil survival** strategies, potentially allowing Maui Wowie to
  tolerate or leverage specific microbial chemistry in its preferred soils.

This provides a specific mechanistic link between a cannabis cultivar and a
functionally relevant microorganism, moving beyond compositional descriptions
to [[bloomfield-fungal-fruiting-body-morphology-ecological-function]].

## Edaphic Factor Rankings

| Rank | Edaphic Factor | Correlation (Weighted) | Notes |
|------|---------------|----------------------|-------|
| 1 | **Nitrogen** | r = 0.465 | Strongest driver of community variation |
| 2 | **Salinity** | Moderate | Second strongest association |
| 3 | **Carbon** | Moderate | Reflects organic matter availability |
| 4 | **Water content** | Moderate | Influences oxygen diffusion and niche space |
| 5 | **pH** | Weaker among the set | Still significant but less explanatory |

Nitrogen's dominance is consistent with its role as the primary limiting
nutrient in terrestrial ecosystems, selecting for copiotrophs at high
availability and oligotrophs at low availability.

## Alpha Diversity Gradients Across Compartments

- **Bulk soil** (~4,947): Highest diversity — largest species pool
- **Rhizosphere** (~4,525): Slight reduction (~8.5% decrease)
- **Endorhiza** (~3,321): Dramatic reduction (~33% decrease from bulk)

- **MB soil** had higher alpha diversity than **OC soil** in bulk soil and
  rhizosphere compartments.
- **Endorhiza diversity was not significantly different** between soils.

This convergence at the endorhiza level suggests the plant host imposes a
filter narrowing community size independently of starting soil diversity — the
plant may support a finite niche space within root tissues, capping diversity
regardless of soil richness.

## Synthesis and Implications

Winston et al. (2014) establish a clear hierarchy: (1) **soil type** determines
available organisms; (2) **compartment** (bulk → rhizosphere → endorhiza)
progressively filters diversity; (3) **strain** modulates abundances; (4)
**edaphic chemistry** (especially nitrogen) further structures communities;
(5) **cannabinoid concentration** correlates strongly with endorhiza composition
but causal direction remains unresolved.

For cultivation, **soil selection** is the single most important decision for
shaping the root-associated microbiome. Manipulating nitrogen levels may offer a
practical lever, though the interplay between soil, plant chemistry, and
microbiome requires further study.
