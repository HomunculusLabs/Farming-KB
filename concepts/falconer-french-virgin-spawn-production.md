of producing virgin spawn, drawing extensively from M. Lachaume's
authoritative book "The Cave Mushroom." Virgin spawn is natural spawn
that has not yet produced mushrooms and is far more vigorous than
manufactured spawn. It is supposed to be produced directly from
mushroom spores rather than from surviving parts of old spawn.

## What Is Virgin Spawn

Virgin spawn, also called natural or wild spawn, is the spawn that
occurs naturally in fields, manure piles, or elsewhere without any
artificial aid. It appears as a netted mass of white strings traversing
the manure, giving off a strong mushroom odor. Falconer noted that
using virgin spawn for spawning beds produced larger and heavier
mushrooms than from "made" spawn, and the beds lasted longer in good
bearing, though the total weight of the crop was not greater.

Spawn makers always endeavored to obtain virgin spawn to use in
impregnating their manufactured bricks and flakes, but it was seldom
used directly for spawning mushroom beds because it was not easy to
obtain. When discovered in a manure pile, Falconer advised securing all
one could find, bringing it indoors to dry, and preserving it as one
would French flake spawn.

## Lachaume's First Method

The most detailed account of producing French virgin spawn comes from
Lachaume's first method. One takes five or six barrow loads of horse
droppings that have lain in a heap for some time and lost their heat,
and mixes them with one-fourth of their bulk of short stable litter.

In April, a trench is opened two feet wide, twenty inches deep, and of
suitable length, at the foot of but eight inches distant from a wall
facing north. In the bottom of the trench, a layer three to four inches
deep of chopped straw is spread, then an equally thick layer of the
prepared manure, all pressed firmly by treading it down.

The two layers are gently watered, and then another double layer of
chopped straw and droppings is added, trodden down and watered, and so
on until the top of the trench is reached. The bed should rise above
the level of the ground and be rounded off like the top of a trunk. To
prevent excessive dampness from heavy rain, the mound is covered with a
thick layer of stable litter.

Three months after filling the trench it should be opened at the side
or end. If the pieces of manure are well covered with masses of
bluish-white filaments giving off the odor of mushrooms, the operation
has succeeded. If the threads are only sparingly scattered through the
mass, the trench should be covered up again and left for another month.

In saving the spawn, the flakes of manure containing the largest amount
of spawn filaments should be retained, and those showing a brown
appearance rejected. To facilitate drying, the flakes should be broken
into pieces weighing from one to two pounds, then placed in a well
ventilated shed without piling upon each other. Properly prepared and
dried, this spawn keeps good for ten years.

## Lachaume's Second Method

The second method accelerates spawn formation by adding pieces of old
spawn here and there throughout the bed. At the beginning of April, a
piece of ground is chosen at the foot of a wall facing north, with very
open and light soil to avoid dampness.

A trench sixteen inches wide is opened about eight inches from the foot
of the wall. Manure prepared for a mushroom bed and just come into
condition is filled into the trench, leaving a space at one end about
two and a half feet long for forming a mushroom bed. The manure is
tossed about and shaken up with the hands, then pressed down with the
hands and knees.

As the layer of manure reaches six inches in thickness, lumps of spawn
are placed along the edge about one foot apart. These lumps are placed
level with the manure on the edge facing the wall. The surface facing
the wall is raised vertically against the earthen wall of the trench,
while the other half slopes gently toward the wall, leaving three or
four inches between it and the side of the trench for trimming.

A second row of lumps is inserted as the bed is filled up. The bed is
finished by covering with a layer of fine, dry soil three or four inches
thick. The spawn must be very dry to avoid a premature crop of mushrooms
instead of fresh spawn. At the end of six weeks or a couple of months,
the new spawn should make its appearance.

## Lachaume's Third Method Using Guano

Lachaume's third method fills a trench with layers of one-third pigeon
or fowl guano and two-thirds short manure containing a large proportion
of spent horse droppings. The material is trodden down firmly, watered
if too dry, and finished with a layer of soil as described in the other
methods. At the end of a couple of months, or even a little longer,
this produces a supply of well-formed cakes of spawn of excellent
quality.

## Spawn Deterioration

Lachaume noted that by using spawn taken from one bed to impregnate
another, "breeding in and in" as it were, the mushrooms show a tendency
to deteriorate after a time. New spawn must therefore be obtained as
soon as any signs of deterioration begin to manifest themselves. This
is an important observation about the limitations of vegetative spawn
propagation and the eventual need to refresh the mycelial line.

## Stratification Before Use

Much stress was laid upon stratifying the spawn before using, when dry
spawn was employed. About eight days before a bed is to be spawned, the
dry spawn is spread out in a row on the floor of the cave or cellar so
that it may absorb moisture and the mycelium begin to run. At spawning
time these cakes or flakes are broken up and used in the ordinary way.
It is claimed this gives a week's difference in favor of the early
appearing of the crop. However, no more spawn than is necessary for
use should be stratified, for it will not bear being dried and damped
again.
