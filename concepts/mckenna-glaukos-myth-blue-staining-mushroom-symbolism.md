# The Glaukos Myth: Blue Staining, Honey Preservation, and Mushroom Symbolism

## The Myth of Glaukos

Among the most intriguing passages in Terence McKenna's *Food of the Gods* is his analysis of the little-known Greek myth of **Glaukos**, son of King Minos and Pasiphae (the Moon Goddess). McKenna argues that this myth encodes a barely-remembered memory of [[eleusinian-mysteries-minoan-mushroom-cults]], preserved in symbolic form after the original religious practices had been forgotten.

The myth, preserved in its most complete form by Apollodorus and Hyginus, tells the following story:

While Glaukos was still a small child, he died from falling into a jar (a *pithos*) filled with honey while pursuing a rat or a fly. His father Minos consulted diviners, who told him that a man who could offer the best simile for a three-colored cow in his herds would also know how to restore the boy. The seer Polyidos ("the man-who-has-many-ideas") compared the cow's colors to the fruit of the bramble, and was compelled to find the boy. He did so through divination, but Minos then insisted that Polyidos restore the child to life.

Polyidos was shut in a tomb with the dead body. There he saw a snake approach the corpse, killed it with a stone, and watched a second snake return with an herb that revived its dead companion. Polyidos took the same herb and applied it to Glaukos, raising him from the dead. Minos then compelled Polyidos to teach Glaukos the art of divination. As Polyidos was about to sail home, he bade Glaukos spit into his mouth—and thereby the youth unwittingly lost the power of divination.

## Glaukos: "The Blue-Gray One"

McKenna identifies the central clue to the myth's meaning in the name itself. **Glaukos** means "blue-gray" or "gleaming blue-green." This is significant because, as McKenna notes, "it is well known among mycologists that the flesh of *Stropharia cubensis* and other psilocybin mushrooms has the property of staining a bluish color when bruised or broken."

This blue staining reaction is caused by the enzymatic oxidation of psilocybin and its analogs. When the mushroom tissue is damaged, enzymes convert psilocin (the active compound) into blue-colored oxidation products. The reaction is one of the most reliable field indicators of psychoactive *Psilocybe* species and related genera.

The name Glaukos, McKenna argues, identifies the youth as a symbolic representation of the psychoactive mushroom itself—a "blue-gray" child preserved in honey, dead and then restored to life through secret herbal knowledge.

## The Honey Jar: Preservation and Transformation

The motif of Glaukos falling into a honey jar (*pithos*) is multiply resonant:

### Mushroom Preservation in Honey
McKenna notes that "in Mexico honey has long been used to preserve psilocybin-containing mushrooms." The antiseptic and hygroscopic properties of honey make it an excellent preserving medium. Wasson documented similar practices in his ethnographic work on Mazatec mushroom use.

### Bronze Age Funerary Practice
Herodotus mentions that the Babylonians preserved their dead in honey. The use of large storage vessels (*pithoi*) for burials was widespread in the Bronze Age Aegean. The bucket-shaped graves of the Natufian culture (discussed elsewhere in McKenna's analysis) provide an even earlier parallel.

### The Soma Connection
McKenna quotes Wasson's observation that "honey, *madhu*, is mentioned frequently in the Rig Veda but mead never. Honey is cited for its sweetness and also is often applied as a metaphor of enhancement to Soma." Honey was used to mix with Soma but the two substances were never confused—honey was the sweetening agent that enhanced the sacred preparation.

The pithos shape itself, McKenna suggests, echoes the bucket-shaped graves of the Natufian culture, connecting the Minoan material to the deeper prehistory of mushroom use in the ancient Near East.

## The Three-Colored Cow: Linguistic Facility

The bizarre episode of the three-colored cow and the simile contest is one of the most cryptic elements of the myth. McKenna interprets it as a reference to the importance of linguistic facility as a precondition for understanding mushroom mysteries:

The cow (or cattle generally) was the central totemic animal of Minoan religion, as evidenced by the numerous cattle shrines at Qatal Huyuk. The three colors may reference the tripartite nature of the mushroom experience, or the stages of mushroom growth, or the three recognized levels of psilocybin experience that McKenna describes elsewhere. The demand for a simile—"the fruit of the bramble"—suggests that poetic or metaphorical thinking was considered essential to the shamanic art.

## The Serpent and the Resurrection Herb

The serpent motif connects the Glaukos myth to the Genesis story of Eden, where the serpent also possesses secret knowledge concerning plants—specifically, plants that confer immortality (or at least knowledge of good and evil). In both narratives:

- A serpent possesses accurate and secret information about a plant with transformative properties
- The plant confers a form of knowledge or power that threatens established authority
- The knowledge is ultimately elusive or transitory

The resurrection herb that restores the dead snake (and then Glaukos) parallels the shamanic understanding of [[ceremonial-shamanic-use-psychoactive-mushrooms]] as agents of death and rebirth—the ego-death experience followed by psychic renewal. This death-rebirth pattern is one of the most universally reported features of the [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]], lending additional plausibility to McKenna's identification of the herb with psychoactive fungi.

In the broader context of Greek mythology, resurrection herbs appear in several narratives—Medea's herbal knowledge, Circe's pharmacopoeia, and the herbs of Chiron the centaur. The recurring motif suggests that ancient Greek culture preserved a memory of genuine botanical knowledge about plants with remarkable psychoactive or medicinal properties, even as the specific identities of those plants were lost or deliberately obscured.

## The Loss of Divination

Perhaps the most poignant element of the myth is its ending. When Polyidos sails away, he has Glaukos spit into his mouth, and the youth loses all the divinatory power he had been taught. McKenna interprets this as a reference to "the elusive nature of the visions glimpsed during mushroom intoxication."

The knowledge gained through mushroom experience cannot be permanently retained in ordinary consciousness—it is glimpsed and then fades, like a dream that cannot be fully recalled upon waking. The shamanic teacher (Polyidos) retains the understanding, but the student (Glaukos) cannot hold onto it in the absence of ongoing practice or the sacrament itself.

## Parallels with New World Mushroom Cults

McKenna draws a direct parallel to Mesoamerican mushroom traditions: "throughout their range in Mesoamerica, the psychoactive mushrooms are thought of as small children—*los ninos*, 'the dear sweet little ones.'" Maria Sabina, the renowned Mazatec mushroom shamaness, used this formulation. McKenna identifies this as "an instance of the motif of the alchemical children, the elfin denizens of some nearby magical continuum, accessed through psilocybin."

The equation of psychoactive mushrooms with children appears in both Old World and New World traditions, separated by thousands of years and thousands of miles. Whether this represents deep cultural memory of a shared Paleolithic tradition or independent convergent symbolism remains an open question. The universality of the child-mushroom metaphor may reflect the common phenomenological experience of psilocybin—the sense of encountering small, playful, intelligent entities that many users report across cultures.

## The Mycenaean Opium Connection

McKenna notes that the late Mycenaean-Minoan civilization was "more fascinated by opium than by psychedelic plants," citing scholarly analysis suggesting that opium "stimulated the visionary faculty and aroused visions which had earlier been obtained without opium." The transition from mushroom-based visionary practice to opium-based visionary practice represents what McKenna calls a period of "strong medicine"—when the original simpler methods no longer suffice and more potent substitutes are adopted.

This progression mirrors the broader pattern McKenna traces throughout *Food of the Gods*: the replacement of authentic plant-teacher relationships with increasingly destructive drug dependencies, from psilocybin to opium to alcohol to synthetics. Each substitution represents a further alienation from the original symbiotic relationship between humans and psychoactive plants, and each new substance carries a heavier burden of physiological and social harm.

## Assessment

McKenna's reading of the Glaukos myth is speculative but internally consistent. The blue-gray name, the honey preservation, the death-and-rebirth narrative, the serpent with herbal knowledge, and the totemic cattle all align with mushroom symbolism. Whether this represents genuine encoded memory of Minoan mushroom practices or a case of pattern-matching driven by an a priori theory, the analysis illuminates how myths can preserve biological knowledge in symbolic form long after the original practices have been forgotten.

## The Cattle Motif at Qatal Huyuk

The broader Minoan and pre-Minoan context for the cattle symbolism is found at the Neolithic site of **Qatal Huyuk** in Anatolia (modern Turkey), which McKenna discusses extensively. The most common motifs at Qatal Huyuk are cattle and bulls, and secondarily vultures and leopards—all animals of the African grasslands. The cattle shrines feature sculpted heads of cattle protruding into the rooms, sometimes sexually ambiguous, representing bulls or cows or simply "cattle generally."

McKenna argues that "the prevalence of female symbolism in the shrines is overwhelming—for example, the breasts of sculpted stucco that are apparently randomly placed—makes it seem likely that the religious officials were women." This matrifocal religious culture centered on the Great Goddess and her totemic animals, with the mushroom as the "third and hidden member of a kind of shamanic trinity."

## Polyidos: The Shaman Figure

The name Polyidos—"the man-who-has-many-ideas"—identifies him as a classic shamanic figure. Shamans across cultures are characterized by their possession of esoteric knowledge, their ability to travel between worlds (symbolized by Polyidos entering the tomb/the underworld), and their power over life and death. The shaman derives his knowledge from observation of the natural world (watching the snake), not from conventional sources of authority.

The motif of a healer or magician learning a resurrection technique from animal behavior appears in multiple mythological traditions, suggesting a deep archetypal pattern. The animal that knows the secret of life-giving herbs is a widespread figure—from the serpent of Genesis to the bird that brings the healing herb in Norse mythology.

## The Bramble Simile

The specific simile that wins the contest—comparing the three-colored cow to "the fruit of the bramble"—is itself potentially significant. Brambles (blackberries, raspberries) produce aggregate fruits with multiple small drupelets that can display varying colors simultaneously (red, purple, black). This visual resemblance to a multi-colored surface may have made the bramble an apt metaphor for a variegated hide.

But the bramble is also a thorny, wild plant that produces edible fruit—a symbol of the wild, uncultivated knowledge that exists outside the boundaries of civilization, much like psychoactive mushrooms themselves. The simile that wins the contest is drawn from nature, not from the domesticated world, suggesting that true divinatory knowledge comes from close observation of the wild.

## Implications for Understanding Myth

McKenna's analysis of the Glaukos myth exemplifies his broader methodological approach to interpreting ancient religious traditions:

1. **Biological literacy**: Reading myths through the lens of natural history, identifying references to real biological phenomena (blue staining, honey preservation)
2. **Comparative ethnography**: Drawing parallels between Old World and New World mushroom traditions to identify universal patterns
3. **Evolutionary framework**: Situating mythological changes within the larger historical trajectory from mushroom-using partnership societies to alcohol-dominated [[partnership-vs-dominator-societies]]
4. **Symbolic decoding**: Treating mythological motifs as encoded references to actual religious practices rather than as purely literary or psychological constructs

Whether one accepts McKenna's specific interpretation, his approach highlights the possibility that ancient myths preserve genuine biological and ecological knowledge in symbolic form—knowledge that becomes increasingly opaque as the [[ayahuasca-pharmacology-and-cultural-context]] that produced it disappears.

## The Enduring Mystery

McKenna concludes his analysis with characteristic candor: "We may never know with certainty the role that hallucinogenic fungi and plants had in the Minoan world. Much can change over the length of nearly four thousand years." The Glaukos myth stands as a fragmentary record of a religious world that has been almost entirely lost—a world in which a blue-gray child preserved in honey, restored to life by serpent-taught herbal knowledge, carried the memory of humanity's oldest sacramental relationship with psychoactive mushrooms.

## Archaeological Context: Minoan Pillar Worship

The broader religious context of the Glaukos myth includes the Minoan practice of pillar worship. McKenna notes the connection to Soma, which was called "pillar of the World" in the Rig Veda. The broadening columns found in Minoan palaces—particularly the temple tomb near the palace of Knossos—may represent "explicit echoes of the memory of the mushrooms" or "a last aniconic vestige of the mushroom image."

These columns were widely understood to stand for a sacred tree, connected with images and rituals of vegetative significance. Whether they were direct mushroom references or more general vegetative symbols, they testify to a culture deeply invested in the relationship between plant life and religious experience—a relationship that McKenna argues was ultimately rooted in the psychoactive mushroom sacrament.

The myth's emphasis on the transience of the gained knowledge—Polyidos departs with the power, leaving Glaukos empty-handed—serves as a fitting metaphor for the broader historical pattern McKenna describes: the repeated loss of gnosis about the mushroom mystery across cultures and millennia.
