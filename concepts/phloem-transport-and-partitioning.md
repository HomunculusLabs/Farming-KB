# Phloem Transport and Partitioning

The [[phloem]] is the plant's bidirectional distribution system for organic compounds—sugars, starches, proteins, amino acids, hormones, and signaling molecules—produced by photosynthetic cells and delivered to non-photosynthetic tissues throughout the plant. Understanding phloem transport and the partitioning of its cargo is essential to understanding how plants allocate resources for growth, storage, and defense.

## Xylem Sap in Spring: An Exceptional Case

Before examining the phloem in detail, it is worth noting a remarkable seasonal function of the [[xylem]]. In spring, large sugar molecules stored in roots all winter are converted into transportable forms and move **upward through the xylem** to apical meristem tissues. This creates osmotic conditions that draw water into xylem tubes, pushing the sap upward. The resulting xylem sap is what is converted into **maple and birch syrups**.

The mechanism differs between species. In birch trees, root pressure from osmotic water movement drives sap flow. In maples, a freeze-thaw cycle operates: gases (primarily CO₂ from cellular respiration) dissolve in cooling xylem sap at night, lowering pressure and pulling water inward via hydrogen bonding. When morning temperatures warm the sap, trapped gases expand, creating pressure that pushes sap upward. Once leaves and buds form in later spring, phloem takes over sugar transport and xylem sap ceases carrying sugars.

Spring xylem sap also carries amino acids and peptides. Other substances transported in xylem include **hormones**—some triggering stomatal closure during water stress, others carrying growth signals. A single hormone molecule can transmit a message to a cell, making the plant's signaling system remarkably efficient.

## Phloem Sap Composition

Phloem sap is approximately 80% sugar dissolved in water, but its full composition is remarkably complex and varies by species, growth stage, season, and time of day:

- **Sugars**: ~90% of phloem solids; mostly complex forms (sucrose predominates). Glucose, the direct product of photosynthesis, is found in very low concentrations or absent entirely. Some species transport sugar-alcohols (e.g., sorbitol) instead of sugars.
- **Amino acids and proteins**: Up to 12% of sap solids; up to 50% in new seedlings where nitrogen demand for synthesis is extremely high.
- **Organic acids**: Citric, oxalic, piscidic, tartaric, acetic, lactic, and malonic acids—plant-synthesized compounds with specific roles in nutrient acquisition.
- **ATP, potassium ions, hormones**: Essential for cellular energy, signaling, and regulation.
- **Messenger RNA**: Part of a long-distance signaling system coordinating responses to viral attacks, establishing defenses, regulating development, and directing nutrient allocation. RNA levels in sap respond to stress conditions.
- **Viruses**: Can travel through phloem tubes, exploiting the plant's own transport system.

### pH Characteristics

Phloem sap is typically **alkaline** (pH 7.5–8.5), in contrast to the acidic xylem sap and vacuolar contents. The exception is perennials in spring, whose phloem sap becomes acidic during the period of active upward transport.

## Root Exudates and the Rhizosphere

[[root-exudates]]—lipid-, [[carbohydrate]]-, and amino acid-based molecules—travel to the soil through the phloem system. These plant-synthesized compounds serve critical nutritional functions:

- **Organic acids** (citric, oxalic, malonic, etc.) dissolve phosphate minerals and mobilize phosphorus, zinc, iron, manganese, and other nutrients from soil particles. Each acid has different abilities to dissolve specific mineral forms.
- Plants can synthesize specific compounds from their seventeen essential nutrients in response to particular nutrient needs—demonstrating remarkable biochemical adaptability.

Exudate transport across the [[plasmalemma]] requires energy for hydrophilic carbohydrates and amino acids (via transport proteins), while small lipids can diffuse through phospholipid membranes. Larger lipids may require facilitative or active transport.

## Phloem Loading and Unloading

### Loading Mechanisms

Sugars must be actively loaded into phloem, which requires energy expenditure:

- **Apoplastic loaders** (common in temperate herbaceous plants): Hydrogen ions are pumped out of companion cell membranes into the cell wall space, creating a gradient that drives sugar co-transport into the cells.
- **Symplastic loaders** (common in tropical plants and most trees): Sugars pass through plasmodesmata into companion cells, facilitated by transport proteins specific to sucrose or other sugars.

Both pathways ultimately deliver sugars to companion cells, which control the sieve tube's activities. The choice of loading pathway depends on environmental factors, plant type, and the specific sugars being transported.

### Source-Sink Dynamics

The **source-sink model** drives phloem transport direction:

- **Sources**: Photosynthetic tissues (leaves) producing sugars at high concentrations
- **Sinks**: Growing or storage tissues (roots, flowers, seeds, developing fruits) with lower sugar concentrations due to active utilization

At sources, active loading creates high solute concentrations in sieve tubes, drawing water from adjacent xylem and generating **turgor pressure** that pushes sap toward sinks. At sinks, active unloading removes sugars into receiving cells, some entering storage vacuoles via specialized transport proteins. The resulting lower concentration draws more sap flow. Water then exits the phloem and returns to the xylem via transpiration.

## Resource Partitioning

**Partitioning** is the process by which plants divide up phloem-transported resources among competing organs. This is a precisely regulated system:

- Roots grow in proportion to the number of leaves they must supply with water and nutrients. If a plant loses leaves, root growth adjusts to match the reduced demand—experienced gardeners reduce watering after defoliation for this reason.
- Flowers and seeds develop only after sufficient resources exist for the synthesis they require.
- Different phloem tubes carry different cargo: smaller leaf veins primarily transport carbohydrates, while larger feeder veins accommodate amino acids and larger molecules.

### Sugar as a Partitioning Signal

Sugar concentration itself acts as a **regulatory signal**:

1. Growing leaves photosynthesize sugar, which travels to roots (the sink), stimulating new cell growth
2. Root growth increases the root system's capacity, which supports more leaf growth
3. If roots don't demand sugar, it accumulates in leaves, photosynthesis slows or stops, and root growth ceases
4. This feedback loop maintains balanced growth across all plant organs

This elegant self-regulating system means that the plant's architecture is continuously adjusting to optimize resource distribution. No single organ grows faster than the supply chain can support, and all growth is coordinated through the phloem's bidirectional transport network.

## Seasonal Phloem Dynamics

Phloem flow direction changes with the seasons and plant needs:

- **Spring**: Upward flow from roots, delivering stored starches converted to transportable sugars to support new growth at apical meristems
- **Growing season**: Primarily downward from leaves to roots, with lateral distribution to growing fruits, seeds, and developing tissues
- **Autumn**: Strong downward flow as proteins in leaves are broken down and nitrogen is transported to stems and roots for winter storage, along with carbohydrates

Phloem longevity varies by species: some plants replace phloem annually, while palm tree phloem systems can survive for more than 50 years. When phloem dies, meristematic cells at the root apical meristem generate replacements.
