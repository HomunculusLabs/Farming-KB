# Chicken Coops, Tractors, and Breeding Guide

Updated: 2026-04-18
Source: Sheri Faires, The Ultimate Guide to [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]]
and [[natural-farming-sustainable-living-principles]]

## How Chickens Fit into the System

The goal is to feed chickens from Zone 2 and profit from
them in Zone 1, so they are located on the very edge of
Zone 1 or as close as possible to it. They provide meat,
eggs, feathers, fertilizer, [[comparison-pheromone-based-pest-control-vs-beneficial-insect-biocontrol]], and [[fukuoka-textdoc-weed-control-with-weeds-rice-barley-clover-discovery]].

The chickens live in a coop with an attached pen running
along the border of the Zone 1 gardens and the Zone 2
orchard. A second pen for chicks should be surrounded by
spiny shrubs to protect them from hawks.

The trees and ground in their pen should be well mulched
with straw, corn stalks, sawdust, yard waste, or bark,
with wire mesh around the trees holding the mulch in.

The chicken run can be planted with fruit trees, grains,
corn, sunflowers, and greens. Weeding waste from the
garden can simply be thrown over the fence into the run.

The run is divided into several pens planted in succession,
so chickens can be rotated when plants are ready. Each pen
also has a log on the ground that can be flipped over to
reveal pill bugs and worms for the birds to eat.

The fence should be at least five feet high. Chickens
cannot be let into mulched gardens or orchards because
they will destroy the mulch layer. An unmulched orchard
is acceptable, especially for bantams or small chickens.

Stocking rates should not exceed 50-100 chickens per acre
in a [[mollison-designers-mixed-orchard-design-species-selection]] or pasture. Twenty-five light-breed
hens eat about a quarter pound of food per day; heavy
breeds eat even more. Cash-crop operations may support up
to 300 chickens per acre on well-planned forage pasture.

## Chicken Tractors

Chicken tractors (or arks) are small movable coops ideal
for urban dwellers and small-scale operations. The house
typically holds 3-8 chickens, with a pen roughly twice
the size of the coop. Each chicken needs 3 square feet.

The most common design is an A-frame: a triangular house
with a fully enclosed triangular pen covered in chicken
wire. The rectangular pen design is another common option,
with the house taking up one-third of total space.

Wheels make moving easier, but must be set very low to
prevent predator access through bottom gaps. An extra
wood barrier around the base is removed during moves.

Larger designs feature full coops on wheels with removable
ramps, rolled out into pasture. Chickens must be shut in
at night since there is no attached enclosure.

## Coop Design Principles

The coop must provide at least 3 square feet per chicken.
A manageable size is 7 x 7 feet (about 49 square feet),
sufficient for 16 birds. A permaculture coop sits on
stilts so manure drops through slatted floors into a
swale or wheelbarrow below.

Roosts are long poles or boards at least 18 inches from
the wall. Nesting boxes are 12 x 12 inches minimum, about
18 inches from the ground, with one box per two hens.
Boxes can be attached to the outside wall with external
access lids for easy egg collection.

The chicken door should be 12 inches high, incorporated
into the peak of the roof for ventilation and predator
deterrence. A 5-foot-wide ladder with 3-foot rung spacing
is difficult for foxes but easy for chickens to traverse.

In cold climates the coop needs extra insulation and the
ability to be closed at night. A thick layer of hay as
bedding may compensate for gaps in slatted floors.

## Predator Protection Strategies

Predators are the single greatest threat to backyard
flocks. Common predators include foxes, raccoons, hawks,
owls, weasels, minks, skunks, opossums, coyotes, and
domestic dogs and cats.

Hardware cloth (half-inch mesh) is far superior to
chicken wire for predator exclusion. Bury wire 12 inches
deep around the coop perimeter to prevent digging. A
skirt of hardware cloth extending outward along the
ground surface further deters tunneling predators.

Locking mechanisms should be raccoon-proof. These clever
animals can open simple latches and slide bolts. Use
carabiner clips or padlocks on all access points.

Motion-sensor lights and solar-powered predator deterrents
can provide additional protection, especially during peak
predation hours at dawn and dusk. Guard animals such as
livestock guardian dogs or geese can also reduce losses.

## Seasonal Considerations

Summer management focuses on ventilation and heat stress
prevention. Provide shade, ensure water is cool and
abundant, and consider adding frozen treats. Coops with
good airflow prevent respiratory issues in hot weather.

Winter management requires windbreaks, reduced ventilation
(to balance moisture control with warmth), and frost-proof
waterers. A thick layer of the deep litter method generates
composting heat that helps insulate the flock.

Molting typically occurs in autumn as daylight decreases.
Hens stop laying while regrowing feathers, which takes
6-8 weeks. Supplement lighting (14-16 hours) can maintain
egg production through winter if desired.

## Forage Plants and Pasture

Pasture and pens should include trees and shrubs such as
pigeon pea, berries, fruits, acacias, clovers, grasses,
chicory, comfrey, and dandelions. Insects can be boosted
with manure piles or mulches.
