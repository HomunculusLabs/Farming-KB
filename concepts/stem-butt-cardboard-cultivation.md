## Stem Butt and Cardboard Cultivation

Low-tech methods for expanding mycelium without laboratory equipment. From [[paul-stamets]]' *Mycelium Running* (2005). See also [[inoculation-methods]] and [[mycelium-running-techniques]].

## Stem Butt Revolution

### Concept

The stem butt method uses the bottom portion of harvested mushrooms to generate new mycelium. It is the simplest and most accessible form of mushroom propagation, requiring no sterile equipment.

### Method

1. **Harvest**: Collect mature mushrooms from wild patches or cultivated beds
2. **Cut**: Remove the bottom 1-2 inches of the stem (the "butt")
3. **Plant**: Bury stem butts in moist substrate (wood chips, straw, cardboard)
4. **Incubate**: Keep moist and shaded for several weeks to months
5. **Expand**: Once colonized, transplant to larger beds or make bunker spawn

### Advantages
- No laboratory equipment needed
- Free — uses waste from foraging or cultivation
- Wild mycelium is already acclimated to local conditions
- Maintains genetic diversity of local strains

### Disadvantages
- Slower than commercial spawn (typically 1 year longer)
- Not all strains survive the transfer
- Trial-and-error required to perfect technique
- Lower success rate than [[mushroom-spawn-making-and-pure-culture-methods]]

## Cardboard Spawn Method

### Why Cardboard Works

Corrugated cardboard is an excellent substrate for mycelial expansion:
- Sweet wood-based glues in cardboard boost mycelial growth
- Contaminating green molds (Trichoderma species) do NOT grow as well on cardboard as many gourmet and [[anti-cancer-polysaccharides-from-medicinal-mushrooms]]
- Corrugated ridges and valleys provide favorable microenvironments
- Readily available, free, and biodegradable
- Cardboard from US, Canada, and Europe tends to be free of dioxins

### Step-by-Step Method

1. **Prepare cardboard**: Tear apart corrugated cardboard boxes, exposing the corrugated ridges
2. **Soak**: Submerge torn panels in hot water for 1 hour to soften fibers
3. **Contact**: Place exposed-corrugation side against the mycelium (wild patch, stem butts, or commercial spawn)
4. **Cover**: Cover with newspaper or cardboard to prevent contamination from airborne spores
5. **Incubate**: Store in cool, dark place in a container with a lid (snap-on plastic preferred)
6. **Monitor**: Check weekly — cardboard should remain moist; small water pool on bottom maintains humidity
7. **Expand**: After 1 week, when colonies are visible, transfer to a sheet 4x larger
8. **Repeat**: Every 2-3 months, transfer to new cardboard until enough spawn for a mushroom patch

### Mycelial Faces

When the underside of cardboard is fully colonized (mycelium running through corrugations):

1. **Mycelial face method**: Place colonized cardboard mycelial-face-down onto fresh wood chips — the face becomes a "footprint"
2. **Bunker spawn**: Sandwich myceliated cardboard between 2 burlap sacks filled with wood chips
3. **Cardboard tower**: Alternate colonized and uncolonized cardboard panels to build a tower of expanding mycelium

### Surfing the Mycelial Wave

A critical timing concept from Stamets:
- Mycelium has a growth cycle — it colonizes, peaks, then declines
- Transfer mycelium at the "crest of its growth" for best results
- Among saprophytes, mycelial waves may continue for 4 months to 2 years before consuming cardboard and dying back
- Over-incubation = mycelium dies back

## Transplanting Wild Mycelium

### Finding Mycelium

Practically any fallen tree or piece of wood on the ground for a few months will host mycelium. Look for:
- Areas where wood chips are held together in clumps (mycelium binds them)
- Downed logs with white fungal fans on the underside
- Piles of aging firewood
- Leaf and wood chip piles

### Testing Mycelium Vigor

- Healthy mycelium grips wood chips so firmly that strength is required to tear them apart
- Tenacity reflects mycelial mat strength and vigor
- Rhizomorph test: whether rhizomorphs can suspend chips in the air

### Transplantation Steps

1. **Collect**: Scoop up mycelium-rich material (called "virgin spawn")
2. **Store**: Place in cardboard box or paper sack — fungus-friendly containers
3. **Moisture**: Apply water every 2-4 weeks
4. **Timing**: Best transplantation window is March to June
5. **Plant**: Place onto moist substrate in prepared bed
6. **Expansion ratio**: 1:20 works best (1 gallon mycelium → 20 gallons substrate)

### Identification Without Mushrooms

Mycelium "breathes" — emitting species-specific fragrances:
- **[[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]** (oyster): Mildly anise-like
- **Clitocybe odora**: Anise-rich
- **[[tricholoma-matsutake]]**: Pine-like
- **Stropharia rugoso-annulata**: Rich, uniquely sweet fragrance
- **Lepista nuda** (blewit): Brilliant purplish mycelium

## Spore Germination Methods

### Sugar and Salt Broth

1. Add 1/2 teaspoon noniodized salt + 1 tablespoon sugar/molasses to 1 gallon water
2. Boil 10 minutes, cool
3. Add 1 teaspoon (1g, ~500 million to 1 billion) spores
4. Incubate 24-48 hours at 50-80°F, shaking twice daily
5. Use the resulting spore-mass slurry to inoculate substrates

### Spores in Oil (Chainsaw Inoculation)

- Immerse spores in canola, corn, or safflower oil
- Use as chainsaw lubricant — distributes spores to fresh cut surfaces
- Optimal: 100 million spores per liter of oil
- Oils help spores adhere and resist washing away

### Cold Incubation on Straw

1. Immerse 1g spores in 1 liter of water
2. Spread over 10 lbs of moist, untreated wheat straw
3. Place in perforated bag or container
4. Incubate outdoors at 35-50°F
5. In [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]: January inoculation → March fruiting (for oyster mushrooms)

## Expansion Mathematics

The exponential power of mycelial expansion:
- **Inoculation ratio**: 1:20 (1 part mycelium : 20 parts substrate)
- **Annual cycle**: Each year, descendant patches can be amplified at similar ratios
- **3-year projection**: 4x4-foot patch → 128,000+ square feet (3+ acres)
- **Key**: Mycelial islands seek to join with other islands to form contiguous mats
- When spawn is broken up, hyphae fork and refork, extending their range if food is available
