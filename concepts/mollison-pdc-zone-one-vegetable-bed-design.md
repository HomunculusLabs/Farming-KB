# Mollison Zone One Vegetable Bed Design and Spot Rotation

## Overview

In Pamphlet IX of the [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]] Course, [[bill-mollison]] describes
a detailed system for designing and managing zone one [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]].
His approach differs significantly from conventional row-cropping
methods, emphasizing permanent bed systems, spot rotation within beds,
and strategic [[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]] that eliminates the need for traditional
block [[coleman-cover-crop-rotation-and-soil-health]]. The design aims to create a garden that produces
abundantly year-round while requiring minimal maintenance once
established.

## The Vegetable Bed Structure

Mollison recommends designing zone one gardens as a series of permanent
beds that are never dug or turned after initial establishment. Beds
should be narrow enough to reach the center from either side. Pathways
are permanent and mulched. The layout places frequently harvested crops
nearest the house entrance, with progressively less intensively managed
crops further away. The bed system creates a three-dimensional growing
space with tall crops as canopy, medium crops in the understory, and low
crops at ground level.

## The Spot Rotation Method

Mollison's most distinctive contribution to garden management is his
spot rotation system, which he contrasts sharply with traditional
block rotation. In conventional agriculture, entire beds or fields are
rotated: brassicas this year, legumes next year, roots the following
year. Mollison argues this is unnecessarily destructive and wasteful.

Instead, he advocates for a grid-based spot rotation where individual
plants are rotated within the same bed. The gardener divides each bed
into a grid of small planting positions. When a crop finishes in one
position, a different crop family is planted there next season. Adjacent
positions can hold crops from the same or different families. The result
is that every bed contains a diverse mix of crop families at all times,
making whole-bed rotation unnecessary.

Mollison notes that you rarely see a tomato planted twice in the same
spot within a three-year cycle. But the key insight is that the bed
itself is never bare or empty. As one plant finishes, another goes in.
This continuous cropping is fundamentally different from the conventional
approach of clearing and replanting entire areas.

## Advantages of Spot Rotation

The spot rotation approach offers several advantages. It eliminates bare
soil periods vulnerable to erosion, weed invasion, and nutrient loss.
It maintains continuous root networks supporting [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]]. It
makes [[query-what-is-biofumigation-and-how-does-it-work]] easier because scattered host plants prevent pests
from building up the populations they achieve in block plantings.
Mollison also notes that spot rotation allows more intensive space use,
as every position can be productive in every season, unlike block
rotation where some fraction is always in fallow.

## Companion Planting Within Beds

The spot rotation system naturally integrates companion planting. Since
different crop families are interspersed throughout each bed, the
opportunity for beneficial plant interactions increases. Mollison
describes several classic companion combinations:

- Tomatoes interplanted with basil and marigolds, which deter nematodes
  and whitefly
- Carrots and onions planted in adjacent spots, as each repels the
  other's primary pests
- Beans climbing corn stalks, fixing nitrogen for the heavy-feeding
  maize
- Nasturtiums at bed edges to trap aphids away from vulnerable crops
