### Impalpable Yields

These are the invisible yields related to health and nutrition, security, and a satisfactory social context and lifestyle. Not surprisingly, it is the search for these invisible yields that most often drives people to seek good design or to take up life on the land. These yields are understood in terms of values and ethics, which govern the concept of needs and set the limits of "enough." An ethical basis is a vital component of yield.

## The Role of Life in Yield

Living things, including people, are the only effective intervening systems to capture resources on this planet and to produce a yield. The sum and capacity of life forms decide total system yield and surplus. Only life and flow can yield continually, or as long as they persist. The energy stocks of any system are the flows and lives within it.

While flow may exist without life (as on the moon, where only technology can intervene to obtain a yield), on earth life is the intervening strategy for capturing flow and producing yield. Technology depends on the continuation of life, not the opposite. This means that biological systems — forests, soils, wetlands, grasslands — are the primary yield-generating mechanisms, and all technological systems are ultimately dependent on their continued function.

## Yield Is Not a Fixed Quantity

A critical insight of permaculture is that **yield is not a fixed sum in any design system**. It is the measure of the comprehension, understanding, and ability of the designers and managers of that design. Defined in this way, yield has no known limits, as designers cannot know all ways to conserve, store, and save energy, nor can they fail to improve any system they build and observe.

There is always room for another plant, another cycle, another route, another arrangement, another technique or structure. Designers can thus continually shrink the area needed to survive. The critical yield strategy is in governing appetites and expectations.

Some biologists define yield in narrower terms, accepting that a forest, lake, or crop has a finite upper limit of surplus due to substrate conditions and available energy. Permaculture does not accept this passive approach, which is inapplicable to active and conscious design or active management using fertilizers, windbreaks, or selected species. Even more narrowly defined is the yield of agricultural economists, who regard a single product per unit area as the yield — Mollison suggests that this narrow approach itself may be the true limit to yield.

A true accounting of yield takes into consideration both upstream costs (energy) and downstream costs (health). The "product yield" may create problems of pollution and soil mineral loss, and cost more than it can replace.

## Yield Reduction by Cultural Impediments

Just as yield can be increased, so it can be decreased. The perverse aims of some politicians, developers, and even religious dogmatists limit yield by disallowing certain products. Just as neighbors may refuse the snail and eat the lettuce, refuse the blackbird and eat the strawberry, societies may only "allow" certain types of toilets, or certain plants in gardens or parks. People are thus the main impediment to using their potential yields.

## Strategies That Create Yield

Permaculture employs abundant strategies across several broad categories to create yields instead of incurring costly inputs:

### Foundational Strategies

- **Creation of a niche in space** and provision of critical resources
- **Rehabilitation and creation of soils** for long-term productivity
- **Diversion of water** and water recycling to capture and reuse this essential resource
- **Integration of structures and landscape** for mutual benefit
- **Selection of low-maintenance cultivars and species** suited to a particular site
- **Investigation of other species** for usable yields beyond conventional selections
- **Supplying key nutrients** and biological waste recycling (mulch, manure)
- **Assembly of beneficial and cooperative guilds** of plants and animals
