# Light Wavelength Effects on [[psilocybe-cubensis]]
# Basidiocarp Initiation

The Badham (1980) study in *Mycologia* (Vol. 72, No. 1, pp. 136-142)
established the first formal action spectrum for basidiocarp initiation
in [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]]. This page
examines the detailed spectral data, dose-response mechanics,
cross-species comparisons from the literature reviewed in that paper,
and the photoreceptor implications of the findings.

## Action Spectrum: Wavelength-Specific Photoinduction

The spectral sensitivity experiment used a 300-watt General Electric
quartz-iodine projector lamp coupled with a 0.25-m Jarrell-Ash
monochromator set to 2-nm slits (15-nm bandwidth). Light energy was
calibrated to 23 ergs/sec/cm^2 at all wavelengths using an
International Light #783 spectroradiometer. Approximately 30 cultures
per treatment were illuminated at each wavelength from 370 to 510 nm,
receiving 30 seconds of monochromatic light per day for 5 days.

The resulting action spectrum revealed two regions of peak sensitivity:

| Wavelength | Region     | Response Level |
|-----------|------------|----------------|
| 370 nm    | UV-A       | High           |
| 440 nm    | Blue       | High           |
| 460 nm    | Blue       | High           |
| >510 nm   | Green/Red  | None           |

Wavelengths of 370, 440, and 460 nm were the most effective for
triggering fruitbody initiation. No photoinduction occurred above
510 nm. This bimodal UV-blue sensitivity is characteristic of fungal
photoresponses broadly (Tan, 1978).

## Bunsen-Roscoe Reciprocity and Dose-Response

A critical prerequisite for determining a valid action spectrum is
establishing that the dose-response obeys the Bunsen-Roscoe law of
reciprocity: the biological effect depends on total energy dose
(intensity x time) rather than on intensity or duration independently.
Without confirming reciprocity, action spectra measured at saturating
doses would falsely show all wavelengths as equally active.

Using monochromatic light at 460 nm, Badham tested eight dosages (10
cultures per treatment). The response was approximately linear between
0.086 x 10^4 and 0.345 x 10^4 ergs/cm^2. Saturation occurred at
0.345 x 10^4 ergs/cm^2 (23 ergs/sec/cm^2 per day for 5 days). All
subsequent spectral experiments were conducted within this linear range.
Doses above saturation used increased illumination time; doses below
used neutral-density filters (Kodak).

## Cross-Species Comparison of Action Spectra

Badham's paper reviewed published action spectra for three other
basidiomycetes, revealing both similarities and discrepancies:

### Favolus arcularis (Kitamoto et al., 1970)

Kitamoto et al. found a half-maximal initiation response at 1.8 x 10^3
ergs/cm^2 at 398 nm. Photoinduction occurred between 350 and 560 nm
with six distinct peaks -- a considerably broader active range than
the 370-510 nm window observed in P. cubensis.

### Coprinus domesticus (Chapman and Fergus, 1973)

Blue-end spectrum light at intensities above 1.5 x 10^4 ergs/cm^2/sec
induced mature basidiocarp formation. Green, red, and far-red
wavelengths failed to induce initials, consistent with P. cubensis.

### [[schizophyllum-commune]] (Perkins and Gordon, 1969)

Dose-response was linear up to approximately 1.1 x 10^5 ergs/cm^2 at
440 nm, with spectral sensitivity peaking in blue and UV. No light
above 525 nm was photoinductive. This species requires roughly 30x
more energy to reach saturation than P. cubensis.

### Agreement Points

All four species share strong UV-blue activity and green/red
ineffectiveness. Strong activity at 370 nm was reported in both the
Favolus and Schizophyllum studies, matching P. cubensis.

### Key Discrepancies

Kitamoto (1972) reported peaks at 400 and 520 nm and a notable lack
of activity at 460 nm in Favolus arcularis -- results Badham called
"very unlike" his own, where 460 nm was highly effective. Perkins and
Gordon (1969) found peaks at 420 and 480 nm in S. commune, broadly
consistent but shifted relative to the 440 and 460 nm peaks in P.
cubensis. These discrepancies may reflect genuine species-level
variation in photoreceptor spectral tuning.

## Photoreceptor Implications

The consistent UV-blue sensitivity across basidiomycetes points to a
conserved photoreceptor mechanism. Badham referenced Briggs (1976), who
suggested that blue-light reactions in plants and fungi are
fundamentally similar, likely mediated by flavin-based photoreceptors.
The LOV (Light-Oxygen-Voltage) domain containing flavin chromophores
is now recognized as the molecular basis for these responses.

Badham raised an important caveat about using red light as "safe"
light for culture manipulation. Chon and Briggs (1966) demonstrated
interactions between blue-light and red-light (phytochrome) responses
in plants. While red light did not directly initiate fruitbodies in
P. cubensis, it may modulate blue-light sensitivity. Assuming red
light is truly "safe" may be incorrect.

## Unresolved Questions

1. The action spectrum for basidiocarp *development* after initiation
   is unknown and may differ from initiation requirements.
2. The phototropic blue pigment zone in low-light primordia coincides
   with Plunkett's (1961) phototropic zone. Singer (1958) linked this
   pigment to hallucinogenic indoles, but the functional relationship
   between indole alkaloids and photomorphogenesis remains untested.
3. The molecular photoreceptor was unknown in 1980. White Collar-1
   (WC-1) LOV-domain proteins from other fungi provide candidates,
   but direct confirmation in P. cubensis is still needed.

## Experimental Limitations

All experiments were conducted at 21 degrees C with 85% relative
humidity. The scoring system combined a size/development index (0-4)
with a density index (1-4) into a composite "degree of initiation"
score. Second-order spectra from the monochromator were eliminated
during calibration but not during culture illumination, a minor
methodological limitation. Most cultures were scored on day 6.
