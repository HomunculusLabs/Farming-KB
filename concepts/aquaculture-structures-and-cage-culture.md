## Aquaculture Structures and Cage Culture

The physical structures used within and around aquaculture systems—breeding substrates, rafts, screens, cages, inlets, and outlets—are as important to productivity as the ponds themselves. These "furniture" elements create specialized habitats, enable species management, and dramatically influence the efficiency of both extensive and intensive fish-rearing operations.

## Breeding Substrates

Different fish species require specific substrates for egg deposition. By providing a variety of breeding structures, pond managers can support diverse species and increase overall reproduction rates. Key substrate types include:

- **Grassy slopes** that can be flooded for carp spawning
- **Thatched shelters** for cave-breeding species
- **Logs and drums** for large perch and Murray River cod
- **Gravels, sands, and rock piles** for species that spawn on hard substrates
- **Mud caves** for species requiring sheltered breeding sites
- **Floating weed and bundles of reeds or twigs** for species that attach eggs to vegetation

Many cannibalistic crayfish and territorial fish species defend such breeding homes, and their population density is directly limited by the availability of these refuges. Tyre heaps or piles of broken pipe effectively serve as "condominiums" for territorial species, dramatically increasing the [[fukuoka-textdoc-land-requirements-per-diet-type-carrying-capacity]] of a given water area.

For small species such as shrimp, snails, notonectids, and small fish, **bundles of brush** perform a dual function: breeding substrate and refuge from larger predators. All such refuges can be designed to be hauled out, operating as traps—octopus, eels, and crayfish remain in their holes or tyres, while shrimp and freshwater crab cling to brush piles. They can also be used to collect eggs and fry systematically.

## Rafts

Rafts are among the most versatile structures in aquaculture, serving multiple simultaneous functions:

- **Floating docks** in tidal waters for access to fish cages and ring-nets
- **Supports for houseboats or pumps**
- **Walkways** to reach cage systems
- **Surface floats** for organisms cultured on ropes, brush, and in mesh bags
- **Observation platforms** for monitoring species interactions
- **Depth control** for aquatic plants requiring specific water levels
- **Invertebrate culture platforms** for breeding, attracting, or supporting food organisms
- **Solar panel arrays** that can be steered to follow the sun

With fluctuating surface levels from tides or dam draw-down, only raft arrangements can maintain the steady water level needed by certain plants and nesting birds. Cultures on rafts range from light-and-lure traps for insect foods to insect incubators of [[biodiversity-of-fungi-leaf-litter-microfungi-survey-methods]] or animal wastes. Multiple uses are frequently feasible on a single raft.

Rudolf Doernach, a German architect, built **raft houses in cistern ponds** that could literally follow the sun. Heavy arrays of solar cells and solar collectors are most economically oriented to the sun on raft structures. Rafts also hold self-feeders for fish and waterfowl.

## Screens and Fences

Shallow-water fences and screens are essential tools in polycultural stocking systems:

- **Predator exclusion**: Keeping predatory species away from forage fish cultures
- **Population management**: Allowing stunted fish to be culled by predators through selective openings
- **Migration control**: Outlet and inlet screens prevent or regulate species movement
- **Species separation**: Enabling brood fish and immatures to coexist in the same pond, or two antagonistic species to be reared together

### Screen Types

Screens can be configured in several geometries, each with advantages for self-cleaning:
- **Horizontal screens** for skimming surface debris
- **Sloping screens** that deflect solid particles
- **Vertical screens** for standard flow-through applications
- **Cylindrical and cone screens** for omnidirectional flow

**Rotating drum screens** can be made entirely self-cleaning and self-turning where a small head of water is available. They are particularly useful for skimming ponds or collecting floating plants and algae for use as manures or forages.

Fences are a cost-effective way to screen shallow areas or separate zones between deeps. Screen fences beside deeps in marshes prevent fish from escaping into shallow waters, or permit frogs to breed in shallows without excessive predation from trout.

## Outlets and Inlets

### Water Level Controls

Modern production of reliable flexible pipe has made water level control simple, using either elbow fittings or upturned flexible pipes. These systems allow precise management of [[pond-depth-and-series-arrangements]], which is critical for:

- Managing spawning cycles through controlled flooding
- Harvesting fish through draw-down
- Maintaining appropriate depth for different species and seasons

### Harvest Systems

Outlets can double as harvest infrastructure:
- **Basket outlets** into which water falls, collecting fish migrating downstream
- **Smaller ponds with screened spillways** that gather fish from complex swamp systems that are difficult to harvest by nets

Both systems are used extensively for gathering eels or trout.

### Inlet Design

Inlets require careful design to prevent the entry of silt, weeds, or unwanted organisms. Inlet systems can range from very complex arrangements of filters and screens (where water quality is poor) to simple pipes (where unpolluted, fish-free water is available).

## Cage Culture

Cages of wood, woven [[building-with-natural-materials]], metal mesh, and modern synthetic meshes have been used since antiquity for trapping, harvesting, and rearing fish. They are in widespread current use for intensive fish rearing in both still and flowing waters.

### Cage Applications

- **Egg and fry protection**: Species such as sturgeon are hatched in cages with flow-through water
- **Live holding**: Fish, crayfish, prawns, molluscs, and eels held in cages, corfs (floating barges), or wet wells for the fresh fish market
- **Algal taint reduction**: Holding eels, oysters, crayfish, carp, and weed or algae eaters to reduce off-flavours in fish flesh
- **Intensive rearing**: Fed fish in floating cages produce some of the highest known yields in aquaculture

### Cage Design Considerations

Where flow is rapid, cage mesh is large, or wave motion exists, oxygenation is not a significant problem. However, cage shape matters in tidal flows—shaping cages to induce water circulation within the cage significantly improves conditions. Normally, cages are circular (as in salmon ring-net culture) or plain square (to suit slatted wood construction).

Typical rearing cages where fish are fed rely on the **swimming action of the fish themselves** to maintain water flow. Such cages produce the largest yields known to aquaculture.

### Mooring Systems

Cages can be moored by:
- Individual anchors
- Sets of cages attached to floating docks or walkways
- Gangs of cages fastened to lines, stretched across bays or anchored to float-lines in open water (allowing boat-width between pairs)

Old tyres serve well as spacers between cage units. All cages require **annual inspection and watchful maintenance**—predators such as octopus and seals can cause large mortalities in caged fish, whereas pond fish can evade such threats.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Provide diverse breeding substrates** matched to target species to maximize reproduction
2. **Use rafts for multi-purpose platforms**—solar, housing, observation, and culture simultaneously
3. **Design screens for self-cleaning** where possible to reduce maintenance burden
4. **Configure outlets as harvest systems** to simplify fish collection from complex water bodies
5. **Protect cage investments** with regular inspection and predator exclusion measures
6. **Choose cage shape based on water flow**—circular for active current, square for still water with fish-induced flow
