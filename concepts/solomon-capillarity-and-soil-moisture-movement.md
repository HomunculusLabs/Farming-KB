    10|# Capillarity and Soil Moisture Movement
    11|
    12|Steve Solomon devotes considerable attention in *Gardening When It Counts* to the
    13|mechanics of how water moves through soil, arguing that most gardeners fail to
    14|understand these fundamental physical processes. The result is overwatering,
    15|improper bed preparation, and crops that underperform because their root zones
    16|are either waterlogged or parched despite frequent irrigation.
    17|
    18|## The Physics of Capillary Rise
    19|
    20|Capillarity is the force that causes water to rise in narrow tubes against the
    21|pull of gravity. In soil, this phenomenon operates continuously through the
    22|microscopic pores between soil particles. The smaller the pore spaces, the
    23|higher water can climb through capillary action.
    24|
    25|Fine-textured soils like clay have very small pore spaces, which means water
    26|can rise considerable distances through capillary forces alone, sometimes
    27|measuring many feet. However, the rate of movement is extremely slow. Clay soil
    28|might wick moisture upward only inches per day, even though the total
    29|theoretical rise could be substantial.
    30|
    31|Sandy soils, by contrast, have large pore spaces that limit capillary rise to
    32|only a few inches. But water moves through those large pores very quickly when
    33|gravity pulls it downward. This is why sandy soils drain rapidly but cannot hold
    34|moisture near the surface for long.
    35|
    36|Loam soils represent a middle ground, with a mixture of particle sizes creating
    37|a range of pore sizes. This gives them moderate capillary rise at moderate
    38|speeds, which is one reason loams are considered ideal for most crops. The
    39|balanced pore structure allows both good drainage and adequate moisture
    40|retention.
    41|
    42|## The Capillary Fringe and Root Zone Dynamics
    43|
    44|Below the water table, all soil pores are filled with water. Above the water
    45|table lies the capillary fringe, a zone where water has been drawn upward by
    46|capillary action from the saturated zone below. The height of this fringe
    47|depends on soil texture: it may extend several feet in clay but only a few
    48|inches in coarse sand.
    49|
    50|Plant roots draw moisture primarily from the capillary fringe and the moist
    51|soil above it. When a gardener waters, the water percolates downward through
    52|gravity until it reaches the existing moisture level, then spreads laterally
    53|through capillary action.
    54|
    55|Solomon emphasizes that frequent shallow watering encourages roots to stay
    56|near the surface where they are vulnerable to drying out. Deep, infrequent
    57|watering is superior because it pushes moisture down to where roots will follow,
    58|developing a deep root system that can access the more stable moisture supplies
    59|in the capillary fringe.
    60|
    61|## Mulch and the Disruption of Capillary Flow
    62|
    63|Solomon is notably critical of heavy mulching practices, arguing that thick
    64|organic mulches can interfere with natural capillary water movement. When soil
    65|is covered with a deep layer of mulch, the capillary connection between the
    66|soil surface and the atmosphere is broken.
    67|
    68|In bare soil, moisture continuously moves upward through capillary action and
    69|evaporates at the surface. This evaporation actually creates a pull that helps
    70|draw more moisture up from below. When mulch covers the surface, this
    71|evaporative pull is eliminated, and the upward movement of water slows or stops.
    72|
    73|While mulch does reduce surface evaporation, Solomon argues that the net effect
    74|on the root zone can be negative if the capillary supply from below is also
    75|reduced. The soil beneath deep mulch can become unexpectedly dry, creating a
    76|false sense of security for the gardener who believes the mulch is conserving
    77|moisture.
    78|
    79|He notes that thin mulches used primarily for weed suppression are less
    80|problematic than thick blankets of organic matter. The key is maintaining enough
    81|capillary connection between the soil and the atmosphere to allow the natural
    82|upward flow of moisture to continue.
    83|
    84|## Soil Structure and Moisture Availability
    85|
    86|The availability of soil moisture to plants depends not just on how much water
    87|is present but on how tightly it is held. Water in large pores is loosely held
    88|and easily extracted by roots, while water in very small pores is held so
    89|tightly that plants cannot pull it free.
    90|
    91|This distinction is measured as field capacity versus wilting point. Field
    92|capacity is the amount of water remaining in soil after gravity has drained
    93|away the excess, typically one to two days after a soaking rain or irrigation.
    94|The wilting point is the moisture level at which plants can no longer extract
    95|water and permanently wilt.
    96|
    97|The difference between field capacity and wilting point is the plant-available
    98|water, and this varies enormously by soil type. Sandy soils hold relatively
    99|little plant-available water despite draining quickly. Clay soils hold large
   100|quantities of water but much of it is held too tightly for plants to use.
   101|
   102|Ideal garden soil maximizes plant-available water by having a granular
   103|structure with a range of pore sizes. Solomon advocates for generous compost
   104|incorporation and minimal tillage to build and maintain this crumb structure,
   105|which creates both drainage channels and water-holding micro-pores.
   106|
   107|## Practical Implications for Gardeners
   108|
   109|Understanding capillarity leads directly to several practical conclusions that
   110|Solomon stresses throughout his book.
   111|
   112|First, raised beds may dry out faster than flat ground because their elevated
   113|position puts them above the capillary fringe, cutting off the natural upward
   114|supply of moisture. Raised beds in sandy soil are particularly vulnerable.
   115|
   116|Second, watering should penetrate deep into the root zone rather than merely
   117|wetting the surface. A single deep watering that pushes moisture six to twelve
   118|inches down is far more beneficial than several shallow waterings that only wet
   119|the top inch.
   120|
   121|Third, soil surface management matters. A soil surface that is slightly rough
   122|and open allows better capillary connection than a surface that is sealed smooth
   123|by rain impact or compaction.
   124|
   125|Fourth, plant spacing should account for the moisture availability in the soil.
   126|Plants grown wider apart in dry conditions can develop larger root systems that
   127|access more soil volume, making them more resilient during dry periods.
   128|
   129|Fifth, compost improves both the water-holding capacity and the structure of
   130|soil, but it should be incorporated into the soil rather than simply layered on
   131|top, to maximize its effect on the soil's capillary properties throughout the
   132|root zone.
   133|
   134|## Solomon's Departure from Organic Orthodoxy
   135|
   136|Solomon's views on soil moisture place him at odds with much conventional
   137|organic gardening wisdom. He challenges the standard recommendation of permanent
   138|mulching, arguing instead that gardeners should understand and work with the
   139|natural physics of water movement rather than trying to override them with
   140|organic matter.
