## Endophytic Fungi — Bark, Root, Xylotropic, and Specialized Host Endophytes

[[endophytic-fungi]] colonize living plant tissues asymptomatically, representing one of the most diverse and poorly understood components of [[fungal-biodiversity]]. Beyond the well-studied foliar endophytes of grasses and herbaceous plants, woody perennials harbor rich communities of endophytes in bark, xylem, roots, and specialized host tissues including lichens, mosses, liverworts, and pteridophytes. This page catalogs these communities by tissue type and host group, drawing primarily from comprehensive surveys of woody perennials and associated non-vascular plants.

The endophytic lifestyle represents a fundamentally important ecological strategy
among fungi, encompassing species that range from latent pathogens to mutualists.
Understanding the full diversity of endophytes across different host tissues and
plant groups remains one of the major challenges in fungal biology.

## Woody Perennial Endophytes

### Foliage Endophytes (Table 12.5)

- **Diatrypales:** Libertella, Diatrypella, Diatrype, Eutypa

- **Xylariales:** Coniochaeta, Hypoxylon, Biscogniauxia, Camillea, Geniculosporium, Nodulisporium, Virgariella, Periconiella, Xylaria
- **Sordariales:** Chaetomium, Sordaria, Gelasinospora

### Notable Taxonomic Patterns

Several groups are repeatedly isolated from woody perennials:

- **Leotiales:** Tiarosporella and Ceuthospora (Phacideaceae); Pezicula, Dermea, and Mollisia (Dermateaceae); Chloroscypha (Leotiaceae) are common and frequently isolated.
- **Dothidiales:** Phyllosticta (teleomorph: Guignardia) and Hormonema (associated with Dothiora and Pringsheimia) are widespread. Phomopsis and various Hypocreales are essentially ubiquitous.
- **Xylariaceae** are among the most important endophytic groups: ubiquitous, exceptionally speciose, and especially diverse in the tropics (Whalley 1993; Petrini et al. 1995), though temperate regions also harbor significant diversity. Anamorphic Xylariaceae were the predominant endophytes in two tropical host studies (Rodrigues 1994; Lodge et al. 1996a). Anamorphic states are usually encountered in culture and are differentiated relatively easily, with common temperate species identifiable by morphology. Teleomorphs appear more restricted in distribution; their apparently host-specific occurrence may be misleading, as broad anamorphic recovery from diverse substrata suggests wider ecological amplitude (Petrini et al. 1995; Rogers 2000). Members of this family are notable producers of bioactive metabolites, though their ecosystem-level significance remains largely unelucidated. The Xylariaceae thus exemplify a recurring theme in endophyte biology: the disconnect between the restricted ecological appearance of sexual (teleomorph) states and the broad ecological distribution of asexual (anamorph) counterparts.

## Terrestrial Aquatic Hyphomycetes

A fascinating category of endophytes comprises the so-called "terrestrial aquatic
fungi." **Ingoldian hyphomycetes** produce tetraradiate, sigmoid, or appendaged
conidia specialized for aquatic dispersal and are typically associated with
senescent or decaying leaf litter in and near streams (Webster 1981). However,
they have also been recovered from rainfall beneath forest canopies far from
streams (G. C. Carroll), from building roofs (Czeczuga and Orlowska 1997), and
from rain-washed tree trunks (Bandoni 1981). These unexpected terrestrial
recoveries challenge the traditional view of Ingoldian fungi as strictly
aquatic organisms.

Bandoni (1981) proposed that some [[aquatic-hyphomycetes]] may function as
endophyte-like early leaf colonists or even parasites on land plants. Ando
(1992) documented "terrestrial aquatic fungi" from living leaves, finding
staurosporous conidia produced from minute endophytic conidiophores that
discharge into fog, dew, or rain droplets. The distinctive tetraradiate
conidial shape was originally interpreted by Webster and Descals (1981) as an
adaptation for stable attachment in flowing currents; Ando (1992)
alternatively proposed it as a water retention adaptation.

**Collection method:** Water droplets are collected from leaf and stem surfaces in
plastic bags, centrifuged gently, then either fixed for microscopy or spread on
isolation media. This simple technique has proven remarkably effective for
recovering these inconspicuous endophytes from living plant surfaces.

Key genera include *Geminoarcus*, *Kodonospora*, *Tetraspermum*, *Trifurcospora*, and *Trinacrium* (Ando and Tubaki 1984a,b; Ando et al. 1987; Ando 1993a,b). These terrestrial aquatic hyphomycetes blur the ecological boundary between aquatic and terrestrial fungal communities, suggesting many Ingoldian fungi may have originated as endophytes of living plant surfaces.

## Lichens as Substrata for Endophytes

Cryptic endophytic microfungi have been isolated at high frequencies from lichen thalli (Petrini et al. 1990; Girlanda et al. 1997). In one survey, 17 fruticose lichen samples yielded 506 fungal taxa, with the majority (306 taxa) isolated only once (Petrini et al. 1990), illustrating extraordinary microfungal diversity. When two lichen species from a common site were compared, differences in assemblage composition were found despite similar overall biodiversity levels (Girlanda et al. 1997). Most isolates were not [[lichenicolous-fungi]] but rather genera known from other substrata. The exceptionally high diversity is attributed to the highly porous and heterogeneous physical structure of lichen thalli, which provide numerous micro-niches for fungal colonization. This raises important questions about whether lichen-associated fungi represent a specialized community or a subset of the general environmental fungal spore rain.

## Mosses, Hepatics, Liverworts, and Pteridophytes

Bryophyte and pteridophyte endophyte associations remain intriguing but incompletely studied. Comprehensive surveys are largely lacking.

- **Mosses:** *Selenospora guernisacii* is an inconspicuous Discomycete associated with mosses in northwestern North America (Weber 1995b). Döbbler (1979) documented pyrenocarpous and pezizalean parasites of mosses in Europe.
- **Hepatics and liverworts:** Intracellular associations between achlorophyllous hepatic and pteridophyte gametophytes and various fungi are widespread (Pocock and Duckett 1984; Ligrone and Lopes 1989; Ligrone et al. 1993). *Phanoceros laevis* (an anthocerote) harbors endophytic Ascomycetes, Basidiomycetes, and Zygomycetes with various cytological specializations. The general term "endomycothalli" has been proposed for fungal colonization of hepatics (Ligrone et al. 1993). Schmid and Oberwinkler (1993) described the "lycopodioid mycothallus interaction" for fungal endophytes colonizing *Lycopodium clavatum* gametophytes.
- **Pteridophytes:** Root colonization is well known (Boullard 1957, 1979); most associations have been considered endomycorrhizal, but septate hyphae are also numerous. Roots of *Pteridium aquilinum* harbor Zygomycetes (*Absidia cylindrospora*, *Mortierella*), anamorphic Ascomycetes, and a sterile Basidiomycete (Petrini et al. 1992a). Tropical arboreal epiphytic ferns host an undetermined Ascomycete that invades epidermal and cortical cells in a manner resembling ericoid mycorrhizae (Schmid et al. 1995).

These non-vascular and seedless vascular plant hosts remain critically understudied. The diversity of fungal associations — from intracellular parasitism to mutualistic mycorrhiza-like structures — suggests endophytism in these ancient plant lineages may represent evolutionary precursors to the more complex endophyte symbioses seen in seed plants.

## Bark Endophytes

Many fungal species colonize living bark on twigs and small branches of both conifers and broad-leaved trees. Notable examples include:

- Nonlichenized **Arthopyreniaceae** colonize resinous young conifer bark and smooth deciduous bark (e.g., *Alnus*), including *Arthopyrenia plumbaria*, *Mycoglaena subcoerulescens*, and other *Mycoglaena* species.
- *Arthonia impolita* is ubiquitous on young *Pinus strobus* bark in eastern North America.
- *Vestigium felicis*, an unusual coelomycete producing distinctive "cat's paw" conidia, has been found exclusively from young living *Thuja plicata* twigs (Pirozynski and Shoemaker 1972).

Several bark endophytes colonize healthy young tissue and subsequently fruit on recently dead attached twigs, including **Rhytismataceae** (*Tryblidiopsis pinastri* on *Picea*, *Discocainia treleasei* on *P. sitchensis*), *Lachnellula* species (Hyaloscyphaceae), and *Pezicula* and *Mollisia* (Dermateaceae). *Therrya pini* and *T. fülii* on *Pinus*, and *Coccomyces strobi* on *P. strobus*, follow this same strategy.

## Xylotropic Endophytes

Xylotropic endophytes form a distinct guild of xylem-colonizing species spanning
a wide taxonomic range. They are predominantly xylariaceous (*Hypoxylon*,
*Biscogniauxia*, *Camillea*, *Nemania*), Diaporthales (*Phragmoporthe*,
*Amphiporthe*, *Phomopsis*), Hypocreales (*Nectria*), with few Basidiomycetes
(*Coniophora*) and some species from the periderm mycobiota. Species diversity
and abundance are generally lower than in bark, shoot, or foliar endophyte
communities, but the ecological significance of xylotropic species may be
disproportionately large given their direct access to the host's vascular
transport system.

Some xylotropic endophytes are notably host-specific: germination of *Hypoxylon fragiforme* is triggered only by host-specific monolignol glucosides (Chapela et al. 1990, 1991, 1993). Peculiar mycobiota characterize different hosts colonizing healthy attached branches, documented for alder (Fisher and Petrini 1990), conifers (Sieber 1989; Kowalski and Kehr 1992), and oak and beech (Chapela and Boddy 1988b; Boddy 1992).

The life history of xylotropic endophytes is analogous to that of foliar endophytes: they infect early, interrupt growth, and then grow rapidly during host stress or senescence. Disjunct infections remain quiescent in healthy wood because high water content prevents active invasion. When stress, injury, or death reduces water content, active colonization resumes (Chapela and Boddy 1988b). Active growth and sporulation respond directly to substratum drying (Chapela 1989; Boddy 1992). This strategy has been adopted by facultative pathogens such as *Entoleuca mammata* on aspen (Manion and Griffin 1986) and by various wood-decaying fungi.

## Root Endophytes

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are technically endophytes but are treated separately (see
Chapter 15). Nonmycorrhizal microfungi that infect roots or associate with
mycorrhizae represent a distinct category. Soil fungi, saprobic rhizosphere
fungi, root pathogens, and root endophytes overlap considerably in taxonomy
and ecology, making it difficult to draw clear boundaries between these
functional groups. Forest tree roots support diverse nonmycorrhizal
endophytes, though detailed investigations exist for only a few hosts.

Common root endophytes in alpine, ericoid, and perennial hosts include *Phialocephala fortinii*, *P. dimorphospora*, *P. finlandia*, *Oidiodendron*, *Geomyces*, and *Scytalidium vaccinii*. **Mycelium radicis atrovirens** (MRA) is a heterogeneous taxonomic concept used as a name for sterile dematiaceous isolates with a broad host range. On ericoid hosts, MRA produces unique morphological structures and has sometimes been termed ericoid mycorrhizae, though its host range extends far beyond ericaceous plants (Stoyke and Currah 1991; Stoyke et al. 1992).

MRA hyphae are rhizoctonialike, featuring "monilioid hyphae" that form a loose weft on the root surface with relatively extensive colonization but limited intracellular penetration. On conifer hosts, MRA can form ectomycorrhiza-like structures with an intercellular colony resembling a Hartig net (Wilcox and Wang 1987; O'Dell et al. 1993). In culture, MRA produces thick-walled, dark-pigmented, septate hyphae and is usually sterile or very slow to sporulate. Its ecological role remains uncertain — it may form functional mycorrhizae or act as a root pathogen depending on circumstances.

*P. fortinii* and MRA have been recovered from hardwoods (*Fagus*), conifers (*Abies*, *Picea*, *Pinus*), and alpine perennials (Wang and Wilcox 1985; Holdenrieder and Sieber 1992; Stoyke et al. 1992; O'Dell et al. 1993; Ahlich and Sieber 1996). Summerbell (1989) serially washed mycorrhizal *Picea mariana* roots and recovered primarily MRA and *Penicillium*. Holdenrieder and Sieber (1992) documented 120 taxa from *Picea abies* roots, with MRA, *Penicillium*, *Cylindrocarpon destructans*, and *Cryptosporiopsis* as the most frequent isolates.
