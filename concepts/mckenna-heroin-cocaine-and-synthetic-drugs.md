# Heroin, Cocaine, and Synthetic Drugs

## The Pattern of Hard Drugs

Distilled alcohol and white sugar preceded morphine as examples of high purity addictive compounds, but morphine set the pattern for the modern "hard drugs" — highly addictive injectable narcotics. Once heroin, invented as a cure for morphine addiction, was introduced, it quickly replaced morphine as the synthetic opiate of choice.

Heroin replaced all other drugs in the public fantasy concerning the evils of drug addiction. Even today, with statistics showing that alcohol kills ten times more often than heroin, heroin addiction is still viewed as the depths of drug depravity.

## The Nature of Heroin Addiction

Immediately after his shot the heroin addict is cheerful, almost ebullient. This quickly gives way to the "nod" — the detached state of twilight sleep where the long reveries of the opiates can unfurl. There is no pain, no regret, no distraction, and no fear. Heroin is the perfect drug for anyone who has been damaged by lack of self-esteem or traumatized by historical upheaval. It is a drug of battlefields, concentration camps, cancer wards, prisons, and ghettos.

## Coca: From Divine Plant to Scourge

For millennia the peoples of the montane rain forests of South America have held cultural values promoting the ritual and religious use of the stimulant/food coca. Locals will immediately tell you: "Coca no es un droga, es comida" — Coca is not a drug, it is food. The self-administered doses contain a significant percentage of daily vitamin and mineral requirements. Coca is also a powerful appetite suppressant, crucial in the protein-poor Amazonian environment.

To the ancient Inca culture of Peru, coca was a goddess — Mama Coca, a New World echo of Graves's white goddess Leucothea. In 1859 cocaine was isolated for the first time. Sigmund Freud was among the early researchers, hailing coca as a possible cure for morphinism — a pattern that would repeat endlessly.

## Cocaine and Modern Empire

Cocaine followed the pattern set with coffee, tea, and chocolate — quickly attracting entrepreneurial attention. Chief among those who saw commercial opportunities was M. Angelo Mariani, who marketed Vin Mariani, a coca-based wine. The most famous derivative was Coca-Cola, originally containing both cocaine and caffeine.

## Drugs, Governments, and Intelligence

The relationship between governments, intelligence agencies, and the drug trade is one of the most disturbing aspects of modern dominator culture. Nearly every drug that appeared on the black market during the 1960s had previously been scrutinized, tested, and in some cases refined by CIA and army scientists. The pattern of exploitation cycles — from sugar through tea, opium, and cocaine — reveals a consistent dynamic:

1. A natural plant substance is discovered with useful properties
2. It becomes a commodity of trade and colonial exploitation
3. Technology is developed to refine and concentrate the active principles
4. The refined product becomes far more destructive than the original
5. The trade becomes entangled with intelligence operations and geopolitical strategy

## Television as Electronic Drug

McKenna extends his analysis to include what he calls "electronic drugs" — particularly television. Television shares many properties with addictive drugs: it provides passive stimulation, alters consciousness, creates dependency, and serves the interests of dominator culture by pacifying and distracting the population. The combination of television for the poor and cocaine for the rich represents the management agenda that emerged after the suppression of the psychedelic sixties.

## The Hidden Persuader

The drug problem is not what it seems. We do not have a drug problem; we have a problem with the management of our technologies and with the denial of our fundamental human need for altered states of consciousness. The real question is not how to suppress drugs but how to create a society in which the legitimate human need for ecstasy and transcendence can be safely and meaningfully fulfilled.

McKenna's work represents one of the most ambitious attempts to construct a comprehensive theory of the role of psychoactive plants in human history and culture. While academic anthropologists have been skeptical of many specific claims, his ideas have been enormously influential in psychedelic culture and continue to inspire research into the relationship between psychoactive substances and human consciousness. ## The Opium-Morphine-Heroin Progression
McKenna identifies the opium-to-heroin progression as the paradigmatic example of what happens when [[dominator-culture-and-partnership-society]] refines a plant medicine into a commodity of addiction. Opium, the dried latex of Papaver somniferum, has been used for thousands of years across Eurasia as a medicinal and recreational substance. In its raw form, opium contains a complex mixture of alkaloids — morphine, codeine, thebaine, papaverine, and noscapine — that modulate each other's effects. The presence of additional alkaloids in raw opium creates a self-limiting quality: the combination of psychoactive compounds produces nausea at doses approaching toxicity, providing a natural ceiling on consumption.
The isolation of morphine from opium in 1804 by the German pharmacist Friedrich Sertürner removed this natural safeguard. Morphine is approximately ten times more potent than raw opium by weight and, administered by injection, bypasses the gastrointestinal tract entirely, producing an intensity of effect that raw opium smoking or eating cannot match. The hypodermic syringe, introduced in the 1850s, completed the transformation by allowing morphine to be delivered directly into the bloodstream. Heroin (diacetylmorphine), synthesised from morphine by Bayer in 1898 as a purportedly non-addictive cough suppressant, increased the lipid solubility of the molecule, allowing it to cross the blood-brain barrier more rapidly and produce an even more intense euphoric rush. Each refinement step — from opium to morphine to heroin — increased potency, addiction potential, and social harm while reducing the self-regulating properties of the original plant material.

## Cocaine and the Colonial Extraction Model
McKenna's analysis of cocaine follows a parallel structure to his analysis of heroin. The coca leaf, when chewed or brewed as a tea, provides a mild stimulant effect comparable to coffee. Coca contains approximately 0.5-1.0% cocaine alkaloid along with additional compounds that moderate its effects. Indigenous Andean users typically consume 30-60 grams of coca leaf per day through chewing, receiving a gradual, sustained release of cocaine along with vitamins, minerals, and appetite-suppressing compounds that are nutritionally significant in the high-altitude environment.
The isolation of cocaine hydrochloride in 1860 removed this balanced delivery system. Cocaine powder, at 60-90% purity, delivers the entire alkaloid load in a single dose, producing an intense but short-lived euphoria followed by a crash that drives compulsive re-dosing. The development of crack cocaine in the 1980s further intensified this pattern by creating a freebase form that could be smoked, delivering the drug to the brain even more rapidly than intranasal use. McKenna argues that each step in this refinement progression — from coca leaf to cocaine powder to crack cocaine — parallels the progression from opium to morphine to heroin, and from fermented beverages to distilled spirits.
The colonial dimension of cocaine's history is central to McKenna's critique. The demand for cocaine in Europe and North America drove the expansion of coca cultivation in the Andes, displacing subsistence agriculture and creating economic dependencies that mirror the earlier patterns of sugar and opium cultivation. The criminalisation of cocaine in the twentieth century did not eliminate demand but rather transferred enormous profits from legal pharmaceutical companies to illegal cartels, creating the conditions for the drug-related violence that has destabilised much of Latin America. This pattern — in which prohibition creates a black market that enriches criminal organisations while doing nothing to address the underlying demand — is, for McKenna, a predictable consequence of the dominator culture's refusal to acknowledge the legitimate human desire for altered states of consciousness.

## Synthetic Drugs and the Escalation of Harm
McKenna extends his analysis to encompass the wave of synthetic drugs that emerged in the twentieth century. Amphetamine, first synthesised in 1887, was widely distributed to soldiers during World War II to maintain alertness and combat readiness. Methamphetamine, a more potent derivative, became a widespread drug of abuse in the post-war period, particularly in Japan and later in the United States. The development of PCP (phencyclidine) in the 1950s, initially investigated as an anaesthetic agent, introduced a dissociative drug that could produce psychotic states indistinguishable from schizophrenia. MDMA (ecstasy), synthesised by Merck in 1912 and rediscovered by Alexander Shulgin in the 1970s, represented a partial exception — a synthetic compound that, like the classical psychedelics, produced feelings of empathy and emotional openness rather than addiction and withdrawal.
For McKenna, the synthetic drug epidemic represents the logical endpoint of the dominator culture's relationship with psychoactive substances. Having suppressed the natural plant entheogens that once provided safe, culturally embedded contexts for altered states, the culture has created a vacuum that is filled by the least regulated, most destructive substances available. The pattern is consistent: the better the original plant medicine is at providing genuine spiritual or therapeutic benefits — as in the case of [[psychedelic-plants-and-civilization]], ayahuasca, or peyote — the more vigorously the dominator culture suppresses it, while simultaneously tolerating or even facilitating the distribution of drugs that stupefy rather than illuminate.

## The Political Economy of Prohibition
McKenna's analysis of the drug war connects it directly to the broader dynamics of [[mckenna-consciousness-expansion-and-drug-war]]. He argues that the selective prohibition of certain drugs — psychedelics above all — reveals the true agenda behind drug policy. Drugs that promote ego-dissolution, critical thinking, and ecological awareness are criminalised with the harshest penalties, while drugs that reinforce social conformity, consumer behaviour, and political passivity — alcohol, tobacco, prescription sedatives — are actively promoted or at least tolerated.
The scheduling of psychedelic substances in the United States, which placed LSD, psilocybin, and mescaline in Schedule I (the most restrictive category, defined as having "no accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse") while placing cocaine and methamphetamine in Schedule II (acknowledging medical uses), reflects this bias. McKenna notes the irony that the drugs with the lowest addiction potential and the highest capacity for producing transformative experiences received the most severe legal penalties, while genuinely addictive drugs with demonstrated capacity for physical harm received relatively more lenient treatment. This inversion of rational drug policy is, in McKenna's analysis, not a mistake but a deliberate strategy to suppress the substances most likely to catalyse [[mckenna-shamanism-and-the-lost-archaic-world]].

## Television and the Management of Consciousness
McKenna's concept of "electronic drugs" extends the analysis of substance addiction to the domain of media technology. He argues that television functions as a consciousness-altering technology that shares many pharmacological properties with addictive drugs. Both television and addictive drugs produce passive states of receptivity in which critical faculties are suspended. Both create dependency through the provision of regular, predictable rewards. Both serve to distract attention from underlying social and existential problems. And both are actively promoted by institutions that benefit from a pacified, compliant population.
The comparison between television and heroin — television for the poor, cocaine for the rich — is deliberately provocative but grounded in McKenna's broader analysis of how dominator culture manages the human need for altered states. If the genuine psychedelic experience, which promotes critical thinking, ecological awareness, and the dissolution of ego boundaries, is suppressed, then the vacuum must be filled with substitute experiences that do not threaten the established order. Television provides a simulacrum of the psychedelic experience — vivid imagery, emotional engagement, altered sense of time — without any of the transformative content. The viewer is transported to other worlds but returns unchanged, unlike the psychedelic voyager who returns with new perspectives that may challenge the foundations of the social order.
McKenna's analysis anticipates later research on the neurological effects of screen media. The alpha brainwave state induced by television viewing — a passive, receptive state distinct from both the active beta state of waking consciousness and the deep theta state of meditation — has been compared by neuroscientists to a mild hypnotic trance. The dopamine release associated with screen-based media consumption follows the same reward pathway activated by addictive drugs, creating a neurological basis for compulsive viewing behaviour. These findings lend empirical support to McKenna's argument that electronic media should be understood as pharmacological agents rather than merely as entertainment or information delivery systems.

## The Opium Wars and the Geopolitics of Drugs
McKenna's historical analysis of the relationship between governments and psychoactive substances finds its most explicit expression in the Opium Wars of the nineteenth century. The British Empire, having developed a lucrative trade in Indian opium, used military force to compel the Chinese Qing dynasty to accept opium imports despite the devastating social consequences of opium addiction in China. The First Opium War (1839-1842) and the Second Opium War (1856-1860) established the principle that a powerful state could use military coercion to open foreign markets for psychoactive substances regardless of the wishes or welfare of the recipient population.
For McKenna, the Opium Wars are not an historical anomaly but a paradigmatic example of the [[mckenna-sugar-slavery-and-dominance]] that has characterised dominator culture's relationship with psychoactive plants from the sixteenth century to the present. The same pattern can be observed in the British promotion of tea and opium in China, in the Dutch promotion of coffee and opium in the East Indies, in the American promotion of tobacco and alcohol among indigenous populations, and in the modern pharmaceutical industry's promotion of prescription psychoactive drugs in developing countries. In each case, a psychoactive substance is identified, a market is created through colonial or commercial pressure, and the profits from the trade flow to the metropole while the social costs are borne by the colonised or the economically disadvantaged.
The irony, in McKenna's analysis, is that the same imperial powers that aggressively promoted addictive drugs in foreign markets simultaneously criminalised the indigenous use of [[psychedelic-plants-and-civilization]] within their own territories. The British prohibition of cannabis in India (1893-1894), the American prohibition of peyote (despite treaty protections for Native American religious use), and the international scheduling of psychedelic substances under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971) all follow the same pattern: the substances that promote conformity and dependency are encouraged, while the substances that promote critical thinking and ecological awareness are suppressed. This asymmetry, for McKenna, reveals the true function of drug policy in dominator culture — not the protection of public health but the management of consciousness in the service of social control.

## The Addiction Paradigm and Legitimate Needs
McKenna's deepest insight about the drug problem is that addiction is not primarily a pharmacological phenomenon but a cultural one. People do not become addicted to drugs because the drugs are inherently irresistible; they become addicted because the drugs provide something that their culture and social environment fail to provide. Heroin provides relief from trauma and despair; cocaine provides energy and confidence for those who lack both; alcohol provides temporary escape from the loneliness and meaninglessness of life in dominator culture. The root cause of addiction is not the availability of drugs but the unavailability of genuine fulfilment.
This analysis, which McKenna shares with writers such as Gabor Maté and Bruce Alexander (whose "Rat Park" experiments demonstrated that drug consumption is primarily a response to environmental conditions rather than a property of the drugs themselves), has profound implications for drug policy. If addiction is a response to cultural pathology, then the solution is not to suppress the drugs but to address the cultural conditions that drive people to seek relief in them. The [[mckenna-archaic-revival]], in this reading, is not merely a cultural movement but a public health intervention — the recovery of the plant-based ecstatic traditions that once provided genuine spiritual fulfilment and social bonding, replacing them with the counterfeit satisfactions of addictive drugs and electronic media.
The legitimate human need for altered states of consciousness is, in McKenna's analysis, as fundamental as the need for food, shelter, and social connection. Every human society that has ever been studied has developed technologies for altering consciousness — through plant medicines, meditation, fasting, dance, music, or sensory deprivation. The attempt to eliminate this need through prohibition and punishment is not only futile but counterproductive, driving the demand for altered states underground and into the hands of the least regulated and most destructive substances available. A sane drug policy would begin by acknowledging this need and providing safe, culturally supported contexts — informed by the traditions of [[psychedelic-plants-and-civilization]] and validated by modern [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]] research — in which it can be responsibly fulfilled.
