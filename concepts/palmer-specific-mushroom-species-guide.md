# Palmer on Specific Edible Mushroom Species

## Palmer's Personal Experience

Julius Auboineau Palmer tested perhaps forty varieties of toadstools
personally, eating regularly more than half of them whenever found,
and making daily additions to his bill of fare. He described his
method: after tracing a specimen to its family, he broiled it with
salt and butter (no pepper) and ate a small piece on an empty
stomach, increasing the quantity in successive experiments. During
mushroom season he ate them three times a day, sometimes half a
dozen kinds at a meal, enjoying better digestion than at any other
time of year.

## The Meadow and Horse Mushroom

The meadow mushroom (Agaricus campestris) and the horse-mushroom
(Agaricus arvensis) are so similar that cooks and gardeners ignore
any difference between them. Palmer advanced the theory that the
horse-mushroom is propagated from spores of the meadow mushroom
after passing through the horse's digestive system. At first growth
the mushroom resembles a puffball, but soon discloses under the cap
a veil which ruptures to expose gills free from the stem, with a
faint shade of pink deepening through purple into black. The taste
and smell are so peculiar that Palmer felt there was not much danger
of deception once the species was known. He noted that Berkeley and
Curtis, the leading authorities of England and America, had only
twenty years earlier considered the Coprinus comatus poisonous,
showing how knowledge of edibility evolved.

## The Maned Mushroom (Coprinus comatus)

The most easily recognized agaric, shaped like a turtle's egg when
fit for food. The top is brown and smooth; toward the earth the
outer skin ruffles into a white mane of concentric layers. The stem
is hollow, appearing like macaroni when cooked. A ring around the
stem is movable in well-developed plants. Soon after acquiring its
growth the gill points turn black, the mushroom opens umbrella-like
and melts into an inky fluid. This was the first fungus Palmer dared
to eat on his own recognition, and it has no poisonous counterpart.
It should never be eaten stale or with black gills. When found in
lawns it is delicious; specimens from dust-heaps, brick-yards, or
city offal have no flavor and would prove deleterious in quantity.

## The Elm-Tree Mushroom (Agaricus ulmarius)

Growing only in the fall on elm trees, with a thick solid stem
(sometimes set eccentrically), broad white gills, firm white flesh,
and a light-yellow top at first smooth, finally spotted and cracked.
In older specimens the gills turn yellow and the top becomes very
leathery. Palmer knew of no other large fungus with gills and white
flesh growing from the elm. It is most common in a cleft or where a
limb has been sawn off, often reappearing yearly in the same spot.
It has a fresh, tonic smell and tastes much like fish broiled or
fried. Friends always called it the "tree-fish."

## The Fairy-Ring Champignon (Marasmius oreades)

A leathery toadstool growing in great profusion in rich grassy lawns,
entirely cream-colored with a musk flavor. It can be kept any length
of time by drying, expanding again when put in water. Palmer sent
some to a family with an eighteen-month-old infant who manifested
great delight at the taste.

## The Honey-Colored Mushroom (Agaricus melleus)

Palmer once ate plentifully of this species, only to find in
Greville's authority that it was "said to be freely eaten on the
Continent" but that Paulet had tried its effect upon a dog, which
died twelve hours later. Notwithstanding such warnings, it continued
to be a favorite article of Palmer's diet. He thought it might be
noxious raw but that heat killed the virus.

## The Russulae

A brittle family whose gills break into small pieces and whose flesh
snaps suddenly on being bent. If milk or juice comes, Palmer advised
rejection. The novice should taste a small piece when dry: non-edible
kinds bite and sting the tongue, while esculent varieties are
tasteless, mild, or chestnut-flavored. R. alutacea (bright red top,
buff gills) closely resembles R. emetica and R. rosacea, so Palmer
invariably tasted a small piece of each stem while washing.

## The Boleti

The Boleti can only be confused with the Polyporei. The latter grow
mostly on wood with abortive stems, while edible boleti grow from
the ground with distinct stems and easily separable tubes. Brown,
yellowish, or greenish tubes with a russet or brown top and white
flesh when cut indicate an edible variety. Brilliant coloring (red
or yellow) or turning blue when bruised signals rejection. Palmer
regularly ate B. aestivalis and B. pachypus. No recorded death
from toadstool poisoning has been traced to a boletus, though some
contain bitter or acrid principles causing illness.

## The Spreading Hydnum (Hydnum repandum)

Usually yellow, sometimes reddish, always firm-fleshed, with a
deformed or eccentric stem. Smith said all species of any size
enjoy a good character. The teeth or spines (which rub off easily
in young specimens) are sufficient to distinguish them. The Hydnei
are nearly all edible, with none known to contain poisonous
principles.

## Puffballs (Lycoperdons)

The giant puffball (Lycoperdon giganteum) in its infant state
resembles an egg. Skin color varies from pure white to nearly black.
If the knife leaves a yellow stain, it is too old. Palmer believed
all white puffballs may be safely used if cooked at once, as they
change rapidly after gathering. There is no poisonous fungus
resembling them. Those with dark interiors (Scleroderma) are coarse
and uninviting, though not definitely branded noxious.

## The Smeared Cortinarius (Cortinarius collinitus)

A specimen eaten by Palmer's friends who did not pretend to
distinguish varieties. Despite its obscure reputation among English
authorities, they ate whatever had an appetizing flavor and Palmer
never knew them to acknowledge any unpleasant sensation from this
species, reporting only an intense desire to hunt for more.

## Cooking Recommendations

Palmer insisted on two proper methods for cooking mushrooms.
The majority are best broiled on a fine-wire gridiron, sprinkled
with salt and (if the species is well known) red pepper, buttered
as the fire browns them. Otherwise, stew them in milk exactly as
one would make an oyster stew. The elm-tree mushroom, honey-colored
mushroom, and any others tasting raw of grass or trees are only
good broiled. The meadow mushroom, horse-mushroom, and coprinus
are excellent cooked either way.
