# Mushroom Cultivation Contamination

Contamination is the most common cause of failure in mushroom cultivation.
Competing organisms, primarily molds and bacteria, outcompete mushroom mycelium for substrate resources or directly attack fruiting bodies.

## Common Contaminant Molds

### Trichoderma (Green Mold)

The most devastating contaminant. Trichoderma species are aggressive
saprophytes producing dense green spore masses. They are thermotolerant,
surviving pasteurization temperatures. They produce antifungal compounds
that kill mushroom mycelium. Contaminated substrate should be discarded
far from growing areas. Spores persist in the environment for months.
Trichoderma is the primary reason supplemented substrates require full
[[mycotoxins]] posing health risks through spore
inhalation. Any substrate showing these molds should be removed.

### Neurospora (Pink Mold)

A fast-growing, heat-resistant mold producing bright pink-orange mycelium
and spore clouds. Common in tropical and subtropical climates. Survives
pasteurization and brief sterilization exposure. Immediate removal and
isolation of affected blocks is critical.

### Mucor and Rhizopus

These Zygomycete fungi produce fluffy gray-white mycelium filling substrate
bags within days. They thrive in wet, poorly aerated conditions. Less
competitive against healthy mushroom mycelium but can overwhelm stressed
cultures.

## Bacterial Contamination

### Bacterial Blotch (Pseudomonas tolaasii)

The most common bacterial disease. Dark brown, sunken lesions on caps
caused by the toxin tolaasin. Spreads through water splash and handling.
Prevention: reduce surface moisture, improve air circulation, lower
humidity to 85% during later fruiting stages.

### Bacterial Soft Rot

Burkholderia and Pseudomonas species cause substrate to become slimy and
foul-smelling. Occurs in over-wet substrates with poor drainage. Affected
substrate has a sour smell distinct from healthy mycelium's earthy aroma.
