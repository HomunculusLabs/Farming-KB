# Mushroom Cultivation Contamination

Contamination is the most common cause of failure in mushroom cultivation.
Competing organisms, primarily molds and bacteria, outcompete mushroom mycelium for substrate resources or directly attack fruiting bodies.

## Common Contaminant Molds

### Trichoderma (Green Mold)

The most devastating contaminant. Trichoderma species are aggressive
saprophytes producing dense green spore masses. They are thermotolerant,
surviving pasteurization temperatures. They produce antifungal compounds
that kill mushroom mycelium. Contaminated substrate should be discarded
far from growing areas. Spores persist in the environment for months.
Trichoderma is the primary reason supplemented substrates require full
[[sterilization]].

### Aspergillus and Penicillium

These produce black, yellow, blue, or green colonies. Common in indoor
environments, they readily colonize improperly sterilized substrates. Some
Aspergillus species produce [[mycotoxins]] posing health risks through spore
inhalation. Any substrate showing these molds should be removed.

### Neurospora (Pink Mold)

A fast-growing, heat-resistant mold producing bright pink-orange mycelium
and spore clouds. Common in tropical and subtropical climates. Survives
pasteurization and brief sterilization exposure. Immediate removal and
isolation of affected blocks is critical.

### Mucor and Rhizopus

These Zygomycete fungi produce fluffy gray-white mycelium filling substrate
bags within days. They thrive in wet, poorly aerated conditions. Less
competitive against healthy mushroom mycelium but can overwhelm stressed
cultures.

## Bacterial Contamination

### Bacterial Blotch (Pseudomonas tolaasii)

The most common bacterial disease. Dark brown, sunken lesions on caps
caused by the toxin tolaasin. Spreads through water splash and handling.
Prevention: reduce surface moisture, improve air circulation, lower
humidity to 85% during later fruiting stages.

### Bacterial Soft Rot

Burkholderia and Pseudomonas species cause substrate to become slimy and
foul-smelling. Occurs in over-wet substrates with poor drainage. Affected
substrate has a sour smell distinct from healthy mycelium's earthy aroma.

### Anaerobic Conditions

Anaerobic pockets promote bacteria producing hydrogen sulfide (rotten egg)
and butyric acid (vomit) smells. These conditions kill mycelium. Proper [[substrate-preparation]] substrate preparation with adequate structure and aeration prevents this.

## Contamination Sources

### Airborne

Spores are ubiquitous in the air. Concentrations are higher in agricultural
areas, older buildings, and poorly ventilated spaces. Laminar flow hoods
with HEPA filtration (0.3 micron, 99.97% efficiency) are the primary
defense during inoculation.

### Substrate-Related

Raw materials carry their own microbial load. Pasteurization and
sterilization reduce this. Contamination from this source indicates failure
to achieve proper temperature, time, or coverage. Hot or cold spots in the
substrate mass are common causes.

### Equipment and Surfaces

Work surfaces, tools, and containers can harbor contaminants. Effective
disinfectants: 70% isopropyl alcohol, 10% bleach, quaternary ammonium
compounds. Clean before and after each use.

### Spawn-Related

Contaminated spawn introduces organisms directly into substrate, bypassing
heat treatment protection. Use spawn from reputable suppliers and maintain
quality control during production.

## Prevention Strategies

### Aseptic Technique

The single most important prevention measure:

1. Work in a clean, dedicated space
2. Minimize time containers are open during inoculation
3. Flame-sterilize tools between uses
4. Wear clean clothing and gloves
5. Avoid talking or rapid movement during sensitive procedures

### Sanitation Protocols

- Clean grow rooms between crops with disinfectant
- Remove and destroy contaminated materials promptly
- Maintain positive air pressure in clean areas
- Use shoe covers or dedicated footwear
- Filter intake air in grow rooms

### Process Controls

- Monitor and record sterilization temperatures
- Use temperature probes in substrate mass centers
- Verify filter integrity on spawn bags
- Maintain proper spawn rates for rapid colonization

## Integrated Pest Management

Fungus gnats, mites, and other arthropods carry contaminant spores between
substrates. Controlling pests reduces contamination:

- Sticky traps for monitoring and population reduction
- Screened air intakes to exclude flying insects
- Clean floors and surfaces to remove pest-harboring debris

## Economics of Contamination

For commercial operations, contamination has direct financial impact through
lost substrate and spawn costs, reduced yield, cleanup labor, and lost
production capacity. Industry benchmarks suggest acceptable rates below 5%
for well-managed operations. Rates above 10% indicate systemic issues.
