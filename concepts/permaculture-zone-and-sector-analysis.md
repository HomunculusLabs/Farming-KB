# Permaculture Zone and Sector Analysis

Zones and sectors are analytical graphic tools used in [[permaculture-design-process|permaculture design]] to understand a site and make design decisions. From William Ozier's PDC course and "Permaculture Plants: A Selection."

## Zones: What Is a Zone?

In Permaculture Design, zones are NOT the same as urban planning "zoning" (residential, commercial, etc.). They are a graphic tool drawn on paper that helps the designer understand the **amount of human impact** on different areas of a site. Zones are ranked 0-5.

Zones are often represented as concentric circles for demonstration purposes, but in reality they will almost never be concentric circles -- they can be any shape (oblong, globular areas). They represent rough estimates, not literal boundaries.

**Important**: In dense urban environments, a site may only have Zone 0 and Zone 1. Zone 5 (true wilderness) will essentially never exist in an urban area.

## Zone Descriptions

### Zone 0 -- Permanent Built Structures
The house or other buildings. Considered permanently impacted. With green roofs and living walls, some ecological activity can be remediated by a skillful designer.

### Zone 1 -- Daily Impact Area
The area around Zone 0 where humans come and go daily. Place systems that require or attract daily human impact:
- Vegetable gardens
- Herb gardens
- Cob ovens
- Yard/play space
- Parking for automobiles

### Zone 2 -- Weekly Impact Area
Systems requiring human impact a few times per week at most. Should be fairly self-supporting, requiring only occasional intervention or harvesting:
- Beehives
- Compost piles
- Food-producing bushes
- Pump houses for water systems

### Zone 3 -- Monthly/Seasonal Impact Area
Visited roughly monthly or seasonally:
- Orchards
- Seasonally harvested crops
- Main crop fields
- Ceremonial spaces (sweat lodges, etc.)

### Zone 4 -- Minimal Impact Area
Human impact present but minimal. Light interaction such as bird watching, camping, hiking, wildcrafting. Some sustainable forestry (thinning, coppicing). No human-built structures or systems. May include:
- Previously impacted areas now fallow (brownfields)
- Old logging areas
- Wilderness corridors through urbanised areas
- Wooded areas within suburbs

### Zone 5 -- Wilderness
True wilderness: no structures, no roads, no human impact. Left entirely to natural processes. Used for observation, study, and as a reference for what the natural ecosystem looks like.

## Sectors

Sectors analyse **external energies** entering the site -- natural forces that cross property boundaries. These are mapped as wedge-shaped sectors radiating from the centre of the design.

### Common Sectors to Analyse
- Summer sun (solar altitude and azimuth)
- Winter sun
- Prevailing seasonal winds (cold winter winds to block, cool summer winds to encourage)
- Fire hazard
- Noise pollution
- Undesirable views
- Desirable views
- Wildlife movement corridors
- Water flow

### How to Create a Sector Diagram
1. Start with a large circle centered on Zone 0
2. Make concentric rings equal to the number of sectors planned
3. Draw angle lines from the mid-point to the appropriate ring
4. Typically: summer sun on outermost ring, winter sun second, prevailing winds third
5. Color in arcs first, then fill wedges to avoid overlap errors

### Using Sectors in Design
Once complete, the sector analysis tells you where various elements should go to either:
- **Block** a force crossing the site (cold winds, fire)
- **Allow** a force access (cool summer breezes, winter sun)
- **Catch and harness** a force (sun for solar collectors, water for storage)

## Integration with Plant Selection

From "Permaculture Plants: A Selection," zones and sectors directly inform species selection:
- Zone 1: Home garden, grafted fruit trees, intensive vegetable production
- Zone 2: Fruit forests, small animal systems
- Zone 3+: Animal forage systems, timber, large-scale plantings
- Sectors: Fire-resistant plants on fire-hazard sectors, windbreaks on cold-wind sectors
