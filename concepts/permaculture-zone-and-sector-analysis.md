# Permaculture Zone and Sector Analysis

[[zone-and-sector-analysis]] is the foundational spatial planning methodology in [[permaculture-design]]. Developed by [[bill-mollison]], this dual framework provides a systematic approach to placing every element of a design—plants, animals, structures, and activities—according to energy efficiency and environmental factors. Together, zones and sectors form a "spiderweb of placements" that guides the entire ground plan of a permaculture site.

## The Zone System

Zones are concentric rings of management intensity radiating outward from the center of activity—typically the home or settlement (Zone 0). The fundamental principle is one of energy conservation: elements requiring frequent attention or visits are placed close to the center, while those needing less attention are placed further away.

### Zonal Placement by Visit Frequency

The logic of zoning is based on simple arithmetic of human visits. Consider a lemon tree: it needs harvesting 60-100 times per year, while requiring care (pruning, feeding) only 6-12 times. The total annual visits might be 66-112. An apple tree, harvested less frequently, may total only 15 visits per year. Thus, the lemon tree deserves a closer position than the apple tree.

Components and species naturally "space themselves in zones according to the number of visits we make to them annually." The golden rule of zone development is: **develop the nearest area first, get it under control, and then expand the perimeter.** A single perimeter then encloses all needs.

### The Dooryard Garden Model

Edgar Anderson's description of Central Honduran homesteads provides an exemplary model of zone planning in practice. Close to the house is a compact garden-orchard containing:

- Fruit trees (nance, citrus, melias, mango, coffee in shade)
- Root crops (tapioca, taro) at the edges of tree canopies
- Annual crops (corn, beans) in rows or patches
- Climbing vines (squash, chayote, luffa gourd) over the house, fences, and trees
- Flowers and useful weeds (dahlias, gladioli, climbing roses, cannas)
- Self-seeding species like grain amaranth

This polycultural model blurs the boundaries between orchard, field, house, and garden. The house and fence themselves become trellis structures. It is a semi-natural order of plants in their right relationships—not rigorously separated into artificial groups.

### The Problem with Monoculture Thinking

Mollison contrasts this productive complexity with the "monoculture man" mindset—the ordered, linear, segmented thinking that demands simplicity and therefore creates chaos. Monoculture design cannot accommodate the beneficial complexity of polycultural systems. The intrinsic properties of any component (e.g., all trees provide shade) are available regardless of placement, so these "intrinsics" are excluded when assessing specific functional placement in a design.

## The Sector System

While zones organize space by frequency of human use, sectors organize space according to external energies flowing through the site—sun, light, wind, rain, wildfire, water flow, and even views. Sectors are more site-specific than zones and are mapped as compass directions on the ground plan.

### The Energy Flow Model

Sectors represent energies generated elsewhere that pass through the system. The home or settlement is the "through point" for sectors (just as it is ground zero for zones). Energies arrive from outside like "arrows winging their way towards the home," carrying both destructive and beneficial forces.

For each sector, the designer faces a choice:

1. **Block or screen** — Exclude incoming energy (e.g., a windbreak against cold winds, a hedge to hide an unpleasant view)
2. **Channel** — Direct the energy for special uses (e.g., directing water to storage, steering cool breezes to ameliorate heat)
3. **Open** — Allow maximum passage (e.g., opening a sector to maximize sunlight)

### Combining Zones and Sectors

The placement of any component is determined by combining both zone and sector analysis. Mollison describes this as "revolving" zones until each component finds its optimal position relative to sector factors:

> "For any one component, it stops rotating when it is working to govern energies in the sector diagram."

A pine tree, for example, belongs in Zone 4 (infrequent visits), away from the fire-danger sector (pines accumulate fuel and burn intensely), toward the cold-wind sector (pines are hardy windbreaks), and ideally should also bear edible nuts as forage.

A poultry shed should border Zone 1 (frequent visits for egg collection and feeding), be away from the fire sector, border the annual garden (for easy manure collection), back onto the forage system, possibly attach to a greenhouse, and form part of a windbreak system.

### Protective Measures

Against catastrophic fire, wind, or flood, designers use:
- Protective embankments
- Dense tree plantings
- Ponds and water bodies
- Roads and fences
- Stone walls

Free-ranging wildlife is managed through placement of forage systems, fences, nest boxes, and habitat features.

## The Basic Energy-Conserving Rules

The synthesis of zone and sector analysis yields two fundamental rules:

1. **Every element** (plant, animal, or structure) must be placed so that it serves at least two or more functions.
2. **Every function** (e.g., water collection, fire protection) is served in two or more ways.

## The Broad Pattern Approach

If broad initial patterning is well analyzed and good placements made, "many more advantages than we would have designed for become obvious." This is the broad pattern approach: set the scene properly, then observe how animals move, how winds vary, how water flows, and evolve guiding patterns that achieve additional desirable ends—making animals easy to muster, steering manure to where it's needed, directing cool winds, or routing water to storage.

## Key Principles

- **No element should be placed without reference to both zone and sector criteria.**
- Start with the nearest area and expand outward incrementally.
- Any soil, with effort and compost from waste recycling, will grow a good garden—so stay close to home.
- The distinction between intrinsic properties (available everywhere) and functional placement (specific to position) is essential to efficient design.
- Observation and adaptive management refine the initial pattern over time.
