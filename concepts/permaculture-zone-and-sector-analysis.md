# Permaculture Zone and Sector Analysis

Zone and sector analysis are foundational tools [[mollison-lawn-reform-and-alternatives-in-permaculture-design]] that
organize space and manage energy flows on a property. Together they form a
spatial planning framework that places elements according to how frequently
they are visited and how they interact with external natural forces. This
approach minimizes energy waste, maximizes productivity, and creates
efficient, resilient landscapes. Developed by Holmgren and Mollison in the
1970s, the system has been refined through decades of application worldwide
and remains one of permaculture's most practical and widely-used design
methodologies. Zone and sector analysis work in complement: zones organize
from the inside out, while sectors map forces from the outside in.

## Zone Analysis: Organizing by Frequency of Use

Zone analysis arranges design elements in concentric rings based on the
frequency of human attention and interaction they require. Elements needing
daily care are placed closest to the home; those needing less attention are
placed progressively further away. This reduces unnecessary walking, improves
observation, and ensures that intensive management stays manageable without
becoming overwhelming. The zone concept is not rigidly circular—it follows
the actual patterns of human movement on a site, adapting to topography,
access paths, and existing infrastructure.

**Zone 0** is the home or center of activity. This is where people spend the
most time and includes the house, its immediate interior spaces, and the
associated infrastructure of daily living. Zone 0 is the hub from which all
other zones radiate. Energy-efficient building design, passive solar
orientation, water harvesting from roofs, and indoor food preservation are
Zone 0 concerns. The home itself is a design element that interacts with all
other zones.

**Zone 1** is the area immediately surrounding the home visited most
frequently—typically daily. It contains intensive garden beds, culinary herbs,
salad greens, small greenhouse or cold frames, seedling propagation areas,
compost bins, worm farms, and frequently harvested fruits like strawberries.
The keyhole bed design is a Zone 1 innovation that maximizes growing area
while minimizing path space, creating a pie-slice shaped bed accessible from
a central path. Zone 1 elements are those that benefit from constant
observation and regular interaction.

**Zone 2** is less intensively managed and visited less frequently—perhaps
every few days. It includes main fruit trees, berry bushes, larger vegetable
beds, poultry houses, small livestock enclosures, and orchards. These
elements require periodic attention but can function for days without direct
management. Zone 2 is where domestic food production scales up from intensive
gardening to small-scale diversified farming.

**Zone 3** is the main broadscale farming area. It includes larger orchards,
main crop fields, grazing areas, large livestock paddocks, and commercial
production zones. Visited weekly or seasonally, Zone 3 elements are managed
with less intensive input. Pasture systems, grain crops, staple food
production, and timber plantations occupy this zone. The design emphasis
shifts from frequent interaction to periodic management, selective harvest,
and [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]].

**Zone 4** is a semi-wild area used for foraging, timber production, fuel
wood gathering, and occasional grazing. It receives minimal management—
perhaps a few visits per year for harvesting or maintenance. Zone 4 is a
transition between managed and unmanaged landscapes. It provides useful
resources while requiring almost no ongoing input, functioning as a
productive buffer zone between civilization and wilderness.

**Zone 5** is the unmanaged wild area. It is left entirely to natural
processes and serves as a reference ecosystem, a reservoir of biodiversity,
a source of wild genetic material, and a place for observation and learning.
Zone 5 is not neglected but deliberately protected as habitat for wildlife,
a watershed protector, and a reminder of what self-organizing systems look
like. Even small Zone 5 areas provide critical ecological services including
pollination, pest control, and water purification that benefit all other
zones.

## Sector Analysis: Mapping External Energies

While zones organize space from the inside out based on human use, sector
analysis maps external energies that flow across a site regardless of zone
boundaries. Sectors are directional influences that include: sun paths (the
angle and duration of sunlight through seasons), prevailing winds (whether
warm, cold, drying, or moisture-laden), water flows (surface runoff,
groundwater movement, seasonal flooding patterns), fire risk directions and
fuel loads, [[permaculture-designers-manual-wildlife-corridors]] and animal movement patterns, noise and
pollution sources, views and visual privacy considerations, and access
points, roads, and human movement patterns.

Sector analysis overlays these directional forces onto a site map to reveal
opportunities and constraints. A cold winter wind sector might be deflected
with windbreak plantings of dense evergreen trees; a hot summer sun sector
might be shaded with deciduous trees that drop leaves to allow winter sun; a
wildfire approach sector might be managed with fire-resistant vegetation,
cleared buffer zones, and water storage for defense. Each sector interaction
can be actively designed for—captured, deflected, or channeled—rather than
passively accepted as an unavoidable condition.

## Integration at Melliodora

Holmgren's own property, Melliodora in [[holmgren-melliodora-permaculture-demonstration-site-hepburn-springs]], Victoria, serves as
a detailed demonstration of zone and sector analysis in practice. The
1-hectare property integrates zones 0 through 4 with careful sector
management. The house (Zone 0) uses [[passive-solar-design]] oriented to the
northern sun sector. Intensive gardens (Zone 1) surround the house for daily
harvest. Fruit trees, nut trees, and poultry (Zone 2) are positioned for
convenient access while also providing wind protection. The property's slope
is used to gravity-feed water through the landscape and manage drainage
naturally. Windbreak plantings address cold southern winds while allowing
winter sun to penetrate. This integration demonstrates how [[permaculture-beginners-guide-zones-and-sectors]]
work together to create efficient, productive, and beautiful landscapes.

## Practical Applications

The zone system is inherently flexible and scales from suburban backyards to
large rural properties. On a small urban lot, zones compress dramatically
with Zone 1 dominating the available space and Zone 5 reduced to a small
wild corner or a pond. On large farms and ranches, Zone 3 may expand to
hundreds of hectares while Zone 1 remains compact around the homestead. The
principle remains constant: place frequently-used elements close, rarely-used
elements far, and let natural energy flows inform all placement decisions.
