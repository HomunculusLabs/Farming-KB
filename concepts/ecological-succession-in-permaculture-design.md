# Ecological Succession in Permaculture Design

[[ecological-succession]] — the predictable sequence of biological communities replacing one another over time on disturbed or newly formed land — is a fundamental principle that [[permaculture]] designers harness and accelerate. Rather than waiting decades for natural succession to unfold, permaculture compresses the entire sequence into a single planting event, achieving mature-system yields within years rather than centuries.

## Natural Succession Processes

Nature establishes new systems on devastated landscapes — basalt flows, ice-scoured terrain, flood-deposited sediments, overgrazed land, and fire-cleared areas — through a characteristic sequence:

1. **Pioneer species** colonize first: hardy, fast-growing "weeds" (thistles, Lantana, broom, grasses) that tolerate poor soils, full sun, and exposed conditions
2. **Soil stabilization** follows as pioneer roots hold soil, organic matter accumulates from leaf drop and dieback, and microclimate moderation begins
3. **Shrub establishment** occurs as soil improves and pioneers provide shelter
4. **Understory trees** emerge in the moderated conditions
5. **Canopy closure** by long-term climax or crop-tree species completes the succession

Pioneer species perform critical ecosystem services: stabilizing water flow in the landscape, providing shelter for later-succession species, generating mulch and organic matter, fixing nitrogen, and improving soil structure and chemistry for their successors.

## The Permaculture Acceleration Strategy

Unlike natural succession, which unfolds over decades to centuries, permaculture **places all successional stages simultaneously** in a single planting:

- Pioneer annuals and short-lived perennials for immediate ground cover and soil improvement
- Ground covers to suppress weeds and retain moisture
- Understory shrubs and tree legumes for nitrogen fixation and mid-level production
- Herbaceous crops for short-term food yield
- Mulch-producing species for ongoing soil health
- Long-term windbreak trees for microclimate protection
- Climax or tree-crop species for permanent yield (timber, fruit, nuts)

A single hectare may require 4,000–8,000 plants of multiple species, typically necessitating a dedicated plant nursery where seedlings are propagated while site preparation (fencing, soil rehabilitation, earthworks) proceeds.

## Temporal Yield Sequence

A well-designed accelerated succession produces continuous yields across time scales:

| Phase | Timeframe | Primary Yields |
|---|---|---|
| Pioneer establishment | Years 0–2 | Vegetables, herbs, ground-cover mulch |
| Shrub/understory development | Years 2–5 | Berries, fodder, nitrogen fixation |
| Early forest | Years 5–15 | Coppice, pole timber, honey, some fruit |
| Mature system | Years 15–50+ | Fruit, nuts, bark, plank timber, full ecosystem services |

A forest planted this way yields first coppice, then pole timbers, and eventually honey, fruit, nuts, bark, and sawlogs as it evolves from a crowded pioneer plantation to a well-spaced mature stand over 15–50 years.

## System Evolution Stages

### Stage A: Initial Establishment
An area is fenced and a complex of species planted. Ponds and water features are established. Tree guards protect seedlings from grazers. Only small livestock (chickens) and some annual crops can be harvested. The system is vulnerable and requires intensive management.

### Stage B: Semi-Hardy Development
Geese, fish, and shellfish are introduced as the system gains stability. Aquatic plant species become productive. The system begins to generate its own mulch and fertility. Some medium-term crops become harvestable.

### Stage C: Mature Self-Reliant System
The evolved system provides forage, firewood, aquatic products, and animal products. Larger foragers (sheep, pigs) can be grown seasonally. The system generates its own mulch and fertilizers. Management replaces energy input as the primary requirement. Multiple marketable yields emerge, including information and educational value.

## Practical Establishment Principles

### Nucleus Development
The most successful approach is to start small and expand outward:
- Begin with a compact 4m² vegetable garden — in the Philippines, such gardens supply 40–60% of household food
- Plant and intensively care for 10 critical trees (citrus, nuts, storable fruits, oil-producing species)
- Ten well-protected, manured, and watered trees outperform a thousand neglected seedlings (which may suffer 60% mortality)

### Prerequisites to Planting
Before any planting begins, four essentials must be in place:
1. **Fencing** or hedgerow to exclude grazers
2. **Soil rehabilitation** through mulching or mechanical loosening
3. **Erosion control** via earthworks, contour plantings, or physical structures
4. **Water supply** for establishment irrigation

All soil shaping for roads, dams, swales, terraces, and paths must be completed before planting commences.

### Design-to-Implementation Sequence
1. Design the site thoroughly on paper
2. Set priorities based on economic reality
3. Locate and trade for components locally or cheaply
4. Develop a nucleus completely before expanding
5. Expand using species proven suited to the site

The same nucleus-development principle applies universally — to erosion control, wildlife rehabilitation, writing books, and creating nations. Break the job into small, easily achieved stages and complete them one at a time.

## Commercial Considerations

In commercial plantings, it is wise to restrict total commercial species to 3–10 reliable plants and trees for manageable harvesting and marketing. The home garden and orchard, however, can maintain far greater diversity of 25–75 species, providing household resilience and nutritional variety.

## The Philosophy of Evolutionary Design

[[permaculture-succession]] design embodies a philosophy of modest, incremental progress: gain a foothold, stabilize a small area, develop self-reliance, then seek exportable yields or commercial gain. Constant transition from present to future state is inevitable — impressive only if widespread. The designer's role is to steer this transition intelligently, using observation and adaptive management rather than imposing rigid blueprints.
