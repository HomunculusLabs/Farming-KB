# Fungi in Ecosystem Primary Production

Fungi play multifaceted and essential roles in moderating the rate of primary production by plants in terrestrial ecosystems. From their partnership in [[lichen-symbiosis]] to their intimate associations with vascular plant roots as mycorrhizae, fungi are integral to the flow of energy and nutrients that sustains ecosystem productivity. Understanding these roles is critical for predicting how ecosystems will respond to environmental change, land use alteration, and biodiversity loss.

## Lichen Symbiosis and Primary Production

As an essential partner in the lichen symbiosis, fungi regulate the water and nutrient supply for photosynthesis performed by their photobiont partners (algae or cyanobacteria). Lichens contribute significantly to net primary production (NPP) in certain ecosystems, particularly in arctic, alpine, and boreal environments where they serve as important food sources for animals during periods of the year when vascular plants are unavailable.

Most quantitative information on lichen primary production comes from arctic and boreal ecosystems, where lichens can form extensive mats covering large areas of the landscape. However, the literature is far from comprehensive regarding NPP figures across a wide variety of ecosystems. Key knowledge gaps remain:

- How much does total ecosystem NPP decline if the lichen community is reduced?
- What are the consequences of lichen removal for organisms higher up the food web?
- How does lichen NPP compare to vascular plant NPP across ecosystem types?

Lichens are sensitive to land use change and atmospheric pollution, making them both indicators of environmental quality and potentially vulnerable components of ecosystem productivity.



## Mycorrhizal Fungi and Plant Nutrition

The effect of mycorrhizae on plant growth extends well beyond simple nutrient uptake. Mycorrhizal associations are diverse in both structure and function:

- **Not all [[mycorrhizal-types]] function identically**: [[arbuscular-mycorrhizae]] (AM), ectomycorrhizae (ECM), ericoid mycorrhizae, and other types differ substantially in their abilities to access inorganic and organic nutrient sources.
- **Species-level variation**: Even within the same mycorrhizal functional group, species differ in their relative efficiencies and responses to environmental stimuli.
- **Environmental mediation**: Different mycorrhizal types achieve dominance depending on environmental conditions and the plant species present, leading to shifts in community composition along environmental gradients.

These differences have ecosystem-wide consequences, as the dominant mycorrhizal type influences nutrient cycling rates, plant community composition, and carbon allocation patterns.




## Mycorrhizal Networks and Resource Sharing

The existence of linkages among plants via mycorrhizal fungal hyphae in natural ecosystems has been conclusively demonstrated (Simard et al., 1997a, b). This discovery has fundamentally altered our understanding of how plant species interact in communities:

- **Intra-specific sharing**: Nutrients can be transferred between individual plants of the same species through [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]].
- **Inter-specific sharing**: More importantly, nutrients are shared among plants of different species, creating belowground connections that may stabilize plant communities.
- **Carbon transfer**: Labeled carbon studies have shown bidirectional or source-sink patterns of carbon movement between interconnected plants.

Critical questions about mycorrhizal networks remain:

- How much resource sharing actually occurs in natural ecosystems?
- Is sharing conditional on asymmetric supply and demand between connected plants?
- How much does resource sharing increase ecosystem stability and resilience?
- How much functional redundancy exists when both mycorrhizal linkages and mycorrhizal diversity are high?
- Do all mycorrhizal species produce inter-plant linkages?




## Mycorrhizal Plasticity and Partner Switching

Traditional categorization of mycorrhizal associations has not adequately accounted for plant species that share different types of mycorrhizae. Evidence now shows:

- Some tree species change their dominant mycorrhizal associates from arbuscular mycorrhizae to ectomycorrhizae as they age, raising questions about the functional significance of these transitions.
- Some ericaceous plants can support ericoid, ecto-, and possibly arbuscular mycorrhizae simultaneously (Dighton and Coleman, 1992; Smith et al., 1995).

The functional significance of this mycorrhizal plasticity remains poorly understood. Why should a plant need to associate with a variety of mycorrhizal types, and does this flexibility have ecosystem-level consequences for nutrient cycling and plant community dynamics?




## Fungal Pathogens and Plant Productivity

Pathogenic fungi exert negative effects on plant growth by reducing photosynthesis and overall host health. However, much of the evidence for these negative effects comes from agricultural literature or studies involving exotic fungi on native plants (or native fungi on exotic plants). This raises important questions about the role of fungal pathogens in natural ecosystems:

- How important are fungal pathogens in natural ecosystems compared to agricultural settings?
- Have current ecosystems co-evolved with a degree of resistance to fungal pathogens, such that only damaged or weakened individuals are significantly impacted?
- Is the balance between plants and fungal pathogens an evolved equilibrium that maintains plant community diversity?




## Ecosystem Coevolution and Balance

The cumulative evidence suggests that fungi play a central role in plant production, but the subtleties of their interactions and the global consequences of plant–fungus relationships remain incompletely understood. A central question pervades mycorrhizal ecology: have the components of ecosystems co-evolved to produce a fine balance among all components, including fungi, which could be disrupted if the fungal community were significantly altered?

As efforts continue to control fungal pests of economically important crops and to manipulate mycorrhizae for agricultural benefit, the long-term effects of such manipulation on natural ecosystem functioning must be carefully considered. The interconnectedness of mycorrhizal networks, the plasticity of mycorrhizal associations, and the subtle balance between symbionts and pathogens all point to a system of remarkable complexity — one where fungal communities serve as both architects and regulators of ecosystem primary production.
