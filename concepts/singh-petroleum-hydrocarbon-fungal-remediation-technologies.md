# Fungal Remediation Technologies for Petroleum Hydrocarbons

Petroleum-contaminated soils and aquifers represent a significant
environmental challenge, threatening soil fertility, drinking water, and
groundwater quality. A wide variety of physical, chemical, and biological
technologies have been established for the decontamination of petroleum-
contaminated sites. Engineered in [[staycare-fungal-in-situ-bioremediation-formulation-strategies]] has received wide
acclaim because it requires little energy, preserves the soil structure,
and can detoxify contaminants in place.

## Treatment Technology Overview

Multiple treatment technologies are available for petroleum hydrocarbon
removal, applicable to both soil and groundwater, and operating either
in situ or ex situ:

**In situ technologies** include air sparging, air biosparging, soil
vapor extraction, soil flushing, bioventing, natural attenuation, and
bioremediation. These approaches treat contamination without excavation,
reducing costs and disturbance to the site.

**Ex situ technologies** include air stripping, carbon adsorption, soil
washing, incineration, and mycorrhizal and white-rot [[singh-biopile-fungal-treatment-contaminated-soil]].
These involve excavation and above-ground treatment, offering greater
control over process conditions.

Phytoremediation is applicable to both soil and groundwater in situ, and
bioremediation is applicable across all media and locations. Complete
mineralization of hydrocarbons to innocuous end products is preferred
over partial degradation to metabolites whose fate and effects may be
unknown.

## In Situ Fungal Bioremediation

Engineered in situ bioremediation requires the stimulation of indigenous
microflora by oxidants and nutrients. Petroleum can be degraded under
aerobic and/or anaerobic conditions, but rapid degradation occurs under
aerobic conditions. Key considerations for in situ fungal remediation
include:

- Bioavailability of hydrocarbons to fungal mycelium
- Adequate oxygen supply through aeration or bioventing
- Nutrient supplementation, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus
- Moisture content maintenance for fungal growth
- pH adjustment to optimal range for fungal activity
- Proper contact between mycelia and soil aggregates

Natural attenuation has become a popular and preferred cost-effective
remedy for hydrocarbon degradation. This approach relies on naturally
occurring biological, chemical, and physical processes to reduce
contaminant concentrations over time. However, natural attenuation may
not achieve cleanup goals within acceptable timeframes at heavily
contaminated sites.

## Ex Situ Fungal Bioremediation

Ex situ approaches offer greater control over environmental conditions
and process monitoring. Land treatment (landfarming) involves the
application of contaminated soil to a prepared area where it is tilled
and amended with nutrients to enhance fungal and microbial degradation.
Slurry bioreactors provide intensive treatment by mixing contaminated
soil with water in controlled vessels, achieving rapid degradation but
at higher cost.

[[singh-fungal-composting-bioremediation-contaminated-soils]] combines contaminated soil with organic
amendments and bulking agents in piles or vessels, creating thermophilic
conditions that accelerate hydrocarbon degradation. White-rot fungi have
been applied ex situ in composting systems to degrade recalcitrant
petroleum components through their ligninolytic enzyme systems.

Phytoremediation has received wide public acclaim as a cost-effective
remedy. Marsh macrophytes play a role in the cleanup of petroleum
hydrocarbons through a combination of mechanisms including direct uptake,
stimulation of rhizosphere microbial communities, and physical
stabilization of contaminated soil.

## Hydrocarbon-Utilizing Fungi

Hydrocarbons in the environment are degraded primarily by bacteria and
fungi. Bacteria and yeasts are generally predominant hydrocarbon
degraders in aquatic ecosystems, while fungi and bacteria are the main
degraders in soil environments. The extent of hydrocarbon biodegradation
by fungi is a function of the ecosystem and local environmental
conditions.

Hydrocarbon-utilizing yeasts and fungi are widely distributed in marine,
freshwater, and soil habitats. Common marine isolates include species of
Aureobasidium, Candida, Rhodotorula, Mortierella, and Trichoderma. A
group of terrestrial fungi capable of degrading crude oil hydrocarbons
includes strains of Acremonium, Graphium, Fusarium, Penicillium,
Paecilomyces, Trichoderma, and members of Sphaeropsidales.

Cladosporium resinae is ubiquitously distributed in oil-contaminated
environments and grows rapidly on n-alkanes and jet fuels. About 96%
(weight basis) of paraffin oil was utilized by [[staycare-cunninghamella-elegans-in-bioremediation]]
during a 5-day period. No single species of microorganisms will
completely degrade any particular oil; degradation involves a consortium
of microorganisms.

## Assessment Methods

Successful biodegradation depends on the presence and activity of fungal
populations capable of degrading target contaminants. Assessment methods
include fungal enumeration using selective media, respirometric tests
measuring CO2 production, and monitoring of hydrocarbon-degrading fungal
counts. Preliminary analyses of total heterotrophic and specific
hydrocarbon-degrading fungal counts provide understanding of soil
biological conditions and the extent of acclimatization of indigenous
fungal populations.

Acidified wort agar is employed for enumerating yeasts introduced into
soil. Fungi are isolated by placing small pieces of tar balls on potato
dextrose agar, V-8 agar, and Gorodkowa's media. Media specific for
hydrocarbon-adapted fungi (HAF) have been developed, where hydrocarbons
serve as the carbon source instead of traditional substrates like malt.
