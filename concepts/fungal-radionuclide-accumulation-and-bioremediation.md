Fungi demonstrate a remarkable capacity to accumulate radionuclides from contaminated environments, a property with significant implications for both environmental radioecology and the development of bioremediation strategies for radioactive waste sites.

## Radionuclide Uptake by Fungi

Fungi can accumulate a wide range of radionuclides, including cesium-137, cesium-134, strontium-90, beryllium-7, cobalt-60, zirconium-95, niobium-95, silver-110m, antimony-125, cerium-144, radium-226, and uranium-238.

The mechanisms of uptake include both active transport (where radionuclides substitute for essential elements in metabolic pathways) and passive biosorption to cell wall components.

Cesium is taken up by fungi through potassium transport pathways, as the ionic radius of cesium is similar to that of potassium.

The affinity of fungi for alkali metals decreases in the order K > Rb > Cs > Na > Li, with relative ratios of approximately 100:42:7:4:0.5.

Some fungal species, however, take up cesium in preference to potassium.

Strontium behaves similarly to calcium in fungal metabolism, and saprotrophic fungi have been shown to translocate strontium from parent minerals through their mycelial networks.

## Chernobyl: A Case Study in Fungal Radionuclide Accumulation

The 1986 Chernobyl nuclear disaster focused international attention on radionuclide accumulation by fungi.

As some 90-95% of all plant species associate with mycorrhizal fungi for nutrient uptake, mycorrhizae are of considerable importance in plant uptake of radionuclides.

Additionally, mushroom fruit bodies and lichens form a substantial part of the diet of animals and humans in many affected countries.

In countries affected by Chernobyl fallout, mushrooms became a persistent source of radiocesium contamination in the human diet.

In some regions, annual radiocesium intake from mushrooms exceeded that from all other food sources combined.

The European Community limit for radioactivity in foodstuffs is 600 Bq/kg, yet levels of 134Cs and 137Cs in fungi from Slovenia ranged from 0.5 kBq/kg dry weight in Cortinarius praestans to 43-44 kBq/kg in Laccaria amethystina and Cortinarius armillatus, up to 80 times the safe consumption limit.

Pre- and post-Chernobyl comparisons showed dramatic increases in fungal radionuclide content.

In the Netherlands, deposition ranged from 500-600 Bq/m2 and pre-Chernobyl mushroom levels were 230-1000 Bq/kg dry weight, rising to 1700-95,000 Bq/kg post-Chernobyl.

In Germany, with deposition of 10,000-40,000 Bq/m2, post-Chernobyl levels reached 25,160-88,700 Bq/kg dry weight.

## Variation Among Fungal Species and Guilds

There is considerable variation in radionuclide accumulation among fungal species, and consistent patterns emerge when comparing trophic guilds.

Mycorrhizal basidiomycete species generally have higher radionuclide levels than saprotrophic species, a trend that is more pronounced for 134Cs than 137Cs.

The highest concentration factors (ratio of radiocesium content of mushroom to substrate) were found in the genera Boletus, Paxillus, Tylopilus, Lactarius, Leccinum, Amanita, Cortinarius, and Suillus, all mycorrhizal genera.

The lowest concentration factors were found in saprotrophic genera including Scleroderma, Lepista, and Agaricus.

Even within a single species, variation can be dramatic.

In Boletus badius, concentration factors ranged from 7 to 99 among isolates.

In Boletus edulis, radiocesium levels varied from 300 to 1800 Bq/kg, and levels of alpha-emitting isotopes ranged from undetectable to 90 MBq/kg.

Fungi also accumulate more radioactivity than other organisms in the ecosystem, making them excellent indicator species of radionuclide contamination.

Comparative studies in Belgium showed that mushroom radiocesium content far exceeded that of soil leaf litter, soil organic horizons, and tree tissues from the same ecosystem.

## Mechanisms of Accumulation and Translocation

The mechanism of radiocesium accumulation involves more than passive uptake.

Gray et al. (1995) demonstrated that translocation of 137Cs through hyphae of Schizophyllum commune was slower than diffusion, suggesting incorporation of the element into structural components of the cytoplasm or hyphal wall.

This reduced rate of movement effectively traps cesium within fungal tissues, leading to long-term bioaccumulation.

Fungi are nondiscrete organisms with extending hyphal networks capable of translocating elements within the thallus.

If a radionuclide were always translocated away from dying regions, translocation would increase the length of time of immobilization into fungal components.

This capacity for internal redistribution distinguishes fungal radionuclide cycling from that of plants and animals.

## Melanized Fungi at Chernobyl

Studies of the walls of the Shelter over Reactor No. 4 at Chernobyl demonstrated that melanized strains of fungi, particularly Alternaria, Cladosporium, and Aureobasidium, were able to survive and colonize highly radioactive concrete.

These melanized fungi not only tolerated extreme radiation levels but in some cases exhibited radiotropism, growing preferentially toward radiation sources.

The presence of fungi at the Chernobyl site raises concerns about myco-corrosion of concrete barrier materials designed to contain radioactive contamination.

Fungal colonization and deterioration of concrete may reduce the theoretically calculated service life of containment structures, potentially causing release of radionuclides into the environment.

## Bioremediation Potential

The ability of fungi to accumulate and immobilize radionuclides suggests potential applications in bioremediation of contaminated sites.

Mycorrhizal fungi could be used to stabilize radionuclides in soil, preventing their leaching into groundwater.

The capacity of fungi to grow toward radiation sources could theoretically be exploited to concentrate radionuclides from contaminated substrates.

Lichens also play an important role in radionuclide retention and distribution, accumulating metals including lead, copper, and radionuclides from atmospheric deposition.

Their capacity to form metal-organic biominerals during growth on metal-rich substrates provides another potential avenue for bioremediation.
