Fungi demonstrate a remarkable capacity to accumulate radionuclides
from contaminated environments, a property with significant implications
for environmental radioecology and the development of
[[comparison-bioremediation-strategies-vs-biostimulation-techniques]]
for radioactive waste sites.
## Radionuclide Uptake by Fungi
Fungi can accumulate a wide range of radionuclides, including
cesium-137, cesium-134, strontium-90, beryllium-7, cobalt-60,
zirconium-95, niobium-95, silver-110m, antimony-125, cerium-144,
radium-226, and uranium-238. The mechanisms of uptake include both
[[diffusion-osmosis-and-active-transport-in-plants]] (where
radionuclides substitute for essential elements in metabolic pathways)
and passive biosorption to cell wall components. Cesium is taken up
through potassium transport pathways, as its ionic radius is similar
to potassium. The affinity of fungi for alkali metals decreases in the
order K > Rb > Cs > Na > Li, with relative ratios of approximately
100:42:7:4:0.5. Some fungal species take up cesium in preference to
potassium. Strontium behaves similarly to calcium in
[[singh-fungal-metabolism-pahs]], and saprotrophic fungi translocate
strontium from parent minerals through their
[[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]].
## Chernobyl: A Case Study
The 1986 Chernobyl disaster focused international attention on fungal
radionuclide accumulation. As 90-95% of plant species associate with
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] for nutrient uptake,
mycorrhizae are of considerable importance in plant radionuclide uptake.
In affected countries, mushrooms became a persistent source of
radiocesium contamination in the human diet. In some regions, annual
radiocesium intake from mushrooms exceeded that from all other food
sources combined. The European Community limit for radioactivity in
foodstuffs is 600 Bq/kg, yet levels in Slovenian fungi ranged from
0.5 kBq/kg in Cortinarius praestans to 80 kBq/kg in Cortinarius
armillatus, up to 80 times the safe limit. Pre- and post-Chernobyl
comparisons showed dramatic increases: in Germany, post-Chernobyl
levels reached 25,160-88,700 Bq/kg dry weight.
## Variation Among Fungal Species and Guilds
Considerable variation exists in radionuclide accumulation among fungal
species. Mycorrhizal basidiomycetes generally have higher radionuclide
levels than saprotrophic species. The highest concentration factors
were found in Boletus, Paxillus, Lactarius, Leccinum, Amanita,
Cortinarius, and Suillus, all mycorrhizal genera. The lowest factors
were found in saprotrophic Scleroderma, Lepista, and Agaricus. Even
within a single species, variation is dramatic: in Boletus badius,
concentration factors ranged from 7 to 99 among isolates. Fungi also
accumulate more radioactivity than other organisms, making them
excellent indicator species of radionuclide contamination.
## Mechanisms of Accumulation and Translocation
Radiocesium accumulation involves more than passive uptake. Gray et al.
(1995) demonstrated that translocation of 137Cs through hyphae of
Schizophyllum commune was slower than diffusion, suggesting
incorporation into structural components of the cytoplasm or hyphal
wall. This effectively traps cesium within fungal tissues, leading to
long-term bioaccumulation. Fungi are nondiscrete organisms with
extending hyphal networks capable of translocating elements within the
thallus, distinguishing fungal radionuclide cycling from that of plants
and animals.
## Melanized Fungi at Chernobyl
Studies of the Shelter over Reactor No. 4 at Chernobyl demonstrated
that melanized strains of Alternaria, Cladosporium, and Aureobasidium
survived and colonized highly radioactive concrete. These fungi not
only tolerated extreme radiation but exhibited radiotropism, growing
preferentially toward radiation sources. Their presence raises concerns
about myco-corrosion of concrete barrier materials designed to contain
radioactive contamination, potentially reducing containment service life
and causing release of radionuclides into the environment.
## Bioremediation Potential
The ability of fungi to accumulate and immobilize radionuclides suggests
potential applications in bioremediation. Mycorrhizal fungi could
stabilize radionuclides in soil, preventing leaching into groundwater.
The capacity of fungi to grow toward radiation sources could be
exploited to concentrate radionuclides from contaminated substrates.
Lichens also play an important role, accumulating metals and
radionuclides from atmospheric deposition and forming metal-organic
biominerals on metal-rich substrates.
