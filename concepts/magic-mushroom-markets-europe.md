## Markets and Legal Status of Hallucinogenic Mushrooms in Europe

## Overview

The European market for hallucinogenic mushrooms evolved rapidly from the late
1990s through the mid-2000s, driven by smartshops, internet commerce, and a
broader "return to nature" trend among drug users. Legal responses followed
quickly, with six EU countries tightening legislation between 2001 and 2006.

## The Smartshop Model

The **Netherlands** pioneered the commercial model for hallucinogenic mushrooms
through **smartshops** — retail outlets specializing in psychoactive herbal
substances that are (or were) legal, alongside vitamins and supplements. These
shops sold mushrooms both **fresh and dried**, as well as:

- **Grow kits** (complete home cultivation setups)
- **Spore prints** and **spore syringes** for home cultivation
- **Spawn bags** containing inoculated substrate

The smartshop model created a legitimate retail channel that normalized
[[homestead-community-building]]
- Online sales transcended national borders and legal restrictions

## Product Types and Brand Names

Marketed mushrooms came in several forms with various brand/trade names:

| Product Type | Description |
|-------------|-------------|
| Fresh mushrooms | Whole fruiting bodies, sold refrigerated |
| Dried mushrooms | Dehydrated, longer shelf life |
| Mushroom chocolate | Dried mushrooms incorporated into chocolate |
| Truffles/sclerotia | *P. mexicana* and *P. tampanensis* sclerotia marketed as "philosopher"s stones" |
| Grow kits | Pre-inoculated substrate for home cultivation |
| Spore syringes | For inoculating substrate (legal in most jurisdictions) |

Common varieties marketed included *P. cubensis* strains: Mexican, Thai,
Colombian, Amazonian, Hawaiian, and others. The "Philosopher"s Stone"
(truffles from *P. mexicana* and *P. tampanensis*) were marketed as a more
potent and discreet alternative.
