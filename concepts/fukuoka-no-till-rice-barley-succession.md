# No-Till Rice Barley Succession

The no-till rice/barley succession method is the centerpiece of Masanobu Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]] system. Developed over thirty years of experimentation, it allows rice and winter grain to be grown in continuous rotation on the same field without plowing, flooding, transplanting, chemical fertilizers, or pesticides.



## The Basic Method

The annual cycle of the no-till rice/barley succession is:

1. Fall (September-October): Sow clover seed over standing rice heads
2. Late fall (October-November): Sow barley seed two weeks before rice harvest
3. Rice harvest: Cut rice while trampling young barley shoots. Scatter rice straw uncut over field
4. Winter (November-December): Enclose rice seed in clay pellets, scatter over field
5. Spring: Clover grows thick. Rice seedlings emerge through clover. Barley matures
6. Late spring (late May): Harvest barley. Scatter barley straw over field
7. Early summer: Flood field briefly to weaken clover, then drain
8. Summer: Rice grows to maturity with intermittent irrigation
9. Fall: Harvest rice. Begin cycle again

This method requires roughly 3-4 hours of work per quarter-acre per year -- a fraction of the labor required by conventional rice farming.



## The Three Stages of Development

Fukuoka arrived at this method through three stages of experimentation:

Stage 1 (1940s): Tillage, ridging, and drilling -- the conventional method
Stage 2 (1950): Light-tillage, low-ridge cultivation -- Fukuoka discovered that naked barley could be grown with minimal tillage
Stage 3 (1960s): Complete no-tillage with clover ground cover and straw mulching -- the "clover revolution"



## The Role of Clover

White clover is the key to the system. It serves multiple functions:

- Nitrogen fixation: Clover is a legume that fixes atmospheric nitrogen, providing fertility for both rice and barley
- Weed suppression: The thick clover cover prevents winter weeds from establishing
- Soil protection: Clover protects the soil surface from erosion and maintains soil moisture
- Organic matter: When cut and left on the field, clover adds nitrogen and organic matter to the soil



## The Role of Straw

Rice and barley straw scattered uncut over the field serves as mulch, protecting the soil, suppressing weeds, and providing organic matter. Fukuoka was emphatic that straw must be scattered loosely, not neatly arranged. "The more disorderly the scattered straw the better."

The conventional practice of burning or composting straw was, in Fukuoka's view, a catastrophic waste. Straw returned directly to the field maintains soil fertility without the labor of composting.



## Clay Pellet Seed Encapsulation

For winter sowing of rice, seeds are enclosed in clay pellets to protect them from rodents and birds. The simplest method is to mix seeds with five to ten times their volume of clay, add water, knead, pass through a half-inch screen, and dry. The pellets break down naturally in spring, allowing the rice seed to germinate.

This technique was later adapted by Fukuoka for desert revegetation -- the [[fukuoka-clay-pellet-desert-greening]] for sowing seeds in degraded landscapes.



## Yield Performance

Fukuoka reported yields of up to 50 bushels per quarter-acre (approximately 1.25 tons), comparable to or exceeding conventional methods. His old variety of glutinous rice from the Tokugawa Period produced an average of 250 grains per head with 12-18 tillers per plant.

The key to high yields was not variety or inputs but plant health. Rice grown in natural conditions -- with strong roots, robust stalks, and erect leaves -- naturally produced well.



## Water Management

Unlike conventional rice farming, which floods paddies for months, natural rice farming uses intermittent irrigation. The field is flooded for 4-7 days in early summer to weaken the clover, then drained. During the growing season, water is passed briefly over the field once a week to ten days. Soil moisture is maintained at about eighty percent.

This method preserves soil structure, maintains aerobic conditions for soil organisms, and reduces water use by a significant margin.



## Verification by Research Stations

After years of skepticism, agricultural testing stations in Ehime and Aichi Prefectures confirmed that direct-seeded, no-till cultivation produced the highest yields. The Ehime Prefectural Agricultural Testing Station found that winter-sown, direct-seeded no-till cultivation gave the best results -- a method Fukuoka had first reported in 1961.

It took the research establishment twenty years to catch up with what Fukuoka had been practicing all along.
