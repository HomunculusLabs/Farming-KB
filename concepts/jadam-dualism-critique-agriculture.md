# JADAM Dualism Critique in Agriculture

## Overview

"The good and the bad are one" is the third principle of JADAM organic farming, drawn from the Buddhist and Confucian philosophical tradition. It is a direct critique of the dualistic thinking that JADAM argues has been weaponized by agricultural corporations to control farmers and maximize profits.

## The Core Principle

The principle means that good and bad, right and wrong, sacred and secular, heaven and hell are actually one. There is no absolute value in this world. It negates the dualistic, separate view of light versus darkness or good versus evil. The concept is comparable to the "Middle Way" (Doctrine of the Mean) of Confucianism -- living without imbalances, without excess or want.

In practical agricultural terms: "Too much medicine (good) is poison (evil); a little poison (evil), however, can be medicine (good)."

## Applications in Farming

### Microorganisms

The most consequential application concerns microorganisms. Modern agriculture divides microbes into "good" (beneficial) and "bad" (harmful) categories, then sells products containing only the "good" ones. JADAM's critique:

- Less than 1% of soil microorganisms can be cultured by modern science
- Commercial products represent at best a tiny fraction of total microbial diversity
- Over 99% of soil microorganisms are unknown to us
- Selecting only a few species disrupts the natural balance
- Change in microorganism composition leads directly to change in nutrient availability
- "Microorganisms equal nutrients" -- selecting microbes means selecting nutrients

In nature, all microorganisms serve a function. Even Bacillus anthracis (canker cause) and E. coli are not always harmful -- they play specific roles in their ecological context.

### Nutrients

The same principle applies to nutrients. It is tempting to label certain elements as "good" (calcium, phosphate, germanium, selenium, silicate) and add them to soil. But "organic farming is not this simple. You will never succeed in farming with this method of 'selecting the good and putting it in.'"

### Beneficial Insects

Ladybugs are not always good -- too many become pests. A moderate number of apple snails control weeds but overpopulation makes them a nuisance. Nematodes are not always bad -- adequate numbers maintain soil health. No life can be labeled good or evil.

### Fertilizer Balance

"The good and the bad are one" tells us to find the "balance" instead of tilting toward good or bad. JADAM identifies two types of balance:
1. **Balance of microorganisms**: Wide variety and abundance
2. **Balance of fertilization**: Nutrients balanced for both crops and microorganisms

## The Political Dimension

JADAM argues that dualism has been deliberately used as a tool of agricultural control:
- Whatever is in the hands of corporations is labeled "good"
- Whatever farmers make for themselves is labeled "bad, risky, unscientific"
- Science is introduced to provide stronger evidence for corporate claims
- The result: "the victory of dualistic farming over monistic farming"

Traditionally, microorganisms, compost, and liquid fertilizer were all made by farmers for thousands of years. This traditional knowledge was labeled "unscientific," and farmers were pressured to abandon it. "Farmers are deprived of farming tools, methods of farming and finally of confidence in farming."

## Technology Rooted in Philosophy

"Technology is rooted on philosophy. If the way of thinking that provides the foundation for the technology is undermined, then it can no longer stand on its own." The fall of traditional farming began with the introduction of Western epistemology (theory of knowledge), which prioritized what could be measured and categorized over what had been proven through millennia of practice.

Science is not objective and unchanging -- it is constantly evolving. Where does the funding for agricultural research come from? Research funded by private corporations tends to produce findings that serve commercial interests rather than the public good.

## The Farmer as Sage

A farmer who walks the Middle Way will be mindful and not become attached to something labeled good, nor blindly reject something labeled bad. When encountering a new fertilizer hailed as nutrient-rich, the wise farmer maintains caution about the potential danger of overusing it.

This is the ultimate implication: "As this farmer ages, s/he gains more wisdom from farming; that wisdom penetrates through all other facets of life. Knowledge in farming directly becomes knowledge of life."

## The Role of Technology in Non-Dualistic Agriculture

JADAM's approach to agricultural technology exemplifies its philosophical stance on dualism. Rather than accepting or rejecting modern agricultural technology categorically, JADAM practitioners evaluate each technology based on whether it increases or decreases the farmer's dependence on external inputs. Technologies that can be produced on-farm using local materials (such as JMS [[jadam-microbial-solution-jms-preparation-and-use]], JADAM wetting agent, and homemade fertilizers) are embraced because they strengthen the farmer's self-sufficiency. Technologies that create dependency on purchased inputs (such as synthetic pesticides, commercial fertilizers, and patented seeds) are rejected, even if they produce short-term yield increases. This criterion is fundamentally non-dualistic: it does not judge technology as inherently good or bad, but evaluates it in terms of its effect on the farmer's relationship with the agricultural system.

## Comparison with Masanobu Fukuoka's Philosophy

JADAM's dualism critique shares significant philosophical ground with Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming, though the two systems differ in practical application. Both reject the fundamental dualism between human agriculture and nature, arguing that the distinction is artificial and harmful. Both emphasize that the farmer's role is to facilitate natural processes rather than to impose human will upon them. However, Fukuoka advocated for a more radical "do-nothing" approach, minimizing all human intervention, while JADAM allows for more active management through on-farm preparations and biological inputs. Young-sang Cho, JADAM's founder, explicitly acknowledges his debt to Fukuoka while arguing that Fukuoka's approach is too difficult for most farmers to adopt in its pure form. JADAM can be understood as a pragmatic adaptation of Fukuoka's non-dualistic philosophy that makes it accessible to a wider audience.

## Practical Outcomes of the Non-Dualistic Approach

The non-dualistic philosophy produces concrete, measurable outcomes in farm management. JADAM farmers typically report dramatic reductions in input costs (often by 70-90%) because most inputs are produced on-farm. Soil health improves over time as chemical inputs are eliminated and biological inputs restore soil food web function. Pest and disease pressure often decreases as the farm ecosystem achieves greater balance and biodiversity. The farmer's knowledge and observation skills deepen as they shift from following external prescriptions to responding to the specific conditions of their own land. Perhaps most importantly, the non-dualistic approach eliminates the anxiety and uncertainty that characterize conventional agriculture's dependence on purchased inputs and external advice — the farmer becomes the expert on their own farm.

## Criticisms and Limitations

JADAM's philosophical approach has attracted criticism from several perspectives. Conventional agronomists argue that the rejection of synthetic inputs limits yield potential and that on-farm preparations cannot provide the precise nutrient management that modern crops require. Some organic farmers find JADAM's complete rejection of all purchased inputs impractical, particularly for specific nutrient deficiencies or pest outbreaks that exceed the capacity of on-farm biological solutions. Academic researchers have called for more rigorous field trials to quantify the effectiveness of JADAM methods compared to both [[jadam-conventional-and-organic-pesticide-integration]] systems. JADAM practitioners respond that the philosophy is not a rigid dogma but a direction of approach, and that individual farmers must adapt the principles to their specific context rather than applying any system as a fixed formula.

## JADAM Microorganism Solution (JMS)

The JADAM Microorganism Solution (JMS) is the central practical technology of the JADAM system and exemplifies the non-dualistic philosophy in action. JMS is produced by collecting microorganisms from the local natural environment (leaf litter, forest soil, or compost) and culturing them in a solution of boiled potato water and sea salt. Unlike commercial microbial inoculants that contain specific, selected strains, JMS embraces total microbial diversity — the full community of bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes, and other microorganisms present in the local environment. This approach reflects the non-dualistic principle that nature's complexity cannot be improved upon by human selection: rather than trying to identify and culture the "best" microorganisms, JMS preserves the entire natural [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]]. The sea salt in JMS serves dual purposes: it provides minerals that support microbial growth and selectively inhibits human pathogens, creating a safe product for foliar application.

## Scaling JADAM Methods for Different Farm Sizes

JADAM methods are designed to be scalable from backyard gardens to large commercial farms, which reflects the non-dualistic philosophy that good farming practices are not dependent on scale. For small-scale growers, JMS can be produced in 20-liter buckets with minimal equipment. For medium-scale farms, 200-liter drums serve as production vessels. For large commercial operations, JMS production can be scaled to 1,000-liter tanks or larger, using simple aeration systems to maintain oxygen levels. The JADAM wetting agent (a surfactant produced from rapeseed oil and potassium hydroxide) replaces commercial surfactants in pesticide and foliar spray applications, reducing input costs by up to 90% compared to commercial alternatives. This scalability demonstrates that the non-dualistic approach — working with nature's existing diversity rather than substituting commercial products — is economically viable at any scale.

## Non-Dualistic Approaches to Soil Management

The non-dualistic philosophy extends deeply into JADAM's soil management practices. Rather than approaching soil as an inert substrate requiring chemical amendment — the dualistic framework of conventional agriculture — JADAM treats soil as a living system whose health emerges from the interactions of its biological components. JADAM's leaf mold method, for example, does not attempt to provide specific NPK ratios but instead feeds the soil food web with decomposing leaf litter, trusting the resulting microbial community to make nutrients available in appropriate forms and quantities. The philosophy rejects soil testing as a management tool for most growers, arguing that biological indicators (earthworm activity, soil structure, organic matter content, root health) provide more meaningful information than chemical analysis. This does not mean JADAM ignores soil chemistry entirely — Young-sang Cho acknowledges that severely degraded soils may need mineral amendments — but the default approach is to trust biological processes over chemical prescriptions, intervening only when clear evidence of deficiency emerges.

## Comparison with Other Non-Dualistic Agricultural Philosophies

JADAM's non-dualistic critique shares philosophical ground with several other agricultural movements while maintaining distinct practical approaches. Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming similarly rejects the separation of humans from nature in agriculture, advocating for a "do-nothing" approach that parallels JADAM's emphasis on minimal intervention. Rudolf Steiner's [[query-what-is-biodynamic-agriculture-and-how-does-it-differ-from-organic]], while incorporating spiritual elements absent from JADAM, shares the holistic view of the farm as a self-sustaining organism rather than a production machine. Permaculture, as developed by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, similarly seeks to work with natural patterns rather than against them, though permaculture's emphasis on landscape design and element placement differs from JADAM's focus on input self-sufficiency and microbial technology. What distinguishes JADAM from these other approaches is its emphasis on practical technology (JMS, the JADAM wetting agent, JADAM sulfur solutions) that can be produced on-farm at near-zero cost — making the philosophy accessible to farmers regardless of their financial resources or technical expertise.

## The Non-Dualistic View of Pests and Disease

In conventional agriculture, the dualistic framework separates crops from pests, treating the latter as external enemies that must be destroyed with chemical weapons. JADAM's non-dualistic philosophy reframes this relationship entirely: [[query-how-to-manage-pests-and-diseases-in-mushroom-cultivation]] are understood as indicators of ecological imbalance rather than autonomous threats. When a pest outbreak occurs, the JADAM practitioner asks not "how do I kill this pest?" but "what condition in my farm ecosystem has allowed this pest to thrive?" This shift in questioning leads to fundamentally different management responses — instead of reaching for an insecticide, the farmer addresses soil health, plant nutrition, biodiversity, and habitat management. JADAM's natural pesticide solutions (JMS-based sprays, sulfur preparations, oil-based coatings) are used sparingly and as supplements to ecological pest management rather than as primary control strategies. Cho argues that a truly healthy farm ecosystem — one with diverse plantings, active soil biology, and balanced nutrient cycling — naturally suppresses pest and disease pressure to manageable levels without requiring intervention.
