Springtails (Collembola) are among the most abundant and active
microarthropods in soil ecosystems. Belonging to the class Insecta,
springtails range from 0.2 to 2 millimeters in length and can reach
population densities of up to 100 individuals per square inch in
organic-rich soils. Along with mites, springtails are responsible for
recycling up to 30% of the leaves and [[fungal-succession-woody-debris-ecology]] deposited on
[[guzman-magic-mushrooms-temperate-zone-species]] forest floors.

## Morphology and Locomotion

Springtails lack wings but possess a distinctive forked tail structure
called a furcula, which folds beneath the body. When threatened, fluid
rapidly pours into the base of the furcula, causing it to snap outward
and propel the animal up to a meter backward -- hence the common name
"springtail." This escape mechanism allows springtails to rapidly
evade predators such as [[teaming-with-microbes-decomposition-pathways]].

### Nutrient Cycling

When springtails consume fungi and bacteria, they digest what they
need and excrete the excess as waste, contributing to nutrient
[[root-exudates]].

### Microbial Transport

Springtails function as taxis for microorganisms. Bacteria, fungal
spores, and other microbes attach to their bodies or get carried in
debris as springtails move through soil. Since springtails are
themselves prey for larger organisms -- including mites, beetles,
spiders, and other predators -- the microbes they carry can be
transported considerable distances through the food web.

### Food Web Indicators

Springtail populations are useful bioindicators of soil health. Their
presence in good numbers indicates adequate [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], proper
moisture, and a functioning [[soil-compaction-solutions-ingham]] and chemical
applications. Tillage and pesticide use can devastate springtail
populations, reducing their contributions to decomposition and
nutrient cycling.
