# Holzer Irrigation Principles

Holzer's approach to irrigation begins with observing how nature waters
plants. A healthy, mixed forest functions like a sponge. The ground, leaves,
roots, ponds, and puddles are all full of water. Understanding this natural
system is the foundation of all irrigation practice.

## How Nature Irrigates

In a natural forest ecosystem:

1. Saturated ground provides moisture to ground-covering vegetation from
   below
2. Vegetation protects the soil while roots air and activate it, keeping
   the [[comparison-swale-vs-contour-line-water-management]] intact
3. A layer of fallen leaves protects the soil from drying out
4. Fallen wood and root trunks absorb water during decomposition and
   slowly release it to neighbouring plant roots
5. Temperature, air pressure, and humidity influence the evaporation and
   absorption cycle

### The Soil's Natural Moisture Mechanism

Bare ground forms a protecting crust to seal in moisture. Before rainfall,
humidity in the air increases, signalling the ground to open and absorb
moisture. After rain passes, humidity drops and the ground closes again to
retain moisture. This is a universal law that Holzer recommends verifying
in any environment.

## Rules of Thumb for Irrigation

### 1. Hydrological Balance First

The most important and sustainable approach is maintaining a good
hydrological balance. This ensures sufficient dew and vegetation that
prevents soil from drying out. [[holzer-water-landscapes-pond-building]] are the primary tool for achieving this.

### 2. Water Less

Most gardeners water too much too often, which spoils plants and makes them
dependent. Plants need watering after being planted out, but after that,
watering should be reduced. Plants will then develop deeper roots, bringing
up water and nutrients from greater depths, creating healthier root systems
that aerate the soil.

### 3. Keep Ground Covered

Maintain vegetation cover throughout the year. [[holzer-intercropping-by-height-suntraps]]
ensures plants support themselves and maintain soil moisture.

### 4. Mulch Appropriately

Mulching is excellent when the ground is not covered with vegetation:
- Materials: straw, leaves, any organic matter, cardboard, or natural fibres
- Fruit trees benefit from thick mulch around the trunk
- Best time: directly after sowing or planting out
- Mulch suppresses weeds while seeds germinate and seedlings push through
- Important: do not mulch once seeds have already germinated

### 5. Never Water from the Top

Sprinklers are common but do more harm than good. They simulate rain,
causing the ground to open to receive water, but when the sprinkler is
turned off, the ground remains open and water evaporates quickly. Additional
problems with top-watering:
- Damages leaves, leading to mildew
- High water usage with low utilisation
- Frequent watering washes nutrients away from roots into groundwater
  where roots cannot reach them

### 6. Water Close to the Ground

Water in traditional trenches, with drip hoses, or by hand-watering close
to the plant stem. Drip hoses can run above ground or be buried. Only use
them in the evenings.

### 7. Water Only After Sunset

Most water evaporates during the day, and plants cannot utilise enough.
At night, temperatures are lower and humidity is higher, creating ideal
conditions for maximum absorption and effect.

### 8. Do Not Panic About Wilting

Plants wilting slightly during the day is part of their protection
mechanism to stop water from evaporating. Watering wilted plants achieves
the opposite of the desired effect because the plant takes time to reopen.
By the time the plant is ready to absorb water, the sprinkler has been
turned off and moisture has evaporated, leaving the plant worse off.

### 9. Do Not Water Grain

Watering grain is unnecessary unless the region is extremely hot and dry.
Instead, undersow with [[holzer-green-manure-crops]] that
will protect and improve the soil.

### 10. Bury Wood for Desertification Areas

In regions affected by [[desertification-causes-reversal]],
bury wood in trenches and plant on top. Wood binds water like a sponge,
and plants benefit from this moisture reserve. This is the same principle
behind [[holzer-crater-gardens-hugelkultur]].

## Drip Irrigation

Drip irrigation (specifically drip hoses) is the method Holzer recommends
for supplemental watering when natural hydrological balance is insufficient.
Drip hoses:
- Deliver water directly to root zones
- Minimise evaporation losses
- Can be placed above ground or buried
- Should only be operated in the evening

The book describes a project in Spain where replacing sprinkler irrigation
with drip systems achieved approximately 30% water savings while improving
plant health.

## Integration with Broader Water Management

Irrigation is just one aspect of [[holzer-water-landscapes-pond-building]]. The hierarchy of importance is:

1. Establish hydrological balance through [[holzer-water-landscapes-pond-building]] and [[comparison-swale-vs-contour-line-water-management]]
2. Build soil organic matter to increase water retention capacity
3. Maintain permanent vegetation cover
4. Use mulch where vegetation cover is absent
5. Supplement with targeted drip irrigation only when necessary
