# Hallucinogens and Shamanism — Harner Anthology

The 1973 Oxford University Press anthology edited by Michael J. Harner, which brought together groundbreaking ethnographic research on the role of psychoactive plants in [[ceremonial-shamanic-use-psychoactive-mushrooms]] traditions worldwide.

## Context and Purpose

Published in 1973 by Oxford University Press, "Hallucinogens and Shamanism" emerged from a period of renewed anthropological interest in psychoactive substances. As Harner noted in his preface, anthropologists had long been interested in the function of peyote in North American Indian cultures but had generally neglected the ethnological importance of other natural hallucinogens, such as psychotropic mushrooms and solanaceous plants.

Harner identified a key reason for this neglect: very few anthropologists had themselves partaken of native psychotropic materials (other than peyote) or had undergone the resulting subjective experiences, which he considered "so critical, perhaps paradoxically, to an empirical understanding of their meaning to the peoples they studied."

## The Three Sections

The anthology is organized into three thematic sections representing increasing degrees of cultural complexity:

### I. In the Primitive World: The Upper Amazon

The first section presents ethnographic research from relatively isolated Amazonian societies where hallucinogen use remains integral to traditional shamanism. This section includes:

Kenneth M. Kensinger's "Banisteriopsis Usage Among the Peruvian Cashinahua" — documenting communal ayahuasca use among the Cashinahua people of eastern Peru.

Michael J. Harner's "The Sound of Rushing Water" — a landmark account of Jivaro shamanism and the use of natem (ayahuasca) among the Jivaro of Ecuador.

Janet Siskind's "Visions and Cures Among the Sharanahua" — describing the role of ayahuasca in healing among the Sharanahua of Peru.

Gerald Weiss's "Shamanism and Priesthood in Light of the Campa Ayahuasca Ceremony" — comparing shamanic and priestly dimensions of Campa ayahuasca rituals.

### II. In Cultures Undergoing Westernization

The second section examines hallucinogen use in societies experiencing cultural change and Western influence:

L. Bryce Boyer, Ruth M. Boyer, and Harry W. Basehart's study of peyote use among the Mescalero Apache — documenting the unusual case of peyote adoption and subsequent rejection.

Marlene Dobkin de Rios's "Curing with Ayahuasca in an Urban Slum" — describing the use of ayahuasca by mestizo shamans in the Peruvian city of Iquitos.

Henry Munn's "The Mushrooms of Language" — a poetic and phenomenological account of Mazatec mushroom use in Mexico.

### III. In the Traditional Western World

The third section connects Western historical practices to shamanic patterns:

Michael J. Harner's "The Role of Hallucinogenic Plants in European Witchcraft" — arguing that solanaceous plants played a central role in medieval and Renaissance witchcraft, linking European practices to shamanic traditions.

### IV. The Question of a Trans-Cultural Experience

The final section addresses the question of whether hallucinogenic experiences share common features across cultures:

Harner's "Common Themes in South American Indian Yage Experiences" — surveying cross-cultural patterns in ayahuasca visions.

Claudio Naranjo's "Psychological Aspects of the Yage Experience in an Experimental Setting" — comparing the experiences of indigenous ayahuasca users with those of experimental subjects in Chile.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

The anthology made several important theoretical contributions:

### The Centrality of Hallucinogens in Shamanism

Harner argued that hallucinogenic plants, while not universal in shamanism, constitute "the most reliable and culturally widespread method of inducing the shamanic trance state" across the Americas, parts of Africa, and Asia. The use of hallucinogens to achieve trance states constitutes, in his view, one of the four major methods of entering the shamanic trance (along with auditory driving, visual stimulation, and extreme pain).

### The Importance of Firsthand Experience

The anthology implicitly argued that anthropologists who had not experienced hallucinogenic states themselves were poorly equipped to understand the role of these substances in the cultures they studied. Most of the contributors had personally participated in the rituals they described.

### Cross-Cultural Patterns

The anthology documented remarkable similarities in hallucinogenic experiences across widely separated cultures, raising fundamental questions about the relationship between pharmacology, consciousness, and culture.

### The European Connection

Harner's chapter on European witchcraft broke new ground by arguing that the use of psychoactive solanaceous plants in medieval Europe represented a form of shamanism, connecting Western historical practices to the broader cross-cultural patterns documented in the anthology.

## The Harner-Jivaro Experience

Harner's own chapter contains one of the most influential first-person accounts in the anthropological literature. In 1961, while conducting fieldwork among the Conibo Indians of eastern Peru, he drank ayahuasca and found himself "in a world literally beyond my wildest dreams." He met bird-headed people and dragon-like creatures who explained they were the true gods of this world.

This experience led him to return to the Jivaro in 1964 to give particular attention to the drug's use in shamanic practice. He concluded that "anthropologists, including myself, had profoundly underestimated the importance of the drug in affecting native ideology."

## Legacy

The anthology was among the first major academic publications to take seriously the role of psychoactive substances in shamanic and religious experience across cultures. It influenced the development of ethnopharmacology, cognitive anthropology, and the modern study of consciousness.

Its emphasis on the importance of firsthand experience in understanding other cultures prefigured later developments in anthropological method and theory. Its documentation of cross-cultural patterns in hallucinogenic experience contributed to the emerging field of transpersonal psychology.

## Merged: Shamanic Divination and Healing with Entheogens
Shamanic rituals involving hallucinogenic plants represent one of the oldest known systems of healing and knowledge-seeking. Across diverse indigenous cultures worldwide, entheogenic plants and fungi have been used for millennia within carefully structured ceremonial contexts to facilitate healing, diagnosis, and divination. The concept of [[amazonian-plant-teachers-doctores]] is central to Amazonian vegetalismo traditions, where different plants are understood to teach different things: some teach healing, others teach singing, and still others teach about the forest ecosystem or the spirit world. ## Merged: Shipibo Healing Traditions and Icaros
The Shipibo-Conibo people of the Ucayali River in eastern Peru maintain one of the most elaborated and well-documented shamanic healing traditions in the Amazon Basin. ## Merged: Michael Harner
American anthropologist (1929-2018), founder of the Foundation for Shamanic Studies, and editor of the seminal anthology "Hallucinogens and Shamanism" (1973). Michael Harner was an anthropologist who conducted fieldwork among the Jivaro (Shuar) people of the Ecuadorian Amazon and later became one of the most influential figures in the modern neo-shamanic movement. Harner's work forced a serious academic reckoning with the centrality of psychoactive plants in shamanic practice worldwide, and his anthology became a foundational text for the emerging field of [[entheogen-culture]] studies.
