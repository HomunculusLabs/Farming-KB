In *Teaming With Microbes*, Lowenfels and Lewis mount a thorough critique of
chemical fertilizers, arguing that they undermine the very [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] that sustains healthy plants. While
synthetic fertilizers deliver immediate results by feeding plant roots directly,
they bypass soil biology and set off a destructive cycle that progressively
degrades soil health. The authors present chemical fertilizers not as plant food
but as chemicals that kill the microbes holding and cycling nutrients.

## How Chemical Fertilizers Kill Microbes

Chemical fertilizers are salts, and when they come into contact with soil
microorganisms, they cause osmotic shock. Water in the cells of
[[lowenfels-bacteria-role-soil]] and [[lowenfels-fungi-role-soil]] flows toward the higher concentration of salts outside, literally
bursting through cell walls and killing the organisms. A good rule of thumb is
that 100 pounds of nitrogen lawn fertilizer per acre will wipe out a healthy
soil food web. Lesser quantities do proportional damage.  What is not killed
outright is driven away by the lack of food resources or by the odor of the
fertilizers themselves. Nematodes and protozoa that cycle nutrients are also
destroyed.

## The Vicious Cycle

The use of chemical fertilizers creates a self-reinforcing cycle of decline.
When fertilizers kill the microbes that hold and cycle nutrients, nutrients
become unavailable without further fertilizer applications. The natural
buffering action of bacteria and fungi is lost, and soil pH drifts lower as more
nitrate is applied, eventually requiring lime or other amendments. Removing
grass clippings while mowing compounds the problem by depriving soil of organic
matter that could feed remaining microbes. The more fertilizer applied, the more
the soil food web is destroyed, and the more fertilizer becomes necessary,
creating a downward spiral that ends in degraded soil dependent on chemical
inputs.

## Impact on Soil Structure

Chemical fertilizers indirectly destroy [[lowenfels-soil-structure-aggregates]]. The [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] that create burrows and aerate soil leave when salts are applied.
The [[lowenfels-glomalin-soil-aggregation]] that produce glomalin to bind
soil aggregates are eliminated. The [[lowenfels-bacteria-role-soil]]
that produce slime binding individual soil particles into aggregates are gone.
Without these organisms, soil loses its crumb structure, its ability to hold air
and water diminishes, and it becomes increasingly compacted. What was once
living, porous soil gradually becomes inert mineral dust.

## Loss of Natural Disease Defenses

A healthy soil food web provides multiple layers of defense against plant
disease. Fungi and bacteria serve as barriers around plant roots, blocking entry
of pathogens and competing for nutrients, space, and even oxygen that disease
organisms need. When chemical fertilizers destroy this protective community,
plants become vulnerable to opportunistic pathogens.  Lawns treated with
chemical fertilizers are frequently infested with mildew, black spot, rots, and
gray mold. Dollar spot, one of the most troublesome diseases of golf course
greens, can be caused by excessive nitrates in chemical fertilizers.

## Effects on Earthworms

[[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] are particularly sensitive to
chemical fertilizers. Salts are direct irritants to worms, causing them to flee
treated areas. When fertilizers are ingested, the gut microbes responsible for
worm digestion die, effectively starving the worms. Since earthworms are a clear
indicator of a healthy food web, their disappearance signals the collapse of
soil biology. Without worms, the shredding of organic matter, aeration of soil,
aggregation of particles, and movement of microorganisms through soil profiles
all cease, compounding the damage done by fertilizer applications.

## Pollution and Environmental Costs

Chemical fertilizers contribute to significant environmental pollution.
Nitrates that are not absorbed by plants leach into groundwater and run off into
waterways, causing algal blooms and dead zones. The fertilizer industry itself
has a substantial carbon footprint from manufacturing and transportation.
Lowenfels and Lewis point out that a healthy soil food web can actually degrade
many pollutants, including some of the chemicals deposited in soil. By
destroying the soil food web, chemical fertilizers eliminate this natural
remediation capacity, making soils more vulnerable to contamination from all
sources.

## Pesticides, Herbicides, and Fungicides

The damage extends beyond fertilizers. Pesticides, herbicides, and fungicides
are nonselective substances that kill beneficial soil organisms along with
target pests. Both bacteria and fungi can degrade pesticides, but it is mostly
the fungi that handle the more complex chemicals, and their populations are
already suppressed by fertilizer use. Air pollution, fungicides, and herbicides
further damage soil biology.  Materials contaminated with these chemicals should
never be added to [[lowenfels-composting-science-microbiology]] piles
because they kill the organisms that drive the composting process.

## The Alternative

The alternative to chemical dependence is building soil biology through organic
matter, [[lowenfels-composting-science-microbiology]],
[[lowenfels-mulch-cover-cropping]], and [[lowenfels-compost-tea-brewing]]. Once a healthy soil food web is established, plants
receive nutrients through natural cycling, soil structure is maintained by
biological activity, and disease is suppressed by competition from beneficial
organisms. The transition may take months or years, but the end result is soil
that grows plants better with less work and fewer inputs. The initial investment
in building biology pays dividends for years to come.

## Summary

Chemical fertilizers destroy soil food web organisms through osmotic shock,
initiating a vicious cycle of increasing dependency on chemical inputs. They
degrade soil structure, eliminate natural disease defenses, repel earthworms,
and contribute to water pollution. Combined with pesticides and herbicides, they
progressively strip soil of its living component. Building soil biology through
compost, mulch, and compost teas offers a sustainable alternative that produces
healthier plants with less ongoing work and environmental impact.

Conventional farming that relies on chemical inputs enters a destructive
feedback loop: as soil biology declines, plants become more dependent on
synthetic fertilizers, which further damage biology. Breaking this cycle
requires a deliberate transition period of building organic matter and
reintroducing microbial communities through compost and compost tea
applications. The investment in biological soil management pays dividends
in reduced input costs and improved plant resilience over time.
