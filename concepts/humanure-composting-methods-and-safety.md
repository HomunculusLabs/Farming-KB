# Humanure Composting Methods and Safety

The recycling of human waste for agricultural use is one of the most
controversial yet potentially valuable practices in [[solomon-soil-freezing-requirements-sustainable-gardening]].
Steve Solomon addresses it directly in *Gardening When It Counts*, drawing
on Joseph Jenkins' *The Humanure Handbook* and his own wife's childhood
experience growing up on a Tasmanian homestead where humanure was the
standard garden fertilizer. While the subject provokes instinctive
revulsion in many, Solomon argues that the practice is safe, practical,
and may become necessary as industrial societies face increasing resource
constraints.

## The Case for Humanure Recycling

In Tasmanian folk culture, as Muriel Solomon experienced in the 1940s, all
[[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]] were fertilized with animal manure — except in those
places that had no flush toilets. Her family's garden, like those of most
neighbors, depended entirely on humanure. The family had no piped water;
their meager supply came from small roof-capture tanks. In such conditions
of self-sufficiency, wasting the nutrient value of human excrement was not
an option.

Solomon frames the issue in terms of biological necessity: a garden can
never operate as a truly closed system unless *all* organic outputs,
including human waste, are recycled back into it. Even comprehensive
composting of all garden and kitchen waste, supplemented with imported
manure and leaves, still represents an open system with net nutrient
export. Humanure closes this loop more completely than any other single
practice. The nutrients in human waste are substantial — particularly
nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium — and represent a significant fraction
of what a household consumes and could return to the soil.

## The Humanure Handbook: Foundation of Modern Practice

Solomon defers to Joseph Jenkins' *The Humanure Handbook* as the definitive
reference, noting that it fully explores the issues of recycling human
waste and is based on the author's own family's longstanding practice. The
handbook is available both in print and as a free download. Jenkins lives
in a region with severe winters and demonstrates that humanure composting
works even in freezing conditions, contrary to what many would assume.

The handbook advocates a simple system: a bucket toilet (often called a
"dunny" in Australian English) where each deposit is covered with sawdust
or other carbonaceous material. The full buckets are emptied into a compost
bin, layered with straw or other bulky organic matter, and left to
thermophilically compost for an extended period — typically one to two
years — before the finished compost is applied to garden soil.

## Thermophilic Composting and Pathogen Destruction

The critical safety question for humanure composting is whether pathogens
— bacteria, viruses, and particularly parasites — are reliably destroyed.
The answer lies in the thermophilic (heat-loving) composting process.

When organic material is properly assembled in a compost heap with adequate
moisture, aeration, and a balanced carbon-nitrogen ratio, the metabolic
activity of decomposing microorganisms generates substantial heat. Core
temperatures in a well-managed compost pile routinely reach 120 to 150°F
(49 to 66°C). At these temperatures, human pathogens are destroyed
relatively quickly. Most bacterial pathogens, including Salmonella and
E. coli, are killed within hours to days at temperatures above 131°F
(55°C). Parasite eggs and cysts, which are more resistant, require
sustained exposure — typically several days to weeks at temperatures
above 122°F (50°C).

The standard recommendation from public health authorities is that compost
containing human waste should achieve sustained temperatures of at least
131°F (55°C) for a minimum of three days, turned at least twice during
that period to ensure all material is exposed to the lethal temperatures.
An additional aging period of several months to a year after the active
heating phase provides an extra safety margin. During this curing period,
the compost becomes increasingly biologically stable, and any remaining
pathogens are outcompeted by the thriving soil [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]].

## Safety Protocols and Practical Considerations

Solomon offers several practical safety recommendations based on Jenkins'
work and his own family's experience:

**Segregation of visitor waste.** To avoid introducing unfamiliar pathogens
into the household's composting system, it may be wise to segregate the
humanure of visitors and houseguests. The concern is primarily parasitic
infections that household members may not have but that visitors could
introduce. A flush toilet can be maintained for guests while the composting
toilet serves the permanent household.

**Odor management.** The "dunny" must be designed to prevent odors. This
requires an extremely well-ventilated outdoor cubicle — crudely built but
with excellent airflow. Each deposit must be immediately covered with a
generous layer of dry carbonaceous material such as sawdust, dry leaves,
or shredded paper. This cover material serves two purposes: it seals in
odors and provides the carbon needed for proper C/N balance in the compost.

**Composting duration.** Humanure compost should be allowed to decompose
for at least one full year, and preferably two, before being applied to
vegetable gardens. This extended timeline is not primarily about pathogen
destruction — thermophilic heating accomplishes that within days — but
about ensuring complete biological stability and providing multiple
safety margins.

**Proper composting technique.** The compost heap must be properly
constructed with adequate carbon material, moisture, soil (to capture
ammonia), and aeration. Simply piling humanure without these elements
creates a public health hazard, not fertilizer. The heap should heat
adequately and be turned to ensure all material passes through the
thermophilic zone.

**Application timing.** Finished humanure compost should be applied to
the garden well before planting, ideally in autumn, so that any remaining
biological activity completes before food crops are grown. This is
consistent with general organic standards that require raw manure to be
incorporated into soil at least 120 days before harvest of crops where
the edible portion contacts the soil.

## Context and Necessity

Solomon introduces this subject at a time when industrialized countries may
soon need to master the recycling of human waste as a valuable resource.
The prevailing system of flushing humanure into waterways, treating it
expensively, and discharging nutrient-rich effluent into oceans represents
a massive ongoing loss of agricultural fertility. Composting humanure
recovers these nutrients, reduces pollution, and decreases dependence on
external fertilizer inputs.

The practice is not for everyone. It requires a willingness to manage the
process carefully, adequate space for composting, and acceptance of
additional chores. But for gardeners serious about self-sufficiency and
closed-loop fertility, humanure composting represents perhaps the single
most important nutrient recycling strategy available.
