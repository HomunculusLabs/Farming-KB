## Calculating Sowing Dates

### The Reverse Growth Rate Principle

In spring planting, growth rates accelerate as days lengthen and warm. A plant sown on April 15 will reach maturity faster than one sown on March 15, because the later-sown plant benefits from longer days and warmer temperatures during its growth period.

In fall planting, the reverse is true. A plant sown on September 1 will take significantly longer to reach harvestable size than the same plant sown on August 1, even though the temperature difference may be small. This deceleration is primarily driven by day length reduction. Each week closer to the Persephone months adds incrementally more time to the maturity calculation.

### Working Backward from Persephone Months

Coleman recommends a systematic approach to calculating fall sowing dates:

1. **Determine the Persephone months start date** for your latitude
2. **Identify the target maturity size** for each crop at the onset of the Persephone months
3. **Calculate adjusted days to maturity** under fall conditions (see below)
4. **Count backward** from the Persephone months start date to find the sowing date

This backward calculation accounts for the decelerating growth rate and ensures that plants reach adequate size before growth essentially stops.

### Adjusted Days to Maturity

Seed packet days-to-maturity ratings are calibrated for spring and summer growing conditions with increasing day length. For fall planting, Coleman recommends adding a significant buffer to the listed days-to-maturity:

- **Early August sowings**: Add approximately 50% to the listed maturity time
- **Late August sowings**: Add approximately 75% to the listed maturity time
- **September sowings**: Add approximately 100% (double) the listed maturity time

For example, a lettuce variety listed as 45 days to maturity would need approximately 68 days if sown in early August, 79 days if sown in late August, and 90 days if sown in September. These adjustments are approximate guidelines that should be refined through local experience and careful record-keeping.

## Crop-Specific Timing Windows

Different crops have different optimal sowing windows for winter harvest. Coleman's published schedule for coastal Maine (Zone 5, 44°N) provides a reference that can be adjusted for other latitudes and climates:

### Baby Leaf Salad Mix

Multiple sowings from September 1 through October 15. Succession sowings every two weeks provide continuous harvest through the winter. Earlier sowings produce larger leaves; later sowings produce smaller but more tender leaves. The salad mix typically includes a combination of lettuce, brassica greens, and specialty cutting greens.

### Spinach

Sow August 15 through September 15 for harvest from October through March. Spinach is extremely cold-hardy and can survive temperatures well below freezing under double-layer protection. Earlier sowings produce larger plants suitable for cooking; later sowings produce baby spinach for salad.

### Carrots

Sow early August for harvest beginning in December. [[coleman-beet-greens-cold-house-winter-production]], such as 'Bolero' and 'Merida', which maintain sweetness in cold conditions and store well in the ground.

### Asian Greens

Tatsoi, mizuna, and other Asian greens: sow September 1 through October 1. These fast-growing greens reach harvestable size quickly and tolerate cold temperatures well under protection. They are among the most reliable winter crops for new winter growers.

### Mache (Corn Salad)

Sow September 15 through October 15. Mache is one of the most cold-hardy salad greens and can be harvested throughout the winter with minimal protection. It grows slowly but maintains excellent quality through the coldest months.
