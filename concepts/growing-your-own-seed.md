# Growing Your Own Seed

Saving your own seed is the ultimate step toward garden self-sufficiency. It reduces costs, preserves varieties adapted to your specific conditions, and provides a deeper understanding of plant biology. However, [[biointensive-seed-saving]] ranges from simple (for self-pollinating species) to extremely challenging (for outcrossing species), and understanding the difference is essential before attempting it.

## Self-Pollinating vs. Outcrossing Species

The fundamental division among vegetables for seed-saving purposes is their method of pollination.

### Self-Pollinating (Inbreeding) Species

These species self-fertilize or exchange very little pollen with neighbors. They inbreed generation after generation without negative consequences. They are usually quite stable — from generation to generation there is little or no change.

**List of self-pollinating vegetables:**
- Beans (all types)
- Peas (with slight tendency to outcross — isolate by 20 feet/6 m)
- Lettuce
- Tomatoes
- Eggplant
- Endive/escarole/chicory
- Garlic (vegetatively propagated, no true seed)
- Peppers (mostly self-pollinating)

**Seed saving difficulty**: Easy. Simply let the plants go to seed, harvest when dry, and store properly. Seed can be saved from a single plant, from a few pods, or from a single fruit. This can be done year after year with no problems.

### Outcrossing (Pollen-Exchanging) Species

These species must exchange pollen between different plants or they become weak (inbred) and eventually can no longer grow or reproduce. They require more skill, more space, and more plants to maintain vigor.

**List of outcrossing vegetables:**
- Alliums (onions, leeks)
- Asparagus
- Beet (beetroot) and chard (silverbeet)
- Brassicas — unrefined (kale, kohlrabi, rutabaga)
- Brassicas — refined (broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, cauliflower)
- [[growing-chinese-cabbage]]
- Cucurbits (melons, pumpkins, squash, cucumbers)
- Corn
- Carrot
- Spinach

**Seed saving difficulty**: Daunting for most home gardeners. Requires maintaining a sufficiently large and genetically diverse population.

## Inbreeding Depression

When outcrossing species are grown from too small a [[cannabis-gene-pool-preservation-acclimatization-clarke]], each generation becomes weaker. After only a few generations of inbreeding, a variety can become so weak that its own seeds will barely sprout and will grow so poorly that it becomes nonproductive.

### Minimum Population Sizes

The minimum number of individuals needed to avoid inbreeding depression varies by species:

| Species Type | Minimum Plants |
|-------------|---------------|
| Kale, rutabaga (unrefined brassicas) | ~12-20 |
| Corn | ~50 |
| Carrots | ~50-100 |
| Most refined brassicas (cabbage, broccoli) | ~100-200 |

### Why This Matters

To maintain a full-sized cabbage variety when the minimum sustainable number is 200 for making seed:
- Each plant needs roughly 4 square feet (0.4 sq m)
- 200 plants need 800 square feet (75 sq m)
- That is two-thirds of a typical 1,200 sq ft garden

Growing seed for refined outcrossers is clearly not practical for most home gardeners as a routine activity.

## Wet Seed vs. Dry Seed

Beyond pollination method, there is another important division: wet seed and dry seed.

### Dry Seed

Forms in pods, in clusters on the stalk, or in dried flower structures. Examples: beans, peas, lettuce, mustard, spinach, beet, okra.

**Key to getting vigorous dry seed**: Let it mature fully but keep it drying down steadily while it matures. If the ripening process continues over weeks, you may need to yank the entire plant, shake soil from the roots, and move it under cover — spread on a tarp or hang to catch shattering seeds.

**Hand-picking large pods**: For peas and beans, you can pick each pod at the exact point of maturation when the stem end withers and it is clear the plant's sap is no longer flowing to the pod. The seed won't have dried down hard at that stage, but no further nutrition will be added to the seed's food reserve.

### Wet Seed

Forms in juicy fruit that is still full of moisture when the seed has matured. Examples: squash, pumpkin, cucumber, melon, tomato, pepper.

**Key to getting vigorous wet seed**: Let the fruit become fully ripe on the plant before harvesting — allow these dead-ripe fruits to ripen nearly to the point of rotting on the vine before you extract the seed.

**Species-specific tips:**
- **Tomato**: Take seeds from overripe fruit. Let a bowl of soft ripe fruit sit on the counter for a few more days before extracting.
- **Melon**: Make sure the fruit honestly slips the vine (the stem separates easily with gentle pressure).
- **Squash**: Allow the fully ripe fruit to cure for a month or so before extracting seed.

## The Drying Phase

For all seed types, **drying is the most critical step**. If the seed is not dry, then it is moist. At high moisture contents and warm temperatures, seed ages rapidly.

- Dry seed down quickly at moderate temperatures (not above 90°F/32°C)
- Do not dry in direct sunlight (UV can damage the embryo)
- Ensure good [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]]
- Store only when fully dry (snap test: seed should break crisply, not bend)

## Practical Recommendations

### Start With Self-Pollinators

If you are new to seed saving, start with beans, peas, lettuce, or tomatoes. These are forgiving and can be saved from just a few plants.

### Avoid Complex Outcrossers Initially

Don't attempt to save seed from cabbage, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, corn, or carrots until you have experience and adequate space.

### Recommended Resources

- **Seed to Seed** by Suzanne Ashworth — the standard reference for [[vegetable-seed-saving-and-germination]]
- **The Seed Garden** by the Seed Savers Exchange — covers seed-raising methods for each vegetable
- Both are essential reading before attempting serious seed saving

## The Question of "Network" Seed

Amateur seed-saving networks where members share seeds are often well-intentioned but frequently produce disappointing results. The problem is usually too small a gene pool, too few generations of selection, and poor storage practices. Results are consistently bad for outcrossing species. Occasionally a network isn't bad, but this is the exception.
