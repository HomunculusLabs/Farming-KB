# Livestock Drought Management

In all arid and semi-arid areas, large livestock such as draft animals, milking cattle, and buffalo are at serious risk during drought. ### 3. Water Harvesting Earthworks
A careful ground-plan of **multiple cross-slope swales** to catch and infiltrate run-off water during rains. This is a **critical precursor** to planting the forages — without water capture, the system cannot function.

### 4. Integrated Windbreaks
All adjoining fields should be edged and wind-breaked with the same forage species, planted at **20-30 m intervals** in rows throughout all other crops, on bunds, and along swales and ditches.

## System Operation

- In **good years**, the basic survival hectare can be cut and managed normally
- In **drought years**, all choice or essential livestock are penned in or near the forage system for survival feeding
- As no crops can be planted in drought, farmers and their families tend cattle on rotation
- All manure and bedding is carried back to the forage fields as mulch, preferably deposited in the swales
- This mulch develops **cool humus soils with good water capacity** over time
- The forage plants thrive on this humus, creating a positive feedback loop

## Drought Feeding Techniques

### Molasses-Urea Lick
Cattle in drought can be fed on chopped dry stalk material, small branches, straw, crushed cane, and even cardboard or paper, provided they have access to a **molasses-urea lick**:

- Handily made from a **petrol drum floating in a half-drum bath** of molasses-urea mixture
- As cows lick the floating drum, it revolves and picks up more mixture
- The urea-molasses plus high-cellulose cheap bulk food enables cattle to break down cellulose in wood and straw
- The rest of the ration comes from the perennial forages, cut in succession and carried to the pen

## Dangers to Livestock on Range Following Rains

This is one of the most critical and underappreciated risks in arid-area livestock management. Both woody and ephemeral plants in drylands may **concentrate toxic substances** as they start new growth after rains:

### Toxic Compounds
- Nitrates
- Oxalic acids
- Cyanides
- Sodium fluoro-acetate
- Poisonous alkaloids

### Effects
- Infertility
- Liver destruction
- Spasm
- Eventually death in wild and domestic browsers

### Timing
The toxic effect lasts **4-6 weeks after rains**. Livestock should not be released to range containing only new growth for long periods on any one plant stand.

### Critical Safety Rules
- **Mature leaf is usually non-toxic** — the same plants that are toxic in new growth may be good browse at maturity
- Cattle on such range must be **very carefully herded**
- Any **sudden shock or running will cause death** — animals must move at their own pace
- Dogs, whips, horses, and "cowboy tactics" can be **fatal** in these conditions
- Any distant droving should be left until forages mature
- Livestock should have access to a **wide range of foods**, some cut forage, or mature leaf from trees

### The Coppicing-Toxin Link
Browsing is equivalent to severe pruning or coppicing. Combined with wildfire (which removes nutrients and causes stress in regrowth plants), burnt and browsed plant stands may secrete toxins for some time after such stresses. This plant response is linked to cyclic factors that cause hare numbers to sharply decline on over-browsed range. In hot deserts, fire, browsing, coppicing, and firewood gathering can produce poisonous metabolites across a wide range of both woody and ephemeral plants (Bryant, J.P., 1981).

## Key Principles

1. **Preparation before drought**: The survival hectare must be established and maintained in good years
2. **Collaboration**: 6-8 farmers sharing one hectare makes the system economically viable
3. **Closed-loop nutrient cycling**: Manure returns to swales, [[building-soil]] over time
4. **Careful herding**: Post-rain toxicity is a lethal threat requiring knowledge and restraint
5. **Multiple forage species**: Diversity ensures something is always available
6. **Water capture first**: Swales are the foundation — forage planting follows
