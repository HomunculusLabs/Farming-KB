## Overview

The ingredients chosen for [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) determine the microbial composition of the final brew. By selecting specific compost types and supplemental nutrients, gardeners can tailor teas to be bacterially dominated, fungally dominated, or balanced — matching the needs of specific plants through Rule #10 of [[soil-food-web]] gardening.

## Core Ingredients

### Dechlorinated Water (Rule #12)

Compost teas are extremely sensitive to chlorine and preservatives. All brewing water and ingredients must be free of these chemicals, which are designed to kill or discourage microbial life.

- **Municipal chlorinated water**: Fill the brewing container and run air bubbles for 1–2 hours to evaporate chlorine
- **Carbon filters**: Effective for [[aact-water-quality-chlorine-removal-brewing-standards-ingham]]; a 1 cubic foot carbon filter handles approximately 4 gallons per minute
- **[[reverse-osmosis-compost-tea]] systems**: Remove both chlorine and chloramines, ideal for large-volume operations

### Compost

The quality of starting compost directly determines tea quality. Requirements:

- No chemical remnants; must pass the "sniff test" (pleasant earthy smell)
- Fully finished compost — avoid "almost compost" or overheated material
- High microbial diversity in compost yields high diversity in tea
- Biological testing is the most reliable quality check

### Vermicastings

Vermicompost is an excellent substitute for compost. It is rich in beneficial microbes and strongly bacterial in character (reflecting the bacterial digestion role inside worms). Freshness matters — older vermicastings have reduced microbial activity.

## Compost Quantity Scaling

Compost amounts do not scale linearly with brew size:

| Brew Size | Compost Needed |
|-----------|---------------|
| 5 gallons | 5 lbs (20 cups) |
| 50 gallons | 7 lbs (28 cups) |
| 500 gallons | 15 lbs (60 cups) |

While compost amount is nonlinear, all supplemental nutrients scale linearly with brew volume.

## Bacterial Foods

Simple sugars feed bacteria and rapidly increase their populations:

- **Nonsulfured molasses** (powdered or liquid) — do NOT use sulfured molasses as it kills microbes
- Cane syrup
- Maple syrup
- Fruit juices
- More complex sugars and [[fish-emulsion]] (also support some fungal growth)

**Dosage**: 2 tablespoons per 4–5 gallons of water.

## Fungal Foods

Encouraging fungal growth requires more complex nutrient sources:

- **Kelp**: *[[ascophyllum-nodosum]]* (cold-water kelp), available as powdered algae from garden centers and feed stores
- **Humic and fulvic acids**: Provide nutrient value and attachment surfaces for fungal growth
- **Phosphate rock dusts**: Surface area for fungal colonization
- **Fruit pulps**: Orange, blueberry, and apple pulps support fungal proliferation
- **Aloe vera extract**: Must be preservative-free
- **Fish hydrolysate**: Enzymatically digested whole fish (bones and all); can be purchased or made at home by adding papain (papaya peptidase) or kiwi to a fish blend
- **Yucca and zeolites**: Support fungi without promoting bacterial populations

## The Fungal Growth Challenge

Growing sufficient fungi [[molasses-and-sugars-in-compost-tea-brewing]] is the primary difficulty for most brewers. The reason is fundamental:

- **Bacteria** both grow and multiply rapidly in tea given adequate nutrition
- **Fungi** only grow (extend hyphae) during the brew period — the brew time is almost never long enough for fungal reproduction

This asymmetry means that without intervention, teas default to bacterial dominance.

## Fungal Activation Protocol

The solution is to pre-activate fungal populations in compost before brewing:

### Process

1. **3–4 days before brewing**, mix compost with a fungal food at 3–4 tablespoons per cup of compost
2. Suitable foods: soybean meal, powdered malt, oatmeal, oat bran, or powdered baby oatmeal (best option)
3. Ensure adequate moisture — a drop of water should be squeezable from a fistful
4. Place mixture in a container in a warm, dark location
5. A seed-germinating mat under the container provides ideal heat (~80°F / 27°C)

### Expected Results

After approximately 3 days at 80°F, visible white mycelial threads should cover the compost surface, described as resembling "Santa Claus's beard." After a few more days, the entire compost mass will be glued together by fungal hyphae.

## Brewing Timeframe and Indicators

- **Small brewers**: 24–36 hours to develop a good tea
- **Commercial brewers**: As little as 12 hours with high-energy agitation
- **Visual indicator**: Tea turns coffee-brown as microbes are extracted
- **Temperature**: May increase 3–5°F from increased [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]]
- **Aroma**: Properly made tea (especially with molasses) produces a healthy, sweet, earthy smell

## Mycorrhizal Addition

Mycorrhizal fungal spores should be added at the very end of the brew cycle. If added during brewing, either the spores or the hyphae they produce will be destroyed by agitation. Since mycorrhizal fungi depend on root exudates, the tea must reach plant roots quickly after application.
