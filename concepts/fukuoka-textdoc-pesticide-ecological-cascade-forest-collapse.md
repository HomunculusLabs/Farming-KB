The beetles are vectors for pinewood nematodes, microscopic parasitic worms that infect and kill pine trees. With beetle populations booming, nematode transmission increases dramatically, threatening the very pines that man planted.

### Step 6: Botrytis Fungi and Matsutake Disappearance

The nematodes feed on parasitic Botrytis fungi in the trunks of the pine trees. The beneficial matsutake fungus, which lives symbiotically on the roots of red pines, has died off as a result of an increase in the harmful Botrytis fungus in the soil. The matsutake was a key part of the forest's [[bloomfield-amphibian-decline-fungal-disease-climate-interactions]], thickets of bamboo grass rise up in the cleared areas. This fast-growing ground cover further prevents forest regeneration by outcompeting native tree seedlings and creating conditions favorable to its own spread.

### Step 9: Mouse Population Explosion

Mice feed on the abundant bamboo grass berries and multiply rapidly. The bamboo grass, which invaded only because the pines died, now supports a rodent population explosion that threatens any remaining vegetation.

### Step 10: Mouse Damage to Cedar Saplings

The mice attack the cedar saplings that were originally planted by man. The very trees that the forester sought to cultivate are now being destroyed by the indirect consequences of his original intervention.

### Step 11: Rodenticide Application

Man responds by applying rodenticide to protect the cedar saplings. This chemical intervention represents yet another layer of human interference in an already disrupted ecosystem.

### Step 12: Predator Collapse

As mice vanish from the rodenticide, weasels and snakes that feed on them experience a population decline. The natural predators that might have helped control rodent populations in the long term are eliminated by the chemical intervention.

### Step 13: Artificial Mouse Rearing

To protect the now-declining weasel population, man begins raising mice to restore the rodent population that he himself had just poisoned. Fukuoka calls this "the stuff of crazed dreams," illustrating the absurdity of trying to manage nature through ever-more-intricate human interventions.

## The Endless Regression of Cause and Effect

The critical insight Fukuoka draws from this cascade is that causes and effects form an unending chain. Each apparent solution creates new problems, and each new problem demands further intervention. This backward regression from effect to prior cause continues infinitely, leaving one wondering what the true cause is.

Fukuoka argues that in nature, there is no simple cause and effect. The relationships between organisms, soil, climate, and microorganisms form a web so complex that human intervention at any single point sends ripple effects through the entire system in unpredictable directions. Science rarely looks to microorganisms for an understanding of large causal relationships. Botanists are not concerned with microorganisms as these lie outside their field of interest.

## The Rice Paddy Parallel

Fukuoka extends this analysis to rice cultivation. Toxic chemicals are applied at least eight times per year on Japanese rice fields. Yet agricultural scientists have barely investigated why insect damage remains largely the same as in unsprayed fields.

The first pesticide application does not kill rice leafhoppers effectively. Instead, it wipes out tens of thousands of young spiders per square yard and eliminates swarms of fireflies that fly up from the stands of grass. The second application kills chalcid wasps, important natural predators, and also destroys dragonfly larvae, tadpoles, and loaches. Just one look at this slaughter would suffice to show the insanity of the blanket application of pesticides.
