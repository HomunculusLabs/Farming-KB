# Animal Systems in Permaculture Bill Mollison viewed animals as integral components of permaculture systems rather than isolated production units. In *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, he argued that animals convert low-quality plant materials into high-quality protein, cycle nutrients through manure, control pests, cultivate soil, and provide labor for land management. The key principle is that animals should be integrated into the broader system design rather than raised in monocultures. This integration connects to [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]], [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-broadacre-and-rural-design]] through their ecological contributions.

## The Multi-Function Principle for Animals

Mollison insisted that every animal in a permaculture system should fulfill at
least three functions. Chickens produce eggs, manure, and [[comparison-pheromone-based-pest-control-vs-beneficial-insect-biocontrol]] while
cultivating soil through scratching. Pigs convert food waste and crop residues
into meat while rooting and tilling soil for future planting. Goats control
weedy brush, produce milk and fiber, and create clearings through browsing.
When animals serve multiple functions, they become indispensable elements of
the designed ecosystem rather than mere commodity producers. This multifunction
approach contrasts sharply with industrial livestock systems that optimize for
single outputs.

## Poultry Systems

### Chickens in the Garden

Chickens are perhaps the most versatile animal in permaculture. Mollison
described the [[comparison-chicken-tractor-vs-free-range]], a movable enclosure that concentrates chickens
on specific garden beds. The chickens scratch the soil, eat weed seeds and
insects, and deposit manure before being moved to the next bed. This system
provides soil cultivation, pest control, and fertilization in a single
operation, connecting directly to [[integrated-pest-management]]. In [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems]], ducks
fertilize pond water with their manure, stimulating the growth of duckweed
and zooplankton that feed fish. Duck-tilapia-rice polycultures in tropical
systems represent a sophisticated integration of animal, fish, and crop
production.

### Geese as Lawn Mowers

Geese are grazing animals that can maintain lawns and pastures without
mowing equipment. They prefer grasses over broadleaf plants, making them
useful for maintaining grass pathways while leaving crops unharmed. Geese
also serve as alarm animals, alerting keepers to intruders. In

## Forage Systems and Cell Grazing

Mollison drew on the work of Andre Voisin and Allan Savory in advocating
rotational grazing systems. In cell grazing, animals are moved frequently
between small paddocks, allowing each area a recovery period before being
grazed again. This mimics the natural movement of wild herbivores across
grasslands and prevents the overgrazing that degrades pastures under
continuous stocking. The recovery period allows plants to rebuild root
systems and carbohydrate reserves, improving both pasture productivity
and soil structure over time. Electric fencing makes cell grazing
practical even on small permaculture holdings, and the paddock layout can
be designed to integrate with tree crops, waterways, and windbreaks.
