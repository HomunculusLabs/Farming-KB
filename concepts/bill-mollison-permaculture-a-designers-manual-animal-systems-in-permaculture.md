# Animal Systems in Permaculture

Bill Mollison viewed animals as integral components of permaculture systems
rather than isolated production units. In *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*,
he argued that animals convert low-quality plant materials into high-quality
protein, cycle nutrients through manure, control pests, cultivate soil, and
provide labor for land management. The key principle is that animals should be
integrated into the broader system design rather than raised in monocultures.
This integration connects to [[permaculture-design]] of multiple
functions, biological resources, and energy cycling. Animal systems support
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]], [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]], and
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-broadacre-and-rural-design]] through their ecological contributions.

## The Multi-Function Principle for Animals

Mollison insisted that every animal in a permaculture system should fulfill at
least three functions. Chickens produce eggs, manure, and pest control while
cultivating soil through scratching. Pigs convert food waste and crop residues
into meat while rooting and tilling soil for future planting. Goats control
weedy brush, produce milk and fiber, and create clearings through browsing.
When animals serve multiple functions, they become indispensable elements of
the designed ecosystem rather than mere commodity producers. This multifunction
approach contrasts sharply with industrial livestock systems that optimize for
single outputs.

## Poultry Systems

### Chickens in the Garden

Chickens are perhaps the most versatile animal in permaculture. Mollison
described the chicken tractor, a movable enclosure that concentrates chickens
on specific garden beds. The chickens scratch the soil, eat weed seeds and
insects, and deposit manure before being moved to the next bed. This system
provides soil cultivation, pest control, and fertilization in a single
operation, connecting directly to [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]] and
[[integrated-pest-management]]. In [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]],
free-ranging chickens forage for fallen fruit, control codling moth larvae,
and distribute manure throughout the system.

### Ducks for Pest Control

Ducks are exceptional for pest control, particularly in wetland and garden
environments. They consume slugs, snails, and aquatic insect pests that
trouble other livestock. Muscovy ducks are effective in orchards where they
forage for fallen fruit pests. In [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems]], ducks
fertilize pond water with their manure, stimulating the growth of duckweed
and zooplankton that feed fish. Duck-tilapia-rice polycultures in tropical
systems represent a sophisticated integration of animal, fish, and crop
production.

### Geese as Lawn Mowers

Geese are grazing animals that can maintain lawns and pastures without
mowing equipment. They prefer grasses over broadleaf plants, making them
useful for maintaining grass pathways while leaving crops unharmed. Geese
also serve as alarm animals, alerting keepers to intruders. In
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-broadacre-and-rural-design]], geese can manage orchard understories,
reducing mowing costs while producing meat and down feathers.

## Pig Systems

Pigs are powerful soil preparation tools in permaculture design. Mollison
described using pigs to clear land for new gardens or orchards. Confined to
a specific area, pigs root up existing vegetation, eat roots and tubers,
break compacted soil, and deposit large quantities of manure. After the pigs
have prepared the site, they are moved and the area is planted with crops
or trees. In traditional systems, pigs are run beneath fruit and nut trees
where they clean up fallen fruit, control rodents, and fertilize the soil.
Pigs can also be integrated with [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems]] where
their manure fertilizes algae growth in ponds.

## Goat and Sheep Systems

Goats and sheep provide meat, milk, and fiber while managing vegetation. Goats
are browsers that prefer woody plants, making them ideal for clearing brush,
controlling invasive species, and maintaining hedgerows. Sheep are grazers
that can be used for pasture management in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-broadacre-and-rural-design]].
Both species can be managed through rotational grazing systems that prevent
overgrazing, allow pasture recovery, and distribute manure evenly across the
landscape. Mollison recommended combining goats with cattle in multi-species
grazing systems because goats prefer browse that cattle ignore, increasing
total pasture utilization.

## Beekeeping

Honeybees provide pollination services, honey, beeswax, propolis, and royal
jelly. Mollison considered bees essential for fruit and seed production in
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]] and vegetable gardens. Beehives should be
placed near diverse flowering plantings to ensure adequate nectar and pollen
sources throughout the season. [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]] includes
insectary plants specifically to support bee populations. Mollison warned
against the use of agricultural chemicals that harm pollinators and advocated
for designing systems that support native bee species alongside managed honeybees.

## Rabbit Systems

Rabbits are efficient converters of forage to protein and produce excellent
manure for garden use. Mollison described rabbit systems integrated with
vermiculture where rabbit manure is fed to worms that produce castings for
the garden. Rabbit housing can be placed in greenhouses where animal heat
benefits plant growth and the rabbits consume vegetable scraps. In
[[urban-permaculture-trees-water-cycling]], rabbits are one of the most practical livestock
options due to their small space requirements and quiet nature.

## Worm Farming

While not traditionally considered livestock, earthworms are among the most
important animals in permaculture systems. Worm farms process organic waste
into nutrient-rich vermicast, the highest quality organic fertilizer available.
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]] depends on healthy earthworm populations for
soil aeration, nutrient cycling, and organic matter decomposition. Mollison
recommended integrating worm farms into [[zone-and-sector-analysis]] Zone 1
where kitchen scraps are readily available and the castings are needed for
intensive garden beds.

## Aquatic Animals

[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems]] integrate fish, crustaceans, and mollusks
into water-based food production. Fish such as tilapia, carp, and trout
convert aquatic plants and organisms into protein. Freshwater prawns and
crayfish clean pond bottoms and consume detritus. Mussels filter water and
improve water quality. These aquatic animal systems connect to
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-water-harvesting-and-earthworks]] through pond design and management, and
to [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]] through polycultural aquatic guilds that
combine multiple species at different trophic levels.

## Integration Principles

The overarching principle for animal systems in permaculture is that animals
should never be kept in isolation from the plant systems that feed them and
benefit from their manure. Every animal enclosure should be connected to a
composting system, every pasture should be connected to a water source, and
every feeding operation should be designed to minimize labor while maximizing
the ecological services the animals provide. This integration creates closed
nutrient loops where wastes become resources and animals become active
participants in landscape management.
