# Permaculture Landscape and Society Design

## Permaculture as a Design Science

[[permaculture]] is a design system, not a collection of techniques. As Mollison states, "Design is the keyword of this book: design in landscape, social, and conceptual systems; and design in space and time." The system arranges what was always there in a different way, so that it works to conserve energy or to generate more energy than it consumes. What is novel—and often overlooked—is that any system of total commonsense design for human communities is revolutionary.

The design process is structured as a marriage of site constraints to people's needs, aiming for the best flow, function, and yield while conserving resources. It involves selection and assembly of elemental, organic-derived, and socio-legal elements into site-specific patterns that evolve toward maturity.

## Design Components

Mollison's design framework integrates several categories of elements:

- **Inorganic and Organic Elements**: Species, earth-shaping, water supply, and planting patterns
- **Derived Organic Elements**: Guilds (beneficial plant communities), constructed ecosystems
- **Socio-Legal Elements**: People, materials, fuels, technology, and social structures including trusts, companies, cooperatives, and community credit unions

The design process operates through elemental flow control and feedback, with continuous observation, evaluation, and control as the system evolves to maturity.

## Post-Industrial Synthesis

What makes permaculture distinctive is its synthesis of plant and animal systems using a post-industrial approach to system design. It applies the principles of whole-system energy flows as devised by Howard T. Odum (1971) and the principles of ecology as enunciated by [[kenneth-watt]] and others. The approach can be applied to devise permaculture systems for local, regional, and personal needs.

Mollison acknowledges that had this approach been taught from the beginning, humanity would be in a stable and functional landscape. Instead, previous generations set up the continuing "mis-designed households, towns, and cities." The concept of "free" energy, in his view, put the final nail in the coffin of commonsense community.

## Three Parallel Responses to Environmental Crisis

In a world losing forests, species, and whole ecosystems, Mollison identifies three concurrent and parallel responses:

1. **Care for surviving natural assemblies**: Leave wilderness to heal itself. This means implacable opposition to further disturbance of any remaining natural forests where most species are still in balance.

2. **Rehabilitate degraded or eroded land**: Use complex pioneer species and long-term plant assemblies—trees, shrubs, ground covers—to restore stability to damaged systems.

3. **Create our own complex living environment**: Incorporate as many species as we can save or have need for, from wherever on earth they come. This is the primary domain of [[permaculture-design]].

## The Global Species Perspective

Mollison challenges the concept of "native" species. While local flora and fauna may be considered "native," it is equally logical to see all life as "native to earth." While preserving systems that are still local and diverse, permaculturists should also build new or recombinant ecologies from global resources, especially to stabilise degraded lands.

In practice, any garden already contains plants, animals, and soil organisms from every major landmass and many islands. Jet travel has merely accelerated a process already well-established by continental drift, bird migration, wind transport, and the rafting of debris by water. Everything will, in time, either become extinct, spread more widely, or evolve to new forms.

The very chemistry of the air, soil, and water is in flux. Metals, chemicals, isotopes, gases, and plastics are loose on earth that have never before been present, or never present in such form and quantity before human activity made it so.

## Refuges and Conservation

Permaculturists are fast approaching the need for refuges for all global life forms, as well as regional, national, or state parks for indigenous forms. The practical way to proceed (outside the home garden) is to form or subscribe to institutes or organisations whose legal charter aims carry out conservation activities. Even the smallest garden can reserve a few square metres of insect, lizard, frog, or butterfly habitat, while larger gardens and farms can fence off forest and wetland areas of critical value to local species.

## Settled vs. Natural Systems

The real difference between a cultivated (designed) ecosystem and a natural system is that the great majority of species and biomass in the cultivated ecology is intended for the use of humans or their livestock. In nature, humans are only a small part of the total species assembly, and only a small part of its yields are directly available to us. But in our own gardens, almost every plant is selected to provide or support some direct yield for people.

Recycling of nutrients and energy in nature is a function of many species. In gardens, it becomes the gardener's own responsibility to return wastes via compost or mulch. Gardeners actively create soil, whereas in nature many other species carry out that function. Around homes, water can be caught for garden use, but people rely on natural forested landscapes to provide the condenser leaves and clouds that keep rivers running with clean water, maintain the global atmosphere, and lock up gaseous pollutants.

## Philosopher-Gardeners

Mollison celebrates those who combine practical action with reverence for nature. Philosopher-gardeners and farmer-poets are distinguished by their sense of wonder and real feeling for the environment. When religions generalise love and respect to all living systems as a witness to the potential of creation, they join scientists, gardeners, and poets in admiration of the complexity and self-sustaining properties of natural systems, from whole universes to simple molecules.

The ultimate goal: if we can integrate food supply and settlement design to supply our needs from our existing settlements, we can free most of the globe for the rehabilitation of natural systems. These natural areas need never be looked upon as "of use to people," except in the very broad sense of global health.
