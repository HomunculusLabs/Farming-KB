## Permaculture Site Assessment and Report Writing

The [[permaculture-design]] report is the primary deliverable of a professional design engagement. It documents the designer's analysis, recommendations, and implementation guidance in a structured format that enables the client to proceed with confidence. Equally important is the site assessment process — the designer's ability to see resources that the client has overlooked, which is where the real value of professional design lies.

## Report Writing Methodology

### Six-Step Process

1. **Collect all data**: Client requirements, [[maps]], local regulations, site observations.
2. **Analyze client's problems**: Identify constraints, challenges, and opportunities.
3. **Match [[permaculture-principles]]**: Recognize which principles fit this particular situation.
4. **Apply principles specifically**: Address each identified problem with targeted [[permaculture]] solutions.
5. **Design solutions**: Express these as edges, patterning, and increased functional relationships.
6. **Verify completeness**: Check that all client needs are met and all problems addressed before writing.

## Report Structure

### General Description of Site

Begin with a brief overview covering:
- Site orientation
- Existing vegetation
- Existing water features
- Soil conditions
- Regional placement and context

Include a main map with the site broken into identifiable areas, each numbered for reference throughout the report.

### Themes Affecting the Whole Site

Address cross-cutting concerns before detailing individual areas:

- **Fire protection**: Steps the client must take against fire hazards.
- **Legal structures**: Advantages of trusts, land trusts, non-profit organizations.
- **Community formation**: For clients wanting to start community on the site — how to get people there and involve the local community.
- **Commercial crops**: For clients contemplating market production.
- **Water**: If water issues pervade the site, treat as a unified theme.
- **Energy**: Site energy resources and requirements.

### Detailed Area Analysis

For each numbered area on the map:

1. **Define by name**: House site, intensive garden, orchard, chicken forage system, etc.
2. **Locate by number**: Reference the main map consistently.
3. **Develop design in depth**: Planting systems, management systems, animal integration.
4. **Start with Zone 1**: The house site and intensive garden are nearly always first, as this is where the client should begin working.

### Principles of House Design

If no house exists, include:
- Energy requirements and recommendations
- Architectural type suggestions
- Builder recommendations if known

If a house exists, provide retrofit recommendations:
- Climate amelioration with glasshouses, shade houses, trellising, vines
- Earth banks, windbreaks, ponds in the garden
- Rain water collection from roof
- Tank or barn roof at higher elevation for gravity-fed water

### Priority Staging and Cost Analysis

This section is mandatory and must never be omitted:
- How much money the client has to spend
- What they ultimately want to accomplish
- Where to start
- Approximate costs for each stage
- Best sequencing of implementation

### Management Policy

Outline ongoing management direction — how the client should run the place after initial establishment, including succession planning for plant systems and time stacking strategies.

### Bibliography

Include all books referenced in the report plus others relevant to the design. Stay current with new publications. Ideally, one person in the consultancy group serves as information collector, regularly reviewing new literature.

### Plant List

Provide a documented plant list with these cautions:
- Avoid recommending species illegal in that region.
- Avoid species that could become rampant on site.
- Initially requires building a personal filing system for plants and animals.
- Specific job lists may require significant research.
- Familiarize yourself with local experts on plant and animal species.

### Resource List

Include:
- People in the area who can help the client.
- Other clients whose properties can be visited for demonstration.
- Hardware resources with reference to tools and technologies needed.

### Standard Designs

Slip standard designs into reports with bridging text. Standard designs can cover:
- Architectural themes
- Landscaping patterns
- Agricultural systems (e.g., horseshoe-shaped windbreaks, fencing layouts, icehouse construction)

Designers can earn author royalties (typically 10%) on published standard designs.

## The Ten Type-One Errors

Even experienced designers make errors. This checklist should be reviewed frequently:

1. **Environmentally destructive clients**: Undertaking work for people whose aims harm the environment. Always side with the "super client" — the environment.
2. **Unexplained recommendations**: Failing to tell clients the reasoning behind each recommendation.
3. **Overly complex technology**: Recommending technology beyond the client's ability to handle.
4. **Insufficient management data**: Not providing adequate directions for running the place after establishment.
5. **Depersonalized reporting**: Writing in a detached tone. Reports should be direct, friendly, and personal.
6. **Vague language**: Using loose expressions like "fairly large" instead of specific measurements.
7. **Poor patterning**: Failing to think through edges and linkages between design patterns.
8. **Missing pre-treatments**: Failing to recommend essential soil conditioning or site preparation.
9. **Illegal species**: Recommending plant species prohibited in that area.
10. **Overlooked resources**: Failing to identify and explain how on-site resources can be useful.

## Site Resource Assessment

### Client Resources

Three categories of client resources must be assessed:

1. **Skill resources**: What the client knows how to do.
2. **Experience resources**: What the client has done before.
3. **Material and capital resources**: What the client has available to invest.

Additionally, determine:
- Desired lifestyle (partial self-reliance, production on site, food self-sufficiency).
- Economic goals (independence vs. happy with outside employment).
- Capacity for on-site income generation (e.g., a potter whose income isn't land-dependent).

### On-Site Resources

This is where the designer earns their fee — identifying resources the client has never recognized:

#### Biological Resources

- **Rampant species as crops**: Fennel covering 18 acres isn't a weed problem — it's a licorice factory. Simple steam distillation yields fractions worth $1,000 per ounce from a $100/gallon crude oil, using a $600 still that pays for itself in the first run.
- **Eucalyptus oil**: Worth $100/gallon, with three one-ounce fractions worth $1,000/ounce each. A second small step of [[fractional-distillation]] in a one-gallon still yields $3,000 — paying for a $600 apparatus in one distillation.
- **Peat and clay**: Below swamps may lie bluish clay suitable for pottery. A 40-acre swamp purchase revealed two feet of peat over potter-quality blue clay worth $6 per bag.
- **Seed**: The site may contain salable seed of valuable species.
- **Unique crops**: The site might suit a particularly valuable processed plant (quinine at 8% bark content, as other malarial controls fail).
