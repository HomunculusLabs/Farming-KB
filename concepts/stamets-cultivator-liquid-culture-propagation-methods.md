# Liquid Culture Propagation Methods

Liquid culture techniques enable cultivators to dramatically increase the
scale of inoculum production. By suspending fragmented mushroom mycelia in
sterile water, a single agar culture plate can inoculate up to 100 grain
spawn jars, more than ten times the number possible with traditional wedge
transfer methods. This approach is fundamental to efficient
[[stamets-spawn-making-and-grain-culture]].

## Principles of Liquid Inoculation

The technique involves injecting mycelium-enriched water into sterilized
grain jars. As the water seeps down through the grain, mycelial fragments
are evenly distributed, each becoming a point of inoculation. For several
days, little sign of growth may be apparent. By the fourth to fifth day
after injection, given optimum incubation temperatures, sites of actively
growing mycelium become visible. In a matter of hours, these zones
enlarge and the grain becomes engulfed with mycelium. The liquid
inoculation technique eliminates the need for repeated shaking of spawn
jars during colonization.

## Direct Syringe Method

The simplest approach uses an autoclaved glass syringe. Inject 30-50 ml
of sterile water into a healthy agar culture. Scrape the surface of the
mycelial mat, drawing up as many fragments of mycelium as possible. As
little as 5 ml of mycelial suspension adequately inoculates a quart jar
of grain. This method requires only basic equipment: a syringe, sterile
water, and a clean culture plate.

## Blender Method (Eberbach Container)

A more sophisticated method incorporates a blender with an autoclavable
container-stirrer assembly. Several companies sell aluminum and stainless
steel units specifically manufactured for liquid culture work. The
procedure is as follows:

1. Fill the blender container two-thirds to three-quarters full with
   water, cover with aluminum foil, sterilize, and cool to room
   temperature
2. Under aseptic conditions, cut an entire agar culture of vigorously
   grown mycelium into four quadrants or narrow strips
3. Since many contaminants appear along the outer periphery of a dish,
   exclude these regions from the transfer
4. Place all four quadrants or strips into the liquid
5. Turn on the blender at high speed for no longer than 5 seconds.
   Longer stirring times fracture individual cells rather than
   fragmenting cell chains, producing inviable suspensions
6. Draw up 5-10 ml of the mycelium concentrate for inoculation

## Dilution Technique

A further improvement calls for a 10:1 dilution of the concentrated
mycelial solution. Inject 50 ml of mycelial suspension into four vessels
containing 450 ml of sterilized water. Narrow-mouth quart mason jars are
well suited. Gently shaking distributes the mycelium evenly. Next,
inoculate grain jars with 10-15 ml of diluted solution. This method
produces an exponential increase in liquid inoculum, with water acting as
a vehicle for carrying mycelial fragments deep into the grain-filled jar.

## Inoculation of Spawn Jars

When using metal lids, drill a small 1-2 mm hole and cover with tape.
During inoculation, remove the tape, insert the syringe needle, inject
the mycelial suspension, and replace the tape. The aperture is exposed
for only a second or two, minimizing airborne contamination risk. This
technique works well provided the source culture is free from foreign
spores.

## Advantages and Disadvantages

The primary advantage is scale: one plate can inoculate up to 100 jars.
Mycelial fragments are distributed throughout the substrate, speeding
colonization and reducing the need for manual grain shaking. However,
the disadvantage is that contaminated regions of a culture dish cannot
be avoided. The water suspends contaminant spores and mycelia alike. If
a dish is contaminated in one region, the traditional method might lose
only a few jars, while the liquid technique could render an entire set
of up to 100 spawn jars useless.

## Species Considerations

Although mycelial suspensions work for many species, the mycelia of some
mushrooms do not survive the stirring process. Additionally, some
species like Flammulina velutipes tend to form mycelial "pellets" that
complicate liquid culture. Japanese researchers found that adding 5%
corn starch and 2% malt to the liquid solution inhibits pellet formation
in this species. Cultivators should test liquid culture methods with
their specific species before committing to large-scale production.

## Enhanced Fruiting Through Liquid Spawn

Research by Shilo et al. (1974) demonstrated that fresh pieces of
Flammulina velutipes mixed directly into liquid spawn and introduced
into sawdust/bran medium accelerated fruiting, halved the spawning
period, and nearly quadrupled annual yields. The same technique applied
to Pleurotus ostreatus increased yields by a factor of three. Total
production in either case equaled up to one-quarter of the substrate on
a dry weight basis. Urayama (1972) discovered that cell-free extracts of
fresh F. velutipes introduced to distantly related species caused
fruitbody formation, suggesting transferable fruiting hormones exist in
liquid mycelial preparations.

## Storage of Liquid Cultures

Liquid inoculum can be prepared in advance and stored under refrigeration
for limited periods, though viability decreases over time. For long-term
preservation, [[stamets-slant-culture-strain-preservation]] remain the standard. Liquid culture is best used as an
expansion tool for active production cycles rather than as a long-term
storage medium. Maintaining master slant cultures and expanding through
liquid culture for each production run provides both security and
efficiency.
