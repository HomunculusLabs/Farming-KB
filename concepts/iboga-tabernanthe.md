# Tabernanthe iboga

## Overview

Tabernanthe iboga is a perennial rainforest shrub in the Apocynaceae family, native to the Congo Basin of West-Central Africa — particularly Gabon, Cameroon, and the Republic of Congo. The root bark contains the indole alkaloid **ibogaine**, along with dozens of related alkaloids including ibogamine, ibogaline, and tabernanthine. Iboga has been used for centuries in the **Bwiti** spiritual tradition and, since the mid-20th century, has attracted global attention for its potential in treating substance use disorders.

## Botany

- Growth habit: evergreen shrub to small tree, 1-2 m tall
- Leaves: opposite, glossy, obovate, 10-15 cm
- Flowers: small, yellowish-white, borne in clusters
- Fruit: ellipsoid berry, orange when ripe
- Root: the thick, yellowish root bark is the primary psychoactive part
- Habitat: understory of tropical rainforest, often in well-drained soils
- Propagation: primarily from seed; slow-growing, taking several years to reach harvestable root size

## Ibogaine Pharmacology

Ibogaine is a complex tryptamine derivative with multiple receptor interactions:

- **Serotonin transporter (SERT) inhibitor** — similar mechanism to SSRIs but far more complex
- **NMDA receptor antagonist** — may contribute to anti-addictive and oneiric effects
- **Kappa opioid receptor agonist** — implicated in withdrawal interruption
- **Sigma-2 receptor modulator** — neuroprotective and anti-addictive properties
- **hERG potassium channel blockade** — responsible for cardiac risk (QT prolongation)

The most notable property is ibogaine's ability to significantly reduce or eliminate opioid withdrawal symptoms, sometimes after a single dose. This effect is thought to involve both the metabolite **noribogaine** (which has a longer half-life) and a "resetting" of neuroadapted reward pathways.

### Metabolism

- Ibogaine is metabolized in the liver primarily via CYP2D6 and CYP3A4
- Primary metabolite: noribogaine (active, longer half-life ~28-72 hours)
- Half-life of ibogaine: 4-7 hours
- Noribogaine half-life: significantly longer, contributing to sustained effects

## Bwiti Ceremony

The Bwiti tradition, practiced primarily by the Fang, Mitsogo, and Punu peoples of Gabon, is the most well-documented indigenous use of iboga.

### Ceremony Structure

- Initiation ceremonies ( can last days and involve large doses
- Participants consume scraped root bark in a ritual context with music, drumming, and chanting
- The experience is described as a journey to the ancestral realm, confrontation with one's mortality, and rebirth
- Bwiti practitioners consider iboga a sacrament — not a drug — and attribute to it the power to heal, reveal truth, and connect the living with ancestors

### Cultural Context

- Bwiti is recognized as an official religion in Gabon
- The tradition blends indigenous animism with elements of Christianity (a syncretism common in Central Africa)
- Women and men participate in parallel but sometimes distinct roles
- Harp (ngombi) and mouth bow (mungongo) accompany all-night ceremonies
- In 2000, UNESCO recognized Bwiti oral traditions as part of Gabon's intangible cultural heritage

## Addiction Treatment

### History

- 1962: Howard Lotsof, a 19-year-old heroin user in New York, discovered ibogaine's anti-addictive properties after purchasing it from a chemist
- Lotsof subsequently devoted his life to advocating for ibogaine research and treatment access
- 1980s-90s: Underground ibogaine treatment networks emerged in Europe and the Americas
- Clinical research has been conducted in Panama, Mexico, the Netherlands, and elsewhere

### Treatment Models

- **Flood dose**: 15-25 mg/kg in a single session, producing a 12-36 hour visionary experience followed by extended afterglow
- **Staggered dose**: lower total dose administered over 24-48 hours
- **Microdosing/pulse dosing**: small doses (1-5 mg) taken daily for 30-60 days; less studied but popular in informal settings
- Treatment typically includes preparatory counseling and post-experience integration support

### Outcomes and Limitations

- Anecdotal and observational data suggest significant reduction in opioid withdrawal and craving
- Effects on alcohol, cocaine, and methamphetamine dependence are less consistent
- Relapse rates vary; ibogaine is not a "cure" but may provide a window for behavioral change
- The experience itself — confronting trauma, mortality, and the roots of addiction — is considered therapeutically essential by many providers

## Safety and Contraindications

Ibogaine carries significant medical risks:

- **Cardiac**: QT interval prolongation, risk of torsades de pointes, fatal arrhythmias
- **Contraindicated** with: heart disease, prolonged QT, liver impairment, history of stroke or seizures
- Deaths have occurred, primarily from cardiac events, often in individuals with pre-existing conditions or polydrug use
- Pre-treatment ECG and blood work (liver enzymes, electrolytes) are considered minimum safety standards
- Must never be combined with MAOIs, SSRIs, or other serotonergic drugs due to serotonin syndrome risk
- Requires medical supervision by trained providers

## Legal Status

- **United States**: Schedule I; not approved for medical use. Clinical trials ongoing under FDA approval.
- **Gabon**: Protected species; traditional Bwiti use is legal and culturally protected.
- **Canada**: Not scheduled; available through medical clinics in some provinces.
- **Mexico**: Unregulated; ibogaine treatment clinics operate openly.
- **Brazil**: Not scheduled; available at some treatment centers.
- **South Africa**: Previously available; regulatory status has shifted toward restriction.
- **New Zealand**: Restricted substance (Class B), available under medical supervision.
- **United Kingdom**: Class B controlled substance; possession and supply are criminal offenses.
- Many European countries: various restrictions; ibogaine treatment exists in legal gray areas.

## Conservation

Wild T. iboga populations are declining due to:

- Overharvesting for international demand (both traditional and treatment markets)
- Deforestation of Congo Basin rainforest
- Slow growth rate makes natural recovery difficult
- Cultivation efforts are underway but scale remains limited
- Sustainable sourcing and fair trade programs have been proposed to support both conservation and local communities
