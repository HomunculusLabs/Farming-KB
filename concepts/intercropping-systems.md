# Intercropping Systems
Intercropping is the practice of growing two or more crop species simultaneously in the same field or garden bed. Unlike monoculture, intercropping leverages ecological interactions between species to increase total yield per unit area, reduce pest pressure, improve soil health, and manage risk through diversification.
## Types of Intercropping
### Strip Intercropping
Two or more crops are grown in alternating strips wide enough to be managed with standard equipment (typically 6-30 feet per strip).
**How it works:**
- Crops are planted in wide, parallel strips across a field
- Each strip is managed independently (planted, fertilized, harvested separately)
- Adjacent strips interact ecologically at their shared borders
**Advantages:**
- Compatible with mechanized farming (strips wide enough for equipment)
- Reduces pest movement between strips (crop diversity disrupts pest habitat)
- Increases total yield compared to monoculture of either crop alone
- Can be adjusted each season without permanent infrastructure
**Examples:**
- Corn + soybeans: 12-row corn strips alternating with 12-row soybean strips. Corn yields increase from wind protection; soybeans benefit from reduced pest pressure.
- Wheat + clover: Wheat strips with clover understory that takes over after wheat harvest as a living mulch and nitrogen source.
- Tomatoes + basil: 4-row tomato strips with 2-row basil strips. Basil repels tomato pests.
### Relay Cropping
A second crop is planted into an existing standing crop before the first crop is harvested. The two crops overlap in time but share the field for only part of their growing cycles.
**How it works:**
- First crop is established and nearing maturity
- Second crop is planted between rows of the first crop
- First crop is harvested, leaving the second crop to continue growing
- There is a period where both crops occupy the field simultaneously
**Advantages:**
- Maximizes use of the growing season
- The first crop provides some protection for the emerging second crop
- No fallow period between crops
- See [[succession-planting]] for sequential timing
**Examples:**
- Plant winter wheat → overseed clover in early spring → harvest wheat in summer → clover continues as cover crop
- Plant sweet corn → interplant beans between corn rows when corn is knee-high → harvest corn → beans continue producing into fall
- Plant peas in early spring → transplant brassica seedlings between pea rows in late spring → harvest peas → brassicas mature through fall
### Row Intercropping
Two or more crops are planted in alternating rows within the same bed.
**How it works:**
- Crops are arranged in close-row patterns
- Rows may alternate (A-B-A-B) or follow other patterns (A-A-B-B)
- Requires hand cultivation or specialized equipment for narrow rows
**Examples:**
- Carrots + onions: Alternate single rows. Onions repel carrot rust fly; carrots repel onion fly. Both benefit from different root depths.
- Lettuce + radishes: Radishes mature in 25 days and are harvested before lettuce needs the space. Fast return on the radish row.
- Tomatoes + parsley: Parsley between tomato rows. Parsley attracts parasitic wasps that prey on tomato hornworms.
### Mixed Intercropping (Polyculture)
Multiple crop species are planted together without distinct row arrangement. Seeds or transplants are mixed and planted in a single bed or field area.
**How it works:**
- Crops are distributed throughout the bed without formal row structure
- Mimics natural plant communities more closely than row-based systems
- Highest level of spatial diversity
**Examples:**
- **Salad polyculture**: Lettuce, arugula, mustard greens, radishes, and cilantro seeded together and harvested as "cut-and-come-again" baby greens
- **Root polyculture**: Carrots, beets, parsnips, and turnips mixed in the same bed. Different maturity dates allow staggered harvest.
- **Mesa garden** (Oklahoma extension model): Corn, beans, squash, and sunflowers in a 4-foot diameter circle. Corn in center, beans around corn, squash around edge, sunflowers at cardinal points.
### Guild Planting
A type of polyculture where plants are selected to fill specific functional roles: nitrogen fixation, nutrient accumulation, pest deterrence, pollinator attraction, ground cover, and canopy.
**Example: Apple Tree Guild**
- **Canopy**: Apple tree
- **Understory**: Comfrey (dynamic accumulator), currants (fruit)
- **Nitrogen fixers**: Clovers, lupines beneath the tree
- **Pest deterrents**: Garlic, chives, nasturtiums
- **Pollinator plants**: Dill, yarrow, clover flowers
- **Ground cover**: Clover, thyme
- **Root crops**: Daikon radish to break compaction
See [[food-forest-design]] for guild-based perennial systems.
## Ecological Principles Behind Intercropping
### Resource Partitioning
Different species use different resources (light, water, nutrients) at different times, depths, and intensities. When combined, total resource use is greater than in monoculture.
- **Light**: Tall crops (corn, sunflowers) and short crops (squash, lettuce) use different light levels
- **Roots**: Deep-rooted (tomatoes, comfrey) and shallow-rooted (onions, lettuce) access different soil layers
- **Nutrients**: Legumes fix nitrogen; grasses use more nitrogen; brassicas need more sulfur
- **Time**: Fast-maturing (radishes, lettuce) and slow-maturing (winter squash, parsnips) occupy the same space at different times
### Facilitation
One crop benefits another through positive interactions:
- Legumes fix nitrogen for neighboring non-legumes
- Tall crops provide wind protection for shorter, sensitive crops
- Deep-rooted crops bring up subsoil nutrients for shallow-rooted neighbors
- Pest-repellent crops protect vulnerable neighbors (see [[companion-planting-reference]])
### Pest Suppression
Intercropping reduces pest pressure through several mechanisms:
- **Trap cropping**: One crop attracts pests away from the main crop (see [[trap-cropping]])
- **Push-pull strategy**: One plant repels a pest while another attracts it away
- **Disrupting host finding**: Pests have difficulty locating their preferred host when surrounded by non-host plants
- **Increased predator habitat**: Diverse plantings support more beneficial insects (see [[beneficial-insects-guide]])
## Land Equivalent Ratio (LER)
The LER measures the productivity advantage of intercropping over monoculture:
    LER = (Yield of Crop A in intercrop / Yield of Crop A in monoculture)
        + (Yield of Crop B in intercrop / Yield of Crop B in monoculture)
An LER > 1.0 means intercropping produces more total yield than growing the same area in monoculture. Well-designed intercrops typically achieve LER values of 1.2-1.8 (20-80% more productive per unit area).
## Design Considerations
### Matching Growth Habits
- Combine upright crops with sprawling crops (corn + squash)
- Combine fast-maturing with slow-maturing crops (radishes + carrots)
- Combine light-demanding with shade-tolerant crops (tomatoes + lettuce)
### Managing Competition
- Reduce planting density of each component crop by 25-50% compared to monoculture
- Ensure adequate spacing between rows to reduce direct competition
- Choose crops with complementary nutrient requirements
- Provide additional water and fertility for intercrop beds
### Harvest Timing
- Plan for different harvest times to reduce damage to remaining crops
- Use [[succession-planting]] to replace harvested components with new crops
- Consider how harvest of one crop affects the remaining crops (e.g., removing corn stalks exposes beans)
## Challenges
- More complex planning and management than monoculture
- Harvest can be more labor-intensive (different crops mature at different times)
- Mechanization is difficult for mixed and row intercropping (strip intercropping is most equipment-friendly)
- Competition can reduce yields if not properly managed
- Pest and disease identification is harder in diverse plantings
- Seed saving becomes complicated with closely related species nearby
