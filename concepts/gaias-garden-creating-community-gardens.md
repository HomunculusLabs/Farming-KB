# Creating Garden Communities and Guilds

The pieces of an ecological garden -- water, soil, plants, and animals -- create little more than a collection of fragments until assembled in proper relationships. This chapter examines how to connect these pieces into living communities that support, enhance, and benefit from each other.

## From Interplanting to Polyculture

### Interplanting

Vegetable gardeners have long mixed different varieties to save space and avoid monoculture. A classic combination pairs onions, carrots, and lettuce: the three have different leaf forms, light requirements, and rooting depths that minimize competition.

However, interplanting mostly avoids negative interactions rather than creating positive ones. It rarely capitalizes on mutual benefits like pest deterrence or nutrient transport.

### Companion Planting

A step further, companion planting pairs species that enhance each other -- sage near carrots to repel carrot fly, for example. But research has debunked many traditional companion recipes. Some combinations are neutral or even detrimental. In its simplest form, companion planting creates orderly beds of two or three species that remain static and ecologically limited.

### Polyculture: Dynamic Plant Communities

Polycultures are dynamic, self-organizing plant communities composed of several to many species. Unlike simple interplanting, the plants themselves tune their environment, creating niches for each other:

- Heat-shunning lettuces snuggle under a cauliflower canopy
- Slow-germinating wildflowers bide time in the moist shade of a currant bush
- Fast-growing radishes cast shade that protects slow-germinating parsnips
- Scented dill and calendula confuse pest insects searching for specific crops

## Ianto Evans's Polyculture

A seven-variety temperate polyculture developed by Welsh inventor Ianto Evans that provides months of continuous food from a single sowing:

**Species**: Radishes, dill, parsnip, calendula, lettuce, cabbage, bush beans

**How they interact**:
- Radishes sprout fast, casting shade to keep soil cool and protect slow germinators
- Dill and calendula attract beneficial insects and confuse pests with scent
- Lettuce provides early harvest, creating space for later crops
- Cabbages grow through fall and winter, protecting soil from erosion
- Beans add nitrogen to the soil
- Dense planting creates living mulch that conserves soil moisture and excludes weeds

## Jajarkot's Advanced Polyculture

Developed by a village self-development program in Nepal, this expanded polyculture can yield food for six to eight months per year:

**Species**: Mustard greens, arugula, garden purslane, radishes, chard, lettuces, carrots, fennel, dill, coriander, favas/beans/peas, alliums, cabbage/cauliflower/broccoli, basil, buckwheat

The polyculture follows natural succession principles: fast-growing ground cover gives way to herbs and vegetables, which are gradually replaced as openings appear.

### Guidelines for Designing Polycultures

1. Seed several varieties of each species to lengthen harvest and fill niches
2. Don't sow too thickly -- plan to eat the thinnings
3. Begin harvest early, when plants start to crowd, not when mature
4. Mix plant families, not just species -- avoid heavy single-genus plantings
5. Include many fast-growing, shallow-rooted species as ground cover
6. Overlap harvests with multiple varieties ripening at different times
7. Avoid root and light competition from sprawling plants
8. Harvest whole plants to create openings for remaining species
9. Save seed from the healthiest individuals for natural reseeding
10. Examine your polyculture daily -- "the best fertilizer is the gardener's shadow"

## Guilds: Beyond Polyculture

### What Is a Guild?

A guild is a harmoniously interwoven group of plants and animals, often centered around one major species, that benefits humans while creating habitat. Guilds bridge the gap between conventional vegetable gardens (which benefit only humans) and wildlife gardens (which specifically exclude people).

### The Three Sisters Guild

The Native American combination of corn, beans, and squash illustrates guild principles:

- **Corn** forms a trellis for beans and feeds nitrogen-fixing bacteria via root exudates
- **Beans** draw nitrogen from the air and convert it to plant-available form, feeding all three plants
- **Squash** forms a living mulch that shades soil, inhibits weeds, and retains moisture

Research by agronomist Jane Mt. Pleasant at Cornell shows this guild yields about 20 percent more calories than corn grown alone in an equal-sized plot.

In the Southwest, a fourth "sister" -- Rocky Mountain bee plant (Cleome serrulata) -- extends the guild by attracting pollinators for beans and squash. This demonstrates how guilds can draw upon multiple kingdoms (plant, animal, bacterial) to capitalize on enormous sources of energy and experience.

### Guild Design Principles

Guilds are fundamentally cooperative. In an ecosystem, microbes build soil, soil nourishes plants, plants feed animals, and animals disperse seed and leave waste to be transformed by microbes. This cycle of mutual interdependence contains many specific partnerships that can be re-created in gardens:

- **Nitrogen fixers** paired with heavy feeders
- **Insectary plants** attracting pollinators and pest predators near food crops
- **Mulch producers** (comfrey, cardoon) slashed periodically to build soil moisture
- **Dynamic accumulators** (chicory, yarrow, daikon) mining minerals from deep soil
- **Habitat plants** providing shelter for birds that control insect pests

### Super-Guilds

Multiple guilds can be connected into larger community networks. A fruit tree guild (centered on apple or pear) can be linked to a vegetable polyculture beneath it, which connects to a hedge guild providing wind protection and deer deterrence, which in turn connects to a poultry system that provides manure and insect control. These super-guilds approach the complexity and resilience of natural ecosystems.

## The Ecological Imperative

If we ignore the material needs of humans in our urban and suburban landscapes, we are doomed to continue consuming wild land for factory farms and tree plantations. Ecological gardens, using guilds and polycultures, help developed land blossom into nourishing places for both humans and wildlife -- reducing our ecological footprint while creating abundance at home.
