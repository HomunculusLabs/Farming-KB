# Mollison Animal Integration in Permaculture

Mollison argues that animals are essential to any agricultural system and should be utilised in all their energy functions, not just as meat producers.

## The Multiple Functions of Animals

In permaculture, animals provide useful products from materials otherwise inaccessible to man:
- **Producers**: Meat, fibres, eggs, down from materials of little direct use
- **Manure providers**: High quality manures from wastes
- **Pollinators and foragers**: Collecting dispersed materials from the system
- **Heat sources**: Body heat for greenhouses and barns
- **Gas producers**: CO2 and methane for enclosed systems
- **Tractors**: Poultry, pigs, and fish as efficient soil-turning, weeding, and manuring machines
- **Draught animals**: Operating pumps and vehicles
- **Pioneer bulldozers**: Clearing and manuring difficult areas before planting
- **Pest control**: Devouring pupae and eggs of pests in fallen fruit and trees
- **Nutrient concentrators**: Nitrogen and phosphates from flies and wasps
- **Water cleansing filters**
- **Fire control**: Short grazers reducing fuel loads

## Design Principles

"To take advantage of these many functions we need only to design by controlling numbers and space."

### Population Management
- All animal populations produce surplus from natural increase
- Like crowded forests, too many animals become non-productive
- Dense populations are self-thinned by starvation, stress, disease
- If we don't cull, nature does — usually as catastrophic population crashes
- To exclude animals from agricultural areas is to kill them in the long term

### The Breeder Paradox
Mollison challenges minimum-size fishing regulations: "We should throw back the breeders and utilize the small fish. No farmer could survive if he killed breeding cattle, and no fishery will thrive under the same mismanagement."

## Free-Range Foraging

The emphasis is on free-range foraging so that any animal is used in all its functions, not penned or restricted by energy-expensive means.

### Religious Context
Religious proscription against eating cattle has "a sound ecological basis in its native climate — cattle in monsoon areas are an essential adjunct to agriculture." Their role is to 'detour' tree leaves and monsoon grasses to nutrient-rich manures.

### Ecological Integration
"Plants without animal foragers are like strawberries without cream, or men without women." Animals "hold up half the sky" and are always in beneficial interplay with plant species.

## Attractant and Decoy Plants

### Pest Management via Animals
- Nepenthes (catmint) has fatal attraction to cats — combined with owls, proves lethal to feral cats
- Wallaby attracted to "wallaby wood" (Pittosporum bicolor) — useful for trapping
- Meat flies attracted to Arum dracunculus ("dead donkey" lily)
- Solanum grown with potatoes attracts Colorado beetle egg masses as a lethal decoy

### Intercropping for Pest Control
- Alternate or interplanting of non-resistant and resistant plants prevents pest build-up (more effective than endless chase for resistant varieties)
- Spring and summer broad beans attract aphids and hence their predators
- "A little powerful diversity of the right kind is the key component of stability"

## Specific Animal System Designs

Mollison's *Designer's Manual* details several integrated animal systems that demonstrate how multiple functions can be stacked in a single design. These systems illustrate the principle that every element should perform multiple functions:

### Poultry Systems
Chickens in permaculture are managed for far more than eggs and meat. A flock of 15-25 chickens on a quarter-acre can provide pest control in orchards (consuming codling moth larvae and curculio), weed suppression in garden beds, manure for compost systems, and soil aeration through scratching. Chickens following behind grazing ruminants in a rotational system will break up and spread manure patties, reducing parasite load on pasture while converting fly larvae into protein. Mollison specifies that the ideal poultry shelter is mobile, predator-proof, and designed to collect manure — a design later popularized as the "chicken tractor" by Andy Lee.

### Pig Systems
Pigs are among the most versatile animals in the permaculture toolkit. Their rooting behavior, which is destructive in conventional settings, becomes a productive function when directed. Pigs can be used to clear land for planting (rooting out rhizomatous weeds, turning sod, and incorporating organic matter), to harvest crops that are difficult to process mechanically (pigs will sort and eat chestnuts, acorns, and fallen fruit, leaving the hulls as mulch), and to maintain firebreaks through their grazing activity. Mollison recommends confining pigs to specific areas with electric fencing and rotating them through zones to prevent over-concentration of manure and soil compaction.

### Duck and Waterfowl Integration
Ducks provide exceptional slug and snail control in gardens and orchards, a function chickens perform less effectively. When integrated with aquaculture systems, ducks fertilize ponds with their manure, stimulate algal growth that feeds fish, and can harvest aquatic plants and invertebrates. The combination of duck ponds, rice paddies, and fish culture — practiced for millennia in Southeast Asia — represents one of the most productive integrated animal systems ever developed. Mollison adapted these traditional Asian polycultures for temperate climates, designing systems where duck manure fertilizes pond water, pond water irrigates and fertilizes adjacent gardens, and garden waste feeds both ducks and fish.

### Rabbit and Small Livestock
Rabbits convert forage to protein with exceptional efficiency, producing manure that is among the most balanced of any livestock (neither too hot nor too cold for direct application). Their manure can be fed directly to worm farms, creating a high-quality vermicompost. Rabbit manure contains approximately 2.4% nitrogen, 1.4% phosphorus, and 0.6% potassium, making it a valuable amendment without the composting required for other manures. Mollison recommended integrating rabbitries with greenhouse systems, where animal body heat reduces heating costs and CO2-enriched air promotes plant growth.

## Forage Design and Animal Nutrition

Mollison emphasized that productive animal systems require designed forage, not just open pasture. Tree fodders (mulberry, carob, honey locust, tagasaste) provide high-protein leaf during seasons when grass is scarce or dormant. Fodder banks of perennial species along fence lines and waterways reduce the need for imported feed while providing erosion control and wildlife habitat. The concept of the "food forest" for livestock — multistory plantings of trees, shrubs, and ground covers that produce fodder year-round — represents one of Mollison's most significant design contributions, prefiguring modern silvopasture research.

## Zone-Based Animal Placement

Mollison's zone system provides a framework for placing animals according to the frequency of management attention they require and the intensity of their interaction with other system elements:

- **Zone 1** (immediate vicinity of the home): Small animals requiring daily attention — chickens for eggs, rabbits for manure, worm farms for composting kitchen scraps. These animals provide daily products and their management integrates with household routines.
- **Zone 2** (intensive garden area): Poultry in mobile tractors for pest control and soil preparation, bees for pollination and honey production. These animals interact directly with food production systems and require regular but not daily intervention.
- **Zone 3** (orchards and farm woodland): Larger livestock including sheep, goats, and pigs managed in rotational grazing systems. These animals provide grazing management, manure for orchard trees, and periodic income from meat or fiber production.
- **Zone 4** (managed forest and extensive grazing): Cattle, horses, or extensive sheep systems. These animals require the least frequent management attention but provide valuable large-scale vegetation management and nutrient cycling across broad areas.
- **Zone 5** (wilderness): No deliberate animal management, though wild animals contribute to ecosystem function through seed dispersal, pest control, and nutrient cycling.

The key principle is that animals should be placed where their products and services are most needed and where their management requirements match the available labor. Placing chickens in Zone 1 ensures daily egg collection with minimal effort; placing bees in Zone 2 ensures pollination of fruit trees and vegetables; placing cattle in Zone 4 maintains pasture health without demanding daily attention.

## Mollison on Animal Welfare

While Mollison is often perceived as primarily focused on productivity, the *Designer's Manual* contains passages that reflect genuine concern for animal welfare within integrated systems. He argues that animals in permaculture systems experience better conditions than those in conventional confinement operations: free-range access to diverse forage, natural social structures, freedom to perform species-typical behaviors, and integration into ecological processes rather than treatment as industrial inputs. The emphasis on free-range foraging, rotational management, and multi-function design naturally produces conditions that align with animal welfare goals.

Mollison also addresses the ethics of culling directly, arguing that responsible population management is more humane than the alternative of allowing nature to "cull" through starvation and disease. The breeder paradox — protecting breeding stock rather than harvesting the largest individuals — reflects a management philosophy that prioritizes long-term population health and system resilience over short-term extraction.

## Practical Design Guidelines

In practice, Mollison's animal integration principles translate into specific design patterns. [[permaculture-designers-manual-chicken-tractors]] follow the "animal as tractor" concept literally, with mobile coops that daily relocate birds to fresh ground. [[permaculture-duck-pond-systems]] integrate waterfowl with aquaculture-in-permaculture|aquaculture and irrigation. [[permaculture-designers-manual-animal-forage-systems]] using tree crops (silvopasture-systems|silvopasture) provide shade, fodder, and timber while animals cycle nutrients. The key design principle is that every animal should be connected to multiple system elements -- a chicken flock connected to compost, garden, orchard, and pest management functions returns far more value than the same flock in a standalone coop.
