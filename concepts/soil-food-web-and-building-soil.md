# Soil Food Web and Building Soil

## The Living Soil

Soil is not dirt—it is a living ecosystem teeming with billions of organisms
in every teaspoon. The **soil food web** is the community of bacteria, fungi,
protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, earthworms, and other organisms that
cycle nutrients, build soil structure, and maintain plant health. Understanding
and nurturing this community is the foundation of [[ecological-gardening-principles]].

When we apply [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]], we feed plants directly but starve the
soil life that would otherwise provide those nutrients for free. Ecological
gardening reverses this: we feed the soil, and the soil feeds the plants.

## Key Members of the Soil Food Web

### Bacteria

Bacteria are the most numerous soil organisms. They decompose simple organic
compounds, fix atmospheric nitrogen (in legume root nodules), and secrete
slimes that bind soil particles into aggregates. Bacteria-dominated soils are
typical of disturbed or early-successional environments and favor annual
plants and weeds.

### Fungi

Fungi break down tough, woody organic matter that bacteria cannot digest. They
form vast [[ectomycorrhizal-nutrient-cycling-mycelial-networks]] that transport nutrients and water across
distances. Fungal-dominated soils characterize mature ecosystems such as
forests and favor perennial plants and trees—the very plants most ecological
gardeners want to grow.

### Protozoa

Protozoa are single-celled organisms that eat bacteria. By grazing on
bacterial populations, they release plant-available nitrogen in a form
and pace that plants can absorb. This natural [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] is far more
efficient than synthetic fertilization.

### Nematodes

[[fungi-dominant-soil-biomass-ecosystem-engineers]] of the soil world. They burrow,
creating channels for water and roots. Their castings are rich in plant-
available nutrients and [[cation-exchange-capacity]] (CEC),
  holding nutrients in the root zone where plants can access them.
- **Water retention**: One pound of humus can hold several pounds of water,
  dramatically improving drought resistance.
- **Soil aggregation**: Humus glues soil particles into stable crumbs that
  resist erosion, allow root penetration, and maintain pore spaces for air
  and water movement.

Building humus is the single most productive thing a gardener can do.

## Building Soil Life

### Composting

Composting accelerates the [[fukuoka-microbes-scavengers-natural-decomposition-biosystem]] process. A well-managed
compost pile heats up as bacteria and fungi break down organic matter,
killing weed seeds and pathogens. The finished product teems with beneficial
organisms ready to inoculate garden soil. Apply compost as a top-dressing,
side-dress, or incorporate into planting holes.

### Sheet Mulching

Sheet mulching (also called [[sheet-mulching-lasagna-gardening]]) builds soil in place without
tilling. Lay down cardboard or newspaper to suppress weeds, then alternate
layers of nitrogen-rich (green) and carbon-rich (brown) materials. Top with
mulch. Over months, the layers decompose into rich, worm-filled soil.

### Cover Crops

Cover crops protect bare soil, fix nitrogen (legumes), break compaction
(daikon radish), and add organic matter when cut and dropped as green mulch.
They keep [[soil-food-web]] fed during fallow periods query how do i garden on a [[query-how-do-i-garden-on-a-slope-and-prevent-erosion]] erosion.

### Woody Debris

Hugelkultur beds use logs and branches as a buried base. As the wood slowly
decomposes, it acts as a sponge for water and a long-term source of carbon
for soil fungi. This technique is especially valuable in dry climates.

## Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratios

Successful composting and mulching depend on balancing carbon and nitrogen:

| Material | C:N Ratio |
|----------|-----------|
| Leaves | 60:1 |
| Straw | 80:1 |
| Wood chips | 400:1 |
| Grass clippings | 20:1 |
| Kitchen scraps | 15:1 |
| Manure (cow) | 20:1 |
| Manure (chicken) | 10:1 |
| Alfalfa | 12:1 |

Aim for roughly 30:1 overall. Too much carbon slows decomposition; too much
nitrogen causes odors and nitrogen loss as ammonia gas.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots, extending the
root system's reach by up to 700 times. In exchange for carbohydrates from
the plant, the fungi deliver phosphorus, water, and other nutrients from soil
the roots alone could not access. Tilling severs these networks; minimizing
soil disturbance preserves them. Inoculants are available but are rarely
necessary—these fungi are ubiquitous and will colonize undisturbed soil.

## Working With Soil Biology

By composting, mulching, minimizing tillage, and planting diverse species,
ecological gardeners create conditions where soil life thrives. Healthy soil
biology means plants get nutrients on demand, soil retains water without
compaction, and chemical fertilizers become unnecessary. The gardener shifts
from being a laborer to being an ecologist, observing and supporting the
processes that nature has refined over billions of years.
