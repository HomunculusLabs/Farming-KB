# Brix Testing as a Plant Health Indicator in AACT Programs

## Overview

Brix is a measurement of dissolved solids — primarily sugars — in plant sap,
taken using a handheld refractometer. In AACT programs, Brix readings serve as
a field-accessible indicator of plant health and [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]]. Dr. Elaine
Ingham approaches Brix testing with characteristic scientific caution, noting
that while elevated Brix readings can indicate that a plant is obtaining what it
needs, the relationship is complex and requires careful interpretation.

## What Brix Measures

Brix is a measure of sugar content in plant sap. The reading is obtained by
placing a drop of plant juice on the lens of a refractometer and reading the
scale where a shadow line falls. The instrument works by measuring how much
light bends (refracts) as it passes through the plant sap — the more dissolved
solids present, the more the light bends and the higher the Brix reading.

While Brix is most directly a measure of sugar concentration, plant sap
contains other dissolved solids that contribute to the reading, including free
amino acids, proteins, organic acids, minerals, and other soluble compounds.
This is why some practitioners refer to Brix as a measure of "total dissolved
solids" rather than just sugar. However, sugars are typically the dominant
component, and changes in Brix readings most often reflect changes in sugar
concentration within the plant.

Ingham notes that Brix alone cannot measure free amino acids directly —
laboratory extraction and analysis are required for that. But as a rapid field
tool, the refractometer provides a useful snapshot of overall plant sap quality.

## Why Higher Brix Can Indicate Healthier Plants

The theory behind Brix as a health indicator rests on plant physiology. Plants
produce sugars through photosynthesis. When a plant is healthy and its soil
foodweb is functioning properly, it can efficiently produce photosynthates. These
sugars provide energy for metabolism, serve as building blocks for structural
compounds, and fuel [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]] production for defense against pests
and disease.

A plant with adequate mineral nutrition and a healthy root-associated microbial
community tends to produce more photosynthates than it immediately needs for
basic growth. The excess sugars accumulate in plant tissue, resulting in higher
Brix readings. These surplus sugars are available for the plant to invest in
defense compounds, giving the plant greater resistance to insect pests and
[[bloomfield-fungal-pathogens-and-parasites]].

Ingham's testing has shown that vegetables, grapes, and strawberries grown with
healthy foodwebs in the soil contain three to ten times more protein than plants
grown in conventional systems. This strongly suggests that biologically active
soil supports not just sugar production but overall improved nutritional quality
in the harvested crop.
