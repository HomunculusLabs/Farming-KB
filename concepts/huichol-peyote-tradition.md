## Huichol Peyote Tradition
The Huichol (Wixarika) people of the Sierra Madre Occidental in
western Mexico
maintain one of the most elaborate and well-preserved peyote
traditions in the
Americas. The Huichol consider peyote (hikuri) to be the central
sacrament of
their religion, and their annual pilgrimage to collect peyote in the
sacred
land of Wirikuta is one of the most important religious ceremonies in
indigenous [[peyote-ceremony-guide]] is the annual pilgrimage to
Wirikuta, a sacred area in the deserts of San Luis Potosi, some 300
miles
(500 km) from the Huichol homeland. This journey, which can take
several
weeks, is undertaken by a group led by a mara"akame (shaman).
The pilgrimage follows a prescribed route, with specific stopping
points where
offerings and prayers are made. The journey itself is considered a
re-enactment
of the sacred journey of the gods and ancestors. Along the way,
participants
undergo purification, fasting, and preparation for the peyote hunt.
Upon reaching Wirikuta, the pilgrims hunt for peyote by seeking the
"footprints" of the Deer Spirit -- patterns in the desert landscape
that lead
to the peyote cactus. When a peyote plant is found, it is "shot" with
an
arrow (now symbolic) and harvested with prayers and offerings.
## The Peyote Ceremony
Upon returning from Wirikuta with the harvested peyote, the Huichol
conduct
elaborate ceremonies in their community temples (tuki). The ceremonies
typically last several days and include:
- Extensive prayers and offerings to the gods and ancestors
- The consumption of peyote buttons, often in large quantities during
  initiation ceremonies
- All-night singing and dancing around the ceremonial fire
- [[metzner-peyote-ceremonies-native-american-church-and-huichol-traditions]]. Common
motifs
include deer, maize, peyote buttons, snakes, eagles, and the sun --
all
elements of the Huichol sacred cosmology.
These artworks are not merely decorative; they function as maps of the
visionary world and as tools for communicating the insights received
during
peyote experiences. The art serves to document and transmit the
religious
knowledge gained through peyote visions from one generation to the
next.
