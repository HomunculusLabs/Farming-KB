# Evolving Polycultures and Species Richness

## Overview

One of the most significant insights from [[fungal-species-richness-and-diversity-indices|diversity]]. This has profound implications for how we design and manage productive landscapes.

## Species Richness and Zonation

Species richness in a polyculture should be highest near the village or home and simplify to well-tested species of high yields as distance from the home increases. This follows the permaculture zone principle:

- **Zone 1 (nearest home):** Most complex and species-rich; gardens, walls, roof areas, trellis systems, and compounds can support 200-400 managed species
- **Intermediate zones:** Moderate diversity with emphasis on reliable producers
- **Outer zones:** Simplified to proven, high-yielding species suited to lower management intensity

Trellis crops should first be placed to shade the home, livestock, or to make fences, and only later placed on Acacia or other legumes as they age.

## Factors Determining Species Complexity

The number of productive, managed, and effectively-harvested species in a polyculture is determined by a complex of seven factors:

### 1. Labor Availability
The number of people responsible for managing one or two crop or animal species directly limits complexity. More hands allow more species to be tended.

### 2. Proximity (Zoning)
The proximity of the complex to village or settlement determines how much attention each species receives. Species requiring frequent harvest or care must be close to settlement.

### 3. Fiscal Economics
The relative cost-benefit balance on increasing inputs to optimum levels. Financial analysis tends to favor simplicity because diverse systems spread management thin and some products lack markets.

### 4. Plant Guilds
The need for effective plant guilds — harmonious ecological assemblies where species support each other through nutrient cycling, pest regulation, and microclimate modification.

### 5. Processing and Marketing Capacity
The method of marketing and processing must cope with complex products. A diverse harvest is worthless if there is no capacity to process and sell many different products.

### 6. Total Area Controlled
Larger areas demand increasing simplicity, at a cost to ecological complexity. A small intensively-managed garden can support far more species per hectare than a broadacre farm.

### 7. System Maturity
Older systems provide more niches as the canopy develops multiple layers and microclimates diversify. Younger systems produce more regular, predictable product from fewer species.

## Species Inventory for a Complex Village Polyculture

In a well-developed village situation, the following species counts are achievable:

| Usage Class | Species Range |
|---|---|
| Basic food species | 70-90 |
| Mulch and fodder provision | 10-30 |
| Medicinals and biocides | 20-50 |
| Structural and craft | 5-20 |
| Culinary herbs | 20-30 |
| Beverages and export specialties | 10-15 |
| Fuelwood and coppice | 12-30 |
| Special/sacred uses | 8-15 |
| **Total cultivated species** | **155-280** |
| Plus wild-gathered species | 40-80 |
| **Total species utilized** | **195-360** |

Some very useful species fall into 3 or more of these classes simultaneously, which is precisely what makes them so valuable in the system.

## The Fiscal vs. Nutritional Divergence

Perhaps the most important analytical insight in Mollison's treatment of polycultures is the dramatic divergence between two measures of system performance:

### Fiscal (Dollar) Returns Peak at 6-8 Species
When analyzed purely in dollar terms, the economic return from a polyculture system peaks at approximately 6-8 species. This is because:

- Markets reward specialization and volume
- Processing and distribution infrastructure favors standardized products
- Management costs increase with diversity
- Many species have no commercial market despite their nutritional value
- Financial accounting ignores the value of self-sufficiency, ecological services, and resilience

### Nutritional/Total Yield Returns Peak at 50-100 Species
When analyzed for total nutrition and total yield (ignoring dollar returns), the optimal system peaks at 50-100 species, well-distributed over all seasons. This is because:

- Nutritional completeness requires diversity of food types
- Seasonal distribution ensures year-round food availability
- Many species contribute small but critical nutritional components
- Ecological stability and resilience increase with diversity
- Self-reliance eliminates dependency on volatile markets

### The Design Implication

These two factors — extensive-intensive, fiscal-nutritional — must be defined by designers for their specific needs and will have a profound effect on design. What emerges is a sensible zonation: maximum species richness close to the home (where nutritional completeness and self-reliance matter most), and concentration on fewer species of high cash value in outer zones.

**Gardens as trial areas:** The garden is where the value of successful polyculture extension can be learned without sacrificing large amounts of energy and capital. Gardens serve as experimental zones for testing species and assemblies before scaling to outer zones.

## Scale and Manageability

The fundamental tension in polyculture design is between ecological richness and human manageability:

- **Very complex polycultures work best at small scale** with close attention from people
- **Depopulated, dehumanized modern agricultural landscapes** are unable to cope with any but the most basic and simple intercrop systems
- **Sacrifices of yield, quality, and stability** result from the simplification demanded by broadacre mechanization
- **People are essential** to complex polyculture — the depopulation of rural areas is a primary driver of agricultural simplification

## [[village-polyculture-species-richness-and-site-planning|Planning]] the Whole Site

Even in established polycultures, periodic re-survey with special attention to key factors can significantly improve performance:

### Key Re-Assessment Factors
- **Main access and harvesting ways** — can these be optimized to reduce labor?
- **Earth-shaping for rainwater harvest** — is water being captured effectively for each crop?
- **Sufficiency of mulch** — is there enough organic material for soil protection?
- **Water and irrigation strategies** — can these be improved?
- **Village planning** — does the settlement layout support efficient management?
- **Site processing for market** — can processing add value to products?

### Steps for New Site Establishment

1. **Market assessment:** Future prices, potential for processing to higher value, labor availability, legal systems, social necessities, and local self-reliance needs
2. **Soil analysis:** Professional advice on soils and necessary nutrients and trace elements
3. **Layout planning:** Ground layout, windbreak, access, and water (detailing can follow later)
4. **Earthworks:** Plan and carry out essential earthworks (swales, dams, terraces)
5. **Nursery establishment:** Use selected varietal forms for new or replacement crops
6. **Broadscale planting:** Commence with or after windbreak and nurse crop establishment
7. **Continuous assessment:** Constant assessment, consultation, feedback, and innovative trials — fill niches as they evolve

### Priority Considerations for New Sites

- **Windbreaks and earth-shaping** are priorities that precede planting
- **Soil tests** for plant nutrients and trace elements are essential — modest amounts of these give early vigor and early yields
- **Intercrop selection** serves multiple purposes: sheltering delicate crops, providing mulch, nitrogen fixation, and protection from wind, salt, or sun damage

## The Nigerian Polyculture Model

Figure 10.29 in the source illustrates a Nigerian polyculture for the humid tropics that includes:
- Pig or goat pen integrated with the system
- Forage crops on appropriate ridges for cassava
- Strip cropping with zero runoff
- Contour layout throughout

This model demonstrates the integration of animals, annual crops, and tree crops in a single contoured system designed to eliminate runoff and maximize nutrient cycling.
