# Bee Forage and Range Design

## Overview

Bees are among the most valuable integrated elements in a permaculture system, producing honey, wax, pollen, propolis, and royal jelly while performing essential pollination services for a wide variety of food, oil, fruit, and seed crops including mustard, clovers, buckwheat, most small fruits, apples, and grain legumes. Designing an effective bee range requires understanding bee biology, forage preferences, seasonal availability, and the synergistic relationship between beekeeping and crop production.

## Bee Range Biology and Layout

### Range Radius and Hive Spacing
A bee hive or cluster of hives ranges over an area of approximately 2.5 km radius. Commercial registered hive sites are therefore sited 5 km apart to restrict disease transmission between sites. As many as 100–150 hives may be grouped at a single site, depending on the richness of the forage resource.

### Foraging Preferences
Bees prefer to fly 100 meters or more to forage, and the flight distance assists in nectar evaporation into honey. Forage species should therefore be planted at this distance or further from hives. Bees more efficiently harvest clumped nectar sources rather than scattered individual plants, making hedgerows, fields, or clumps of preferred forage species superior to a mosaic of scattered individual plants.

### Shelter Requirements
The hive site must be sheltered from extremes of cold, wet, wind, and sun. However, the site should not be atypical of the regional climate, or bees are tempted out in adverse weather and perish. Cold winds most restrict foraging, so hedgerow shelter (even low hedgerows of 0.5–1 m height) is essential, preferably leading from the hive site (the "bee village") to the forage areas. Suitable hedgerow species include rosemary, Acacia, catmint, capeweed, thyme, and field daisies.

### Water Access
Clustered hives consume very large quantities of water for hive cooling. Bees ventilate hives as a "super organism," inhaling and exhaling in approximately 1.2-minute rhythms. Access to water is most safely achieved by providing soaked mats or hessian at pond edges so bees cannot drown while drinking. Small ponds or troughs free of dragonflies result in fewer losses than large ponds, where dragonflies are efficient bee predators.

### Temperature Management
Normal hive temperatures are around 21°C. In cold climates, hive insulation is critical — in Denmark, bees are over-wintered in thatched attic areas, while other parts of Europe use insulated hive houses. In hot summer climates, hives need shade and good ventilation, or honey is lost as energy expended on cooling (fanning) by the hive.

## Forage Species Selection

### Pollen Requirements
In addition to nectar, bees require pollen to rear replacement worker bees, especially early in the season. Early pollen sources are critical: willows (especially pussy willow), Acacias, pines, and vine crops that yield early pollen are highly beneficial.

### Propolis Sources
Bees gather propolis — a hard waxy substance used to plug wind gaps and repaint hives — from species such as Xanthorrheas, poplars, and pines. Very few propolis sources are needed across the whole range.

### Seasonal Forage Planning
Forage species are best organized into a 3 × 3 matrix by season (early, mid, late) and honey type (classical, bulk, special/crop) to ensure continuous availability:

**Classical (highly preferred) honeys:**
- Heather honeys: sage, thyme, catmint, rosemary
- Pine tree or basswood (Tilia spp.) honeys (Europe and USA)
- Marsh tupelo honeys (subtropics)
- Leatherwood (Eucryphia) honeys (Chile and Tasmania)
- Citrus honeys

**Bulk honeys (for blending):**
- Clovers, eucalypts, Acacias
- Field crops: mustard, buckwheat
- Fruit crops (Rosaceae family): blackberry, raspberry, apples
- Mixed garden honey

**Special and crop honeys:**
- Gooseberry (early season)
- Small fruits and mustard (late season)

### Diversity Target
A minimum of 35 main forage species across the whole season is recommended, as species like clover and eucalypts vary in yield from year to year. Pollen species are selected on the same basis, with pollen traps fitted to hives during high-yield periods. Well-managed hives should yield approximately equal weights of pollen and honey; pollen provides a high-protein additive for flours and starchy foods.

### Special Pollen Notes
Some pollens, such as those from certain Acacia species and goldenrod, may cause asthma if inhaled by susceptible individuals. However, when eaten, these honeys and pollens can act to prevent allergic reactions — a form of natural desensitization similar to how young leaves of poison ivy and poison oak, when consumed, can build immunity.

## Pollination-Dependent Crops

The bee range presents a unique opportunity to grow crops dependent on bee pollination. Crops within approximately 1.5 km of hives can out-yield crops in bee-deficient areas by a factor of 3–10 times. Suitable pollination-dependent crops include:

- Kiwifruit and all bramble berries
- Small fruits in general
- Mustards and Brassicas
- Clovers
- Apples and pome fruits
- Buckwheat
- Acacia species
- Stone pine

## The Ideal Bee Farm

An excellent bee farm combines apiary management with production farming for fruits and seeds. Cross-wind forage hedgerows shelter bee-dependent seed crops, herbs, and vines. The system requires adequate water, diverse pollen sources, proper shelter, and a well-designed honey house. The integration of beekeeping with orchard and seed crop production creates a synergistic system where each element supports the others.
