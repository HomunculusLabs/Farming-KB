## Overview

Bulk substrate is the nutritious, water-retaining medium that mushroom mycelium colonizes to produce fruiting bodies. In home cultivation, the bulk substrate is typically mixed with colonized grain spawn at a defined ratio. The substrate provides the water reservoir and additional nutrition that grain alone cannot supply. The standard bulk substrate for Psilocybe cubensis is CVG — coconut coir, vermiculite, and gypsum — valued for its simplicity, contamination resistance, and consistent results.

## CVG: The Standard Substrate

### Components

**Coconut Coir**
- Processed from coconut husk fibers, usually sold in compressed bricks (650g is standard).
- Naturally resistant to contamination — coconut coir contains tannins and other compounds that inhibit microbial growth.
- Excellent water retention capacity (holds 8-9 times its weight in water).
- Near-neutral pH (6.0-6.8), ideal for mushroom cultivation.
- No nutritional supplementation needed for cubensis, though it can be added.

**Vermiculite**
- A mined mineral that has been expanded through heat treatment.
- Absorbs and retains water while maintaining aeration in the substrate.
- Inert and sterile, adds no nutrition but improves substrate structure.
- Medium or coarse grade preferred over fine.

**Gypsum (Calcium Sulfate)**
- Provides calcium and sulfur, both beneficial for mushroom growth.
- Helps prevent grain kernels from clumping together.
- Buffers pH slightly.
- Agricultural grade (ag-gyp) is fine; do not use drywall.
- Optional but recommended at 5-10% by volume.

### Standard CVG Recipe

For a 66-quart monotub with 4 quarts of spawn:

- 1x 650g compressed coco coir brick
- 2 quarts vermiculite (medium or coarse)
- 1/2 cup gypsum (agricultural grade)

Yields approximately 8-9 quarts of hydrated substrate (enough for a 1:2 spawn ratio).

### Hydration Method: Bucket Tek

The simplest and most popular method. Coco coir is hydrated with boiling water, which simultaneously pasteurizes it.

1. Place the coco coir brick in a large bucket (5-gallon or larger).
2. Add 4 quarts (1 gallon) of boiling water per 650g brick. Some growers use more — up to 5 quarts for fluffier coir.
3. Cover the bucket and let sit for 30-60 minutes. The coir will expand dramatically.
4. Add vermiculite and gypsum, mix thoroughly with clean hands or a sterilized utensil.
5. Check field capacity (see below). Add dry vermiculite if too wet, or a small amount of water if too dry.
6. Allow the substrate to cool to room temperature (70-78F) before spawning.

The boiling water raises the core temperature to approximately 160-180F (71-82C), which is sufficient to pasteurize coco coir. Because coir is naturally clean, this method works reliably despite not reaching true pasteurization temperatures throughout.

### Pasteurization vs. Bucket Tek

True pasteurization involves holding the substrate at 140-170F (60-76C) for 60-120 minutes. This kills most contaminants and their spores while preserving beneficial microorganisms. Methods include:

- **Hot water bath**: Substrate in a filter bag, submerged in a water bath held at 160F.
- **Oven pasteurization**: Substrate in oven-safe bags at 170F for 2 hours.
- **Steam pasteurization**: Using a large pot with a rack and steaming substrate bags.

For plain CVG (unsupplemented), the bucket tek is sufficient in most cases. When using supplemented substrates (added grain, manure, or bran), proper pasteurization becomes critical because the added nutrition is highly susceptible to contamination.

## Field Capacity

Field capacity is the target moisture level for the substrate. It is the point at which the substrate holds maximum water without pooling or dripping.

**Testing field capacity**:
- Grab a handful of substrate and squeeze firmly in your fist.
- A few drops of water (1-3) should emerge between your fingers.
- The substrate should form a loose ball that breaks apart when disturbed.
- If no water emerges, the substrate is too dry — add water and mix.
- If water streams out, the substrate is too wet — add dry vermiculite.

Getting field capacity right is one of the most important skills in cultivation. Too wet leads to bacterial contamination and stalled colonization. Too dry leads to slow growth and poor yields.

## Alternative Bulk Substrates

### Horse Manure

Horse manure is a traditional substrate, especially for outdoor grows. It provides excellent nutrition and water retention. Must be aged (6+ months) or leached and pasteurized. Often mixed with straw (50/50 by volume). Popular with Panaeolus cyanescens growers.

### Straw

Wheat or oat straw, chopped to 1-3 inch pieces. Excellent aeration and decent nutrition. Must be pasteurized thoroughly — straw is highly contamination-prone. Often used for oyster mushroom cultivation. Less common for cubensis monotubs due to the extra preparation work.

### Compost

Mushroom compost or custom compost mixes can produce excellent results but require careful preparation. The composting process itself must reach thermophilic temperatures (130-170F) to kill pathogens and weed seeds. Not recommended for beginners.

### CVG + Supplementation

Adding nutrition to CVG can boost yields but increases contamination risk:

- **Coffee grounds**: 5-10% by volume. Adds nitrogen. Can be added fresh to bucket tek.
- **Wheat bran**: 5-10% by volume. Adds carbohydrates and nitrogen. Requires proper pasteurization.
- **Blood meal or bone meal**: 2-5% by volume. High nitrogen. Requires pasteurization.
- **Millet or ground grain**: 5-10% by volume. Very nutritious but very contamination-prone.

Rule of thumb: any supplemented substrate must be properly pasteurized, not just bucket-tek'd.

## Substrate Depth

Substrate depth affects yield potential and water availability:

- **Minimum 3 inches**: Below this, water reserves are insufficient for a good flush.
- **Sweet spot 4-6 inches**: Optimal balance of water capacity, colonization speed, and yield.
- **7+ inches**: Possible but diminishing returns. Deep substrates take longer to colonize and have higher contamination risk.

For a 66-quart monotub, 4-5 inches of substrate depth is standard, requiring approximately 8-12 quarts of CVG plus spawn.

## Troubleshooting Substrate Issues

- **Sour smell during colonization**: Bacterial contamination, likely from overly wet substrate or contaminated spawn. Monitor closely — may still fruit if caught early.
- **Substrate pulling away from tub walls**: Normal as the mycelium consumes water and the substrate consolidates. Prevent side pinning with a trash bag liner.
- **Dry surface**: Mist lightly. Ensure FAE holes are not too large. Consider a thinner casing layer.
- **Excessive condensation on lid**: Normal. If water is dripping onto the substrate surface, wipe the lid or add a small layer of paper towel.
- **Slow colonization**: Check temperature (should be 70-78F). Substrate may be too dense or too wet. Spawn ratio may be too low.
