# AACT Brewing Techniques and Oxygen Management

[[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) brewing is a controlled biological extraction process that requires careful management of oxygen levels, compost quality, water characteristics, and brewing equipment. The goal is to multiply beneficial aerobic microorganisms extracted from high-quality compost and deliver them to soil or plant surfaces in a viable, active state.

## The 24-Hour Brewing Cycle

The standard AACT brew runs for approximately 24 hours. During this period, microbial populations extracted from compost multiply exponentially when provided with adequate oxygen, food sources, and appropriate environmental conditions. The brewing window is critical: brew too short and organism populations have not peaked; brew too long and the tea can shift toward anaerobic conditions, encouraging harmful pathogens and producing phytotoxic metabolites.

Key stages of a typical 24-hour brew:

1. **Extraction phase (0–4 hours):** Organisms are washed out of the compost into the water. Bacteria begin reproducing immediately.
2. **Growth phase (4–18 hours):** Bacterial populations multiply rapidly. Fungal hyphae begin to extend if fungal foods are present.
3. **Peak diversity (18–24 hours):** Maximum diversity and biomass of beneficial organisms. This is the ideal application window.
4. **Decline phase (24+ hours):** Food resources become depleted. If oxygen drops, anaerobic organisms begin to dominate.

## Washing Biofilm

Biofilm buildup on brewing equipment surfaces is a persistent issue. Biofilm harbors both beneficial and potentially harmful organisms, and if left unchecked, it can contaminate subsequent brews. Equipment should be cleaned thoroughly between brews using non-toxic cleaning methods. Mechanical scrubbing combined with citric acid or hydrogen peroxide solutions helps remove biofilm without leaving residues harmful to the next brew's biology.

## Compost Tea Making Fundamentals

### The "Cake" Method

A common approach involves placing compost into a permeable container (often called a "cake" or "bag") within the brewing vessel. This allows extraction of organisms while keeping compost particulates contained. The compost cake should be loosely packed to maximize surface area contact with water and allow free movement of organisms.

### Making Milk (Compost Extract)

A simpler precursor to full AACT brewing is making a compost extract or "milk" — a liquid suspension of organisms created by briefly agitating compost in water without active aeration or added food sources. This captures existing organisms without the multiplication phase.

### AACT Making Factors

Several variables determine brew quality:

- **Compost quality:** The single most important factor. Only fully aerated, mature compost with verified beneficial biology should be used.
- **Brewing time:** 18–24 hours is optimal for most applications.
- **Water quality:** Chlorine-free water is essential; chlorine kills beneficial organisms.
- **Food sources:** Must match the desired biology (bacterial vs. fungal foods).
- **Temperature:** 65–75°F (18–24°C) is ideal for most beneficial organisms.
- **Aeration:** Must maintain dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L throughout the brew.

## Maintaining Aerobic Conditions

### Dissolved Oxygen Requirements

Dissolved oxygen (DO) must remain above **6 mg/L** throughout the entire brewing process. At this level, strictly aerobic organisms thrive and anaerobic organisms cannot survive. If DO drops below 5.5 mg/L, facultative anaerobes begin to dominate; below 4 mg/L, strict anaerobes produce alcohol, organic acids, and other metabolites toxic to plants.

### Measuring Oxygen

A calibrated dissolved oxygen meter is the most reliable way to monitor brew health. Meters should be calibrated regularly according to manufacturer instructions. As a secondary indicator, the nose is an excellent tool — properly aerobic compost tea has an earthy, sweet, or neutral smell. Any sour, putrid, rotten, or septic odor indicates anaerobic conditions have developed and the tea should not be applied.

### Total Dissolved Gases

The relationship between dissolved oxygen and dissolved carbon dioxide is important. As aerobic organisms respire, they consume O₂ and produce CO₂. In a sealed or poorly ventilated system, CO₂ can build up, lowering the effective oxygen available to organisms even if the DO meter reads adequately. Proper aeration must strip CO₂ from solution while introducing fresh oxygen.

### Aeration Methods

Effective aeration requires both adequate volume of air and sufficiently fine bubble size. Fine bubble diffusers create more surface area for gas exchange compared to coarse bubbles. The air pump must deliver sufficient volume (typically measured in liters per minute) relative to the tea volume and compost mass.

**In shipping:** If tea must be transported, maintaining aeration during transit is critical. Battery-powered aerators or minimal agitation can help, but ideally tea should be applied within hours of brewing completion.

### Aeration and Diversity

Higher aeration rates support greater microbial diversity by maintaining strict aerobic conditions that favor a broader range of beneficial species. Different organisms have different oxygen tolerances, and maintaining high DO ensures the full spectrum of compost biology is represented in the finished tea.

## Machine Size and Compost Ratio

The ratio of compost to water varies with brewing machine size and design. General guidelines:

- Use approximately **1 pound of compost per 5 gallons of water** as a starting point.
- Larger machines require proportionally more compost and food to sustain organism growth.
- Overloading a machine with too much compost can clog filters and create dead zones with poor aeration.

## Water Quality

Water is the foundation of the brew. Key considerations:

- **Chlorine and chloramine:** Municipal water supplies often contain these disinfectants, which are lethal to beneficial organisms. Chlorine can be off-gassed by aerating water for 1–2 hours before brewing. Chloramine is more stable and may require ascorbic acid or a dedicated filtration system.
- **Reverse osmosis water:** Provides a clean slate free of contaminants but also lacks minerals that may benefit microbial growth. Some practitioners add back minerals or use blended water.
- **Well water:** Often excellent for brewing if tested free of harmful contaminants, though mineral content varies widely.
- **Temperature:** Water should be within the optimal brewing range before compost and food are added.
