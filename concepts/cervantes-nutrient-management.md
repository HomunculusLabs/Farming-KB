# Cannabis Nutrient Management and Deficiency Symptoms

**Source:** Marijuana Horticulture: The Indoor/Outdoor Medical Grower's Bible — Jorge Cervantes

## Nutrient Categories

Nutrients are grouped into three categories:
1. **Macronutrients (primary):** Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K)
2. **Secondary nutrients:** Magnesium (Mg), Calcium (Ca), Sulfur (S)
3. **Micronutrients (trace elements):** Iron (Fe), Manganese (Mn), Zinc (Zn), Copper (Cu), Boron (B), Molybdenum (Mo), Chlorine (Cl), Cobalt (Co)

## Mobile vs. Immobile Nutrients

**Mobile nutrients** (N, P, K, Mg, Zn) translocate within the plant. Deficiency symptoms appear first on older, lower leaves because nutrients move to where they are needed most (younger growth).

**Immobile nutrients** (Ca, B, Cl, Co, Cu, Fe, Mn, Mo, Si, S) do not translocate. Deficiency symptoms appear first on upper, new leaves.

## Macronutrient Deficiencies

### Nitrogen (N) — Mobile
**Role:** Essential for proteins, chlorophyll, amino acids, enzymes, nucleic acids. Mainly responsible for leaf and stem growth, overall size and vigor. Required in high levels during vegetative growth.

**Deficiency symptoms:** Most common nutrient deficiency.
- Slow growth
- Lower leaves yellow between veins while veins remain green (interveinal chlorosis)
- Yellowing progresses through entire leaf; leaf dies and drops
- Stems and leaf undersides may turn reddish-purple

**Progression:**
1. Older leaves yellow between veins
2. Bottom leaves turn entirely yellow
3. More leaves yellow; severely affected leaves drop
4. Reddish-purple stems and veins on leaf undersides
5. Progressively younger leaves develop interveinal chlorosis
6. All foliage yellows; severe leaf drop

**Treatment:** Fertilize with N or complete N-P-K fertilizer. Results in 4-5 days. Organic sources: seabird guano, fish emulsion, blood meal.

**Toxicity:** Excessively lush, soft foliage susceptible to stress. Weak stems fold over. Slow root development. Flowers are smaller and sparse. Flush growing medium with mild fertilizer.

### Phosphorus (P) — Mobile
**Role:** Necessary for photosynthesis, energy transfer, DNA, enzymes, proteins. Associated with vigor, resin, and seed production. Highest concentrations in root tips, growing shoots, and vascular tissue. Used most during germination, seedling, cloning, and flowering.

**Deficiency symptoms:**
- Stunted, slow growth
- Small leaves that turn bluish-green with dark blotches
- Stems, petioles, and main veins turn reddish-purple (starting on underside)
- Leaf tips of older leaves turn dark and curl downward
- Severely affected leaves develop large purplish-black necrotic blotches
- Flowering delayed; buds uniformly smaller; poor seed yield

**Treatment:** Lower pH to 5.5-6.2 (hydroponic) or 6.0-7.0 (soil). Mix complete fertilizer before planting. Organic sources: bat guano, steamed bone meal, natural phosphates, barnyard manure.

**Toxicity:** Excess phosphorus interferes with Ca, Cu, Fe, Mg, and Zn uptake. Most common in acidic soils.

## General Nutrient Management

- Fertilize sparingly the first month after transplanting
- Use high-nitrogen formula during vegetative stage
- Change to "super bloom" (high P, K; low N) when flowering begins
- Leach nutrients from growing medium 7-10 days before harvest to avoid fertilizer taste
- Fertilizer with chelated iron helps when excess calcium blocks iron and potassium uptake

## pH and Nutrient Availability

- Soil pH 6.0-7.0 for optimal nutrient availability
- Hydroponic pH 5.5-6.5
- Outside this range, nutrients become "locked out" — present but unavailable to plants
- Humic and fulvic acids chelate metallic ions (Cu, Fe, Mn, Zn), making them readily transportable and available

## Soil Temperature and Nutrient Uptake

- Ideal soil temperature: 65-70°F (18-24°C) for maximum chemical activity
- Cold soil slows water and nutrient uptake
- Soil above 75°F dehydrates roots; above 92°F causes root damage
- Root temperatures below 40°F cause cell damage from freezing water expansion
