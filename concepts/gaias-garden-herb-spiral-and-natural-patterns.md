# Herb Spiral and Natural Patterns in Garden Design

Toby Hemenway's exploration of how natural patterns — spirals, branches, nets, and edges — can be applied to garden design for space efficiency, microclimate creation, and ecological benefit. From Gaia's Garden, Chapter 3.

## The Herb Spiral

An herb spiral coils 20-30 linear feet of pathside herbs into a roundish pattern about 5 feet across, using both a two-dimensional spiral and a three-dimensional mound.

### Construction

1. Begin with a mound of good soil about 3 feet high and 5 feet across
2. Place fist- to head-sized rocks in a spiral pattern winding from bottom to top
3. Leave about 1 foot of soil between tiers of the rock spiral
4. Install herbs winding up the spiral

### How It Works

The spiral combines two-dimensional (spiral) with three-dimensional (mound) patterns to create **multiple microclimates** in a small footprint:

- **South-facing slope:** Hotter and drier — oregano, rosemary, thyme
- **North-facing slope:** Cooler and moister — parsley, chives
- **East-facing side:** Morning sun, protected from afternoon scorch — coriander/cilantro
- **West-facing side:** Afternoon heat
- **Bottom:** Wetter soil
- **Top:** Drier soil

### Building Tips

- Place rocks or subsoil at the mound base to save topsoil
- Run 1/4 or 1/2 inch plastic irrigation tubing inside the mound, emerging from the top, with a mini-sprinkler for easy watering
- Sink a small basin or tiny pond (1-3 feet across) at the bottom for watercress and waterchestnuts
- Built with attractive stone, an herb spiral can be an eye-catching central garden feature

### Benefits

- All herbs accessible without bending far (central ones at waist height)
- Coils 30 linear feet of row into a 5-foot-diameter space
- Eliminates long straight herb rows along paths
- Creates synergistic effects from combining 2D and 3D patterns

## Branching Patterns

Branching patterns in nature are used to collect or disperse nutrients, energy, and water. Tree branches spread leaves to absorb sunlight; forking roots gather nutrients and moisture.

### Application: Garden Paths

Kirk Hansen of Wild Thyme Farm observed that leaf veins use minimal space to deliver nutrients while maximizing light-gathering surface. This principle can design garden paths:

- **Wide central path:** Big enough for wheelbarrows or carts
- **Secondary footpaths:** Branching off the main path for frequent foot traffic
- **Small tracks:** Tiny paths into each bed for occasional access

This pattern minimizes the growing area lost to paths while creating natural flow. A single branch is easy to repair if damaged, and its loss has minimal effect on the whole system.

## Net (Mesh) Patterns

Net patterns are found in spiderwebs, birds' nests, honeycombs, and dried mud cracking. They are patterns of expansion, contraction, and even distribution.

### Application: Seed Spacing

Triangular (hexagonal) spacing of seeds fits more plants in the same space than rectangular spacing. Four rows of seeds at 6-inch rectangular spacing need 18 inches of bed space, while triangular spacing needs only about 15 inches.

### Application: Net-and-Pan Water Harvesting

In drylands, orchardists plant trees in small depressions (pans) connected by a network of shallow trenches. Rain and runoff water falling over a large area is collected by the trench network and delivered to the base of each tree. Mulch also collects in the basins, building soil.

### Application: Gradual Soil Building

On poor soil with few plants, instead of an intensive topsoil-and-plant blitz:

1. Begin with a grid of small trees and shrubs spaced over the yard
2. In the shelter of each sapling, add mulch and plant perennials
3. Create a network of mulched circles
4. Over years, expand each circle until they touch
5. The entire property becomes rich soil and lush vegetation

This mimics how shifting sand dunes are stabilized: scattered plants create sheltered patches, then spread via runners to colonize new ground.

## The Edge Effect

Edges are where things happen — boundaries between two ecosystems are cauldrons of biodiversity. All species from both environments are present, plus new species in the transition zone.

### Applying Edge in the Garden

| Edge Type | Strategy |
|-----------|----------|
| House/yard | South wall = heat-loving plants; north wall = coldest, darkest |
| Pavement/soil | Thirsty plants alongside sidewalks catch runoff; pavement stores heat |
| Fence/yard | Zigzag fencing increases trellis area and wind resistance |
| Plant/soil | Wavy planting patterns increase edge and yield |
| Plant/air | Undulating bed shapes create warm bays and cool lobes |
| Water/soil | Lobed starburst ponds hold more bog plants than round ones |

**Key insight:** Sometimes minimize edge (herb spirals, keyhole beds — less path waste). Sometimes maximize edge (ponds, bed margins — more biodiversity and yield). The decision depends on what lies on either side and what you want from it.

Straight lines and smooth shapes reduce edge. Shapes with lobes, notches, mounds, pits, crinkles, and crenellations increase edge. Extend edge into the third dimension by varying height and depth of plantings, soil, structures, and ponds.
