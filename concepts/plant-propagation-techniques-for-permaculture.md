## Seed Propagation: The Fundamentals

Growing plants from seed is the most common and economical form of propagation. Seeds
contain the complete genetic blueprint for a new plant. While seed-grown plants exhibit
genetic variation — which can be advantageous or not, depending on species and purpose
— seeds are the only practical way to propagate many species and the best way to
maintain [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-in-agroforestry]], Albizia, and Robinia species, have hard seed coats preventing
water penetration and germination. In nature, these seeds pass through animal digestive
tracts, experience fire, or suffer abrasion that wears down the coat. In cultivation,
scarification deliberately damages the seed coat to allow water absorption.

The simplest method is soaking seeds in hot (not boiling) water, then cooling
overnight. Seeds that swell are ready for planting; those remaining hard may need more
aggressive treatment. Mechanical scarification involves nicking the seed coat with a
file, sandpaper, or knife, avoiding damage to the embryo inside. For small seeds,
rubbing between sandpaper sheets or shaking with coarse sand works well. Acid
scarification with dilute [[fruit-tree-varieties]], named cultivars, and any plant where specific characteristics
must be preserved. Cuttings can be taken from stems, roots, or leaves depending on
species.

Softwood cuttings come from new, flexible growth in spring or early summer. They root
quickly but require high humidity and careful management. Semi-hardwood cuttings, from
partially matured wood in late summer to autumn, suit many shrubs and some trees.
Hardwood cuttings, from dormant mature wood in winter, are simplest for many deciduous
[[extending-growing-season]], after which the new plant is severed and transplanted. Serpentine
layering buries a long stem at multiple points for several plants from one stem. Tip
layering buries the growing tip, producing a new plant at the tip — useful for
blackberries and raspberries.
