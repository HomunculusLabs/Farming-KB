# Fungal Cosubstrate-Enhanced Bioremediation The use of cosubstrates in fungal bioremediation is a critical strategy for enhancing the degradation of persistent environmental pollutants. Cosubstrates provide carbon and energy sources that support fungal growth and induce the production of degradative enzymes, particularly [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]] hydrocarbons (PAHs) by white-rot fungi depends on an ample supply of a suitable carbon cosubstrate.

The cosubstrate serves multiple functions: it supports fungal biomass production, induces ligninolytic enzyme synthesis (which is often subject to catabolite repression by readily metabolizable carbon sources), and may modify the bioavailability of target pollutants through surfactant-like effects or solubilization.

The effectiveness of cosubstrate addition depends on the type of cosubstrate, the concentration used, the target pollutant, and the fungal species employed. Finding the optimal balance is essential because excessive cosubstrate can repress [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]], and Dichomitus squalens.

Rice, annual plant stems, wood, and bark have also been used as cosubstrates. The extent of benzo[a]pyrene degradation was improved by the use of hemp stem as cosubstrate. Milled wheat straw at 5% concentration has been effective for supporting the degradation of pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, and benzo[a]pyrene by Pleurotus species.

Potato pulp combined with sawdust from Picea species served as an effective cosubstrate for Pleurotus ostreatus treatment of creosote-contaminated soil, achieving 32% benzo[a]pyrene degradation over 3 months.

### Organic Amendments

Fish oil and spent mushroom compost have been used as cosubstrates. P. ostreatus with fish oil and spent mushroom compost achieved 86% total PAH degradation and 89% three-ring PAH degradation over 7 weeks in creosote-contaminated soil. These organic amendments provide both carbon sources and nutrient supplementation.

Straw and other agricultural residues are inexpensive and readily available cosubstrates. Their lignocellulosic composition naturally induces ligninolytic enzyme production in white-rot fungi, making them ideal for fungal-bioremediation applications.

### Simple Carbon Sources

Glucose and malt extract have been used as cosubstrates in liquid culture systems. Penicillium janthinellum utilized glucose, malt extract, [[marmite-yeast-extract-straw-substrate]], peptone, and dextrose media as cosubstrates for benzo[a]pyrene transformation, producing polar metabolites that could subsequently be degraded by bacteria.

The kinetics of Cunninghamella echinulata var. elegans have been determined for PAH-contaminated soils related to three supplemental nutrient solutions, demonstrating the importance of nutrient optimization in cosubstrate-enhanced bioremediation.

## Cosubstrate Effects on PAH Degradation in Soils

The influence of cosubstrates on fungal PAH degradation in soils has been extensively documented:

Phanerochaete chrysosporium with rice as cosubstrate achieved 80% benzo[a]pyrene degradation with a MnP peak titer of 1500 nM/ml per minute. Bjerkandera sp. strain BOS55 showed similar performance with rice cosubstrate.

P. ostreatus with wheat straw achieved 99% anthracene degradation and 92% benz[a]anthracene degradation within 65 days. P. chrysosporium with wheat straw achieved 98% anthracene degradation with the formation of 9,10-anthracene-dione.

Kuehneromyces mutabilis showed dramatically different results in sterilized versus nonsterile soil: only 5.1% mineralization of pyrene in sterilized soil, but 47.7% in soil inoculation conditions, demonstrating the importance of fungal-bacterial interactions in cosubstrate-supported bioremediation.

Agrocybe aegerita showed similar patterns: 1.5% pyrene mineralization in sterilized soil versus 38.5% in soil inoculation conditions.

## Surfactant Enhancement

Polyoxyethylene (POE) surfactants have been found beneficial for the bioremediation of PAH-contaminated soils. Surfactants can increase the bioavailability of hydrophobic pollutants by solubilizing them from soil particles into the aqueous phase.

Several factors must be considered in the selection of surfactants for soil bioremediation, including their biodegradability, toxicity to microorganisms, and effectiveness at increasing pollutant bioavailability without inhibiting fungal enzyme systems.

Surfactants can be used in combination with cosubstrates to further enhance pollutant degradation. The synergistic effects of cosubstrates and surfactants on fungal PAH degradation represent an active area of research.

## Cyclodextrin-Enhanced Bioremediation

Cyclodextrins offer an alternative approach to solubilizing hydrophobic pollutants without the use of organic solvents or synthetic surfactants. The most common forms are alpha, beta, and gamma cyclodextrins containing six, seven, and eight glucose units, respectively.

These molecules can form inclusion complexes with water-insoluble compounds by attaching them to their hydrophobic cavities. High concentrations of 2-hydroxypropyl-beta-cyclodextrin (hpbetaCD) can dramatically increase PAH solubility: the solubility of naphthalene and benzo[a]pyrene was enhanced by hpbetaCD approximately 224 and 7500 times, respectively.

A distinct correlation exists between PAH solubility and hpbetaCD in terms of aromatic rings in the PAH. LiP oxidation of cyclodextrin-included substrates is similar to that reported for mixed solvent systems. Of several PAHs, LiP oxidizes anthracene, pyrene, and benzo[a]pyrene complexed with hpbetaCD.

The hpbetaCD solubilizes PAHs better than solvent systems when used with LiP for the oxidation of PAHs. Addition of maltosyl cyclodextrin enhanced the degradation of fluorene, which had previously shown only 54% degradation.

## Cosubstrate Interactions with Indigenous Microflora

The interaction between cosubstrate-amended fungal inocula and indigenous soil microorganisms significantly affects remediation outcomes. Synergistic degradation by white-rot fungi and indigenous bacteria can occur in the bioremediation of PAH-contaminated soil.
