# Mushroom Spawn Making History and Methods Duggar 1915

Source: Mushroom Growing (Benjamin Minge Duggar, 1915, Ch. VIII)

Duggar's chapter on spawn-making documents the transition from empirical chance methods to scientifically based pure culture spawn production, a revolution in mushroom cultivation.

## The Problem of Poor Spawn (1901-1904)

From 1901 to 1904, Duggar systematically investigated the reliability of commercial mushroom spawn (English "brick" and French "flake") sold by American seed dealers. Samples were purchased in various cities and tested at Columbia, Missouri. In an unexpectedly large majority of cases, the spawn proved to be dead.

The conclusion was that the most important consideration for American growers was a source of reliable spawn. English and French growers experienced no such difficulties. With imported spawn, the additional time involved in shipment, conditions of transport, and subsequent American storage methods all weakened or ruined the products. Worst of all, spawn was sometimes carried over from one season to the next.

## The Pure Culture Revolution

Duggar developed a method for securing pure cultures of Agaricus campestris and other mushrooms in 1902 at the Bureau of Plant Industry, U.S. Department of Agriculture. This opened the way not merely for production of high-grade mushroom spawn in the United States, but also for production by a selection method.

English growers had claimed that spawn-making would not succeed in America due to climatic conditions, just as the French assured Duggar that extreme conditions would render mushroom culture unsatisfactory. Both claims proved false.

Perfection of the method and its application by spawn growers required several years, and still further time was needed for this to react upon commercial production. The experimental pure culture spawn distributed under USDA auspices was very successful, so certain irresponsible firms took advantage to advertise and sell poor product.

By 1913-1914, 80-90% of mushrooms grown in America were from American pure culture spawn. Some spawn was actually exported to Europe.

## Types of Spawn

### Brick Spawn (English)
- Commonly used in England and the United States
- Compressed into bricks for transport and storage
- Very few growers found it advisable to make their own

### Flake Spawn (French)
- Had a monopoly of the French market
- Most large French growers prepared all material using a "starter" of "virgin" spawn
- Rapidly loses vitality under conditions of importation to America

### Virgin Spawn (Historical)
- From the earliest records, growers depended on spontaneous/native occurrence of spawn in pastures, compost piles, or elsewhere
- Presumably from accidental germination of spores
- Could not be adequately tested for productiveness
- In England, "mill-track" virgin spawn from horse paths was highly sought
- As mushroom growing developed, location and sale of virgin spawn became almost a special industry

## Modern Spawn-Making Principles

By 1915, spawn-making was based on:
1. **Selection principle**: Choosing the most productive strains
2. **Pure culture methods**: Bacteriological technique for isolation and maintenance
3. **Testing**: Systematic evaluation of spawn quality before distribution

Spawn-making had become a special industry requiring laboratory training. It was not practicable for every mushroom grower to make satisfactory spawn without necessary training and tolerance for initial failures.

## Importance of Fresh Spawn

Growers should always insist upon receiving fresh, vigorous spawn. The improvement in conditions between 1901 and 1915 was obvious to anyone of experience. The transition from imported, often-dead spawn to domestic, pure-culture, tested spawn represented one of the most important advances in the history of mushroom cultivation.

## Practical Applications

The principles outlined on this page connect directly to hands-on work in gardens,
farms, and homesteads. Integrating these concepts with complementary techniques from
related disciplines can significantly improve outcomes. Start with small-scale
implementation and scale up as understanding deepens through direct observation.

## Safety and Identification

Accurate identification is the single most important skill for anyone working
with wild mushrooms. Many edible and psychoactive species have toxic lookalikes
that can cause serious illness or death. When in doubt, the correct course of
action is always to leave the specimen unidentified. Building identification
skills requires study of multiple characteristics including spore print color,
gill structure, cap features, stem characteristics, bruising reactions, and
habitat associations.

## Ecological Significance

Fungi play essential roles in ecosystem functioning that extend far beyond
their visibility. As primary decomposers, they break down complex organic
materials and recycle nutrients back into available forms. Mycorrhizal fungi
form symbiotic relationships with plant roots, extending the effective
absorption zone and providing nutrients in exchange for carbohydrates. These
relationships are fundamental to the health of forests, grasslands, and most
terrestrial ecosystems.

## Conservation Considerations

Many fungal species are threatened by habitat destruction, pollution, and
climate change. The slow growth and specific habitat requirements of many
species make them particularly vulnerable. Responsible foraging practices,
including leaving adequate specimens for spore dispersal and avoiding
disturbance of sensitive habitats, help protect fungal biodiversity. Citizen
science efforts to document fungal distributions contribute valuable data for
conservation planning and monitoring.
