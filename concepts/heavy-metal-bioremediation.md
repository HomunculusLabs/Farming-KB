# Bioremediation of Heavy Metal Contaminated Soils

Heavy metal contamination of soil is a pervasive environmental problem resulting from mining, smelting, industrial manufacturing, agricultural chemical use, and waste disposal activities.

Unlike organic contaminants, metals cannot be degraded or destroyed but must be immobilized, removed, or transformed to less toxic or less mobile forms.

Bioremediation of metal-contaminated soils encompasses a range of biological strategies including biosorption, bioaccumulation, precipitation, volatilization, and enzymatic transformation.

This topic provides an overview of biological approaches to heavy metal remediation, complementing the fungal-specific discussions in [[factors-affecting-mycoremediation-efficiency]], [[fungal-metal-biosorption]], and [[fungal-biosorption-mechanisms]].

## Sources and Impacts of Heavy Metal Contamination

The primary anthropogenic sources of heavy metal soil contamination include mining and smelting operations, which release lead, zinc, cadmium, copper, arsenic, and other metals.

Industrial activities including electroplating, battery manufacturing, pigment production, and petroleum refining contribute metals including chromium, nickel, cadmium, and lead.

Agricultural inputs including phosphate fertilizers, pesticides, sewage sludge, and irrigation water introduce cadmium, arsenic, lead, and other metals to agricultural soils.

Atmospheric deposition from fossil fuel combustion, waste incineration, and industrial emissions distributes metals widely, contributing to background contamination even in remote areas.

Heavy metals affect soil ecosystem function by inhibiting microbial activity, reducing enzyme production, altering community composition, and disrupting nutrient cycling processes.

Metal toxicity to plants manifests as reduced growth, chlorosis, root damage, and interference with essential nutrient uptake and metabolism.

Human exposure to soil metals occurs through direct ingestion, inhalation of contaminated dust, and consumption of crops grown on contaminated soil, with children being particularly vulnerable.

The bioavailability of metals in soil, rather than their total concentration, is the primary determinant of environmental and human health risk.

## Biosorption of Heavy Metals

Biosorption is the passive uptake of metal ions from solution by biological materials through physicochemical mechanisms including ion exchange, complexation, adsorption, and precipitation.

[[fungal-biosorption-mechanisms]] describes the chemical and physical processes involved in metal binding to fungal biomass.

The cell walls of fungi contain functional groups including carboxyl, phosphate, amine, thiol, and hydroxyl groups that serve as binding sites for metal cations.

Chitin and chitosan, the structural polysaccharides of fungal cell walls, provide abundant amino and hydroxyl groups for metal binding.

[[fungal-metal-biosorption]] and [[fungal-heavy-metal-anion-biosorption]] provide quantitative data on metal uptake capacities for various fungal species and metals.

Non-living fungal biomass can be used as a biosorbent without the complications of maintaining viable cultures, and may show equal or greater metal uptake than living biomass because metabolic protons do not compete for binding sites.

Biosorption isotherms, as described in [[biosorption-isotherms]], provide the engineering basis for designing biosorption-based treatment systems.

[[fungal-biosorption-desorption-regeneration]] describes the recovery of metals from loaded biosorbents and the reuse of biosorbent materials through desorption cycles.

The competitive effects of multiple metals in solution on biosorption performance are important for treatment of real waste streams that typically contain metal mixtures.

[[fungal-heavy-metal-anion-biosorption]] addresses the different mechanisms involved in biosorption of metal anions such as chromate, arsenate, and selenite.

## Microbial Metal Transformation

Microorganisms can transform metals through oxidation, reduction, methylation, and precipitation reactions that alter metal speciation, solubility, and toxicity.

Microbial reduction of hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) to trivalent chromium (Cr(III)) is one of the most important microbial metal transformations because Cr(VI) is highly toxic and mobile while Cr(III) is relatively insoluble and non-toxic.

Bacteria including Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Shewanella species, as well as certain fungi, reduce Cr(VI) through both enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms.

Microbial reduction of uranium (U(VI)) to U(IV) produces insoluble uraninite (UO2) that precipitates from solution, providing a mechanism for uranium immobilization in contaminated groundwater.

Bacterial mercury resistance involves enzymatic reduction of ionic mercury (Hg2+) to elemental mercury (Hg0), which volatilizes from the treatment zone, and organomercurial lyase that cleaves carbon-mercury bonds in methylmercury and other organomercury compounds.

Microbial arsenic transformation includes oxidation of arsenite (As(III)) to arsenate (As(V)), reduction of arsenate to arsenite, and methylation to produce volatile methylarsine gases.

Microbial selenium transformation includes reduction of selenate and selenite to elemental selenium, which precipitates as red selenium nanoparticles, and methylation to produce volatile dimethyl selenide.

[[fungal-metal-transformations-biosorption]] describes fungal contributions to metal transformation processes including reduction, oxidation, and precipitation.

## Phytoremediation of Metal-Contaminated Soils

Phytoremediation provides several mechanisms for addressing metal contamination, as described in [[phytoremediation-mechanisms]].

[[phytoextraction-mechanisms]] involves the use of hyperaccumulator plants to absorb and concentrate metals in aboveground tissues, which are then harvested and removed from the site.

[[phytostabilization]] uses plants to immobilize metals in the root zone through precipitation, adsorption, and changes in rhizosphere chemistry that reduce metal mobility and bioavailability.

[[rhizofiltration]] uses plant roots to absorb metals from contaminated water, and can be applied to treatment of metal-contaminated groundwater or surface water.

[[phytovolatilization]] converts certain metals and metalloids, particularly selenium and mercury, to volatile forms that are released to the atmosphere.

[[rhizofiltration]] are the key biological resource for phytoextraction, with known species capable of accumulating nickel, zinc, cadmium, arsenic, and other metals at exceptionally high concentrations.

[[mycorrhizal-fungi-bioremediation]] can enhance phytoremediation of metals by extending the root absorption zone, improving plant nutrition on poor soils, and directly contributing to metal immobilization through fungal biosorption.

## Bioremediation Strategies for Metal-Contaminated Sites

In situ bioremediation of metal-contaminated soils typically involves amendments and biological treatments that reduce metal bioavailability and mobility.

Phosphate addition promotes formation of insoluble metal phosphate minerals, particularly for lead, uranium, and zinc.

Organic matter addition improves soil structure and provides binding sites for metal cations through complexation and adsorption, while also supporting microbial and plant growth.

Lime addition raises soil pH, which reduces the solubility of most heavy metals and creates conditions more favorable for plant establishment.

Sulfate-reducing bacteria can be stimulated to generate sulfide that precipitates metals as insoluble sulfides, which are extremely stable under anaerobic conditions.

[[biostimulation-techniques]] for metal-contaminated sites include nutrient addition, pH adjustment, and redox management to enhance the activity of indigenous metal-transforming microorganisms.

[[bioaugmentation-strategies]] may involve introduction of microorganisms with enhanced metal transformation capabilities, such as chromium-reducing bacteria or sulfate-reducing bacteria.

Ex situ treatment approaches including soil washing followed by biological treatment of wash water, biopile treatment of excavated soil, and constructed wetland treatment of metal-contaminated water provide alternatives when in situ treatment is not feasible.

[[acid-mine-drainage-treatment]] describes passive biological treatment systems specifically designed for metal-laden acidic water from mining operations.

## Monitoring and Long-Term Management

Monitoring bioremediation of metal-contaminated sites requires assessment of both total metal concentrations and metal bioavailability.

Bioavailability assessment methods include chemical extraction procedures, diffusive gradients in thin films (DGT), and biological assays using indicator organisms.

[[bioremediation-monitoring-and-assessment]] protocols should integrate chemical measurements of metal concentrations and speciation with biological indicators of soil health and ecological recovery.

The long-term stability of metal immobilization is a critical concern because changes in environmental conditions, particularly pH and redox potential, can remobilize previously immobilized metals.

Institutional controls including deed restrictions, groundwater use prohibitions, and engineering controls such as caps and covers may be required to ensure that metal immobilization is maintained indefinitely.

The transition from active remediation to long-term stewardship requires monitoring programs that can detect early signs of treatment failure and trigger corrective action.

Integration of bioremediation with [[bioremediation-ecosystem-restoration]] principles ensures that treated sites achieve both risk reduction and ecological recovery objectives.
