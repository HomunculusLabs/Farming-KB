# Season Extension Structures

Season extension structures protect plants from cold, wind, and excessive rain, allowing gardeners to start earlier in spring, harvest later in fall, and grow through winter. They range from simple cloches and row covers to full greenhouses. This page covers hoop houses, high tunnels, and greenhouse basics. See [[winter-gardening-techniques]] for cold frames, row covers, and low tunnels.

## Hoop Houses (Low-Cost Poly Houses)

A hoop house is a structure made of metal or PVC hoops covered with polyethylene plastic sheeting. It provides passive solar heating and wind protection without artificial heat or ventilation systems. Hoop houses are the most cost-effective season extension structure for home gardeners and small farms.

### Design Specifications

- **Width**: 10-30 feet (wider is more stable and cost-effective per square foot)
- **Length**: Any length; common sizes are 20-50 feet
- **Height**: 8-12 feet at the peak for walk-in access
- **Frame**: Metal conduit (EMT), PVC pipe (Schedule 40 or 80), or galvanized steel tubing
- **Covering**: 6 mil greenhouse-grade polyethylene (UV stabilized, 4-year lifespan)
- **End walls**: Poly over a wood frame, or metal siding for durability

### Construction Steps

1. **Site preparation**: Level the site, orient the long axis east-west (for maximum light in winter, when the sun is low on the southern horizon)
2. **Ground posts**: Drive 2-3 foot long ground posts (rebar or pipe) into the soil at 4-foot intervals along both sides
3. **Bend and install hoops**: Slide hoop pipes over the ground posts. For PVC, use a pipe bender; for metal conduit, bend around a form. Hoops should form a Gothic arch or semi-circle.
4. **Purlins**: Install 2-3 horizontal purlins (ridge purlin at the peak, one on each side at mid-height) running the length of the house. These stabilize the hoops and provide structure for the plastic.
5. **End walls**: Frame and cover both ends with poly or wood. Include a door for access and ventilation.
6. **Plastic covering**: Stretch the poly tight over the hoops. Attach to end frames with wiggle wire in lock channels (the standard professional method). Pull tight enough to prevent flapping in wind.
7. **Anchor**: Bury the bottom edge of the plastic in a trench on both sides, or attach to a baseboard with wiggle wire.

### Hoop House Management

- **Ventilation**: Roll up sides on warm days (above 60°F) using manual or automatic roll-up sidewalls. End wall doors provide cross-ventilation.
- **Temperature**: Can be 15-30°F warmer inside than outside on sunny days, even without heat. Must be ventilated to prevent overheating.
- **Season extension**: Typically adds 4-8 weeks to each end of the growing season. In mild climates, enables year-round growing.
- **Crop rotation**: Rotate crops between hoop house and field to prevent soil-borne disease buildup.
- **Soil management**: Hoop houses receive no rainfall — irrigation is essential. See [[drip-irrigation-guide]].

## High Tunnels

High tunnels are structurally similar to hoop houses but are larger, more permanent, and may have additional features. The term "high tunnel" is generally used for USDA-funded structures and commercial operations.

### Differences from Simple Hoop Houses

- **Height**: 12-16 feet at peak (enough clearance for tractors and tall crops)
- **Frame**: Heavier gauge steel or aluminum; engineered for snow and wind loads
- **Roll-up sides**: Standard, with manual or motorized crank systems
- **End walls**: Often include roll-up doors for equipment access
- **Foundation**: May have a permanent foundation or ground anchors
- **No permanent heating**: By definition, high tunnels are passively heated. Supplemental heat is occasional and temporary.
- **Cost**: $2-8 per square foot depending on size and features

### High Tunnel Advantages

- Earlier spring planting (4-6 weeks ahead of field)
- Later fall harvest (4-6 weeks after field)
- Higher quality fruit (protected from rain, reducing disease on tomatoes and peppers)
- Higher yields per square foot than field production
- Protection from wind, hail, and excessive rain
- Reduced irrigation needed compared to open field (less evaporation)

### High Tunnel Crop Planning

**Spring (February-April)**
- Start cold-tolerant crops first: lettuce, spinach, kale, radishes, carrots, peas
- Follow with warm-season transplants: tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers (4-6 weeks before last frost)

**Summer (May-September)**
- Tomatoes are the most profitable high tunnel crop (higher quality, earlier harvest, reduced disease)
- Cucumbers, peppers, eggplant, and cut flowers
- Remove or roll up sides fully for maximum ventilation
- Consider shade cloth (30-50%) for mid-summer heat management

**Fall (September-November)**
- Succession plantings of cool-season crops
- Extend summer crops with row covers inside the tunnel
- See [[succession-planting]] for fall crop timing

**Winter (December-February)**
- Hardy greens survive with row cover inside the tunnel (double protection)
- Spinach, kale, mache, claytonia, Asian greens
- Garlic and shallots overwintering
- See [[winter-gardening-techniques]] for crop selection

## Greenhouse Basics

A greenhouse is a permanent, heated structure with environmental controls (heating, cooling, ventilation, sometimes supplemental lighting). Greenhouses are more expensive than hoop houses but provide complete climate control.

### Types

**Freestanding Greenhouse**
- Standalone structure, typically 12x20 to 30x100 feet
- Maximum light exposure on all four sides
- Higher heating cost per square foot (more exterior surface per volume)
- Easier to orient for optimal light

**Attached Greenhouse (Lean-To)**
- Built against an existing building wall
- Shares one wall with the building, reducing cost
- Can use building heat through the shared wall
- Limited orientation options (must face south or east)

**Gothic Arch Greenhouse**
- Pointed arch roof sheds snow efficiently
- Good headroom along the sides
- Most common commercial design

### Covering Materials

| Material | Light Transmission | Lifespan | Cost | Notes |
|----------|-------------------|----------|------|-------|
| Single poly film | 85-90% | 4 years | Low | Most common for hobby; replace regularly |
| Double poly (inflated) | 75-85% | 4 years | Low | Air layer between films provides insulation |
| Polycarbonate (twin-wall) | 75-85% | 10-15 years | Medium | Good insulation, lightweight, shatterproof |
| Glass | 90%+ | 25+ years | High | Maximum light transmission, fragile, heavy |
| ETFE film | 95%+ | 10-20 years | High | Excellent light, self-cleaning, very durable |

### Heating Systems

**Electric Heaters**
- Simple, clean, precise temperature control
- Expensive to operate (electricity is the most expensive heat source)
- Best for small greenhouses (under 200 sq ft) or as supplemental heat

**Gas Heaters (Natural Gas or Propane)**
- Most common for mid-sized greenhouses
- Require ventilation to prevent carbon monoxide buildup
- Thermostat-controlled for automatic operation
- Cost-effective for regular heating needs

**Wood Heat**
- Low fuel cost if wood is available
- Labor-intensive (stoking, cleaning)
- Temperature fluctuations harder to control
- Combines with [[compost-tea-aact]] heating (compost piles inside the greenhouse generate heat)

**Radiant Floor Heat**
- Hot water pipes in the floor, heated by a boiler or water heater
- Most even heat distribution
- Expensive to install but efficient to operate
- Ideal for plant benches with bottom heat

### Heating Cost Estimation

    BTU needed per hour = (Greenhouse surface area in sq ft) × (Temperature difference °F) × 1.1

Example: A 20x30 foot greenhouse (surface area = 1,200 sq ft) maintaining 60°F when it is 20°F outside (40°F difference):
    1,200 × 40 × 1.1 = 52,800 BTU/hr

This is the peak heating requirement. Average heating needs are typically 50-70% of peak.

### Ventilation

Proper ventilation is as important as heating. Greenhouses can reach 120°F+ on sunny days without ventilation.

- **Natural ventilation**: Roll-up sides and roof vents that open by convection (hot air rises and exits through roof vents, drawing cool air through side vents). Adequate for mild climates.
- **Forced ventilation**: Exhaust fans + intake louvers controlled by a thermostat. Required for larger greenhouses and warm climates. Size fans to exchange the entire greenhouse air volume once per minute.
- **Shade cloth**: 30-50% shade cloth reduces summer temperatures significantly. Install on the outside of the greenhouse.

## Comparing Season Extension Options

|-----------|------|-----------------|------|------------|----------|
