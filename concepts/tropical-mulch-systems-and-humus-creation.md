# Tropical Mulch Systems and Humus Creation

Mulch provision is the cornerstone of tropical home gardens, and green mulch plus tree legumes are the essential accompaniment of main crops and tree crops in humid, wet-dry, and monsoon tropical environments. In the tropics, where soils are deeply leached and nutrients are held primarily in living biomass, mulch serves as the critical bridge between plant production and soil fertility. Every type of organic mulch increases yields, and all available materials should be used for soil restitution.

## Why Mulch Is Critical in the Tropics

In the wet tropics, heat and high rainfall leach most mobile nutrients from soils. The biomass of diverse plants contains 80–90% of available nutrients, and humus is an essential soil fraction. Without continuous mulch application, tropical soils rapidly degrade from productive forest soils to desolate, baked clays—brick-like and hostile. Humus provides cation exchange capacity (CEC), which enables soils to retain calcium, sodium, and phosphates against leaching. The mulch-to-humus pathway is therefore the primary mechanism for maintaining soil fertility in tropical systems.

## Sources of Humus for Tropical Soils

### Logs and Branches

Logs and branches of trees are among the most valuable mulch sources. They can be rotted in wet terraces or piled as rough mulch around new tree plantings. Palm wastes are often plentiful. The rapid breakdown of wood under the combined influence of rain, heat, termites, and fungi means that whole logs can be leveraged together or arranged inline cross-slope to act as planting sites. This technique is most useful on bare clay soils, eroded areas, and isolated atolls (using trunks of old palm trees).

In Hawaii, a traditional strategy is to rot the logs of the kukui tree (*Aleurites moluccensis*) in the shallow water of taro terraces. As logs rot, an edible fungi appears which is harvested as a crop. The remaining log is then crushed and spread in the taro terrace. Leaves and branches of kukui and other forest trees were gathered for the same purpose.

Marjorie Spears, in Queensland, Australia, successfully built temporary rough-wood terraces across a deforested slope using rejected logs, creating a complex and rich garden based on this strategy and green legume mulch. Logs are available from palms, and fast-growing Acacia species can be previously close-planted specifically for this purpose.

### Detritus from Stands of Bamboo, Pines, and Casuarina

Several plant species provide silica-rich mulch, including most palms, bamboo thickets, Casuarina, and many Acacia species. Grain and nut husks and residues from copra operations also contribute silica. Applied as shredded or chopped mulch to crops or to the base of newly-planted trees, the silica is released for growth and has the secondary effect, in alkaline island soils, of reducing pH (from 8.5 to 6.5 in trials on coral islands).

Of particular value are the fronds and spathes of palms (shredded or whole) and the stems and spathes of bamboo. Both have essential structural uses, and larger stands can serve dual purposes as mulch production and structural material.

### Aquatic Weeds

Floating aquatics provide excellent crop mulch:

- **Azolla** (several species): Has largely replaced kukui as a taro mulch in Hawaii; provides nitrogen through its symbiotic relationship with *Anabaena* (one species of which "nests" in the glutinous sacs in Azolla)
- **Water lettuce** (*Pistia*): Successfully used as mulch in Africa
- **Water hyacinth** (*Eichhornia*): Used in many tropical areas
- **Algal mats**, fern fronds, reeds, and rushes gathered from ditches

In the wet-dry tropics, shallow flood-water bunds collect or produce these plants, which can be gathered as rolls of dry material when water dries out in the winter period, providing garden mulch in abundance from temporarily impounded water. John Selman of Cooktown, Australia, has used algal rolls in this way for garden plants.

### Crop Wastes and Manures

Corn husks, kitchen wastes (including bones), and human and animal manures are invaluable tropical garden mulches and nutrients. Most need only burial near tree roots or in growing mounds for safe disposal. Cardboard and newspaper, where available, are valuable grass-suppressing weed mulches, and a cover of nut husks completes the job. In the Seychelles, cinnamon leaves and branches from pollarded stumps are considered excellent mulch for vegetable crops, and the bark is a valuable spice.

### Hedgerow and Mulch Plants

All hedgerow species are nearly continual mulch sources:

- **Windbreak hedgerows**: Hibiscus, Casuarina, banana grass, palms, leguminous trees (Gliricidia, Acacia, Prosopis)
- **Garden windbreak**: Lemongrass (*Cymbopogon citratus*) and comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*) are as useful for preventing kikuyu grass intrusion as they are for repetitive cutting for mulch in the vegetable garden

Soft herbaceous plants around gardens and trees—nasturtium, comfrey, marigolds, tobacco plants, and the tops of mature taro plants and other Araceae—suppress grass and provide a constant source of "slash" mulch.

Soft legumes are particularly valuable: Sesbania, vetch, Haifa clover, cowpea, lablab bean, soya bean, Desmodium, Siratro, and Centrosema. These can be slashed or interplanted with grains in wet-dry tropics to give a nitrogenous ground cover aiding in grass suppression. Lablab dies down just before grains ripen in the winter dry season.

## Mulch Yield Data

Research by B.N. Okigbo and R. Lal demonstrates the yield benefits of various mulch materials compared to bare ground:

| Mulch | Maize (t/ha) | Cowpea (t/ha) | Soyabean (t/ha) | Cassava (t/ha) |
|----|-------------|--------------|-----------------|----------------|
| Rice husks | 3.7 | 1.1 | 0.8 | 28.3 |
| Pennisetum straw | 3.3 | 1.2 | 1.4 | 14.2 |
| Elephant grass | 3.3 | 0.9 | 1.3 | 16.6 |
| Millet straw | 3.6 | 2.4 | 1.5 | 15.5 |
| [[legume-nitrogen-fixation-mechanism|Legume]] wastes | 4.3 | 1.9 | 1.4 | 15.5 |
| Sawdust | 3.7 | 0.9 | 1.9 | 20.5 |
| Bare ground | 3.0 | 0.6 | 0.6 | 16.4 |

Maize showed a marked positive response to legume straws or [[water-wise-vegetable-gardening|water]] plant (*Pistia*) [[mulching-guide|mulch]]. Legumes responded well to grass and sawdust mulch. [[permaculture-plants-comfrey-chop-and-drop|Cassava]] responded to both legume and grain husk wastes. No-tillage systems maintained or gained yields for maize in Nigeria, and mulching increased yields for cowpea, soya bean, and cassava.

## Application Guidelines

Mulch should be thickly applied—20–25 cm (8–10 inches) deep—when first establishing home gardens. Later mulch can be derived from green herbage, borders, and windbreaks. Every type of organic mulch increases yields.

Special mulches may be used to provide specific nutrients or modify pH:

- **Nitrogen-rich**: Legumes, comfrey, *Poulzolania*
- **Acidifying**: Pine mulch may benefit bromeliads (pH 4–5)
- **Alkalinizing**: Buckwheat, nut husks, bark mulches raise garden [[soil-ph|soil]] pH

## Managing Problematic Mulch Materials

Some mulch materials—hibiscus, Lantana, and weeds that resprout from cuttings or seed if mulched (several grasses and hedge species)—require special handling:

1. **Animal processing**: Route through poultry or cattle pens where seeds are destroyed and foliage is eaten
2. **Anaerobic digestion**: Shred for biogas plants
3. **Solarization**: Bag in large plastic bales exposed to the sun where they "cook" to weed-free silage
4. **Water immersion**: Bundle and immerse to rot in covered water pits
5. **Pig processing**: Pigs eliminate or eat nut-grasses, rhizomes, bulbs, and sedges that resprout from [[hot-composting-vs-vermicompost-vs-bokashi|compost]]

## Fire Control and Mulch Routing

For fire control, it is important to rake under bamboo and clump canes and re-route leaf mulch through animal bedding or poultry strawyards. Branches of legumes and forage trees may also be used in the same way, on their path to the garden.

In rampant grasslands that replace fallen forests, strip-mowing and mulching while trees re-establish is the primary strategy. The timing of slashing is important—seed-free mulch (called second-cut grass) is best for placing around valuable crops. Seed-head mulch should not be placed in gardens or areas where it might become a nuisance. All species of weeds and grasses give weed-free mulch when not in seed.

## Coconut Husks

Coconut husks have multiple uses including as mulch for valued crops such as vanilla orchids. Their drawback is that they hold small sections of water which breed mosquitoes, but on many islands they are available (with palm fronds) to shred into first-class mulch of high potash value, to burn and steam to activated (filter) charcoal, or to use as solid fuel. Shredded bark and broken shells are ideal mulches for ginger, turmeric, and vines.

## Plastic Mulch Considerations

Plastic sheet mulch has excellent effects on row crops, preventing rain splash and [[plant-micronutrient-functions|nutrient]] leaching while condensing groundwater at night. However, it does not add to [[green-manure|humus]] content or cation exchange capacity of soil structure and may release unwanted chemicals. It is therefore not as appropriate to remote village situations where soils must be built up from wastes and mulch.

## The Complete Mulch Strategy

A combination of growing and gathering mulch enables creation of rich humus for gardens over clays, sands, loose volcanic cinder, on lava, and in loose coral atoll sands. Each of these situations can successfully produce mulch. A pioneer [[cover-crops|crop]] of quick-growing [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs|tree]] Acacias, bananas, legumes such as lablab, deep-rooting comfrey, and a grove of bamboo and palms will provide continuous mulch for gardens, main crops, fruit trees, and valued plants. There is absolutely no excuse for burning any organic wastes in the tropics, as even large logs quickly rot under the onslaught of fungi, termites, and beetle larvae.
