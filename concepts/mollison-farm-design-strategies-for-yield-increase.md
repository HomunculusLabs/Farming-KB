# Farm Design Strategies for Yield Increase

## Overview

Mollison presents a comprehensive catalog of farm-level strategies that increase yield beyond the "fencepost-to-fencepost" monoculture approach. These strategies operate across water management, land forming, soil building, forestry, windbreaks, and market processing — and their interactions multiply the benefits further. Real-world examples demonstrate that primary yield increases come from site design and development, not merely from varietal selection.

## Water Storage (12–20% of Landscape)

Water is more productive per unit area than land, and fish are more efficient at food conversion than cattle. Water storage strategies deliver multiple interacting benefits:

1. **Direct product increase:** Animal protein from aquaculture (fish, ducks)
2. **Land productivity increase:** Irrigation plus nutrient-rich water from fish manure
3. **Ecological interaction:** Ducks on water control pests and weeds, provide manure for pond edges
4. **Microclimatic buffering:** Water bodies moderate temperature extremes in surrounding areas

## Land Forming

Reshaping land topography to maximize water distribution and minimize waste:

1. **Product increase:** Even irrigation eliminates dry patches and waterlogging
2. **Land stability:** Reduced soil loss from runoff and salting
3. **Energy efficiency:** Gravity flow replaces pumped water (site-dependent)
4. **Water recycling:** Captured runoff returned to productive use through the system

## Soil Building and Keyline Design

Deep soil preparation following keyline principles:

1. **Product increase:** Deeper root penetration accesses more nutrients and moisture
2. **Zero runoff:** Water infiltration through absorption, eliminating wasteful surface flow
3. **Microclimate buffering:** Improved soil structure moderates [[mushroom-growing-temperature-and-moisture]] fluctuations
4. **Nutrient supply:** Decomposition of [[humus]] releases essential elements

## Forestry and Tree Systems (20–30% of Landscape)

Integrated tree systems provide shelter, products, and ecological services:

1. **Shelter effects:** Increased plant yields, animal protein, and microclimate buffering above and below ground
2. **[[fukuoka-textdoc-land-requirements-per-diet-type-carrying-capacity]] increase:** Shrubs and trees expand the productive base
3. **Nutrient recycling:** Legumes and deep-rooted trees cycle minerals from subsoil
4. **Intrinsic products:** Nectar for honey, seeds, firewood from fallen timber
5. **Biological control:** Insect and bird habitat for pest predator populations
6. **[[permaculture-designers-manual-wildlife-corridors]]:** Connected habitat enabling species movement

## Windbreak Design

Strategic tree plantings for shelter (not industrial forestry):

1. **Precipitation increase:** Night condensation on foliage, improved water penetration
2. **Perennial superiority:** Perennials outperform annuals in bulk, energy savings, and yield duration
3. **Cross-wind rainfall:** Trees positioned across prevailing winds increase local precipitation
4. **Self-forage browse:** Selected drought-proof stockfeed with medicinal qualities
5. **On-farm timber:** Durable fence posts and construction material, reducing purchased inputs
6. **Reduced carcass loss:** Less shivering, sweating, and exposure in livestock
7. **Increased crop production:** Sheltered areas produce more
8. **Increased carcass weight:** Cattle graze all day on shaded pasture instead of sheltering from sun
9. **Reduced pond evaporation:** Less wind over water surfaces

## Market and Processing Strategies

Value addition beyond raw commodity production:

1. **Specialty crops:** Fresh herbs near restaurant concentrations command premium prices
2. **Direct marketing:** Self-pick, mail order, direct dispatch, roadside sales — cutting out middlemen
3. **Higher-order processing:** Seed pressed to oil, crude eucalyptus oil fractionated into specialized products
4. **On-farm fuel processing:** Energy self-sufficiency plus sale of surplus fuel

## Social and Financial Strategies

Community and economic structures that support sustainable yield:

1. **Farm-link contracts:** Urban groups contracting to buy specific produce from farmers
2. **Education income:** Field days and educational courses
3. **Agri-tourism:** Guest houses and holiday farms
4. **Direct investment:** City people investing in particular farms
5. **Local credit systems:** Credit unions and banks recycling money within the district
6. **Equipment pools:** Vehicle and implement sharing with neighbors (90% capital savings)
7. **Labor exchange:** Cooperative work arrangements
8. **Produce cooperatives:** Collective marketing and distribution

## No-Tillage Farming Benefits

Low or no-tillage approaches deliver compounding savings:

- Energy conservation from [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]] operations
- Soil preservation (no structural destruction)
- Water conservation through reduced evaporation
- Time savings between crop cycles

## Real-World Evidence: Norm Sims Farm

Mollison cites farmer Norm Sims (Weekly Times, 5 Jan 1983) applying these strategies:

- **"We expect to double production over the next few years, using half the irrigation water"** — a four-fold benefit
- **"Salinity is reduced"** even in severe drought
- **"Pasture production has never looked better and water is available"**
- **"It took us six days to irrigate what we now do in two"**
- Aiming to milk 185 cows on 24 hectares using 26 paddocks in a 21-day rotation

Specific strategies in use: laser levelling of fields, water reticulation, storage and recycling, grazing rotation, central access road, concentrated rations grown on-farm, and reduced pasture area for optimal watering.

## The Political Economy of Yield

Mollison argues that primary yield increases from site design are not in the commercial interest of seed companies, patent holders, researchers, or extension officers to encourage — the continuous benefit accrues to farmers alone while reducing the role of middlemen. In the Western world, the 4–6% in essential production are "enslaved" while the remaining 96% derive secondary benefits without adequate return to primary producers. Benefits, like wastes, must be returned or recycled to keep any system going.
