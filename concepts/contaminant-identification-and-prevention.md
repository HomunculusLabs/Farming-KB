# [[mushroom-contaminant-identification-key]] and Prevention

## Overview

Contamination is the primary enemy of successful [[oyster-mushroom-cultivation-economics]]. Any
invading organism that competes with or consumes the mushroom mycelium will
ruin the culture. Understanding how to identify, diagnose, and prevent
contamination is essential for consistent results with the PF TEK.

## Types of Contaminants

Contaminant invaders appear in various colors from pastels to black. Common
molds include green, blue-green, pink, orange, and black varieties, typically
appearing as fuzzy or powdery growths. If any colored growth appears, the
culture is doomed and the jar should be discarded.

### Bacteria Contamination

Bacteria contamination is particularly dangerous and requires special handling.
It is detectable through the dry vermiculite layer as a sour, foul odor
within two days after inoculation, accompanied by no visible spore
germination. The bacteria produce a distinctive rancid smell that is easily
identifiable.

### Safety Precautions for Bacteria

If a jar is bacteria contaminated, exercise caution during cleaning and
disposal:

- Keep a safe distance from the contaminated substrate
- Do not inhale the bacteria
- Wash hands thoroughly after touching contaminated material
- Bacteria can be dangerous to human health

## The Control Jar Technique

The control jar technique is a diagnostic method for identifying the source of
contamination problems. It helps determine whether contamination originated
from the sterilization process or from the inoculation procedure.

### Procedure

After the jars are steam sterilized and cooled, tighten the lids and set aside
one or more jars uninoculated for several days. These are the "control jars."
Watch them carefully for any signs of contamination:

- **Colored growths**: Any mold appearing on the substrate or vermiculite
- **Odor changes**: Loosen the jar lid and check for the tell-tale rancid
  odor of bacteria
- **Substrate changes**: Any visible changes in the appearance of the
  substrate

### Interpreting Results

**If the control jar shows contamination**: The sterilization technique needs
to be checked. The most likely cause is insufficient sterilization time. The
remedy is to lengthen the steam sterilization time and try again.

**If the control jar remains clean**: The jars are properly sterilized and
ready for spore syringe inoculation. If contamination occurs after
inoculation, the cause is either a contaminated syringe or the dry vermiculite
barrier layer was breached during inoculation.

This systematic approach eliminates guesswork and allows the cultivator to
target the specific problem.

## Common Contamination Sources

### Inadequate Sterilization

The most common cause of contamination is insufficient sterilization. Steam
sterilization must be performed at a gentle boil for a full hour in a tightly
covered pot. If the pot lid does not fit tightly, steam escapes and
sterilization is incomplete. Short sterilization times are the most frequent
error.

### Breached Vermiculite Barrier

During inoculation, if the needle disturbs the dry vermiculite layer too much,
the underlying wet substrate is exposed to airborne contaminants. This layer is
the primary defense against contamination and must be disturbed as little as
possible.

### Contaminated Spore Syringe

If the spore syringe itself carries contamination, every jar inoculated with
it will likely become contaminated. This is why the control jar technique is
valuable: if control jars remain clean but inoculated jars contaminate, the
syringe is the problem.

### Poor Inoculation Technique

Touching the needle to non-sterile surfaces introduces contaminants. Use the
alcohol flaming technique to resterilize the needle if accidentally touched.

## Dry Substrate vs. Contamination

A common confusion is distinguishing halted growth from contamination. If
mycelium germinates and spreads but then stops with no visible contamination,
this is typically dried substrate, not contamination. The remedy is to
increase the water content of the substrate formula.

## Prevention Best Practices

### During Jar Preparation

- Use professional grade masking tape that holds during steaming
- Ensure the dry vermiculite barrier is at least 1/2 inch thick
- Wipe jar interiors clean before adding the dry vermiculite layer

### During Sterilization

- Steam for a full hour at the lowest possible boil
- Ensure the pot lid fits tightly
- Keep jar lid bands loose during steaming

### During Inoculation

- Shake the syringe well to redistribute spores
- Flame sterilize the needle if touched
- Minimize disturbance of the dry vermiculite layer
- Use the control jar technique to verify sterilization

### During Spore Work

- Wash hands before any sterile procedure
- Sterilize work surfaces with rubbing alcohol
- Minimize drafts and aim for still air
- Run a HEPA air cleaner for several hours before sterile work

### Spore Storage

- Store spore syringes in a dark, cool place
- Spores degrade after a few months; germination ceases after ~1.5 years
- Fresh spores germinate massively and quickly
