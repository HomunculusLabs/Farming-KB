# Permaculture Principle 4: Apply Self-Regulation and Accept Feedback

*"The sins of the fathers are visited on the children unto the [[seventh-generation-principle-mollison]]"*

This principle deals with self-regulatory aspects of [[permaculture-design]] that limit or discourage inappropriate growth or behavior. With better understanding of how positive and negative feedbacks work in nature, we can design systems that are more self-regulating, reducing the need for harsh corrective management. Self-maintaining and regulating systems might be said to be the [[amanita-muscaria-holy-grail-theory-detailed]] of [[permaculture]].

## Feedback Systems

### Positive vs. Negative Feedback

- **Positive feedback** (accelerator): Amplifies processes, pushes system toward freely available energy
- **Negative feedback** (brake): Prevents system from falling into scarcity and instability from overuse

Organisms and individuals adapt to negative feedback from large-scale systems by developing **self-regulation** to pre-empt and avoid harsher consequences of external negative feedback.

### Modern Society's Feedback Failure

Modern society takes for granted enormous dependence on large-scale remote systems while expecting freedom without consequences. "Our whole society is like a teenager who wants to have it all, have it now, without consequences." John Lennon's "Instant Karma" suggests consequences arrive faster than we think.

## The Gaia Hypothesis

The [[gaia-hypothesis]] of Earth as a self-regulating system, analogous to a living organism, makes the whole Earth the suitable image for this principle. Evidence of Earth's remarkable homeostasis over hundreds of millions of years highlights it as the archetypical self-regulating whole system.

## Nurture and Control in Nature

### Nature as Nursing Mother

Ecosystems provide for survival and health of member species by maintaining beneficial environments. Specific positive feedback from species high in the food chain encourages lower species — birds eating berries spread seeds in fertilizer packages. The nursing mother image captures this: the baby's sucking stimulates milk flow; the mother provides nurturing environment and essential sustenance.

### Negative Feedback as Regulation

Predators, pests, and diseases control particular species while providing a service to the ecosystem as a whole. These mechanisms regulate rather than destroy. They are everywhere in nature.

### The Cancer Analogy

Cells within an organism that grow and reproduce without control can be fatal — we call this cancer. Similarly, at all levels of nature including ecosystems and the living planet, the larger system controls its constituent parts for the good of that system. A hierarchy of large-scale controls flows back down the [[holmgren-trophic-pyramid-energy-hierarchy-societies]] to constrain exuberant smaller-scale systems.

### Life Sciences Lag

Biologists are often reluctant to accept higher-order system control beyond the organism level, reflecting Cartesian mechanistic worldview that physicists have largely abandoned. Systems thinkers in management, computer science, and engineering regularly use biological metaphors and find biologists' reluctance incredible.

## Self-Regulation in Nature and Culture

### Biological Self-Regulation

Important evolutionary responses to higher-order control include internal self-regulation mechanisms that control excessive growth before harsher external controls activate:

- **Fish in ponds:** Growth suppressed by their own wastes, decreasing likelihood of disease or starvation killing all
- **Kangaroos:** Can slow embryo development in response to seasonal conditions, partially regulating numbers before poor feed favors disease and predators

### Cultural Self-Regulation

Traditional societies had social and ethical constraints on population growth and resource use allowing communities to persist without destroying environments. Holmgren believes these self-controlling aspects of human culture, rather than expansion of technology, represent the highest evolutionary development of *Homo sapiens*.

## Tripartite Altruism

[[howard-odum]] described a "tripartite altruism" in nature for how organisms allocate available energy:

- **One-third** for metabolic self-maintenance (individual or population)
- **One-third** fed back to maintain lower-order system providers
- **One-third** contributed upward to higher-order system controllers

### Rabbit Example

Rabbits eat grass to live, grow, reproduce. Manure fertilizes grass. "Sacrifice" of weak rabbits to predators keeps population fit and balanced. If feedback mechanisms fail (e.g., manure deposited under brambles instead of on pasture), the population progressively declines from overpopulation, overgrazing, and habitat loss.

## Designing Self-Regulating Systems

### The Garden as Child Metaphor

- **Establishment phase:** Totally dependent on care (newly planted trees need protection, water, fertilizer)
- **Growing independence:** Becomes progressively more self-reliant, may need intervention for external/internal dangers
- **Maturity:** If design is inspired, develops self-regulation and balance analogous to responsible adults
- **Uniqueness:** Myriad circumstances create gardens that differ from plans and hopes

### The Real Work of Management

In any managed natural system, much management work is not making things grow but **preventing excessive and inappropriate growth**:
- Using goats to control rangeland weeds
- Thinning dense forest stands
- In gardens: removing or cutting back unwanted growth (not planting)

By using wild, hardy, self-reproducing species, we avoid high input needs and focus intelligence on harvesting and controlling excess growth — preferably as another yield.

### Feedback from Failure

Failures in obtaining yield are useful if we pay attention:
- Harvesting a little in season and shopping the rest of the year may mask storage problems
- All pumpkins or garlic rotting can reveal soil mineral imbalances
- Growing your own firewood makes wasteful use obvious; buying it hides the real cost
- Flick-of-a-switch power makes impacts seem remote and abstract

## Scale and Feedback Effectiveness

### Remote vs. Local Resources

In modern urban lifestyles, electricity, water, and salary arrive remotely, making it hard to know when we're exploiting common resources. Market and law are crude mechanisms for providing negative feedback.

In smaller communities, more intimate social mechanisms exist:
- Casual interaction in the pub, volunteer fire brigade, or sporting club
- Neighbors understand each other's views
- The more we depend on own and local resources, the more likely we recognize problems and institute corrective behaviors

### The Disconnect Problem

When consequences are remote, we can't feel the feedback. If we buy firewood instead of growing it, we never consider how much forest is required. If power comes from a switch, global warming seems abstract. Self-reliance creates direct feedback loops that make inappropriate behavior obvious.

## Key Takeaways

1. Self-regulation pre-empts harsher external negative feedback — it is the Holy Grail of permaculture
2. Modern society is like a teenager wanting everything without consequences
3. The Gaia hypothesis illustrates the ultimate self-regulating whole system
4. Nature simultaneously nurtures (positive feedback) and constrains (negative feedback)
5. Cancer is the analogy for uncontrolled growth at any system level
6. Tripartite altruism: organisms allocate energy roughly equally to self-maintenance, lower-order support, and higher-order contribution
7. In gardens, most management work is preventing excess, not promoting growth
8. Growing your own needs creates direct feedback that buying masks
9. Smaller communities provide more effective social feedback mechanisms than markets or law
10. Self-controlling aspects of culture represent humanity's highest evolutionary development
