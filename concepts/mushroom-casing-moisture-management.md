# Casing Moisture Management

## Overview

Moisture within the [[gourmet-medicinal-mushroom-casing-layer-techniques]] has a direct effect on the diameter and degree
of branching in growing mycelium. These characteristics serve as visual
indicators of moisture content and can guide the cultivator in proper watering
practices. Proper casing moisture management is critical for successful
[[mushroom-pin-formation]] and sustained flush production in [[agaricus-brunnescens]] cultivation.

## Water Retention Properties

The casing must have the capacity to both absorb and release substantial
quantities of water. Not only does the casing sustain [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]], but it
must also supply sufficient moisture for successive generations of fruitbodies.
Common [[mushroom-cultivator-casing-materials-buffering-agents]] vary in water retention capacity:

| Material | Absorption (ml/g) | Retention |
|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| Vermiculite | 5.0 | 84% |
| Peat | 2.5 | 79% |
| Potting Soil | 0.7 | 76% |
| Loam | 0.3 | 25% |
| Chalk | 0.6 | 37% |
| Limestone Grit | 0.2 | 15% |
| Sand | 0.2 | 18% |

Vermiculite is the most effective water-retaining material available for casing
amendments. Values vary according to source and quality of material.

## Determining Moisture Saturation

To determine moisture saturation, completely saturate a casing sample and allow
it to drain. Cover and wait 30 minutes. Weigh out 100 grams and dry in an oven
at 200 degrees F for two to three hours until completely dry. The difference in
weight reveals the percent moisture at saturation. Optimum moisture content is
normally 2-4% below saturation. Peat-based casings are typically balanced to
70-75% moisture content.

## Three Moisture Conditions

### Optimum Casing Moisture

Under ideal moisture conditions, mushroom mycelium thrives in a moist humid
casing, sending out minute branching networks that expand and grow. This
[[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]] is characterized by many thick, white rhizomorphic strands
branching into mycelia of smaller diameters, fine capillary networks, and a
lush, dense overall appearance. The casing holds firmly together when examined
but separates with little effort.

### Overly Dry Casing

In a dry casing, the mycelium shows a lack of rhizomorphs and abundance of fine
capillary-type mycelia. The fine growth totally permeates the casing layer,
which becomes hard, compact, and unreceptive to water. Puddles commonly form on
a dry casing that has just been watered. Dry casing rarely permits primordia
formation and is susceptible to overlay, where a dense mycelial mat covers the
surface showing little inclination to form pinheads.

### Overly Wet Casing

In a saturated casing, mycelial growth is coarse and stringy with very little
branching. Few capillaries form and mycelial growth is slow and sparse. The
casing remains largely uncolonized, and saturated casing can leach onto the
substrate surface causing waterlogging. Further growth is inhibited and
contamination is promoted. Subsequent drying may reactivate mycelium, but yield
reduction is expected.

## Casing Preparation

To prepare a casing of proper moisture content, remove 10-20% of the volume of
the dry mix, then saturate the remaining 80-90%. Add the reserved dry material
back, which brings the moisture content to near optimum. Vermiculite can be
added (one-half to one part per casing mixture) to improve water-retaining
capacity. When used, it must be presoaked to saturation before mixing with other
ingredients.
