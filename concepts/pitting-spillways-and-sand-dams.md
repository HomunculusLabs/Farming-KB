## Pitting, Spillways, and Sand Dams

In arid and semi-arid landscapes, managing water movement downslope and capturing sediment-laden floodwaters requires specialised techniques. Pitting creates distributed infiltration points across broad areas, carefully designed spillways prevent erosion when water must be discharged downslope, and sand dams convert ephemeral flood flows into permanent subsurface water reserves.

## Pitting in Sands and Light Soils

### The Disc Pitting Technique

By cutting a quarter-section slice off a set of disc blades, long pits are created with a quarter of their length as solid breaks. As discs are drawn along, a series of mini-swales is produced, staggered like contour swales but without requiring strict surveying — they can be drawn by eye.

### Specifications

- **Spacing**: 0.5–0.75 m apart
- **Length**: 1.0–1.5 m each
- **Depth**: Maximum 0.3 m
- **Soil placement**: Ideally sidecast downhill or downwind (not critical on near-flat sites)

### Advantages

- No single pit is long enough to spill excessive water and create stream flow
- Seed can be added directly to pits, broadcast, or blown from residual vegetation
- Fast and effective; can be completed on broadacre scale
- Suitable for grasses, shrubs, trees, and forbs
- Eliminates or greatly decreases dust storms immediately upon creation
- Enormous cumulative storage capacity across many acres

### Enhanced Establishment

Pelleted desert shrub seed combined with a light fertiliser dressing creates conditions for permanent soil stability around settlements. Various pitting machines exist, but pits can also be hand-dug for smaller areas.

### Cordon"s Recommendations

Cordon (1975), working in semi-arid hills, recorded average annual runoff of 20%, with up to 80% in eroded and compacted soils. For slopes exceeding 2%, he recommends:

- Series of contour furrows or mini-swales at 2 m intervals
- Only every 20–30 contours need actual surveying
- Swales are "broken" to allow flooding through to the next series
- Vegetation in furrows remains green during drought
- Soils must have absorption capacity (primarily sandy soils)
- Scale is flexible — as large as required at 2–4 m spacing

## Spilling Water Downslope in Fragile Soils

Steep-sided gullies often result from road culverts, animal tracks exiting wadis, or water discharged from diversion drains after fields are flooded. There are only a few safe methods for spilling water downslope in desert environments:

### 1. Very Long Spreader Banks

Long, gently-sloping banks that take concentrated flow and sheet the water harmlessly downhill. In important areas, a solid concrete core ensures even flow distribution across the hill. Cross-walls eventually block the bank ends to prevent discharge. These must be maintained and inspected during rain events.

### 2. Grassed Spillways with Steering Banks

Below a spreader bank, a well-grassed and fenced spillway leads water safely downslope. Side or "stern" banks prevent sideways scour. These systems take a few years to fully evolve as grass establishes.

### 3. Sealed Spillways or Pipes with Splash Pools

Water is conveyed downslope through sealed channels (concrete or stone) or pipes to splash pools, which then feed into a [[dam-construction]]**: Wall keyed in 4–6 m to side banks (prevents scouring around ends)
2. **Upstream preparation**: Series of 2 m deep bays cut into upstream banks before construction
3. **Filter drain**: Several loads of coarse stone placed against the upstream wall face
4. **Clearwater dam**: Located downstream, less than one-ninth of the total area backed up by the wall

### How It Functions

- First floods rush down and over the wall
- Silt and coarse sands settle out, filling storage within a few rains
- Shingle/rocks on the inside of the wall act as filtered seepage to the clearwater dam
- The dam intentionally leaks water through the riverbed
- Bays fill with sand, creating flat silt beds for planting large trees (pecan, palm, avocado, mango)
