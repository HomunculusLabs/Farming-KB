# Fungal Plant Pathogens

## Overview

Fungi cause more plant diseases than all other enemies combined — bacteria, viruses, nematodes, and insects. Since the beginning of agriculture, billions of livelihoods and lives have been lost to rusts, smuts, bunts, mildews, potato blight, and rice blast. Annual losses to agriculture are incalculably high, and chemical fungicides represent a tremendous investment by farmers and consumers. Chapter 9 of "Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard" explores the most devastating fungal pathogens of crops and trees. ## Rust Fungi

### Black Stem Rust of Wheat (Puccinia graminis)

One of the most complex life cycles in biology, involving two hosts and four spore types:

**On wheat (uredial stage)**:
- Uredospores are reddish, spiny, and wind-dispersed
- A single infected crop can spawn an epidemic — spores carried hundreds of miles by wind
- Germ tubes germinate in morning humidity when stomata are open
- Germ tubes grow *across* the leaf (not along it), crossing epidermal hills and valleys to find stomatal openings — only 1 in 5-7 cell files contains stomata
- Harvey Hoch at Cornell proved fungi use physical cues alone: plastic replicas of leaf surfaces with ridges matching stomatal lip height (0.5 micrometers) trigger appressorium formation; lower or higher ridges are ignored
- Robby Roberson at Arizona State used similar methods to control hyphal growth patterns and passed electrical currents through mycelia, dreaming of biochip "mycocomputers"

**Cellular mechanism for surface sensing**:
- Stretch-activated protein channels in the fungal membrane open and close as the hypha navigates ridges
- Channel opening allows calcium to flood into the cell, triggering a biochemical cascade
- Related to the mechanism providing humans with tactile sense — but fungal sensitivity is tuned to perceive features less than a millionth of a meter tall (smaller than a bacterium)

**Plant defense — hypersensitive reaction**:
- When a rust lands on a resistant plant, the plant destroys its own cells around the penetration point
- Forms a tiny fleck in the epidermis, starving the fungus of living cytoplasm
- Creates an evolutionary arms race: rust evolves new races, plant evolves new defenses
- Rust species contain specialized races (formae speciales): P. graminis f. sp. tritici (wheat), f. sp. avenae (oats), f. sp. secalis (rye)

**On barberry (sexual stage)**:
- Teliospores (black, thick-walled, overwintering in frozen stubble) germinate in spring warmth
- Meiosis produces four daughter nuclei packaged into bean-shaped basidiospores
- Buller's drop appears at the base of each spore before launch — proving rusts are basidiomycete fungi (distant cousins of mushrooms)
- Basidiospores must land on barberry leaves, where they penetrate directly through the cuticle (barberry leaves are less tough than silica-reinforced grass leaves)

**Spermagonia and insect-mediated sex**:
- Tiny flask-shaped spermagonia rupture the barberry leaf's upper epidermis
- Exude sugary nectar and spermatia (function as sperm cells, cannot germinate)
- Flies and insects transfer spermatia between spermagonia while feeding on nectar
- Spermatial nucleus migrates down the receptive hair, creating a dikaryotic mycelium
- Some rusts ([[bloomfield-puccinia-monoica-pseudoflowers-rust-mimicry]]) manipulate host plants to produce yellow spermagonia that mimic real flowers — deceiving insects and outcompeting actual flowers for pollinator attention

**Aeciospore production and return to wheat**:
- Cup-shaped aecia on barberry leaf undersides produce aeciospores
- These reinfect wheat through stomata, completing the cycle

**Why such complexity?**
- Two hosts with different growing seasons boost survival resilience
- Maximizes opportunities for multiplication (asexual cloning) and genetic recombination (sexual)
- Some rusts have even more unrelated alternate hosts: fir trees and ferns, white pines and gooseberries
- Speculative hypothesis: rust species may be ancient hybrids of two distinct parasites

**Control methods**:
- Chemical: sulfur mixtures to synthetic fungicides (e.g., triadimefon disrupts ergosterol biosynthesis)
- Cultural: eradicating barberry from wheat regions removes the sexual reproduction site, retarding evolution of new rust races
- Genetic: resistant wheat cultivars
- Future: [[singh-genetically-engineered-fungi-bioremediation]] crops with built-in antifungal defenses

### Coffee Rust (Hemileia vastatrix)
- Causes devastating losses to coffee crops in South and [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]], Africa, and Asia
- Has an "edited" life cycle: produces uredospores, teliospores, and basidiospores, but lacks spermatium and aeciospore stages
- No alternate host — thrives on coffee alone
- Control limited to fungicide spraying and resistant varieties (no barberry-equivalent to eradicate)

### Hawthorn/Cedar Rust (Gymnosporangium globosum)
- Ifruit trees, and evergreens (cedars, junipers)
- Produces entirely different spore types on different hosts
- Berries transformed into pale galls covered with spore-filled horns
- Elongating towers of infectious cells continuously replace uppermost escaping spores

## Rice Blast (magnaporthe grisea)

### The Appressorium — Most Powerful Pathogen on Earth

- An ascomycete; sexual phase (perithecia) rarely or never develops in crops
- Pear-shaped conidium lands on rice leaf surface; impact triggers sticky mucilage release
- Germ tube grows a short distance, then adheres and swells into a dome-shaped appressorium
- Within hours: blackened with melanin, filled with glycerol, pressurized to 80+ atmospheres
- Can puncture bulletproof vest material
- Force compared to a 400-pound dancer's toe engaged in a brief pirouette, or a lighter woman wearing stiletto heels

**Pressurization mechanism**:
1. "O-ring" of glue seals appressorium to leaf surface
2. Melanin-rich pigment layer deposited against inner cell wall
3. Melanized wall becomes impermeable — traps glycerol inside
4. Water diffuses inward via osmosis in response to rising glycerol concentration
5. Pressure rises until base yields; slender penetration hypha forced into leaf
6. If melanin synthesis is inhibited (by fungicide or albino mutation), penetration fails completely

**Force measurement**: German researchers allowed appressoria to indent metal sandwiches filled with breast-implant gel, using lasers to measure precise indentation depth.

**Disease impact**:
- Leaves look as if struck by a bomb
- "Neck blast" attacks the panicle, bleaching and withering it — grain cannot ripen, severe crop loss
- Most prevalent in intensive agriculture with nitrogen fertilizers; subsistence farmers relying on [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] are less affected
- Genome: 7 chromosomes, 3x more DNA than yeast, 80x less than humans

**Genomic research**: Sequencing targets distinctive Magnaporthe genes for host interaction — controlling conidium germination timing, melanin synthesis, epidermis penetration triggers, cytoplasmic feeding activation.

## Potato Blight (Phytophthora infestans)

### The Irish Famine Pathogen

- An oomycete (water mold), not a true fungus
- Caused the Irish famine of the 1840s: population dropped from 8 million to below 4 million through starvation and emigration
- 6 million Irish were utterly dependent on potatoes for sustenance and rent payments
- Wet, foggy conditions in late summer 1845 allowed Phytophthora to devastate the crop
- A million starved; a million or more emigrated
- Still causes $3 billion in annual losses — the most devastating living enemy of global agriculture

**Infection cycle**:
1. Wind-dispersed sporangia land on wet potato leaf
2. Zoospores expelled into water film, charge across waxy cuticle, form cyst
3. Germ tube forms appressorium, pierces epidermis
4. Initially makes haustoria (feeds without destroying tissue — membrane dimples around haustorium like a glove)
5. After a few days: profound tissue damage, feeds on soupy remains
6. Reemerges through open stomata after 5 days; releases up to 300,000 sporangia per day per leaf blotch
7. Washed sporangia release spores into soil to infect buried tubers
8. Healthy-looking potatoes can rot in storage (accelerated by bacteria melting starchy tissue)

**Historical control**: A Belgian scientist discovered in 1845 that removing infected leaves/stems when blotches appear can save tubers (fungus doesn't usually spread directly from leaves to tubers). Nobody in Ireland knew this.

**Origin and evolution**:
- Current widespread strain is NOT the same one that caused the 1840s famine (determined by DNA extraction from [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]])
- Wild potatoes that co-evolved with Phytophthora likely have strong defenses
- Greatest strain diversity found in Mexico's Toluca Valley — probable origin
- Over 50 Phytophthora species recognized, infecting almost every broad-leaved plant
- P. ramorum causes "sudden oak death" along 200 miles of northern California coastline
