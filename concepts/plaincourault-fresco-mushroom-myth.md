# The Plaincourault Fresco and Mushroom Myth

The fresco at the Chapel of Plaincourault in central France, depicting the
temptation of Adam and Eve, has become one of the most enduring pieces of
evidence cited in support of theories about the [fly-agaric mushroom in Christian art.
Despite being rejected by art historians, it continues to circulate in mushroom
enthusiast literature as proof that early Christians knew of the mushroom's
psychoactive properties.

## The Fresco

The Romanesque Chapel of Plaincourault, located in the Indre department of
central France, contains a fresco depicting the Temptation in the Garden of Eden.
In the fresco, a serpent is coiled around a large, distinctive tree-like form
that bears a striking visual resemblance to the fly-agaric mushroom ([[teonanacatl-and-mesoamerican-mushroom-cults]] throughout human history and the survival
of mushroom knowledge in European religious art.

Wasson had presaged Allegro in suggesting that the stories of
the temptation and of the tree of knowledge were a cultural memory of
fly-agaric-based spirituality. He placed this suggestion at the very end of his
work, which allowed him to avoid the controversy that Allegro later attracted.

## Art Historical Rejection

Art historians convincingly demonstrated that the fresco depicts nothing more
exotic than a common, if bizarrely rendered, Romanesque tree. The Romanesque
artistic style frequently produced stylised, non-naturalistic representations of
trees and plants that can appear strange to modern eyes but were well within the
artistic conventions of the period.

The conventions of Romanesque art did not prioritise botanical accuracy. Trees
were often rendered schematically, with emphasis on symbolic rather than
realistic representation. The particular style of the Plaincourault tree, while
unusual, falls within the known range of Romanesque artistic variation.

Wasson himself was eventually convinced by the art historians' arguments and
accepted that the fresco did not depict a mushroom.

## Allegro's Adoption and Abandonment

[Allegro, however, enthusiastically adopted the
Plaincourault fresco as evidence for his theory. A colour photograph of the
fresco was included in his book, and a stylised graphic rendition was catchingly
placed on the inside cover.

For the general public, this was the only piece of evidence they needed. The
visual impact of a medieval fresco apparently depicting Eve being tempted by a
mushroom rather than an apple was enormously powerful and far more convincing
than Allegro's complex philological arguments.

However, Allegro dropped the fresco from the revised edition of his book, printed
in 1973, presumably having been convinced of the art historians' position. The
damage, however, was done.

## The Enduring Myth

Despite its rejection by scholars, the Plaincourault myth persists in popular
consciousness. It resurfaces regularly in field guides, coffee-table books, and
online discussions about mushrooms and religion. The image has a power that
transcends its scholarly debunking.

Several factors explain the myth's endurance:

**Visual persuasion**: The resemblance between the fresco's tree-form and a
fly-agaric is genuinely striking. Even knowing the art-historical explanation,
the visual similarity is hard to dismiss.

**Confirmation bias**: For those already inclined to believe in ancient mushroom
cults, the fresco provides seemingly incontrovertible visual evidence. It
confirms what they already believe.

**Simplicity**: The fresco is far easier to understand and communicate than
Allegro's complex philological arguments. A picture really is worth a thousand
words.

**Scholarly rejection as evidence**: For some enthusiasts, the fact that the
academic establishment rejects the mushroom interpretation is itself taken as
evidence of a cover-up, reinforcing rather than undermining the theory.

## Subsequent Mushroom-in-Art Claims

The Plaincourault precedent has encouraged numerous other claims about mushroom
imagery in Christian and pre-Christian art. Various authors have identified
supposed fly-agaric representations in everything from Gothic cathedral carvings
to illuminated manuscripts to ancient cave paintings.

Most of these identifications suffer from the same problem as Plaincourault:
they rely on superficial visual resemblances that can be adequately explained by
established art-historical and iconographic conventions.

## Lessons from Plaincourault

The Plaincourault episode illustrates important lessons about the intersection
of scholarship and popular enthusiasm:

**Visual evidence is easily misinterpreted**: Images that appear to support a
theory often have simpler alternative explanations that are only visible to
specialists.

**Myths are resilient**: Once a claim enters popular culture, scholarly
rejection is rarely sufficient to dislodge it.

**The burden of proof**: Those making extraordinary claims bear the burden of
proof, and resemblance alone is not proof.
