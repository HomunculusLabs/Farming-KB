# The Plaincourault Fresco and Mushroom Myth

The fresco at the Chapel of Plaincourault in central France, depicting the
temptation of Adam and Eve, has become one of the most enduring pieces of
evidence cited in support of theories about the
[[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]] in Christian art. Despite being
rejected by art historians, it continues to circulate in mushroom enthusiast
literature as proof that early Christians knew of the mushroom's psychoactive
properties.

## The Fresco

The Romanesque Chapel of Plaincourault, located in the Indre department of
central France, contains a fresco depicting the Temptation in the Garden of
Eden. In the fresco, a serpent is coiled around a large, distinctive tree-like
form that bears a striking visual resemblance to the fly-agaric mushroom
([[mckenna-food-gods-mesoamerican-mushrooms]]). The resemblance is striking
enough that it has fueled decades of speculation about whether the artist
intentionally depicted a mushroom rather than a tree.

The chapel itself dates to approximately the 12th century and is one of the
smaller Romanesque churches in the region. The fresco was executed in tempera
on plaster and has undergone restoration at various points in its history.
The depiction shows Adam and Eve flanking the central tree form, with the
serpent wound around its trunk, following the standard iconography of the
Temptation narrative familiar from [[solanaceous-hallucinogens-of-medieval-european-witchcraft]] art.

## The Mushroom Interpretation

The mushroom interpretation was popularized in the mid-20th century by scholars
investigating the possible role of psychoactive mushrooms in religious history.
John Marco Allegro, in "The [[allegro-the-sacred-mushroom-amanita-identification]] and the Cross" (1970), cited the
Plaincourault fresco as evidence that early Christians preserved knowledge of
psychoactive mushrooms and encoded this knowledge in their art. Allegro argued
that Christianity itself was rooted in an ancient fertility cult centered on
the consumption of the [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]] mushroom.

R. Gordon Wasson, the ethnomycologist who first brought the fly-agaric
hypothesis to wide attention through his work on soma in Vedic India, also
noted the Plaincourault fresco's resemblance to a mushroom. Wasson suggested
that certain elements of Christian art might represent a survival of
mushroom-based religious practices from earlier periods. However, Wasson was
more cautious than Allegro and positioned his suggestions as speculative
rather than definitive.

## Art Historical Rejection

Art historians have convincingly demonstrated that the fresco depicts nothing
more exotic than a common, if bizarrely rendered, Romanesque tree. The key
arguments against the mushroom interpretation include:

- Romanesque art frequently produced stylized, non-naturalistic
  representations of trees and plants that can appear strange to modern eyes
- The conventions of Romanesque art did not prioritize botanical accuracy
- Trees were often rendered schematically, with emphasis on symbolic rather
  than realistic representation
- The particular style of the Plaincourault tree, while unusual, falls within
  the known range of Romanesque artistic variation
- No corroborating evidence exists that medieval Christians used fly-agaric
  in religious rituals

Notably, Wasson himself was eventually convinced by the art historians'
arguments and accepted that the fresco did not depict a mushroom. This
retraction is often omitted from popular accounts that continue to cite the
fresco as evidence.

## Cultural Significance of the Myth

Despite its rejection by scholars, the Plaincourault myth persists because it
serves several functions in popular mycological culture. It provides an
apparently concrete visual artifact connecting modern psychedelic interest
with deep historical religious traditions. The image of a medieval fresco
depicting a forbidden mushroom is compelling and has been reproduced in
countless books, articles, and online discussions.

The persistence of the myth illustrates a broader pattern in
[[mescaline-ethnobotany]]: the tendency to retroactively interpret historical artifacts
through the lens of contemporary interests. Similar patterns can be observed
in interpretations of ancient cave art, pre-Columbian sculpture, and medieval
manuscript illuminations, all of which have been claimed to depict mushrooms
with varying degrees of plausibility.

The Plaincourault case also demonstrates the tension between disciplinary
expertise and popular enthusiasm. Art historians bring knowledge of artistic
conventions, iconographic traditions, and comparative analysis that
non-specialists may lack, while enthusiasts bring passion and a willingness
to consider unconventional interpretations. Both perspectives contribute to
the ongoing dialogue about the role of psychoactive substances in human
[[shroom-cultural-history-hippie-revolution-and-counterculture]].

## Lessons for Mycological Historians

The Plaincourault affair offers several cautionary lessons for anyone
investigating the historical role of mushrooms in human culture:

- Visual resemblance alone is insufficient evidence for intentional
  depiction of specific organisms
- Artistic conventions of other eras may produce forms that appear meaningful
  through modern eyes but were conventional or accidental in their original
  context
- Corroborating evidence from multiple independent sources is essential
  before accepting extraordinary claims
- Scholarly retraction should be given as much attention as original claims
