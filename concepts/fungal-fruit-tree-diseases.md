## Foliar Diseases

Foliar fungal diseases are among the most visible and economically damaging
problems in [[biodiversity-powdery-mildews]], caused by species of Podosphaera, Erysiphe, and related
genera, produce characteristic white-gray growth on leaves, shoots, and
fruit surfaces. Unlike most fungal pathogens, powdery mildew does not
require free water for infection and can develop under relatively dry
conditions, making it particularly problematic in arid and semi-arid
growing regions. The fungi overwinter as cleistothecia (closed fruiting
bodies) on bark and bud scales, releasing spores that infect new growth
in the spring during periods of moderate temperature and high humidity.

## Fruit Rots

Fungal fruit rots cause direct loss of marketable fruit and can spread
rapidly through stored fruit after harvest. Brown rot, caused by
Monilinia fructicola and related species, is the most important
post-harvest disease of stone fruits including peaches, plums, and
cherries. The fungus infects through wounds in the fruit skin and
produces tan-brown lesions that rapidly expand and sporulate, producing
conidia that spread to adjacent fruit. In severe cases, infected fruit
mummify and remain attached to the tree, serving as a source of inoculum
for subsequent seasons if not removed during winter pruning operations.

## Canker and Wood Diseases

Fungal canker diseases affect the woody tissues of fruit trees, causing
sunken, dead areas on branches and trunks that can girdle and kill
affected parts. Cytospora canker, caused by species of Leucostoma and
Cytospora, is a common problem on stone fruits, particularly on trees
stressed by drought, cold injury, or poor nutrition. The fungus enters
through wounds and pruning cuts, gradually killing the cambium and
underlying wood. European canker, caused by Neonectria ditissima, is a
serious disease of apples in cool, wet regions and can cause extensive
damage to both branches and fruit.
