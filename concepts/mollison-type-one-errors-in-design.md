## Advanced Checklist Additions

Experienced permaculture designers have proposed additions to Mollison's original ten errors that address issues not covered in the initial framework. Common additions include: failing to design for climate change and future uncertainty; recommending imported materials when local equivalents exist; neglecting to include succession planning so the design evolves over time; overlooking the social and economic dimensions of the design in favour of purely technical elements; and failing to build in monitoring and feedback mechanisms that allow the system to be adjusted as conditions change. These additions reflect the maturation of permaculture design practice since Mollison first formulated his list, incorporating insights from decades of implementation experience. The [[mollison-wet-dry-tropical-design-strategies]] and [[faires-permaculture-ethics-and-design-principles]] of earth care, people care, and fair share require designers to prioritise the long-term health of the system over short-term convenience, to serve the client's genuine needs rather than imposing the designer's preferences, and to distribute the benefits of the design equitably among all stakeholders including the non-human ones. Type-one errors, when viewed through this ethical lens, are not merely technical mistakes but ethical failings that undermine the credibility of permaculture as a professional discipline and betray the trust that clients place in designers.

## Type-One Errors and the Super-Client

Mollison's concept of the "super-client" -- the environment itself -- provides an overarching ethical framework for evaluating all design decisions, including the ten type-one errors. When a designer makes any of these errors, the super-client suffers. Recommending destructive clients (Error 1) directly harms the environment. Missing on-site resources (Error 10) leads to unnecessary extraction from elsewhere. Poor patterning (Error 7) wastes energy and creates pollution. Every type-one error, without exception, represents a failure to serve the super-client's interests. This reframing elevates the checklist from a practical quality assurance tool to a statement of ecological ethics. The [[pdc-design-implementation-and-maintenance]] process in modern permaculture practice has expanded to include monitoring protocols, adaptive management plans, and succession strategies that were not part of Mollison's original framework. Yet the fundamental wisdom of the ten type-one errors remains relevant: any design error, regardless of its specific form, can be traced back to one or more of the underlying failures that Mollison identified -- failures of observation, communication, specificity, ecological awareness, or ethical commitment.
## The Designer's Growth Journey

Mollison acknowledged that avoiding type-one errors is a developmental process, not a one-time achievement. Novice designers are likely to commit several type-one errors in their early work, and the ability to consistently avoid all ten errors comes only with years of practice, honest self-reflection, and feedback from clients and peers. The [[mollison-designers-design-client-interview-and-site-assessment-process]] that Mollison describes includes multiple meetings, site walks, and written documentation precisely because this education process cannot be accomplished in a single presentation. Clients who understand why a particular design element has been chosen are far less likely to insist on modifications that would compromise the design's integrity.
## The Relationship Between Type-One and Type-Two Errors

Mollison distinguishes type-one errors (errors in design) from type-two errors (errors in maintenance). While the ten type-one errors occur during the design phase and can theoretically be prevented by thorough design practice, type-two errors occur after installation and result from inadequate maintenance, monitoring, or adaptation to changing conditions. The relationship between the two error types is synergistic: a design that contains type-one errors will be harder to maintain and more prone to type-two errors, while diligent maintenance can partially compensate for design shortcomings. The [[ozier-pdc-design-process-analysis-frameworks]] in permaculture education builds this habit through repeated practice in design exercises, so that by the time students begin professional practice, the checklist has become an automatic part of their design workflow. Mollison recommended keeping a physical copy of the checklist visible in the design workspace as a constant reminder, and crossing off each item only after it has been explicitly addressed and documented in the design report.
