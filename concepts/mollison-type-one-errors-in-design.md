# Type-One Errors in Permaculture Design

Type-one errors are the fundamental mistakes that permaculture designers make. Even an experienced designer can make errors. Mollison advises keeping a checklist and going over it frequently. These ten errors represent the most common and serious design failures.

## Error 1: Designing for Destructive Clients

Undertaking a design job for people whose aims are environmentally destructive -- for example, a group that wants to hack out a hole in the forest for themselves. When approached by such people, always side with the "super-client" -- the environment. More often than not, the people you deal with are really good people, but the designer must maintain integrity.

## Error 2: Not Explaining Reasons

Not telling your client the reasons for your recommendations. You must always explain why you have recommended something -- for example, why you placed a drain in a particular location to carry greywater to a specific secondary use. Without explanation, the client cannot maintain or adapt the design, and may undo your work through ignorance.

## Error 3: Recommending Beyond Client Ability

The recommendation of a difficult technology beyond the client's ability to handle. A design that requires skills, tools, or knowledge the client does not possess and cannot acquire will fail. The designer must match the technology to the client's capacity.

## Error 4: Inadequate Management Data

Not supplying adequate management data, sufficient directions about how to run the place after the client gets it going. For example, the orchard needs a specific management strategy, as does changing over from one system to another. A design without management instructions is like a car without a manual.

## Error 5: Depersonalized Reporting

Writing a report with a depersonalized approach. Your reporting style should be direct, friendly, and fairly personal. The client needs to feel that the design was made for them specifically, not generated by a formula.

## Error 6: Vague and Imprecise Language

Failing to be specific, employing expressions that are loose and vague like "fairly large." Every recommendation should be specific enough to implement: exact dimensions, specific species names, precise locations, clear quantities.

## Error 7: Poor Patterning

Very carefully think through those edges and the link-ups between the different patterns incorporated in your design. Poor connections between design elements waste energy, create maintenance problems, and reduce overall system function. The design should flow as an integrated whole.

## Error 8: Missing Pre-Treatments

Failing to recommend essential pre-treatments. For example, explain how the client should condition the soil for the uses you recommend. Skipping soil preparation before planting, or not addressing existing problems (compaction, contamination, erosion) before implementing new elements, leads to failure.

## Error 9: Illegal Plant Species

Recommending the use of plant species illegal in that area. This can result in ecological damage, legal consequences for the client, and reputational damage for the designer. Always check local regulations regarding invasive species and restricted plants.

## Error 10: Missing On-Site Resources

Failing to fully define the resources that are on site, and to explain how they can be useful. This requires the designer's ability to see resources -- a core permaculture skill. Overlooking on-site resources means the client spends money unnecessarily and the design is less efficient.

## The Checklist Approach

Mollison recommends keeping a checklist of these errors and reviewing it frequently throughout the design process. Each error can be addressed at multiple stages:

- During the [[mollison-co-action-analysis-and-species-interaction-in-guild-design]] (Errors 8, 10)
- During the [[mollison-windbreak-design]] (Errors 6, 7)
- During the [[mollison-design-report-structure]] (Errors 2, 4, 5, 6, 9)

## Error Prevention Through Design Methodology

The systematic design methodology inherently prevents many of these errors:
- Client interview addresses Errors 1, 3, and 5
- Site analysis addresses Errors 8 and 10
- The principle-based approach addresses Error 7
- Standard report structure addresses Errors 2, 4, 5, and 6
- Species research addresses Error 9

## The Bigger Picture

These errors are not just technical mistakes -- they reflect failures in the designer's relationship with the client, the site, and the design process itself. The best prevention is thorough preparation, honest communication, and genuine care for both the client and the super-client (the environment).
## How Type-One Errors Compound

Individual type-one errors are damaging enough, but the real danger lies in how they compound when multiple errors occur simultaneously. A designer who fails to explain reasons (Error 2) and uses vague language (Error 6) produces a report that the client cannot understand or implement. If this is combined with recommending technology beyond the client's ability (Error 3) and missing on-site resources (Error 10), the design is essentially doomed to fail. The client will spend money on inappropriate technologies while overlooking free resources available on their own land, and will lack the knowledge to maintain even the elements that were correctly specified. Understanding how these errors interact helps designers prioritise their checklist reviews: errors that compound with others -- particularly Errors 2, 6, and 10 -- deserve the most attention.
## Teaching Type-One Errors in PDC Courses

Type-one errors are a standard teaching topic in the [[holmgren-permaculture-education-training-and-pdc]] curriculum, where they serve as a memorable framework for design ethics and professional standards. Effective PDC teaching presents these errors not just as abstract principles but through concrete examples drawn from real design failures. Students can learn to recognise type-one errors by reviewing and critiquing existing designs -- both good and bad -- identifying which errors are present and how they could be corrected. This practical exercise builds the critical thinking skills needed to avoid these errors in their own design work. The [[pdc-design-project-methodology-and-case-study]] course module typically includes a session where students role-play both designer and client, practicing the communication skills that prevent Errors 2, 4, 5, and 6.
## Self-Evaluation Framework

Beyond the basic checklist, experienced designers develop a personal self-evaluation framework that extends the type-one errors into more nuanced self-assessment questions. This framework might include: Have I visited the site at different times of day and in different seasons? Have I spoken to long-term residents about the site's history and microclimates? Am I recommending this species or technology because it genuinely serves the client's goals, or because it is familiar to me? Does my design address what the client asked for, or what I wanted to design? These deeper questions probe the same underlying issues as the ten type-one errors -- client alignment, specificity, resource awareness, and site sensitivity -- but in a more reflective and self-critical way. The [[mollison-observation-design]] that Mollison advocated requires this kind of ongoing self-questioning throughout the design process.
## Advanced Checklist Additions

Experienced permaculture designers have proposed additions to Mollison's original ten errors that address issues not covered in the initial framework. Common additions include: failing to design for climate change and future uncertainty; recommending imported materials when local equivalents exist; neglecting to include succession planning so the design evolves over time; overlooking the social and economic dimensions of the design in favour of purely technical elements; and failing to build in monitoring and feedback mechanisms that allow the system to be adjusted as conditions change. These additions reflect the maturation of permaculture design practice since Mollison first formulated his list, incorporating insights from decades of implementation experience. The [[permaculture-designers-manual-designer-checklist]] in the Designers' Manual incorporates many of these extended considerations, providing a more comprehensive quality assurance framework for professional design work.
## Type-One Errors in Different Climate Zones

The specific manifestations of type-one errors vary significantly across climate zones, though the underlying principles remain the same. In tropical climates, Error 8 (missing pre-treatments) often manifests as failing to address the rapid leaching of nutrients from tropical soils before planting, while Error 9 (illegal plants) may involve recommending invasive species that could spread rapidly in the absence of frost. In arid climates, Error 10 (missing on-site resources) frequently involves overlooking the value of seasonal water flows, shade from existing vegetation, or thermal mass in rocks and earth that could moderate extreme temperatures. In cold climates, Error 3 (recommending beyond client ability) commonly involves specifying tender perennial species or season extension structures that require more maintenance than the client can sustain through the winter months. The [[mollison-wet-dry-tropical-design-strategies]] and [[mollison-drought-proofing-strategies]] in the Designers' Manual provide climate-specific guidance that helps designers avoid these regionally specific versions of the type-one errors.
## The Ethics Dimension

Underlying all ten type-one errors is a question of professional ethics. A designer who cuts corners, uses vague language, fails to explain their reasoning, or takes on clients whose goals are environmentally destructive is violating the ethical foundations of permaculture practice. The [[faires-permaculture-ethics-and-design-principles]] of earth care, people care, and fair share require designers to prioritise the long-term health of the system over short-term convenience, to serve the client's genuine needs rather than imposing the designer's preferences, and to distribute the benefits of the design equitably among all stakeholders including the non-human ones. Type-one errors, when viewed through this ethical lens, are not merely technical mistakes but ethical failings that undermine the credibility of permaculture as a professional discipline and betray the trust that clients place in designers.

## Type-One Errors and the Super-Client

Mollison's concept of the "super-client" -- the environment itself -- provides an overarching ethical framework for evaluating all design decisions, including the ten type-one errors. When a designer makes any of these errors, the super-client suffers. Recommending destructive clients (Error 1) directly harms the environment. Missing on-site resources (Error 10) leads to unnecessary extraction from elsewhere. Poor patterning (Error 7) wastes energy and creates pollution. Every type-one error, without exception, represents a failure to serve the super-client's interests. This reframing elevates the checklist from a practical quality assurance tool to a statement of ecological ethics. The [[mollison-permaculture-design-philosophy]] positions the designer as an advocate for the super-client within the human design process, ensuring that environmental considerations are not merely included but prioritised. When faced with a difficult design decision, Mollison advises asking: "What does the land want?" -- a question that, if honestly answered, prevents most type-one errors before they occur.
## The Evolution of Design Practice

Mollison's ten type-one errors were formulated in the 1980s and reflect the design challenges most commonly encountered in that era. As permaculture design practice has matured and spread globally, the error landscape has evolved. Contemporary designers face additional challenges that Mollison's original list does not explicitly address: designing for climate uncertainty and extreme weather events, integrating renewable energy systems into productive landscapes, navigating complex regulatory environments that may not accommodate permaculture approaches, and communicating the value of whole-systems design to clients and stakeholders accustomed to reductionist thinking. The [[pdc-design-implementation-and-maintenance]] process in modern permaculture practice has expanded to include monitoring protocols, adaptive management plans, and succession strategies that were not part of Mollison's original framework. Yet the fundamental wisdom of the ten type-one errors remains relevant: any design error, regardless of its specific form, can be traced back to one or more of the underlying failures that Mollison identified -- failures of observation, communication, specificity, ecological awareness, or ethical commitment.
## The Designer's Growth Journey

Mollison acknowledged that avoiding type-one errors is a developmental process, not a one-time achievement. Novice designers are likely to commit several type-one errors in their early work, and the ability to consistently avoid all ten errors comes only with years of practice, honest self-reflection, and feedback from clients and peers. The [[pdc-design-methods-examples]] curriculum builds this development through progressive exercises: students begin with simple design exercises where the errors are easy to avoid, and gradually take on more complex projects where the temptation to commit type-one errors is greater. Mollison encouraged designers to review their past work periodically through the lens of the type-one errors checklist, identifying patterns in their own thinking that predispose them to specific errors. This self-awareness, combined with the systematic use of the checklist during every design project, gradually transforms the ten errors from a list to remember into an internalised design sensibility that operates automatically.
## Client Education and Type-One Error Prevention

Many type-one errors originate not in the designer's failings but in the client's unrealistic expectations or incomplete understanding of permaculture principles. Mollison emphasised that client education is an essential part of the design process and that designers must invest significant time in explaining the rationale behind design decisions, the expected timeline for system maturation, and the ongoing management requirements of the proposed system. A well-educated client is more likely to accept the designer's recommendations, maintain the system as intended, and resist the temptation to make ad hoc modifications that could introduce new errors. The [[mollison-designers-design-client-interview-and-site-assessment-process]] that Mollison describes includes multiple meetings, site walks, and written documentation precisely because this education process cannot be accomplished in a single presentation. Clients who understand why a particular design element has been chosen are far less likely to insist on modifications that would compromise the design's integrity.
## The Relationship Between Type-One and Type-Two Errors

Mollison distinguishes type-one errors (errors in design) from type-two errors (errors in maintenance). While the ten type-one errors occur during the design phase and can theoretically be prevented by thorough design practice, type-two errors occur after installation and result from inadequate maintenance, monitoring, or adaptation to changing conditions. The relationship between the two error types is synergistic: a design that contains type-one errors will be harder to maintain and more prone to type-two errors, while diligent maintenance can partially compensate for design shortcomings. The [[ozier-pdc-plant-guilds-and-polyculture-design]] means that the best designs are those that are easiest to maintain correctly -- designs that work with natural processes rather than against them, that have built-in redundancy and resilience, and that provide clear feedback when something is going wrong. Mollison's most important teaching on this topic is that a design that requires constant heroic effort to maintain is itself a type-one error, regardless of how well it may satisfy all ten checklist items on paper.
## Using the Checklist in Practice

Mollison intended the type-one errors checklist to be used actively during every design project, not merely read once and filed away. The practical application involves reviewing each of the ten errors at key decision points throughout the design process: during the initial client consultation, during site analysis, during the concept design phase, during the detailed design phase, and during the final design review before presenting to the client. At each review point, the designer asks: "Have I committed any of these errors?" If the answer is yes for any item, the design must be revised before proceeding. The [[ozier-pdc-design-process-analysis-frameworks]] in permaculture education builds this habit through repeated practice in design exercises, so that by the time students begin professional practice, the checklist has become an automatic part of their design workflow. Mollison recommended keeping a physical copy of the checklist visible in the design workspace as a constant reminder, and crossing off each item only after it has been explicitly addressed and documented in the design report.
