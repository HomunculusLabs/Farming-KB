# Acidifying Pollutants, Saprotrophic Fungi, Lichens, and Fungal-Faunal Interactions

Beyond their effects on mycorrhizae, acidifying pollutants influence saprotrophic fungal decomposition, lichen community health, phylloplane fungal communities, and the faunal food webs that depend on fungi. This page synthesizes the diverse impacts of atmospheric pollutants on non-mycorrhizal fungal guilds and their ecological interactions.

## Effects on Saprotrophic Fungal Activity

### Sulfite Toxicity to Decomposer Fungi

Part of the effect of sulfur-containing pollutants on fungi comes from the toxic solubility product sulfurous acid (H₂SO₃):
- At soil pH 2–7: bisulfite (HSO₃⁻) is the main toxic product
- At soil pH ≥7: sulfite (SO₃²⁻) predominates (**Dursun et al., 1996a**)

Since sulfur solubility products are used in the brewing and food industries for sterilization, their negative effects on fungi in natural ecosystems under atmospheric deposition are expected. **Dursun et al. (1996a)** and **Boddy et al. (1996)** demonstrated that sulfite at environmentally realistic concentrations (12.5–100 μM) had negative effects on mycelial growth and spore germination of:
- *Mycena galopus*
- *Phoma exigua*
- *Cladosporium cladosporioides*
- *Aureobasidium pullulans*

### Differential Species and Substrate Responses

**Dursun et al. (1996b)** investigated SO₂ fumigation at 40 nl l⁻¹ on fungal respiration across different leaf litter species:

| Fungal Species | Sitka Spruce | Hazel | Scots Pine | Ash |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Mycena galopus* | Significant reduction | Reduction after 10 weeks | Little reduction | Little reduction |
| *Phoma exigua* | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected |
| *C. cladosporioides* | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected |
| *A. pullulans* | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected |

The greatest effect was on Sitka spruce litter decomposition, where respiration of *M. galopus* was reduced by over one-half. This species- and substrate-specificity makes generalizations about pollutant impacts on decomposition extremely difficult.

### SO₂ Effects on Phylloplane Community Structure

**Newsham et al. (1992a,b)** fumigated leaf litter from ash, birch, hazel, oak, and sycamore with 10–30 nl l⁻¹ SO₂ and found:
- **Decreased:** *Cladosporium* spp., *Epicoccum nigrum*, *Fusarium* spp., *Phoma exigua*
- **Increased:** *Coniothyrium quercinum*, *Cylindrocarpon ortosporum*, *Penicillium* spp.
- No change in the leaf litter niches occupied — resource utilization patterns remained similar despite species turnover

### Mesocosm Evidence for Complex Responses

**Shaw (1996)** exposed plants and leaf litter in experimental mesocosms to combined ozone and acid mist (2:1 H₂SO₄:HNO₃). Contrary to expectations of inhibition:
- Metabolically active fungal hyphae (FDA-stained) were significantly greater in acid-misted than control systems
- Stimulation was attributed to nitrogen addition from the acid mist, aiding decomposition of high C:N litter resources

This paradoxical result highlights that the nitrogen component of "acidifying" pollutants can simultaneously inhibit some processes (through acidity) while stimulating others (through nutrient addition).

## Liming as Remediation

### Effects on Fungal Community Composition

**Veerkamp et al. (1997)** studied lignicolous fungal fruit bodies in a limed Scots pine forest on acidic soils:
- Liming increased the total number of fungal species
- The resulting community became more similar to deciduous woodland fungi than coniferous woodland fungi
- **Increased species:** *Amphinema byssoides*, *Hyphodontia breviseta*, *Hypochnicium geogenium*, *Sitotrema octosporum*
- **Decreased species:** *Trechyspora farinacea*

The mechanism: elevated soil pH increases nitrogen availability, stimulating decomposition of the high C:N ratio coniferous wood. This makes the resource quality of coniferous wood more similar to that of angiosperm wood, favoring deciduous-associated fungal species.

### Liming Effects on Mycorrhizal Function

**Antibus & Linkins (1992)** showed that liming reduced acid phosphatase activity of the mycorrhizal community in the forest floor litter layer. This likely reflects a synergistic effect of liming on increasing both N and P availability, reducing phosphatase activity through negative feedback — when more inorganic P is available, fungi produce less phosphatase enzyme.

**Kuyper (1989)** found that:
- Nitrogen addition and liming stimulate saprotrophic fungi and litter decomposition where leaf litter N is low
- The same treatments suppress saprotrophic activity where leaf litter N is already high
- The effect of liming on mycoflora is very similar to that of nitrogen fertilization

## Effects on Fungal-Faunal Interactions

### Nematode Community Responses

**Ruess et al. (1993)** correlated changes in nematode trophic groups to shifts in the bacteria:fungi ratio of forest soils treated with sulfuric, nitric, and oxalic acids. Using the maturity index (**Bongers, 1990**), they showed a shift from bacterial-feeding to fungal-feeding nematodes with acid addition, suggesting that bacteria are more sensitive to soil pH changes than fungi.

In a 3-year acid precipitation experiment on Sitka spruce in Scotland (**Ruess et al., 1996**):
- Soil acidity increased from pH 5.0 to 4.0 in the upper 2 cm
- Fungal biomass (measured by soil ergosterol) and ectomycorrhizal root colonization increased significantly
- This supported elevated nematode populations
- **Decreased:** Omnivorous and predatory nematodes (*Filenchus* spp., *Aporcelaimellus obtusicaudatus*)
- **Increased:** Fungal-feeding nematodes (particularly *Aphelenchoides* spp.)

### Mite Population Responses

**Esher et al. (1992)** showed that in pine forests receiving acid precipitation (pH 4.3 and 3.6):
- Numbers of oribatid, prostigmatid, and astigmatid mites increased with soil acidity
- The shift in the balance between saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal fungi may alter feeding preferences — saprotrophic fungi may be more palatable (**Shaw, 1988, 1992**) or provide higher nutrient levels than mycorrhizal fungi

## Effects on Phylloplane Fungi

### Conifer Needle Surfaces

**Magan et al. (1995)** investigated SO₂ and O₃ effects on the phylloplane fungal community of Scots pine, Sitka spruce, and Norway spruce over 3 years using the open-air fumigation system:
- Low SO₂ levels markedly reduced total phylloplane fungal populations on Sitka spruce needles
- O₃ caused an increase in fungi on Scots pine (measured as FDA-stained hyphae)
- Community changes were slight and differed among tree species, pollutants, and isolation methods (dilution plating vs. direct plating)

Species-level responses on needle surfaces varied:
- **SO₂ effects:** Pink yeasts decreased on Sitka spruce; *Sclerophoma pythiophila* increased
- **O₃ effects:** *Epicoccum nigrum* increased on Scots pine; *Aureobasidium pullulans* and *Rhizosphaera kalkhoffii* increased on Sitka spruce

The hypothesis that pollutants increase fungal colonization by damaging leaf surfaces and exposing more resources received mixed support.

### Cereal Crop Leaves

**Magan & McLeod (1991)** showed more pronounced effects on barley flag leaves:
- Significant reduction in pink and white yeasts under elevated SO₂
- *Cladosporium* spp. (weakly parasitic) increased
- In leaves with less surface wax, SO₂ may increase susceptibility to pathogenic fungal invasion

## Effects on Lichens

Lichens are among the most pollution-sensitive organisms and have long been used as bioindicators of air quality:

### Historical Decline and Recovery

Lichens showed dramatic decline near industrial centers during the industrial revolution, when sulfur dioxide concentrations were highest. Only recently have some lichen communities shown recovery in species abundance and diversity (**Gilbert, 1992; Bates et al., 2001**) as air quality regulations have reduced SO₂ emissions.

### Variable Species Tolerance

**Brown (1996)** reviewed human impacts on lichens, showing that lichen species vary enormously in their tolerance to pollutant loading (**Richardson, 1988**). This differential sensitivity allows lichens to serve as quantitative bioindicators, with community composition reflecting the pollution history of a site.

### Mechanisms of Sensitivity

Lichens lack protective cuticles and absorb nutrients (and pollutants) directly across their entire thallus surface. This makes them particularly vulnerable to:
- SO₂ toxicity (direct sulfite damage to algal photobionts)
- Acid deposition (disruption of pH-dependent physiological processes)
- Heavy metal accumulation (from deposition on bark substrates)

## Aquatic Fungal Communities

The impact of acidifying pollutants on aquatic fungal communities appears relatively minor, probably due to the large dilution factor in water bodies (**Bärlocher, 1992a,b**). However:
- Reduction in water pH from 6 to 4 decreases fungal growth on alder leaves
- The buffering capacity of most water bodies provides some protection, but poorly buffered streams and lakes in sensitive geological areas may experience more significant effects

## Synthesis: No Universal Pattern

The overarching conclusion from studies of acidifying pollutants on non-mycorrhizal fungi is that **there is no consistent trend** in fungal response. Responses depend on:
- **Fungal species identity** and its physiological characteristics
- **Substrate quality** (C:N ratio, lignin content, nutrient status)
- **Pollutant type and concentration** (SO₂ vs. O₃ vs. NOₓ, alone or combined)
- **Host plant species** and its inherent resistance or sensitivity
- **Soil and climate context** (pH buffering capacity, moisture, temperature)
- **Assessment method** (fruit body surveys, root tip analysis, molecular tools, culture-based approaches)

This complexity underscores the need for multi-factor, multi-method approaches when assessing anthropogenic impacts on fungal communities and the ecosystem processes they mediate.
