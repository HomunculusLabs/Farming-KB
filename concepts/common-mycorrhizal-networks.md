## Common Mycorrhizal Networks Common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs), sometimes called mycorrhizal networks or myconets, are [[mycelium-running-mycelium-natures-internet-fungal-network-theory]] to neighboring plants. This has been demonstrated
using isotopic labeling techniques, where carbon-13 or carbon-14 fed to a
"donor" plant is later detected in connected "receiver" plants.

The direction and magnitude of carbon transfer appear to follow source-sink
dynamics: carbon moves preferentially toward plants with higher demand, such as
shaded seedlings growing beneath mature canopy trees. This has led to the
"mother tree" hypothesis, which suggests that mature trees may subsidize the
growth of their offspring through shared mycorrhizal networks. Suzanne
Simard's pioneering work in Douglas-fir forests provided some of the most
compelling evidence for this phenomenon, showing preferential carbon transfer
from older trees to seedlings of the same species.

Nutrient transfer through CMNs includes nitrogen, phosphorus, and water.
Plants with access to different nutrient pools can effectively share resources,
creating a more efficient nutrient economy at the community level. For example,
nitrogen-fixing plants connected to non-fixing neighbors via CMNs may transfer
a portion of their fixed nitrogen to the network. Water transfer through CMNs
has also been documented, potentially helping drought-stressed plants survive
through hydraulic redistribution mediated by fungal hyphae.

## Signaling and Communication

Beyond resource transfer, evidence suggests that CMNs facilitate chemical
signaling between plants. Plants attacked by herbivores or pathogens can
produce [[gadd-fungal-biodiversity-conservation]] and ecosystem resilience.
