# Atonement Sacrifice, Agape, Necromancy, and the Garden of Adonis

## Substitution and Atonement

Christian theology centres on Jesus as the atoning sacrifice who reconciles God
and the world (II Cor 5:18). In the fertility-cult framework Allegro
reconstructs, a state of "sin" or cosmic imbalance arises whenever crops are
plundered from the earth — most critically when the Holy Plant itself is snatched
prematurely from the womb of the ground. This rupture demands reconciliation
through compensatory offerings, and only the god himself can fully atone for
the violation.

Josephus records that the [[allegro-sacred-prostitute-and-the-mushroom-cult]] in the Jerusalem Temple brought
"a replica of the thing itself, hanging from her hand" — a substitute
phallus-shaped fungus offered to the goddess in place of the living plant torn
from the soil. The principle is one of substitution: the god must receive back
something equivalent to what was taken, or the cosmic order remains disrupted.

An Ugaritic hymn from c. 1400 BCE Syria names the appeaser of the violated
furrow '—r—b—d—d, "Furrow-appeaser" (Sumerian *URU_BAD), which corresponds
to the Greek Orobadion — a known mushroom name. The Greek Pithoigia, the
festival of opening the wine-jars, is itself a compound of Sumerian GI_DU
"table of offerings" and IGI "face," yielding "bread of the presence" — the
very Shewbread described in Exodus 25:30.

The atoning gifts listed in cultic contexts are "cakes," "loaves," and
"honeycombs," intended to fill the hole vacated by the Holy Plant. Josephus
further reports that a mandrake's "equivalent" was necessary for safe removal
of the root, echoing the same principle of substitution. The bun-shape of the
mushroom cap was recognised as "loaves"; when dried and skewered on sticks they
became massoth, the unleavened bread of Passover.

I Corinthians 5:7 declares "Christ our paschal lamb has been sacrificed," but
Allegro argues pesakh derives not from p_s_kh "pass over" but from p_s_kh
"appease, quieten." Passover thus commemorates peace after the parturition
agony of the earth — an expression of gratitude combined with ritualistic
atonement for the "rape of the womb."

## Akeldama — "Food of Compensation"

In Matthew 27:3-8 and Acts 1:18-19, Judas Iscariot's death produces the
so-called "Field of Blood" (Akeldama). The narrative elements — thirty pieces
of silver, the potter's field, Judas bursting open — are borrowed from
Zechariah 11:12-13 and Jeremiah 32:6-15, stitched together by the evangelists
to construct a fulfillment scene.

Allegro notes that Judas embodies the arch-māsōr, "betrayer," itself a mushroom
name; the mushroom's function as a powerful purge explains the graphic image
of "bursting open" as lavatorial humour rather than literal description.

Akeldama is conventionally presented as a pseudo-translation of Aramaic khaqal
demā' "field of blood," but Allegro reads it as 'akal dāmē', "food of price,
compensation" — fitting the established pattern of Orobadion ("furrow appeaser"),
"bread of the presence," and the Anthesteria ("offerings of the presence").
The field is not a scene of murder but a site of ritual compensation, where
substitute food-offerings atone for the taking of the sacred fungus.

## Necromancy and Raising the Dead

The Greek Anthesteria was devoted to tending the souls of the departed; the
dead were believed to rise to the upper world during the festival. In Allegro's
reading, the cult of [[allegro-star-of-the-morning-venus-and-the-sacred-mushroom]] is itself a manifestation of necromancy
— "divination by the dead." The mushroom springs from the grave of the god and
carries his revived presence.

The souls who return to the bowels of the earth dwell closer to the "waters of
knowledge," the subterranean abyss from which the mushroom springs. The dead are
not departed but are the source of hidden wisdom, accessible through the
sacred plant.

I Samuel 28:7-14 describes Saul consulting the witch at En-Dor, where Samuel's
ghost appears as an 'Elohim "coming out of the ground," wearing a robe that
ancient versions describe "like an erection" — a phallic mushroom image. The
ghost rises from the earth itself, like the fungus pushing through soil.

The Sumerian NAM-TAR, "plague demon," passes into Greek as Nektar, the divine
drink of the gods. Lilith, the "night-hag" of Isaiah 34:14, is probably another
mushroom word — a chthonic female spirit associated with the underworld and
with the mushroom that grows in darkness.

The Rephaim, described as those "cast down from heaven," correspond to the
fallen angels of Genesis 6 who, according to Enoch, "taught them charms,
cutting of roots, acquaintance with plants." Isaiah 26:19 promises "Thy dead
shall live…on the land of the Shades (Rephaim) thou wilt let dew fall" — the
dew of light associated with Venus, the Morning Star, whose cult is inseparable
from the mushroom.

## The Agape Feast

The New Testament "Agape" or Love Feast (Jude v.12; II Peter 2:13) was
connected by Syriac translators with rites of comforting the dead. The verb
agapaō was used by tragedians for affection shown toward the dead; it derives
from Sumerian AG_AG "love" combined with "stretch, measure" (corresponding to
Semitic m-sh-kh).

Related terms reinforce the funerary context: nekragogos, "leading forth the
dead," and psukhagogeo, "to conjure up souls from the nether world." The Agape
feast is fundamentally a meal shared with the dead — a communion between the
living and the departed god.

If Agape is itself a mushroom name, then the feast consists of eating the
mushroom's flesh and drinking its juice — identical to the "Lord's Supper" and
the consumption of the "crucified Christ." As Paul writes in Galatians 2:20:
"I have been crucified with Christ; I live, yet it is no longer I, but Christ
lives in me" — the mushroom-eater becomes one with the god who dies and rises
again.

## Speaking in Tongues

Necromancers and ventriloquists — literally "belly-speakers" — produced what
Isaiah 8:19 calls "chirping and muttering" when consulting the dead. The
practice was widespread in [[allegro-fertility-philosophy-of-the-ancient-near-east]] East as a means of channelling
voices from the underworld.

The New Testament phenomenon of "speaking with tongues" (I Corinthians 14:1-23)
describes how "one speaking in a tongue utters mysteries in the Spirit."
Outsiders witnessing the practice asked, "will they not say you are mad?"
(I Cor 14:23), while at Pentecost onlookers mocked the apostles as "filled with
new wine" (Acts 2:13) — the mushroom's effects mistaken for drunkenness, its
divine speech for madness. The connection between ecstatic speech, fungal
intoxication, and communication with the dead runs throughout Allegro's
reconstruction.

## The Garden of Adonis

Ezekiel's vision of women bewailing Tammuz at the north gate of the Temple
(Ezekiel 8:14) connects Jerusalem worship with the dying-and-rising god of
Near Eastern fertility religion. The famous "Gardens of Adonis" — shallow trays
of [[solomon-sprouting-seeds-and-early-season-nutrition]] that wither quickly — represent, in Allegro's interpretation,
the raising of the fungus itself: lamenting and simultaneously raising the dead
god.

Allegro claims to be the first to decipher the names involved and to draw
together the scattered references to [[mckenna-food-gods-minoan-crete-mushroom-religion]] in this context. The
worship encompassed every aspect of nature — it was not metaphor or symbol but
a life-or-death matter for the ancients.

The mushroom god's fate is summarised in a single image: "In the morning it
appeared, and by nightfall the worms had consumed it." This transient deity,
born from the earth at dawn and devoured by dusk, is the original of every
crucified and resurrected saviour in Western religion.
