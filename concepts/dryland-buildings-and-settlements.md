# Dryland Buildings and Settlements

Dryland [[permaculture]] settlements require specialized building and shelter strategies that respond to extreme temperatures, water scarcity, high evaporation, dust, and often salinity problems. Traditional and experimental approaches from arid regions worldwide demonstrate that underground, earth-sheltered, and wind-protected structures can achieve remarkable comfort with minimal energy input.

## Underground and Earth-Sheltered Structures

Underground architecture is one of the most effective strategies for dryland habitation. In Fresno, California, underground orchards have been hand-dug into open shaded pits containing fig, grape, citrus, and pomegranate trees. Looking up from underground, the sky is covered by the tree canopy; the roots in the pits remain cool and are easily mulched. The ground surface above can be funnel-shaped to catch water and direct it to the plant containers. One such house covers 2.8 hectares and was entirely hand-dug by M. Forestieri.

In Greenock, South Australia, traditional buried dairy sheds keep milk and food cool, with vine, earth, or tree shade assisting the cooling process. In the Canary Islands, a deep mulch (0.3 m) of volcanic cinder technique produces fruit, potatoes, and other crops in areas receiving mainly night fog and condensation — demonstrating that even underground structures can support productive agriculture.

## Passive Cooling and Shelter Screens

At St. Theresa Mission in Central Australia, house verandahs are screened with climbing plants such as Bryonia (a cucurbit), Lab-lab bean, and tomato vines. These living screens cool west and sunward walls and are sustained by waste wash-water and grey water. At Village Homes in Davis, California, earth-sheltered houses with solar hot water, turf roofs, and slab floors provide excellent comfort control. The roof of one such house uses ice plants and herbs to provide active cooling through transpiration and shade.

The Windstar Foundation's experimental "Biodome" near Old Snowmass, Colorado (7,500 feet elevation) uses solar electricity, heating ponds, and a domed greenhouse to grow tender crops year-round in a high, cold, dryland where the growing season averages only 60 days. Frogs and finches assist with pest control inside the greenhouse, and tilapia and hydroponic vegetables are grown in the ponds — an integrated system within a harsh climate envelope.

## Desert Tree Species for Settlements

Several desert tree species play critical roles in dryland settlement design. Opuntia (prickly pear) in the Canary Islands provides fruit (tuna), vegetable food, cattle forage, hedge material, and hosts the cochineal scale insect used locally for food dye. Many desert [[acacia]] species provide abundant poultry seed, as do Lycium and Solanum species. Gidgee (Acacia cambagei) creates desert mounds (nebkas) about 1.5 m high and 12 m across — sessile or weeping trees in deserts cause dust accretion in this way, providing deep absorbent soils and mulch for vines, other trees, and burrowing animals. Acacia species in the Kalahari perform the same function.

Ocotillo (Fouquieria splendens) near Tucson, Arizona, serves as a living spiny hedge around traditional Papago gardens. It can be cultivated for fencing, live-set to grow gated, and is widely used as reinforcement or base for mud wall construction — a genuinely useful crop plant for desert structures.

## Salting and Interceptor Banks

Deforestation, grazing, and wheat cultivation in dryland areas produce widespread soil salting, visible as collapsed soil at seepage lines. The sea barley grass (Hordeum maritimum) is an indicator species of soil collapse from salinization. Once-forested marshes in wheatlands can become complete saltpans.

Interceptor banks are the primary remediation tool. Farmers develop these banks to run surface water and throughflow off fields to natural valleys, isolating soil blocks for rain leaching and preventing a "cascade" effect of salty overland flow or throughflow moving downhill. A classic interceptor bank ends on the streamline as marked by distant trees. Construction involves a bulldozer ramming subsoil on the downhill wall, with dimensions of approximately 1.5 m depth and 4 m width, spaced no more than 100 m apart (or 1 m vertical separation on slopes). Soil pits below properly constructed interceptor banks show no throughflow and no rising salted groundwater.

Spike rush (Juncus) is an indicator species of imminent soil collapse, surface flooding, and anaerobic soils. Where interceptors have been properly installed, rushes grow on the upslope side, indicating successful flooding prevention that protects downslope crops. Series of interceptor banks in wheatfields can isolate entire soil blocks from overland flow and salting effects.

## Extensive Discing for Dust Control

In Alice Springs, Central Australia, extensive discing over 600 square kilometers has significantly reduced dust storms around the town. This large-scale earthworking demonstrates that even broad landscape management can improve settlement conditions in arid environments.

## Key Principles for Dryland Settlement

- **Use earth as insulation**: Underground and earth-sheltered structures leverage the thermal mass of soil for natural temperature regulation
- **Maximize water capture**: Funnel-shaped surfaces, swales, and mulched pits direct every available drop of moisture to plants
- **Use living screens**: Climbing plants on walls provide evaporative cooling and food production simultaneously
- **Select multi-purpose desert species**: Opuntia, Acacia, and Ocotillo serve as food, forage, fencing, and windbreak simultaneously
- **Monitor indicator species**: Sea barley grass and spike rush signal salinity problems before catastrophic soil collapse
- **Install interceptor banks early**: Prevent the cascade of salt-laden water downhill before it destroys agricultural land
- **Integrate livestock into structures**: Chickens, frogs, and fish within greenhouse structures create pest control and nutrient cycling
