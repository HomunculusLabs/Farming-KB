# Trichocereus Mescaline Cacti: San Pedro and Peruvian Torch

## Overview

Trichocereus cacti are mescaline-containing columnar cacti native to the Andes Mountains of South America.
The two most commonly encountered species are [[trichocereus-pachanoi]] (San Pedro) and [[trichocereus-peruvianus]] (Peruvian torch).
Although not indigenous to the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], these cacti are extremely common ornamental plants sold in garden shops across the country.
Their widespread availability as decorative plants, combined with significant [[gottlieb-peyote-botany-and-mescaline-content]],
makes them one of the most accessible naturally occurring hallucinogenic plant sources in America.
Many [[solomon-garden-centers-seedling-traps]] sell them without any awareness of their psychoactive properties.

## Botanical Description

Trichocereus pachanoi is a fast-growing, columnar cactus that can reach heights of 3-6 meters (10-20 feet) under ideal conditions.
It is bluish-green in color, with 4-8 ribs and small white areoles from which spines emerge.
The spines are typically short, ranging from yellowish-brown to brown, and are fewer in number compared to many other columnar cacti.
The plant produces large, fragrant white flowers that open at night.

Trichocereus peruvianus is similar in appearance but tends to have longer, more prominent spines and a darker green coloration.
It can grow even taller than T. pachanoi under optimal conditions, sometimes reaching 6 meters or more.
Both species are relatively easy to cultivate, tolerating a wide range of conditions and growing rapidly compared to many other cactus species.

## Traditional Use in South America

Trichocereus cacti have a long history of traditional use in the Andean region,
particularly in Peru, Ecuador, and Bolivia.
Archaeological evidence suggests that San Pedro has been used in shamanic ceremonies for over 3,000 years.
The cactus is known by several indigenous names, including "Huachuma" and "Achuma,"
and is considered a sacred teacher plant in Andean traditional medicine.

Pre-Columbian cultures including the Chavin (circa 900-200 BCE)
depicted the cactus in their art and architecture,
suggesting its central role in religious and [[blesching-cannabis-healing-practices-setting-intention-affirmations]].
Stone carvings at Chavin de Huantar show figures holding San Pedro cacti,
and the cactus iconography appears on textiles, ceramics, and monolithic stones throughout the region.

[[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] typically involves slicing the cactus into sections,
removing the spines, and boiling the flesh for extended periods to produce a concentrated tea.
The resulting brew, sometimes called "cimora" when combined with other admixture plants,
is consumed in ceremonial contexts for healing, divination, and spiritual purposes.
The ceremonies are typically led by a curandero (healer)
and involve chanting, prayers, and often an all-night vigil.

## Mescaline Content and Alkaloid Profile

The principal psychoactive compound in Trichocereus cacti is mescaline
(3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine), though these cacti contain numerous other alkaloids as well.
Mescaline content is highly variable between individual plants, populations, and growing conditions.

Trichocereus pachanoi has been found to contain anywhere from 0.33% to 2.375% mescaline by dry weight.
The wide range reflects genetic variation, growing conditions, age of the plant, and the part analyzed.
Crosby and McLaughlin (1973) reported 0.33%, while Helmlin and Brenneisen (1992) found up to 2.375%.

Trichocereus peruvianus has shown even more variable results,
with mescaline content ranging from 0% to 0.82% by dry weight.
Agurell (1969) found no detectable mescaline in Peruvian torch specimens,
while Pardanani et al. (1977) confirmed its presence at up to 0.82%.

### Comparison with Peyote

Peyote ([[lophophora-williamsii]]) contains approximately 1.5% mescaline by dry weight,
which falls within the range reported for Trichocereus pachanoi.
However, peyote contains over 60 identified alkaloids compared to roughly 30 in San Pedro,
and the overall alkaloid profile differs substantially.
Many users report qualitative differences in the experience between peyote and Trichocereus preparations,
which may be attributable to these different accompanying alkaloids.

## Preparation Methods

### Traditional Boiling Method

The most common preparation involves slicing the cactus into disks or strips
(after removing spines and the waxy outer skin),
then boiling in water for several hours.
The liquid is reduced and strained through multiple boilings to concentrate the active material.
This produces a bitter, nausea-inducing tea.
Because of the large volume of plant material required,
emesis is even more likely with Trichocereus preparations than with peyote.

### Dried Powder Method

The cactus flesh can be sliced thin and dried, then ground into a powder.
This powder can be encapsulated or mixed with liquid for consumption.
Drying concentrates the active material by removing water weight,
making it easier to consume the required dose.

### Chemical Extraction

Chemical [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-spore-extraction-methods]] can isolate mescaline from the plant material,
producing a relatively pure crystalline substance through acid-base extraction.
