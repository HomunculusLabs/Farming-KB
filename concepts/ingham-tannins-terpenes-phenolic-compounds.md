# Tannins, Terpenes, and Phenolic Compounds in Soil Systems

## Plant Defense Chemistry

All woody material contains some level of hard-to-break-down
material. These compounds represent the plant's effort to protect
itself against pathogens and herbivores. Tannins, terpenes, and
phenolic compounds are secondary metabolites that serve as chemical
defenses. They make plant material resistant to decomposition and
can inhibit microbial growth, which is precisely why plants produce
them.

## Black Walnut and Juglone

Black walnut produces a particularly effective terpene called
juglone, which is allelopathic to many other plant species. This
compound inhibits the growth of competing vegetation near walnut
trees. The terpene is present in walnut leaves, hulls, roots, and
wood chips. When these materials are used in compost or as mulch,
the terpene must be managed to avoid damage to sensitive plants.

## Volatilization as a Management Strategy

When working with woody materials high in terpenes or phenols, you
have choices for management. One approach is to pile the chipped
material and let the terpene or phenol volatize over time. This
process takes about four weeks or possibly longer. Turning the pile
helps speed the volatilization process by exposing more surface
area to air. The volatile compounds evaporate into the atmosphere,
reducing their concentration in the material.

## Biological Decomposition Approach

The alternative to volatilization is biological decomposition.
Apply a tea with good fungi in it and use material from an old wood
chip pile to enhance the fungal community. The fungi and bacteria
can chew up the plant defense compounds over time. However, this
approach requires that the right organisms are present and active.
Fungal-dominated teas are particularly useful for this purpose
because fungi are better adapted to breaking down complex woody
compounds than bacteria alone.

## Weed Suppression Potential

Terpenes and phenolic compounds may also provide weed suppressant
effects that could be desirable in certain situations. If you place
materials like black walnut chips in some areas but not others, you
can assess the impact of the walnut terpenes on weed species. This
represents an area where more research is needed, as there is little
scientific literature assessing the impact of black walnut compounds
specifically on weed species.

## Tannins in Composting

Tannins are polyphenolic compounds found in many plant species,
particularly in oak leaves, pine needles, and certain legumes. They
bind proteins and can make nitrogen less available to microorganisms
during the initial stages of composting. This is one reason why
materials high in tannins decompose slowly. Over time, as the
microbial community adapts and tannin-degrading organisms multiply,
decomposition proceeds normally.

## Impact on Soil Biology

When fresh materials containing high levels of these compounds are
added to soil, they can temporarily suppress microbial activity.
This is why fresh wood chips are often recommended as a mulch
rather than being incorporated into the soil. As a surface mulch,
the compounds volatize and break down gradually without directly
contacting the [[soil-food-web]]. If incorporated, they can tie up
nitrogen and slow the decomposition process significantly.

## Compost Tea Interactions

When compost tea is brewed using compost that contains residues of
these plant defense compounds, the tea may carry some of these
materials along. In most cases, the concentrations are low enough
that they do not cause problems for the organisms in the tea or for
the plants receiving the application. However, using compost made
from materials that have been properly aged allows these compounds
to break down before they enter the tea system.

## Practical Recommendations

For woody materials with high terpene or phenol content: age the
material for at least four weeks before using in compost or as
mulch, turn piles regularly to promote volatilization, or inoculate
with fungal-dominated compost tea to accelerate biological
breakdown. Avoid using fresh black walnut material around sensitive
plants. Consider the potential weed suppression benefits as a
secondary benefit when using these materials intentionally.

## Phenolic Compounds in Tea Brewing

When compost containing residual phenolic compounds is used for tea
brewing, trace amounts of these compounds may be present in the tea.
In most cases, the concentrations are low enough that they do not
harm the organisms in the tea. However, if the compost is made from
fresh materials that have not been adequately aged, the phenolic
content may be high enough to suppress microbial activity in the
brew. Using well-cured compost that has been through a complete
composting process ensures that most phenolic compounds have been
broken down or volatized before they enter the tea system.

## Tannins and Nitrogen Immobilization

Tannins bind proteins through a process called protein precipitation.
In compost piles with high tannin content, this can temporarily
immobilize nitrogen by making it unavailable to microorganisms.
The nitrogen is not lost but is temporarily locked in tannin-protein
complexes. Over time, as tannin-degrading fungi and bacteria
multiply, these complexes are broken down and the nitrogen becomes
available again. This is why compost piles high in oak leaves or
pine needles may take longer to heat up initially.

## Terpenes as Fungal Foods

While terpenes can be inhibitory at high concentrations, at lower
levels they may serve as carbon sources for certain fungi. Some
fungal species have evolved the ability to metabolize terpenes and
other plant defense compounds. This is one reason why fungal-
dominated compost teas are recommended for working with woody
materials: the fungi in the tea can include species capable of
breaking down the very compounds that make the wood resistant to
decomposition. The biological approach works with nature's own
decomposition pathways.
