### Identifying OP vs Hybrid
- Seed catalogs should clearly label OP and F1 hybrid varieties
- "Heirloom" usually (but not always) indicates open-pollinated
- If seed packet says "do not save seed" — it is likely a hybrid
- Purchase from seed companies that specialize in OP varieties

## Seed Saving by Plant Family

### Dry Seed Processing (Easy)
Plants where seeds dry on the plant and require minimal processing:
- **Legumes**: beans, peas, lentils — let pods dry completely on plant
- **Grains**: corn, wheat, oats, amaranth — let seed heads dry
- **Alliums**: onions, leeks — allow umbels to dry on stalk
- **Brassicas**: kale, radish, mustard — let pods dry and shatter

### Wet Seed Processing (Moderate)
Plants where seeds are enclosed in fleshy fruit:
- **Solanaceae**: tomatoes, peppers, eggplant — ferment tomato seeds to remove gel coating
- **Cucurbits**: squash, cucumber, melon — rinse seeds, dry thoroughly
- **Nightshades**: tomatillos, ground cherries

### Fermentation for Tomato Seeds
- Squeeze seeds and gel into a jar with water
- Ferment 3-5 days until mold forms on surface
- Viable seeds sink; pour off floating debris and mold
- Rinse seeds, spread on paper towel, dry completely (2-3 weeks)

## Regional Adaptation

Seeds adapt to local conditions over generations through natural selection:

### How Adaptation Works
- Plants that thrive in your climate produce more viable seed
- Each saved generation shifts the population toward local fitness
- After 3-5 generations, noticeable adaptation to local conditions
- Includes adaptation to: day length, temperature range, soil type, pest pressure, rainfall patterns

### Strategies for Accelerating Adaptation
- Save seed from the best-performing plants (not just the earliest or largest)
- Save from multiple plants to maintain genetic diversity (minimum 20 plants for outcrossing species)
- Rogue out (remove) plants that are diseased, stunted, or off-type
- Maintain a population size large enough to avoid inbreeding depression

## Seed Sovereignty

Seed sovereignty is the right of communities to control their own seed supply:

### Why It Matters
- Consolidation of seed industry: 4 companies control 60%+ of global seed market
- Intellectual property on seeds (patents, plant variety protection) restricts saving
- Loss of crop genetic diversity reduces food system resilience
- Corporate seed breeding prioritizes transportability and shelf life over nutrition and taste

### Practical Seed Sovereignty
- Grow and save open-pollinated varieties
- Support regional seed companies rather than multinational corporations
- Participate in seed swaps and exchanges
- Learn to breed for local conditions
- Maintain diverse varieties, not just "the best" one

## Community Seed Banks

Community seed banks are shared repositories of locally adapted seeds:

### Organizational Models
- **Seed library** — seeds are "borrowed," grown, and returned (with interest)
- **Community seed bank** — seeds are stored centrally and distributed to members
- **Seed swap events** — periodic gatherings where gardeners exchange saved seeds
- **Online seed networks** — regional and national networks for seed exchange

### Seed Library Model
- Physical collection housed in a public library, community center, or coop
- Gardeners "check out" seed packets for growing
- At season's end, return saved seeds to replenish the library
- Include educational materials on seed saving techniques
- Track varieties and their local performance
- Common challenges: maintaining seed viability, genetic drift, volunteer management

### Best Practices for Community Seed Systems
- Document variety names, source, year saved, and growing notes
- Store seeds in cool, dry, dark conditions (refrigerator or freezer for long-term)
- Conduct periodic germination tests
- Maintain a backup collection in a separate location
- Include simple seed-saving instructions with each variety

## Seed Storage

### Optimal Conditions
- Temperature: 40°F (5°C) or below; each 10°F reduction doubles storage life
- Humidity: below 50% relative humidity
- Darkness: UV light degrades seed viability
- Moisture content: 5-8% for most vegetable seeds

### Storage Methods
- Paper envelopes in airtight glass jars with desiccant packets
- Freezer storage for long-term (5+ years) with proper drying
- Label every packet with variety, year, and source
- Silica gel or powdered milk as desiccant in storage containers

### Viability by Species
- **Short-lived (1-3 years)**: onion, parsnip, leek, corn, spinach
- **Medium-lived (3-5 years)**: pepper, carrot, peas, beans, cabbage
- **Long-lived (5-10+ years)**: tomato, cucumber, melon, radish, squash
