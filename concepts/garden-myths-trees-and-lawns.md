# Garden Myths About Trees and Lawns

Trees and lawns are the dominant elements in most landscapes, and both are surrounded by persistent myths that affect how they are planted, maintained, and managed. These myths can lead to unnecessary expense, wasted resources, and poor plant health outcomes.

## Myth: Trees Have Deep Taproots That Reach Deep Groundwater

The image of a tree with a massive taproot reaching deep into the earth is one of the most persistent misconceptions in horticulture.

### What the Evidence Shows

- **Most tree roots are shallow**: The vast majority of tree roots (80-90%) are in the top 18-24 inches of soil. This is where oxygen, nutrients, and moisture are most available
- **Taproots are rare in mature trees**: While many tree seedlings produce a taproot, most trees lose their taproot as they mature. The taproot is replaced by a spreading lateral root system that is far more effective at gathering resources
- **The "root plate" model**: Trees are better understood as being held in place by a broad, relatively shallow root plate -- a pancake of roots spreading outward from the trunk, typically extending well beyond the drip line
- **Root spread is extensive**: Tree roots commonly extend 2-3 times the diameter of the crown (drip line). In some cases, roots extend 4-7 times the crown radius
- **Implications for management**:
  - Trenching or excavation within the drip line can sever major roots, destabilizing the tree
  - Soil compaction over the root zone (from construction, parking, foot traffic) damages the feeding roots that are concentrated near the surface
  - Mulch (not turf) under trees protects the shallow root system
  - Irrigation should be applied broadly under the canopy, not just at the trunk

## Myth: Trees Automatically Send Roots Deep to Find Water During Drought

A related myth is that during drought, trees simply grow deeper roots to access groundwater. This is not how trees respond to water stress.

### What Actually Happens

- **Trees cannot grow new deep roots quickly enough** to respond to drought. Root growth is slow and depends on the tree's current energy reserves, which are depleted during drought stress
- **Drought response is primarily physiological**: Trees close stomata to reduce water loss, shed leaves, and allocate resources to survival rather than growth. Root growth actually decreases during severe drought
- **Shallow roots are the first to die**: During drought, the fine feeder roots in the upper soil profile are the first to desiccate and die, reducing the tree's capacity to absorb water when it does become available
- **Recovery takes time**: After drought, trees must regrow their root systems before they can fully recover canopy function. This is why drought-stressed trees may take years to regain full vigor

### Implications for Tree Care During Drought

- **Proactive watering** is essential: Do not wait for visible stress symptoms, which indicate that damage has already occurred
- **Water broadly and slowly** under the entire canopy to reach the root plate
- **Mulch** to conserve soil moisture and protect feeder roots
- **Avoid fertilization** during drought: Fertilizer stimulates canopy growth that the compromised root system cannot support
- **Prioritize young trees**: Established mature trees have larger root systems and greater drought tolerance than recently planted trees

## Myth: Grass Clippings Cause Thatch Buildup

Thatch is a layer of partially decomposed organic matter between the soil surface and the green grass blades. Many gardeners believe that leaving grass clippings on the lawn causes thatch to build up to harmful levels.

### What the Evidence Shows

- **Grass clippings decompose rapidly**: Fresh grass clippings are approximately 80-90% water and contain readily decomposable nitrogen. They break down quickly and contribute to soil organic matter without building thatch
- **Thatch is primarily caused by**: (1) Excessive use of nitrogen fertilizer that stimulates rapid shoot growth; (2) Overuse of fungicides and insecticidesdecompositions the soil organisms that decompose thatch; (3) Growing grass species with high lignin content (like Bermuda grass and zoysia) that decompose slowly; (4) Compacted or poorly drained soils where decomposition is slow; (5) Infrequent mowing that leaves long clippings that mat
- **Returning clippings is beneficial**: Grasscycling (leaving clippings on the lawn) returns nitrogen and organic matter to the soil, reducing fertilizer needs by 25-30% and improving soil health
- **When clippings ARE a problem**: If clippings are long (more than 1 inch), wet, and left in thick mats, they can smother the grass beneath. This is a mowing frequency problem, not a fundamental problem with returning clippings

### Best Practice

Mow frequently enough that no more than one-third of the grass blade height is removed at each mowing. This produces short clippings that decompose quickly and return nutrients to the soil. Use a mulching mower that recuts clippings into even smaller pieces for faster decomposition.

## Myth: Lawns Require Constant Watering, Fertilizing, and Chemical Treatment

The conventional American lawn maintenance regime (weekly mowing, frequent irrigation, regular fertilizer applications, and preventive pesticide treatments) is often presented as necessary for a healthy lawn. This is not true for most lawn situations.

### What a Reasonable Lawn Needs

- **Appropriate species selection**: Choosing grass species adapted to local climate, soil, and light conditions is the single most important factor in lawn health. Cool-season grasses (Kentucky bluegrass, fescue) for northern regions; warm-season grasses (Bermuda, zoysia, buffalo grass) for southern regions. Buffalo grass and fine fescues require dramatically less water and fertilizer than Kentucky bluegrass
- **Mow high**: Set mowing height at 3-4 inches (for cool-season grasses) or as high as the species allows. Taller grass develops deeper roots, shades out weeds, and retains soil moisture
- **Water deeply and infrequently**: This encourages deep root growth and drought tolerance. Most lawns need 1-1.5 inches of water per week during active growth, applied in 1-2 deep waterings rather than daily light sprinkling
- **Fertilize based on soil test**: Most established lawns need nitrogen fertilizer 1-2 times per year (fall for cool-season grasses, late spring for warm-season grasses). Excessive fertilizer promotes thatch, disease, and rapid growth that requires more mowing
- **Accept some weeds and imperfection**: A lawn that is 90% grass and 10% clover and dandelions is still a functional lawn. Clover fixes nitrogen, reducing fertilizer needs. A few weeds do not constitute a crisis requiring chemical intervention

### The Environmental Case for Reduced Lawn

- **Lawns are the single largest irrigated crop in the United States**: Approximately 40 million acres of lawn
- **Pesticide and fertilizer runoff** from lawns is a significant source of water pollution
- **Gas-powered lawn equipment** produces approximately 5% of US air pollution
- **Consider alternatives**: Where appropriate, replace portions of lawn with native meadow, ground covers, food gardens, or low-maintenance landscape plantings

## Myth: Newly Planted Trees Need to Be Staked

Staking is so common that many people assume it is always necessary. In fact, unnecessary staking can harm tree development.

### When Staking Is NOT Needed

- **Most landscape trees do not need staking**: Trees establish stronger trunks and root systems when allowed to move freely in the wind. Wind movement stimulates trunk caliper growth and triggers root development
- **The risks of staking**:
  - Girdling from ties that are not removed or loosened as the trunk grows
  - Reduced trunk taper (staked trees grow taller but with thinner, weaker trunks)
  - Delayed root development (the tree does not "feel" the need for anchorage)
  - Reduced canopy movement reduces wind acclimation, making the tree more vulnerable when stakes are eventually removed

### When Staking IS Appropriate

- **Bare-root trees** with insufficient root ball to stand on their own
- **Top-heavy trees** in very windy, exposed sites
- **Trees with very flexible trunks** that cannot support their canopy
- **Vandalism-prone sites** where the tree needs physical protection

### Correct Staking Practice

If staking is necessary:
- Use flexible ties that allow trunk movement
- Place stakes low (6-12 inches above ground) to allow the upper trunk and canopy to move freely
- Remove stakes after one growing season (maximum two seasons)
- Never leave staking materials in place permanently -- they will girdle the tree

## Myth: Tree Roots and Lawns Are Naturally Compatible

The common landscape pattern of grass growing right up to the trunk of a tree is actually detrimental to both the tree and the lawn.

### The Conflict

- **Grass is a fierce competitor for water and nutrients**: Grass roots are dense and efficient, competing directly with tree feeder roots in the upper soil profile
- **Mowing equipment damages trees**: String trimmers and lawnmowers damage tree bark, creating wounds that can lead to decay, canker diseases, and girdling
- **Lawn irrigation is inappropriate for trees**: Frequent shallow watering (good for grass) encourages shallow tree roots, making the tree more drought-vulnerable
- **Lawn chemicals affect trees**: Herbicides, fungicides, and insecticides applied to lawns can affect tree roots growing in the same soil zone

### The Better Approach

- **Create a mulched root zone**: Extend a ring of organic mulch (arborist wood chips, 2-4 inches deep) from the trunk to the drip line (or as far as practical). Keep mulch pulled back from the trunk
- **Underplant with shade-tolerant species**: Where a full mulch ring is not desired, plant shade-tolerant ground covers that are less competitive than turf grass
- **Separate irrigation zones**: Water trees deeply and infrequently; water lawn separately with its own schedule
- **Protect trunks**: Install physical barriers (tree guards, mulch rings) to prevent equipment damage

## Myth: Fertilizing Stressed or Declining Trees Will Help Them Recover

When a tree appears stressed (yellowing leaves, dieback, sparse canopy), a common response is to apply fertilizer. This can make the problem worse.

### Why Fertilizing Can Harm Stressed Trees

- **Fertilizer stimulates growth, not recovery**: If a tree is stressed from drought, root damage, compacted soil, or disease, adding nitrogen fertilizer stimulates the canopy to grow without addressing the underlying cause. The tree expends limited energy reserves on new foliage it cannot support
- **Root stress means inability to take up nutrients**: Compacted, waterlogged, or drought-stressed roots cannot efficiently absorb nutrients. Applied fertilizer may simply accumulate in the soil, potentially causing salt damage
- **Diagnostic first**: Before fertilizing, diagnose the actual cause of stress. A soil test, root inspection, and site assessment will reveal whether nutrient deficiency is actually the problem (it rarely is)

### When Fertilization IS Appropriate

- **Confirmed nutrient deficiency** identified through soil and/or foliar testing
- **Young trees** during their establishment phase
