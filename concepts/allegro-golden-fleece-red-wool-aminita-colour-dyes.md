# Allegro: Golden Fleece, Red Wool, and Amanita Colour Dyes

The distinctive red-and-white colouration of the *[[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]* mushroom was so striking to ancient peoples that it became embedded in dye terminology, mythological imagery, and colour-related epithets across multiple cultures. [[john-allegro]] argues that the mushroom's vivid red cap, flecked with white spots, served as the prototype for red dyes, golden fleeces, and numerous personal and ethnic names throughout [[allegro-fertility-philosophy-of-the-ancient-near-east]] East and Mediterranean.

## Dioscouroi Quelling Storms

The closing section of the "Star of the Morning" chapter links the Dioscouroi—the divine twins Castor and Pollux—to the mushroom's power over storms. In Greek tradition the Dioscouroi were celebrated as saviours of sailors, appearing as St Elmo's fire atop masts during tempests. The New Testament preserves this association directly: in Acts 28:11, Paul boards a ship "whose figurehead was the Dioscouroi," underscoring that the Christian narrative inherits this ancient storm-quelling symbolism.

The magical principle at work is *like repels like*: the sacred fungus was regarded as the very source of the "Sons of Thunder" (Boanerges), and therefore could repel the tempest it itself engendered. Pliny records the folk belief that menstrual fluid drives away hail and whirlwinds. In [[allegro-death-and-resurrection-in-the-mushroom-cult]], the Amanita was thought to receive the "[[allegro-menstrual-blood-creative-process-purple-fertility]]" of the cedar tree—the reddish resin or exudate that stained its stem and volva—thereby inheriting the power to still storms through sympathetic magic.

## Jonah and Jesus Storm Stories

Two of the most famous storm narratives in scripture encode the same mushroom motif. In Jonah 1:4–15, the prophet sleeps in the hold of a ship while a great storm rages. The sailors cast lots, determine Jonah is the cause, and throw him overboard, whereupon the sea immediately grows calm. In Mark 4:35–41, Jesus is asleep on a cushion in the stern when a fierce gale threatens to swamp the boat. He wakes, rebukes the wind and sea, saying "Peace! Be still!" (Greek: *siōpa, phimōthē*), and a great calm follows.

Both stories are allegories of the mushroom's ability to quieten storms. Jonah's name itself contains a pun: it plays on the Semitic *BAR_USh_TAR_IAU_NA*, yielding the Hebrew bar-setārā'-yōnā', "Jonah—son-of-hiding," referencing the mushroom's hidden, subterranean growth. The very phrase "silencing the storm" is traced to a Sumerian wordplay on *\*MASh_BA(LA)G*, which gives the Hebrew root sh-b-kh, "to pacify" (as in Psalm 89:9, where God "stills the raging of the sea"). Even the name Galilee derives from *\*GI-LI-LI(LI-LI-GI)*, "reed with two cones," whose root g-l-l yields the Hebrew word for "waves." The mushroom was thus understood as a being of two worlds—the celestial and the chthonic—a child of Venus who quelled storms by its very nature.

## The Golden Fleece

The red top of the Amanita, flecked with white warts, was the original model for the celebrated Golden Fleece of Greek myth. In [[allegro-plants-and-drugs-in-the-ancient-world]] "golden" frequently meant the red gold that predominated before refined yellow gold became common, so a "golden fleece" was in fact a red woollen cloak. The myth tells how Jason and the Argonauts sailed to Colchis to recover the fleece of a miraculous ram. This ram could fly and speak; it had carried Phrixus and his sister Helle to safety, though Helle fell into the sea (the Hellespont). King Aeetes hung the fleece in a sacred grove and set a dragon to guard it. The Argo itself sailed with the Dioscouroi aboard, and the sorceress Medea assisted Jason in obtaining the prize—only to later destroy his new bride with a robe of fire, a detail recalling the burning, consuming quality of the red-cap itself.

## Red Wool and the Name "Thracian"

The Sumerian word DARA means both "ram" and "red dyed hair," linking wool, colour, and animal from the earliest recorded period. The Greek word thrix ("hair") is derived from the Semitic *\*tra_igs*, "red hair." From this root comes Thraikos, "Thracian," literally meaning "red-headed people." Dionysus, the mushroom god par excellence, was a Thracian deity, and his ecstatic female followers the Maenads were also called Threiciae. In Allegro's reading, "Thracian" becomes a coded epithet for the red-cloaked Amanita muscaria. The Jewish historian Josephus records that Alexander Jannaeus crucified eight hundred Pharisees and was called "the Thracian" for the cruelty of the spectacle—a name that may carry this older fungal symbolism. Even the mandrake plant was called "Thracian" by Dioscorides, further evidence that the term denoted a red-coloured, pharmaceutically potent plant.

## Jacob and Esau

The Genesis narrative of Jacob and Esau encodes the mushroom's anatomy in the two brothers' names and descriptions. Esau represents the mushroom cap: his name derives from *\*E-ShU-A*, "raised canopy," the dome-like pileus lifted above the stem. Jacob represents the stem: from *\*IA_A_GUB*, "pillar," the upright stalk. Esau is described at birth as "all red, all his body like a hairy mantle" (Genesis 25:25)—the rough, scabby texture of the Amanita's spotted cap. In Genesis 27, Jacob tricks the blind Isaac into bestowing the blessing by wearing Esau's garments and covering his hands and neck with kid skins. Isaac's famous remark—"The voice is Jacob's voice, but the hands are the hands of Esau"—perfectly captures the mushroom's dual nature: the smooth white stem (Jacob) hidden beneath the rough red canopy (Esau).

## Amanita and Red/Purple Dyes

The mushroom's colour pervaded the vocabulary of ancient dyes. The Greek word phoinix—used for the phoenix bird, the palm tree, the Phoenician coast, and the costly purple-red dye—derives from the Sumerian *\*GEShPU_IMI*, "strong man holding up the sky," a description of the mushroom stem bearing its cap. The Latin tablion, the purple fringe worn on Roman garments as a mark of authority, comes from *\*TAB_BA_LI*, "double-cone," referring to the split volva halves that frame the emerging mushroom. The Sumerian GAN-NU denoted the red dye cochineal; GAN alone means "cone, hemisphere, breast," and the compound AGAN ("breast") gives the Greek Amanita, literally "breast-shaped object." Finally, the Hebrew khinnān, meaning "red cap on ewes," describes the mushroom's pileus directly. It is homophonous with the root kh-n-n, "be gracious," from which come personal names such as Hanan, Hannah, and John—names that thus carry within them the image of the red-capped fungus.

## Reading the Colour Complex as a Cluster

Allegro's argument is cumulative rather than dependent on a single pun. He places the red cap, white flecks, wool, fleece, dye, storm, and twin imagery into one symbolic field.

Within that field, the mushroom is not merely compared to red wool; it is treated as the hidden prototype that makes red wool ritually meaningful.

The same logic links the Dioscouroi, the Argo, Jonah, Jesus, Jacob, Esau, and the names derived from grace or red caps.

Each story preserves a different facet of the fungus: its sudden appearance, its dangerous power, its red canopy, its white support, and its ability to mediate between sky and earth.

## Mythic Motifs Emphasized

- Redness marks the cap, the dye, the fleece, the garment, and the dangerous robe.
- Hair and wool encode the shaggy or warted surface of the pileus.
- Twins encode the cap-and-stem duality and the above/below polarity of fungal growth.
- Storm quelling expresses the belief that a substance can master what it generates.
- Hiddenness links Jonah, subterranean growth, and the mushroom's sudden emergence.
- Grace names such as John and Hannah are read through the same red-cap wordplay.

## Summary

For Allegro, the Golden Fleece is not simply a heroic prize.

It is a red, living, wool-like emblem of the sacred fungus.

The Dioscouroi and storm stories preserve its heavenly force.

Jacob and Esau preserve its divided body.

Dye terminology preserves its colour.

Personal names preserve its cap.

Together these strands form one of the densest examples of Allegro's mushroom-symbol reading.
