# PDC Water Conservation Techniques

The 1981 [[permaculture-design]] Course devotes extensive coverage to water conservation across multiple pamphlets. This page consolidates the specific water harvesting, storage, and conservation techniques Mollison teaches, complementing the existing [[pdc-water-harvesting-design]] page.

## Swales: The Core Technology

Swales are described as "a critical technology for winter-wet America" that is "not much used." A swale is a broad, shallow blade trench cut on true contours with no movement of water along the trench.

### Construction Specifications
- Never less than four feet wide, often much wider
- Not used on steep slopes; moderate to shallow slope systems only
- In clay: deepen the swale in profile
- In sandy/gravelly places: widen it
- Where clay is found: create little ponds within the swale
- Where water will soak in: widen the whole water system for maximum surface area

### The Aging Effect
The efficiency of absorption in swales increases as they age and as trees grow along them, because tree roots penetrate the subsurface and carry water down. Mollison cites the Davis, California example: initially absorbed about 40% of water, then 85%, and now 100%. It now absorbs water from off-site.

### Multiple Functions of Swales
- Water infiltration and groundwater charging (17-20 feet of fully charged soil in 3-4 years)
- Composting system on site (leaves rot quickly in the moist environment)
- Fire risk reduction (collects and rots fuel quickly)
- Mulch production for garden
- Salamander habitat
- Ginseng and blueberry growing sites (moist, rich, highly mulched)
- Children's play areas ("great places for children to run in the storms")

### Urban Swales
Mollison describes a suburban design for semi-dry climates:
- Hard-top roads, swales, little bridges, houses back to back, footpaths, down pipes
- All roof and road run-off goes into swales -- no gutters, no curbs
- Swales sometimes pass under roads
- Double rows of houses sitting between swales
- No pipes to repair, no gutters, no curbs -- cheap

## Keyline [[irrigation-systems]]

### The Gravity-Fed Plug System
For flat lands, Mollison describes a detailed irrigation system using side channels and plug barriers:
1. Run a side channel (drain) down the side of the field with a slight fall
2. Block the channel at intervals with removable plug barriers
3. Lead short 2-inch pipes from the main drain into graded channels across the field
4. Trees planted on banks between channels
5. When one section saturates, move plugs to the next area
6. Hundreds of trees irrigated with very little effort

### The Diversion Drain
A diversion drain above fields works efficiently both ways: to irrigate in summer (plugged) and to remove excess water in winter. A "well-keylined and combed landscape that has been soil conditioned doesn't get boggy in winter and doesn't get dry in summer."

## Net and Pan Systems

For stony, steep slopes where normal terracing is impossible:
- Set up very shallow guide lines for drainage
- Hand cut the drains
- Clear vegetation at junctions
- Make little flat planting platforms
- Dribbles of water come down drains, gathering in pans that absorb overflow
- Interrupt with planting bands
- Effective particularly in very rocky country normally not used for orchards

## Drip Irrigation Methods

Mollison describes drip irrigation ranging from primitive to sophisticated:

**Primitive forms:**
- Ostrich egg with a single hole bored near the plant
- Inverted one-gallon wine flagon (abundant around desert camps)
- Water leaks from bottom in little drips

**Modern forms:**
- Israeli drip irrigation lines

Critical principle: drip irrigation must be combined with a moisture barrier (vertical plastic sheets or clay) penetrating at least three feet around the garden, preventing lateral water transfer. Without this, water travels away from plants.

## Gley Ponds: Sealing Without Concrete

Gley is described as "by far the most satisfactory solution" for sealing ponds, especially in gravel pits.

### The Process
1. Collect green sappy material (mowings, green leaves, second-cut hay) -- six inches thick
2. Pack it down over the leak area
3. Cover with sand, plastic, old carpets, or a combination
4. When it ferments (becomes slimy), fill with water
5. The pond fills without any trouble and will never leak again

### Advantages
- Permanent seal
- Can be done in gravel pits where concrete fails
- Uses locally available materials
- Cost-effective compared to bentonite (clay that swells 14x)

### Verification
If it does not work, you did not do it properly. Go back and find the spots you missed, redo them, and the rest of it is permanent.

### Alternative Sealing Methods
- Bentonite clay (spread and roll in) -- costly but very satisfactory
- Plastic sheeting
- Concrete
- Water dynamite (three packages, boom -- bumps the bank and seals cracks with water pressure; may need 2-3 attempts)

## Earth Banks in Flat Landscapes

Earth banks serve multiple purposes on flat land:

1. **Windbreaks**: Fastest way to establish windbreak -- four to six foot bank with quick-growing plantings (pampas grass, bamboos)
2. **Livestock shelter**: Well-insulated shelter with plantings on top
3. **Fire radiation shield**: Decreases fire risk
4. **Noise reduction**: Deflects street noise; vegetation alone needs 100 meters, but "a well-insulated house with an earth bank protection can be near a fairly noisy system and be quite quiet"
5. **Mulch collection**: Earth banks accumulate leaf and silt from water flowing across landscape
6. **Silt defense**: Earth banks between garden and flooding rain stop silt from reaching the dam

### The Road-to-Bank Rule
From the crown of the road to the top of the Earth bank, deflect in a straight line so that you clear the roof of the house. This is "one main rule to follow."

## Storage Tanks

### Placement Principles
- Never fill a tank from a house roof if you can avoid it
- Put all tanks up on the slope above house roof level when possible
- Tanks below buildings: water runs down by gravity flow to lower areas
- Hot water collectors: below sill level on the shaded side for thermo-siphon

### Structural Uses
- Bury tanks to get them out of the way
- Build over them
- Use as bold structures -- trellis off them for summer shade with central ivy-covered tank
- Integrate into barns: large tanks as base of buildings
- Underground glass houses in caves behind tank dams

## Biological Dam Structures

When building dams, Mollison emphasizes creating biological diversity:

- **Islands in lakes**: Increase shoreline, provide nesting sites for water birds (fox-free except in winter)
- **Barrier islands**: Create quiet patches of water in front of wind exposure
