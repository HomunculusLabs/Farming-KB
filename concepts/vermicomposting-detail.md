# Vermicomposting Detail

Advanced guide to worm farming for homestead-scale waste processing. Builds on [[vermicompost]] with deeper species selection, bin design, feeding science, population management, and troubleshooting. See [[composting-systems]] for how vermicomposting fits alongside hot composting, bokashi, and sheet mulching.

## Worm Species Comparison

| Species | Common Name | Temp Range | Processing Speed | Reproduction | Best Use | Drawbacks |
|---------|------------|------------|-----------------|-------------|----------|-----------|
| Eisenia fetida | Red wiggler | 55-77F | Fast | High (cocoons every 7 days) | Standard bins, all-purpose | Dies below 40F; common escapee |
| Eisenia hortensis | European nightcrawler | 50-70F | Moderate | Moderate (cocoons every 10-14 days) | Deeper bins, cooler climates, fishing bait | Slower processing; less tolerant of heat |
| Perionyx excavatus | Indian blue / Malaysian blue | 70-85F | Very fast | Very high | Tropical/subtropical, enclosed systems | Highly migratory (escapees); intolerant of cold (<50F) |

E. fetida is the gold standard for most homesteads. E. hortensis handles cooler conditions and goes deeper — good for outdoor pits. P. excavatus processes waste fastest but requires warm, stable conditions and secure lids.

## Bin Designs

| Design | Capacity | Harvesting | Cost | Best For |
|--------|----------|-----------|------|----------|
| **Stacked tray** (Worm Factory, VermiHut) | 3-5 trays, 5-10 lb worms | Bottom tray finished; lift off | $80-150 | Small spaces, indoor, continuous harvest |
| **Continuous flow-through** (Urban Worm Bag, Worm Inn) | 10-30+ lb worms | Scrape castings from bottom hatch | $50-120 | Serious homestead waste processing; easiest harvest |
| **Single tote** (plastic bin with holes) | 1-5 lb worms | Light migration or screen | $10-30 | Beginners, low budget |
| **In-ground pit** (buried bin or trench) | Variable, climate-buffered | Partial harvest, natural migration | $0-20 | Outdoor, frost protection, orchard integration |
| **Windrow** (outdoor row on soil) | 50-500+ lb worms | Front-to-back migration | $0-50 (materials) | Large-scale, manure processing, field waste |

## Feeding Guidelines

### What to Feed (green/nitrogen inputs)
- Vegetable scraps (most kitchen waste)
- Fruit (avoid large citrus quantities)
- Coffee grounds and filters (excellent — worms love them)
- Crushed eggshells (grit + calcium, see [[animal-byproducts]])
- Tea bags (remove staples)
- Aged manure (horse, rabbit, duck — pre-composted 1-2 weeks)

### What to Avoid
- Meat, dairy, oils, bones (attracts pests, goes anaerobic)
- Large amounts of citrus (lowers pH)
- Alliums in quantity (onions, garlic — worms avoid)
- Glossy/coated paper
- Fresh grass clippings in bulk (heats up, can kill worms)

### Feeding Rate by Worm Population
- **Rule of thumb**: Worms eat roughly half their body weight daily
- 1 lb worms = 0.5 lb food/day at peak
- **Start slow**: feed 1/4 rate for first 2 weeks; double as population establishes
- **Pre-process**: Freeze/thaw or chop scraps — speeds decomposition by 2-3x
- **Bury food** 2-3 inches under bedding in rotating zones to prevent fruit flies
- **Surface area matters more than depth** — feed in a thin layer across the bin

### C:N Balance in the Bin
Maintain bedding-to-food ratio of roughly 3:1 by volume. When bin smells or gets wet, add carbon bedding (shredded paper, leaves, cardboard). See [[composting-systems]] for C:N ratios of common materials.

## Population Management

### Density
- **Optimal**: 1 lb worms per sq ft surface area (roughly 1000 worms)
- **Maximum**: 2-3 lb/sq ft — beyond this, reproduction slows and stress increases
- A healthy bin doubles its population every 2-3 months

### Reproduction
- Cocoon hatch time: 23-72 days depending on temperature
- Each cocoon contains 3-7 embryos
- Sexual maturity: 40-60 days after hatching
- Population self-regulates based on food, space, and conditions

### Harvesting Worms (population control)
- When bin is overcrowded, worms slow feeding and attempt escape
- Divide bin: move half the contents (worms + castings) to a new bin
- Sell or give away excess worms — they sell for $30-50/lb locally
- Feed excess to ducks or chickens (high protein)
- Start new bins for different locations (greenhouse, orchard pit)

## Harvesting Methods

| Method | Effort | Purity | Time | Best For |
|--------|--------|--------|------|----------|
| **Light migration** | High | High | 1-2 hours | Small bins, getting clean worms |
| **Screen (1/4 inch)** | Medium | High | 30 min | Medium batches, separating cocoons |
| **Side-to-side divide** | Low | Medium | 2-4 weeks | Low-effort continuous systems |
| **Bottom hatch (flow-through)** | Very low | Medium | Ongoing | Large-scale, best for continuous use |

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| **Worms escaping** | Bin too acidic, hot, wet, or anaerobic; new worms acclimating | Check pH (add eggshells), temp (<77F), moisture; leave light on for 48h; don't feed first 3 days |
| **Foul smell (sulfur/ammonia)** | Anaerobic conditions, overfeeding, food rotting | Add dry bedding, stop feeding 1-2 weeks, fluff bin for aeration, remove rotting food |
| **Fruit flies** | Exposed food, sweet scraps on surface | Bury food deeper, add dry newspaper layer on top, use vinegar traps, freeze scraps before feeding |
| **Protein poisoning (sprinkle disease)** | Too much nitrogen-rich food, sour bin | Remove uneaten food, add carbon bedding, flush with water if severe, reduce feeding |
| **Mite outbreak** | Excess moisture, overfeeding | Reduce moisture, expose bin to light (mites flee), add dry bedding, reduce food |
| **Acidic conditions** | Too much citrus, coffee, or food overall | Add crushed eggshells or dolomite lime; pH should be 6.5-7.5 |
| **White worms (enchytraeids)** | Excess moisture | Reduce watering, add dry bedding; harmless but compete with worms |
| **Slow processing** | Temperature too low, not enough worms, food too large | Move to warmer spot, increase population, chop/blend food |

## Using Vermicompost in Potting Mixes

- **Seed starting**: 10-20% castings by volume in mix (higher can burn seedlings)
- **Potting mix**: 15-25% castings; mix with peat/coir, perlite, and compost
- **Top-dress established plants**: 1/4 inch layer on soil surface, water in
- **Transplant amendment**: 1/4 cup castings per planting hole
- **Living soil beds**: 1-2 inches worked into top 4 inches of soil annually

Castings provide biology (beneficial microbes, enzymes), humic acids, and plant growth hormones — see [[soil-food-web]] for what these microbes do.

## Worm Tea vs Compost Tea

- **Bin leachate** (drainage from bin bottom): Can contain anaerobic pathogens and phytotoxic compounds. Dilute 10:1 for soil drench only — never foliar spray. Avoid if bin smells off.
- **AACT with castings**: Actively aerated compost tea using worm castings as the compost source. Aerates for 24-36 hours with molasses. Safe for foliar spray. See [[compost-tea-aact]] for full brewing method. Always use AACT — never raw leachate as a spray.

## Scaling Up for Homestead Waste

A 2-3 person household produces 3-5 lbs kitchen scraps daily. To process this:
- **Goal**: 5-10 lb worm population across 4-8 sq ft surface area
- **System**: 2-3 flow-through bags or a small windrow
- **Plus**: Duck/goat manure pre-composting (see [[animal-byproducts]]) feeds worm population
- **Orchard pits**: Bury 5-gal buckets with holes under fruit trees — worms process fallen fruit and return nutrients to root zone
- **Greenhouse integration**: Worm bin under greenhouse bench provides warmth and CO2 for plants
- **Timeline**: 6-12 months to reach full processing capacity from 1 lb starter
