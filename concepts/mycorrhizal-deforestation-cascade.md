## The Cascade Sequence

The deforestation cascade follows a predictable sequence:

1. **Immediate loss**: Tree removal eliminates root
   exudates that fed mycorrhizal networks. The mycelium
   begins to die back within days.

2. **Soil exposure**: Without tree canopy, soil is exposed
   to sun and rain. Surface soil temperatures rise, killing
   heat-sensitive organisms. Rain compacts bare soil and
   washes away topsoil.

3. **Nutrient loss**: Without mycorrhizal networks to
   capture and cycle nutrients, minerals leach from the
   soil. The nutrient-retention capacity of the land
   declines dramatically.

4. **Water cycle disruption**: [[mycoforestry]] practices can
accelerate recovery by inoculating seedlings with
mycorrhizal fungi before planting and by introducing
saprophytic fungi to rebuild soil organic matter. However,
restoring a fully functional mycorrhizal network with its
full complement of associated organisms is one of the
most challenging tasks in ecological restoration.

## Economic Implications

The economic costs of the deforestation cascade are enormous
but largely unaccounted for: reduced agricultural
productivity from soil degradation, increased flooding and
erosion downstream, loss of non-timber forest products
(mushrooms, medicinals), reduced water quality requiring
expensive treatment, and loss of biodiversity with unknown
consequences for ecosystem services.

## Prevention and Restoration

The most effective approach is prevention: preserving
existing forests and their fungal networks intact. Where
clearing has already occurred, restoration should
prioritize reestablishing mycorrhizal communities. This
requires inoculating planting sites with appropriate
mycorrhizal fungi, reducing soil disturbance, and
maintaining continuous tree cover to support the fungal
networks as they recover.
