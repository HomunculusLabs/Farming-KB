# Mycorrhizal Contributions to the Terrestrial Carbon Cycle

## Overview

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] receive substantial amounts of photosynthetically-fixed carbon from host plants and represent a major, functionally distinct component of the terrestrial carbon cycle. Over 80% of plant species invest significant below-ground carbon into mycorrhizal symbionts. At the global scale, the annual carbon flux through [[soil-respiration]] is ten times greater than fossil fuel combustion, and roots plus mycorrhizas are the single most important component of this flux. Mycorrhizal type (ericoid, ectomycorrhizal, or arbuscular) varies systematically across biomes and is a key driver of carbon cycling and storage patterns.

## Three Major Mycorrhizal Types and Their Biomes

### Ericoid Mycorrhizas (ERM)

- **Dominant biomes**: Heathlands, northern tundra.
- **Host plants**: *Cassiope*, *Vaccinium*, *Erica*, *Calluna*, *Rhododendron*.
- **Fungal structures**: Dense coils occupying 10%–80% of hair root volume.
- **Key features**: High carbon demand; peak activity in autumn; fungi turn over faster than roots.

### Ectomycorrhizas (EM)

- **Dominant biomes**: Boreal/coniferous forest (taiga), temperate deciduous forest.
- **Host plants**: *Pinus*, *Picea*, *Fagus*, *Quercus*, *Betula*, *Larix*, *Salix*.
- **Fungal structures**: Complete sheath around root tips; >90% of tips normally symbiotic.
- **Key features**: Receive 10–30% of net photosynthate; extensive extra-radical mycelium; produce persistent hydrophobic cords and melanized hyphae.
- **Boreal forest**: The world's largest vegetation system — circumpolar belt 1000–2000 km wide.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizas (AM)

- **Dominant biomes**: Temperate grasslands, subtropical/tropical savannas and rainforests.
- **Host plants**: Most herbaceous plants and grasses; some trees (*Fraxinus*, *Acer*).
- **Key features**: Most ancient and widespread type; external mycelium cannot assimilate exogenous sugar.
- **Decomposition**: Soils under AM-dominated ecosystems tend to have higher decomposition rates than ERM/EM systems.

## Direct Carbon Inputs via Mycorrhizal Mycelium

### Functional Distinctiveness from Saprotrophs

Mycorrhizal mycelia are chemically and functionally distinct from both their host plants and saprotrophic fungi:

| Feature | Mycorrhizal Mycelium | Host Plant | Saprotrophs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cell walls** | Chitin | Cellulose | Chitin |
| **Protective compounds** | Melanin | Lignin | Various |
| **C source** | Plant photosynthate | Photosynthesis | Decomposing organic matter |
| **Effect on soil C** | Adds new C | Adds new C | Only transforms existing C |

This functional difference is reflected in distinct δ¹³C signatures: mycorrhizal fungi are much closer to host plant values than are saprotrophs.

### Ericoid Mycorrhizal Carbon Flux

- Combined root + ERM respiration dominates soil respiratory fluxes in heathlands and tundra.
- ¹⁴C pulse-labelling showed very marked seasonality with **peak C allocation in autumn**.
- Strong linear correlation between ecosystem net C fixation and ergosterol (fungal sterol) concentration in hair roots one week later.
- Only 20%–40% of below-ground ¹⁴C allocation found in ERM hair roots; at mire sites, only 3%–10% of total ¹⁴C in top 5 cm hair roots.
- Estimated ~50% of C reaching hair roots passes to fungi; at seasonal peak, mycorrhizal allocation = up to 4.8% of net fixation.
- These estimates significantly **under-estimate** true C costs (don't include external mycelium, respiratory losses, or exudation).

### Ectomycorrhizal Carbon Flux

- Receives **10–30% of net photosynthate** in many forest ecosystems.
- Conservative estimate of EM contribution to soil microbial biomass in boreal forest: **32%**.
- Girdling experiment (Högberg & Högberg, 2002): soil fungal biomass decreased 47%–84% within 5 weeks, attributed primarily to EM mycelium senescence.
- External EM mycelium production: **420–590 kg ha⁻¹ year⁻¹** (hyphal in-growth bags).
- Combined EM mycelium + root biomass: **700–900 kg ha⁻¹**, with ~80% being extra-radical mycelium.
- EM mycelium accounts for **>70% of total soil microbial biomass** in some boreal forests.
- At least **50% of soil respiration** and half of dissolved organic C attributed to mycorrhizal mycelium plus roots.

## Carbon Quality and Persistence

### High C:N Ratios Promote Sequestration

- EM mycelium has very high C:N ratios (18.5–21.9), similar to or exceeding Norway spruce forest organic matter.
- These values are unusually high for soil microbial biomass.
- High C:N, combined with melanin production, hydrophobic cords, and chemical defences, promotes C persistence in soil.

### Chemical Defences Against Decomposition

Several features of EM mycelium resist decomposition:

1. **Melanized hyphae**: *Cenococcum geophilum* produces heavily melanized hyphae and sclerotia that decay very slowly, directly implicated in soil C sequestration.
2. **Hydrophobic cords**: Robust multicellular structures persistent for months.
3. **Polyphenolic compounds**: Contribute to chemical resistance.
4. **Toxins**: Some EM fungi kill fungivorous microarthropods with immobilizing toxins and predate them, reducing decomposition rates of EM necromass.

### Evidence for Reduced Decomposition

- Mycorrhizal colonization resulted in **30% lower decomposition rate** of *Pinus edulis* roots compared with non-mycorrhizal roots (Langley & Hungate, 2003).
- EM fungi secrete nutrient-mobilizing organic acids (oxalic, citric) and form calcium oxalate crystalline deposits coating hyphae and rhizomorphs.

## Mycorrhizas and Soil Carbon Sequestration

### Biome-Level Patterns

- **Heathland and boreal forest** together cover ~70% of the vegetated land mass of the Northern Hemisphere and contain the majority of global soil C stocks, mainly in peat and raw humus.
- The extent of soil C sequestration varies systematically across biomes, correlated with vegetation type, mycorrhiza type, soil characteristics, and climate.
- Variations in dominant mycorrhizal types are **important drivers** of ecosystem C cycling and storage.

### AM vs. ERM/EM Decomposition

- Rates of soil organic matter decomposition tend to be **higher in AM-dominated** ecosystems than in ERM/EM systems.
- Combined root + mycorrhiza contributions to soil respiration are generally **less for AM** than for ERM or EM.

## Methodological Challenges

1. **Standard microbial biomass techniques** (substrate-induced respiration) discriminate against mycorrhizal contributions — sieving fragments mycelia, and detachment from plant C supply causes 60%–95% decrease in mycorrhizal respiration within hours.
2. **External AM mycelium** cannot assimilate exogenous sugar, causing systematic under-detection.
3. **Seasonal dynamics**: Mycorrhizal C allocation peaks at end of growing season, time-lagged from photosynthesis rates.
4. **Distinguishing live from dead mycelium**: Most biomass measurements cannot separate these fractions.
5. **External mycelium excluded**: Many estimates capture only root-attached structures, missing the bulk of extra-radical mycelium.

## Carbon Cycling and Plant Community Composition

Mycorrhizas create **positive feedback mechanisms** affecting both biogeochemical cycles and plant community composition:

- The magnitude of below-ground C flow through mycorrhizal networks has been so large that it has driven the **evolution of cheater plants** — more than 10% of plant species depend on exploiting mycorrhizal fungal partners for carbon.
- Mycorrhizal type influences decomposition rates, nutrient availability, and competitive interactions among plant species.
- The importance of mycorrhizas in C cycling demands **radical re-appraisal** of ecosystem C flux models and concepts of plant competition.
