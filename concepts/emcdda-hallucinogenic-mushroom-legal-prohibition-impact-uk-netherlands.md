# EMCDDA Hallucinogenic Mushroom Legal Prohibition Impact: UK and Netherlands

## Overview

Between 2001 and 2006, six EU Member States tightened legislation on
[[hallucinogenic-mushroom-online-retail-markets]] for [[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]].
- **November 2002**: Netherlands Supreme Court clarified that the Opium Act
  applied to prepared (dried, mashed, or processed) mushrooms but not to
  fresh mushrooms.
- **March 2005**: Germany changed definition of controlled substance from
  "plant" to "organism" to include mushrooms.
- **May 2005**: Estonia clarified that cultivation of hallucinogenic
  mushrooms is an offence.
- **July 2005**: UK added all fungi containing psilocin as Class A
  controlled substances, removing the previous "if treated or prepared"
  qualification.
- **February 2006**: Ireland criminalised possession or sale of psilocin-
  [[tryptamine-chemistry-psilocin-and-psilocybin-synthesis]] are controlled under the 1971 UN Convention on
Psychotropic Substances and listed in Schedule I. All EU Member States
control them accordingly. However, the application to unprocessed fresh
mushrooms was ambiguous. The Dutch Supreme Court ruled in November 2002 that
the Opium Act, which specifically prohibits psilocin, psilocybin, and
"preparations" containing these substances, applied to hallucinogenic
mushrooms that had been "prepared" by being dried, mashed, or processed in
food but not to fresh mushrooms.

### Market Impact

This ruling created a legal distinction that allowed smartshops to continue
selling fresh mushrooms while prohibiting dried or processed products. The
effect was to preserve the Dutch [[guzman-magic-mushrooms-african-species]] made up
approximately 50% of smartshop turnover, meaning the ruling preserved a
significant commercial sector.

### How to Control Wild Fungi

The Dutch case highlighted the legal challenge of controlling naturally
occurring organisms. Some countries had legal difficulties not wanting to
unjustly criminalise people who may own land where mushrooms grow wild. The
initial approach was to state that mushrooms were illegal if "treated or
prepared," indicating intent of use. The UK later argued that even packaging
constituted a form of "preparation," but ultimately changed the law in 2005
to apply to mushrooms of any kind regardless of their state.

## United Kingdom: July 2005 Ban

### Pre-Ban Market Scale

Prior to July 2005, the UK had a substantial open market:

- Approximately 300 shops and market stalls across the UK sold
  hallucinogenic mushrooms (The Economist, 2004).
- Annual turnover estimated at approximately £1 million (€1.46 million).
- The UK Treasury collected sales tax estimated at up to £175,000 (€255,421)
