## Boundary Conditions and the Edge Effect

## Overview

Boundary conditions \u2014 the interfaces between different media, systems, or states \u2014 are among the most productive and species-rich zones in nature. [[permaculture]] itself acts as a translator between disciplines, fitting many forms of knowledge into relation with each other. It is a synthesis \u2014 and it functions at the boundaries between conventional areas of knowledge, exactly where the richest insights emerge.

Any boundary may be between media or within media. Boundaries present a place for things to happen, for events to locate. They present an opportunity to place a translatory element in a design, or to deform the surface for specific flow or translation to occur.

### Living Translators

At every natural boundary, living translators can be found: trees at the forest edge, fish at the thermocline, molluscs at the sediment-water interface, water striders at the air-water surface. The establishment of complex boundary conditions is a **primary strategy** for generating complex life assemblies and energy translators.

As Vogel (1981) noted: "Most biologists seem to have heard of the boundary layer, but they have a fuzzy notion that it is a discrete region, rather than the discrete notion that it is a fuzzy region."

## Motion at Boundaries

When we concentrate attention on the boundary condition, there are two common motions or particle flows:

- **Longitudinal (along boundaries):** Shear lines between media where deflections and turbulence may be caused by local friction or cosmic Coriolis (spin) forces.
- **Transverse (across boundaries):** Where surfaces may resist invaders chemically or physically, or where nets, sieves, or criteria must be bypassed.

### Why Boundaries Are Rich

Boundaries in nature are rich places for organisms for several reasons:

1. **Particle accumulation** \u2014 The boundary itself acts as a net or blockade, trapping materials in transit.
2. **Unique niches** \u2014 Special or unique niches are available in space or time within the boundary area itself.
3. **Dual resource access** \u2014 Resources of two (or more) media systems are simultaneously available at the boundary or nearby.
4. **Third-medium creation** \u2014 Special physical, social, or chemical conditions exist because of the reaction between adjacent media. Since all boundary conditions have some fuzzy depth, they constitute a third medium (the boundary zone itself).
5. **Turbulent mixing** \u2014 Turbulence creates a mix of two or more media, which may form another recognizable medium (e.g., foam on water, an emulsion of oil and water).

## The Edge Effect

The **[[edge-effect]]** is one of the most important factors in permaculture design. More events occur at boundaries than elsewhere because of these special conditions. It is common to find more different types of living species at any boundary or edge than within the adjoining systems.

### Ecological Recognition

Ecologists recognize that the interface between two ecosystems represents a third, more complex system which combines both. At interfaces:

- Species from both systems can exist
- The boundary supports its own unique species
- Gross photosynthetic production is higher than in either parent system

**Estuaries and coral reefs** \u2014 the complex systems of land/ocean interface \u2014 show the highest production per unit area of any major ecosystem (Kormondy, 1959).

### Traditional Use of Edge

- **Tasmanian Aborigines** burnt forest to maintain a large interface of forest/plain, since transitional areas provided great variety and abundance of food.
- **Forest/pasture interfaces** show greater complexity than either system alone in both producers (plants) and consumers (animals).
- **Fire mosaic landscapes** are rich in species. Such mosaics were the basis of Australian Aboriginal landscape management.

## Design Implications

### Maximize Interface

Given the edge effect, it is worthwhile to increase interface between particular habitats to a maximum. A landscape with a complex edge mosaic is:

- Interesting and beautiful
- The basis of the art of productive landscape design
- More stimulating ecologically
- Richer in species and niches

### Creating Harmonic Edge

Designers can create harmonic edge with plants, water, or buildings. The geometry or harmonics of any particular edge \u2014 how we crenellate it \u2014 is a key design variable. Edges and surfaces may be:

- **Sinuous** \u2014 Smooth, flowing curves
- **Lobular** \u2014 Rounded protrusions and embayments
- **Serrate** \u2014 Zig-zag or toothed patterns
- **Notched** \u2014 Indented or cut-out sections
- **Smoothed** \u2014 For more efficient flow where that is desired

### Practical Applications

- **Keyhole beds** use lobular edges to create sheltered, spacious garden habitats while minimizing path area (parallel paths take up to 50% of area; keyhole beds less than 30%).
- **Crenellated pond edges** can double the plantable pond-edge area without changing pond or field size.
- **Contour-following edges** (notched or lobular) afford sheltered, wetter, drier, hotter, or more exposed micro-habitats.
- **Serrate or zig-zag fences** resist wind-throw much better than straight barriers.

## Accumulations at Boundaries

When a boundary separates things which differ, there is opportunity for trade, transactions, or translation. Where the boundary acts as a trap or net, accumulations occur:

- Stranded shells on beaches
- Brush piled up against fences
- People at water edges (80% of human population lives at water edges)
- Birds perching on fences, depositing seed and manure
- Coral reefs accumulating ocean energy and oxygen
- Tree canopies accumulating wind energy

### Designing for Accumulation

We can arrange edges to net, stop, or sieve-through animals, plants, money, and influence. Forest edges collect aerial plankton passing in winds. Designers can use these natural accumulation processes to gather a variety of yields. However, there is a danger of accumulating so much material that systems become smothered \u2014 translators keep flow on the move, relieving stresses and building new life systems.

## Diffusion at Boundaries

Between all media, some **diffusion** takes place. This is greatly enhanced by surface turbulence, wave overturn, temperature differences, and pressure differentials. Boundaries between diffusing media are blurred, often seasonally different or sporadic, and always in flux:

- Plants give pollens and chemicals to air, actively intervening in radiative, gaseous, liquid, and energy transactions with the atmosphere.
- Between plant groups, leaf, root, and mulch exudates diffuse as chemical messengers.
- Water is the "universal solvent" of substances diffusing through earth\u2019s crust, plant systems, and atmosphere.
- A tree may have many acres of leaf; evapotranspiration can exceed evaporation by a factor of 40 or more. Growing many trees on one acre can increase the diffusion effect by factors of 1000 or more.

Diffusion is a quiet process operating on a broad front, analogous to but distinct from active transport systems (events or translators). Once an event has occurred, it also uses diffusive processes to gather or distribute materials, enlarging the total diffusive area available.

## Key Principles

1. **More happens at boundaries** \u2014 boundaries concentrate events, species, and resources.
2. **Edge = third system** \u2014 the interface between two systems creates a third, more complex system.
3. **Differences create trade** \u2014 every medium seeks to gather what it lacks from adjacent media.
4. **Translators relieve stress** \u2014 living organisms at boundaries keep flows moving, preventing destructive accumulation.
5. **Increase edge, increase yield** \u2014 designing differences into a system is a resource-building strategy; smoothing out differences is a deprivation of potential resources.
6. **Stupidity is ironing out differences** \u2014 not using or valuing differences creatively is the fundamental design error.
