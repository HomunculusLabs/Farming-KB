# Composting Temperature Phases

Composting proceeds through three distinct temperature phases, each driven by different groups of microorganisms. Understanding these phases is essential for producing high-quality compost efficiently and for ensuring that pathogens and weed seeds are destroyed during the process. The heat that drives these phases comes not from the sun but from the [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] of the soil microorganisms themselves, primarily bacteria.

## Core Requirements for Composting

Before examining the temperature phases, it is important to understand the five essential ingredients for successful composting:

1. **Organic materials** — the carbon and nitrogen sources that feed the [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]]
2. **Moisture** — required for microbial transport and life functions; organisms go dormant or die without adequate water
3. **Air (oxygen)** — beneficial decomposers are aerobic and require oxygen; [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] produce alcohols toxic to plants
4. **Heat** — generated by microbial metabolism, not external sources; drives population succession and pathogen killing
5. **Carbon-to-nitrogen ratio** — ideally 25:1 to 30:1 for efficient decomposition

## Phase 1: Mesophilic Stage (68–104°F / 20–40°C)

The mesophilic stage is the initial phase of composting, dominated by organisms adapted to moderate temperatures. Even in this early stage, significant decomposition work begins:

- **Cellulose breakdown** — straight, difficult-to-digest cellulose chains are depolymerized into smaller glucose chains, primarily by bacteria
- **[[brown-rot]] fungi** (basidiomycetes) and certain bacteria (*Bacillus* spp., *Heliospirillum* spp.) break down other resistant materials
- **Survival strategy** — [[mesophilic-organisms]] produce endospores, which are resistant to chemicals and heat, allowing them to survive the upcoming high-temperature phase and return when conditions cool
- **Larger organisms** join the decomposition effort — soil arthropods and other invertebrates physically break apart organic matter, and microbial activity in their guts contributes further chemical breakdown

**[[psychrophilic-bacteria]]** — those thriving at temperatures just above freezing (some as low as 32°F/0°C) — play a critical role in starting compost piles in spring after frozen winters. Their metabolic activity generates enough heat to wake up mesophilic organisms, which then take over.

As metabolic activity accumulates, temperatures rise to approximately 104°F (40°C), at which point mesophilic organisms can no longer function and [[thermophilic-organisms]] take over.

## Phase 2: Thermophilic Stage (104–150°F / 40–65°C)

The thermophilic phase is the high-temperature stage where the most rapid and complete decomposition occurs:

- **Complex carbohydrates** are fully broken down, including hemicelluloses (more resistant structures)
- **Proteins** are decomposed
- **Diverse microbial community** — *Arthrobacter* spp., *Pseudomonas* spp., *Streptomyces* and other actinomycetes, plus many fungi, play prominent roles
- **Pathogen destruction** — sustained temperatures of 140–150°F (60–65°C) for several days kill pathogenic microorganisms
- **Weed seed destruction** — at 150°F (65°C), weed seeds are also destroyed

### Timing and Monitoring

A properly constructed compost pile should reach 135°F (57°C) within 24–72 hours. With the correct carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, the center typically reaches 135°F in one day and 150°F in three days. If the pile fails to heat:

1. **Turn the pile** — mixing inside and bottom materials with outside and top materials adds oxygen
2. **Add fresh green material** — easy-to-digest sugars supply bacteria with needed food
3. **Add newspaper, fruit pulps, or commercial compost inoculants** — these provide additional microbial starter populations and carbon sources

### Critical Temperature Thresholds

| Temperature | Significance |
|---|---|
| Below 104°F (40°C) | Pile may need more green material; decomposition is too slow |
| 104°F (40°C) | Transition from mesophilic to thermophilic |
| 140–150°F (60–65°C) | Ideal range for pathogen and weed seed destruction |
| Above 155°F (68°C) | **Danger zone** — carbon begins burning off; pile must be cooled immediately |

### Cooling an Overheated Pile

- **Turn the pile** — introduces air and temporarily cools the pile while ensuring all material gets treated
- **Add water** — more drastic but effective cooling measure
- **Add brown materials** — reduces the proportion of green (bacterial food), slowing the primary heat-generating organisms

Monitoring can be done by hand (inserting into the pile), with a metal rebar or gutter nail (which transmits heat), or most precisely with a soil or oven thermometer.

## Phase 3: Maturation Stage

As complex proteins and carbohydrates are consumed, metabolic activity declines and temperatures decrease. The mesophilic organisms, whose protected spores survived the thermophilic phase, reassert themselves:

- **Lignin decay** — the most resistant plant component, with extremely strong bonds holding alcohol chains together, is finally broken down
- **Actinomycetes** — chain-like bacteria that resemble fungi, they attack these difficult-to-digest remnants and produce the characteristic earthy smell of good compost (from decay of cellulose, lignin, chitin, and protein)
- **Basidiomycetes** — continue their work on resistant materials
- **Physical decomposers** — nematodes, springtails, centipedes, worms, ants, snails, slugs, mites, spiders, rove beetles, and sow bugs shred organic matter, graze on microbes (stimulating their populations), and create soil aggregates

### Maturation Management

After the thermophilic peak, the pile should ideally be kept between 104–131°F (40–55°C):

- **Below 104°F** — add green, high-nitrogen material
- **Above 131°F** — add brown, carbon-containing material
- **Turning** — always initially cools the pile but stimulates renewed microbial activity; repeated turning is the single most effective management tool
- **Moisture** — maintain throughout; don't let the pile dry out or become saturated

### Knowing When Compost Is Finished

Compost is mature when you can no longer recognize the original ingredients. Additional quality indicators:

- **Smell test** — good compost smells clean and earthy; bad smells (vomit, putrefaction, vinegar) indicate anaerobic conditions; ammonia smell means it's not finished
- **Plant growth test** — plant seeds in the compost; good compost supports healthy growth, while incomplete compost shows nutrient [[knf-calcium-deficiency-symptoms]] due to locked-up nutrients
