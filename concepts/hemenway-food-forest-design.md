# Hemenway Food Forest Design

A food forest is a multi-layered edible ecosystem that mimics the structure and function of a natural forest. It is one of the most productive and self-maintaining designs in permaculture.

## What Is a Food Forest

A food forest is a perennial polyculture of multipurpose plants that mimics a natural forest's structure. Unlike a conventional orchard with rows of single-species trees over mowed grass, a food forest contains plants at multiple vertical levels, each performing multiple functions. The goal is a self-maintaining ecosystem that produces food, fuel, fiber, fodder, fertilizer, and pharmaceuticals.

## The Seven Layers

### Canopy (Overstory) -- 40-60+ feet
- Large fruit and nut trees
- Examples: standard apple, pear, walnut, chestnut, oak (acorns for flour), honey locust (pods for animal feed), mulberry

### Understory (Low Tree) -- 15-30 feet
- Smaller fruit and nut trees
- Examples: semi-dwarf fruit trees, plum, cherry, peach, apricot, persimmon, pawpaw, serviceberry

### Shrub Layer -- 6-12 feet
- Berry bushes and fruiting shrubs
- Examples: blueberry, raspberry, blackberry, currant, gooseberry, elderberry, hazelnut, goumi, seaberry

### Herbaceous Layer -- 1-6 feet
- Perennial vegetables, herbs, and flowers
- Examples: comfrey, artichoke, rhubarb, asparagus, Jerusalem artichoke, culinary herbs, medicinal herbs, insectary flowers

### Ground Cover -- 0-6 inches
- Creeping plants that suppress weeds and protect soil
- Examples: clover, strawberry, creeping thyme, sweet woodruff, violet, mint (contained), vinca

### Root Layer
- Edible and nutrient-accumulating root crops
- Examples: daikon radish, burdock, Jerusalem artichoke, potatoes (in early succession), groundnut, sunchokes

### Vine Layer
- Climbing plants on trellises, trees, or structures
- Examples: grape, kiwi, passionflower, hops, climbing beans, hardy kiwi, akebia, vining berries

## Designing a Food Forest

### Start with Observation
- Observe existing vegetation, sunlight patterns, water flow, soil conditions
- Work with existing trees if possible (adding understory plants beneath them)
- Note existing wildlife corridors and patterns

### Begin with the Bones
1. Plant canopy and understory trees first
2. Use [[hemenway-sheet-mulching-guide]] to prepare planting areas
3. Protect young trees with mulch and ground covers
4. Fill in shrub and lower layers as trees establish

### Guild-Based Planting
Each tree can be surrounded by a [[hemenway-guild-design-roles-and-assembly]] of supporting plants:
- Nitrogen fixers beneath each tree
- Nutrient accumulators at drip line
- Insectary plants for pollination and pest control
- Mulch plants for in-place soil building
- Ground covers for weed suppression

### Progression Over Time
- Year 1-2: Plant trees and establish ground covers
- Year 3-5: Shrub layer fills in, herbaceous plants spread
- Year 5-10: Canopy begins closing, forest microclimate develops
- Year 10+: Self-maintaining system with minimal input needed

## Food Forest Functions

- **Food production**: fruits, nuts, berries, vegetables, herbs at multiple levels
- **Habitat**: shelter and food for birds, beneficial insects, amphibians
- **Soil building**: constant leaf fall and root turnover builds humus
- **Water management**: canopy slows rain, roots prevent erosion, mulch holds moisture
- **Microclimate**: shade, wind protection, moderated temperatures
- **Carbon sequestration**: perennial woody plants store carbon long-term
- **Beauty**: flowering, fruiting, and changing seasons provide aesthetic value

## Integration with Zones

- Food forests are primarily Zone 2-3 elements
- Edge areas near the house (Zone 1-2) get the most intensive food forest design
- Larger food forests in Zone 3-4 may emphasize nut trees, firewood, and forage
- Zone 5 wilderness areas serve as reference and genetic reservoir

## Common Mistakes

- Planting too densely without regard for mature sizes
- Using only fruit trees without supporting guild members
- Ignoring shade patterns as trees mature
- Not providing adequate water during establishment
- Planting invasive species that will outcompete desired plants
- Forgetting access paths for harvesting and maintenance
