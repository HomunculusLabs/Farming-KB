## Palmer"s Mushroom Charts and Mycological Education

## Overview

Julius A. Palmer Jr. collaborated with the renowned publisher [[louis-prang]] to produce the first illustrated American guide to [[classification-of-edible-and-poisonous-mushroom-families]] mushrooms. Published as a set of two charts with twelve colored plates, this work represented a landmark effort to make mycological knowledge accessible to the general public. The charts condensed both descriptive text and botanical illustrations into a format that enabled "any person with no botanical knowledge to distinguish delicious food from deadly poison."

## Publication and Design

### The Publisher: Louis Prang and Company

The charts were published by L. Prang and Company, well-known publishers of "Flowers and Ferns of the United States." Louis Prang, a pioneer of chromolithography in America, was noted for the exceptional quality of his color printing. The mushroom charts benefited from Prang"s expertise in botanical illustration, producing plates that contemporary reviewers considered superior to both French and English competitors.

### Chart Contents

The publication consisted of:

- **Two charts** serving as the complete guide
- **Twelve colored plates** depicting various mushroom species
- **Succinct, non-technical descriptions** enabling identification without botanical training
- Coverage of both **edible species** (for safe consumption) and **poisonous or suspicious species** (for avoidance)

### Illustrative Quality

The *North Carolina Medical Journal* praised the illustrations as "remarkably fine," noting that a casual comparison placed them above those in Cordier (French press) on one hand and the chart of [[worthington-smith]] (English press) on the other. Cordier"s illustrations, though exquisitely rendered, were considered too highly colored, while Smith's were deemed inferior in quality.

### Scope and Ambition

The charts described and figured four poisonous or suspicious species — going beyond the earlier unpublished manuscript work of Dr. M.A. Curtis, which had illustrated forty colored species but described only edible fungi. The publishers intended, should the charts receive a positive public reception, to issue supplements over time "until their illustrations comprise nearly or quite all of the mushrooms of America."

## Historical Context

### Dr. Curtis"s Unpublished Manuscript

The late Dr. M.A. Curtis of North Carolina had prepared a manuscript work on edible fungi, illustrated with forty colored species drawn by Rev. Charles J. Curtis. Despite its obvious value, this work "had never found a publisher." Palmer"s chart project thus filled a significant gap in American mycological literature, being the first published illustrated guide to mushroom use in the United States.

### Educational Purpose

Palmer envisioned the charts as educational tools for schools and households. One enthusiastic student sent twenty-five copies to as many different schools, an example Palmer called "surely worthy of imitation on the part of those in charge of the education of the young." The charts were specifically designed for the era of "object-teaching" in American education, where visual aids were increasingly recognized as essential pedagogical tools.

## Design Philosophy and Misunderstandings

### Intended Audience

The charts were explicitly "never designed as a scientific or botanical manual." They were precisely what they professed to be: a practical identification guide for non-specialists. This distinction was important because some botanical reviewers criticized the charts for not conforming to scientific conventions.

### Artistic Arrangement vs. Natural Growth

A recurring source of misunderstanding involved the artistic arrangement of specimens on the plates. Where a mushroom in its expanded state would occupy the entire available plate space, Palmer placed smaller specimens showing earlier growth stages beneath the cap. Where specimens of different species were crowded together for spatial efficiency, some botanical critics laboriously pointed out that mushrooms "could not have grown in the manner depicted."

Palmer responded that the designer would have "cheerfully admitted" this — the arrangements were made for the artist"s pencil to fit a definite amount of space, not to depict natural growing conditions. He noted that artistically considered, "there could be no finer or more appropriate ornament for every dining-room in the land than these exquisite pictures from Mr. Prang"s studios."

## The Amatoxin Poisoning Table

### Amanitine Toxicology

Palmer"s later work included detailed clinical observations on amatoxin ([[amanitine]]) poisoning from *Amanita* species. Key findings documented in his records included:

- **Liver discoloration** as a primary pathological sign
- **Symptoms resembling cholera** during the initial phase
- **Delayed onset** — symptoms appeared long after ingestion, unlike most other food poisonings
- **Variable latency period** recorded across cases from different geographic regions
- **Potential for recovery** if treated promptly with appropriate remedies

### Treatment Approaches

Palmer advocated for the use of atropine as the specific antidote for [[amanitine-poisoning-and-absorption-mechanisms]], based on his clinical observations. The diagnostic key was the timing of symptom onset: delayed symptoms indicated amanitine (requiring atropine), while immediate symptoms suggested less virulent toxins treatable with milder remedies such as whiskey and sweet oil.

### Geographic Records

Palmer's files contained records of mushroom poisoning cases from across the northeastern United States, including incidents documented in:

- The *Boston Daily Globe*
- The *Scientific American* (which had published erroneous accounts of [[mushroom-poisoning-incidents-and-remedies]])
- The *North Carolina Medical Journal*
- Various local newspapers

The *Scientific American* incident was particularly instructive: a New York family was badly poisoned in September 1879, with two deaths. The surviving member sent a specimen to the journal claiming they had eaten a common edible mushroom. The resulting article erroneously stated that the common mushroom could become poisonous. Palmer sent a colleague to interview the writer, who could only say "that he had received the variety he named" — leading Palmer to observe that a person "whose ignorance was already proven" could hardly be trusted to have correctly identified a specific variety among hundreds.

## Legacy

### Copyright Transfer

Although the copyright of the mushroom charts eventually passed from Palmer"s hands — Louis Prang having "generously assumed all the risks of its success" — Palmer maintained a deep personal interest in the work"s continued circulation and educational impact.

### Palmer's Personal Reflections

Palmer concluded his writings on mushrooms with a lyrical tribute to the fungi he had spent decades studying, describing them as "curious creatures" surrounded by "holy mystery." He noted their vivid color changes at dissolution, their subtle poisons that "defied all the re-agents known to the laboratory," and the delicate tracery of their veils. His final image was poetic: fungi as "commas in sweet nature"s book, strewn up and down her emerald-tinted page," appearing anew each spring through "some [[allegro-menstrual-blood-creative-process-purple-fertility]] hidden below the moss of the bank or under the grass of the lawn."

### Broader Significance

Palmer's work represented a critical transition point in [[early-american-mycology-and-palmer]] — from folk superstition and blanket condemnation of wild fungi toward systematic, empirical study. His emphasis on practical testing, his collaboration with chemists like [[paul-casamajor]], and his efforts to create accessible educational materials all contributed to the gradual popularization of mycophagy in the United States.
