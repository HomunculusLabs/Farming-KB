# Gourmet and Medicinal Mushroom Oyster Cultivation Methods

Oyster mushrooms (genus Pleurotus) are by far the easiest and least expensive gourmet mushrooms to cultivate. Few other mushrooms demonstrate such adaptability, aggressiveness, and productivity. They grow on a wider array of forest and agricultural wastes than any other mushroom group and can serve to reduce hunger in developing nations while revitalizing rural economies. Worldwide production surged from 169,000 metric tons in 1987 to 909,000 in 1990.

## Overview of Cultivation Approaches

Oyster mushrooms can be cultivated using several methods, each suited to different scales, climates, and available resources:

- **Straw-based cultivation**: Pasteurized straw in bags, columns, or trays. Most accessible for beginners.
- **[[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] blocks**: Sterilized sawdust/bran mix in bags or bottles. Higher yields, more equipment needed.
- **Log cultivation**: Hardwood logs inoculated with spawn. Low-tech, long-term production.
- **Shelf/bed cultivation**: Pasteurized substrate on shelving or in beds. Common in Asia.
- **Outdoor bed cultivation**: Substrate in shaded outdoor beds. Seasonal production.

## Straw-Based Cultivation

Straw is the most forgiving substrate for small to mid-scale cultivators. It accepts limited numbers of contaminants while selectively favoring mushroom mycelium.

### Straw Preparation

1. Chop straw into 1-4 inch lengths using a shredder or chipper
2. Wheat, rye, oat, and sorghum straws are the best; hay with seed kernels should be avoided
3. Straw can be supplemented with up to 20% alfalfa to enhance yields without increasing contamination risk (per Royse, 1988)

### Hot Water Pasteurization

1. Stuff chopped straw into a wire basket
2. Submerge in a cauldron of 160 degrees F (71 degrees C) water for 1-2 hours
3. Heat with a propane burner; use a probe thermometer to monitor core temperature
4. After two soakings, discard the water as it becomes toxic ("straw tea") to mycelium
5. Drain well and spread on clean surfaces to cool rapidly
6. Broadcast grain spawn by hand over the cooled straw (wear gloves, wash hands every 15 minutes)
7. Mix spawn and straw thoroughly and pack into bags, trays, columns, or wire racks

### Phase II Steam Pasteurization (Commercial Scale)

For larger operations, a dedicated steam room (Phase II chamber) is used:

1. Moisture chopped straw to 75% over 2-4 days using sprinklers; turn daily
2. Load straw into insulated chamber with raised false floor and steam pipes
3. Fill room to no more than 1/8 capacity (7:1 air-to-substrate ratio)
4. Inject live steam; monitor temperature at low, mid, and high points
5. Maintain minimum 2 hours above 160 degrees F (71 degrees C)
6. Do not allow temperature to exceed 200 degrees F (93 degrees C) or beneficial organisms are killed
7. Cool under HEPA-filtered positive pressure to below 105 degrees F (38 degrees C) before inoculation
8. Clean inoculation area with 10% bleach solution before opening chamber

One ton of chopped, soaked wheat straw occupies approximately 250 cubic feet and functionally fills a 1,000 square foot growing room.

## Supplemented Sawdust Cultivation

For indoor controlled-environment cultivation, supplemented sawdust provides higher and more consistent yields:

### Formula

| Component | Amount |
|-----------|--------|
| Hardwood sawdust | 100 lbs |
| Wood chips (1/2-4 inches) | 50 lbs |
| Rice/wheat/oat bran | 40 lbs |
| Gypsum | As needed |
| Water | To 65-75% moisture |

### Process

1. Mix dry components thoroughly
2. Add water to achieve 65-75% moisture
3. Pack into polypropylene bags with filter patches
4. Sterilize in autoclave or retort (not merely pasteurized, due to nitrogen supplements)
5. Cool and inoculate with grain or sawdust spawn
6. Incubate until fully colonized
7. Initiate fruiting by adjusting environmental parameters

### Bag Cultivation (MushWorld Method)

1. Fill polypropylene bags with moistened substrate
2. Compress firmly and create a 2-3 cm diameter center hole for deeper inoculation
3. Seal with breathable plugs (cotton balls or micro-filters)
4. Heat treat: normal pressure at 90-95 degrees C for 5-8 hours, or 100 degrees C for 4 hours; high pressure at 121 degrees C for 1.5-2 hours
5. Cool to below 30 degrees C and inoculate
6. Incubate at appropriate temperature (typically 3-5 degrees C below species optimum to account for respiration heat)
7. Upon full colonization, expose to fruiting conditions

## Environmental Parameters for Fruiting

Oyster mushrooms require specific environmental triggers to shift from vegetative growth to fruiting:

| Parameter | Spawn Run | [[growing-gourmet-primordia-formation-pinning]] | Fruitbody Development |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Temperature | 75-85 degrees F (24-29 degrees C) | Species-dependent | Species-dependent |
| Humidity | 90-100% | 98-100% | 85-95% |
| CO2 | 5,000-20,000+ ppm | Below 1,000 ppm | Below 1,000 ppm |
| Fresh Air Exchanges | 1-2 per hour | 4-8 per hour | 4-8 per hour |
| Light | Not needed | 500-1,000 lux | 500-1,000 lux |

A sharp temperature drop is the most effective stimulus for fruiting induction. CO2 above 1,000 ppm during fruiting causes long stipes with tiny caps; proper ventilation produces short stipes with broad caps.

## Advantages and Disadvantages

### Advantages

- Low cost of entry and operation
- Wide substrate adaptability (over 200 documented waste materials)
- High [[stamets-biological-efficiency-yield]] (frequently exceeds 100%)
- Fast cropping cycle (10-14 days between flushes for many species)
- Suitable for small-scale and commercial operations
- Spent substrate has value as animal feed, compost, and soil amendment
- Several species secrete metabolites toxic to parasitic nematodes

### Disadvantages

- Short shelf life: mushrooms spoil within days, supporting local market arguments
- Spore load can be a health hazard to workers; sporeless strains are highly sought
- Attracts Sciarid and Phorid flies far more than other [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]]
- Protein content varies significantly with substrate, spawn media, and strain (15-35% dry weight)

## Nutritional Value

Oyster mushrooms contain significant protein (15-35% dry weight), vitamin C (30-144 mg per 100g), niacin (109 mg per 100g), and various free amino acids. [[dighton-fungal-nutritional-composition-animal-diets]] varies with substrate type, spawning rate, and strain.

## By-Products of Cultivation

After the crop cycle, spent oyster mushroom substrate has multiple uses:

- **Animal feed**: Nutritious fodder for cattle, chickens, and pigs
- **Soil amendment**: Excellent ingredient for composts and building soils
- **Nematicide**: At least five Pleurotus species secrete metabolites toxic to root-knot nematodes
- **Secondary cultivation**: Spent straw can support outdoor growth of Stropharia species
- **Enzyme recovery**: The decomposition process secretes commercially valuable enzymes
