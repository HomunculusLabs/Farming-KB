# Tools for the Small Farm

Eliot Coleman's philosophy on farm tools is rooted in the belief
that everyone who grows plants has a favorite tool, and given the
number and ingenuity of gardeners, there is no end to the many
simple solutions for making almost any garden task easier. Tools
should work as extensions of the grower's wishes, translating
intention into action. This chapter covers Coleman's approach to
finding, evaluating, modifying, and creating tools for the small
farm.

## The Lesson of the Broom

Coleman opens with a formative experience: while demonstrating an
"ideal planting hoe" to a [[community-garden-mushroom-integration]] group, an older woman
left, returned with an ancient broom with short worn bristles, and
proceeded to outperform him. Holding it upside down, she used the
tip of the wooden handle to make a neat furrow and dropped in bean
seeds. Turning it right side up, she deftly brushed soil to cover
and firm them. The broom was inexpensive (recycled), did multiple
jobs (furrow, cover, firm) with no moving parts, saved bending, and
could cultivate small weeds. The lesson: the best tool is one that
fulfills your sense of the job you wish to do.

## Finding Good Tools

The [[hamilton-choosing-garden-tools]] in hardware stores are mass-market items whose
shapes and sizes are limited to minimize costs and maximize profits.
Ease of use is not the goal. Years ago, when farm and garden tools
were used by professionals, choices were far broader and designs
were based on efficient use. Old tool catalogs and historical
museum collections reveal the nuances that once abounded: shovel
catalogs offered all conceivable blade sizes, shapes, and angles.
Regional peculiarities were acknowledged.

This [[fungal-functional-diversity]] still exists on small farms in Europe.
Over many years Coleman has photographed different styles of
wheelbarrows, noting that farmers had added cuts, bends, or welds
to standard models to fit them more precisely to their needs.

## The Collineal Hoe

The collineal hoe was developed at Four Season Farm through
systematic experimentation. Starting with standard hoes (large
blade at 90 degrees to the handle) and a few with smaller blades
at 80 degrees, Coleman and his team went to the workbench with
sacrificial hoes and proceeded to cut, bend, and file through
repeated field trials.

The final design features a narrow blade (7 inches side to side,
1 inch front to back) with the neck bent to hold the cutting edge
in line with the handle at a 70-degree angle. The name "collineal"
(meaning "in line with") reflects this alignment. The hoe cuts
weeds best when sharpened like a chisel, with the sharp edge next
to the soil. Sharpening is done by holding the handle upright and
filing across the upper edge of the blade.

Coleman is fascinated by the great improvement in tool performance
from such seemingly small refinements.

## The Modified Transplant Trowel

When Coleman switched from bare-root plants to soil-block-grown
seedlings, the standard transplant trowel became uncomfortable to
use. The solution was to modify a sturdy bricklayer's trowel by
shortening the blade and changing the angle of the handle to
parallel the blade. This created a tool comfortable to hold blade-
down for jabbing and pulling rather than digging, making a perfect
hole for inserting a soil block. The modification changed the wrist
position from strained (conventional trowel) to natural (modified
trowel).

## Tool Modification as Standard Practice

Coleman advocates viewing any purchased tool as a point of
departure rather than a finished product. Modifying existing tools
or inventing your own is not complicated. Creativity means escaping
traditional patterns to see things differently. Wonders can be
accomplished with a hacksaw, a file, a drill, a pair of pliers,
and a vise.

Tool modifications used to be common practice. Many have been
forgotten. Examples include:

- Scythe blades: newly purchased blades have a standard angle
  between blade and tang. When scythes were commonly used, everyone
  understood that bending that angle to fit their mowing style was
  the user's responsibility.
- Handle length: store-bought tools come with one length, but it
  is illogical to assume the same handle length works for users
  from 5-foot-4 to 6-foot-4. Don't struggle; cut it off or find
  a longer one.

## Handle Selection and Care

The preferred woods for tool handles have traditionally been ash
for long handles (hoe, shovel) and hickory for short handles (axe,
maul). The grain lines running down the handle should be straight
from one end to the other. If grain lines run at an angle, stress
from tool use will likely initiate a break at that point.

Once a good handle is found, keep it in shape by coating twice a
year with pure linseed oil or a beeswax-linseed mixture. This
prevents drying, which causes wood to lose its liveliness, become
brittle, and fail.

Historically, handle length often served purposes beyond matching
worker height. Long-handled hayforks were used for pitching hay up
onto wagons, while shorter forks were used on the wagon to stabilize
loads. Exchange those tools and both workers would be awkward and
inefficient.

Coleman emphasizes that simply learning by name that there are
hayforks, manure forks, spading forks, and garden forks will help
you find the right tool for the job.

## The Spirit of Innovation

Enormous amounts of time, money, thought, and ingenuity go into
perfecting sports equipment. Think what a difference graphite shafts,
larger sweet spots, and metal laminates have made to golf clubs.
Farmers should apply the same innovative spirit to their tools.

Coleman encourages all growers to look beyond readily available
tools. Make farmwork easier and more pleasant by searching out
unique tools or creating your own. There are many other growers
pioneering new systems and crops who can benefit from your ideas
