# Plant Propagation Methods
Plant propagation is the process of creating new plants from existing ones. Methods fall into two broad categories: sexual propagation (from seeds) and asexual/vegetative propagation (from plant parts). Vegetative methods produce clones genetically identical to the parent plant, preserving desired traits. [[green-cannabis-grow-bible-cloning-and-seed-propagation]] introduces genetic variation, which is valuable for breeding but may not preserve specific characteristics.
## Seed Propagation
Seed propagation is the most common method for annual vegetables, herbs, and many perennials. See [[seed-starting-reference]] for detailed seed-starting guidance.
### Advantages
- Large numbers of plants from minimal material
- Genetic diversity (important for adaptation and breeding)
- Low cost per plant
- Seeds store well for years
### Disadvantages
- Does not preserve exact genetics of hybrid parents (F1 hybrids do not breed true)
- Some species have long juvenile periods before flowering/fruiting
- Some species are difficult or slow from seed (e.g., many [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]])
### Seed Trepawpaw Methods
- **Stratification**: Cold, moist treatment to break dormancy. Place seeds in moist sand or paper towel in a plastic bag, refrigerate for 30-120 days. Common for: apples, peaches, cherries, pawpaw, many native perennials.
- **Scarification**: Breaking or weakening the seed coat to allow water absorption. Methods: nick with a file, rub with sandpaper, soak in hot (not boiling) water for 12-24 hours. Common for: morning glories, locust, redbud, moonflower.
- **Soaking**: Soak seeds [[ingoldian-hyphomycetes-spore-dispersal-in-water]] for 6-24 hours before planting to speed germination. Common for: beans, peas, corn, beets, squash.
## Cuttings
Cuttings are pieces of stem, leaf, or root that are encouraged to form roots and grow into a new plant. This is one of the most widely used vegetative propagation methods.
**Softwood Cuttings (Spring-Summer)**
- Take from new, flexible growth that snaps when bent
- Best for: herbs (basil, mint, rosemary, sage), geraniums, hydrangeas, fuchsia, coleus
- Cut 3-6 inch sections just below a node
- Remove lower leaves, keep 2-3 leaves at the top
- Dip the cut end in rooting hormone (optional for easy-to-root species)
- Insert into moist rooting medium (perlite, vermiculite, or 50/50 peat-perlite)
- Cover with a humidity dome or plastic bag to maintain high humidity
- Keep in bright, indirect light at 65-75°F
- Roots form in 2-4 weeks; test by gently tugging
**Semi-Hardwood Cuttings (Late Summer-Fall)**
- Take from partially mature wood (current season's growth that has begun to firm)
- Best for: camellias, rhododendrons, azaleas, holly, boxwood, many fruit trees
- 4-6 inch sections, treated similarly to softwood but slower to root (4-8 weeks)
**Hardwood Cuttings (Late Fall-Winter)**
- Take from mature, dormant wood after leaf drop
- Best for: grapes, figs, currants, gooseberries, willow, poplar, forsythia
- 6-12 inch sections, pencil-thickness
- Make a straight cut at the top (just above a bud) and an angled cut at the bottom (just below a bud)
- Bury bottom 2/3 in moist sand or soil outdoors
- Roots form by spring; transplant in fall or following spring
- Can be bundled and stored in moist sand in a cool location until spring planting
### Root Cuttings
Sections of root that produce new shoots. Works for species that naturally sucker.
- Best for: comfrey, horseradish, oriental poppies, blackberries, raspberries, elderberry
- Take 2-4 inch root sections in late fall or early spring during dormancy
- Plant horizontally 2 inches deep in pots or nursery beds
- Keep moist; shoots emerge in 4-8 weeks
### Leaf Cuttings
Some plants can be propagated from a single leaf or leaf section.
- **African violets, saintpaulia**: Cut leaf with 1 inch of petiole, insert petiole into moist medium. New plantlets form at the base in 4-8 weeks.
- **Succulents (echeveria, sedum, jade)**: Remove leaf, let callus for 1-3 days, place on moist medium. New plantlets form at the leaf base.
- **Begonias**: Cut leaf with veins, lay flat on moist medium, pin down. New plantlets form at vein intersections.
## Division
Division is the separation of a clumping plant into smaller sections, each with roots and shoots, to create new plants. This is the simplest and most reliable vegetative propagation method for many perennials.
### When to Divide
- **Spring division**: As growth begins. Best for: most perennials, ornamental grasses, hostas, daylilies, iris
- **Fall division**: After flowering or foliage dieback. Best for: peonies, bearded iris, oriental poppies, bleeding heart
### Division Method
1. Dig up the entire plant clump
2. Shake off excess soil to see the root structure
3. Identify natural division points (separate crowns with visible roots and shoots)
4. Separate using hands, two garden forks back-to-back, or a sharp knife
5. Each division should have 3-5 shoots and a healthy root system
6. Replant divisions immediately at the same depth as the original
7. Water thoroughly and mulch
### Best Candidates for Division
- Perennial herbs: chives, oregano, thyme, mint, lemon balm
- Flowers: daylilies, hostas, coneflowers, rudbeckia, asters, irises
- Vegetables: asparagus (crown division), rhubarb, horseradish, Jerusalem artichokes
- Berries: blackberries, raspberries (dig suckers), strawberries (runner separation)
## Layering
Layering encourages a stem to form roots while still attached to the parent plant. The new plant is severed once roots are established.
### Simple Layering
1. Select a flexible, low-growing branch
2. Bend the branch down to the ground
3. Wound the underside of the stem at the contact point (scrape or notch)
4. Bury the wounded section 4-6 inches deep [[singh-fungal-interactions-with-heavy-metals-in-soil]]
5. Leave the tip of the branch exposed above ground
6. Anchor with a U-shaped wire or stone
7. Keep moist; roots form in 6-12 months
8. Sever from the parent plant once well-rooted
**Best for**: raspberries, blackberries, honeysuckle, forsythia, rhododendrons, magnolias, hazelnut
### Air Layering
Used [[query-how-to-make-lactobacillus-serum-for-plants]] that cannot be easily bent to ground level.
1. Select a healthy branch, 1-2 feet from the tip
2. Make an upward diagonal cut 1/3 through the stem
3. Insert a toothpick or matchstick to keep the cut open
4. Apply rooting hormone to the cut
5. Wrap the wounded area with moist sphagnum moss
6. Cover the moss with plastic wrap and secure at both ends with tape or twist ties
7. Keep the moss moist; check monthly
8. Roots are visible through the plastic in 2-6 months
9. Sever below the rooted section and plant
**Best for**: figs, citrus, avocado, magnolia, rubber plant, difficult-to-root woody plants
### Tip Layering
Similar to simple layering but the tip of the branch is buried.
1. Bend the growing tip of a branch down to the soil surface
2. Bury the tip 4-6 inches deep
3. The tip grows downward initially, then curves upward
4. New shoots emerge from the buried tip
5. Sever and transplant the following season
**Best for**: blackberries, raspberries, trailing blackberry varieties
## Grafting
Grafting joins two plant parts so they grow as one. The upper part (scion) provides the desired fruit variety; the lower part (rootstock) provides disease resistance, size control, and soil adaptation. See [[grafting-fruit-trees]] for detailed techniques.
### Common Grafting Methods
- **Whip-and-tongue**: Scion and rootstock of similar diameter, joined with interlocking tongues. Used for bench grafting in winter.
- **Cleft graft**: Scion inserted into a cleft in the rootstock. Used for top-working established trees.
- **Bud grafting (T-budding)**: A single bud is inserted under the bark of the rootstock. Used for summer budding of fruit trees.
## Tissue Culture (Micropropagation)
Tissue culture produces large numbers of identical plants from small tissue samples in a sterile laboratory environment.
### Basic Process
1. **Explant selection**: Take a small tisagarsample (meristem, leaf, stem node) from the parent plant
2. **Surface [[sterilization]]**: Clean the explant in bleach or alcohol to eliminate contaminants
3. **Initiation**: Place explant on nutrient agar medium with growth hormones (auxin + cytokinin)
4. **Multiplication**: Subculture to fresh medium every 4-6 weeks; shoots multiply
5. **Rooting**: Transfer shoots to rooting medium (higher auxin, lower cytokinin)
6. **Acclimatization**: Gradually transfer rooted plantlets from sterile agar to potting soil, increasing humidity control
### Applications
- Mass production of disease-free planting material
- Propagation of species difficult to propagate by other means (orchids, bananas, potatoes, strawberries)
- Preservation of rare or endangered species
- Production of genetically identical clones for research or commercial orchards
### Limitations for Home Gardeners
- Requires sterile laboratory conditions (laminar flow hood, autoclave)
- Specialized media and growth hormones
- Significant startup cost and technical knowledge
- Not practical for most home growers
## Choosing a Propagation Method
| Method | Speed | Genetic Fidelity | Skill Level | Best For |
|--------|-------|-----------------|-------------|----------|
| Seed | Slow to fast | Low (except open-pollinated) | Beginner | Annuals, vegetables |
| Stem cuttings | Moderate | High | Beginner-Intermediate | Herbs, softwood perennials |
| Division | Fast | High | Beginner | Clumping perennials |
| Layering | Slow | High | Beginner | Woody shrubs, berries |
| Grafting | Moderate | High | Advanced | Fruit trees |
| Tissue culture | Moderate | High | Expert | Mass production, difficult species |
