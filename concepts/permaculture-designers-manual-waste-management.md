# Permaculture Designers Manual - Waste Management

updated: 2026-04-18

Waste management in the Designers Manual is not treated as a single topic but integrated throughout Chapters 7, 12, 13, and 14. Mollison's fundamental principle is that "waste" is a concept foreign to natural systems -- every output of one process is an input for another. The manual consistently reframes waste as a resource in the wrong place.

## Sewage and Water Recycling (Chapter 13-14)

### Sewage in Aquaculture

The manual notes that the highest fish production per hectare was achieved in a rapid canal flow of mainly sewage. While cautioning about biocide contamination, Mollison presents sewage lagoons and secondary treatment ponds as productive aquaculture resources when properly managed.

Ponds are classified by oxygen status: aerobic, facultative, and anaerobic (primary sewage). Water quality management involves:
- Regular monitoring for waste removal
- Draining, liming, and taking a season of dryland crop (clover, fenugreek) before re-flooding
- Flow-on pond water that doubles land crop yields downstream
- Reed bed filtration for excess nutrients from pond overflow
- Forage food ponds of crustaceans to filter high nitrate status

### Village-Scale Systems

Village infrastructure should include methane generators, ferment tanks, sewage, sullage, and biomass conversion systems. All sites for energy and water provision are public. The village trust (Trust B) can undertake these as trading operations that are both productive and waste-eliminating.

## Composting and Organic Waste

### Animal Manures

Pig, duck, and second-stage sewage are all used in fish ponds. Any bird or animal manures are useful as fertiliser. The stocking of waterfowl, perches for birds, pigeon lofts, pig pens, and chicken roosts over water are all devices to bring complex plant nutrients into aquatic systems.

Chicken manure is deliberately cultivated: farmers pile manure for flies to lay in, then let chickens eat the maggots. Mulch piles breed cockroaches and termites for poultry food. Mealworms in bran/root mixtures provide additional protein.

### Plant Waste

In aquaculture systems, wastes reduce water quality if in excess or as dead material. Detritus feeders -- herbivorous fish, nutria, or shrimp -- convert wastes to useful protein. In padi and terrace systems, 2-5 cm of mulch is retained over the clay base for cropping.

### Pond Mud and Silt

Waste removal from ponds is traditionally layered with green crop for compost. Mud, silt, and humus from pond bottoms is nutrient-rich material that can be deposited near ponds as retaining walls or low islands. Jet pumps can vacuum detritus out of reservoirs for this purpose.

## Lawn Waste Critique

Mollison's critique of lawns (Section 12.12) is effectively a waste management argument: lawns produce "a massive public disposal problem consisting of poisoned grass waste rich in Dieldrin, DDT, biocides, and nitrogen." Millions of litres of petrol are used in maintenance. The yields of lawn agriculture are waste, not product.

## Biogas and Energy from Waste

Methane conversion from organic wastes is presented as a village-scale energy strategy (see [[permaculture-designers-manual-energy-systems]]). Cow dung converted to methane provides useful fertiliser residue and achieves significant conversion efficiency. The manual lists biogas conversion efficiency alongside other energy systems.

Methane generators in village systems convert:
- Animal manures (pig, cattle, poultry)
- Kitchen and food processing waste
- Sewage and sullage
- Green crop and forestry waste

The residue from biogas digestion is a superior fertiliser compared to raw dung burning, as it retains nitrogen that would otherwise be lost to the atmosphere.

## Design Principles for Waste Systems

1. Every output should be an input to another system (no waste)
2. Design waste treatment as productive systems, not disposal systems
3. Use biological processes (composting, digestion, filtration) before mechanical or chemical ones
4. Keep waste treatment local and visible
5. Design for nutrient cycling within the site boundaries
6. Use waste heat from energy generation for space heating or crop drying
7. Treat water at the highest practical level for on-site reuse
8. Design sewage systems that produce food (fish, plants) rather than requiring disposal
9. Integrate animal systems with crop systems for manure cycling
10. Design for materials reuse and eventual decomposition rather than landfill

## Recycling and Resource Conservation

The manual advocates for:
- Seed saving and exchange as resistance to multinational seed control
- Local materials use for building (eliminating industrial waste streams)
- Tool sharing and leasing (reducing per-household resource consumption)
- Vehicle leasing systems at village scale (reducing individual ownership)
- Community freezers, laundrettes, and processing facilities (reducing household energy waste)
