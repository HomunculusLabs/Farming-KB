# Cannabis and Encephalitis

## Overview

Encephalitis is an acute inflammation of the brain most commonly caused by a
virus (e.g., live virus vaccination, herpes, West Nile, or equine encephalitis),
although it may also be produced by bacteria (e.g., syphilis, bacterial
meningitis, Lyme disease), parasites (e.g., malaria), or an over-reactive
immune response to secondary infection (e.g., measles, mumps, or rubella).

Symptoms may include headaches, blurred vision, fever, confusion, generalized
weakness, seizures, stiff neck, and projectile vomiting. Diagnosis is done by
physical examination, history, blood, urine or mucosa tests, spinal tap
(examining cerebrospinal fluid), electroencephalogram (EEG), brain biopsy, and
MRI or CT scan imaging.

## Conventional Treatment

Depending on the underlying cause, treatment may include:
- Antiviral agents such as acyclovir (only for herpes-related cases)
- Other antimicrobial pharmaceuticals
- Anti-inflammatory drugs to reduce swelling and inflammation
- Analgesics for pain
- Anti-emetics for vomiting
- Supportive care (IV fluids, seizure medications, ventilation)

## Research Evidence

### CB2 Signaling Controls CNS Inflammation

A 2013 study conducted on rodents by researchers at the University of Manitoba,
Winnipeg, Canada, discovered that CB2 signaling is a mechanism for controlling
CNS (central nervous system) inflammation during viral encephalitis. The
synthetic CB2 agonist HU308 (5 mg/kg intraperitoneally once daily) mitigated
inflammation during viral encephalitis by:

1. Limiting viral infection
2. Reducing microglia activation

Microglia are the resident immune cells of the central nervous system. When
overactivated, they contribute to neuroinflammation and neuronal damage. By
reducing microglia activation through CB2 signaling, cannabinoids may help
protect brain tissue from the inflammatory cascade that characterizes
encephalitis.

CBD, CBC, and CBG.

## Clinical Significance

Encephalitis can cause severe and potentially permanent neurological damage.
The finding that CB2 receptor activation can control CNS inflammation during
viral encephalitis is significant because it suggests a targeted
anti-inflammatory approach that works through the body's own endocannabinoid
system rather than broad-spectrum pharmaceuticals. CB2 receptors are primarily
expressed in immune cells, meaning CB2-targeted therapies may reduce
inflammation without the psychoactive effects associated with CB1 activation.
