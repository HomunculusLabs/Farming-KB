# Mushroom Spawn Making and Pure Culture Methods

## The Fundamental Role of Spawn

Spawn is the essential inoculum of commercial mushroom cultivation — the living mycelium of Agaricus that is introduced into prepared compost beds to initiate the mushroom crop.

Without spawn, there is no mushroom crop.

The quality, purity, and vigor of the spawn are among the most critical factors determining the success or failure of a cultivation enterprise.

Duggar devoted an entire chapter of his 1915 treatise to spawn-making, reflecting its central importance to the industry.

The term "spawn" refers to the vegetative phase of the mushroom organism growing in visible or extensive quantity within a suitable substratum.

It is not a seed, not a cutting, but a living network of fungal threads ready to colonize fresh compost and ultimately produce mushrooms.

The development of reliable spawn-making methods was the key technological advance that made commercial mushroom cultivation possible.

## Traditional Brick Spawn

Historically, mushroom spawn was produced in the form of bricks — compacted blocks of manure and soil that had been naturally colonized by Agaricus mycelium.

The process began when growers observed vigorous patches of white mycelium in spent mushroom beds or in pastures where mushrooms had grown.

This colonized material was collected, mixed with fresh manure and loam, pressed into brick-shaped molds, and allowed to dry slowly.

The resulting bricks could be stored for months and shipped over long distances.

When a grower wished to start a new bed, the bricks were soaked in water to rehydrate them, broken into smaller pieces, and buried in the prepared compost.

If conditions were favorable, the mycelium would revive from dormancy and spread through the bed.

## Limitations of Brick Spawn

This system had serious limitations.

The spawn was of unknown genetic purity — it might contain mycelia of multiple fungal species, some beneficial and some harmful.

The vigor of the mycelium was unpredictable.

Different batches of brick spawn varied enormously in quality.

The grower had no way of assessing the viability of the spawn until weeks after planting, when it either grew or failed to grow.

Often, inferior or useless spawn was responsible for complete crop failures.

The American market was particularly plagued by inconsistent spawn quality, as the country lacked the long tradition and tight quality controls of the French champignonnières.

## Flake Spawn (French Method)

The French developed a form of spawn known as flake spawn, which differed from brick spawn in both preparation and form.

Rather than being compressed into bricks, the colonized compost was broken into irregular flakes or pieces and used in a fresh state.

French growers considered flake spawn superior to brick spawn because it was not subjected to the stresses of drying and rehydration.

The mycelium remained in a more active state, leading to faster colonization of new beds.

Flake spawn was prepared in caves and champignonnières, where conditions for maintaining active mycelium were ideal.

It could not be stored or transported as conveniently as brick spawn, which limited its use primarily to regions near Paris where the cave system was concentrated.

The French method produced spawn of excellent quality but was not easily adaptable to the needs of American growers, who often received their spawn from distant suppliers.

## The Pure Culture Revolution

Duggar's principal contribution to mushroom cultivation was the improvement of spawn-making methods through the establishment of pure culture techniques.

Pure culture spawn is produced from a single isolated strain of Agaricus mycelium, grown under sterile conditions in the laboratory, and then propagated into a suitable substratum.

This approach eliminates the uncertainty of traditional spawn and provides a product of known genetic identity and proven vigor.

The pure culture method begins with the collection of a perfect, mature mushroom specimen.

Under sterile conditions, a small piece of tissue is excised from the interior of the cap or stem and transferred to a nutrient agar medium in a Petri dish.

If the tissue is from a healthy specimen and the transfer is performed without contamination, the mycelium will grow out from the tissue fragment and colonize the agar surface within a few days.

Once a clean culture is established, it can be maintained indefinitely through serial transfers to fresh agar plates.

The mycelium can also be used to inoculate larger quantities of sterilized grain, compost, or other substrata to produce commercial quantities of spawn.

## Advantages of Pure Culture Spawn

The advantages of pure culture methods over traditional brick spawn were substantial and transformative for the industry.

**Known identity:** The grower knows exactly which strain of mushroom has been planted. Different strains may vary significantly in yield, quality, and disease resistance.

**Freedom from contaminants:** Pure culture spawn is free from the mycelia of competitor fungi and from bacterial and viral pathogens that may have been present in traditional spawn.

**Vigor and reliability:** Spawn produced from actively growing laboratory cultures is far more vigorous than spawn that has been dried, stored, and rehydrated.

**Strain improvement:** Pure culture methods allow the selection and propagation of superior strains, opening the possibility of genetic improvement through selective breeding.

Duggar noted that the improvement of American spawn under the stimulus of the pure culture method was directly responsible for the increased reliability and profitability of the American mushroom industry.

Before pure culture methods were adopted, growers frequently experienced complete failures due to poor-quality or contaminated spawn.

## Quality Assessment

The quality of spawn could be assessed by several criteria.

Fresh, undried spawn of Agaricus campestris had a pleasing aroma of fresh mushrooms combined with a slight degree of powdered almond scent.

The mycelium should be white, vigorous, and spreading actively through the substratum.

Any discoloration, off-odor, or patchy growth indicated contamination or deterioration.

Commercial spawn was graded by the density and uniformity of mycelial colonization.

A brick of high-quality spawn would show white threads throughout when broken open, with no dark or discolored patches.

The substratum itself should be properly fermented compost — short, trashy, or poorly prepared material would not support vigorous mycelial growth.

## Application in Beds

Spawn was introduced into prepared compost beds by burying pieces of brick spawn at regular intervals throughout the bed, typically at a depth of 2 to 3 inches.

Flake spawn was scattered on the surface and lightly covered with compost.

The spacing between spawn pieces affected the speed of bed colonization and the uniformity of the resulting crop.

Closer spacing gave faster, more even colonization but increased the cost of spawn.

After spawning, the bed was covered with a layer of soil or "casing" material, which provided moisture retention and created conditions favorable for fruiting.

The spawn then grew through the compost for several weeks before pin heads appeared, signaling the beginning of the harvest period.

## Historical Significance

The development of pure culture spawn-making represented one of the most important advances in the history of applied mycology.

It transformed mushroom cultivation from an uncertain, experience-based craft into a more predictable and scientifically grounded agricultural practice.

The principles established by Duggar and his contemporaries — sterile technique, strain selection, quality control — remain the foundation of modern mushroom spawn production, even though the specific methods and substrata have been refined over the subsequent century.
