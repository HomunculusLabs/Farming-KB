# Neoshamanic Medicine Circles — Structure and Practice

## Overview

Ralph Metzner coined and extensively documented the phenomenon of "neoshamanic
medicine circles" — hybrid ritual forms that blend elements of traditional
shamanic healing ceremonies with Western psychotherapeutic approaches. Over
more than a decade of participant-observation research, Metzner attended over
100 such circle rituals in both Europe and North America, involving several
hundred participants, many of them repeatedly. His analysis provides the most
detailed ethnographic account available of contemporary Western entheogenic
practice outside of organized religious contexts.

## Definition and Context

Neoshamanic medicine circles represent what Metzner called a "hybrid of the
psychotherapeutic and traditional shamanic approaches." They are distinguished
from both clinical psychedelic therapy sessions and traditional indigenous
ceremonies by their intentional synthesis of elements from multiple traditions.
Participants in these circles tend to be experienced in one or more
psychospiritual practices — including shamanic drum journeying, Buddhist
vipassana meditation, tantra yoga, holotropic breathwork, and various forms of
psychotherapy and body-oriented therapy.

## Core Structural Elements

Metzner identified a consistent set of structural elements that characterize
most neoshamanic medicine circles:

- **Circle formation**: Participants sit or lie in a circle, the universal
  shape of ceremonial gathering across cultures.

- **Central focus**: An altar in the center of the circle (or a fire if
  outside or in a tipi) serves as the ceremonial focal point. The altar
  typically holds objects of personal and spiritual significance.

- **Experienced guidance**: An experienced elder or guide presides, sometimes
  with one or more assistants. The guide's role combines elements of the
  traditional shaman (ceremony leader, singer) and the Western therapist
  (facilitator, holder of space).

- **Low-light environment**: A preference for semidarkness or low light
  facilitates the emergence of visions. Eye-shades are sometimes used to
  deepen the interior focus.

- **Musical accompaniment**: Drumming, rattling, singing, or evocative
  recorded music provides a rhythmic and emotional container for the
  experience. The music is not merely atmospheric but actively structures
  the flow of consciousness during the ceremony.

- **Sacred space dedication**: The ritual space is dedicated through
  invocation of spirits of the four directions and elements, a practice
  drawn from both Native American and European pagan ceremonial traditions.

- **Respectful attitude**: Participants cultivate a spiritual, reverent
  approach to the experience, distinguishing it from recreational drug use.

## The Council Practice

A distinctive feature of many neoshamanic circles is the use of the "talking
staff" or "singing staff," a practice that appears to have originated among
the Indians of the Pacific Northwest and is now more broadly referred to as
"council." In this format, only the person who holds the circulating staff may
sing or speak. There is no discussion, questioning, or interpretation of the
kind that would occur in standard group psychotherapy.

This structure has several important effects:

1. It gives each participant undivided attention and space for expression.
2. It prevents the analytic, interpretive patterns of group therapy from
   overriding the direct, experiential quality of the entheogenic state.
3. It creates a contemplative, non-judgmental atmosphere that supports
   vulnerability and honesty.
4. It honors the indigenous roots of the practice by preserving the
   ceremonial form of council.

Some group sessions, however, involve minimal or no interaction between
participants during the time of the expanded state of consciousness, allowing
each person to engage fully with their own interior process.

## Preparation and Integration

Neoshamanic circles typically include structured preparation and integration
phases:

**Preparation** may include sharing of intentions and purposes among
participants, meditation, solo time in nature, or expressive arts modalities
such as drawing, painting, or journal work. This preparatory phase helps
participants clarify their intentions — a crucial factor given the importance
of "set" in determining the quality of the entheogenic experience.

**Integration** usually occurs the morning after the ceremony and involves
participants sharing something of the lessons learned and how they might be
applied in their daily lives. This phase addresses one of the central
challenges of entheogenic work: translating extraordinary experiences into
lasting personal transformation.

## Plant Medicines Used

The entheogenic substances used in these circles have included:

- **Psilocybe mushrooms**: The most commonly used medicine in contemporary
  Western neoshamanic circles, valued for their accessibility and relatively
  manageable duration of effect.

- **Ayahuasca**: Used in circles that have direct connections to Amazonian
  traditions, or that have developed their own brewing and ceremonial
  practices.

- **San Pedro cactus** (*Echinopsis pachanoi*): Used in circles influenced
  by Andean curanderismo traditions.

- **Iboga**: Used less frequently, typically in circles with specific
  connections to Bwiti or iboga treatment traditions.

## Participant Demographics and Belief Systems

Metzner noted that the majority of Westerners who develop an ongoing practice
of working with entheogenic plant substances have expanded their belief
systems beyond the boundaries of conventional materialistic science and
psychology. While accepting the validity of many Western psychological
insights — including those of Freud, Jung, and Reich — these practitioners
have come to accept the reality of nonmaterial spirit beings and to recognize
that human beings exist within multiple worlds or dimensions of consciousness.

This expansion of belief is not a regression to pre-scientific superstition,
in Metzner's view, but a pragmatic response to direct experience. The
entheogenic state produces phenomenological content — encounters with beings,
visions of other worlds, experiences of unity and transcendence — that cannot
be adequately accounted for within a purely materialist framework.
