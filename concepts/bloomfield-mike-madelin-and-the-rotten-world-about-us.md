# Mike Madelin and The Rotten World About Us

Among the mycologists who shaped [[nicholas-money]]'s career, Mike
Madin at Bristol University holds a special place as the teacher
whose influence converted a young biology student into a lifelong
mycologist. Money's account of Madelin reveals how a single
inspiring educator, combined with the power of televised science,
can redirect the trajectory of an entire scientific life.

## Madelin as a Teacher and Mentor

Money describes Madelin as his favorite teacher at Bristol
University, a star among a group of quite brilliant botany
lecturers. What distinguished Madelin was his simple, understated
style, which suited the material he taught about fungi. Money
hung on every word Madelin uttered. This kind of intellectual
captivation, occurring at age eighteen, is more often associated
with the work of an evangelist than a university lecturer.

Madelin's influence extended beyond Money himself. Nick Read,
the cell biologist who tested the limits of spore durability in
the book's discussion of ascospore resilience, was similarly
awestruck by Madelin and credits him with his own career choice.
The conversion of multiple students to mycology through contact
with a single teacher speaks to Madelin's exceptional ability to
communicate the wonder of [[bloomfield-fungal-biology-and-hyphal-growth]].

## The Rotten World About Us

Madelin's impact on Money was amplified by a remarkable piece of
timing. Shortly before Money left home for university, a British
television documentary called "The Rotten World About Us"
appeared. In this film, Madelin was featured as a mycologist
explaining the role of [[query-what-is-the-role-of-fungi-in-decomposition]] and ecological
processes. The documentary brought [[fungal-biology-fundamentals]] to a mass
audience, showing time-lapse sequences of mushrooms growing,
[[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]] dissolving, and the relentless work of decay
that fungi perform in forests and fields.

For Money, seeing his future teacher on television before
meeting him in person created a sense of destiny. The
documentary served as a bridge between public science
communication and personal mentorship. Madelin was not merely
an academic lecturing from a podium; he was a figure who had
already entered Money's imagination through the medium of
broadcast television.

## The Chain of Mentorship

Money's narrative reveals a chain of mentorship that connects
generations of mycologists. Madelin taught Money at Bristol.
Money then pursued doctoral work under John Webster at Exeter,
whose own career had been shaped by earlier mentors. Webster
introduced Money to field mycology on forays in Devon, and
through Webster, Money eventually met [[terence-ingold-mycologist]], the
retired mycologist who had been his neighbor in Benson,
Oxfordshire, without Money realizing it.

This chain illustrates how scientific knowledge and enthusiasm
are transmitted through personal relationships. The technical
content of mycology can be learned from textbooks, but the
passion that sustains a research career is typically ignited
by direct contact with an inspiring individual. Each link in
the chain both preserves the tradition and adds something new,
as Money did by bringing the narrative approach of popular
science writing to mycology.
