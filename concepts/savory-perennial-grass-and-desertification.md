# Perennial Grass and Desertification

In [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]],
perennial grass is identified as the key indicator species for land health
in [[savory-brittle-environments]]. The fate of
perennial grasses symbolizes the issue of [[savory-desertification-causes]], because the most seriously deteriorating parts of the
world lack the rainfall to support any other kind of stable soil cover.
Annual grass populations fluctuate greatly and may leave ground bare for
months, sometimes failing to grow at all.

## Perennial Grass as Foundation

In a very direct sense, perennial bunch grass underpins the fortunes of
the politicians, bankers, businessmen, ranchers, tribespeople, and citizens
who have a stake in over 50 percent of the earth's surface, which is
brittle environment and serves as the world's watersheds. Where perennial
grass communities thrive, all four [[savory-ecosystem-foundation-blocks]] function effectively.

Perennial grasses provide year-round soil cover, deep root systems that
cycle water and minerals, continuous [[savory-energy-flow-and-solar-capture]] through all seasons, and a stable base for
[[savory-plant-succession-and-community-dynamics]] to
advance toward complexity.

## The Severe Grazing Paradox

Consider a healthy perennial grass plant in a brittle environment. A large
animal appears and bites all stem and leaf down to an inch or two. This is
severe grazing, but not unusual or bad. Certain animals evolved to graze
in such a manner in harmony with such grasses over millions of years.

In the growing season the plant receives a short-term setback but a
long-term boost. The bitten plant demands time for recovery but may still
finish the season better off and less encumbered than its neighbors. The
growth points at the base remain intact, and no old growth stands in the
way of regeneration.

If the bite comes in the dormant season, when the plant has no use for the
leaves and stems, it loses nothing and gains an uncluttered start on the
new season. On balance, severe grazing benefits the grazed plant.

## Overgrazing: The Time Problem

[[savory-holistic-resource-management-overgrazing]] occurs when a plant bitten
severely in the growing season gets severely bitten again before it
completely recovers from the first bite. This can happen because the plant
is exposed to the animals for too long or because they return too soon.

The consequences are serious because of how grasses use and store energy.
Most perennial grasses transfer energy and protein from leaves and stems
to stem bases and roots at the end of the growing season. This reserve
carries the plant through the dormant period and supports the next year's
first growth.

If bitten during the growing season, when reserves have been used in
early growth, grasses can only take energy from growing roots, allowing
them to die back to put out new shoots. Repeated severe defoliation kills
roots and eventually most plants.

## Root Sacrifice and Soil Life

The organic material in the sacrificed root benefits the organisms living
within the soil, adds to soil structure, and provides a medium for
regrowth. All goes well if the root has time to regrow once the new leaf
can supply adequate energy. A second bite before root regrowth requires
the sacrifice of yet more root. Subsequent bites without root regeneration
may demand sacrifices to the point of plant death.

## Annual vs Perennial Grasslands

The shift from perennial to annual grasslands is a clear symptom of
desertification. In California, for example, annual grasses now dominate
most rangelands where perennials once thrived. The annual grasslands
produced by poor management illustrate the problem clearly: prolonged
periods exist in every year when no growth at all is taking place, as the
perennial grasses that could and should be converting energy have
disappeared.

Remnant patches of perennial grassland continue to grow actively and
convert energy to fuel the rest of the ecosystem, while surrounding annual
grasses have long since finished their cycle, dried off, and died. A
change in management could bring back the perennials and restore months of
productivity to the area.

## Perennial Grass and Energy Flow

The annual grassland situation dramatically illustrates lost [[savory-energy-flow-and-solar-capture]]. A high and diverse level of
succession includes enough cool-season and warm-season grasses to ensure
that some part of the plant community will perform at peak capacity as
long as any growth is possible. Perennial grasses provide this year-round
capability that annuals cannot match.

The growing time side of the energy equation is directly affected by the
perennial vs annual composition. Perennial grasses can begin growing the
instant temperature and moisture conditions allow, while annuals must first
germinate from seed, extending the period of zero energy conversion.

## Perennial Grass Die-Off from Overrest

In brittle environments, the accumulation of old growth around perennial
grass plants can be as deadly as overgrazing. Without some form of
disturbance, such as [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]], old oxidizing material blocks sunlight from reaching growth
points, reduces air circulation, and favors the establishment of mosses
and algae that compete with the grass.

Large-scale death of perennial grasses has been documented on sites that
have been rested for many years. The plants become grey and dead, wide
spaces open between tufts, and new seedlings become scarce. This is the
delayed effect of [[savory-rest-as-management-tool]] in brittle
environments.

## Perennial Grass and Water Cycles

Perennial grass communities support effective [[savory-water-cycle-management]] through their deep, persistent root systems.
These roots create channels for water infiltration, add organic matter to
the soil, and maintain soil structure that prevents capping. When
perennial grasses die and are replaced by annuals or bare ground, the
water cycle deteriorates dramatically.

## Management for Perennial Grass Health

Maintaining perennial grass health in brittle environments requires
disturbance through [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]]
combined with adequate [[savory-recovery-and-grazing-periods]]. The goal is severe grazing without overgrazing, which removes
old growth, stimulates new tillering, and maintains root vigor while
allowing sufficient time for root regrowth between defoliations.
