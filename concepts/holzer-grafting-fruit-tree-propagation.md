# Grafting and Fruit Tree Propagation

Sepp Holzer's approach to fruit tree propagation through grafting reflects
his broader philosophy of working with nature rather than against it. While
he employs standard grafting techniques, his choices of rootstock, scion
selection, and overall strategy differ significantly from commercial fruit
growing practices. His methods prioritise tree longevity, hardiness, and
independence over early fruiting and ease of harvest. This approach is
central to [[holzer-grafting-fruit-forest]] and [[sepp-holzer-fruit-tree-varieties]].

## Why Graft?

Cultivated fruit trees are not usually propagated using seeds, because the
characteristics of the desired variety will not be passed on true through the
seeds. Many fruit cultivars can now only be pollinated by different varieties,
meaning every flower can produce different seeds and consequently different
characteristics. To preserve the qualities of a certain variety, it must be
propagated vegetatively through [[fruit-tree-grafting-guide]].

Grafting joins a shoot or scion from a desired variety to a rootstock. The
practice was developed because a tree with beautiful and aromatic fruit does
not necessarily grow well. A grafted tree consists of at least two different
plants with qualities which complement each other, combining the positive
growing characteristics of the rootstock with the qualities of the cultivated
fruit.

## Rootstock Selection: Vigorous vs Dwarf

For grafting to be successful, both partners must "get along" with each
other. Only certain plants can be used as a rootstock, mostly members of the
same species, although related species can sometimes be used. The rootstock
generally determines the growing characteristics of the grafted plant and
affects characteristics such as disease resistance and frost tolerance.

Today, dwarf rootstocks are used for most fruit trees because they keep the
tree small and make it fruit earlier. An example is the practice of grafting
many pear varieties on quince as a rootstock, because it does not grow as
vigorously and slows down the growth of the pear.

Holzer, however, prefers rootstocks from vigorous growing seedlings and wild
varieties for his method of grafting. He rejects dwarf varieties as
rootstocks for several reasons:

- Dwarf rootstocks do not develop strong root systems, which is one of the
  most important conditions for an independent tree
- The weak roots mean trees frequently have to be tied to stakes to prevent
  them being knocked over by wind or snow
- They cannot supply themselves as well with nutrients, requiring good soil
  or even fertiliser
- They are more sensitive to drought and usually much more susceptible to
  disease and frost
- They have shorter lifespans compared to vigorous rootstocks

## Benefits of Vigorous Rootstocks

For Holzer's requirements he needs hardy and independent trees which can
thrive on poor soil and in unfavourable locations. These needs are best
fulfilled by rootstocks from vigorous and hardy seedlings. Their strong
growth means they will fruit a few years later and also grow taller, which
makes harvesting the fruit a little more difficult. But Holzer accepts all of
this happily.

Dwarf rootstocks would not grow so well on the farm. On the other hand, the
characteristics of vigorous rootstocks and the cultivation they make possible
would save a great deal of work in terms of maintenance. If the amount of
work saved in terms of maintenance by grafting onto vigorous rootstocks is
taken into consideration, the greater amount of work required to harvest them
is put into perspective.

A further point which speaks for using vigorous growing rootstocks is the
fact that they live for much longer. So Holzer can plant a [[holzer-fruit-forest-establishment]] that will continue to provide food for the next
generation. This long-term perspective is fundamental to the [[sepp-holzer-method]].

## Scion Selection

Strong and sturdy perennial shoots should be used for scions. Water sprouts
are not suitable. The middle part of the shoot is used for grafting, which
should have three to five buds on it. Scions should be cut during the dormant
months in winter (January is best) and stored until they are grafted in
spring.

It is best to store scions in a cellar in wet sand. Scions can also be cut
and used fresh for grafting. If you do this, it is best to use the shoots
straight after they are cut. If you find a nice tree somewhere and want to
take a scion from it, you must stop the scion from drying out with a damp
cloth. If scions are cut in spring or summer, cut the leaves off and leave
about 1cm on each leaf stalk.

## Grafting Techniques

The aim of grafting is to bind the rootstock and the scion so that they grow
together. It is necessary to achieve good contact between the cambium layers
of the rootstock and scion. The cambium is the cell layer between the bark
and wood, responsible for growth by cell division. Only when these layers are
well joined will the graft be successful.

### Whip and Tongue Grafting
For this method the rootstock and scion must be of the same thickness. Holzer
usually whip and tongue grafts young plants after the first or second year of
growth in spring. The graft is made at the root collar, cutting the rootstock
to around 10cm above the ground at an angle. The cut must be three to four
centimetres long and done in a single stroke.

A tongue is cut in both the rootstock and the scion. Both cut areas must fit
together flush so that the cambium layers join properly. The graft is wrapped
with raffia, keeping the buds uncovered. To stop the graft from drying out or
getting infected, it and any open cuts are painted with grafting wax.

### Cleft Grafting
A cleft graft is used when the rootstock is thicker than the scion. The
simplest type is the bark graft, generally carried out in May when the bark
can easily be peeled back. The stem of the rootstock is cut straight at the
desired height and most of the twigs are removed, leaving a few to draw sap.
Two scions are then inserted into the bark, with the cambium layers aligned.

## The No-Pruning Philosophy

Holzer's approach to grafting is closely connected to his [[holzer-fruit-trees-no-pruning]] philosophy. By using vigorous rootstocks and allowing
trees to grow to their natural size, he avoids the need for the extensive
pruning that dwarf trees require. The resulting trees may be taller and
slightly harder to harvest, but they are far more self-sufficient, disease-
resistant, and long-lived.

This approach also means that grafted trees are better suited to
integration with livestock. Tall, vigorous trees can withstand browsing
pressure better than small dwarf trees, making them more compatible with
[[holzer-livestock-integration]] systems.
