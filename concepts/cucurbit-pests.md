## Cucurbit Pests

The [[solomon-cucurbit-family-growing-squash-melons-cucumbers]] — cucumbers, melons, squash, pumpkins, and related species — faces a distinctive set of insect pests across [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]]. From cucumber beetles to squash borers, these insects can devastate crops, but smart strategies exist to minimize losses without resorting to heavy chemical use.

## Cucumber Beetles (Striped and Spotted)

### Identification

Two types of this [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] pest, about 1/4 inch (6 mm) long:
- **Striped** (Acalymma vittatum/trivittatum): Three parallel lines running head to tail
- **Spotted** (Diabrotica spp.): Various patterns on their backs

### Life Cycle

Both species overwinter in spring. In the north, striped beetles overwinter while southern populations migrate northward. Weather and garden location can greatly alter their numbers.

### Damage

- **Seedlings**: Chew on leaves, somewhat stunting plants
- **Roots**: Their larvae feed on roots, weakening plants
- **Adults on fruit**: Scarring of fruit skins and reduced yields
- **Virus transmission**: Overwintering beetles sometimes transmit a virus wilt disease that kills seedlings

### Food Preferences

Their favorite food is cantaloupe and similar melons. They also feed on cucumbers, then squash, and watermelon last. This knowledge of [[invertebrate-fungivory-and-feeding-preferences]] can inform crop placement.

### Control Strategies

- **Sow later**: Be a few weeks later than most in your area to sow all cucurbits — lets seedlings get growing more vigorously before beetles arrive
- **Plant 4 seeds per hill**: Thin to 2 plants per spot only after seedlings have made a true leaf and are growing fast (where beetles aren"t a problem, 1 plant per hill is better)
- **Row covers**: Protect each hill of emerging seedlings under a carefully anchored [[floating-row-cover]] until plants begin blooming, then remove to allow bee pollination
- **[[comparison-free-living-vs-parasitic-nematodes]]**: Control larvae if applied by mixing into soil when seedlings are small
- **Avoid excessive nutrients**: Make seedlings less succulent and thus less attractive
- **Direct seed**: Greenhouse-grown seedlings are always lush and succulent, making them more vulnerable

### Resistance

Yellow Crookneck shows the highest resistance to the cucumber beetle.

## Squash Vine Borer (Melittia satyriniformis)

### The Threat

In North America east of the Rockies, the borer can be highly destructive because a single one can cause the loss of an entire plant.

### Life Cycle

- Adult moth emerges from soil in spring
- Lays eggs singly on stems at the bases of squash plants
- A few dozen moths can infect an entire commercial pumpkin field
- Hatched larvae enter the plant near the base (marked by a "gob" resembling sawdust)
- Larvae tunnel "upstream" toward the center, destroying the vascular system
- Causes the runner or whole plant to wilt and die
- Tunnelling continues for 4-6 weeks
- If the vine dies before the borer completes its life cycle, it migrates to another plant

### Preference

The borer prefers squash and pumpkins; usually has little interest in cucumbers or melons.

### Resistant Varieties

- Butternut
- Green-Striped Cushaw
- Yellow Crookneck
- Dickinson pumpkin

Yellow Crookneck may be the best garden choice because its sprawling habit makes inspection easier and it shows the highest resistance to both borer and cucumber beetle.

### Control

- **Patrol plants**: Inspect every few days for signs of borers (sawdust-like frass at stem base)
- **Surgical removal**: If signs appear, slice the vine starting at the entry hole toward the center to find and destroy the borer
- **Soil burying**: For vining squash, toss a shovelful of soil on top of each point where leaves emerge from vines — encourages secondary roots to form at each point
- **Bt injection**: Some gardeners use a syringe to inject Bt var. kurstaki into the stem
- **Sanitation**: After harvest, promptly burn winter squash vines or [[compost]] them thoroughly
- **Later planting**: May allow you to evade the moths, which only fly and lay eggs for a short period

### Why Multiple Plants Per Hill

The borer is the reason squash is traditionally grown several plants per hill — as insurance — even though all cucurbits grow much better if only one plant occupies each hill.

## Squash Bug (Anasa tristis)

### Identification

About 5/8 inch (1.5 cm) long, dark brown or mottled, with an unpleasant odor. Children call them stinkbugs.

### Damage

Adults and larvae suck plant juice and inject a toxin that causes:
- Wilting
- Individual runners may blacken and die back
- Resembles bacterial wilt, but plants recover when bugs are brought under control

Considered the most destructive American cucurbit pest.

### Life Cycle

Overwinter under whatever cover is available. Emerge in spring to feed and lay eggs. Eggs are about 1/16 inch (1.5 mm) long, placed in neat rows on leaf undersides. Larvae start feeding upon hatching. In the south, multiple generations occur each summer.

### Control

- **Handpicking**: The home gardener's primary remedy
- **Sanitation**: Burn or thoroughly compost all squash vegetation. Dig up the root mass at the center and compost that too
- **Board traps**: Place boards on the ground overnight — bugs gather underneath and can be collected in the morning
- **Beneficial habitat**: Grow a mixed garden with lots of cover for beneficials, including buckwheat stands for tachinid fly parasites
- **Bt**: Not useful — biological pesticides are effective only against small larvae, and egg-laying continues for months

### Why Pesticides Don't Work

- Biological pesticides only work on small larvae
- Egg-laying goes on for several months
- Most organic pesticides last only a short time
- Vines grow rapidly, requiring repeated spraying on leaf undersides
- [[comparison-parasitoid-wasps-vs-chemical-pesticides]] kill pollinating bees
- Numerous beneficials eat squash bugs — spraying kills them too
