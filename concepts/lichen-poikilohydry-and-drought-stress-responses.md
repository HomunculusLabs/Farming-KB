## Poikilohydric Water Relations

Lichens possess poikilohydric water relations, meaning their water
content varies passively with environmental conditions. This contrasts
with homoiohydric plants that regulate their internal water status. Most
lichen-forming fungi and their photobionts are fully adapted to
continuous wetting and drying cycles, as experienced during diurnal and
seasonal fluctuations in water availability. However, they die off under
continuously moist conditions or when prevented from drying out at
elevated temperatures.

### Metabolic Activity Windows

Polar, alpine and desert lichens all face principally the same problem:
photosynthetic activity of their photoautotrophic partner is restricted to
a narrow time window when thalli are at least partly hydrated and solar
radiation is available at temperatures within the range suitable for
photosynthesis. Thalline growth rates depend on the frequency and length
of such time windows per day and year.

In the Negev and Namib Deserts, lichen photobionts photosynthesize
during a short period at sunrise, when thalli are partly hydrated from
fog and/or nocturnal dewfall before both symbionts dry out and fall into
dormancy. Annual rates of gas exchange were measured in Ramalina
maciformis (photobiont, Trebouxia sp.) in the Negev Desert. A positive
balance was recorded on 218 days, a negative balance on 88 days and
balance zero (due to drought-induced dormancy) on 49 days.

## Photobiont Types and Habitat Preferences

Approximately 85% of lichen-forming ascomycetes have green algal
photobionts (chlorolichens), 10% have cyanobacterial photobionts
(cyanolichens) and roughly 4% have both simultaneously (cephalodiate
lichens). Drought-stressed chlorolichens with Trebouxia photobionts can
rehydrate and re-establish at least partial metabolic activities with
water vapour alone, but cyanolichens require liquid water for full
recovery.

Accordingly, chlorolichens with Trebouxia spp. dominate in arid, hot or
cold ecosystems, but cyanolichens with internally stratified thalli
(Peltigera, Sticta and Nephroma spp.) are abundant in moist habitats.
Gelatinous cyanolichens (Collema spp.) dominate in soil crust communities
in arid ecosystems with erratic rainfall events.

## Drought-Stress-Induced Structural Changes

Drought-stressed lichen thalli shrink without wilting, but reveal a
different consistency and colour from the fully hydrated state. The
cortex of fully hydrated thalli is elastic and glassy-translucent, but
becomes opaque and brittle, usually greyish, in the desiccated state.
The severity of drought-stress-induced shrinkage depends on thalline
morphology: crustose and squamulose thalli shrink mainly in the vertical
axis, but thalli with no tight contact to the substratum shrink in all
dimensions.

## Symplastic Cavitation

Drought-stress-induced cytoplasmic gas bubbles in fungal cells were first
described by de Bary (1866). Cells with thin, hyaline walls undergo
cytorrhysis (collapse): they shrivel and shrink during desiccation, often
achieving a boat-like shape. This applies to the green algal and
cyanobacterial photobiont cells of lichen thalli.

### Reversible Cavitation in Living Cells

Cells with thick, rigid walls cannot be sufficiently deformed during
water loss and therefore cavitate (implode). Reversible symplastic
cavitation is a regularly occurring feature in lichen-forming fungi under
drought stress. Unlike xylem cavitation in plants, which affects dead
cells, drought-stress-induced cavitation of fungal cells affects the
living cell itself.

Desiccated lichen thalli contain less than 20% water per unit dry mass.
The cortex rehydrates within less than 30 seconds when water is applied
to the thallus surface, but it takes more than a minute to fully
rehydrate the whole cortex. A gradient from complete desiccation to full
hydration can therefore be studied among cortical cells of samples that
have been cryofixed during rehydration.

## Polar and Alpine Adaptations

Temperature and water availability are also the limiting factors in polar
and alpine lichens. Frost is the main water source available to lichens
of high Andean Paramos. Trebouxia photobionts of polar lichens are
photosynthetically active even at sub-zero temperatures, at low photon
flux densities and in a partly hydrated state. Covered by snow, ideally
with ice windows due to melting and freezing cycles above darkly pigmented
thalli, lichens live as in a greenhouse, well protected from the harsh
climate. Very low growth rates were recorded among lichens of the
continental Antarctic, but surprisingly fast growth was documented in
lichen communities in the maritime Antarctic.

## Water Retention Mechanisms

Water can temporarily be retained in the thallus by hydrophilic, amorphous
fungal wall components such as lichenin, a linear (1,3),(1,4)-beta-glucan,
which overlies the cell wall proper. Lichen-forming fungi do not protect
their photobiont from desiccation, as is often assumed. Both symbionts
are fully adapted to continuous wetting and drying cycles, which are an
integral part of their lifestyle and the driving force behind passive
fluxes of solutes between the partners.

## Fog Oasis Communities

The most conspicuous chlorolichen communities develop in desert areas
with very low precipitation, but frequent periods of high atmospheric
moisture as found in coastal fog oases (Atacama Desert in South America,
Namib Desert in South Africa). These harbour high proportions of fog
comb lichens: band-shaped (Ramalina or Roccella spp.) to finely branched
species (Usnea or Teloschistes spp.) with high surface to volume ratios
that maximize water capture from fog droplets.
