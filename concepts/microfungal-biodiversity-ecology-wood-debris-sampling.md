# Microfungal Biodiversity — Ecology, Limitations, and Sampling on Wood and Debris

Microfungi — the often-overlooked microscopic fungi — constitute the vast majority of fungal diversity. They colonize wood, leaf litter, soil, living plant surfaces, and virtually every other substratum. Their study presents unique challenges due to their small size, complex life cycles, and the paucity of ecological data.

## Scale of Microfungal Diversity

- Microfungi likely represent the majority of undescribed fungal species
- Individual tropical leaves can harbor up to **30 different species** of hyphomycetes
- Temperate substrata (Fagus/Quercus cupules, Castanea spines) regularly support 5-10 species
- Most species known only from isolated collections; ecological information almost entirely lacking

## Major Challenges

### Size
- Fruiting bodies as small as 50 um diameter
- Conidiophores of some hyphomycetes only ~10 um long
- Spores may be 2-3 um long, often <1 um wide
- Few species exceed 100 um spore length
- Direct field observation impossible; requires compound microscopy

### Teleomorph-Anamorph Connections
Many microfungi have multiple morphologically distinct reproductive stages:
- **Teleomorph:** Sexual (meiotic) spore-bearing stage
- **Anamorph:** Asexual (mitotic) spore-bearing stage
- **Synanamorphs:** Multiple asexual stages in same species

Challenges:
- Anamorph may or may not precede teleomorph
- Timing of different morphs often unknown
- Two or three synanamorphs may form (even in same conidioma)
- Example: Phomopsis species with different anamorphs at different seasons
- Example: Diplocarpon rosae — anamorph (Marssonina) forms on living leaves; teleomorph forms on fallen, overwintering leaves

### Current Knowledge Gaps
- No comprehensive database of species distributions or ecological preferences
- Most ecological data from temperate agricultural systems
- Tropical microfungal ecology almost entirely unstudied
- Species concepts for many necrotrophic genera (Colletotrichum, Phomopsis) under revision based on DNA evidence

## Ecological Patterns

### Substratum Specialization
- Many saprobes specialize on substrata with particular physical characteristics, NOT particular host taxa
- Key substratum features: longevity, topography (spininess, venation), rigidity, water retention
- Climate patterns (rainfall extent/distribution) also promote specialization

### Host Specificity Reassessment
- DNA analysis shows traditional host-based classifications are often artificial
- Colletotrichum and Phomopsis species concepts being revised
- Didymosphaeria on dicotyledonous plants: species do not correlate with host identity
- Necrotrophs traditionally regarded as strongly host-specific; evidence increasingly contradicts this

### Succession
Fungal succession varies at very fine scales:
- Upper vs. lower internodes of grass stems (Hudson and Webster 1958)
- Standing vs. uprooted culms (Webster and Dix 1960)
- Upper vs. lower surfaces of individual fallen leaves (A. Rambelli, Sardinia)
- Characteristic sequences on dung pats, decaying wood, leaf litter

### Extreme Environments
Distinctive guilds in:
- **Desert leaf axils and rosettes** (Agave): moisture-dependent colonization
- **Bromeliad water traps** (wet tropics): specialized aquatic communities
- **Aeroaquatic habitats:** Periodic inundation specialists
- **Cactus surfaces:** Restricted diversity despite available substratum

## Sampling Methods

### Direct Microscopic Examination
- Most reliable for detecting what is actually present
- Painstaking mounting of vegetative and spore-forming structures
- Compound microscopy essential
- Only way to assess mixed populations without culturing

### Culture-Based Methods
- Particle washing or particle filtration (borrowed from soil mycology)
- Allows identification through sporulation in culture
- Overcomes nonsporulating field collections
- May select for fast-growing species; miss slow-growers

### Moist Chamber Technique
- Place substratum in humid chamber
- Induces fruiting of fungi present but not sporulating
- Particularly useful for litter-inhabiting ascomycetes and small agarics
- See [[moist-chamber-technique-fungal-fruiting]]

## Accessory Vegetative Structures

Many microfungi produce persistent vegetative structures for survival:
- **Melanized fruiting structures:** Protect spores from drought, cold, UV
- **Melanized hyphae:** Withstand adverse conditions
- **Chlamydospores:** Thick-walled resting spores
- **Sclerotia:** Compact masses of hyphae
- **Bulbils:** Small propagules for asexual reproduction
- **Appressoria:** Infection structures
- **Setae:** Stiff sterile hairs

Species in vegetative condition are often unidentifiable and must be cultured to induce sporulation.

## Biomass Considerations

- Microfungal biomass difficult to measure
- Hyphal length and biovolume measurements possible but laborious
- Ergosterol assays estimate total fungal biomass
- Phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis distinguishes fungal from bacterial biomass
- Substrate-induced respiration provides functional measure
