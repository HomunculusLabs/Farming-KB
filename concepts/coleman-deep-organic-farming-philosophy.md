# Coleman's Deep-Organic Farming Philosophy

[[soil-life]].

## Deep Organic vs. Shallow Organic (Input-Substitution)

Coleman draws a sharp distinction between "deep-organic" and
"shallow-organic" farming. This distinction is not about certification
status but about underlying philosophy and practice.

**Deep-organic farmers**, in addition to rejecting agricultural
chemicals, look for *better ways to farm*. Inspired by the elegance
of Nature's systems, they try to mimic the patterns of the natural
world's soil-plant economy. They understand that the underlying cause
of pest problems is plant stress, and they avoid pest problems by
managing soil tilth, nutrient balance, organic matter content, water
drainage, air flow, crop rotations, and varietal selection to reduce
that stress. Their goal is to grow the most nutritious food possible
and to respect the primacy of a healthy planet.

**Shallow-organic farmers**, after rejecting agricultural chemicals,
look for *quick-fix inputs*. Trapped in a belief that the natural
world is inadequate, they end up mimicking the patterns of chemical
agriculture. They use bagged or bottled organic fertilizers to
temporarily treat symptoms of infertile soil. They treat insect and
disease symptoms by arming themselves with the latest natural organic
weapons. Their goal is merely to follow approved guidelines and
respect the primacy of international commerce.

The difference is a difference in life views. The shallow view regards
nature as consisting of mostly inadequate, usually malevolent systems
that must be battled or modified. The deep-organic view understands
that nature consists of elegant, impeccably designed, smoothly
functioning systems that must be studied and nurtured.

## The Question: "Is It Nutritious?"

Coleman argues that the consumer question should not be "is it
organic?" but "is it nutritious?" Organic farming must be more than
the absence of negatives — it must be the presence of positives: the
full nutritional complement that Nature designed food to contain.

He draws an analogy from music: hearing a great artist perform
directly, without a sound system, is a fundamentally different
experience from hearing the same artist through speakers. Fresh food
from a local deep-organic grower is like that unfiltered music. The
same food shipped from far away is like hearing it through a sound
system — much is lost in transmission.

## Real Food and the Future of Small Farms

Coleman's philosophy culminates in a vision of "real food" — food
that is fresh, local, minimally processed, and grown with the
nutritional intention that deep-organic farming provides. He
believes that small, committed, organic family farms are the fastest
growing segment in U.S. agriculture, and that old-time deep-organic
farming will save these farms because there will always be demand for
exceptional food by customers who can see past the hype of labels and
make fully informed decisions about food quality.

## Practical Considerations

Successful implementation of Coleman's Deep-Organic Farming Philosophy requires attention to
several practical factors including environmental conditions,
resource availability, and timing. Careful monitoring and
adaptive management help optimize outcomes across varying
conditions. Integration with other system elements enhances
overall effectiveness and creates beneficial synergies that
improve resilience and productivity over time.
