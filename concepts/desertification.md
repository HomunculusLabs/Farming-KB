## Desertification

Desertification is the process by which fertile land gradually transforms into
desert due to climate variations and human activities. It represents one of
the most significant environmental threats globally, affecting over 2 billion
people across every continent. The United Nations Convention to Combat
Desertification (UNCCD) estimates that 12 million hectares of productive land
are lost annually to desertification and
drought.
## Causes

- **Overgrazing**: Removing vegetation cover faster than it can regenerate, exposing soil to wind and rain
- **Deforestation**: Loss of tree cover reduces rainfall interception and soil stability, disrupting local hydrological cycles
- **Poor irrigation practices**: [[masanobu-fukuoka]] observed that "rain does not fall from the heavens; it issues
forth from the ground. Deserts do not form because there is no rain; rather,
rain ceases to fall because the vegetation has disappeared." This insight
challenges conventional approaches that treat the symptom (lack of water)
rather than the cause (loss of vegetative cover and soil
biology).
Building dams in the desert, Fukuoka argued, is an attempt to treat symptoms
rather than address root causes. The strategy for reversing desertification
must focus on restoring vegetative cover, which in turn restores the
microclimate and hydrological cycle that generate
rainfall.
## The Role of Soil Microbiology

One gram of healthy soil can contain approximately 100 million nitrogen-fixing
bacteria and other soil-enriching microbes. Fukuoka believed that soil
containing seeds and these microorganisms could be the spark that restores the
deserts. Modern [[fukuoka-desert-greening-projects]] and

- **Holistic grazing**: Mimicking natural herd movement patterns to stimulate plant growth

## Global Scale and Impact

Desertification affects approximately one-third of the Earth's land surface.
In Africa's Sahel region, the advancing Sahara Desert has displaced millions
of people. In China, the Gobi Desert expands by thousands of square kilometers
annually. The economic costs include lost agricultural productivity, displaced
populations, and increased conflict over diminishing
resources.
