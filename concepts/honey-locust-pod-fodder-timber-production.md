Honey locust (Gleditsia triacanthos) is a moderately fast-growing,
long-lived leguminous tree native to North America. It combines
high-quality stock fodder from its pods with valuable timber, making
it a dual-purpose tree of exceptional value in permaculture systems.
Requiring 425 to 1,500 mm of rainfall for good growth, it survives
up to altitude 2,100 metres and is tolerant of both alkaline and
acid soils, as well as salt.

## Growth Form and Characteristics

The tree commonly reaches 24 metres, with trunk diameters of 60 to
90 cm. Its deep root system provides exceptional drought resistance.
Being deciduous, it allows light through to pasture or understorey
during winter months, making it ideal for silvopastoral systems.
Mature trees are resistant to frost.

Branches generally form thorns that protect against ring-barking by
stock. Thornless cultivars do exist, although seedlings may revert
to thorny forms. The thorns themselves can serve as a livestock
deterrent in specific fencing applications.

## Pod Fodder: Nutritional Analysis

Honey locust pods fall from female trees in autumn and are readily
eaten by pigs, sheep, cattle, and goats. Nutritional analysis shows
impressive values: protein 14.3 to 16 percent, sugar 15.5 to 26.5
percent, carbohydrate 60.5 percent, fat 7.5 percent, calcium 0.2
percent, and crude fibre 18.4 percent. Pods are considered superior
to oats as a stock feed.

Yield increases significantly with age. At year 12, expect 9 tonnes
per hectare per year at a density of 36 trees per hectare. By year
15, yield reaches 18 tonnes. Mature groves have achieved 50 to 60
tonnes per hectare per year in selected North American plantings.

Pods can also serve for human food and alcohol fuel production. The
sweet pulp can be eaten fresh or processed into various products.

## Timber Qualities

Honey locust timber is hard, heavy, and strong, with heartwood
reported to last 100 years untreated in the ground. It is a valued
furniture timber. The tree coppices freely and suckers from damaged
roots, providing renewable wood supplies. It is also a good fuelwood.

## Role in Permaculture Systems

The honey locust fits naturally into silvopastoral systems where its
winter leaf-drop allows pasture growth beneath it while summer canopy
provides shade. The pods provide autumn fodder without any
harvesting or processing cost, as stock simply eat fallen pods
directly from the ground.

In a food forest system, honey locust occupies the upper canopy
layer while permitting productive understorey layers. Its nitrogen-
fixing capacity improves soil fertility for companion plantings. The
deep taproot accesses subsoil minerals and moisture unavailable to
shallow-rooted species, cycling these nutrients to the surface
through leaf fall.

## Propagation

Propagation from seed is similar to carob. Seeds require scarification
to break dormancy, typically achieved by soaking in hot water or
acid treatment. The tree can also be propagated by transplanting root
suckers, which is useful for maintaining thornless cultivars.

For pod production, both male and female trees are needed. A ratio of
approximately one male to eight or ten females ensures adequate
pollination. Selecting for heavy pod-bearing females during
establishment ensures maximum fodder returns.

## Companion Planting Considerations

Because honey locust is deciduous and provides dappled shade, it
combines well with pasture grasses, winter-active annuals, and
shade-tolerant perennials. In orchard systems, it can serve as a
windbreak that does not compete with fruit trees for winter light.

The nitrogen fixed by root nodules benefits neighbouring plants.
In mixed livestock systems, the tree's pods feed ruminants while
poultry forage for insects in the leaf litter beneath. This multi-
layer utilisation exemplifies permaculture's principle of stacking
functions within a single element.

## Comparison with Related Fodder Trees

Unlike carob, which requires Mediterranean climates, honey locust
tolerates much colder winters and a wider range of soil conditions.
Its faster growth and higher pod yields make it more productive in
temperate permaculture systems. Combined with black locust for
fence posts and nitrogen fixation, honey locust provides the fodder
component of a self-reliant livestock system that requires minimal
minimal external inputs.

## Establishing a Honey Locust Fodder System

Site preparation for honey locust should include deep ripping if
soils are compacted, as the tree develops a deep taproot in its
early years. Planting at 36 trees per hectare allows adequate
space for pod production while leaving room for pasture or crops
beneath. Closer initial spacing of 100 to 200 trees per hectare can
be used, with thinning for fence posts and fuel as the canopy
closes.

Protection from livestock during establishment is essential.
Tree guards or temporary fencing prevent browsing damage while
young trees develop their canopy. Once trees are tall enough that
cattle cannot reach the leading shoots, they can be safely
integrated into grazing systems.

The economic case for honey locust is compelling. At maturity, pod
yields replace tonnes of grain and supplementary feed annually. The
timber provides a perpetual supply of fence posts, and the tree
requires no annual planting or cultivation costs. Combined with
the soil improvement from nitrogen fixation and leaf litter, honey
locust represents one of the highest-return long-term investments
in a permaculture farm design.
