# Honey Locust Pod Fodder and Timber Production

Honey locust ([[gleditsia-triacanthos]]) is a moderately fast-growing,
long-lived leguminous tree native to [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]]. It combines
high-quality stock fodder from its pods with valuable timber, making it
a dual-purpose tree of exceptional value in permaculture systems.

The species requires 425 to 1,500 mm of rainfall for good growth and
survives up to altitude 2,100 metres. It tolerates both alkaline and
acid soils, as well as saline conditions. These traits make it one of
the most adaptable nitrogen-fixing trees available for temperate and
subtropical permaculture designs.

## Growth Form and Characteristics

The tree commonly reaches 24 metres in height, with trunk diameters
of 60 to 90 cm. Its deep root system provides exceptional drought
resistance once established. Being deciduous, it allows light through
to pasture or understorey during winter months, making it ideal for
silvopastoral systems. Mature trees are resistant to frost.

Branches generally form thorns that protect against ring-barking by
stock. Thornless cultivars do exist, although seedlings may revert to
thorny forms. The thorns themselves can serve as a livestock deterrent
in specific fencing applications. Young trees bear formidable thorns
up to 20 cm long on the trunk and major branches.

## Pod Fodder: Nutritional Analysis

Honey locust pods fall from female trees in autumn and are readily
eaten by pigs, sheep, cattle, and goats. The pods are sweet-tasting
and palatable to all classes of livestock. Nutritional analysis shows
impressive values:

- Protein: 14.3 to 16 percent
- Sugar: 15.5 to 26.5 percent
- Carbohydrate: 60.5 percent
- Fat: 7.5 percent
- Calcium: 0.2 percent
- Crude fibre: 18.4 percent

Pods are considered superior to oats as a stock feed in many feeding
trials. The high sugar content makes them an excellent energy source
for ruminants during autumn and early winter when pasture quality
declines. The protein content, while moderate, complements low-protein
dry pasture effectively.

### Yield Over Time

Pod yield increases significantly with age:
- At year 12: expect 9 tonnes per hectare per year at 36 trees/ha
- At year 15: yield reaches 18 tonnes per hectare per year
- Mature groves: 50 to 60 tonnes per hectare per year reported

Pods can also serve for human food and [[permaculture-on-farm-alcohol-fuel-production]]. The
sweet pulp can be eaten fresh, dried, ground into flour, or processed
into [[comparison-mead-vs-kvass-fermented-beverages]]. Indigenous peoples traditionally used the
pulp as a sweetener before the availability of cane sugar.

## Timber Qualities

Honey locust timber is hard, heavy, and strong, with a specific
gravity of approximately 0.65 to 0.70. The heartwood is reddish-brown
and is reported to last 100 years untreated in the ground. This
exceptional durability makes it ideal for fence posts, outdoor
construction, and landscape timbers.

The timber is a valued furniture wood with a fine, close grain that
takes a high polish. It machines well and is dimensionally stable.
The tree coppices freely and suckers from damaged roots, providing
renewable wood supplies without replanting. It is also an excellent
fuelwood with high energy density and relatively low ash content.

## Nitrogen Fixation and Soil Improvement

Like many leguminous trees, honey locust forms symbiotic associations
with rhizobial bacteria in root nodules. While its nitrogen fixation
rates are modest compared to black locust or tagasaste, the cumulative
effect of deep-rooted [[fungal-role-in-nitrogen-cycling]] is significant. Leaf fall
returns nitrogen and minerals to the soil surface, benefiting
understorey plants and pasture species.
