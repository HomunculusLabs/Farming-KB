# Vegetable Garden Watering Systems and Plant Spacing

The relationship between how closely vegetables are spaced and how much water
they require is the single most important decision a food gardener will make.
[[steve-solomon]]'s *Gardening When It Counts* presents four distinct spacing
systems, each calibrated to different [[solomon-climate-zones-and-growing-seasons]] water availability.
Understanding these systems allows gardeners to match their plant density to
their actual resources, avoiding both water waste and crop failure.

## The Four Spacing Systems

Solomon organizes plant spacing into four columns, ranging from intensive to
extensive. Each column reflects a fundamentally different approach to water
management and soil utilization.

**Column 1 — Intensive raised beds (Jeavons method):** These are the closest
spacings, typically found in [[coleman-british-adoption-of-french-intensive-gardening]] manuals. Plants are set
inches apart in massed plantings on deeply dug raised beds. A crop canopy
forms quickly, completely covering the bed with leaves. Once this canopy
forms, moisture loss accelerates so rapidly during sunny weather that the
gardener must water at least half an inch (1.25 cm) every single day. In
sandy soil during hot weather, daily watering may be insufficient. Solomon
does not recommend this system for serious food production.

**Column 2 — Semi-intensive raised beds (Solomon method):** This is the
system Solomon uses for most of his own garden. Plants are given roughly five
times more room per plant than in intensive beds. The crop does not form a
closed canopy so quickly, so each plant has far more root space and soil
moisture to draw from. Such a garden needs watering only every few days,
and in humid climates may go a week or more without irrigation. Yields per
[[comparison-biointensive-vs-square-foot-gardening]] are comparable to [[row-gardening-vs-intensive-methods-solomon]] because the individual
plants grow much larger and more productive.

**Column 3 — Extensive with good rainfall:** Small plants are grown on wide
raised beds, larger ones in slightly raised single rows, and sprawling crops
in hills far apart. Even after crop growth makes walking between rows
difficult, the garden may pass through rainless weeks without significant
moisture stress because plant roots have spread sufficiently to access
subsoil moisture through capillarity. Weeds are easy to hoe because rows
are wide. This system has been used by [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] gardeners since
colonial times.

**Column 4 — Extensive with little rain or fertigation:** These are the
widest spacings, designed for gardeners with limited water or poor soils.
This approach only works if the soil is open to considerable depth, weed
competition is minimal, and the soil surface is kept loose or mulched.
After intense summer thunderstorms, the gardener should loosen the surface
soil to accept the next rainfall and prevent evaporation of existing
moisture. It is vital that the surface inch of soil contain enough organic
matter to prevent crust formation.

## Drought: A Matter of Management, Not Providence

Solomon draws on John Widstoe's 1911 work *[[comparison-dry-farming-vs-xeriscaping]]*, which defined
drought not as a weather event but as a condition where crops fail to mature
due to insufficient water — and where the cause is usually human error.
Widstoe identified four causes of drought: (1) improper or shallow
preparation of the soil, (2) failure to use methods for keeping moisture
in the soil, (3) failure to apply proper cultural methods, and (4) sowing
too much seed for the available soil moisture.

The practical implication is that many so-called droughts are self-inflicted.
If a gardener senses dry conditions developing, the first response should be
to thin every row immediately — harvest every second carrot or beet, removing
half the plants. This instantly doubles the capillary moisture available to
each remaining plant. The result may not be losing half the crop at all; by
preventing severe moisture stress, the gardener may harvest more total
production from fewer, healthier plants.

## Fertigation: Water-Efficient Feeding

Fertigation combines watering with fertilizing by delivering a nutrient-rich
solution directly to the root zone of individual plants. A bucket with a
small drain hole is placed near the base of each plant and filled with
dilute manure tea, [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]], or soluble fertilizer. The water sinks
straight down, and the moisture penetrates deeply into the subsoil.

The volume and frequency depend on soil type. On clay soils (20 percent clay
or more), about five gallons (20 liters) every three weeks is appropriate.
On sandy soils with less moisture-holding capacity, perhaps half that amount
— 2.5 gallons (10 liters) every ten days — works better. The results can
be remarkable: Solomon reports that a single fertigated [[winter-squash-guide]] vine
yielded 50 pounds (22 kg), compared to 20 pounds (9 kg) for un-fertigated
vines. Thirty pounds of produce from 20 gallons of water represents an
exceptionally efficient exchange.

Fertigation is especially valuable for large plants: tomatoes, melons,
cucurbits, peppers, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, and broccoli. It is also
wise practice even when plants otherwise have enough water, because the
[[solomon-fertigation-bucket-drip-water-nutrient-delivery]] goes directly to the root zone where it is most effective.
