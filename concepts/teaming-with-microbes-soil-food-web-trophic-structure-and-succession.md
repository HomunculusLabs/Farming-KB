The teaming with microbes soil food web trophic structure and succession is organized into
trophic levels -- layers of feeding relationships where energy flows from
primary producers through consumers and decomposers. Understanding this
structure is essential for managing soil biology to support specific
types of plants.

## Trophic Levels in Soil

### Level 1: Primary Producers

Photosynthetic organisms form the base of the soil food web. Plants are
the dominant primary producers, capturing solar energy and converting it
to organic compounds through photosynthesis. They release root exudates
(sugars, proteins, carbohydrates) into the
[[root-exudates]], fueling the
entire belowground food web.

Algae (including [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-tea-and-microbial-inoculants]])
are photoautotrophic -- they also capture solar energy and produce their
own food, contributing to primary production particularly on bare soil
surfaces.

### Level 2: Decomposers and Mutualists

[[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria]] and [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi]]
are the primary decomposers. They consume organic matter (dead plant
material, root exudates, and other organisms) and break it down into
simpler compounds. In the process, they incorporate nutrients into their
biomass -- a process called [[teaming-with-microbes-mineralization-and-immobilization]].

A teaspoon of good garden soil contains approximately 1 billion bacteria
and several yards of fungal hyphae. These organisms serve as nutrient
reservoirs, holding nitrogen, phosphorus, and other minerals in their
cells until released by higher trophic levels.

Mycorrhizal [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] occupy this
level as mutualists, trading mineral nutrients and water for plant
carbohydrates in a symbiotic relationship with roots.

### Level 3: Grazers and Microbial Feeders

[[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-types-amoeba-flagellate-ciliate]]
(amoebae, flagellates, ciliates) and
[[teaming-with-microbes-nematodes]] feed on bacteria and fungi. This
grazing is the key mechanism of
[[teaming-with-microbes-mineralization-and-immobilization]]
-- when these predators consume bacteria and fungi, they absorb the
nutrients they need and excrete the excess as plant-available ammonium.

As much as 80% of the nitrogen a plant needs comes from the wastes
produced by bacteria- and fungi-eating protozoa and nematodes.

### Level 4: Higher Predators

Larger arthropods including [[teaming-with-microbes-mites-and-soil-arthropod-predators]],
[[teaming-with-microbes-springtails-and-collembola]], beetles,
centipedes, and spiders feed on the microbial grazers. Some are generalist
predators (centipedes, spiders), while others like gamasid mites
specialize in hunting soil microarthropods. Their predation regulates
populations at lower trophic levels.

### Level 5: Top Predators

 Birds, moles, shrews, and other small mammals feed on the larger soil
arthropods and earthworms. While these vertebrates are often considered
aboveground organisms, they play an important role in the soil food web
by redistributing nutrients (through guano) and dispersing microorganisms
(on their feet and in their digestive tracts).

## Bacterial vs Fungal Dominance: The Succession Continuum

A central insight from the soil food web framework is the bacterial-to-
fungal succession continuum. This concept describes how the ratio of
fungal to bacterial biomass (F:B ratio) shifts along an ecological
gradient from disturbed, early-succession soils to mature, late-succession
ecosystems.

### The Succession Sequence

1. **Bare soil / Disturbance** (weeds, pioneer plants):
   Bacterially dominated. F:B ratio near 0.1:1. Bacteria rapidly colonize
   disturbed sites, feeding on simple sugars and easy-to-digest organic
   matter.

2. **Grassland / Prairie** (perennial grasses):
   Transitional, with roughly equal bacterial and fungal biomass.
   F:B ratio approximately 1:1. This is the zone where lawn grasses
   naturally thrive.

3. **Shrubland** (shrubs, soft hardwoods):
   Increasingly fungally dominated as woody plant material accumulates.
   F:B ratio 5:1 to 10:1.

4. **Forest** (mature hardwoods):
   Strongly fungally dominated. F:B ratio 10:1 to 100:1. Large amounts
   of lignin-rich woody debris support extensive fungal networks.

5. **Old-growth / Conifer forest**:
   Maximum fungal dominance. F:B ratio 50:1 to 1000:1. Conifers produce
   the most recalcitrant litter, supporting vast mycorrhizal networks.

### Why This Matters for Gardening

The plant-soil food web relationship is co-evolved:

- **Annual vegetables and flowers** evolved in disturbed, bacterially
  dominated soils. They prefer nitrate nitrogen and an F:B of 0.3:1 to
  0.8:1.
- **Tomatoes, corn, wheat** prefer a moderate F:B of 0.8:1 to 1:1.
- **Lawns** (perennial grasses) prefer an F:B of 0.5:1 to 1:1.
- **Deciduous trees and shrubs** require F:B of 10:1 to 100:1.
- **Conifers** (rhododendrons, spruce, pine) require F:B of 50:1 to
  1000:1.
- **Riparian trees** (alder, beech, aspen) prefer bacterial soils when
  young but shift to fungal soils (F:B 5:1 to 100:1) when mature.

### Managing the F:B Ratio

Gardeners can shift the F:B ratio by adjusting inputs:

- **Green materials** (grass clippings, kitchen scraps, fresh weeds):
  High in simple sugars and nitrogen; feed bacteria
- **Brown materials** (autumn leaves, bark, wood chips, sawdust):
  High in [[fungal-decomposition-ecology]] and
  [[fungal-cellulose-decomposition-fungal-enzymes]]; feed fungi
- **Humic and fulvic acids, kelp, phosphate rock dust**: Support fungal
  growth
- **Molasses, simple sugars**: Support bacterial growth
- **Tillage**: Destroys fungal hyphae, shifting balance toward bacteria
- [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-tea-and-microbial-inoculants]] can
  be brewed with either bacterial or fungal dominance depending on
  ingredients
