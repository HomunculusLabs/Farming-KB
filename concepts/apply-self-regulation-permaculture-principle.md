## Positive and Negative Feedback

In systems theory, positive feedback acts as an accelerator, amplifying a
process or effect. Negative feedback acts as a brake, preventing systems from
overrunning their limits. Both types of feedback are essential for healthy
system function. In systems well adapted to their energy base, positive
feedback pushes the system toward freely available energy, while negative
feedback prevents falling into holes of scarcity from overuse.

Examples of positive feedback include:
- In a bushfire, heat from burning preheats green vegetation, making it more
  readily combustible; extreme cases create massive updrafts drawing in more
  oxygen
- Plants use captured sunlight to grow more leaves, which capture more energy
- Reed growth in a billabong slows water flow and increases sedimentation,
  leading to more plant growth and conversion to swamp

## The Gaia Hypothesis as Archetype

The Gaia hypothesis of James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis provides the most
powerful example of self-regulation at a planetary scale. The earth is
understood as a self-organised system that has maintained conditions suitable
for life over billions of years through complex feedback mechanisms involving
the atmosphere, oceans, biosphere, and geology.

Microbial life plays a core role in Gaia. The hypothesis suggests that if
humanity seriously affects fundamental life-support systems, co-evolutionary
mechanisms such as climate change or disease will act to neutralise the threat.

adaptive management.

## Top-Down Thinking, Bottom-Up Action

This principle restates the well-known environmental slogan "think globally,
act locally" through the lens of systems thinking. Top-down thinking involves
stepping back to consider the large-scale systems and connections within which
a design problem is embedded. Bottom-up action focuses on the leverage points
available to individuals and small groups to influence those larger systems.

The prevailing mode of action in modern society is characterised as "top-down"
(dominant) action by governmental and corporate management. What is needed is
more bottom-up (participatory) action at all levels in natural and human
systems. This applies equally to managing rangelands and forests and to
fostering appropriate community change.
