## Pond Water Quality Management

Water quality is the single most critical factor determining the success or failure of aquaculture systems. Dissolved oxygen levels, pH, temperature, salinity, and the management of wastes and nutrients all interact in complex ways that directly affect fish growth, survival, and reproduction. Understanding these parameters and their management is essential knowledge for any [[permaculture]] aquaculture designer.

### Dissolved Oxygen

All life processes and [[decomposition]] in aerated waters consume oxygen. The general level of 5 parts per million (ppm) dissolved oxygen is considered very satisfactory for pond production. At 1 ppm, many fish species will die, except for a select group of adapted air-breathers used in stagnant and weedy tropical still ponds.

#### Air-Breathing Fish Species

Several remarkable fish species have evolved specialised respiratory structures that allow them to survive in oxygen-depleted waters:

- **Snakeheads (Ophiocephalus)** — predatory air-breathers common in Asian rice paddies
- **Singhi (Heteropneustes)** — Indian catfish with accessory respiratory organs
- **Walking catfish (Clarias)** — highly adaptable air-breathing catfish used widely in aquaculture
- **Mahi (Notopterus)** — featherback fish with air-breathing capability
- **Climbing perch (Anabas)** — labyrinth organ fish that can survive in stagnant conditions

These species possess air sacs, labyrinth organs, or special air chambers near the gills from which they can derive oxygen by breathing atmospheric air. While most other fish die in the anaerobic conditions of weedy tropical shallows, these adapted species — along with lungfishes and many minor species — not only survive but can produce remarkable yields. Derelict swampy ponds can yield 2,400 kg of protein per hectare per year when stocked with a polyculture of three or more air-breathing species.

#### Oxygen Dynamics

Less oxygen dissolves in warm water than in cold water, creating a critical interaction between temperature and dissolved oxygen. Additionally, aquatic plants that produce oxygen through photosynthesis during daylight hours consume it through respiration at night. This creates a dangerous situation: healthy, weed-filled shallow ponds may develop severe oxygen deficiency on warm summer nights when plant respiration depletes dissolved oxygen while warm water holds less of it.

If valuable fish stocks are maintained in such conditions, management must address both the reduction of organic waste levels and the provision of automatic aeration at critically low dissolved oxygen levels. These dangers are less severe in clear, cold, open, or flowing pond systems where oxygen deficiency is rarely a concern.

Oxygen supply becomes a limiting factor in fish stock density at levels above approximately 5,000 kg per hectare, beyond which supplemental oxygen as air bubbles must be supplied.

#### Aeration Methods

Several strategies exist for maintaining adequate dissolved oxygen:

- **Commercial aerators** — many models available, including solar-powered options
- **Gravity-fed systems** — wherever water can be led from a head of 2 metres or more, fountains, showers, and "Flowforms" (sculpted flow channels that create vortical mixing) will oxygenate ponds
- **Partial aeration** — aerating only part of the pond at critical periods; fish will naturally gather ("kettle") in the oxygenated zone
- **Combined feeding-aeration stations** — locating feeding areas in oxygenated zones so food wastes are oxidised, simplifying waste management

### Water Classification

Waters are generally classified according to their oxygen status:

**Aerobic** — oxygen is well-supplied by wind overturn, turbulent flow, aerators, or rapids, with a light load of decaying organic material. These are the most productive and manageable waters for general aquaculture.

**Facultative** — many swamps and weed-clogged ponds, and secondary treatment sewage ponds. The surface may be aerated, but sediment or sludge collects during cold periods and the pond base becomes anaerobic. Sulphur-fixing bacteria decompose sediments under these conditions. These waters can be productive but require careful management.

**Anaerobic** — primary sewage ponds and over-fertilised shallows where dissolved oxygen is 1 ppm or less. Few species survive these conditions, though the adapted air-breathers noted above can thrive.

### Acidity and Alkalinity (pH Management)

Pond pH is ideally maintained between 6.0 and 8.5. Most fish grow well within this range, though salmonids are among the few satisfactory species in monoculture and can tolerate somewhat more acidic conditions. Lethal limits for most fish are pH 3.7 (acid) and pH 10.5 (alkaline).

#### Acidic Water Sources

Ponds situated in areas of peats, mangroves, cordgrass flats, samphires, and with water derived from heaths and granites or siliceous soils, can be very acidic (pH 4.0 or less). The acids involved include humic acids, tannins, and minor organic acids. In such environments, hydrogen sulphide may also be released by ponded peats or swamps, creating sulphuric acid.

Remediation strategies include:
- Peat stripping and mounding
- Freshwater or rain leaching
- Liming with burnt lime (initial dressing) followed by unburnt lime (maintenance)
- Siting ponds where run-off from limestone areas can be captured
- Selecting sites with naturally high pH levels

#### The pH Mosaic Approach

In practice, a pH mosaic across ponds and pond series can be established. Many valuable food organisms prefer soft (acid) water, while fish, molluscs, and freshwater lobsters and prawns prefer hard (alkaline) waters. Crushed dolomite, marble chips, hard limestone gravels, and oyster or mussel shells provide slow-release calcium in tanks, small ponds, intake filter systems, and the upper sections of canal systems.

For breeding soft-water species, only organic acids are used — chiefly hydrochloric, humic, or phosphoric acids. The same effect can be achieved by using peat or dense moss areas to filter water, laying peats on the pond base, or creating swampy conditions around the culture pond.

#### pH Interactions

There is an important interaction between pH, respiration, and plant density. pH may rise at night or in clogged coastal waters with Chara and other algal forms, or fall as peats form and anaerobic conditions develop. These fluctuations require monitoring, especially in densely planted or organically enriched systems.

### Mud, Silt, Humus, and Waste Management

Wastes of any nature in ponds reduce water quality if they occur in excess or as dead material. A useful distinction can be made between rotting wastes (which create anaerobic conditions) and fertilisers (which stimulate growth). The latter need not create excessive growth if herbivorous fish, nutria (a large aquatic rodent), or shrimp are available to consume the detritus or weeds that would otherwise die and decompose anaerobically.

#### Waste Removal Strategies

In flowing canals or well-aerated ponds, waste management is less problematic. Still ponds, however, need regular monitoring and maintenance:

- **Drain and lime** — draining the pond, applying lime, and taking a season of dryland crop (clover, fenugreek) before reflooding
- **Clear bank access** — maintaining at least one bank free of trees for dredging or bucket removal of muck
- **Composting** — traditionally, muck is layered with green crop for compost in Asia
- **Mulch retention** — in padi and terrace systems, 2–5 cm of mulch is retained over the clay base for cropping

In deeper non-draining reservoirs, jet pumps serve as vacuum cleaners to pump detritus from the bottom, removing material up to gravel size. This material can be deposited as adjacent retaining walls, low islands, or causeways where silt settles out and water flows back to the pond free of detritus. These pumps are typically raft-mounted and liquid-fuel powered.

### Integrated Rice-Fish Systems

The integration of fish with rice cultivation represents one of the oldest and most productive polyculture systems. Fish refuges in rice paddies are constructed either as peripheral canals or as deep sumps to contain fish between flooding for crop.

Temperatures in rice pond water may reach 34°C, with optimum fish growth at 22–28°C. In tropical Indonesia, fish reach pan size at 10–12 weeks, while in cooler Japan, it may take 2–3 years of summer seasons.

Weedy rice paddies are partly cleared by stocking species such as:
- **Trichogaster pectoralis** — gourami that consumes weeds
- **Clarias macrocephalus and C. batrachus** — walking catfish for weed control
- **[[ophiocephalus-striatus]]** — snakehead acting as predator on small fish
- **Anabas testudineus** — climbing perch for additional weed control

Optimal polyculture mixtures have been established through extensive trials:
- **Mixture 1:** Helostoma temmincki (35%), Osteochilus hasselti (15%), [[cyprinus-carpio]] (15%), Puntias gonionotus (35%)
- **Mixture 2:** Sarotherodon mossambicus (50%), Helostoma (20%), Osteochilus (15%), Puntias (15%), Cyprinus carpio (10%)

With such combinations, the extra crop of fish in rice reaches 70–400 kg per hectare per year across water temperatures of 10–34°C. The fish also help control malarial mosquitoes, providing multiple benefits beyond protein yield. Crayfish ([[cambarus-clarkii]]) are additionally stocked in some rice padi systems.
