# Mycorrhizal Carbon Sequestration

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the vast majority of
terrestrial plant species, fundamentally shaping both the terrestrial carbon
cycle and the composition of plant communities worldwide. Jonathan Leake's
chapter in "Fungi in the Environment" examines how mycorrhizas serve as a
critical conduit for carbon flow from the atmosphere into long-term soil carbon
pools, and how their influence extends beyond nutrient exchange to determine
which plant species dominate in given ecosystems.

## Mycorrhizal Types and Carbon Dynamics

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF)
Arbuscular mycorrhizas, formed by Glomeromycota fungi, associate with
approximately 80% of all terrestrial plant species. In this symbiosis:

- The fungal partner receives 10-20% of the host plant's photosynthetically
  fixed carbon (recent estimates suggest up to 30% in some systems)
- Carbon is transferred primarily as glucose or fructose across the
  arbuscule interface within root cortical cells
- AMF produce copious amounts of glomalin, a glycoprotein that contributes
  significantly to stable soil carbon pools
- Glomalin persists in soil for 7-42 years, making it one of the most
  persistent forms of soil organic carbon

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (EMF)
Ectomycorrhizas, formed primarily by Basidiomycota and some Ascomycota, are
characteristic of temperate and boreal forest trees (pine, oak, beech, spruce):

- EMF receive a substantial proportion of host photosynthate (often
  15-25% of net primary production in forest ecosystems)
- Unlike AMF, EMF produce extensive external [[mycelial-networks-and-intelligence]]
  differentiated mycorrhizal roots (short roots) with a Hartig net
- EMF produce significant quantities of recalcitrant organic compounds
  including melanized hyphal walls that resist decomposition
- These melanized tissues contribute to long-term carbon storage in forest
  soils, particularly in boreal and temperate ecosystems where decomposition
  rates are slow

## Carbon Sequestration Mechanisms

### Direct Pathways
- **Hyphal turnover**: Mycorrhizal hyphae continuously grow and die in soil,
  contributing carbon directly to soil organic matter. The total mycorrhizal
  mycelial biomass in many ecosystems rivals or exceeds that of fine roots
- **Exudates and necromass**: Fungi release organic compounds (exudates)
  during growth and leave behind structural residues (necromass) upon death,
  both of which contribute to soil carbon pools
- **Glomalin production**: AMF-specific glomalin contributes to soil
  [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]], physically protecting organic carbon from microbial
  decomposition

### Indirect Pathways
- **Nutrient mining**: By accessing nutrients (especially phosphorus) that
  are unavailable to plant roots alone, mycorrhizas enhance overall plant
  productivity and therefore total ecosystem carbon fixation
- **Soil aggregation**: Mycorrhizal hyphae and their exopolymer secretions
  bind soil particles into stable aggregates, physically protecting
  enclosed organic matter from decomposition
- **Decomposition priming**: Mycorrhizal exudates can stimulate or suppress
  the activity of free-living decomposer organisms, altering the rate at
  which existing soil organic matter is broken down

## Influence on Plant Community Composition

Mycorrhizal fungi do not benefit all plant species equally. This differential
effect shapes plant communities through several mechanisms:

### Differential Mycorrhizal Responsiveness
- Plant species vary enormously in their dependence on mycorrhizal
  associations, ranging from obligately mycorrhizal species to facultative
  and non-mycorrhizal species
- Highly mycorrhizal-responsive species (many grasses and legumes) gain
  competitive advantages in mycorrhizal soils
- Non-mycorrhizal species (many Brassicaceae and Cyperaceae) may be
  competitively excluded from mycorrhizal-dominated communities

### Common Mycorrhizal Networks (CMNs)
- Mycorrhizal hyphae can interconnect multiple plants of different species,
  creating common mycorrhizal networks
- Through CMNs, carbon and nutrients can be transferred between plants,
  potentially supporting seedlings and suppressed individuals
- These networks can mediate plant-plant interactions, sometimes
  facilitating coexistence and sometimes amplifying competitive dominance

### Soil Feedback Mechanisms
- [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] can generate plant-soil feedback loops that
  either promote or inhibit specific plant species
- Changes in mycorrhizal community composition following disturbance or
  vegetation change can redirect successional trajectories
- Invasive plant species often form novel mycorrhizal associations that
  confer competitive advantages over native species

## Implications for Climate Change

The role of mycorrhizal fungi in carbon cycling has significant implications
for understanding and responding to climate change:

- Rising atmospheric CO2 concentrations generally increase belowground
  carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi, potentially enhancing soil
  carbon sequestration
- However, warming temperatures may accelerate decomposition of existing
  soil carbon, partially offsetting increased inputs
- Changes in precipitation patterns may shift the balance between AMF-
  and EMF-dominated ecosystems, with cascading effects on carbon storage
  dynamics
- Understanding mycorrhizal carbon dynamics is essential for accurate
  global carbon cycle models and for developing land management practices
  that maximize soil carbon storage

## Measurement and Quantification Challenges

Quantifying mycorrhizal contributions to [[savory-soil-carbon-sequestration]] remains
methodologically challenging. Techniques include isotopic tracing with 13C or
14C labelling, molecular quantification of fungal biomarkers (ergosterol,
phospholipid fatty acids, and glomalin-related soil protein), and direct
microscopy of root colonization. Each approach has limitations in temporal
and spatial resolution, and reconciling estimates across methods remains an
active area of research.
