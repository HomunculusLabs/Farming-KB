# Gymnosporangium Cedar-Apple Rust

## Overview

Gymnosporangium globosum, the cause of hawthorn rust (cedar-hawthorn
rust), is a basidiomycete fungus requiring two completely different
host plants to complete its life cycle. On hawthorns and fruit trees
it produces yellow-spotted leaves and galled berries covered with
horn-like spore structures. On evergreens including cedars and
junipers it produces entirely different spores. This alternation
between two unrelated hosts, called heteroecism, is characteristic
of many rust fungi and represents one of the most complex life
cycles in the fungal kingdom.

## Disease Symptoms on Hawthorn

Fungi are at war with the plants in gardens. An ornamental hawthorn
is attacked by a rust in wet years. The visible symptoms include:

- Leaves become covered with yellow spots
- Red berries are transformed into pale galls
- Galls are covered with spore-filled horn-like structures
- Masses of spores are produced at the base of hollow chimneys
- Elongating towers of infectious cells continually replace the
  uppermost spores that escape into the air

Hawthorn rust is caused by Gymnosporangium globosum, which also
infects fruit trees and evergreens including cedars and junipers.
The nearest apples may be less than 100 feet away, and there are
usually plenty of ornamental junipers in managed landscapes. By
showering spring and summer breezes with spores, the rust has no
problem finding food. Although its diet of diverse tree species
argues for broad infection mechanisms, Gymnosporangium cannot
compete with the dietary flexibility of generalist fungi.

## The Heteroecious Life Cycle

Gymnosporangium behaves similarly on hawthorns and fruit trees, but
produces entirely different spores on evergreens. This alternation
between two unrelated hosts is called heteroecism.

### Stage 1: Overwintering on Juniper (Telial Host)

The fungus overwinters as spherical galls on evergreen branches and
twigs, persisting for more than a year. In spring, triggered by warm
rains, the galls swell and produce gelatinous orange telial horns.
Within these horns, thick-walled resting teliospores are produced,
serving as the bridge between hosts. Teliospores germinate to
produce basidiospores, released into the air and carried by wind.

### Stage 2: Infection of Hawthorn or Apple (Aecial Host)

Basidiospores land on rosaceous host leaves or fruit and germinate,
establishing new infection through two sub-stages:

- **Spermagonial stage:** Flask-shaped pycnia form on upper leaf
  surfaces, producing spermatia and receptive hyphae for
  cross-fertilization between compatible mating types
- **Aecial stage:** Following fertilization, cup-shaped aecia
  develop on leaf undersides and fruit, producing aeciospores
  that must find a juniper host to complete the cycle

## Comparison with Black Stem Rust

Puccinia graminis, causing black stem rust of wheat, shares the
heteroecious lifestyle but has multiple distinct life cycle stages.
Only one stage, the uredial pustule on wheat, is actually
rust-colored, exposing reddish uredospores. In heavily diseased
crops, a cloud of uredospores blankets the field after a wind gust.
With permissive winds, spores travel hundreds of miles, so one
infected crop can spawn an epidemic. Rusts are obligate biotrophs,
growing only on living host tissue, which adds to the difficulty of
studying and controlling them.

## Infection Mechanisms

Rust spores germinate on the waxy leaf surface, sending out a germ
tube that hunts for an opening. The leaf surface is rippled with
microscopic hills and valleys. At intervals, stomata open and close
to regulate gas exchange. When open, the plant is vulnerable because
each stoma is a portal to succulent interior tissues.

Spores germinate in morning humidity when stomata are open. Germ
tubes grow across the leaf, rising over parallel hills and falling
into intervening valleys, maneuvering to find stomata. By growing
across rather than along the leaf, the rust is far more likely to
find a stoma, since only one in five or fewer cell lines contain
stomata.

## Physical Cue Recognition

When the germ tube detects the thin lips around a stoma, it inflates
over the opening and plunges inside. How does it find the stoma?
The lips are raised less than a millionth of a meter; a stray
bacterium is taller. Cornell plant pathologist Harvey Hoch made
plastic replicas of leaf surfaces and found germ tubes recognized
and swelled over model stomata, demonstrating that physical cues
alone suffice. Harvey fabricated films with defined ridge heights;
the fungus recognized ridges matching stomatal lip height (0.5
micrometers) but crawled over lower or higher ones. He then
manufactured mazes for his rusts, predicting growth patterns before
seeding surfaces with spores.

## Practical Management

Gymnosporangium rusts are economically significant. Management
strategies include removing junipers near orchards, fungicide
applications timed to telial spore release, and resistant cultivars.
The visual drama of orange gelatinous telial horns emerging from
juniper galls after spring rains makes this one of the most
memorable plant diseases.
