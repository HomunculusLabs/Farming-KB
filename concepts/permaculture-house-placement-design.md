# Permaculture House Placement Design

updated: 2026-18-04

House placement in permaculture is one of the most critical design decisions, as it determines energy efficiency, comfort, productivity of surrounding zones, and resilience to fire, wind, and flood. Mollison's Designers' Manual provides detailed climate-specific guidance for house siting and design.

## General Principles

Settlement site choice and surrounding plantings provide approximately 70% of heat energy conservation in cool climates; structural techniques handle the rest. The house is Zone 0, the origin from which all zones radiate. Good house design includes attached glasshouse or shadehouse, integration of living components such as sod roofs, vines, trellis, pot plants, roof gardens, and companion animals.

## Cool and Cold Climate Housing

Settlement design factors:
- Village or streets aligned east-west at mid-slope (thermal belt) of a sun-facing slope, with forests and high water storages above
- Housing closely placed or conjoined at east and west walls, preferably two to four stories
- Dense windbreak polewards or uphill of settlement, and to east and west (suntrap structure)
- Dense attached vines on masonry walls for insulation
- Deciduous trees to sunwards chosen to admit low winter light
- Siting above local frostlines, avoiding unstable soils, avalanche tracks, and flooding
- Settlements should present stepped aspect so each dwelling presents full facade to winter sun

House design specifics:
- 30% (Latitude 30-35 degrees) to 100% (Latitude 60 degrees) glazing on sunward walls
- No glazing on west walls; minimal double-glazing on poleward walls
- Houses no more than two rooms (10 m) deep on north-south axis, 1.5 times longer east-west
- Concrete slab, mud brick, or tiled floors in contact with ground for heat absorption
- Insulate entire earth mass below floor: 1 m deep foundation trench with 5-15 cm insulation around periphery
- 5-15 cm ceiling insulation (fibre, wool, sawdust, feathers, shredded paper)
- Reverse brick veneer walls (bricks inside, timber outside) with insulated cavity
- Double entries essential in cold wind areas
- In alpine areas, conjoin housing to barns, feed storages, and fuel storage

## Desert Housing

Desert housing needs twin qualities of summer cooling and winter warmth. Key features:
- Cool courtyards in building interiors, narrow and tall to preserve shade
- Evaporation strategies from water in tunnels, unglazed pots, fountains, bark mulch
- Narrow east-west streets maximised; broad north-south streets minimised
- White-painted massive walls (often mud) as cool surfaces
- Small windows or stone grilles, direct from inner courtyards
- Towers, vanes, and airscoops for ventilation
- Cooking outdoors under shade trellis
- Earth-sheltered or underground housing

Close-clustered dwellings with long axis of streets east-west ensure neither wind nor heat easily penetrates. Cool air in shaded narrow wells is always available in courtyards. Roads and car parks must be shaded and narrow.

Site selection: wadis are excellent in very hot deserts, narrow east-west wadis particularly well-shaded. A thermal belt exists some 10-20 m above the peneplain. The main consideration is potential for water harvest and storage on or near the house site.

## Tropical and Subtropical Housing

Tropical housing emphasizes ventilation, shade, and rain protection. Key strategies:
- Raised floors for airflow beneath
- Wide overhanging eaves
- Open or screened walls for cross-ventilation
- Shade from trees and climbing vines
- Integration with outdoor living spaces
- Attached shadehouses rather than glasshouses

## Thermal Mass and Passive Solar

The interactions of thermal mass (heat storage) and insulation (buffering for temperature extremes) plus sensible siting permit efficient and safe housing over broad climatic ranges. The floor area and earth beneath it are the critical heat stores for winter, radiating solar heat at night. Insulation adds 5-8% to house costs but pays for itself in energy savings.

## Fire-Resistant Design
In fire-prone areas (Mediterranean, subtropical, and temperate regions
with dry summers), house placement must account for fire sector analysis.
Mollison recommends siting the house within a fire-retardant green zone
of broadleaf deciduous trees, irrigated gardens, and mown grass.
Buildings should be set into the slope (earth-sheltered on the uphill
side) with metal roofing, no wooden decks facing the fire sector, and
shaded walls. Water tanks should be placed above the house for
gravity-fed fire fighting. The [[permaculture-sector-analysis-detailed]] should identify prevailing fire directions and design
defensive vegetation zones accordingly. A 20-40m fuel-reduced buffer zone
of short green sward, succulents, and deciduous species surrounds the
house, backed by a wider zone of fire-resistant trees such as oak,
elm, and willow. See [[permaculture-fire-protection-design]] for complete strategies.

## Humid Tropical Housing
Beyond basic tropical design, Mollison emphasises specific strategies for
humid tropical climates where mould, pests, and heavy rainfall are
primary concerns. Houses should be oriented to capture prevailing
breezes, with rooms arranged for cross-ventilation through low-level
inlets and high-level outlets (stack effect). Bamboo and thatch, while
traditional, must be treated or replaced regularly due to pest
vulnerability. Raised timber or concrete floors prevent flooding and
reduce termite access. Verandas on all sides provide shaded outdoor
living space that doubles as a rain protection zone. The roof pitch
should be steep (30-45 degrees) to shed heavy tropical rain rapidly,
and gutters should feed into [[permaculture-energy-storage-systems]] sized for both wet and dry seasons.

## Earth-Sheltered and Underground Construction
Earth-sheltered housing provides excellent thermal performance across
multiple climate zones. In cool climates, earth berming on poleward
walls reduces heat loss by 50-80% compared to fully exposed walls. In
hot climates, underground construction maintains stable temperatures
close to the earth's mean annual temperature (typically 10-15 degrees C
at 2-3m depth). The key design consideration is waterproofing: a
drainage layer of gravel and perforated pipes must surround the
earth-contact surfaces. [[mollison-thermal-mass-passive-building]] is inherently optimised in earth-sheltered
construction, as the surrounding earth acts as an enormous thermal
buffer.

## House-Garden Integration
The Zone 0-1 interface is where daily human-plant interaction is most
intense. Mollison specifies that the house should open directly onto
intensive garden beds, with the kitchen adjacent to the herb spiral,
salad garden, and small greenhouse. Paths between house and garden
should be covered (pergola, vine trellis) for weather protection.
Poultry housing in Zone 1 allows daily management and egg collection as
part of the household routine. [[permaculture-zone-planning-detailed]] ensures that the house functions as the productive
centre of the design rather than an isolated structure surrounded by
ornamental landscaping. Compost systems, worm farms, and small animal
housing should be placed within 10-20m of the kitchen door to maximise
the efficiency of daily organic waste recycling.

## Wind Protection Strategies
Wind is one of the most significant factors in house comfort and energy
efficiency. Mollison's design approach uses a layered defence: a primary
windbreak of dense evergreen trees at 5-10x their mature height
upwind, a secondary windbreak of medium-height deciduous trees, and
perimeter hedges close to the house that filter remaining wind without
blocking winter sun. The permaculture-windbreak|windbreak design
must account for both the prevailing wind direction and cold air
drainage from slopes. Sites at mid-slope (the thermal belt) are both
warmer and less windy than sites at the bottom of a valley, where
cold air pools at night.
