# Psilocybin Mushroom Sclerotia: Philosopher's Stone and Truffles

## What Are Sclerotia?

Sclerotia are hardened masses of mycelium which are more resistant to adverse
environmental conditions than normal mycelium. They represent a specialised
survival structure formed by certain fungi as a defence mechanism against
dryness, cold, heat, or excessive moisture. In the context of hallucinogenic
mushrooms, sclerotia are produced by some species of Psilocybe, most notably
Psilocybe mexicana and Psilocybe tampanensis.

Unlike the familiar [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-species-classification-and-chemistry]], only a limited number
are known to produce sclerotia. The two most notable species in this regard
are Psilocybe mexicana and Psilocybe tampanensis. Both of these species are
native to subtropical and tropical regions, particularly in Mexico and the
southern United States, which may explain the evolutionary development of
sclerotia as an adaptation to surviving dry seasons and other environmental
challenges.

Psilocybe mexicana has a particularly significant cultural and historical
importance. It is one of the mushroom species traditionally used in
indigenous Mesoamerican ceremonies, and it was Psilocybe mexicana that
was identified and studied by R. Gordon Wasson and Roger Heim in the
1950s, leading to the famous 1957 Life magazine article that introduced
hallucinogenic mushrooms to a wide Western audience. The species was
given the name 'teonanacatl' by the Aztecs, meaning 'divine flesh' or
'the flesh of god', a name that has also gained popularity with modern
users [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] as their primary
psychoactive compounds, the same active substances found in other
hallucinogenic mushrooms of the Psilocybe genus.

## Cultivation and Production

The cultivation of sclerotia differs from the cultivation of mushroom
fruiting bodies. While cubensis varieties are typically grown in
terrariums to produce the familiar cap-and-stem mushroom structures,
sclerotia-producing species can be cultivated specifically for their
underground masses without necessarily producing fruiting bodies at all.
This cultivation method can be more discreet and may require different
growing conditions and timeframes.

Growkits for Psilocybe tampanensis were available through smartshops and
online retailers, typically containing a box with colonised substrate, a
bag with an air filter, and paperclips. The cultivation process for
sclerotia generally involves inoculating a substrate with fungal spores
or mycelium, then allowing the organism to grow and form the hardened
sclerotial masses over a period of weeks to months.

The Free Spore Ring Europe, a non-profit making organisation, provided
various strains of hallucinogenic mushroom spores, including those for
sclerotia-producing species, at a cost of approximately EUR 1 per print.
Instructions on how to grow hallucinogenic mushrooms using a spore print
and a substrate could be easily found on the internet, lowering barriers
to home cultivation for interested consumers.

## Potency Comparison: Sclerotia vs Fruiting Bodies

The potency of sclerotia compared to the fruiting bodies of the same
species is an important consideration for users. Both sclerotia and
fruiting bodies of Psilocybe mexicana and Psilocybe tampanensis contain
the same primary psychoactive compounds, psilocybin and psilocin, but
the concentrations may differ between the two forms.

As with all hallucinogenic mushrooms, potency depends on multiple factors
including the species, origin, growing conditions, and age of the
specimen. Mushroom potency is inherently variable, and the psychoactive
psilocybin doses are believed to be between 45 micrograms per kilogram of
body weight and 315 micrograms per kilogram of body weight. Given this
variability, users face challenges in determining appropriate doses
regardless of whether they are consuming sclerotia or fruiting bodies.

The sclerotia form may offer certain practical advantages that influence
consumer preference. Their hardened, dense structure makes them more
durable and potentially easier to store and transport than fresh
fruiting bodies, which have a limited shelf life of approximately seven
to ten days. The different physical form also means that sclerotia may
be prepared and consumed differently, with users chopping them up or
brewing them in tea, similar to the preparation methods used for
fruiting bodies of hallucinogenic mushrooms.
