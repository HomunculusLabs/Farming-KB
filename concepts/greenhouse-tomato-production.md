# Greenhouse Tomato Production

Tomatoes are the highest-value single crop at the farm stand, generating roughly three times more dollar value than any other crop. In cool climates like coastal Maine, where outdoor tomatoes don't begin ripening until early August, greenhouse production is essential for a competitive farm-stand business. This section covers the complete system for greenhouse tomato production, from seed starting through vertical growing and season extension.

## The Business Case

Tomatoes serve as the **traffic driver** for the farm stand. Even when the unheated hoop houses produce an impressive array of crops by early June—[[arugula]], beets, carrots, chard, fennel, herbs, kale, lettuce, pak choi, parsley, potatoes, radishes, scallions, spinach, and turnips—it is the tomato and cucumber signs that catch the public eye. The goal is to have tomatoes and cucumbers ready when the stand opens, with peppers following two to three weeks later.

## Variety Selection

Three basic types are grown to cover the full market:

1. **Early round red standard** (6–10 oz): The first to ripen, providing early sales
2. **Later-maturing red beefsteak**: Larger fruit, extends the main-season offering
3. **Cherry tomato**: For basket sales and impulse purchases

Modern greenhouse-adapted cultivars consistently outperform heirlooms in growth, yield, and eating quality under greenhouse conditions. Given the constraints of climate and greenhouse space, there is no room for underperforming varieties. New candidates should be trialed every year.

## Starting Tomato Plants from Seed

Tomato plants receive the most elaborate seed-starting treatment of any crop in the system:

1. **Germination**: Sow seeds in mini-blocks on heat pads at 70°F. Germination takes 7–8 days
2. **First repot**: Move to 2-inch soil blocks as soon as seedlings can be handled. Leave on heating pads for 10 days
3. **Final potting**: Transplant to 5-inch square pots before leaves overlap. This is the only crop for which pots are used (rather than soil blocks), because the extra soil volume guarantees sturdy, well-rooted plants

### Spacing During Propagation

Keep pots spaced so that leaves of adjacent plants never shade each other. Plants that are crowded stretch and become leggy. It is much easier to handle and transplant younger, shorter plants.

### Timing

Start seeds **six weeks before** the planned greenhouse transplant date. In the standard rotation, this means sowing in late February for an April 7 transplant.

## Bed Layout

Tomatoes use the standard 30-inch-wide beds with 12-inch paths between them. In a 30-foot-wide greenhouse with eight beds:

- **Middle six beds**: Planted to tomatoes
- **Two edge beds**: Planted to companion crops (early celery and Tuscan kale), which tolerate lower headspace and don't compete with the staked tomatoes

Plants are set **24 inches apart** down the center of each bed. Tighter spacing (down to 14 inches) is possible with better native soil, but the wider spacing eases pruning, harvesting, and monthly [[compost]] topdressing, while also improving air circulation in the humid hoop-house environment.

## Vertical Growing System

The vertical support system more than triples yield per square foot compared to unsupported crops and is essential for greenhouse tomato production.

### Support Structure

- Horizontal wires run the length of the greenhouse at approximately 8 feet above ground level
- Small spools of plastic twine hang from wire frames attached to these horizontal wires
- Commercial tomato trellis clips secure the stem to the twine, placed every 12 inches as the plant elongates

### Pruning

- **Single stem**: All suckers (shoots between the leaf branch and main stem) are removed
- **Fruit thinning**: Beefsteak varieties limited to 4 fruits per cluster; medium varieties to 5 fruits per cluster
- **Cherry tomatoes**: Once the first fruit in a cluster begins ripening, pinch off further blossoms at the end of that cluster

### Lowering and Layering

When the growing tips reach the support wire (~8 feet high):

1. Harvest the lowest fruit and remove lower branches up to the height of the lowest remaining fruit cluster
2. Unroll a turn or two of twine from each spool, lowering the plant top by about 12 inches
3. Slide the wire frame holding the spool horizontally along the support wire
4. At the row end, curve the plants around the corner and over to the partner row moving in the opposite direction

This creates paired rows resembling the circular hanging-rail system in commercial dry-cleaning establishments. The process is repeated every time the tops reach wire height. The bare stems end up lying along the ground while the top 8 feet of each vine continues producing.

This lowering-and-layering system allows a single planting set out in early spring to remain in vigorous production until late fall—potentially six months or more of continuous harvest.

## Soil Temperature Management

Getting the soil warm enough before transplanting is critical for tomato success in a cold-climate greenhouse.

### When Following a Winter Spinach Crop

The house has been unheated all winter. To maximize soil warming:

1. During March, pull back the inner covers protecting the spinach on every sunny day to allow direct solar heating of the soil
2. After pulling the spinach in late March/early April, spread 1 inch of compost over the beds
3. Turn on a propane heater for a few days before transplanting to keep night temperatures above 60°F

The income from the late spinach harvests more than compensates for the slightly slower tomato start compared to a house prepared earlier.

### When the House Is Available Earlier

1. Remove previous crop, wickets, and row-cover inner layer a month or more before tomatoes
2. Prepare the beds and lay **clear plastic sheeting directly on the soil surface**
3. This traps solar heat effectively and also stimulates weed-seed germination
4. Flame off the weed seedlings before transplanting
5. Soil temperature at 4-inch depth can reach 65°F with solar heat alone using this method

## Monthly Compost Topdressing

Because the plants produce for many months, they need ongoing fertility. A monthly topdressing of compost along the beds is applied throughout the growing season. This is one reason for the generous 24-inch spacing—it provides access to the plants for both topdressing and harvest.

## Companion Crops

The two edge beds in a tomato house are planted to:
- **Early celery**: Tolerates lower light at the edges
- **Tuscan kale transplants**: Similarly shade-tolerant and productive

Both crops mature before they would interfere with tomato production.
