# Soil Building in Ecological Gardens

**Updated:** 2026-04-18

Soil building is the foundation of ecological gardening. Rather than treating soil as an inert medium that receives chemical inputs, the ecological approach treats soil as a living ecosystem that, when properly nourished, builds its own fertility, structure, and water-holding capacity.

## The Living Soil

Healthy soil teems with life: billions of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and arthropods inhabit every cubic inch. These organisms form the soil food web -- a complex hierarchy of interactions that drives decomposition, [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] (a leaf, for example) falls to the ground:
1. **Primary decomposers** (bacteria, fungi, mites, springtails) shred, chew, and chemically dissolve the material
2. Sugars and starches are quickly converted to energy, CO2, or more organisms
3. **Celluloses and proteins** decompose more slowly
4. **Lignins and chitins** (the toughest compounds) are broken down primarily by specialized fungi
5. Secondary and tertiary decomposers (beetles, centipedes, spiders) feed on the primary decomposers

## Humus: The Heart of Soil Health

### What Is Humus?
Humus is a stable, complex group of molecules made of carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen, bonded in ways that resist further decomposition. It is the end product of the decomposition cascade -- but not a dead end. In healthy soil, fresh organic matter arrives as fast as old humus breaks down, maintaining a constant buildup.

### Why Humus Matters

**Moisture retention:** Humus absorbs four to six times its weight in water. One foot of rich, moist soil blanketing a backyard holds as much water as a 3-inch-deep lake the same size. As humus swells when wet and shrinks when dry, it creates air spaces, lightens soil, and acts like gentle tilling.

**Nutrient holding:** Humus presents a bristling array of negatively charged oxygen atoms that attract positively charged nutrients: potassium, calcium, magnesium, ammonium, copper, zinc, manganese. At pH near 7, humus can store enormous quantities of nutrients.

**Soil structure:** Soil microbes secrete gums, waxes, and gels that hold tiny earth particles together into crumbs. These mini-clumps give microbially rich soil its good tilth -- the loose, crumbly structure gardeners strive for.

**Nutrient delivery to plants:** Plant roots secrete mild acids that break bonds holding nutrients onto humus, washing them into soil moisture. Alternatively, roots release sugars and vitamins that feed beneficial bacteria and fungi, which in turn release humus-bound nutrients and share them with plants.

## Mineralization: Nature's Fertilizer Factory

As decomposers finish processing organic matter, most consumable carbon is gone. What remains are inorganic compounds -- phosphate, nitrate, sulfate, and other chemicals recognizable as plant fertilizer. Microbes literally make fertilizer in the soil through mineralization.

Key insight: [[comparison-bokashi-vs-hot-composting-method]] creates finished humus quickly; cold composting preserves more soil life. Both add organic matter and nutrients.

### Green Manures and Cover Crops
Living plants that are later cut or tilled in add organic matter and nitrogen (legumes). Protect soil from erosion during fallow periods.

### Mulch
Any organic material (leaves, straw, [[dighton-mycorrhizal-networks-carbon-transfer]], and soil organism populations. Use sheet mulching, chop-and-drop, and direct planting instead.

### Dynamic Accumulators
Deep-rooted plants (comfrey, dandelion, chicory, yarrow) draw minerals from subsoil and deposit them on the surface as leaves decompose.

### Mycorrhizal Fungi
Fungi that form symbiotic relationships with plant roots, extending the root network dramatically. They transport water and nutrients to plants in exchange for sugars. Disturbed by tilling.
