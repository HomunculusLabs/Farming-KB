## Canopy Layers and Vertical Stacking

Mollison identifies five principal layers in a productive mixed
orchard:

1. **Overstory (tall canopy):** Large nut trees, timber trees, or
   tall fruiting species such as chestnut, walnut, pecan, or
   standard apple and pear on seedling rootstock. These cast light
   shade and create the microclimate for lower layers.

2. **Mid-canopy (main crop trees):** The primary fruit and nut
   producers -- apples, pears, plums, peaches, cherries, figs,
   persimmons, citrus in suitable climates. These are typically on
   semi-dwarf or semi-vigorous rootstocks spaced for full canopy
   development.

3. **Understory (shrub layer):** Berry bushes, currants, gooseberries,
   hazelnuts, bamboo, and nitrogen-fixing shrubs such as Siberian pea
   shrub or tagasaste. These fill gaps between trees and produce in
   the partial shade of the canopy above.

4. **Herbaceous layer:** Comfrey, borage, herbs, annual vegetables,
   and green manure crops that suppress weeds, cycle nutrients, and
   attract [[zone-1-intensive-garden]] systems.

Spacing follows the mature canopy diameter plus 1-2 meters for air
circulation and access. In a mixed orchard, Mollison recommends
irregular spacing that follows landform contours using [[dighton-pine-needle-litter-decomposition-fungal-succession]],
reducing or eliminating the need for imported fertilizers.

Black locust also provides excellent fence posts and firewood. Honey
locust produces pods that serve as high-protein livestock fodder.
These multi-functional outputs align with the multiple functions
principle.

## Pollination Groups

Successful fruit set requires compatible pollinizers. Mollison
emphasizes that most apple, pear, plum, and sweet cherry varieties
are self-incompatible and require cross-pollination from a different
variety in the same pollination group (flowering at the same time).
The mixed orchard naturally addresses this by including multiple
varieties, but the designer must verify compatibility.
