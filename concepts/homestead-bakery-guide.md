## Overview

A homestead bakery transforms home-milled or purchased grains into bread and baked goods for family consumption or sale. This guide covers sourdough and whole grain bread techniques using whole wheat, rye, and mixed-grain flours — the grains most accessible to homesteaders growing their own grain or purchasing in bulk.

## Sourdough Fundamentals

### What Is Sourdough

Sourdough is bread leavened by wild lactobacilli and wild yeast (naturally occurring in flour and the environment) rather than commercial baker's yeast. The symbiotic relationship between these microorganisms produces:

- **Carbon dioxide** — Leavens the bread (from wild yeast)
- **Lactic and acetic acid** — Flavor and preservation (from lactobacilli)
- **Enzymes** — Break down complex carbohydrates and gluten, improving digestibility
- **Lower glycemic index** — The acid and fermentation slow sugar absorption
- **Extended shelf life** — Acidic environment inhibits mold growth (sourdough keeps 5-7 days without preservatives)

### Creating a Sourdough Starter

A starter is a stable culture of wild yeast and lactobacilli maintained with regular flour and water feedings:

**Day 1:** Mix 1/2 cup whole wheat flour + 1/2 cup warm water (80-85F) in a glass jar. Stir well, cover loosely. Whole wheat flour works best initially because the bran hosts more wild organisms.

**Day 2-3:** Stir the mixture. You may see bubbles. Do not feed yet.

**Day 4:** Discard half the starter. Feed with 1/2 cup flour + 1/2 cup water. Stir well.

**Day 5-7:** Repeat the discard-and-feed cycle once daily. The starter should show consistent bubbling, rise within 4-8 hours of feeding, and smell pleasantly sour (like yogurt or tangy fruit).

**Day 7+:** The starter is ready to use when it reliably doubles in volume within 4-6 hours of feeding.

### Maintaining a Starter

- **Daily use:** Feed 1:1:1 ratio (equal weights starter, flour, water). Example: 50g starter + 50g flour + 50g water.
- **Weekly storage (refrigerator):** Feed the starter, let it rise for 2 hours, then refrigerate. Feed once a week by discarding half and refreshing. Bring to room temperature and feed 2-3 times before baking.
- **Long-term storage (freezer):** Dehydrate a portion of active starter on a sheet of parchment. Break into flakes, store in a jar. To revive, soak flakes in warm water and begin regular feeding.
- **Hydration:** Most recipes call for 100% hydration (equal weights flour and water). Whole grain starters may benefit from slightly higher hydration (110-120%) because bran absorbs more water.

## Whole Wheat Bread

### Characteristics

Whole wheat bread behaves differently from white bread:

- **Higher absorption** — Bran absorbs more water. Expect to use 10-15% more water than white flour recipes.
- **Lower gluten strength** — Bran particles physically cut gluten strands, reducing rise. Whole wheat bread is typically denser.
- **Faster fermentation** — Bran provides more nutrients for yeast, accelerating fermentation. Watch carefully to avoid over-fermentation.
- **Stiffer dough** — Add water gradually until the dough feels tacky but not sticky.
- **Longer mixing** — Autolyse (resting flour and water before adding starter and salt) is especially helpful for whole wheat dough.

### Basic Whole Wheat Sourdough Recipe

**Ingredients:**
- 500g whole wheat flour (freshly milled preferred)
- 350g water (70% hydration — adjust to 75% if dough is too stiff)
- 100g active sourdough starter (20% inoculation)
- 10g sea salt (2%)

**Process:**
1. **Autolyse:** Mix flour and water until no dry spots remain. Rest 30-60 minutes.
2. **Add starter and salt:** Mix starter into dough, then sprinkle salt on top and mix until incorporated.
3. **Bulk fermentation:** 4-6 hours at room temperature (70-78F). Perform stretch-and-folds every 30 minutes for the first 2 hours (4 sets total). This develops gluten without heavy kneading.
4. **Pre-shape:** When dough has risen 50-75% and feels airy, turn onto a lightly floured surface. Shape into a round. Rest 20 minutes.
5. **Final shape:** Shape tightly and place seam-side up in a floured banneton (proofing basket).
6. **Cold proof:** Refrigerate 8-16 hours. This slow fermentation develops flavor and makes scoring easier.
7. **Bake:** Preheat oven to 500F with a Dutch oven inside for 45 minutes. Score the dough with a sharp blade. Bake covered for 20 minutes at 500F, then reduce to 450F, remove lid, and bake 20-25 minutes more until crust is deeply browned.
8. **Cool:** Completely cool on a wire rack (at least 1 hour) before slicing.

### Improving Whole Wheat Loaf Quality

- **Vital wheat gluten** — Add 2-3 tablespoons per loaf to improve rise and texture (available commercially or can be separated from fresh flour)
- **White flour blend** — Mix 70% whole wheat with 30% bread flour for lighter texture
- **Soaker** — Soak coarse grains, seeds, or additional bran in water overnight and add to dough. This softens the bran and improves moisture retention
- **Higher hydration** — Whole wheat performs better at 72-78% hydration than lower
- **Longer fermentation** — Extended bulk fermentation (6-8 hours) develops more complex flavor and better structure

## Rye Bread

### Working with Rye

Rye flour presents unique challenges and rewards:

- **Very low gluten** — Rye contains some gluten proteins, but they do not form the elastic network that wheat gluten does. Rye breads rely on starches and pentosans for structure, not gluten
- **High pentosan content** — These complex carbohydrates absorb enormous amounts of water (up to 8x their weight). Rye doughs are sticky, soft, and require different handling than wheat doughs
- **Acidic environment is essential** — Rye flour is more prone to spoilage organisms. The sourdough acid is necessary for both flavor and safety
- **Lower baking temperature** — Rye benefits from lower oven temperatures (400-425F) and longer baking times

### Basic Sourdough Rye Bread

**Ingredients:**
- 400g medium rye flour
- 100g bread flour (helps with structure)
- 350g water (70% hydration)
- 150g active rye sourdough starter (fed with rye flour)
- 10g salt
- Optional: 2 tablespoons caraway seeds, 1 tablespoon ground coriander

**Process:**
1. Mix all ingredients until incorporated. Do not knead — rye dough does not benefit from traditional kneading.
2. Bulk ferment 3-5 hours at room temperature. Rye ferments faster than wheat.
3. Shape into a round or oval (rye dough is sticky — wet hands help).
4. Place in a floured banneton or on a parchment-lined baking sheet.
5. Proof 1-2 hours at room temperature, or refrigerate overnight.
6. Score with a single deep slash (rye expands less than wheat).
7. Bake at 425F for 30-35 minutes. Rye bread is done when the internal temperature reaches 195-200F.
8. Cool completely (at least 2 hours). Rye bread benefits from a 24-hour rest before slicing — the crumb sets and flavor develops.

### Rye Varieties

- **Light rye** — Finely milled, sifted. Produces lighter bread with mild rye flavor.
- **Medium rye** — Standard baking rye. Good balance of flavor and texture.
- **Dark rye** — Coarsely milled with bran included. Strong flavor, dense texture, highest nutrition.
- **Pumpernickel** — Very coarse rye meal. Used in traditional German pumpernickel bread, which is steamed and baked for many hours.
- **Whole rye berries** — Can be soaked and added to bread for texture.

## Commercial Kitchen Requirements

### Selling Baked Goods Legally

In most US states, cottage food laws allow limited home baking sales without a commercial kitchen:

**Typical cottage food allowances:**
- Annual sales cap: $10,000-$50,000 (varies by state)
- Direct sales only (farmers markets, direct to customer, not retail stores)
- Specific approved foods (bread, cookies, jams — varies by state)
- Labeling requirements (ingredients, allergens, producer info, "home-produced" statement)
- No perishable items (cream-filled, meat-filled, or dairy-heavy products)

**Requirements for cottage food operation:**
- Clean, sanitary home kitchen
- Food handler certification (most states)
- Business license
- Product liability insurance (recommended)
- State registration or permit (varies)

### Scaling to a Commercial Kitchen

If cottage food limits are too restrictive:

- **Shared commercial kitchen** — Rent time in an existing licensed commercial kitchen. $15-40/hour in most areas.
- **Build a dedicated commercial kitchen** — Requires health department approval, commercial-grade equipment, three-compartment sink, hand-washing sink, separate food prep areas, proper ventilation, and more. Cost: $20,000-$100,000+ depending on scope.
- **Co-packing** — Partner with an existing bakery to produce your recipes in their facility.
