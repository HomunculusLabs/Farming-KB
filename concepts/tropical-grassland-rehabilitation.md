## Pioneering Ethics

Pioneering in the tropics is only ethical when directed at rehabilitating degraded lands. Suitable target sites include:

1. **Grasslands** developed by burning/grazing sequences and monsoon grasslands.
2. **Semi-forested clearings and old monoculture plantations** of sugarcane, banana, pine, eucalypt, or pineapple — often with "weed" invasion by Lantana, tobacco bush, vines, or shrubs.
3. **Logged and burnt forest** with reject logs, branches, stumps, and weedy regrowth.

The end results envisioned for rehabilitation range from terrace culture and water absorption systems, through extensive aquaculture or substantial dams, to polycultural forests and managed or rehabilitative forestry for perpetual yields. In practice, all of these are planned in appropriate combinations for each specific site. The approach of extending small and successful trials is basic to success; broadscale trials have unstable social [[fungal-allelopathy-mechanisms-and-ecological-effects]] from the beginning.

## The Tropical Grassland Problem

The management of deforested grassland areas is the main problem of the wet-dry tropics. Soil erosion, rank grasses in the wet season, and inflammable or low-nutrition feed in drought all result from burning and over-grazing. Once deforested, pastures are exposed to summer winds, and the nutrient cycle of trees/grass/browsing is broken. Fire — often out of control — only accelerates the process. Although very few trees can survive in tropical grasslands, it is essential to re-establish tree legumes as the primary rehabilitation strategy. The broken nutrient cycle means that without intervention, grasslands degrade further with each burning cycle, losing both organic matter and soil structure.

Heavy cattle browsing is a major cause of pasture deterioration and soil loss. Their extensive grazing is probably the most common destructive use of tropical lands. No nation can support destructive grazing agriculture on the agribusiness/cowboy/pyromaniac model so general in tropical countries, in America, and wherever "cheap" beef is produced. The long-term cost makes such systems uneconomic in any terms. The first step in rehabilitation is therefore to relieve the land of the weight of too many cattle.

## Cover Crop Legumes

Vigorous grassland [[cover-crop]] legumes will help reduce the grasses and eventually lay down a mulch. Key species include **Desmodium** and **Stylosanthes** (also called Suratro). Under trees, a short-stemmed Desmodium will defeat the grasses, but it is then essential to supply dry-season water, as the legume also competes with young trees for moisture. These legumes serve as the first biological barrier against grass re-invasion, and their nitrogen-fixing capacity begins the process of soil restoration even before tree canopy closure is achieved.

## Fast-Growing Leguminous Trees

Fast-growing leguminous trees — **Albizzia**, **Acacia**, **Inga**, and **Leucaena** — will quickly establish and can be grown in the shelter of banana grass or [[elephant-grass]] (*Pennisetum*). If these grow vigorously, they also provide green mulch for surrounding plantings. Their rapid canopy closure is the key to shading out rank tropical grasses that otherwise prevent forest succession.

## Intensive Forage Systems vs. Extensive Grazing

A positive approach is to re-establish either a multi-species system ecology (trees and a variety of browsers), or to intensify cattle rearing. Cliff Adam, Chief Research Officer at Grand Anse, Mahé, in the Seychelles, developed an intensive system using *Pennisetum atropurpureum* (7 parts) plus *[[leucaena-leucocephala]]* — the low-mimosin type available in Australia — (1 part), with the addition of Bocking strain comfrey. This cut-and-carry "pasture" supports **seven milk cows per acre**. All manure and washings from the stable/dairy are returned to the irrigated field. Imported artificial fertilisers have been reduced to one-tenth, with further reductions expected as soil builds.

By contrast, in the same climate in Australia, **one cow per square mile is enough to lay waste to the land**. This stark comparison underscores the futility of extensive cattle systems in the tropics and the power of intensive, closed-loop forage systems.

## Seasonal Strategies and Broadcast Planting

Innovative practitioners have developed clever seasonal techniques that work with natural growth cycles:

- One innovator who bought a degraded cattle property north of the Daintree River (Queensland, Australia) gathers coconuts from the beaches and, travelling the ridges of old fields just before the wet season, throws dozens of coconuts at intervals into stream-lines and gullies. About **4% take root** and grow into sheltered, pioneering palms — a low-effort, high-reward strategy for establishing long-lived tree crops in degraded waterways.

- Another innovator rolls down monsoon grasses as they begin to die off in the dry season, then broadcasts tall-stalk rye and field legumes (**Fava**, **Dolichos**, **Vigna**) into the thick resulting mulch. Enough moisture persists over the dry winter to grow these crops; after harvest, the monsoon grasses regrow for next year's rye crop.

This clever use of seasons and growth is possible for the establishment of many species, some of which become permanent and grass-defeating pioneers for later successional stages.

## Nucleus Pioneer Tree Clumps

The most reliable strategy for regenerative forestry in grasslands proceeds as a scattered set of pioneer tree and herbaceous nuclei — the steady establishment of **clumped pioneer trees in open grassland**. This duplicates the natural process of grassland seeding by fruit pigeons and frugivorous birds, which carry seed from forest edges into grassland.

A **dense 2 × 2 m planting** of nuclei containing legumes, palms, shrubs, ground covers, and bulbs, plus stone or stick mulch, quickly shades out grasses and produces a **closed canopy within 10 years**. These nuclei then self-generate and spread, eventually joining to form continuous forest.

### Nuclei Clump Composition

Excellent nucleus clumps in grassland are built up from a close-planted (1–2 m spacing) mixture of:

- **Acacia mearnsii** and **A. melanoxylon** — fast-growing nitrogen fixers and timber
- **Inga** and **Gliricidia** — leguminous shade trees providing mulch and fodder
- **Nicotiana** and **Casuarina** — pioneer species for rapid establishment
- **Vigna** and **Tagetes** — leguminous and pest-repellent ground covers
- **Comfrey** and nasturtium (box, soil, and all) — mineral accumulators
- A few handfuls of fertiliser at establishment
- Occasional slashing of grass and tall weeds during early growth

### Natural Aids

Natural aids greatly assist establishment and should be incorporated into every nucleus:

- **Stumps** — plant directly into the decaying wood
- **Large rocks** — keep central to clumps for thermal mass and moisture retention
- **Logs and log piles** — pocket soil and plant into the decaying wood
- **Old buildings and rock walls** — provide shade, wind protection, and moisture traps
- Any stones, logs, cardboard, or old carpets laid in the clump help suppress grass

It is within and around these pioneer nuclei that re-afforestation or productive tree crop can commence, using nuclei as ongoing mulch sources.

## Topographic Strategy: Valleys, Slopes, and Ridges

Planting strategy follows topography to match species to their optimal microclimate:

- **Steep moist valleys**: Plant patches of **bamboo** and **rattan** palm for later wild harvest; large nut trees of heavy water demand (**macadamia**, **coconut**, **pecan**); wind-sensitive large fruits (**avocado**); and high-value timber trees (**rosewood**, **teak**, **cedar**, tropical conifers, **balsa**, **mahogany**).
- **Gentle and accessible slopes**: A mixture of productive tree and palm crop planted in the shelter of pioneer species.
- **Ridges**: A protective windbreak of hardy palms, **Casuarina**, and wind-fast legumes to shield lower plantings from desiccating winds.

## Legume Nitrogen Fixation

Most legumes, and other genera such as alder and Casuarina, have **mycelial root associates** which fix atmospheric nitrogen. As these organisms and their attached roots are in constant process of death and replacement over a growing season, much nitrogen is released for use by other plant species. Clover and tree legumes perform the same benefit for pastures. Trees such as the rain tree (*Samanea saman*) can preserve green grass below even in dry seasons.

### Nitrogen Yields

Nitrogen fixation amounts have usually been underestimated at **75–100 kg N/ha/year** for general legumes. Efficient legumes such as **lucerne (alfalfa)** may provide **250–500 kg N/ha/year**, and tree legumes such as **Albizzia** as much in quite poor sandy soils (Iseky, D., 1982, *Economic Botany* 36(1)). Every part of legumes such as **Leucaena**, **Acacia**, **Albizia**, **Gliricidia**, and **Tephrosia** may contain high nitrogen levels — one can actually smell the ammonia from the trees in rain or when roots are crushed.

Cut green material from such trees (green mulch), lightly turned into crop, water-mulched, or even used as interplant, supplies much of the nitrogen for crops. It is necessary to ensure trees are **inoculated with the correct root associates** in the nursery or field. Most agricultural departments can supply inoculant strain lists or the inoculum itself, or soil from nodulating trees can be washed around newly planted trees or mixed with potting soils.

### Nitrogen Distribution and Slow Release

Nitrogen is distributed around root zones. Some shrubs and trees lay down about a **9-year supply**; if cut or ringbarked, the slow decay of the roots releases nitrogen for **6 years or more**. Artificially supplied nitrogen quickly leaches in warm rains, so legumes with their slow nitrogen release are of critical importance in any tropical crop situation.

### Tephrosia as a Nitrogen Powerhouse

*Tephrosia* yields **13.5 t/ha in 4 cuts**; leaf nitrogen is about **20–30 kg/t**, equating to **1,000–1,500 kg N/ha/year** — many times higher than root nitrogen yield. This is the whole rationale for avenue cropping and legume mulch. Phosphate and potash levels in green mulch are also satisfactory for crop production.

## Management Considerations for Leguminous Tree Crops

Management of leguminous tree crops must be carefully assessed for local conditions. The following factors are critical to long-term productivity:

- **Spacing**: For shrubs and small trees, **0.5 m apart** is best for foliage production, with a trimming height of 0.5–1.5 m recommended. Closer spacing maximises leaf yield per unit area.
- **Season**: Only in frost-free tropics can trimming occur all year (4–5 cuts). Wherever cold is a seasonal problem, two months of growth must be allowed to harden the plant before winter, or it will weaken as shoots are cold-killed. Trees in drought can be part-trimmed.
- **Forage**: Young coppice shoots may have higher levels of metabolic poisons than 2–3 year old shoots. If stock do not thrive, this factor should be assessed.
- **Shelter**: Trees can be more widely spaced for root nitrogen, seed production (for poultry and bees), and in-crop shelter. Flowering and seed production is better at **2–20 m spacing** (depending on tree size). A full canopy may be needed to reduce or eliminate frost.
- **Replacement**: Although many trees will coppice for **4–30 years**, any sign of loss of vigour should indicate the need to replant. Replant for small shrubs may be necessary every 2–3 years, while some shrubs and ground covers are annuals or become annuals in cold-season areas. A diverse age structure among planted legumes ensures continuous nitrogen supply even as individual plants age out.

## Summary of Key Species

The core species for tropical grassland rehabilitation form a layered system:

- **Ground covers and legumes**: Desmodium, Stylosanthes, Vigna, Tagetes
- **Shrub legumes**: Tephrosia, Gliricidia (also managed as small tree)
- **Fast-growing canopy trees**: Albizzia, Acacia (mearnsii, melanoxylon), Inga, Leucaena
- **Support and pioneer species**: Casuarina, Nicotiana, Pennisetum
- **Mineral accumulators and companions**: Comfrey, nasturtium
- **Long-term productive species**: Bamboo, rattan, macadamia, coconut, pecan, avocado, rosewood, teak, cedar, balsa, mahogany

To assess total nitrogen yield, combine soil nitrogen from mycelia (approximately 200 kg/ha/year) with leaf and slash nitrogen per hectare — the leaf yield from coppiced legumes typically far exceeds root nitrogen contribution. This makes leguminous tree crops the cornerstone of any sustainable tropical rehabilitation programme.
