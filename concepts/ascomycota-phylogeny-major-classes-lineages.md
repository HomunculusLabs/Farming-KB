The Saccharomycetes is a monophyletic clade that probably shares
a most recent common ancestor with the Euascomycetes. No true
yeast produces a sporocarp, and the class generally lacks
ascogenous hyphae, with life histories including a budding growth
phase. These generalizations do not apply to all members; some
display filamentous growth or produce ascophores.

## Euascomycetes (Filamentous Ascomycetes)

The Euascomycetes is the largest class of Ascomycota and arguably
the most successful group of fungi, including parasites, pathogens,
and mutualists of plants, algae, and animals, as well as saprobes
able to decompose virtually all known organic substrata. The [[fungal-decomposition|fungal decomposition]] pathways used by these saprobes are critical to [[dighton-fungi-elevated-co2-global-carbon-cycling]] cycling, alongside [[pezizales-discomycete-ascomycete-diversity]], with
macroscopic forest species such as Morchella, Gyromitra, and
Helvella.

## Convergent Evolution

modern methods continues to challenge traditional groupings. Morphological traits of ascomata
have been repeatedly lost and gained during the evolutionary
history of the group. Convergent evolution in ascus morphology
is apparently common, especially among modes of dehiscence
(operculate versus inoperculate).

The Euascomycetes is characterized by numerous well-supported
terminal clades linked by poorly supported basal nodes. The poorly
resolved base of the euascomycete clade may represent a radiation
event in which many major clades originated over a relatively
short period, though this hypothesis is based on analyses of a
single gene.

## Nomenclatural Flux

The nomenclature for major groups within the Euascomycetes is in
a state of flux. Various classifications have been proposed with
the goal of accurately reflecting monophyletic clades. The names
used in current literature represent a composite of formal and
informal designators. Resolution will require analyses of
multiple independent loci.
