# Tropical Savanna Forestry Rehabilitation

Tropical savannah forestry addresses the rehabilitation of degraded lands where overgrazing, fire, and slash-and-burn agriculture have destroyed native forests, replacing them with fire-prone grasslands of low productivity. These degraded areas—often dominated by stubborn species like blady grass (*Imperata cylindrica*)—present both a challenge and an opportunity for systematic ecological restoration using pioneer legume species.

## The Problem of Savannah Degradation

When native tropical forests are cleared or burned repeatedly, a successional regression occurs. Closed-canopy forest gives way to tough, fire-adapted grassland species that create a self-reinforcing cycle of degradation:

- **Fire-prone grasses** establish and prevent tree seed germination
- **Soil erosion** accelerates as tree cover is lost
- **Soil acidity** increases (often reaching pH 4–4.5)
- **Nutrient states** decline severely
- **Stock carrying capacity** drops during dry periods
- **Bare patches** of eroded, nutrient-poor soil develop

Blady grass (*Imperata cylindrica*) and similar tropical forage grasses are particularly problematic. They form dense, nearly impenetrable barriers that resist both manual clearing and natural forest regeneration. However, they do provide good mulch material when cut.

## The Rehabilitation Approach

Given rainfall of 60–150 cm, a systematic set of pioneer legumes can restore exhausted sites, including mine spoils and road embankments. The rehabilitation proceeds through several phases:

### Phase 1: Pioneer Establishment

Direct seeding in scratch holes or chiselled strips establishes fast-growing pioneer legumes. The key principle is planting **small assemblies** of species rather than spacing out individual specimens—clustered plantings support each other through shared microclimate and mycorrhizal networks.

### Phase 2: Grass Suppression and Fertility Restoration

Pioneer legumes accomplish several critical functions simultaneously:
- **Leaf drop and root nodulation** rebuild soil nitrogen and organic matter
- **Canopy closure** shades out fire-prone grasses, reducing or eliminating fire risk
- **Mulch production** from pruned branches and leaf fall improves soil structure
- **Microclimate creation** enables subsequent plantings of more sensitive species

### Phase 3: Introduction of High-Value Species

Once the pioneer canopy has suppressed grasses and improved soil conditions, high-value timber species such as rosewood, mahogany, and ebony can be introduced in lines or clearings within the first crop. The system gradually transitions to either high-value forestry or sustainable strip cultivation.

## Key Pioneer Species for Savannah Rehabilitation

### Acacia auriculiformis
The most important pioneer for exhausted savannah and tropical soils. It defeats blady grass across a very wide range of soils and sites, restores fertility, provides firewood, and acts as a nurse crop for subsequent tree plantings. It reduces fire intensity, provides good paper pulp, coppices readily, and self-seeds. Widely used as a shade and street tree throughout the tropics.

### Acacia mangium
Similar to *A. auriculiformis* but with straighter stems, making it better suited for forestry operations. Shares the same grass-defeating and soil-restoring properties.

### Sesbania grandiflora
An exceptionally fast-growing tropical pioneer reaching 10 meters. It coppices readily, serves as forage, green manure in rice paddies, and reinvigorates worn-out land. Features exceptional nitrogen nodulation. Young leaves, pods, and flowers provide human food (36% crude protein; seeds 40% protein). Used as a light shade crop and vine support. Frost and wind tender, with approximately 20-year lifespan. All food from this tree should be cooked before consumption.

### Calliandra colothyrsus
A stick-wood coppicing species that defeats grasses and provides abundant firewood while repairing exhausted soils and restoring fertility.

### Dalbergia sissoo
Salt and frost tolerant, fast growing, and effective at defeating grasses. Tolerates a wide range of soil types and can be established from large cuttings (quickset method), as practiced in India.

### Enterolobium cyclocarpum
A durable timber tree with large pods that defeats grasses. Native to and widely used in Central America.

### Mimosa scabrella
A subtropical pioneer from Brazil that provides good humus and functions effectively as a living fence.

### Samanea saman (Rain Tree)
A very fast-growing large tree for tropics and subtropics, producing sugary pods. Grass grows well beneath its canopy. The wood is valuable and durable.

## Nitrogen Dynamics of Tree Legumes

Tree legumes create distinct zones of nitrogen intensity around their root systems (Figure 10.48 in the source). Nitrogen continues to diffuse from the site for up to 6 years after a tree is cut down, meaning the soil fertility benefits persist long after the tree itself is harvested. The intensity of nitrogen concentration falls off in the outer root zones, creating a gradient that benefits understory plantings.

## Establishing Perennial Grass Swards on Degraded Land

Establishing productive perennial grass on weedy or eroded areas requires a one to three year process:

1. **Land-forming**: Implement swaling, interception banks, or pitting. Establish dams for irrigation above good soil types.
2. **Pioneer grass sowing**: Sow pioneer grass such as molasses grass, *Sorghum almum*, or silk sorghum mixed with sawdust into slashed weeds, or drill selected grasses and legumes after slashing.
3. **Transition**: Burn molasses grass or drill selected perennials and broadcast *Sorghum almum*.
4. **Tree legume planting**: Concurrently with earth-forming, plant leguminous trees along swales at 30–100 metre strip spacing. Allow 2–3 years of growth with light grazing beginning in year 3.
5. **Managed grazing**: Commence rotational grazing with 15–18 fields necessary for rotation. Drill or broadcast forage legumes into established grasses.

## Support Species for Long-Term Systems

For long-term forage and forest systems, additional species complement the pioneers:
- **Leucaena leucocephala** and **Albizia** species for ongoing nitrogen fixation and forage
- **Inga edulis** for shade and mulch production
- **Prosopis pallida** for pod production and firewood on dry sites
- **Pterocarpus indicus** and **P. erinaceus** for valuable timber (first seed-planted in nursery, then coppiced for 2m quickset planting)

## Pioneering on Difficult Terrain

On man-made or natural landslide areas and volcanic terrain in the tropics, initial establishment requires pocketing the area with soil-mulch mixtures. Coconut and macadamia nut husks are excellent materials for creating these initial planting pockets. Pioneer species then prepare the area for palms, figs, and useful fruit trees by providing shelter and mulch.

On volcanic terrain, a heavy spiked roller can crush soft lava (a'a), making it accessible and easily weathered to soil. Soil pockets should be amended with trace elements (boron, manganese, zinc, copper, molybdenum) if analysis shows deficiencies.

## Moisture Harvesting on Coastal Ranges

On sea-facing slopes in tropical tradewind areas, saturated ocean winds carry heavy moisture loads. This moisture is available as dew on grasslands but is far more effectively trapped on the myriad leaf surfaces of an uneven tree canopy. A ragged or spiky forest canopy—where palms and tall pines or fruit trees rise above the general canopy layer—ensures more condensation from sea air than a level, closed canopy. Even small garden tree patches "rain" softly on clear nights when sea breezes blow, channeling water down leaf midribs to fibrous trunks of palms and tree-ferns. Once this vegetation is cleared, effective precipitation drops dramatically, rivers cease to flow, and the land becomes truly arid.

## The Role of Tree Nurseries and Seed Sources

The primary constraint on [[savannah-forestry-rehabilitation]] is often the lack of tree nurseries and seed sources for appropriate species. This gap presents a significant enterprise opportunity in the humid tropics. The National Academy of Sciences publication on tropical legumes (referenced in the source) provides an excellent selection of potential species for anyone establishing such a nursery operation.
