# Fungal Mineral Transformations and Biogeochemical Cycling

Fungi are among the most consequential biological agents of mineral
weathering and element cycling in terrestrial ecosystems. Their capacity to
dissolve rocks, mobilize metals, and precipitate secondary minerals positions
them at the interface of geology and biology, driving processes that shape
soil formation, nutrient availability, and even the long-term carbon cycle.
Although bacterial geomicrobiology has historically received more attention,
accumulating evidence demonstrates that fungi can dissolve minerals and
mobilize metals at higher pH values and over a wider redox range than many
bacteria, making them uniquely important in a broad spectrum of environments.

## Fungi as Weathering Agents

Bioweathering is defined as the erosion, decay, and decomposition of rocks
and minerals mediated by living organisms. Fungi are exceptionally well
suited to this role because they tolerate extreme environmental conditions
including metal toxicity, UV radiation, and desiccation. Their filamentous
growth habit provides a high surface-area-to-mass ratio that can be
strategically increased or decreased depending on whether the fungus is
exploring or exploiting resources. Many fungi also form mutualistic
associations with plants, algae, or cyanobacteria, extending their reach into
environments where free-living organisms cannot persist. The ability to
translocate nutrients within a mycelial network is a further critical feature
that enables fungi to explore and exploit heterogeneous environments
effectively.

Fungi have been components of terrestrial microbial communities since the
Ordovician period, approximately 480 to 460 million years ago. Rock-dwelling
communities must cope with extremes in light, salinity, pH, and water
potential. Protection is achieved through melanin pigments and mycosporines
in cell walls, and by embedding colonies into mucilaginous polysaccharide
slime that can entrap clay particles for additional shielding.

## Rock-Dwelling Fungal Communities

Rock- and stone-dwelling microorganisms are classified into several ecological
categories. Epiliths occur on rock surfaces, often as microcolonial
black-coloured fungi forming spherical clusters of tightly packed cells with
thick pigmented walls. Hypolithic microorganisms are associated with pebbles.
Endoliths inhabit the rock subsurface and include chasmoliths, which grow in
pre-existing cracks and fissures; cryptoendoliths, which grow inside cavities
among crystal grains; and euendoliths, which actively bore into submerged
rock. Filamentous fungi across the zygomycetes, ascomycetes, and
basidiomycetes have been found in limestone, soapstone, marble, granite,
sandstone, andesite, basalt, gneiss, dolerite, amphibolite, and quartz.

## Mechanisms of Fungal Weathering

Fungal weathering operates through two synergistic pathways: biomechanical
and biochemical. Biomechanical weathering can be direct, occurring through
extensive hyphal penetration into decayed rocks and tunnelling into intact
mineral matter along crystal planes, cleavage, cracks, and grain boundaries.
The considerable mechanical force derives from osmotically generated turgor
pressure within hyphae. Thigmotropism, or contact guidance, directs fungal
growth towards grooves, ridges, and pores in solid material, enabling hyphae
to exploit weakened sites. Mucilaginous slime provides lubrication and may
contain acidic and metal-chelating metabolites. Indirect biomechanical
weathering arises from the shrinking and swelling of hydrated mucilage.

Biochemical weathering is generally considered more important than mechanical
degradation. Three primary mechanisms drive mineral solubilization. Acidolysis,
or proton-promoted dissolution, occurs when fungi acidify their microenvironment
through excretion of protons and organic acids, and through formation of
carbonic acid from respiratory carbon dioxide. Complexolysis, or
ligand-promoted dissolution, involves the excretion of metal-complexing
metabolites including carboxylic acids, amino acids, siderophores, and
phenolic compounds. Fungal-derived carboxylic acids such as oxalic and citric
acid perform aggressive attack on mineral surfaces, providing both protons for
solubilization and chelating anions to complex metal cations. Redoxolysis
involves oxidative and reductive transformations of metal species. Metal
immobilization through biosorption, intracellular accumulation, and
extracellular precipitation further drives dissolution by reducing external
free metal activity and shifting equilibria.

## Secondary Mycogenic Minerals

Fungi precipitate a remarkable diversity of secondary minerals. Carbonate
precipitation, particularly calcite and whewellite (calcium oxalate
monohydrate), occurs in limestones and calcic soils and may act as a sink for
atmospheric carbon dioxide. Chitin, the major component of fungal cell walls,
serves as an effective substrate for calcite nucleation. Oxalate minerals
include weddelite (calcium oxalate dihydrate) and whewellite, which occur in
various crystalline forms and serve as calcium reservoirs that influence
phosphate availability. Toxic metal oxalates, such as moolooite (copper
oxalate), can form through fungal metabolism and represent a detoxification
mechanism. Reductive precipitation yields elemental silver, selenium, and
tellurium, while oxidative processes produce manganese and iron oxide patinas
associated with desert varnish. Other mycogenic minerals include birnessite,
ferrihydrite, goethite, montmorillonite, and calcium formate.

## Mycorrhizal Contributions to Mineral Weathering

Mycorrhizal fungi amplify mineral weathering through their symbiotic
partnerships with plants. Ectomycorrhizal fungi dissolve soil weatherable
calcium-bearing minerals and excrete low-molecular-mass carboxylic acids
including malic, succinic, gluconic, and oxalic acid in a process termed
heterotrophic leaching. In podzol E horizons beneath European coniferous
forests, weathering of hornblendes, feldspars, and granitic bedrock has been
attributed to organic acid excretion by ectomycorrhizal hyphae operating at
micro- to millimolar concentrations within micropores of weatherable
minerals. These fungi also filter toxic metals in the hyphal sheath and Hartig
net, complex metals through released organic acids, and can ameliorate metal
toxicity to host plants, making them valuable for re-vegetation and
remediation of contaminated soils.

## Environmental Significance

Fungal mineral transformations have broad environmental implications. Their
ability to immobilize metals via biosorption and precipitation can be harnessed
for bioremediation of contaminated land and treatment of industrial wastes.
Conversely, mineral-solubilizing activity may undermine in situ chemical
remediation technologies such as phosphate-induced metal stabilization by
transforming the final mineral products. Fungi also contribute to deterioration
of building materials including concrete, raising concerns about nuclear waste
storage integrity. At the Chernobyl reactor, melanized microfungi including
Alternaria, Cladosporium, and Aureobasidium colonized highly radioactive
concrete, demonstrating fungal adaptation to extreme contamination.
