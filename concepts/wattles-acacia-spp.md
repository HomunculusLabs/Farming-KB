# Wattles (Acacia spp)

Wattles are a large genus of some 850 species native to Australia, in the
family Mimosaceae. They are among the most important [[permaculture-plants-nitrogen-fixing-trees]]
plants in [[permaculture]] systems, providing fodder, timber, shelter,
soil stabilisation, and [[permaculture-bee-forage-system]]. The seed of some species is
edible and some are traditional foods of Australian Aboriginal people.

## General Characteristics

Most Acacia species are good sources of pollen but not nectar. Seed is
generally good [[integrated-poultry-forage-systems-zone-two]] fodder. Most species are smaller plants whose
wood is significant for fuel, craft uses, fence posts, and mine timbers,
but some species are prime furniture timbers. Many wattles are extremely
drought tolerant and adapted to Australia's arid interior.

## Key Species for Permaculture

### Acacia aneura (Mulga)

An outstanding [[permplant-tagasaste-pioneer-fodder-tree]] tree native to all Australian states except
Victoria and Tasmania. Survives 200-500 mm annual rainfall. Trees range
from straight-trunked (to 9 m) to shrubs in extreme conditions. Prefers
red clayey loams or red sands but will survive most soil types. Can
withstand some frost.

A daily ration of 1.4 kg of mulga leaf supplies sheep with sufficient
protein (12%), calcium, and vitamin A. The mulga diet needs supplementing
with foods containing sulphur and phosphorus. Wood has striking colour,
is one of the hardest in the world, and is used for fence posts, mine
timbers, and craft wood. Companions include Eucalyptus spp, Atriplex
spp, and other Acacia species.

### Acacia mearnsii (Black Wattle)

A fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing tree to 6-10 m, adapted to 600-925 mm
rainfall at altitudes from near sea-level to 200 m. Native to
south-eastern Australia. Leaves have 15% protein content but palatability
trials showed milled leaves unpalatable on their own.

In Indonesia, it yields 21-28 tonnes/ha/year of wet leaves containing
240-285 kg of nitrogen. Indonesian farmers report vegetables and tobacco
grown in rotation with A. mearnsii give twice the yield due to the
green manure the trees provide. Yields high quality tannin (36-44%) from
bark, used for adhesives in plywood and particle board. Wood is hard and
used for house poles, mine timbers, tool handles, cabinet making, and
charcoal.

Widely cultivated overseas: 160,000 hectares in South Africa, over
125,000 in Brazil, yet almost ignored in Australia.

### Acacia melanoxylon (Blackwood)

A nitrogen-fixing tree attaining 35 m with trunk diameters up to 1.5 m
in ideal conditions in Tasmania. Occurs naturally from lowland swamps to
mountain tops in 750-1500 mm rainfall. Timber is easily worked, turns
well, has a sheen, dresses to a smooth finish, takes a high polish, and
is well suited to quality furniture and cabinet making. Also regarded as
a good bending timber. Not a good firewood. Considered slightly salt
tolerant. Ability to coppice and form root suckers.

### Acacia saligna (Golden Wreath Wattle)

A fast-growing, fire-retardant, salt-tolerant legume (3-8 m) from
south-west Western Australia, withstanding rainfall as low as 300 mm.
Leaves are palatable to livestock fresh or dried into hay, although have
low digestibility. Damaged bark exudes large amounts of very acidic gum
showing exceptional promise for use in pickles and foodstuffs. Suitable
for soil stabilisation and does well in sand near coastal areas. The
suckering variety readily forms thickets, ideal for low windbreaks and
erosion control.

### Acacia salicina (Cooba)

A tall shrub or tree to 20 m on flood plains on most soils, including
alkaline and saline. Leaves and pods are readily eaten by sheep, although
high tannin levels in leaves have been suspected of poisoning hungry
cattle. Wood is tough with attractive grain, takes a high polish, and
rivals blackwood in quality.

## Fodder Species for Arid Zones

### Acacia ampliceps (Salt Wattle)

A large dense shrub or small tree (2-8 m) for semi-arid to arid tropical
areas (250-700 mm rainfall). Fast growing and tolerates alkaline, highly
saline, and waterlogged conditions. Timber is good as fuel. Heavily
grazed by cattle.

### Acacia victoriae (Gundabluey)

A short-lived (10-15 years), moderately fast-growing shrub or small tree
(1-5 m) in 125-300 mm rainfall. Leaves have moderate palatability and
digestibility, and are a good protein source for cattle, poultry, and
humans. Good source of pollen for bees. Twigs exude a clear, tasteless
gum with outstanding food and industrial uses.

### Acacia stenophylla (River Cooba)

A nitrogen-fixing small tree (4-10 m) adapted to saline and alkaline,
heavy clay soils in arid and semi-arid areas (125-600 mm rainfall).
Palatable to sheep as reserve fodder. Produces very hard timber, heavy
and close-grained, suitable for furniture and excellent firewood.

## Fire Retardant Species

Several wattles have fire-retardant properties useful in [[fire-resistant-plants]] systems: A. baileyana, A. cyclops, A. glandulicarpa,
A. ileaphylla, A. melanoxylon, A. pravissima, A. prominens, and
A. saligna.

## Propagation

Most wattles propagate readily from seed. Many species benefit from
scarification (pouring boiling water over seeds) before planting. Some
species sucker freely or coppice well after cutting.
