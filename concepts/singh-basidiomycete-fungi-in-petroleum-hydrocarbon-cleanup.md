# Basidiomycete Fungi in Petroleum Hydrocarbon Cleanup

## Overview

Petroleum hydrocarbon contamination of soil and groundwater is a
widespread environmental problem resulting from oil exploration,
refining, transportation, and storage activities. The Singh source
provides extensive evidence that basidiomycete fungi, particularly
white-rot species, can colonize petroleum-contaminated soils and
degrade complex hydrocarbon mixtures including aliphatic, aromatic,
and [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]] components. Their extracellular, nonspecific
enzyme systems give them advantages over bacterial degraders for
weathered and recalcitrant petroleum fractions.

## Petroleum Hydrocarbon Classes

Crude oil and refined petroleum products contain thousands of
individual hydrocarbon compounds spanning several classes:

- Aliphatic hydrocarbons: n-alkanes, branched alkanes (isoalkanes),
  and cycloalkanes. These are generally the most biodegradable
  fraction but become recalcitrant as [[phenethylamine-chain-length-effects]] increases beyond
  approximately C30.

- Monoaromatic hydrocarbons: Benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and
  xylenes (collectively BTEX). These are volatile, water-soluble,
  and highly toxic, representing priority pollutants at fuel spill
  sites.

- Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons: Naphthalene, phenanthrene,
  anthracene, pyrene, and benzo-a-pyrene among others. High-molecular-
  weight PAHs are the most persistent fraction of petroleum
  contamination.

- Asphaltenes and resins: High-molecular-weight, polar constituents
  that resist biodegradation and complicate remediation.

- Diesel and fuel oil: Complex mixtures dominated by C10-C25 alkanes
  with significant aromatic content.

## Fungal Colonization of Contaminated Soils

Basidiomycetes face unique challenges in colonizing petroleum-
contaminated soils:

- Hydrophobic hydrocarbons limit water availability and create
  nutrient-poor microenvironments.

- Toxic components (BTEX, naphthenic acids) inhibit microbial growth.

- Existing microbial communities may be competitive or antagonistic.
