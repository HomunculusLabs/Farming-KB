# Falconer Gathering and Marketing Mushrooms

Chapter XVII of William Falconer's *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* (1891)
covers the final stages of mushroom cultivation: knowing when to pick,
proper harvesting technique, and the practicalities of getting mushrooms
to [[falconer-mushroom-marketing-economics]] in saleable condition.
Falconer considered this "an important point in the cultivation of this
esculent, and should be attended to with painstaking discretion."

## When to Pick

Timing depended on purpose. For fresh, attractive market appearance,
mushrooms were picked when plump and just before the frill connecting cap
and stem broke apart. French mushrooms should always be gathered before
the frill burst; English varieties were also best at this stage but
admissible when the frill began to burst, provided the cap had not opened
flat. Mushrooms displaying long stems should be picked earlier, as long
stems were disliked in market. Dark, discolored, or old mushrooms of any
sort were rejected.

For soups, mushrooms were gathered before bursting their gills, typically
in button state (marble-sized), to retain white appearance and avoid
discoloring the soup. For home use in baking, stewing, or broiling,
mushrooms were allowed to attain full size and burst their frills, gathered
before caps opened flat or gills lost their bright pink color.

Falconer strongly advised against leaving mushrooms ungathered to grow
larger for the next day's harvest. This produced an unfavored, unequal
lot. Far better to pick each mushroom the moment it was ready and keep
them safe in a cool place until enough accumulated.

## Pulling vs. Cutting

The cardinal rule was: always pull mushrooms out by the root, never cut
them with a knife if it could be avoided. The proper technique was to
take hold of the mushroom, give a sharp but gentle twist while pressing
down, and they would generally part from the bed without trouble. Pulled
mushrooms were placed in baskets root-end down to keep them clean and
free from grit. When several mushrooms shared one root-stock and one
could not be removed without disturbing the whole, it was cut out. The
same applied to clumps where pulling would displace smaller mushrooms.

## Advantages of Pulling

Pulling benefited the bed in several ways. Cut stumps left in the ground
developed decay and a fluffy, spongy substance that destroyed surrounding
mushrooms and mycelium threads. These stumps had to be scooped out with
a knife before decay set in, and holes filled with fresh loam.

Pulled mushrooms also kept fresh longer than cut ones. For market growers,
there was a commercial advantage: mushrooms were sold by weight, and
retaining the full stem preserved both quality and selling weight.

## Gathering Field Mushrooms

Wild mushrooms were gathered in the morning before sunshine warmed them.
For preserving in perfect condition, they were pulled by the roots, soil
removed, and laid orderly in baskets root-end down, with stout paper
between layers. For immediate use, ketchup, or drying, the common method
of cutting and carrying in bulk sufficed.

## Packing for Market

Most growers near New York City sold direct to hotels, restaurants, and
fruiterers. Distant growers sold through commission merchants. Mushrooms
were sold by the pound in strong undressed paper boxes, or light wooden
boxes holding one to four pounds each for express shipping.

Mr. Gardner's baskets held twelve pounds each. For commission merchants,
who dealt out in various quantities, mushrooms should always be packed in
one, two, three, or four-pound boxes or baskets, preferably one-pound.
Unlike potatoes or apples, mushrooms could not be handled and repacked
without discoloration and damage.

## Packing Best Practices

Falconer's packing instructions were meticulous:

- Gather the moment mushrooms were in best condition.
- Never let them blow open before gathering.
- Never cut off short stems until ready to pack.
- Brush earth from caps with a soft hair brush.
- Rub earth from root end with a harder brush or knife back.
- Sort by size: large, middle-sized, and button separately.
- Pack each kind by itself, very firmly without bruising.
- Show pretty caps to best advantage.
- Never pack more than two deep without soft paper between layers.
- Never put a heavy bulk in one box -- about a pound was safest.

Mr. Barter of London used punnets (the same used for strawberries or
peaches) holding one pound, though larger boxes of three to five pounds
were becoming customary for long journeys.
