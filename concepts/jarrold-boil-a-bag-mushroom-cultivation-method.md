# Jarrold Boil-a-Bag [[mushroom-cultivation]] method developed by D.H. Jarrold. It uses heat-resistant plastic bags as both the [[spore-print]] to harvest.

## Equipment Required

### Essential Items
- **Boil-a-Bags**: Heat-resistant plastic bags (20cm x 30cm / 8" x 12") that withstand pressure cooking and microwaving. Lakeland Plastics brand was specified
- **Pressure cooker**: For substrate sterilisation at 15 psi
- **Syringe and needle**: Standard syringe; a long needle can be fabricated from stainless steel tubing inserted into the needle holder
- **Spore print**: In a glass jar with metal screw lid
- **Propyl alcohol**: For [[microwave-trace-water-surface-sterilisation-protocol]]
- **Spirit burner**: With methylated spirits for flame sterilisation
- **Pyrex glass container**: For sterile water preparation
- **Cotton wool buds**: For alcohol swabbing
- **Flat-edged metal scraper**: For harvesting spores from prints
- **Metal tweezers and scissors**: For sterile cap handling
- **Breathing mask or clean handkerchief**: To prevent the operator's breath contaminating spore solutions

## Step 1: Making the Filter Can

The filter can provides gas exchange during colonisation while preventing contamination:

1. Obtain a clean 3-4 inch round food can — open it leaving the lid attached by about 1 inch to serve as a closable lid
2. Punch holes throughout the bottom with a hammer and nail for airflow
3. Line the inside bottom with ~1 cm of rock wool (or loft insulation fibreglass)
4. Fill the can with vermiculite to within 1 cm of the top (alternatively, use small pieces of rockwool instead of vermiculite)
5. Top with a thin layer of rock wool to hold everything in place
6. Close the lid when ready for use

The filter can sits inside the open top of each bag, creating a sterile breathing pathway.

## Step 2: Preparing the Straw Substrate

### Straw Preparation
1. Chop straw to 3-10 cm lengths (pre-cut straw is available from pet stores)
2. Place straw in a large watertight container

### Marmite Solution
3. Dissolve one heaped tablespoon of Marmite ([[marmite-yeast-extract-straw-substrate]]) in boiling water
4. Add to a bucket with approximately 6 litres of additional boiling water
5. Pour the Marmite solution over the straw and weigh the straw down
6. Soak for 1-2 hours, checking water level and adding more boiling water if needed

The yeast extract serves as a nutrient supplement that helps speed spore germination.

### Draining and Supplementing
7. Turn the soaked straw out into a bathtub and drain for 1 hour
8. Spread straw evenly and squeeze out excess water by hand if too wet
9. Sprinkle brown rice flour over the straw (~3-4 handfuls for 6 bags' worth) and mix in thoroughly

The brown rice flour provides additional carbohydrates for mycelium growth.

## Step 3: Filling the Bags

1. Firmly pack straw mixture into the bags until almost full
2. Leave enough room at the top to fit the filter can
3. Insert the filter can into the neck of each bag
4. Secure by wrapping wide masking tape around the neck of the bag three times, pressing it firmly onto both bag and can

## Step 4: Sterilisation

1. Place bags in the pressure cooker (1-2 bags depending on cooker size)
2. Add water and bring to maximum pressure (15 psi)
3. Sterilise for 60 minutes (can be done as two 30-minute cycles)
4. Allow bags to cool completely before inoculation

## Step 5: Preparing Bags for Inoculation

Two inoculation approaches are described:

**Short needle method**: Tape support strips from can to bag base. Mark 2 inoculation points per bag with tape, adding a looped piece to quickly seal after needle withdrawal.

**Long needle method (preferred)**: Tape the filter can on both sides of the bag. Inoculation passes the needle through the filter can itself — described as easier, quicker, and more trouble-free.

## Step 6: Preparing the Spore Syringe

1. Fill a Pyrex jug with 80 ml water, cover with tin foil
2. Wrap the connected syringe and needle in tin foil
3. Sterilise both in the pressure cooker at 15 psi for 20 minutes
4. Allow to cool, then add water to the spore jar
5. If spores do not readily mix, use a flame-sterilised flat-ended scraper to loosen them from the jar bottom
6. Load the spore suspension into the syringe
7. Flame sterilise the needle before each refill or inoculation

## Step 7: Inoculation

### Short Needle Method
1. Swab inoculation points with propyl alcohol
2. Flame sterilise the needle tip until red hot
3. Insert fully and release 5 cc of spore solution at each point
4. Roll pre-placed tape over the hole as the needle is withdrawn

### Long Needle Method
1. Lift the filter can lid and pass the flame-sterilised needle through the filter
2. Inoculate 10 cc of spore solution into the straw
3. Remove needle, close lid, and secure with tape if needed

## Step 8: Colonisation and Fruiting

1. Place inoculated bags in a cardboard box in a warm location
2. Mycelium growth should be visible within a few days
3. Once the mycelium has fully colonised the bag, remove the filter can
4. Transfer bags to a [[mushroom-humidity-chamber-design-operation]] (e.g., a 4ft x 2ft fish tank)
5. Add a layer of soaked hydro pellets to the tank bottom for humidity
6. Cover the tank with plastic sheeting to seal in humidity and exclude flies
7. Fan fresh air into the tank twice daily
8. When mushrooms begin forming, cut the bag down the sides, leaving ~2 cm at the bottom to stand the block upright
9. Continue fanning twice daily until mushrooms are ready to pick

## Step 9: Taking Spore Prints

1. Sterilise small glass jars with metal lids in an oven at half heat for 30 minutes
2. Cool, loosely place lids on, and remove tray from oven
3. At the growing tank (wear breathing mask), flame sterilise tweezers and scissors
4. Insert tweezers into a mature spore-loaded cap, cut from stem, place gills-down in jar
5. Replace lid loosely — do not tighten (moisture must evaporate)
6. Leave 24+ hours for spores to deposit
7. Remove caps with tweezers, leave jars open in a clean draught-free area 24 hours to dry
8. Tighten lids firmly for storage

## Advanced Chamber Designs

### Self-Humidifying Chamber
Built from hardware and car accessory shop materials. Uses an air stone, tubing, and small aquarium air pump with a small hole near the top feeding pipe to a water container. Inner angled drip lids from 2 mm plastic sheet provide continuous humidity without manual misting.

### Warm Humid Air Delivery System
Designed for cold climates. A container with water, lid, and input fan; multiple small ducts as outlets, one large duct as main input. Water heater with timer controls [[greg-green-temperature-and-humidity-control]]. Growing chambers are plumbed in once calibrated.

## Key Advantages

- **Low cost**: Bags cost approximately £2.50 per pack of 50; most other materials are household items
- **Self-contained**: Each bag is an independent growing unit, reducing cross-contamination risk
