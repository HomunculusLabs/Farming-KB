## Grain Spawn Production

[[grain-spawn]] is the living bridge between sterile laboratory agar cultures and the bulk substrates used for mushroom fruiting. It consists of cereal grains colonized by mushroom mycelium, providing a nutrient-rich, easily distributable inoculum for large-scale cultivation.

## Purpose of Grain Spawn

Grain spawn transitions mycelium from a small agar plate into a robust, voluminous inoculum capable of colonizing large fruiting substrates such as straw, wood chips, sawdust, or composted materials. Each individual grain kernel acts as a discrete point of inoculation, so when grain spawn is mixed into bulk substrate, hundreds or thousands of colonization fronts advance simultaneously — dramatically reducing the time needed for full colonization compared to direct agar inoculation.

## Grain Selection

Choosing the right grain is critical to successful [[spawn-production]]. The grain must provide adequate nutrition, hold moisture without becoming mushy, and allow mycelium to colonize between individual kernels.

**Recommended grains:**

- **Rye** — widely regarded as the gold standard; plump, holds moisture well, separates easily after cooking
- **Wheat** — excellent alternative with similar performance characteristics
- **Millet** — very small kernels provide an enormous number of inoculation points per volume; ideal for fast colonization
- **Milo (sorghum)** — durable, holds up well through pressure cooking, good moisture retention
- **Corn** — large kernels provide substantial nutrition but fewer inoculation points per volume

**Grains to avoid:**

- **Hulled grains** — loose hulls harbor contamination and interfere with [[mycelial-colonization]]
- **Sticky or gummy grains** — clump together after cooking, restricting gas exchange and creating anaerobic pockets
- **Explosive grains** — burst open during pressure cooking, creating a starchy mess that encourages bacterial contamination

## Container Options

Spawn containers must withstand high-pressure sterilization while allowing gas exchange to prevent anaerobic contamination.

- **Mason jars with filter disks** — most common option; 0.5 or 0.3 micron filter disks fitted under the ring allow breathing while excluding contaminants
- **Cotton or polyester fill** — budget-friendly alternative; polyfil or cotton packed into the jar lid opening provides adequate gas exchange
- **Tyvek covers** — postal or construction Tyvek cut and fitted over jar mouths; breathable and effective at filtering spores
- **Autoclavable bags** — polypropylene bags with built-in filter patches; essential for commercial-scale production

## Grain Preparation: The Precooking Method

Precooking grain before loading into containers is strongly preferred over adding dry grain and water directly to jars, which often results in uneven moisture distribution.

1. Measure the desired quantity of grain into a large pot
2. Cover with ample water and bring to a rolling boil
3. Boil for 10–15 minutes, then remove from heat
4. Soak in hot water for 30–60 minutes to allow full kernel hydration without bursting
5. Pour through a strainer and drain thoroughly
6. Spread grain on a clean surface and air-dry for 15–30 minutes
7. Grain is ready when the surface is dry to the touch but kernels remain plump internally — no moisture should remain on your fingers after squeezing a handful

## 10-Step Master Spawn Production

1. **Measure grain** — a standard quart jar holds approximately 1.5 cups of dry grain before cooking
2. **Boil and soak** — follow the precooking method to achieve proper hydration
3. **Strain and cool** — drain and air-dry until surface moisture is eliminated
4. **Fill containers** — load grain to roughly **half full**; headspace is critical for shaking and gas exchange
5. **Sterilize** — pressure cook at **12–15 PSI for 2 hours** to eliminate resistant endospores
6. **Cool under laminar flow** — move containers into a still-air box or [[laminar-flow-hood]]; cool completely (8–12 hours)
7. **Inoculate** — transfer approximately **10 agar cubes per quart jar** using a flame-sterilized scalpel
8. **Initial distribution** — gently shake to distribute agar pieces throughout the grain
9. **Shake after colonization visible** — once white mycelium is clearly visible (5–10 days), shake vigorously to redistribute colonized grains
10. **Monitor and final shake** — continue monitoring until fully colonized (2–4 weeks); final shake produces an even, easily distributable spawn

## Spawn Expansion Generations

| Generation | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **G0** | Isolate | Original pure culture on agar from spore germination, tissue clone, or culture slant |
| **G1** | Master grain spawn | Grain inoculated directly from G0 agar; the most genetically faithful generation |
| **G2** | Expanded grain spawn | Grain inoculated from G1; used to further increase volume |
| **G3** | [[substrate-spawn]] | Spawn used to inoculate the final fruiting substrate; may be grain or sawdust-based |

Limit expansion to G2 for most purposes. Each successive generation introduces additional contamination risk and genetic senescence.

## Contamination Inspection

**Mold indicators:**

- **Green or blue-green** — *Trichoderma* or *Penicillium*; the most common and aggressive contaminants
- **Black** — *Aspergillus niger*; dark speckled patches
- **Pink or salmon-colored** — *Neurospora*; extremely fast-spreading with vast spore production
- **White but distinctly different** — some molds mimic mycelium; look for powdery textures versus rhizomorphic growth

**Bacterial indicators:**

- Greasy, wet, or slimy patches on the grain surface
- Ropy or stringy textures when grains are disturbed
- Sour, rotten, or off-putting odors — healthy mycelium smells clean and earthy

## Troubleshooting and Quality Control

### Control Jars

Always prepare **un-inoculated control jars** alongside each batch. These go through every step but receive no inoculant. If contamination appears in a control jar, the problem lies in sterilization, grain preparation, or filter integrity rather than inoculation technique.

### HEPA Filter Testing

Periodically test laminar flow hoods by exposing open agar plates to the airflow and incubating them to check for colony growth, or use a particle counter to verify the filter captures particles at its rated efficiency.

## Liquid Culture Production

Liquid culture (LC) grows mycelium in a nutrient-rich liquid medium rather than on solid grain.

**Production method:** Prepare a sterile nutrient solution (~4% malt extract or honey by weight), sterilize in a flask or jar with a filter, inoculate with colonized grain or agar, and optionally blend once established to fragment and distribute the mycelium evenly.

**Advantages:**

- **Faster colonization** — liquid flows into every crevice of the receiving grain or substrate
- **Easy to scale** — a single LC jar can inoculate dozens of grain jars
- **Reduced labor** — no individual grain-to-grain transfers needed for expansion

**Disadvantages:**

- **Higher bacteria risk** — contamination is harder to detect in opaque liquid; a contaminated LC can silently ruin an entire batch
- **Shorter shelf life** — degrades faster than grain spawn or agar plates
- **No visual isolation** — unlike agar plates where contamination is immediately visible

## Streak Tests for Liquid Culture Screening

Before using liquid culture to inoculate production grain, screen it with a **streak test**:

1. Flame-sterilize an inoculation loop and dip it into the liquid culture
2. Streak across a nutrient agar plate in a zigzag or quadrant pattern
3. Incubate for 3–7 days at the appropriate temperature
4. Healthy mycelium produces uniform white growth along streak lines; colored colonies, sliminess, or irregular growth indicate contamination and the LC should be discarded

## Substrate Spawn: Sawdust-Based Alternative

For large-scale production, **sawdust-based spawn** offers a cost-effective alternative. Hardwood sawdust (often supplemented with bran) is sterilized and colonized by mushroom mycelium.

**Advantages:** Lower material cost, better compatibility with wood-based fruiting substrates, and reduced pest attraction from grain residues.

**Disadvantages:** Slower colonization (fewer discrete inoculation points), harder to break up and distribute evenly, and generally shorter shelf life.

For small-to-medium operations, grain spawn remains the most practical choice. Sawdust spawn becomes attractive at commercial scales where material costs are significant and production focuses on wood-loving species.
