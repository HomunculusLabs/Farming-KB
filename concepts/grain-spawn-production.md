# Grain Spawn Production

Grain spawn is the living mushroom mycelium grown on a sterilized grain
substrate, used as the inoculum for bulk substrate cultivation. It serves as
the bridge between a small, clean agar culture and a large-scale fruiting
substrate. Producing high-quality grain spawn is one of the most critical skills
in mushroom cultivation, because the health and vigor of the spawn directly
determines the success of the entire grow.

## Grain Selection

Several grains work well for spawn production. Rye grain is often considered the
gold standard because its individual kernels are large, nutrient-dense, and
resist clumping after hydration. Hard red winter wheat berries are an excellent
and widely available alternative. Millet and sorghum work well for species that
benefit from many inoculation points, since these small grains provide far more
individual kernels per volume. Brown rice, while usable, tends to become mushy
and is generally avoided. Popcorn can work but is less nutritious and more
expensive than standard grain choices.

The grain should be whole and undamaged, free from excessive dust, and stored
in a cool, dry place. Old grain that has been stored for years may have reduced
nutritional value and higher levels of natural contaminants.

## Hydration Methods

Grain must be hydrated to approximately 50 percent moisture content before
sterilization. There are several approaches, each with advantages:

The soak method involves submerging grain in water for 12 to 24 hours at room
temperature, then boiling it for 10 to 20 minutes until the kernels are swollen
and soft but not bursting. After boiling, the grain is drained well and spread
thinly to steam-dry and cool. The soaking period allows endospores of bacteria
present on the grain to germinate, making them more susceptible to destruction
during sterilization.

The boil method skips the long soak and simply boils the dry grain in water for
30 to 45 minutes, then drains and dries. This is faster but less effective at
eliminating bacterial endospores. A 24-hour soak is strongly recommended for
grain that will be pressure sterilized at standard durations.

The no-prep method involves measuring grain and water directly into the jar at
a specific ratio, typically around 1 part grain to 0.8 parts water by volume.
The jar is then sterilized, and the grain hydrates during the pressure cycle.
This is the simplest method but produces slightly less consistent results.

## Loading Jars

Hydrated grain is loaded into glass jars, typically quart-sized Mason jars, to
a fill level of about two-thirds full. This leaves adequate headspace for shaking
the jar later. Overfilled jars cannot be shaken effectively, which slows
colonization and increases the risk of anaerobic bacterial growth in compacted
areas.

Each jar lid should be modified with at least one gas exchange port. A hole
punched in the center of the lid and stuffed tightly with polyfill is the most
basic approach. More refined setups use synthetic filter discs, Tyvek sleeves,
or commercial self-healing injection ports. The filter must allow CO2 to escape
and oxygen to enter while blocking airborne mold spores and bacteria.

## Pressure Sterilization

Grain spawn must be sterilized in a pressure canner or autoclave, not a boiling
water bath. The target is 15 PSI (approximately 121 degrees Celsius or 250
degrees Fahrenheit) held for a minimum of 90 minutes for quart jars. Larger
volumes require longer times. This temperature and duration are necessary to kill
bacterial endospores that survive normal boiling.

The canner should be brought up to pressure on high heat, then the heat reduced
to maintain 15 PSI without excessive rocking or venting. A timer is started
only after the target pressure is reached. After the cycle, the canner is
removed from heat and allowed to depressurize naturally. Rapid depressurization
can cause jars to boil over, drawing unfiltered air through the filters.

## Inoculation

Once jars have cooled to room temperature, they are ready for inoculation. This
must be done inside a still air box or in front of a laminar flow hood using
proper sterile technique. Each jar receives a small amount of inoculum, either
from a spore syringe (2 to 5 cc), an agar wedge, or a scoop of colonized grain
from an existing jar (grain-to-grain transfer).

After inoculation, the jars are shaken vigorously to distribute the mycelium
throughout the grain. The jar is then placed in a warm location, ideally 75 to
80 degrees Fahrenheit, to begin colonization.

## Colonization

During colonization, mycelium spreads from each inoculation point through the
grain, digesting the starches and proteins. Visible white growth typically
appears within 3 to 7 days. Jars should be inspected daily for signs of
contamination. Any jar showing unusual colors, slimy textures, or foul odors
should be discarded immediately.

Once colonization reaches 20 to 30 percent, the jar can be shaken again to
redistribute colonized grain kernels throughout the uncolonized portions. This
significantly speeds up the process. Full colonization typically takes 2 to 4
weeks depending on species, temperature, and inoculation method.

## Storage

Fully colonized grain spawn can be stored in a refrigerator at 35 to 38 degrees
Fahrenheit for several months. Cold temperatures slow mycelial metabolism to a
near-standstill, preserving the vitality of the spawn. Spawn older than three
months may show reduced vigor and slower colonization when used to inoculate
bulk substrate. Some species, particularly aggressive colonizers like oyster
mushrooms, may continue to grow slowly even under refrigeration.
