## Tryptamine Biotransformation in *Psilocybe cubensis*

## Overview

The biotransformation of exogenous tryptamine into psilocin by *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]*
represents one of the most significant findings in fungal biochemical research. First
demonstrated by Gartz (1989) in *Planta Medica*, this process revealed that the
fruiting mycelia of *P. cubensis* possess powerful enzymatic machinery capable of
converting fed tryptamine into psilocin at concentrations far exceeding natural
levels—up to **3.3% dry mass**, the highest psilocin values ever reported in any
mushroom species.

*Psilocybe cubensis* is a subtropical indole-alkaloid-producing mushroom that
naturally contains psilocybin as its primary active compound, with only trace amounts
of psilocin. The discovery that supplementation with tryptamine hydrochloride
redirects the biosynthetic pathway toward massive [[psilocin-accumulation-flushes]] has profound
implications for understanding fungal secondary metabolism, enzymatic promiscuity,
and the biosynthetic route from simple indoles to psychoactive alkaloids.

## Biosynthetic Pathway Implications

### Natural Pathway: Tryptophan → Tryptamine → Psilocin → Psilocybin

In *P. cubensis*, [[baeocystin-and-norbaeocystin-in-psilocybin-biosynthesis]] proceeds from L-tryptophan through
decarboxylation to tryptamine, followed by hydroxylation at the 4-position of the
indole ring, and finally N-methylation and O-phosphorylation to produce psilocybin
(4-PO-HO-DMT). Earlier submerged-culture experiments using radioactive tracers
established that labeled tryptamine is a better precursor for psilocybin than
tryptophan itself, with no less than 22.4% of synthesized psilocybin deriving from
labeled tryptamine. Notably, psilocin levels in mycelial tissue from those
submerged-culture experiments were generally zero—suggesting that under normal
conditions, psilocin is rapidly phosphorylated to psilocybin rather than
accumulating as a free base.

### Pathway Redirection with Exogenous Tryptamine

When exogenous tryptamine HCl (25 mM) is introduced into the fruiting substrate,
the metabolic landscape shifts dramatically. The mycelium channels the abundant
tryptamine through hydroxylation and methylation steps, producing enormous quantities
of psilocin while [[psilocybin-synthesis]] is severely suppressed—dropping from typical
levels of ~0.5% to only 0.01–0.2% dry mass.

This pattern suggests a **bottleneck at the phosphorylation step**: the enzyme
responsible for converting psilocin to psilocybin (a phosphotransferase) becomes
saturated under high psilocin flux. Gartz proposed that reduced phosphate availability
in the culture media may further limit phosphorylation, causing psilocin to
accumulate as the terminal product rather than being converted to its phosphate ester.

### Enzymatic Capacity and Promiscuity

The enzyme systems of *P. cubensis* demonstrate remarkably **high hydroxylation and
methylation capacity**. The complete absence of detectable tryptamine in both fruit
bodies and [[maitake-spawn-run-vegetative-mycelial-phases]] extracts confirms that conversion is essentially
quantitative—the fungus does not store tryptamine but processes it immediately.

This enzymatic promiscuity aligns with broader mycological observations: many fungal
enzyme systems are non-specific and can oxidize exogenous compounds as readily as
their natural intermediates. Repke et al. reported trace baeocystin in some *P.
cubensis* strains approximately a decade before Gartz's work, supporting the
presence of multiple, flexible alkaloid-modifying enzyme systems.

## Experimental Results

### Substrate and Cultivation Conditions

Gartz used a dried cow dung and rice-grain mixture (2:1 ratio) hydrated at twice
the dry weight in water, enabling fast fructification without a casing layer. This
substrate outperformed both rye (with casing) and rice alone, producing the first
flush earliest. Tryptamine HCl was added at 25 mM concentration, with parallel
unsupplemented controls. First sporocarps appeared in 3–4 weeks; five flushes were
harvested per cycle. Mushrooms were freeze-dried, sealed, and stored at −10°C.

Average yield was ~3 g dry mass per 10 g substrate. Fructification times, yields,
fruit body sizes, and the characteristic bluing reaction were all equal between
supplemented and unsupplemented cultures—tryptamine did not impair normal fruiting
morphology at 25 mM.

### Alkaloid Profiles: Tryptamine-Supplemented vs. Control

Analysis by HPLC and TLC revealed striking differences across all five flushes:

| Flush | Psilocin (+Trp) | Psilocybin (+Trp) | Psilocin (Ctrl) | Psilocybin (Ctrl) |
|-------|-----------------|-------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| 1     | 2.1%            | 0.01%             | absent          | 0.55%             |
| 2     | 3.3%            | 0.02%             | 0.01%           | 0.48%             |
| 3     | 2.8%            | 0.2%              | 0.02%           | 0.51%             |
| 4     | 3.1%            | 0.07%             | 0.09%           | 0.46%             |
| 5     | 2.9%            | 0.13%             | 0.15%           | 0.61%             |

**Psilocin levels of 2.1–3.3% dry mass** are unprecedented—all previously reported
values in dried mushrooms fall below 1%. The peak psilocin (flush 2: 3.3%) coincided
with near-minimal psilocybin (0.02%), reinforcing the inverse relationship. Controls
showed the expected profile: moderate psilocybin (0.46–0.61%) with consistently low
or absent psilocin, particularly in the first flush.

### Complete Conversion and Baeocystin Observations

Tryptamine was never detected in any mushroom sample or in methanolic extracts of
vegetative mycelia from the substrate—confirming **complete biochemical conversion**
of the supplemented precursor into downstream alkaloids with no residual accumulation.

Gartz (1987) was unable to detect baeocystin in *P. cubensis*. Repke et al. had
reported traces in other strains ~10 years earlier, but the absence of baeocystin in
supplemented cultures suggests the tryptamine-to-psilocin pathway does not
significantly shunt through baeocystin intermediates. *[[inocybe-aeruginascens]]*, by
contrast, contains only traces of psilocin but high baeocystin—illustrating
species-specific differences in alkaloid biosynthetic routing.

## Practical Cultivation Notes
### PF TEK Application

The tryptamine supplementation technique has been adapted for PF TEK: tryptamine HCl
is added to the standard 1/2 pint jar of vermiculite, [[brown-rice-powder]], and water.
A notable observation is that **fruit bodies grow dwarfed** when supplemented.
Despite reduced size, bio-assay indicated at least **3× the usual potency**, likely
from metabolic resource diversion toward alkaloid biosynthesis. The recommended dose
is **0.1 g or less tryptamine HCl per jar** for the lighter PF substrate; the
originally suggested 0.16 g is considered excessive and may worsen dwarfing without
proportional potency gains.

### Preservation and Storage

For optimal preservation of psilocin-rich fruit bodies, Gartz recommended a **cool
desiccation technique**: dry mushrooms in a refrigerator under cold conditions to
minimize oxidative degradation of psilocin (less stable than psilocybin due to the
absent phosphate group); store dried fungi in an airtight container with desiccant;
maintain freezer storage at −10°C or below. This is especially critical for
tryptamine-supplemented fruit bodies, where elevated psilocin makes oxidative
degradation a greater concern than in specimens dominated by stable psilocybin.

## Significance and Broader Implications
The Gartz (1989) study is foundational for several reasons:

- **Biosynthetic proof of concept:** Confirmed the tryptamine-to-psilocin pathway is highly active in fruiting mycelia in vivo.
- **Enzyme promiscuity:** Quantitative conversion of exogenous substrate reveals flexibility of fungal alkaloid-modifying enzymes at non-native concentrations.
- **Phosphorylation as rate-limiting step:** Dramatic psilocin accumulation at psilocybin's expense identifies phosphorylation as the key regulatory bottleneck.
- **Analytical benchmark:** Psilocin up to 3.3% remains the highest documented in any mushroom species and serves as an upper reference for method validation.
- **Practical [[cannabis-potency-enhancement-intact-flowers]]:** The 3-fold potency increase in PF TEK adaptations demonstrates [[growing-gourmet-substrate-supplementation]] can modulate alkaloid profiles, though with morphological trade-offs.
