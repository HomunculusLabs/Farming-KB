## Overview

Fungal degradation of textile dyes is a major application of [[mycoremediation-techniques]]. The textile dye industry produces over 700,000 tons of synthetic dyes annually, with 10-15% lost directly to wastewater. These dyes — including azo, anthraquinone, phthalocyanine, triphenylmethane, and heterocyclic classes — are environmentally persistent, potentially carcinogenic, and resistant to conventional wastewater treatment.

## The Problem

Textile processing (desizing, scouring, bleaching, dyeing, printing, finishing) generates complex wastewater containing more than 100,000 commercially available synthetic dyes. Key concerns include:

- **Toxicity**: Many dyes contain benzidine and other aromatic carcinogens; azo dyes can form toxic amines under anaerobic conditions
- **Environmental persistence**: Complex aromatic structures resist conventional biological treatment
- **Heavy metals**: Metal-complex dyes release chromium and other metals
- **Bioaccumulation**: Some disperse dyes accumulate in aquatic systems

## Fungal Degradation Mechanisms

Fungi decolorize and degrade dyes through several mechanisms (Table 10.4, Singh 2006):

1. **Adsorption** — Biosorption to fungal cell walls (rapid, non-specific)
2. **Biodegradation** — Enzymatic breakdown of chromophore structures
3. **Adsorption + biodegradation** — Sequential processes in living mycelium
4. **Mineralization** — Complete breakdown to CO2 (rare; 23-48% achieved for some radiolabeled azo dyes)
5. **Carbon source utilization** — Use of dyes as sole carbon source (limited)

## Key Fungal Species

### White-Rot Fungi (Most Effective)

About 50% of dye decolorization research involves white-rot fungi:

- **Phanerochaete chrysosporium** — First established for polymeric dye degradation (Glenn and Gold, 1983); achieves 100% removal of Orange II, Azure B, Congo Red via ligninolytic enzymes
- **Trametes versicolor** — Superior to P. chrysosporium in many cases; 96% removal of Remazol Blue/Red; produces laccase, MnP, and LiP
- **Bjerkandera adusta** — Reduces toxicity (EC50) of Reactive Blue 38 from 37 to 8-9 against Vibrio fischeri
- **Pleurotus ostreatus** — 49% decolorization of olive mill wastewater; effective on multiple dye classes
- **Irpex lacteus** — 90-100% removal of Reactive Black 5, RBBR, Cu-phthalocyanine, and Bromophenol Blue

### Non-White-Rot Fungi

- **Aspergillus foetidus** — >98% removal of Remazol dyes via adsorption
- **Cunninghamella elegans** — Transforms Malachite Green via cytochrome P450-mediated N-demethylation
- **Geotrichum sp.** — >99% removal of Reactive Black 5, Red 158, Yellow 27 via MnP and laccase

### Yeasts

- **Candida zeylanoides** — Cleaves azo bonds, producing aromatic amines
- **Kluyveromyces marxianus** — >94% removal of Remazol Black B in 24 hours
- **Pseudozyma rugulosa** — 99% removal of Reactive Brilliant Red

## Enzymatic Systems

The primary enzymes involved are [[mycoremediation-enzymology|laccases, lignin peroxidase (LiP), and manganese peroxidase (MnP)]]. Nitrogen limitation generally enhances ligninolytic enzyme production and dye degradation. Laccase from Pleurotus pulmonarius during solid-state fermentation correlates with high decolorization of structurally different dyes.

## Regulatory Context

Color discharge limits exist worldwide: <200 ADMI units (US), absorbance-based standards (Germany), "water-white" discharges (UK), forbidden azo colorants (Denmark). See [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]] for details.

## Practical Applications

Field applications have demonstrated measurable results across diverse environments. In industrial settings, primary uses include contaminated water treatment, soil amendment at brownfield sites, and integration into wastewater treatment infrastructure. The approach scales from household systems to municipal installations, with cost-effectiveness improving at scale. Pilot programs in agricultural regions show particular promise where the technology integrates with existing farming practices.
