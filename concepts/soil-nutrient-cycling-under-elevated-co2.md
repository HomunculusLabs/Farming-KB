## Soil Nutrient Cycling Under Elevated CO₂

Elevated atmospheric CO₂ concentrations alter plant tissue chemistry, decomposition dynamics, and soil nutrient availability in ways that cascade through fungal communities and ecosystem processes. The effects are complex, often inconsistent, and depend on plant species, ecosystem type, and local soil conditions.

## Changes in Plant Litter Chemistry

### Carbon:Nitrogen Ratios

Elevated CO₂ generally increases the C:N ratio of plant tissues:

- A doubling of CO₂ produces approximately a 15% increase in leaf C:N ratio.
- Oak leaf litter in Mediterranean forests showed increased C:N and lignin:N ratios under elevated CO₂, retarding decomposition by 5–8% in field experiments and 12.5% in microcosm experiments.
- Beech twigs showed dramatic changes: C:N ratio increased from 45.6 to 72.7, and lignin:N from 16.3 to 22.4.
- Grassland species (*Lolium perenne*, *Agrostis capillaris*, *Festuca ovina*) showed increased C:N ratios when CO₂ was doubled from ambient (350 ml l⁻¹).

### Nitrogen Content Reduction

Reduced nitrogen content in leaves grown under elevated CO₂:

- Sweet chestnut (*Castanea sativa*): 50% reduction
- Sycamore (*Acer platanoides*): 19% reduction

Lower nitrogen content stimulated grazing by isopods (*Oniscus asellus* and *Porcellio scaber*), suggesting complex cascading effects on detritivore communities.

### Phosphorus Ratios

C:P ratio responses to elevated CO₂ are highly variable with no consistent trend in the literature.

## Decomposition Responses

### Reduced Saprotrophic Activity

Lower leaf litter resource quality (higher C:N, lignin:N) under elevated CO₂ reduces saprotrophic community activity, including fungi:

- The reduction in nitrogen content directly limits decomposer microbial growth.
- Higher lignin:N ratios slow enzymatic breakdown of structural compounds.
- This creates a potential bottleneck in nutrient recycling.

### Inconsistent Decomposition Rates

Despite changed litter chemistry, decomposition rates don't always respond predictably:

- Beech twig decomposition rates and N/lignin dynamics were unchanged despite significant C:N and lignin:N increases.
- Gorisen and Cotrufo (2000) cautioned that leaf chemistry changes may not correlate with decomposition rates.
- Respired CO₂ during decomposition could not be attributed to labeled carbon accumulated under elevated CO₂ treatment, suggesting internally allocated carbon follows different pathways.

## Carbon Partitioning to Below-Ground Systems

A critical finding is that carbon sequestered under elevated CO₂ is preferentially translocated to roots rather than retained in leaves:

- Leaves contributed approximately 6% to remaining carbon during 222-day decomposition.
- Roots contributed approximately 26% to remaining carbon.
- This highlights the importance of plant root material for below-ground carbon storage.

### Root System Responses

Root responses to elevated CO₂ are case- and species-specific:

- *Bromus madritensis* (Mediterranean old field): 31% root biomass increase, 88% root length increase.
- No consistent pattern exists for root:shoot ratios, root architecture, nutrient absorption capacity, or nutrient use efficiency across studies.
- More branched root systems have higher energy demands due to greater meristematic tissue, which has higher metabolic rates than other root tissue.

## Nitrogen Cycling Under Elevated CO₂

### Nitrogen Limitation on Carbon Sequestration

McMurtrie et al. (2000) demonstrated that long-term forest carbon sequestration is limited by nitrogen availability. This is where mycorrhizal fungi become critical:

- Ponderosa pine forests under elevated CO₂ accessed more nitrogen from surface soil horizons or from recalcitrant soil nitrogen forms to support increased biomass.
- Ectomycorrhizal [[fungal-enzymatic-capabilities]] for acquiring nitrogen from organic sources could be increasingly beneficial in a high-CO₂ world.
- *[[suillus-bovinus]]* (N-poor environment specialist) doubled plant nitrogen content compared to *[[laccaria-laccata]]* (N-rich specialist), irrespective of CO₂ concentration.

### Below-Ground Nitrogen Dynamics

Different soil types channel carbon and nitrogen through different pathways:

- **Mor soils** (distinct surface organic horizons): Carbon investment in roots supports soil exploitation and nutrient uptake where nitrogen is readily supplied.
- **Mull soils** (organic matter incorporated into mineral horizons): Photosynthates are used to stimulate N mineralization through rhizodeposition of easily assimilated carbon, priming the microbial community to decompose organic nitrogen (the "priming effect").

### Fungal Food Web Effects

Elevated CO₂ increases fungal biomass, which cascades through soil food webs:

- Increased fungal-feeding nematodes suggest higher energy transfer through fungal-based food webs at elevated CO₂.
- However, [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] are less favored food sources for soil fauna than saprotrophic fungi (Klironomos and Kendrick, 1996; Klironomos and Ursic, 1998), potentially allowing more carbon to be retained as fungal biomass or glomalin rather than being consumed and mineralized.

## Implications for Mycorrhizal Function

### Enhanced Mycorrhizal Importance

Under elevated CO₂, several factors increase the importance of mycorrhizal fungi:

1. **Higher C:N litter**: Makes organic nutrient sources more critical; mycorrhizal fungi with enzymatic capabilities for organic N and P acquisition become more valuable.
2. **Greater carbon supply**: Enhanced photosynthesis provides more carbohydrates to support mycorrhizal development and function.
3. **Nutrient limitation**: As plant growth increases demand for nutrients, mycorrhizal access to soil nutrients becomes proportionally more important.

### Species Shifts

The effect of enhanced CO₂ differs considerably among mycorrhizal fungal species, potentially shifting community structure:

- Infection rates and extraradical hyphal growth varied between *Glomus* species, *[[acaulospora-denticulata]]*, and *[[scutellospora-calospora]]* under elevated CO₂.
- Only the two *Glomus* species increased plant growth under elevated CO₂; other genera changed the form of mycorrhizal association without affecting plant growth.
- Douglas fir ectomycorrhizal communities shifted toward *Rhizopogon* and *Cenococcum* morphotypes with elevated temperature.

## The Carbon-Fertilizer Feedback Loop

A potential positive feedback loop connects elevated CO₂, mycorrhizal function, and nutrient acquisition:

1. Increased atmospheric CO₂ → enhanced photosynthesis
2. Greater carbohydrate supply to roots and mycorrhizae
3. Increased mycorrhizal growth and soil exploration
4. Enhanced capture of limiting nutrients (P for AM, N for ECM)
5. Alleviation of nutritional stress → further increased plant growth
6. More CO₂ fixation and carbohydrate production

However, this loop may be moderated by:
- Increased saprotrophic decomposition under warming (reducing plant dependence on mycorrhizae)
- Rapid AM hyphal turnover increasing soil respiration
- Species-specific responses that don't universally enhance nutrient acquisition

## Knowledge Gaps

Several critical uncertainties remain:

- The mycorrhizal compartment is rarely separated from root components in carbon studies.
- Quantitative measures of mycorrhizal contribution to long-term carbon storage are limited.
- Most studies use controlled microcosms; mesocosm and field studies with multiple interacting factors are needed.
- Synergistic and antagonistic interactions among environmental change factors are difficult to disentangle in single-factor experiments.
