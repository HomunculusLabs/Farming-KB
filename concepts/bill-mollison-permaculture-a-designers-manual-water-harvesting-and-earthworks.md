# Water Harvesting and Earthworks

Water is the most critical limiting factor in most land-based systems, and
Bill Mollison devoted significant attention to water harvesting and earthworks
in *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*. The fundamental principle is that
water should be captured, stored, and distributed across the landscape using
gravity and natural processes rather than pumped or imported energy. Effective
water design transforms erosion into fertility, drought into abundance, and
runoff into groundwater recharge. These techniques are foundational to
[[permaculture-design]] of energy cycling and biological resource
use, and they integrate closely with [[zone-and-sector-analysis]] and
[[faires-climate-zones-and-microclimate-design]].

## The Hydrological Cycle and Design

Mollison framed water harvesting within the context of the complete
hydrological cycle. Rain falls on a landscape and follows one of several
paths: it may infiltrate into the soil, run off across the surface, evaporate
back to the atmosphere, or be transpired by plants. In degraded landscapes,
most rain runs off quickly, carrying topsoil and nutrients with it. The
permaculture designer's task is to slow, spread, and sink water so that it
infiltrates the soil, recharges groundwater, and becomes available to plants
over extended periods. Every drop of rain that falls on a property should be
accounted for in the design.

## Swales on Contour

Swales are one of the most important earthworks in the permaculture toolkit.
A swale is a shallow ditch excavated on the exact contour of a slope, with
the excavated soil placed on the downhill side to form a berm. When rain falls,
water flows downhill and is intercepted by the swale, where it spreads along
the contour and infiltrates into the soil. The berm above the swale is
planted with trees and deep-rooted perennials that access the stored moisture.
Swales convert runoff into groundwater recharge, halt erosion, and create a
moist planting zone that supports [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]] on what
might otherwise be dry slopes. Mollison recommended spacing swales according
to the slope gradient and rainfall intensity of the site.

## Keyline Design

Keyline design was developed by P.A. Yeomans in Australia and was heavily
promoted by Mollison as a sophisticated approach to water management on
undulating terrain. The keyline is a contour line identified by finding the
point where the valley floor changes from convex to concave shape. Plowing
parallel to the keyline, regardless of whether moving uphill or downhill from
it, creates a pattern of cultivation that directs water from ridges into
valleys, evenly distributing moisture across the landscape. Keyline plowing
uses a subsoil-ripping implement called a Yeomans plow that fractures compacted
soil layers without inverting the soil profile, improving [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]]
by allowing deeper water penetration and root growth.

## Dams and Water Storage

Mollison provided detailed guidance on the design and construction of dams for
water storage. The choice of dam site depends on the shape of the catchment,
the soil type, and the intended use of the stored water. A good dam site has a
narrow valley with broad sides, impermeable clay subsoil, and a sufficient
catchment area above it. Mollison classified dams by their position in the
landscape: high dams for gravity-fed irrigation, mid-slope dams for general
use, and valley floor dams for large-scale storage and [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems]].
The dam wall must be constructed with proper compaction, adequate freeboard,
and a spillway designed to handle maximum flood events without erosion. Dam
sites should be integrated into the overall [[zone-and-sector-analysis]] plan.

## Contour Banks and Diversion Drains

Contour banks are raised earth embankments built on contour to slow runoff and
reduce erosion on grazing land and cropland. They are simpler than swales and
are typically used in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-broadacre-and-rural-design]] where the scale makes
swale construction impractical. Diversion drains intercept water flowing across
a slope and redirect it to storage or safe discharge points. Both structures
are fundamental to erosion control and water management on agricultural land.

## Gabions and Check Dams

Gabions are wire cages filled with rocks placed in watercourses to slow water
flow and trap sediment. Check dams serve a similar purpose and can be
constructed from rocks, logs, or earth. These structures are used in gullies
and erosion channels to halt headward erosion and rebuild soil. Over time,
sediment accumulates behind gabions and check dams, raising the channel floor
and creating new planting areas. Mollison recommended these as low-cost,
low-technology solutions for degraded landscapes.

## Terracing for Steep Slopes

On steep slopes, terraces create level planting surfaces that prevent erosion
and make cultivation possible. Mollison described several terrace types
including bench terraces for crops, orchard terraces for trees, and
retaining-wall terraces for structural stability. Terraces can be constructed
from stone, earth, or timber, and should always incorporate drainage to
prevent waterlogging and wall failure. Terraced landscapes appear throughout
traditional agriculture in Asia, the Mediterranean, and the Andes, validating
the effectiveness of this approach across diverse cultures and climates.

## Urban Water Harvesting

In urban and suburban settings, water harvesting focuses on rooftop catchment,
greywater recycling, and stormwater management. Rainwater tanks connected to
rooftop gutters provide potable or irrigation water. Greywater from showers,
sinks, and laundry can be treated through simple reed bed systems and used
to irrigate gardens. [[urban-permaculture-trees-water-cycling]] designs incorporate permeable
surfaces, rain gardens, and bioswales to manage stormwater on-site rather
than channeling it to storm drains. These strategies connect to
[[energy-efficient-house-design]] through integrated water management systems.

## Integration with Other Systems

Water harvesting earthworks are never designed in isolation. Swales feed water
to tree crops in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]]. Dams supply water to
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems]] and irrigation for [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-broadacre-and-rural-design]].
Keyline design improves soil conditions for [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-soil-and-humus-management]].
The permaculture designer integrates all water management into a comprehensive
plan that supports every other element of the system, creating a self-reinforcing
network of water, soil, plants, and animals that becomes more productive and
resilient over time.
