# Natural Farming Soil Ecology

Masanobu Fukuoka's understanding of soil ecology forms the scientific foundation of his
[[fukuoka-natural-farming-method]]. Rather than treating soil as an inert substrate requiring human
management through tilling, fertilizing, and amending, Fukuoka demonstrated that soil
is a living system capable of self-enrichment and self-regulation when left to its own
devices. His observations challenge the fundamental assumptions of conventional soil
science and offer a radically different model for plant-soil relationships.

## The Living Soil

Fukuoka's farm provides dramatic evidence of soil's inherent fertility. One gram of
soil on his farm contains about 100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria and other
soil-enriching microbes. This [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] exists naturally in undisturbed soil.
Fukuoka felt that soil containing seeds and these microorganisms could be the spark
that restores even the world's deserts.

Hundreds of millions of microorganisms crowd each gram of soil. Bacteria are present,
but so are other bacteria that kill these bacteria, and yet other bacteria that kill
the killer bacteria. The soil contains bacteria harmful to man, but also many that are
harmless or beneficial. The soil in the fields under the sun is not only healthy but
pure. No amount of research can teach man everything about the soil, and he will never
create soils more perfect than those of nature.

## Nature's Tillers

A central insight is that plowing is unnecessary because nature provides its own
tillage system: "No plant on the face of the earth is so weak as to germinate only in
plowed soil. Man has no need to plow and turn the earth, for microorganisms and small
animals act as nature's tillers."

The conventional approach — flooding a field, stirring with a plow — causes the ground
to set as hard as plaster. This dead, compacted soil then requires annual plowing to
soften it. Farmers create the conditions that make a plow useful, then rejoice at the
utility of their tool. In natural farming, the soil remains loose and crumbly because
it is alive with organisms that maintain its structure from within.

## Soil Self-Enrichment

One of Fukuoka's most radical claims: "Soil enriches itself year in and year out
without man having to lift a finger." This is achieved through several natural
mechanisms operating simultaneously in healthy, undisturbed soil.

### Rice Straw as Fertilizer

Rice straw applied to the fields half a year before rice is sown eliminates the need
for [[cultivator-compost-preparation-phase-methods]] entirely. As straw decomposes on the soil surface, it feeds the
microbial community, which makes nutrients available to plants. This differs
fundamentally from prepared compost, which requires labor-intensive turning, watering,
and monitoring. The straw simply lies where it falls after harvest.

### Green Manure Plants

Leguminous green manure plants such as clover, alfalfa, and bur clover play a vital
role. Through their symbiotic relationship with nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root
systems, these plants convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms available to other plants.
In Fukuoka's rice/barley succession system, clover serves as both a ground cover that
suppresses weeds and a continuous nitrogen source for grain crops.

### The Microbial Economy

The soil functions as a complex economy where microorganisms, small animals, plant
roots, [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] exchange nutrients in a continuous cycle. Some organisms
break down organic matter, others fix nitrogen, still others make minerals available
through metabolic processes. When this system is intact, no external fertilizer inputs
are needed.

## Natural Pest Balance

In a healthy ecosystem, pest populations are naturally regulated by predators and the
overall biological community balance: "No need for pesticides because nature strikes a
balance among a thousand diseases."
