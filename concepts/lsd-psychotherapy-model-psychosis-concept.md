# LSD in Psychotherapy and the Model Psychosis Concept

## Overview

The introduction of [[lsd]] into psychiatric research in the late 1940s and 1950s opened two major lines of investigation: its use as an adjunct to psychotherapy and its use as an experimental model for psychosis. This concept page examines both applications, the researchers who pioneered them, and the evolving understanding of LSD's relationship to mental illness.

## LSD in Psychotherapy

### The Sandoz Delysid Program

Sandoz distributed LSD under the trade name Delysid to qualified researchers, along with a prospectus that listed two primary indications. The first was "analytical psychotherapy"—the use of LSD to facilitate access to unconscious material during therapeutic sessions.

### Pioneer Researchers

Hofmann credits several researchers who "deserve to be named in the front rank" for their contributions to LSD psychotherapy:

**United States:**
- **A. K. Busch and W. C. Johnson**: Early clinical investigators
- **S. Cohen and B. Eisner**: Pioneers of psychedelic therapy
- **H. A. Abramson**: Conducted extensive research on LSD's effects
- **H. Osmond and A. Hoffer**: Canadian researchers who coined the term "psychedelic" and developed the "psychotomimetic" model; worked with Abram Hoffer on the adrenochrome hypothesis of schizophrenia

**England:**
- **R. A. Sandison**: Pioneer of LSD-assisted psychotherapy at Powick Hospital, treating hundreds of patients

**Germany:**
- **W. Frederking and H. Leuner**: Developed psycholytic therapy approaches using lower doses of LSD in repeated sessions

**Czechoslovakia:**
- **G. Roubicek and S. Grof**: Stanislav Grof would later become the most influential theorist of psychedelic therapy, developing the framework of "transpersonal psychology" based on his LSD research

### Therapeutic Mechanisms

The therapeutic value of LSD in psychotherapy was attributed to several mechanisms:
- **Lowering of psychological defenses**: LSD appeared to reduce the ego's resistance to confronting painful or repressed material
- **Enhanced access to unconscious content**: Patients reported vivid recall of forgotten memories, childhood experiences, and traumatic events
- **Emotional intensification**: Feelings associated with recovered memories were experienced with unusual vividness and immediacy
- **Cognitive flexibility**: New perspectives on longstanding problems became available during the LSD state

## The Model Psychosis Concept

### Early Enthusiasm

"In the early days of LSD research, it was often claimed that LSD inebriation has something to do with a type of 'model psychosis.'" The reasoning was straightforward: if LSD could produce states that resembled schizophrenia and other psychoses, then studying the LSD experience might reveal the biochemical basis of mental illness.

### The Theory of Endogenous Psychotoxins

The model psychosis concept was linked to the hypothesis that "various mental disturbances could be produced by psychotoxic metabolic products that have the power, even in minimal quantities, to alter the functions of brain cells." LSD represented "a substance that certainly does not occur in the human organism" but that could mimic the effects of hypothetical endogenous psychotoxins.

This was the "pink spot" hypothesis—the idea that schizophrenics produced abnormal metabolic byproducts (like adrenochrome, identified by Osmond and Hoffer) that caused their symptoms. If LSD could replicate these symptoms, it might serve as a tool for identifying and studying the natural psychotoxins.

### Dismissal of the Model

"This idea was dismissed, however, because extended comparative investigations showed that there were essential differences between the manifestations of psychosis and the LSD experience." The key differences included:

1. **Duration**: Psychotic episodes last days, weeks, or indefinitely; LSD experiences last 8-12 hours
2. **Insight**: LSD users typically retain insight that their experience is drug-induced; psychotics genuinely believe their delusions
3. **Content**: LSD experiences tend toward the visionary and cosmic; psychotic experiences tend toward the paranoid and personal
4. **Aftermath**: LSD experiences resolve completely; psychotic episodes may cause permanent cognitive changes

### The Residual Value

Despite the failure of the strict "model psychosis" hypothesis, the LSD model retained value as a tool for "studying deviations from the normal psychic and mental condition, and to observe the biochemical and electrophysiological alterations associated with them." Even if LSD did not exactly replicate psychosis, it produced consistent and reproducible alterations of consciousness that could be studied under controlled conditions.

## The Two Paradigms

### Psychotomimetic vs. Psychedelic

The tension between the "model psychosis" (psychotomimetic) and "mind-manifesting" (psychedelic) interpretations of LSD shaped the entire history of its clinical use:

- **Psychotomimetic**: LSD mimics psychosis; its value is in studying mental illness (Osmond, Hoffer)
- **Psychedelic**: LSD reveals aspects of the mind not normally accessible; its value is in therapy and self-understanding (Grof, Leuner)

Hofmann himself eventually came to favor the psychedelic interpretation, recognizing that the similarities between LSD states and psychotic states were superficial compared to the profound differences in meaning, content, and outcome.

## Legacy

The psychotherapy research program, though interrupted by the criminalization of LSD in the 1960s, laid the groundwork for the modern revival of psychedelic therapy. The current research on [[psilocybin]] for depression, MDMA for PTSD, and LSD for anxiety in terminal illness all build on the clinical paradigms developed by the pioneers Hofmann names.
