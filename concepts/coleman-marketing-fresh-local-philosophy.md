# Marketing Philosophy — Fresh, Local, and Beyond Organic

## The Industrialization of Organic

[[coleman-deep-organic-farming-philosophy]] gave
small growers a unique marketing advantage. That advantage
disappeared when organic became industrialized. As more and more
organic produce became available from distant sources, the word
"organic" lost its power to distinguish truly superior food from
merely certified commodities.

Industrial organic produce, while meeting the legal requirements
for certification, is nowhere near as well grown or as flavorful as
the organic produce from a small family farm. The profit-at-all-costs
mentality of industrial organics has undermined the traditional
values that motivated the organic pioneers. Coleman concluded that
the word "organic" is no longer an adequate distinction, and that
small farmers need a new way to advertise the superiority of their
produce.

## The Keywords: Fresh and Local

The important words that Coleman stresses are "fresh" and "local."
No matter who grew a product or how it was grown, produce shipped
from far away is at least a week old by the time it reaches the
customer. Long-distance produce is not fresh by any meaningful
definition. Astute eaters who shop at roadside stands and farmers
markets know the superiority of truly fresh vegetables and actively
seek out local growers.

To attract and keep appreciative food lovers as loyal customers,
Coleman focused on establishing a reputation for quality and further
branding produce through imaginative packaging. The combination of
unmatched freshness, local provenance, and beautiful presentation
creates a product that industrial organic simply cannot compete
with, regardless of scale or price.

## Packaging as Brand Identity

Coleman packs for delivery in distinctive 12-by-16-by-8-inch wooden
boxes, handmade from native Maine cedar trees cut on the farm and
milled at a neighbor's sawmill. The reusable box idea came from
reading that 25 percent of the volume in the average landfill was
food-packaging waste. Since all sales are to local markets, it was
easy to avoid contributing to that waste stream.

The boxes are sanded smooth and coated with a food-grade finish.
The trademark "Seasonal Salads" is burned into the end boards with
a branding iron. These boxes look attractive in produce coolers,
catch the customer's eye, establish brand loyalty, and help sell
whatever they contain. The box dimensions were determined by
measuring both produce coolers in stores and the cargo space of the
delivery vehicle.

## The Quality Advantage

Beautiful crops and professional presentation are described as the
keys to selling everything a grower can produce. The eating quality
of locally grown, freshly harvested organic vegetables creates
customers for life. Coleman recounts that parents in neighboring
towns report that their children, notorious for hating vegetables,
specifically ask for the farm's carrots and spinach. He puts great
faith in the honest and unspoiled taste buds of children, who can
detect quality differences that older palates may miss.

This quality advantage extends to nutritional value as well.
Coleman quotes Aldo Leopold's observation from A Sand County
Almanac: "The products of fertile soil may be qualitatively as well
as quantitatively superior. We can bolster poundage from depleted
soils by pouring on imported fertility, but we are not necessarily
bolstering food-value." Fresh, locally grown organic produce from
living soil offers nutritional benefits that industrial systems
cannot replicate.
