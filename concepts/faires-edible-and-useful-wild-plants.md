# Faires Edible and Useful Wild Plants

Wild and semi-wild plants represent some of the most resilient and low-maintenance food sources in the permaculture landscape. The Faires guide catalogs numerous edible wild plants that grow spontaneously, require minimal care, and provide substantial nutrition. These plants are often the first to establish in [[faires-ecological-succession-and-edge-design]] and serve as pioneer species in new garden systems.

## Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale)

The dandelion is perhaps the most underappreciated plant in the modern landscape. Every part is edible and extremely nutritious:

- **Flowers:** Made into wine or added to pancakes
- **Roots:** Brewed into a tea that tastes like coffee — a caffeine-free coffee substitute
- **Leaves:** Added to salads (young leaves are less bitter)
- **Growing:** Grows spontaneously in everyone's yard, needs no care at all

Dandelions represent the principle that food is already growing around us — we simply need to recognize and harvest it. Their deep taproots break compacted soil and bring minerals to the surface, making them valuable even where they are not actively harvested.

## Daylily (Hemerocallis fulva)

The Tawney Daylily is a remarkably versatile edible plant:

- **Entire plant is edible:** flowers, buds, roots, and shoots
- **Flower buds and flowers:** Most commonly used in Asian stir-fry dishes
- **Cooking method:** Cut off the base with the ovary, use like mushrooms
- **Growing:** Enjoys partial shade to full sun, requires little care
- **Wetland tolerance:** The Tawney variety tolerates wet conditions
- **Placement:** Too large and expansive for near-other-plants, best in outer zones
- **WARNING:** Only cultivated varieties are edible; others are toxic. Verify your species before eating.

Daylilies demonstrate the importance of correct species identification when foraging. The edible cultivated varieties and toxic wild varieties can look similar to the untrained eye.

## Duck Potato (Sagittaria latifolia)

Also known as Broadleaf Arrowheads or Wapato:

- Produces a root vegetable eaten like potatoes
- Grows directly in water or in marshy soil
- Can be planted from eyes, just like potatoes
- **Harvest limit:** No more than 1/4 should be collected per year — the plants will spread to replace losses
- Harvest anytime during summer and fall until the first frost

Duck potato is an excellent aquatic food plant for [[faires-pond-design-and-aquaculture]], providing a staple carbohydrate crop from the water's edge.

## Duckweed (Lemna spp.)

A tiny floating aquatic plant:

- Edible and extremely productive — doubles its biomass in 2-3 days under ideal conditions
- Can be harvested daily as animal feed (especially poultry and fish)
- High protein content makes it valuable as a livestock supplement
- Helps purify pond water by absorbing excess nutrients
- Can become invasive — manage carefully in small ponds

## Acorn Processing

Acorns from oak trees have been processed into flour for thousands of years, predating any grain flour:

**Collection:** Gather acorns when fully formed and beginning to fall. Don't take acorns without "hats" (caps). Discard any with holes (likely containing weevils).

**Drying:** Dry in the oven, [[faires-food-drying-and-no-energy-storage]], or in the sun.

**Shell removal:** Crack off the shell to access the nutmeat.

**Tannin removal (essential — tannins are toxic):**

*Boiling method:*
1. Put nutmeat in a large pot of water, bring to boil
2. Water will turn dark as tannin leaches out
3. Pour off dark water, refill, boil again
4. Repeat for 5-6 pots of water until water stays clear

*Soaking method:*
1. Grind nut into flour first
2. Soak in 1 part water to 3 parts flour
3. Keep in fridge for a week, switching water daily
4. Once tannin is gone, dry the nuts again

**Final processing:** Grind into fine flour. Use to replace up to half the flour in any recipe.

## Groundnut (Apios americana)

A nitrogen-fixing climbing plant that produces protein-rich tubers:

- Somewhat like a "new" potato, similar size, with many small roots
- Unlike potatoes, groundnuts are full of protein
- Climbs and sends out roots, invading neighboring areas — must be kept in check
- Roots cooked exactly like any other root vegetable
- The nitrogen-fixing legume trait makes it a soil builder as well as a food crop

## [[carob]] (Ceratonia siliqua)

A large evergreen shrub reaching 50 feet (15.2 meters):

- Prefers hot climates; commercially grown in the Mediterranean and Australia
- Not frost hardy (mature tree can withstand 20°F / -7°C if not blooming)
- Grows in any soil, tolerant to drought
- Won't tolerate heavy rain until end of growing season
- Pods are sweet and have many uses

**Harvest:** Shake tree with a long pole before winter rains. Catch pods on a tarp. Dry in sun for a couple of days until seeds rattle.

**Processing:**
- Crush pods to break up seeds for animal feed
- Ground into varying consistencies for syrup, jam, fine flour, and coffee substitute
- Animals should not eat too much crushed pods (growth stunting)
- Chickens cannot eat them at all

## Gooseberry (Ribes spp.)

A hardy shrub producing tart berries:

- Used in jams, preserves, and desserts
- Cold hardy and reliable producer
- Often found in hedgerows and forest edges
- Benefits from [[faires-orchard-pruning-methods]] for productivity

## Sloe / Blackthorn (Prunus spinosa)

A large shrub to 15 feet (5 meters) producing purple fruit:

- Fruit used in juice, wine, or jam
- Traditional wisdom: harvest after first frost for better flavor
- Large thorns make it an excellent hedge plant
- Wood useful for firewood and carpentry projects
- Flowers are cream-colored (not white like cherry plum) and bloom later

## Dew Collection with Stone Stacks

A technique for providing water to plants in desert conditions:

- Stack stones in a circle around a plant
- Stones collect dew from the air
- Moisture drips to the ground below, keeping grape vines or small trees alive
- This is most effective in the desert where clear skies and gentle breezes prevail

## Foraging Principles

The guide implies several principles for incorporating wild plants into the permaculture system:

1. **Identify correctly:** Toxic look-alikes exist (daylilies being the prime example)
2. **Harvest sustainably:** Never take more than a fraction (duck potato: max 25%)
