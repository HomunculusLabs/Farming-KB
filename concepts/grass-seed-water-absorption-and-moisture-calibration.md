# Grass Seed Water Absorption and Moisture Calibration

Dry grain may appear to colonise normally on the surface but harbours uncolonised kernels
in the interior of the jar, creating latent contamination risks that emerge during later

## Factors Affecting Water Absorption

Several factors influence how much water a given batch of grass seed will absorb during
[[cotter-compost-substrate-preparation-manure-mushroom-cultivation]]:

**Seed variety**: Different grass species and cultivars have different hull compositions
and endosperm structures that affect water uptake rates and total capacity. Rye grass,
Lolium perenne, is the most commonly used species, but mixtures of different grass
species have also been used with great success.

**Batch variation**: Even seeds of the same variety from the same supplier can vary
between harvests, [[vegetable-storage-conditions-by-temperature-and-humidity]], and age. A batch purchased one month may absorb
water very differently from the same product purchased six months later from the same
store. This variability is one of the primary reasons that cultivators should never
assume a previously successful water ratio will work unchanged with a new bag of seed.

**[[jadam-seed-treatment-and-nursery-management]]**: Seeds sold for agricultural planting are sometimes treated with
fungicides, which can affect both water absorption and [[brassinosteroid-psilocybe-cubensis-mycelial-growth-research]]. If seed has
been treated with fungicides it should say so on the packaging. Treated seed should
be avoided entirely for cultivation purposes, as the fungicide residues may inhibit
or prevent mycelial colonisation entirely.

**Hull integrity**: Mechanical damage during harvesting or transport can create fissures
in seed hulls that alter absorption patterns. Seeds with damaged hulls absorb water
more quickly but may also release nutrients prematurely, potentially favouring bacterial
over fungal growth during the early stages of colonisation.

**Storage conditions**: Seeds stored in humid environments may have pre-absorbed
atmospheric moisture, while very old seeds may have reduced absorption capacity as the
endosperm dries and hardens over time. Seeds stored in airtight containers in cool, dry
conditions maintain more consistent absorption characteristics.

## Sourcing Grass Seed

The source recommends purchasing grass seed from an animal feed store where it is sold
as bird feeding supply. This is typically the most economical option available to home
cultivators. Feed-grade seed intended for caged birds and poultry is rarely treated with
the fungicides that are commonly applied to agricultural planting seed, making it safer
for mycological use.

When selecting grass seed, check the packaging carefully for any indication of fungicide
treatment. Avoid seed that lists chemical treatments among its ingredients. The seed
should look clean, free of excessive dust, and should have a fresh grassy smell rather
than a musty or chemical odour that might indicate poor storage conditions or the
presence of mould contamination.

Garden centres and agricultural suppliers are alternative sources, but their seed is
more likely to be treated and is typically more expensive per kilogram than feed-store
bird seed. Online suppliers specialising in mycology supplies may offer pre-tested grass
seed guaranteed to be free of fungicide treatment, though at a premium price.

## Direct Addition Method

The standard approach involves measuring water and seed directly into the [[mushroom-cultivation-jar-lid-management-loose-fit-vacuum-gas-exchange]].
For 720 ml jars, combine 110 grams of grass seed with 180 ml water. The measurements
should be reasonably precise, as even small deviations in water volume can shift the
substrate from ideal to too wet or too dry.

After the water and seed have been filled into the jars, the lids are placed on but NOT
screwed tight. The lids must remain loose to allow steam to escape during sterilisation
and to prevent the jars from exploding under pressure. A double layer of tinfoil is
crumpled over the lid and top part of the jar, providing an additional barrier against
contamination during the sterilisation process.
