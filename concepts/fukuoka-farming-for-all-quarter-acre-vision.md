## Economic Independence

Small-scale natural farming offered economic independence that commercial farming could not. Without the need to buy seeds, fertilizer, pesticides, or fuel, the natural farmer's expenses were minimal. Most of what the family needed could be produced on the farm itself. Money was needed only for a few items that could not be made or grown locally.

Fukuoka noted that this independence was precisely what made natural farming unattractive to the agricultural establishment. Chemical companies, equipment manufacturers, seed suppliers, and government agencies all had a vested interest in keeping farmers dependent on external inputs. Natural farming threatened this system by demonstrating that farmers did not need any of it.

## The Village Economy

Fukuoka envisioned a revival of the traditional Japanese village economy, in which each community produced most of what it needed locally. Rice, vegetables, fruit, firewood, [[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-order-food-chain-unity]].

## Self-Sufficient Communities

Fukuoka envisioned a world of small, self-sufficient communities where all matters would be taken care of at the level of the family farm. The home, the community, and the natural environment -- all products of the nature in that region -- would be capable of self-sufficiency and self-reliance. When this was achieved, he believed, all the people of the world would at last be able to join hands in a position of equality.

This was not utopian dreaming but a practical observation. Before chemical agriculture and global trade, most human communities did feed themselves from their immediate surroundings. The traditional Japanese village, the Indian pueblo, the European peasant farm -- all were proof that small-scale self-sufficiency was not only possible but historically normal. What was abnormal, Fukuoka argued, was the current system in which food travels thousands of miles and farmers go into debt to buy chemical inputs.
