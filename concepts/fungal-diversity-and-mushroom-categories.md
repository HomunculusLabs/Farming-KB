# Fungal Diversity and Mushroom Categories

The fungal kingdom is staggeringly diverse — estimated at 1 to 2 million species, outnumbering plants at least 6 to 1. Only about 10% of mushroom species have been identified. From *Mycelium Running* by [[stamets-mycoremediation-principles]].

## Evolutionary Context

- Animals are more closely related to fungi than to any other kingdom (diverged ~600+ million years ago)
- Fungi colonized land more than a billion years ago
- Fungi enabled plants to inhabit land ~700 million years ago through symbiotic partnerships
- After mass extinctions (Permian-Triassic, Cretaceous), fungi surged to recycle debris fields
- Fungi respond to environmental changes with waves of mycelium, reflecting deep-rooted ancestry and diversity

## The Four Nutritional Strategies

### 1. Saprophytic (Decomposers)
Break down dead organic matter into simpler molecules. The premier recyclers.

**Primary decomposers**: First on fresh debris. Fast-growing. Include oyster (*Pleurotus*), shiitake (*Lentinula edodes*), maitake (*Grifola frondosa*), enoki (*Flammulina velutipes*).

**Secondary decomposers**: Work after primary fungi and bacteria. Include button mushroom (*Agaricus bisporus*), garden giant (*Stropharia rugoso-annulata*).

**Tertiary decomposers**: End-stage decomposers in complex microbial soil. Include *Agrocybe*, *Mycena*, *Clitocybe*.

Most gourmet and medicinal mushrooms are wood decomposers. Building soil is the primary outcome of saprophytic fungal activity.

### 2. Parasitic
Feed on living hosts. Often transition to saprophytic after host death. Honey mushrooms (*Armillaria*) are the classic example — they form the largest organisms on Earth (2,400 acres, 2,200+ years old). Some parasitic fungi can devastate forests of firs and oaks in months.

### 3. Mycorrhizal
Symbiotic partnerships with plant roots. The norm in nature, not the exception. A forest's vitality is directly related to the presence, abundance, and variety of mycelial associates.

**Ectomycorrhizal**: Form sheath around root tips (matsutake, boletus, chanterelles, truffles). More than 2,000 species associate with Douglas firs alone; 250 species with hemlocks.

**Endomycorrhizal (VAM)**: Invade root cell interiors. The roots of a young pine tree enveloped with mycelium extend the tree's range for absorbing nutrients and water while conferring a fungal defense against invasive diseases.

### 4. Endophytic
Live inside plant tissues without causing disease. Enhance growth and disease resistance. Produce specialized mycotoxins (often alkaloids) that protect host plants from insect attack. Still poorly understood — may be the largest and most important group. Most described endophytes are ascomycetes.

### Medicinal Mushrooms
Species with documented therapeutic properties — antibiotics, anticancer chemotherapeutic agents, immunomodulators, antivirals, and more.

Key species: reishi (*Ganoderma lucidum*), turkey tail (*Trametes versicolor*), shiitake (*Lentinula edodes*), maitake (*Grifola frondosa*), lion's mane (*Hericium erinaceus*), chaga (*Inonotus obliquus*), agarikon (*Fomitopsis officinalis*), cordyceps (*Cordyceps sinensis*), oyster (*Pleurotus ostreatus*), agaricus (*Agaricus brasiliensis*)

### Mycoremedial Mushrooms
Species useful for degrading toxins: oyster, turkey tail, maitake, shiitake, reishi, artist conk, crust fungus (*Phanerochaete chrysosporium*), garden giant

## Old-Growth Forest Mushrooms

More than 527 mushroom species grow in old-growth forests, with at least 109 native to the Pacific Northwest. One-tenth to one-fifth of the total biomass in the topsoil of a healthy Douglas fir forest may be mycelium. Rare old-growth species include:

- **Noble polypore** (*Bridgeoporus nobilissimus*): once the largest known mushroom, found on stumps in Oregon Cascades
- **Agarikon** (*Fomitopsis officinalis*): restricted to old-growth forests; produces antiviral compounds active against pox viruses; less than 5% of old-growth forests survive today

## The Mycelial Archetype

The branching network pattern of mycelium appears throughout nature — neurons in the brain, the Internet, dark matter filaments, hurricanes, blood vessels, river deltas. Stamets argues this reflects an energy-conserving architecture that the universe tends toward.
