# Natural Order and Microbial Ecology

Masanobu Fukuoka's concept of "natural order" is fundamentally rooted in microbial ecology. He saw the invisible world of soil microorganisms as the foundation upon which all terrestrial life depends, and believed that human agriculture goes wrong precisely when it disrupts this microbial foundation. Understanding natural order, for Fukuoka, meant understanding the role of microbes in maintaining the health and balance of ecosystems.






## The Food Chain as Unified System

Fukuoka viewed the entire food chain — from soil microbes through insects, plants, animals, and humans — as a single integrated system. In this view, microbes are not merely decomposers but the primary drivers of nutrient cycling, soil structure formation, plant health, and disease suppression. When microbial communities are healthy and diverse, the entire ecosystem above them functions efficiently without human intervention.

This perspective contrasts sharply with conventional agriculture, which treats microbes as either irrelevant or as problems to be managed through sterilization. Fukuoka argued that killing soil microbes through tillage, chemicals, or sterilization destroys the foundation of the food chain, requiring ever-increasing human intervention to compensate for the lost natural functions. See [[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-order-food-chain-unity]] for Fukuoka's detailed exposition of this idea.






## Microbes as Maintainers of Natural Order

In Fukuoka's philosophy, microbes are the agents of natural order — they maintain soil structure, cycle nutrients, suppress pathogens, and regulate plant growth through complex biochemical signaling. A healthy soil contains billions of microorganisms per gram, representing thousands of species, each performing specialized functions that together create the conditions for plant growth.

When humans add chemical fertilizer, they bypass the microbial nutrient cycling system, causing microbial populations that depend on organic matter decomposition to decline. When they apply pesticides, they kill not only target pests but also the microbial and invertebrate communities that naturally suppress pest populations. Each intervention reduces the complexity and resilience of the soil ecosystem, creating a dependency spiral that requires more intervention to maintain productivity.






## Contrast with Scientific Agriculture

Scientific agriculture approaches soil as a physical substrate that holds roots and nutrients, managing it through chemical analysis and amendment. Fukuoka approached soil as a living ecosystem that manages itself, provided it is not disturbed. The scientific approach asks "what nutrients does this soil lack?" while Fukuoka's approach asks "what is preventing this soil from cycling nutrients naturally?"

The difference is not merely philosophical but practical. Scientific agriculture can achieve high yields in the short term by supplying nutrients directly, but it degrades the soil ecosystem over time. Natural farming may produce lower yields initially but builds soil health and ecosystem complexity over time, eventually matching or exceeding conventional yields while requiring zero inputs. See [[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-order-microbial-ecology]] for Fukuoka's specific observations on microbial communities in his fields.






## Practical Examples from Fukuoka's Systems

In Fukuoka's rice paddies, the no-till approach preserved the complex microbial communities in the floodwater and soil. These communities included nitrogen-fixing bacteria associated with the rice roots, decomposer fungi that broke down straw mulch, and photosynthetic organisms in the floodwater that contributed to soil fertility. The duck-rice-fish polyculture system, described in [[fukuoka-duck-rice-fish-polyculture]], further enhanced microbial diversity through the introduction of animal manures and the physical disturbance created by duck activity.

In his orchards, the continuous ground cover of clover and weeds maintained a moist, cool soil environment favorable to microbial activity. The undisturbed soil developed a rich humus layer through years of leaf litter decomposition, creating conditions where fruit trees could access nutrients through mycorrhizal associations rather than requiring fertilizer application.






## The Philosophy of Non-Intervention

Fukuoka's microbial ecology philosophy ultimately supports his broader principle of non-intervention. If microbes are the true managers of soil fertility and plant health, then the farmer's role is not to manage but to create conditions where microbial communities can thrive. This means minimizing soil disturbance, maintaining continuous plant cover, returning all organic residues to the soil, and avoiding chemical inputs that harm microbial populations. See [[fukuoka-textdoc-man-sole-heretic-natural-order]] for Fukuoka's reflections on being misunderstood as a heretic for these views.
