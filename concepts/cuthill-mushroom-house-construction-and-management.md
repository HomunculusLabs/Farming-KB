Cuthill devoted considerable attention to the structures used for
cultivating mushrooms, ranging from purpose-built houses to improvised
shelters. His descriptions provide a detailed picture of 19th-century
mushroom.

## The Oldacre Mushroom House

The ideal purpose-built mushroom house, as described by Cuthill, was
introduced to England by Mr. Oldacre upon his return from service as
gardener to the Emperor of Russia. The first Cuthill saw was at Sir
Joseph Banks' estate. Its features included:

- Built against the wall of a vinery or other south-facing building,
  so the mushroom house itself faced north.
- Enclosed on all sides by walls.
- Internal shelving with side boards nine inches to a foot deep.
- Shelves placed approximately two feet apart, one above another.
- A central gangway running down the middle.
- A flue passing through for heating (though Cuthill considered this
  prone to overheating).
- Hot water pipes as a superior alternative to flues, used only in
  severe weather.

Cuthill considered this the safest design for severe weather, but noted
that overheating from flues was a real danger: "very often this dry
heat does more harm than good, especially if the house happens to get
overheated."

## Vineries Adapted for Mushrooms

Since purpose-built Oldacre houses were rare, Cuthill more commonly used
old vineries. His own setup included:

- Two old houses, each forty-five feet long.
- A central pathway with beds on each side, four feet wide and about
  one foot four inches deep.
- Beds made up of dung and mould.

## Span-Roofed Houses

Cuthill also used a span-roofed house with beds on each side at the
same manure depth. This design was particularly useful for summer
cultivation:

- A covering of straw placed on top during hot weather.
- The straw was watered every night to keep the interior cool.
- This prevented maggot infestation; Cuthill had mushrooms at five
  shillings per pound in July using this method.
- On July 12, 1856, during very hot weather, a mushroom grown in this
  house reached three feet six inches in circumference and weighed four
  and a half pounds.

## Brick Pits

Cuthill praised brick pits as excellent places for mushroom beds because
of the ease of covering them:

- Covered with glass, straw, and mats during cold weather.
- Could be kept very cold during early spring by watering the surface.
- The covered design prevented excessive evaporation.

## Sheds

Sheds ranked "second only to pits or frames" in Cuthill's estimation:

- Beds were convenient to access and easily covered or uncovered.
- Beds typically sloped from the wall (about three feet high next to
  the wall) down to the ground.
- An improvement was a foot-plank fixed on the floor in front to keep
  dung and mould firm and create a flatter surface better suited for
  watering.
- A flat bed was considered more natural than the ridge system.
- Cuthill preferred beds higher in the centre by six or eight inches,
  giving more surface area and allowing boiling water to run into the
  crevice between wall and bed where wood-lice hid.

## Other Structures

Cuthill listed many alternative locations for mushroom beds:

- Cucumber frames.
- Railway arches near towns ("capital places both for winter and
  summer mushrooms").
- Stables, lofts, coach-houses, yards.
- Large boxes and cellars.
- Orchard houses (a concept Cuthill claimed to have originated in the
  Gardeners' Chronicle).

## Temperature Management

Temperature control was central to house management. Cuthill's key
principles:

- The ideal surface temperature of a bed was "September heat," or about
  60-70 degrees Fahrenheit.
- Hay and mats were used to maintain this heat.
- In summer, water sprinkled on straw and mats extracted heat from the
  bed surface, preventing maggot breeding.
- At approximately 60 degrees F the maggot would not breed in mushrooms.
- Hot water pipes were preferred to flues for gentle supplemental heat
  in winter.
- The house needed to be "shut out from sun and air" -- mushroom beds
  could not evaporate moisture like plants with leaves.

## Underground Cold Houses for Summer

Cuthill proposed that "it would be as easy to have an underground cold
house for summer as a warm house for winter," ensuring year-round supply
without maggot problems. This was presented as a remedy for the fact
that the wealthy were "deprived of this luxury for several months during
summer."

## Pest Control in Houses

The primary pest was the wood-louse (also called slater, cheese-bug, or
pea-bug). Cuthill's method:

1. Uncover the bed and allow wood-lice to retreat to cracks and
   crevices between the bed and wall.
2. Fill cracks in the bed with fine mould before the bed comes into
   bearing.
3. Pour boiling water (from a watering-pot) into the cracks between
   bed and wall.
4. Repeat periodically.

He had tried arsenic on bread and butter, toads, and bantams, all
without success, before discovering the boiling water method.
