# Pinoline, MAO Inhibitors, and Entheogenic Plant Combinations

## Overview

The intersection of endogenous neurochemistry and entheogenic plant pharmacology reveals a compelling framework for understanding both ancient sacramental brews and naturally occurring altered states of consciousness. At the center of this framework is **pinoline**, a naturally occurring monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) produced by the pineal gland, which acts in concert with endogenous DMT to facilitate visionary and psychic experiences.

This endogenous mechanism mirrors the pharmacological logic behind some of the world's most powerful entheogenic preparations, particularly **Ayahuasca** and its analogues, and provides a biochemical key to one of the oldest mysteries in ethnobotany: the identity of the Vedic sacrament known as **Soma**.

The concept that visionary experiences can arise both from within the brain (through endogenous neurochemistry) and from without (through entheogenic plant combinations) bridges the gap between ancient spiritual wisdom and modern neuroscience, suggesting that the two traditions have been describing the same fundamental phenomenon from different vantage points.

## Pinoline as an Endogenous MAO Inhibitor

Pinoline (6-methoxy-tetrahydro-beta-carboline) is a beta-carboline compound synthesized naturally in the human pineal gland. As a monoamine oxidase inhibitor, pinoline functions by temporarily disabling the MAO enzymes—primarily MAO-A—that normally break down tryptamine compounds in the digestive tract and brain. This inhibition is pharmacologically significant because it allows otherwise orally inactive compounds, such as DMT, to reach the central nervous system in psychoactive concentrations.

Without MAO inhibition, DMT and similar tryptamines are metabolized almost immediately upon entering the body, rendered inactive before they can cross the blood-brain barrier. Pinoline effectively removes this metabolic barrier, allowing tryptamines to circulate and bind to serotonin receptors in the brain, producing their characteristic visionary effects.

The implications of pinoline extend beyond mere biochemistry. Research suggests that pinoline may be directly responsible for **naturally occurring psychic experiences**, including dream states, mystical visions, and even what some researchers have described as UFO contact visualizations. These experiences, rather than being purely pathological or hallucinatory, may represent the brain's own entheogenic capacity—moments when the pineal gland's natural chemistry replicates the effects of carefully constructed plant brews. The fact that the human body produces its own MAO inhibitor suggests that visionary states are not anomalies but rather an integral part of human neurochemistry, one that ancient cultures may have learned to amplify through botanical means.

## The DMT-Pinoline Connection to the Pineal Gland

The pineal gland, long revered as the "third eye" in esoteric and spiritual traditions, occupies a unique position in neurochemistry. Located deep within the brain, this small endocrine gland has been associated with [[mystical-experience]] across cultures for millennia. Modern research has begun to confirm what these traditions always intuited: the pineal gland is capable of producing powerful psychoactive compounds.

The pineal gland's capacity to synthesize DMT was proposed by Rick Strassman and has since gained traction through accumulating biochemical evidence. The gland contains the necessary enzymes—specifically indolethylamine N-methyltransferase (INMT)—to convert tryptamine into DMT. The co-secretion of pinoline alongside DMT creates a natural entheogenic cocktail within the brain itself.

During sleep—particularly during rapid eye movement (REM) phases—the pineal gland secretes both DMT and pinoline simultaneously. This endogenous co-release is of profound significance: pinoline inhibits the MAO enzymes that would otherwise degrade DMT, allowing it to remain active in the brain and produce the vivid, immersive visionary experiences characteristic of dreaming. The dreaming state, in this framework, can be understood as a naturally occurring entheogenic experience facilitated by the brain's own pharmacology.

The phenomenon of **somnambulism** (sleepwalking) is particularly relevant to this discussion. The term itself contains the Latin root "somn," linking it etymologically to both *Soma*—the legendary divine substance of Vedic tradition—and *[[papaver-somniferum]]*, the opium poppy. During somnambulistic episodes, DMT and pinoline excretion by the pineal gland is believed to be at its peak, reinforcing the hypothesis that the visionary states described in ancient religious texts may have been referring to chemically specific experiences that the human brain is capable of generating on its own.

The significance of this connection cannot be overstated: the very word "somnambulism" contains the root shared by both Soma and somniferum, suggesting that ancient cultures may have recognized a relationship between sleep-states, visionary experience, and their sacramental substances—a relationship that modern neurochemistry is only now beginning to validate.

This endogenous DMT-pinoline pairing serves as the biological template for understanding how entheogenic plant combinations achieve their effects. The ancient brewers of sacramental substances may have been, in essence, replicating and amplifying a natural brain process through botanical means—discovering plants that contained the same classes of compounds the pineal gland produces endogenously.

## Ayahuasca and the Principle of MAO Inhibition

The Amazonian brew **Ayahuasca** represents the most well-documented example of entheogenic plant synergy. Traditionally prepared from *[[banisteriopsis-caapi]]* (which contains harmine and harmaline—beta-carboline MAO inhibitors) combined with DMT-containing plants such as *Psychotria viridis* or *Diplopterys cabrerana*, Ayahuasca demonstrates a pharmacological principle that is central to understanding many ancient entheogenic traditions.

Research by Jace Callaway and others has confirmed that DMT-containing plants, when consumed alone, produce little to no psychoactive effect because the MAO enzymes in the gut rapidly deactivate the DMT before it can cross the blood-brain barrier. However, when DMT-containing plants are combined with MAO inhibitor-containing plants—whether *Banisteriopsis caapi* from the Amazon or **Syrian Rue** (*Peganum harmala*) from the Middle East—the resulting brew becomes powerfully and profoundly psychoactive.

This principle extends beyond Ayahuasca itself. The existence of **Ayahuasca analogues**—combinations of local DMT-containing plants with local MAOI-containing plants found across different cultures and continents—suggests that the discovery of this pharmacological synergy may have occurred independently in multiple regions throughout human history. Syrian Rue, native to the Mediterranean and Middle East, has been proposed as a key ingredient in Near Eastern entheogenic traditions and may have played a central role in the original Soma preparations of ancient Persia and India.

The global distribution of this pharmacological discovery is remarkable. In South America, *Banisteriopsis caapi* provides the MAO inhibition while *Psychotria viridis* supplies the DMT. In the Middle East and Central Asia, Syrian Rue (*Peganum harmala*) serves as the inhibitor, paired with locally available DMT-containing plants. In Africa, similar preparations using *Acacia* species have been documented. Each culture arrived at the same pharmacological insight through its own botanical resources—a testament to the universality of this particular chemical synergy.

The pharmacological elegance of this system—the requirement that two specific types of plants must be combined to produce the desired effect—also helps explain why the mystery of Soma persisted for so long. Researchers who tested individual plants in isolation would consistently be disappointed, not because any particular candidate was wrong, but because the magic lies in the combination.

## Historical Context: The Soma Debate

The search for Soma has engaged scholars for well over a century. The Rigveda contains over a thousand hymns, many of them dedicated to Soma, describing it as a divine substance that bestows immortality, visionary insight, and ecstatic union with the gods. Despite the richness of these descriptions, no single plant candidate has ever satisfied all the criteria implicit in the Vedic accounts.

R. Gordon Wasson's identification of *Amanita muscaria* as Soma in the 1960s was a landmark contribution, supported by linguistic analysis, ethnographic parallels with Siberian shamanic practices, and the mushroom's distinctive cultural iconography. Yet Wasson's theory, while convincing in many respects, could not fully account for the intensity and specificity of the visionary states described in the Vedas.

Terence McKenna championed the [[psilocybin-mushrooms]], documenting their capacity to produce ecstatic visionary states of extraordinary depth and complexity. Flattery and Schwartz argued for Syrian Rue, noting its MAO-inhibiting properties and its linguistic connection to Haoma. Each researcher identified a genuine piece of the puzzle. The five-plant theory proposes that all of these pieces belong to the same picture. Rather than a single botanical identity, Soma may represent an early example of what modern pharmacology would recognize as a multi-target therapeutic formulation—a combination of agents working across different receptor systems to produce a unified experiential outcome greater than the sum of its parts.

## The Five-Plant Soma Theory

A unified theory proposes that Soma was not a single plant but a **carefully calibrated combination of five plants** (four specific species and a fifth group of green plants), each contributing a specific pharmacological function to the overall experience. Five plants depicted in one picture, when understanding why each plant could play an important part in inducing the ultimate brew, may be the key to unlocking the mystery of Soma.

### 1. Syrian Rue (*Peganum harmala*)

Syrian Rue contains the beta-carboline alkaloids harmine and harmaline, which function as potent MAO inhibitors. In the Soma combination, Syrian Rue would serve the same role that *Banisteriopsis caapi* serves in Ayahuasca—enabling the oral activity of DMT and other tryptamine compounds. Flattery and Schwartz have made a strong case for Syrian Rue as a Soma candidate in its own right, noting the linguistic connection between Harmala and the Persian Haoma (a cognate of Soma). Without Syrian Rue or an equivalent MAOI, the other psychoactive components of the brew would be largely ineffective when consumed orally.

### 2. Psilocybin Mushrooms (*Psilocybe* species)

Terence McKenna and others have exhaustively documented the ecstatic visionary states produced by psilocybin-containing mushrooms. The Psilocybe mushrooms contribute potent serotonergic psychedelic effects, producing vivid visual imagery, altered cognition, and mystical-type experiences. Their validity as a Soma candidate cannot be discounted, nor should it be—McKenna speaks on this with authority. However, even McKenna, like many researchers, considered the question unresolved when evaluating single-plant theories. Within a multi-plant framework, Psilocybe mushrooms provide the primary visionary engine of the experience.

### 3. Amanita Muscaria

R. Gordon Wasson presented a convincing case for the iconic red-and-white *Amanita muscaria* mushroom as Soma, based on textual analysis, geographic distribution, and cultural evidence spanning Siberia, India, and the ancient Near East. While Amanita muscaria produces distinctive psychoactive effects—primarily through [[ibotenic-acid]] and muscimol acting on GABA receptors rather than serotonin receptors—its effects alone differ significantly from the visionary ecstasy described in Vedic literature. Within a multi-plant framework, however, Amanita muscaria may contribute a unique dimension of dissociative or sedative-hypnotic experience that complements and deepens the serotonergic effects of the other ingredients.

### 4. Opium Poppy (*Papaver somniferum*)

The opium poppy has rarely been proposed as a Soma candidate, likely overshadowed by the stronger evidence for other plants. However, its inclusion in a multi-plant theory addresses a critical practical consideration: the euphoria induced by opiate-containing poppies enables one to **relax enough to let go**—a prerequisite for fully experiencing the intensive visionary state produced by the other components. Without this relaxation component, the overwhelming intensity of the combined psychoactive effects could be difficult or impossible to surrender to and integrate. The opium poppy's role is one of facilitation—it opens the psychological door that allows the other compounds to deliver their full visionary potential.

The linguistic connection is also noteworthy: *Papaver somniferum* shares the root "somn" with Soma, and somnambulism—the state during which the pineal gland excretes DMT and pinoline—further reinforces this etymological bridge between sleep, dreaming, and the divine sacrament. This connection, which the other proposed candidates lack (with the exception of the Harmala/Haoma relation), lends unique weight to the poppy's inclusion.

### 5. Green Plants: Cannabis and DMT-Containing Species

The fifth component is represented not by a single species but by a category of green plants, symbolized in ancient depictions by an underscoring vine that visually connects the other four elements. This group includes:

- **Cannabis** (*Cannabis sativa/indica*): Used traditionally to prevent nausea (a common side effect of entheogenic preparations), enhance and modulate the effects of other psychoactive compounds, and reactivate neural pathways initially opened by other entheogens. Cannabis serves both a practical antiemetic function and a synergistic psychoactive one, deepening and prolonging the visionary state.

- **DMT-containing grasses and plants**: Various green plants worldwide contain DMT, including species of *Phalaris* (giant reed grasses), *Acacia* species, *Mimosa* species, and others with deep histories of indigenous usage across continents. When combined with the MAO inhibition provided by Syrian Rue, these DMT-containing plants become orally active, directly mimicking the endogenous DMT-pinoline combination of the pineal gland and completing the pharmacological circuit.

## Pharmacological Logic of the Five-Plant Combination

The five-plant theory is not arbitrary. Each plant addresses a specific pharmacological gap that the others cannot fill. Syrian Rue provides the essential MAO inhibition that makes oral DMT activity possible. Psilocybin mushrooms deliver serotonergic psychedelic effects through direct 5-HT2A receptor agonism. Amanita muscaria contributes GABAergic modulation through muscimol, producing a qualitatively different altered state that interacts with the serotonergic effects in complex ways. The opium poppy provides opioid-mediated euphoria and physical relaxation, addressing the practical challenge of surrendering to an overwhelming experience. Cannabis serves as both an antiemetic and a modulator, preventing nausea while reactivating neural pathways opened by the other entheogens.

This pharmacological logic mirrors the pineal gland's own production of multiple neurochemicals simultaneously. Just as the brain does not rely on a single compound to produce its most extraordinary states, the Soma brew would have combined multiple plant-derived compounds to achieve a multi-layered, synergistic entheogenic experience that no single plant could produce alone.

## Synergy Over Singularity: The Combination Principle

The fundamental insight of the five-plant theory is that **these plants must be used in combination**, as their psychoactivity individually is limited. Each candidate proposed for Soma over the years has genuine merit and pharmacological relevance, yet each falls short when evaluated against the complete descriptions found in ancient religious texts. The problem in concluding the discussion of what exactly Soma was rests precisely in this disappointment—the experience achieved by each candidate individually has never matched the transcendent state described in the source material.

This mystery, however, may not be as unsolvable as it has seemed. The Soma brew was obviously a mixture of several plants. The answer may be that **none of the proposed candidates is wrong**. Individually, each falls short of the mark in producing the described state of consciousness expected after reading the descriptions in the various religious texts. However, a mixture of these five plants may produce the long-sought-after and mysterious experience that has remained an enigma for so many years.

The combination addresses every pharmacological requirement simultaneously: MAO inhibition (Syrian Rue), serotonergic visionary effects (Psilocybe mushrooms), additional dissociative or hypnotic dimensions (Amanita muscaria), physical relaxation and euphoric surrender (Opium Poppy), and antiemetic support plus supplementary DMT (Cannabis and DMT-containing green plants). Together, these components replicate and amplify the pineal gland's natural DMT-pinoline secretion, producing the profound, immersive visionary state that ancient texts describe as communion with the divine.

## Philosophical Implications

This pharmacological framework carries a deeper philosophical message about the nature of entheogenic experience itself. As the source material notes:

> "All roads do not lead to Rome. Different keys open different doorways, and different combinations open different locks."

The trap of reductionism—assuming that any single compound or single plant can account for the full spectrum of entheogenic experience—is a fundamental error in both ethnobotanical research and psychedelic exploration. The reality of plant entheogens is one of extraordinary specificity and complexity. A chemical is designated a name to distinguish it from all others, and the specific combinations, proportions, and contexts in which these chemicals are encountered produce fundamentally different experiential outcomes.

One should not fall into the trap of thinking that any experience is the same as any other—this is not the reality of plant entheogens, and it never has been. The mystery of Soma may ultimately teach us that the most powerful entheogenic experiences arise not from any single substance but from the **synergistic interplay** of multiple compounds working in concert—much as the brain itself produces its most extraordinary states through the coordinated release of multiple neurochemicals simultaneously.

The five-plant theory honors the contributions of every researcher who has proposed a Soma candidate—Wasson, McKenna, Flattery and Schwartz, and others—by suggesting that each was partly right, and that the truth was always a synthesis waiting to be assembled from the pieces each had independently discovered. In this light, the Soma mystery is not a failure of scholarship but a testament to the complexity of the subject itself—a complexity that mirrors the intricate pharmacological dance between pinoline, DMT, and the myriad other compounds that produce the most extraordinary states of which the human brain is capable.
