# Soil Food Web: Bacteria-Fungi Succession Cycle

## Overview

In the soil food web model developed by Dr. Elaine Ingham, soil ecosystems
undergo a predictable succession from bacterial dominance to fungal dominance
over time. Understanding this cycle is fundamental to managing soil health,
producing effective compost teas, and matching biological amendments to
specific crop requirements. Succession reflects the changing availability of
organic materials and the evolving physical and chemical conditions of the
soil environment.

## The Succession Process

When soil is disturbed — whether by tillage, excavation, compaction, or
chemical application — the microbial community is set back to an early
successional stage dominated by bacteria. Bacteria are the first colonizers
because they reproduce rapidly (doubling every 20-30 minutes under ideal
conditions), require relatively simple carbon sources, and tolerate a wide
range of environmental conditions including reduced oxygen availability. Their
small size allows them to occupy the tiny pores in disturbed, compacted soils
where larger organisms cannot survive.

As succession progresses and soil structure improves through bacterial
activity, fungal populations begin to establish and expand. Fungi require
more stable soil conditions with good aggregation, continuous air channels,
and more complex organic matter. Their hyphal networks grow more slowly than
bacterial colonies but cover far greater distances and can access resources
in larger soil pores. This [[query-how-does-soil-naturally-transition-from-bacterial-to-fungal-dominance]] to fungal dominance is
gradual and reflects the maturation of the soil ecosystem.

## Conditions Favoring Each Group

Bacterial dominance is favored by disturbed soils, high simple-carbon inputs,
compact soils with limited macroporosity, lower oxygen availability, near-neutral
pH, and warm moist conditions with frequent wet-dry cycles.

Fungal dominance is favored by undisturbed stable soils, complex carbon inputs
(woody debris, leaf litter), well-aggregated soils with good macropore structure,
consistent aerobic conditions, slightly acidic pH, and steady moisture.

## Succession and Disease Suppression

Disease suppression in the Ingham model is directly linked to microbial
[[savory-plant-succession-and-community-dynamics]] diversity. As succession progresses, the soil food
web develops increasingly complex predator-prey relationships. Bacteria and
fungi compete with pathogens for space and resources on root surfaces and
in the rhizosphere. [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] that feed on bacteria and fungi
release plant-available nutrients through the microbial loop, supporting
vigorous plant growth that further resists disease.

Early successional soils with only [[cannabis-endorhiza-bacterial-communities]] are vulnerable to
bacterial pathogens. As fungi establish, they create physical barriers to
pathogen movement, produce antifungal compounds, and occupy niches that
would otherwise be available to disease organisms. Anaerobic pathogens —
organisms that cause putrefaction and plant disease — are effectively "put
to sleep" or suppressed by the dominant aerobic community. When beneficial
aerobic organisms occupy all available niches and consume available oxygen,
[[ingham-anaerobic-organisms-role-soil]] cannot become active and remain dormant.

## Tailoring AACT for Bacterial or Fungal Dominance

Actively aerated compost tea (AACT) can be produced with a bacterial or
fungal bias depending on the food sources added during brewing and the
brewing duration. The tea microbiology will reflect the conditions provided
during extraction and multiplication.

### Bacterial-Dominant Teas

To produce a bacterial-dominant tea, use shorter brew times (12-24 hours),
add simple sugars as bacterial food (molasses, fruit juice, kelp), use compost
rich in bacterial biomass (thermophilic compost), and maintain water temperature
between 65-75°F (18-24°C). Target crops include brassicas, leafy greens,
annual vegetables, and row crops that evolved in bacterial soils.

### Fungal-Dominant Teas

To produce a fungal-dominant tea, use longer brew times (24-48 hours or more),
add complex fungal foods (fish hydrolysate, humic acids, kelp meals), include
small amounts of oatmeal or bran as fungal stimulants, and use compost with
visible fungal hyphae (forest-floor compost). Target crops include perennials,
fruit trees, timber trees, vines, shrubs, and ornamental woody plants.

## Indicator Organisms

Bacterial-dominant communities are indicated by high total bacterial biomass,
dominance of small rod-shaped bacteria, active bacterial movement under
microscopy, presence of cyanobacteria in surface soils, and a low
fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratio (below 1:1).

Fungal-dominant communities are indicated by visible hyphal networks in
compost and soil, diverse fungal morphotypes under microscopy, active hyphal
growth and branching, saprophytic fungi decomposing woody materials, and a
high fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratio (above 1:1, up to 100:1 in
old-growth forest soils).

## Crop-Specific Applications

The plant families and growth forms a farmer grows determine which tea
profile is most appropriate. Brassicas, lettuce, and most annual vegetables
perform best in soils with bacterial-dominant biology. These plants evolved
in [[biodiversity-fungi-grassland-ecosystems]] where rapid nutrient cycling by bacteria supported
fast, above-ground growth.

Perennials, fruit trees, nut trees, vines, and forest species perform best
in fungal-dominant soils. These plants evolved in woodland ecosystems where
slow, steady nutrient release through fungal decomposition channels energy
into root establishment and long-term woody growth.

Many agricultural systems benefit from a balanced approach, applying
bacterial teas during early-season establishment and transitioning to
more fungal teas as the season progresses and soil structure matures.

## Key Takeaways

- Soil succession follows a predictable pattern from bacterial to fungal
  dominance as ecosystems mature and stabilize.
- Both groups play essential roles; the goal is matching biology to the
  crop, not maximizing one group at the expense of the other.
- AACT allows practitioners to shift soil biology toward the community
  profile that best supports their specific crops.
- Aerobic, diverse microbial communities suppress both anaerobic organisms
  and plant pathogens through competition and predation.
