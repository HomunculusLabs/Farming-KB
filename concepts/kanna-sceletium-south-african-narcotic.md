## Kanna (Sceletium tortuosum): South African Narcotic Plant

Kanna is the common name for several species of Mesembryanthemum (or Sceletium) from South Africa that were reportedly used by the Khoikhoi (Hottentots) as vision-inducing narcotics. One of the most ancient documented psychoactive plant traditions in Africa, kanna has recently attracted renewed scientific and commercial interest as a potential antidepressant and anxiolytic.

## Botanical Profile

Kanna is associated primarily with Mesembryanthemum expansum and M. tortuosum, two species of succulent plants native to the drier parts of South Africa. About two dozen species of Mesembryanthemum are considered by some botanists to represent a separate genus, Sceletium, distinguished by the skeletal appearance of the dry capsule. All belong to the carpetweed family, Aizoaceae, mainly South African, and are believed to be related to the pokeweed, pink, and cactus families.

In the drier parts of South Africa, there are altogether about 1,000 species of Mesembryanthemum, many with bizarre forms. The genus includes the well-known "ice plants" and "living stones" (lithops), which are popular among succulent collectors. The psychoactive species are relatively inconspicuous members of this large and diverse genus.

## Historical Documentation

There is strong evidence that one or both of these species were used by the Hottentots of southern Africa as vision-inducing narcotics. More than two centuries ago, it was reported that the Hottentots chewed the root of kanna, or channa, keeping the chewed material in the mouth, with remarkable results.

The early account describes the effects in vivid terms: "Their animal spirits were awakened, their eyes sparkled and their faces manifested laughter and gaiety. Thousands of delightsome ideas appeared, and a pleasant jollity which enabled them to be amused by simple jests. By taking the substance to excess, they lost consciousness and fell into a terrible delirium."

This description suggests that kanna produces a dose-dependent spectrum of effects: at moderate doses, euphoria, sociability, and enhanced mood; at higher doses, confusion, loss of consciousness, and delirium. This pharmacological profile is consistent with the effects of mesembrine and related alkaloids, which are now known to be the primary active constituents of Sceletium.

## Identity Debate

Since the narcotic use of these two species has not been observed directly by modern ethnographers, various botanists have suggested that the hallucinogenic kanna may actually have been cannabis or other intoxicating plants, such as several species of Sclerocarya of the cashew family. These alternative identifications have been proposed because the alkaloid profile of Mesembryanthemum was not well characterized at the time the historical accounts were recorded.

However, these two species of Mesembryanthemum do have the common name kanna in South Africa, and they do contain alkaloids that have sedative, cocaine-like properties capable of producing torpor in man. The presence of mesembrine-type alkaloids in these plants provides a plausible pharmacological basis for the traditional use, even if the "vision-inducing" description in the historical account may involve some degree of exaggeration or cultural interpretation.

## Chemical Composition

Sceletium tortuosum contains a group of alkaloids known as mesembrine-type alkaloids, including mesembrine, mesembrenone, mesembrenol, and tortuosamine. These alkaloids are serotonergic agents that act primarily as serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SRIs), similar in mechanism to modern antidepressant drugs like fluoxetine (Prozac). They also possess PDE4 inhibitory activity, which may contribute to their mood-enhancing effects.

The discovery of these serotonergic compounds in kanna has generated considerable scientific interest, as they represent a novel class of natural antidepressants. The traditional use of kanna as a mood enhancer and social facilitator is consistent with the pharmacological profile of serotonin reuptake inhibition, which typically produces feelings of well-being, reduced anxiety, and increased social confidence.

## Traditional Preparation Methods

Traditional preparation of kanna reportedly involved fermenting or drying the plant material before use. The fresh plant was sometimes crushed and allowed to ferment for several days, which may enhance the concentration or bioavailability of the active alkaloids. The dried and fermented material was then chewed, sometimes with the addition of other substances.

The chewing method of administration, with the material retained in the mouth, allows for sublingual absorption of the alkaloids through the oral mucosa. This route of administration bypasses first-pass metabolism in the liver and may produce more rapid and reliable effects than oral ingestion. The traditional practice of keeping the chewed material in the mouth for extended periods suggests a sophisticated understanding of this pharmacological principle.

## Modern Revival and Commercialization

In recent years, kanna has experienced a significant revival of interest, both as a commercial dietary supplement and as a subject of scientific research. Sceletium extracts are now marketed as natural antidepressants, anxiolytics, and cognitive enhancers, available in various forms including capsules, tinctures, and chewing gums.
