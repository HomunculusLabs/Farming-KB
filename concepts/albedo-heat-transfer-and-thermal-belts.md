## Frost Formation and Cold Air Drainage

### Radiation Frost

On clear, still nights, surfaces lose heat rapidly through longwave radiation. When the air is calm, this cooling is concentrated at ground level, producing **radiation frost**. Frost-prone sites include:

- Hollows and depressions where cold air settles
- Flat, open areas with no windbreaks
- Forest clearings of 9–30 meters or more

The larger the clearing in a forest, the greater the frost risk, as the insulating canopy effect is removed.

### Advection Frost

**Advection frost** occurs when cold air flows downhill under gravity and pools in flat areas or valleys below. Cold air is denser than warm air, so it drains like water off the landscape, collecting in the lowest available positions. Frost can also form rapidly on **high plateaus** exposed to cold air movement.

A useful field indicator: **dense autumn fogs** reveal the extent of winter frost zones. Where fog settles and lingers in autumn, frost will form in winter — these are the cold pockets to avoid for sensitive plantings.

## Temperature Inversions

A **temperature inversion** occurs when a layer of cool air becomes trapped beneath warmer air, typically in valleys, basins, and broad plains. Under normal conditions, air temperature decreases with altitude, but during an inversion this gradient reverses near the surface.

Inversions cause fog, smog, and pollution to accumulate and persist, since the cooler air layer prevents vertical mixing. They also intensify frost damage by holding cold air in place. Sites prone to inversions are poor choices for housing, animal shelters, or sensitive crops unless deliberate ventilation or elevation is provided.

## Temperature Extremes and Biological Limits

Temperature directly governs what can survive and thrive in a given location:

- **Above 43°C**: Poultry and birds cannot survive prolonged exposure.
- **Above 36°C soil temperature**: Many plants suffer transplant shock and root damage.
- **At or below 0°C**: A wide range of plants experience frost damage.

Designing for thermal moderation — through shading, windbreaks, water bodies, and thermal mass — extends the viable range for both plants and animals.

## Global Temperature Profile

The Earth's mean temperature at depth is approximately **5°C**. Air near the ground surface averages around **14°C**, while the upper atmosphere ranges from **−50°C to −80°C**. As a general rule, temperature decreases by roughly **1°C per 100 meters of altitude gain**. This lapse rate means that a site 500 meters above a valley floor may be 5°C cooler — a difference that shifts entire plant hardiness zones.

## Thermal Belts: The Sweet Spot on Slopes

One of the most valuable insights for permaculture site placement is the concept of **thermal belts** — the mid-slope positions on hillsides that offer the most favorable temperature conditions.

Cold air drains downhill at night and pools in valley bottoms, while hilltops are exposed to chilling winds and lack the insulating effect of surrounding land. The **thermal belt** sits between these two extremes, above the valley frost pocket and below the exposed summit.

### Thermal Belt Placement by Landscape Type

| Landscape | Thermal Belt Position |
|---|---|
| Hill country | 1,000–5,000 m elevation |
| Lower slopes | 100–200 m above valley floor |
| Hot deserts | 10–15 m above valley floor |

These mid-slope positions are the ideal locations for gardens, orchards, housing, and animal shelters. They receive cold air drainage from above but escape the pooling of frost at the bottom. In hot desert environments, even a modest 10–15 meter rise above the valley floor provides meaningful frost protection and temperature moderation.

## Design Implications

The interplay of albedo, heat transfer, and thermal belts offers designers powerful leverage:

- **Modify albedo** with mulches, gravel, or reflective surfaces to increase or decrease heat absorption.
- **Use thermal mass** (stones, water, earth) to store daytime heat and release it at night.
- **Plant windbreaks** to reduce convective heat loss and slow cold air drainage.
- **Position settlements and gardens in thermal belts** to avoid frost pockets while retaining access to water and soil in valley floors.
- **Observe autumn fog patterns** to map frost zones before committing to permanent plantings.

By [[reading-the-landscape]]'s thermal behavior — its albedo, its slopes, its cold air drainage paths — the permaculture designer can place elements where the climate does the work of heating, cooling, and protecting, reducing external energy inputs and increasing system resilience.
