# Beneficial Insects and Ecological Pest Control

Eliot Coleman's approach to [[cannabis-pest-management-guide]] is rooted in the ecological
principles articulated by Lady Eve Balfour and Aldo Leopold: create the
conditions for natural biological balance, and pest problems will
minimize themselves. In the [[coleman-aphid-management-winter-greenhouse-nitrogen-flushing]], this philosophy proves
remarkably effective, as [[solomon-insects-and-diseases-integrated-approach]] have "not been a real
consideration" in his [[coleman-claytonia-winter-harvest-cultivation]] system. His method prioritizes
prevention through superior soil and [[mushroom-crop-management-flush-harvesting]] over any form of
direct pest intervention.

## The Ideal of Biological Balance

Lady Eve Balfour's vision of organic cultivation centered on providing
"conditions in which natural biological balance will prevent the
multiplication of any one species to pest proportions." Coleman embraces
this ideal completely. Rather than responding to pests with treatments,
he seeks to understand and correct the conditions that allowed pest
pressure to develop. The appearance of insects or disease is "just as
certain an indication of inadequate growing conditions as are yellowed
leaves." The remedy is to improve growing conditions by determining what
the soil or environment is missing.

## Winter Greenhouse Pest Profile

The winter harvest environment is remarkably free of pest pressure.
Coleman reports finding "little new to report" on insects and diseases
since his earlier writing. The cold temperatures, low light levels, and
continuous plant growth in the greenhouse create conditions that suppress
most pest populations naturally. The major pest that has developed is not
an insect but a mammal: the meadow vole, which tunnels under the soil
and into the greenhouse, attracted by the year-round food supply and
insulated environment.

## The Aphid Example

The one significant plant pest in the winter greenhouse is aphids, which
appear on spinach starting in early to mid-February. At first, Coleman
did not mind a few aphids since they washed off easily. But then aphid
predators arrived, and the carcasses of preyed-upon aphids stuck to the
backs of spinach leaves, creating a quality problem.

Research into conditions favoring aphids consistently pointed to soil
nitrogen levels as the main factor. Nitrogen builds up in greenhouse soil
when there is no rain or irrigation to flush it through. Coleman's
solution was to begin irrigating thoroughly once a week on sunny mornings
starting in mid-January, flushing out excess nitrogen. The aphid problem
disappeared without any direct pesticide application. This case
exemplifies his ecological approach: identify the root cause and correct
the environmental conditions.

## Prevention Through Soil Health

Coleman's diagnostic framework for pest problems focuses on soil
conditions. When problems appear, he asks: Is the soil ready for that
crop? Should the rotation or cultural practices be changed? Are there
mineral deficiencies, particularly trace elements? Was the compost
mature? Were transplants stressed? Has the soil been aerated adequately?
The answers to these questions usually point to correctable soil
management issues rather than requiring direct pest intervention.

## The Role of Observation

Learning from plants is central to Coleman's pest management philosophy.
He encourages growers to closely observe not just the affected plants but
the entire ecosystem: Are all plants equally affected, or only those in
certain areas? What soil differences exist between affected and
unaffected zones? Is there a pattern related to compost sources, liming,
or drainage? Wild vegetation on undisturbed land is "an excellent
indication of how differing soil conditions favor the physiological
needs of some plants over others." The same principle applies to crops
and their pests.
