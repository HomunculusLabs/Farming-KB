# Wicking Bed Materials and Construction Variants

## Overview

Wicking beds can be constructed from a wide range of materials, from recycled containers to purpose-built timber frames, and the choice of construction method significantly affects cost, durability, aesthetics, and performance. This page covers the primary construction variants, material options for each component, and comparative guidance for selecting the best approach for different budgets, climates, and site conditions.

## Bed Container Options

### Timber Frames

Timber is the most popular material for [[passive-humidity-fruit-chamber-polyethylene-sheeting]] (200 μm minimum) to create a waterproof barrier between the soil and treated timber. Cedar, redwood, and cypress naturally resist decay and do not require treatment, making them ideal for organic growers, but they are significantly more expensive.

### Corrugated Metal (Colorbond)

Corrugated steel raised beds are increasingly popular for their modern appearance, slim profile (maximizing growing area), and durability (20+ year lifespan). They require a waterproof liner (EPDM pond liner or heavy-duty polyethylene) as the metal is not watertight at seams. In hot climates, the metal can heat the soil significantly — install an insulating layer of 20 mm closed-cell foam between the liner and the metal, or shade the exterior with a vertical garden or climbing plants. The thin walls provide minimal insulation compared to timber, so soil temperatures fluctuate more with ambient conditions.

### Concrete Blocks (Besser Blocks)

Concrete blocks offer extreme durability and thermal mass, moderating soil temperature fluctuations. Standard hollow blocks (390 mm × 190 mm × 190 mm) are laid in a running bond pattern and can be filled with gravel for stability. Waterproof the interior with a cement-based sealer or flexible membrane. The rough interior surface helps grip the liner. Concrete blocks are inexpensive per unit but labor-intensive to lay, and the heavy weight means the bed must be built in its final position. They are well suited to institutional and community garden settings where permanence is valued. The block cores can be planted with strawberries, herbs, or flowers for additional growing space.

### IBC Totes (Intermediate Bulk Containers)

Repurposed IBC totes (1000 L plastic cubes with metal cage frames) are a popular low-cost option for wicking beds. Clean the tote thoroughly and verify that it previously contained food-grade materials (never use totes that held chemicals). Cut the top off with a reciprocating saw or angle grinder to create an open-top container. The existing outlet valve at the base serves as the overflow drain, and the fill port at the top becomes the inlet. The built-in metal cage provides [[fukuoka-critique-organic-certification]], verify that the geotextile does not contain synthetic binders or coatings. Some growers use natural fiber alternatives such as coir matting, burlap (hessian), or shade cloth, but these degrade within 1–3 seasons and require regular replacement. Polyester geotextiles offer similar performance to polypropylene but are generally more expensive.

## Safety and Regulatory Considerations

### Food Safety

When using recycled materials, verify they are free from chemical contamination. Avoid materials that have held pesticides, herbicides, motor oil, or industrial chemicals. Treated timber should be lined with food-grade polyethylene to prevent direct contact between soil and preservative. Galvanized metal can leach zinc at low pH — maintain soil pH above 6.0 and use a liner if growing acid-loving crops. Test recycled containers by filling with water and testing for pH, conductivity, and visual signs of leaching (discoloration, sheen on water surface) before installing the growing medium.
