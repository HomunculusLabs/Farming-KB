# Creating Wildflower Meadows

updated: 2026-04-18

Wildflowers can be grown in the borders or, in large gardens, as a wildflower meadow. In many cases, what is a wildflower, or even a weed, in some countries is a desirable border plant in others. Some native wildflowers looked upon with scorn by gardeners can, when grown in the fertile conditions of the border, become very attractive indeed. And, even when cultivated in this way, they are just as attractive to native insects and birds, helping to [[coleman-unheated-greenhouse-design]].

### Direct Sowing

If sowing straight into the ground, ensure the site is well-drained. Ideally, choose a piece of soil that has not been cultivated in the recent past; in a new garden, subsoil dug up by builders is suitable. Wildflowers thrive on poor land, so do not add fertilizer or the coarser weeds will take over.

### Greenhouse Sowing

Harden off seedlings raised in the greenhouse before planting in the border.

## Creating a Wildflower Meadow

A wildflower meadow is suitable for larger gardens. One of the advantages of this method is the ease of maintenance; in a large garden this could be a boon. The flower population will change a little as conditions generally favor one species or another, but there always needs to be a varied selection to attract the insects and birds that help control pests.

### Site Selection

Choose a patch of land with low fertility if possible. Low fertility is key -- fertile soil encourages grasses to outcompete wildflowers. If the site has been fertilized in the past, you may need to reduce fertility by repeated cutting and removal of the cuttings for a season or two.

### Sowing the Meadow

Prepare and sow as you would for a lawn, using a mixture of grasses and flowers suitable for your soil type. Sow in spring or early fall.

### Meadow Maintenance

The meadow only needs cutting twice a year:
1. **Early spring**: Cut just as growth starts. This removes dead material and gives wildflowers room to grow.
2. **Late summer**: Cut after the wildflowers have set seed. Use the seed for next year's meadow. Remove the cuttings -- leaving them on adds nutrients and encourages grass dominance.

This minimal maintenance regime is one of the main advantages of the wildflower meadow approach. A summer meadow of scarlet poppies, blue cornflowers, and white ox-eye daisies attracts butterflies and other insects. Later, the seedheads will attract a wide variety of birds.
