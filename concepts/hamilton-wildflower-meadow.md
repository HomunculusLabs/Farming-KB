# Creating Wildflower Meadows

updated: 2026-04-18

Wildflowers can be grown in the borders or, in large gardens, as a wildflower meadow. In many cases, what is a wildflower, or even a weed, in some countries is a desirable border plant in others. Some native wildflowers looked upon with scorn by gardeners can, when grown in the fertile conditions of the border, become very attractive indeed. And, even when cultivated in this way, they are just as attractive to native insects and birds, helping to control pests by attracting their natural predators.

## Choosing Wildflowers

There are several specialists who sell wildflower seeds and most retail seed catalogs carry a selection. Special mixtures are available, aiming to attract butterflies, bees, or birds. Other seed mixtures may include old-fashioned cornfield flowers such as the corncockle (Agrostemma githago) and cornflower (Centaurea cyanus).

Some wildflower suppliers sell rooted plants that are ready for planting. Never take plants from the wild -- this is how they become rare or extinct. Check the soil and habitat preferences of each species before planting.

## Growing Wildflowers in Borders

Wildflowers that blow in from nearby fields can be encouraged. Red campion (Lychnis dioica) and white campion (Lychnis alba) are good examples. When they grow in manured and fertilized borders they become much bigger plants bearing many more flowers, while remaining just as attractive to native insects and birds.

Bluebells bring color to a woodland border and also suppress weeds. Seedlings can be left to naturalize and spread.

## Planting Wildflowers

Wildflowers can be raised from seed, either sown directly in the ground in the spring, or in seed trays in an unheated greenhouse.

### Direct Sowing

If sowing straight into the ground, ensure the site is well-drained. Ideally, choose a piece of soil that has not been cultivated in the recent past; in a new garden, subsoil dug up by builders is suitable. Wildflowers thrive on poor land, so do not add fertilizer or the coarser weeds will take over.

### Greenhouse Sowing

Harden off seedlings raised in the greenhouse before planting in the border.

## Creating a Wildflower Meadow

A wildflower meadow is suitable for larger gardens. One of the advantages of this method is the ease of maintenance; in a large garden this could be a boon. The flower population will change a little as conditions generally favor one species or another, but there always needs to be a varied selection to attract the insects and birds that help control pests.

### Site Selection

Choose a patch of land with low fertility if possible. Low fertility is key -- fertile soil encourages grasses to outcompete wildflowers. If the site has been fertilized in the past, you may need to reduce fertility by repeated cutting and removal of the cuttings for a season or two.

### Sowing the Meadow

Prepare and sow as you would for a lawn, using a mixture of grasses and flowers suitable for your soil type. Sow in spring or early fall.

### Meadow Maintenance

The meadow only needs cutting twice a year:
1. **Early spring**: Cut just as growth starts. This removes dead material and gives wildflowers room to grow.
2. **Late summer**: Cut after the wildflowers have set seed. Use the seed for next year's meadow. Remove the cuttings -- leaving them on adds nutrients and encourages grass dominance.

This minimal maintenance regime is one of the main advantages of the wildflower meadow approach. A summer meadow of scarlet poppies, blue cornflowers, and white ox-eye daisies attracts butterflies and other insects. Later, the seedheads will attract a wide variety of birds.

## Benefits in the Organic Garden

Wildflowers provide several important functions in the organic garden:
- **Pest control**: Attract predatory insects (ladybirds, hoverflies, lacewings) that control aphids and other pests
- **Pollination**: Attract bees and other pollinators for fruit and vegetable crops
- **Wildlife habitat**: Provide food and shelter for birds, small mammals, and beneficial insects
- **Aesthetic value**: Beautiful, naturalistic displays that change throughout the seasons
- **Low maintenance**: Once established, wildflower areas require minimal care compared to lawns

## Forget-Me-Not Propagation

Do not buy forget-me-not (Myosotis alpestris) seeds. Simply pull up a seeded plant and shake it over the border to disperse its seeds. They will naturalize readily and provide a reliable display of blue flowers in spring.

## Establishment Timeline and Management

Establishing a wildflower meadow is a multi-year process that requires
patience and appropriate management at each stage. In the first year,
annual weeds typically dominate the site while perennial wildflower seeds
establish their root systems. Regular mowing at 6-8 inches height during
the first growing season prevents weeds from going to seed without
damaging the developing perennial seedlings. The second year shows
significant improvement as perennial plants begin to fill in, and by
the third year, a well-established meadow should be largely
self-sustaining with minimal weed pressure.

Ongoing management includes annual mowing in late winter or early spring
(before new growth begins) to remove accumulated thatch and prevent woody
plants from establishing. Some meadow managers practice rotational mowing,
cutting only one-third to one-half of the meadow each year to maintain
diverse habitat structure for wildlife. Periodic spot treatment of
invasive species is necessary, and overseeding with native wildflowers
every 3-5 years helps maintain species diversity as individual plants
age and die. The reward for this management is a low-maintenance landscape
that supports pollinators, birds, and beneficial insects while requiring
a fraction of the inputs of a conventional lawn.
