## Arthur Heffter and the Isolation of Mescaline

In 1897, the same year as Weir Mitchell"s peyote account, Arthur Heffter — a rival of Lewin's — became the first human being to isolate and ingest pure mescaline. Mescaline is a powerful visionary phenethylamine that occurs in the peyote cactus. It had been used for at least several centuries by the indigenous peoples of Northern Mexico, and its use in South America — derived from other cactus species such as *[[hallucinogenic-mushroom-acute-psychological-effects-and-bad-trips]]*. Kluver, whose work built on the observations of Weir Mitchell and Havelock Ellis, reintroduced the English-speaking world to the notion of visionary pharmacology. His contribution was especially important because he took the hallucinogenic content of the experiences seriously and became the first to attempt a systematic phenomenological description of the psychedelic experience.

Kluver documented the form constants — geometric patterns that recur across different mescaline experiences and different subjects: tunnels, spirals, cobwebs, honeycombs, and lattices. These form constants later proved important to the neurobiology of visual processing, as they correspond to the geometric patterns generated by the visual cortex when its normal filtering mechanisms are disrupted.

## The Ethical Dimension of Self-Experimentation

The tradition of self-experimentation established by Lewin, Heffter, and their successors raises important ethical questions that remain relevant to contemporary psychopharmacology. These researchers ingested unknown compounds with uncertain effects, risking their health and sanity in pursuit of knowledge. While modern institutional review boards would never approve such experiments, the self-experimentation tradition produced insights that external observation alone could never yield.

The paradox is that the very subjectivity that makes self-experimentation scientifically problematic is also what makes it scientifically indispensable. Psychoactive compounds affect consciousness — the one phenomenon that cannot be adequately studied from the outside. The first-person reports of Lewin, Weir Mitchell, Heffter, Ellis, Beringer, and Kluver remain valuable not despite their subjectivity but because of it. They are primary sources for the phenomenology of [[mckenna-food-gods-peganum-harmala-soma]]*, the Syrian rue from which it had been previously isolated — the telepathy reference was erased. The older, more conservative name was given "official precedence," a decision that McKenna implicitly criticizes. The renaming represents a broader pattern in which Western science systematically strips psychoactive compounds of their more provocative associations, preferring taxonomic neutrality to the preservation of indigenous knowledge and extraordinary claims.

## The Parke-Davis Connection: Corporate Pharmacy and Indigenous Knowledge

The role of the Parke-Davis Company in the peyote story highlights the complex relationship between corporate pharmaceutical interests and indigenous plant knowledge. Parke-Davis, one of America's oldest pharmaceutical companies, was the source of the peyote buttons that Lewin brought back to Berlin. The company had obtained the peyote through its connections with traders and suppliers who had access to indigenous plant materials from the American Southwest and Mexico.

This commercial pipeline — from indigenous communities through corporate pharmacies to European research laboratories — established a pattern that would repeat throughout the 20th century. The knowledge and plant materials of indigenous peoples flowed to Western science through corporate intermediaries, generating pharmaceutical products and academic publications while rarely benefiting the communities from which the knowledge originated. McKenna's history of psychopharmacology is, in this sense, also a history of bioprospecting and cultural appropriation.

## The Phenomenological Tradition and Its Descendants

Beringer and Kluver established a phenomenological tradition in psychopharmacology that would have descendants throughout the 20th century. The emphasis on careful first-person description of subjective experiences, rather than purely behavioral or physiological measurement, became a hallmark of psychedelic research. This tradition is visible in the self-reports of Albert Hofmann's first LSD experience, in the clinical observations of Humphry Osmond and Abram Hoffer, and in the elaborate trip reports of the 1960s counterculture.

The phenomenological approach also influenced the development of psychometric scales for measuring altered states of consciousness, such as the Hallucinogen Rating Scale (HRS) and the [[turner-harmala-alkaloids-preparation-and-dosage]] and their Amazonian sources were largely forgotten until the 1950s, when the discovery of their MAO-inhibiting properties rekindled interest in ayahuasca.

This interruption had consequences that extended beyond the loss of research momentum. The suppression of psychopharmacological research meant that when LSD was discovered in 1943, there was no established institutional framework for studying it responsibly. The absence of the phenomenological tradition represented by Beringer and Kluver contributed to the chaotic and ultimately disastrous way that psychedelic research was conducted in the 1960s.
