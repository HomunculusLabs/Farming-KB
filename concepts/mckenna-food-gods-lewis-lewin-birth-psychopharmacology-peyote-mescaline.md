# Lewis Lewin and the Birth of Psychopharmacology — Peyote, Mescaline, and the Modern Renaissance

His work established the methodological template for all subsequent psychopharmacological research: isolate the active compound, characterize its chemistry, test it on human subjects (including himself), and document the subjective effects with as much phenomenological precision as possible.

Lewin's broader contributions include his influential classification of psychoactive substances into categories that are still used today: euphoriants (stimulants), phantasticants (hallucinogens), inebriants (depressants), and hypnotics (sedatives). This taxonomy represented a significant advance over earlier, less systematic approaches to drug classification.

## Silas Weir Mitchell: The First Gringo Peyote Experience

Within a decade of Lewin's return to Berlin, peyote had attracted sufficient attention in the United States that in 1897, Philadelphia novelist and physician Silas Weir Mitchell became the first non-Native American to publish a detailed account of peyote intoxication. Mitchell's description is remarkable for its literary quality and its departure from the clinical detachment that characterized most contemporary medical writing:

> "The display which for an enchanted two hours followed was such as I find it hopeless to describe in language which shall convey to others the beauty and splendor of what I saw. Stars... delicate floating films of color... then an abrupt rush of countless points of white light swept across the field of view, as if the unseen millions of the Milky Way were to flow a sparkling river before the eyes."

Mitchell's account is significant not only for its vividness but for its implicit argument: peyote visions are not pathological hallucinations but genuinely aesthetic experiences worthy of serious attention. His use of architectural imagery — "a tall, richly finished Gothic tower of very elaborate and definite design, with many rather worn statues standing in the doorways or on stone brackets" — anticipates the phenomenological descriptions that would later characterize the [[mckenna-suppression-psychedelic-research-lsd-therapy]] of the mid-20th century.

## Arthur Heffter and the Isolation of Mescaline

In 1897, the same year as Weir Mitchell's peyote account, Arthur Heffter — a rival of Lewin's — became the first human being to isolate and ingest pure mescaline. Mescaline is a powerful visionary phenethylamine that occurs in the peyote cactus. It had been used for at least several centuries by the indigenous peoples of Northern Mexico, and its use in South America — derived from other cactus species such as *[[alexander-shulgin]]'s systematic exploration of hundreds of psychoactive compounds in the late 20th century.

## Havelock Ellis and the Phenomenology of Mescaline

The psychologist and pioneer sexologist Havelock Ellis, following Weir Mitchell's example, published his own account of mescaline's effects. Ellis's contribution was particularly important for his observation about the nature of mescaline visions: "The visions never resembled familiar objects; they were extremely definite, but yet always novel." He noted that while elementary visions were followed by visions of more complex character, "any scheme which, in a detailed manner, assigns different kinds of visions to successive stages of the mescal state must be viewed as extremely arbitrary."

This observation — that psychedelic experiences resist systematic staging — was a crucial insight that anticipated the phenomenological approach of later researchers. Ellis recognized that mescaline did not produce a fixed sequence of effects but rather a fluid, responsive interaction between the substance and the user's consciousness. The visions were "constantly approaching, and yet constantly eluding, the semblance of known things" — a description that captures the paradoxical familiarity and strangeness of the psychedelic experience.

## Kurt Beringer and the Foundation of Psychedelic Psychiatry

Dr. Kurt Beringer, Lewin's student and an acquaintance of both Hermann Hesse and Carl Jung, became what McKenna calls "the father of psychedelic psychiatry." Beringer's phenomenological approach stressed the reportage of internal vistas beheld under the influence of mescaline. He conducted hundreds of experiments with mescaline in human subjects, producing accounts of remarkable vividness and detail.

His subjects described "endless passages in Moorish style moving like waves" alternating with "pictures of curious figures," designs in the form of crosses present in "unceasing variety," and crystals that "appeared again and again, changing form and color." Most remarkably, they described "two immense cosmic systems... shining with their own light... From the interior new rays appeared in more luminescent colors, and gradually becoming perfect, they assumed the form of oblong prisms." These descriptions anticipate the cosmic and transcendental imagery that would become characteristic of later psychedelic literature.

In 1927, Beringer published his magnum opus *Der Meskalinrausch* (The Mescaline Intoxication), translated into Spanish but never into English. McKenna considers this "an inspired work" that "set the stage for the science of investigative pharmacology." The book's untranslated status in English represents a significant gap in the accessible literature of psychopharmacology.

## Heinrich Kluver and Mescal, the Divine Plant

The following year, 1928, saw the publication in English of Heinrich Kluver's *Mescal, the Divine Plant and Its [[hallucinogenic-mushroom-acute-psychological-effects-and-bad-trips]]*. Kluver, whose work built on the observations of Weir Mitchell and Havelock Ellis, reintroduced the English-speaking world to the notion of visionary pharmacology. His contribution was especially important because he took the hallucinogenic content of the experiences seriously and became the first to attempt a systematic phenomenological description of the psychedelic experience.

Kluver documented the form constants — geometric patterns that recur across different mescaline experiences and different subjects: tunnels, spirals, cobwebs, honeycombs, and lattices. These form constants later proved important to the neurobiology of visual processing, as they correspond to the geometric patterns generated by the visual cortex when its normal filtering mechanisms are disrupted.

## The Renaissance of Psychopharmacology in Germany

McKenna characterizes the 1920s as a "veritable Renaissance of psychopharmacology" in Germany. This period saw not only the intensive study of mescaline but also the investigation of harmine, an indole alkaloid derived from *[[harmala-alkaloids]] largely vanish, along with much of the interest in ethnopharmacology. One notable exception was Blas Pablo Reko (born Blasius Paul Reko), an Austrian expatriate living in Oaxacan Mexico. Reko was a person of wide-ranging interests whose "wandering life took him to the United States, to Ecuador, and finally to Oaxacan Mexico." There he became interested in ethnobotany and what is today called archaeo-astronomy.

Reko was an astute observer of plant usages among the native people with whom he lived. In 1919, in rebuttal to an article by William Safford (who had notoriously claimed that teonanacatl was not a mushroom but the *Mammillaria* cactus), Reko wrote that it was indeed a [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-amanita-muscaria-emerging-alternative]]. This correction was an early salvo in the scholarly debate over the identity of the Aztec sacred mushroom — a debate that would not be conclusively resolved until the 1950s work of R. Gordon Wasson.

Reko's significance in McKenna's narrative is that he represents the bridge between indigenous plant knowledge and Western scientific investigation. He was neither a detached laboratory scientist nor a passive cultural observer but someone who moved between worlds, translating ethnobotanical knowledge into a form that Western science could eventually investigate.

## The Ethical Dimension of Self-Experimentation

The tradition of self-experimentation established by Lewin, Heffter, and their successors raises important ethical questions that remain relevant to contemporary psychopharmacology. These researchers ingested unknown compounds with uncertain effects, risking their health and sanity in pursuit of knowledge. While modern institutional review boards would never approve such experiments, the self-experimentation tradition produced insights that external observation alone could never yield.

The paradox is that the very subjectivity that makes self-experimentation scientifically problematic is also what makes it scientifically indispensable. Psychoactive compounds affect consciousness — the one phenomenon that cannot be adequately studied from the outside. The first-person reports of Lewin, Weir Mitchell, Heffter, Ellis, Beringer, and Kluver remain valuable not despite their subjectivity but because of it. They are primary sources for the phenomenology of [[weil-the-natural-mind-altered-states-normalcy]], and no amount of brain imaging data can substitute for the direct experiential knowledge they contain.

## The Naming of Telepathine: Science and Wonder

The story of telepathine illustrates a tension in psychopharmacology between scientific conservatism and the preservation of wonder. When Perrot and Raymond-Hamet named the *Banisteriopsis caapi* alkaloid "telepathine" in 1927, they were honoring the indigenous claims about the plant's telepathy-inducing properties. The name encoded a hypothesis about the compound's effects that was consistent with indigenous knowledge but beyond the reach of conventional scientific verification.

When the compound was later identified as harmaline — a name derived from *[[mckenna-food-gods-peganum-harmala-soma]]*, the Syrian rue from which it had been previously isolated — the telepathy reference was erased. The older, more conservative name was given "official precedence," a decision that McKenna implicitly criticizes. The renaming represents a broader pattern in which Western science systematically strips psychoactive compounds of their more provocative associations, preferring taxonomic neutrality to the preservation of indigenous knowledge and extraordinary claims.

## The Parke-Davis Connection: Corporate Pharmacy and Indigenous Knowledge

The role of the Parke-Davis Company in the peyote story highlights the complex relationship between corporate pharmaceutical interests and indigenous plant knowledge. Parke-Davis, one of America's oldest pharmaceutical companies, was the source of the peyote buttons that Lewin brought back to Berlin. The company had obtained the peyote through its connections with traders and suppliers who had access to indigenous plant materials from the American Southwest and Mexico.

This commercial pipeline — from indigenous communities through corporate pharmacies to European research laboratories — established a pattern that would repeat throughout the 20th century. The knowledge and plant materials of indigenous peoples flowed to Western science through corporate intermediaries, generating pharmaceutical products and academic publications while rarely benefiting the communities from which the knowledge originated. McKenna's history of psychopharmacology is, in this sense, also a history of bioprospecting and cultural appropriation.

## The Phenomenological Tradition and Its Descendants

Beringer and Kluver established a phenomenological tradition in psychopharmacology that would have descendants throughout the 20th century. The emphasis on careful first-person description of subjective experiences, rather than purely behavioral or physiological measurement, became a hallmark of psychedelic research. This tradition is visible in the self-reports of Albert Hofmann's first LSD experience, in the clinical observations of Humphry Osmond and Abram Hoffer, and in the elaborate trip reports of the 1960s counterculture.

The phenomenological approach also influenced the development of psychometric scales for measuring altered states of consciousness, such as the Hallucinogen Rating Scale (HRS) and the [[mystical-experience]] Questionnaire (MEQ). These instruments, while more quantitative than Beringer's literary descriptions, share the same fundamental assumption: that the subjective experience of the user is the primary data of psychedelic research, not an inconvenient artifact to be explained away by neurochemistry.

The loss of this phenomenological tradition during the decades of psychedelic prohibition (roughly 1970–2000) is one of the great intellectual casualties of the drug war. When psychedelic research resumed in the 21st century, modern investigators had to largely reconstruct the phenomenological vocabulary that Beringer, Kluver, and their contemporaries had developed a century earlier.

## The Interruption and Its Consequences

McKenna notes with evident frustration that the promising psychopharmacological research of the 1920s and 1930s was interrupted by political events — the rise of the Nazi regime in Germany dispersed the research community, and the Second World War shifted scientific priorities toward military applications. The [[turner-harmala-alkaloids-preparation-and-dosage]] and their Amazonian sources were largely forgotten until the 1950s, when the discovery of their MAO-inhibiting properties rekindled interest in ayahuasca.

This interruption had consequences that extended beyond the loss of research momentum. The suppression of psychopharmacological research meant that when LSD was discovered in 1943, there was no established institutional framework for studying it responsibly. The absence of the phenomenological tradition represented by Beringer and Kluver contributed to the chaotic and ultimately disastrous way that psychedelic research was conducted in the 1960s.

## The Aztec-Mexica Context: Teonanacatl and the Spanish Suppression

The peyote and mushroom research of the early psychopharmacologists was, in a sense, a recovery project — an attempt to reclaim knowledge that had been violently suppressed during the Spanish colonization of the Americas. The Aztec (Mexica) people had used both peyote (*peyotl*) and psychoactive mushrooms (*teonanacatl*, "flesh of the gods") in religious ceremonies for centuries before the Conquest. The Spanish Inquisition, recognizing these substances as threats to Catholic conversion, banned their use and systematically destroyed the indigenous knowledge surrounding them.

William Safford's 1915 claim that teonanacatl was not a mushroom but the *Mammillaria* cactus — a claim that Reko rebutted in 1919 — can be understood as a late echo of this suppression. By denying that the Aztecs used psychoactive mushrooms, Safford was, whether consciously or not, continuing the colonial project of minimizing indigenous botanical knowledge. Reko's correction, and the later confirmation by Wasson and Heim in the 1950s, represented the restoration of a truth that had been obscured for four hundred years.

The German psychopharmacologists of the 1920s were working within this historical context, though they may not have fully appreciated it. Their laboratory investigations of peyote and harmine were, in McKenna's framing, acts of cultural recovery — retrieving fragments of indigenous knowledge from the ruins of colonialism and reinterpreting them through the lens of Western science.

## Legacy

The lineage from Lewis Lewin's peyote buttons to modern psychedelic science is direct and traceable. Every major development in psychopharmacology — the isolation of mescaline, the discovery of harmine/telepathine, the mapping of form constants, the phenomenological method — was present in nascent form in the German psychopharmacological renaissance of the 1920s. McKenna's account of this period serves as both historical recovery and implicit argument: the knowledge that Western science suppressed for political reasons is the same knowledge that indigenous cultures had preserved for millennia, and its recovery is essential to understanding the full scope of human consciousness and the role that psychoactive plants have played in shaping it.
