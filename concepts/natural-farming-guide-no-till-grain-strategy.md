# No-Till Grain Strategy for Sustainable Farms

The no-till grain strategy, based on Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming
methods, grows grain and legumes together continuously without plowing,
applying chemical fertilizers, weeding, or using pesticides. This system
requires remarkably low energy input while producing high yields on small
plots of land, making it ideal for self-sufficient homesteads.

## Why No-Till Grain?

Soil should not be turned or disturbed much at all. Grain is a staple of
most people's diets, and yet modern grain farming uses monstrous machines
to turn and fertilize the earth, and even bigger machines to harvest
perfect rows of grain, leaving the land to sit over the winter.

Many sustainable farms follow a rotational planting schedule with legumes
before and after the grain crop and a fallow period to let the soil rest.
The Fukuoka system, on the other hand, grows grain and legumes together
continuously, eliminating the need for fallow periods entirely.

## Energy Efficiency

This system is not only sustainable but requires extremely low energy
input. Not only is there no mechanization, the energy used in human labor
is also very low. A farmer eating an average 2,000-calorie per day diet
can produce 1,300 pounds (590 kg) of rice on a quarter acre. Using
animals for labor requires at least 5 times as many calories. Using a
tractor requires at least 10 times as many.

## Land Efficiency Comparison

Many self-sufficient homesteads avoid growing grains because they assume
it requires vast expanses of land. But the reality of land required to
keep one human alive entirely on one food is striking:

- 1,800 square feet of grain alone
- 5,400 square feet of potatoes alone
- 13,500 square feet of dairy farming alone
- 36,000 square feet of pigs alone
- 90,000 square feet of beef alone

This comparison is the greatest argument for a plant-based diet and
demonstrates that grain production on small plots is highly practical.

## Setting Up the Rice Paddy

First, level the ground and build a low mud retaining wall around the
plot that can hold 2 inches (5 cm) of water. You may need to use a chisel
plow the first year if the soil is extremely compacted. Spread lime or
dolomite and a thin layer of chicken manure over the area and water it
in. This soil disturbance and fertilizing needs to be done only once.

On the outer perimeter, grow a band of weed-control plants such as
comfrey, lemongrass, or citrus. Mulch these with sawdust for additional
protection. If you cannot have a paddy that fills with water, dry rice
species exist that can survive on monsoon rains alone.

## Step-by-Step No-Till Method

**Step 1:** A variety of plants can be grown together. Each plot will
always grow rice and white clover. Then add rye, barley, millet, winter
wheat, or oats. Use approximately 1 pound of clover, 6 to 14 pounds
of grains, and 5 to 10 pounds of rice seed per acre.

**Step 2:** Rice seed is sown in early fall. It can be broadcast and
covered with straw or made into seed balls. Seed balls can be made by
mixing seeds with mud and pressing through a wire mesh, or by wetting
seeds and rolling them in fine clay dust until they form a ball shape.

**Step 3:** In mid-fall, harvest last year's rice and lay it out on rice
racks to dry for a couple of weeks. Thresh off the husks and straw and
save it. Within a month, sow unhusked rice in the field and spread the
saved husks and straw over it.

**Step 4:** In winter, if the rice has grown to 6 inches, allow 40 ducks
per acre to graze in the field. If you notice thin spots, plant more seed
quickly. Keep the rice drained so water does not freeze.

**Step 5:** In spring, check again for thin spots and sow more seed if
needed. In late spring, harvest the rye or barley. Stack the grain to dry
for about a week and then thresh it.

**Step 6:** Spread threshed straw and husks on the field. If you have
more than one plot, do not spread it in the same plot where it was grown.
Cross-spread between plots to balance nutrients.

**Step 7:** By early summer, only rice and weeds should be growing. Flood
the paddy for about a week until the clover turns yellow (without killing
it). Prepare seeds of the grains for sowing in fall.

## Cold Climate Adaptations

In areas too cold for rice, use a system with shorter cycles. Spring
wheat can be planted in the spring with oats or barley or wheat as the
winter crop. You can also experiment with squash, melons, tomato, cotton,
vetch, or sunflowers as no-till crops.

## Understanding Grain Maturity

Grain goes through several stages: seed, seedling, tiller, booting,
heading, flowering, milk phase, dough stage, and finally dead ripe.
Ideally grain should be harvested when in the late dough stage, when it
is far past the milk stage but still able to be dented.

## Traditional Harvesting Methods

Harvest with a scythe, sickle, or horse-drawn mower. Sheave and shock
the grain and let it dry in the field. Thresh with a hand flail on the
barn floor. Winnow by tossing grain in the air with a basket on a windy
day. Save the chaff for livestock feed and the straw for mulch.

## Seed Saving and Storage

Save seed from your second crop (unless using a hybrid). Choose the best
seed heads, unbroken and healthy. Let them dry in the shock for at least
a month. Store all grains in rodent-proof containers in the house with
cats around to deter mice. Grain will keep for a year or more until
ground. Once ground, flour must be used immediately.
