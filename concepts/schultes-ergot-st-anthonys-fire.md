# Ergot and St. Anthony's Fire

Ergot (*[[claviceps-purpurea]]*) is a fungal parasite of cereal grasses,
primarily rye, that has played a dark but pivotal role in the history of
Europe. Unlike most psychoactive plants discussed in Schultes and
Hofmann's compendium, ergot is not a plant but a sac fungus
(Ascomycete) whose accidental consumption caused some of the most
devastating mass poisonings in European history. Yet from this toxic
fungus emerged one of the most significant discoveries in
psychopharmacology: the lysergic acid derivatives, including LSD.

## Botanical Description and Life Cycle

Ergot is a fungal disease of certain grasses and sedges, primarily rye.
The name "ergot" means "spur" and refers to the sclerotium or fruiting
body of the fungus. The spur is a purplish or black, curved,
club-shaped growth measuring half an inch to two and a half inches (1-6
cm) long, which parasitically replaces the endosperm of the rye kernel.
The fungus produces both psychoactive and toxic alkaloids.

There are two distinct periods in the life cycle of this fungus: an
active and a dormant stage. The ergot spur represents the dormant
stage. When the spur falls to the ground, it sprouts globular heads
called ascocarps from which grow asci, each with threadlike ascospores
that are disseminated when the asci rupture. The entire cycle perpetuates
through infected grain, spreading from field to field during harvest.

## St. Anthony's Fire: The Medieval Plague

In the Middle Ages, especially where rye was used in bread-making,
whole areas of Europe were frequently poisoned by ergotism. When
fungus-infected rye kernels were milled into flour, entire districts
suffered plagues known as St. Anthony's Fire. Hundreds of citizens
would go mad and suffer hallucinations, often causing permanent
insanity, gangrene, or death. The disease manifested in two principal
forms: convulsive ergotism, characterized by violent spasms and
hallucinations, and gangrenous ergotism, marked by vasoconstriction and
tissue death in the extremities.

Ergot was apparently never purposefully used as a hallucinogen in
medieval Europe, though it was employed extensively as a medicine by
midwives in cases of difficult childbirth. The fungus induced
contractions of involuntary muscles and was a strong vasoconstrictor,
making it one of the most important obstetric drugs before modern
medicine.

## The [[eleusinian-mysteries]] Connection

It has been convincingly argued that ergot played a role in the
Eleusinian mysteries of ancient Greece, the most important religious
initiation ceremony of the ancient Hellenic world. The mysteries at
Eleusis, conducted for nearly two thousand years, may have involved
the controlled use of ergot-containing preparations to induce visionary
experiences among initiates. The goddess Demeter and her daughter
Persephone were central to these rites, which promised revelations about
the afterlife to those who underwent the secret ceremonies.

## Chemical Constituents and the Discovery of LSD

The pharmacologically active constituents of ergot are ergoline
alkaloids, mainly derivatives of lysergic acid. These alkaloids form
the basis of important medicines used today in obstetrics, internal
medicine, and psychiatry. The most potent hallucinogen known, lysergic
acid diethylamide (LSD), is a semisynthetic derivative of ergot.

[[albert-hofmann]] first synthesized LSD in 1938 while studying ergot
alkaloids at Sandoz Laboratories in Switzerland. He accidentally
discovered its extraordinary psychoactive properties in 1943. This
discovery, born from research on a humble rye parasite, would transform
modern understanding of consciousness, brain chemistry, and the nature
of perception itself.

## Significance in Ethnobotany

Ergot occupies a unique place in ethnobotany as a fungus rather than a
plant. Its accidental psychoactive effects shaped European history,
inspired religious speculation about ancient mysteries, and ultimately
led to the synthesis of LSD. The ergot alkaloids demonstrate how natural
substances, even those never intentionally used by human cultures, can
open new frontiers in pharmacology and the study of altered states.

The story of ergot also illustrates the thin line between poison and
medicine, between accidental intoxication and deliberate visionary
practice. While no culture is known to have purposefully cultivated
ergot for its psychoactive properties, its impact on human history is
unquestionable, from medieval plague to modern psychopharmacology.

## Dosage and Toxicity

Dosage is extremely difficult to determine and can be dangerous. Ergot
alkaloids in uncontrolled quantities are highly toxic, and ingestion of
ergot-contaminated grain has historically caused death. The line between
a psychoactive dose and a lethal one is very narrow, which explains why
no culture appears to have developed a safe, intentional use of ergot
as a hallucinogen despite its potent psychoactive properties.

## Modern Applications

Ergot derivatives continue to be important in modern medicine. Ergot
alkaloids and their synthetic derivatives are used in the treatment of
migraine headaches, postpartum hemorrhage, and Parkinson's disease.
The discovery that LSD could model psychotic states in otherwise
healthy individuals revolutionized psychiatric research and contributed
to the development of serotonin receptor theory in neuroscience.
