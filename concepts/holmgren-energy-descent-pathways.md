# Holmgren's Energy Descent Pathways

Energy descent is the foundational context within which David Holmgren frames permaculture design. Rather than treating sustainability as a static end state, Holmgren argues that modern industrial civilisation represents a temporary energy pulse driven by fossil fuels, and that the inevitable decline of these fuels will force a return to patterns observable in nature and pre-industrial societies dependent on renewable energy.

## The Fossil Fuel Pulse

The Industrial Revolution was fundamentally powered by the extraction of ancient solar energy stored in coal, oil, and gas over hundreds of millions of years. This one-time inheritance allowed human populations, technology, and consumption to expand at rates unprecedented in planetary history. Holmgren, drawing heavily on the systems ecology of Howard T. Odum, describes this period as a "pulse" — a temporary surge in available energy that transforms the system receiving it.

Graphs of self-organising systems across all scales — from microbial populations to galaxies — show this same pattern: rapid growth followed by climax and decline. The fossil fuel pulse is no different. Industrial culture and permaculture are "stable only in their direction of energy use," with the current globalised economy representing "chaotic climax and transition from growth in population and energy use to decline."

We live in a world of unprecedented wealth resulting from the harvesting of enormous storages of fossil fuels created by the earth over billions of years. We have used some of this wealth to increase our harvest of the earth's renewable resources to an unsustainable degree. Most of the adverse impacts of this over-harvesting will show up as available fossil fuels decline. In financial language, we have been living by consuming global capital in a reckless manner that would send any business bankrupt.

## Beyond Sustainability

Holmgren deliberately titled his book *Principles & Pathways Beyond Sustainability* to challenge what he sees as naive notions of sustainability. For any human culture to be truly sustainable, it must demonstrate the capacity to reproduce itself across generations without catastrophic breakdown — something high-energy society cannot do by definition, since it is energetically impossible.

"Sustainability" has been appropriated by corporate spin doctors, stripped of meaning, and turned into a marketing label. Even the concept of "permanence" at the heart of permaculture is problematic when we accept the reality of continuous change. Holmgren argues that our task is not to achieve sustainability as a stable state but to use our familiarity with continuous change to adapt to energy descent.

The implicit assumption of sustainability is that current patterns of civilisation can be maintained indefinitely with appropriate modifications. Holmgren rejects this, arguing that the patterns themselves are products of a specific energetic context and will necessarily change as that context changes.

## The Mountain Metaphor

Holmgren uses the metaphor of a mountain expedition to frame energy descent. Humanity has climbed a spectacular but dangerous peak. The climb involved heroic effort and sacrifice, but also exhilaration and new views at every step. From the summit, the view reconnects us with the wonder and majesty of the world. But we cannot dally — we must chart our way down while we have favourable weather and daylight.

The descent will be more hazardous than the climb. We may need to camp on plateaus to rest and sit out storms. Having been on the mountain so long, we can barely remember the valley home we fled as it was progressively destroyed by forces we did not understand. But each step brings us closer to a sheltered valley where we can make a new home.

This metaphor captures the ambivalence of the transition: it involves both loss and return, both danger and homecoming. The skills and perspectives gained on the ascent will be valuable on the descent, but they must be applied differently.

## Permaculture as Adaptation Strategy

Permaculture, in Holmgren's framing, is a "whole-hearted adaptation to the ecological realities of decline, which are as natural and creative as those of growth." This distinguishes it from "green tech" optimists like Amory Lovins, whose natural capitalism assumes continued growth with improved efficiency. While there are overlaps, permaculture differs in critical ways:

- It prioritises using existing wealth to rebuild natural capital, especially trees and forests
- It emphasises bottom-up redesign processes starting with individuals and households
- It was predicated on the likelihood of some degree of societal collapse and breakdown
- It sees pre-industrial sustainable societies as providing relevant models

## Third Wave Environmentalism

Holmgren identifies three waves of modern environmental awareness. The first wave emerged from the Club of Rome report (1972) and oil shocks of 1973 and 1975 — the context in which permaculture was born. The second wave followed public awareness of the greenhouse effect in the late 1980s. By 1999, signs were in place for a third wave, which Holmgren saw as involving the mainstreaming of earlier innovations while throwing up new insights that challenge previous assumptions.

Each wave has carried environmental ideas further into the mainstream while also generating new understandings that go beyond the original formulations. The third wave may be characterised by a convergence of environmental awareness with practical action at the household and community level, driven by the growing gap between optimistic projections and lived experience.

## Industrial vs Sustainable Culture

Holmgren contrasts two cultural systems across polarised characteristics:

| Characteristic | Industrial Culture | Sustainable Culture |
|---|---|---|
| Energy base | Non-renewable | Renewable |
| Material flows | Linear | Cyclical |
| Natural assets | Consumption | Storage |
| Organisation | Centralised | Distributed network |
| Scale | Large | Small |
| Movement | Fast | Slow |
| Feedback | Positive | Negative |
| Focus | Centre | Edge |
| Activity | Episodic change | Rhythmic stability |
| Thinking | Reductionist | Wholistic |

The dynamic balance between these polarities is a recurring theme throughout Holmgren's twelve design principles. Neither pole is inherently superior; rather, the creative tension between them generates the conditions for adaptive change. The beam balance model and the pulsing model of ecosystem dynamics both illustrate these balances.

## The Role of Affluence in Transition

Affluence has paradoxically enabled the experimentation needed for energy descent. The safety net of generational wealth, the welfare state, and philanthropy has provided insurance against the consequences of experimental failures. This has led to an explosion of creativity in the arts, sciences, and lifestyle innovation — much of it outside institutional constraints.

However, this same affluence has generated enormous waste and dysfunction. When energy supply again becomes constraining, negative feedback controls will operate to constrain or eliminate system elements that do not contribute to survival. The "fast breeder" effect of free energy creates evolutionary possibilities that could never emerge under constraint, some of which may prove adaptive once energy is again limited.

If energy supply is too great, the system usually dies from self-pollution or through cancerous growth that causes major system malfunction. Eventually energy supply again becomes constraining, and negative feedback controls operate to constrain or eliminate elements that do not contribute to system survival in an energy-efficient way.

## Energy Quality Hierarchy

A critical insight from Odum's energy analysis is that energy comes in different qualities. The embodied energy (EMERGY) of goods and services reflects the total amount of solar energy that went into producing them. Industrial agriculture uses enormous quantities of high-quality fossil fuel energy to produce food with relatively low EMERGY value compared to traditional agriculture powered by renewable sources.

Understanding this energy quality hierarchy helps explain why many technological "solutions" to sustainability problems actually accelerate energy descent. Solar panels, for example, may have EMERGY yield ratios less than 1, meaning they consume more energy in their manufacture than they produce in their lifetime. Holmgren cautions against technological optimism that ignores energetic accounting.

## Pulsing Systems and Adaptive Change

All natural systems exhibit pulsing behaviour — cycles of growth, climax, and decline. This is not failure but the fundamental pattern of energy flow in self-organising systems. When any natural system receives a sudden increase in net energy supply, it is transformed by rapid growth. If the high-energy supply is maintained, diversification takes over from simple growth.

Eventually energy supply again becomes constraining, and negative feedback controls operate to constrain or eliminate elements that do not contribute to system survival. Permaculture gardeners experimenting with many new and climatically marginal crops can be seen as a hobby activity reflecting affluence, but this experimentation will also lead to advances useful in a low-energy future.

## Philosophical Underpinnings

Holmgren grounds energy descent thinking in a critique of Enlightenment rationalism and Cartesian dualism. The philosophical frameworks that emerged with a rising energy base are likely to be dysfunctional — even destructive — in a world of limited and declining energy. Individualism, for example, is better understood as an outcome of material well-being rather than its source.

This does not mean rejecting modern knowledge wholesale. Rather, it means recognising that our most cherished beliefs and values are not universal truths but products of a specific energetic context. The transition to energy descent requires us to evaluate every idea, technology, and institution against the question: does this help or hinder adaptation to declining energy availability?

## Traditional Societies as Energy Descent Models

Indigenous and peasant societies that have existed in relative balance with their environments for millennia provide working models of energy descent cultures. These cultures were not static — they evolved and changed over time — but they operated within energetic constraints that modern society has temporarily escaped.

Holmgren notes that the permaculture focus on learning from indigenous tribal cultures is based on evidence that these cultures have existed in relative balance with their environment and survived longer than any of our more recent experiments in civilisation. This does not mean romanticising indigenous life or advocating a return to pre-technological existence. Rather, it means studying the design principles that allowed these cultures to persist: local self-reliance, feedback-rich social structures, and economies based on renewable resources.

## Permaculture Ethics as Foundation

The three permaculture ethics — Care for Earth, Care for People, and Fair Share — provide the ethical foundation for energy descent pathways. Holmgren treats these not as abstract moral principles but as practical design constraints that emerge from energetic realities. The more power available to a civilisation, the more critical ethics become in ensuring long-term survival. This ecologically functional view of ethics makes them central to the development of a culture for energy descent.

## Practical Implications

For practitioners, energy descent thinking means:

- Prioritising the rebuilding of natural capital (soil, water, forests) over consumption
- Designing systems that function with minimal external energy inputs
- Valuing traditional and indigenous knowledge as repositories of low-energy solutions
- Accepting that the transition will be uneven, with local variations depending on resources
- Recognising that personal and household action is the most powerful lever for change
- Understanding that diversity of experiments increases the chance that adaptive solutions emerge
- Learning to identify which aspects of modernity will be valuable in a low-energy future
