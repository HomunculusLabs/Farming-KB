## Overview

Agar work is the practice of growing mushroom mycelium on nutrient agar in petri dishes. It is the foundation of advanced mushroom cultivation, enabling spore germination, contamination identification, strain isolation, and long-term culture storage. Working with agar transforms cultivation from a gamble with multi-spore genetics into a precise, repeatable process. Every serious cultivator eventually adopts agar work as a core skill.

## What is Agar?

Agar is a gelatinous substance derived from red algae. It serves as a solid growth medium that holds nutrients and water while allowing mycelium to grow visibly on its surface. Agar melts at 185F (85C) and solidifies at approximately 100-106F (38-41C). This property allows it to be poured as a liquid and then set into a firm gel.

## Agar [[mushroom-agar-media-recipes|Recipes]]

### Malt Extract Agar (MEA) — The Standard

The most widely used agar recipe for mushroom cultivation. Simple, reliable, and promotes good mycelial growth.

- 10g light malt extract (not hopped malt extract used for brewing)
- 10g agar powder
- 500ml distilled water

Light malt extract (LME) provides sugars, amino acids, and minerals. Dried malt extract (DME) works but may produce slightly darker media. Use the lightest color LME available.

### Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA)

A classic microbiological medium that supports excellent fungal growth.

- 200g sliced potato (peeled)
- 500ml distilled water
- 10g agar powder
- 10g dextrose (corn sugar or honey can substitute)

Boil the potato slices in water for 30 minutes. Strain the broth through a coffee filter. Add water back to 500ml. Add agar and dextrose, bring to a boil while stirring. Pour.

### Dog Food Agar (DFA)

A non-traditional but highly effective recipe that produces exceptionally rhizomorphic (branching, rope-like) growth. Developed by cultivators seeking better isolation characteristics.

- 200ml water
- 20g dry dog food (any brand, kibble type)
- 10g agar powder

Boil dog food in water for 20 minutes. Strain through a coffee filter. Add water back to 200ml. Add agar, boil to dissolve. Pour.

### Nutrient Yeast Agar (NYA)

Good for recovering stressed or slow-growing cultures.

- 10g light malt extract
- 1g nutritional yeast
- 10g agar powder
- 500ml distilled water

### Variations and Additives

- **Activated charcoal**: 0.5-1g per 500ml. Darkens the agar, making it easier to see translucent contaminants.
- **Antibiotics**: Gentamicin or chloramphenicol (for bacterial contamination issues). Use sparingly and only as a diagnostic tool — mycelium can develop resistance.
- **Food coloring**: Helps identify which side of the plate is up. Add a drop to the cooled agar before pouring.
- **Wood chips or sawdust**: For wood-loving species, adding sterilized wood to the agar can help select for the desired organism.

## Pouring Plates

### Materials

- Prepared agar solution (still hot/liquid)
- Sterile petri dishes (pre-sterilized plastic or glass)
- Still air box (SAB) or laminar flow hood
- Isopropyl alcohol (70%)

### Process

1. Prepare agar solution and bring to a boil. Stir until all components are fully dissolved.
2. Allow the solution to cool to approximately 120-130F (49-54C). Pouring too hot causes condensation on the lid. Pouring too cool causes the agar to solidify prematurely.
3. In the SAB or flow hood, lift the lid of each petri dish just enough to pour.
4. Pour approximately 15-20ml of agar per plate (about 1/8 to 1/4 inch deep).
5. Immediately replace the lid. Gently swirl the plate to distribute the agar evenly.
6. Allow plates to solidify completely (30-60 minutes).
7. Stack plates and wrap with plastic wrap or store in ziplock bags to prevent drying.
8. Let plates sit for 24-48 hours at room temperature before using. This allows any residual moisture to settle and reveals any contamination that was present.

### Plate Storage

- Unused plates store in the refrigerator (sealed in bags) for 2-4 weeks.
- Let plates warm to room temperature before inoculating — condensation can cause issues.
- Older plates may dry out and pull away from the edges; this is normal but reduces usable surface area.

## Inoculation Techniques

### Spore Inoculation

Drop 0.1-0.5 cc of spore solution onto the agar surface. A single drop from a spore syringe contains thousands of spores. Multiple germination points will appear over 3-7 days.

### Agar Wedge Transfer

The primary technique for moving mycelium between plates or from plates to grain:

1. Flame-sterilize a scalpel or inoculation loop.
2. Allow to cool (10-15 seconds).
3. Cut a small wedge (approximately 5mm x 5mm) from the leading edge of the mycelium on the donor plate. The leading edge is the most vigorous and least likely to harbor hidden contamination.
4. Transfer the wedge to the receiving plate, placing it mycelium-side down.
5. Position the wedge near the edge of the plate for directional growth, or in the center for radial expansion.
6. Close the plate immediately.

### Tissue Culture (Cloning)

Taking a piece of tissue directly from a mushroom fruiting body to create a genetically identical culture:

1. Select a healthy, vigorous mushroom with desirable characteristics.
2. Tear (do not cut) the stem to expose clean inner tissue. The outside of the mushroom is heavily contaminated with spores and environmental organisms; the inside is relatively sterile.
3. Flame-sterilize tweezers and grasp the exposed inner tissue.
4. Place the tissue sample on the agar surface.
5. Seal and incubate. Growth typically appears in 3-5 days.

## Strain Isolation

Strain isolation is the process of selecting and separating individual genetic strains from a multi-spore germination. This is how cultivators develop consistent, high-performing cultures.

### The Process

1. Germinate spores on agar. Multiple distinct sectors of mycelium will appear, each representing a different genetic individual.
2. Identify sectors with desirable characteristics: fast growth, rhizomorphic morphology, thick leading edge.
3. Transfer a small piece from the leading edge of the selected sector to a fresh plate.
4. Repeat this transfer process 3-5 times, each time selecting the most vigorous sector.
5. After several transfers, the culture stabilizes and grows uniformly — this is your isolated strain.

### Characteristics to Select For

- **Rhizomorphic growth**: Rope-like, organized growth indicates strong genetics. Cottony (tomentose) growth is weaker and less organized.
- **Speed**: Faster-growing sectors are generally more vigorous.
- **Sectoring**: Avoid sectors that continue to show multiple growth patterns — these are not yet isolated.
- **Cleanliness**: Select sectors that are clearly separated from any contamination.

## Long-Term Storage: Agar Slants

Agar slants are test tubes partially filled with solidified agar, stored at cool temperatures. They allow long-term culture preservation (6-12 months) without the need for frequent transfers.
