## Mycofabrication: Engineering Materials from Mycelium

Mycofabrication is the use of fungal mycelium as a biological manufacturing platform to produce structural materials, textiles, packaging, and construction components. Mycelium, the vegetative body of fungi, consists of a network of hyphal filaments that can colonize organic substrates and self-assemble into dense, load-bearing composites.

The field emerged from the intersection of mycology, materials science, and sustainable design. Commercial interest has grown rapidly since the 2010s, with companies such as Ecovative Design, MycoWorks, and Mogu producing mycelium-based products at industrial scale.

## Mycelium Growth and Material Formation

The mycofabrication process begins with selecting a fungal species and a lignocellulosic substrate. Common substrates include agricultural waste fibers such as hemp hurds, straw, sawdust, corn stalks, and cotton gin waste.

Fungal mycelium is inoculated onto the substrate and incubated under controlled temperature and humidity conditions. Over 5-14 days, the mycelium ramifies through the substrate, secreting extracellular polymers that bind the particles together into a cohesive mat.

The extracellular matrix secreted by mycelium contains chitin, glucans, proteins, and hydrophobic compounds. This matrix acts as a natural adhesive, gluing substrate particles into a rigid composite without synthetic resins or chemical binders.

Species selection is critical for material properties. Ganoderma lucidum produces dense, woody composites with high compressive strength. Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster mushroom) colonizes rapidly but produces lighter, less dense materials suitable for packaging applications.

Trametes versicolor has been investigated for its ability to produce composites with enhanced fire resistance. Species from the genus Schizophyllum produce materials with unusual flexibility, potentially useful for applications requiring impact absorption.

Growth rate and substrate compatibility vary significantly between species. Some industrial processes use mixed-species inocula to combine rapid colonization speed with desirable final material properties, though interspecific competition can complicate process control.

## Material Properties and Characterization

Mycelium composites exhibit mechanical properties that fall between those of polystyrene foam and natural wood. Compressive strength typically ranges from 0.1 to 2.5 MPa depending on species, substrate, and processing conditions.

The density of mycelium composites can be tuned by adjusting substrate particle size, packing density, and incubation duration. Higher density materials achieve compressive strengths approaching those of low-density particleboard.

Thermal conductivity of mycelium composites is remarkably low, ranging from 0.04 to 0.07 W/m·K. This places them in the same insulating performance category as expanded polystyrene, making them attractive as sustainable building insulation alternatives.

Water absorption is a significant limitation. Untreated mycelium composites can absorb 200-400% of their dry weight in water. Post-treatment with natural oils, waxes, or bio-based coatings substantially improves water resistance.

Acoustic absorption coefficients for mycelium composites range from 0.2 to 0.8 across the 500-4000 Hz frequency range, comparable to commercial acoustic ceiling tiles. This makes them viable for sound dampening applications.

Electrical properties of mycelium composites have received limited study. Initial measurements indicate dielectric constants in the range of 2-5, suggesting potential for lightweight electronic substrate applications if conductivity can be enhanced through doping or metallization.

## Mycelium Leather and Textiles

Mycelium leather represents one of the most commercially advanced applications of mycofabrication. Companies like MycoWorks have developed proprietary processes to grow thin, flexible mycelium sheets that mimic the hand feel and durability of animal leather.

The production process involves growing mycelium on a liquid or solid medium under tightly controlled conditions that promote horizontal hyphal growth rather than three-dimensional penetration. After 5-10 days of growth, the mat is harvested, compressed, and tanned using plant-based processes.

Mycelium leather can be produced in a range of thicknesses, colors, and textures. The material is biodegradable, avoids the ethical concerns of animal leather, and eliminates the chromium tanning chemicals used in conventional leather processing.

Fire resistance is a notable advantage of mycelium textiles. Unlike synthetic leather materials derived from petroleum, mycelium is inherently flame retardant due to its chitin content and does not melt or drip when exposed to flame.

## Packaging and Disposable Applications

Mycelium packaging was the first commercial application of mycofabrication. Companies such as Ecovative Design partnered with IKEA and Dell to replace polystyrene foam packaging with mycelium-based protective packaging.

The production process involves molding mycelium-inoculated substrate into custom shapes using form-fitting molds. As the mycelium grows, it takes the shape of the mold, producing custom-fit protective inserts for shipping electronics, furniture, and other products.

End-of-life disposal is straightforward. Mycelium packaging is fully compostable and breaks down in soil within 45-90 days. This contrasts sharply with polystyrene, which persists in landfills for centuries.

The carbon footprint of mycelium packaging is estimated at 60-80% lower than expanded polystyrene on a life-cycle basis. The mycelium sequesters carbon during growth, and the substrate is typically derived from agricultural waste that would otherwise decompose anaerobically.

## Construction and Architecture

Mycelium composites are being explored as structural and insulating materials for the built environment. Research prototypes include mycelium bricks, wall panels, ceiling tiles, and even load-bearing structural elements.

Compressive strengths of 0.5-2.5 MPa are adequate for non-load-bearing applications such as interior partition walls and insulation. For load-bearing applications, mycelium composites are typically combined with timber or concrete to create hybrid structural systems.

The Living, an architectural design studio, created the Hy-Fi tower for the Museum of Modern Art's PS1 courtyard in 2014. This 12-meter-tall structure was built entirely from mycelium bricks grown from corn stalk substrate and coated with a reflective brick film.

Fire performance of mycelium building materials is favorable. The material self-extinguishes without flame retardant treatment due to its high moisture content and chemical composition. ASTM E84 testing has shown Class A or Class B fire ratings for certain mycelium composites.

## Biomedical and Emerging Applications

Mycelium scaffolds are being investigated for tissue engineering applications. The porous, three-dimensional structure of mycelium composites provides a template for cell attachment and tissue growth that mimics the extracellular matrix.

Filtration is another emerging application area. The mycelial network can trap particles from air and water, and the living organism can be engineered to degrade specific contaminants. Mycelium water filters have shown effectiveness at removing heavy metals and organic pollutants.

3D printing of mycelium composites is an active research frontier. Direct ink writing of mycelium-laden bioinks enables the fabrication of complex geometries that cannot be achieved through mold-based growth methods. Printable formulations typically combine live mycelium with a gel carrier medium.

Bioremediation applications exploit the metabolic capabilities of living mycelium composites. Mycelium bricks containing pollutant-degrading fungi have been used to create biological filtration walls that passively treat contaminated air or water.

## Limitations and Challenges

Variability in material properties remains a significant challenge for quality control. Biological growth introduces batch-to-batch variation that is difficult to eliminate, even under tightly controlled manufacturing conditions.

Scale-up from laboratory to industrial production requires overcoming challenges in sterilization, contamination control, and process consistency. Large-scale mycelium reactors must maintain aseptic conditions over thousands of square meters of growth surface.

Water sensitivity limits outdoor applications without protective coatings. While natural finishes improve water resistance, achieving the durability of synthetic materials for exterior exposure remains difficult.

Cost competitiveness with conventional materials is improving but not yet achieved for most applications. Mycelium leather and packaging are approaching price parity with premium alternatives, but structural building materials remain more expensive per unit of performance.

## Practical Applications

The concepts discussed here have direct applications in mushroom cultivation, mycoremediation, and fungal ecology research. Understanding these principles enables practitioners to optimize growing conditions and develop innovative approaches to fungal biotechnology.

## Key Considerations

When applying these concepts, several factors warrant attention: environmental conditions, regional climate variations, available resources, and long-term sustainability goals. Success depends on careful observation and adaptive management based on feedback from the system.
