## Fungal Melanin Biosynthesis and Ecological Functions

## Introduction

Melanin is a broad class of dark, insoluble pigments produced by virtually all groups of fungi. Fungal melanins are not a single chemical entity but a family of heterogeneous polymers with diverse biosynthetic origins, structures, and functions. In fungi, melanin serves as a critical survival molecule — providing protection against UV radiation, [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]], heavy metals, and host immune responses. Melanized fungi are among the most resilient organisms on Earth, and melanin production is a key virulence factor in pathogenic species.

## Types of Fungal Melanin

### DHN Melanin (1,8-Dihydroxynaphthalene Melanin)

DHN melanin is the most common type in filamentous ascomycetes and basidiomycetes. It is produced through the polyketide synthase (PKS) pathway starting from acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA:

1. **PKS activity**: A reducing polyketide synthase (R-PKS) assembles a polyketide chain that undergoes cyclization to form 1,3,6,8-tetrahydroxynaphthalene (T4HN).
2. **Reductive steps**: T4HN is sequentially reduced by specific reductases (T4HN reductase, scytalone dehydratase, vermelone reductase) to produce 1,8-dihydroxynaphthalene (DHN).
3. **Polymerization**: DHN is oxidized by laccases or tyrosinases, and the resulting quinone radicals undergo non-enzymatic polymerization to form melanin.

Key enzymes and corresponding genes:
- **THN reductase** (*thr1*): T4HN → scytalone
- **Scytalone dehydratase** (*scd1*): scytalone → vermelone
- **Vermelone reductase** (*vr1*): vermelone → DHN
- **PKS** (*pks1* or *alb1* in *Aspergillus*): initiates the polyketide chain

Inhibition of DHN melanin synthesis (e.g., with tricyclazole, which inhibits reductases) produces albino mutants that are dramatically more sensitive to environmental stresses, demonstrating melanin"s protective role.

### DOPA Melanin

DOPA melanin is produced from the amino acid L-tyrosine through the DOPA (3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine) pathway, analogous to mammalian eumelanin biosynthesis:

1. Tyrosine → L-DOPA (via tyrosinase)
2. L-DOPA → dopaquinone (via tyrosinase)
3. Dopaquinone → cyclodopa → dopachrome
4. Dopachrome → 5,6-dihydroxyindole (DHI) and/or 5,6-dihydroxyindole-2-carboxylic acid (DHICA)
5. Oxidation and polymerization of DHI/DHICA to melanin

DOPA melanin is characteristic of melanized yeasts (*[[exophiala-dermatitidis]]*) and some dimorphic fungi. *Cryptococcus* melanin is specifically deposited in the cell wall, creating a distinctive concentric layered structure visible by electron microscopy.

### Pyomelanin

Pyomelanin is derived from homogentisic acid (HGA) through the tyrosine catabolic pathway. HGA accumulates when the enzyme homogentisate dioxygenase is impaired, and the resulting HGA auto-oxidizes and polymerizes to form a brown-black pigment. Pyomelanin is produced by some opportunistic pathogens (*[[soil-physicochemical-properties-cannabis-microbiome-assembly-winston]] from DHN and DOPA melanins.

### Melanin-Like Compounds

Some fungal pigments share chemical features with melanins but have distinct biosynthetic origins:

- **Ferricrocin and other siderophore-melanin complexes**: Iron-chelating melanin-like pigments
- **Glycoprotein-melanin complexes**: Covalently bound to cell wall structural proteins
- **Allomelanin**: A nitrogen-free melanin produced from catechol or other simple phenols

## Ecological Functions

### UV Radiation Protection

Melanin absorbs UV radiation across a broad spectrum (200–800 nm), with peak absorption in the UV-B and UV-C ranges. The absorbed energy is dissipated as heat through rapid non-radiative decay processes, preventing DNA damage. Melanized fungi survive UV doses 10–100× higher than non-melanized counterparts. This UV protection is critical for airborne spores and fungi colonizing exposed surfaces.

### Oxidative Stress Resistance

Melanin is a potent free radical scavenger. Its extended conjugated π-electron system can delocalize and neutralize [[cervantes-hydrogen-peroxide-sterilization]] and other oxidative agents used by host immune systems.

### Desiccation and Temperature Tolerance

Melanin reduces water permeability of fungal cell walls, helping maintain cellular hydration during desiccation. Melanized structures (sclerotia, stromata, dark-walled spores) survive extreme temperature fluctuations, including repeated freeze-thaw cycles. Melanin's contribution to thermotolerance is hypothesized to involve both structural reinforcement of the cell wall and dissipation of thermal energy.

### Heavy Metal Binding and Radiotrophic Growth

Fungal melanin has exceptional metal-binding capacity due to its abundance of phenolic, carboxyl, and amine functional groups. Melanin can concentrate uranium, cesium, and other radionuclides at levels 100–1000× above environmental concentrations. Remarkably, melanized fungi at Chernobyl and other radioactive sites appear to "harvest" ionizing radiation energy through melanin-mediated electron transfer, using radiation as an energy source for growth — a phenomenon termed **[[bloomfield-cryptococcus-neoformans-opportunistic-mycoses-aids]]*, melanin deposition in the cell wall directly protects against macrophage oxidative bursts and antimicrobial peptides.

### Enzyme Immobilization and Protection

Melanin can bind and stabilize enzymes on fungal surfaces, creating catalytically active zones that function even under hostile environmental conditions. This enzyme-immobilizing capacity has been exploited in biotechnology for creating melanin-based biocatalysts.

## Melanin in Fungal Pathogenicity

### Cryptococcus neoformans

Melanin is a major virulence factor in *C. neoformans*, the causative agent of cryptococcal meningitis. The fungus produces DOPA melanin from host-derived catecholamines (L-DOPA, dopamine, norepinephrine) during brain infection. Melanized *Cryptococcus* cells are more resistant to:

- Macrophage phagocytosis and killing
- Antifungal drugs (amphotericin B, caspofungin)
