# Fungal Antibiotics, Bacterial Predation, and Nitrogen Acquisition in Soil

## Overview
Fungi face a fundamental nutritional challenge when feeding on plant tissues: while
plant material provides abundant sugar (carbohydrates), it yields negligible
protein and therefore insufficient nitrogen for building fungal proteins and nucleic
acids. To overcome this nitrogen limitation, many fungi have evolved predatory
strategies including the secretion of antibiotics to kill soil bacteria, the
trapping of nematode worms, and the parasitism of other fungi. These behaviors
reveal fungi not merely as decomposers but as active predators in soil ecosystems.

## The Nitrogen Problem for Fungi
Without proteins or alternative nitrogen-containing molecules, fungi cannot build
their own proteins or nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). Plant tissues are particularly
poor nitrogen sources—they yield plenty of sugar but negligible protein. This
creates a serious bottleneck for fungi that depend primarily on dead or living
plant material as their food source. The nitrogen gap between what plants provide
and what fungi need to grow and reproduce drives the evolution of supplementary
nutritional strategies.

## Antibiotic Secretion as Predation
By secreting antibiotics, some fungi benefit not only by removing competing
microorganisms but also obtain an extra shot of nutrients in the form of dead
bacteria. This is not merely a defensive or competitive strategy—it is a form of
predation. The antibiotics kill bacteria in the vicinity of the fungal hyphae, and
the resulting dead cells leak their contents into the surrounding environment. The
fungus then absorbs these nutrient-rich fluids through its hyphal walls, gaining
access to the bacterial proteins, nucleic acids, and amino acids that plant
tissues lack.

## Penicillin and Cephalosporin in Nature
The same natural role has been suggested for antibiotics such as penicillin and
cephalosporin, which we employ against [[allelopathy-and-chemical-warfare-in-plants]] soil ecosystems.

## Mycoparasites: Fungi That Eat Fungi
The ultimate expression of fungal predation is mycoparasitism—fungi that infect
other fungi. The most conspicuous mycoparasites form fruiting bodies on fruiting
bodies. Asterophora probes the blackening tissues of old milk caps and russulas
and buds its own powdery mushrooms at the surface. Some Cordyceps species infect
the underground fruiting bodies of truffles. Farmed mushrooms are also at risk from
fungal parasites that cause dry and wet bubble, shaggy stipe, and cobweb disease.

## Trichoderma: The Biocontrol Agent
The soil fungus Trichoderma is deliberately introduced as a biological control
agent against fungal parasites of [[endophytic-fungi-in-agriculture]].

## Ecological Implications
The predatory behavior of fungi has profound implications for soil ecology and
nutrient cycling. By killing bacteria and nematodes, fungi release nitrogen and
other nutrients that would otherwise remain locked in living biomass. This
predatory activity accelerates nutrient turnover in soil and makes nitrogen
available for plant uptake. The fact that some fungi eat other fungi is symptomatic
of the behavior of all microorganisms discussed in this context: they penetrate
their foods with invasive hyphae, feed by absorbing nutrients, reproduce by spore
formation, and show a marked propensity for attacking other organisms.
