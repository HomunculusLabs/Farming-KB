## Japan

Japan's relationship with psychoactive mushrooms extends deep into its
cultural history:

- **Stropharia venenata** (formerly Stropharia caerulescens) Imai: Now
  classified within [[armillaria-mellea-entity]]). Singer and
  Smith mistakenly cited these incidents as examples of fatal effects,
  unjustly branding the species as highly dangerous for decades.

- **Psilocybe subcaerulipes** Hongo: A bluing species that caused
  intoxications when mistaken for honey mushrooms. Its discovery led to
  isolation of psilocybin in crystalline form.

- **Psilocybe argentipes** Yokoyama: Systematically studied by Yokoyama
  in 1973. No "urge to dance" was noted in controlled experiments,
  despite Japan's legendary "laughing mushroom" tales dating to the 11th
  century. Effects were comparable to Psilocybe species from other
  continents at similar dosages.

- **[[gymnopilus-spectabilis-entity]]) are now known not to
contain psychoactive substances. Mushroom cultivation in Japan has a
2,000-year tradition.

## Thailand and Southeast Asia

Thailand represents the epicenter of contemporary psychoactive mushroom
use:

- **Psilocybe cubensis**: Common across Thailand, growing on water
  buffalo dung and partially decomposed cow manure in valley areas at
  about 3,000 feet altitude. Fruits year-round, most abundantly in May
  and June (rainy season preceding monsoon). The first specimens from
  outside Mexico used by Hofmann to confirm psilocybin presence came from
  Thailand and Cambodia.

- **Psilocybe subcubensis** Guzman: Very similar to P. cubensis but with
  smaller spores and more limited tropical habitat.

- **Psilocybe samuiensis** Guzman, Bandala & Allen: Discovered in
  Thailand in August 1991. Similar in appearance to P. semilanceata but
  does not contain baeocystin. A bluing species that grows on fertilized
  soil but not directly on dung. Successfully cultivated on a mixture of
  rye, horse dung, and water (2:1:2) with a peat and chalk casing layer.

- **Panaeolus cyanescens**: Common dung-inhabiting species. Mushroom
  omelettes made with this species were famous house specialties at Bali
  restaurants during the 1970s and 1980s.

- **Panaeolus cambodginiensis** Ola'h & Heim: Found only in Cambodia.
  According to Ola'h, all fruiting bodies contain psilocybin.

- **Panaeolus tropicales** Ola'h: A close relative of P. cyanescens,
  found in mainland Asia. Extremely difficult to differentiate from
  P. cyanescens.

Mushroom restaurants in Thailand offer mushrooms as omelettes, soups,
teas, pizzas, or juices. Similar practices were reported from Sumatra,
Java, and the Philippines. Schroeder and Guzman proposed that the ancient
Vedic "soma" may have been a psychedelic Psilocybe species rather than
Amanita muscaria, based on distribution patterns and effects.

## Nepal

Mushrooms of the Psilocybe species are widespread throughout Nepal,
possibly classifiable as P. cubensis and P. subcubensis. No evidence of
indigenous mushroom use was found; consumption was limited to mycophilic
Western visitors who required relatively large doses (forty or more
mushrooms).

## Australia

Australia's psychoactive mycoflora attracted scientific attention early:

- **Psilocybe subaeruginosa**: Described by Cleland in 1927 from South
  Australia. Grows in New South Wales, Victoria, and South Australia.
  Colloquially called "gold top." Picker and Rickards isolated 0.45%
  psilocybin from dried specimens in 1970. Found in flat-bottomed valleys
  and on gentle slopes with moist, grassy soil.

- **Psilocybe cubensis**: Documented in southeastern Queensland and New
  South Wales valleys by Aberdeen and Jones in 1958. Likely introduced by
  early settlers. Also called "gold top." A 1969 bumper crop near
  Queensland's coast attracted thousands of collectors and spawned
  specialized transportation companies. Nearly displaced LSD on the black
  market by 1972.

- **Panaeolus cyanescens**: Called "blue meanies" in Australian
  counterculture (likely named after characters in the 1968 Beatles film
  "Yellow Submarine"). Contains psilocybin along with high levels of
  psilocin and serotonin. Originally misidentified as P. ovatus.

Usage in Australia was widespread by the late 1960s, with comprehensive
reports making it the first country outside Mexico to produce detailed
accounts of psychoactive mushroom use.

## New Zealand

Customs evolved around the same species found in Australia, with criminal
penalties modeled after Australian law. Psilocybe cubensis is not native
to New Zealand; all psychoactive species are generally referred to as
"magic mushrooms."

## Pacific Islands

- **Samoa**: Teenagers discovered psychoactive effects of Panaeolus
  cyanescens during the 1970s. Police persecution was halted when it
  became clear the mushrooms posed no significant health risk.

- **Bali**: Mushroom restaurants serving P. cyanescens omelettes became
  famous during the 1970s-1980s. Commercial cultivation developed using
  naturally-grown mycelia transferred onto fresh buffalo manure.
