# Nurse Crop Species in Permaculture

Nurse crops are plants deliberately grown to protect, shelter, and improve conditions for other more valuable or demanding species during their establishment phase. They are a practical application of [[pioneer-species-permaculture]] and [[permaculture-succession]] principles, accelerating the development of productive plant communities by simulating natural succession processes.

## How Nurse Crops Work

Nurse crops create favourable conditions for target species by providing shade and shelter from wind, suppressing competing grasses and weeds, fixing nitrogen and building soil fertility, producing mulch from leaf litter, attracting beneficial insects and birds, and moderating temperature extremes.

As the target species grow and become established, the nurse crops are eventually removed, shaded out, or managed to a minor role in the system. This mirrors the natural process where pioneer species prepare the ground for longer-lived climax species.

## Tagasaste as a Nurse Crop

Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus palmensis) is one of the most versatile nurse crop species. In trials conducted in New Zealand with various fruit and nut trees, trees growing in close association with tagasaste (as close as 1 meter) consistently grew better than the controls without tagasaste.

When tagasaste is grown close to other trees, it may influence the tree's shape. If a tree is grown for timber, the tagasaste will tend to force the tree up for light, and provided it is surrounded with tagasaste, it should produce a good straight, long timber tree. This is valuable for [[permaculture-forestry-systems]] establishment.

If the nursed tree is required to produce fruit or nuts, a spreading habit is usually desired for easy access to fruit. In this case the tagasaste should be clipped and used as mulch around the nursed tree or fed out to stock if necessary. This dual approach (timber vs fruit production) demonstrates the importance of understanding the target species' growth form when designing nurse crop systems.

## Tree Lupin as a Nurse Crop

Tree lupin (Lupinus arboreus) is an evergreen, perennial shrub or small tree to about 2 meters. It is nitrogen fixing and a good nurse crop, tolerant of frost and preferring deep loose soil. It provides bee forage from late winter to early summer.

Tree lupin can be cut hard to supply mulch rich in nitrogen, making it useful for [[soil-building-permaculture]] during the establishment of target species. Its ability to thrive in poor conditions makes it an excellent first-stage nurse crop for degraded sites.

## Acacia Species as Nurse Crops

Many Acacia species serve as nurse crops in permaculture systems. Acacia mearnsii (black wattle) is an efficient nitrogen fixer and an ideal tree for regenerating impoverished soils. In Indonesia, it has been widely planted to improve soil conditions for subsequent crops.

Acacia aneura (mulga) survives in harsh conditions and provides shelter for establishing plants. Its deep roots bring moisture and minerals to the surface. In the wild, mulga grows in association with Eucalyptus species, other Acacia species, and various understory plants, demonstrating the [[permaculture-plant-guilds]] concept.

Acacia adsurgens (mintilpi) is a moderately fast-growing large shrub for hot dry zones, with good potential for fuel and soil stabilisation. Acacia ampliceps (salt wattle) is fast growing and tolerates alkaline, highly saline, and waterlogged conditions, making it a nurse crop for [[permaculture-difficult-sites-plant-guide]].

## Black Locust as a Nurse Crop

Black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia) is a nitrogen-fixing tree widely used as a nurse crop in temperate permaculture systems. It grows rapidly, fixes nitrogen efficiently, provides excellent firewood, and produces flowers that are valuable bee forage.

Black locust can be coppiced to provide both nitrogen-rich mulch and fuel wood. Its ability to grow on poor soils and improve them for subsequent plantings makes it a classic nurse crop species in [[permaculture-earthworks-guide]] and land rehabilitation projects.

## Pioneer Forest as Nurse System

On large areas of degraded land, a pioneer forest can be established as a comprehensive nurse system. Particularly hardy and fast-growing trees create a kind of pioneer forest to protect the land from erosion and prevent it from drying out.

Later on, more demanding fruit trees can grow safely amongst the initial ones. Once they are stable and have grown large enough, the pioneer trees will no longer be required and can be cut down and used for timber. This large-scale nurse crop approach is described in [[microclimate-creation-permaculture]] and connects to [[permaculture-earthworks]].

## Nurse Crops with Livestock Integration

Nurse crop species often produce fodder that can be used to feed livestock during the establishment phase of a food forest or orchard. Tagasaste is a prime example, providing high-protein fodder (17-31 percent crude protein) that can support sheep, cattle, pigs, horses, rabbits, and poultry.

When nurse crops are used as fodder, the livestock return manure to the system, further building soil fertility. This integration of plant and animal systems is central to [[permaculture-livestock-forage-systems]] and [[sepp-holzer-livestock-integration]].

## Managing the Transition

The key challenge with nurse crops is managing the transition from nurse-dominated to target-species-dominated community. If nurse crops grow too vigorously, they may outcompete target species rather than supporting them.

Management techniques include regular cutting or coppicing of nurse crops, selective removal as target species establish, using nurse crops only on the windward side of target trees, and choosing nurse species that naturally decline as conditions improve (many short-lived pioneers).

The timing of nurse crop removal is important. Removing them too early leaves target species exposed. Removing them too late means they may have already suppressed the target species. Observation and responsive management, following [[permaculture-principle-observe-and-interact]], are essential.

## Nurse Crops in Food Forest Establishment

In [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]], nurse crops play a critical role during the establishment phase. A new food forest is essentially a degraded site (lawn, pasture, or bare soil) that needs to be converted to a multi-layered perennial system.

Nurse crops bridge the gap between the initial degraded state and the mature food forest. They provide the protective, fertile conditions that young fruit trees, shrubs, and perennial plants need to establish. Without nurse crops, establishing a food forest on exposed, degraded land is much more difficult and expensive.

## Nurse Crops in Shelterbelt Establishment

Nurse crops are also valuable for establishing [[permaculture-windbreak]] systems. Fast-growing nurse trees protect slower-growing, longer-lived windbreak species while they establish. For example, fast-growing poplars or casuarinas can nurse slower-growing oaks or ash in a windbreak row.

As the long-lived windbreak species mature, the nurse trees can be coppiced for firewood or mulch, or removed entirely to make room for the permanent windbreak. This staged approach ensures continuous wind protection while building a permanent, multi-species windbreak system.

The choice of nurse crop for shelterbelts depends on the climate, soil conditions, and the target windbreak species. Nitrogen-fixing nurse species provide the additional benefit of improving soil fertility for the windbreak trees, creating a self-fertilising shelterbelt system over time.
