# Mollison: Composting Methods and Systems

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 8)

## The Role of Compost in Permaculture

Mollison places compost and mulch at the centre of soil health management.

Compost or mulch is critical to preserving soil crumb structure, buffering pH, and improving sugar content and flavour of vegetable products.

The gums and gels produced by soil organisms create crumb structure, aerate the soil, and darken it so that it heats up faster in spring.

Humic acids from compost assist root development dramatically even at levels of 60 ppm carbon.

## Hot versus Cold Composting

Mollison identifies two fundamental composting approaches with different trade-offs.

Hot (aerobic) heaps heat up to 55-60 degrees C and kill weed seeds and pathogens, but nitrogen (ammonia) losses are severe.

Cold heaps (pitted or silage) do not lose nitrogen but neither do they kill weed seeds.

One percent of superphosphate added to a hot compost heap prevents ammonia escape.

## The Chinese Combined Method

Chinese scientists have developed a method that achieves the best of both worlds.

First, an aerated heap is built with bamboo poles inserted as air tunnels.

This is covered with mud and the heap heats up to 55-60 degrees C for a few days, killing weed seeds and pathogens.

Then all holes are sealed and the rest of the decay proceeds anaerobically, preserving nitrogen.

With sealed boxes, either hot or cold processes can take place depending on whether the box is ventilated.

## Rapid Composting Techniques

In the UK, liquid manures sprinkled on straw in silos or tanks, together with a forced air draught, produce compost in about one week.

Efficient open piles encased in straw need 10 days.

The liquid effluent system plus straw is suited to treatment of manurial sludge such as that from biogas digesters.

It is best to use dry twiggy or straw material as hot compost to kill weed seeds and produce useful heat.

## Winter Heat from Compost

In severe winter areas, the efficiency of rapid composting is counter-productive because slower heat release from large compost piles of 10-50 cubic metres can provide heat over a long period.

This greatly reduces glasshouse and house heating costs.

The compost itself is best applied to soil in spring after it has released its heat.

## Comfrey as a Biological Filter

Domestically, a comfrey bed is one way to absorb the faecal products of animals where wash-water from yards or pens is available.

Comfrey can stand heavy inputs of raw faeces in solution.

The crop may then be used for fodder or trenched for instant compost under other species of plants such as potatoes.

Flowthrough systems for methane production take little plant nutrient from faecal matter, and comfrey or algae ponds deal with the residues while producing useful by-products for compost and stock feed.

## Care in Selection of Compost Materials

Kevin Handreck (Organic Growing, 1987) identified potential mineral contamination from compost materials:

Galvanized, copper, or brass containers produce excess zinc and copper.

Clay, stainless steel, glass, or iron containers are preferable.

Manures from pigs, poultry, cattle, or sewage sludges can add excessive copper, zinc, nickel, boron, lead, or cadmium to soils, especially acid sandy soils.

Animals penned in galvanized areas can produce excess zinc in pen wastes.

Handreck recommends a limit of 10% worm casts in a potting mix due to potential mineral accumulation.

## Leaf Material Quality

Leaf material cut and mulched or composted green will contain more nutrients than fallen leaves.

Fallen leaves are still useful for humus production but should not be relied upon as the sole source of nutrients.

## Integration with Biogas Systems

The effluent from biogas digesters is a rich sludge full of nutrients but still containing seeds.

This sludge is ideally treated through the rapid composting system using straw and forced air.

The resulting compost is seed-free and nutrient-rich, completing the cycle from waste to resource.

## Compost as Soil Building Strategy

Compost is one of three primary methods gardeners use to build soil (along with bed shaping and mulching).

The process involves mixing compost or humus materials into the soil, supplying clay, sand, or nutrients to bring it to balance.

Accessory systems include growing compost materials as hedgerow, herbs, or soft-leaf plots within or around the garden.
