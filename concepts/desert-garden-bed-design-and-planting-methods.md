## Desert Garden Bed Design and Planting Methods

[[desert-home-gardens]] in drylands return so much in health, cash, and quality of life that they deserve intensive, bed-by-bed planning. In this approach, companion plants, [[mushroom-seasonal-succession-perennial-spawn]], bed soil treatments, and a permanent watering method are all designed together as an integrated system.

## Earth-Shaping Within Gardens

A variety of garden bed forms can be used in desert gardens, each suited to different conditions and plant needs:

- **Cool mulched pits** for trees — deep holes filled with [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] to provide cool root zones
- **[[mollison-gangammas-mandala-and-banana-circle-gardens]]** around a central mulch hole — efficient use of water and space
- **North-south ridges** to shade seedlings from intense lateral sun
- **Mulch "baskets"** at clump plantings — contained mulch systems for individual plant groups
- **Log boxes of mulch** over alkaline sands — raised beds using coarse woody material
- **Broad flood bays** for carrot crops — wide shallow beds designed for periodic flooding
- **Top-watered raised beds** for salty water supply (up to 1,200 ppm) — elevated beds that allow salt to drain away from root zones

## Irrigation Substitutes

Where plastic irrigation pipes are absent or expensive, adequate substitutes can be made from locally available materials:

- Unglazed pots buried to seep water slowly to root zones
- Bamboo pipes, drilled to leak water
- Flagons or bottles suspended with perforations
- Simple bucket drip systems
- Gravel beds below or on the surface, covered with flat materials

## Categories of Plant Establishment

Four main categories of plants require different establishment techniques:

### 1. Advanced Perennials and Trees in Pots

Including shrubs, flowers, and advanced tomatoes. Establishment procedure:

- Keep under shade until rains have fallen
- Transplant in wet weather
- Dig holes to accept the full root system
- Soak the hole and add a small handful of fertiliser (lime in acid areas, [[compost]], dibble a hole for the seedling
- If in thick mulch, pour one-quarter of a bucket (about a litre) of good soil into a hole made in the mulch
- Place a flat stone upwind as a windbreak
- Plant in the soil hole

### 3. Tubers and Bulbs

Including potato, yam, sweet potato, flower bulbs, and sunroot:

- Greened and if possible sprouted tubers can be pushed down into mulch
- Alternatively, bury and then mulch (in damp soils)
- Root crops prefer organic dry soils with pH 5.0–6.8, achieved by compost, perhaps some sulphur, and a thick damp mulch
- Subsurface water is ideal; a few marigold interplants aid root health

### 4. Large or Fine Seeds

Two main families of [[clay-seed-pellets-direct-seeding]]:

**Method 1 — Scatter over fine-tilth bed:**
- Scatter seeds over a bed ideally of sieved compost
- Wet and press down
- Sometimes hessian (burlap) is laid above the bed before watering
- Remove the hessian on day 3 or 4 after the first signs of germination appear
- This suits larger crop areas

**Method 2 — Fine soil lens over mulch:**
- A lens of fine soil 4–5 cm thick is patted down over a thick (12 cm) base mulch
- Fine seeds are sprinkled on this, forming patches of crop in a general mulch
- This suits repetitive sowings in home gardens

## Specific Crop Bed Designs

### Carrots and Onions

- Allocate 3–6 beds (4 m long × 1 m wide) for carrots and onions, depending on family size
- An additional 2–3 beds for chives, garlic, and shallots for year-round Allium supply
- Keen gardeners include beds for leeks, sand leeks, and multiplier onions
- Build raised beds dead level by levelling with a straight-edge
- Both onions and carrots prefer free sandy soils with moderate drainage and modest fertility (pH 5.5–7.5)
- Mix old compost and dry fine organic material (powdered grazing-animal manures) at ratio of 1:9 or 1:7 (organic material to soil)
- Sprinkle seed mixed with sawdust or coloured sand to show coverage
- Flood the bed to the rim with water not exceeding 1,100 ppm salt

**Carrot specifics:**
- A scatter of small salad radish breaks the soil crust and "thins" the crop as radishes mature in weeks and are removed
- Plant 3 beds: mid-summer, just before the cool season, and just after
- Surplus carrots keep well layered in cool sand in pits 0.5–1 m deep (green tops removed to 1 cm from crown, pitted unwashed)

**Onion specifics:**
- Onions are hung in dry airy plaits for storage
- Gladioli inhibit most pests and provide a small cash crop

**[[crotalaria]], or marigold ([[tagetes]]) reduces wind effects
- Tagetes reduces nematodes
- Crotalaria provides soil nitrogen and mulch

### Tomatoes and Peppers

- Both appreciate pH of about 5.5–6.8
- Tomatoes tolerate about twice the salt in soils compared to peppers
- Peppers may need more tank water at salt levels exceeding 1,100 ppm
- Both are best arranged for underground or subsurface (root level) irrigation via unglazed pottery or leaky pipes
- Both are unsuited to spray irrigation — sprays aid the spread of leaf mould, mildew, and fungi
- The efficient bed layout is a **"keyhole" system** holding 20–50 plants in 3 rows deep (1.5 m wide)
- Both benefit from Tagetes interplant and a wind shelter of sunflower, sunroot, or Crotalaria
- Basil is an excellent culinary companion

### Keyhole Beds

Keyhole beds provide easy access with minimum path-to-bed ratio and wasted space. They are densely planted raised beds, often circular or wedge-shaped with a central access path. Both tomatoes and peppers do well when set out as strong seedlings in soil pockets in deep mulch.

### Root Crops

- Broad beds, keyholes, or long rows are suitable
- The one essential is thick coarse mulch to shield tubers and cool root runs while buffering pH
- A family needs 2–3 successions of potato and sweet potato, with areas of about 10 square metres per bed
- Total: 20–30 square metres devoted to staple root crops
- Yam beans, jicama, sweet potato, and sunroot ([[helianthus-tuberosus]]) supply more reliable yields than potatoes in desert conditions

## Creating "Instant" Gardens

Such instant gardens have commonly been established in a few hours using thick mulch systems. They give satisfactory results in the first year, but good garden soils do not form until a few years of continuous gardening have passed, when the biological content of the soil is stabilised and crumb structures have formed.

## Economic Returns

Planning a garden repays itself quickly. A good gardener expects to amortise establishment costs (fences, beds, seed, mulch, micronutrients) in 6–8 months, thereafter gaining ongoing profit in health, food, and cash.
