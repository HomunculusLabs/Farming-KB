## Soil Rehabilitation — Water Control and Conditioning

Soil rehabilitation is the process of restoring degraded, compacted, eroded, or lifeless soils to productive health. In permaculture, soil rehabilitation is a priority wherever degradation has occurred, as all productive systems depend on stable and fertile soil. No matter on what substrate we start, we can create rich and well-structured soils in gardens with some input of labour and the addition of organic material or green manures.

## The Four Pillars of Soil Rehabilitation

Effective soil rehabilitation proceeds through four interconnected stages:

1. **Water control**: Drainage and irrigation management to correct water imbalances.
2. **Soil conditioning**: Mechanical and biological aeration of compacted soils.
3. **Fertilisation**: Transition from wasteful chemical inputs to biological nutrient cycling.
4. **Crop and plant species selection**: Choosing plants suited to site conditions that contribute to soil building.

## Water Control

Water is the primary agent of both soil degradation and soil recovery. Proper [[masanobu-fukuoka]] demonstrate patient and effective biological soil conditioning by casting out strong-rooted radish seed (daikon varieties), tree legume seed, and deep-rooted plants such as comfrey, lucerne, and Acacias, eventually followed by forest trees. The same subsurface shattering that mechanical implements achieve occurs slowly and noiselessly through root action. The soil regains structure, aeration, and permits water infiltration without the energy input and potential disruption of mechanical methods.

## Crop and Plant Species Selection

Many older varieties of both annual and perennial crops yield with less fertiliser and water than recently developed varieties. There is a growing trend among farmers and gardeners to preserve and cultivate these heritage varieties for multiple reasons:

- **Lower input requirements**: Older varieties often produce well under less-than-optimum conditions.
- **Flavour**: Many older apple varieties, such as Pippin and Russet types, are more flavourful than market varieties like Red Delicious.
- **Pest resistance**: Many heritage varieties have natural resistance to pests and diseases.
- **Nutritional value**: Older varieties frequently have higher concentrations of vitamins and minerals.

There are different species of plants that can live in almost any type of soil, starting the process of rehabilitation. So-called noxious weeds often colonise eroded landscapes, beginning a slow march towards stabilisation. These pioneer species can be used as mulches once cut, contributing organic matter and beginning the succession toward productive soil.

## Building Soil at Garden Scale

Gardeners build soil through three primary processes:

1. **Shaping the earth**: Raise or lower beds to facilitate watering or drainage, and carefully level bed surfaces where needed.
2. **Mixing amendments**: Incorporate compost or humus materials into the soil, and supply clay, sand, or nutrients to bring the soil to balance.
3. **Mulching**: Apply surface mulch to reduce water loss, moderate sun exposure, and prevent erosion.

Accessory systems include growing compost materials as hedgerows, herbs, or soft-leaf plots within or around the garden, and using trellis, shadecloth, glasshouses, and trickle irrigation to assist specific crops while regulating wind, light, and heat effects. By observing plant health, gardeners can continuously adjust the system for productive food production.

## Large-Scale Soil Rehabilitation

Large-scale systems on farms cannot be treated exactly as gardens unless they produce high-value products. Farmers create soils through broadscale drainage or soil conditioning. Most degraded soils are compacted, eroded, or waterlogged, requiring primary aeration by modern machines or biological agents, followed by careful plant and livestock management to keep the soil open and provided with humus.

Key biological agents for farm-scale rehabilitation include daikon radish for deep soil penetration, tree and shrub legumes for nitrogen fixation, earthworms for aeration and nutrient cycling, and rhizobia for root-associate nutrient transfer. The management of livestock for minimal compaction and controlled grazing is a critical part of soil building and preservation. Many organic farmers introduce specific worm species to pastures and sow deep-rooted chicory, radish, or comfrey as green manures.

## Setting Priorities

In any specific soil rehabilitation project, priorities should be set for erosion control on specific sites and slopes, with earthworks or planting sequences designed to establish soil stability first. If soil losses are allowed to continue or worsen, all other investments are at risk. The next stage is to assess the capacity of soils for dams, swales, foundations, or specific crops, which may require further analysis through test holes, auger borings, or soil pit inspection.

When a predetermined set of values based on soil and water conservation and appropriate use of sites is adopted, any site with its water lines and soil types noted starts to define itself in appropriate usages. This approach allows the landscape itself to guide design decisions, rather than imposing arbitrary land use patterns.
