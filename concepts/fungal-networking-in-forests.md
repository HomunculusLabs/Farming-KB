## Fungal Networking in Forests

Fungal networking in forests refers to the vast underground systems of mycelial connections that link trees, plants, and soil organisms into integrated communities. These networks -- sometimes called the "wood wide web" -- enable [[plant-hormones-and-chemical-signaling-systems]], and coordinated responses across entire forest ecosystems.

Paul Stamets describes mycelium as "Nature's Internet," a biological network predating any human communication technology by hundreds of millions of years. He sees mycelium as the living network that manifests the natural intelligence imagined by Gaia theorists.

## Evolutionary Origins

Animals are more closely related to fungi than to any other kingdom, sharing common ancestry more than 600 million years ago:

- Fungi evolved external digestion by secreting acids and enzymes into their environs
- They absorb nutrients using netlike cell chains
- Fungi marched onto land more than a billion years ago
- Many partnered with plants around 700 million years ago, enabling terrestrial plant life
- The mycelium retained its net-like form and went underground
- It formed the vast food web upon which terrestrial life flourished

The classic cap and stem mushrooms common today are descendants of varieties that predated the Cretaceous mass extinction 65 million years ago. After each mass extinction, fungi surged to recycle postcataclysmic debris fields.

## Resource Sharing Between Trees

Mycorrhizal networks enable trees to share resources across distances impossible for roots alone:

- Mycelium absorbs phosphorus and transports mineral salts over distance
- Nutrients are released when mushrooms rot or mycelium dies
- Bacteria absorb released phosphorus and redeposit it into the nutritional bank
- Trees growing on thin soil gather nutrients from afar through fungal partnerships
- Old-growth trees near Mount Rainier grow from thin soil thanks to distant fungal networks

In old-growth forests the network is extraordinarily dense. Most plants are supported by vast colonies of fungi working in concert. Mushrooms contribute phosphorus and other ecological benefits to riparian and forest ecosystems.

Mushrooms become launching platforms for bacterial populations critical for plant health. The network functions as a biological redistribution system, moving resources from surplus areas to areas of need.

## Chemical Communication and Sensing

Stamets describes mycelium as an "exposed sentient membrane, aware and responsive to changes in its environment":

- Organisms moving across the network leave impressions mycelia sense and respond to
- Chemical signals can travel between connected plants through the mycelial web
- Stressed plants may trigger defensive responses in neighbors through fungal connections
