## Fungal Carbon Storage in Soils

Fungi play a central role in soil carbon storage through multiple pathways including root-[[mycorrhizal-carbon-allocation]], extraradical hyphal biomass production, glomalin deposition, and [[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]]. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for predicting how soils will respond to elevated atmospheric CO₂ and climate change.

## Decomposition and Carbon Loss Under Warming

Elevated atmospheric temperatures accelerate saprotrophic activity, increasing decomposition rates and potentially releasing stored soil carbon:

- A 5°C temperature rise in boreal forests increased soil respiration losses of 439 g C m⁻² (birch) to 675 g C m⁻² (black spruce).
- Increased photosynthesis under warming effectively counteracted decomposition losses for paper birch (+23 g C m⁻²) and black spruce (+46 g C m⁻²), but white spruce showed a net loss of −129 g C m⁻² after 25 years.
- Soil temperature increases of 300–500 degree days were predicted across different boreal forest types.

## Root and Mycorrhizal Carbon Budgets

### Below-Ground Biomass Allocation

Root and mycorrhizal components are frequently overlooked in carbon accounting. Of 24 forest examples surveyed by Vogt (1991), only nine included both above- and below-ground biomass data. Below-ground biomass carbon ranged from:

- 16% of total tree biomass carbon in *Pinus menziesii*
- 64% in *Pinus elliottii*

Root respiration alone accounts for 35–62% of total soil respiration across species, with coniferous trees allocating approximately twice as much carbon to roots as deciduous species.

### Root Carbon Turnover

Fine roots of eastern US forest trees have a mean age of 3–18 years, with dead roots persisting 10–18 years. Carbon in organic soil horizons was fixed 3–8 years earlier, while mineral soil horizon carbon was fixed 11–18 years earlier, establishing root biomass and necromass as midterm carbon stores.

## Mycorrhizal Carbon Dynamics Under Elevated CO₂

### Extraradical Hyphal Responses

- Arbuscular mycorrhizal extraradical mycelium showed a five-fold increase under elevated CO₂ in experimental conditions.
- Along a natural CO₂ gradient near cold springs in New Zealand, mycorrhizal root colonization (r² = 0.47, P = 0.0016) and soil hyphal length (r² = 0.76, P < 0.0001) increased linearly with CO₂ concentration.
- However, elevated CO₂ did not always increase phosphorus uptake through expanded hyphal networks.

### Species-Specific Carbon Allocation

Carbon fate under elevated CO₂ depends on mycorrhizal fungal species:

- *[[suillus-bovinus]]* (N-poor specialist): Additional carbon translocated below ground into root and fungal biomass → increased nitrogen uptake → plant N content twice that of *Laccaria*-colonized trees.
- *[[laccaria-laccata]]* (N-rich specialist): Additional carbohydrate respired rather than stored → no biomass accumulation benefit.
- Different *Glomus*, *Acaulospora*, and *Scutellospora* species showed variable infection rates and extraradical hyphal growth under elevated CO₂.

### Long-Lived Ectomycorrhizal Mycelium

Ectomycorrhizal mycelium may resist microbial decomposition and enter the "slow pool" of soil carbon, potentially contributing to long-term carbon sequestration. This contrasts with arbuscular mycorrhizal hyphae, which turn over rapidly and contribute to soil respiration that may counter enhanced carbon fixation.

## Glomalin and Soil Carbon

The glycoprotein glomalin, produced by [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], represents a major soil carbon pool:

- Glomalin may account for 30–60% of carbon in undisturbed soils.
- Elevated CO₂ increased glomalin content in grassland soils, enhancing soil aggregate stability.
- This carbon is physically protected within soil aggregates and represents a potential long-term carbon sink.

### Soil Aggregate Formation

Soil aggregates physically protect carbon from rapid decomposition. In prairie restoration:

- Rate constant for aggregate formation was 35× that for bulk soil carbon accumulation.
- Time to reach 99% equilibrium: 10.5 years for aggregates vs. 384 years for whole soil.
- Aggregate carbon had low C:N ratios, indicating recent fungal and bacterial biomass origin.
- A significant fraction consists of fungal cell wall residues that bind microaggregates into macroaggregates.

### Carbon Partitioning in Restored Prairie

| Carbon Fraction | Under Corn | 4-Season Restoration | 10-Season Restoration | Virgin Prairie |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mineral-associated macroaggregate C | 1,181 | 2,548 | 3,348 | 4,692 |
| Intra-macroaggregate POM C | 77 | 131 | 138 | 250 |
| Macroaggregate associated C | 1,258 | 2,679 | 3,485 | 4,924 |
| Total C in aggregates <212 μm | 1,918 | 837 | 576 | 567 |
| Total C in whole soil | 3,517 | 3,996 | 4,733 | 6,106 |

Under elevated CO₂, tallgrass prairie showed increased soil C and N stocks, with heavy particulate organic matter accumulating over time while light POM turned over more rapidly.

## Exotic Plantations and Carbon Loss

The assumption that [[plantation-forestry-carbon-dynamics]] creates carbon sinks may be incorrect for exotic tree species:

- Radiata pine plantations in Ecuador showed greater carbon loss from soil than photosynthetic gain, with 30% loss of former paramo grassland soil carbon during the first 20 years of rotation.
- Only three dominant ectomycorrhizal species (*Suillus luteus*, *[[thelephora-terrestris]]*, *Rhizopogon vulgaris*) were found, compared to ~100 species in the tree's natural range.
- Prolific fruiting (1,200 kg dry weight ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹) contributed to respiratory carbon loss as these fungi metabolized organic forms of nutrients and non-host carbon.

## Land-Use Change and Soil Carbon

Land-use changes in England and Wales between 1984–1990 led to a predicted net loss of ~106,747 × 10³ t C by 2020, with losses from permanent grass, upland grass, bog, and ley systems exceeding gains from arable, coniferous, and deciduous cover. This loss, combined with temperature-driven increased soil respiration, could create a positive feedback loop exacerbating global warming.

## Agricultural Practices and Carbon Sequestration

No-till and minimal-till practices increase soil carbon stocks by 3.2–4.7 t C ha⁻¹ in 10 years. As soil aggregates are larger and more stable in no-till systems, understanding fungal contributions to aggregate formation and carbon protection could improve sustainable agricultural practices and global carbon fixation.

## Fast vs. Slow Carbon Pools

The balance between carbon entering "slow" turnover pools (protected in aggregates, glomalin, resistant fungal biomass) and "fast" turnover pools (labile root exudates, rapidly decomposing hyphae) determines overall soil carbon accretion. AM fungi producing glomalin and contributing to aggregate formation may be particularly important for shifting carbon into protected pools.

The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.

Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
