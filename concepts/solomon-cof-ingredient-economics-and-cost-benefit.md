# COF Ingredient Economics and Cost-Benefit Analysis

## The Complete Organic Fertilizer Recipe

Solomon's Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) is a blended [[azomite-mineral-amendment]]
designed to provide balanced nutrition to [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]] without relying on
compost alone. The standard recipe for a large garden producing significant
food consists of approximately three sacks of seed meal (cottonseed, soybean,
or canola meal) as the primary nitrogen source, two sacks of kelp meal for
trace minerals and micronutrients, one to two sacks of high-phosphate Peruvian
guano as a phosphorus source, 25 pounds of agricultural lime, 25 pounds of
gypsum, and 50 pounds of dolomitic lime for [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]]. This
quantity is intended to fertilize a substantial garden area for an entire
growing season. The exact proportions can be adjusted based on soil test
results, but the general framework remains consistent across different garden
situations.
## Cost Analysis at 2005 Prices

Solomon provides a detailed cost breakdown of the COF ingredients at mid-2000s
prices. The total annual cost for all COF ingredients runs approximately $300
USD for a garden large enough to produce a meaningful portion of a family's
food supply. This $300 investment, when applied to a well-managed garden of
1000 to 2000 square feet, produces vegetables with an estimated retail
replacement value of roughly $4,000. This represents a return on investment of
approximately 13 to 1, making home [[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]] with COF one of the
most economically efficient food [[hashish-production-methods]] available to the home
gardener.
## Ingredient Availability and Substitution

One of Solomon's practical strengths is his discussion of ingredient
substitution when specific products are unavailable. Seed meal can be sourced
from cottonseed, soybean, canola, or other oilseed processing byproducts. The
key criterion is protein content, which correlates with [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]].
If seed meal is unavailable, alfalfa meal or blood meal can serve as
substitutes, though at higher cost per unit of nitrogen. Kelp meal is harder
to substitute because of its unique trace mineral profile, but Solomon
suggests that a combination of other organic trace mineral sources can
partially compensate. Peruvian guano can be replaced with rock phosphate or
bone meal, though the [[phosphorus-availability]] differs significantly between
these sources.
## Bulk Purchasing Strategies

Solomon strongly recommends purchasing COF ingredients in bulk quantities
rather than in small bags from garden centers. The price per pound drops
dramatically when buying 50-pound sacks from feed stores or agricultural
suppliers compared to 5-pound bags from retail outlets. He notes that many
gardeners are put off by the large upfront outlay for bulk ingredients but
fail to calculate the per-season cost, which is actually quite reasonable when
amortized over the garden's productive life. The bulk ingredients also store
well if kept dry, so a two-year supply can be purchased at once to take
advantage of favorable pricing.
## COF vs. Compost-Only Approaches

A central theme in Solomon's economics discussion is the comparison between
COF-based fertilization and compost-only approaches. While compost provides
valuable [[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] some nutrients, Solomon argues that it cannot
supply adequate minerals for [[intensive-vegetable-spacing-systems]] production unless the compost
itself is made from mineral-rich materials. Most home compost is made from
kitchen scraps and yard waste, which are inherently deficient in key minerals
like calcium, phosphorus, [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]]. COF fills these gaps at modest
cost. The gardener who relies solely on compost will eventually see declining
yields and [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] as soil minerals are depleted by harvest and
leaching.
## Scaling COF for Different Garden Sizes

Solomon provides scaling guidance for different garden sizes, from small
backyard plots to substantial homestead gardens. The per-square-foot cost
remains roughly constant across scales, but the absolute cost and the
practicality of bulk purchasing change significantly. For very small gardens
under 200 square feet, the fixed costs of purchasing multiple ingredients make
COF relatively more expensive per unit of production, and Solomon suggests
that compost-based approaches may be more practical at this scale. For gardens
over 500 square feet, COF becomes increasingly economical and the bulk
purchasing strategy works well.
