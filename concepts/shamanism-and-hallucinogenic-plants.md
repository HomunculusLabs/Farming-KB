# Shamanism and Hallucinogenic Plants

## Overview

Shamanism represents the oldest known human tradition of working with
hallucinogenic plants for healing, divination, and the exploration of
nonordinary states of consciousness. Both Terence McKenna and Ralph Metzner —
drawing on decades of fieldwork, anthropological literature, and personal
experience — emphasized that shamanism is fundamentally a set of techniques
rather than merely ritual, and that indigenous hallucinogenic plant use reflects
a profound knowledge of pharmacology developed over millennia. The shamanic
worldview radically diverges from Western materialism in two key respects: the
recognition of [[metzner-spirit-beings-multiple-realities-entheogens]] ("worlds") accessible in expanded states of
consciousness, and the belief that spirits encountered in dreams and visions are
as real as physical organisms.

## Shamanism as Technique

McKenna stressed that "shamanism is primarily techniques, not ritual." It is a
set of methods worked out over millennia that make it possible for people of
predilection to explore nonordinary dimensions of consciousness. In archaic
societies, signs of shamanic potential include:

- Oddness or uniqueness in an individual
- Epilepsy (often considered a signature in preliterate societies)
- Survival of an unusual ordeal — lightning strikes, near-fatal illness, or
  extended periods in an indeterminate zone between life and death
- Hypersensitivity to trance states

The distinguishing characteristic McKenna observed among shamans worldwide was
"extraordinary centeredness." Despite the anthropological literature presenting
shamans as embedded in tradition, he found them to be intellectually
sophisticated about their practice — "the true phenomenologists of this world"
who understand plant chemistry but call the energy fields "spirits."

## The Shaman's Social Position

Shamans occupy a paradoxical social position. In traditional societies, they are:
- Not permitted political power, because they are considered sacred
- Present at council meetings but returning afterward to their hut at the edge
  of the village
- Peripheral to ordinary social life in every sense
- Called upon in crisis — illness, psychological difficulty, marital quarrels,
  theft, weather prediction, or death

Metzner confirmed that the role of the guide or healer is "always described as
central and essential" in entheogenic rituals. This must be a person with
extensive personal experience who provides initiatory experiences to seekers or
training to apprentices. In virtually all entheogenic rituals, the guide or
shaman does the singing, which "profoundly shapes the quality and content of the
experience."

## Shamanic Healing Modalities

Metzner identified three main variations of shamanic healing:
1. **Extraction**: Removal of a toxin implanted through sorcery
2. **Soul retrieval**: Recovery of a split-off psychic fragment
3. **Dismemberment and reconstitution**: Destruction followed by reconstitution
   with a healthier, stronger "body"

These healing modalities operate on physical, psychic, and spiritual levels
simultaneously; traditional healers do not make analytic distinctions between them.

## Divination and the Plant Teacher Concept
Shamanic divination — the equivalent of Western "diagnosis" — determines the
cause of illness and appropriate remedy through visionary states. Indigenous
healers refer to entheogenic plants as "plant teachers," recognizing an
intelligence associated with the medicine that communicates interiorly.

## Traditional Entheogenic Ceremonies

Metzner documented key ceremonial forms across cultures:

**Peyote ceremonies** ([[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] Church, Huichol): Participants sit around
a central fire in a tipi; ceremonies last all night with a roadman, drummer,
firekeeper, and cedar-man; rapid rhythmic peyote songs are sung continuously.

**[[metzner-mazatec-mushroom-veladas-darkness-singing-and-christian-syncretism]]** (Mazatec, Mexico): Participants sit or lie in near-total
darkness with a small candle; the healer sings almost uninterrupted throughout
the night, weaving Christian saints, spirit allies, and nature spirits into
chants.

**[[metzner-amazonian-ayahuasca-ceremonies-icaros-and-healing-methods]]** (Amazon): Small groups sit in semidarkness while
healers sing *icaros* — songs with rapid rhythmic pulse that keep the visionary
experience moving; shamanic "sucking" methods extract toxic psychic residues.

**San Pedro ceremonies** (Andes): Around a fire or at an altar with figurines
representing light and dark spirits.

**Bwiti ceremonies** (Gabon/Zaire, iboga): Temples with ancestral images;
initiates daubed with white mud; mirrors allow them to "see" ancestral spirits.

### Common Ceremonial Features
- Minimal talking among participants during the experience
- Singing or chanting considered essential to success
- Ceremonies are almost always conducted in darkness or low light
- Small groups (typically 6–12 participants)

## Multiple Worlds and Spirit Beings

The two elements of shamanic cosmology most radically challenging to the
Western worldview are:

1. **Multiple worlds**: "Inner world," "spirit world," "upper/lower world,"
   "dreamtime," or "otherworld." Access may come through journeying or by
   seeing into the spirit world while remaining aware of ordinary reality.

2. **Spirit beings**: Nonmaterial entities associated with particular animals,
   plants, trees, places, deceased ancestors, or nonordinary entities such as
   "extraterrestrials or elves." Healing is experienced as done by or with
   the assistance of such spirits, also called "allies," "power animals,"
   "guardians," or "helpers."

## The Word "Spirit" as Technical Language

McKenna noted that Westerners hear "spirit" through "narrowing declensions of
meaning" that obscure its original richness. Shamans use "spirit" as a
technical gloss for a complicated concept — the way a quantum physicist uses
"charm."

McKenna concluded that the absence of a living shamanic tradition in modern
Western culture represents a profound loss. The tragedy is that "we have no
shamanic tradition" to guide exploration of these states.

> Our normal waking consciousness, rational consciousness as we call it, is but
> one special type of consciousness, whilst all about it, parted from it by the
> filmiest of screens, there lie potential forms of consciousness entirely
> different. No account of the universe in its totality can be final which
> leaves these other forms of consciousness quite disregarded.
