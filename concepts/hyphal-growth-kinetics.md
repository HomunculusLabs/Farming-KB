## Temperature Effects on Growth Rate

Temperature is the major factor influencing the rate of mycelial growth.
Studies on [[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]] have demonstrated
distinct temperature optima for hyphal extension, with growth rates declining
sharply above and below the optimum range. Different mushroom species have
characteristic temperature ranges:

- Agaricus species typically favor 75 to 77 degrees F for spawn running

- Tropical species generally require higher incubation temperatures

The temperature within a spawn jar or substrate is typically several degrees
higher than the surrounding air temperature due to metabolic heat generated
by the growing mycelium. This must be accounted for when designing
incubation environments, particularly when jars or containers are densely
packed.

## Nutrient Effects on Growth

The availability and type of carbon and nitrogen sources significantly
affect hyphal growth rates. Glucose and other readily metabolized sugars
promote rapid extension but can also trigger catabolite repression of
enzyme systems. Cellulose and more complex carbon sources support slower
but more sustained growth.

Nitrogen availability is particularly important for white rot fungi. High
nitrogen levels suppress the ligninolytic enzyme system while promoting
rapid vegetative growth. Low nitrogen conditions trigger secondary
metabolism and expression of lignin-degrading enzymes, which is why
nitrogen limitation is often used to induce pollutant degradation in
bioremediation applications.

## Water Potential and Growth

Water potential affects hyphal extension in all environments. Different
fungal species show varying tolerances to water stress. Some wood-decomposing
fungi in the ascomycete order Sphaeriales, including Xylaria and Hypoxylon,
can grow at water potentials as low as 10 to 12 MPa, explaining their
successful colonization of dry lignocellulose resources. Most cultivated
mushroom species require much higher water potentials for optimal growth.
