## The Nitrogen Cycle in Aquaponics

The biological engine of every aquaponics system is the nitrogen cycle, mediated
by nitrifying bacteria that convert toxic fish waste into plant-available
nutrients. Fish excrete ammonia (NH3) primarily through their gills, with
additional ammonia produced by the decomposition of uneaten food and fecal matter.
Ammonia is highly toxic to fish at concentrations above 0.5 ppm, depending on
temperature and pH.

Nitrosomonas bacteria oxidize ammonia to nitrite (NO2-), also toxic to fish
above 0.5 ppm. Nitrobacter and Nitrospira bacteria then oxidize nitrite to
nitrate (NO3-), which is relatively non-toxic and serves as the primary
nitrogen source for plants. This two-step process requires dissolved oxygen,
alkalinity (carbonate hardness), and surface area for bacterial colonization.

Nitrifying bacteria are slow-growing and sensitive to environmental conditions.
Optimal temperature is 25-30°C, with activity declining sharply below 15°C.
They require pH between 7.0-8.5. System cycling — establishing sufficient
bacterial populations — typically takes 4-6 weeks, during which fish are added
gradually or ammonia is added artificially to feed developing colonies.

## System Configurations

Aquaponics systems are classified by their hydroponic component into several
configurations. Deep water culture (DWC), also called raft systems, float plant
roots directly in nutrient-rich water. Plants are supported by Styrofoam rafts
on the water surface, with roots dangling freely below. DWC systems provide
excellent plant growth rates and are the simplest to build and operate, but
require large water volumes and careful oxygen management to prevent root
suffocation.

## Plant Selection and Nutrition

Leafy greens and herbs are the most successful aquaponics crops due to moderate
nutrient needs and short growing cycles. Lettuce, basil, mint, watercress, and
kale thrive and harvest in 4-6 weeks from transplanting. Fruiting crops —
tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers — have higher phosphorus and potassium demands that
may exceed fish waste output. Potassium, calcium, and iron are typically
deficient and must be supplemented via potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide,
and chelated iron (Fe-DTPA).
