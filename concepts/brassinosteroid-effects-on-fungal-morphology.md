# Brassinosteroid Effects on Fungal Morphology

The 1990 study by Gartz, Adam, and Vorbrodt documented not only growth
promotion but also a striking morphological alteration in [[psilocybe-cubensis]]
fruiting bodies treated with 22S,23S-homobrassinolide. The treated mushrooms
consistently lacked the typical membranous annulus on their stems, a
diagnostic feature of the species. This observation provides evidence that
brassinosteroids influence fungal developmental gene expression rather than
merely accelerating growth rate.

## The Annulus as a Developmental Landmark

The membranous annulus (also called a veil ring or partial veil remnant) is
a structure formed during the development of agaric mushrooms. As the
cap expands from the button stage, the partial veil that originally
connected the cap margin to the stem ruptures and typically remains as a
ring of tissue on the stem. In Psilocybe cubensis, this annulus is a
characteristic taxonomic feature used for species identification.

The formation of the annulus involves precisely timed developmental
processes including differential cell expansion, tissue thinning, and
programmed structural weakening at specific zones. The fact that
brassinosteroid treatment abolished this structure suggests that the
hormone interferes with one or more of these developmental programs.

## Morphological Change Versus Growth Acceleration

The distinction between morphological change and growth acceleration is
biologically significant. Growth promotion involves quantitative changes
in the rate or extent of biomass accumulation. Morphological alteration
implies qualitative changes in the pattern of development, suggesting
that brassinosteroids affect gene regulatory networks controlling
tissue differentiation in fungi.

In the Gartz study, both types of effects were observed simultaneously:
mycelial growth increased two to three times (quantitative), and the
annulus failed to form (qualitative). This dual action indicates that
brassinosteroids interact with multiple targets in fungal cells, affecting
both metabolic pathways that drive growth and developmental pathways
that control tissue patterning.

## Suppression of Incomplete Fruiting Bodies

Beyond the annulus effect, the study noted that the formation of
incomplete fruiting bodies was "almost completely suppressed" by
brassinosteroid treatment. In mushroom cultivation, incomplete or
abortive fruitbodies represent a significant source of yield loss, as
primordia may initiate development but fail to mature into harvestable
mushrooms.

The suppression of abortive fruitbodies could be interpreted in several
ways. Brassinosteroids may improve the efficiency of nutrient allocation
to developing primordia, ensuring that initiated fruitbodies receive
sufficient resources to complete development. Alternatively, the hormone
may strengthen the developmental commitment of primordia, reducing the
likelihood that initiated structures will arrest. A third possibility
is that brassinosteroids improve the synchrony of fruiting body
development, reducing competition between simultaneously developing
primordia.

## Comparison with Hormone-Induced Morphological Changes in Other Fungi

The observation of hormone-induced morphological changes in fungi has
precedents in other hormonal systems, though primarily with fungal
hormones rather than plant hormones:

**Fungal Sex Hormones:** In species such as Achlya and other
oomycetes, steroidal sex hormones (antheridiol and oogoniol) control
the formation of sexual reproductive structures. These fungal sterols
are structurally related to brassinosteroids, suggesting possible
mechanistic parallels in steroid perception across kingdoms.

**Gibberellin Effects:** Gibberellins, another class of plant hormones
originally discovered as fungal metabolites (from Gibberella fujikuroi),
can affect fungal growth patterns when applied exogenously. This
cross-kingdom hormonal activity parallels the brassinosteroid effect
documented by Gartz and colleagues.

## Implications for [[fruiting-body-development]] Biology

The absence of the annulus in brassinosteroid-treated P. cubensis raises
interesting questions about the regulation of fruiting body development
in basidiomycete fungi. Fruiting body morphogenesis is a complex process
involving the coordinated differentiation of multiple tissue types
including pileipellis (cap skin), hymenophore (gill tissue), stipe
(stem), and veil tissues.

The specific loss of the annulus while other structures (cap, gills,
stem) developed normally suggests that brassinosteroids may selectively
affect the developmental program governing partial veil tissue. This
selectivity could indicate that veil formation is regulated by a distinct
genetic pathway that is particularly sensitive to brassinosteroid
signaling, or that the timing of veil development overlaps with a
brassinosteroid-sensitive window of morphogenetic competence.

## Possible Mechanisms of Morphological Action

Several mechanisms could explain how brassinosteroids alter fungal
morphology:

**Membrane Effects:** As steroidal compounds, brassinosteroids may
incorporate into fungal cell membranes and alter membrane fluidity or
receptor function, indirectly affecting morphogenetic signaling cascades
that operate through membrane-localized components.

**Receptor-Mediated Signaling:** Fungi may possess receptors that
recognize brassinosteroid molecules, triggering intracellular signaling
pathways that intersect with endogenous developmental programs. The
structural similarity between brassinosteroids and fungal sterols
(ergosterol-derived compounds) makes receptor cross-reactivity plausible.

**Transcriptional Regulation:** Brassinosteroids could influence the
expression of genes involved in tissue patterning during fruiting body
development. In plants, brassinosteroids are known to regulate hundreds
of genes through the BZR/BES1 transcription factor family; analogous
regulatory networks may exist in fungi.

**Metabolic Crosstalk:** The brassinosteroid molecule or its metabolites
could interact with fungal endogenous hormone systems (such as those
involving mating pheromones or other sterol-derived signals), producing
altered developmental outcomes through hormonal pathway crosstalk.

## Significance for Taxonomic Identification

The loss of the annulus has practical implications for taxonomic
identification of treated mushrooms. Since the annulus is a key
diagnostic character for Psilocybe cubensis and related species,
brassinosteroid-treated specimens could potentially be misidentified
if reliance is placed solely on macromorphological features. This
observation underscores the importance of considering cultivation
conditions and any applied growth substances when using morphological
characters for species identification.
