# Forest-Atmosphere Interactions and the Hydrological Cycle

## Overview

Forests exert profound and complex influences on the atmosphere, rainfall, wind, humidity, temperature, and the entire hydrological cycle. [[bill-mollison]] described these interactions as "very, very complicated — not simple," emphasizing that the full scope of forest-atmosphere dynamics is still poorly understood by science. What is clear, however, is that forests are among the most powerful regulators of climate and water on Earth, and their removal has catastrophic consequences that extend far beyond the loss of trees themselves.

## Wind and the Forest

### Wind Absorption

Wind completely disappears in an effective forest within approximately 1,000 meters (about 3,280 feet). The forest absorbs the total force of even gale-force winds, except at the crown level where winds continue to have some effect. The energy of the wind is converted within the forest — Mollison hypothesized it may become wood, noting that anchored trees maintain constant stem diameter while exposed trees rapidly increase theirs, suggesting wind aids transpiration, pumping, or cell production.

### Rainfall Enhancement

The forest forces approximately 60% of the wind upward. This upward deflection creates:
- **High pressure on the windward side**: Decreased evaporation, and the windward face catches significantly more rain than the leeward side
- **Increased rainfall**: Measured increases of 15-20% have been documented in Holland and Sweden
- **Sidewise spiraling**: Creates belts of rain across the wind direction, producing wet-dry patterns at intervals of approximately five tree lengths past the forest edge

When forests are cut, regional rainfall decreases by 10% to 30%. The descending winds past a forest are warmer, less humid, and turbulent, causing drying on the leeward side.

### Windbreak Design

For effective windbreaks:
- **Width**: About five trees wide is needed for full effectiveness
- **Penetrability**: A single belt at 40% penetrability has measurable effect
- **Distance**: Wind diminishes rapidly within 100 to 200 meters of a tree belt at 40% penetration, becoming negligible
- **Trimming**: Trimming tree belts can create wind tunnels below the canopy — it is generally better not to trim windbreaks
- **Hedge rows**: Should reach the ground (or start above a stone wall) to prevent wind tunneling

## Humidity Interception

### Positive Interception

On foggy nights with humid air moving into a forest, the forest reduces humidity in the air by about 50% within 100 meters. This is called positive interception and represents a major factor for all coastal forests and ridges within fifty miles of the coast. The moisture precipitates out rapidly on the myriad leaf surfaces of individual trees, which collectively represent many acres of leaf area per tree.

In Tasmania, up to 60% of total precipitation is attributed to this effect, with only 14% falling as rain — trees catch the remaining 86%. Screens erected to imitate trees create similar high precipitation, confirming the mechanism.

### Evaporation from Canopy

The canopy absorbs almost all the energy of falling rain. Even a violent thunderstorm enters a forest in leaf as nothing but a fine mist. Tons of water and thousands of pounds of kinetic energy dissipate in the crown. This means:
- No erosion from pelting rain within forests
- In light rain, no water reaches the ground at all — roads under trees remain dry
- The absorbed water evaporates from the crown, creating a profound cooling effect

## Precipitation Nucleation

### Organic and Inorganic Particles

Forests interact dramatically with airborne particles:
- **Inorganic particles** (dust, industrial pollutants) are reduced to about a quarter of their previous concentration after passing through 100 meters of forest
- **Organic particles** (pollens, bacteria, oil droplets) are released by forests in enormous quantities

Early voyagers approaching North America in spring reported pollen coating ship decks up to 80 miles out in the Atlantic. The sea turned yellow, and crews mistook the pollen for sulfur from volcanic eruptions, advancing with trepidation toward "yellow skies."

Organic particles are far more effective precipitation nuclei than inorganic particles. Forests absorb tons of inorganic materials while releasing tons of organic materials. About 60% of inland rain falls from forest clouds, not sea clouds.

### Mineral Cycling

There is likely no shortage of any mineral or element anywhere on Earth, because everything is on the move — particularly off seacoasts. Forests net minerals from the air. While minerals may be used and fixed as fast as they are netted, this atmospheric deposition is a real and significant pathway for nutrient input to forest ecosystems.

## The Forest as a Water Mass

### Water Content of Forests

Eighty-six percent of a forest's mass is water. Ninety-six percent of its leaves and twigs are water. This represents an enormous weight on the Earth — effectively, the forest is a collection of vertical water tanks. The implications of this mass are profound:

- **Geological effects**: Loading and unloading the crust of the Earth through forest removal can cause Earth movements, similar to how modest dams cause local earthquakes
- **Branch failure**: On still, misty nights when trees cannot transpire, the enormous weight of leaf water can smash branches down without warning
- **Water storage**: The forest functions as a massive water storage system distributed across the landscape

### Soil Water Retention

Water entering the forest floor follows a complex retention cascade:

1. **Litter and humus absorption**: No water seeps down until the forest floor fully charges
2. **Mineral soil absorption**: Every foot of soil holds approximately one inch of rain
3. **Percolation through root channels**: Forest soils are "totally bored out" by old rotted roots, forming conduits to deeper levels

With 30 inches of soil depth (minimal for many forests), a 3-inch rainfall produces zero surface runoff. Sometimes up to 60 inches of rain can be held. Water percolates easily through old root traces. Evaporation from the soil surface below forests is essentially zero because deep roots draw water downward.

### Forest Floor Hydrology

On the forest floor:
- **No straight runoff**: Twigs, leaves, and debris accumulate in immense quantities, creating impediments that repeatedly halt water flow
- **Slow transit**: Water performs "weird convolutions" getting through the forest — its time on landscape is vastly greater than in open ground
- **Downward movement only**: Water entering the forest travels downward, and only returns upward as pure water released to the atmosphere through transpiration

## Prevention of Salinization

Because water never travels upward to the soil surface for evaporation within a forest, there is no upward migration of salts. Salts that enter trees as essential nutrients are fixed in the forest biomass. When forests are cut, even if streams continue to run clear, they contain enormous amounts of dissolved salts — particularly calcium. Measurements in Tasmania showed more tonnage running off cut-over forest land as dissolved salts than as actual silts.

## Snow Retention

Forests catch and hold snow, delaying melt by at least a month compared to open ground. Without forest cover, winter snow melts extremely quickly on pastures and open ground, causing sudden flooding. The forest effectively takes all winter precipitation accumulated as snow and ice, holds it, and releases it slowly over a much longer period.

## Light and Energy Interactions

### Individual Tree Responses

Trees cannot be treated as a mass — they are collections of individuals that do individual things with light:
- **Sumac**: On a sunny day with light wind, it switches from being a light absorber to a reflector, changing its entire energy balance
- **Aspen**: Uses wind as an energy basis and has an orientation system for solar energy
- **Ivy**: Continuously adjusts its orientation surface relative to sunlight
- **Multi-surfaced leaves**: Some trees have shiny underleaves with matte tops, performing wind-energy trade-offs

### Light Interception

In some Tasmanian forests, light cannot be measured at depth — there is total interception. You must carry torches even in brilliant daylight. The forest intercepts ultraviolet light and passes through more red light, creating a different quality of light within. Dark trees become radiators (heat stores), while birches act as reflectors.

The heat storage system operates at 86% water heat storage. Even on very cold nights, thick forests produce warm downdrafts — cold air enters the upper canopy, passes slowly through thousands of enormous water storages that absorbed heat all day, and descends as warmed air.

### Air Quality

The quality of air moving through forests changes markedly:
- **Negative ions increase sharply**: Negative ions are excellent precipitators, causing particle clumping and precipitation. They can remove cigarette smoke from large rooms efficiently.
- **Gas absorption**: A relatively small plant in an office can handle all CO₂ and much of the CO problems
- **Carbon monoxide absorption**: Forests absorb many gases that are obnoxious to humans

## Root Systems and Soil

At least 40% of a tree's mass is below the surface. Foresters' estimates of forest weight typically account for wood volume but neglect roots, which are:
- **Enormous storage organs**: Busy performing many soil functions
- **Dynamic**: Roots throw masses toward the surface and pull them back, while sending others deeper; they live and die within the soil, leaving channels and pathways
- **Rock-breakers**: Tree roots break down primary rock material
- **Nutrient cyclers**: They fix minerals brought down from the atmosphere and cycle them through the tree

## The Consequences of Deforestation

Mollison stated with certainty:

> "By the removal of ridge forests alone, we can produce deserts in any climate. By the removal of forests alone, we can remove soils."

> "I am certain that the removal of the forest has been the main cause of the collapse of nations. Because when the forests go, they just haven't the water, the soil, or the climate quality to sustain human life thereafter."

These statements reflect the integrated understanding that forests govern water, soil, climate, and atmosphere in ways that are interconnected and self-reinforcing. Removing forests sets in motion cascading failures across all these systems simultaneously. The lesson is not how to live without forests, but how to live with them.

## Tree Establishment Techniques

### The Nuclei Method

Two elderly women near Sydney re-established 1,500 acres of native Australian bush in a badly managed forest using only spoons and blunt table knives, working from age 68 to 75. Their method — the nuclei approach — is the most important principle in forest establishment:

1. **Don't scatter**: Planting individual elements across a large area is extremely slow and many species will perish without their associates
2. **Create dense nuclei**: Plant small areas (half a room size) very densely, with all desired species
3. **Close out exotics**: Remove unwanted species from within the nuclei
4. **Patch disturbed soil**: Use mulch to repair any disturbance
5. **Expand outward**: Work from the perimeter of established nuclei — the ecology helps itself expand
6. **Begin where desired species already exist**: Start with remaining patches of natives

The same approach was used by Marjorie Spear (over 80 years old) in reverse — she expanded an exotic [[food-forest]] into degraded native forest using densely planted nuclei.

### Pioneer Species

Pioneer species are trees that will stand alone in grassland or hostile areas. They share common characteristics:
- They don't mind grass competition
- They are very hardy and drought resistant
- They change soil nature toward forest soils
- They range from acacias (nitrogen fixers, hundreds of species) to eastern and western red cedars

The strategy is to run pioneer species across land while planning for the desired final forest. When ready, pioneers can be cut down as manurial crop for the succeeding forest. Both western and eastern red cedar are useful as crops while pioneering and reducing competitors before forest establishment.

### Nutrient Amendments

In sandy soils, basic nutrients may need to be added — particularly on acid or alkaline soils. Superphosphate in sands and dunes can produce three to four times normal growth rates from a single handful per tree, though additional superphosphate provides no further benefit. In general, pH adjustment is more often the issue than missing elements — except in sands, where zinc, iron, and other minerals are locked up by high calcium.
