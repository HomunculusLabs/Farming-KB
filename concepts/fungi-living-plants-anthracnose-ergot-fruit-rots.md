# Fungi on Living Plants — Anthracnose, Ergot, and Fruit Rots

Fungi that infect living plant tissues — leaves, stems, flowers, and fruits — include some of the most destructive plant pathogens known. They range from biotrophs that can only grow on living tissue to facultative saprobes that kill tissue then sporulate.

## Obligate vs. Facultative Biotrophs

### Obligate Biotrophs
- Cannot be cultured away from living host
- Obtain nutrients only from living cells via haustoria
- Examples: rusts, powdery mildews, downy mildews

### Facultative Biotrophs
- Cause disease in living plants but can sporulate after plant tissue dies
- Often cultured easily in vitro
- Sporulation occurs in dead tissue islands or after host death
- Symptoms can be confused with obligate biotrophs

## Anthracnose (Colletotrichum / Glomerella)

### The Pathogens
- **Glomerella** (teleomorph, Phyllachoraceae, Ascomycota)
- **Colletotrichum** (anamorph): 39 species + numerous subspecies
- **G. cingulata / C. gloeosporioides:** Most common tropical pathogen

### Hosts and Impact
Causes anthracnose on avocado, citrus, mango, papaya, passion fruit, guava, cocoa, rubber, and many other wild and cultivated plants. One of the most important causes of preharvest and postharvest disease worldwide.

### Symptoms
- Sunken, irregular-shaped necrotic lesions on fruits, leaves, and stems
- Lesions may be raised, cankerous, scablike, or wartlike
- Branch die-back
- Mucilaginous masses of pink/salmon-colored conidia oozing from acervuli under humid conditions

### Identification
- Acervuli with dark brown setae
- Hyaline, aseptate conidia
- Appressoria produced on germination (induced by placing conidia in water drop on slide, moist chamber overnight)
- Glomerella teleomorph production inconsistent in culture

## Ergot (Claviceps — Clavicipitaceae)

### The Pathogen
Clavicipitaceae (Hypocreales, Ascomycota): 27 genera, mostly systemic endophytes or insect parasites. Claviceps contains ~35 species worldwide.

### Life Cycle
1. Infects grass ovaries, replacing seed with dark sclerotium (same size as grain)
2. Sclerotia overwinter, germinate to produce stromata with perithecia
3. Ascospores infect new grass flowers during bloom
4. Honeydew stage: conidial exudation (can be parasitized by Cerebella and Fusarium)

### Notable Species
- **C. purpurea:** Ergot of rye and other grasses — causes ergotism (toxic alkaloids) in humans and mammals ingesting contaminated grain

### Collection Challenges
- Sclerotia may need ripening periods up to 9 months
- Hyperparasites (Cerebella, Fusarium) can inhibit sclerotium development
- Cerebella fruiting bodies can serve as indicators of cryptic early Claviceps infections

## Fruit Rot Pathogens

### Major Genera
- **Monilinia:** Brown rot of stone fruits; blossom blights; "mummy" fruits (dried, shriveled fruits remaining attached)
- **Botrytis:** Gray mold; blossom blights; pre- and post-harvest rots
- **Aspergillus:** Aspergillus rot; mycotoxin production
- **Colletotrichum:** Anthracnose fruit rot
- **Dothiorella, Guignardia, Phytophthora, Rhizopus:** Additional fruit rot agents

### Blossom Blights
- Discoloration, shriveling, wilting, premature flower drop
- Predominant agents: Botrytis, Monilinia
- Often same fungi that cause fruit rots later

### Scab Diseases
- Roughened, crustlike diseased areas on fruits/nuts
- Tropical: Elsinoe / Sphaceloma anamorphs (Dothideales)
- Temperate: Venturia species
- Wide host range including avocado, cashew, castor, papaw, soybean (tropics) and Rosaceae (temperate)

## Gall-Forming Fungi

### Fungal Galls
- **Apiosporina morbosa:** Prunus galls in temperate climates
- **Phomopsis species:** Galls on woody temperate angiosperms
- **Nectriella pironii, Sphaeropsis tumefaciens:** Galls on woody and herbaceous plants in subtropics
- **Cyttaria:** Parasitic discomycete on Nothofagus branches — 12 south-temperate species; 3 groups by geography, host range

### Chytrid Galls
- **Synchytrium:** ~120 species worldwide; infect algae, mosses, ferns, flowering plants
- >1,350 host species in >800 genera, 165 families
- S. macrosporum infects >1,300 species under greenhouse conditions
- Galls: scattered to large/compound; some brightly colored
- Identification primarily by host determination
