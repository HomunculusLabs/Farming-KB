# Fungal Soil Crust Communities and Primary Production

## Overview

Fungal soil crust communities, encompassing [[lichens]], free-living fungi,
and their associated bacterial and algal partners, represent a
significant but often overlooked component of primary production in
many ecosystems. These communities are particularly important in cold,
arid, and nutrient-poor environments where vascular plant growth is
limited. Through photosynthesis by their photobiont partners and the
ecosystem engineering activities of their fungal components, soil crust
communities contribute to carbon fixation, nitrogen inputs, soil
formation, and [[dighton-fungal-roles-soil-erosion-prevention]] across diverse habitats.

## Community Composition

### Biodiversity of Soil Crust Fungi

Soil crusts are formed by diverse assemblages of organisms including
mosses, [[lichens]], free-living fungi, green and blue-green algae
(Cyanobacteria), and other bacteria. States and Christensen (2001)
identified 33 species of fungi associated with the [[lichens]], bryophytes,
and graminoids of surface crusts in semidesert grassland ecosystems of
Utah and Wyoming. These included a new species of basidiomycetes, five
new loculoascomycetes, and three mitosporic species not previously
identified from soil crust communities.

### Mat-Forming Lichens

Mat-forming [[lichens]] (Cladonia, Cetraria, Stereocaulon, and Alectora)
are major components of plant biomass in cold, wet environments and
arid ecosystems. These [[lichens]] grow in elevated microsites of boreal
Arctic peatlands, growing upward and dying off at the base. The dead
bases input considerable organic matter into developing soil
(Crittenden, 1991, 2000). These [[lichens]] form up to 60% of winter food
for caribou and reindeer and are resistant to trampling effects.

## Carbon Fixation and Primary Production

### Arid Environment Productivity

Lange et al. (1994) demonstrated that soil crust [[lichens]] in the
Namibian desert perform significant primary production in response to
pulses of water and light availability. Following nocturnal dew or fog,
[[lichens]] rapidly absorb water, photosynthesize, and shut down as
daytime temperatures reduce water availability. Individual fog events
can sustain photosynthesis for approximately 3 hours 20 minutes,
producing net carbon gain of approximately 126 mg C per square meter
per day.

With approximately 250 foggy days per year at their research site,
annual net carbon gain by 100% lichen cover is on the order of 16 g C
per square meter per year. Similar responses have been found for
cyanobacterial soil crust [[lichens]] in arid Utah soils (Lange et al.,
1998). The rapid response of [[lichens]] to short-term pulses of optimal
conditions, combined with fungal ability to survive long periods of
inhospitable conditions (S strategists), provides competitive
advantages over vascular plants.

### Semiarid Forest Systems

Beymer and Klopatek (1991) showed that approximately 28 kg C per
hectare was fixed by lichen crust communities in a pinyon pine and
juniper forest in a semiarid Grand Canyon environment. Using
radioactive tracer techniques, they estimated that approximately 34 to
36% of this fixed carbon becomes incorporated into soil organic matter.

### Growth Rates

Growth rates of terricolous lichen species range from 0.2 to 0.4 g
per gram dry weight (30 to 70 g per square meter) per year in Sweden
(Palmqvist and Sundberg, 2000). Epiphytic [[lichens]] in the same
locality produce only 0.01 to 0.02 g per gram (1 to 4 g per square
meter). The greater biomass accumulation of ground-inhabiting species
is attributed to better water-holding capacity and greater light
levels.

In the Norwegian high arctic, Cooper and Wookey (2001) measured growth
of fruticose [[lichens]] at 2.4 to 10.6 mg per gram per week, or 2.5 to
11.2% of original biomass per season. Peck et al. (2000) showed that
the arctic tumbleweed lichen Masonhalea richardsonii increased in
biomass by about 10% per year in Alaska.

## Nitrogen Fixation

### Cyanobacterial Contributions

The nitrogen-fixing Cyanobacteria photobiont of Stereocaulon paschale
fixes approximately 20 kg N per hectare per year. This rate could
provide a large proportion of the 10 to 40 kg N per hectare per year
plant demand in upland spruce boreal forests of Canada and Russia
(Crittenden, 2000). In desert crust communities of Utah, between 1 and
13 kg N per hectare per year can be fixed by crust communities (Belnap,
2002).

### Nutrient Interception

Mat-forming [[lichens]] are highly efficient at intercepting nutrients
from precipitation. The efficiency of nitrogen interception can often
approach 100% (Crittenden, 1989), though at certain times [[lichens]] can
be a source of leached nitrogen and potassium for other plants. This
form of nitrogen capture can be equivalent to the fixation capacity of
[[lichens]] containing nitrogen-fixing bacterial phycobionts.

Growth of mat-forming [[lichens]] is severely limited by nitrogen
availability. Crittenden et al. (1994) showed significant positive
relationships between nitrogen availability and chitin content (a
measure of fungal biomass) of [[lichens]], and Crittenden (1989) reported
that very little nitrogen is available in the substratum on which
these [[lichens]] grow.

## Landscape-Scale Nutrient Effects

In an Alaskan taiga ecosystem mosaic, Lamontagne (1998) demonstrated
that net nitrogen mineralization was seven times higher and nitrate
nitrogen 40 times higher in lichen patches than adjacent forest
islands. Lichen-dominated areas of upper slopes are important producers
of soluble mineral and dissolved organic nitrogen that runs off into
lower-lying forest islands, where nutrients are utilized through
immobilization into tree biomass. This spatial coupling between
lichen-dominated and forest areas represents an important
landscape-scale nutrient transfer mechanism.

## Adaptations to Environmental Stress

Fungi in soil crust communities are subject to rapid and wide changes
in water availability, temperature, and light on the soil surface.
Gauslaa and Solhaug (2001) showed that melanin content of fungal
tissue within [[lichens]] reduced ultraviolet B and ultraviolet A light
penetration, acting as a natural sunscreen. These pigments shift the
wavelength of light entering the lichen to higher wavelengths that
cause less structural damage, protecting both the fungal symbiont and
the photosynthetic algal partner. For related topics, see [[dighton-fungi-desert-arid-ecosystems]] and [[singh-mycelial-mats-and-biological-soil-crusts]].
