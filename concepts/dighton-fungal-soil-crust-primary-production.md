## Carbon Fixation and Primary Production

### Arid Environment Productivity

Lange et al. (1994) demonstrated that soil crust [[lichens]] rapidly absorb water, photosynthesize, and shut down as
daytime temperatures reduce water availability. Individual fog events
can sustain photosynthesis for approximately 3 hours 20 minutes,
producing net carbon gain of approximately 126 mg C per square meter
per day.

With approximately 250 foggy days per year at their research site,
annual net carbon gain by 100% lichen cover is on the order of 16 g C
per square meter per year. Similar responses have been found for
cyanobacterial soil crust [[lichens]] to short-term pulses of optimal
conditions, combined with fungal ability to survive long periods of
inhospitable conditions (S strategists), provides competitive
advantages over vascular plants.

### Semiarid Forest Systems

Beymer and Klopatek (1991) showed that approximately 28 kg C per
hectare was fixed by lichen crust communities in a pinyon pine and
juniper forest in a semiarid Grand Canyon environment. Using
radioactive tracer techniques, they estimated that approximately 34 to
36% of this fixed carbon becomes incorporated into soil organic matter.

## Nitrogen Fixation

### Cyanobacterial Contributions

The nitrogen-fixing Cyanobacteria photobiont of Stereocaulon paschale
fixes approximately 20 kg N per hectare per year. This rate could
provide a large proportion of the 10 to 40 kg N per hectare per year
plant demand in upland spruce boreal forests of Canada and Russia
(Crittenden, 2000). In desert crust communities of Utah, between 1 and
13 kg N per hectare per year can be fixed by crust communities (Belnap,
2002).

## Landscape-Scale Nutrient Effects

In an Alaskan taiga ecosystem mosaic, Lamontagne (1998) demonstrated
that net nitrogen mineralization was seven times higher and nitrate
nitrogen 40 times higher in lichen patches than adjacent forest
islands. Lichen-dominated areas of upper slopes are important producers
of soluble mineral and dissolved organic nitrogen that runs off into
lower-lying forest islands, where nutrients are utilized through
immobilization into tree biomass. This spatial coupling between
lichen-dominated and forest areas represents an important
landscape-scale nutrient transfer mechanism.
