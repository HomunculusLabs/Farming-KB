# Faires Hugelkultur and Forest Garden Strategy

Comprehensive guide to two key permaculture techniques: hugelkultur beds and forest garden design, including plant communities and companion planting strategies.

## Hugelkultur

### What It Is
A method of burying old wood in the ground under a garden bed. It patterns the exact processes of the forest, returns massive organic material to soil, retains huge quantities of water, and breaks down over time leaving air pockets for plant roots.

### Wood Selection
- Avoid: treated wood, cedar, black locust (don't rot well), black walnut (toxic to plants)
- Fresh wood robs soil of nitrogen during decomposition — add nitrogen sources
- Best: wood that has been laying around for a couple of years (brush, stumps, roots, debris)
- Well-rotted wood may actually release nitrogen

### Construction Steps
1. Remove a strip of sod (saved for later mulch layer)
2. Lay down wood layer, at least a couple of feet deep
3. Sandwich layers of mulch and soil (leaves, grass clippings, compost, straw, moldy hay)
4. Flip removed sod upside down on top, add another soil layer
5. Line sides with rocks to prevent erosion
6. First planting: grow a cover crop like clover to fix nitrogen
7. Follow with root vegetables: potatoes, carrots, radishes

### Nitrogen Strategy for Fresh Wood
- Key ingredient: **urine** from separating compost toilet diverted to beds
- Straw from animal bedding as mulch layer
- Fungi naturally grow and speed decomposition process

### Integration
- Can function like a [[comparison-swale-design-vs-contour-swale-irrigation-design]] with trees planted between beds
- On larger scale: applicable to chinampa systems

## Soil Teas

Quick nutrient delivery method (particularly for tomatoes and peppers):
1. Mix 1 part manure to 3 parts water
2. Ferment 2+ weeks with loosely fitting lid
3. Dilute to 10-15 parts more water ("weak tea" appearance)
4. Keep adding manure and water for continuous supply
5. Extras: comfrey (potassium), seaweed, kitchen compost

## Forest Garden Strategy

### Core Principle
In natural ecosystems, plants grow at different heights. Mimic the forest with canopy trees, smaller trees, shade-loving shrubs, groundcover herbs, and sun-loving edge plants. This self-sufficient community maintains itself with less effort.

### Why It Works
- Trees are huge biomass affecting everything around them
- Roots have fungi that benefit soil; trunks and leaves provide wind shelter
- Trees change temperature/climate: a large oak transpires 40,000 gallons/year
- Structures store water in canopy and bark, runoff waters plants below

### Planting Guidelines
- Dry/cool climate: space plants farther apart
- Warm/humid: closer (but not too close — fungus risk)
- Very fertile/water-rich: plant all species at once (walnuts + fruit trees + legumes + perennials)
- Total yield from all crops > monoculture yield, despite individual plants being smaller
- Diversity = security: bad year for vegetables still leaves tree fruits/nuts

### Establishment Steps
1. Pioneer weeds become mulch — cut woody weeds to ground
2. Dig out large perennial roots (don't dig too much — encourages more weeds)
3. Cardboard/old carpet on top to mulch and decompose weeds
4. Fence area, grow legumes and useful shrubs (comfrey)
5. Plant tree nucleus as garden center
6. Once established: simple management with animals and ongoing harvest
7. After main harvest: plant cover crop/green manure (rye, clover, buckwheat, barley, oats, vetch)

### Choosing Species — Key Questions
- What stage? (pioneer, Stage 2 or 3?)
- Deciduous or evergreen?
- Height, root behavior, growth rate?
- Shade density, disease resistance, pruning sensitivity?
- Climate/soil suitability, invasiveness?
- Multiple products? (leaves, roots, seeds, fruit)
- Self-storing? (nuts, roots harvested on demand)
- Record all species on 3x5 index cards with growing instructions and uses

### Polyculture vs Monoculture
- Monoculture: concerned with individual plant, pushes each to maximum yield
- Polyculture: concerned with the whole; total yield greater
- Forest gardens: "more food for less work"
- Warning: experimental, at least a decade to establish, no strict formula
- Running successful organic market farm: 80+ hours/week
- Forest garden: few people working part time

## Plant Communities (Guilds)

### The Three Sisters
Corn, beans, squash — corn supports beans, squash shades ground and suppresses weeds.

### Apple Tree Community
Rings of plants under the canopy:
- Base: legume (fava beans)
- Groundcover: clover, dandelions, chicory
- Ring: comfrey, artichoke, yarrow, nasturtiums, dill
- Enclosure: daffodils (stop grass encroachment)
- Each plant serves a purpose: attracting bees, providing mulch, fixing nitrogen, hindering grass

### Coaction Study
- Three ways species interact: positively (+), negatively (-), or not at all (0)
- Observe and record how each species does relative to others
- "Every plant should benefit from something, and it should be removed from any species that affects it negatively"

### Strategic Plant Placement
- **Attract predators**: plants providing food/shelter to pest-eating insects
- **Sacrifice themselves**: attract pests away from other crops
- **Trap pests**: attract and kill or trap pests for hand removal
- **Provide nutrients**: nitrogen fixers or nutrient accumulators for mulch
- **Create shelter**: prevent frost, stop wind, make mulch, create microclimates

### Animal Integration
- **Foraging**: pigs/birds clean fallen fruit and fertilize
- **Insect control**: flowers/herbs attract insects which attract birds that eat larvae/eggs
- **Slug/snail control**: ducks from fall to spring
- **Predator control**: dogs (raised with chickens) protect from foxes, deer, rabbits
