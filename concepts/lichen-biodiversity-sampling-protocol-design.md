# Designing Sampling Protocols for Lichen Biodiversity Inventory

Designing effective sampling protocols for lichen biodiversity inventory requires careful
attention to personnel, equipment, field procedures, data archiving, and the alignment of
survey goals with available resources. Lichens present unique challenges compared to vascular
plants, including microscopic identification requirements and sensitivity to microhabitat
variation.

## Resources Required

### Personnel

- **Trained lichenologists** should participate in protocol design and identify/confirm all
  voucher specimens. Specialist teams conducting field work maximize species capture,
  particularly for crustose lichens requiring microscopic examination.
- **Non-specialist field personnel** allow larger areas to be inventoried for the same cost,
  but species capture is less complete. Nonspecialists become proficient with macrolichens
  (fruticose, foliose, squamulose) through experience (McCune et al. 1997b).
- **Low-intensity inventories**: properly trained observers can distinguish many species
  in the field. **Moderate-intensity inventories** (regional status/trend surveys):
  non-specialist personnel are appropriate with adequate training.
- Training should be conducted by a lichenologist familiar with the region. Habitat
  selection criteria and morphological variation in lichens differ substantially from
  vascular plants.

### Collecting Equipment

- **Hand lens** (10–20×); **rock hammer and chisel** for hard mineral substrata; **stout
  knife or wood chisel** for woody/soft substrata; **hand pruning shears** for twigs;
  **paper packets/bags** (never watertight containers); **small card boxes** for fragile
  soil-surface specimens.
- Special equipment may be required for vertical cliff faces or tree canopies (Pike et al.
  1975; ter Steege and Cornelissen 1988).

## Field Procedures

### Specimen Handling and Preservation

- Do **NOT** place lichens in watertight containers — mold overgrowth occurs within hours.
- Store **one specimen per paper bag/packet**; record substratum, location, and date in
  pencil or waterproof ink. Air-dry wet/damp specimens thoroughly within hours of
  collection; never store damp.

### Collection Strategy

- **Minimum collection**: one voucher specimen per species per study area.
- Species distinguished in the field: record presence/abundance on data sheets; collect
  2–4 specimens from widely separated sites as multiple vouchers.
- Difficult-to-distinguish species (most crustose lichens): at least one specimen per
  species per site. In poorly surveyed regions: one specimen per species per site.
- Nonspecialist technicians can be trained to distinguish some species, but an expert
  decides which. Many lichens require laboratory study for species-level identification.

### Conservation Considerations

- Avoid massive collection from small areas; if only one specimen is seen, collect only
  half. Overcollection is rarely a problem on large plots with years between repeat surveys.
- Epiphytic lichens from recent litterfall have no impact on abundance.
- **Threatened/endangered species**: record but do not collect.
- When collecting is not permitted, inventory goals must be planned carefully; only lichen
  experts should conduct surveys. Historical buildings/monuments: microscope preparations
  in the field facilitate identification without material removal.

## Information Processing and Archiving

- Most taxa must be identified to species for a meaningful inventory. Maintain a minimum of
  one voucher per species in an accessible permanent collection.
- **Required data**: scientific name, lat/long, substratum, date, collector/identifier
  names, unique collection number. Vouchers are critical for data validity; reports without
  vouchers are considered unreliable.
- When nonspecialists sort specimens, an expert must identify each sorted "species";
  resorting involves reassigning abundance values.
- Vouchers archived dry in acid-free paper packets. Soil specimens: dip substratum in
  water-soluble glue, dry, glue to stiff card (Rosentreter et al. 1988).
- Reports should describe protocols in sufficient detail for duplication, including expert
  names, taxonomic authorities, and voucher locations. Archive data in standard format
  (ASCII minimal); translatability is key.
- Typical ratio of field time to lab/office time: approximately **1:4 to 1:10**.

## Criteria for Design of Sampling Protocols

Three critical aspects require explicit attention:

1. **Matching the sampling protocol to survey goals**
2. **Selecting sites for sampling**
3. **Selecting the within-site sampling protocol**

### Key Concepts

- A **sample site** is an independently chosen geographic location; number of sites =
  sample size for the inventory.
- The **within-site protocol** specifies area searched and subsampling method; it has no
  effect on overall sample size (number of independent replicates).
- Two fundamental trade-offs: (1) many sites with low precision vs. fewer sites with high
  precision; (2) many small subplots for quantitative precision vs. fewer large subplots for
  species capture (Fig. 9.5).

### Matching Survey Goals with Protocols

- First step: **state the overall purpose and specific objectives**.
- **Maximize species capture**: focus on within-site completeness with minimum replicate
  sites (suited to richness estimation and checklist development).
- **Monitor trends**: require adequate site replication, accepting some sacrifice of
  within-site completeness for geographic coverage and statistical power.
- Three constraining factors: (1) size/diversity of the area including human influence;
  (2) time/resources; (3) need for multiple independent sites per classification stratum.
- Lichen surveys must be conducted slowly, intensively, at close range (often with hand
  lens) across all microhabitats. A careful survey of a fixed-size site is more repeatable
  than a superficial inventory of a large diffuse area.
- Two major attributes vary with resources: (1) number of independent sites; (2) degree of
  within-site quantification.
- **Increased within-site precision does NOT automatically improve between-site accuracy.**
  Excessive within-site effort at the expense of sampling more sites can reduce overall
  inventory utility.
