# Saltpans, Gilgais, and Gully Erosion Control

## Overview

In arid and semi-arid landscapes, soil degradation manifests in several characteristic landforms: saltpans, gilgais, and gullies. Each represents a different stage or type of land deterioration, and each requires specific strategies for rehabilitation. These landforms are interconnected through the hydrological processes of erosion, salt accumulation, and water mismanagement. Understanding their formation and control is fundamental to dryland [[permaculture-design]].

## Saltpans and Salt Lakes

### Formation

Saltpans and salt lakes form when water collects in depressions during flood events, then evaporates over time. As water evaporates, dissolved salts concentrate and precipitate, sometimes accumulating to depths of several metres. A thin clay top may form over the salt deposits. Vegetation is limited to salt-tolerant (halophytic) plants on the margins.

### Sources of Salt Contamination in Drylands

Salt enters freshwater zones in sandy deserts and atolls from several pathways:

- **Downward infiltration**: Salt is washed down through saline surface sands by infiltrating water
- **Upward diffusion**: Salt diffuses up from deep, heavily-saline groundwaters (often saltier than seawater)
- **Advection from rains**: Salt is carried in rains from the sea or from saltpans after rain, particularly severe when freshwater has been overdrawn from groundwater
- **Forceful pumping**: Local pumping draws salt upward into surface soils, contaminating productive land

### Rehabilitation Strategies

Non-saline eroded claypans can be rehabilitated through:

- **Pitting**: Creating small depressions to catch and hold water
- **Flood retention banks**: Low banks (0.5–1 m) that support adapted trees and assist desalination
- **Chequerboard ridging**: A pattern of ridges that slows runoff and promotes infiltration
- **Ripping and ploughing**: Breaking compacted clay to allow faster water infiltration
- **Strategic planting**: Using herbaceous or needle-leaf groups (rather than dry-leaf mulch species) to keep infiltration crevices open

The key principle is that trees planted on or near saltpans actively assist desalination through transpiration and root-zone modification.

## Gilgais

### Formation and Characteristics

Gilgais are "puff and hollow" micro-relief patterns formed by patches of clay 3–5 m across. These clay patches expand and contract on a "swell-shrink" regime, creating hollows 6–20 cm deep. They occur in solonetic (sodic) clay soils, which shed water into the hollows. The hollows can link up in chains or complex patterns across the landscape.

### Ecological Importance

Where gilgai country is intact, the area is remarkably rich in plant species. The micro-topography creates diverse microhabitats — from the elevated "puffs" to the moisture-collecting hollows — supporting a wide range of species.

### Degradation

When gilgai areas are overgrazed, the hollows become sediment-filled, the solonetic clays wash away, and a series of sandy mounds replaces the original pattern. The area loses most of its plant diversity and becomes unstable. This represents a significant loss of ecological complexity that is difficult to reverse.

## Gully Erosion

### Formation

Gullies develop from flood-outs (also called flat-outs or run-ons) — the ever-widening flattish floors of valleys as they leave the hills. These "streams" are only 2–8 cm deep but 20 m or more wide, braided, and naturally stable. Water absorbs over a wide area of such plains.

When these areas are overgrazed, deeper channels erode first, then develop into steep gullies. The transition from stable flood-out to active gully represents a critical threshold in landscape degradation.

### Causes of Gully Formation

Gullies form from a combination of factors:

- Overgrazing of fragile flood plains
- Rabbit tunnelling into loose subsoils
- Vehicle or cattle tracks creating concentrated downhill flow paths
- Poorly constructed roads directing sheet flow to culverts that concentrate water
- Recent sediments or loose shaley ground exposed to sudden overland flows

### Gully Control Principles

Controlling gullies involves two fundamental strategies:

1. **Relieving the causes**: Addressing overuse and lack of water control through fencing, reduced stocking, pest control, and water spreading
2. **Preventing further cut-back**: Stabilising gully heads and sidewalls to halt upstream migration

### Technical Interventions

#### Diversion and Spreading

- In valleys above gullies, stream flow is diverted to flood spreader channels
- Riplines are drawn downhill from gully banks towards ridges at slopes of approximately 1:1000
- Small gullies can be bulldozed full or intercepted by cross-gully drains

#### Weirs and Gabions

A series of low weirs (0.5 m high) trap sediment to enable tree establishment. Weirs can be constructed from:

- Well-braced wire-mesh fences across gully floors
- Low stone bunds
- Stone-filled wire baskets (gabions) that spread floodwater flow

Weirs higher than 0.5 m require splash aprons at their foot to prevent plunge-pool scour. However, if interceptor banks above the gully-head divert water and reduce flow volume, weirs of 1.0–1.5 m become feasible without scour damage.

#### Construction Sequence

All weirs are started at the gully outlet and built in sequence upstream. This allows each weir to create a stable sediment platform before the next one is built above it.

#### Vegetative Stabilisation

- [[vetiver-grass]] or similar tough plant barriers hold erosion below weirs
- Stone weirs are more permanent and easily maintained than wire fences
- All weir structures should extend well above any expected flood level up the gully sides

### Case Study: Assomada, Cape Verde

A documented project in the Sahel demonstrated effective gully stabilisation through:

- Terrace building and stone-filled wire mesh gabion check dams in stream beds
- A shift from sugar cane cropping to tree and vegetable culture
- Pigeon pea (*[[cajanus-cajan]]*) supplying both food and ground cover
- Cooperative organisation for growing and marketing food with credit provision
- Wells and galleries tapping groundwater for tree and vegetable crops
- Silt from erosion building terraces 6–8 m deep and ~200 m wide across eroded valleys
- A change from goats and sheep to rabbits and pigs, replacing animals that overgraze hills

Over approximately 2,000 hectares were reclaimed across two target valleys, integrating biosocial strategies including revolving government loans, community employment and education, and replacement of export crops with local nutritional food crops.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Small and frequent**: Gully retention banks must be small, frequent, and well-constructed to effectively spread flow
2. **Start downstream, work upstream**: Weir construction always begins at the gully outlet
3. **Address causes first**: Vegetation and land management changes must accompany any structural intervention
4. **Work with water, not against it**: Diversion and spreading are preferred over containment
5. **Expect iteration**: Extensive gullied badlands may be too expensive to treat conventionally; fallback to creating small silt dams in valley floors
