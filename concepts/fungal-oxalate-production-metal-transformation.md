# Fungal Oxalate Production and Metal Transformation

Oxalic acid is one of the most commonly produced organic
acids by fungi and plays a central role in fungal
interactions with minerals, metals, and soil
environments. Fungal oxalate production drives mineral
weathering, [[staycare-phytochelatins-and-intracellular-metal-detoxification-in-fungi]], soil aggregation,
and the formation of secondary minerals with
significant biogeochemical consequences.

## Mechanisms of Oxalate Production

Fungi produce oxalic acid through metabolic pathways
involving the oxidation of glyoxylate, hydrolysis of
oxaloacetate, or the glyoxylate cycle. Oxalate
excretion varies among species and is influenced by
environmental conditions including nitrogen source,
pH, and the presence of metal ions.

Oxalate excretion is enhanced with nitrate as a nitrogen
source compared to ammonium, and is also stimulated by
bicarbonate, calcium, and certain toxic metals such
as copper and aluminum or metal-bearing minerals like
pyromorphite and zinc phosphate.

Oxalic acid functions simultaneously as a source of
protons (promoting acidolysis) and a chelating agent
(promoting complexolysis or ligand-promoted
dissolution), making it a dual-purpose agent for
mineral dissolution.

## Key Fungal Genera

Many fungal genera are known for significant oxalate
production. Among the most studied are Aspergillus,
Penicillium, and Sclerotium in the Ascomycota, and
Serpula, Trametes, and Fomitopsis among the
Basidiomycota. [[query-what-are-ectomycorrhizal-fungi-and-which-plants-do-they-associate-with]] including
Paxillus, Laccaria, and Pisolithus produce oxalate
at hyphal tips in contact with mineral surfaces.

The saprotrophic fungus Beauveria caledonica is notable
as an oxalate-overexpressing species capable of
transforming cadmium, copper, lead, and zinc from
toxic metal minerals into insoluble oxalate forms
while remaining tolerant to all tested minerals.

## Types of Calcium Oxalate Crystals

Fungi produce two primary forms of calcium oxalate:
calcium oxalate dihydrate (weddellite, CaC2O4.2H2O)
and calcium oxalate monohydrate (whewellite,
CaC2O4.H2O). Whewellite is the more stable form
and predominates in natural environments.

Depending on physicochemical conditions, fungal
calcium oxalate can exhibit various crystalline forms
including tetragonal, bipyramidal, plate-like,
rhombohedral, or needle-shaped crystals. The specific
crystal morphology is influenced by pH,
supersaturation, and the presence of other ions.

## Functions of Calcium Oxalate Precipitation

Calcium oxalate precipitation serves multiple
ecological functions. It acts as a calcium reservoir
in ecosystems, regulates intracellular calcium
concentrations, influences phosphate availability
through calcium binding, promotes soil aggregation
through crystal formation between soil particles,
and contributes to carbon cycling through CO2
release during oxalate degradation.

The formation of calcium oxalate crystals on and
within fungal hyphae and mycelial mats creates
physical structures that influence soil architecture
and water retention. These biogenic crystals persist
in soil long after fungal hyphae have decomposed.

## Toxic Metal Oxalate Formation

Fungi produce metal oxalates with a variety of
metals including Ca, Cd, Co, Cu, Mn, Sr, Zn, Ni,
and Pb. The formation of toxic metal oxalates
provides a mechanism for fungal tolerance of
environments containing high concentrations of
toxic metals.

The oxalate overproduction appears to be a
constitutive [[mycelial-metabolite-excretion-stress-response-grain-jar-cultivation-guide]] that precipitates
toxic metals as stable, insoluble oxalate crystals.
Specific mycogenic metal oxalates include moolooite
(copper oxalate) formed on copper-bearing substrates
and zinc oxalate hydrate formed on zinc phosphate
media.

Both saprotrophic and mycorrhizal fungi participate
in metal oxalate formation, suggesting this mechanism
is widespread across [[dighton-fungal-nutritional-composition-animal-diets]] strategies
and plays a significant role in metal cycling in
natural and contaminated environments.

## Oxalate in Mineral Weathering

Oxalic acid is a key agent in fungal weathering of
rocks and minerals. The acid dissolves minerals
through proton-promoted dissolution and metal
chelation. When the fungus excretes large amounts of
oxalate, the solubilization mechanism shifts from
acidolysis to ligand-promoted dissolution.

In podzol E horizons under European coniferous
forests, the weathering of hornblendes, feldspars,
and granitic bedrock has been attributed to oxalic,
citric, succinic, formic, and malic acid excretion
by ectomycorrhizal hyphae. Hyphal tips produce
micro- to millimolar concentrations of these
organic acids and are associated with micropores
(3-10 micrometers) in weatherable soil minerals.

## Oxalate-Carbonate Pathway

Calcium oxalate can be transformed to calcium
carbonate (calcite) through bacterial degradation
of the oxalate moiety, releasing CO2 that
precipitates as carbonate in the presence of
calcium ions. This oxalate-carbonate pathway
represents a potential biological sink for
atmospheric CO2.
