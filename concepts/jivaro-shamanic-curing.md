## Jivaro Shamanic Curing Practices

## Overview

Among the [[jivaro]] peoples of the upper Amazon, shamanic curing is the primary means
of treating illness. Disease is understood not in biomedical terms but as the result
of hostile sorcery — specifically, the injection of a supernatural projectile
(*[[tsentsak]]*, or "darts") into the victim's body by an enemy shaman. The curer's task
is to extract this invisible intruding object and restore the patient to health, all
while navigating extraordinary personal danger.

## The Setting: Darkness as a Prerequisite

Curing ceremonies take place **only at night**, in the darkest area of the house.
Darkness is not incidental — it is essential. The hallucinogenic beverages that the
shaman consumes produce visions, and these visions are believed to be visible only
in darkness. A ceremony conducted in daylight would be ineffective, as the shaman
would be unable to perceive the spirit world and the invisible darts he must
confront.

## The Shaman's Preparation

Before beginning the actual extraction, the shaman undergoes rigorous preparation:

1. **Drinking [[natem]]** — the ceremonial preparation of *[[banisteriopsis-caapi]]*,
   the [[ayahuasca]] vine. Natem serves as what Harner calls "the chemical door to
   the otherwise invisible world of the Jivaro shaman."
2. **Drinking tobacco juice** — a powerful emetic and purgative that also
   activates the shaman's supernatural defenses.
3. **Drinking piripiri** — a cultivated sedge (*Cyperus articulatus*) believed
   to confer specific magical powers.
4. **Whistling the curing song tune** — establishing the sacred sonic framework
   for the ritual.
5. **Singing for approximately fifteen minutes** — after whistling, the shaman
   sings the full curing song before commencing the physical act of sucking.

## The Sucking Technique

The central act of the cure is **sucking** (*wetisua*). Under natem's influence,
the shaman regurgitates two of his own *tsentsak* into his mouth — positioning
one in front and one in back. These darts serve as supernatural instruments to
catch the essence of the intruding enemy dart lodged in the patient's body.

### Danger to the Shaman

This is extraordinarily perilous. If **both** tsentsak fail to catch the
supernatural essence, it passes into the shaman's own stomach, killing him. The
shaman literally risks his life in every curing session, which is why the
profession carries such profound weight in Jivaro society.

## The Display: Proof of the Cure

After capturing the intruding dart, the shaman "vomits" the caught tsentsak out
of his mouth, displaying it to the patient and family as **proof** of the cure.
Laypeople believe the material object shown is the actual projectile extracted
from the patient's body. In reality, the shaman is displaying his own tsentsak
that caught the supernatural essence — but the distinction is lost on the
non-shamanic audience.

## The Shaman's Protective Armor

When under natem's influence, the shaman is covered by **hundreds of tsentsak**
forming a supernatural armor over his entire body. This living armor continuously
repels hostile darts shot by enemy shamans. Without it, the shaman would be as
vulnerable as any layperson.

To keep tsentsak alert and ready to repel attacks, the shaman must **constantly
consume tobacco juice**. This daily practice is practically necessary — a shaman
who neglects it finds his defensive darts sluggish, leaving him exposed.

## Confronting Different Types of Sorcery

### Dealing with Pasuk (Armed Spirit Helpers)

When the bewitcher has sent *pasuk* — powerful armed spirit beings — the shaman
must engage in direct **tsentsak duels** with these supernatural entities. His
own armored spirit helpers do battle with the incoming pasuk, and the outcome
depends on the relative strength and number of each combatant's defensive darts.

### Dealing with Wakani Birds

Some illnesses are caused by *wakani* birds — malevolent supernatural bird
spirits sent by enemy shamans. To combat these, the shaman drinks **maikua**,
prepared from *Datura arborea* or *Datura suaveolens*. Maikua is considered **even
more powerful than natem**, producing visions of exceptional intensity. Under its
influence, the shaman ventures into the forest in a trance to locate and kill the
wakani bird, destroying the source of the affliction.

## Post-Extraction Cleanup

After extraction, the shaman performs thorough cleansing: **blowing all over the
house** to remove the lingering supernatural "atmosphere," and **sucking out
remaining "dirtiness"** (*pahuri*) from the patient's body and surroundings. This
residual contamination, if untreated, could cause further illness.

## Identifying the Bewitcher

A critical social dimension is the shaman's ability to **"see" who bewitched the
patient** under natem. The family demands to know the sorcerer's identity, and
considerable pressure is placed on the shaman to name someone, as the family
needs a target for outrage and potential retaliation. The identification can have
serious political consequences within the community.

## Tsentsak Recycling and Return

Extracted enemy darts are **thrown into the air**, at which point they fly back
through the supernatural realm to their original owner. This automatic return
means every captured dart returns to the adversary's arsenal, creating a complex
economy of supernatural munitions. Tsentsak also return upon **the death of
former apprentices** to whom they may have been transferred, maintaining the
personal bond between shaman and dart.

## The Shamanic Weakening Cycle

Curers progressively **lose power**, especially after treating victims of shamans
with fresh, abundant tsentsak supplies. Each curing session drains the shaman's
defensive arsenal, and excessive patient load — particularly from powerful
adversaries — thins his protective armor and increases vulnerability.

### Tsentsak Recovery via the Rainbow Bridge

To replenish his supply, a shaman may create a supernatural **rainbow "bridge"**
extending to another shaman. Along this bridge he shoots a tsentsak to **surprise
the target**, and in the moment of confusion, sucks back a quantity of the
target's darts. This aggressive technique blurs the line between healer and
bewitcher.

## Loss of Tsentsak and Illness

When tsentsak are depleted, the shaman becomes ill himself — the loss of
supernatural protection manifests as physical sickness. To recover, he can
**purchase a new supply from another shaman**, an important feature of the
shamanic economy.

## Natem: The Chemical Door

Underlying all these practices is the central role of **natem** (*Banisteriopsis
caapi*), described by Harner as "the chemical door to the otherwise invisible
world of the Jivaro shaman." Without natem, the shaman cannot see supernatural
darts, communicate with spirit helpers, identify the bewitcher, or perform any
of the extraordinary feats defining his craft. The entire shamanic system depends
on this single hallucinogenic preparation bridging the mundane and supernatural.
