# Raised Beds, Wide Rows, and Hills

## Overview

[[steve-solomon]] presents three distinct planting layout systems in Gardening
When It Counts, each suited to different crops, soil conditions, and water
availability. Rather than prescribing a single "best" method, Solomon
argues that the choice of layout depends on whether the garden is
irrigated, how much rainfall it receives, and what types of vegetables
are being grown. The three systems — raised beds, raised wide rows, and
hills — can be mixed within a single garden to match each crop's needs.

Solomon explicitly rejects the [[coleman-british-adoption-of-french-intensive-gardening]] orthodoxy of deeply
and doubly dug raised beds with vegetables planted in precise hexagonal
interplanted patterns. He considers this approach unnecessarily laborious
and argues that easier methods produce higher-quality food with far less
effort. The key is matching the right layout to the right crop and water
situation rather than following a rigid system.

## Raised Beds

Raised beds are Solomon's preferred layout for small-sized vegetables
such as lettuce, beets, carrots, bush beans, and bush peas, especially
in gardens that receive irrigation or reliable rainfall. The standard
bed is four feet (120 cm) wide, which allows a person standing on the
adjacent path to reach the center while bending over or squatting. For
someone inflexible or short-armed, beds can be as narrow as three feet,
but below four feet it becomes impossible to run two parallel rows of
large plants lengthwise. Very flexible gardeners with long arms may go
to 4.5 feet. Paths between beds are about one foot (30 cm) wide.

Solomon advises raising beds no more than two to three inches (5-8 cm)
above the surrounding paths. Anything higher is unnecessary effort unless
the gardener cannot squat or bend over. Creating the elevation is simple:
after digging, move along the paths with a combination shovel, scooping
up about two inches of soil and tossing it onto the growing bed. In
subsequent seasons, beds are maintained by restoring soil from the paths
after each crop.

The primary advantage of raised beds is that the growing areas stay
looser because nobody walks on them. Walking on loosened soil compacts
it and makes root development difficult for vegetables. A secondary but
critical advantage is that elevated beds make it far easier to create a
fine seedbed — essential for tiny seeds like lettuce and carrots that
cannot push past clods or lumps. When you raise a bed a few inches, you
can rapidly rake clods and lumps off the surface and push them into the
paths, where they break down over the following months.

## Why Double-Digging Is Unnecessary

Most intensive gardening advocates say raised beds need to be double-dug
to a depth of 24 inches (60 cm). Solomon strongly disagrees. Digging 12
inches (30 cm) deep is plenty; loosening the second foot produces little
additional benefit in exchange for a heap of effort. Over time, the
second foot becomes looser without extra effort as worms transport
[[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] downward and as plant nutrients leach into the subsoil.
This [[green-cannabis-soil-flushing-nutrient-leaching]] chemically loosens clay through a process called
"flocculation," gradually improving soil structure at depth naturally.

## Raised Wide Rows

Raised wide rows are narrow raised beds, typically about two shovel-
blades wide, or 20 to 24 inches (50-60 cm). With one-foot paths between
them, row centers end up about 36 inches (90 cm) apart. This layout is
better suited to gardens without irrigation or with unreliable rainfall,
because the wider spacing between row centers allows each plant access
to more soil moisture.

In dry climates, Solomon recommends increasing [[wide-row-spacing-intensive-gardening-comparison]] significantly.
When he lived in western Oregon, where summer rain is almost nonexistent,
he used row centers of four to five feet (120-150 cm) depending on the
crop. The wider spacing ensures that each plant's root zone can draw
from a larger soil volume, reducing the need for supplemental watering.

Raised wide rows are well-suited for larger plants such as Swiss chard,
cucumber varieties, cabbage, kale, and Brussels sprouts. Less-aggressive
broccoli varieties also work in wide rows.

## Hills

Hills are the preferred system for large, sprawling plants such as
squash, melons, and some kinds of cucumbers, as well as for crops that
grow in large clusters like sweet corn (which needs a cluster for wind
pollination). Hills are also used for large brassicas like kale,
Brussels sprouts, and broccoli when planted on raised beds.

The standard hill spacing is usually four feet from center to center
when planted on top of a four-foot-wide raised bed, or 24 by 30 inches
for slightly smaller plants. For sprawling crops like squash and melons,
hills can be spaced five to six feet apart, or even eight feet where
rainfall is low and the plants grow aggressively.

To make hills, spread amendments (lime, compost, manure, and COF) over
the whole area. Then at each hill location, dig a circle about 12 to 18
inches (30-45 cm) in diameter, blending additional amendments to the
full depth of the shovel blade. The result is a concentrated mound of
highly fertile soil. For extra fertility, place a shovelful or two of
manure or compost at the center and sow seeds around the outside.

The concept of placing concentrated fertilizer in a localized zone —
called "banding" by agronomists — helps young plants get a fast start.
As their roots grow outward, they immediately encounter a zone of highly
fertile soil, which helps them outgrow environmental threats like
insects that can devastate newly emerged seedlings.

## Matching Layout to Crop and Water

Solomon organizes his garden so that small plants are grown on wide
raised beds, larger ones in slightly raised single rows, sprawling
species in hills spaced far apart, and crops that tolerate being walked
nearby are placed at bed edges. The choice between [[solomon-raised-beds-and-bed-preparation]] wide
rows depends primarily on water availability. Irrigated gardens can use
closer spacing and wider beds. Unirrigated gardens should mainly use
hills and wider row spacing, except for small-sized vegetables that can
still be grown in raised beds. The wider spacing in unirrigated gardens
allows plants to access moisture from a larger soil volume and can carry
the garden through rainless periods that would devastate closely spaced
plantings.

## Key Points

- Three layout systems serve different purposes: raised beds for small
  vegetables with irrigation, wide rows for unirrigated gardens, and
  hills for large sprawling plants
- Raised beds are four feet wide with one-foot paths; elevate only 2-3
  inches above path level
- Double-digging to 24 inches is unnecessary; 12 inches deep is plenty
  because the subsoil loosens naturally over time
- Wide rows (20-24 inches) with increased spacing are essential for
  unirrigated gardens to maximize moisture per plant
- Hills concentrate fertility for large plants; banding fertilizer gives
  seedlings a fast start and competitive advantage
- Not walking on growing beds is the primary reason raised beds stay
  loose and productive
