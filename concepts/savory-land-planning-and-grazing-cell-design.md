# Land Planning and Grazing Cell Design

Until recently, land on farms, ranches, forests, national parks, and tribal lands was
rarely planned with any long-term vision. Forests were planned for easy extraction,
national parks for the needs of tourists. Farms were planned around the original home
site and fields often dictated by roads, tracks, hedgerows, or the machinery used to
work them. Ranches were planned around home sites and handling facilities, with fencing
going in according to where water was and where range sites differed.

Under [[savory-holistic-management-overview]], land planning is based on the
[[savory-holistic-goal-definition]] and considerable forethought goes into ensuring that
it will lead toward that goal. The previous restraints imposed by past developments or
enforced by modern convenience are ignored during creative planning to avoid stifling
innovation.

## The Strategic Nature of Land Planning

Land planning falls under the [[savory-flexibility-in-management]] guideline at the
strategic level. It deals with things that have truly long-term consequence and cannot
be changed easily on a whim. Land planning is generally applied once in one hundred to
two hundred years or so, with long-term consequences not covered by other guidelines.

The process for developing long-term land plans in livestock situations follows a
structured approach that begins with thorough preparation and ends with a plan that
passes all [[savory-testing-and-management-guidelines]], including the society and
culture test.

## Steps in Land Planning

### Preparation

The people involved prepare by considering all factors that bear on the situation,
beginning with quality of life, production, and landscape goals and proceeding to
boundaries, croplands, crop rotations, topography, weather, wildlife, predation,
recreational uses, and more. A short brainstorming session often uncovers factors that
might otherwise be forgotten.

### Herd and Cell Sizing

Special thought goes to the likely size of livestock herds and areas in which the herds
probably will graze regularly in normal seasons. This gives an idea of the size of the
grazing cells, the areas that will be planned as a unit to regulate plant exposure and
reexposure through [[savory-recovery-and-grazing-periods]].

### Overlay Mapping

Information on wildlife, weather, sources of water, croplands and rotations, timber
extraction routes, recreational access areas, and other factors is displayed on
transparent overlays placed on clean maps. Separate overlays for each possible use of
the same land help draw attention to important features. One overlay will show clearly
the essential features of the landscape projected in the landscape goal.

### Creative Brainstorming

A fast and furious brainstorming session is held to produce as many trial layouts as
possible. Land can be subdivided in many ways, and all those remotely reasonable are
recorded on paper or separate maps. Throughout this process all existing land divisions,
fences, water points, and other developments are ignored in order not to stifle
creativity.

### Selection and Integration

When all possibilities are exhausted, a plan is drawn up taking into account all
existing developments that the brainstorming ignored. The best plan is selected through
a necessarily long process covering every possible aspect and all previously prepared
overlays. It requires the participation of people familiar with both the land and its
problems, but also understanding of the biological planning that will function through
the final design.

## The Radial Grazing Cell Design

The radial design consists of separate paddocks radiating out from a focal point that
might be a village, water point, handling facility, or merely a convenient point
through which livestock can move from one paddock to any other. This design works
particularly well in [[savory-brittle-environments]].

While Andre Voisin found radial layouts led to severe trampling where fences converged
under high densities on frequently wet soils such as those in France, they worked well
in brittle environments. The drier soils compact less, and [[savory-recovery-and-grazing-periods]]
last long enough for root growth to sustain a healthy community. In fact, once timing
is well-planned, the higher [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] near the center
produces better water and mineral cycles and thus higher [[savory-energy-flow-and-solar-capture]].

This contradicts our experience going back thousands of years of dust bowls developing
around livestock concentration points. Where radial layouts have existed for prolonged
time under heavy stocking in brittle environments, the growth of vegetation and
stability is highest near the center.

## Water Distribution and Fencing

The quantity of edge between different habitats, together with water distribution,
counts for much in land planning. Equal blocks of land containing two different habitats
offer far more opportunity for [[savory-wildlife-livestock-integration]] when one
layout provides more edge than another.

Throughout the planning process, the financial implications of fencing and water
development must be tested against the [[savory-ranch-financial-planning]] guidelines.
Developing a ranch in a planned manner should not cost money; on the contrary, the land
should generate the capital for each step of development, and each investment should
make the next step possible.

## Herding as an Alternative to Fencing

If the final plan involves herding rather than fences, any number of methods can
demarcate land divisions. Line-of-sight divisions, ridge lines, blaze-marked trees,
and periodic rock piles (cairns) will all serve. Where a radial layout does not include
fencing, short barriers near the center help funnel the herds as they approach. Given
the high cost of fencing and its disadvantages to wildlife, herding has much to
recommend it.

## Who Should Plan?

Much experience shows that the person who knows the land best, by having so many
preconceptions, is least qualified to do the creative planning, but is the best and
sometimes the only person capable of selecting the ideal plan. Those who feel they know
their land so well they don't need creative planning simply grab the nearest old map
and start drawing fencelines. When tested against collaborative planning, very seldom
do those knowledgeable people produce the best plan.
