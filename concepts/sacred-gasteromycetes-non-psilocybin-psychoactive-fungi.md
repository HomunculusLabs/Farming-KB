# Sacred Gasteromycetes and Non-Psilocybin Psychoactive Fungi

Beyond the well-known psilocybin-containing Agaricales, the Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (2000) worldwide survey documented several groups of fungi that are considered sacred or psychoactive by indigenous peoples but either lack confirmed chemical analysis or contain entirely different classes of psychoactive compounds. These include gasteromycetes (puffballs), boletes, and other groups that fall into their Group 4 classification of "sacred fungi without chemical studies."

## The Mixtec Narcotic Puffballs

One of the most intriguing entries in the Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz survey concerns the use of puffballs by the Mixtec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico. The authors noted that certain gasteromycetes were used as narcotic fungi by the Mixtec, although the specific species involved had not been definitively identified or chemically analyzed at the time of their publication.

The use of puffballs in indigenous ceremonies is documented in the Codex Vindobonensis, a Mixtec pre-Columbian manuscript that depicts scenes of mushroom worship including figures associated with gasteromycete-like forms. Lowy (1972) also discussed puffball symbolism in Maya codices, and Burck (1983) documented puffball usages among North American Indians more broadly.

The Mixtec puffball tradition is particularly significant because it suggests that the concept of sacred fungi in Mesoamerican cultures was not limited to the gilled mushrooms of the genus Psilocybe. The diversity of fungal forms used in ritual contexts may have been much broader than is currently appreciated, with puffballs, earthstars, and other gasteromycete forms playing roles that have been largely overlooked by modern researchers focused on psilocybin-containing species.

## Dictyophora Indusiata

Dictyophora indusiata (syn. Phallus indusiatus), commonly known as the veiled lady mushroom or long-net stinkhorn, is a striking tropical fungus found across Asia and parts of the Americas. Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz listed this species in their survey based on its traditional use in Asia and its presence in Mexico.

In Asia, particularly in China and Japan, D. indusiata has a long history of use. Yu (1959) described a Chinese mushroom called "Hsiao-Tuan" (laughing mushroom) that may correspond to this species. Imazeki and Hongo (1983, 1987) included it in their illustrated works on Japanese fungi. In Hong Kong, Griffiths (1977) documented its occurrence.

In Mexico, Guzmán, Montoya, and Bandala (1990) studied the distribution of Dictyophora in Mexico and Latin America, confirming its presence in the country. Its inclusion in the sacred fungi list raises interesting questions about whether this stinkhorn was used for psychoactive purposes, consumed as food, or revered for its dramatic appearance in indigenous Mexican cultures. In China, D. indusiata is primarily valued as an edible delicacy, but its categorization alongside known psychoactive fungi in the Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz survey suggests possible ceremonial use that warrants further investigation.

## Boletes as Sacred Fungi

The survey includes several species of Boletus that are traditionally considered sacred or psychoactive by indigenous peoples, despite the absence of confirmed indole alkaloid chemistry in most of them.

The New Guinea boletes represent the most extensively documented case. Heim (1963, 1965, 1978) and Singer (1978) described several Boletus species from Papua New Guinea that were associated with the Kuma people's "mushroom madness" phenomenon. These include Boletus flammeus, B. kumaeus, B. manicus, B. nigroviolaceus, B. nigerimus, and B. reayi. Heim and Wasson (1958, 1965) documented the Kuma tradition of consuming these mushrooms, which they linked to ceremonial practices.

However, Ott (1993) later expressed doubt about the neurotropic properties of several of these boletes, including B. flammeus and B. nigroviolaceus, noting that there is no scientific evidence confirming psychoactive constituents. The case illustrates the tension between ethnographic reports of psychoactive use and the absence of confirming chemical analysis.

In Malaysia and Singapore, Corner (1972) documented Boletus flammeus and B. nigroviolaceus, while noting their presence in local ecosystems. The sacred status of certain boletes in New Guinea appears to be related to their visual properties and cultural associations rather than their chemical content.

## Russula Species of New Guinea

Several species of Russula from New Guinea are listed in the Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz survey based on their association with Kuma ceremonial practices. These include Russula agglutina, R. kirinea, R. maenadum, R. nondorbingi, R. pseudomaenadum, and R. wahgiensis, all documented by Singer et al. (1958) and Heim and Wasson (1958, 1965).

Ott (1993) specifically noted that there is no scientific evidence that any of these Russula species are neurotropic, despite their inclusion in Kuma mushroom traditions. Their sacred status may derive from their visual resemblance to other psychoactive fungi, their bright colors, or their association with the broader "mushroom madness" complex of beliefs and practices.

## Heimiella Species

Heimiella anguiformis and H. retispora, both from New Guinea, were documented by Heim and Wasson (1958, 1965) and Singer (1978) as part of the Kuma mushroom complex. Ott (1993) expressed doubt about the neurotropic properties of H. anguiformis. These genera, like the boletes and Russula discussed above, remain chemically uncharacterized with respect to psychoactive compounds.

## Chlorophyllum Molybdites Confusion

An interesting case documented in the survey is the confusion between Chlorophyllum molybdites and Copelandia species. Portugal, Acosta-Urdapilleta, López, and Montiel (1992) reported a case of gastrointestinal intoxication in Morelos, Mexico, caused by C. molybdites that was mistaken for a Copelandia species. This highlights the practical dangers of misidentification that can occur when indigenous users or inexperienced collectors confuse toxic species with psychoactive ones.

## Significance of Group 4 Fungi

The inclusion of these non-psilocybin sacred fungi in the Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz survey is significant because it broadens the concept of "neurotropic fungi" beyond the narrow definition of species containing psilocybin, psilocin, ibotenic acid, or ergot alkaloids. The authors recognized that the indigenous concept of sacred fungi encompasses a wider range of organisms, some of which may contain undiscovered psychoactive compounds, while others may derive their sacred status from cultural rather than pharmacological properties.

## Modern Chemical Investigation of Group 4 Fungi

The development of sensitive analytical methods such as liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) has made it possible to screen fungi for a wider range of psychoactive compounds than was feasible during the initial chemical exploration period. Modern screening can simultaneously detect psilocybin, psilocin, baeocystin, norbaeocystin, aeruginascin, and other tryptamine derivatives, as well as ibotenic acid, muscimol, muscarine, and ergot alkaloids.

Despite these advances, many of the Group 4 species remain chemically uncharacterized. The practical challenges include obtaining fresh specimens from remote tropical locations, the legal restrictions on handling species suspected of containing controlled substances, and the difficulty of securing research funding for ethnopharmacological investigations of fungi whose psychoactive status is uncertain. Several research groups have called for systematic chemical screening of the species listed by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz in their Group 4, arguing that the potential discovery of novel psychoactive compounds justifies the effort.

Recent work on the Boletus species from New Guinea has been particularly revealing. Chemical analysis of herbarium specimens of Boletus manicus and B. kumaeus has failed to detect psilocybin, psilocin, ibotenic acid, muscimol, or any other known psychoactive compound, lending support to Ott's skepticism. However, the possibility remains that these species contain unknown compounds with psychoactive properties, or that the psychoactive effects reported by the Kuma are attributable to a combination of species consumed together rather than to a single compound.

## Puffballs in North American Indigenous Traditions

Beyond the Mixtec tradition, puffballs held significance for numerous indigenous peoples of North America. The Lycoperdon and Calvatia genera were used medicinally by many tribes, primarily as wound dressings and hemostatic agents due to the spore mass's absorbent properties. However, some traditions hint at psychoactive or visionary uses that remain poorly documented.

The Apache and Navajo peoples of the American Southwest reportedly used puffballs in ceremonial contexts, though the specific details of these practices were closely guarded and many have been lost. The Pawnee and other Plains tribes incorporated puffball imagery into their visual arts and cosmology, sometimes associating puffballs with stars, clouds, or spirits. The extent to which these cultural associations reflect genuine psychoactive use, as opposed to symbolic or medicinal use, is difficult to determine from the available ethnographic record.

Burck (1983) documented a case from the Pacific Northwest in which indigenous peoples reportedly used puffballs to induce visionary experiences, though the identification of the species involved and the reliability of the report have been questioned. The ambiguous status of these accounts illustrates the broader challenge of interpreting ethnographic evidence for psychoactive fungi use: the boundary between medicinal, symbolic, and genuinely psychoactive use is often unclear in the source material.

## Earthstars and Other Gasteromycetes

The earthstar fungi (genus Geastrum) represent another group of gasteromycetes that have attracted ethnobotanical interest. While no Geastrum species has been confirmed to contain psychoactive compounds, several species have traditional uses that suggest possible bioactivity. Geastrum triplex and related species have been used in traditional Chinese medicine, and some Amazonian peoples reportedly associate earthstars with spiritual entities, though the nature of these associations has not been thoroughly investigated.

The basket stinkhorn (Clathrus ruber) and related Phallaceae, while not gasteromycetes in the strict sense, share the enclosed spore-producing habit and have been culturally significant in various traditions. The dramatic appearance of these fungi at fruiting, emerging seemingly overnight from the soil in striking colors and forms, may contribute to their association with supernatural or sacred concepts even in the absence of psychoactive properties.

## Implications for Future Research

The Group 4 fungi challenge researchers to broaden their conception of what constitutes a "psychoactive" or "sacred" fungus. The possibility that some of these species contain novel compounds with psychoactive properties that have not yet been characterized by modern chemistry remains open. The example of the recent discovery of aeruginascin in Inocybe aeruginascens and Psilocybe cyanescens demonstrates that new psychoactive fungal alkaloids continue to be identified, suggesting that the chemical diversity of sacred fungi is not yet fully mapped.

Future research should prioritize chemical screening of Group 4 species using modern analytical methods, combined with ethnographic fieldwork to document remaining indigenous knowledge before it is lost. The interdisciplinary approach pioneered by Wasson, Heim, and Hofmann — combining ethnography, taxonomy, and chemistry — remains the most productive framework for investigating these enigmatic sacred fungi.
