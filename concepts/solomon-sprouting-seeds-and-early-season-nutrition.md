# Sprouting Seeds and Early Season Nutrition

[[steve-solomon]] addresses [[solomon-seed-sprouting-conditions-laboratory-germination-technique]] and early season growing from
two angles: the practical techniques for starting seeds successfully
in the garden, and the broader strategy of getting food production
started as early as possible in the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. His approach
emphasizes simplicity and avoids unnecessary equipment.

## Understanding Capillarity for Seed Sprouting

A key concept Solomon teaches is capillarity, the movement of water
through soil. Understanding capillarity helps gardeners sprout seeds
more successfully. When soil has good structure with stable crumbs,
water moves upward from deeper layers through [[capillary-action-in-soils]],
keeping the seed zone consistently moist without surface watering that
could wash away tiny seeds or create crusts.

This is why preparing a fine, level seedbed is essential. The surface
must be raked smooth and the soil structure must allow capillary
moisture to reach the seeds. After sowing, lightly firming the soil
above the seeds restores capillary contact between the seed and the
moist soil below.

## Direct Seeding: The Preferred Method

Solomon strongly prefers direct seeding over transplanting for most
crops. Most vegetables can be successfully direct-seeded, and this
avoids the [[query-how-to-prevent-transplant-shock-when-moving-cannabis-plants]] that sets plants back. You will not need
greenhouses, hot frames, heat cables, or [[faires-season-extension-cold-frames]]. Most vegetables
will come up handsomely if you follow proper [[soil-tilth-and-seedbed-preparation]] and
use strong, vigorous seeds.

He reserves transplanting for species that genuinely benefit from being
given every possible frost-free day: mainly tomatoes, peppers, and
eggplants. In short-season areas, a couple of melon or [[winter-squash-guide]]
transplants may also be worthwhile.

## Seed-Sprouting Parties

Solomon describes a traditional community practice called "seed-
sprouting parties," where gardeners would gather to prepare and plant
large quantities of seeds. This social approach made the work of spring
planting more enjoyable and efficient. The concept reflects a time when
gardening was a shared community activity rather than an individual
pursuit.

## Pre-Sprouting Techniques

For seeds that are slow or unreliable to germinate in open ground,
pre-sprouting (also called chitting) can give them a head start. This
involves moistening the seeds and keeping them at the right temperature
until the first root emerges, then carefully planting the sprouted seed.

Solomon's chitting method involves placing seeds between layers of
moist paper towel or in a container of moist sand, keeping them at room
temperature, and checking daily. Once the radicle (first root) emerges,
the seed is ready to plant. This technique works particularly well for
peas, beans, corn, and large-seeded crops.

## The Spring Gap: Early Nutrition

In climates with a defined winter, there is a gap between the end of
winter storage crops and the first fresh harvest from the garden.
Solomon suggests several strategies for bridging this period:

-  **Overwintered crops:** Certain hardy vegetables like kale, Brussels
    sprouts, leeks, and parsnips can survive winter in the garden and
    provide [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] harvests before new plantings are ready.

-  **Quick spring crops:** Radishes, lettuce, spinach, and other
    fast-maturing crops can be planted as early as the soil can be
    worked, providing fresh food within 30 to 45 days.

-  **Sprouted seeds for eating:** Growing edible sprouts (alfalfa,
    mung beans, radish, broccoli) indoors provides fresh, nutritious
    greens within days, regardless of outdoor conditions.
