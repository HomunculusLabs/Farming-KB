## Seed-Sprouting Parties

Solomon describes a traditional community practice called "seed-
sprouting parties," where gardeners would gather to prepare and plant
large quantities of seeds. This social approach made the work of spring
planting more enjoyable and efficient. The concept reflects a time when

## Pre-Sprouting Techniques

For seeds that are slow or unreliable to germinate in open ground,
pre-sprouting (also called chitting) can give them a head start. This
involves moistening the seeds and keeping them at the right temperature
until the first root emerges, then carefully planting the sprouted seed.

Solomon's chitting method involves placing seeds between layers of
moist paper towel or in a container of moist sand, keeping them at room
temperature, and checking daily. Once the radicle (first root) emerges,
the seed is ready to plant. This technique works particularly well for
peas, beans, corn, and large-seeded crops.

## The Spring Gap: Early Nutrition

In climates with a defined winter, there is a gap between the end of
winter storage crops and the first fresh harvest from the garden.
Solomon suggests several strategies for bridging this period:

-  **Overwintered crops:** Certain hardy vegetables like kale, Brussels
    sprouts, leeks, and parsnips can survive winter in the garden and
    provide [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] harvests before new plantings are ready.

-  **Quick spring crops:** Radishes, lettuce, spinach, and other
    fast-maturing crops can be planted as early as the soil can be
    worked, providing fresh food within 30 to 45 days.

-  **Sprouted seeds for eating:** Growing edible sprouts (alfalfa,
    mung beans, radish, broccoli) indoors provides fresh, nutritious
    greens within days, regardless of outdoor conditions.
