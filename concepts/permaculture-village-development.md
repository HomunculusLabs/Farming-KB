# Permaculture Village Development

[[permaculture]] village development joins the idea of client design work with earth repair and real estate development. Finance managers join forces with good design groups to purchase lands, capitalize them, and organize them as complexes of lake, forest, and village settlement. The world needs well-designed villages more than any other enterprise: villages to re-locate refugees from sea-level rise, villages to house people from urban slums, and villages where people of like mind can find community.

## Village Aims

An intentional village should have a group ethic acceptable to all who come there. Ethics, if shared, discussed, and acknowledged, give unity to groups and control the use of [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-earthworking-and-earth-resources]]. The aims of a sensible village group include:

1. **Reduce the need to earn** — by developing food, energy, and shelter self-reliance
2. **Earn within the village if possible** — reducing transport and travel needs by recruiting people who fill essential village occupations or are self-employed
3. **Produce a surplus** from services to others, maintaining a strong economy and outreach potential
4. **Provide non-material needs** — meaningful work, relevant education, and a rich natural environment, especially for children
5. **Cooperate** in various enterprises and small associations

## What a Village Can Provide

A well-designed village provides far more than isolated households ever could:
- **Privacy** in homes and gardens
- **Access to tools** — leased, rented, or easily accessed equipment from computers to tractors
- **Entertainment** — from local folk groups to shared media
- **Conservation** — village wildlife, water, and forest reserves
- **Recreation** in the near environment
- **Basic life essentials** — shelter, food, and energy

No isolated or scattered group can self-provide all of this, but about 30 to 200 houses can support these services and facilities, especially with planning for cooperative funding. What is easy for a group may be impossibly stressful for a nuclear family.

## Methods of Village Creation

### Land Acquisition Approaches
1. **Gift of land** to a land trust intended for village development
2. **Option to buy** from an owner, dependent on pre-sale of the idea to buyers (options can be as little as $50 per year if the price offered is about 20 to 30% of developed site value)
3. **Tribal or trust lands** already communally owned
4. **Investor partnership** with a finance group to purchase and [[basidiocarp-four-stage-development-classification-psilocybe-cubensis-badham-1982]]
5. **Group capital** — a convened group uses their own capital to stage development

The first options presume a developer and are faster and probably easier. All need careful forward planning. Development may yield considerable profit, and fair profits can be used to benefit both people and land, give young or poorer people titles, and rehabilitate neglected landscapes.

## Title Allocation Model

For 100 titles in the village:

### Development Stage
| Allocation | Percentage | Purpose |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Sold to village group | 60% | 30% covers all costs and sets land price |
| Given to village group | 30% | Reserved for community use |
| Labour equity | 10% | Surveyors, earthmovers, landscapers |

### Village Trust Stage (the 30% given to village group)
| Allocation | Percentage | Purpose |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Sold for village projects | 20% | Can be sold at 50% or less value to low-income families |
| Reserved for crucial recruits | 10% | Medical, computer, energy-producing people |

**Key principle**: 30% of titles should cover all land and development costs, so that surplus titles remain for community access, profits, gifts, labour equity, and new project development.

## Site Types for Village Development

| Type | Description | Real Estate Approach |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| Type 1 | City block or suburbs | Gradual takeover/buy-in |
| Type 2 | Adjoining an existing village | Outright purchase or option (easiest to plan) |
| Type 3 | Within a part-vacant village | Gradual takeover/buy-in |
| Type 4 | Isolated from existing settlement | Pioneering/kibbutz approach (most intensive planning) |
| Type 5 | Site of a pre-existing vacant/destroyed village | Outright purchase |
| Type 6 | New suburban development | Purchase or option |
| Type 7 | Specialized coastal or wilderness settlements | Purchase or option |

**Type 2 is probably the easiest** to plan and administer. It allows a whole graduation of involvement and commitment, attaches to pre-existing essential services, and purchase of existing homes results in little delay in the pioneering stage.

## Essential Site Criteria

1. **Water self-sufficiency** — must be able to catch, store, reticulate, and clean up its own water supply
2. **Clean energy availability** — wood, wind, solar, or high-pressure water for energy production
3. **Transport access** — road, rail, boat, and not-too-distant air access for trade and travel
4. **Mixed ecologies** — access to or ability to develop forest, aquatic, marine, agricultural, and market areas gives far more options than a village marooned in a simple ecology
5. **Telecommunications** — computer and data networks for connectivity

## Procedural Stages

1. **Formation of a group** or location of a site
2. **Arrange site option or purchase terms** — options are cheaper; the seller may retain a house title on the land with a discounted price
3. **Obtain "agreement in principle"** from local shire or planning authority, establishing road type, number of allotments, and services the developer must provide
4. **Design the village** layout, infrastructure, and enterprise mix
5. **Stage development** sequentially as funds and approvals allow
6. **Build community** through shared ethics, cooperative enterprises, and social structures

## Cooperation and the Developmental Credit Union

Villages can pool surplus or current financial resources in a developmental credit union, creating land titles to sell in order to develop public service facilities. With good management, this approach comes very close to a sane village development model that balances ecological responsibility with economic viability.
