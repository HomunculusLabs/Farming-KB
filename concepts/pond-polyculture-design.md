# Pond Polyculture and Integrated Aquaculture Design

## Overview

Pond polyculture in permaculture involves designing interconnected aquatic systems where each element supports the others, creating cascading yields from ponds, marshes, and tidal areas. Rather than monoculture fish farming, Mollison advocates for linked systems where ducks, wild rice, mussels, shrimp, fish, and edge plants form integrated food webs. The design principle is to have small specialized ponds at different conditions that flow into each other, with each producing different crops while enriching water for downstream systems.

## The Polyculture Model

### Sequential Pond Systems

The polyculture model uses a sequence of specialized ponds flowing one to another:

1. **Forage ponds**: Small ponds at varying pH levels suited to small forage organisms (snails, phreatocids, diatoms)
2. **Crop ponds**: Produce wild rice, duck eggs, rice, and edge plants
3. **Shrimp ponds**: Support invertebrates that trout feed on
4. **Trout pond**: The terminal harvesting pond, receiving enriched water carrying shrimp on migration

The key insight is that trout in the harvest pond can't swim up through the trickle connections (which pass through small grilles), so they receive food from upstream without being able to access or deplete the source organisms.

### pH Differentiation

Different organisms require different pH conditions:
- **Low pH ponds**: Suit phreatocids and other trout food organisms
- **Heavily limed ponds**: Support mollusk production
- **Variable pH across the system**: Each pond optimized for its specific inhabitants, with food flowing through connecting waterways to the trout

This is polyculture, but not the naive version of putting everything together. Some crops genuinely cannot grow together because their pH requirements conflict.

## Duck Integration

### Stocking Rates and Benefits

- **Eight ducks per quarter acre** is the standard stocking rate
- More ducks produce more manure, accelerating nutrient cycling in the pond
- Ducks provide an additional crop (eggs, meat) beyond the pond's primary products
- They greatly assist energy turnover within the pond ecosystem

### Duck Forage Systems

Wild rice is ideal in duck ponds because:
- It grows in the vegetative stage above duck height
- The three-week dropping period provides superb duck food (harvest what you want, the rest falls for ducks)
- It can be integrated into the dry cycle rotation

### Duck Protection

Where foxes and dogs are prevalent:
- **Islands**: The best protection — ducks can swim to safety
- **Fenced pond sections**: Top-netted fences extending into the water with duck sheds behind them work where islands aren't feasible
- Ducks learn to use these shelters from their first night

### Edge Yields

The highest known yields from home farm ponds come from blueberries and mulberries on the pond edge. White mulberries are used extensively throughout Asian ponds and paddies for both leaf and fruit as stock feed.

## Fish Systems

### Trout

Trout are the premium extensive fish for permaculture ponds:
- Found naturally in beaver ponds with shaded escape areas
- Optimal above 60°F but remain active at lower temperatures
- Low on the trophic chain when feeding on natural forage
- **Extensive vs. intensive**: Extensive trout in large ponds require minimal management. Intensive trout in small ponds are "a curse" requiring fine adjustments and constant attention.

### The Grasshopper-to-Trout Transformation

A striking case study: A cattle farmer whose land was covered with erupting annual grasshoppers built a pond. Within twelve months, eight-pound trout were thriving — feeding on grasshoppers blown into the water. The farmer converted over 100 acres to trout pond and retired.

### Catfish

Catfish are ideal because they are very low on the trophic ladder — they are bottom feeders and way down the chain. Good eating and easy to maintain.

### Feeding Strategies

- **UV lamp traps**: Ultraviolet patio lights placed over ponds with small fans blow attracted insects into the water, eliminating the need for manure input
- **Forage cascades**: Tiny organisms breed in upstream ponds and flow downstream to feed fish
- **Stick insects**: Yellow balloons floated beyond grasshopper leap range can attract and capture grasshoppers that blow into the water (note: insects see spectral yellow differently than humans — use known spectral yellow or test with insects)

### Smelt Systems

In one Australian location with clay soil unsuitable for trout breeding, thousands of stocked trout grew well by feeding on a small fish called smelt. The smelt bred in only one tiny quarry site — demonstrating how a single small forage habitat can support a much larger fishery.

## Tidal and Marine Aquaculture

### Tide Traps

Ancient stone-walled tide traps — possibly aboriginal in origin — are found throughout Tasmania and across Polynesia:
- Stone walls built about six inches below the high tide surface
- Fish swim over the walls at high tide
- When the tide drops, fish are trapped in the lagoon
- They work best at night (by day, fish often sense and avoid them)

### Salt Marsh Ponds

Salt marshes represent enormous untapped aquaculture potential:
- **Spartina** (cord grass): Heavy seed producer, excellent goose forage, and the base food for most cool water fish. Without Spartina, there are no bluefish. The bluefish industry depends entirely on Spartina and on there never being an oil spill.
- **Salt marsh plants**: Various fat plants at grass-root level provide goose fodder
- **Honey plants**: Sea lavender produces salt marsh honey
- **Pickling plants**: Samphire and other salt marsh plants can be made into pickles
- **Goose grazing**: Salt marshes with tidal flushing are the best goose grazing areas known — the origin of many domestic goose breeds

### Constructed Tidal Ponds

On land with salt flats behind the high tide mark:
- Drive a simple channel through the reserve area (tractor backwards and forwards, or existing channels)
- Between tides, dig inland ponds with gentle 3:1 side slopes
- Rocks can be thrown up on the tide side to block sea winds
- Plant salt-resistant shrubs around the pond edges
- Regulate tide range by adjusting boards in the channel (3 inches to one foot range twice daily)
- The result: the cheapest swimming pool possible — self-flushing, always warm, inland, and sheltered

### Oyster Culture

Oysters grow best at 60% air exposure:
- Above ideal exposure: much less meat, more shell
- Below ideal exposure (immersed): much more shell, less meat
- At ideal exposure: modest shell with lots of meat

With a constructed tidal pond, a client can set up inland oyster rafting or supported culture — much easier than tide-land operations and self-governing for ideal exposure. This can be a significant income source through selling spat.

### Lobster Culture

Lobsters will not tolerate another lobster in the same hole — they will fight and lose legs. However, in artificial structures with fish processing waste for food, they can be grown inland. Lobsters and oysters are largely immune from predation in these systems.

### Sponges and Flounder

Sponges can be grown in tidal pond systems. Flounder can be raised if fed adequately, though cormorant predation may be an issue in open ponds.

### Longshore Drift Traps

Natural scour holes behind logs, wrecks, or other obstructions demonstrate how longshore drift builds up deep and permanent ponds. By directing tides into these situations using simple barrier systems (fences, logs, dragged materials), permanent productive ponds form without digging or maintenance.

**Additional yields from longshore drift:**
- Broken shell accumulates — a 1,100-ton deposit observed at one jetty
- Shell is salable in 50-pound bags to poultry keepers as lime
- It is a continually renewable source of good lime for fields
- Octopus will colonize provided habitats (pots) are available

## Sewage Treatment Through Aquatic Systems

### The Marsh-Pond Sequence

Sewage should be turned into a marsh, not a pond:
1. **Holding pond**: Receives raw sewage; necessary because winter dormancy prevents marsh plants from purifying water
2. **Marsh with trees**: Grows mints, bog plants, and other species with phenomenal sewage demand. After passing through, water has no solids left
3. **Nutrient pond**: Receives the still nutrient-rich water (mainly phosphates and nitrates) for aquaculture production

### Case Study: Town of 8,000

Mollison designed a sewage disposal system for a town of 8,000 people that:
- Saved $30,000 annually in engineering costs
- Saved equivalent amounts in fuel costs
- Created an industrial base and employment through mint and bamboo production
- Originally designed for 50 acres; the town later purchased 1,500 additional acres for raw material production
- Incorporated waterfowl habitat with underwater shelving
- Became so successful it attracted vast numbers of black swans and teal

This contrasts sharply with a $700,000 mechanical sewage treatment plant in Canberra that produces poisonous water.

## System Design Principles

### The Complete Pond Ecosystem

A well-designed pond integrates approximately 20 species of plants and small animals low on the trophic ladder:
- Shrimps and yabbies (decomposers/herbivores)
- Crayfish and catfish (omnivores)
- Edge plants and pond plants (primary producers)
- Ducks (nutrient cyclers and egg producers)
- Mussels (filter feeders and phosphorus fixers)
- Wild rice and vegetable crops (human food)

### Minimum Viable Pond

A very small area — six feet in diameter — is well worth having as a production pond with these integrated elements. The aquatic vegetables produced are as good as any land vegetables, with watercress and cattails as prime examples.

### Even a Small Production Pond is Worthwhile

In Australia, concrete production tanks from 5,000 to 25,000 gallons are available off the shelf. The most handy model costs only about $40 — a six-foot diameter, two-foot deep concrete pond that serves as an excellent small production unit.

### Multiple Functions Always

Every pond should be designed to serve:
- Aquaculture production
- Barrier/fencing function (saves fencing costs)
- Reflection for adjacent gardens
- Fire protection
- Heat storage
- Recreation
- Water purification and nutrient collection
- Wildlife habitat
