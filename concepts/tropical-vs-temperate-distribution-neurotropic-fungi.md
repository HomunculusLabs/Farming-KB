## Tropical Centers of Diversity

associated with tropical and subtropical forests. The states of Oaxaca,

(Guatemala to Panama) contributes additional species, particularly in
the genera Copelandia and Psilocybe.

**South America**: Brazil has approximately 20 species, Colombia 12,
with additional species in Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Argentina, Chile,
and Venezuela. The Amazon basin and Andean cloud forests are major
diversity centers, though they remain undersampled.

**Southeast Asia**: Thailand, Cambodia, Indonesia, the Philippines,
and Sri Lanka host numerous tropical species. [[psilocybe-azurescens]], P.
baeocystis, P. cyanescens, P. pelliculosa, P. stuntzii, P.
semilanceata, P. silvatica, and P. strictipes. The cool, wet climate
and abundance of wood debris in coastal forests create ideal
conditions for neurotropic fungi.

**Europe**: Approximately 30 species are documented from Europe. P.
semilanceata is the most widespread European species. The British
Isles, Scandinavia, France, Germany, and the Netherlands are
particularly rich.

**Japan**: The Japanese archipelago hosts a temperate-subtropical
mycoflora including P. argentipes, P. subcaerulipes, P. venenata,
P. septentrionalis, and several Gymnopilus species.

## Ecological Differences

Tropical and temperate neurotropic fungi differ in several key
ecological characteristics:

**Substrate**: Tropical species are predominantly coprophilous
(dung-inhabiting), growing on the dung of herbivores in pastures and
grasslands. Copelandia and Panaeolus species are almost exclusively
coprophilous. Temperate species are more commonly lignicolous
(wood-inhabiting), growing on dead wood, wood chips, forest debris,
or in grassland soils (P. semilanceata).

**Seasonality**: Tropical species fruit year-round with peaks
corresponding to wet seasons. Temperate species have more defined
