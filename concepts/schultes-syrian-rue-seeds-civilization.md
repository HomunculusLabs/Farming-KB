# Syrian Rue Seeds of Civilization

Syrian Rue (*Peganum harmala* L.) is a bushy shrub of the Caltrop family
(Zygophyllaceae) native to desert areas from western Asia across to
northern India, with a range extending through Mongolia and Manchuria.
Though less celebrated than New World hallucinogens, Syrian Rue possesses
psychoactive beta-carboline alkaloids of profound pharmacological
significance, serving both as a traditional medicine across vast regions of
the Old World and as a key ingredient in modern ayahuasca analog
preparations.

## Botanical Description

Syrian Rue attains a height of approximately three feet (1 m). The leaves
are cut into narrowly linear segments, and the small white flowers occur
in the axils of branches. The globose, deeply lobed fruit contains many
flat, angled seeds of a brown color, bitter taste, and narcotic odor. The
plant is remarkably hardy, adapted to the arid and semi-arid environments
of its native range, thriving where few other plants survive.

## Chemistry: Beta-Carboline Alkaloids

The plant possesses psychoactive principles: beta-carboline alkaloids,
primarily harmine, harmaline, and tetrahydroharmine, along with related
bases. These constituents are concentrated in the seeds, which constitute
the Indian drug known as Harmal or Harmala. These same beta-carboline
alkaloids are known to occur in at least eight families of higher plants,
including the Malpighiaceae, the plant family that contains
*Banisteriopsis caapi*, the primary ingredient of ayahuasca.

The beta-carbolines are notable for their monoamine oxidase inhibitor
(MAOI) properties. This pharmacological characteristic is central to their
role both in traditional medicine and in ayahuasca preparation, where they
function to inhibit the enzymatic breakdown of dimethyltryptamine (DMT) in
the gut, allowing the visionary compound to become orally active.

## Folk Medicine Across Continents

The high esteem that *Peganum harmala* enjoys in folk medicine wherever
the plant occurs may indicate a former semisacred use as a hallucinogen in
native religion and magic. Syrian Rue has many uses in traditional
medicine and is also valued as an aphrodisiac. The dried seeds are often
used as incense. From Asia Minor across to India, the plant is held in
"extraordinary esteem," suggesting a history of religious use as a
hallucinogen extending far back into antiquity.

The plant has recently been postulated to have been the legendary Soma of
ancient Vedic ritual, though this identification remains debated among
scholars. Regardless of the Soma question, the ubiquity of Syrian Rue in
Old World folk medicine points to a long and culturally significant
relationship between the plant and human societies across Central Asia,
the Middle East, and the Indian subcontinent.

## Ayahuasca Analogs

In connection with research on so-called ayahuasca analogs, the
beta-carboline alkaloids of Syrian Rue have taken on new significance. The
seeds of *Peganum harmala* can serve as a substitute for *Banisteriopsis
caapi* in ayahuasca-type preparations, providing the necessary MAOI
activity to activate orally ingested DMT-containing plants.

For example, an extract made from the leaves of certain reed grass
species with high DMT content, in combination with *Peganum harmala*,
produces visionary effects and can be drunk as a substitute for
ayahuasca. This combination of Old World MAOI plant and New World DMT
plant represents a fascinating pharmacological bridge between continents
and traditions, demonstrating the universal chemistry underlying visionary
plant use.

## Pharmacological Significance

The discovery that beta-carboline alkaloids from Syrian Rue could
substitute for the Amazonian *Banisteriopsis caapi* in activating oral DMT
represented a major insight in ethnopharmacology. It revealed that the
key to ayahuasca's oral activity lay not in any unique Amazonian plant
property but in the universal biochemistry of monoamine oxidase inhibition.
This understanding opened the door to the study of ayahuasca analogs
worldwide and deepened scientific appreciation of the pharmacological
sophistication embedded in indigenous plant knowledge.

The beta-carbolines harmine and harmaline are themselves psychoactive,
producing mild visionary effects, altered states of consciousness, and in
higher doses, nausea and purgative effects. In the context of ayahuasca
preparations, however, their primary role is facilitatory, enabling the
DMT from companion plants to reach the bloodstream intact.

## Toxicological Considerations

Many of the approximately fifteen species in the genus *Peganum* are
potent cardiac poisons; others are emetics; one is said to cause death by
paralysis of the central nervous system. While *P. harmala* is the most
widely used and culturally significant species, proper identification and
dosage are essential. The seeds contain the highest concentration of
active alkaloids, and traditional preparations have been refined over
centuries to balance efficacy with safety.

## Cultural and Historical Context

The distribution of Syrian Rue across Central Asia, the Middle East, and
the Indian subcontinent places it at the crossroads of some of the
world's oldest civilizations. Its persistent role in folk medicine, its
use as incense, and its reputation as an aphrodisiac all suggest a
cultural importance that extends well beyond any single ceremonial
application. The plant's beta-carboline chemistry connects it to a global
network of visionary plant use that includes ayahuasca in the Amazon and
ergot in Europe.
