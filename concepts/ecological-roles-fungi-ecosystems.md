# Ecological Roles of Fungi in Ecosystems Fungi are fundamental to the functioning of terrestrial ecosystems, playing irreplaceable roles in nutrient cycling, plant nutrition, soil formation, and food web dynamics. As the primary decomposers of lignocellulosic material and essential symbionts of most land plants, fungi mediate the flow of carbon and nutrients through virtually all terrestrial habitats from arctic tundra to tropical rainforests. ## Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling ### Lignocellulose Degradation Fungi are the dominant decomposers of plant- derived organic matter, particularly the recalcitrant components lignin and cellulose that bacteria cannot efficiently degrade. White-rot fungi (primarily Basidiomycota) are unique in their ability to enzymatically degrade lignin, using a suite of oxidative enzymes including lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and laccase. Brown-rot fungi primarily degrade cellulose while modifying lignin through non- enzymatic Fenton chemistry. Without fungal decomposition, terrestrial ecosystems would be buried under accumulations of undecomposed plant material. ### Carbon and Nitrogen Cycling Fungi process an estimated 1.5 gigatons of carbon annually through decomposition. This fungal- mediated decomposition releases CO2 through respiration while converting a portion of plant carbon into stable soil organic matter (fungal necromass). Fungal residues contribute significantly to soil carbon sequestration, with glomalin-related soil protein from arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi being particularly persistent in soil. Fungi also influence nitrogen availability through mineralization of organic nitrogen from decomposing material and access to organic nitrogen sources unavailable to plants directly. ## Mycorrhizal Symbiosis ### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF) arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (phylum Glomeromycota) form associations with the roots of most herbaceous plants, many shrubs, and some tropical trees. The fungus penetrates root cortical cells, forming highly branched structures called arbuscules that facilitate nutrient exchange. The plant provides carbohydrates (up to 20% of total photosynthate) to the fungus, while the fungus supplies phosphorus, nitrogen, zinc, and other nutrients scavenged from soil. AMF also improve plant resistance to pathogens and drought stress. ### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (ECM) [[fukuoka-textdoc-biological-control-fallacy-pheromone-critique]] agents in agriculture.

## Fungi in Soil Food Webs

Fungi occupy multiple trophic levels within soil food webs, connecting
primary production to higher organisms through complex feeding
interactions:
- **Saprotrophic fungi** serve as primary decomposers, converting
  dead organic matter into fungal biomass that becomes food for
  fungal-feeding nematodes, microarthropods, and collembola
- **Mycorrhizal fungi** function as [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]], disease
  resistance, and [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]] persist underground through fire, drought,
  and logging events, providing inoculum sources that accelerate
  plant community regeneration after disturbance
- Fungal [[dighton-nitrogen-deposition-mycorrhizal-community-shifts]], makes them reliable indicators
  of air quality in both urban and remote environments
- Crustose lichens contribute to biological soil crust formation
  in arid and semi-arid ecosystems, stabilizing surface soils and
  fixing atmospheric nitrogen through their cyanobacterial partners
- Epiphytic lichens on tree bark contribute to canopy nutrient
  cycling by trapping atmospheric particulates and dissolved
  nutrients that are released to the forest floor during rainfall
