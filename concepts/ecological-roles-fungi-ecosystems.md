# Ecological Roles of Fungi in Ecosystems

Fungi are fundamental to the functioning of
terrestrial ecosystems, playing irreplaceable
roles in nutrient cycling, plant nutrition, soil
formation, and food web dynamics. As the primary
decomposers of lignocellulosic material and
essential symbionts of most land plants, fungi
mediate the flow of carbon and nutrients through
virtually all terrestrial habitats from arctic
tundra to tropical rainforests.

## Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling

### Lignocellulose Degradation
Fungi are the dominant decomposers of plant-
derived organic matter, particularly the
recalcitrant components lignin and cellulose that
bacteria cannot efficiently degrade. White-rot
fungi (primarily Basidiomycota) are unique in
their ability to enzymatically degrade lignin,
using a suite of oxidative enzymes including
lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and
laccase. Brown-rot fungi primarily degrade
cellulose while modifying lignin through non-
enzymatic Fenton chemistry. Without fungal
decomposition, terrestrial ecosystems would be
buried under accumulations of undecomposed plant
material.

### Carbon and Nitrogen Cycling
Fungi process an estimated 1.5 gigatons of carbon
annually through decomposition. This fungal-
mediated decomposition releases CO2 through
respiration while converting a portion of plant
carbon into stable soil organic matter (fungal
necromass). Fungal residues contribute
significantly to soil carbon sequestration, with
glomalin-related soil protein from arbuscular
mycorrhizal fungi being particularly persistent in
soil. Fungi also influence nitrogen availability
through mineralization of organic nitrogen from
decomposing material and access to organic
nitrogen sources unavailable to plants directly.

## Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF)
arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (phylum
Glomeromycota) form associations with the roots of
most herbaceous plants, many shrubs, and some
tropical trees. The fungus penetrates root
cortical cells, forming highly branched structures
called arbuscules that facilitate nutrient
exchange. The plant provides carbohydrates (up to
20% of total photosynthate) to the fungus, while
the fungus supplies phosphorus, nitrogen, zinc,
and other nutrients scavenged from soil. AMF also
improve plant resistance to pathogens and drought
stress.
