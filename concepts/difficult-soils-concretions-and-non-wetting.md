# Difficult Soils — Concretions, Pans, and Non-Wetting Sands

## Overview

Many soils present physical barriers to plant growth in the form of cemented subsurface layers (concretions or pans) or hydrophobic surface conditions (non-wetting sands and clays). Understanding these difficult soil types and their remedies is essential for successful land management, especially in arid, tropical, and coastal environments.

## Types of Soil Concretions and Pans

### Calcrete (Caliche, Platin, Kunkar)

[[calcrete-caliche]] is a hard, mainly level subsurface concretion found about 0.5–1.0 m below granular or sandy topsoil. It is typical of coral islands (where it contains calcium triphosphate) and downwind areas of desert borders. On atolls, fresh-water deposits develop below the caliche layer.

**Characteristics:**
- Whitish to creamy color
- Calcium/magnesium concretion, worsened by superphosphate addition
- Releases bubbles of carbon dioxide when exposed to acid (vinegar)
- Must be broken open for tree planting, otherwise roots spread laterally and become vulnerable to wind-throw

**Uses:** Broken caliche can serve as building material and forms a safe roof for tunnels or dugouts.

### Silcrete (Cangagua)

Silcrete is a grey to red, shiny, hard layer developed below some tropical forest soils. The soil is concreted by silica deposits and gives a glassy surface if forests are cleared. If silica-cemented deposits lie below forests, it is unwise to clear the forest, as topsoil loss follows rapidly.

### Ferricrete

Ferricrete encompasses iron-cemented pans and soil layers of varying thickness, including:
- Thin sandy layers of 5–10 mm
- Alumina-iron laterites (often capping desert hills with silcrete veins)
- Iron-manganese nodular horizons
- **Ortstein:** Iron-organic hard B horizons in podzols
- **Coffeerock:** Thick, sandy, coffee-coloured horizon rich in iron, common in humic podzols, easily broken
- **Duricrusts:** Hard silica-iron caps on desert hills

Ferricrete may lie over pale bauxites and is also called ironstone or plinthite.

### Plough Pans

Plough pans are usually clay-based compacted layers developed below croplands during wet periods, often caused by mouldboard ploughs. These impermeable layers restrict root penetration and water movement.

### Solcrete

Sodium in soils may develop a "collapsed" cemented, greyish, gravelly pan (solcrete) that is impermeable to water. Only deep-rooted trees, salt reduction, and humus can relieve these conditions.

## Remedies for Concreted Soils

All concreted soil layers require aggressive intervention:
- **Ripping** with heavy equipment
- **Explosive shattering** in extreme cases
- **Deep mulch pits** to establish trees
- **Deep drainage** of 1–2 m is essential for salted soils

In Ecuador, an iron-silica pan called *cangagua* lies about 3 m below tropical rainforest. Where cangagua is known to exist, perpetual forests used for products other than wood (honey, fruits, medicines) are the only sustainable land use.

## Non-Wetting Sands

Some classes of very fine blackish sands, and sands invaded by hydrophobic soil fungi, resist wetting — water sits on top as droplets rather than infiltrating.

### Garden-Scale Remedies

- **Ridge soil** to create basins that hold water
- **Core out sand** every square metre and drop in a loam or clay-loam plug (4–10 cm by 30 cm deep)
- **Compost thoroughly** and build organic material to 8% of surface soil
- **Add bentonite** — one handful per square metre, or use powdered clay from clay pans
- **Mulch thoroughly** before planting

### Broad-Acre Remedies

On a larger scale, deep ploughing in autumn (to 45 cm) is used, followed by rotary plough or chopper to mix the top non-wetting profile of 10–20 cm with subsoils. A [[cover-crop]] is immediately sown to prevent erosion, used as [[green-manure]] for deep-rooting crop or tree species. Successful tree establishment permanently curbs the problem.

### Plastic Liner Technique for Deep Sands

For acid or deep silica sands, a practical garden technique involves:
1. Laying plastic sheeting at 0.5 m depth in garden beds
2. Adding clay above the plastic to hold water at root level
3. Mulching on top to prevent surface evaporation

## Sodium-Rich Clays

Clays that seal on the surface in light rains are often sodium-rich and "melt" in rain. Remedies include:
- Making low banks across run-off
- Adding gypsum at 2–3 handfuls per square metre
- Flushing out sodium as sulphate with fresh water if possible
- Placing sand over the surface to 4 cm depth

For deep cracking clays and lumpy soils, add a sand layer, scatter gypsum at a handful per square metre, and mulch.
