# Phototropism, Photoperiodism, and the Discovery of Phytochrome

## Overview

Daniel Chamovitz's "What a Plant Knows" presents the history of plant light perception as a series of landmark discoveries, beginning with Charles Darwin's experiments on phototropism and culminating in the identification of phytochrome as the molecular switch governing photoperiodic responses. Together, these discoveries reveal that plants possess sophisticated light-sensing systems that are in many ways more complex than human vision, capable of discriminating between wavelengths that humans cannot perceive and using different photoreceptor systems for fundamentally different purposes.

## Darwin's Phototropism Experiments

For twenty years following the publication of "On the Origin of Species," Charles Darwin and his son Francis conducted experiments on plant movement that remain foundational to plant biology. Their central finding was that **almost all plants bend towards lateral light**, a behavior called phototropism.

### The Tip Is the Eye

In their classic experiment, the Darwins grew canary grass seedlings in total darkness for several days, then exposed them to a gas lamp so dim that the researchers themselves could barely see. After only three hours, the seedlings had bent toward the light, with the curvature consistently occurring about two centimeters below the tip.

They then tested five conditions:

1. **Untreated seedling**: Bends toward light normally.
2. **Tip pruned off**: No bending occurs. The plant is "blind."
3. **Tip covered with an opaque cap**: No bending occurs.
4. **Tip covered with a clear glass cap**: Bending occurs normally. Light passes through the glass.
5. **Middle section covered with an opaque tube**: Bending occurs normally.

The conclusion was definitive: the tip of the seedling perceives light direction and sends a signal to the midsection, which responds by differential growth, causing the plant to bend. This was the first experimental demonstration of a dedicated light-sensing organ in plants.

### Blue Light and Phototropism

Julius von Sachs, a contemporary of Darwin, had previously established that **blue light** is the primary wavelength inducing phototropism. Plants are generally insensitive to other colors for directional bending purposes. This discovery established that plants differentiate between colors of light and use specific wavelengths for specific purposes.

## Photoperiodism and the Maryland Mammoth

The next major advance came from an unexpected source. In 1906, farmers in southern Maryland noticed a tobacco strain that grew to four and a half meters, produced nearly a hundred leaves, and flowered only when killed by frost. This strain, called Maryland Mammoth, was agronomically useless because it produced no seed.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of Agriculture discovered the reason: Maryland Mammoth was a **short-day plant**. It required long nights to trigger flowering. When grown under natural summer conditions, the long days kept it vegetative. When moved to a dark shed each afternoon to artificially shorten the day, it flowered normally.

This discovery, called **photoperiodism**, established that plants measure the duration of light and dark periods and use this information to regulate major developmental transitions.

### From Tobacco to Global Agriculture

The Maryland Mammoth discovery had implications far beyond tobacco. It revealed that flowering, one of the most critical transitions in a plant's life cycle, is regulated by an environmental cue that can be easily manipulated. This opened the door to the entire field of photoperiodic control of crop development. Once scientists understood that plants measure night length, they could breed varieties adapted to different latitudes, manipulate flowering schedules in greenhouses, and optimize seed production for any growing region. The discovery also raised the fundamental question of how plants perceive and measure light, which would occupy plant biologists for the next century.

### Day or Night?

A critical follow-up discovery came around World War II: scientists found that they could manipulate flowering by briefly turning lights on and off in the middle of the night. A few minutes of light during the night was sufficient to prevent a short-day plant from flowering or to induce flowering in a long-day plant. This proved that **plants measure the length of the continuous dark period, not the length of the day**.

## The Red-Far-Red Switch

The photoperiodism researchers then asked which color of light plants use to measure darkness. The answer was surprising: only **red light** was effective. Blue or green flashes during the night had no effect on flowering, but a few seconds of red light were sufficient to disrupt the dark period measurement.

### Far-Red Reversal

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues made an even more remarkable discovery. **Far-red light**, with wavelengths slightly longer than bright red and barely visible at dusk, could completely cancel the effect of red light. The sequence operates as a reversible switch:

- Red light pulse during the night: induces flowering response in long-day plants.
- Far-red pulse immediately after red: cancels the red effect entirely. The plant behaves as if it never saw the red light.
- Another red pulse after the far-red: restores the flowering response.
- And so on, repeatedly.

A few seconds of either color is sufficient. On a philosophical level, as Chamovitz notes, **the plant remembers the last color it saw**.

### Phytochrome

By 1960, Warren L. Butler and colleagues had demonstrated that a single photoreceptor protein, called **phytochrome** (meaning "plant color"), was responsible for both the red and far-red responses. Phytochrome exists in two interconvertible forms:

- **Pr form**: Absorbs red light and converts to the Pfr form. This is the active state that promotes flowering in long-day plants.
- **Pfr form**: Absorbs far-red light and converts back to the Pr form. This is the inactive state.

Ecologically, this system is perfectly adapted to natural conditions. The last light at the end of the day is enriched in far-red wavelengths (because red light is scattered more by the atmosphere), signaling the plant that night is approaching. Morning light is enriched in red, activating the system for the day.

## Separate Receptors for Separate Functions

A key finding is that phototropism and photoperiodism use **different photoreceptors in different parts of the plant**:

- **Phototropism**: The "eye" is in the shoot tip. Blue light receptors in the tip detect direction and send a growth signal downward.
- **Photoperiodism**: The receptors are in the **leaves**, not the tip. Illuminating any single leaf is sufficient to regulate flowering in the entire plant. If all leaves are removed, leaving only stem and apex, the plant is blind to photoperiodic signals regardless of how much light hits it.

The phytochrome in a single leaf receives the light cue and initiates a mobile signal that propagates throughout the plant to induce or suppress flowering. This demonstrates that plants have evolved multiple, independent light-sensing systems optimized for different ecological tasks.

## Plant Vision Versus Animal Vision

Chamovitz draws a deliberate analogy between plant and animal light perception. While plants lack the imaging capability of animal eyes, they possess photoreceptor systems that detect:

- **Ultraviolet light** (UV-A and UV-B wavelengths), which humans cannot see
- **Blue light** (used for phototropism and stomatal opening)
- **Red light** (used for photoperiodism via phytochrome)
- **Far-red light** (used to detect shading by other plants and to reset phytochrome)
- **Infrared radiation** (detected as heat, influencing thermotropic responses)

Human vision covers only a narrow band of the electromagnetic spectrum. Plants effectively "see" a broader range of wavelengths than humans, using different receptor proteins for different purposes. The concept of "plant vision" is therefore not merely metaphorical. Plants possess the ability to detect, discriminate between, and respond to different wavelengths of light, which constitutes a legitimate form of environmental perception.

## The Mobile Floral Signal

The discovery that phytochrome in a single leaf can regulate flowering throughout the entire plant implies the existence of a mobile signaling molecule. When phytochrome in a leaf detects the appropriate light conditions, it triggers the production of a signal (later identified as the protein FT, or FLOWERING LOCUS T) that travels through the phloem to the shoot apex, where it activates the genetic program for flowering. This was one of the first documented examples of a long-distance signaling molecule in plants and remains one of the best-characterized.

## Short-Day and Long-Day Plants

Garner and Allard's work with Maryland Mammoth established a classification system that remains in use:

- **Short-day plants**: Flower when the continuous dark period exceeds a critical length. Examples include chrysanthemums, soybeans, and Cannabis (under natural conditions). These plants are more accurately described as "long-night plants."
- **Long-day plants**: Flower when the continuous dark period falls below a critical length. Examples include irises, barley, and spinach. These are more accurately "short-night plants."
- **Day-neutral plants**: Flower regardless of photoperiod, responding primarily to other cues such as plant age or temperature. Examples include tomatoes and rice.

## Agricultural Applications

These discoveries have major agricultural significance. Flower farmers routinely manipulate photoperiod to control production schedules. Chrysanthemums, which naturally flower in autumn as days shorten, can be kept vegetative through autumn and winter by providing brief red light pulses at night. Two weeks before the desired market date, the night lighting is stopped, and the entire crop flowers simultaneously. Indoor cannabis cultivators use the inverse approach, maintaining long light periods (18 hours or more) during vegetative growth and switching to short days (12 hours) to trigger flowering.
