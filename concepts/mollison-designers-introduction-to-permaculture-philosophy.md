# Introduction to Permaculture Philosophy

Permaculture, as articulated by Bill Mollison, is far more than a set of
agricultural techniques. It is a comprehensive design philosophy rooted in a
sense of personal responsibility for earth care. The philosophy emerges from
values and ethics, and from a deep conviction that we are in danger of
perishing from our own stupidity and lack of personal responsibility to life.

## Core Philosophical Statement

The philosophy behind permaculture is one of:

- **Working with, rather than against, nature**
- **Protracted and thoughtful observation** rather than protracted and
  thoughtless action
- **Looking at systems in all their functions**, rather than asking only one
  yield of them
- **Allowing systems to demonstrate their own evolutions**

This philosophy is close to Taoism in its emphasis on cooperation with natural
processes rather than domination of them.

## The Prime Directive

> The only ethical decision is to take responsibility for our own existence
> and that of our children. Make it now.

Most thinking people would agree that we have arrived at final and irrevocable
decisions that will abolish or sustain life on this earth. We can either ignore
the madness of uncontrolled industrial growth, or take the path to life and
survival.

## People as the Critical Resource

Mollison deeply believes that people are the only critical resource needed by
people. We ourselves, if we organise our talents, are sufficient to each other.
What is more, we will either survive together, or none of us will survive. To
fight between ourselves is as stupid and wasteful as it is to fight during
times of natural disasters, when everyone's cooperation is vital.

A person of courage today is a person of peace. The courage we need is to
refuse authority and to accept only personally responsible decisions. Like war,
growth at any cost is an outmoded and discredited concept.

## Cooperation, Not Competition

Life is cooperative rather than competitive, and life forms of very different
qualities may interact beneficially with one another and with their physical
environment. Even bacteria live by collaboration, accommodation, exchange, and
barter.

**Principle of Cooperation:** Cooperation, not competition, is the very basis
of existing life systems and of future survival.

## The Gaia Hypothesis and Tribal Wisdom

Lovelock sees the earth and the universe as a thought process, or as a
self-regulating, self-constructed and reactive system, creating and preserving
the conditions that make life possible. For every scientific statement on
energy, Aboriginal tribespeople of Australia have an equivalent statement on
life: life is a totality neither created nor destroyed, imagined as an egg from
which all tribes (life forms) issue and to which all return.

The totality of this outlook leads to a meaningful daily existence, in which
one sees each quantum of life eternally trying to perfect an expression towards
a future, and possibly transcendental, perfection.

## The Transition to Sustainable Systems

The characteristic that typifies all permanent agricultures is that the needs
of the system for energy are provided by that system. Modern crop agriculture
is totally dependent on external energies. Without permanent agriculture there
is no possibility of a stable social order.

There are three approaches to permanence:

1. **Feudal permanence** (peasant annual grain culture with nutrient hauling)
2. **Baronial permanence** (large pastoral holdings with low land use)
3. **Communal permanence** (forest-based, tribal reverence, intergenerational)

The further we depart from communal permanence, the greater the risk of
tyranny, feudalism, revolution, and the more work for less yield.

## Information and Integration

The present great shift in emphasis is on how the parts interact, how they
work together with each other. The development of different sciences and
disciplines has led to a proliferation of specialists and a consequent
inability to foresee results or to design integrated systems. Permaculture
seeks to reverse this fragmentation by designing integrated systems that
model themselves on natural ecosystems.

## Summary

The permaculture philosophy asks us to shift our fundamental question from
"What can I get from this land, or person?" to "What does this person, or
land, have to give if I cooperate with them?" The former leads to war and
waste; the latter leads to peace and plenty.

Most conflicts arise in how such questions are asked, not in the answers. We
should be alert to rephrase or refuse the "wrong" question. Unity in people
comes from a common adherence to a set of ethical principles, each going
their own way at their own pace, yet all leading to the same goals: that of a
living, complex, and sustainable earth. Those who agree on such ethics form a
global nation.
