## Salinity Control Strategies for Drylands

## Overview

Effective salinity control in dryland landscapes requires a layered approach combining earthworks, vegetation management, irrigation efficiency, and careful species selection. The specific combination of strategies depends on local soil conditions, slope, groundwater depth, and the mechanism of salinization at work. This page details the practical strategies available for treating and preventing salt-affected land.

## Integrated Strategy Framework

After assessing local conditions through back-hoe pits or piezometer installation, farmers and designers can choose from a set of complementary strategies:

### 1. Swales for Tree Establishment

Swales constructed at high elevations infiltrate rainwater and establish trees, which reduce net infiltration. Swales are particularly valuable aids to tree crops, concentrating fresh water at root zones. However, they are appropriate only if trees are planted along them to reduce net infiltration above deep clay layers. Without trees, swales can actually contribute to water table rise.

### 2. Interceptor Drains to Natural Waterways

Interceptor drains at midslope and on lowlands capture overland flow and shallow seepage, routing salt-affected and fresh water to natural waterways. This prevents the downhill cascade of water that drives both [[interceptor-banks]] on upper slopes, they form a comprehensive [[eucalyptus-camaldulensis]]* (river red gum) — general dryland use and lowland sewage/wastewater treatment
- *[[eucalyptus-sideroxylon]]* (ironbark) — dryland conditions
- *[[eucalyptus-wandoo]]* — specific dryland adaptation
- *[[eucalyptus-globulus]]* (blue gum) — cool, wet areas
- *[[casuarina-cunninghamii]]* — combined with *E. camaldulensis* for surplus wastewater in lowlands

Natural tea-tree vegetation in swampy valleys (*Leptospermum*, *Melaleuca*, *Banksia*) is killed by salt but can re-establish if uphill water effects are controlled.

Species selection requires local trials; no single set of species is universally applicable.

### 4. Subsurface Drains

Subsurface drains ending in natural waterways or interceptor drains are worthwhile where the crop is of high value. They remove excess water from the root zone without the evaporative losses associated with open drains.

### 5. Bores and Pumps

Bore and pump systems to lower water tables in irrigation areas should be regarded as a last resort. They may be supplanted by trees if headwater infiltration can be controlled. Mechanical pumping is effective for wetland salting but is insufficient for the scale of dryland salting via aquifer discharge.

## Stream Health Considerations

Whatever methods are used, salt levels will rise in streams as soils are flushed out. This is an unavoidable transitional effect. Fresh water for settlements may need to be supplied by rainwater tanks and hill sand storage dams until streams regain health — a process that could take decades.

## Irrigation Efficiency

A critical component of salinity control is eliminating wasteful water use that contributes to water table rise:

### Channel Replacement

In some areas of Australia, only 26% of channel water actually reaches the farm. On-farm use may waste 60% or more of the remainder through extensive spray or flood irrigation without close supervision. Replacing leaky channels with impermeable pipes addresses multiple problems:

- Eliminates land lost to open channels
- Prevents sand drift problems from channel banks
- Preserves water quality
- Reduces excess infiltration that raises water tables

### Irrigation Management

- Use sophisticated irrigation at root level
- Closely monitor irrigation water to minimum levels
- Avoid flood irrigation where possible — it can cause 60% evaporative water loss and 2–7 fold salt concentration increase
- In ridge and furrow systems, flood the ridges by creating beds with raised edges and flush salt down to furrows

## Salt-Tolerant Plant Species

Salt-tolerant plants can supplant affected crops as a transitional or complementary measure:

| Plant | Type |
|-------|------|
| Saltbushes (*Atriplex*) | Shrub |
| Bluebush (*Maireana* spp.) | Shrub |
| *Acacia* spp. | Tree |
| *Prosopis* spp. | Tree |
| Puccinellia grass | Grass |
| Tall wheat grass (*Agropyron*) | Grass |
| Wimmera rye grass (*Lolium rigidum*) | Grass |
| Samphire (*Crithmum*, *Salicornia*) | Herb |
| Buckthorn plantain (*Plantago cornopus*) | Herb (indicator species) |
| Sea barley grass (*[[hordeum-maritimum]]*) | Grass (indicator species) |

Plants in this group will thrive in soils with more than 0.4% sodium chloride. However, over-reliance on salt tolerance can delay investigation into root causes and treatments. Salt-tolerant planting should complement, not replace, earthworks and hydrological correction.

## Land Use Transition

### From Annual Crops to Perennial Systems

Changes from shallow-rooted annual or pasture crops to forage tree crops and fuelwood supply represent a long-term but currently profitable shift toward sustainable land use. Practices that exacerbate salinity include:

- Fallow or bare-soil cultivation
- Stubble burning (destroys humus, the key to soil crumb structure)
- Shallow-rooted crops (insufficient transpiration to control water tables)

### Economic Reassessment

Careful assessment of local factors, a return to more natural yields, and true economic assessment of farming costs versus aid to gardeners will likely reveal the benefits of closing down most marginal agriculture and returning the area to a naturally managed system of sustainable yield. It is cheaper to reclaim salted lands than to clear new country for short-term cropping, which merely extends the problem.

## Cold and Montane Desert Considerations

In high-altitude arid plateaus and interior valleys, additional challenges compound salinity management:

- Extreme temperature variation (hot days, icy nights) demands woolly or silvery-haired plants and animals for insulation
- Snow at high altitudes sublimates directly to vapor; swales for snow-melt and trees as snow traps are essential
- Stone piles and mulch preserve moderate air layers over plant roots
- Rocky soils are favorable growing sites as rocks help preserve ground warmth and moisture
- Growing seasons are measured in days (40–70 days common), making cold hardiness more important than drought hardiness
- Few succulents survive at high altitude — cold freezes thick-leaved plants and ruptures cells
- Housing requires Trombe walls, earth sheltering, massive fire-flue walls, and efficient stick-wood cookers

## Designers' Checklist: Broad Strategies

- Commence freshwater infiltration operations at the top of catchments, working downstream as plants establish
- Give upwind areas first attention for soil pitting and windbreak trees
- Establish plant nuclei or oases at favored sites, then extend along corridor plantings
- Spread pelleted seed over larger areas to await favorable seasons
- Never use deep bore or pumped well water beyond the ability of recharge areas to resupply
- Wherever water is infiltrated into the ground, plant trees to keep salt levels down
- Reduce dust in settlements via trees and soil pitting
- Supply ample vitamins via home garden vegetables and fruits
- Check plants, soils, and blood levels for essential minerals, especially zinc and iron
