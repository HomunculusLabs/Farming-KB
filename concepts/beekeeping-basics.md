# Beekeeping Basics

Beekeeping (apiculture) is the maintenance of honey bee colonies for [[faires-beekeeping-and-honey-production]], pollination services, and wax harvesting. Honey bees (Apis mellifera) are essential pollinators for many food crops, and a single hive can significantly improve fruit set and yields in your garden and orchard. This page covers the fundamentals for beginning beekeepers.

## Why Keep Bees

- **Pollination**: Honey bees dramatically increase fruit, vegetable, and seed set in home gardens and orchards. They pollinate apples, pears, berries, squash, cucumbers, melons, and many other crops.
- **Honey**: A healthy hive produces 30-60 pounds of surplus honey per year in most climates.
- **Beeswax**: Used for [[candle-making|candle]] s, salves, food wraps, woodworking, and cosmetics.
- **Propolis and pollen**: Valuable bee products with medicinal and nutritional properties.
- **Ecological benefit**: Supporting pollinators contributes to broader ecosystem health.
- **Education and connection**: Beekeeping connects you to the natural world and provides a deeper understanding of ecology and seasonal cycles.
- [[companion-planting-reference]] and [[food-forest-design]] systems benefit enormously from nearby bee colonies.

## Honey Bee Biology

### Colony Structure

A honey bee colony is a superorganism consisting of three types of bees:

- **Queen bee**: The single reproductive female. Lays up to 2,000 eggs per day during peak season. Lives 2-5 years. Her pheromones regulate colony cohesion and behavior.
- **Worker bees**: Sterile females that perform all colony tasks — foraging, nursing brood, building comb, guarding, cleaning, [[blesching-cannabis-fever-temperature-regulation]]. Live 4-6 weeks in summer, 4-6 months in winter.
- **Drones**: Male bees whose sole function is mating with virgin queens from other colonies. Drones do not forage or perform hive work. Ejected from the hive in fall.

### Life Cycle

1. **Egg**: Queen lays a single egg in each cell. Hatches in 3 days.
2. **Larva**: Fed by nurse bees. Worker larvae are fed royal jelly for the first 3 days, then pollen and honey. Queen larvae are fed royal jelly exclusively. Larval stage: 6 days (worker), 7 days (drone).
3. **Pupa**: Cell is capped. Larva transforms into adult. 12 days (worker), 14 days (drone).
4. **Adult emerges**: Total development time is 21 days (worker), 24 days (drone), 16 days (queen).

### Seasonal Colony Cycle

- **Spring**: Colony builds up population. Queen increases egg-laying. Foragers bring in nectar and pollen. Risk of swarming.
- **Summer**: Peak population (50,000-80,000 bees). Maximum honey production. Workers forage intensely.
- **Fall**: Colony reduces population. Drones are expelled. Bees focus on storing winter provisions.
- **Winter**: Colony clusters around the queen, vibrating flight muscles to maintain ~93°F in the cluster center. Survives on stored honey. No brood rearing for much of winter.

## Hive Types

### Langstroth Hive
The standard modern beehive, invented in 1851. Uses removable frames that maintain "bee space" (3/8 inch gap) — the space bees naturally leave between combs.

- **Components**: Bottom board, entrance reducer, deep boxes (hive bodies) for brood, medium or shallow boxes (supers) for honey, inner cover, outer (telescoping) cover.
- **Frames**: Wood or plastic frames with wax or plastic foundation. Bees build comb on the foundation.
- **Advantages**: Standardized equipment, widely available, easy to inspect and manage, modular expansion.
- **Standard configuration**: 2 deep boxes for brood + medium supers for honey.

### Top Bar Hive
A horizontal hive with no frames. Bees build comb from wooden bars suspended across the top.

- **Advantages**: No heavy lifting (horizontal management), bees build natural comb (no foundation), lower cost, simpler construction.
- **Disadvantages**: Less honey production, comb is fragile and cannot be extracted with a spinner, less standardized equipment.

### Warre Hive
A vertical [[top-bar-hive]]. Boxes are added to the bottom (nadiring) rather than the top (supering). Bees build natural comb downward.

- **Advantages**: Minimal disturbance to bees, natural comb, good [[query-best-kiwifruit-varieties-for-cold-climates]] (bees cluster at the top near warmth).
- **Disadvantages**: Lower honey production, heavy boxes when full, less common equipment.

**Recommendation for beginners**: Langstroth hives are the most practical choice due to standardized equipment, availability of supplies and education, and ease of management.

## Essential Equipment

### Protective Gear
- **Veil**: Protects face and neck. Essential even for experienced beekeepers.
- **Bee suit or jacket**: Full suit or half-jacket with attached veil. Provides protection from stings.
- **Gloves**: Leather or nitrile gloves. Many experienced beekeepers eventually stop using gloves for better dexterity, but beginners should wear them.
- **Boots**: Tuck pants into boots or use beekeeping boots.

### Tools
- **Smoker**: Produces cool smoke that calms bees by masking alarm pheromones. Fuel: dry grass, pine needles, burlap, wood chips. Essential equipment.
- **Hive tool**: Metal pry bar for separating frames, scraping propolis, and lifting boxes. You will use this every inspection.
- **Bee brush**: Soft brush for gently moving bees off frames and comb.
- **Frame grip**: Helps lift frames, especially when full and heavy.

### Other Equipment
- **Feeder**: Boardman entrance feeder, top feeder, or frame feeder for feeding sugar syrup during dearth or when establishing new colonies.
- **Queen excluder**: Metal or plastic grid that allows workers through but not the queen. Placed between brood boxes and honey supers to prevent the queen from laying eggs in honey comb.
- **Extractor**: Mechanical centrifuge that spins honey out of comb without destroying it. Shared or rented for small-scale beekeepers.

## Getting Started

### Ordering Bees

- **Package bees**: 2-3 pounds of worker bees plus a mated queen in a screened box. Shipped in spring. Installed into your equipment.
- **Nuc (nucleus colony)**: A small, established colony — 5 frames of drawn comb, brood, honey, pollen, and a laying queen. More expensive but much faster to establish than a package. Recommended for beginners.
- **Order in winter**: Suppliers sell out early. Place orders December-February for spring delivery.
- **Local sources**: Best option if available — bees adapted to your local climate.

### Hive Placement

- **Sun exposure**: Face the hive southeast for morning sun warmth. Some afternoon shade in hot climates.
- **Wind protection**: Block prevailing winter winds with a fence, building, or hedgerow.
- **Water source**: Provide a water source within 50 feet (birdbath with rocks, dripping hose). Otherwise bees will find your neighbor's pool.
- **Forage**: Ideally within 2 miles of diverse nectar and pollen sources.
- **Access**: Easy year-round access for inspections and honey harvest. Space for equipment storage.
- **Distance from neighbors**: Consider bee flight paths. Face the hive away from high-traffic areas. A fence or hedge forces bees to fly upward before reaching neighbors.

## Seasonal Management

### Spring (March-May)
- **Inspection**: Check colony health, queen presence (look for eggs), food stores, and population.
- **Feed**: If stores are low, feed 1:1 sugar syrup (1 part sugar : 1 part water by weight or volume) to stimulate comb building and brood rearing.
- **Swarm prevention**: Provide adequate space (add a super). Consider reversing hive bodies if the colony is building down.
- **Monitor for disease**: Check for varroa mites, nosema, foulbrood.

### Summer (June-August)
- **Honey supers**: Add supers as needed. A strong colony can fill a super in 1-2 weeks during a good nectar flow.
- **Pest management**: Monitor and treat for varroa mites. Monitor for small hive beetle, wax moths.
- **Water**: Ensure water source remains available.
- **Inspections**: Every 2-3 weeks. Check for queen, brood pattern, disease, and stores.

### Fall (September-November)
- **Harvest honey**: Remove and extract honey supers before temperatures drop. Leave enough honey for winter (60-80 pounds [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]], 40-60 pounds in mild climates).
- **Varroa treatment**: Critical fall treatment to ensure healthy winter bees. Oxalic acid vaporization or glycerin strips are common treatments.
- **Feeding**: Feed 2:1 sugar syrup (2 parts sugar : 1 part water) if stores are insufficient. Stop feeding when temperatures drop below 50°F.
- **Reduce entrance**: Install entrance reducer for winter to prevent mice and cold drafts.
- **Ventilation**: Ensure upper ventilation to prevent moisture buildup in winter (condensation kills bees faster than cold).

### Winter (December-February)
- **Minimal disturbance**: Open the hive only when temperatures are above 50°F and only for quick checks.
- **Emergency feeding**: If the colony is starving (bees at the top of the frames, cluster small), place dry sugar (Mountain Camp method) or fondant above the inner cover.
- **Equipment maintenance**: Clean, repair, and paint equipment during the off-season.
- **Education**: Read, take classes, join your local beekeeping association.

## Common Problems

### Varroa Mites (Varroa destructor)
The most serious threat to honey bees worldwide. Mites feed on adult bees and brood, transmitting viruses that weaken and kill colonies.

**Monitoring**: Sugar shake test, alcohol wash, or sticky board. Threshold for treatment is typically 3 mites per 100 bees (3%).
**Treatment**: Oxalic acid vaporization, formic acid (MAQS), amitraz (Apivar), or mechanical methods (drone brood removal). Rotate treatments to prevent resistance.

### Small Hive Beetle (Aethina tumida)
Beetles that lay eggs in hives. Larvae tunnel through comb, defecating and fermenting honey, which runs out of the hive.
**Management**: Maintain strong colonies that can [[query-how-do-plants-defend-themselves-chemically]]. Beetle traps (oil traps, beetle blasters). Keep apiary clean and remove debris.

### Wax Moths
Larvae that destroy comb, especially in weak colonies or stored equipment.
