## Quarter-Acre Farmer Training

Long before any formal institution existed, Fukuoka's own farm on **Shikoku** served as
a living prototype. Young people from Japanese cities and from other countries came to
live what Fukuoka described as a "crude, simple life" growing crops without electricity
or running water. They were self-sufficient on brown rice and vegetables, sleeping in
mud-walled huts nestled in the hilltop orchard overlooking the Inland Sea.

Fukuoka called these young people **"quarter-acre farmers."** The name captured the
modest scale at which he believed meaningful agricultural reform could begin. One did
not need vast tracts of land or expensive technology. A [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]] farming, was sufficient to sustain a human life while restoring
the soil and supporting local ecosystems.

This informal community was the embryo of the International Nature University — proof
that the concept was not abstract idealism but a lived practice already producing
results.

## The Paris Peace March

Fukuoka's involvement in the **Paris Peace March** was a natural extension of his
agricultural philosophy. For Fukuoka, peace was not a political arrangement to be
negotiated between nations but a way of life rooted in humanity's relationship with
the natural world. War, industrial agriculture, and environmental destruction were, in
his view, symptoms of the same fundamental error: the belief that human beings must
conquer and control nature rather than live within its patterns.

The peace march provided Fukuoka with a platform to articulate his conviction that
"the only road for man is to do nothing" and that "if everyone were to do nothing, the
world would become a place of peace and plenty." This was not laziness or nihilism but
a radical reorientation — an insistence that human prosperity comes from ceasing to
intervene destructively in natural systems.
