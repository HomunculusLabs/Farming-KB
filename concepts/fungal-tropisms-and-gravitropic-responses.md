## Fungal Tropisms and Gravitropic Responses

## Definition and Scope

Fungal tropisms are growth responses in which a fungus orients its development toward or away from a directional stimulus. Unlike motile organisms that reposition themselves, fungi respond to environmental cues by polarizing their tip growth — directing the extension of hyphae, rhizomorphs, or fruiting body structures along gradients of light, gravity, chemicals, or physical contact. These tropic responses are fundamental to how fungi colonize substrates, locate nutrients, and position reproductive structures for effective [[query-how-do-plants-use-chemical-signals-to-defend-against-insects]] during infection.

The chemotropic response involves G-protein-coupled receptors at the hyphal tip membrane, which detect extracellular signals and activate intracellular signaling cascades that redirect vesicle delivery and cytoskeletal organization.

### Thigmotropism and Contact Responses

Thigmotropism is the response to physical contact or surface texture. Fungi exhibit several contact-related behaviors:

- **Hyphal avoidance**: Hyphae growing on solid surfaces frequently turn to avoid contacting other hyphae from the same individual (self-non-self recognition via [[fungal-hyphae]] in response to electric fields. Both natural (endogenous bioelectric currents) and applied electric fields influence hyphal growth direction. In experiments, field strengths of 1–10 V/cm can redirect hyphal tip growth, with the hyphae typically growing toward the cathode. This response may be relevant to the endogenous electric currents measured along growing hyphae and within fruiting bodies.

### Aerotropism

Aerotropism is growth toward oxygen or air. This response is particularly important in:

- **Stipe elongation**: Mushrooms in low-oxygen environments elongate their stipes more rapidly to reach better-aerated zones.
- **Hyphal emergence**: Submerged mycelium produces aerial hyphae directed toward the air-substrate interface.
- **Perithecial neck orientation**: In some ascomycetes, the necks of perithecia (fruiting bodies) orient toward air channels to facilitate spore release.

## Molecular Mechanisms

### The Cytoskeleton and Tip Growth

Fungal tropisms are executed through the **Spitzenkörper** — a dense, vesicle-rich organelle at the hyphal tip that serves as the vesicle supply center. The Spitzenkörper position determines the direction of tip extension. Tropic signals are transduced into repositioning of the Spitzenkörper, which redirects vesicle delivery containing cell wall synthases and membrane material.

The actin cytoskeleton plays a central role:

- **Actin cables** serve as tracks for myosin-driven vesicle transport to the Spitzenkörper.
- **Arp2/3 complex** mediates actin branching near the tip.
- **Formins** nucleate linear actin filaments that guide tip extension.

Microtubules provide the structural framework and are involved in long-distance transport of organelles, including the statolith-like bodies involved in graviperception.

### Signal Transduction

Tropic signal transduction involves:

1. **Perception**: Receptor proteins (photoreceptors, mechanosensors, GPCRs) detect the directional stimulus.
2. **Signal amplification**: Second messengers (Ca²⁺, cAMP, IP₃) amplify and propagate the signal.
3. **Cytoskeletal reorganization**: Calcium gradients and Rho-family GTPases reorganize the actin cytoskeleton asymmetrically.
4. **Vesicle redirection**: Vesicle trafficking is redirected to one side of the tip, shifting the Spitzenkörper and changing the growth axis.
