# Spore Syringe Inoculation

Spore syringe inoculation is the process of introducing fungal spores into a prepared substrate to initiate mycelial colonization. In the PF TEK method, this is accomplished using a **spore syringe** — a sterile medical syringe containing a suspension of mushroom spores in distilled water. The inoculation step is the most critical point in the cultivation process for [[cervantes-cannabis-contamination-prevention]], as it is the moment when the sterile substrate is exposed to the external environment.

## The Spore Syringe

### Composition

A spore syringe consists of:

- **Spores**: Reproductive cells harvested from the gills of a mature mushroom
- **Sterile distilled water**: The suspension medium
- **Medical syringe** (typically 10-12cc): The delivery device
- **Hypodermic needle** (typically 18-20 gauge): For piercing jar lids

The spores are microscopic (approximately 10-15 micrometers in diameter for *Psilocybe cubensis*) and are suspended in the water at a concentration designed to deliver millions of spores per milliliter. A single 10cc syringe contains enough spores to inoculate 10-12 half-pint jars.

### Spore Syringe Quality

The PF TEK emphasized that spore syringes should be:
- **Clean**: Free from bacterial and fungal contamination
- **Viable**: Spores must be alive and capable of germination
- **Fresh**: Older spore syringes may have reduced germination rates

Fanaticus offered a money-back guarantee on his spore syringes, replacing or refunding any that failed to germinate. This guarantee reflected confidence in proper [[pf-tek-spore-syringe-preparation-and-inoculation]] storage techniques.

### Legal Status

Under US federal law at the time of publication, **psilocybin mushroom spores were not scheduled** as [[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]]. Spores do not contain psilocybin or psilocin, placing them in a legal gray area. However, cultivation produces material containing scheduled substances. Fanaticus distributed spores "for scientific and entheogenic research only."

## Inoculation Technique

### Preparation

Before inoculation, the following preparations are essential:

1. **Allow jars to cool completely** after sterilization — hot jars can kill spores on contact and create thermal currents that draw in contaminants
2. **Prepare a clean workspace** — wipe surfaces with alcohol, minimize air currents (close windows, turn off fans)
3. **Assemble tools**: Spore syringe, alcohol lamp or lighter, paper towels with alcohol
4. **Wash hands thoroughly** with antibacterial soap

### The Alcohol Flaming Technique

The PF TEK specifies a flame sterilization technique for the needle between each jar inoculation:

1. **Shake the spore syringe** vigorously to distribute spores evenly in suspension (spores settle over time)
2. **Flame the needle tip** until it glows red-hot using an alcohol lamp or lighter
3. **Allow the needle to cool** for several seconds (a hot needle will kill spores; some cultivators touch the needle to the sterile substrate surface to cool it)
4. **Insert the needle** through the inoculation hole in the jar lid
5. **Inject spore solution** (approximately 1cc per inoculation point) against the glass at an angle, allowing it to run down the inside surface onto the substrate
6. **Withdraw the needle** and **reflame** before the next jar
7. **Cover inoculation holes** with tape or leave the dry vermiculite barrier exposed (the barrier provides sufficient filtration)

### Angle Injection

Fanaticus recommended injecting the spore solution against the inside glass of the jar at an angle rather than straight down into the substrate. This technique:

- Distributes spores across a larger surface area of the substrate
- Allows the cultivator to observe where the spore solution lands
- Creates multiple colonization points visible through the glass
- Reduces the risk of creating a deep pocket of moisture in the substrate center

### Inoculation Without Lids

The PF TEK also described inoculating jars without lids in a still-air environment. The needle is flamed as usual, spore solution is injected onto the dry vermiculite barrier, and the lid is replaced immediately. This approach requires extremely clean conditions and is generally considered riskier than through-lid inoculation.

## The Canning Jar Lid Configuration

### Standard PF Lid Setup

The PF TEK uses standard canning jar lids modified with **four inoculation holes**:

1. The metal disc of a standard two-piece canning lid is punctured with 4 holes (using a small nail or drill)
2. Holes are spaced evenly around the lid, roughly 1/4 inch from the edge
3. The metal ring holds the disc in place on the jar
4. The dry vermiculite barrier inside the jar filters air through the holes

### Lid Tightness

Fanaticus addressed the question of whether jar lids should be **loose or tight** during colonization:

- During **sterilization**: Lids should be loose enough to allow steam penetration but secure enough to keep the vermiculite barrier in place
- During **incubation**: Lids should be loose to allow gas exchange; mycelium produces CO₂ which must escape
- During **fruiting**: Lids are removed entirely when cakes are transferred to the fruiting chamber

Many cultivators place a layer of aluminum foil over the lids during sterilization to prevent water from entering the inoculation holes, then remove the foil after cooling.

## Spore Germination

### Process

After inoculation, spores germinate through the following process:

1. Spores absorb water and swell
2. Germ tubes emerge from the spore surface (typically within 3-7 days at room temperature)
3. Germ tubes elongate and branch to form **hyphae** — the individual filamentous cells of fungal mycelium
4. Hyphae from multiple spores interconnect to form a **mycelial network**
5. The mycelial network expands through the substrate, digesting nutrients

### Visible Colonization

Mycelial growth typically becomes visible within **3-5 days** as white, thread-like growth spreading from inoculation points. Full colonization of a half-pint jar takes **14-21 days** depending on temperature and conditions.

### Optimal Temperature

Colonization proceeds optimally at **room temperature** (approximately 70-78°F / 21-26°C). Higher temperatures accelerate colonization but also increase the risk of contamination. Fanaticus recommended against using external heat sources for incubation, noting that room temperature provides adequate conditions without the added contamination risk of heating equipment.

## Spore Printing and Syringe Preparation

### Spore Printing

Mature mushrooms with open veils can create **spore prints** by placing the cap gills-down on clean foil, covering with a glass for 12-24 hours. The resulting dark purple-brown print (*P. cubensis*) contains millions of spores for future cultivation.

### Syringe Preparation

Spore prints are converted to syringes by scraping spores into sterile boiled water and drawing the suspension into a sterile syringe. Properly stored syringes remain viable for **several months to over a year**, though germination rates decline with age.

## Non-Germination of Spores

The PF TEK addressed the common problem of spores failing to germinate:

- **Dead spores**: Old or improperly stored spores may lose viability
- **Excessive heat**: Exposure to temperatures above 100°F can kill spores
- **Contamination**: Bacterial contamination may outcompete fungal germination
- **Insufficient moisture**: Overly dry substrate prevents spore hydration
- **Chemical inhibitors**: Tap water chlorine or substrate contaminants may inhibit germination

If no growth is visible after 14 days, jars should be carefully inspected for contamination. If clean, a second inoculation may be attempted, or the substrate should be discarded and the process restarted with fresh materials.
