# Fungal Molecular Community Analysis

Molecular methods have revolutionized the study of fungal community structure
and diversity, overcoming the severe limitations of traditional
culture-dependent approaches. It is well documented that, at best, perhaps
only 5% of environmental fungi can be cultivated, meaning that reliance on
morphological and physiological identification of cultivable species will
never elucidate the inherent complexities of community dynamics. Culture-
independent approaches are now essential tools for characterizing fungal
communities in soils, rocks, plant roots, and other environmental substrates.

## Limitations of Culture-Dependent Methods

Traditional cultivation-based methods for assessing fungal diversity are
generally recognized as insufficient because:

- Many environmental species are refractory to laboratory cultivation,
  including some biotrophs (rust and smut fungi), many basidiomycetes,
  arbuscular endomycorrhizas (particularly the Glomales), and rock-dwelling
  fungi with low metabolic activity.
- There is a tendency to underestimate the complexity and diversity of
  fungal communities due to bias associated with traditional methods.
- Species that grow rapidly on artificial media may outcompete and obscure
  slower-growing or more specialized taxa.

Nevertheless, cultivation-dependent studies have proven useful for
characterizing some communities. For example, microscopical analyses of
phototrophic epilithic communities from the Roman Catacombs found that
Sporotrichum and Aspergillus species were the most frequently encountered
fungal isolates.

## DNA Extraction from Environmental Samples

The majority of culture-independent studies rely on analysis of rRNA genes
amplified from DNA extracted directly from environmental samples. Soil DNA
extraction kits are available, but are often unsuccessful for rock samples.
Extracting DNA from mineral substrates requires extraction of high-molecular-
mass DNA, free from inhibitors, that represents the full microbial consortium
present.

DNA extraction methods typically include combinations of bead beating,
detergents, enzymatic lysis, and solvent extraction. However, bias remains
a problem: lysis efficiency may vary between different species and between
spores and mycelia. Different extraction methods (soft lysis, hard lysis,
blending, and cation exchange) can yield significantly different results as
measured by community fingerprinting profiles.

## PCR-Based Approaches and Target Regions

PCR amplification of specific regions of the ribosomal RNA gene is the main
methodology for characterizing fungal populations. Characterization of fungal
community members usually involves PCR amplification of either:

- **SSU (18S) rRNA gene**: widely used but has limited sequence resolution
  due to relative lack of evolutionary variation between closely related
  fungal species.
- **ITS (Internal Transcribed Spacer) region**: located between the 18S and
  28S rRNA genes and incorporating the 5.8S rRNA gene. Has a higher degree
  of heterogeneity between species, allowing improved community resolution.

Problems with PCR-based approaches include PCR bias from cycling conditions,
formation of chimeric sequences, introduction of sequence error, and primer
choice. Additionally, the number of copies of the rRNA operon varies between
different fungal species, making quantification from mixed communities
difficult.

## Phylogenetic Methods: Cloning and Sequencing

Phylogenetic methods identify the species present in a community. Fine-scale
information is obtained by cloning the amplified rRNA gene pool, followed by
sequencing and database comparison. Although prokaryotic bioinformatic
databases are more extensive, fungal databases are growing in size.

Ectomycorrhizal fungal communities have been studied extensively by molecular
techniques. Studies of rhizosphere communities have revealed marked
differences caused by host plant health: healthy plants had a higher
proportion of Homobasidiomycete clones, whereas diseased samples had more
Sordariomycetes. Rhizospheres of young maize plants selected for the
ascomycete order Pleosporales, while senescent maize plants hosted different
Ascomycetes and basidiomycetous yeasts.

Fungal diversity in grassland soils studied by 18S rDNA cloning followed by
ARDRA (amplified ribosomal DNA restriction analysis) showed that Zygomycetes,
Ascomycetes, and Basidiomycetes were all present. However, the 18S region
was not variable enough to distinguish between closely related species,
highlighting the need for more variable genetic markers.

## Fingerprinting Techniques

Fingerprinting methods allow multiple sample analysis to study spatial and
temporal variation in community composition:

- **DGGE/TGGE**: Denaturing/Temperature gradient gel electrophoresis
  exploits differences in sequence composition to separate community members.
  Used to study communities from decomposing leaves, coniferous forests,
  and wheat rhizospheres.
- **RISA**: Ribosomal intergenic spacer analysis separates gene fragments by
  length differentiation of the ITS region.
- **ARISA**: Automated ribosomal intergenic spacer analysis is a
  high-throughput, automated version of RISA using fluorescently labelled
  primers. Community complexity has been reported to range from approximately
  30 to over 200 fungal peaks per profile.
- **T-RFLP**: Terminal restriction fragment length polymorphism provides
  high-resolution automated capillary electrophoresis profiles.

These techniques have somewhat superseded cloning-based approaches for large
sample numbers due to the laborious and costly nature of gene fragment
cloning.

## Application to Lithic Communities

Studies addressing the molecular analysis of fungal communities in rocks and
minerals remain extremely rare. This represents a significant gap in
geomycological research, given the ubiquity and ecological importance of
[[endolithic-fungi-rock-colonizing-communities]] and epilithic
fungal communities. Lithic fungal DGGE profiles generated from DNA extracted
directly from sandstone demonstrated considerable variations in banding
patterns between samples, suggesting that rock-dwelling fungal populations
are highly diverse and that selection of indigenous populations is a complex
process.
