## Thermotolerant and Thermophilic Fungi

### Temperature Ranges and Definitions

The vast majority of known fungi are mesophilic, growing within a temperature range of approximately 5–35°C, with optima between 20–30°C. Beyond this range, two categories of heat-adapted fungi are recognized:

- **[[thermophilic-fungi]]** are defined as those that grow only above 20°C and continue to grow at temperatures exceeding 50°C (Cooney and Emerson 1964; Crisan 1973). They are obligately adapted to warm environments and cannot proliferate at ambient temperatures.
- **Thermotolerant fungi** are capable of growth below 20°C but also tolerate and grow at temperatures of 40–50°C and above. This broader thermal range gives them greater ecological flexibility.

### Habitats and Distribution

Thermophilic and thermotolerant fungi have been isolated from a diverse array of heated environments: compost piles, mushroom beds, municipal waste processing facilities, wood chip piles, bird and alligator nests, coal tips, and even volcanic hot springs. Their distribution is not limited to obviously heated substrates — they are ubiquitous in soils wherever solar radiation heats the surface, making them ecologically significant in arid, tropical, and temperate regions alike.

A reservoir of thermophilic propagules persists in soils year-round, even in temperate climates where surface heating is seasonal (Tansey and Jack 1976). This persistence ensures rapid colonization when conditions become favorable.

### Taxonomic Distribution and Diversity

Thermophilism is strongly developed within the Zygomycetes, Ascomycetes, and mitosporic ascomycetes, but is notably absent from the Basidiomycetes (Cooney and Emerson 1964). Approximately 70 thermophilic species have been estimated globally (Abdullah and Al Bader 1990; Mouchacca 1995), of which more than 20 are considered cosmopolitan in distribution.

## Psychrotolerant and Psychrophilic Fungi

### The Cold Biosphere

Cold environments represent the dominant condition on Earth: approximately 80% of the biosphere maintains temperatures between −3 and −7°C, and 90% of marine waters are below 5°C (Gounot 1986). Fungi inhabiting these environments face fundamental physiological challenges including reduced membrane fluidity, slower enzymatic reaction rates, and ice crystal formation.

### Definitions

Two categories of cold-adapted fungi are distinguished:

- **True psychrophiles** are restricted to permanently cold habitats. They have temperature optima reaching up to 16°C, maxima no higher than 20°C, and the ability to grow at 0°C or below.
- **Psychrotolerant fungi** possess maximum growth temperatures above 20°C but retain the ability to grow at 10°C and below. This group is far more common than obligate psychrophiles and includes many genera also found in temperate environments: *Alternaria*, *Cladosporium*, *Keratinomyces*, *Leptomitus*, *Mucor*, *Penicillium*, *Rhizopus*, and *Typhula* (Gounot 1986).

### Community Shifts with Temperature

Temperature exerts a profound influence on fungal community composition. Carreiro and Koske (1992) demonstrated that forest soil communities at 0°C are completely different from those at 25°C: cold conditions select for *Mortierella* and *Mucor* species, while warmer temperatures favor mitosporic ascomycetes. This temperature-dependent community turnover has important implications for nutrient cycling in seasonally cold ecosystems.

### Glacial and Polar Habitats

Petrini et al. (1992b) isolated 18 fungal strains from glacial cones, snow fields, and a glacial pond in Switzerland, recovering 5 mitosporic ascomycete species and 5 sterile strains. In polar regions, fungal occurrence is tightly coupled to water availability. In the dry interior valleys of Antarctica, yeasts predominate, with *Cryptococcus albidus* being the most prevalent species (Cameron et al. 1976).

Broader Antarctic surveys have documented 2 phycomycetes, 2 ascomycetes, 27 mitosporic ascomycetes (with *Chrysosporium*, *Penicillium*, and *Phialophora* forming the majority), and 10 yeast species across diverse cold habitats.

### Snow Molds

Snow molds are a ecologically and economically significant group of low-temperature-tolerant, soil-borne fungi that damage grasses and cereals beneath snow cover (Lebeau and Logsdon 1958; Nelson and Sturges 1982). Common species include *Coprinus psychromorbidus*, *Microdochium nivale*, *Myriosclerotinia borealis*, *Typhula ishikariensis*, and other *Typhula* species (Hsiang et al. 1999). These pathogens exploit the insulated, humid microclimate beneath snow to attack dormant plant tissues during winter and early spring.

## Heavy-Metal Tolerant Fungi

### Essential and Toxic Metals

Several heavy metals — including copper (Cu), nickel (Ni), and zinc (Zn) — are essential trace elements required for fungal metabolism as enzyme cofactors and structural components. However, overabundance of these and other metals becomes toxic. Metals of particular concern in environmental contamination include class B and borderline elements: cadmium (Cd), copper (Cu), lead (Pb), nickel (Ni), mercury (Hg), silver (Ag), and zinc (Zn).

### Effects on Soil Mycota

Metal contamination of soils leads to measurable declines in overall fungal diversity and significant shifts in community structure (Brown and Hall 1989). Sensitive species are eliminated, while tolerant species may persist or even become dominant. Notably, some metal-tolerant fungi can be isolated from the most heavily contaminated sites, suggesting strong selective pressure favoring tolerance mechanisms.

### Tolerance in Uncontaminated Environments

An important finding is that metal tolerance is not exclusively a product of contaminated environments. Cu-tolerant fungi have been recovered from uncontaminated sites (Arnebrant et al. 1987), and lead sensitivities of *Aureobasidium pullulans* strains were found to be similar whether isolated from contaminated or uncontaminated environments (Mowll and Gadd 1985). Similar results were reported for Cu-contaminated soils (Yamamoto et al. 1985).

These observations suggest that intrinsic metal tolerance mechanisms — including extracellular sequestration, intracellular chelation, cell-wall binding, and active efflux — are widely distributed among soil fungi and may be maintained in populations even in the absence of strong selective pressure from contamination. This pre-existing tolerance has important implications for [[bioremediation-strategies]], as it expands the pool of candidate organisms beyond those isolated from contaminated sites.

## Summary

Fungi have colonized virtually every stressful environment on Earth through a combination of three fundamental life strategies — competitive, stress-tolerant, and ruderal — that can be deployed flexibly depending on conditions. Thermophilic and thermotolerant species exploit heated substrates from compost to volcanic springs, psychrotolerant and [[psychrophilic-fungi]] dominate the vast cold biosphere from glacial ice to polar soils, and metal-tolerant populations persist in both contaminated and pristine environments. The ecological understanding of these adaptations directly informs their application in industry, agriculture, and environmental management.
