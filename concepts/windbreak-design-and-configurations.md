# Windbreak Design and Configurations

Windbreaks are structures of trees and shrubs planted to reduce wind speed and modify microclimates for agricultural benefit. In [[permaculture-design]], windbreaks serve multiple functions: protecting crops from mechanical damage, reducing soil erosion, conserving water, trapping snow moisture, and significantly increasing crop yields.

## Critical Design Factors

The **height** and **density** (or permeability) of windbreak trees are the most critical factors determining shelter effectiveness. Windbreaks should be spread at no more than 20 times the hedgerow height apart in areas with severe winds. For wind-sensitive tree crops, continuous interplant windbreaks may be necessary.

**Permeability** is essential for reducing frost risk and extending the windbreak effect. Species should permit 40–70% of wind through, preventing turbulent wind overturn on the leeward side. The ideal windbreak height is one-fifth of the space between windbreaks, though it remains effective for low crops at one-thirtieth of the interspace.

Windbreak length must exceed the length of the field being protected, as wind funnels around the ends in regular flow patterns.

## Windbreak Configuration Types

### A. Dense Windbreak with Bare Stem
- **Species:** Cupressus, Pinus, Casuarina
- **Effects:** Good summer shade for livestock; poor or useless winter shelter. Clumps on knolls allow animals to escape heat while reducing flies and mosquitoes.

### B. Alternate (Zig-Zag) Permeable Planting
- **Species:** Araucaria, Pinus, Casuarina
- **Effects:** Excellent "front-line" seafront system to reduce salt burn and provide shelter for denser trees on islands and coasts.

### C. Compound High-Density Windbreak
- **Ground cover:** Convulvulus, Phyla (Lippia), Mesembryanthemum
- **Low shrubs:** [[echium-fastuosum]], wormwood
- **Shrubs:** Coprosma repens
- **Trees:** Lycium, Cedrus, Cupressus
- **Effects:** Best protection for eroding beaches — lifts wind smoothly over the beach berm and traps sand. Also effective as a dust trap in dust-storm areas.

### D. Permeable Low Leguminous Hedgerow
- **Species:** Acacia, Leucaena, Prosopis, Albizia, Glyricidia, tagasaste
- **Effects:** Good for grass and crop growth; allows air movement to reduce frosts.

### E. In-Crop Windbreak Systems
1. **Savannah-style:** Open-spaced light-crowned trees in crop or pasture. Excellent forage in arid areas, especially when trees provide fodder. Species: fodder palms, Inga, Acacia, tagasaste, baobab, Prosopis.

2. **Complete crown cover:** Interplanting fruit trees with leguminous trees as shelter and mulch, with Casuarinas as borders. Suited to humid climates and irrigated areas. Species: fruits (palms, avocado, Inga, banana, citrus); legumes (tagasaste, Acacia, Albizia, Glyricidia, Leucaena); borders (Casuarina, [[phoenix-canariensis]], Prosopis).

## Integral Windbreak Strategy

For tree [[mollison-designers-windbreak-configurations-for-crops-and-orchards]], windbreaks can be composed of trees compatible with the protected system, making the windbreak integral with the crop itself. This strategy has proven successful for wind and frost moderation in susceptible crops such as citrus, avocado, macadamia nuts, and chestnuts. Hardy species like Acacia, Casuarina, Glyricidia, tagasaste, and Prosopis — all of which fix nitrogen or phosphates, provide firewood, radiate heat, and shelter crops — serve as interplant barriers within the crop.

## Effects on Soil and Water

### Soil Conservation
Windbreaks reduce dust and blown sand to **1/1000th** of unsheltered situations within 10 times the windbreak height (Chang, 1968). Soil loss at 20 times windbreak height is still 18% of open situations, which is significant when windstorms can cause losses of 8–40 tonnes per hectare.

### Water Evaporation
Evaporation can be **halved** in strong winds (32 km/h or more) for distances up to 10 times windbreak height. Over 24 km/h winds, a 30% gain in soil water conservation is achieved.

### Snow Moisture
Windbreaks of types A or B trap snow on fields. Snow depth in winter closely correlates to dry matter yields in spring and summer, making windbreaks essential in snow-field areas. Savannah configurations create spring soil moisture traps wherever snow blows across the landscape.

## Crop Yield Increases

[[windbreak-establishment-and-benefits]] on exposed sites produces remarkable yield increases:
- **Avocado:** up to 100% increase
- **Alfalfa:** 60–70% increase
- **Corn:** 45% increase
- **Wheat:** 30% increase
- **Lettuce and low crops:** 7–18% increase
- **Garden vegetables** (cucurbit, tomato, potato): great benefit

Effects are naturally less pronounced in sheltered situations or areas of normally low wind speed. A spiral pattern of low leguminous windbreak in crop areas allows continual tractor access while providing wind protection.

## Ecological Significance
The principles described in relation to windbreak design and configurations
are fundamental to understanding how natural systems maintain equilibrium.
Forests and vegetation play critical roles in water cycling, temperature
regulation, and atmospheric processes that sustain regional climate patterns.

## Practical Applications
These concepts apply directly to permaculture design, agroforestry systems,
[[ozier-pdc-hydro-cycle-and-watershed-management]]. Understanding the interactions between vegetation,
water, and atmosphere enables designers to create more resilient and productive
landscapes that work with natural processes rather than opposing them.
