# Thermal Belts, Thermosiphons, and Convective Heat

The movement and storage of heat in landscapes follows predictable physical laws that can be harnessed for building design, agricultural planning, and microclimate creation. Understanding convection loops, thermal belts, and thermosiphon effects allows permaculture designers to position structures and plantings for optimal thermal performance.

## Convection Loops

Convection is the transfer of heat by the movement of fluids (air or water) over heated surfaces. The fundamental principle is simple: **heated air or water is less dense and rises, while cooled fluid is denser and sinks**.

### Atmospheric Convection

In the atmosphere, columns of heated air over land ascend as an "[[overbeck-jet]]." At the top of this column, condensation and rain may occur as the rising air is cooled in the upper atmosphere. Such **convective rains** are responsible for the mosaic rainfall pattern characteristic of deserts, where isolated thunderstorms create patchy precipitation.

### Valley Winds

Convection operates prominently in valley climates:
- During the day, valley floors and slopes heat up, causing air to rise along slopes (anabatic winds)
- At night, cooled air from hillsides drains downward to pool in valley floors (katabatic winds)
- This daily cycle creates predictable air movement patterns that affect frost distribution, fog formation, and plant growing conditions

### Indoor Convection Limitations

Convection loops will **not** occur effectively in closed rooms, where hot air (at 8-10°C higher temperature) stratifies into a quiet layer below ceilings. Air is difficult to heat and stores little heat, making air convection an inefficient method for heating building interiors despite being the main engine of global atmospheric circulation.

## Thermosiphons

A **thermosiphon** is a closed-loop circulation system driven by buoyancy, where heated fluid in a pipe or duct rises naturally and is replaced by cooler fluid descending on the other side of the loop.

### Design Requirements

For a thermosiphon to function:
- A minimum height difference (rise) of **40 cm (about 18 inches)** must be built into the loop between the heated section and the top of the loop
- Any greater height is also effective
- The fluid must be contained in pipes or ducts to maintain directed flow

### Applications in Permaculture

Thermosiphons are useful for:
- **Transferring heat** from solar ponds or flat plate collectors to home radiators or hot water tanks
- **Self-regulated circulation** — no pumps needed if collectors are sited at least 0.5 m (1.6 feet) below the storage or use points
- **Refrigeration** — thermosiphon principles drive absorption refrigerators powered by flames or heat sources

The key design principle is to **site heat collectors below** the point of use, so that natural thermosiphon circulation provides self-regulating heat transfer without mechanical pumps or external energy input.

## Thermal Belts

The **thermal belt** is a critical concept for settlement and agricultural siting in hill country. It refers to the midslope position that offers the most moderate temperatures, lying between:

- **Chilly hilltops** — exposed to high winds and rapid radiation loss
- **Cold valley floors** — where dense cool air pools at night (frost pockets)

### Why Thermal Belts Work

Sun-facing slopes in the thermal belt:
- **Absorb more heat** from direct solar radiation due to optimal angle of incidence
- **Drain off cold air** at night — cold air flows downhill and away from the midslope
- **Lie above the frost line** — avoiding the coldest temperatures that accumulate in valleys
- **Are sheltered from extremes** — protected from both hilltop exposure and valley cold pooling

### Thermal Belt Altitudes

The altitude of thermal belts varies dramatically by region:

| Region | Thermal Belt Altitude |
|--------|----------------------|
| Hill country / mountains | 1,000-5,000 m (3,280-16,400 feet) |
| Lower hill slopes | 100-200 m (330-650 feet) |
| Hot deserts (mesa slopes) | 10-15 m (33-49 feet) above valley floor |

### Locating the Thermal Belt

Each situation needs specific local information, obtainable from:
- **Local anecdotes and farmer knowledge** — long-time residents know where frost settles
- **Observation of existing plants** — the transition from frost-sensitive to frost-hardy species marks the thermal belt boundary
- **Trial plantings** of frost-susceptible species to map frost boundaries before committing to permanent plantings

## Advected Heat and Cold

**Advected** (exotic or out-of-area) warmth and cold are carried by winds travelling between regions of different temperatures:

- **Advection fogs** — warm moist air moving inland from coasts condenses when encountering cooler land surfaces
- **Advection frosts** — cold air flowing down mountain slopes to pool in hollows and valleys
- **Warm air invasions** — when warm advected air enters cool areas, moisture condensation occurs, critical to precipitation in forests but a nuisance in enclosed buildings

### Managing Advected Moisture

Designers should:
- Attempt to bring only **dry warm air** into wooden houses
- Provide ways to **direct condensation moisture** to the house exterior
- Understand that warm advected air entering cool forest environments is a primary moisture source for cloud forest precipitation

## Heat Transfer Mechanisms Summary

### Low-Grade Heat: Conduction
Transmitted by direct solid-to-solid or fluid-to-fluid contact. Insulation is effective at trapping conducted heat. Basis of slab floor heating efficiency.

### Medium-Grade Heat: Convection
Transmitted by the movement of fluids and gases — air, wind, water. Draft-proofing conserves convective heat losses. Heated fluids rise.

### High-Grade Heat: Radiation
Travels by straight-line radiation across space. Can only be conserved by reflective (dust-free) surfaces. This is how the sun heats the Earth.

## Plants as Heaters

Some plants generate their own heat through biochemical processes:

- **Arum lilies** and **Philodendron selloum** store fats that are metabolically "burnt" to create heat
- **Philodendrons** may register 46°C (115°F) when ambient air is only 4°C (39°F)
- **Crocuses** heat up to 15°C (27°F) above ambient air temperature
- **Skunk cabbage** (*Symplocarpus foetidus*) uses metabolic heat to melt holes in spring snow, protecting blooms from cold

The warmth generated by these plants probably evolved to attract flies and heat-seeking insects to pollen. In permaculture design, understanding thermogenic plants suggests possibilities for using plant assemblies to modify local temperatures, however modestly.

## Practical Design Guidelines

1. **Site homes and gardens in the thermal belt** on sun-facing slopes — the single most important energy-conservation strategy in non-tropical climates
2. **Use thermosiphon principles** for water heating and space heating — site collectors below use points
3. **Study frost patterns** before planting — use autumn fog observations from high points to map frost zones
4. **Design for convective airflow** in buildings — avoid stratified warm air under ceilings; use stack ventilation
5. **Consider advected air** — position buildings to receive warm dry breezes but screen cold downhill air drainage
6. **Exploit conduction** through thermal mass — slab floors, stone walls, and earth-sheltered designs store and slowly release heat
