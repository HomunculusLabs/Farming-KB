# Game Ranching and Wildlife Conversion

Game ranching -- substituting wild game for domestic livestock as a
production enterprise -- was one of Savory's early ideas in the 1960s.
While the concept has merit, subsequent experience revealed that wild
game without their natural predators can be as damaging to vegetation
as domestic stock. The key insight is that game ranching must account
for the time and movement factors central to holistic management  overview.

## Origins of the Game Ranching Concept

As a research officer in Zimbabwe's Game Department, Savory promoted
game ranching based on several observations: ranchers considered
wildlife vermin competing with cattle, so converting wildlife to an
economic asset would change this perception; wildlife adapted to local
conditions might cause less damage; and the approach could save both
game and land while providing returns.

The idea was unpopular in the early 1960s. Savory was eventually forced
from his government position and turned to consulting with private
landowners in southern Africa.

## The Complication: Loss of Natural Behavior

Experience revealed a critical flaw: wild game evolved with predators.
Their grazing patterns, herd movements, and population dynamics are
shaped by predator pressure. When predators are removed, game animals
lose natural wariness, concentrate in favored areas, overbrowse
preferred species, and destroy seedlings that would have survived under
natural browsing. The [[savory-elephant-culling-and-wildlife-population-policy]]
case study illustrates this dramatically.

## Game Ranching vs. Wildlife-Livestock Integration

Savory distinguishes pure game ranching (complete substitution) from
wildlife-livestock integration (running both together). The latter,
covered under [[savory-wildlife-livestock-integration]], better replicates
natural multi-species grazing patterns. In African ecosystems, buffalo,
zebra, wildebeest, and various antelope each favored different plants
at different levels, distributing impact across the plant community.

## Economic Considerations

Game ranching must be evaluated using enterprise overhead and gross  profit analysis:

- Compare gross margins against livestock, crops, or recreation.
- Fixed costs (land, infrastructure) remain the same regardless of
  enterprise type.
- Variable costs differ: game typically requires less supplemental
  feeding and health intervention, but may need different fencing.
- Market development for game products is a critical variable.

## Ecological Challenges

Game ranching must address several challenges:

- **Time management**: Without predators or herders, how is animals'
  time on any area managed? This is the critical time and timing   guideline challenge.
- **Stock density**: Can adequate density be achieved for needed
  animal impact and herd effect? In fenced game ranches, animals
  may spread thinly across large areas, producing partial rest.
- **Species mix**: What combination best replicates natural grazing
  patterns and distributes impact across the plant community?
- **Population regulation**: Without predators, how are populations
  prevented from exceeding carrying capacity and degrading land?
- **Water distribution**: Water point placement affects game movement
  and grazing distribution just as with livestock.

## The Biodiversity Argument

Mixed wildlife communities contribute to ecosystem health: more complex
plant succession and community dynamics through diverse grazing
pressures; enhanced mineral cycle management through varied dung
distributions; greater stability through population complexity; and
aesthetic and recreational values commanding premium prices through
eco-tourism and hunting.

## Game Ranching in Practice

Implementation has varied widely. In southern Africa, game ranching is
a significant industry with developed markets for meat, hides, and
trophy hunting. In North America, wildlife enterprises are typically
secondary to livestock, with income from hunting leases and eco-tourism.
Some operations successfully integrate both, using livestock to create
animal impact that wildlife alone cannot provide. The success of any
game ranching operation depends on the quality of its planning, the
available markets, and the specific environmental conditions of the
land being managed.

## The Planning Challenge

The fundamental challenge is managing time. livestock grazing planning
techniques can be adapted for wildlife, but the inability to physically
move wild animals creates complications. This is why wildlife livestock  integration is often preferred -- livestock can be moved to create
desired patterns, and wildlife benefits from improved conditions.
