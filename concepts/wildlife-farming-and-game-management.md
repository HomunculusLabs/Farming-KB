## Wildlife Farming and Game Management

Managed wildlife farming represents a paradigm shift from single-species livestock
production toward integrated polycultural systems that harness the productivity of
diverse animal guilds. These systems can far exceed the yields of conventional
pasture-based operations while simultaneously improving ecological health.

## Integrated Wildlife Farming Systems

The concept of managed wildlife farming involves simultaneously farming **40 to 80
species** of birds, mammals, fish, and lower vertebrates within an integrated product
system. Such systems predictably exceed one-species yields and may actually improve
tree and prairie cover. By the mid-1960s, wildlife researchers already had good
estimates of yields under managed systems and could have devised excellent game farms.

Pioneer operations already demonstrating [[holmgren-economic-and-social-succession]] benefits include farms
raising:
- **Crocodiles** — for leather and meat
- **Emus** — for oil, leather, and meat
- **Kangaroos** — for premium low-fat meat (58% assimilable protein)
- **Deer** — for venison and antler products
- **Waterfowl** — for meat, eggs, and down

## Population Monitoring and Assessment

Long-term measurement of natural mortalities on range — due to drought, predation,
plant response, parasitism, and territorial behaviour — enables prediction of natural
losses with reasonable accuracy. This knowledge allows **pre-emptive culling** to make
use of age and sex classes of animals that would in any case perish in the field.

Population estimates are developed through multiple complementary methods:
- **Capture and marking** — dyes, collars, ear tags, leg bands, tattoos, ear notches
- **Scat counts** — tracking droppings as a population index
- **Browse effect surveys** — measuring impact on range vegetation
- **Herd size observations** — direct counts and aerial surveys
- **Breeding success monitoring** — tracking offspring survival rates

## Capture Techniques for Game Species

The first priority in game management is developing harmless live-capture methods.
These range widely in scale and technology:

### Large-Scale Capture
- **Drift (non-return) traps** — for migratory, moulting, or surface-driven species
  including jackrabbits, geese, deer, gazelle, and gnu
- **Set nets in flyways** — effective for many birds and some mammals, often operated
  at night when heat stress is reduced
- **Yard traps at waterholes** — effective for goats, horses, and kangaroos

### Small-Scale Capture
- **Baited box traps** — effective for most rodent species
- **Cage traps** — suitable for many bird species
- **Individual capture using fast-acting knockout drugs or chemical pellets** — for
  large or dangerous species such as bear, rhino, and elephant

### Direct Culling
Large species may also be culled by shooting where culling is essential, as sexes and
ages are easily distinguished in the field. Many capture methods operate at night using
lights, traps, and nets.

## Habitat Enhancement for Wildlife

Free-range species are attracted to or benefit from areas providing:
- **Special forage or crop plantings** — strategically placed food sources
- **Shade** — essential in hot climates for stress reduction
- **Winter shelter** — rock piles, brush piles, and forest debris provide critical
  refuge
- **Water points** — critical resource for many species; placement determines range
  utilization patterns

Scattering seed or forage plants of particular value during periods of browse shortage
can dramatically increase wildlife [[fukuoka-textdoc-land-requirements-per-diet-type-carrying-capacity]] on depleted range. Special forage
areas and water points are among the most effective habitat enhancements.

## The Case Against European-Style Pasture Systems

Deer (red deer and roe) are now farmed in New Zealand, but are being raised on
improved pasture using the same hay-and-concentrate system designed for sheep. This is
remarkably inappropriate, as these animals are well suited to **managed montane tussock
and tree browse systems** and are most efficiently raised in that environment.

Only in very recent years has intelligent management of game species in their preferred
habitat resumed. The benefits of such systems:
- Exceed those of improved pasture and selected domestic breeds of sheep and cattle
- Require **less capital cost**
- Deliver **better feed [[energy-conversion-efficiency]]**
- Produce **healthier products** (less fatty, fewer chemical residues)

## Capital Philosophy: Encouraging Nature

Mollison advocates redirecting capital currently misused in producing "fatty and
chemicalised meats" toward the intelligent management of healthier rangelands and
products. He invokes the tribal concept of **"increase"** — encouraging nature to
demonstrate her capacity rather than dictating directions and species.

European cultures have historically prevented the use of kangaroo and a variety of
antelope as farmed species, despite their status as preferred local foods with superior
feed conversion ratios. This cultural bias has suppressed more productive and
ecologically sound food systems.

## Fencing Strategies for Cattle Range

Fence alignment on cattle range has profound hydrological consequences. Fences aligned
to create **valley-to-ridge flow** of water following cattle tracks allow natural water
distribution across the landscape. In contrast, straight fences accelerate erosion by
concentrating runoff and disrupting natural drainage patterns.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Polyculture over monoculture** — a judicious balance of species always exceeds
   the yield of any single-species system
2. **Species-specific habitat** — match animals to their preferred environments rather
   than forcing them into artificial pasture systems
3. **Pre-emptive management** — use population data to cull surplus animals before
   natural mortality claims them
4. **[[integrated-pest-management]]** — use insectivorous birds, fish, and other
   predators as biological controls rather than chemical interventions
5. **Habitat provision** — water, forage, and shelter additions to depleted range
   multiply wildlife carrying capacity
6. **Cultural flexibility** — overcome biases against native game species as food
   sources

## Integration with Broader Permaculture Systems

Wildlife farming integrates naturally with other [[permaculture]] elements: water
[[mollison-designers-swale-design-and-water-harvesting-earthworks]] provide wildlife water points, [[forest-gardens]] supply browse and
shelter, and [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]] systems manage both domestic and wild herbivores.
The key insight is that managed wildlife farming works *with* natural systems rather
than attempting to replace them, yielding both [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]] and productive
harvest simultaneously.
