# MushWorld Oyster Spawn Production Guide

A comprehensive guide to producing oyster mushroom (Pleurotus spp.) spawn from agar culture through grain spawn, as described in the MushWorld Mushroom Growers' Handbook 1. Spawn is to mushroom what seed is to crop, and its quality is one of the most decisive factors for successful cultivation.

## Overview of Spawn Types

Mushroom spawn comes in several forms, each suited to different scales of production:

- **Grain spawn**: Mycelium grown on sterilized grains such as rye, sorghum, or millet. Most commonly used for commercial production. Easy to distribute through substrate.
- **Sawdust spawn**: Mycelium on sterilized sawdust, typically used for [[log-cultivation]] of oyster mushrooms.
- **Plug spawn**: Wooden dowels colonized with mycelium, used primarily for log inoculation.
- **Liquid spawn**: Mycelium suspended in a nutrient broth, allowing rapid inoculation of large volumes.

For oyster mushroom cultivation, grain spawn is the standard choice due to its fast colonization rate and ease of handling. One jar of grain spawn can inoculate 10 times its own mass in subsequent grain, enabling rapid scale-up.

## Agar Media Preparation

The starting point for spawn production is a pure mushroom culture, either obtained from a culture laboratory or cloned from a wild specimen. Agar media provides a solid surface for mycelial growth and allows assessment of culture health before scaling up.

### Malt Extract Yeast Agar (MEYA) Formula

The standard recipe used in the MushWorld handbook:

| Ingredient | Amount |
|---|---|
| Water | 1 liter |
| Agar agar (culturing grade) | 20 grams |
| Barley malt extract | 30 grams |
| Nutritional yeast | 2 grams |

Higher-grade agar is recommended over food-grade, as it contains fewer impurities and has better gelling properties.

### Preparation Steps

1. Weigh ingredients, add to a flask with non-chlorinated sterile water
2. Stop the flask with non-absorbent cotton or aluminium foil (not a tight stopper, to prevent blow-off from pressure buildup)
3. Sterilize in a pressure cooker at 250 degrees Fahrenheit (15 psi) for 45 minutes
4. Let pressure return to zero before opening; place domestic pressure cookers in a clean room during cooling to prevent contaminated air from being drawn in
5. Pour media into petri dishes within a laminar flow cabinet at approximately 50 degrees Celsius

### Clean Room Protocols for Pouring

Proper hygiene is critical. Shower and wear freshly washed clothes before entering the clean room. Hair carries significant contamination and should be washed, tied back, or covered with a hair net. Clean the work surface with isopropyl alcohol before pouring and periodically during the process.

Pour dishes closest to the HEPA filter first, as the clean air stream picks up contaminants from the operator and blows them away from the work area. Never place hands between the filter surface and open dishes. Do not breathe over open dishes, as breath is laden with bacteria. Lift dish lids with one hand while pouring with the other, keeping fingers away from dish rims.

## Inoculating Petri Dishes

1. Heat a scalpel tip in an alcohol lamp until glowing red
2. Cool the scalpel by dipping into the center of the dish to be inoculated
3. Open the culture tube without letting fingers hang inside the opening
4. Tease out a wedge of mycelium and quickly transfer to the agar surface
5. Place the mycelial wedge in the dish center so growth can radiate outward
6. Seal with laboratory film (parafilm) if available, or leave dishes in front of laminar flow filters until colonized
7. Reheat the scalpel between dishes to prevent cross-contamination

Oyster mushroom mycelium typically shows fuzzy growth within 2-3 days and fully colonizes a petri dish within approximately 10 days at 24 degrees Celsius. Blue-green mold or yellow slime indicates contamination, and affected dishes should be discarded immediately.

## Grain Spawn Preparation

### Grain Selection

Many grains can serve as spawn carriers. Rye grain is preferred because it is relatively soft, cooks easily without becoming sticky, and does not clump. Sorghum seed is the most commonly used carrier in Thailand and many tropical regions.

### Container Preparation

Jars (glass or plastic) or custom bags with filter patches are used. Jars should be wide-mouthed for easy inoculation, with tight-fitting lids drilled with an 8mm hole. Filter discs (soaked in 5% bleach solution for at least 1 hour) are fitted over the holes to allow gaseous exchange while blocking contaminants.

The filter allows oxygen to reach the growing mycelium without drawing in contaminants. Without adequate filtration, mycelium becomes anaerobic and will contaminate regardless of material cleanliness.

### Grain Cooking Process

1. Weigh grain; 500g or less of dry grain per container is optimal for petri dish inoculation
2. Boil water in a large pan, add dry grain, and simmer for approximately 30 minutes until firm but soft enough to squash
3. Do not overcook, as exploded kernels increase contamination susceptibility
4. Drain in colanders or improvised containers; mix periodically to promote evaporation
5. Add calcium sulfate (gypsum) at approximately 4g per 1kg dry grain to prevent clumping after sterilization
6. Fill containers to three-quarters capacity (jars) or two-thirds (bags)
7. Sterilize at 250 degrees Fahrenheit (15 psi): 105 minutes for 1kg bags of sorghum, 60 minutes for 1L jars with 400g grain, up to 3 hours for tightly packed bags

### Inoculation of Grain

Each fully colonized petri dish can inoculate up to 20 containers of grain. The MushWorld protocol recommends using one-half to one whole dish per 1kg grain jar for rapid colonization.

1. Clean the laminar flow cabinet work surface with alcohol
2. Cut a criss-cross pattern of 9 or more wedges from the colonized dish
3. If not using laboratory film, leave a 5mm strip of mycelium around the dish edge in case edge contamination is present
4. Pick up wedges with a hot scalpel and quickly drop into waiting containers
5. Reheat the scalpel between dishes
6. Shake jars thoroughly using a rolling, see-sawing motion to distribute mycelium through grain
7. For bags, trap clean air by holding the bag open toward the laminar filter before sealing
8. Incubate at 24 degrees Celsius

First signs of growth appear within 3 days. After 7 days, shake containers again to redistribute colonized kernels. Grain is fully colonized when completely white or off-white, and should be used immediately at peak vigor. Over-incubated spawn forms a solid lump that is extremely difficult to break up.

## Spawn Scale-Up

Grain spawn can serve two purposes after full colonization:

1. **Further grain inoculation**: One jar can inoculate 10 times its own mass, enabling exponential scale-up. However, do not add more than 20% of the target mass, as growing mycelium produces heat that can cause contamination.
2. **Final substrate inoculation**: Colonized grain spawn is used to inoculate bulk substrates (sawdust, straw, cotton waste, etc.) from which mushrooms will be fruited.

## Key Quality Considerations

- Always use proven, productive strains from reputable culture laboratories until experience in cloning and assessing wild specimens is gained
- Spawn must maintain strain characteristics and is propagated by subcultures; new strains are developed through genetic methods such as variation and mating
- The faster grain becomes fully colonized, the less chance of contamination, so generous inoculation rates are worthwhile
- Cool all sterilized materials completely before inoculation, as hot grain will kill introduced mycelium
- Contaminated cultures should never be used; mold spores produced by contamination can easily spread throughout the clean room if disturbed
