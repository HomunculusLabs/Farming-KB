## Nutrient Available Forms — Microbial Chelation and Plant Uptake

Elaine Ingham's soil food web framework emphasizes that [[chelation-and-nutrient-availability]] is not simply about total mineral content in soil, but about whether those minerals exist in forms that plant roots can actually absorb.

## The Problem with Total Soil Tests

Conventional soil tests measure total elemental concentrations — the sum of all forms of a given nutrient present in a soil sample.

However, most minerals in soil are locked in unavailable forms: bound in rock particles, precipitated as insoluble compounds, or adsorbed onto clay and organic matter surfaces.

A soil may test high in calcium, iron, or phosphorus while plants growing in it show [[knf-calcium-deficiency-symptoms]], because the nutrients are present but not accessible to root membranes.

## Plant-Uptakeable Forms

Plants absorb nutrients primarily through root hairs and [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]]. The forms they can take up are limited and specific.

Nitrogen is absorbed as ammonium (NH₄⁺) or nitrate (NO₃⁻). Organic nitrogen compounds like amino acids and amino sugars can also be absorbed directly by some plants, especially in conjunction with mycorrhizal fungi.

Phosphorus is taken up primarily as orthophosphate ions (H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻), but these readily bind with calcium, iron, and aluminum in soil to form insoluble precipitates.

Potassium is absorbed as the K⁺ cation, which can become fixed between clay layers and become temporarily unavailable.

Micronutrients including iron, manganese, zinc, copper, and boron must be in ionic or chelated soluble forms to cross root membranes.

## The Role of Chelation

Chelation is the process by which organic molecules bind to mineral ions, forming stable but soluble complexes that keep nutrients in solution and available for plant uptake.

Microorganisms are the primary chelators in soil. Bacteria and fungi produce organic acids, siderophores, and other chelating compounds that solubilize minerals from rock particles, organic matter, and mineral precipitates.

Bacterial chelation tends to produce shorter-chain organic acids like citric acid, oxalic acid, and lactic acid, which work quickly but have limited reach.

Fungal chelation, especially from mycorrhizal fungi and saprophytic fungi, produces longer-chain chelators including humic and fulvic acids, glomalin, and specialized siderophores that can access nutrients from greater distances.

## Microbial Nutrient Cycling

[[soil-food-web]] converts nutrients from unavailable to available forms through a continuous cycle of consumption, digestion, and excretion.

Bacteria and fungi absorb nutrients from their surroundings, incorporating them into their biomass. When protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods consume these microbes, they excrete excess nutrients in plant-available ionic forms.

This predator-prey nutrient cycling is the primary mechanism by which organic matter [[soil-phosphorus-and-mineral-nutrients]] become available to plants in healthy soil ecosystems.

The rate of nutrient cycling depends on the activity and diversity of [[soil-food-web]]. A diverse, active microbial community cycles nutrients faster and more completely than a depleted one.

## Nutrient Pools in Soil

Soil nutrients exist in several distinct pools, each with different availability characteristics.

The **structural pool** contains nutrients locked in primary mineral crystals (parent rock material). These are essentially unavailable on human timescales without microbial intervention.

The **organic pool** contains nutrients incorporated into organic matter, humus, and living biomass. These are moderately available and released through decomposition.

The **available pool** contains dissolved ions and chelated complexes in the soil solution. This is the pool that plants draw from directly, but it is typically very small and turns over rapidly.

The **labile pool** contains nutrients adsorbed onto exchange sites on clay and organic matter surfaces. These are in equilibrium with the soil solution and can replenish available nutrients as they are depleted.

## pH and Nutrient Availability

Soil pH dramatically affects nutrient availability because it controls the solubility and speciation of mineral ions.

At low pH (acidic conditions), aluminum, manganese, and iron become more soluble and can reach toxic concentrations, while calcium, magnesium, and molybdenum become less available.

At high pH (alkaline conditions), phosphorus, iron, manganese, zinc, and boron become less available through precipitation reactions, while calcium and molybdenum are more soluble.

The optimal pH range of 6.0-7.0 for most crops represents a compromise where the majority of essential nutrients remain reasonably available.

Ingham's approach emphasizes that biological management can buffer pH extremes more effectively than lime or sulfur amendments alone, because microbial communities maintain nutrient cycling across a wider pH range.

## Mycorrhizal Nutrient Access

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF) extend the effective nutrient-accessing zone of plant roots by 10-100 times through their extensive hyphal networks.

Mycorrhizal hyphae can access phosphorus from soil volumes that root hairs cannot reach, and they produce phosphatase enzymes that solubilize organic phosphorus compounds.

Mycorrhizal fungi also access micronutrients including zinc and copper from soil micropores and transport them back to the host plant in exchange for carbon compounds (sugars and lipids).

The mycorrhizal nutrient pathway is particularly important in low-fertility soils and in perennial systems where established mycorrhizal networks persist year-round.

## Compost Tea and Nutrient Availability

[[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) contributes to nutrient availability primarily by inoculating the soil and foliar surfaces with diverse microbial communities.

The microbial inoculum from compost tea establishes or augments [[soil-food-web]], accelerating nutrient cycling from organic matter [[plant-nutrients-and-mineral-sources]].

Foliar applications of compost tea can supply directly absorbable nutrients in chelated forms, bypassing soil fixation processes entirely.

[[compost-tea-ingredients-and-recipes]] like kelp, fish hydrolysate, and humic acids also contribute chelated micronutrients and growth-promoting compounds that enhance [[plant-nutrient-uptake]] capacity.

## Implications for Fertilization

Ingham's framework challenges the conventional approach of correcting nutrient deficiencies by adding soluble fertilizers.

Adding soluble nitrogen, phosphorus, or potassium fertilizers without addressing the biological mechanisms of nutrient cycling is a short-term fix that can actually degrade soil biology over time.

High concentrations of soluble salts from fertilizers can create osmotic stress that kills beneficial microbes, reducing the soil's capacity to cycle nutrients naturally.

The preferred approach is to feed [[soil-food-web]] — ensuring adequate organic matter, appropriate microbial inoculum, and conditions that support diverse microbial communities — and let the biology make nutrients available.

## Key Takeaways

- Total nutrient content in soil does not equal available nutrient content for plants.
- Microorganisms are the primary agents that convert unavailable nutrients into plant-available forms.
- Chelation by bacteria and fungi is essential for keeping micronutrients in soluble, absorbable forms.
- Predator-prey cycling in [[soil-food-web]] is the main pathway for nutrient release.
- Compost tea supports nutrient availability by building the microbial community that drives cycling.
