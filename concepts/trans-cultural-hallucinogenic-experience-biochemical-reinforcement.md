## Trans-Cultural Hallucinogenic Experience and Biochemical Reinforcement

## Overview

In the concluding chapter of "[[hallucinogens-and-shamanism]]," Michael J. Harner addresses a fundamental question: to what extent are the similarities between shamanic experiences across different cultures the result of cultural diffusion, and to what extent do they reflect a common human response to [[cultural-evolution-and-psychoactive-substances]]? Harner proposes the concept of "biochemical reinforcement" to explain why certain beliefs and practices associated with hallucinogen use are found in widely separated cultures with no apparent historical connection.

## The Problem of Cross-Cultural Regularities

Anthropologists have long observed "widespread and obviously long-standing thematic regularities" in shamanic beliefs and practices across the world. The shamanistic journey, the encounter with animal spirits, the experience of flight, the transformation into animals, the healing through supernatural means—these themes appear in cultures as diverse as the Mazatecs of Mexico, the Jivaro of Ecuador, the Siberian peoples, and the European witches of the Middle Ages.

### The Diffusion Problem

Normally, anthropologists view such regularities as "part of the totality of the ancient cultural heritage of man" and do not attempt to explain their existence. Attempts to explain "origins" have fallen into disrepute as antiquated anthropology associated with nineteenth-century figures like Edward B. Tylor.

The difficulty with purely diffusionist explanations is that many of these regularities appear in cultures that had no contact with each other. The similarity between Siberian shamanism [[mckenna-ayahuasca-and-amazonian-shamanism]], for example, cannot be easily explained by cultural transmission across the Bering Strait, given the vast differences in time, environment, and social organization.

### Redefining the Search

Harner redefines the search for origins as "the search for causality of regularities." Rather than asking "where did this belief come from?" he asks "why does this belief persist across cultures?" The answer, he proposes, lies in the biochemical properties of the psychoactive substances themselves.

## The Biochemical Reinforcement Thesis

### Cultural Maintenance Requires Reinforcement

Harner's central argument is that "culture is learned and transmitted through human effort; therefore it seems unlikely that cultural institutions and traits can be successfully passed on through centuries and millennia without having some regular reinforcement for their maintenance."

If a belief or practice is maintained across many generations, there must be something that reinforces it—something that makes people who adopt it continue to find it valuable. For beliefs and practices associated with hallucinogen use, the most obvious candidate for this reinforcement is the direct experience of the substances themselves.

### The Mushroom Knows

When a Mazatec shaman says "the mushrooms know where his spirit is," he is expressing an intuitive understanding of biochemical reinforcement. The mushroom experience provides direct, experiential confirmation of the belief system in which it is embedded. The spirits that the shaman encounters, the journeys he undertakes, the healing he performs—these are not abstract beliefs but lived experiences that reinforce the entire framework of shamanic cosmology.

### The "Trip" as Linguistic Parallel

A striking piece of evidence for the biochemical reinforcement thesis is the independent invention of the concept of the "trip" in the United States during the early 1960s to describe the nature of the hallucinogenic drug experience. This concept has a "precise linguistic parallel among the Cashinahua of the Amazon rain forest," who use a term with the same meaning to describe their own hallucinogenic experiences.

The convergence of English "trip" and Cashinahua terminology is remarkable because these cultures had no contact when the terms were coined. The parallel suggests that the experience of hallucinogen use is sufficiently consistent across cultures that it generates similar descriptive vocabulary independently.

### "A Good Trip"

Even the specific contemporary concept "to have a good trip" has its linguistic parallel among indigenous peoples. The universality of the distinction between positive and negative hallucinogenic experiences suggests that the biochemical properties of the substances produce a limited range of possible experiences, which cultures then interpret within their own frameworks but cannot fundamentally alter.

## The Implications for Anthropology

### Beyond Cultural Relativism

The biochemical reinforcement thesis challenges a strict cultural relativist position by suggesting that some cultural phenomena have a basis in human biology that transcends cultural variation. While the specific content of hallucinogenic experiences is culturally shaped (a Mazatec sees the Virgin of Conception; a Siberian shaman encounters a spirit bear), the underlying structure of the experience—the sense of journey, the encounter with other beings, the transformation of identity—appears to be biologically constrained.

### The Shamanistic Journey

The shamanistic journey, found in virtually every shamanic culture, may be the most fundamental example of a biologically reinforced cultural institution. The experience of traveling to another world, encountering supernatural beings, and returning with knowledge or healing power is consistently produced by a wide variety of psychoactive substances across many different chemical classes. This consistency suggests that the shamanistic journey is not a cultural invention but a discovery—a recognition of a universal human potential that is activated by specific chemical agents.

### Causality vs. Diffusion

The search for causality, as Harner frames it, does not deny the role of cultural diffusion. Shamanic practices certainly spread through contact between cultures. But the biochemical reinforcement thesis explains why certain elements persist even in the absence of contact, and why they take such similar forms across cultures.

A belief that is merely transmitted by cultural diffusion can be lost or distorted over time. A belief that is regularly reinforced by direct experience—the experience of a psychoactive substance confirming the reality of spirits, journeys, and transformations—is far more likely to persist and maintain its core features.

## The Scope of the Thesis

### Which Beliefs Are Biochemically Reinforced?

Harner's thesis applies specifically to beliefs and practices associated with hallucinogen use. Not all cultural regularities require biochemical explanation—many can be adequately explained by diffusion, independent invention, or universal features of human cognition and social organization.

The thesis is most compelling for beliefs that:
1. Are found in cultures with no historical connection
2. Are associated with the use of psychoactive substances
3. Describe experiences that match the known pharmacological effects of those substances
4. Are maintained over long periods despite cultural change

### The Witches' Flight

The European belief in witches' flight, for example, is biochemically reinforced by the flying ointment's capacity to produce sensations of floating, movement through space, and altered perception of the body. The belief persists because the experience of the ointment directly confirms it.

### Shape-Shifting

The belief in werewolf transformation is biochemically reinforced by the capacity of hallucinogens to alter the experience of bodily identity, producing feelings of being an animal. The harmaline experiment demonstrates that this is not merely a cultural interpretation but a direct experiential effect of the substance.

## Contemporary Relevance

The rediscovery of psychedelic substances by Western culture in the 1960s provides a natural experiment for testing the biochemical reinforcement thesis. The independent invention of terms like "trip" and "good trip," the spontaneous emergence of experiences resembling shamanic journeys, and the cross-cultural consistency of psychedelic phenomenology all support Harner's argument that the human response to hallucinogens is constrained by biology as well as shaped by culture.
