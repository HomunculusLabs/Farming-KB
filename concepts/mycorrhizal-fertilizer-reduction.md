## Overview

One of the most compelling practical benefits of mycorrhizal fungi for agriculture and horticulture is their potential to reduce or eliminate the need for synthetic fertilizers, particularly phosphorus. As fertilizer costs rise and phosphorus supplies become constrained, mycorrhizal partnerships offer a sustainable alternative that can maintain or increase crop yields while reducing pollution from nutrient runoff.

Source: [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-methods-agriculture]])

***

## The Inhibition Threshold

### Critical Phosphorus Levels

The amount of phosphorus in the soil greatly affects mycorrhizal spore germination and formation:
- With too much phosphorus, spores are less likely to germinate
- Mycorrhizal growth is hindered
- Some experts recommend less than **80 parts per million** of phosphorus for establishing arbuscular mycorrhizae
- Other experts recommend fewer than **75 micrograms of phosphorus per gram** of soil
- These figures are contested, as some plants readily form mycorrhizae even with high phosphorus

### Nitrogen Effects

Excess nitrogen similarly inhibits mycorrhizal formation. The relationship is dose-dependent: as more fertilizer is added, mycorrhizal colonization generally declines, and as fungal colonies decrease, phosphorus concentration in plant tissues also decreases.

## Understanding Fertilizer Labels

### N-P-K Numbers

Most fertilizers are labeled with N-P-K percentages, but the numbers are misleading:
- The nitrogen number represents actual nitrogen percentage by weight
- The phosphorus number represents phosphorus pentoxide (P2O5) — not actual phosphorus
- The potassium number represents potassium oxide (K2O) — not actual potassium

**To convert to actual nutrient weight:**
- Multiply phosphorus number by **0.44**
- Multiply potassium number by **0.83**

This matters for calculating actual phosphorus application rates and staying below levels that inhibit mycorrhizae.

### Organic vs. Inorganic Fertilizers

Studies indicate that:
- **Organic fertilizers** are generally compatible with mycorrhizae
- **Phosphorus-rich inorganic fertilizers** can inhibit mycorrhizal growth
- Some organic amendments (manure-heavy composts) can build up excessive phosphorus over time

## Agricultural Economics

### Cost-Benefit Analysis

The economic case for replacing chemical fertilizers with mycorrhizal management:
- Rising fertilizer costs make alternatives increasingly attractive
- Mycorrhizal fungi are self-replicating once established — a one-time or periodic investment
- Reduced fertilizer applications save money and reduce pollution liability
- Mycorrhizal plants often have higher yields and better quality

### Runoff Reduction

Inoculating seed and/or soil with mycorrhizal fungi can be a cost-effective way to minimize nutrient runoff:
- Mycorrhizal plants absorb nutrients more efficiently
- Less excess nutrient is available to wash into waterways
- This protects downstream ecosystems from eutrophication
