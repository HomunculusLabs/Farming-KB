## Foundational Principles: Dunbar and Alexander

The CHI traces modern mind-body medicine to Helen Flanders Dunbar, MD, and psychoanalyst Franz Alexander, MD, founders of modern psychosomatic medicine. Dunbar established that "many physiological processes which are of significance for health of the individual can be controlled by way of emotion," while Alexander demonstrated that "emotional influences can stimulate or inhibit the function of any organ."

Both researchers argued that emotions must be identified and targeted on equal footing with biological parts or systems affected by disease. Alexander identified nine factors in the development of illness: hereditary constitution, birth trauma, nature of infant care, physical and emotional traumatic experiences of infancy and childhood, family emotional climate, later physical injuries, later emotional experiences in intimate and occupational relations, and organic diseases of infancy that increase organ vulnerability.

## Three Destructive Emotions: Guilt, Martyrdom, and Harbored Anger

While all appropriately expressed emotions are healthy, the CHI identifies three emotions that by definition have only negative health consequences:

### Guilt

Guilt functions like a virus that infects the capacity to think and feel. When guilt infects anger, it turns a normal human emotion into something repressed, forcing a person to live a lie while hidden anger boils and injects toxic stress into body and mind. Without guilt, anger flows freely and powerfully without hurting anyone.

A University of California study showed that negative self-talk and self-blame increase pro-inflammatory cytokines and raise tumor necrosis factor-alpha receptor levels, directly linking guilt to systemic inflammation. Guilt also freezes grief in place and serves as unhealthy motivation based on purification through punishment.

### Martyrdom

Martyrdom is a form of fanatical anger that is arrogant, self-important, and destructive. It emerges from feeling unappreciated, treated wrongly, and misunderstood. People who use self-sacrifice and self-inflicted suffering as a front to punish others (or who consistently suffer for another and call it self-sacrifice) are caught in martyrdom. The CHI notes that it is impossible to feel martyrdom when in love, feeling grateful, or providing gentle caring intimacy.

### Harbored Anger

Harbored anger is recycled as soon as it is expressed, never released, continuously impacting body and mind. This self-feeding loop of the same emotional reaction to a specific trigger is commonly called "ruminating." Releasing it requires changing underlying beliefs such as "without this anger I am weak" or "without this anger I lose my family."

## Three Unhealthy Mental Habits: Bias, Prejudice, and Judgment

The CHI defines unhealthy habits as those both physically and psychologically detrimental to well-being. Biases can prevent clear thinking and fuel chronic fear, anger, anxiety, defensiveness, or power struggle. Over one hundred different types of biases have been described, and all automatically influence judgments without reason, logic, or critical thinking.

Key examples include:
- **Optimistic bias**: smokers acknowledging smoking kills "most people" while doubting it will kill them personally
- **Negativity bias**: it is easier to imagine things going wrong than right, an evolutionary hangover that fuels chronic fear states

The CHI provides a four-step process for overcoming bias: (1) recognize biases without guilt, (2) give them emotional dimensions, (3) let them be okay without self-condemnation, and (4) respond differently when triggers arise.

## Three Healing Habits: Gratitude, Compassion, and Forgiveness

### Gratitude

Gratitude changes negative affect (guilt, hostility, anxiety), reduces cortisol, increases positive affect (caring, happiness, vigor), and produces significant increases in DHEA, a suspected anti-aging molecule. In a University of California Davis study, patients who wrote five things they were grateful for daily experienced heightened well-being, more sleep, and reduced pain after two months, compared to a control group writing about grudges.

Research on 287 heart-attack survivors found that patients who learned from their experience were at significantly less risk for a second attack compared to those who blamed others. A meta-analysis of 45 studies confirmed hostility as an independent risk factor for coronary heart disease.
