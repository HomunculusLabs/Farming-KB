# Ectomycorrhizal Morphological Structures

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) associations form a distinctive set of anatomical features that distinguish them from all other mycorrhizal types. These structures -- the fungal mantle, [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]] occurs between fungus and host plant.

## The Fungal Mantle

The mantle is a dense sheath of fungal hyphae that completely encases the short lateral roots of the host plant. In well-developed ECM associations, this sheath can range from a few cell layers to several hundred micrometers thick. The mantle serves multiple critical functions:

- **Physical barrier**: It acts as a selective filter controlling which soil organisms and substances reach the root surface, providing a degree of pathogen protection.

- **Storage organ**: The mantle accumulates carbohydrate reserves (glycogen, trehalose, and lipids) derived from host photosynthate.

- **[[mycorrhizal-phosphatase-activity-elf97-detection]] has been localized to the mantle using ELF-97 substrate staining, demonstrating its role in phosphorus acquisition (Van Aarle et al. 2001, Alvarez et al. 2005).

- **Morphological diversity**: Mantle morphology varies extensively between fungal taxa -- some form smooth, compact sheaths while others produce loose, weft-like or cottony coverings. These characteristics are used in morphotyping for ECM identification.

The mantle surface may also produce cystidia, specialized sterile cells that project into the soil environment and may function in defense or ion exchange.

## The Hartig Net

The Hartig net is the definitive structure of ectomycorrhizae -- a labyrinthine network of fungal hyphae that penetrate between the cortical cells of the host root without actually entering the cells themselves. This intercellular interface represents the primary site of nutrient exchange.

Key features of the Hartig net include:

- **Hyphal branching pattern**: In most angiosperm hosts, the Hartig net forms a highly branched, palmetti-type (palm-tree shaped) configuration. In gymnosperms (particularly Pinaceae), it typically develops as a less branched, more linear structure.

- **Protoplasmic contact**: The fungal plasma membrane is separated from the host plasma membrane by only the fungal and plant cell walls, creating a very narrow interface optimized for metabolite transfer. In some contacts, the combined wall thickness can be less than 100 nm.

- **Surface area amplification**: The dense branching of the Hartig net dramatically increases the contact area between symbionts, enhancing the efficiency of bidirectional nutrient exchange.

- **Phosphatase localization**: Acid phosphatase activity has been demonstrated in the Hartig net using confocal laser scanning microscopy combined with ELF substrate methods (Alvarez et al. 2005), confirming its direct role in nutrient processing.

The Hartig net depth varies with host plant. In angiosperms like oak and beech, it may extend through all cortical cell layers to the endodermis. In many conifers, it is typically restricted to the epidermal and outer cortical layers.
