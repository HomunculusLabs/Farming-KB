## Overview

In [[permaculture-design]], **zones** are a spatial planning tool that organizes elements based on the frequency of human use and the energy required to maintain them. **Zone one** is the area closest to the home — the space of daily presence and the highest energy flow-through. **Zone two** extends outward and supports the main crop gardens, orchards, and integrated animal systems. Together, these two innermost zones form the productive heart of any [[permaculture]] site.

## Zone One: The Intensive Garden

Zone one is defined by constant human interaction. It is the area you walk through every day, multiple times a day, and therefore receives the most attention, the most nutrients, and the most careful design. This is where **energy flow-through** is at its absolute highest — every pass through the garden is an opportunity to observe, harvest, weed, or tend.

### Gardening Techniques

Two primary soil preparation methods serve zone one, and the choice between them depends on the gardener's **occasion, age, and inclination**:

- **Full mulch gardening** — Layering organic matter (straw, leaves, compost) on top of the soil surface, suppressing weeds and feeding the soil biology from above. This is a low-effort, high-reward technique ideal for long-term soil building.
- **Triple digging** — A traditional intensive method involving three layers of soil excavation and compost incorporation. Physically demanding but produces rapid results in depleted soils.

Neither technique is universally superior; both are tools appropriate to the situation. A young, energetic gardener with poor soil might triple dig once and then shift to mulch. An older gardener might rely entirely on mulch from the start. The key insight is that **the best method is the one the gardener will actually sustain** over time — consistency matters more than intensity.

### Two Classes of Annual Garden Plants

Annual vegetables in zone one fall into two practical categories based on harvest pattern:

- **Continual pick plants** — Crops harvested repeatedly over a season: beans, peas, tomatoes, zucchini, parsley, leafy greens. These belong **along the pathways** where they are encountered daily.
- **Single harvest plants** — Crops picked once at maturity: potatoes, onions, carrots, cabbage, corn. These can be placed further from paths since they don't demand daily attention.

A classic example is the **parsley border**: planting parsley along the edge of pathways so that every walk past the garden yields a handful of fresh herbs. This placement principle — put high-frequency-harvest plants where feet already travel — is fundamental to zone one efficiency.

### Border Plants for Zone One

The perimeter of zone one requires special plants that serve multiple functions. Ideal border plants are:

- **Fireproof** (low flammability)
- **Dark underneath** (providing shade and mulch conditions)
- **Adapted to low light** (thriving in the shadow of taller elements)
- **Useful in both zone one and zone two** (dual-purpose plants)

Specific **barrier plants** recommended for zone one borders include:

- **Sunflowers** — Quick-growing, productive seeds, mulch material, and visual screen
- **[[jerusalem-artichoke]]** — Hardy tuber crop, excellent windbreak, very tall and dense
- **Siberian pea tree ([[caragana-arborescens]])** — Nitrogen-fixing, edible seeds, drought-hardy hedge
- **Comfrey** — Deep taproot miner, dynamic accumulator, mulch factory, medicinal herb

These plants form living fences that produce food, medicine, and mulch while protecting the garden interior.

### Fencing Strategies

Protecting zone one from animals requires layered fencing:

- **Netted fence** — Physical barrier against birds and small mammals
- **Electric wire** — Psychological deterrent for larger animals; minimal material, maximum effect
- **Underground layer** — Wire or mesh buried at the base to prevent digging animals (rabbits, foxes)

The principle is deterrence through design rather than brute force — each layer addresses a different threat level.

### Greenhouse and Small Animals in Zone One

A **greenhouse attached to the kitchen** is a classic zone one element, providing passive solar heating, seedling propagation space, and year-round growing conditions. The greenhouse also serves as habitat for small beneficial creatures:

- **Quail** — Compact egg producers suited to small spaces
- **Frogs** — Pest control in the humid greenhouse environment
- **Turtles** — Sluggish but effective slug hunters
- **Geckos** — Insect control in warmer climates

These small animals integrate pest management into the greenhouse ecosystem without the complexity of larger livestock. The greenhouse itself becomes a **microclimate node** — a warm, humid zone within zone one that extends the growing season and creates conditions for species that would not otherwise thrive in the local climate.

## Zone Two: Main Crops and Orchards

Zone two is less intensively managed than zone one but still visited regularly. It houses the **main crop gardens**, the **orchard**, and most of the **animal systems**. Crops here are **spot-mulched** rather than fully mulched — targeted composting around individual plants rather than blanket coverage, reflecting the reduced daily attention.

### Animal Integration

Zone two is the primary zone for **livestock integration**:

- **Chickens** — Scratching, manuring, egg production, pest control in orchards
- **Goats** — Browse management, milk production, clearing brush
- **Ducks** — Slug control in orchards, eggs, foraging
- **Milk cow** — Possible on systems up to **2 acres**, providing dairy and manure

Animals in zone two are not pets — they are functional elements that convert pasture and weeds into protein and fertility.

### The Nutrient Funnel

A critical design principle connecting zones one and two is the **nutrient funnel** (also called the **biological funnel** or **nutrient vortex**). Animals housed in zone two produce manure, which is collected and carried into zone one to fertilize the intensive garden. The system is designed so that **nutrients flow inward** — from the broader, less-managed outer zones toward the intensive center.

This nutrient vortex creates a self-reinforcing cycle: zone two animals eat zone two and zone three forage, deposit manure, and that manure feeds zone one vegetables, which in turn feed the household and produce kitchen scraps that feed the chickens. Nothing is wasted; everything cycles.

The **biological funnel concept** extends beyond manure alone. It includes all organic materials — prunings from zone two fruit trees become mulch for zone one beds; duck pond water becomes irrigation loaded with nutrients; even autumn leaves from zone two orchard trees are raked inward to build zone one soil. The design deliberately structures the site so that gravity, daily movement patterns, and animal behavior all cooperate to move fertility toward the center of production.

### Intensive Production on Small Areas

One of the most striking insights from permaculture design is how little land is needed for genuine self-sufficiency when zones one and two are properly designed:

- **1/8 acre** — Sufficient for a productive zone one garden with chickens
- **1/4 acre** — Supports intensive vegetables, small orchard, and poultry
- **1 acre** — Full self-sufficiency with vegetables, fruits, chickens, and a goat
- **2 acres** — Complete system including a milk cow, orchard, and all zone one and two elements

These figures are not theoretical — they are drawn from documented examples of working permaculture farms. The implication is profound: **a standard suburban lot of 1/4 acre can feed a family** if designed with zone one and zone two principles rather than lawn.

### Strawberries and Blueberries

These berry crops illustrate the zone placement principle well. **Strawberries** can work in zone one due to their continual picking habit, but they are often better placed in **zone two** where they can spread and receive spot-mulching. **Blueberries**, as perennial shrubs with a concentrated harvest window, clearly belong in zone two within the orchard system.

The broader lesson: **zone placement is determined by harvest frequency and management intensity**, not by crop category. A tomato belongs in zone one; a blueberry bush belongs in zone two — even though both are fruits.

## The Cultural Problem of Lawns

[[bill-mollison]] strongly criticized the **lawn-as-status-symbol** tradition inherited from British landscape design — vast expanses of manicured grass that consume water, fertilizer, and labor while producing nothing edible. This tradition, exported worldwide through colonialism, established an aesthetic of **conspicuous non-productivity** where the ideal landscape was one that required constant work yet yielded no harvest. The lawn represents a fundamental misunderstanding of land stewardship: maintaining a sterile monoculture where a productive food system could exist. Converting lawn to zone one and zone two gardens is often the single most impactful action a suburban household can take.

## The Flower-Growing City Clerk

Mollison tells the story of a city clerk who discovered that the land around his small property could generate significant income through flower cultivation. By intensively planting **cut flowers** in what had been ornamental lawn, this clerk turned a marginal suburban plot into a productive enterprise. The story illustrates that even the smallest zone one and zone two designs can produce economic returns far exceeding the cost of maintenance. It also demonstrates Mollison's broader point: **most people are sitting on productive capacity they don't recognize**, simply because they have never been taught to see their land as a system rather than a decoration.

## Key Design Principles

- Place elements by **frequency of use** — daily crops at the doorstep
- Design **nutrient flow inward** through the biological funnel
- Match gardening technique to **the gardener's capacity**
- Integrate **animals as functional components**, not ornaments
- Every border plant should serve **multiple functions**
- The most productive land is land **walked past every day**
- **Lawns are wasted zone one** — the single most common design error in Western households
- **Stack functions** in every element: a fence that also produces food, a pond that also irrigates
- **Start with zone one** — it is the highest-leverage investment of time and energy on any site
