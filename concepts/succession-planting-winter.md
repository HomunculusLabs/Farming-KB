# Succession Planting for Winter Harvest

Succession planting for winter harvest is the practice of sowing vegetables at staggered intervals
from late summer through fall (and continuing through winter into spring) to maintain a continuous
supply of harvestable crops throughout the cold months. Unlike spring succession planting, where
[[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] is expanding, fall and [[winter-succession-planting]] operates in reverse — day
length is contracting, temperatures are falling, and growth is slowing. This fundamental difference
requires a distinct approach to scheduling, [[coleman-crop-selection]], and harvest management.

## The "Second Spring"

[[eliot-coleman]] designates August 1 as the beginning of the "second spring" — the start of the
winter-harvest planting season. This is not arbitrary. August 1 in a USDA Zone 5 climate
(approximately 44°N latitude) provides roughly 14.5 hours of daylight and warm soil temperatures,
conditions under which seeds germinate rapidly and seedlings establish quickly. The crops sown in
early August have the longest possible growing window before the onset of the persephone period
(when days drop below ten hours, around early November in Maine).

The second spring continues through the fall. Sowing dates become progressively later for different
crop groups, with the latest sowings timed so that plants reach minimal harvestable size just as
growth effectively stops.

## The Persephone Period

The persephone period — named after the Greek goddess who spent winter in the underworld — is the
time when day length drops below ten hours and plant growth effectively ceases. The exact dates
vary by latitude:

| Latitude | Persephone Period Begins | Persephone Period Ends |
|----------|------------------------|----------------------|
| 30°N     | Mid-December           | Early January        |
| 35°N     | Late November          | Mid-January          |
| 40°N     | Mid-November           | Late January         |
| 45°N     | Early November         | Early February       |
| 50°N     | Late October           | Mid-February        |

During the persephone period, plants do not die — they enter a state of dormancy or extremely slow
growth. Cold-hardy vegetables maintain their quality in this state and can be harvested as needed.
The key to successful [[winter-succession-planting-schedule]] is ensuring that crops reach sufficient size before
this period begins.

## Crop Groups and Scheduling

### Quick-Maturing Greens (20-35 days)

Crops like arugula, radishes, mizuna, and tatsoi mature quickly and can be sown multiple times
through the fall. These provide the last harvests before the persephone period and the first
harvests after it ends. Sowing window: mid-August through late October.

### Medium-Season Greens (35-50 days)

Spinach, lettuce, mustard greens, and pak choi require a longer growing window. These are the
workhorses of [[coleman-beet-greens-cold-house-winter-production]], providing cut-and-come-again harvests throughout the cold months.
Sowing window: August through early October. Later sowings produce smaller plants that are
harvested as baby greens.

### Long-Season Crops (50-80+ days)

Chicories (radicchio, endive, escarole), leeks, carrots, beets, and celery require the longest
growing window. These must be sown earliest — mid-July to early August — to reach adequate size
before growth slows. Carrots sown in early August reach full size by November and store in the
ground through winter.

### Overwintering Crops

Some crops are sown in fall specifically to overwinter and produce an [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] harvest.
Garlic (planted in October), certain onion varieties, and some brassicas are planted in fall,
survive the winter in a dormant state, and resume growth in February when days lengthen. These
crops bridge the gap between the end of winter stores and the first spring harvests.

## The Cut-and-Come-Again Strategy

During the persephone period, the primary harvest method for [[lettuce-and-leafy-greens]] is cut-and-come-again.
Rather than harvesting entire plants, individual leaves or the top portion of the plant is cut,
leaving the growing point intact. The plant regrows slowly even in the shortest days, providing
repeated harvests from the same planting.

This strategy dramatically extends the productive life of each planting. A single bed of spinach
sown in September might provide four to six harvests between November and March. Each harvest
removes perhaps one-third of the leaf area, and the plant regrows the removed portion over
several weeks.
