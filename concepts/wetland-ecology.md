# Wetland Ecology

## Overview

Wetlands are ecosystems where water saturates the soil for at least part of the growing season, creating conditions favoring hydrophytic (water-adapted) vegetation. Often called "nature's kidneys," wetlands filter water, store floodwaters, and support extraordinary biodiversity. On the homestead, understanding wetland ecology is valuable for [[riparian-zone-restoration]], [[watershed-management]], and [[graywater-systems|greywater]] treatment systems.

## Wetland Types

### Marshes
Marshes are dominated by herbaceous plants (grasses, sedges, rushes) with no peat accumulation. They occur in depressions, along stream margins, and in shallow lake basins.

- **Freshwater marshes**: Cattails, bulrushes, pickerelweed; habitat for waterfowl, amphibians, muskrats
- **Tidal marshes**: Salt marsh cordgrass, salt hay; critical nursery habitat for fish and shellfish
- **Wet meadows**: Grasses and sedges in seasonally saturated areas; important for ground-nesting birds

Marshes are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth, often exceeding agricultural productivity per acre.

### Swamps
Swamps are wetlands dominated by woody vegetation (trees and shrubs). Water levels fluctuate seasonally.

- **Forested swamps**: Bald cypress, tupelo, red maple; deep water with seasonal flooding
- **Shrub swamps**: Willow, dogwood, buttonbush; shallower water with dense shrub thickets
- **Floodplain swamps**: Bottomland hardwood forests along rivers; critical for flood storage

Swamps provide critical wildlife corridors and flood attenuation. Old-growth swamp forests store enormous amounts of carbon.

### Bogs
Bogs are peat-accumulating wetlands fed primarily by precipitation (ombrotrophic). They are acidic and nutrient-poor, creating unique conditions:

- **Sphagnum moss** forms the dominant ground cover and builds peat layers
- **Carnivorous plants** (sundews, pitcher plants, bladderworts) supplement nutrient-poor conditions
- **Acid-tolerant trees** like black spruce and tamarack form the bog forest margin
- **Orchids** and other specialized plants thrive in the low-nutrient, high-light environment

Bogs accumulate peat at rates of 0.5-1.0 mm/year and can be thousands of years old. They are significant global carbon stores.

### Fens
Fens are peat-accumulating wetlands fed by groundwater (minerotrophic), making them less acidic and more nutrient-rich than bogs:

- **Rich fens**: Higher pH, diverse plant communities including sedges, wildflowers, and mosses
- **Poor fens**: Lower pH, similar to bogs but with some groundwater influence
- **Calcareous fens**: Fed by calcium-rich groundwater; among the most biodiverse wetland types

Fens support rare plant species and are among the most threatened wetland types due to groundwater depletion.

## Wetland Hydrology

### The Hydroperiod
The seasonal pattern of water level fluctuation is the primary factor determining wetland type and function:

- **Permanently flooded**: Open water year-round; supports aquatic vegetation and fish
- **Seasonally flooded**: Saturated during growing season, dry in late summer/fall
- **Temporarily flooded**: Brief saturation after rain or snowmelt; supports wet meadow plants
- **Saturated but not flooded**: Water table at or near surface; supports forested wetlands

### Hydrologic Functions
Wetlands perform critical water management functions:

- **Flood storage**: Absorb and slowly release floodwaters, reducing downstream damage
- **Groundwater recharge**: Water percolates through wetland soils into aquifers
- **Streamflow maintenance**: Baseflow released from wetlands sustains streams during dry periods
- **Sediment trapping**: Slow water velocity allows suspended sediments to settle

## Wetland Plants

### Adaptations to Saturated Soils
Wetland plants have evolved several strategies for root zone anoxia:

- **Aerenchyma tissue**: Spongy tissue with air channels that transport oxygen from leaves to roots
- **Shallow root systems**: Roots concentrated in oxygenated surface layers
- **Adventitious roots**: Roots emerging from stems above the waterline
- **Pneumatophores**: Specialized vertical roots (cypress knees) that facilitate gas exchange

## Wetland Wildlife

### Amphibians
Wetlands are essential breeding habitat for most amphibians. Species using wetlands include:

- Frogs and toads (spring peepers, bullfrogs, tree frogs)
- Salamanders (spotted, Jefferson's, marbled)
- Newts (eastern, red-spotted)

See [[amphibian-habitat-creation]] for creating amphibian breeding habitat.

### Birds
Wetlands support diverse bird communities:

- **Wading birds**: Herons, egrets, bitterns
- **Waterfowl**: Ducks, geese, swans
- **Shorebirds**: Sandpipers, plovers, rails
- **Songbirds**: Red-winged blackbirds, marsh wrens, swamp sparrows

### Mammals
Wetland-associated mammals include beavers, muskrats, mink, river otters, and various bat species that forage over open water. See [[bat-conservation-and-housing]] for bat habitat guidance.

### Invertebrates
Wetland invertebrates form the base of the food web:

- Dragonflies and damselflies (adults and aquatic nymphs)
- Mayflies, caddisflies, and stoneflies (water quality indicators)
- Freshwater mussels (water filtration, indicator species)
- Aquatic snails and clams (nutrient cycling)

## Ecosystem Services

### Water Quality
Wetlands remove pollutants through several mechanisms:

- **Sediment trapping**: Suspended particles settle in slow-moving water
- **Nutrient removal**: Plants and microbes take up nitrogen and phosphorus
- **Pathogen removal**: UV exposure, predation, and filtration reduce bacteria
- **Heavy metal binding**: Organic matter and mineral soils sequester metals

A single acre of wetland can store 1-1.5 million gallons of floodwater and remove significant nutrient loads from agricultural runoff.

### Carbon Storage
Wetlands, particularly peatlands, store disproportionate amounts of carbon:

- Peatlands contain approximately 30% of global soil carbon on 3% of land area
- Saturated conditions slow decomposition, allowing organic matter to accumulate
- Drainage of wetlands releases stored carbon, converting sinks to sources

### Shoreline and Bank Protection
Wetland vegetation stabilizes shorelines and stream banks by:

- Absorbing wave energy with dense stems and root masses
- Binding soil with extensive root systems
- Reducing flow velocity during flood events

This function connects directly to [[soil-erosion-control]] and [[riparian-zone-restoration]] practices.

## Threats to Wetlands

- Agricultural drainage and conversion
- Urban development and fill
- Hydrologic alteration (dams, diversions, groundwater pumping)
- [[invasive-species-management]] needs (purple loosestrife, phragmites, hydrilla)
- Climate change (altered precipitation patterns, sea level rise)
- Pollution (nutrient loading, sedimentation, contaminants)

## Wetland Creation and Restoration

Creating or restoring wetlands on the homestead can serve multiple functions: wastewater treatment via [[graywater-systems|greywater]] systems, wildlife habitat, flood control, and aesthetic value. Key considerations include hydrology (ensuring consistent saturation), substrate, and appropriate native plant selection. Even small constructed wetlands (100-500 sq ft) can provide significant ecological benefit.
