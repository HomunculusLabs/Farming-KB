# Ingham — Glomalin and Biological Soil Glues

Elaine Ingham views glomalin as part of a much broader category of biological glue compounds produced by many soil organisms, not just [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]. While the recognition of glomalin has been important for soil science, Ingham argues it represents only the "tip of the iceberg" of sticky materials that build soil structure.

## What Is Glomalin

Glomalin is a general term for compounds produced by soil organisms that stick things together. The name was coined in relation to vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi, but:
- VAM are not the only organisms making glomalin
- Many other soil organisms produce these materials
- Glues are produced by many bacteria as well as fungi
- Glomalin likely encompasses many different structures and materials under varying conditions

## Historical Context

R.C. Foster's work in Australia ("The Ultrastructure of the Rhizosphere") documented these sticky compounds long before the term "glomalin" was coined. The recognition of these materials is important because they are finally being acknowledged for their critical role in soil structure [[fungal-peat-decomposition-and-carbon-storage]].

## Bacterial Glues

Bacteria produce their own glues to:
- Hold themselves to soil particles
- Form microaggregates (the smallest soil structural units)
- Create biofilms on root surfaces and soil particles
- Improve oxygen diffusion into soil by opening pore spaces

When bacteria have adequate food resources (simple sugars), they produce these glues as part of their growth. This is the mechanism by which sugar additions can [[query-how-do-fungi-improve-soil-structure]] — bacteria make glues, form microaggregates, and help oxygen diffuse more rapidly.

## Fungal Glues and Macroaggregate Formation

Fungi are the primary builders of larger soil structures:
- Fungal hyphae physically bind soil particles together
- Mycorrhizal fungi produce glues that stabilize macroaggregates
- Fungal residues become humic acid (the most complex [[knf-leaf-mold-humus]])
- Earthworms and microarthropods assist fungi in maintaining dominance over bacterial processes in structure building

## The Role in Soil Structure

The full process of soil structure building requires all organism groups:
1. **Bacteria** create microaggregates through glue production
2. **Fungi** bind microaggregates into macroaggregates with hyphae and glues
3. **Protozoa** cycle nutrients through bacterial grazing, maintaining bacterial activity
4. **Nematodes** create pore spaces through movement and root channels
5. **Microarthropods** fragment [[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] create larger pore networks
6. **Earthworms** mix and aerate soil, creating stable aggregates

Real humic acid — the most complex, stable soil organic matter — requires all of these organisms working together.

## Glomalin and Carbon Storage

The sticky compounds that include glomalin represent a significant carbon sink in soil. They:
- Bind carbon into stable, long-lasting soil aggregates
- Protect organic matter from rapid decomposition
- Can persist in soil for years to decades
- Are part of the reason healthy soils store more carbon than degraded soils

## Glomalin and Soil Aggregation Mechanisms

Glomalin operates at the microaggregate level, coating hyphal surfaces and soil
particles with a sticky, hydrophobic protein layer. This coating binds tiny mineral
particles together into stable microaggregates, which serve as building blocks for
larger soil structures. The hydrophobic nature of glomalin helps soil resist
wetting and slaking during intense rainfall on exposed agricultural fields.

Research shows glomalin contributes 27-60% of total carbon in stable soil
aggregates, making it a major long-term carbon stabilization mechanism. The
compound persists in soil for 7 to 42 years, providing [[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]] long
after the fungal hyphae that produced it have decomposed.

## Relationship to Mycorrhizal Networks

Glomalin production is closely linked to mycorrhizal network health. Arbuscular
mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) are the primary producers of glomalin-related soil
proteins, and soil glomalin levels correlate strongly with AMF hyphal length
and root colonization intensity. Healthy mycorrhizal networks directly translate
to improved soil structure through continuous glomalin deposition on hyphal
surfaces throughout the root zone.

When mycorrhizal networks are disrupted by tillage, fungicides, or prolonged
fallow periods, glomalin production declines and existing pools begin to degrade.
This creates a feedback loop: reduced mycorrhizal activity means weaker aggregates,
poorer root environments, and further reduced colonization potential over time.

## pH Considerations

An open question Ingham raises is whether VAM-produced glomalins have the same pH characteristics as bacterial glues. This has implications for:
- Which organisms dominate in different soil pH conditions
- How different glues interact with mineral particles
- The stability of different aggregate types across pH ranges
