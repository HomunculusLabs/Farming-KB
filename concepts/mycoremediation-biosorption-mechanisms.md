# Biosorption Mechanisms in Fungal Biomass

## Overview

Biosorption is the passive uptake of metal ions or organic compounds by biological materials. Fungal biosorption — termed mycosorption — uses fungal biomass (mycosorbents) to sequester toxic metals from waste streams. Mycosorption offers a promising and economical alternative for treating metal-containing industrial effluents, with conservative market estimates of $27 million per year in North America alone. The process exploits the abundant functional groups present in fungal cell walls.

## Cell Wall Composition and Binding Sites

Fungal cell walls are composed of chitins, chitosans, and glucans, and also contain proteins, lipids, and other polysaccharides. Yeast cell walls consist mainly of glucans with an outer layer of mannoprotein. These structural components provide a large number and variety of functional groups or binding sites, exceeding those of monofunctional group ion-exchange resins. Key binding sites include carboxyl, sulfate, phosphate, hydroxyl, amino, imino, sulfonate, imidazole, sulfydryl, carbonyl, and thioether moieties. The amino group of Rhizopus nigricans cell walls is involved in Cr(VI) binding, while -COOH groups contribute to lead biosorption in Mucor rouxii.

## Metabolism-Independent vs. Metabolism-Dependent Mechanisms

Biosorption mechanisms divide into two categories:

### Metabolism-Independent (Passive)
Employs live or dead biomass. Includes ion exchange, chelation, adsorption on cell surfaces, crystallization, precipitation, and ion entrapment in inter- and intrafibrillar capillaries and spaces of polysaccharide material. Nonliving biomass has strong affinity for metal ions due to the lack of protons produced during metabolism. Metal toxicity does not affect nonliving biomass, representing a major advantage. Dead biomass can be generated as waste byproduct of industrial fermentation, pretreated by washing with acids or bases, then dried and granulated.

### Metabolism-Dependent (Active)
Requires living cells. Includes intracellular uptake of metal ions and chemical transformation. Living cells accumulate metals through energy-dependent transport systems and can transform metals through reduction, methylation, and dealkylation. The three-step mechanism of Pb(II) accumulation in [[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]] involves initial surface binding (metabolism-independent), followed by energy-dependent transport into cytoplasm (metabolism-dependent), and further accumulation.

## Metal Transformation Mechanisms

Fungi can chemically transform metals through reduction, methylation, and dealkylation:

- **Reduction**: [[aureobasidium-pullulans]] reduces Ag(I) to Ag(0); Fusarium spp. reduce Se(IV/VI) to Se(0); [[candida-albicans]] reduces Hg(II) to volatile Hg(0).
- **Methylation**: [[alternaria-alternata]] volatilizes selenium to dimethylselenide; Penicillium spp. methylate arsenic compounds to volatile dimethyl- or trimethylarsine.
- **Dealkylation**: Penicillium sp. MR-2 dealkylates organic mercury to Hg(II), then reduces to Hg(0).
- **Precipitation**: [[fusarium-oxysporum]] reduces silver ions to form stable silver hydrosol with nanoparticles of 5-15 nm stabilized by fungal proteins.

## Organic Acid-Mediated Leaching

Many heterotrophic fungi produce organic acids that assist in solubilization and complexation of metal cations. Oxalic acid is a primary leaching agent for Al, Fe, and Li, forming soluble metal oxalate complexes. White-rot fungi Bjerkandera fumosa, Phlebia radiata, and [[trametes-versicolor]] produce oxalate crystals on ZnO, Co3(PO4)2, and CaCO3. Brown-rot fungi maintain oxalic acid concentrations up to 600 uM/g, related to copper tolerance mechanisms. [[penicillium-simplicissimum]] adsorbs zinc accompanied by citric acid production, and [[aspergillus-niger]] filtrate solubilizes 18% Cu, 7% Ni, and 4% Co from contaminated materials.

## Specific Biosorbent Organisms

### White-Rot Fungi
[[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]] removes 69.8 mg/g Pb(II), 23 mg/g Cd(II), and 20.2 mg/g Cu(II) from artificial wastewater. P. chrysosporium resting cells achieve 80 mg/g Pb(II) uptake. Polyporus versicolor removes 57 mg/g Ni(II). See [[mycoremediation-heavy-metals-detailed]] for full uptake data.

### Molds
Aspergillus niger binds silver to 10% of dry weight through stoichiometric exchange with Ca(II) and Mg(II). Nickel localizes as nickel oxalate dihydrate crystals on the cell wall and inside cells. Live A. niger biomass removes nickel more effectively than alkali-boiled biomass.

### Yeasts
Saccharomyces cerevisiae shows binding affinity in the order Pb(II) > Cu(II) > Ni(II). Candida utilis exhibits uptake capacities of 39 mg/g Pb(II), 23 mg/g Cu(II), and 19 mg/g Cd(II). The thicker mannan layer and larger surface area of certain S. cerevisiae strains are responsible for higher Cd(II) uptake.

## Biosorption Reactor Systems

Several types of fungal biosorption reactors have been developed, though not yet fully commercialized:

- **Batch stirred-tank reactors**: The earliest type used for metal-biosorbent effectiveness testing. A plug-flow biosorption reactor with 2-5 minute retention time achieved 87% copper reduction using polyelectrolyte-induced flocculation of biomass.
- **Continuous-flow stirred-tank reactors (CFST)**: Continuous feed of metal solution with either continuous fresh biosorbent supply or retained biosorbent batch. Heavy metals removed using [[rhizopus-arrhizus]].
- **Fixed packed-bed reactors**: Pycnosporus sanguineus adsorbs heavy metals from aqueous solution in fixed-bed columns. These are the most common configuration for biosorption due to simple operation.
- **Fluidized-bed reactors**: Provide better contact between metal solution and biosorbent particles.
- **Pulsating-bed and multiple-bed reactors**: Advanced configurations for improved mass transfer.

## Factors Affecting Biosorption

Multiple factors influence biosorption extent and efficiency:

- **pH**: The most critical parameter, affecting metal speciation, functional group ionization, and biomass surface charge. Optimum pH varies by metal and fungal species (typically pH 4-6 for most metals).
- **Temperature**: Biosorption tends to be exothermic; however, moderate temperature increases can improve kinetics.
- **Biomass concentration**: Higher biomass generally increases total metal removal but decreases per-unit uptake.
- **Initial metal concentration**: Higher concentrations drive greater uptake until saturation.
- **Biomass preparation**: Pretreatment with NaOH, formaldehyde, or other agents can significantly enhance biosorption capacity by increasing active binding sites.
- **Competing ions**: In multimetal systems, competitive adsorption reduces individual metal uptake. Pb(II) generally shows highest affinity, followed by Cu(II) and Ni(II).
- **Contact time**: Rapid initial uptake followed by slower equilibrium approach; most biosorption completes within 2-6 hours.

## Sorption Performance Models

Equilibrium data are evaluated using adsorption isotherms. The Langmuir model applies to monolayer sorption onto a finite number of identical sites. The empirical Freundlich model describes sorption on heterogeneous surfaces. Other models include Scatchard-Langmuir, Redlich-Peterson, Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET), and Dubinin-Radushkevich equations. These isotherms do not always provide meaningful interpretation of biosorption data and may not apply under variable environmental conditions. Rapid uptake is desirable, and two sorption systems must be compared at the same equilibrium concentrations for accurate assessment.

## Heavy Metal Uptake by Major Fungal Groups

### White-Rot Fungi
Phanerochaete chrysosporium demonstrates broad-spectrum metal uptake: 25% Pb(II), 15% Cr(III), 10% Cu(II) and Cd(II) removal at 150 mg/l. Resting cells achieve 80 mg/g Pb(II) uptake. P. chrysosporium achieves 100% Cu(II) removal at 100 mg/l with 1N HCl regeneration over 6 cycles. Fomitopsis pinicola removes 51% Cd(II) at 1120 mg/l concentration.

### Yeasts
Saccharomyces cerevisiae achieves 84-98% uranium removal. Debaryomyces hansenii shows 137 mg/g UO2(II) uptake. Kluyveromyces marxianus achieves the highest uranium uptake at 343 mg/g. Candida utilis shows the highest Cr(VI) uptake among yeasts tested.

### Aspergillus niger
A. niger achieves 98% Ni(II) removal at 6.5 mM. Pretreatment with formaldehyde, DMSO, NaOH, and detergent improves removal of lead, copper, and cadmium compared to live biomass. A. niger biomass produces weak organic acids (mostly citric acid) for leaching heavy metals from contaminated soils, achieving remediation to Quebec Provincial Standards within 20-25 days at approximately $5.00 per cubic meter.
