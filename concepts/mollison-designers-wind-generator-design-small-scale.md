## Site Assessment and Wind Resource

Wind power increases with the cube of wind speed, making accurate
and site assessment critical. Mollison emphasizes that small differences
in average wind speed produce large differences in energy output.
A site with 6 m/s average wind produces roughly twice the energy of
a 5 m/s site.

## Blade Design Principles

Blade design determines turbine efficiency and performance:

- **Tip speed ratio (TSR):** The ratio of blade tip speed to wind
  speed. Optimal TSR depends on blade count: two-blade designs
  target TSR 6-8, three-blade designs target TSR 5-7, multi-blade
  designs target TSR 2-4.
- **Pitch and twist:** Blades are angled (pitched) and twisted from
  root to tip to maintain optimal angle of attack across the rotor
  span. Simple flat or carved blades are less efficient but adequate
  for small systems.
- **Airfoil shape:** NACA airfoil profiles (NACA 4415, NACA 2412)
  are common for small turbine blades. Even simple curved shapes
  outperform flat plates significantly.

Mollison encourages experimenting with carved wooden blades using
traditional windmill patterns as a starting point. Accuracy matters
less at small scale than robustness and ease of construction.

## Tower Height and Siting

Tower height is critical for performance. Wind speed increases with
height above ground due to reduced surface friction (wind shear). As
a rule, the turbine should be at least 10 meters above any obstacle
within 150 meters. Mollison recommends:

- Minimum tower height of 10 meters for open terrain
- 15-25 meters for sites with nearby trees or buildings
- Tilt-up guyed towers for ease of erection and maintenance
- Lattice or monopole towers for permanent installations

Tower materials include steel pipe, galvanized tubing, and treated
timber. Guyed towers using wire rope anchors are the most economical
for heights up to 20 meters. The tower must withstand wind loads of
50+ m/s (survival wind speed) without failure.

## Battery Storage Systems

Wind generators require battery storage for consistent power supply,
as wind is intermittent. Mollison describes lead-acid battery systems
as the standard for small-scale wind power:

- Deep-cycle lead-acid batteries (golf cart, L-16, forklift types)
  provide reliable energy storage at reasonable cost
- System voltage: 12V for very small systems, 24V or 48V for
  moderate systems to reduce wiring losses
- Charge controllers prevent overcharging and protect batteries
- Dump loads (water heating elements) divert excess power when
  batteries are full
- Battery bank sizing: typically 3-5 days of autonomy

Battery care is essential: avoid deep discharges below 50% state of
charge, equalize periodically, maintain electrolyte levels, and
keep batteries at moderate temperatures. Mollison connects wind-battery
systems to [[holmgren-renewable-resources-detailed]] design and recommends hybrid
wind-solar systems for year-round reliability.

## Maintenance and Safety

Small wind generators require regular maintenance: annual inspection
of blades, bolts, and guy wires; lubrication of bearings; checking
electrical connections; and monitoring battery health. And safety considerations include fall protection for tower work, proper grounding
for lightning protection, braking or furling mechanisms to prevent
overspeed in storms, and sufficient clearance from dwellings and
paths.
