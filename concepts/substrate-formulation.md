# Substrate Formulation A substrate is the material on which mushroom mycelium grows and from which it draws nutrients to produce fruiting bodies. Selecting and preparing the right substrate is essential for successful cultivation. Different mushroom species have evolved to decompose different materials, and matching substrate to species is one of the foundational skills in mushroom growing. ## Substrate Categories Mushroom substrates fall into three broad categories based on the type of organic matter they are composed of: - **Lignocellulosic**: Wood-based substrates including hardwood sawdust, wood chips, straw, and agricultural waste. These support species that degrade cellulose and lignin — the primary decomposers. - **Composted**: Mixtures of manure and straw that have undergone a thermophilic composting process. Required by species like Agaricus bisporus that need a partially broken-down substrate. - **Supplemented**: Base substrates enriched with nitrogen-rich additives like bran, gypsum, or seed meal to boost yields. ## Primary Substrates **Hardwood sawdust** is the backbone of commercial shiitake, oyster, and lion's mane cultivation. Oak, maple, beech, and alder are excellent choices. Softwoods like pine and cedar contain resins and phenolic compounds inhibitory to most cultivated species. Sawdust should be from recently cut trees and stored dry to prevent premature decomposition. **Straw** is the preferred base substrate for oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus species). Wheat straw is most common, but oat, rye, and rice straw all work well. Straw is inexpensive, widely available, and has a high surface-area-to-volume ratio that promotes rapid colonization. It must be chopped or shredded to 2-5 cm lengths for optimal hydration and packing. **Wood chips** are used for outdoor log-style cultivation and as a component in mixed substrates. Beyond the choice of materials, the physical preparation of substrate
affects colonization speed and yield. Sawdust should be sifted to remove
oversized particles that can create air pockets, and mixed thoroughly
with supplements to ensure even distribution. Straw must be chopped or
shredded intact straw stems hydrate unevenly and pack poorly.

For bagged substrates, packing density matters. Overly tight packing
restricts [[bulk-substrate-preparation]]. Most mushroom substrates perform best at 60 to
65 percent moisture content by weight, measured as the ratio of water
mass to total substrate mass. This range provides sufficient water
for mycelial metabolism while maintaining adequate air space within
the substrate matrix for gas exchange.

The squeeze test provides a practical field method for assessing
moisture content. A handful of properly hydrated substrate should hold
together when squeezed firmly and release only a few drops of water.
If no water is released, the substrate is too dry. If water streams
out freely, the substrate is too wet and may become anaerobic.

Moisture content can be measured precisely by weighing a sample before
and after drying in an oven at 105 degrees Celsius for 24 hours. The
formula is: moisture percentage equals wet weight minus dry weight,
divided by wet weight, multiplied by one hundred. This measurement
should be taken after hydration and again after sterilization, as the
process can alter moisture levels.

## Substrate Pasteurization and Sterilization

Substrate must be treated to eliminate or reduce competing organisms
before inoculation. The choice between pasteurization and sterilization
depends on the substrate type, supplementation level, and the
[[growing-gourmet-phase-ii-steam-pasteurization]] chambers, and in-container
pasteurization are common methods. Pasteurized substrates are
suitable for low-supplement recipes and outdoor beds.

Sterilization involves heating substrate to 121 degrees Celsius at
15 psi pressure for 60 to 120 minutes in a pressure vessel or
autoclave. This eliminates all [[savory-living-organisms-as-tools]] and is necessary for
supplemented substrates, which are highly susceptible to
contamination due to their enriched nutrient content. Sterilized
substrates must be handled in clean conditions to prevent
recontamination during inoculation.

## Supplementation Strategies

Supplements are nitrogen-rich additives that significantly boost
mushroom yields by providing additional nutrients for mycelial growth
and fruiting. The most common supplements include wheat bran, rice
bran, soybean meal, and various seed meals. Gypsum (calcium sulfate)
