# Substrate Formulation A substrate is the material on which mushroom mycelium grows and from which it draws nutrients to produce fruiting bodies. Selecting and preparing the right substrate is essential for successful cultivation. Different mushroom species have evolved to decompose different materials, and matching substrate to species is one of the foundational skills in mushroom growing. ## Substrate Categories Mushroom substrates fall into three broad categories based on the type of organic matter they are composed of: - **Lignocellulosic**: Wood-based substrates including hardwood sawdust, wood chips, straw, and agricultural waste. These support species that degrade cellulose and lignin — the primary decomposers. - **Composted**: Mixtures of manure and straw that have undergone a thermophilic composting process. Required by species like Agaricus bisporus that need a partially broken-down substrate. - **Supplemented**: Base substrates enriched with nitrogen-rich additives like bran, gypsum, or seed meal to boost yields. ## Primary Substrates **Hardwood sawdust** is the backbone of commercial shiitake, oyster, and lion's mane cultivation. Oak, maple, beech, and alder are excellent choices. Softwoods like pine and cedar contain resins and phenolic compounds inhibitory to most cultivated species. Sawdust should be from recently cut trees and stored dry to prevent premature decomposition. **Straw** is the preferred base substrate for oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus species). Wheat straw is most common, but oat, rye, and rice straw all work well. Straw is inexpensive, widely available, and has a high surface-area-to-volume ratio that promotes rapid colonization. It must be chopped or shredded to 2-5 cm lengths for optimal hydration and packing. **Wood chips** are used for outdoor log-style cultivation and as a component in mixed substrates. They provide structural air space and break down slowly, making them suitable for long-term beds and raised beds. ## Supplementation Supplements add readily available nitrogen and minerals that accelerate mycelial growth and increase biological efficiency (grams of fresh mushrooms per gram of dry substrate). Common supplements include: - **Wheat bran** (5-15% by dry weight): The most widely used supplement. Provides nitrogen, vitamins, and growth factors. - **Rice bran** (5-15%): Similar to wheat bran, slightly higher in fat content. - **Soy hulls** (5-20%): Extremely effective for oyster cultivation, capable of doubling yields on straw substrates. - **Gypsum** (1-3%): Calcium sulfate that improves substrate structure, buffers pH, and provides calcium and sulfur. - **Lime** (1-2%): Raises substrate pH, reducing contamination risk from Trichoderma and other acid-loving molds. Higher supplementation rates produce higher yields but also increase the risk of contamination, particularly during spawn run. Supplemented substrates must be sterilized, not merely pasteurized. ## Pasteurization vs. [[substrate-preparation]] Methods

Beyond the choice of materials, the physical preparation of substrate
affects colonization speed and yield. Sawdust should be sifted to remove
oversized particles that can create air pockets, and mixed thoroughly
with supplements to ensure even distribution. Straw must be chopped or
shredded — intact straw stems hydrate unevenly and pack poorly.

For bagged substrates, packing density matters. Overly tight packing
restricts [[mushroom-cultivation-jar-lid-management-loose-fit-vacuum-gas-exchange]] and slows colonization. Loose packing leaves too
much air space, reducing the nutrient density available to the mycelium.
The ideal density allows the bag to hold its shape when compressed
gently while still feeling somewhat springy.

## Common Problems

**Souring**: A sour or foul odor from substrate indicates bacterial
contamination, usually from over-hydration or insufficient
pasteurization. Affected substrate should be discarded.

**Dry patches**: Areas of substrate that do not colonize are often
caused by uneven hydration or over-compression. Proper mixing and
moderate packing density prevent this.

**Premature pinning**: Mushrooms forming inside the bag during spawn
run are triggered by light exposure, temperature fluctuations, or full
colonization of a localized area. Minimize light during incubation and
maintain stable temperatures.
