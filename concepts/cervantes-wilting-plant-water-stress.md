# Wilting and Plant Water Stress in Cannabis

Wilting is a visible sign that cannabis plants are experiencing water
stress -- either too much or too little. Jorge Cervantes describes
wilting across many contexts in cannabis cultivation, from normal
transient wilting in cuttings to catastrophic wilt caused by vascular
fungal diseases. Understanding the different causes of wilting and
their remedies is critical for preventing crop loss.

## Causes of Wilting

### Under-Watering

The most straightforward cause of wilting is insufficient water
availability. When roots cannot draw enough moisture to replace what
is lost through transpiration, cells lose turgor pressure and leaves
droop. This is often seen when growers let soil dry out excessively
between waterings. Cervantes notes that some growers deliberately
let soil dry enough that plants wilt one or two days before harvest
to speed drying time, but warns this should not affect end-product
quality.

### Over-Watering

Paradoxically, over-watering also causes wilting. When roots stand in
saturated, un-aerated water or medium, they are deprived of oxygen
and cannot function properly. Without oxygen, roots cannot absorb
water efficiently, even though it surrounds them. This is
particularly common in heavy soils, poorly drained containers, and
hydroponic systems with stagnant solution.

### Root Problems

Damaged, diseased, or underdeveloped root systems cannot supply
adequate water to foliage. Root rot fungi cause roots to turn from
healthy white to dark brown, followed by leaf chlorosis and wilting
of older leaves. Root wounds from transplanting, pests, or physical
damage invite wilt fungi and reduce water uptake capacity.

### Vascular Wilt Diseases

Fusarium wilt, Verticillium wilt, and Pythium wilt are fungal
diseases that block the plant's internal fluid transport system
(xylem). When fungi plug the fluid flow in plant tissue, entire
portions of the plant or the whole plant wilts -- often rapidly and
without warning. These diseases are discussed in detail in the
vascular wilt diseases topic.

### Heat Stress

Excessive temperatures accelerate transpiration beyond what the root
system can supply. During heat events, plants may wilt during the
hottest part of the day and partially recover in the evening.
Prolonged heat stress causes persistent wilting and can be fatal.

### Transplant Shock

Newly transplanted cannabis may wilt as roots recover from
disturbance. Cervantes notes that with proper transplanting
technique, there should be no signs of transplant shock or wilt.
Allowing adequate recovery time and maintaining high humidity around
recently transplanted plants helps minimize shock.

## Wilting in Clones

Cuttings routinely wilt after being taken from the mother plant.
Cervantes describes that some cuttings may wilt but regain rigidity
in a few days. Clones should look close to normal by the end of the
first week. Cuttings that are still wilted after seven days may root
so slowly that they never catch up with others and should be culled
or returned to the cloning chamber.

During cloning, transpiration management is critical. Cut leaves in
half to lower the transpiration surface area. Keep root-zone
temperature 5-10 degrees F warmer than ambient air to slow
transpiration while stimulating root growth. Misting with water cools
foliage and slows transpiration to help traumatized clones retain
moisture unavailable from nonexistent roots.

## CO2 and Wilting Resistance

Cervantes notes an interesting effect of CO2 enrichment on plant
turgor. Foliage on CO2-enriched plants is measurably thicker, more
turgid, and slower to wilt than leaves on non-enriched plants. This
is because CO2 causes stomata to partially close, reducing water
loss while maintaining adequate gas exchange for photosynthesis.

## Diagnosing Wilting

When plants wilt, check these factors in order:

1. Soil or medium moisture: is it bone dry or waterlogged?
2. Root health: are roots white and healthy or brown and slimy?
3. Temperature: is the grow room excessively hot?
4. Recent events: was the plant recently transplanted, trained, or
   exposed to stress?
5. Vascular symptoms: cut a stem and check for discolored (brown or
   reddish) xylem tissue, which indicates vascular wilt disease
6. Nutrient status: extreme EC levels can cause osmotic stress that
   mimics underwatering
