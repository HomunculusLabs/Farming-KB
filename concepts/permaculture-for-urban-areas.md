## Permaculture for Urban Areas

[[permaculture-design-principles]] apply as rigorously to tiny urban spaces as to large rural properties. In urban settings, the primary design challenges are limited space and reduced solar access, but these constraints can be overcome through strategic plant selection, [[coleman-greenhouse-tomato-vertical-growing]] systems, and creative use of available light and resources.

## Design Principles for Small Spaces

### Vertical Space as the Primary Growing Area

In urban areas with very little ground-level growing space, the vertical dimension becomes the greatest asset. Strategies include:

- **Trellising**: As much growing surface as possible should be trellised. Tall walls can support substantial crop production on vertical surfaces. High-yielding climbing vegetables like pole beans, cucumbers, and indeterminate tomatoes are preferred over bush varieties.
- **Roof systems**: Modest turf roofing on small roofs can support herbs and shallow-rooted crops. Glasshouses within roof structures can [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] season, though they require active ventilation with fans and heat storage systems.
- **Under-utilized spaces**: Under-floor areas, stairwell spaces, and basement areas with light access can all be pressed into service for growing.

### Light Maximization

Old houses in urban areas often have poor solar orientation. Strategies to address this include:

- **Reflecting surfaces**: Old mirrors and aluminum foil can light up dark corners. One documented example involved a person who obtained hundreds of mirror strips and built a reflector system that dramatically increased light in a tiny yard, effectively doubling the usable growing area of a very small property.
- **Glassed-in areas**: Maximizing glassed-in growing space provides more continuity and controllability in production. Even small cold frames or cloches significantly extend growing seasons.

### Plant Selection Strategy

In restricted urban spaces, low-yielding and slow-bearing plants should be abandoned in favor of:

- **High-yielding crops**: Lettuce, zucchini, peppers, and other long-producing vegetables
- **Continuous harvesters**: Plants that produce over extended periods rather than single harvests
- **Herbs**: High value per unit area and many thrive in small spaces and partial shade

### Wind Protection

Wind shielding is critical in urban environments, particularly around brick and masonry buildings where cold winds channel between structures:

- **Ivy as insulation and windbreak**: Ivy grown on brick or masonry walls provides significant benefits. It can prevent 70% of heat gain during spring and autumn in variable climates, and provides approximately 40% efficiency in preventing radiant heat escape. Most importantly, it checks the rapid lateral flow of cold winds along walls.
- **Trellis windbreaks**: Where ivy is not suitable, trellises supporting fast-growing vines can serve as windbreaks. Boxing trellises in helps redirect wind away from growing areas.
- **Evergreen hedges**: Where space permits, small evergreen hedges guard against cold winter winds.

## Optimal Lot Size

Research indicates that suburban lots between approximately one-quarter acre and one-half acre are the most productive for food production. The dynamics are notable:

- **Too much land** tends to be converted to lawns rather than food gardens
- **Too little land** discourages gardening entirely
- **One-quarter acre** appears to be the ideal size that motivates food production
- Such lots can produce 28% more than the equivalent area of conventional crop land

Poultry can be incorporated on quarter-acre suburban lots, though roosters and peacocks should be avoided due to noise.

## Social Strategies for Urban Land Access

One of the most important aspects of urban permaculture is securing access to growing land. Multiple proven systems exist:

### The Allotment System

Standard in Britain and available through community gardening organizations in America, allotments typically provide one-eighth acre plots. In Britain, a post office listing system matches people who want land with people (often elderly) who have land they can no longer maintain. The arrangement is typically informal: no rent is charged, but the gardener shares some of the produce. This system has been extraordinarily successful in locating urban growing space.

### The Food Park Strategy (Australia)

In Australia, land frequently reverts to local authorities when absentee owners fail to pay rates and taxes. The process for converting these neglected lots:

1. Identify neglected, boarded-up lots owned by the local council
2. Consult with local residents — they almost universally support the idea
3. Organize heavy initial landscaping: stone block beds, paths, water systems
4. Arrange for the council to drop off sawdust for paths and leaves for mulch
5. Hold a community planting day involving council, residents, and garden volunteers
6. Formally present the completed garden to surrounding house owners

The property remains with the council, but in practice is ceded to residents. After 3-4 years, these gardens remain well-maintained and productive. They transform depressed industrial urban neighborhoods.

### Garden Clubs

Particularly effective for low-income groups, including high-rise residents. Key requirements:

- A dedicated organizer
- Collective purchase of a farm with formally issued shares (e.g., a $100,000 farm funded by 100 people with $1,000 shares, $100 down)
- The farm must be accessible by public transport
- Overnight camping facilities and central toilet facilities can be installed
- Most participants visit on weekends

### City Farms (Britain)

Britain has established 46 city farms, each occupying deprived urban areas where children have never visited a working farm. These typically feature:

- Milk cows being milked and giving birth to calves
- Goats, geese, and rabbits
- Worm beds and [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]]
- They serve an educational purpose, connecting urban children with agricultural reality
- Most are voluntarily staffed but require at least a manager's wage

### Community Farm Link

A system linking small growers to 20-30 urban families on a personal basis:

- Families guarantee to buy the grower's crop
- The grower guarantees to try to meet their production needs
- The whole year is planned together with accurate harvest time estimates
- Families often assist with labor at critical periods (planting, harvesting, hilling potatoes)
- Weekend work schedules are arranged, with labor credited to workers
- The grower receives better than wholesale price; families pay less than retail

This is essentially the [[community-supported-agriculture-csa]] (CSA) model that has since spread globally.

## Cooperative Processing

Urban groups can establish shared processing infrastructure:

- **Wine presses and vats**: Immigrant communities in Australia (Greeks, Italians) have installed cooperative pressing facilities where people weigh in their grapes and receive juice back or a proportion of vat wines
- **Nut processing cooperatives**: Centralized cleaning and packaging machinery shared among nut growers
- **Grain mills, oil presses, distillation equipment**: These can be placed on rental in a district, giving everyone local access to food processing
- **Town stills**: In Yugoslavia, town halls house large brass stills where residents bring plums for community distillation into slivovitz

## The City as an Existing Farm

A [[science-and-living-systems-permaculture-perspective]] views cities as farms that already exist:

- **Grazing**: Cities contain very large areas suitable for grazing. One documented example in Adelaide, Australia involves a man running 9,000 sheep, each rented out at $6 per week to homeowners with overgrown yards. He shears and drenches them using a suburban shearing shed and pickup truck. Demand exceeds supply.
- **Fruit and nut trees**: Melbourne and San Francisco each contain approximately half a million citrus trees in private back yards, most of whose fruit falls to the ground unused. Organizing harvest of this existing resource represents enormous potential.
- **Building site surpluses**: Scouting groups can locate glass, fencing, [[gadd-fungal-biodegradation-concrete-building-materials]], doors, and windows at scrap prices from construction sites — materials that are expensive in rural areas.

## WWOOF (Working Weekends on Organic Farms)

Originally British, now operating in many countries, WWOOF connects urban people with organic farms for weekend working visits. Approximately 68% of people express desire to leave urban areas but are uncertain about their rural capabilities. WWOOF provides hands-on experience in fencing, firewood cutting, crop planting, harvesting, and machinery maintenance. Participants pay to work and learn, and the system serves as a pathway to rural transition.

## Food Cooperatives

Food cooperatives represent another urban strategy, with approximately 40,000 operating in the United States alone. Each typically involves 400-500 people. Many have evolved to purchase farms and hire farm managers, creating direct urban-rural food supply chains that bypass conventional distribution systems.
