# Mobile Greenhouse Crop Rotation Designs

## Philosophy of Year-Round Multi-Crop Production

The core aspiration behind mobile greenhouse crop rotation is to supply the widest
possible range of fresh vegetables to local customers through a broad-based
multiple-crop system across the longest possible season. Mobile greenhouses make
this achievable by enabling multi-crop, multi-season rotations that a static
greenhouse cannot support. Rather than dedicating a fixed structure to a single
crop or season, the farmer moves protection where it is needed most at each
point in the year, effectively multiplying the productive capacity of every
square foot of covered ground.

This approach transforms the greenhouse from a simple shelter into a dynamic
tool that follows the rhythm of the seasons and the varying hardiness of
different crops. The result is a farming system where spatial, temporal, and
biological relationships between crops are fully exploited for maximum yield
and continuous harvest.

---

## Two-Plot, One-Move Rotation

The simplest rotation pattern uses a pair of greenhouses, each moving once per
year from one plot to an adjoining plot. The two pairs of plots are adjacent,
and the greenhouses are arranged in a **diagonal offset** so neither shades the
other. Greenhouses are moved in the **fall** from summer crops to winter crops
— as late as possible to extend the summer season, but early enough to protect
winter salad crops before cold damage.

| Plot | Summer (Under Cover) | Summer (Uncovered) | Winter (Under Cover) |
|------|---------------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| A1   | Tomatoes            | —                   | —                    |
| A2   | —                   | Onions → Spinach    | Spinach              |
| B1   | —                   | Peas → Carrots      | Winter Carrots       |
| B2   | Arugula, pak choi, parsley, flowers | — | — |

In the fall, houses shift to cover A2 (spinach) and B1 (carrots). Come spring,
tomatoes replace the spinach on A2, and arugula replaces the carrots on B1.
The rotation then continues into the B plots. By cycling through all four
plots, the same crop returns to any given plot only **once every four years**,
providing a solid disease and soil-fertility break.

## Three-Plot, Three-Move Rotation

This intermediate design uses three plots and three annual moves, governed by
the principle of **progressive hardiness** — each successive crop can withstand
colder outdoor conditions than the last, allowing the greenhouse to move on at
the right moment.

1. **Mid-October** — Move from plot 1 (tomatoes) to plot 2, covering fall
   spinach for late November harvest.
2. **End of November** — Move to plot 3 to protect leeks for midwinter harvest.
   Leeks are hardy enough for outdoor temperatures until then.
3. **February 1 onward** — As leeks are sold, beds are re-prepared and sown to
   early carrots. By mid-March the entire house is in carrots.
4. **May 1** — Carrots no longer need protection; move to plot 2. Soil was
   prepared and covered with clear plastic in mid-April. Tomatoes go out around
   **May 5** after a few days of closed-up greenhouse warming.
5. **Mid-May** — New leek transplants go into plot 1 (last year's tomato plot).
6. **After carrot harvest from plot 3** — Summer lettuce, then spinach sown in
   early September to restart the cycle.

Crop flow: Plot 1 (tomatoes → leeks), Plot 2 (spinach → tomatoes), Plot 3
(leeks → carrots → lettuce → spinach). Progressive hardiness is key: tomatoes
need the most protection, spinach less, leeks survive uncovered until late
November, and carrots are fine on their own by May 1.

## Four-Plot, Five-Move Rotation

The most intensive design uses four plots and five moves per year, the fullest
expression of progressive hardiness determining move timing.

| Date         | Move                    | Purpose                                     |
|--------------|-------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| Winter       | House on Plot 1         | Protect overwintered onions or spinach      |
| March 25     | Move to Plot 2          | Warm soil for April 1 carrots and beets     |
| April 25     | Move to Plot 3          | Warm soil for May 1 zucchini and bush beans |
| Late May     | Move to Plot 4          | Cover summer tomatoes                       |
| Mid-July     | Move back to Plot 2     | Cover cucumbers for fall harvest            |
| October      | Plot 2: cucumbers → lettuce transplants |                          |
| Late August  | Plot 3: plant onions for overwintering   |                          |
| Thanksgiving | Move to Plot 3          | Cover overwintered onions for winter        |
| Late Fall    | Plot 1: compost/fertilize for next April 1 |                        |

Full sequence: Plot 1 (overwintered onions → early carrots/beets), Plot 2
(early carrots/beets → fall cucumbers → lettuce), Plot 3 (zucchini/beans →
overwintering onions), Plot 4 (summer tomatoes). The cycle closes when the
house covers Plot 3's onions at Thanksgiving and Plot 1 is readied for spring.

## Cumulative Benefits

### Spatial, Temporal, and Biological Relationships

- **Spatial**: Crops rotate across plots, breaking disease cycles and balancing
  soil nutrient demands.
- **Temporal**: The greenhouse moves in sync with seasonal progression, placing
  protection where it delivers the greatest value.
- **Biological**: Progressive hardiness determines the move sequence — each
  crop's cold tolerance dictates when it can be left uncovered.

### Extraordinary Yield from Minimal Infrastructure

One 1,000-square-foot [[mobile-greenhouse]] influences the production of 1,000 sq ft
each of onions/scallions, early carrots and beets, and early zucchini and beans,
plus earlier tomatoes, late-fall cucumbers, and late lettuce — all in one year.
This dramatically amplifies the value of the slight additional capital investment
for a movable house versus a static one.

### Extended Sales Season

Crops are harvestable **five weeks earlier** in spring and/or **five weeks
later** in fall, expanding the total sales season by approximately **two and a
half months** — a significant advantage for any small farm selling direct to
local customers. This system is designed for generalist producers who grow a
wide diversity of crops; the more diverse the mix, the more opportunities to
exploit the relationships that [[movable-greenhouses]] unlock.

---

## Key Principles

1. **Move as late as possible** from summer to winter crops to maximize benefit.
2. **Progressive hardiness** governs move timing — know each crop's cold limits.
3. **Diagonal offset** layout prevents shading between adjacent greenhouses.
4. **Four-year return cycles** provide adequate disease and fertility breaks.
5. **More plots and moves yield exponentially more production** per greenhouse.
6. **Soil preparation and warming** (clear plastic, closed-up house) should
   begin before the house arrives at each new plot.
