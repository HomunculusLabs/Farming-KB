## Advantages of Natural Culture

The key advantage of growing outdoors compared to indoors is that competitors are not
concentrated in a tight space. When cultivating mushrooms outdoors, the cultivator has
entropy as an ally. The ratio of hours spent per pound of mushrooms grown becomes quite
efficient. Outdoor beds need minimum maintenance and are controlled by volatile weather
patterns — meaning outdoor crops are not as frequent as indoor crops. However, they can
be just as intense, sometimes more so, especially if one pays modest attention to the
needs of the mushroom mycelium at critical junctures throughout its life cycle.

Generally, when mushrooms are fruiting in the wild, the inoculated patches also produce.
Summer-fruiting species like King Stropharia (Stropharia rugoso-annulata) require
frequent watering. Shaggy Manes (Coprinus comatus) prefer cool fall rains and need
little attention. The rate of growth, time to fruiting, and quality of the crop depend
upon the spawn, substrate materials, and weather conditions.

## Wild Mushroom Competition

substrates. For gilled mushrooms, caps are severed from stems and laid gills-down on
clean paper or glass. A glass jar is placed over the mushroom to reduce water loss. After
12 hours, thousands of spores fall in a radiating symmetry pattern called a Spore Print.

For immediate outdoor use, a more efficient method is spore mass slurry: submerge fairly
mature mushrooms in a 5-gallon bucket of water with a gram or two of table salt (to
inhibit bacteria) and 50 ml of molasses (to stimulate germination). After four hours,
remove the mushrooms. Allow the broth to sit 24-48 hours at 50-80°F. Spores begin
germinating within minutes to hours. The slurry can be expanded by a factor of ten in
48 hours and then broadcast onto prepared outdoor beds.

### 2. Transplantation (Mycelial Mining)

Transplantation is the moving of mycelium from natural patches to new habitats. Most
wild mushroom patches have a vast mycelial network beneath each mushroom. Portions can
be gathered and transferred to a new location, ensuring quick establishment without
having to germinate spores or buy commercial spawn. Paper sacks or cardboard boxes work
well for transport; disturbed mycelium dries quickly unless kept moist in a cool, dark
place.

The best sites for finding mycelium are sawdust piles at depths of 2-6 inches, where
mushroom mycelia run vigorously. Fans of mycelium are more often found along the
periphery than within the depths of piles. Ideal locations include sawmills, nurseries,
composting sites, recycling centers, and rhododendron gardens.

**Important caveat:** Gathering wild mycelium of mycorrhizal mushrooms could endanger
the parent colony. Mycorrhizal species should not be transplanted unless the parent
colony is imminently threatened with habitat loss. Digging up mycelium from a healthy
forest root zone can jeopardize the symbiotic relationship between mushroom and host
tree, leaving exposed roots vulnerable to disease and insect invasion.

### 3. Pure Cultured Spawn Inoculation

Commercial spawn provides mycelium of higher purity than can be harvested from nature.
For outdoor, unpasteurized substrates, wood-based spawn (sawdust or plugs) is far
superior to grain spawn. Grain spawn is attractive to rodents and other animals, and
its nutritional profile is poorly suited to outdoor environments.

Stamets recommends an inoculation rate of 5-50%, with an ideal of 20%. This means one
gallon of spawn can inoculate five gallons of prepared substrate. Although this rate
may seem high, rapid colonization is assured. More skilled cultivators can use a less
intensive rate of 10%, but dilute inoculation below 5% risks losing the patch to wild
competitors before full colonization is achieved.

## Species Succession Sequencing

Species succession can be accomplished indoors as well. After Shiitake stops producing on
logs or sawdust, the substrate can be broken apart, re-moistened, re-sterilized, and re-
inoculated with Oyster mushrooms. Once the Oyster life cycle is completed, the substrate
can be sterilized again and inoculated with King Stropharia, and finally Shaggy Manes.
All four species can be grown on the same substrate in sequence, increasingly reducing
the substrate mass without the addition of new materials. The majority of substrate mass
that does not evolve into gases is regenerated into mushrooms — a conversion that
Stamets describes as "mind-boggling."
