# Radiant Floor Heating

Radiant floor heating warms a space by circulating heated fluid (hydronic) or electric current through elements installed in or beneath the floor. Heat radiates upward from the floor surface, warming objects and people directly rather than heating air. This creates even, comfortable warmth with lower operating temperatures than forced-air systems.

## How Radiant Heating Works

Unlike forced-air systems that heat air and blow it into a room, radiant heating warms the floor surface to 75-85F. This warmth radiates in all directions, heating people and objects directly. The result is:

- Even temperature distribution (no hot/cold spots)
- No drafts or fan noise
- No dust circulation (better [[bloomfield-stachybotrys-indoor-mold-trichothecenes-air-quality]])
- Comfort at lower air temperatures (you feel warm even if air is cooler)
- Compatibility with [[passive-solar-design|passive solar]] [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]] floors

## Types of Radiant Floor Heating

### Hydronic (Water-Based) — Most Efficient for Whole Home

Circulates heated water through PEX tubing embedded in the floor.

**System Components:**
- Heat source: boiler, tankless water heater, wood boiler, or solar thermal
- PEX tubing: Cross-linked polyethylene, embedded in floor (typically 1/2 inch)
- Manifold: Distributes water to multiple zones
- Pump: Circulates water through the system
- Thermostat: Controls each zone independently
- Expansion tank: Absorbs pressure changes in the closed loop

**Installation Methods:**

1. **Slab-on-grade (Most Common for New Construction)**
   - PEX tubing stapled to wire mesh or rebar before pouring concrete
   - Tubing spacing: 6-12 inches on center (tighter [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]])
   - 2-4 inches of concrete above the tubing provides thermal mass
   - Insulation (2 inches rigid foam) beneath the slab prevents downward heat loss

2. **Thin-Slab Over Subfloor**
   - PEX attached to subfloor with aluminum [[albedo-absorption-and-heat-transfer]] plates
   - 1-1.5 inches of lightweight concrete or gypcrete poured over
   - Lower thermal mass but faster response time
   - Good for retrofits

3. **Staple-Up (Retrofit)**
   - PEX stapled to the underside of the subfloor between joists
   - Aluminum plates improve heat transfer upward
   - Insulation below the tubing prevents heat loss downward
   - Lowest installation cost but slowest response time

**Heat Sources for Hydronic Systems:**

- **Condensing boiler**: 90-98% efficient, natural gas or propane
- **Electric boiler**: Simple, no venting needed, higher operating cost
- **Tankless water heater**: Dual-purpose (domestic hot water + heating)
- **Wood gasification boiler**: Off-grid capable, uses firewood
- [[masonry-heater-design|Masonry heater]] with water coil: Heats water as a byproduct
- **Solar thermal**: Pre-heats water, reduces boiler load

### Electric Radiant — Best for Small Areas

Electric resistance cables or mats installed under flooring.

**Types:**
- Cable systems: Loose cable laid in serpentine pattern, embedded in thin-set or self-leveling compound
- Mat systems: Cable pre-attached to mesh mat, rolled out and embedded
- Film systems: Carbon-film radiant panels (for floating floors)

**Pros:**
- Simple installation — no boiler, pumps, or manifolds
- Individual room control
- No maintenance (no moving parts)
- Fast response time

**Cons:**
- Higher operating cost than hydronic (electricity vs. gas/wood)
- Not suitable for whole-home heating in cold climates
- Difficult to repair if embedded in concrete

### Air-Heated Radiant — Rare

Circulates heated air through tubes in the floor. Less common, lower efficiency, but can tie into a furnace system. Generally not recommended for new installations.

## Floor Covering Considerations

Floor covering directly affects radiant performance:

| Floor Covering | R-Value | Radiant Performance |
|---------------|---------|-------------------|
| Tile/Stone | 0.2-0.3 | Excellent — best conductor |
| Concrete (exposed) | 0.1-0.2 | Excellent |
| Hardwood | 0.7-1.0 | Good — use engineered wood (more stable) |
| Laminate | 0.5-0.8 | Good — check manufacturer's max temperature |
| Vinyl/LVT | 0.1-0.3 | Good — check temperature rating |
| Carpet | 1.0-2.5 | Poor — acts as insulation, blocks heat transfer |
| Cork | 0.5-1.0 | Good — natural insulator but conductive enough |

Maximum floor surface temperature: 85F for comfort and to protect flooring. Hardwood floors should not exceed 80F to prevent drying and warping.

## Zoning

Divide the floor into zones controlled by individual thermostats:

- Living areas: Independent zone
- Bedrooms: Independent zone (often cooler at night)
- Bathrooms: Independent zone (often warmer)
- Unused rooms: Zone can be turned off completely

Each zone requires its own thermostat, zone valve (hydronic), or relay (electric). Manifold systems make zoning straightforward in hydronic installations.

## Integration with Off-Grid Systems

For off-grid homesteads, radiant heating works well with:

- **Solar thermal panels**: Pre-heat water for the radiant system
- **Wood boiler**: Heat water with firewood, store in a large buffer tank
- **[[masonry-heater-design|Masonry heater]]**: Some designs include a water jacket for radiant heating
- **Battery system**: Electric radiant requires significant battery capacity (calculate carefully)

## Maintenance

- **Hydronic**: Annual pump inspection, periodic boiler maintenance, check for leaks
- **Electric**: Virtually maintenance-free
- **PEX tubing**: Rated for 50+ year lifespan when properly installed
- **Boiler**: 15-25 year lifespan depending on type
- **Pumps**: 10-15 year typical lifespan
