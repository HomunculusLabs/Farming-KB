# Food Processing, Preservation, and Storage

Mollison addresses food processing and storage as essential components of
permaculture self-reliance in Permaculture: A Designer's Manual. A productive
garden or farm generates seasonal abundance that must be preserved for lean
periods. Without processing and storage capacity, much of the harvest is lost
to spoilage, and the system cannot support year-round food security.

## Principles of Permaculture [[mollison-designers-temperate-zone-food-forest-design]]

Mollison's approach favors low-energy, low-technology methods that can be
practiced at household and community scale. The hierarchy of preference:

1. Eat fresh from the garden (primary strategy)
2. Extend the season with [[zone-1-intensive-garden]]
   and protected cropping
3. Store whole foods in their natural state ([[mollison-designers-cold-caves-root-cellar-storage]]ing, grain storage)
4. Process with minimal energy input (drying, fermentation, pickling)
5. Process with moderate energy input (canning, freezing)
6. Use high-energy methods only when necessary

The goal is to minimize external inputs while maximizing food availability
throughout the year.

## Solar Drying

Solar drying is Mollison's preferred preservation method for fruits,
vegetables, herbs, and fish. It requires no fuel, uses freely available solar
energy, and produces lightweight, shelf-stable products.

**Direct sun drying:** Simple and ancient. Lay food on screens or trays in
full sun with good airflow. Protect from insects with cheesecloth or mesh
covers. Best for arid and semi-arid climates with low ambient humidity.

**Solar dryers:** Enclosed structures that capture solar radiation and
concentrate heat while maintaining airflow. Mollison describes several designs:

- **Box dryer:** An insulated box with a transparent cover (glass or
  polycarbonate), inlet vents at the bottom, and outlet vents at the top.
  Food trays sit inside the heated chamber. Effective for small quantities.
- **Chimney dryer:** A tall, narrow drying chamber with a solar collector at
  the base. Warm air rises through the chimney, drawing fresh air through the
  collector and across the food trays. The chimney effect provides passive
  airflow without fans.
- **Concentrating dryer:** Reflective surfaces concentrate solar radiation
  onto the drying chamber, achieving higher temperatures for faster drying.

Foods suitable for solar drying: tomatoes, peppers, apples, pears, apricots,
figs, herbs, mushrooms, fish, and meat (jerky). Dry to 10-15% moisture content
for long-term storage. Condition dried foods by placing them in sealed
containers for several days and checking for moisture condensation; if present,
continue drying.

## Fermentation

Fermentation preserves food through the action of beneficial microorganisms
(lactic acid bacteria, yeasts) that produce acids, alcohol, or other
compounds inhibiting spoilage organisms. Mollison highlights fermentation as
particularly valuable in permaculture because it enhances nutritional value
(increases B vitamins, creates probiotics, improves mineral bioavailability)
while preserving food without heat or refrigeration.

**Lactic acid fermentation (pickling):** Vegetables are submerged in a brine
solution (2-5% salt by weight) that selects for lactic acid bacteria.
Cabbage becomes sauerkraut; cucumbers become pickles; daikon, carrots, and
turnips become traditional fermented vegetables across many cultures. Ferment
at room temperature for 1-4 weeks, then store in a cool place. Properly
fermented vegetables keep for 6-12 months or longer.

**Dairy fermentation:** Yogurt, kefir, and cheese preserve milk proteins and
fats while extending shelf life from days to months.

**Alcoholic fermentation:** Wine, cider, and mead preserve fruit sugars as
stable, long-lasting beverages. Mollison notes that fruit wines and ciders are
a practical use for windfall and surplus fruit.

**Other fermented foods:** Miso, tempeh, kimchi, sourdough bread, kombucha,
and vinegar each preserve different food substrates through unique microbial
processes.

## Canning

Canning provides shelf-stable food storage for years. Mollison covers both
water-bath and pressure canning.

**Water-bath canning:** Suitable for high-acid foods (pH below 4.6): fruits,
tomatoes (with added acid), pickles, jams, chutneys. Process in boiling water
for the time specified for the food type and jar size.

**Pressure canning:** Required for low-acid foods (vegetables, meat, fish,
beans, soups). Higher temperature (115-121 degrees C) destroys Clostridium
botulinum spores. Mollison cautions strict attention to hygiene, processing
times, and equipment. Use tested recipes and current guidelines.

**Root cellaring:** Stores whole fruits and vegetables in a cool, humid, dark,
well-ventilated environment. Optimal conditions: 0-5 degrees C, 85-95% humidity
for roots (60-70% for onions/garlic/squash), complete darkness, and air exchange.
Earth-sheltered construction provides natural thermal stability. Suitable crops:
potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips, onions, garlic, squash, pumpkins,
apples, pears, and cabbages.

**Grain and legume storage:** Dry to 12-14% moisture. Use metal drums, glass
jars, or food-grade containers. Diatomaceous earth (1 cup per 50 kg) kills
insect pests without chemical residues. Freezing at -18 degrees C for 72 hours
kills all stored product pest life stages. Neem leaf powder is a traditional
deterrent. For long-term storage, oxygen-absorbing packets or CO2 flushing
extends viability.

## Other Methods

**Freezing:** Energy-dependent but effective. Best for vegetables that lose
quality in other methods (peas, beans, corn, greens). Blanch before freezing.

**Smoking:** Preserves fish and meat through dehydration and antimicrobial
compounds in wood smoke. Requires fuel and skill but produces excellent flavor.

**Salt curing and oil preservation:** Salt inhibits microbial growth in fish,
meat, and vegetables. Submerging vegetables in olive oil extends shelf life
for several months (requires refrigeration).

## Integration with [[permaculture-design]]

Mollison recommends locating food processing and storage facilities in Zone 1
for daily access. A dedicated processing area with work surfaces, water supply,
fuel (for canning), and storage shelving should be part of every permaculture
homestead design. The [[mollison-designers-plant-nursery-propagation-bed-design]]
and food processing areas can share infrastructure (work surfaces, water,
shelter).

Seasonal processing activities create a rhythm that connects household members
to the productivity cycle of the land, reinforcing the relationship between
design, production, and consumption that is central to [[permaculture-ethics]].
