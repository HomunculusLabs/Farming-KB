# Mycorrhiza Bioassays for Soil Infectivity Assessment

Mycorrhiza bioassays are standardized experimental procedures used to quantify the infectivity of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] at a single point in time provides limited understanding of interactions between AM fungi and host plants.

The diversity and dynamics of mycorrhizal associations vary in space and time, making single-time-point measurements insufficient.

Calibrated bioassays of infectivity enable interpretation of mycorrhizal function by integrating the effects of propagule density, viability, and soil environmental conditions on the colonization process.

## Soil Infectivity Concept

The concept of soil infectivity was originally developed in plant pathology to describe the ability of soil containing pathogen propagules to initiate infection of host plants.

Applied to mycorrhizal fungi, infectivity refers to the ability of AM fungal propagules in soil to colonize roots of a host plant.

Infectivity can differ among host plant species even when grown in the same soil, reflecting compatibility between specific plant-fungal combinations.

## Most Probable Number Method

The most probable number (MPN) method is a quantitative bioassay approach that estimates the number of infective AM fungal propagules in a soil sample.

It involves preparing a dilution series of the soil sample and using a trap plant (typically a highly mycorrhiza-dependent species) to detect colonization at each dilution level.

The number of positive colonization readings across dilutions is used with statistical tables to estimate propagule density.

This method accounts for all types of infective propagules including spores, hyphal fragments, and root pieces containing viable fungal material.

## Direct Assessment Methods

Direct assessment of AM fungal colonization involves microscopic observation of stained root samples.

Root length colonized and percentage of root length colonized are the most common parameters.

The magnified intersection method developed by McGonigle and colleagues assesses the proportion of root length containing specific mycorrhizal structures: arbuscules, vesicles, and [[cervantes-fertilizer-application-strain-feeding]], and pesticide use on [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-inoculum-production-best-practices]] potential.

In restoration ecology, bioassays help evaluate the recovery of mycorrhizal communities after disturbance and the effectiveness of inoculation strategies.
