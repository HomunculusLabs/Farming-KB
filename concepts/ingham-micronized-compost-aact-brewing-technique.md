# Micronized Compost in AACT Brewing

## Overview

Micronized compost is compost that has been mechanically processed to extremely fine particle sizes, typically passing through screens of 200 micrometers or smaller. In the context of [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) brewing, micronized compost serves as a superior inoculum base because the dramatically increased surface area allows for more rapid and complete extraction of beneficial microorganisms, humic substances, and nutrients into the tea solution.

## Rationale and Benefits

### Surface Area and Extraction Efficiency

The primary advantage of micronizing compost before brewing AACT is the exponential increase in surface area. When compost particles are reduced from typical sizes (several millimeters) to sub-millimeter dimensions, the available surface area for microbial extraction increases by orders of magnitude. This means that:

- Beneficial bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes attached to compost particles are more readily dislodged into suspension
- Humic and fulvic acids dissolved from organic matter are extracted more completely
- Nutrient minerals bound in compost particles become more accessible to the brewing solution
- The total biomass of extracted organisms per unit of compost input increases significantly

Elaine Ingham emphasizes that the quality of the compost inoculum is the single most important factor in AACT brewing. Micronization represents a processing step that maximizes the biological potential of any given batch of compost, effectively getting more value from the same volume of starting material.

### Improved Organism Extraction

Standard compost tea extraction methods rely on the agitation and aeration of the brewer to pull organisms off compost particles and into suspension. With micronized compost, the organisms are already distributed across a vastly larger surface area and are present on much smaller particles. The energy required to dislodge them into solution is therefore lower, and the proportion of organisms successfully extracted is higher.

This is particularly important for fungal extraction, which has historically been one of the more challenging aspects of AACT brewing. Fungal hyphae tend to be more tenaciously attached to larger compost particles. Micronization breaks up the physical matrix that traps these hyphae, making them more available for suspension in the tea.

### Shorter Extraction Times

Because the extraction process is more efficient with micronized compost, the initial extraction phase of AACT brewing can be shortened. In standard brewing, Ingham recommends an extraction period at the beginning of the brew cycle where compost is placed in a mesh bag and allowed to steep while aeration begins. With micronized compost, this phase can be reduced because the organisms and soluble materials are more readily liberated.

However, it is important to note that the total brew time should not be shortened proportionally. The multiplication phase of brewing, where extracted organisms reproduce in the nutrient-rich aerated solution, still requires adequate time (typically 18-24 hours for bacterial teas, 24-36 hours for fungal teas) to reach optimal population levels.

## Production Methods

### Mechanical Grinding

The most common method for producing micronized compost is mechanical grinding. Various types of equipment can be used:

- Hammer mills can reduce compost to fine particles, though they may generate heat that could harm some organisms
- Ball mills provide gentle but thorough particle size reduction through tumbling action
- Screen-based sifters can separate already-fine fractions from coarser material
- Modified food processors or industrial grinders may be used at small scale

### Sieving and Screening

After grinding, the compost should be passed through appropriate screens to ensure uniform particle size. A 200-micrometer (approximately 80 mesh) screen is commonly recommended. Material that passes through this screen is considered adequately micronized for AACT brewing.

The coarser fraction that does not pass through the screen is not wasted; it can be returned to the composting process, used as a mulch, or incorporated into soil amendments where larger particle size is acceptable or even desirable.

### Moisture Considerations

The moisture content of compost at the time of micronization affects both the grinding process and the biological quality of the result. Compost that is too dry may become dusty and lose volatile biological components, while compost that is too wet may clog grinding equipment and form clumps rather than fine particles.

A moisture content of approximately 30-40% is generally optimal for micronization. Compost at this moisture level will form a weak ball when squeezed but will crumble easily. If the compost is too wet, it should be allowed to air-dry partially before processing. If it is too dry, a light misting with dechlorinated water can restore workable moisture.

## Quality Control

### Organism Viability

A critical concern with micronized compost is maintaining the viability of beneficial organisms through the grinding process. Mechanical grinding generates heat through friction, and excessive heat can kill or stress bacteria and fungi. This concern must be balanced against the benefits of increased surface area.

To minimize heat damage:
- Use equipment designed for processing biological materials
- Process in small batches to prevent heat accumulation
- Allow equipment to cool between batches if necessary
- Work in a cool environment when possible
- Monitor output temperature and stop if excessive heating occurs

### Contamination Risks

The grinding equipment itself can be a source of contamination if not properly maintained. Equipment that has been used for processing non-compost materials may introduce chemical residues or unwanted organisms. Dedicated equipment for compost micronization is ideal.

Between uses, equipment should be cleaned to prevent cross-contamination between different compost batches. This is especially important if compost from different sources or with different biological profiles is being processed.

### Storage of Micronized Compost

Once micronized, compost has a larger surface area exposed to the environment, which means it is more susceptible to drying, oxidation, and potential contamination. Micronized compost should be used promptly for AACT brewing rather than stored for extended periods.

If storage is necessary, it should be kept in a cool, dark, slightly moist environment in a breathable container. Sealed plastic bags are not recommended because they can create anaerobic conditions that favor undesirable organisms. Paper bags, burlap sacks, or open bins in a humidity-controlled environment are better choices.

## Application in AACT Brewing

### Inoculum Rates

Because micronized compost provides more efficient extraction, the volume of compost required per gallon of tea may be reduced compared to standard compost. However, Ingham cautions against simply reducing the compost amount proportionally, because the total number of organisms needed for adequate soil or foliar inoculation remains the same.

A practical approach is to use the standard recommended compost-to-water ratio but verify through microscopy that adequate organism levels are achieved. If organism counts are higher than needed with standard compost rates, the micronized compost amount can be reduced in subsequent brews.

### Mesh Bag Considerations

Micronized compost may pass through the mesh bags typically used to hold compost during brewing. A finer mesh bag (smaller pore size) may be needed to contain micronized compost while still allowing organisms to pass through into the tea.

Alternatively, some practitioners simply add micronized compost directly to the brewer without a containment bag, allowing the fine particles to circulate freely. This approach maximizes extraction but means that the finished tea will contain suspended particulate matter that may clog spray equipment. If this method is used, adequate filtration before application is essential.

### Compatibility with Other Ingredients

Micronized compost is compatible with all standard AACT brewing ingredients, including molasses, kelp, fish hydrolysate, humic acids, and rock dust. The increased extraction efficiency may mean that less of these supplemental ingredients are needed to achieve target organism populations, though this should be verified through testing rather than assumed.

## Limitations and Considerations

### Cost and Equipment

Micronization requires additional equipment and processing steps beyond standard compost preparation. For small-scale growers, the investment in grinding equipment may not be justified by the marginal improvements in extraction efficiency. For larger operations or commercial AACT brewers, the improved consistency and extraction rates may justify the added complexity.

### Not a Substitute for Compost Quality

Micronization cannot compensate for poor-quality compost. If the source compost lacks adequate microbial diversity, contains pathogens, or is otherwise deficient, micronizing it will simply produce a larger quantity of deficient material. The same [[compost-quality-standards]] that apply to standard AACT brewing apply equally to micronized compost: the compost must be aerobic, mature, biologically diverse, and free of contaminants.

### Potential for Over-Extraction

In some cases, the increased extraction efficiency of micronized compost can lead to excessive levels of certain compounds in the tea. For example, higher concentrations of phenolic compounds, tannins, or humic substances from more complete extraction could potentially inhibit some beneficial organisms at elevated concentrations. Monitoring tea chemistry and organism levels through regular testing helps identify and correct such issues.
