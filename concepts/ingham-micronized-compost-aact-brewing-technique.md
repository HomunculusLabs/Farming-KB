## Micronized Compost in AACT Brewing

## Overview

Micronized compost is compost that has been mechanically processed to extremely fine particle sizes, typically passing through screens of 200 micrometers or smaller. In the context of [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]], humic substances, and nutrients into the tea solution.

## Rationale and Benefits

### Surface Area and Extraction Efficiency

The primary advantage of micronizing compost before brewing AACT is the exponential increase in surface area. When compost particles are reduced from typical sizes (several millimeters) to sub-millimeter dimensions, the available surface area for microbial extraction increases by orders of magnitude. This means that:

- Beneficial bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes attached to compost particles are more readily dislodged into suspension
- [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] are extracted more completely
- Nutrient minerals bound in compost particles become more accessible to the brewing solution
- The total biomass of extracted organisms per unit of compost input increases significantly

Elaine Ingham emphasizes that the quality of the compost inoculum is the single most important factor in AACT brewing. Micronization represents a processing step that maximizes the biological potential of any given batch of compost, effectively getting more value from the same volume of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-spore-extraction-methods]] rely on the agitation and aeration of the brewer to pull organisms off compost particles and into suspension. With micronized compost, the organisms are already distributed across a vastly larger surface area and are present on much smaller particles. The energy required to dislodge them into solution is therefore lower, and the proportion of organisms successfully extracted is higher.

This is particularly important for fungal extraction, which has historically been one of the more challenging aspects of AACT brewing. Fungal hyphae tend to be more tenaciously attached to larger compost particles. Micronization breaks up the physical matrix that traps these hyphae, making them more available for suspension in the tea.

### Shorter Extraction Times

Because the extraction process is more efficient with micronized compost, the initial extraction phase of AACT brewing can be shortened. In standard brewing, Ingham recommends an extraction period at the beginning of the brew cycle where compost is placed in a mesh bag and allowed to steep while aeration begins. With micronized compost, this phase can be reduced because the organisms and soluble materials are more readily liberated.

However, it is important to note that the total brew time should not be shortened proportionally. The multiplication phase of brewing, where extracted organisms reproduce in the nutrient-rich aerated solution, still requires adequate time (typically 18-24 hours for bacterial teas, 24-36 hours for fungal teas) to reach optimal population levels.

## Production Methods

### Mechanical Grinding

The most common method for producing micronized compost is mechanical grinding. Various types of equipment can be used:

- Hammer mills can reduce compost to fine particles, though they may generate heat that could harm some organisms
- Ball mills provide gentle but thorough particle size reduction through tumbling action
- Screen-based sifters can separate already-fine fractions from coarser material
- Modified food processors or industrial grinders may be used at small scale

### Sieving and Screening

After grinding, the compost should be passed through appropriate screens to ensure uniform particle size. A 200-micrometer (approximately 80 mesh) screen is commonly recommended. Material that passes through this screen is considered adequately micronized for AACT brewing.

The coarser fraction that does not pass through the screen is not wasted; it can be returned to the composting process, used as a mulch, or incorporated into soil amendments where larger particle size is acceptable or even desirable.

### Moisture Considerations

The moisture content of compost at the time of micronization affects both the grinding process and the biological quality of the result. Compost that is too dry may become dusty and lose volatile biological components, while compost that is too wet may clog grinding equipment and form clumps rather than fine particles.

A moisture content of approximately 30-40% is generally optimal for micronization. Compost at this moisture level will form a weak ball when squeezed but will crumble easily. If the compost is too wet, it should be allowed to air-dry partially before processing. If it is too dry, a light misting with dechlorinated water can restore workable moisture.

## Quality Control

### Organism Viability

A critical concern with micronized compost is maintaining the viability of beneficial organisms through the grinding process. Mechanical grinding generates heat through friction, and excessive heat can kill or stress bacteria and fungi. This concern must be balanced against the benefits of increased surface area.

To minimize heat damage:
- Use equipment designed for processing biological materials
- Process in small batches to prevent heat accumulation
- Allow equipment to cool between batches if necessary
- Work in a cool environment when possible
- Monitor output temperature and stop if excessive heating occurs
