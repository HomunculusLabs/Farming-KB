## Ecological Functions and Symbioses

Fungi in Southeast Asia perform essential ecological functions that sustain the
region's forests and agricultural systems. Decomposer fungi break down the
enormous volumes of plant litter produced by tropical forests, facilitating
nutrient cycling and maintaining soil fertility. In peat swamp forests, which
store vast quantities of carbon, [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] form mutualistic associations with the roots of most
tropical trees, enhancing nutrient and water uptake while receiving
carbohydrates from their plant partners. Dipterocarp forests, which dominate
much of lowland Southeast Asia, depend heavily on ectomycorrhizal fungi for
regeneration and growth. Research has shown that dipterocarp seedlings
establish more successfully when inoculated with appropriate mycorrhizal
partners.

An unusual and ecologically significant group of fungi in Southeast Asia are
the Termitomyces, which are cultivated by fungus-growing termites
(Macrotermitinae) within their nest structures. These termites maintain fungal
gardens that provide food for the colony and contribute to the decomposition of
plant material. The mutualism between termites and Termitomyces is one of the
most sophisticated examples of insect-fungus symbiosis and has significant
impacts on nutrient cycling in tropical ecosystems.

## Ethnomycology and Economic Importance

Southeast Asian cultures have a long and intimate relationship with fungi.
Wild mushroom collecting is a widespread practice, particularly in rural and
highland communities where mushrooms provide both food and supplemental income.
Seasonal mushroom markets in northern Thailand, Vietnam, and the Philippines
offer a dazzling variety of wild-harvested species, reflecting deep local
knowledge of fungal identification, ecology, and preparation.
