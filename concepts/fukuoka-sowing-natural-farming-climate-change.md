# Natural Farming and Climate Change — Fukuoka's Analysis

*Fukuoka's analysis of desertification, climate change, and environmental
degradation, and [[fukuoka-why-natural-farming-blocked-in-japan]] is the only viable response.*

## Desertification as a Human-Caused Crisis

Fukuoka believed that most of the world's deserts were created by human activity
based on incomplete understanding. He cited the rapid loss of vegetation across
the globe:

- Africa was largely devoid of vegetation, while just a few hundred years
earlier it was covered by deep forests - India's vegetation had disappeared
rapidly over forty to fifty years, now covering less than 10 percent of the
land's surface - The Himalayas had become bald, treeless mountains in just
twenty years - The Philippines, Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia saw land
conditions deteriorate as nature-protecting [[natural-farming-methods-comparison]] were swallowed by
modern civilization - Tropical rain forests in Asia and Brazil were being
deforested at rates that would make oxygen scarce

The immediate cause was indiscriminate deforestation and large-scale agriculture
to support the materialistic cultures of developed countries, but the remote
cause stretched back thousands of years. The natural world did not become a
desert on its own.

## Why Conventional Countermeasures Fail

Fukuoka systematically criticized every major approach to combating
desertification:

**Dam building and irrigation canals.** The Aswan Dam on the Nile, planned dams
on the Yellow River and Narmada River — all would serve as short-term measures
but become hundred-year mistakes. The main reason water disappears from rivers
is that rain has stopped falling. Building dams to redirect river flow puts the
cart before the horse.

**[[comparison-drip-irrigation-vs-passive-irrigation]] and water-retaining materials.** While locally effective,
these require materials and energy that make them questionable as long-term
solutions. Super-absorbent resins plowed into soil in Egypt, plastic pipes in
Mexico and Israel — all short-term expedients.

**[[roger-heim-scientific-study-psilocybin]] and computer simulation.** China's massive effort to halt the
Taklamakan Desert used satellites, airplanes, remote surveys, deep boring, soil
analysis, and computer simulations. Every plan for revegetation created this way
failed. Governments then said they needed to go back and reanalyze the data —
costing more time and money.

**Fast-growing tree monocultures.** Eucalyptus and similar fast-growing trees
require large amounts of water. When watered only to shallow depth, soil becomes
compacted, roots cannot extend, and many trees wither and die.

**Pivot farms on aquifer water.** In Saudi Arabia and the American desert east
of the Rocky Mountains, water pumped from aquifers that filtered down for tens
of thousands of years was creating green circles in the desert. When the
aquifers are depleted, these farms will collapse.

**Salinization from irrigation.** Irrigation water quickly evaporates,
precipitating salt into surface soil layers. The salts must be drained
elsewhere, causing toxic conditions there. The Kesterson Wildlife Refuge in
California's San Joaquin Valley became lethally contaminated with selenium from
agricultural drainage water.

## Vegetation Causes Rain

Fukuoka proposed a revolutionary understanding of the water cycle. His
conjecture was that deforestation and the change from perennial bunchgrasses to
annuals contributed directly to decreased rainfall. "Rain doesn't only fall from
the sky," he suggested. "It also falls up from below." The vegetation,
especially trees, actually causes rain to fall.

The first step in countering desertification is not to redirect the flow of
rivers but to cause rain to fall again through revegetation. Vast stretches of
desert must be revegetated at one time, causing rain clouds to rise from the
earth. When a large area becomes green, even temporarily, it protects against
radiant heat and soil temperature drops. One tree acts as a pump bringing up
underground water; its transpired mist acts as both sprinkler and fan.
