# Wind Patterns and the Beaufort Scale

Wind is one of the most powerful and pervasive forces affecting landscape design, agriculture, and human comfort. Of all natural elements, designers have least control over wind in terms of storage or generation, but its behaviour on site can be controlled by excluding, reducing, or increasing its force using windbreaks and wind funnels. Understanding wind patterns, their seasonal variations, and their effects on living [[mollison-designers-soil-water-storage-in-forest-systems|systems]] is essential for effective [[permaculture-design]].

## The Beaufort Scale

The [[beaufort-scale]] is the standard system for reporting wind speed and its effects. It provides a practical framework for understanding the relationship between wind speed and damage potential.

### Wind Speed Classifications

| Beaufort No. | Knots | km/h | mph | Description |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---|
| 0 | <1 | <1 | <1 | Calm |
| 1 | 1 | 1 | 0–1 | Light airs |
| 2 | 1–3 | 1–5 | 1–3 | Light breeze |
| 3 | 4–6 | 6–11 | 4–7 | Light breeze |
| 4 | 7–10 | 12–19 | 8–12 | Gentle breeze |
| 5 | 11–16 | 20–28 | 13–17 | Moderate breeze |
| 6 | 17–21 | 29–38 | 18–24 | Fresh breeze |
| 7 | 22–27 | 39–49 | 25–31 | Stormy breeze |
| 8 | 28–33 | 50–61 | 32–38 | Near gale |
| 9 | 34–40 | 62–74 | 39–46 | Gale |
| 10 | 41–47 | 75–88 | 47–55 | Strong gale |
| 11 | 48–55 | 89–102 | 56–64 | Storm |
| 12 | 56–63 | 103–117 | 65–73 | Violent storm |
| 13 | 64+ | 118+ | 74+ | Hurricane |

### Effects on Crops and Structures

| Beaufort No. | Effects |
|:---:|:---|
| 0–2 | Slight; no damage to crops or structures |
| 3–4 | Damage to very susceptible species; useful energy produced |
| 5–6 | Mechanical damage to crops, some damage to structures |
| 7–12 | Severe structural and crop damage; damage to windmills |

## Wind Speed Thresholds for Agriculture

Specific wind speed thresholds have critical implications for food production:

- **8 km/h**: Harmless to crops; no significant effects
- **24 km/h**: Reduces crop production and causes weight loss in animals
- **32–40 km/h**: Sheer mechanical damage to plants exceeds all other effects — plants may be uprooted, torn, or bowled along like tumbleweeds

Trees are severely wind-pruned by a combination of mechanical damage and salt burn near coasts, and by the additional factors of sandblast in dunes (desert or coasts) and iceblast in cold climates. Wind transport of sands in deserts and incipient deserts buries fences, buildings, trees, and crops.

## Wind Reduction by Forests

Forests are extraordinarily effective at reducing wind velocity. The reduction follows a predictable pattern based on penetration depth:

| Penetration into Forest | Remaining Wind Velocity |
|:---:|:---:|
| 30 m | 60–80% |
| 60 m | 50% |
| 120 m | 15% |
| 300–1,500 m | Negligible wind |

This data demonstrates that even relatively narrow forest belts provide significant wind protection, and that substantial forests essentially eliminate wind entirely within their interior.

## Wind Directions and Seasons

### Global Wind Patterns by Latitude

Winds are fairly predictable and often bi-modal in their directions and effects in local areas. For the landscape designer, wind-flagging of older trees and wind-pruning tell the story — the site itself has summed total wind effects over time.

**Latitudes 0° to 35° (North and South) in oceanic areas:**
- Winds are bi-modal and seasonal
- Southeast or northwest in the southern hemisphere
- Southwest or northeast in the northern hemisphere
- Local directions are modified by landscape, but windward and leeward coast phenomena are almost universal

**Latitudes 35° to limits of occupied coasts:**
- Westerlies prevail in winter
- Easterlies occur more sporadically as highs or lows pass over the site and remain stationary to the east or west
- Cold winds blow from continental interiors in winter
- Warmer but still chilling winter winds blow in from the seas

### Tropical Island and Peninsula Winds

Islands and peninsulas from latitudes 0° to 28° experience two main wind modes:

1. **Trade winds** (winter winds): Southeast in the southern hemisphere, northeast in the northern hemisphere
2. **Monsoon winds**: Cross-equatorial monsoon winds deflect to blow from the northwest in the southern hemisphere and as southwest monsoons in the northern hemisphere

In effect, these latitudes see two seasons of winds and two short periods of relative calms or shifting wind systems. Southeast Asia and the Pacific or Indian Ocean islands are most predictably affected by these bi-modal systems.

## Wind Loads and Carried Materials

### Salt and Chemical Deposits

On warm sea coasts, where onshore winds carry salt and evaporate moisture, salt deposits on vegetation are the limiting factor on species selection. Only selected hardy species with fibrous or waxy surfaces can escape death or deformation by salt burn.

Beyond inorganic materials, wind may transport organisms ranging from almost impalpable spores of fungi and ferns to very weighty insects. Plague locusts are swept aloft by heated air columns and carried as frozen or chilled swarms to down-draught areas. Mosquitoes, fruit flies, wasps, and spiders deliberately spin aerial floatlines and migrate over mountain and oceanic barriers on windstreams. Flocks of migrating birds take advantage of windstreams as they circle the globe.

### Acid Rain and Industrial Pollution

More severe than mechanical damage are the minute sulphur and nitrogen particles carried by wind. In industrial areas and for many miles downwind:

- Dry sulphur falling on leaf and soil converts to acid in misty rains
- On parts of the northeast coast of America and Canada, acid rains can burn gardens and forests, or make holes in garments and tents in a few days
- Near acid production factories, even paints and roofing are pitted and holed
- In areas downwind of nuclear waste stores, tests, and accidents, plutonium and other radioactives add to the wind hazard factor

## Wind Harmonics

Wind does not blow steadily but varies as gusts and calms in a predictable and locality-specific way. Research by [[frank-bethwaite]], a New Zealand-born Australian boat designer, outlined characteristics of ground winds:

### Gust and Lull Patterns

- A **49–60 minute frequency of gusting** is typical of mid-latitudes
- Gusts are approximately **40% stronger** than lulls
- In lulls, the wind direction changes, with light crosswinds at about **15° to the main wind direction**
- The variation in wind speed and direction is systematic and regular
- Both frequencies, durations, and amplitudes can be measured using combinations of stopwatches, anemometers, and wind vanes

### Visible Wind Patterns

Such "waves" of wind are made visible in:

- **Grasslands**: Wave fronts may come at every 14 seconds, with gusts at longer intervals
- **Water surfaces**: Viewed from a cliff, wave patterns reveal wind structure
- **Cloud formations**: Reflected as "rank and file" systems where lulls show as spaces between cloud ranks, and in these spaces, light clouds of different alignment represent the direction change typical of lulls

### Site-Specific Wind Periods

- **Bahrain**: 5.25 minute periods
- **Sydney**: 6–12 minute periods
- **Toronto**: 10 minute periods
- **Sea waves**: Usually about 5–12 per minute, the period lengthening in storms

### Prevalent vs. Energy Winds

In the westerly wind belts, two categories are distinguished:

- **Prevalent winds** (8–24 km/h): Blow for five out of seven windy days
- **Energy winds** (16–40 km/h): Blow on the other two days, coming from between 15°–20° off the direction of the prevalent winds

## Transpiration and Wind

Flow of air (wind) over leaf surfaces promotes rapid transpiration, as does high light intensity. When both effects occur together, shrubs and trees may lose too much water. Trees guard against this combined factor by:

- Presenting **whitish undersides of leaves** to the light as wind blows
- **Altering leaf angles** to reflect light, trap air, or reduce the area of leaf exposed
- **Closing leaf pores (stomata)** to prevent moisture loss
