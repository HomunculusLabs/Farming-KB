## Wind Patterns and the Beaufort Scale

Wind is one of the most powerful and pervasive forces affecting landscape design, agriculture, and human comfort. Of all natural elements, designers have least control over wind in terms of storage or generation, but its behaviour on site can be controlled by excluding, reducing, or increasing its force using windbreaks and wind funnels. Understanding wind patterns, their seasonal variations, and their effects on living [[permaculture-design]].

## The Beaufort Scale

The [[frank-bethwaite]], a New Zealand-born Australian boat designer, outlined characteristics of ground winds:

### Gust and Lull Patterns

- A **49–60 minute frequency of gusting** is typical of mid-latitudes
- Gusts are approximately **40% stronger** than lulls
- In lulls, the wind direction changes, with light crosswinds at about **15° to the main wind direction**
- The variation in wind speed and direction is systematic and regular
- Both frequencies, durations, and amplitudes can be measured using combinations of stopwatches, anemometers, and wind vanes

### Visible Wind Patterns

Such "waves" of wind are made visible in:

- **Grasslands**: Wave fronts may come at every 14 seconds, with gusts at longer intervals
- **Water surfaces**: Viewed from a cliff, wave patterns reveal wind structure
- **Cloud formations**: Reflected as "rank and file" systems where lulls show as spaces between cloud ranks, and in these spaces, light clouds of different alignment represent the direction change typical of lulls

### Site-Specific Wind Periods

- **Bahrain**: 5.25 minute periods
- **Sydney**: 6–12 minute periods
- **Toronto**: 10 minute periods
- **Sea waves**: Usually about 5–12 per minute, the period lengthening in storms

### Prevalent vs. Energy Winds

In the westerly wind belts, two categories are distinguished:

- **Prevalent winds** (8–24 km/h): Blow for five out of seven windy days
- **Energy winds** (16–40 km/h): Blow on the other two days, coming from between 15°–20° off the direction of the prevalent winds

## Transpiration and Wind

Flow of air (wind) over leaf surfaces promotes rapid transpiration, as does high light intensity. When both effects occur together, shrubs and trees may lose too much water. Trees guard against this combined factor by:

- Presenting **whitish undersides of leaves** to the light as wind blows
- **Altering leaf angles** to reflect light, trap air, or reduce the area of leaf exposed
- **Closing leaf pores (stomata)** to prevent moisture loss
