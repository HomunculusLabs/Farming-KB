# Propagation Methods Organic

updated: 2026-04-18

The best way to ensure everything in your garden has been cultivated organically is to grow as much as possible from seed. However, some plants will not produce exact replicas from seed, so alternative vegetative techniques — division, layering, and cuttings — are required to ensure new plants are exactly the same as the parent.

## Growing from Seed

### Sowing Outside

The cheapest method of raising plants is by sowing seeds directly into prepared soil. Use this for most vegetables, hardy annuals, and many herbaceous perennials.

**Soil Preparation:** Normal organic methods produce a good, workable structure. Rake soil level, sprinkle fertilizer at the recommended rate. A firm bed is usually required — compress by walking over the surface with weight on your heels. Rake to a fine tillage. Never tread on wet soil or risk destroying soil structure. On deep beds, consolidation is unnecessary — leave three or four weeks after digging for soil to settle.

**When to Sow:** Varies by plant (normally shown on seed packets). Seeds sown in soil below 45F (7C) will not germinate until it warms up. Seeds sown in mid-spring often germinate at the same time as, or even before, those sown in cold, wet soil earlier. Exceptions include alpine seeds and some trees that require a period of freezing. Use cloches to start sowing outside in early spring — place cloches two weeks before sowing to warm soil.

**The Stale Seedbed:** Cultivate the seedbed a few weeks before sowing. Let dormant weed seeds germinate, then hoe them out immediately before sowing. This eliminates competition from native weeds that germinate faster and grow quicker than cultivated varieties.

**Encouraging Germination:** Seeds with hard coats can be soaked overnight in water; very hard seeds can first be filed with a nail file. Seeds like beets have natural germination inhibitors — remove by washing under cold tap or soaking overnight.

**Depth and Distance:** The main cause of seed failure is sowing too deeply. Make "shallow" rows — furrows as shallow as possible. Space seeds and rows correctly to avoid overcrowding. Root crops will "fork" if transplanted. 60-90% of seeds will germinate, so sow thinly.

**Sowing Techniques:** Always sow seed thinly. Hold seeds in the palm of your hand and sow a pinch at a time. Large seeds can be sown singly or in "stations" (groups of two or three at required distances). With deep beds, use block-sowing technique. If soil is very dry, water rows before sowing and allow to drain — never water after sowing as this causes "capping" (soil crust preventing water entry or seedling emergence).

**Drawing Seed Rows:** Narrow rows — use one corner of a draw hoe, a broom handle pressed along a planting line, or a short stick. Wide bands — put a hoe flat on the ground and pull toward you, ensuring uniform depth (used in deep-bed cultivation for selectively thinned crops).

**Covering:** Cover seeds by running the back of a rake down the center of the row. Tap down lightly with the back of the rake for good seed-soil contact. Always label rows with commercial plant labels.

**Pre-germinating:** Sow seeds onto moistened tissue in a plastic container. When first roots show (no longer than 1/8in/3mm), sow immediately. Large seeds can be picked with tweezers; small seeds need to be fluid-sown (suspended in alginate gel or wallpaper paste and sown in a seed row). If soil is dry, water after sowing to prevent gel setting too hard.

### Sowing Inside

Start much earlier in the year — vegetables in midwinter, planted out under cloches in early spring for first crops in late spring.

**Containers:** Use pots, seed trays, module trays, or any shallow container with drainage holes. Clay pots "breathe" (good for alpines needing drainage). Plastic pots need less frequent watering. Module trays have individual cells — roots are never disturbed, eliminating transplant shock. Peat-free coir blocks are also available.

**Procedure:** Fill tray/pot with moist seed [[organic-composting-methods]], firm lightly (peat-free needs very little firming). Level top, firm with a firming board or bottom of another pot. Moisten compost thoroughly before sowing. Sow seeds onto surface. Cover all except very small seeds with their own depth of vermiculite. Cover with opaque plastic, put in a warm place. Check daily — when first seedling appears, move to a light place (not direct sunlight). Cover with newspaper if sun may scorch seedlings.

**Light Box:** On windowsills, light from one direction causes spindly growth. Make a "light box" from a cardboard box lined with kitchen foil to reflect light all around. Bring into room center at night in winter.

**Sowing in Seed Trays:** Fill tray with moistened sowing compost. Press compost into edges with fingers, consolidate with firming board. Pour seed into palm, cup hand to form channel, tap to move seeds along. Cover with vermiculite or seed compost.

**Sowing Small vs Large Seeds:** Very small seeds — mix with silver sand as a spreading agent. Large seeds (beans, cucumbers) — sow two to a pot, on their sides. Remove weaker seedling if both germinate.

### Thinning and Transplanting

**Outside Seedlings:** Water rows well before handling. To thin, pull up unwanted seedlings leaving plants at required spacing. Put thinnings on compost heap. For transplanting, water the day before, lift seedlings with trowel leaving as much root as possible, transplant to new rows using a planting board for spacing, water with fine nozzle. Handle seedlings by their leaves, not stems — stems bruise easily and invite fungus attack.

**Container Seedlings:** Thin or transplant ("prick out") into larger trays when large enough to handle. Fill larger tray with compost, lift seedlings by pushing a dibber under roots (handle by leaves only), make holes with dibber in new compost, insert seedlings, firm compost. Water well and place in light spot out of direct sunlight.

**Hardening Off:** Before planting out, acclimatize greenhouse seedlings to colder conditions. Place first in closed cold frame, then gradually open during the day, then at night, until left completely open.

### Saving Seed

Store opened packets in a dry, airtight container in a cool, frost-free place. Most last at least one more season. You can harvest your own seeds from many vegetables and ornamentals — but never save F1 hybrid seeds (they will not grow true to type). Let seeds mature and ripen on plants, remove pods just before they burst. For difficult seeds, place paper bags over almost-mature pods to catch seeds as they ripen, or cut whole flowering stems and hang upside down over a sheet.

## Propagation by Division

The simplest and most effective vegetative method, producing good, sizable plants quickly. Used to increase perennials.

**Dividing Perennials:** Plants forming spreading clumps should be divided every few years to perpetuate stocks and prevent overcrowding. Most perennials: divide in fall. Cut back old flower stems, lift whole clump with a fork, divide in half, remove young shoots from the outside of the clump. Discard the center (older, less vigorous part). Cut all leaves back to within 1in (2.5cm) of roots and replant immediately, or pot in potting compost in a shady area.

**Dividing Plants with Fleshy Roots:** Plants like hostas have fleshy roots and should be divided in spring, just before growth starts, when new buds are visible. Each new piece needs at least one good bud. Cut through the root with a sharp spade or large knife. Small clumps can be divided by hand; larger or very old clumps may need garden forks pried back-to-back through the center.

## Softwood Cuttings

Used to increase any shrubs. Take cuttings of soft, new growth about 4in (10cm) long, any time during summer when the shoot is still growing. Success rate should be about 80%.

**Procedure:** Fill a tray with peat-free propagation compost. Trim cutting by half, just below a leaf joint. Carefully trim away all side leaves. Dip in copper fungicide solution. Dip end in organic rooting powder (safe to use — no synthesized hormones), shake off excess. Space cuttings in tray 1in (2.5cm) apart each way. Wrap tray in light plastic sheeting so it touches the tops of cuttings and is sealed under the tray. Put into a softwood cuttings frame.

**Shading:** Vital to provide the right amount of light — cuttings feed through their leaves and need some sunshine but too much causes wilting. Use shade netting or onion sacks. Two layers for very sunny weather, one for fairly sunny, none for dull days.

**Clematis Cuttings:** Take cutting at least 12in (30cm) long. First cut immediately above a leaf joint, second about 1.5in (4cm) below it. Dip in copper fungicide and insert into compost up to the leaves.

**Conifer Cuttings:** Same method but tear leaves away from shoots (stems need to be bruised before rooting).

**Half-Hardy Perennials:** Best taken in late summer or early spring when tubers produce new shoots. They root more readily than shrubs but need 55-60F (13-15C) at the roots. Cut with at least three leaves and a new shoot. Trim stem below lowest leaf joint. Dip in organic rooting powder. Insert in pot of peat-free compost using a dibber. Cover with plastic bag or put pot in a plastic bag inflated with air.

## Hardwood Cuttings

Used to increase deciduous shrubs (black currants, gooseberries, dogwoods, mock orange, Japanese rose) in the fall. Take in fall after leaves drop (some like gooseberries can be taken earlier). Takes time to establish but is simple and cheap.

**Procedure:** Cut off stem length about 8-9in (20-23cm) using sharp knife or secateurs. Trim below the lowest bud, cut off soft top growth by trimming just above a bud. Make a narrow trench lined with sharp sand. Place cuttings in trench leaving 3in (7.5cm) of top above ground. Refill trench, press in cuttings, leave for one year. The following winter, plant approximately 6-9in (15-23cm) apart in rows in a corner of the garden. Leave another year, then transplant to permanent position.

## Propagation by Layering

Involves putting part of the plant into the ground while leaving it attached to the parent until it develops sufficient roots to support itself. Three methods: tip layering, normal layering, and serpentine layering.

**Tip Layering:** Used mainly for plants that root readily, particularly bramble fruits (blackberries, loganberries, hybrid berries). In late summer, pull down shoots, make a hole where the tip rests, place tip in hole, pin in position with a forked stick, cover with soil. Leave until parent plant's leaves fall, then cut the layer from the parent, lift and transplant to permanent position.
