# Singh Ligninolytic Enzymes in Fungal Bioremediation
#mycoremediation #enzymology #white-rot-fungi #biodegradation #lignin

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Harbhajan Singh, Mycoremediation: Fungal Bioremediation (2006), Chapter 9

## Overview

Ligninolytic enzymes are the primary enzymatic arsenal that white-rot and litter-decomposing fungi deploy to degrade lignin and a vast array of xenobiotic pollutants. These enzymes include lignin peroxidase (LiP), [[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]] (MnP), laccase, and hydrogen peroxide-generating enzymes. Their nonspecific oxidative mechanisms make them uniquely suited for bioremediation of recalcitrant [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]]. See also [[brown-rot-and-white-rot-fungi-in-mycoremediation]], fungal-ecology-mycoremediation.

## White-Rot Fungi Classification by Enzyme Profile

Hatakka (1994) classified white-rot fungi into three major categories based on enzyme production patterns:

1. **Lignin-Manganese Peroxidase Group** (e.g., [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]], Phlebia radiata) -- produces LiP and MnP
2. **Manganese Peroxidase-Laccase Group** (e.g., Dichomitus squalens, Rigidoporus lignosus) -- produces MnP and laccase
3. **Lignin Peroxidase-Laccase Group** (e.g., Phlebia ochraceofulva, Junghuhnia separabilima) -- produces LiP and laccase

## Lignin Peroxidase (LiP; EC 1.11.1.14)

### Discovery and Distribution
- Discovered in Phanerochaete chrysosporium (Glenn et al., 1983; Tien and Kirk, 1983)
- Produced by most white-rot fungi including Bjerkandera adusta, [[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]], Phlebia radiata, Trametes trogii, Phlebia ochraceofulva, Phlebia tremellosa
- NOT produced by Ceriporiopsis subvermispora (despite being an active biopulping fungus)
- NOT detected in marine fungi

### Properties
- Glycosylated heme protein, ~40 kDa molecular weight
- Low optimum pH: 2.5-3.0
- Acidic pIs, high redox potential
- Crystal structure established at 2-2.5 Angstrom resolution
- Secreted during secondary metabolism in nutrient-limited cultures

### Catalytic Cycle
Three-step H2O2-dependent oxidation:
1. Resting peroxidase [Fe3+] + H2O2 -> Compound I [Fe4+=O] + H2O
2. Compound I + Substrate -> Compound II + Substrate radical
3. Compound II + Substrate -> Resting peroxidase + Substrate radical + H2O

### Substrate Range
LiP is relatively nonspecific: oxidizes veratryl alcohol, beta-O-4 lignin dimers, benzyl alcohols, PAHs, and unrelated compounds. Catalyzes cleavage of beta-O-4 linkages, C-alpha-C-beta linkages, ring-opening reactions, demethoxylations, and oxidative dechlorination. Cannot oxidize lignin to CO2 in cell-free systems.

### Genetic Aspects
- At least 10 structural genes (lipA through lipJ) in P. chrysosporium
- LiP genes also characterized from T. versicolor, B. adusta, P. radiata
- Gene expression regulated by carbon starvation (lipD) and nitrogen limitation (lipA, lipC, lipJ)
- Heterologous expression difficult (low yields in E. coli); homologous expression developed
- Putative regulatory elements include xenobiotic response elements (XREs) and cAMP-mediated response elements

## Manganese Peroxidase (MnP; EC 1.11.1.13)

### Distribution
- 56+ fungi known to produce MnP (Hofrichter, 2002)
- Produced by families Coriolaceae, Meruliaceae, Polyporaceae, Strophariaceae, Tricholomataceae
- 11 isozymes known from Ceriporiopsis subvermispora; 5 from P. chrysosporium

### Properties
- Glycosylated heme-containing extracellular peroxidase
- Molecular weight: 38-62.5 kDa (most purified: 45 kDa)
- Lower redox potential than LiP
- Uses Mn(II) as obligatory substrate (widespread in lignocellulose and soil)
- Two calcium ions maintain active site structure
- Nitrogen-deficient conditions favor production (similar to LiP)

### Catalytic Cycle
1. H2O2 binding -> MnP Compound I (Fe4+-oxo-porphyrin-radical)
2. Reduction through Compound II -> Mn(II) oxidizes to Mn(III)
3. Generation of native enzyme and water release
4. High H2O2 causes reversible inactivation (Compound III formation)

### Key Functions
- Chelates of Mn(III) with organic acids oxidize substrates
- Generates reactive radicals (superoxide) via autocatalytic reactions
- Can oxidize organic sulfur cooxidants (glutathione, l-cysteine)
- With unsaturated fatty acids (linoleic acid), generates thiyl and peroxyl radicals
- Hybrid MnP/LiP forms exist in Bjerkandera sp. BOS55, P. eryngii -- oxidize both phenolic and nonphenolic compounds

### Genetic Aspects
- MnP genes cloned from T. versicolor, P. ostreatus, C. subvermispora, D. squalens, G. applanatum
- In Pleurotus, MnP produced even without Mn(II); addition of Mn(II) inhibits production (unusual)
- In P. chrysosporium, transcription of MnP genes requires Mn(II) presence

## Laccase (EC 1.10.3.2)

Laccase is a multicopper oxidase that oxidizes phenolic compounds and, with mediators, nonphenolic compounds. It is the most widely distributed ligninolytic enzyme among fungi. See separate [[singh-laccase-mediated-biodegradation-pahs-dyes]] for detailed PAH degradation data.

### Key Mediators
- HBT (1-hydroxybenzotriazole)
- ABTS (2,2'-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonic acid))
- Mediators enable laccase to oxidize high-redox-potential nonphenolic substrates

### Remarkable PAH Degradation with Mediators
- T. versicolor laccase + HBT: 91-100% degradation of benzo[a]pyrene, anthracene, acenaphthene, fluorene, perylene (Majcherczyk et al., 1998)
- Pycnosporus cinnabarinus laccase + ABTS: 95% degradation of benzo[a]pyrene (Rama et al., 1998)
- Pleurotus eryngii laccase + HBT: 70% degradation of benzo[a]pyrene (Rodriguez et al., 2004)
- Coriolopsis gallica laccase: 100% degradation of anthracene (Pickard et al., 1999)

## Synergistic Enzyme Action

Increased lignin depolymerization occurs using enzyme combinations:
- MnP + laccase from Rigidoporus lignosus
- MnP + laccase from [[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]]
- MnP + LiP from P. chrysosporium
- LiP + MnP + laccase together (most effective)

## White-Rot vs. Brown-Rot vs. Soft-Rot

### White-Rot
- Only effective lignin mineralizers
- Several hundred species of Basidiomycetes + few Ascomycetes
- Decompose hardwood more extensively than softwood
- Can completely mineralize lignin and carbohydrates to CO2 and water
- Examples: T. versicolor (simultaneous rot), P. chrysosporium (selective delignification), Ceriporiopsis subvermispora, Phlebia radiata, Ganoderma spp.

### Brown-Rot
- Remove cellulose and hemicellulose, leaving modified lignin as brown residue
- Lignin demethylation, partial oxidation, depolymerization
- Employ Fenton-type catalytic system (hydroxyl radicals)
- Two groups: Gloeophyllum trabeum (oxalic acid accumulation) and Coniophora puteana/Poria placenta
- Common in softwood

### Soft-Rot
- Caused by Ascomycetes and Imperfect Fungi
- Two types: Type I (cavities in secondary walls) and Type II (erosion)
- Better lignin degradation in hardwood than softwood
- Species: Allescheria, Graphium, Monodictys, Paecilomyces, Papulospora, Thielavia, Daldinia, Hypoxylon, Xylaria
- Daldinia concentrica: 53% weight loss in birch wood within 2 months

## Ganoderma lucidum Specifics

Ganoderma lucidum produces MnP in poplar wood media but NOT in pine wood media (D'Souza et al., 1999), demonstrating that enzyme expression is substrate-dependent and related to the ecological niche of the fungus.

## Soil Fungi as Lignin Degraders

- Penicillium chrysogenum: mineralized 27.4% of 14C-labeled lignin from milled wheat straw in 4 weeks
