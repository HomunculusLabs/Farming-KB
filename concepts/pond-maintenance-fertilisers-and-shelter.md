# Pond Maintenance, Fertilisers, and Shelter

## Overview

Ongoing pond management—encompassing mud and waste removal, fertiliser application, and provision of shelter and refuges—is what separates productive aquaculture systems from failed ones. These maintenance activities directly influence water quality, [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]], fish health, and ultimately the total useful yield from pond systems.

## Mud, Silt, Humus, and Waste Removal

### Distinguishing Waste from Fertiliser

Wastes of any nature in ponds reduce water quality if they occur in excess, or if they occur as dead material. It is useful to distinguish rotting wastes from fertilisers: fertilisers need not create excessive growth if herbivorous fish, nutria (a large aquatic rodent), or shrimp are available to eat the detritus or weeds that would otherwise die and create anaerobic conditions.

### Flowing vs. Still Systems

- **Flowing canals and well-aerated ponds**: Little or no problem with waste accumulation
- **Still ponds**: Require regular monitoring for waste removal, most handily achieved by draining and liming the pond and taking a season of dryland crop (clover, fenugreek) before reflooding

### Dredging and Muck Removal

Where ponds cannot be drained, care should be taken to keep at least one bank clear of trees for dredging or bucket removal of muck. This material is traditionally layered with green crop for [[compost]] in Asia. In paddy and terrace systems, 2–5 cm of mulch is retained over the clay base for cropping, and all terraces can be drained to a dryland phase.

### Jet Pumps for Deep Reservoirs

In deeper non-draining reservoirs, a jet pump serves as a vacuum cleaner to pump detritus out. These pumps can remove soils up to gravel size. The extracted material can be deposited near the pond as:

- Adjacent retaining walls
- Low islands and causeways where silt settles out
- Water flows back to the pond free of detritus

Jet pumps are typically raft-mounted and liquid-fuel powered.

## Fertilisers for Aquaculture

### Testing and Initial Application

After testing waters, it is likely that minor elements and phosphates may be needed. Key cautions include:

- Avoid adding nitrates to waters where natural manures are used
- Be careful in desert basins where water nitrate levels can already be high
- Avoid boron-containing detergents and any biocides not recommended for fish
- Salt, copper sulphate, weak formalin, and some antibiotics can control diseases or parasites without lasting harm

### Integrated Manure Systems

Pig, duck, and second-stage sewage are all used in fish ponds, and any bird or animal manures are useful. The three main [[pond-fertilisation-and-nutrient-management]] strategies correspond to fish food preferences:

1. **Heavily fertilised ponds**: Intended for intensive algal growth to feed milkfish or prawns
2. **Moderately vegetated ponds**: Higher vegetation encouraged to feed grass carp or [[tilapia]]
3. **Nearly clear-water ponds**: For bass, perch, and salmonids (trout)

In any polyculture, a larger proportion of fish should be algal or plant feeders, with a usual ratio of **5:1 herbivore to predator**.

### Devices for Bringing Nutrients to Ponds

A variety of design elements bring complex plant nutrients to pond systems:

- **Waterfowl stocking**: Ducks contribute phosphatic fertilisers directly
- **Perches for gulls and land birds**: Bird droppings enrich pond water
- **Pigeon lofts**: Positioned over water for direct manure input
- **Pig pens and chicken roosts**: Sited over or adjacent to ponds
- **Martin and swift nest sites**: Erected in or over water
- **Bat roosts**: Used in Holland for nutrient cycling

This integration of land animals with pond systems creates a closed [[williams-pdc-composting-and-nutrient-cycle]] where land nutrients are returned to ponds and pond water irrigates land crops.

### Fertiliser Uptake Dynamics

Phosphates, potash, and minor elements are fixed and held in water within **12–14 hours** in ponds, or **3–4 days** in oceans. This rapid uptake means:

- Fertile ponds need to be stocked with shrimp or large numbers of small forage fish while fingerling pan fish are growing, or yields are lost
- Excess nitrate can be filtered via forage food ponds of crustaceans or through reed beds
- Land crops downstream remove excess nutrients from pond overflow

### The Fertiliser Factor

Even modestly fertilised ponds can alter yields by factors of **2 to 10 times**. Swingle (1966) recorded a difference in mussel yield (same pond) from 52.8 kg unfertilised to 1,012 kg fertilised (whole weight; meats are 35% of total). Well-managed fertiliser application is a critical yield strategy.

In flow-on pond series, a **doubling in yield** of species such as melons, tomatoes, or [[dryland-settlement-vegetation-and-fuel-forests]] is commonly achieved, making fish ponds a key feature in raising land-based yields.

### Long-Term Liming Materials

Once selected nutrient levels have been achieved, phosphatic rock and granite dust can supply ongoing lime needs. These slow-release materials maintain calcium levels without the rapid pH swings associated with burnt lime application.

## Shelter and Refuges

### Types of Refuges

Stacks of pots, pipes, tyres, and bundles of rope, reeds, or brush can have a decisive yield effect on predation losses of young fish, crayfish, and species subject to predation by their fellows. Crab and crayfish culture, in particular, benefits from such refuges.

### Forage Species Protection

Forage species in ponds need breeding refuges to persist. While exact figures are hard to establish, documented yield increases include:

- **10–30% increase** for crustaceans with shelter
- **20–100% increase** for forage fish protected by shallows or weedy areas

Mortality of young and fry is always greater in ponds of simple design and without escapements.

### Cage Culture

Cage culture serves a dual purpose:

1. **High-yield system in itself**: Concentrated production in controlled environments
2. **Strategic separation**: Enables separation of predator and prey, or adults and juveniles

This approach mimics the natural pond dynamic where screened-off predatory fish continually cull overbreeding populations, allowing harvest at all times rather than in single annual events.

### Deep Water Refuges (Kettles)

Deep "kettles" in shallow ponds serve multiple functions:

- Prevent fish dying from freezes or excessive heat
- Protect fish from accidental pond water loss
- Assist capture during draw-down conditions
- Provide temperature refuge when surface conditions become extreme

## Integrated System Benefits

The combination of proper [[community-recycling-waste-management]], targeted fertilisation, and adequate shelter creates synergistic effects. A pond with good refuges, appropriate fertiliser input, and integrated [[historical-mushroom-manure-sources]] can achieve yields an order of magnitude higher than a poorly managed equivalent. The key principle is treating the pond as a living system where nutrient cycling, habitat complexity, and species interactions are designed rather than left to chance.
