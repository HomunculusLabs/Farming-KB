# Tribal Pattern Application and Land Division

## Overview

Pattern application in permaculture has two aspects: perceiving existing patterns and how they function, and imposing pattern on sites to achieve specific ends. Traditional and indigenous societies have long demonstrated sophisticated pattern literacy—using visual patterns, songs, dances, and navigational systems to encode complex environmental, genealogical, and practical knowledge. Studying these traditional approaches provides permaculture designers with proven models for sensible land division, resource management, and knowledge transmission that work with natural patterns rather than against them.

## Two Aspects of Patterning

### Perceiving Existing Patterns

The first skill of sophisticated design is the ability to read the patterns already present in a landscape:
- Geological formations and their influence on water flow
- Vegetation patterns that indicate soil conditions, microclimates, and succession stages
- Animal movement corridors and habitat preferences
- Wind patterns, sun exposure, and seasonal variations

### Imposing Pattern on Sites

The second skill is the deliberate application of pattern to achieve specific design goals:
- Creating access systems that maximize nutrient exchange (branching, keyhole)
- Shaping earthworks to retain water and slow erosion (contour, swales)
- Arranging plantings in beneficial spatial relationships (guilds, zones, sectors)

Both perception and application can result in specific strategies, harmonious resolution of problems, or the production of local resources.

## The Problem of Rectilinear Land Division

### Critique of Grid-Based Systems

A bird's-eye view of centralized societies reveals a strictly **rectilinear network** of streets, farms, and property boundaries. This pattern is designed to suit survey instruments rather than to serve human or environmental needs. The consequences include:

- **Prevention of sensible landscape planning**: Straight-line boundaries ignore watersheds, soil types, microclimates, and ecological corridors
- **Functional deficiency**: Neither aesthetically nor functionally satisfactory landscapes or streetscapes result
- **Institutional inertia**: Once entered into law, such patterning is stubbornly defended despite its inadequacy

The key insight is that rectilinear patterning is created by people and can therefore be dismantled by people—it is not an immutable constraint but a cultural choice.

## Traditional Land Division Models

### Hawaiian Ahupua'a System

Hawaiian villagers developed a far more sensible approach by taking **natural ridgelines as their boundaries**. This created land units that:

- **Contained complete watersheds**: Each land unit extended from dense cloud-forests at the ridge crest to outer reefs in the ocean
- **Enabled integrated resource management**: A whole valley of people could maintain a clean catchment, store and divert mid-slope water resources, catch lost nutrients in shallow ocean enclosures (converting first to algae, then to crabs and fish), and preserve offshore reef areas and the marine environment
- **Worked with volcanic landscapes**: The conic and radial volcanic landscape with radial water lines naturally suited this method of land division
- **Created stable, resource-rich landscapes**: By managing entire watershed-to-reef systems, these societies achieved long-term sustainability

### Other Circular and Zonal Models

- **Zulu settlements** on the plains adopted circular or zonal modes of spatial organization
- **American Indian** plains settlements similarly used circular arrangements
- Both models reflect natural patterns—circular forms arise from radial expansion from a central point, zonal forms from concentric gradients of resource availability

These models can be studied and adopted by future bioregional societies as people committed to earth-care become the majority in their regions and set about the task of landscape rehabilitation. **Sensible land division is a long-delayed but essential precursor to a stable society.**

## Tribal Uses of Patterning

### Pattern as Literature and Recording System

Tribal peoples worldwide use an enormous variety of traditional patterns that decorate weapons, houses, skin, and woven textiles or baskets. Many patterns carry sophisticated meaning, and almost all have associated songs or chants. The pattern complexity of tribal art—including Celtic designs and ancient engravings—may have encoded important information for their peoples.

**Critical insight**: To call such peoples "illiterate" is only valid if one ignores their patterns, songs, and dances as a valid literature and accurate recording system. Having evolved alphabetical symbols, modern societies have abandoned pattern learning in education—a mistake, because learning even one significant pattern (such as the permaculture pattern model) is like learning an underlying principle applicable to all specific data and situations.

### Polynesian Navigation Systems

Polynesians used **pattern maps** that lacked scale, cartographic details, and trigonometric measures, yet sufficed to find 200–2,000 island specks across the vast Pacific Ocean. These maps were constructed from:

- **Sticks and flexed strips** representing wave interference patterns
- **Cowrie shells** marking island positions and current interactions
- **Song cycles** encoding navigational knowledge for specific routes

A complete traditional Polynesian navigation system integrated multiple sensory inputs (Figure 4.26):

| Navigator Role | Method |
|---------------|--------|
| Helmsman | Sings to time the log line; feels for current deflections through the steering oar; sings to record journey stages |
| Lookouts | Note star patterns, bird flight, cloud trains, and light reflected from coastal lagoons |
| Hull listener | Sits in a "black box" near the keel, listens to wave refraction from the hull to predict storms or islands |
| Navigator | Consults voyage charts of sticks and cowries; sings navigation songs for specific search patterns |
| Solar observer | Judges solar and star elevations (latitude) via a water eye |
| Night watch | Awaits bioluminescent glow from deep-sea trenches ("lightning from the deeps"), listens for bird song and headland echoes, watches for phosphorescent forest glow on shore |
| Temperature reader | Lowers testicular sac into the sea to accurately gauge water mass temperature |
| Scent observer | Notes wind-carried scents from forests, rock lichen, bird colonies, and fish schools |
| Faunal sampler | Catches indicator organisms related to the water mass |
| Lead line operator | Uses a depth line with soft grease to find banks and sample bottom fauna |
| Crystal navigator | Uses a calcite crystal to predict rain or find the sun on overcast days by polarization of light |

### Maori Tattoo and Carving

The Maori of New Zealand use tattoo (*moko*) and carved patterns to **record and recall genealogical and saga information**. The intricate spirals, curves, and geometric forms encode family lineages, tribal histories, and personal achievements in a wearable, permanent format.

### Pitjantjatjara Sand Patterns

The Pitjantjatjara people of Australia sing over **sand patterns** (Figure 4.27) and are able to "sing" strangers to a single stone in an apparently featureless desert. Their designs accurately reflect:

- **Lobular shapes** and elaborate micro-elevations of the desert landscape
- Vegetation changes embroidered across the terrain
- Fire patterns, pollen distributions, and flowering stages of specific plants
- Rain tracks and cloud streets
- Hunting grounds, saga information, and climatic data

### Song as Encoding System

Children of many tribes are taught hundreds of simple chants where the words hide deeper, secondary meanings about:

- **Medicinal knowledge**: Plant properties, preparation methods, dosages
- **Sacred information**: Ritual procedures, spiritual teachings, cultural law
- **Navigational knowledge**: Star positions, current patterns, island locations

The meaning only becomes accessible when the initiate is given the decoding system or discovers it through personal revelation (intuition). A pattern map may have little meaning without its **song keys** to unlock that meaning.

### Dance as Knowledge Transmission

Dances, involving muscular learning and memory coupled with chants, carry accurate long-term messages including:

- **Saga details**: Historical narratives preserved in choreographed movement
- **Planting knowledge**: Agricultural calendars encoded in seasonal dances
- **Species interpretation**: Dance-imitations of animal species interpret their postural meanings in a non-verbal, universally transmittable way
- **Cultural postures**: Many formal attitudes of prayer and submission are basic imitations of primate postures taken from other species

Many dances and chants evolved directly from work and travel movements, embedding practical skills in memorable physical form.

## Lessons for Permaculture Designers

1. **Pattern literacy is a design skill**: Learning to read and create patterns is as fundamental as learning to read text—it encodes principles applicable across all specific situations
2. **Land division should follow natural boundaries**: Ridgelines, watersheds, and soil zones make more sense than arbitrary grid lines
3. **Multi-sensory observation is superior to single-channel data**: Traditional navigators integrated sight, sound, touch, smell, and temperature perception simultaneously
4. **Knowledge should be embedded in practice**: Songs, dances, and patterns that are used daily retain knowledge more effectively than stored documents
5. **The whole watershed is the design unit**: The Hawaiian ahupua'a model demonstrates that managing from ridge to reef creates stable, resource-rich systems
6. **Rectilinear patterns are cultural, not natural**: They can be replaced by patterns that serve ecological and human needs
