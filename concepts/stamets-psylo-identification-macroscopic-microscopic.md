# Identification of Psilocybin Mushrooms
Identifying psilocybin mushroom identifications to species is difficult unless you are a
trained mycologist. Microscopic examination and comparison with known
species can be a long and laborious process. However, determining whether
a candidate is part of the psilocybin-containing group is not at all
difficult.
## The Stametsian Rule
The two primary criteria for identifying a psilocybin [[gartz-mushroom-identification-dangers-and-tips]] field guide:
1. **Purplish brown to black [[psilocybin-mushroom-spore-print-identification]]**
2. **Flesh that bruises bluish when damaged**
If a gilled mushroom has purplish brown to black spores AND the flesh
bruises bluish, the mushroom is very likely a psilocybin-producing species.
Stamets reports knowing of no exceptions to this rule, but cautions that
does not mean there are none.
## The Spore Print
The psilocybin mushroom spore print identification is the single most important identification tool. The
majority of distinctions between gilled mushroom genera are at least
partially based on spore color.
### Spore Print Colors by Genus
- **Psilocybe and Panaeolus**: Purplish brown to black (the psilocybin
  genera)
- **amanita genus morphology**: Rusty brown (deadly poisonous)
- **Entoloma**: Pinkish brown
- **Hebeloma and Inocybe**: Yellowish brown to clay brown to dull brown
- **Amanita and Galerina**: Typically whitish
- **Gymnopilus**: Rusty orange
### Technique
Separate the cap from the stem, place the cap gills-down on white paper.
Cover with a glass to lessen dehydration and disturbance from air currents.
In a few hours, spores will deposit according to the radiating symmetry of
the gills. Spore printing is best done within the first few hours after
picking, as spore production declines as the mushroom dries.
Perceiving the difference between a purple-brown spore print and a
rusty-brown spore print can be a distinction with deadly potential. People
who are color impaired should not make critical decisions about spore color.
## The psilocybin mushroom bluing reaction chemistry
A feature common to many psilocybin mushrooms is the psilocybin bluing reaction.
Many Psilocybe and Panaeolus species will turn bluish or bluish green
when bruised. The blue pigmentation results from the degradation of
unstable psilocin to presently unknown compounds by enzymes within the
mushroom cells.
The psilocybin bluing reaction is a co-indicator that psilocin is or was present.
Since it appears to be a parallel decomposition sequence, the more the
mushrooms are bruised, the less potent they become. No one has been able
to pinpoint the chemical structure of the bluing compound.
### Limitations of the Bluing Reaction
- Many active Psilocybe and Panaeolus species will NOT blue no matter how
  much they are abused
- Several poisonous species exhibit near-bluing without psilocybin or
  psilocin (Hygrophorus conicus, some Inocybes)
- Some Galerinas have stems that darken blackish from the base, which
  could be confused with bluing
- The less psilocin a species contains, the more subtle the bluing
  reaction (P. semilanceata is extremely potent but rarely bruises bluish)
## Macroscopic Features for Identification
### Cap (Pileus)
Key features to observe in Psilocybe species:
- **Gelatinous pellicle**: A separable, gelatinous skin covering the cap
  surface that becomes viscid when moist. This is a characteristic feature
  of many Psilocybe species. Test by gently peeling the cap surface.
- **Cap shape progression**: Observe the progression from young to old.
  Many psilocybes start conic or campanulate and expand to convex or
  plane. An acute umbo (nipple-like protrusion) is characteristic of P.
  semilanceata.
- **Hygrophanous color**: Caps are often dark brown when moist, fading to
  lighter straw or tan colors as they dry. This two-tone coloration is
  characteristic.
- **Translucent-striate margin**: Many species have margins that appear
  striped when moist due to translucency of the gills showing through.
### Gills (Lamellae)
- **Attachment**: Observe how gills meet the stem (adnate, adnexed,
  sinuate, subdecurrent, or free). This is a key taxonomic feature.
- **Color progression**: Gills typically progress from pale/grayish in
  youth to dark purplish brown to nearly black at maturity.
- **Whitish fringed edges**: Gill edges are usually whitish/pallid even
  when the gill faces are dark with spores.
### Stem (Stipe)
- **Bluing at the base**: The bluing reaction is often first seen at the
  stem base.
- **Rhizomorphs**: Many species have coarse white aerial tufts of mycelium
  or cordlike rhizomorphs at the stem base.
- **Annular zone**: Remnants of the partial veil may leave a fibrillose
  annular zone in the upper regions of the stem.
- **Veil characteristics**: The nature of the partial veil (membranous vs.
  cortinate) is important. P. cubensis has a well-developed membranous
  annulus, while P. semilanceata has only a thinly cortinate, evanescent
  veil.
### Odor and Taste
- P. azurescens: extremely bitter
- Some Inocybe species: disagreeable, soapy smelling, or urine-like
- Most Psilocybes: odorless to farinaceous (starchy)
## Microscopic Features
For absolute certainty, microscopic examination is often necessary:
- **Spore size and shape**: Measured in micrometers (um). Spore morphology
  is the most definitive microscopic feature. Psilocybe spores are
  typically ellipsoid, 9-17 um long.
- **Basidia**: The spore-bearing cells, usually 4-spored (occasionally
  2-spored in some species).
- **Pleurocystidia and cheilocystidia**: Sterile cells on the gill face
  and gill edge. Their presence, absence, size, and shape are critical
  for species determination.
- **Cap cuticle (pileipellis)**: Psilocybes and Galerinas have filamentous
  cap cuticles. Conocybes and Panaeolus have cap cuticles composed of
  inflated rounded cells (cellular/sphaerocystic).
- **Germ pore**: Some species have a distinct germ pore on the spore.
