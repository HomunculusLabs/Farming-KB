# Soil Fertility Gradient

## Overview

[[steve-solomon]] does not prescribe a single fertility program for all
gardeners. Instead, he presents a gradient of escalating approaches to
soil improvement, each step more effective than the last. This gradient
acknowledges that gardeners have widely varying resources: some have
little money to spend, some lack access to manure or [[duggar-stable-manure-selection-compost-materials]],
and others can readily afford to invest in their soil. By offering
multiple tiers, Solomon ensures that every gardener can start wherever
they are and progressively improve their results.

The fundamental principle underlying the gradient is that soil fertility
directly determines the [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] of harvested vegetables.
Simply spreading manure and compost, regardless of how rich the soils
around you are, is no guarantee of producing highly nutritious food.
The gradient approach provides a structured pathway from minimal input
gardening to full nutritional optimization using COF and compost.

## Step One: No Soil Improvers

The most basic level involves simply spading up the ground, keeping weeds
hoed out, and growing only low-demand crops. Solomon considers this
approach viable only for someone starting a new garden who has no
completed compost heap and cannot face using humanure. At this level, the
gardener is entirely dependent on the inherent fertility of the native
soil and can only succeed with the most undemanding vegetables — things
like certain root crops, beans, and peas that tolerate poor conditions.

While this level is viable only for someone starting a new garden with
zero compost and who cannot face using humanure, it represents a realistic
starting point for anyone who needs to grow food immediately. Even here,
[[coleman-cover-crop-rotation-and-soil-health]] helps by preventing soil-borne [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]] buildup.

## Step Two: Minimal Manure or Compost

The next step up is to spread a layer of manure or compost about one-
quarter inch (6 mm) deep, and preferably at least a half inch, before
spading the garden in spring. This thin layer of well-decomposed ruminant
manure or finished compost allows the gardener to grow both low- and
medium-demand vegetables. The modest [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] input feeds soil
microbes, improves soil structure slightly, and provides a baseline level
of nutrients.

If the gardener does not have enough compost or manure to cover the
entire garden, Solomon recommends concentrating it on the areas that
will grow medium-demand crops and attempting low-demand vegetables on the
unamended soil. A somewhat better approach is to rotate: grow low-demand
vegetables on ground that received manure the previous year, since some
fertility will still be present in the soil from the prior season's
application.

## Step Three: Full Manure, Compost, and Lime

At this level, the gardener spreads and digs in appropriate materials
for each crop category before planting. Solomon's "Soil Improving in a
Nutshell" table specifies the exact amounts for low-, medium-, and
high-demand vegetables. For low-demand crops, a 1/4-inch layer of
steer manure or finished compost plus 50 pounds of lime per 1,000 square
feet suffices. Medium-demand vegetables get the same plus a 1/8-inch
layer of composted chicken manure. High-demand crops receive a 1/4-inch
layer of finished compost, 1/4-inch layer of composted chicken manure,
50 pounds of lime, and 4-6 quarts of COF per 100 square feet.

At this level, Solomon prefers COF over manure as the primary nutrient
source, using only the minimum amount of manure necessary to supply the
soil ecology's requirement for [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]. This is because the
[[solomon-manure-quality-variable-nutrient-content-horse-steer-chicken]] of manure is wildly unpredictable.

## Manure Quality Variability

Solomon devotes considerable attention to the problem of manure quality.
[[coleman-french-maraicher-horse-manure-hotbed-system]], for example, can range from 0.5 percent nitrogen to 2
percent nitrogen depending entirely on what the horse was fed. A horse
fed high-protein hay, oilseed meals, and vitamin supplements by a
knowledgeable farmer produces superb manure. A horse fed low-quality
local grass hay from nutrient-depleted pasture produces manure so poor
that it is useful only as bedding straw. The same variability applies to
all animal manures — cattle, sheep, poultry — depending on the quality
of their feed.
