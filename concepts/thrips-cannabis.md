# Thrips on Cannabis

Thrips are small, winged, fast-moving insects more common in greenhouses than in [[cannabis-indoor-grow-lighting-systems]] rooms. They are difficult to see individually but not hard to spot in groups, making them a pest that can establish before growers notice the damage. Thrips can be very vexing to control once they get established, so early detection and rapid response are essential.

## Identification

- Size: 0.04-0.05 inch (1-1.5 mm) long — very small but visible to the naked eye
- Colors: white, gray, dark colors, often with petite stripes
- Wings present on adults; they fly but prefer to jump and run
- Check for them under leaves by shaking plant parts — if many are present, they will scatter as a herd of specks
- Females make holes in soft plant tissue where they deposit virtually invisible eggs
- Winged adults easily migrate from infested plants to the entire garden
- With a 10X magnifying glass, thrips appear as tiny, slender, fast-moving insects

When disturbed, thrips often choose to jump and run rather than fly to safety. Their behavior of thundering across foliage in groups makes them distinctive once you know what to look for. Adults are the most visible stage; larvae are smaller and tend to stay hidden in feeding sites.

## Life Cycle

Thrips have a life cycle that includes egg, larval, pupal, and adult stages:

- Females deposit eggs inside soft plant tissue through small holes they create
- Eggs hatch into tiny larvae that begin feeding immediately on leaf tissue
- Larvae pass through two instars (growth stages) before dropping to the growing medium to pupate
- Pupation occurs in the soil or growing medium for several days
- Adults emerge, mate, and begin laying eggs within days
- The complete life cycle can take 2-4 weeks depending on temperature
- Warmer temperatures accelerate the life cycle and increase reproductive rates
- Multiple generations can overlap, making control more difficult
- Thrips can reproduce continuously indoors where conditions remain favorable year-round

## Damage Symptoms

Thrips scrape tissue from leaves and buds, then suck out plant juices for food:

- **Stippling**: Whitish-yellowish specks appear on top of leaves as tissue is destroyed
- **Chlorophyll loss**: Diminished chlorophyll production as infestation progresses
- **Brittle leaves**: Leaves become brittle and damaged as tissue is scraped away
- **Black specks**: Thrip feces visible on foliage along with the tiny insects — a characteristic sign
- **Bud damage**: Thrips feed inside flower buds, causing distortion and reduced quality
- **Leaf distortion**: Thrips wrap up and distort leaves as they feed on tender tissue
- **Silvering**: Affected leaf areas may develop a silvery or bronzed appearance from tissue damage
- **Growth reduction**: Severely affected plants show stunted growth and reduced vigor

The combination of stippling and black fecal spots is characteristic of thrips damage and helps distinguish it from spider mite damage, which produces webbing but not fecal spots. Thrips damage is often most visible on the oldest leaves first, as populations build over time.

## Cultural and Physical Control

- **Cleanliness**: First line of defense in any [[spider-mites-cannabis]], but their damage should not be underestimated:

- Stippling damage reduces photosynthetic capacity across affected leaves
- Bud damage from thrips feeding inside flower buds directly impacts harvest quality
- Leaf distortion can interfere with light penetration to lower canopy
- The black fecal spots left on foliage can harbor mold spores
- Heavy infestations during flowering can reduce final yield
- Thrips serve as vectors for certain plant viruses, though this is less common in cannabis

## Resistant Varieties

While no cannabis varieties are specifically bred for thrips resistance, some factors influence susceptibility:

- Plants with thicker, waxier leaf cuticles may be less attractive to thrips
- Healthy, vigorously growing plants tolerate thrips damage better than stressed plants
- Some growers report that certain sativa-dominant varieties with narrow leaflets are less prone to severe thrips damage
- Maintaining optimal growing conditions reduces plant stress and makes plants less attractive to thrips

## Monitoring

- Install blue or pink [[cervantes-sticky-traps-pest-monitoring]] near plants to capture adults and monitor population trends
- Shake plant parts over a white surface to check for thrip activity — the contrast makes tiny insects easier to see
- Inspect undersides of leaves regularly, especially on new growth where thrips prefer to feed
- Check flower buds for feeding damage and distortion — bud damage is often the first noticed symptom
- Use a 10X magnifying glass for detailed inspection of suspected feeding damage
- Track sticky trap counts over time to identify whether populations are increasing or decreasing
- Pay special attention to plants near doors, vents, or other entry points where thrips may enter
