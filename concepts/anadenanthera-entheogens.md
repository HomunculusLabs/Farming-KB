# Anadenanthera Entheogens

The genus Anadenanthera comprises several species of leguminous trees
whose seeds have been used as psychoactive snuffs and smoked
preparations for thousands of years across South America and the
Caribbean. The most significant species are [[anadenanthera-peregrina]]
(source of yopo and cohoba) and [[anadenanthera-colubrina]] (source of
cebil and villca). These plants represent one of the most ancient and
culturally significant entheogenic traditions in the Americas, with
archaeological evidence of use extending back over 4,500 years.

## Taxonomy and Distribution

Anadenanthera belongs to the family Fabaceae (legume family), a large
and economically important plant family that also includes beans,
peas, and [[acacia]]. Two principal species are recognized for their
psychoactive use:

- **Anadenanthera peregrina**: Distributed in the Orinoco basin of Ven
  ezuela and Brazil, and extending into the Caribbean. Source of [[yopo-snuff]] and [[cohoba-snuff]].
- **Anadenanthera colubrina**: Found in southern Peru, Bolivia, and no
  rthern Argentina. Source of cebil and villca.

Both species are medium to large trees with distinctive bark that
flakes off in papery strips, revealing a greenish inner layer. They
produce bean-like seed pods that collect beneath the leaf canopy when
ripe, containing numerous small seeds rich in tryptamine alkaloids.

## Historical Antiquity

Archaeological evidence indicates that Anadenanthera seeds have been
used psychoactively for over 4,500 years, making this one of the
oldest documented entheogenic traditions in the world. In the Puna
region of northwest Argentina, numerous ceramic pipes containing
cebil seed residues have been discovered in caves, providing direct
evidence of ritual or shamanic use. These finds date to approximately
2500 B.C. and represent some of the oldest archaeological proof of
psychoactive substance use anywhere.

The psychoactive use of Anadenanthera appears to have particularly
influenced the culture of Tiahuanaco (literally "City of the Gods"),
considered the "mother" of Andean civilizations. Pre-Columbian snuff
paraphernalia displaying the iconography of the Tiahuanaco culture
have been found throughout the Puna region and the Atacama Desert,
suggesting that cebil use was integral to the religious and political
life of this civilization.

## Ethnographic Use

### Yopo (A. peregrina)

Indigenous tribes of the Orinoco basin use yopo snuff prepared from
toasted, pulverized seeds of A. peregrina mixed with alkaline lime
derived from snail shells or plant ashes. The snuff is blown into the
nostrils through elaborate tubes by an assistant. The practice is
widespread among tribes of the region and is used in divination,
healing, hunting magic, and initiation rites. See [[yopo-snuff]] for
detailed coverage.

### Cohoba (A. peregrina in the Caribbean)

The indigenous peoples of the West Indies, particularly the Taíno,
used cohoba snuff in religious ceremonies prior to European contact.
The practice was among the first entheogenic traditions observed by
Europeans in the Americas, documented from the very first voyage of
Columbus in 1492. See [[cohoba-snuff]] for detailed coverage.

### Cebil/Villca (A. colubrina)

In the southern Andean region, A. colubrina seeds are known as cebil
or villca. The use of cebil as a snuff powder was first mentioned in
1580 by the Spanish chronicler Cristobal de Albornoz, who described
its use by indigenous peoples of the Andes in magical and religious
contexts.

The shamans of the Wichi (Mataco Indians) of northwest Argentina
continue to use cebil snuff and smoke the dried or roasted seeds
today. The practice persists as an integral part of Wichi spiritual
life.

Villca had great ritual and religious significance in Peru before the
arrival of the Spaniards. It was known to the Incan high priests and
soothsayers (umu) as Villca or Villca camayo. The "juice" of Villca
was added to a fermented corn beverage and taken by soothsayers to
look into the future. Villca was also the name for enemas used for
medicinal or shamanic purposes, a practice shared with other
indigenous groups of the Amazon.

## Chemistry

The psychoactive constituents of Anadenanthera seeds are tryptamine
alkaloids. Chemical analysis varies by species and variety:

**A. colubrina (cebil) seeds:**
- Bufotenine (5-hydroxy-DMT): 4-12% in dried seeds, the major active c
  ompound
- 5-MeO-DMT, DMT, DMT-N-oxide: Found in some samples
- 5-OH-DMT-N-oxide: Present in some varieties

**A. peregrina (yopo) seeds:**
- Bufotenine: Major active constituent, though concentrations vary
- DMT and 5-MeO-DMT: Present in some varieties
- Various tryptamine derivatives and related compounds

The addition of alkaline lime to the snuff preparation serves to
convert the tryptamine alkaloids to their free-base form,
facilitating absorption through the nasal mucosa. This technique is
shared with the preparation of [[virola-snuff]].

## Cultural Impact on Art

The hallucinations triggered by cebil seeds appear to have been very
influential in the iconography of the Tiahuanaco style. The art of
Chavin de Huantar is full of similar motifs, including intertwined
snakes coming out of the head of the oracle god, which are clearly
interpreted as cebil hallucinations. Many examples of pre-Columbian
snuff paraphernalia display visionary imagery apparently inspired by
the cebil experience, including depictions of jaguars, serpents, and
transformed human figures.

## Modern Context

Some Mataco Indians who have converted to Christianity have come to
identify cebil with the biblical Tree of Knowledge, but they do not
see it as a "forbidden fruit"; rather, they view it as the fruit of a
holy tree used by shamans for healing. This syncretic interpretation
illustrates the resilience of indigenous entheogenic traditions in
the face of cultural change.
