# Mushroom House Design

The design and construction of [[duggar-mushroom-growing-history-and-methods]]
facilities is a critical factor in commercial success.
Any situation in which temperature and moisture may be
controlled according to the requirements of the mushroom
should be satisfactory. Facilities range from natural
caves to purpose-built structures.

## French Cave Systems

In France, the mushroom industry has fallen heir to the
subterranean quarries constituting extensive arrays of
artificial caves, especially under Paris and its environs.
These caves were formed by mining limestone for building
construction. The suburban caves are from a few feet to 50
feet beneath the surface, varying from 5 to 25 feet in
width and 5 to 20 or more feet in height. The cave
systems are ventilated by means of chimneys or chutes,
with air change facilitated by small charcoal fires.
Entrance may be through an open quarry or through a hole
accessed by ladder with a windlass for materials.

## American Mushroom Houses

In the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], production has been confined to
special houses, caves, and cellars. Near Philadelphia,
Chicago, Boston, and other production centers, mushrooms
are grown wholly in special mushroom houses. The major
part of the American product is grown in this way. Some
commercial growers in Minnesota, Ohio, Indiana, New York,
and Missouri have secured underground quarries, caves, or
storage cellars of considerable extent. Natural caves in
the limestone section of Missouri, Kentucky, Indiana,
Ohio, Virginia, and Arkansas have been used successfully.

## House Construction

For a permanent investment, the mushroom house should be
built with due consideration for convenience, durability,
and economy. Perfect drainage is a factor of first
importance. For convenience in heating and more uniform
temperature, it is often desirable to construct the house
over an excavation several feet below the natural surface.
The greatest economy is attained by constructing houses
high enough to accommodate beds in tiers of two to five.

About 2.5 feet of free space between bed tiers is
necessary, since the bed is at least 8 to 10 inches deep.
More than 3 feet in height must be allowed for each bed.
Better class mushroom houses have double walls packed
with fresh shavings. More expensive houses may be made
of hollow tile or concrete, which have much to recommend
them given the constant moisture-laden atmosphere.

## Bed Supports and Tiers

For commercial production where every foot of space is
used, the construction of supports for different tiers
requires particular attention. Upright supports for beds
in four or five tiers should be scantling not less than 2
by 6 inches, placed about 4 feet apart. Experienced
growers now use large gas piping for supports, which has
the advantage of permanency and is perfect from a sanitary
standpoint. Wall beds should be no more than 3 feet
across, but beds approachable from either side may be 6
feet across. Ample aisle space of 3 feet should be left.

## Ventilation

Arrangement should always be made for ventilation, and
it is usually advisable to place the ventilators in the
roof. A sufficiently sloping roof provided with air space
will correct, to a large extent, the difficulty of
moisture condensation on cold walls. The main point is
that the purpose of ventilation is accomplished with less
inconvenience from direct drafts when roof ventilators
are used.

## Heating

A heater is necessary since winter is the important
season for mushroom work. Many commercial mushroom houses
that pay well are not particularly elaborate. For
temporary use, a very simple boarded shed or unused barn
may serve, provided temperature can be maintained. The
exact form of heating will depend entirely upon local
conditions and the scale of operations.

## Open Air Culture

In Southern England, where winter temperature extremes
are moderate, culture in modified hot beds or [[hamilton-cold-frames-and-hardening-off]]
has been successful. Simple frames are employed, covered
with sheet iron or boards and straw. Open air culture
cannot be regarded as having great possibilities in most
regions, but sections of California with mean monthly
temperatures of 50 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit and without
pronounced daily range might be suitable. Experiments
with spawning pastures and lawns have shown inconsistent
results and are not to be depended upon.
