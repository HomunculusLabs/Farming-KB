# Tidal Aquaculture Systems

Permaculture design extends beyond the shoreline into intertidal zones, salt marshes, and shallow marine environments. These areas offer extraordinary productivity for food production, building materials, and mineral harvesting — yet they remain among the most underutilized landscapes in modern agriculture. Tidal systems require minimal external inputs because the sea delivers nutrients, organisms, and water movement for free.

## The Tidal Zone

Tide ranges vary from two to 27 feet globally, but productive tidal aquaculture requires as little as **one foot of tidal range**. Tides may occur once or twice daily depending on location. Along the American coast, tide ranges are well-documented in tide tables.

### Intertidal Plant Communities

#### Front Line Plants

- **Mangroves** — in tropical and subtropical climates, mangroves stabilize coastlines, trap sediments, and provide nursery habitat for marine species
- **Salt marsh grasses** — various low-growing species providing goose fodder and erosion control

#### Salt Marsh Plants

- **Samphire (*Salicornia*)** — fat, succulent plants at grass-root level; excellent goose fodder; can be made into pickles that sell well in England
- **Sea lavender (*Limonium*)** — a good honey plant for salt marsh apiaries
- **Cord grass / Spartina** — perhaps the most important salt marsh plant; produces heavy seed crops; excellent forage for geese; the base food for most cool-water fish including bluefish; without Spartina, there are no bluefish. The bluefish industry depends on Spartina, and Spartina depends on there never being an oil spill. Spartina also provides material for woven chair seats and cordage

### Salt Marsh Grazing

Saltings (salt marsh grazing land) provide some of the finest goose grazing available. If you can obtain access to salt flats — even where governments have struck off 100-foot tide reserves above the high tide mark — hundreds of yards of salt marsh often extend inland. These areas can support geese, and collecting Spartina seed for propagation is a worthwhile endeavor.

## Constructed Tidal Ponds

### Inland Tide Ponds

A remarkably simple and productive system can be created by digging ponds inland of salt marsh areas:

1. **Channel construction** — drive a tractor back and forth through the reserve area (or dig a channel if one doesn't exist)
2. **Side slope** — maintain a gentle 3:1 slope so rocks don't collapse
3. **Rock armoring** — place rocks on the tide side to block sea winds
4. **Salt-resistant shrubs** — plant around the pond perimeter
5. **Board regulation** — shift a board up or down in the channel to control the tidal range entering the pond (3 inches, 6 inches, or 1 foot twice daily)

**Benefits of inland tide ponds:**

- The cheapest swimming pool possible — self-flushing, always warm, inland, shelterable
- Tide brings only a few inches of water over warmed water
- Self-governing system requiring no pumps or external energy

### Longshore Drift Traps

Natural scour-hole formations demonstrate how tidal ponds create themselves:

- A log, wreck, or other obstacle on the shoreline causes longshore drift to build up sediment
- A deep permanent pond forms behind the obstacle, with a shallow pond in front
- Nobody digs or maintains these — they are self-creating and self-sustaining

Designers can replicate this by placing simple barrier systems — fences, logs, or any dragable material — to direct tide into scour-hole situations. These make excellent growing ponds.

## Tidal Fish Traps

### Ancient Stone Wall Traps

Mud and stone traps found in Tasmania and built by Maori and other Polynesian peoples are among the oldest known aquaculture structures:

- Stone walls extend just below the surface at high tide (about six inches below)
- Fish swim freely over the walls at high tide
- When the tide drops, fish are trapped in the lagoon
- The tide drains out through the rocks, but fish cannot follow

These traps work **best at night**, which is why most people who observe them by day think they don't work.

### Funnel Traps

Funnels can be constructed to lead sea water into ponds at night. Small round fish enter through the funnels but cannot find their way back out. Every tide brings a fresh supply of fish into the ponds. If predator fish or lobsters inhabit the ponds, they are kept in continuous food supply.

## Marine Organisms in Tidal Systems

### Oysters

Oysters grow best at **60% air exposure** — meaning they spend 60% of the tidal cycle exposed to air and 40% submerged. Local fisheries guides specify ideal exposure levels for each species:

- Above ideal exposure: much more shell, less meat
- Below ideal exposure (fully immersed): much more shell, less meat
- At ideal exposure: modest shell with abundant meat

Inland oyster cultivation using tide ponds is **far easier** than open-ocean raft culture and is self-governed to provide ideal exposure conditions. Clients can generate significant income breeding oysters and selling spat.

### Lobsters

Lobsters are territorial — they will not tolerate another lobster in the same hole and will lose legs in territorial disputes. If a client has access to broken pots (common from fish processing waste), they can establish a "lobster city":

- Stack broken pots in tidal ponds
- Feed lobsters with fish processing waste
- Grow a marine animal entirely inland

In dense populations, lobster growth reaches a standstill at approximately 3.25-inch carapace. Managing density is essential for optimal production.

### Sponges

Sponges can be grown in tidal pond systems, providing additional marine crop diversity.

### Flounder

Flounder can be raised in tidal ponds if adequate food is provided. However, they face predation from **cormorants**, which can devastate open pond fish populations. Solutions include:

- Hanging 5.5-inch nets well off the bottom — cormorants drown when they dive and become fish food
- Providing shelter where fish can escape when cormorants attack
- This measure is only appropriate for intense fish production operations

### Octopus

Octopus colonize deep scour-hole ponds naturally. Providing pots for shelter is all that's needed to establish them.

### Smelt

Smelt are small fish that serve as the primary food for trout in some systems. They breed in specific quarry sites and migrate into connected water bodies. Establishing smelt breeding habitat can substantially reduce or eliminate the need to feed trout.

## Integrated Tidal Polyculture

The most productive approach combines multiple elements:

1. **Duck marsh** with mussels and wild rice in 18-inch deep flat water
2. **Rice paddies** receiving nutrient-enriched water from the duck marsh
3. **Shrimp ponds** growing invertebrates that flow into trout habitat
4. **Trout ponds** receiving migration of shrimp and small fish from upstream systems

This **trickle system** uses small grilles that allow downstream movement of food organisms while preventing fish from moving upstream to consume the production organisms. Each pond operates at its optimal pH and conditions, with food flowing naturally to the harvest species.

## Mineral Harvesting from Longshore Drift

### Shell Collection

Longshore drift traps accumulate **broken shell**, sometimes by the ton. Shell is essentially limestone and has multiple uses:

- **Chicken grit** — sold in 50-pound bags to poultry keepers; quite expensive
- **Lime for fields** — a continually renewable source of agricultural lime
- One documented site had an estimated **1,100 tons of broken shell** accumulated alongside a jetty — on an island where limestone was being shipped in by boat

This represents a self-renewing mineral resource that the sea delivers, sorts, and deposits without any energy input from the farmer.

## Edge Design in Tidal Systems

Maximizing edges through islands and peninsulas increases the productive interface between water and land. When designing tidal ponds:

- Plant buffer trees to block rushes from extending onto lawns
- Establish edges quickly with desired species before undesirable species colonize
- Decide on edge species deliberately rather than allowing random colonization
- If rushes begin to dominate, they can close off the entire marginal surface

## The Design Philosophy

The fundamental insight of tidal aquaculture is that **the sea does the work**. It delivers water, nutrients, organisms, and energy for free. The designer's role is to create the structures — channels, ponds, barriers, funnels — that capture and organize this natural abundance into productive systems. This stands in sharp contrast to conventional aquaculture, which pumps, feeds, heats, and medicates at enormous energy cost.

A single bulldozer and a sloping site are, in Bill Mollison's estimation, sufficient to design and build sewage treatment and food production systems for entire towns — replacing millions of dollars in engineering infrastructure with productive wetland systems that generate employment, food, and raw materials while purifying water.
