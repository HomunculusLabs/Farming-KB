### Gymnopilus: 14 Species

Medium to large mushrooms with rusty orange to yellow-orange spore prints, preferring wood substrates.

Confirmed active species include [[gymnopilus-purpuratus-profile]], [[gymnopilus-purpuratus-profile]] is also likely active based on its bluing reaction.

A Mexican species, Gymnopilus subpurpuratis, is suspected active due to its green bruising reaction.

### Panaeolus: 13 Species

Dark-spored mushrooms of the family Coprinaceae, many growing on dung.

Species with mottled or spotted gills caused by uneven ripening of spore-producing cells.

Key species include [[panaeolus-subbalteatus-profile]], [[copelandia-genus-species-guide]] for detailed coverage.

Species include Copelandia cyanescens, C. tropicalis, C. mexicana, and others.

C. cyanescens sometimes grows in disturbed zones of temperate regions, including central Europe and Mexico City at 2,220 meters altitude.

### Hypholoma: 6 Species

Wood-inhabiting species, some of which produce bluing reactions.

Hypholoma naematoliformis is considered an endemic species.

The original species described by Maire from Algeria as "Hypholoma cyanescens" was later reclassified as Psilocybe mairei.

### Pluteus: 6 Species

Light-spored mushrooms of the Pluteaceae family, the only psilocybin-containing genus with rose-colored spore prints.

[[inocybe-aeruginascens-profile]] is the best-known active species, found in Central Europe.

Other active species include Inocybe corydalina, I. haemacta, I. tricolor, and I. coelestium.

### Conocybe: 4 Species

Small, fragile mushrooms of the Bolbitiaceae family.

[[conocybe-cyanopus-profile]] are confirmed active.

Conocybe siliginoides is considered endemic (Mexico).

Conocybe siligineoides was reportedly used by Mazatecs but has not been chemically confirmed since circa 1956.

### Panaeolina: 4 Species

Panaeolina foenisecii is cosmopolitan but poorly known in its true distribution.

Several Panaeolina species described by Hongo from Japan and by Natarajan and Raman from India are considered endemic.

### Single-Species Genera

Gerronema: 2 species (G. fibula reported from Malaysia, New Guinea, Solomon Islands, South America, and Europe).

Agrocybe: 1 species.

Galerina: 1 species.

Mycena: 1 species.

## Group 2: Species with Ibotenic Acid (3 Species)

Amanita muscaria: The classic fly agaric, mycorrhizal with Pinus and Betula in northern hemisphere forests, also found in pine plantations in tropical regions including Brazil, Colombia, Africa, Australia, and New Zealand.

Amanita pantherina: The panther cap.

Amanita regalis: The king bolete, found in northern regions.

## Group 3: Ergot Fungi (7 Species)

Claviceps purpurea and allies: 5 species of Claviceps.

Cordyceps: 2 species (C. capitata and C. ophioglossoides).

## Group 4: Sacred Fungi Without Chemical Studies (22 Species)

Boletes: Two genera with 8 species.

Russula: 6 species.

Gasteromycetes (Lycoperdales and Phallales): 6 species in 3 genera.

These species are used as sacred fungi by various ethnic groups but lack reliable chemical studies confirming their active compounds.

## Taxonomic Challenges

Several species have been subject to taxonomic disputes.

Psilocybe coprinifacies was considered doubtful by Guzman (1983) but recognized by European authors.

Psilocybe bohemica was described by Sebek (1983) from Czech specimens identified as P. coprinifacies.

Krieglsteiner considered P. coprinifacies, P. bohemica, P. mairei, and P. serbica to all be synonyms of P. cyanescens.

Copelandia mexicana is considered a good species despite being placed as a nomen excluded by Gerhardt (1996).

Panaeolina castaneifolia is considered by some to be a synonym of Panaeolus olivaceus.
