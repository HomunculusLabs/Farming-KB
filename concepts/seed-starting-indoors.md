# Seed Starting Indoors

How to raise your own vegetable transplants indoors, from [[steve-solomon]]"s *[[gardening-when-it-counts]]*. Solomon prefers direct seeding for most crops but acknowledges that raising your own seedlings is essential for tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, and other heat-loving crops in shorter-season areas.

## Why Raise Your Own

Garden center transplants are deeply suspect for several reasons:

- **Mislabeled varieties**: Unless it's a red cabbage labeled as green, you can rarely tell if the label is correct
- **Pot-bound or under-rooted**: Commercial growers face a narrow 10-day window between "too young to sell" and "too old to sell"
- **Soft and leggy**: Grown at high temperatures without wind, producing weak-walled, water-filled cells
- **Not hardened off**: Going from greenhouse to garden is a severe shock
- **Wrong varieties**: Grown for commercial appeal, not garden performance

## Container Selection

Use pots that hold slightly more than a half pint (250 ml) of soil. Options include:
- Clean plastic pots from earlier purchases
- **Newspaper pots**: 3-inch-wide (8 cm) strip of newspaper rolled into a squat cylinder, filled with soil mix, held together with a rubber band and/or string. The entire pot can be planted without disturbing roots.

For fast-growing cucurbits (melons, squash, cucumbers) that have delicate roots and are grown indoors for no more than one week, newspaper pots are ideal — the whole thing goes into the ground.

## Germination Cabinet (Germ Box)

Unless you have a place in your house where the temperature stays over 75°F (24°C) all day and all night, you need a germ box. It is the easiest tool to provide and makes seed starting far more reliable.

### Construction

- A container roughly the size of a cardboard apple box or case
- A lightbulb on the bottom (25-40 watts — beware of fire risk with larger bulbs in cardboard)
- A sliding glass top (cut from scrap window glass) that sits in grooves/tracks
- Control temperature by changing bulb wattage or sliding the top

### Temperature Control

- Target 80°F (27°C) — temperatures exceeding 80°F may lessen germination
- Adjust by changing bulb size or sliding the top open
- Use an old cookie sheet or perforated rack for heat distribution

## Seed Starting Mix

Use soil at the ready-to-till moisture content (as described for garden beds in the tools chapter). This is the same moisture content you'd want for direct seeding — the soil should be damp enough to form a ball when squeezed but not dripping wet.

### Fertilizer Strategy

- **Before first true leaf**: Minimal NPK (nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium) but plenty of calcium. Excess soil nutrients encourage damping-off diseases that attack emerging seedlings.
- **After first true leaf**: Begin fertilizing. Organic liquid fertilizers are often deficient in phosphorus — look for [[fish-emulsion]] that has been fortified with phosphoric acid or use a complete liquid fertilizer.
- **Coffee grounds**: Worth reckoning as about half-strength [[chicken-manure]]. Free espresso grounds from restaurants are an excellent amendment.

## Sowing Procedure

1. Fill pots with seed mix, press down to settle and create air spaces
2. Use a pencil to poke a small hole for each seed (2-3 seeds per pot)
3. Flick loose soil into the hole to cover seeds — nothing weighty to oppose emergence
4. Slip a thin, clear plastic bag over the pot, large enough to twist closed
5. Place sealed pots on the germination cabinet

### Why the Plastic Bag

Each time you water a sprouting seed, the medium gets too wet and the moisture makes the temperature drop. Inside a nearly airtight bag there is almost no moisture lost. The soil starts at the perfect moisture level and stays that way until seedlings emerge.

## Sowing Schedule

- **Solanums (tomatoes, peppers, eggplant)**: Start about 6 weeks before you'll transplant
  - Sow tomatoes first
  - When tomatoes are up and growing, start peppers and eggplant
  - When those are up, consider starting hardiest cucurbits
  - Start zucchini about 2 weeks after solanums
  - Start tender cucurbits (melons, etc.) when it's warm enough to put tomatoes out

This schedule works with one small germination cabinet.

## After Germination

1. **As soon as some seedlings emerge**: Remove from germ cabinet, take off the plastic bag, move to the brightest, sunniest area available. Begin watering as needed.

2. **Strong, vigorous seedlings**: Should appear within 4 days. Seeds that haven't emerged after the entire week are likely dead — remove them.

3. **Thinning**:
   - When seedlings fully open their seed leaves (cotyledons): thin to all but the two strongest using small scissors or fingernails
   - When seedlings develop three true leaves: thin down to two per pot
   - Later: thin to one seedling per pot

4. **Hardening off**: When thinned to one seedling and weather is pleasant, keep outside during the day. Before planting, keep outside overnight too.

## Light

Small batches of seedlings can be raised in a sunny window. If light is insufficient:

- Seedlings incline strongly toward the light
- Stems become thin and spindly
- **Solution**: Make a reflector from a large piece of cardboard about 14 inches (35 cm) tall, somewhat longer than the line of pots. Cover one side with aluminum foil (glue or wrap it) or paint it bright white. Prop the reflector immediately behind the seedlings.

## Transplanting Seedlings to the Garden

Solomon's [[transplanting-technique]]:

1. Dig a hole large enough for the rootball
2. Pour a quart (liter) of liquid fertilizer or compost/[[manure-tea]] into the hole
3. Gently enough to not wash soil away from the rootball, but rapidly enough to fill the hole before it soaks in
4. Quickly push loose soil back around the rootball, creating a muddy slurry that settles into all nooks and crannies
5. This creates a tight connection between roots and surrounding soil
6. Done properly, the seedling will not need more frequent watering than anything else in the garden and will grow really fast
