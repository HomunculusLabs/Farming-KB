## Thigmomorphogenesis: Growth Adaptation to Mechanical Stress

Beyond rapid movements, the more pervasive effect of mechanical sensing in
plants is thigmomorphogenesis, the alteration of growth patterns in response to
chronic mechanical stimulation. Trees growing in windy environments develop
shorter, thicker trunks with more extensive root systems than those growing in
sheltered locations. This is not merely a physical consequence of being blown
about; it is an active developmental response initiated by the plant's
mechanosensory system.

The biochemical pathway involves several key steps. Mechanical stimulation
activates mechanosensitive channels, leading to calcium influx. This calcium
signal triggers the production of [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]] causes the tendril to
curl around the object. The cells on the side away from the contact elongate
more than those on the contact side, creating a coiling response.

The coiling occurs in two phases. First, the tendril rapidly forms a hook
around the support through differential growth. Then, over hours or days, the
tendril develops a stable coil that provides a strong mechanical grip. The
tendril can also sense the texture and diameter of the support, coiling more
tightly around thin, rough objects than around thick, smooth ones. This
sensory discrimination allows the plant to invest energy in securing itself
to supports that provide the best structural advantage.

## Root Thigmotropism

Roots also respond to touch, but their response is opposite to that of shoots:
roots grow away from touch (negative thigmotropism). When a root encounters
an obstacle in the soil, it changes direction and grows around it. This
ability allows roots to navigate the complex underground environment, avoiding
rocks and seeking out pockets of nutrients and water. The root cap, a
specialised structure at the root tip, is the primary site of touch
perception in roots.

Roots can also distinguish between living and dead objects in the soil. When
a root encounters another living root, it typically alters its growth pattern
differently than when it encounters a stone or dead root. This suggests that
roots can detect chemical signals associated with living tissue, integrating
touch perception with chemical sensing.
