# Tidal Aquaculture and Marsh Systems

Permaculture design extends beyond the shoreline into intertidal zones, salt marshes, and shallow coastal waters. These edge environments are among the most productive on Earth, offering opportunities for polyculture aquaculture, forage production, and mineral harvesting that require minimal external inputs once established.

## Tide Range Requirements

Effective tidal aquaculture requires surprisingly little tidal movement — less than one foot of tide range is sufficient. Tides range from 2 to 27 feet globally (Bay of Fundy, Darwin), but even minimal daily fluctuation supports productive systems. In some locations, tides occur only once per day. Tide tables and local observation determine specific site conditions.

## Ancient Tidal Traps

Indigenous peoples worldwide developed highly effective tidal fish traps:
- **Tasmanian mud traps**: Ancient stone walls built about 6 inches below high tide surface — fish swim over at high tide and are trapped when water recedes through the rocks
- **Maori and Polynesian traps**: Similar designs with no recorded construction date, indicating great antiquity
- **Nocturnal effectiveness**: These traps work best at night when fish are less wary

The design principle is elegantly simple: a barrier that allows fish entry at high tide but prevents escape as the tide drops. No maintenance, no bait, no fuel — just permanent passive harvest.

## Salt Marsh Plant Communities

### Front Line Plants
- **Mangroves**: In sufficiently warm climates, mangroves form the primary coastal barrier
- **Fat salt-tolerant grasses**: Excellent goose fodder; the historic origin of many domestic goose breeds

### Spartina (Cord Grass)
*Spartina* is one of the most important salt marsh plants:
- **Primary forage**: Base food for most cool water fish — no Spartina means no bluefish
- **Heavy seed production**: Valuable forage crop for geese
- **Nurse ground**: Provides essential habitat for juvenile fish and their prey
- **Bluefish industry dependency**: The entire bluefish fishery depends on Spartina marshes remaining free of oil spills
- **Weaving material**: Traditionally used for cord and fancy seat chairs
- **Pickling**: Samphire pickles sell well in England
- **Seed collection**: Advise collecting Spartina seed and distributing it to restore degraded marshes

### Honey and Other Crops
- **Sea lavender**: Excellent salt marsh honey plant
- Various small salt marsh plants provide useful yields for foraging, crafts, and specialty foods

## Inland Tidal Pond Construction

Where clients have salt flats or salt marsh behind the high tide mark:

### Site Selection
- Look for salt marsh extending hundreds of yards behind the tide reserve
- Existing tidal channels are ideal; if absent, a channel can be cut by driving a tractor back and forth

### Construction
- **Side slopes**: 3:1 ratio (gentle) so rocks naturally settle
- **Rock placement**: Throw rocks up on the tide side to block sea winds
- **Salt-resistant shrubs**: Plant around the pond perimeter
- **Tide regulation**: Adjust a board up or down in the channel to control water depth — 3-inch, 6-inch, or 1-foot tidal range twice daily over the pond

### Benefits
- **Cheapest swimming pool possible**: Self-flushing, always warm, inland and sheltered
- **Permanent water**: Fills with the next tide and maintains itself
- **No maintenance**: No digging, no pumping, no fuel costs

## Oyster and Mussel Cultivation

### Optimal Exposure
Oysters and mussels grow best at approximately 60% air exposure. Local fisheries guides specify ideal air-to-water ratios for each species:
- **Above ideal exposure**: Much less meat, more shell
- **Below ideal exposure (immersed)**: Much more shell, less meat
- **At ideal exposure**: Modest shell with abundant meat

### Inland Oyster Rafting
A client with access to broken pots can establish productive oyster cultivation inland — far easier than tidal flat operations. The tidal pond system self-governs to provide ideal exposure cycles. Breeding oyster spat can become a significant income source.

## Lobster Cultivation

- **Territorial behavior**: Lobsters will not tolerate another lobster in the same hole — they will lose legs in confrontations
- **Habitat provision**: Abundant broken pot or similar cavities support dense populations
- **Feed source**: Fish processing waste provides food; this amounts to growing marine animals inland
- **Density management**: Dense populations bring growth to a standstill — none exceed 3.25-inch carapace
- **Predator protection**: Lobsters and oysters are fairly immune from predation in pond systems

## Flounder and Finfish

- Flounder require feeding and are vulnerable to cormorant predation
- **Cormorant control**: Hang a 5.5-inch net well off the bottom to drown cormorants; fish eat the carcasses
- **Shelter importance**: Any shelter allows fish to escape cormorants; in open ponds, cormorants devastate populations
- Only implement cormorant control during intense fish production

## Longshore Drift Traps

### Natural Scour Holes
Study natural formations — logs, wrecks, old boats — where longshore drift accumulates. The tide builds up deep permanent ponds behind obstacles and shallow ponds in front. Nobody digs or maintains these; they form naturally and permanently.

### Shell Collection
Longshore drift traps accumulate broken shell, sometimes by the ton:
- **Case study**: An island with acidic soil had been importing limestone by boat for generations, spending enormously on jetty maintenance. Yet approximately 1,100 tons of broken shell (essentially limestone) had accumulated naturally at the jetty base
- **Commercial value**: Shell sells in 50-pound bags to poultry keepers as grit and lime source — quite expensive and continuously renewable
- **Agricultural use**: Excellent lime source for fields

### Artificial Drift Traps
Simple barrier systems — fences, logs, anything dragged into position — can create permanent productive ponds from longshore drift accumulation. These ponds require no excavation or maintenance.

## Octopus and Sponges

- **Octopus**: If simple pots are provided, octopus will colonize tidal ponds
- **Sponges**: Can be grown in appropriate tidal conditions
- These represent additional low-input crops for tidal systems

## Tidal Fish Ponds

### Funnel Design
- Construct funnels leading into ponds so sea water enters through narrow openings at night
- Small round fish enter with the tide but cannot find their way back out through the funnels
- With predator fish or lobsters in the pond, incoming fish become continuous feed

### Night Operation
These traps work best at night, which is why casual daytime observation leads people to conclude they don't work — the fish are already inside.

## Geese on Salt Marshes

Salt marshes with appropriate tide range provide the best goose grazing available. If you acquire saltings (thousands of acres exist), you have premium goose habitat. Combined with Spartina as forage base, these areas support productive goose operations with minimal input.

## Polyculture Integration

The full tidal system integrates multiple trophic levels:
1. **Primary producers**: Spartina, sea lavender, salt-tolerant grasses
2. **Herbivores**: Geese, ducks
3. **Filter feeders**: Oysters, mussels (at ideal tidal exposure)
4. **Predators**: Lobsters, flounder, trout
5. **Decomposers**: Scavenging species in the benthic zone

Each level supports the next, creating self-regulating food webs that produce multiple yields with minimal management once established.

## Design Philosophy

The overarching principle of tidal aquaculture in permaculture is to work with natural tidal energy rather than against it. The tide brings water, nutrients, organisms, and shell material. The designer's role is to direct these flows into productive configurations using simple barriers, channels, and substrate provisions — not to fight the sea with pumps, concrete, and energy-intensive systems. As Mollison emphasizes: permaculture does not stop at the shoreline.
