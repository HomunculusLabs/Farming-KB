# Old-Growth Forest Fungi and Conservation

Old-growth forests harbor some of the most diverse and ecologically significant fungal communities on Earth. These ancient ecosystems support mushroom species that may exist nowhere else, including organisms with potentially invaluable medicinal and bioremedial properties. The destruction of old-growth forests for timber represents an irreversible loss of fungal biodiversity that may have profound consequences for human welfare.

## Fungal Diversity in Old-Growth Forests

The old-growth forests of the Pacific Northwest — the dominant ecosystem where [[mycoremediation-stamets-approach]] conducted his research — support extraordinary fungal diversity. More than 2,000 species of mushrooms live symbiotically with Douglas firs alone. Randy Molina and colleagues (1997) estimated that approximately 250 species of mycorrhizal fungi associate with hemlocks. Of the more than 527 mushroom species documented growing in old-growth forests, at least 109 are native specifically to the Pacific Northwest (USDA 1993).

The fungal community of an old-growth forest includes all four major ecological types: mycorrhizal fungi forming symbiotic partnerships with tree roots, saprophytic fungi decomposing dead organic matter, parasitic fungi obtaining nutrients from living hosts, and endophytic fungi living within plant tissues. The health of a forest ecosystem's foundation is an interplay of mycelial networks from all four types.

## Rare and Endangered Fungal Species

One of the rarest old-growth forest mushroom species is Bridgeoporus nobilissimus (formerly Oxyporus nobilissimus), the noble polypore. This mushroom once held the record for the largest mushroom in the world before being surpassed by an individual of Rigidoporus ulmarius estimated to weigh more than 660 pounds (approximately 300 kg).

Both species are associated exclusively with old-growth habitats, and many other rare species likely thrive in old-growth forests but remain undiscovered. The slow growth rates, specific habitat requirements, and dependence on ancient tree hosts make these species extremely vulnerable to logging.

## The National Security Argument for Forest Conservation

Stamets made a provocative argument that preserving old-growth forests is a matter of national security. He discovered that many of the most potent antiviral and antimicrobial compounds come from fungi native to old-growth forests. If a mushroom species exclusive to the old-growth forest could prevent a viral epidemic that could kill millions and cost billions, the value of that forest becomes incalculable.

Specifically, mushroom species producing enzymes to break down VX nerve agent and antibiotics effective against pox viruses and HIV have been isolated from old-growth forest fungi. Losing these mycomedicinal species to improve the quarterly reports of lumber companies may cost civilization far more in economic terms than it gains for a single industry.

The natural capital flowing from old-growth forests exceeds short-term economic gains. Current economic models comparing timber and mushrooms as two mutually exclusive commodities fundamentally fail to account for the full range of ecosystem services these forests provide.

## Forests Are Not Renewable Resources

Stamets drew on his personal experience as a logger in Washington's northern Cascades during the 1970s to argue against the timber industry's claim that "trees are America's renewable resource." With hundreds of tons of trees harvested per acre in one week from soils that have been built over 10,000 years, the loss of biomass cannot be called renewable in any human timeframe.

Carbon cycles that fuel the food chain and build forest soils move at a much slower pace than logging operations. With each generation of trees harvested, soils become increasingly shallow, jeopardizing forest health. The richness and depth of soil is humanity's legacy from centuries of mycelial activity, and with each harvest-replant cycle, the soil loses nutrients and gradually becomes overtaxed.

Many logging companies in the Pacific Northwest have sold their lands because return on investment declined to unattractive ratios after three generations of harvest. The fourth replanting does not provide yields comparable to the first two. Trees prematurely climax, falling over as root systems can no longer hold them upright in the depleted soils.

## Economic Valuation of Forest Fungi

A study by Alexander and colleagues (2002) using a soil evaluation equivalent (SEV) formula found that in south-central Oregon, harvesting timber and matsutake mushrooms each yielded close to the same economic benefit from the land over the same time period. This evaluation did not factor in additional benefits of not cutting timber: thickening soils, reducing erosion, supporting stream health, increasing biodiversity, improving air quality, providing regional cooling (offsetting global warming), and the experiential pleasure of wild mushroom foraging.

A United Nations report by Eric Boa (2004) confirmed that wild mushrooming has worldwide socioeconomic significance, supporting communities across multiple continents.

Selective harvesting of developing second- and third-growth forests, when done with the intention of preserving secondary forest products such as mushrooms, may prove to be the best practice for sustaining both ecological and economic value. These principles form the cornerstone of an emergent management strategy called ecoforestry.

## Sustainable Mushroom Harvesting Practices

The manner in which mushrooms are harvested has dramatic impacts on subsequent crops. A study in southern Oregon showed that when matsutake patches were raked and the divots from harvested mushrooms were not re-covered with duff, crops in subsequent years plummeted 75 to 90 percent. When harvesters re-covered the divots after shallow raking, yields were not adversely affected (Eberhart et al. 1999).

Similarly, no adverse effects were noticed over a decade of chanterelle harvesting in a forest west of Portland, Oregon (Pilz et al. 2003), where the preferred practice was to cut mushrooms while picking, leaving stem butts undisturbed. Chanterelles often emerge in pairs, and if harvesters cut only one visible mushroom, the hidden partner — often a resting primordium — can grow to maturity.

## The Cortes Island Mycoforestry Research Project

In 2003, Stamets purchased land on Cortes Island, British Columbia, half of which had been clear-cut. The property, a mixture of old-growth and second-growth Douglas fir, hemlock, and western red cedar, became an experimental site for demonstrating mycoforestry strategies as an alternative to traditional slash-and-burn methods.

The clear-cut portion was divided into four types of test plots, replanted with 35,000 Douglas fir and cedar seedlings. Half of all seedlings were root-clipped in mycorrhizal spore-mass slurries: cedars received approximately half a million spores of the endomycorrhizal species Glomus intraradices, while Douglas firs received the ectomycorrhizal species Rhizopogon parksii.

Half of all trees in each treated and untreated group received a topdressing of wood chips (approximately one gallon per tree) made from the logging debris. The potential benefits include regional soil cooling, enhanced moisture retention, and slow nutrient streaming to root zones as saprophytic fungi decompose the wood chips.

## Recycled Wood Debris and Forest Recovery

When trees are killed for logging, mycorrhizal fungal communities die back. The vast debris fields that remain — stumps, brush, and unmarketable trees — represent locked-away biomass unavailable to the food web. Stamets proposed that rather than burning this debris (the conventional practice), it should be chipped and distributed as a nutrient resource.

Wood chips serve as valuable "ecological currency" that can be reinvested into the forest's "ecobank" to enhance sustainability. Inoculated wood chip beds decompose into rich soil within 2-3 years, accelerating the recovery of soil depth and nutrient content that would otherwise take decades.

## Historical Context

The relationship between old-growth forests and fungal diversity has been recognized since the early days of forest ecology, but the full extent of this relationship has only become apparent in recent decades. Old-growth forests — those that have developed over long periods without significant disturbance — harbor fungal communities that are far more diverse and structurally complex than those found in younger, managed forests.

The recognition that old-growth forests are irreplaceable reservoirs of fungal biodiversity has been a driving force behind conservation efforts, particularly in the Pacific Northwest of North America, where logging of old-growth temperate rainforests has eliminated vast areas of habitat for fungi that are dependent on the specific conditions found only in ancient forests.

## Modern Significance

Old-growth forest fungi are increasingly recognized as essential components of forest ecosystem function. Mycorrhizal networks in old-growth forests connect individual trees into a "wood wide web" through which nutrients, water, and chemical signals are shared. These networks, which may take centuries to develop fully, are believed to contribute to the stability, resilience, and productivity of old-growth forest ecosystems.

Conservation of old-growth fungal communities requires preservation of the forest structural features on which they depend: large dead wood in various stages of decomposition, deep organic soil layers, diverse microhabitats, and a complex canopy structure that creates a range of light and moisture conditions.
