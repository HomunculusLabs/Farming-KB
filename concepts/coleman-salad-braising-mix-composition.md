
# Winter Salad Mix and Braising Mix Composition

## Overview

[[salad-crops-growing-guide]] are far more cold tolerant than mature ones. This means the grower can harvest tender, delicate, nutritionally dense greens during months when most farms have nothing fresh to sell—a powerful competitive advantage.

The distinction between salad mix and braising mix also reflects a deep understanding of culinary use. Rather than offering a generic "mixed greens" product, Coleman designed two distinct products for two distinct purposes, each with its own quality standards and ingredient palette. This segmentation allows the grower to use a wider range of crops, reduce waste, and serve different customer preferences from the same production base.

## The Baby-Leaf Salad Mix

### Ingredient Selection

Coleman's winter salad mix has included: red lettuces, green lettuces, broadleaf arugula, sylvetta arugula, endive, narrow-stem chard, claytonia, minutina, spinach, mache, watercress, and beet leaves. All are carefully chosen as naturally hardy winter-salad varieties. The mix was designed to be mild and child-friendly—Coleman deliberately excluded the hardy oriental greens (mustards) from the salad mix because his customers preferred a salad without the mustard tang.

### Nutritional Superiority

Based on nutritional data from the U.S. Department of Agriculture, Coleman's salad blend contains at least 5 times more calcium, 4 times more iron, 12 times more vitamin A, and 6 times more vitamin C than a salad of head lettuce. This nutritional advantage is a strong marketing point and reflects the superior nutritional density of diverse, immature, cold-grown leaves compared to the watery, mature heads of conventional lettuce. The USDA data provides a scientific basis for what experienced winter growers have long observed: that cold-stressed plants concentrate sugars and nutrients, producing leaves that are both sweeter and more nutritious than their warm-season counterparts.

### Quality Standards

Quality was the primary concern. Coleman established strict standards for his salad mix:

- **Size**: Leaves should be no more than three inches in length. They must easily fit onto a fork and into the mouth.
- **No overgrown leaves**: Leaves with seven- to eight-inch-long stems (as seen in some commercial mixes) are better consigned to a braising mix or sold bunched as cooking greens.
- **No chopping**: Coleman did not chop mature plants into small pieces like the cut-up endive and radicchio seen in supermarket mixes. All ingredients are whole baby leaves.
- **No tough, bitter, or stringy leaves**: Any ingredient not meeting quality standards is left out of the mix until conditions improve.
- **No frost damage**: Damaged leaves are excluded entirely.

### Specific Cultivar Choices

Coleman used specific cultivars chosen for winter performance:

- **Endive**: 'Indivia Bianca Riccia da Taglio' (white curly endive for cutting), sold by Johnny's Seeds as 'Bianca Riccia'—nice light-colored frilly leaves at the baby-leaf stage.
- **Red color**: 'Bull's Blood' beet leaves for red color, rather than radicchio. Cool winter temperatures intensify the deep maroon, and the flavor lacks radicchio's bitter edge (which can ruin a whole salad under poor conditions).
