## Shamanic Initiation Through Illness

## Overview

Across cultures, one of the most well-documented pathways to becoming a shaman is the experience of serious personal illness—often an illness that conventional medicine fails to cure. The Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico, provide a particularly detailed ethnographic example of this pattern. The shaman [[roman-estrada]]'s account of his initiation, as documented by Henry Munn, reveals a structured process in which physical illness becomes psychological crisis, which becomes spiritual calling, which becomes professional vocation—and in which the acceptance of the calling is itself the final stage of the cure.

## The Universal Pattern

The illness-initiation pattern is found in shamanic traditions worldwide:

1. **Physical or mental crisis**: The future shaman experiences a serious illness, accident, or psychological breakdown
2. **Failure of conventional treatment**: Doctors, medicines, and ordinary remedies prove ineffective
3. **Call to the shaman**: A traditional healer is consulted as a last resort
4. **Mushroom/plant medicine**: The shaman administers psychoactive preparations
5. **Visionary cure**: The patient experiences a dramatic recovery during or after the visionary session
6. **Identification of vocation**: The curing shaman declares that the patient has been "called" to become a shaman
7. **Resistance**: The patient initially refuses, wanting only to be cured
8. **Compelling acceptance**: Through further visionary experiences, the patient accepts the calling
9. **Training and transmission**: The elder shaman transmits knowledge to the new initiate
10. **Completion of cure**: Full recovery coincides with acceptance of the new vocation

## The Case of Roman Estrada

### The Original Illness

Estrada's crisis began with appendicitis—a straightforward physical condition. He was taken to a hospital, where he underwent surgery. The physical surgery was successful, but the psychological trauma of the experience devastated him:

- He had never been in a hospital in his life
- He was suffocated with an ether mask—an experience of near-asphyxiation
- He "gave up the ghost" during the operation (experienced clinical death or near-death)
- Upon regaining consciousness, he was "frightened and depressed, without any will to live"

### The Development of Fright

What began as physical illness became *susto*—soul loss or fright. The traumatic experience of surgery—particularly the anesthesia and the sense of dying—caused his spirit to flee. His symptoms were characteristic of severe post-traumatic stress:

- He lay "like a dead man with his eyes wide open"
- He was "not saying anything to anyone"
- He felt his "life had been a failure"
- He "had never become the important man he had aspired to be all his life"
- He felt it was "too late"; his "life was over"
- He had "done nothing that his children might remember with respect and awe"

The hospital doctors could not help because "there was nothing wrong with him physically." The appendectomy had healed, but the psychological dimension of his illness—what the Mazatecs call the loss of spirit—was beyond the scope of biomedical treatment.

### The Failed Pilgrimage

Estrada sought help from multiple sources: "No matter how much the doctors treated me, I didn't get well. I went to the Latin American Hospital. I went to Cordoba as well. I went to Mexico. I went to Tehuacan and wasn't alleviated." His unsuccessful medical pilgrimage through multiple institutions mirrors the experience of many patients with treatment-resistant psychological conditions who eventually turn to alternative healing systems.

### The Mushroom Cure

Finally, Estrada called a renowned shaman from the nearby village of San Lucas—a man described as "short, slight, wizened" and 105 years old. The old shaman gave Estrada the "mushrooms of vitality," and the therapy worked through a dramatic mechanism:

1. **Imaginal re-surgery**: Under the influence of the mushrooms, Estrada vividly relived his operation. "The mushrooms cut him open, arranged his insides, and sewed him up again."
2. **Correction of perceived errors**: He believed the hospital doctors had made mistakes responsible for his lethargy. In his mushroom vision, the mushrooms "performed another surgical intervention and corrected the mistakes of the profane doctor."
3. **Active integration**: By intensely envisioning with heightened consciousness what had happened under anesthesia, he "assumed at last the frightening event he had previously been unable to integrate into his experience."
4. **Resurrection**: "The dead man came back to life, he wanted to live because he felt once again that he was alive and had the force to go on living."

### The Transmission of Vocation

The elder shaman immediately identified Estrada as a potential successor. The transmission followed a precise ritual structure:

#### The Altar of Signs

The old shaman prepared three objects on a table:
- **The table itself**: An altar at which to officiate (mushroom sessions are called "masses")
- **Ground tobacco** (*San Pedro*): Believed to have powerful magical and remedial values
- **A cross**: Symbolizing both the Christian crucifixion/resurrection and a "crossing of the ways"—an intersection of existential paths indicating change

#### The Choice

The shaman told Estrada to choose among these signs. Estrada refused—he wanted only to be cured, not to become a shaman. The shaman's response was decisive: "Here you don't give the orders. I am he who is going to say whether you receive this work or not."

#### The Negation of Will

The shaman's strategy was to negate the patient's will in order to arouse it. "It is no longer you who command," he was told. "You are too far gone to have any say in the matter." This paradoxical intervention—denying the patient's autonomy to stimulate autonomous desire—is reminiscent of therapeutic techniques in both psychoanalysis and hypnotherapy.

#### The Voice of the Dead Father

At the critical moment, Estrada heard the voice of his dead father—forty-three years deceased—speaking to him during the mushroom experience: "This work that is being given to you, I am he who tells you to accept it." He could not identify the source of the voice until the shaman interpreted it for him.

This phenomenon—contact with deceased family members during altered states of consciousness—is widely reported in both shamanic traditions and psychedelic therapy research. It suggests that the mushroom experience activates deep psychological structures related to family, identity, and personal destiny.

#### The Diploma

The shaman declared that Estrada would receive his "diploma in the presence of God." In an oral culture without writing, where skills are transmitted from father to son and mother to daughter rather than contained in books, the "diploma" represents initiation through direct experiential knowledge. Wisdom is gained during the mushroom experiences themselves—"experiences of vision and communication that impart knowledge."

#### Teaching of Techniques

Having accepted the vocation, Estrada was taught specific shamanic techniques. The most notable was "sucking through space"—using a hollow tube of cane to draw sickness out of a patient by suction from a distance. This technique, combining physical gesture with spiritual intention, exemplifies the Mazatec understanding that healing operates simultaneously on material and immaterial planes.

## The Psychology of Initiation

### The Inseparability of Cure and Vocation

The crucial insight of the Mazatec system is that the cure is inseparable from the acceptance of vocation. The patient's neurosis—the repression of his creative forces—cannot be resolved by passive treatment alone. The patient must actively assume a new role in the community that releases these repressed forces. The "impulse to die" must be counterbalanced by a new "impulse to live" that comes from outside the self.

### Compensatory Vocation

The new profession serves as compensation for the patient's sense of failure. Estrada had spent his life as a storekeeper, feeling he had never become the "important man" he aspired to be. The shamanic vocation offered him a role of genuine respect and authority—one that would cause his children to "remember him with respect and awe."

### Personality Reintegration

The acceptance of the calling is the mechanism by which the shattered personality is reintegrated around a new center. The fragmented self—alienated from its own spirit, depressed, purposeless—is reconstructed around the identity of healer, speaker of mighty words, intermediary between the human and spiritual worlds.

### The "I Who Is Another"

The initiation process enacts Rimbaud's dictum "I is another" (*Je est un autre*). The voice of the dead father, heard during the mushroom experience, is the patient's own super-ego speaking in a form he cannot recognize as his own. The shaman interprets this voice, giving the patient permission to accept a new identity that is simultaneously himself and something more than himself.

## Resistance as Necessary Stage

The patient's initial resistance to the vocation is not an obstacle to be overcome but a necessary stage in the process. The resistance represents the dying self's last grip on its familiar identity. Only by exhausting this resistance—by demonstrating that the old self has nothing left to offer—can the new self emerge. The shaman's authoritarian negation of the patient's will ("Here you don't give the orders") paradoxically creates the conditions for genuine autonomous choice.

## The Shaman as Secular Priest

A notable feature of Mazatec shamanism is that the shaman is a secular figure, unordained by the Catholic Church. Yet he assumes a sacerdotal role: mushroom sessions are called "masses," and each father of a family is considered the religious priest of his own household. The shaman's authority derives not from institutional appointment but from personal experience—specifically, the experience of illness, cure, and initiation described here.
