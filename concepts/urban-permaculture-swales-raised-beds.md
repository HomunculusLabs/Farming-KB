# Swales and Raised Beds

Swales and raised beds are complementary earthwork strategies that manage water, build soil, and create productive growing spaces. In [[permaculture]] Water Systems, they work together to transform sloped land into a series of fertile, water-retentive planting zones.

## Swales: Capturing Water on Slopes

Swales are long, level excavations dug on contour across a slope. They differ fundamentally from ditches: ditches divert water elsewhere, while swales intercept water flow, hold it for hours or days, and let it slowly infiltrate into the groundwater, recharging soils and tree root systems.

A swale system typically includes:

- **The swale berm** -- the mound of soil on the downhill side of the excavation
- **The swale trench** -- the level basin that fills with water during rain events
- **Planted trees** -- established on the berm, where roots access the stored water
- **Connecting channels** -- linking swales to ponds or overflow points

On one permaculture site, a sequence of swales connects to a small dugout pond that carries nutrient water down the slope. At the bottom, raised beds made of sticks and mud hold the garlic crop. The entire system functions as a self-contained ecosystem that does not need inputs from the outside.

## Alder-to-Fruit Tree Succession

One documented site was a big swamp full of alder trees a couple years before the permaculture crew began work. Alders are pioneer trees that thrive in wet, nitrogen-poor conditions -- they fix nitrogen through root-associated bacteria and tolerate waterlogged soils. They are nature's first step in converting swamp to forest.

The crew's process:

1. **Assess** -- the alder swamp holds water but is not productive for food crops
2. **Reroute water** -- carefully redirect water flow to make wet areas smaller and surrounding areas more fertile for ground crops
3. **Harvest alders selectively** -- cut trees to harvest wood while preserving younger trees' access to old root systems and sunlight from the opened canopy
4. **Replace with food trees** -- plant fruit and nut trees in the improving soil, using the nitrogen left behind by the alders
5. **Allow succession** -- the system transitions from alder swamp to productive mixed orchard over several years

This succession follows a natural pattern: pioneer species (alders) prepare the site, and climax species (fruit trees) replace them. The permaculture designer accelerates this process by selectively harvesting pioneers and planting desired species.

## Raised Beds at the Base

At the bottom of the slope, raised beds are constructed from sticks and mud -- simple, local-material construction that creates well-drained growing areas above the wet ground. Garlic and other crops that need good drainage thrive in these beds.

Raised bed construction methods include:

- **[[hugelkultur]]** -- beds built over logs and branches that decompose over time, creating long-term fertility and water retention (see [[chop-and-drop-mulching]])
- **Wattle and daub** -- woven stick frames filled with mud (the traditional method described at this site)
- **Sheet mulch beds** -- layers of cardboard, compost, and mulch on top of existing ground
- **Rock walls** -- stone-lined beds that provide thermal mass in addition to drainage

## Intercropping by Height

The swale system supports intercropping by vertical layer. On the berm:

- **Canopy** -- fruit and nut trees
- **Understory** -- berry bushes and small fruit trees
- **Herbaceous** -- herbs, vegetables, and [[dynamic-accumulators]]
- **Ground cover** -- clovers and other living mulches in the swale trench itself

Each species is placed according to its shade tolerance, height, and water requirements. The taller trees access deeper groundwater; the ground covers protect the soil surface and fix nitrogen; the herbs attract pollinators and accumulate nutrients.

## The Self-Contained Ecosystem

The goal of [swale-and-bed design is to create a system that functions without external inputs. When properly established:

- Rainwater is captured and stored on-site
- Nitrogen is fixed by leguminous ground covers and trees
- Biomass is cycled through pruning and mulching
- Pests are managed by diverse plantings that attract beneficials
- Fertility is maintained through decomposition and nutrient cycling

The system produces its own water, fertility, and pest management. Trees are essential parts of swale planting systems -- their deep roots access stored groundwater and their leaf drop contributes to the organic matter cycle.

## [[swale-design]] Guidelines

For effective swale installation:

- **Always dig on contour** -- use an A-frame level or laser level to mark exact contour lines
- **Space swales according to slope** -- steeper slopes need more closely spaced swales
- **Size to rainfall** -- larger swale capacity for higher rainfall areas
- **Plant immediately** -- never leave bare swale berms; plant trees and ground covers as soon as the earthwork is done
- **Plan overflow** -- every swale needs a spillway for extreme rain events to prevent blowout
- **Start at the top** -- work from the top of the slope downward so upper swales don't overload lower ones

## Urban Applications

Swales can be scaled to any property:

- **Backyard food forests** -- A single swale across a gentle slope captures enough water to support [fruit trees
- **Rain gardens** -- miniature swales in urban yards that capture roof runoff
- **Curb cuts** -- redirecting street runoff into sidewalk planting strips via small swale-like basins
- **Contour hedgerows** -- planting dense hedges on contour with a slight swale behind them

Even on flat urban lots, the principles apply: slow, spread, and sink water rather than letting it run off. Every drop of water captured on-site is a drop that does not need to be imported.
