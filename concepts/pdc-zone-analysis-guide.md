# Zone Analysis Guide

Permaculture zones are an analytical graphic tool used to understand the impacts on a site and to help design. Zones rank areas by frequency and intensity of human interaction, guiding the placement of elements based on how often they need attention.

## Zones Are a Tool, Not Reality

Zones are something you draw on paper -- they do not really exist on the ground. By drawing permaculture zones onto a base map or design, it helps the designer understand the site. Zones are a rough estimate of impacted areas, not the literal truth. While zones appear as lines on paper, in reality they will not have sharp lines of delineation.

## Important Distinctions

### Not Urban Zoning
Permaculture zones must not be confused with "zoning" used in modern urban planning (residential zoning, commercial zoning, etc.). These have nothing to do with permaculture.

### Not Concentric Circles
When described for beginners, zones are often represented as concentric circles for demonstration and explanation purposes only. In reality, zones will almost never be concentric circles. They can be any shape but will usually be big oblong or globular areas. The shape depends on the site's topography, access paths, and existing features.

### Not All Zones Required
Many novice permaculture designers believe every zone analysis has to have all five zones. This is not true:
- In dense urban environments, a typical home with a yard may only have Zone 0 and Zone 1
- It may have a Zone 2 and 3, but rarely Zone 4
- It will NEVER have a Zone 5 (true wilderness)
- Larger urban neighborhoods may have up to Zone 4

### Before Planning Anything
Check with the city or county for local zoning ordinances to find out what is and is not legal to do on the site.

## The Five Zones

### Zone 0: Permanent Structures

The zone for permanent built structures, usually the house or other buildings. This zone is considered to be permanently impacted because the building will never move. It is often considered ecologically inert. However, with systems such as green roofs and living walls, some of the ecological activity of this zone can be remediated by a skillful designer.

### Zone 1: Daily Impact

The area impacted by humans on a daily basis, directly around Zone 0 and around any other place where humans come and go. Zone 1 is where the designer should place systems that either require or will attract daily human impact.

**Typical elements**: Gardens, herb spirals, salad greens, seedling nurseries, compost bins close to the kitchen, clotheslines, parking, entry paths, sitting areas, cob ovens, play spaces.

### Zone 2: Weekly Impact

Systems that require or attract human impact only a few times a week at most. Zone 2 systems should be fairly self-supporting, requiring only occasional human intervention or occasional harvesting, or receiving sporadic human attention.

**Typical elements**: Beehives, compost piles (larger), food-producing bushes and small fruit trees, pump houses for water systems, poultry houses, berry patches, intensively managed orchards.

### Zone 3: Monthly or Seasonal Impact

Zone 3 is impacted roughly monthly or seasonally. It is less intensively managed and more self-maintaining than Zones 1 and 2.

**Typical elements**: Orchards, crops that are seasonally harvested, pastured livestock, ceremonial sweat lodges, large-scale composting areas, main crop fields, grazing paddocks.

### Zone 4: Minimal Impact

The area where human impact is present but minimal. Zone 4 may have some light impact such as bird watching, camping, hiking, or wildcrafting, and some sustainable forestry practices such as thinning and coppicing. Zone 4 has no human-built structures or systems. This is where humans interact with the natural world as is.

**Additional possibilities**: Areas that were heavily impacted by humans in the past but now lie fallow (brown fields, old logging areas), wilderness corridors cutting through urbanized areas, or wooded areas within suburbs.

### Zone 5: Wilderness

True wilderness -- no structures, no roads, no human management. This zone exists for its own sake and for the ecological services it provides (wildlife habitat, water purification, genetic diversity, carbon sequestration, seed sources). Zone 5 is left entirely to natural processes.

## The Relative Location Principle

Understanding zones is important to designing the "relative location" of elements. The key question is: how often does a human need to visit this element? Place frequently-visited elements close to Zone 0 and infrequently-visited elements farther away.

## Zone Interactions with Sectors

Zones and [[pdc-sector-analysis-guide]] work together:
- Zones determine WHERE (based on frequency of use)
- Sectors determine HOW (based on directional forces)
- An element's placement must satisfy both zone and sector requirements

For example, a greenhouse needs daily attention (Zone 1) and maximum winter sun (south-facing sector). Place it in Zone 1 within the winter sun sector.

## Urban Zone Considerations

Urban designers must be especially careful:
- Don't force all five zones where they don't fit
- Recognize that Zone 5 may be a nearby park rather than on-site wilderness
- Access paths may create non-concentric zone shapes
- Apartment buildings have different zone patterns than standalone houses
- Community gardens can serve as shared Zone 1-2 for multiple households

## Drawing Zones on the Base Map

1. Start with Zone 0 (the house/structures)
2. Work outward based on actual paths of movement and areas of activity
3. Use the shapes that naturally form from topography, access, and existing features
4. Remember that zones are tools, not rigid boundaries
5. The resulting map guides element placement in the design
