# Slope Measurement and Stability

Slope measurement and stability are foundational topics in permaculture land design. Understanding how to measure, interpret, and work with slopes is critical for safe earthworks, road construction, water management, and site planning. This concept covers measurement systems, material stability, slope profiles, hazard assessment, and practical design guidelines.

## Slope Measurement Systems

Different professions express slope in different units, and designers must be fluent in converting between them:

- **Degrees (°):** Measured as the angle from the horizontal plane. Common in general surveying and topographic maps.
- **Percentage (%):** The ratio of vertical rise to horizontal run, expressed as a percentage. Widely used by engineers and road builders. A 5% slope means 5 units of rise per 100 units of run.
- **Proportions/Ratios:** Expressed as a ratio of horizontal distance to vertical drop (e.g., 1:500). The standard notation among hydrologists and drainage engineers. Irrigation drains typically range from 1:500 to 1:2000 depending on the required flow rate and soil conditions.

### Conversion Reference

| Percentage | Approximate Degrees |
|------------|-------------------|
| 1%         | 0.6°              |
| 3%         | 1.7°              |
| 5%         | 2.9° (≈3°)        |
| 10%        | 5.7°              |
| 15%        | 8.5°              |
| 20%        | 11.3°             |
| 50%        | 26.6°             |
| 100%       | 45°               |

As a rule of thumb, for small angles, the percentage figure is roughly 1.75 times the degree figure. The approximation of 5% ≈ 3° is a commonly used field conversion.

## Irrigation Grades by Soil Type

The appropriate grade for irrigation channels and drains depends on soil texture:

- **Sandy soils:** Steeper grades needed to maintain flow velocity and prevent percolation losses (roughly 1:200 to 1:500).
- **Loam soils:** Moderate grades (roughly 1:500 to 1:1000).
- **Clay soils:** Gentler grades since clay carries water with less seepage (roughly 1:1000 to 1:2000).

Steep grades on erosive soils cause scouring; flat grades on heavy soils cause siltation and waterlogging.

## Angle of Rest

The angle of rest is the steepest angle at which a material can be piled without slumping. This varies by material composition and moisture content:

| Material            | Ratio (H:V) | Approx. Angle |
|---------------------|-------------|---------------|
| Gravel              | 1 : 1.5     | ~34°          |
| Free-drained clay   | 1 : 2       | ~27°          |
| Wet clay            | 1 : 3       | ~18°          |
| Dry sand            | 1 : 1       | 45°           |
| Rock rubble         | 1 : 1 to 1 : 1.5 | 34°–45°   |

Wet clay has a significantly lower angle of rest than free-drained clay, which is why landslides are far more common during or after heavy rainfall on clay slopes. These values are essential for designing embankments, dams, swales, and road cuts.

## Slope Profiles: Concave vs. Straight

In nature, most stable slopes are **concave** — steeper at the top and flattening toward the base. This profile:

- Distributes gravitational forces more evenly
- Slows water velocity as it moves downhill, reducing erosion
- Accumulates soil and organic matter at the toe
- Provides natural benching for cultivation and access

**Straight (linear) slopes** are less stable because water accelerates uniformly, reaching maximum erosive velocity at the bottom. They are also harder to traverse, cultivate, and vegetate.

In road construction and earthworks, **bench-cut concave curves** are preferred. The road is cut into the slope with a curve that starts steep and flattens, mimicking natural geomorphology. This reduces maintenance, improves drainage, and is far more resilient to slumping.

## Slump and Mass Movement

**Slump** occurs when a coherent mass of soil moves downslope along a curved failure surface. It is the most common form of slope failure in permaculture sites and is triggered by:

- Saturation of the soil profile (especially in clay)
- Removal of vegetation that stabilises the slope
- Loading the top of the slope (buildings, fill)
- Undercutting the toe of the slope (road cuts, excavation)

**Mass movement** encompasses a broader range of failures including slides, flows, and creep. Recognising warning signs — tension cracks, tilted trees, bulging toes, seepage zones — is essential before undertaking earthworks or construction on sloped land.

## Hazards of Building on Unstable Hillsides

Building on steep, unstable slopes is one of the most dangerous decisions in land development. The Los Angeles canyon lands are a well-documented example: homes built on steep, geologically young slopes with unstable alluvial soils have repeatedly been destroyed by landslides, slumps, and debris flows, especially during El Niño rainfall events.

Key risks include:

- **Structural failure** from slow creep or sudden slump
- **Debris flows** that can bury structures entirely
- **Foundation undermining** from subsurface water movement
- **Increased insurance costs** and uninsurability in high-risk zones

Permaculture design strongly advocates placing settlement and infrastructure on stable, gently sloping land (0–13%) and restricting steep slopes to forestry, conservation, and carefully engineered terrace systems.

## Fire, Rainfall, and Mud Flows

In Mediterranean and similar climates, a dangerous cycle exists: **fire → hydrophobic soil → heavy rain → mud flow**.

### The Fire-Rain Sequence

1. **Wildfire** burns vegetation on steep slopes, destroying the root systems that bind soil.
2. **Intense heat** causes organic matter in the soil to volatilise, creating a **hydrophobic (water-repelling) crust** below the surface.
3. **Rainfall** cannot infiltrate through this crust and instead runs off rapidly across the surface.
4. **Surface runoff** gathers soil, ash, and debris, becoming a heavy slurry.
5. On slopes steeper than approximately 15–20°, this slurry accelerates into a **mud flow or debris flow** capable of destroying structures, blocking roads, and causing fatalities.

This sequence is a well-documented hazard in Southern California, Mediterranean Europe, southeastern Australia, and parts of South Africa.

### Mud Flow Emergency Response

- Never attempt to cross an active debris flow
- Move perpendicular to the flow path (uphill or to the side), never directly downhill
- Listen for warning sounds: rumbling, roaring, or the sound of approaching debris
- After fires, monitor weather forecasts and evacuate proactively before heavy rain events
- Design post-fire landscapes with check dams, contour swales, and rapid revegetation to slow runoff

## Slope Stability for Road Design

Roads are one of the most significant slope-modifying features on a permaculture site. Poorly designed roads are a primary cause of erosion, slumping, and water management problems.
