## Ecological Niches and Substrate Preferences

The ecological diversity of Psilocybe species reflects their evolutionary radiation into multiple niches:

**Dung-inhabiting (coprophilous) species** represent a significant ecological guild:
- [[psilocybe-coprophila]] is another dung-inhabiting species, though its neurotropic status is debated
- Copelandia species are predominantly coprophilous, including [[copelandia-tropicalis]]

**Lignicolous (wood-inhabiting) species** are particularly diverse:
- [[psilocybe-azurescens]] grows on coastal dune grasses in the Pacific Northwest
- [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] grows in rich, acidic soils in pastures and meadows, often associated with Agrostis and Festuca grasses
- [[psilocybe-mexicana]] grows in mossy trails and pastures in montane forests

**Ericaceous and alpine species** occupy specialized habitats:
- [[psilocybe-baeocystis]] is associated with coniferous forests and sphagnum bogs in the Pacific Northwest

The relationship between substrate preference and biogeography is significant. Coprophilous species tend to have wider distributions because their spores are dispersed through the gut of herbivorous mammals, which can carry them over long distances. Terricolous and lignicolous species tend to have more restricted distributions tied to specific vegetation types.

## Bluing Reaction as a Field Character

The characteristic blue bruising reaction displayed by many neurotropic Psilocybe species results from the oxidation of psilocin and related phenolic compounds. When the fungal tissue is damaged, enzymatic activity converts psilocybin to psilocin, which then oxidizes to form blue-colored quinones. This reaction serves as the most practical field identification character for distinguishing neurotropic from non-neurotropic Psilocybe species.

However, the bluing reaction is neither necessary nor sufficient for identifying neurotropic activity:

- Several confirmed neurotropic species do not blue at all, including some Panaeolus species and [[psilocybe-azurescens]] and [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] often shows only slight or delayed bluing
- Some non-neurotropic mushrooms can also develop blue-green discoloration due to unrelated pigments (notably some Boletus species with xerocomoid pigmentation)
- Environmental factors including temperature, humidity, and age of the fruiting body affect bluing intensity

Among Psilocybe, the bluing species are concentrated in Sections Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae, Cubensies, Mexicanae, Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae, and Zapotecorum. Species in other sections may or may not blue, making microscopic examination of spore morphology and cystidia essential for accurate identification.
