# Fungal Biosorption of Heavy Metals by Filamentous Fungi

Biosorption by filamentous fungi represents a promising approach for removing heavy metals from contaminated effluents. Dead fungal biomass offers several advantages over live systems: no nutrient requirements, no toxicity limitations, easy regeneration, and predictable performance.

## Biosorbent Selection Criteria

The characteristics determining suitability of fungal biomass for biosorption are:

1. Biosorption efficiency and metal uptake capacity/affinity for metal ions

2. Specificity for metal ions

3. Flocculation and sedimentation efficiency

4. Desorption efficiency

5. Preservation of biosorptive capacity

Fungal melanins of filamentous fungi contribute significantly to metal removal. The cell wall, particularly chitin and chitosan components, serves as the predominant target for metal binding.

## Mechanisms of Metal Binding

Fungal biosorption of heavy metals involves several physicochemical mechanisms operating at the cell wall surface:

- Ion exchange: Metal ions displace lighter ions (H+, Na+, K+) from cell wall binding sites

- Complexation: Metal ions form coordination complexes with functional groups such as carboxyl, amino, sulfhydryl, and phosphate groups

- Surface adsorption: Physical adsorption onto the cell wall surface driven by van der Waals forces

- Precipitation: Metal ions may precipitate on the cell wall as hydroxides or other insoluble compounds

The cell wall composition varies among fungal species and includes chitin, chitosan, glucans, mannans, and proteins, all of which provide binding sites for metal ions.

## Metal Uptake Performance by Species

### Mucorales (Highest Priority -- Uranium)

Fungi belonging to the order Mucorales are excellent biosorbents of uranium. Notable uptake capacities (mg/g dry weight):

- Mucor javanicus: 343 mg/g UO2(II)

- Rhizopus arrhizus: 170 mg/g UO2(II)

- Rhizopus oryzae: 227 mg/g UO2(II)

- Neurospora sitophila: 285 mg/g UO2(II)

### Lead Biosorption

- Aspergillus niger: 580 mg/g Pb(II)

- Phellinus badius: 170 mg/g Pb(II)

- Rhizopus nigricans: 68 mg/g Pb(II) at 500 mg/l

- Penicillium chrysogenum: 116 mg/g Pb(II)

### Chromium Biosorption

- Rhizopus nigricans (APTS-treated biomass): 212 mg/g Cr(VI)

- Rhizopus nigricans (PEI-treated biomass): 160 mg/g Cr(VI)

- Mucor miehei: 1.15 mM/g Cr(III) at pH 4.0

### Multi-Metal Systems

Mucor rouxii (dead biomass) showed markedly higher uptake than live biomass for multiple metals simultaneously: Pb(II) 53.75 mg/g, Ni(II) 20.49 mg/g, Cd(II) 20.31 mg/g, Zn(II) 53.85 mg/g.

## Metal Enrichment Selectivity

Metal enrichment selectivity by Mucor hiemalis follows the order: Cr(III) > Cu(II) > Pb(II) > Ag(I) > Al(III) > Co(II) > Zn(II) > Ni(II) > Fe(II) > Mo(V) > Cd(II) > Cs(I) > Cr2O7(2-) > CrO4(2-) > VO3(-)

## Biomass Pretreatment and Regeneration

Common metal eluants for regeneration include:

- **Counterions**: Sodium/potassium/calcium salts (chlorides, carbonates, bicarbonates, hydroxides) at 0.1-1 N for 1-2 hours

- **Proton exchangers**: Mineral acids (HCl, H2SO4, HClO4, HNO3) at 0.01-1 N for 1-2 hours

- **Complexing agents**: EDTA, nitriloacetic acid at 0.01-0.1 N for 2 hours

- **Heat treatment**: Boiling for 5 minutes

- **Combinations**: Basic followed by acidic treatment or vice versa

Pretreatment with alcohol, acetone, and HCl improved uptake capacity of Rhizopus nigricans. Boiling of Saccharomyces uvarum biomass enhanced lead adsorption. More than 90% of biosorbed metal was eluted, equivalent to a 25-fold increase in copper concentration.

## Competition in Multi-Metal Effluents

In biosorption with multiple metal species, competition exists for limited binding sites. In column experiments, affinities and concentrations of metals play important roles. Metals in trace amounts may reach breakthrough before others. The criteria for specific metal breakthrough in multimetal solutions remain unexplained.

## Metal Ion Resistance

Some fungi survive in environments with excessive metal ion concentrations and develop metal resistance. Metallothioneins (MTs) are gene-encoded cysteine-rich metal-binding proteins found in fungi. Several metals induce phytochelatin synthesis, and intracellular chelation is an established mechanism in metal tolerance. Glutathione, metal-binding peptides, and sulfide ions play roles in these protective mechanisms.

## Reactor Types for Biosorption

Several reactor configurations have been applied for fungal biosorption:

- Batch stirred-tank reactors: Simple operation, useful for small-scale treatment

- Continuous-flow stirred-tank reactors: Better for continuous effluent treatment

- Fixed packed-bed reactors: Most common for commercial applications; high surface area contact

- Immobilized reactors: Enhanced stability and reusability of biomass

Mathematical models for process development include Langmuir, Freundlich, and Redlich-Peterson isotherms to predict sorption performance under different operating conditions.

## Applications in Environmental Remediation

Fungal biosorption has been applied to treat mineral-processing wastewater, electroplating effluents, and contaminated groundwater. The use of dead biomass is established as a substitute for ion-exchange resins, offering cost advantages. Understanding translocation within the mycelium is essential for enhanced metal sequestration. The introduction of filamentous fungi into contaminated soils enables absorption and translocation of metals, which eventually concentrate in basidiocarps that can be harvested.
