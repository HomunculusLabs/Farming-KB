# Fungal Wood Decay Types: White Rot, Brown Rot, and Soft Rot

The decomposition of wood by fungi occurs through three principal decay types: white rot, brown rot, and soft rot. Each type is characterized by a distinct pattern of wood component removal, a specific suite of degradative enzymes, and a different group of predominantly basidiomycete or ascomycete fungi. Understanding these decay types is fundamental to forest ecology, carbon cycling, and the management of wood in service.

## Wood Composition

Wood is a complex composite material consisting primarily of cellulose (40 to 50 percent), hemicellulose (20 to 30 percent), and lignin (20 to 30 percent), along with smaller amounts of extractives, pectins, and ash minerals. Cellulose is a linear polymer of glucose that provides tensile strength. Hemicelluloses are branched heteropolymers of various sugars that form a matrix around the cellulose microfibrils. Lignin is a complex, irregular phenolic polymer that provides rigidity and resistance to microbial attack.

The relative proportions of these components vary among wood types, with softwoods (gymnosperms) generally having higher lignin content than hardwoods (angiosperms). The chemical composition of wood, particularly the lignin-to-cellulose ratio, influences the rate and pattern of fungal decay.

## White Rot

White rot is the most complete form of wood decay and is caused primarily by basidiomycete fungi. White rot fungi degrade all major wood components, including lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose, though the relative rates of degradation of these components can vary among species. In some white rot species, lignin is removed preferentially, leaving a bleached, cellulose-rich residue, while in others, all components are degraded simultaneously.

The key enzymes of white rot decay are lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidase (MnP), and laccase, which are collectively termed ligninolytic enzymes. Lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase are heme-containing peroxidases that require hydrogen peroxide as a co-substrate and can oxidize the non-phenolic components of lignin, which constitute approximately 90 percent of the lignin polymer. Laccase, a copper-containing oxidase, can oxidize phenolic lignin components and, in the presence of redox mediator molecules, can also attack non-phenolic lignin.

Additional enzymes produced by white rot fungi include cellobiose dehydrogenase, aryl alcohol oxidase, glyoxal oxidase, and glucose oxidase, which generate hydrogen peroxide and other reactive oxygen species that support the peroxidase enzymes. The synergistic action of this complex enzyme system enables the oxidative depolymerization of lignin, a feat that few other organisms can achieve.

White rot fungi produce a bleached appearance in decaying wood because the removal of brown lignin reveals the white cellulose underneath. The decayed wood becomes soft, fibrous, and often lighter in color than the original wood. Examples of white rot fungi include species of Phanerochaete, Trametes, Pleurotus, and Resinicium.

## Brown Rot

Brown rot is caused by a group of basidiomycete fungi that selectively remove cellulose and hemicellulose while leaving lignin largely unmodified. The result is a brown, cracked, cubically fractured wood residue that consists primarily of modified lignin. Brown rot decay is particularly important in coniferous forests, where brown rot fungi are major decomposers of softwood timber.

Brown rot fungi employ a non-enzymatic mechanism for cellulose degradation that involves the generation of hydroxyl radicals through the Fenton reaction. In this reaction, reduced iron (Fe2+) reacts with hydrogen peroxide to produce hydroxyl radicals, which are extremely reactive oxidants that can cleave cellulose chains non-specifically. The fungus generates hydrogen peroxide through extracellular oxidases and reduces iron through secreted reductants.

The initial phase of brown rot involves a rapid depolymerization of cellulose that occurs before the fungus has extensively colonized the wood. This non-enzymatic depolymerization creates a diffusible sugar pool that the fungus can then absorb. The subsequent decay phase involves enzymatic hydrolysis of the partially depolymerized cellulose by cellulases and hemicellulases.

Brown rot fungi do not produce lignin peroxidases or manganese peroxidases, and their ability to degrade lignin is limited to minor modifications such as demethylation. The lignin that remains after brown rot decay is chemically modified but largely intact, giving the decayed wood its characteristic brown color.

## Soft Rot

Soft rot is caused primarily by ascomycete fungi and is characterized by the formation of cavities or erosion within the secondary cell wall of wood. Soft rot is particularly important in environments where moisture and temperature conditions are unfavorable for basidiomycete activity, such as in water-saturated wood, in arid environments, and in wood exposed to marine conditions.

There are two types of soft rot. Type 1 soft rot is characterized by the formation of cylindrical cavities within the secondary cell wall, aligned with the cellulose microfibril direction. Type 2 soft rot involves a more generalized erosion of the cell wall from the lumen outward. Both types are caused by the production of cellulases and, in some cases, laccase enzymes.

Soft rot fungi do not produce lignin peroxidases or manganese peroxidases, limiting their ability to degrade lignin. However, as demonstrated by the saltmarsh cordgrass decomposition system, some soft rot ascomycetes can achieve significant lignocellulose breakdown through laccase-mediated mechanisms involving redox mediator molecules. Type 2 soft rot is morphologically very similar to white rot.

## Ecological Significance

The distinction between white rot and brown rot has profound implications for carbon cycling in forest ecosystems. White rot, which degrades lignin completely, results in the release of most of the carbon in wood as carbon dioxide. Brown rot, which leaves lignin behind, contributes to the accumulation of modified lignin residues in soil organic matter. The relative importance of white rot and brown rot in a forest ecosystem influences the rate of carbon turnover and the composition of soil organic matter.

The evolution of white rot basidiomycetes, which are the only organisms capable of substantial lignin degradation, is considered one of the most significant events in the evolutionary history of terrestrial ecosystems. The ability to degrade lignin opened up the vast reservoir of woody plant material for decomposition, fundamentally altering the global carbon cycle.

## Wood Decay and Nutrient Cycling

Wood has a very high carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, typically ranging from 300:1 to 1000:1. During fungal decomposition, nitrogen and other mineral nutrients are immobilized into fungal biomass, often imported from the surrounding soil. The duration of this immobilization phase can be substantial, particularly for brown rot fungi that may take years to decades to fully decompose a large woody resource.

The immobilization of nutrients during wood decay creates competition between decomposer fungi and plant roots for available soil nutrients. In practical forestry, this competition between wood-decomposing fungi and second-rotation tree crops has led to the use of burning or removal of post-harvest woody residues to reduce nutrient immobilization.

## Ecological Consequences

The distinction between white-rot and brown-rot decay has major implications for forest carbon cycling. White-rot fungi decompose lignin completely, releasing all carbon from wood, while brown-rot fungi primarily target cellulose and hemicellulose, leaving modified lignin residues that contribute to long-term soil carbon storage. In boreal forests, brown-rot fungi are particularly important for forming humus and building soil organic matter.

## Enzymatic Machinery

White-rot fungi produce an array of extracellular enzymes that enable them to decompose all major components of wood. The lignin-degrading enzyme system includes lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidase (MnP), and laccase, which work together in a complementary fashion to oxidize the complex phenylpropane units of lignin. These enzymes require hydrogen peroxide as a co-substrate and generate highly reactive radical intermediates that attack lignin non-specifically.

Brown-rot fungi employ a fundamentally different strategy. Rather than producing the full complement of ligninolytic enzymes, brown-rot fungi use a Fenton chemistry-based system. They generate extracellular hydrogen peroxide and reduce ferric iron to ferrous iron, producing hydroxyl radicals through the Fenton reaction. These hydroxyl radicals depolymerize cellulose and hemicellulose through an oxidative mechanism that does not require direct enzymatic contact with the substrate. This non-enzymatic system is highly efficient at depolymerizing cellulose while leaving lignin largely chemically modified but not mineralized.

## Representative Species and Their Decay Patterns

Different fungal species within each decay category show distinct preferences for wood type (hardwood vs softwood) and decay strategy:
- **White rot:** *Phanerochaete chrysosporium* preferentially degrades lignin, leaving cellulose-rich residue (selective delignification). *Trametes versicolor* simultaneously degrades all components (simultaneous rot). *Pleurotus ostreatus* (oyster mushroom) shows intermediate behavior. *Irpex lacteus* is notable for degrading both hardwoods and softwoods.
- **Brown rot:** *Serpula lacrymans* (the dry rot fungus, a major building pathogen) and *Gloeophyllum trabeum* are the most studied species. *Fomitopsis pinicola* (red-belted polypore) is a common brown rotter of conifer logs in temperate forests. *Postia placenta* has the smallest known genome among brown rot fungi, having lost many cellulase genes through evolution, relying almost entirely on Fenton chemistry.
- **Soft rot:** *Chaetomium globosum* and *Humicola insolens* are common soft rot ascomycetes. These fungi are the primary decomposers of wood in extreme environments (tropical marine, hot deserts, Antarctic soils) where basidiomycetes cannot survive.

## Decay Detection and Assessment

Wood decay is detected through several methods in both forestry and building management contexts. Visual inspection reveals characteristic color changes, cracking patterns (cubical fracture in brown rot, fibrous texture in white rot), and fruiting body formation. Sounding (tapping with a mallet) reveals hollow or degraded areas by changes in acoustic properties. More sophisticated methods include resistance drilling (measuring torque required to advance a fine drill into wood), Pilodyn testing (measuring penetration depth of a spring-loaded pin), and ultrasonic wave propagation, which detects changes in wood density and elasticity caused by decay.

Moisture content measurement is critical since all wood decay requires water activity above approximately 0.6 (equivalent to about 20% wood moisture content for most decay fungi). Brown rot fungi are notable for their ability to transport water through rhizomorphs (dense mycelial cords), enabling them to conduct water from moisture sources to otherwise dry wood, a behavior that makes *Serpula lacrymans* particularly destructive in buildings.

## Economic Significance

Wood decay causes enormous economic losses worldwide. In buildings, fungal decay is responsible for more damage than fire, termites, and floods combined. The dry rot fungus *Serpula lacrymans* alone causes an estimated hundreds of millions of dollars in building damage annually. In forestry, wood decay reduces timber quality and yield, with estimates suggesting that 10-30% of standing timber volume in managed forests may be affected by decay at any given time. In the shipping and utility pole industries, brown rot and soft rot cause significant service life reduction of wooden structures.

Conversely, white rot fungi have valuable industrial applications. Their lignin-degrading enzymes are used in biopulping (reducing energy requirements for paper production), biobleaching (replacing chlorine-based bleaching agents in paper manufacturing), and the bioremediation of recalcitrant organic pollutants. The edible mushroom industry also depends on white rot fungi, as *Pleurotus*, *Lentinula* (shiitake), and *Flammulina* (enoki) are all white rot decomposers cultivated on lignocellulosic substrates.

## Moisture Requirements and Decay Rates

The rate of wood decay is strongly influenced by moisture content, with each decay type having different moisture optima:
- White rot fungi typically require wood moisture content of 40-80% (wet basis) for active decay. *Trametes versicolor* thrives at 60-70% moisture. Below the fiber saturation point (approximately 30%), enzymatic activity ceases.
- Brown rot fungi can operate at lower moisture contents than white rot, with some species like *Serpula lacrymans* capable of decay at moisture contents as low as 20-30% through water transport via rhizomorphs. This ability makes brown rot fungi particularly damaging in buildings where wood may be only intermittently moistened.
- Soft rot fungi tolerate the widest range of moisture conditions, from water-saturated wood to relatively dry wood, and are the dominant decomposers in environments with extreme or fluctuating moisture. This ecological flexibility explains their importance in marine, desert, and polar wood decay.

Temperature also significantly affects decay rates, with most wood-decay fungi showing optimal activity between 25-30°C and greatly reduced activity below 5°C or above 40°C. However, some cold-adapted species continue to decompose wood at temperatures near 0°C, contributing to slow but continuous decomposition in boreal and alpine environments.

## Wood Decomposition in the Global Carbon Cycle

Wood decomposition by fungi is a critical component of the global carbon cycle, mediating the transfer of approximately 50-60 billion tonnes of carbon per year from dead woody material back to the atmosphere as CO2. The balance between white rot and brown rot decomposition has profound implications for long-term carbon storage:
- White rot fungi decompose lignin completely, releasing virtually all wood carbon as CO2 within years to decades, depending on wood size and environmental conditions. This rapid carbon turnover contributes to atmospheric CO2 levels.
- Brown rot fungi leave modified lignin residues that are chemically resistant to further decomposition and can persist in soil for centuries to millennia. These residues form a significant component of stable soil organic matter (humus), particularly in boreal and temperate forest soils where brown rot dominates.
- The proportion of carbon transferred to stable soil pools versus released as CO2 depends on the decay type, wood chemistry, climate, and soil conditions. In boreal forests, brown rot may contribute more to long-term soil carbon storage than all other decomposition pathways combined.

Climate change may alter this balance by shifting fungal community composition. Warming temperatures may favor brown rot fungi in some boreal regions (accelerating carbon release from currently slow-decaying wood) or expand the range of white rot fungi into higher latitudes (potentially increasing decomposition rates of previously recalcitrant wood). Understanding and predicting these feedbacks is an active area of global change biology.

## Evolution of White Rot

The evolution of lignin-degrading white rot fungi represents one of the most consequential events in the history of terrestrial life. Molecular clock analyses suggest that white rot basidiomycetes evolved approximately 290-300 million years ago, coinciding with the end of the Carboniferous period. This timing is significant because the Carboniferous is characterized by vast accumulations of undegraded woody material that formed the world's major coal deposits. The emergence of efficient lignin-degrading fungi is hypothesized to have ended the conditions that allowed coal formation on such a massive scale.

Comparative genomics has identified class II peroxidases (including lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase) as the key enzymes that distinguish white rot fungi from other fungi. Gene loss events in brown rot fungi, which lost class II peroxidases while retaining the Fenton chemistry system, demonstrate that the transition from white rot to brown rot involved genome reduction rather than innovation. The independent evolution of brown rot from white rot ancestors occurred at least five times, making it one of the most striking examples of convergent evolution driven by lifestyle adaptation in the fungal kingdom.
