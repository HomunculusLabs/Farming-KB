# Integrated Farm Design

updated: 2026-04-18

Integrated farm design is the application of permaculture principles to create whole-farm systems where every element supports every other element. The goal is a farm that functions as an ecosystem -- producing food, fiber, and fuel while building soil, conserving water, enhancing biodiversity, and generating income. The integrated farm is not a collection of separate enterprises; it is a single, interconnected system.

## The Whole Systems Approach

### Energy Cycling

In a conventional farm, energy flows in one direction: inputs (fertilizer, fuel, feed) come in, products go out, and waste is generated. In an integrated farm, energy cycles within the system:

- Animal manure becomes compost for crops
- Crop residues become animal feed or mulch
- Kitchen and garden waste feeds chickens or compost worms
- Water flows through multiple uses (house to garden to livestock to orchard)
- Wood from forestry provides fuel, building material, and mushroom substrate
- Leguminous trees fix nitrogen for surrounding crops

### Multiple Yields

Every element should produce multiple yields. A chicken, for example, provides:
- Eggs
- Meat
- Manure for fertilizer
- Pest control (eating insects and weed seeds)
- Tillage (scratching and turning compost)
- Heat (in a greenhouse chicken coop)

A dam provides:
- Water storage for irrigation
- Fire protection
- Aquaculture (fish and water plants)
- Wildlife habitat
- Recreation
- Microclimate creation (moderating nearby temperatures)
- Duck habitat

### Multiple Functions for Each Element

Each function should be supported by multiple elements. Water security, for example, should not depend on a single source. Design for:
- Multiple water sources (dams, wells, rainwater tanks, springs)
- Multiple food sources (vegetables, fruits, grains, eggs, meat, fish)
- Multiple income streams (direct sales, value-added products, education, tourism)
- Multiple fertility sources (compost, green manures, animal manure, nitrogen-fixing plants)

## Integrating Animal Systems

### Poultry Integration

Chickens are one of the most integrative elements on a farm:
- **Chicken tractors**: Mobile coops moved daily over garden beds to prepare soil, eat pests, and add manure
- **Free-range in orchards**: Chickens control codling moth and other orchard pests while fertilizing the trees
- **Deep litter system**: Chickens in a stationary coop over a deep bed of carbon material (straw, leaves, wood chips) that composts in place
- **Following ruminants**: Chickens follow cattle or sheep through pastures, spreading manure and controlling fly larvae

### Ruminant Integration

Cattle, sheep, and goats can be integrated with:
- **Pasture cropping**: Direct-seeding crops into perennial pastures
- **Silvopasture**: Grazing under trees (timber, fruit, or nut)
- **Manure management**: Collecting manure for composting or directing grazing to fertilize specific areas
- **Rotational grazing**: Planned grazing that stimulates pasture growth and cycles nutrients

### Pig Integration

Pigs can be used for:
- **Land clearing**: Pigs effectively clear vegetation and root up soil for garden preparation
- **Compost turning**: Pigs will turn and mix compost piles
- **Manure production**: Pig manure is high in nutrients for composting
- **Forage harvesting**: Pigs can harvest crops (fallen fruit, root vegetables) that would otherwise go to waste

## Integrating Crop Systems

### Polyculture Design

Instead of monoculture, design mixed plantings that:
- Fill different root zones (deep-rooted with shallow-rooted)
- Fix nitrogen while others need it (legumes with grains)
- Provide shade while others need it (tall crops with understory crops)
- Attract beneficial insects while others need pest control
- Produce at different times of year (succession planting)

### Food Forest Design

A food forest mimics the structure of a natural forest with up to seven layers:
1. **Canopy**: Tall fruit and nut trees
2. **Understory**: Shorter fruit trees
3. **Shrub layer**: Berry bushes
4. **Herbaceous layer**: Herbs and small plants
5. **Ground cover**: Spreading food plants
6. **Root layer**: Root vegetables
7. **Vine layer**: Climbing plants through all layers

By stacking food production vertically, a food forest produces the same amount of food on far less surface area than conventional row cropping, leaving the rest of the area open to just be wild.

## Integrating Water Systems

Design water to flow through the farm in a connected series:

1. **Catchment**: Roofs, dams, swales, and contour banks capture rainwater
2. **Storage**: Ponds, tanks, and soil moisture hold water for dry periods
3. **Distribution**: Gravity-fed pipes, channels, and swales move water where needed
4. **Use**: House, garden, livestock, orchard, crops -- each in sequence
5. **Recharge**: Excess water infiltrates to groundwater through swales and contour planting
6. **Greywater**: House water flows through biological treatment (reed beds, constructed wetlands) before irrigating crops

## Farm Forestry Integration

Trees integrated throughout the farm provide:
- **Shelter**: Windbreaks for crops, livestock, and buildings
- **Fertility**: Nitrogen-fixing trees (leguminous species) for adjacent crops
- **Fodder**: Tree leaves and pods for animal feed (especially during dry periods)
- **Timber**: Long-term production of building materials and firewood
- **Water management**: Tree roots increase infiltration and reduce erosion
- **Biodiversity**: Native tree corridors connect wildlife habitat patches

## The Designer's Role

The integrated farm designer must:
- Think in systems, not in separate enterprises
- Understand the connections between elements
- Design for energy cycling and nutrient cycling
- Plan for succession and evolution over time
- Start small, observe, and adapt
- Design for redundancy and resilience
