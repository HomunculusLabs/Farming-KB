# Glomalin and Soil Aggregation in AACT Systems

## Overview

Glomalin is a sticky, glycoprotein-like substance produced by soil organisms—most notably [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF), but also by a wide range of bacteria and other fungi—that plays a fundamental role in binding soil particles together into stable aggregates. In the context of Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) and soil foodweb management, glomalin [[ingham-glomalin-soil-aggregation-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-glue]] represents one of the key mechanisms by which [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] create and maintain the soil structure that supports healthy plant growth.

## What Is Glomalin?

Glomalin was initially identified in research on vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizae (VAM), now called arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF). Early work assumed that glomalin was exclusively a product [[query-what-are-the-different-types-of-mycorrhizal-fungi]]. However, Dr. Elaine Ingham and other soil microbiologists have recognized that many organisms in soil produce similar glues and sticky compounds. The term "glomalin" has become a general descriptor for a family of compounds that share the ability to stick things together. (see [[aact-microbial-chelation-of-minerals-ingham]]).

Key characteristics of glomalin and related glues:

- They are produced by mycorrhizal fungi, saprophytic fungi, and many bacteria
- They function as biological binding agents that aggregate soil particles
- They contribute significantly to soil carbon pools and are remarkably stable
- They are found on hyphal surfaces and in soil where organisms have been active
- Their production is stimulated by healthy, diverse microbial communities

## The Role of Glomalin in Soil Structure

### Aggregate Formation

Soil aggregates are clumps of sand, silt, clay, and organic matter held together by biological, chemical, and physical forces. Glomalin acts as one of the primary biological "glues" in this process. When fungal hyphae grow through soil, they deposit glomalin along their length and at branch points. This sticky material binds mineral particles together, creating microaggregates that can then be further stabilized into macroaggregates by roots, fungal networks, and other biological agents. (see [[comparison-rhizosphere-ecology-vs-mycorrhizal-fungi]]).

### Water Stability

Aggregates bound with glomalin demonstrate enhanced water stability—they resist breakdown when wetted. This is critical for maintaining soil porosity, infiltration, and resistance to erosion. Soils with high glomalin content drain better, hold moisture more effectively, and resist compaction.

### Carbon Sequestration

Glomalin is remarkably resistant to decomposition, with residence times measured in years to decades. This makes it an important contributor to stable soil organic carbon pools. The more fungal activity in a soil system, the more glomalin is produced and the more carbon is sequestered in stable forms.

## Glomalin and AACT Applications

### Stimulating Glomalin Production

AACT can contribute to glomalin production in several ways:

1. **Introducing fungal biomass**: Well-made AACT containing significant fungal biomass delivers living fungi and their glomalin-coated hyphae directly to soil and plant surfaces
2. **Feeding existing fungi**: The food resources in ACT (humic acids, fish hydrolysate, kelp) feed existing fungal populations, stimulating them to grow and produce more glomalin
3. **Supporting mycorrhizal colonization**: By improving overall soil biology, AACT creates conditions favorable for mycorrhizal fungi to colonize plant roots and begin producing glomalin

### The Fungal Connection

Fungi are the primary producers of glomalin in most soils. Dr. Ingham emphasizes that getting fungal biomass into soils is essential for building soil structure:

- Saprophytic fungi produce glues as they decompose organic matter
- Mycorrhizal fungi produce glomalin along their hyphal networks
- Bacterial glues complement fungal glomalin in binding finer particles
- The combination of fungal and bacterial glues creates the most stable aggregates

### Bacterial vs. Fungal Glues

While mycorrhizal fungi are famous for glomalin production, bacteria also produce sticky extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) that help them attach to soil particles. These bacterial glues are particularly important for binding clay particles and creating the microaggregates that serve as the building blocks for larger soil structure.

The interaction between [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] glues creates a hierarchy of aggregation:
- Bacterial EPS binds clay and silt particles into microaggregates
- Fungal hyphae and glomalin bind microaggregates into macroaggregates
- Roots and macrofauna further stabilize these structures

## Factors Affecting Glomalin Production

### Soil Biology

The diversity and activity of soil organisms directly affect glomalin levels:
- Higher fungal biomass correlates with higher glomalin
- Diverse fungal communities produce more varied glues
- [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] that graze on fungi stimulate new fungal growth
- Microarthropods help distribute fungal hyphae and their glues through soil

### Soil Chemistry

- Calcium availability influences fungal growth and glomalin deposition
- pH affects the production and stability of glues
- Excessive nitrogen can suppress mycorrhizal colonization and glomalin production
- Balanced mineral nutrition supports the organisms that produce glomalin

### Management Practices

Practices that support glomalin production include:
- Minimizing tillage (which disrupts fungal networks)
