# Psilocybin Mushroom Cultures in Mesoamerica

Henry Munn's chapter "The Mushrooms of Language" in Harner's "Hallucinogens and Shamanism" provides an extraordinary account of psilocybin mushroom use among the Mazatec people of Oaxaca, Mexico. Based on fieldwork in Huautla de Jimenez, Munn documents the discourse of Mazatec medicine men and women during mushroom sessions, revealing the mushrooms as agents of communication, healing, and existential insight. This chapter stands as one of the richest single-source descriptions of an indigenous psychedelic practice available in the anthropological literature.

## The Mazatec Context

The Mazatec people, self-described as the "People of the Deer," inhabit the mountainous terrain of Oaxaca, Mexico. Their society was undergoing significant transition during Munn's research period. Until recently isolated from the modern world, the Indians "lived in their mountains as people lived in the neolithic." Roads had been blasted through rock to connect the community with the society beyond. The younger generation lives between "two times": the timeless, cyclical time of recurrence of the People of the Deer and the time of progress, change and development of modern Mexico.

The children are "people of opposites": just as they speak two languages, Mazatec and Spanish, they inhabit two worlds. The mushrooms serve as a bridge between these worlds, and the medicine woman's discourse encompasses both the ancient and the modern, the indigenous and the national.

## The Mushrooms as Medicine and Language

The Mazatec do not call the mushrooms "mushrooms." They call them "prayer," "well-being," "wisdom," and "the holy ones." For the shamaness, the mushroom experience is synonymous with language, with communication on behalf of her people with the supernatural forces of the universe. The mushrooms are described not as hallucinogens or drugs but as agents of plenitude, perception, insight, and knowledge: "They aren't called mushrooms. They are called prayer. They are called well-being. They are called wisdom. They are there with the Virgin, Our Mother, the Nativity."

Munn highlights the striking inversion between indigenous and modern understandings: "It is paradoxical that the rediscovery of such chemicals should have related their effects to madness and pejoratively called them drugs, when the shamans who used them spoke of them as medicines and said from their experience that the metamorphosis they produced put one into communication with the spirit." The shamaness insists the mushrooms are not an anachronistic vestige: "their power to awaken consciousness and cure existential ills is not any the less relevant now than it was in the past."

## The Mushroom Session: Structure and Content

The medicine woman's discourse during a mushroom session is described as comprehensive and continuous. She "goes on talking and talking, non-stop; there are lulls when her voice slows down, fades out almost to a whisper; then come rushes of inspiration, moments of intense speech; she yawns great yawns, laughs with jubilation, claps her hands in time to her interminable singsong." The mushroom experience lasts approximately six hours, and it is usually impossible to sleep until dawn.

### Physiological Self-Knowledge
A distinctive feature is the mushrooms' capacity to teach "the workings of the organism." Munn writes: "There is a very definite physiological quality about the mushroom experience which leads the Indians to say that by a kind of visceral introspection they teach one the workings of the organism: it is as if the system were projected before one into a vision of the heart, the liver, lungs, genitals, and stomach."

### Landscape and Spirit Geography
The shaman maps a landscape of emotional and spiritual significance through his discourse: "Where the dirty gulch is, says. Where the dangerous gulch is, says. Where the big gulch is, says. Where the fear and the terror are, says." He situates locations by naming them, calling them into being: "Where is, says. I am, where is." Different places are inhabited by presences: "Gnome of Cold Water, says. Gnome of Clear Water, says. Gnome of Big River, says. White Gnome. Delicate Gnome."

### The Return to Everyday Consciousness
Each session concludes with a return from the visionary state: "We are going to return without mishap, along a fresh path, a path of the veins of our blood. The path of the Master of the World. Let us go in a path of happiness." The shaman calls back his spirits "from their flight into the beyond in order to become his ordinary self again."

## The Shamanic Function

The Mazatec medicine man defines his function in clear terms: "I am he who puts together, he who speaks, he who searches... I am he who fixes, he who cures the person that is sick. Herbal medicine. Remedy of the spirit. Remedy of the atmosphere of the day, says. I am he who resolves all, says." The mushrooms enable the shaman to heal the divided personality, to synthesize solutions to problems through sudden intuition, and to function as "the doctor, not only of the body, but of the self."

Munn notes a critical discrepancy between indigenous and modern psychological understandings: whereas experimental reports describe depersonalization and schizophrenia, the Mazatec shaman "considers himself endowed with the power of bringing together what is separated." The shaman is "a signifier in pursuit of significance, intent upon bringing forth the hidden, the obscure into the light of day, the lucid one, intrepid enough to realize that the greatest secrets lie in regions of danger."

The mere eating of mushrooms does not make a shaman. The Mazatec recognize that the mushrooms speak only to some--those with "a longing for awakenment, a disposition for exploring the surrealistic dimensions of existence, a poet's need to express themselves in a higher language than the average language of everyday life: for them in a very particular sense the mushrooms are the medicine of their genius."

## Curing the Lost Spirit (Susto)

Central to Mazatec practice is the concept of "fright" (susto)--the loss or alienation of the spirit. The shaman, as "hunter of extravasated spirits," uses the mushrooms to locate and retrieve the lost spirit:

"Under the effect of the mushrooms, the lost spirit is whistled for through space for the spirit is alienated, but by means of the mushrooms one can call for it with a whistle. If the person is frightened, the mushrooms know where his spirit is. They are the ones who indicate and teach where the spirit is. Thereby one can speak to it. The sick person then sees the place where his spirit stayed. He feels as if he were tied in that place. The spirit is like a trapped butterfly. When it is whistled for it arrives where one is calling it. When the spirit arrives in the person, the sick one sighs and afterwards is cleaned."

The sick person, freed from inhibitions and repressions by the "mnemonic power of the mushrooms," recalls the traumatic event, experiences catharsis, and is reintegrated. The parallels with modern psychotherapy are unmistakable.

An alternative curing method involves bargaining: the shaman becomes a "transcendental bargainer" who learns from supernatural powers how much they demand as ransom for the expropriated spirit, then undertakes to transact the deal.

## Connection to Linguistic Centers

Munn observes that the mushroom experience seems connected to "the linguistic centers of the brain." The discourse produced is characterized by extraordinary fluency, with the shaman culminating in a remarkable identification: "I am the mushroom. I am the fresh mushroom. I am the large mushroom. I am the fragrant mushroom. I am the mushroom of the spirit."

## The Spiritual Ancestor and the Hunt

The shaman recalls his spiritual ancestor, an old wise man from San Lucas who taught him the use of the mushrooms. Vestiges remain of the days when the shaman "intermediary between man, nature, and the divine was a thaumaturge who presided over fertility and the hunt." The old shaman could "transform a tree or a stone into a deer once he has exchanged its value for it with the Lord of the Mountain."

## Mazatec Shamans in the Classical Sense

Munn explicitly positions Mazatec medicine men within the classical shamanic tradition. Following Alfred Metraux's definition of the shaman as "an individual who, in the interest of the community, sustains by profession an intermittent commerce with the spirits or is possessed by them," Munn concludes that the Mazatec are shamans "in every sense of the word." Their means of entering the shamanistic condition--the mushrooms--"disclose, by their psychoactive power, another modality of conscious activity than the ordinary one."
