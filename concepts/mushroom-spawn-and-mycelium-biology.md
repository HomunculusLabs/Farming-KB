# Mushroom Spawn and Mycelium Biology

## Overview

Mushroom spawn is the vegetative growing material used by cultivators to
establish fungal colonies in prepared beds. It is not a seed but an extensive
network of living fungal threads — the vegetative body of the organism — that
has been propagated in a nutrient substratum and can colonize fresh compost
to produce a mushroom crop.

## Mycelium: The Vegetative Body

### Structure and Growth

Mycelium consists of microscopic, thread-like filaments called hyphae that
elongate at their tips, branching repeatedly to form an extensive web through
the substratum. Hyphae secrete enzymes that break down complex organic
compounds, absorbing the resulting molecules as nutrients. The mycelium of
Agaricus campestris is white, forming dense cottony to strand-like growth in
well-prepared compost. It serves as both the feeding and storage phase of the
organism.

### From Spore to Mycelium

The cycle begins when a spore germinates under favorable moisture, temperature,
and nutrients, producing a germ tube that branches into hyphae. Germination is
notoriously difficult even under laboratory conditions — a major obstacle to
early cultivation. In nature, mycelia of various species invade diverse
substrata: rich earth, leaf litter, fallen timber, and living tree trunks.
Each species has specific habitat requirements.

## Spawn as a Cultivation Tool

### Definition and Characteristics

"Spawn" refers to mycelial growth extensive enough to be visible and
manageable. Fresh, undried A. campestris spawn has a distinctive pleasant
aroma of fresh mushrooms with slight almond quality, largely lost during
drying. Different species produce different aromas — A. fabaceus spawn, for
example, has a far more pronounced almond scent.

### Traditional Spawn-Making

Historically, spawn was collected from established beds of successful growers.
Two dominant European traditions developed:

- **French flake spawn**: Naturally colonized compost broken into flakes, used
  fresh
- **English brick spawn**: Dried, colonized manure pressed into bricks for
  storage and shipping

Both relied on natural airborne spore contamination, introducing variability
in quality and species purity.

### The Pure Culture Revolution

Pure culture methods, pioneered in America in the early 20th century,
represented a watershed advance:

1. Spores germinated on sterile agar media
2. Resulting mycelium isolated from contaminants
3. Clean mycelium propagated through successive transfers
4. Sterilized grain or compost inoculated to produce commercial spawn

Advantages over traditional methods:
- **Known identity**: Species and variety definitively established
- **Freedom from contaminants**: No competing organisms
- **Consistent vigor**: Uniform growth and yield
- **Improved varieties**: Selection of superior strains becomes possible

Duggar noted that the stimulus of pure culture methods directly elevated
American spawn quality above imported European products.

### Tissue Culture

Spawn can also be produced from living mushroom tissue. Small pieces of cap
or stem placed on sterile media produce mycelium identical to the parent —
preserving exact genetic identity of a selected superior strain.

## Spawn in the Bed

### Planting and Colonization

Spawn pieces are inserted in prepared compost at regular intervals (8-12
inches apart, 2-3 inches deep). The mycelium colonizes outward from each
point until the entire bed is permeated by a white network, typically taking
2-3 weeks.

### The Casing Layer

After colonization, a fine soil or loam layer ("casing") is applied:
- Provides humid microclimate at the bed surface
- Supplies minerals and modifies pH
- Stimulates fruiting body formation
- Protects mycelium from temperature fluctuations

### Triggering Fruit

The transition to mushroom production is triggered by mycelial maturity,
reduced temperature, increased fresh air exchange, and the casing layer. The
mycelium then forms dense knots that develop into pin heads and mature mushrooms.

## Quality Indicators

Spawn quality directly determines crop success:
- **Vigor**: Rapid, robust colonization of fresh compost
- **Purity**: Freedom from molds, bacteria, and competitor fungi
- **Strain traits**: Yield potential, cap color, size, disease resistance
- **Freshness**: Active mycelium with adequate moisture content

Duggar observed that many growers were sold inferior spawn by unscrupulous
dealers, and that pure culture methods were the most effective remedy.
