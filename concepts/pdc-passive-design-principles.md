    10|# PDC Passive [[permaculture-house-design-principles]] for Buildings and Landscapes
    11|
    12|Passive design is the art and science of harnessing natural existing forces to accomplish tasks without external energy input. The Williams PDC devotes extensive coverage to passive design as a core permaculture competency, treating it as an application of Permaculture Principle #2: catch and store energy. Williams teaches that passive design is not limited to architecture -- it is a way of thinking applicable to water movement, cooling, heating, ventilation, and lighting.
    13|
    14|## The Three Mechanisms of Heat Transfer
    15|
    16|Understanding passive design begins with understanding how heat moves. Williams identifies three mechanisms:
    17|
    18|### Radiation
    19|Heat travels through space via electromagnetic waves. The Sun is the primary source of radiant heat through microwave and infrared radiation. Key principles:
    20|- Radiation travels in spherical pulses, not straight beams like a laser
    21|- Darker objects absorb more radiation and heat up faster
    22|- Materials absorb radiation and re-emit it as infrared (which is what we perceive as heat)
    23|- For design purposes, direct solar rays produce massive radiant heat
    24|
    25|### Conduction
    26|Heat transfers between any two solid objects in direct physical contact, moving from hot to cold until thermal equilibrium is reached. Key principles:
    27|- The rate of transfer depends on material density, atomic composition, and contact surface area
    28|- Greater surface area contact = greater heat transfer
    29|- Dense materials (stone, concrete, brick) conduct heat effectively
    30|
    31|### Convection
    32|Heat transfers through the movement of a medium such as air or water. Key principles:
    33|- Hot air rises; cool air sinks
    34|- A closed heated room needs both a top vent (for hot air exit) and a bottom vent (for cool air intake) to create airflow
    35|- This intake-driven airflow is called "drafting"
    36|- Cross-breezes require windows on opposite sides of a structure
    37|- Cross-breeze direction follows prevailing wind patterns
    38|
    39|## The Four Fundamentals of [[passive-solar-design]]
    40|
    41|Williams identifies four elements that must be correctly combined for effective passive solar design:
    42|
    43|### 1. Orientation
    44|
    45|The structure's longest wall should face solar south (in the northern hemisphere). The Sun tracks through the sky with these critical angles:
    46|- **Solar altitude**: The vertical angle of the Sun above the horizon, varying from its lowest point at the winter solstice (December 21) to its highest at the summer solstice (June 21)
    47|- **Solar azimuth**: The horizontal angle of sunrise/sunset measured off solar south. Sunrise and sunset angles are always equal -- one angle describes both.
    48|- **Shadow length formula**: Length = Height / tan(solar altitude). This formula is used to design overhangs that keep summer sun out while admitting winter sun.
    49|
    50|### 2. Insulation
    51|
    52|Insulation blocks all three forms of heat transfer. Key concepts:
    53|- **R-Value**: Measures thermal resistance. Higher R-Value = better insulation. R-Value per inch allows calculation of total insulation for any wall thickness.
    54|- **Target R-Value**: Minimum R-20, with R-24 as a common design target
    55|- **Insulating envelope**: The entire interior must be surrounded by insulation at the target R-Value
    56|- **Thermal bridges**: Areas where insulation is significantly below target R-Value. These are generally undesirable, though strategic thermal bridges may be used to transfer heat between zones (e.g., between a greenhouse and living space)
    57|- **Outsulation/Exsulation**: Complete exterior insulation wrapping with no gaps in the envelope
    58|
    59|### 3. Glass (Glazing)
    60|
    61|The proportion of south-facing glazing is critical:
    62|- **Minimum**: 12% of total surface area (for optimal designs with high R-Value, perfect orientation, minimal glazing on other sides)
    63|- **Maximum**: 20% of total surface area (beyond this, heat loss through glazing exceeds heat gain)
    64|- **Calculation example**: A 2,000 sq ft structure needs 240-400 sq ft of south-facing glazing
    65|- As the design becomes "less perfect" (lower R-Value, more east/west/north glazing), increase the percentage toward 20%
    66|
    67|### 4. Thermal Mass
    68|
    69|Thermal mass stores heat and releases it slowly:
    70|- Dense materials (stone, concrete, brick) make excellent thermal mass
    71|- **Heat travel rate**: Approximately 1 hour per inch of thickness
    72|- A 4-inch thermal mass absorbs heat for ~4 hours, then releases it for ~4 more hours
    73|- Thermal mass must be positioned within the thermal envelope where winter sun will strike it
    74|- Placement is calculated using solar altitude and azimuth data -- the mass goes where sun tracks will shine
    75|
    76|## Natural Ventilation Design
    77|
    78|Beyond the basic concepts of drafting and cross-breeze, Williams covers advanced ventilation considerations:
    79|
    80|### Ventilation Point Placement
    81|- Hot air exhaust vents at the highest point of the structure
    82|- Cool air intake vents at the lowest point
    83|- The line of flow between intake and exhaust should pass through occupied spaces
    84|- Vent sizing should allow adjustable flow control
    85|
    86|### Cross-Breeze Design
    87|- Windows placed across from each other create cross-breeze paths
    88|- Cross-breeze direction follows prevailing wind, but windbreaks can prevent excessive gusts
    89|- Windows should be openable when outside temperature is more comfortable than inside, and closed when it is not
    90|
    91|### Convective Cooling Strategies
    92|- Design "cool bowls" where settled cool air accumulates
    93|- Use stack-effect ventilation in multi-story structures
    94|- Night flushing: open vents at night to cool thermal mass, close during day
    95|
    96|## Landscape Integration with Passive Design
    97|
    98|Williams stresses that passive design begins OUTSIDE the structure. The surrounding landscape creates the microclimate that the passive design works within.
    99|
   100|### Trees as Passive Design Elements
   101|
   102|- **Evergreens**: Provide year-round windbreak and shade. Best for consistent protection from prevailing winds.
   103|- **Deciduous trees**: Provide summer shade (full canopy) and winter sun penetration (bare branches). Ideal for south and west sides of structures.
   104|- **Placement consideration**: Trees grow. Account for mature canopy size when positioning. Many foundations have been damaged by trees planted too close.
   105|
   106|### Ground Covers and Vegetation
   107|
   108|- Grasses and ground covers shade the ground surface, reducing ambient temperature
   109|- Evapotranspiration from plants cools surrounding air
   110|- Plant selection can provide year-round shade or seasonal variation
   111|
   112|### Water Features
   113|
   114|- Ponds provide cooling through evaporation
   115|- Water surfaces can reflect sunlight into structures
   116|- Positioned correctly, water features can contribute to passive cooling strategies
   117|
   118|### Hardscape as Thermal Mass
   119|
   120|- Sands, rocks, stones, and concrete walkways function as landscape thermal mass
   121|- Can be exposed to sun year-round for maximum heat absorption
   122|- Or shaded by deciduous plants for seasonal variation
   123|
   124|### Secondary Structures as Topography
   125|
   126|- Chicken coops, greenhouses, gazebos, garages, and yurts can create shade zones and wind protection similar to hills
   127|- Their placement relative to primary structures is a passive design decision
   128|
   129|## Roof Design Considerations
   130|
   131|### Solar Reflective Index (SRI)
   132|
   133|- SRI measures how much sunlight a roof material reflects
   134|- Lower SRI = more heat absorption = hotter structure
   135|- Higher SRI = more reflection = cooler structure
   136|- Choose SRI based on whether you want heat gain or avoidance at the roof
   137|
   138|### Roof as Multi-Function Element
   139|
   140|- Green roofs maximize growing space and replace ecological footprint
   141|- Solar panels provide shade while generating electricity
   142|- Solar water heaters serve double duty as shade devices
   143|- Decks on flat roofs protect underlying roof membrane
   144|- Rainwater catchment from appropriate roof surfaces
