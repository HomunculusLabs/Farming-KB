# PDC Passive [[permaculture-house-design-principles]] for Buildings and Landscapes

Passive design is the art and science of harnessing natural existing forces to accomplish tasks without external energy input. The [[williams-pdc-permaculture-ethics-and-principles-in-practice]] devotes extensive coverage to passive design as a core permaculture competency, treating it as an application of [[permaculture-design-principles]] #2: catch and store energy. Williams teaches that passive design is not limited to architecture -- it is a way of thinking applicable to water movement, cooling, heating, ventilation, and lighting.

## The Three Mechanisms of Heat Transfer

Understanding passive design begins with understanding how heat moves. Williams identifies three mechanisms:

### Radiation
Heat travels through space via electromagnetic waves. The Sun is the primary source of radiant heat through microwave and infrared radiation. Key principles:
- Radiation travels in spherical pulses, not straight beams like a laser
- Darker objects absorb more radiation and heat up faster
- Materials absorb radiation and re-emit it as infrared (which is what we perceive as heat)
- For design purposes, direct solar rays produce massive radiant heat

### Conduction
Heat transfers between any two solid objects in direct physical contact, moving from hot to cold until thermal equilibrium is reached. Key principles:
- The rate of transfer depends on material density, atomic composition, and contact surface area
- Greater surface area contact = greater heat transfer
- Dense materials (stone, concrete, brick) conduct heat effectively

### Convection
Heat transfers through the movement of a medium such as air or water. Key principles:
- Hot air rises; cool air sinks
- A closed heated room needs both a top vent (for hot air exit) and a bottom vent (for cool air intake) to create airflow
- This intake-driven airflow is called "drafting"
- Cross-breezes require windows on opposite sides of a structure
- Cross-breeze direction follows prevailing wind patterns

## The Four Fundamentals of [[passive-solar-design]]

Williams identifies four elements that must be correctly combined for effective [[permaculture-designers-manual-passive-solar-design]]:

### 1. Orientation

The structure's longest wall should face solar south (in the northern hemisphere). The Sun tracks through the sky with these critical angles:
- **Solar altitude**: The vertical angle of the Sun above the horizon, varying from its lowest point at the winter solstice (December 21) to its highest at the summer solstice (June 21)
- **Solar azimuth**: The horizontal angle of sunrise/sunset measured off solar south. Sunrise and sunset angles are always equal -- one angle describes both.
- **Shadow length formula**: Length = Height / tan(solar altitude). This formula is used to design overhangs that keep summer sun out while admitting winter sun.

### 2. Insulation

Insulation blocks all three forms of heat transfer. Key concepts:
- **R-Value**: Measures thermal resistance. Higher R-Value = better insulation. R-Value per inch allows calculation of total insulation for any wall thickness.
- **Target R-Value**: Minimum R-20, with R-24 as a common design target
- **Insulating envelope**: The entire interior must be surrounded by insulation at the target R-Value
- **Thermal bridges**: Areas where insulation is significantly below target R-Value. These are generally undesirable, though strategic thermal bridges may be used to transfer heat between zones (e.g., between a greenhouse and living space)
- **Outsulation/Exsulation**: Complete exterior insulation wrapping with no gaps in the envelope

### 3. Glass (Glazing)

The proportion of south-facing glazing is critical:
- **Minimum**: 12% of total surface area (for optimal designs with high R-Value, perfect orientation, minimal glazing on other sides)
- **Maximum**: 20% of total surface area (beyond this, heat loss through glazing exceeds heat gain)
- **Calculation example**: A 2,000 sq ft structure needs 240-400 sq ft of south-facing glazing
- As the design becomes "less perfect" (lower R-Value, more east/west/north glazing), increase the percentage toward 20%

### 4. Thermal Mass

Thermal mass stores heat and releases it slowly:
- Dense materials (stone, concrete, brick) make excellent thermal mass
- **Heat travel rate**: Approximately 1 hour per inch of thickness
- A 4-inch thermal mass absorbs heat for ~4 hours, then releases it for ~4 more hours
- Thermal mass must be positioned within the thermal envelope where winter sun will strike it
- Placement is calculated using solar altitude and azimuth data -- the mass goes where sun tracks will shine

## Natural Ventilation Design

Beyond the basic concepts of drafting and cross-breeze, Williams covers advanced ventilation considerations:

### Ventilation Point Placement
- Hot air exhaust vents at the highest point of the structure
- Cool air intake vents at the lowest point
- The line of flow between intake and exhaust should pass through occupied spaces
- Vent sizing should allow adjustable flow control

### Cross-Breeze Design
- Windows placed across from each other create cross-breeze paths
- Cross-breeze direction follows prevailing wind, but windbreaks can prevent excessive gusts
- Windows should be openable when outside temperature is more comfortable than inside, and closed when it is not

### Convective Cooling Strategies
- Design "cool bowls" where settled cool air accumulates
- Use stack-effect ventilation in multi-story structures
- Night flushing: open vents at night to cool thermal mass, close during day

## Landscape Integration with Passive Design

Williams stresses that passive design begins OUTSIDE the structure. The surrounding landscape creates the microclimate that the passive design works within.

### Trees as Passive Design Elements

- **Evergreens**: Provide year-round windbreak and shade. Best for consistent protection from prevailing winds.
- **Deciduous trees**: Provide summer shade (full canopy) and winter sun penetration (bare branches). Ideal for south and west sides of structures.
- **Placement consideration**: Trees grow. Account for mature canopy size when positioning. Many foundations have been damaged by trees planted too close.

### Ground Covers and Vegetation

- Grasses and [[mollison-designers-living-mulches-ground-covers]] shade the ground surface, reducing ambient temperature
- Evapotranspiration from plants cools surrounding air
- Plant selection can provide year-round shade or seasonal variation

### Water Features

- Ponds provide cooling through evaporation
- Water surfaces can reflect sunlight into structures
- Positioned correctly, water features can contribute to passive cooling strategies

### Hardscape as Thermal Mass

- Sands, rocks, stones, and concrete walkways function as landscape thermal mass
- Can be exposed to sun year-round for maximum heat absorption
- Or shaded by deciduous plants for seasonal variation

### Secondary Structures as Topography

- Chicken coops, greenhouses, gazebos, garages, and yurts can create shade zones and wind protection similar to hills
- Their placement relative to primary structures is a passive design decision

## Roof Design Considerations

### Solar Reflective Index (SRI)

- SRI measures how much sunlight a roof material reflects
- Lower SRI = more heat absorption = hotter structure
- Higher SRI = more reflection = cooler structure
- Choose SRI based on whether you want heat gain or avoidance at the roof

### Roof as Multi-Function Element

- Green roofs maximize growing space and replace ecological footprint
- Solar panels provide shade while generating electricity
