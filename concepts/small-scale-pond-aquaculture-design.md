Pond aquaculture scales from tiny garden containers of one to ten square metres through mid-size family production units of 100 to 500 square metres, up to commercial enterprises of several hectares. At every scale, the same ecological principles apply, but the appropriate species, management intensity, and integration with surrounding landscape differ significantly. Understanding the productive potential and design requirements at each size order enables farmers and gardeners to select the right pond system for their site, resources, and food production goals.

## Garden Ponds (1–10 Square Metres)

Very small garden ponds of two to sixty centimetres depth can be constructed from old baths, stock watering tanks, plastic-lined holes with protective clay or earth covers, and pre-cast units in plastic, fibreglass, or concrete. Despite their modest size, these ponds support productive polycultures of edible and useful species. In shallow ponds, Chinese water chestnut (Eleocharis dulcis), kangkong (Ipomoea aquatica), watercress (Rorippa aquatica), taro (Colocasia esculentus), frogs, and small fish all thrive.

Frogs are excellent predators in the garden ecosystem, consuming insects by day and night, while Hylid tree frogs inhabit the leaves of plants and provide additional pest control. A single square metre of taro produces 20–30 kg of starchy food, making it one of the most productive leaf crops available for small water features. Deeper garden ponds support Indian water chestnut (Trapa natans), lotus (Nelumbo), and arrowhead (Sagittaria).

These tiny ponds also serve as nurseries for forage fish stocks destined for larger ponds, housing mussels, useful molluscs, basic plant stocks, and shrimp. Boiled taro, cassava, or plantain can be added to fish food, creating a closed-loop system where garden waste feeds pond organisms and pond organisms provide protein and vegetables in return.

The [[new-alchemy-institute]] on Cape Cod demonstrated that clear fibreglass ponds of approximately ten square metres and two metres depth produce fish and products valued at $4.50 to $17 per square foot (1984 values), amortising costs in three to five years. These ponds yield fish, shrimp, and enriched water for semi-hydroponic crops. They also provide useful heat storage for night and winter heating, functioning effectively in greenhouses or outdoors for much of the year. Outdoor ponds placed in front of reflective white walls on white gravel maximise solar input, increase yields, and lengthen growing seasons.

In glasshouses, such ponds give good yields and moderate temperature extremes while providing algae-rich waters for terrestrial plant beds. Fish wastes and nitrates are reduced in the water and routed to plants, creating a simple aquaponic system that requires no external nutrient inputs. The Rodale organisation has also made extensive trials on small tank production of fish, confirming the viability of these micro-scale systems for domestic food production.

There is a significant business opportunity for standard-sized domestic pond kits, equipment, and fish stocks. Subsidiary uses include water storage for fire suppression, tank supplies for gardens during dry periods, productive disposal systems for food wastes fed to omnivorous fish, and sources of recreational interest. Fish tanks are recommended even for workaholics as a peaceful and contemplative hobby, providing psychological benefits alongside food production.

## Family Production Ponds (10–100 Square Metres)

Ponds of ten to 100 square metres are very useful for rearing fish fry for sale or stocking, breeding forage fish, producing large quantities of vegetable crop, supplying aquaculture nurseries and aquariums with plant and animal stocks, and creating significant fire-breaks, water reserves, and heat moderation effects in the surrounding landscape. For a family, an intensively-managed fish pond of 100 square metres comes close to providing a full protein and vegetable resource if carefully designed and stocked with a beneficial polyculture.

Aeration via a photovoltaic cell and air pump may be necessary for high yields, but the modest yields of 300–2,000 kg of protein that can realistically be expected represent a significant contribution to household food self-reliance. These are specialty ponds requiring very careful planning and subsequent modification, much like the home garden of which they may form a part. They can serve as important components of waste food disposal systems and form part of a total wastewater treatment chain.

In line with a biogas or septic tank unit, these ponds grow a fairly constant mass of green manures that can be reaped or gathered for garden mulch or biogas recycling, closing nutrient loops within the household system. One special pond of 100 square metres can supply house heating and hot water as a solar pond, or be built just to heat a larger pond through thermal exchange.

## Nursery and Income Ponds (100–500 Square Metres)

Ponds of 100 to 500 square metres are regarded by Chakroff (1982) and others as ideal for fish culture. Easily harvested, netted, or drained, and capable of holding specific age, size, or species assemblies, these ponds give maximum flexibility in management. Even a single 500 square metre pond, or five 100 square metre ponds in series, can provide income from baitfish, aquarium fish, special plant crops, and intensively managed high-value species such as prawns.

A nursery providing eggs, seeds, fry, or fingerlings to growers need not exceed this size. In 1983, incomes of about $30,000 were possible from open-air ponds of this size stocked with prawns in Hawaii, demonstrating the commercial viability of small-scale intensive aquaculture. These ponds can form part of a total wastewater system and generate green manures for garden mulch or biogas recycling.

## Semi-Intensive Family Farms (500 Square Metres to 5 Hectares)

About five hectares is estimated to provide a full family income in high-value product. Ten to twenty prawn ponds with ducks, mussels, and edge plants represent two full-time jobs akin to but more easily managed than a market garden. Many successful enterprises never exceed this family-farm scale. The scale permits multiple use and leaseholds of other activities, supplies a relatively large area of land crop, and forms a complete fire-break for homesteads and fuel forests. Sewage ponds of this size provide safe disposal for small towns.

Ponds of all sizes can be arranged as isolated units, in parallel, in series, or annidated (nested) one within another. They can be the same or different sizes, and all these factors must be selected for particular landscapes or for specific production sequences. Depths too may need to be varied for forage production, breeding, or predator protection within the same complex.
