## Vernalization: Winter Memory

Perhaps the most dramatic example of plant long-term memory is
vernalization -- the requirement for prolonged exposure to cold
temperatures before a plant will flower. Many temperate plant species,
including wheat, barley, and Arabidopsis, will remain in a vegetative
state indefinitely unless they experience a period of winter cold lasting
several weeks. Once vernalized, the plant retains the memory of that cold
exposure through subsequent growth, even if conditions become warm, and
proceeds to flower when days lengthen in spring.

The molecular mechanism of vernalization centers on the gene FLOWERING
LOCUS C (FLC), which encodes a repressor of flowering. In non-vernalized
plants, FLC is actively transcribed and suppresses the flowering program.
During cold exposure, progressive modifications to the chromatin
chromatin surrounding the FLC locus -- specifically, the addition of
histone marks such as H3K27me3 -- gradually silence FLC expression. This
epigenetic silencing persists through cell division, meaning that every
cell in the growing plant "remembers" the winter it experienced, even
after dozens of rounds of mitosis.

Remarkably, vernalization memory is reset each generation. In the seeds
of a vernalized plant, FLC expression is reactivated, ensuring that each
new generation must independently measure winter before committing to
flowering. This reset mechanism prevents premature flowering in offspring
that germinate in autumn rather than spring.

## Priming and Defense Memory

Plants can be "primed" by prior exposure to stress, resulting in faster,
stronger, or more effective responses to subsequent stress encounters.
This form of memory, sometimes called systemic acquired resistance (SAR)
or [[comparison-jasmonate-vs-salicylic-acid-defense-signaling]] (SA). After the
initial infection is resolved, the plant maintains elevated levels of
defensive proteins and a heightened state of alertness. If the same or
a related pathogen attacks again, the primed plant mounts a defense
response more rapidly and with greater intensity than a naive plant.
The molecular basis involves lasting changes in chromatin accessibility
at defense-related gene loci, keeping these genes in a transcriptionally
poised state ready for rapid reactivation.
