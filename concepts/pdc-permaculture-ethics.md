The ethics are stated formally in the Professional Permaculture Designers' Ethics (Pamphlet VIII) and inform every lecture throughout the course. They form the core principle set that distinguishes permaculture from other design systems.

## The Designers' Eight Ethics

In Pamphlet VIII, Mollison lays out eight formal ethics for professional permaculture designers:

1. **Cooperation over competition**: "As a group of designers, we cooperate; we do not compete."
2. **No duplication of work**: "We do not duplicate each other's work. As far as possible, we create and use individual designs."
3. **Responsibility to clients**: "We care for our clients and are responsible to them."
4. **The ultimate end -- reforestation and soil restoration**: "The end to which we are working is the reforestation of the Earth and the restoration of its soils to health."
5. **Care for environment and wildlife**: "We care for the environment and wildlife. In all our design work, we side with that 'super client,' Gaia."
6. **Appropriate energy paths**: "We seek the best, the most appropriate energy paths, utilizing appropriate energy."
7. **Recycle at the highest level**: "We recycle at the highest level possible. Everything can be a resource. You must know how to use it. Use what you have. Try to make the least change produce the most effect."
8. **Personal divestment**: "A final ethic that we practice in our community in Tasmania is that we divest ourselves of everything surplus to our needs. But we don't ask you to do that."

## Gaia as Super-Client

Mollison introduces a critical concept: the "super-client." While designers serve human clients, they must always "side with that 'super client,' Gaia, which is an old Greek word for the Mother Earth Goddess. Earth was conceived of then as a living, thinking organism, a biological entity."

This means the designer's ultimate loyalty is not to the paying client but to the Earth itself. If a client's aims are "environmentally destructive, for example, a group that wants to hack out a hole in the forest for themselves... you should always side with the super-client -- the environment." This is formalized as "Error 1" -- undertaking a design job for people whose aims are environmentally destructive.

## The Three Ethics (Earth Care, People Care, Fair Share)

While Mollison's 1981 course emphasizes the eight designers' ethics, the three core permaculture ethics permeate every lecture:

### Earth Care
Explicit throughout: "The end to which we are working is the reforestation of the Earth and the restoration of its soils to health." This ethic drives every design recommendation -- from swales that build soil to forests that store water. Mollison states: "What we are looking at is complete acceptance, acceptance of a real situation with a methodology to which the investor can link."

### People Care
The designer "cares for our clients and is responsible to them." This means designing for genuine human needs, not just productivity. It means explaining why recommendations are made (Error 2: not telling the client the reasons). It means recommending technologies within the client's ability to handle (Error 3: recommending difficult technology beyond the client's ability).

### Fair Share (Return of Surplus)
The eighth ethic -- "divest ourselves of everything surplus to our needs" -- captures this. Mollison also emphasizes distributing yields: the Babassu palm project is designed so that "the investor can let all of these go. All the investor wants is to earn a return from the alcohol production capacity of the site. All the other people own these other capacities."

## Cooperation as Foundational Principle

The first ethic -- cooperation over competition -- is radical. Mollison explicitly instructs designers not to compete with each other, not to duplicate work, and to create individual designs. This extends to the community level: "It is functional stacking. The original meaning of the word is to put one painting on top of another. It is like laying on colors. What we are laying on is functions."

## Least Change, Most Effect

A key recurring ethical principle: "Try to make the least change produce the most effect." This is Mollison's version of the Hippocratic oath for land -- first, do no harm. The designer's role is to "know where to place things, and why" so that "you use their multiple functions" and "create low energy inputs for high yield and stability."

This principle appears repeatedly across the course:
- Use existing resources on site (fennel becomes a cash crop rather than a weed to clear)
- Work with natural patterns rather than against them (keyline follows natural water flow)
- Stack functions (a pond provides water, grows food, breeds frogs for pest control, reflects light to extend growing season)

## The Creative Observer

Mollison defines the designer's role as both technical and philosophical: "your function is to know where to place things, and why" but simultaneously "your role is that of a creative observer. You must learn to observe nature, to recognize how to develop potential uses so that humans may benefit."

## Application in Design Practice

The ethics translate into concrete design behaviors:

- **Site observation before intervention**: Spend time on your own at the site with a spade, looking at soil, water, resources
- **Resource identification before design**: Find what's already productive (fennel, clay, wind) before importing solutions
- **Type-one error avoidance**: Never place clients in fire funnels, wind tunnels, or in the bush where they'll become wildlife targets
- **Honest communication**: Direct, friendly, fairly personal reporting style; never vague or loose expressions
- **Follow-up responsibility**: "Assure the client that you will answer further questions at no extra charge"
- **Ecological succession awareness**: Design for natural succession rather than fighting it

## The Ethical Investment Proposition

In the "Permaculture for Millionaires" lecture (Pamphlet XV), Mollison extends ethics into economics. He proposes convincing wealthy investors to fund large-scale permaculture by appealing to enlightened self-interest: "They are not immune from acid rain; they are not immune from environmental disaster... There is nobody to tell them what to do about the environment."

The ethical investment model: the investor earns returns from one product (e.g., alcohol from Babassu palm) while all other yields (food, honey, aquaculture, housing) belong to the people living and working the land. "In the end, what you have is levels of function. All the investor buys is the product in which he is interested. The rest of it is the people's."
