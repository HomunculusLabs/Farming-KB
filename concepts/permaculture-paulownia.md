Paulownia (Paulownia fortunei) is one of the fastest-growing hardwood trees in the world, capable of producing harvestable timber in as little as 5 years. It is promoted as a sustainable alternative to tropical hardwoods.

## Uses

- Wood and timber (harvestable in 5 years)

- Edible leaves (high-protein animal forage)

- Bare soil erosion control

- Pest control

- Bee forage

- Intercropping (with grains and other crops)

## Growing Conditions

- Full sun

- Deep, regular watering when soil is dry

- Additional fertilizer throughout the year

- Not suitable for the very coldest climates (susceptible to frost when young)

- First year: tree should reach at least 10 feet (3 m)

## Varieties

Paulownia fortunei is the most commonly cultivated species, known for its straight trunk and rapid growth. Paulownia elongata is similar but slightly more cold-tolerant. Paulownia tomentosa (Empress Tree) is the most cold-hardy species, surviving temperatures down to -5°F (-20°C) once established, though young trees need protection. Hybrid varieties have been developed for specific characteristics, including faster growth rates and improved form for timber production.

## Propagation

Paulownia is most reliably propagated from root cuttings or tissue culture. Seed propagation is possible but results in high genetic variability. For root cuttings, dig sections of root 0.5-1 inch in diameter and 3-6 inches long in early spring before bud break. Plant horizontally 2-3 inches deep. Commercial growers often use tissue-cultured plantlets for uniform, disease-free stock. Direct planting of seed is possible but requires careful weed control during establishment. Trees can also be propagated by stump sprouts after harvesting.

## Growth Management

If the tree does not reach 10 feet in the first year, cut it down in early spring before leaves form. This will make it grow twice as fast the next year. The tree can be cut back like this almost every year.

## Companion Planting

Paulownia's open canopy allows significant light penetration, making it suitable for [[permaculture-tree-crop-systems]] intercropping. In [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]], paulownia can serve as a fast-growing nurse tree that provides shade during the establishment phase, then is harvested to open space for slower-growing canopy species. Its leaves make excellent [[mulch-producing-plants]] material when chopped and dropped. The tree's flowers are highly attractive to bees, supporting [[permaculture-beekeeping]] operations with abundant nectar in spring.

## Culinary Uses

While not commonly consumed by humans, paulownia leaves are valued as high-protein animal forage, containing 18-22% crude protein. Leaves can be fed fresh to livestock or dried for winter feed. Rabbits, goats, and cattle readily consume paulownia foliage. In some regions, young leaves are occasionally used in salads or cooked as greens, though this practice is not widespread. The flowers are edible and can be used in teas or as a garnish.

## Permaculture Design Role

In [[permaculture-design-principles-beginners]], paulownia exemplifies rapid biomass production and succession management. As a [[permaculture-pioneer-species]], it quickly establishes on degraded land, providing erosion control and soil improvement through leaf litter. Its deep taproot breaks compacted soil layers, improving conditions for subsequent plantings. In [[permaculture-wildlife-corridors]], paulownia serves as a biomass generator, timber producer, bee forage, and animal fodder source — a true multi-function element.

## Pests and Diseases

Paulownia is relatively disease-resistant but can be affected by several issues. Paulownia witches' broom is the most serious disease, caused by phytoplasma — infected trees develop dense clusters of stunted branches and should be removed and destroyed. Caterpillars, particularly the paulownia leaf-eating caterpillar, can defoliate young trees. Spider mites may attack in hot, dry conditions. Root rot can occur in poorly drained soils. Regular watering during establishment and proper site selection prevent most problems.

## Intercropping

Paulownia leaves make excellent high-protein animal forage. The trees can be intercropped with grains and other plants, as their open canopy allows sufficient light through.

## Timber Value

Paulownia wood is lightweight, strong, and rot-resistant. It is marketed as a sustainable alternative to tropical hardwoods that typically take decades to mature.

## Carbon Sequestration

Paulownia is among the fastest-growing trees for carbon sequestration, absorbing CO2 at rates significantly higher than most hardwoods. A single paulownia tree can absorb approximately 10 times more CO2 than an equivalent oak tree. This makes it valuable in [[permaculture-design-principles-beginners]] for climate mitigation. After harvest, the carbon stored in the timber remains locked away in wood products. The rapid growth cycle means repeated harvests can continuously capture carbon over time.

## Practical Tips for Growers

Site selection is critical — paulownia needs well-drained soil and full sun. Avoid planting in frost pockets or waterlogged areas. Young trees benefit from weed control within a 3-foot radius for the first two growing seasons. Fertilizer requirements are moderate to high, as the tree's rapid growth is nutrient-demanding. Staking young trees prevents wind damage during the first year. For timber production, prune lower branches early to encourage straight, clear trunk development. Harvest timing depends on intended use — 5-7 years for small timber, 8-12 years for larger dimension lumber.
