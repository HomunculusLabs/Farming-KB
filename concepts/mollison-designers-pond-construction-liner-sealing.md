# Pond Construction: Liners, Sealing, and Edge Treatment

In Permaculture: A Designer's Manual, Mollison treats ponds as multi-functional
elements within permaculture landscapes. A well-designed pond provides water
storage, irrigation supply, aquaculture habitat, fire protection, microclimate
moderation, wildlife habitat, and aesthetic value. Proper construction
technique determines whether a pond holds water reliably or becomes a
maintenance burden.

## Site Selection

Site selection is the most critical factor in pond success. Mollison provides
the following criteria:

**Catchment area:** The pond must have sufficient contributing catchment to
fill and maintain water levels. As a rule of thumb, 1 hectare of catchment
yields roughly 10,000 cubic meters of runoff per year in areas with 1,000 mm
annual rainfall, though actual yield depends on slope, soil, and vegetation
cover.

**Soil suitability:** Clay-rich soils (at least 20-30% clay content) are ideal
for natural sealing without liners. Sandy, gravelly, or rocky sites require
imported clay or artificial liners. Test soil by excavating a test pit and
filling it with water. If the pit drains within 24-48 hours, sealing will be
necessary.

**Topography:** Locate ponds where the landscape naturally concentrates water --
valley floors, depressions, saddles, or areas below [[swale-design-construction]]
diversions. Excavation costs are lowest where a small dam at a narrow point
impounds a broad valley.

**Depth:** A minimum depth of 1.5-2 m prevents excessive warming, controls
aquatic weed growth, and provides thermal refuge for fish during hot weather.
Deeper ponds (3-5 m) offer greater drought resilience and stratification
opportunities for aquaculture.

**Orientation:** In temperate climates, orient the longest axis of the pond
perpendicular to prevailing summer winds to maximize wave aeration. Avoid
shading the entire pond surface -- partial shade from edge trees is beneficial,
but full shade limits productivity.

## Excavation

Excavation shape matters ecologically. Mollison recommends irregular shorelines
with varying depth zones: shallow shelves (0-30 cm) for emergent and marginal
plants, intermediate zones (30-100 cm) for submergent plants and small fish,
and deep zones (100+ cm) for open water and fish refuge.

Avoid steep underwater banks that may slump. Slope underwater sides at 3:1 or
flatter. Create at least one shallow zone for wildlife access and plant
establishment.

Topsoil should be stripped and stored separately before excavation. It is
replaced over the completed liner or seal as a substrate for plant growth.

Excavated material can be used to build the dam (in embankment ponds), form
islands, create adjacent [[mollison-designers-terracing-slope-stabilization]], or
level surrounding land.

## Clay Lining (Puddled Clay)

Where adequate clay is available on-site or nearby, puddled clay is the
traditional and most durable sealing method. Clay particles swell when wet,
creating an impermeable layer.

1. Spread 15-30 cm of clay over the excavated pond floor and banks
2. Compact the clay in 5-10 cm layers using a sheepsfoot roller or by
   "puddling" -- working the wet clay with feet or tools to eliminate air
   pockets and create a dense, homogeneous layer
3. Protect the clay layer from drying, cracking, and root penetration with a
   5-10 cm cover of topsoil or sand

Clay liners are self-healing to some degree: if cracked, rewetting causes the
clay to swell and reseal. They are the preferred method in [[permaculture-earthworks]]
where long-term durability with minimal maintenance is desired.

## Bentonite

Bentonite is a highly expansive sodium clay that can be applied as a dry powder
or mixed with soil to create an impermeable layer. It swells to 10-15 times its
dry volume when wet.

Application rate: 5-15 kg per square meter depending on soil type and
application method. On sandy soils, mix bentonite with the top 10-15 cm of soil
and compact. On gravelly soils, apply as a blanket layer beneath protective
cover.

Bentonite is sensitive to disturbance -- roots, burrowing animals, and
mechanical damage can breach the seal. It is less durable than puddled clay for
large or heavily used ponds but is convenient for small garden ponds and repair
of leaking clay liners.

## Synthetic Liners (LDPE, HDPE, EPDM, PVC)

Where natural sealing is impractical, synthetic liners provide reliable
waterproofing. Mollison covers the common types:

**LDPE:** Flexible, inexpensive, UV-sensitive (must be covered). 10-20 year
life with UV protection. Suitable for small to medium ponds.

**HDPE:** Rigid, durable, UV-resistant. 20-50 year life. Requires professional
installation. Preferred for large earthwork ponds.

**EPDM:** Rubber liner, highly flexible, UV resistant. Expensive but long-lived
(30-50 years). Preferred for aquaculture ponds.

**PVC:** Moderately priced but contains plasticizers that may leach. Less
preferred for food production systems.

Installation: Remove all rocks, roots, and sharp objects from the subgrade.
Lay a geotextile cushion beneath the liner. Overlap seams 15-30 cm and seal
with manufacturer's adhesive. Cover the liner with 10+ cm of soil or sand to
prevent UV damage and provide a rooting medium for aquatic plants.

## Edge Treatment

The edge is where most pond problems originate. Mollison recommends creating a
marginal shelf (0-30 cm deep, 50-100 cm wide) around the perimeter for emergent
aquatic plants that stabilize banks, filter runoff, and provide wildlife habitat.
Plant with native marginal species: reeds, rushes, sedges, irises, cattails.
Extend the liner at least 30 cm above the expected maximum water level and
anchor in a trench. Rock or stone edging protects the liner from UV and damage.

## Overflow and Spillway

Every pond must have a designed overflow to safely convey excess water during
heavy rainfall. Without proper overflow, water can overtop and erode the dam or
banks, causing catastrophic failure.

The spillway should be at the lowest point of the embankment, constructed of
erosion-resistant materials (stone, concrete, dense turf), and sized for the
design storm (typically 1-in-25 to 1-in-50 year event). Ensure discharge onto
stable, non-erodible surfaces connected to a drainage system or natural waterway.
Mollison also recommends an anti-siphon pipe or "monk" outlet for controlling
water level and draining the pond for maintenance.

from shallow to deep zones. Start with oxygenating submergents, then marginal
