Site selection is the most critical factor in pond success. Mollison provides
the following criteria:

**Catchment area:** The pond must have sufficient contributing catchment to
fill and maintain water levels. As a rule of thumb, 1 hectare of catchment
yields roughly 10,000 cubic meters of runoff per year in areas with 1,000 mm
annual rainfall, though actual yield depends on slope, soil, and vegetation
cover.

**Soil suitability:** Clay-rich soils (at least 20-30% clay content) are ideal
for natural sealing without liners. Sandy, gravelly, or rocky sites require
imported clay or artificial liners. Test soil by excavating a test pit and
filling it with water. If the pit drains within 24-48 hours, sealing will be
necessary.

**Topography:** Locate ponds where the landscape naturally concentrates water --
valley floors, depressions, saddles, or areas below [[mollison-designers-small-dam-site-selection-design]] osts are lowest where a small dam at a narrow point
impounds a broad valley.

**Depth:** A minimum depth of 1.5-2 m prevents excessive warming, controls
aquatic weed growth, and provides thermal refuge for fish during hot weather.
Deeper ponds (3-5 m) offer greater drought resilience and stratification
opportunities for aquaculture.

**Orientation:** In temperate climates, orient the longest axis of the pond
perpendicular to prevailing summer winds to maximize wave aeration. Avoid
shading the entire pond surface -- partial shade from edge trees is beneficial,
but full shade limits productivity.

## Excavation

Excavation shape matters ecologically. Mollison recommends irregular shorelines
with varying depth zones: shallow shelves (0-30 cm) for emergent and marginal
plants, intermediate zones (30-100 cm) for submergent plants and small fish,
and deep zones (100+ cm) for open water and fish refuge.

Avoid steep underwater banks that may slump. Slope underwater sides at 3:1 or
flatter. Create at least one shallow zone for wildlife access and plant
establishment.

Topsoil should be stripped and stored separately before excavation. It is
replaced over the completed liner or seal as a substrate for plant growth.

Excavated material can be used to build the dam (in embankment ponds), form
islands, create adjacent [[permaculture-earthworks]]
where long-term durability with minimal maintenance is desired.

## Overflow and Spillway

Every pond must have a designed overflow to safely convey excess water during
heavy rainfall. Without proper overflow, water can overtop and erode the dam or
banks, causing catastrophic failure.

The spillway should be at the lowest point of the embankment, constructed of
erosion-resistant materials (stone, concrete, dense turf), and sized for the
design storm (typically 1-in-25 to 1-in-50 year event). Ensure discharge onto
stable, non-erodible surfaces connected to a drainage system or natural waterway.
Mollison also recommends an anti-siphon pipe or "monk" outlet for controlling
water level and draining the pond for maintenance.
