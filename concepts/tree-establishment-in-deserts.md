# Tree Establishment in Deserts

## Overview

Establishing trees in desert environments requires careful attention to a combination of factors: timing, soil temperature, water delivery, wind protection, mulching, and pest exclusion. Valuable fruit, forage, and seed-source trees need to be carefully established in dryland areas, partly as graft, bud, and seed sources for broader reafforestation. Success depends on following a comprehensive set of precursors that address the extreme challenges of arid environments.

## Essential Precursors to Success

### Timing and Temperature

Plant in relatively cool periods and verify that soil temperature is not lethally high. Shallow plant roots can be cooked at temperatures above 30°C. In many desert environments, this restricts planting to early spring or autumn when both air and soil temperatures are moderate enough for root establishment before extreme heat or cold arrives.

### Mulching

Supply mulch in quantity in pits near the plant and around its roots. In extreme conditions, mulches 45 cm or deeper may be needed to be effective. [[stone-mulch]] is particularly valuable for tree root areas, as it simultaneously provides condensation, weed suppression, wind protection, and thermal mass that moderates soil temperature fluctuations.

### Water Collection

Plant in a long [[cover-crops]] around the tree to cool the root area through shade and transpiration. Hardy tree legumes as intercrop aid with wind protection, sun shading, nitrogen fixation, and mulch production. Trees are pit or slot-planted in a "mud planter" hole where the mud walls conserve moisture in both the mud and surrounding sand, and insulate roots from surface heat.

This can be line, drip, or pot-irrigated. The mud must be locally available, and the whole process is worthwhile only if the trees are themselves of great value.

## Soil Amendments for Desert Tree Planting

### Gypsum for Clay Soils

Just as gypsum helps roots penetrate clay, it breaks up compacted clay layers that would otherwise create impenetrable barriers to root growth. In desert soils with hardpan layers, gypsum application at planting can dramatically improve tree survival and growth rates.

### Bentonite for Sandy Soils

Bentonite clay assists sands to hold moisture. When mixed into sandy planting holes, bentonite swells on contact with water, creating a gel-like matrix that significantly increases the water-holding capacity of otherwise freely-draining desert sands.

### Dried Seaweed

Dried seaweed crumble added to planting holes forms a gel in rain, enabling seedlings to penetrate to deeper levels in the first season. The gel also retains moisture around the root zone and slowly releases minerals and trace elements as it decomposes.

### Commercial Soil Gel Additives

Commercial superabsorbent polymer products are available for adding to soils of potted plants in the nursery and for direct field application. Some of these function effectively for many months, significantly reducing irrigation frequency during the establishment period.

## Calcrete and Hardpan Management

In the calcrete (calcium carbonate-cemented) soils of coasts and islands, the encrusted layer must be broken open at every tree site. This is essential for coconut and date cultivation, where the calcrete layer prevents root penetration to moisture below. A mulch pit plus a scatter of elemental sulphur and mineral elements should be prepared for the tree.

A few fibrous-rooted species will establish on calcrete, but they are at risk from drought and windthrow. Tree growth keeps the calcrete open and cracked for interplanting. A fast way to achieve this is with an auger and about one-third of a plug of dynamite. The same technique can be used to place fence posts or shatter concretions in shales and mudstones.

## Bulldozer Rip Lines

When a bulldozer is available, rip lines can be made for tree rows. Companion crops of small legumes can be seeded between trees along the rip. This approach is efficient for large-scale afforestation projects and allows intercropping during the tree establishment phase, providing both ground cover and additional yields.

## Integrated Establishment Approach

The ideal desert tree establishment combines multiple protective strategies simultaneously:

1. **Fencing** to exclude browsers
2. **Shading** from morning sun and midday heat
3. **Mulching** with organic matter and stone for moisture retention
4. **Conservative irrigation** designed to wet 1 m depth of soil at each application
5. **Wind protection** from surrounding vegetation or artificial barriers
6. **[[companion-planting]]** with legumes and ground covers
7. **Soil amendment** appropriate to local soil conditions (gypsum, bentonite, seaweed)
8. **Sun protection** of stems until bark thickens
9. **Proper timing** during cool periods with acceptable soil temperatures

No single technique is sufficient on its own—the integrated approach dramatically improves survival rates and growth rates compared to any individual method applied alone.
