Fukuoka struggled throughout his life with the question of what to call his approach to farming. This page explores his reflections on naming, self-expression, and the impossibility of codifying natural farming.

The naming problem is not a minor semantic issue but a window into the deepest tensions in [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy]]. If nature cannot be captured in concepts, then any name given to a way of farming that works with nature will inevitably distort it.

## The Problem of Naming

"I have not the slightest idea what I should put it. To tell the truth, I began calling this 'natural farming' just for lack of a better name. There never really was any way to name this."

Fukuoka tried various names — "Gandhian farming," "Lao Tzu farming" — but felt that none of these served the purpose more or less. He saw no point in getting worked up over calling it one thing or another.

Each rejected name reveals something about what Fukuoka wanted to avoid. "Gandhian farming" would tie his approach to a specific cultural and political tradition. "Lao Tzu farming" would make it sound like a philosophical system rather than a direct engagement with the living world. Even "natural farming" — the name that stuck — troubled him, because the word "natural" had already been co-opted by the organic food industry and stripped of its radical implications.

This connects to [[fukuoka-what-is-nature]]: if we cannot say what nature is, then we cannot say what "natural" farming is either. The name points toward something real but can never fully capture it.

## No Schools of Natural Farming

"What can he mean by saying 'my way of natural farming?' There is no X or Y school of natural farming."

When a young man who had stayed on the farm for a year announced he was returning to Kyushu to start "my own way of natural farming," Fukuoka saw this as proof the young man had not succeeded in shedding his ego. Anyone who creates their own "method" or "school" has already failed to return to nature.

The creation of a school implies a set of principles that can be transmitted from teacher to student. But if natural farming cannot be taught — because it is not a body of knowledge but a mode of being — then any school is already a betrayal of the fundamental principle. This is why [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-and-spiritual-life]] emphasizes that the farmer must discover the way through their own direct experience.

The young man's error was not in wanting to farm naturally but in wanting to own the method. The ego that says "my way of natural farming" is the same ego that says "I will improve upon nature through my knowledge." Both assert the primacy of the human will over the greater intelligence of the natural world.

## The Self-Expression Trap

Before a sculptor even knew what the face of the Buddha was, he sculpted wood and proclaimed himself a "sculptor of Buddhist images." Is it really possible to call this a Buddhist image one sculpted oneself? This is self-expression. If a sculptor who understood truth and beauty were to fashion a sculpture, then even a terrible piece could be called an image of Buddha. But the question is: can any ordinary human being sculpt something that is absolute virtue, and beauty?

Fukuoka's analysis of the sculptor applies equally to the natural farming "expert" who develops a branded method. The person who truly understands nature might practice farming in any number of ways — even ways that look messy or inefficient to the outside observer. The person who does not understand will create a polished, marketable system that looks impressive but misses the essence.

This critique anticipates the contemporary problem of natural farming being packaged and sold as a lifestyle brand. The very act of systematizing natural farming for mass consumption risks transforming it into just another product of human ego.

## Artists and Nature

People who have entered an unnatural world long for the beauty of nature. They are moved by fond recollections of a distant past and take up their play to lay these idyllic memories down on canvas. This self-expression through abstract paintings is all an absurd mistake. It is the height of arrogance to use nature as a springboard to draw, write, sculpt, or engrave the soul of the Buddha into a statue.

This passage connects directly to [[fukuoka-learning-about-nature-distances-one-from-nature]]. The artist who paints nature from memory is not closer to nature than the city dweller who never thinks about it at all. Both are operating through the medium of human concepts and images. The difference is that the artist believes they are engaging with nature, while the city dweller makes no such claim.

Fukuoka's target here is not art itself but the illusion that art can serve as a bridge back to nature. In his view, the only bridge is direct, unmediated participation in the natural world — which is exactly what natural farming provides.

## The Paradox of Teaching

Before people try to understand God, they should first see that man is not in a position to understand things. When man attempts to know something, his thought and wisdom collapse into incoherence. The only thing Fukuoka felt good at was pointing out to people contradictions between what they say and what they do.

This self-assessment is both humble and precise. Fukuoka did not claim to understand nature better than anyone else; he claimed only to see the contradictions in how others related to it. The person who advocates environmental protection while driving a car to work, the person who eats organic food while supporting industrial agriculture — these contradictions were Fukuoka's constant targets.

## Naming and the Ego

The desire to name is ultimately an expression of the ego's need to control. To name something is to place it within a conceptual framework, to make it manageable and predictable. Nature, which is by definition uncontrollable and unpredictable, resists all attempts at naming. The farmer who truly practices natural farming must relinquish not only the desire to control the land but the desire to name what they are doing.

This connects to [[fukuoka-god-nature-and-man-as-one]]: just as God cannot be named or conceptualized, the practice of aligning with nature's intelligence cannot be captured in a name. Any name inevitably reduces the practice to a method, and any method is a departure from the spontaneity that nature requires.

## Implications for the Natural Farming Movement

The naming problem has practical consequences for anyone who wants to spread natural farming practices. If Fukuoka is right that any codified method betrays the essence of natural farming, then how can the practice be shared at all? Fukuoka's answer seems to be that it cannot be shared through teaching — only through example. The farmer who lives and works in harmony with nature becomes a living demonstration, and those who are ready to see will see.

This connects to [[fukuoka-nature-as-teacher]]: the real teacher is not Fukuoka or any human expert but nature itself. The role of the experienced farmer is simply to step aside enough to let nature do the teaching.

## The Marketplace of Names

In the decades since Fukuoka first raised the naming problem, the natural farming movement has splintered into dozens of named approaches: permaculture, biodynamic farming, regenerative agriculture, agroecology, holistic management, and many more. Each carries the name of its founder or a branded methodology. From Fukuoka's perspective, this proliferation of names is a symptom of the underlying problem — the human need to own, systematize, and market what should remain nameless.

This does not mean these approaches are worthless; many of them incorporate genuine insights about working with nature. But Fukuoka would caution that the moment any approach becomes a "brand" with certified practitioners and standardized methods, it has already departed from the spirit of natural farming. The [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-philosophy]] was always a provocation rather than a product.
