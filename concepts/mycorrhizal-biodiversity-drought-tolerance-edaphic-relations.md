## Overview

The functional diversity of ectomycorrhizal fungal communities on plant root systems is a critical factor in ecosystem resilience [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]] efficiency. Individual forest trees can simultaneously host communities of many ectomycorrhizal species, and this biodiversity provides **functional plasticity** — the ability to shift mycorrhizal species composition in response to changing soil conditions, nutrient availability, and environmental stresses. Additionally, mycorrhizal associations play significant roles in plant drought tolerance and mediate plant responses to disturbances such as fire.

## Species-Specific Effects on Nutrient Uptake

### Laboratory and Greenhouse Evidence

Evidence for functional differences among ectomycorrhizal (ECM) species is abundant. In many comparative studies, different ECM associates produce significantly different growth responses in the host plant (Villeneuve et al., 1991a). Key patterns include:

- Mycorrhizal plants consistently outperform non-mycorrhizal plants, especially in **disturbed situations**
- Inoculated mycorrhizal species are frequently **replaced by native mycorrhizal flora** over time (Villeneuve et al., 1991b)
- Soil type influences ECM performance: **Jones et al. (1990)** showed *[[laccaria-proxima]]* induced higher tissue phosphorus content in willow (*[[salix-viminalis]]*) than *[[thelephora-terrestris]]*

### Field Evidence: Phosphorus Uptake by Different ECM Communities

**Dighton et al. (1990)** conducted a landmark field study on birch trees using **radioactive ³²P** injection:

- Radioactive inorganic phosphorus was injected into soil zones dominated by different ECM species (identified by fruit body surveys)
- **³²P influx into leaves was highest** under trees dominated by *Hebeloma* spp.
- Lower influx was observed under trees dominated by *Laccaria* spp. or *Lactarius* spp.

Despite complexities including isotopic dilution, non-uniform canopy translocation, and imperfect correspondence between fruit bodies and actual root mycorrhizal communities, the study demonstrated that **mycorrhizal species composition significantly affects nutrient acquisition efficiency**.

### Enzyme Production Variability

Differences in enzyme production among ECM species further support functional diversity:

- **Dighton (1983)**, **Antibus et al. (1992, 1997)**, and **Leake and Read (1990b)** showed significant interspecific variation in enzyme production
- **Sinsabaugh and Liptak (1997)** demonstrated that available inorganic nutrients exert **negative feedback** on enzyme production, meaning fungi adjust their enzyme output based on ambient nutrient conditions

## Functional Plasticity of Mycorrhizal Communities

The ability of a single tree root system to maintain **multiple ECM species simultaneously** has been well documented:

| Reference | Host | Number of ECM Species Co-occurring |
|---|---|---|
| Zak and Marx (1964) | Pine | Multiple |
| Gibson and Deacon (1988) | Various | Multiple |
| Palmer et al. (1994) | Various | Multiple |
| Allen et al. (1995) | Various | Multiple |
| Shaw et al. (1995) | Various | Multiple |

This coexistence suggests that the ECM community on root systems is **functionally plastic** — capable of changing locally at both spatial and temporal scales to optimize resource utilization as local environmental conditions change. **Tibbett (2000)** confirmed that extraradical hyphae of both ericoid and ectomycorrhizal symbioses exhibit significant morphological and physiological plasticity (Bending and Read, 1995a, b; Cairney and Burke, 1996), ideally suiting them for exploiting patchily distributed nutrient resources.

## Environmental Gradients and Mycorrhizal Function

### Oligotrophic vs. Fertile Soils

Mycorrhizal function has a **more dramatic effect on plant growth in oligotrophic (nutrient-poor) systems** than in fertile systems:

- **Gehring and Whitham (1994)**: Growth of pinyon pine on cinder soils was **doubled** by ECM addition, compared to minimal effect on adjacent loam soil
- Cinder soil had half the moisture, one-third available phosphorus, and no mineralizable nitrogen compared to loam — the ECM fungi overcame **multiple simultaneous stresses**
- **Jumpponen et al. (1998)**: Growth of lodgepole pine on glacial outwash was enhanced by the dark septate mycorrhizal fungus *[[phialocephala-fortinii]]*, which improved phosphate acquisition in the nutrient-poor ecosystem

## Fire and Mycorrhizal Community Dynamics

Fire is a natural disturbance that maintains both plant and fungal diversity in forested ecosystems. Changes in ECM species composition following fire have been documented by **Visser (1995)** and **Jonsson et al. (1999a)**, revealing a succession of mycorrhizal fungi during forest re-establishment (Frankland, 1992, 1998; Boerner et al., 1996).

### Factors Influencing Post-Fire Mycorrhizal Recovery

The nature of the post-fire ECM community depends on:

1. **Degree of damage to the former mycorrhizal community** — particularly impacts on soil surface organic matter
2. **Speed of forest re-establishment** — if rapid, dying roots of the former forest act as an **inoculum source**, maintaining species diversity similar to the pre-fire community (Baar et al., 1999; Jonsson et al., 1999b)
3. **Post-fire nutrient conditions** — fire-induced mineralization and/or organic matter loss alter nutrient availability
4. **Physical soil changes** — darkened soil surface absorbs more solar radiation, increasing soil heating and affecting species survival

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae After Fire

In Australian dry sclerophyllous shrub communities, **Torpy et al. (1999)** found that fire effects on [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] were more closely related to **host plant density** than to direct fire effects on the mycorrhizae themselves. This suggests that AM community recovery is mediated primarily through changes in the host plant community rather than direct thermal effects on fungal propagules.

## Drought Tolerance Mediated by Mycorrhizae

Mycorrhizal associations significantly impact **plant-water relations** and can help alleviate drought stress through multiple mechanisms (Sánchez-Díaz and Honrubia, 1994):

### Mechanisms of Drought Protection

1. **Direct water transport**: Water flows through fungal hyphae, extending the effective root absorptive surface
2. **Improved phosphate nutrition**: Better P status improves root [[water-infiltration-soil-hydraulic-conductivity]] and osmotic adjustment
3. **Altered hormonal balance**: Mycorrhizal colonization can modify abscisic acid and other hormone levels affecting stomatal regulation

### Evidence from Experimental Studies

**Augé (2001)** reviewed the effects of arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization on drought response and found that AM fungi improved water relations under **both drought conditions and adequate water supply**.

**Michelsen and Rosendahl (1990)** showed that AM inoculation of *Acacia nilotica* and *Leucaena leucocephala* had species-specific effects:

- AM inoculation mirrored the effect of adding phosphorus for both species
- The AM effect was **greater than P addition alone** for *Leucaena* under drought stress
- *Leucaena* benefited most from AM under drought conditions

**Cruz et al. (2000)** demonstrated drought protection in papaya: the decrease in leaf water potential during drought was **less severe** in AM plants than in non-mycorrhizal plants.

### Endophytic Fungi and Drought Tolerance

Drought protection is not limited to mycorrhizal endophytes. **Belesky and Malinowski (2000)** showed that leaf endophytes of grasses confer drought tolerance through production of **loline alkaloids** that act as osmoregulators. However, **Cheplick et al. (2000)** found no drought benefit from endophytes in *Lolium perenne* — in fact, growth was lower with the endophyte under both drought and normal conditions, indicating that fungal endophyte effects are **environmentally context-dependent**.

### Saprotrophic Fungi and Drought Tolerance

**Osonubi et al. (1990)** demonstrated that inoculation of *[[gmelina-arborea]]* (a Nigerian pulpwood tree) with the saprotrophic fungus *[[chaetomium-bostrychoides]]*:

- Increased seed germination
- Significantly increased drought tolerance

| Treatment | Shoot Biomass After Drought (g) | Root Biomass After Drought (g) |
|---|---|---|
| Inoculum onto seed, droughted | **9.36** | **4.47** |
| Inoculum onto seed, undroughted | 5.89 | 2.65 |
| Inoculum at seedling, droughted | 6.92 | — |
| Inoculum at seedling, undroughted | 5.67 | — |

The fact that inoculated droughted plants outperformed uninoculated undroughted plants demonstrates the profound effect of fungal associations on drought resilience.

## The Broader Significance of Mycorrhizal Diversity

**Fitter (1985)** suggested that because the nutritional benefit of mycorrhizae in natural ecosystems is often less dramatic than in controlled experiments, other benefits — such as drought tolerance, pathogen protection, and soil stabilization — may be the primary evolutionary rationale for maintaining the mycorrhizal habit. The diversity of mycorrhizal fungal species on individual root systems provides:

- **Functional redundancy**: If one species is compromised by environmental change, others may compensate
- **Resource optimization**: Different species can be favored as local conditions change spatially or temporally
- **Stress resilience**: Diverse communities are better able to buffer against multiple simultaneous stresses (drought, nutrient limitation, disturbance)

## Summary

Mycorrhizal biodiversity provides critical functional plasticity to forest ecosystems. Individual trees host diverse ECM communities that can shift composition in response to changing nutrient availability, soil conditions, and disturbances like fire. Mycorrhizal associations enhance drought tolerance through direct water transport, improved nutrition, and hormonal effects. The mycorrhizal habit likely persists in many ecosystems not because of its nutritional benefits alone, but because it provides a suite of services — drought protection, pathogen defense, soil stabilization, and nutrient access — that collectively enhance host plant fitness across variable environmental conditions.
