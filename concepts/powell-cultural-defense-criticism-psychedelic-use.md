# Cultural Defense and Criticism of Psychedelic Use

The reception of psilocybin in Western intellectual culture has been
marked by both enthusiastic advocacy and fierce criticism. Simon G.
Powell's The Psilocybin Solution examines the key figures who shaped the
debate, exploring why some embraced the mushroom as a tool of
transformation while others rejected it, often on grounds that reveal
more about the critic's psychological state than about the substance
itself.

## Koestler's Rejection of Psychedelic Technology

Arthur Koestler, the renowned author of Darkness at Noon, represents one
of the most significant intellectual rejections of psychedelics. Despite
acknowledging mankind's violent, paranoid, destructive streak in his
Ghost in the Machine, and calling for techniques to supplant biological
evolution, Koestler refused to endorse natural psychedelic plants as a
solution. He argued that it was fundamentally wrong to expect that drugs
could confer free gifts upon the mind, assuming that drugs cannot put
something into the mind that is not already there.

Koestler perceived psilocybin as a technological product, largely because
his experience came in the form of a Sandoz pill, the perfect symbol of a
modern technological fix. This contrasts sharply with the wild mushroom
as a natural symbol. Powell suggests that Koestler's negative attitude
stemmed principally from his painful memories as a prisoner of war and
unresolved conflicts in his psyche. Leary concluded that Koestler's
objections arose from a deep-seated Catholic guilt, a guilt that arises
easily in the face of pleasure, ecstasy, and human freedom.

## Baudelaire and the Pattern of Rejection

The pattern of initial enthusiasm followed by vehement rejection is not
unique to Koestler. The 19th century French poet Baudelaire eventually
came to oppose psychoactive substances after having once used opium and
cannabis for creative purposes. Like other writers of his era including
Byron, Shelley, Balzac, De Quincey, and Coleridge, Baudelaire had
experienced psychoactive plant products but later came to despise them as
the root of evil and misleading, treating them as the cunning tools of
the Devil for thwarting mankind from reaching God.

Powell argues that the point is almost deliberately missed. These plant
substances are not inherently evil; rather, they become destructive if
used in excess or for the wrong reasons, much as any benign substance can
become harmful if used beyond sensibility. The stream of psilocybin's
gloriously numinous potential can hit the wrong human mind, or at least
the wrong state of mind, causing the stream to be blocked so that it
cannot flow and blossom.

## Robert Graves and the Warning of Self-Knowledge

The poet Robert Graves, who originally notified R. Gordon Wasson of the
secret mushroom ceremonies in Mexico, tried psilocybin in Wasson's New
York apartment in 1960. Graves was apprehensive about perceiving demons
behind his closed eyes. As it transpired, he need not have worried. He
saw a mountain-top Eden and experienced the bliss of innocence and the
knowledge of good and evil. He felt capable of solving any problem in
the world.

Graves predicted that a once sacred substance entrusted to an elite few
would soon be sought out by jaded sensation seekers, though they would
likely be dissatisfied since psilocybin was not a drug in the stupefying
sense. His warning remains relevant: whoever prepares to eat
hallucinogenic mushrooms should take as careful stock of their maldous-huxleyal well-being as initiates took before attending the Eleusinian
Mysteries. The peculiar virtue of [[psilocybin]], the power to enhance
personal reality, turns Know Thyself into a practical precept.

## Huxley's Optimistic Vision

[[aldous-huxley]] explicitly summed up the early mood of optimism surrounding
psychedelics in a 1961 speech to psychologists. He predicted that
psychological science would inevitably be confronted with data on the
visionary experiences induced by substances like [[psilocybin]]. If these
experiences were cooperated with, and if some deep meaning was ascertained
and acted upon, this could be crucial in changing the lot of humanity.

Huxley conjectured that our mode of consciousness could be altered by
psychedelics, unleashing a psychological force enabling transformation.
His Utopian novel Island, published in 1962, elaborated this vision of a
society that had integrated psychedelic substances into its spiritual
and therapeutic practices. Huxley's vision in The Doors of Perception
of a mass-marketed psychedelic that enlightens the world cannot be
faulted on its technological methodology.

## The Problem of Sensation Seekers

Both Wasson and Graves expressed dismay at the hordes of thrill seekers
and riff-raff descending upon Mexico in search of the divine mushrooms.
Graves remarked that jaded sensation seekers would likely be dissatisfied
with [[psilocybin]] since it was not a stupefying drug like alcohol. This
reaction to popular interest was inevitable, but Powell argues that to
deny the mushroom to the masses is an impractical, short-sighted reaction
to basic human nature.

Knowledge of [[psilocybin]]'s potentially supra-mundane power is best laid
open to all who might wish to seek it out. If this be considered casting
pearls before swine, so be it. The end justifies the means: a culture
transformed with revitalized veneration for the natural systems of the
Earth and a deeper insight into the ultimate nature of the reality
process.

## The Scientific Blind Eye

The scientific study of entheogens has been hampered by the
compartmentalization of science into separate disciplines. To properly
understand the entire complex of the entheogenic experience, a marriage
must be made between psychology, phenomenology, anthropology,
ethnobotany, and even metaphysics. Powell calls this proposed enterprise
neo-shamanic phenomenology.

It is almost unheard of for the witnessing ethnobotanist or anthropologist
to actually experience the visionary brew for themselves. The most
important ingredient, the experience, is generally not witnessed. The
discipline of phenomenology, the study of immediate experience and its
implications for psychology, provides a legitimate scientific framework
for personal exploration. To personally partake of shamanic substances
is to glean insight into the psychological forces they set in motion.

## Psychedelic Substances as Cultural Healing Agents

Despite the obvious dangers posed by psychedelic substances, such as
their capacity to induce intense psychical terror, native cultures have
managed to tame them through ritual, preparation, and cultural
integration. The psychedelic link between humans and Nature operates at
a different level from purely utilitarian relationships with
environmental resources, offering spiritual nourishment that can have a
transformative cultural role.

In native societies without access to modern healthcare or pharmaceutical
products, the curative role of the shaman becomes essential, with natural
plant allies serving as tools of the healing trade. This Gaian
psychotherapy, where one part of the environment acts upon another to
restore harmony, represents a model of healing that Western culture has
largely abandoned but may need to rediscover.
