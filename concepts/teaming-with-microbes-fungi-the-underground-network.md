# Fungi — The Underground Network

Fungi play a key role in the [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]] and are an essential tool for gardeners using soil food web principles. Over 100,000 species are known, with perhaps a million more undiscovered. Once classified as plants without chlorophyll, fungi are now placed in their own kingdom in the domain Eukarya because they cannot photosynthesize and build their cell walls from chitin instead of cellulose.

A teaspoon of good garden soil contains several yards of fungal hyphae, invisible to the naked eye. Millions upon millions merge to produce mushrooms and other fruiting bodies.

## Structure and growth

Fungi are eukaryotes with cells containing distinct, enclosed nuclei. Each cell can have more than one nucleus. They grow from spores into thread-like structures called hyphae (singular: hypha). A single hyphal strand is divided by walls (septa) that allow liquids to flow between cells. Masses of invisible hyphae growing in proximity form visible threads called mycelia (singular: mycelium). Fungi reproduce by spores, never by seeds.

A fungal hypha is considerably larger than a bacterium — 2 to 15 micrometers long with a diameter of 0.2 to 3.5 micrometers. It takes hundreds of thousands of individual hyphal strands to form a network thick enough to see. Unlike bacteria, fungal hyphae can grow in length measured in feet or meters. Fungi also do not need a film of water to spread through soil, allowing them to bridge gaps and transport nutrients over considerable distances.

Fungi can grow up to 40 micrometers per minute — incredibly fast for such tiny organisms. Compare this to a typical soil bacterium that may travel only 6 micrometers in its entire life.

## The decay powerhouse

Fungi are the primary decay agents in the [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]]. While some prefer simple sugars, most fungi specialize in tougher foods. They produce phenol oxidase, a strong enzyme that dissolves even lignin — the woody compound that binds and protects cellulose. No other soil organism can break down lignin effectively.

Fungi can penetrate hard surfaces including the chitin shells of insects, animal bones, and even human fingernails. [[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria-the-smallest-workers]] can hold their own, but they require simpler-to-digest foods, often the by-products of fungal decay. Compared to fungi, bacteria are in the Minor Leagues of decaying ability.

### Apical growth

Fungal growth occurs at the hyphal tip through apical growth — an engineering feat akin to building a tunnel under a river. A dark spot called the Spitzenkorper in the tip controls or directs this growth. Cytoplasm transports vesicles loaded with construction supplies to the advancing tip while powerful enzymes capable of dissolving all but the most recalcitrant carbon compounds are released. These enzymes convert lignin, cellulose, and tough organic matter into simple sugars and amino acids, yet they do not decay the fungi's own chitin cell walls.

## Nutrient transport

Fungal hyphae contain cytoplasm circulated throughout their cells. When a hyphal tip invades a nematode, it drains the victim of nutrients and distributes them through the hyphal cytoplasm to locations several yards away — functioning like a conveyor belt. Once inside the fungus, nutrients are immobilized and will not be lost from soil.

This ability to transport nutrients from one location to another is a key difference from bacteria. Fungi can locate new food sources and bring nutrients from distant locations back to the root zone.

## Fungal feeding and [[teaming-with-microbes-mineralization-and-immobilization]]

Fungi produce acidic digestive substances that break organic materials into compounds they ingest through cell walls via diffusion (osmosis) and active transport. Nutrients taken in are usually immobilized, just as with bacteria. Much of the nitrogen released by fungi is in ammonium form (NH4+).

Some fungi trade nutrients for root exudates directly. Nutrients not delivered to plant roots are locked up in the fungi and released when the fungi die and are decayed by other organisms.

## Impact on [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-ph-and-biology]]

The enzymes produced by fungi are decidedly acidic and lower soil pH. As soils become dominated by fungi, populations of nitrifying bacteria diminish because pH drops below 7. More ammonium therefore remains as plant-available ammonium instead of being converted to nitrates.

This means [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]] tend to have nitrogen in ammonium form — preferred by trees, shrubs, and perennials (Rule #3). In bacterially dominated soils (Rule #2), bacterial slime keeps pH above 7, nitrifying bacteria thrive, and nitrogen converts to nitrate — preferred by vegetables and annuals.

## Soil structure contributions

When fungi die, their hyphae leave a subway system of microscopic tunnels (up to 10 micrometers in diameter) through which air and water can flow. These tunnels also serve as safety zones for bacteria trying to elude protozoa — protozoa are considerably bigger than the tunnels.

Fungal hyphae travel through soil particles, binding them together into aggregates, much like bacterial biofilm but on a larger scale. This contribution to soil structure is one of the most important but underappreciated functions of soil fungi.

## [[teaming-with-microbes-mycorrhizal-associations]]

Among the most important soil fungi are mycorrhizae — symbiotic associations between plant roots and fungi. At least 90-95% of all plants form mycorrhizae, relationships that began 450 million years ago. In return for root exudates, mycorrhizal fungi extend effective root surface area by 700 to 1000 times and mine phosphorus, copper, calcium, magnesium, zinc, and iron from locations roots cannot reach.

## Clever adaptations

Soil fungi have evolved remarkable strategies:
- **Arthrobotrys dactyloides** creates three-cell hyphal rings that swell in a tenth of a second to trap nematodes, attracted by chemical lures
- **Pleurotus ostreatus** (oyster mushroom) emits toxic drops from hyphal tips that immobilize nematodes on contact
- Other fungi use adhesives to stick to nematodes, protozoa, and even springtails
- Some fungi send out scout filaments and demonstrate chemical-sensing capabilities to locate prey

These predatory fungi play an important role in the soil food web as regulators of nematode and microarthropod populations. In agricultural systems, nematode-trapping fungi can serve as biological control agents against plant-parasitic nematodes, reducing crop damage without nematicides. The presence of predatory fungi is generally an indicator of a mature, balanced soil ecosystem, as they require stable conditions and adequate organic matter to sustain their predatory lifestyle.

## Ectomycorrhizal vs. Endomycorrhizal Fungi

The two major categories of mycorrhizal associations differ in structure and host range:

- **Ectomycorrhizal fungi** (ECM) form a dense sheath (mantle) around root tips and a Hartig net between root cells. They associate primarily with trees in the Pinaceae, Fagaceae, and Betulaceae families — pines, oaks, beeches, and birches. ECM fungi produce visible fruiting bodies (many wild mushrooms, including chanterelles, boletes, and truffles, are ectomycorrhizal). They are particularly effective at mining phosphorus and nitrogen from organic matter in forest soils.
- **Endomycorrhizal (arbuscular mycorrhizal) fungi** (AMF) penetrate root cells internally, forming arbuscules (tree-like structures) that facilitate nutrient exchange. They associate with the vast majority of herbaceous plants, grasses, and many tropical trees. AMF do not produce visible fruiting bodies and are identified primarily through root staining and microscopic examination. They are especially important in agricultural and grassland soils.

Understanding which type of mycorrhizal association a plant forms helps guide companion planting and inoculation strategies in permaculture and ecological design.

## Pathogenic fungi

Soil contains both beneficial and pathogenic fungi. Common garden pathogens include botrytis (gray mold), powdery mildew, fusarium wilt, downy mildew, root rots (Phytophthora), and Armillaria mellea (oak root fungus). Beneficial fungi and bacteria compete with these pathogens for nutrients, space, and oxygen. Mycorrhizal fungi form protective webs that physically block invasion.

Managing pathogenic fungi in a soil food web approach focuses on prevention rather than eradication. A diverse and active fungal community provides competitive exclusion — when beneficial species occupy available niches and consume root exudates, pathogens find fewer footholds. Practices that support beneficial fungi (woody mulch, minimal disturbance, appropriate moisture) simultaneously suppress pathogen establishment. This contrasts with the chemical approach of broad-spectrum fungicides, which often kill beneficial fungi along with pathogens and can create vacated niches that pathogens colonize faster than beneficial species recover.

## Functional overlap with bacteria

In a healthy soil food web, fungi and bacteria share many functions. Both produce vitamins and antibiotics that kill soil pathogens. Both retain and mineralize nutrients. Both contribute to soil structure. The key difference is that fungi handle complex, lignin-rich materials while bacteria prefer simpler sugars and green materials.

## Fungal succession in soil development

Soil fungal communities shift predictably through ecological succession. In recently disturbed or barren soils, pioneer fungi are typically fast-growing opportunists (zoochores) that colonize from spores deposited by wind or animals. These early successional fungi tolerate higher pH and bacterial competition. As organic matter accumulates and perennial plants establish, the community shifts toward slower-growing, cellulose- and lignin-decomposing fungi. Mature ecosystems such as old-growth forests develop highly diverse fungal communities dominated by ectomycorrhizal species, with extensive mycelial networks connecting individual trees into a shared resource grid.

This succession pattern has direct implications for [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]]. Tilling disrupts hyphal networks and sets succession back, favoring bacteria over fungi. No-till practices, perennial plantings, and surface applications of woody mulch promote fungal dominance — which is why forest gardening and food forest approaches tend to develop fungally dominated soils over time.

## Glomalin and long-term carbon storage

A specific contribution of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) to soil health is the production of glomalin, a glycoprotein secreted by hyphae that coats soil particles and binds them into stable aggregates. Glomalin is remarkably persistent in soil, with a turnover time estimated at 7 to 42 years, and contributes significantly to soil organic carbon pools. It improves water retention, reduces erosion, and protects soil carbon from microbial decomposition. This mechanism represents one of the most important pathways by which perennial agricultural systems sequester atmospheric carbon, making fungal conservation a key strategy in climate-adaptive farming.

## Gardening with fungi in mind

Practical steps to encourage beneficial soil fungi include:

- **Minimize soil disturbance**: Tilling breaks hyphal networks. No-till or reduced-till methods preserve the fungal subway system described above.
- **Apply woody mulch**: Wood chips, leaf litter, and straw provide the complex carbon substrates that fungi specialize in decomposing. A diverse mulch layer supports diverse fungal species.
- **Maintain perennial plantings**: Perennials support mycorrhizal networks year-round, unlike annuals that die back and leave roots to decompose bacterially each season.
- **Avoid excessive phosphorus fertilization**: High available phosphorus suppresses mycorrhizal colonization, as plants no longer need the fungal partnership for phosphorus uptake. This is a common unintended consequence of synthetic fertilizer use.
- **Use compost teas and fungal inoculants**: Aerated compost teas can introduce beneficial fungal propagules to soil, and commercial mycorrhizal inoculants are available for transplanting and establishing new plantings.

## Endophytic Fungi and Plant Health

Beyond mycorrhizal associations, endophytic fungi live within plant tissues — leaves, stems, and roots — without causing visible disease symptoms. These fungi contribute to plant defense by producing alkaloids and other secondary metabolites that deter herbivorous insects and grazing animals. In grasses, endophytes such as *Epichloë festucae* confer drought tolerance, disease resistance, and improved phosphorus uptake. Some endophytic relationships are mutualistic under moderate stress but can become parasitic under high-nutrient conditions, illustrating the context-dependent nature of fungal-plant symbioses described throughout the [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]] literature.
