# [[cannabis-safety-profile|Cannabis Safety Profile]] and Subjective Therapeutic Window

## Overview

[[uwe-blesching|Uwe Blesching]]'s *The [[cannabis-health-index|Cannabis Health Index]]* (2015) provides a comprehensive safety assessment of cannabis, drawing on his 20 years of experience as a paramedic and extensive review of government health statistics. His analysis places cannabis at the extreme low-risk end of the psychoactive substance spectrum, while emphasizing the importance of dosing discipline through the concept of the "subjective therapeutic window."

## Mortality Data — Cannabis vs. Other Substances

### Zero Cannabis-Only Deaths

Blesching cites U.S. government sources reporting **zero deaths due to the exclusive use of cannabis** during the study period of January 1997 through June 2005. This finding is contrasted with mortality data for other commonly used substances:

| Substance | Average Annual Deaths | Period |
|---|---|---|
| Tobacco (cigarettes) | 110,750 | 2000-2004 (CDC) |
| Alcohol | 75,766 | 2001 (CDC) |
| 17 FDA-approved pharmaceuticals | 10,008 total | 1997-2005 (FDA) |
| Cannabis (alone) | 0 | 1997-2005 |

### Emergency Services Perspective

From Blesching's two decades in emergency medical services, the frequency of 911 calls related to substance use followed a clear hierarchy:

1. **Nicotine/tobacco** — most frequent, due to lasting organ damage
2. **Alcohol abuse** — close second
3. **Heroin, stimulants** (cocaine, crack, methamphetamine, diet pills), PCP
4. **Psychogenic substances** (LSD, ecstasy, psilocybin) — occasional calls
5. **Cannabis alone** — virtually no calls in 20 years of experience

Blesching notes that while government statistics show large numbers of "cannabis-related" emergency room visits, closer examination reveals that the majority involved cannabis combined with other drugs, or were anxiety-related incidents rather than medical emergencies.

## Adverse Effects Profile

### Common Adverse Effects

| Effect | Frequency | Severity | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Increased appetite | Very common | Mild | "Munchies" — often beneficial for patients with cachexia |
| Reduced attention span | Common | Mild | Transient, resolves after effects wear off |
| Red sclera (bloodshot eyes) | Common | Cosmetic only | No health risk |
| Dry mouth (cottonmouth) | Common | Mild | Manageable with hydration |
| Decreased cognitive/motor skills | Common | Moderate | Do not drive or operate machinery |

### Less Common Adverse Effects (Higher Doses)

| Effect | Trigger | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Ataxia (unsteady gait) | Higher doses, oral ingestion | Sit or lie down; effects pass |
| Aphasia (difficulty speaking) | High oral doses | Reassurance; temporary |
| Unusual sensory perceptions | Very high doses | Not inherently dangerous |
| Anxiety | Exceeding therapeutic window | Reassurance, calming environment |
| Slight heart rate increase | Normal pharmacological response | Self-limiting |
| Blood pressure shifts | Position-dependent | Change position slowly |
| Panic (first use) | Novelty + high dose | Reassurance most effective |

### Critical Insight: Dose-Dependent Adverse Effects

Blesching emphasizes that the vast majority of adverse effects are directly related to exceeding the subjective therapeutic window. The same cannabis that reduces anxiety at the proper dose can multiply anxiety if taken too rapidly or in excessive quantity.

## The Subjective Therapeutic Window

### Definition

The subjective therapeutic window is the personalized dose range at which cannabis produces desired [[cervantes-terpenoid-pharmacology-therapeutic-effects]] without adverse effects. It is "subjective" because it varies between individuals based on:

1. **The body's present endocannabinoid state** — relative balance or deficiency
2. **The cannabinoid profile** in the specific medicine being used (THC:[[cannabis-thc-cbd-ratio-guide-for-conditions]])
3. **The form of consumption** (inhalation vs. ingestion vs. topical)

### Establishing the Window — Inhalation Protocol

Blesching provides a precise step-by-step protocol for vapor inhalation:

1. Take **one short to medium inhalation**
2. **Wait 5 to 10 minutes** — do not be impatient
3. Evaluate: did this achieve the desired effect?
4. If not, take another inhalation of approximately the same length
5. Wait another 5 to 10 minutes
6. Continue incrementally until the "sweet spot" is found
7. **Stop** — do not inhale more. "More is not better."
