# Faires Stock and Bone Broth

Stock and bone broth are among the most valuable and overlooked foods in a permaculture kitchen. They transform animal bones, vegetable scraps, and other kitchen leftovers into nutrient-dense liquid that serves as the foundation for soups, stews, sauces, and gravies. In a system that values producing no waste, making stock from bones and scraps is an essential practice that extracts maximum nutrition from every animal harvested and every vegetable processed.

## The Difference Between Stock and Broth

While the terms are often used interchangeably, there are practical distinctions:

Broth is typically made from meat and simmered for a shorter time (1-2 hours). It has a lighter flavour and thinner consistency. It can be eaten on its own as a clear soup.

Stock is made primarily from bones and simmered for a much longer time (6-24 hours or more). The long simmering extracts collagen, gelatin, minerals, and other nutrients from the bones and connective tissue. Stock has a richer mouthfeel (from gelatin) and deeper flavour.

Bone broth is essentially the same as long-simmered stock, with particular emphasis on the health benefits of the extracted collagen and minerals. The term has become popular in recent years, but the practice is ancient and universal.

## The Value of Bones

Bones are often discarded after butchering, but they contain significant nutritional value:
- Collagen and gelatin: Supportive for joint health, skin, hair, and digestive system
- Minerals: Calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, and trace minerals are leached from bones during long simmering
- Marrow: Rich in healthy fats and fat-soluble vitamins
- Glycine and proline: Amino acids that support immune function and tissue repair

In the context of the whole animal utilisation that permaculture demands, bones represent a significant portion of the animal's embodied energy. Discarding them wastes this resource. Making broth captures and makes available these nutrients.

## Making Basic Stock

### Ingredients

The beauty of stock is that it can be made from almost anything:
- Bones from any animal (chicken, beef, pork, fish, rabbit, goat, deer)
- Vegetable scraps (onion ends, carrot peels, celery leaves, garlic skins)
- Herbs and spices (bay leaf, peppercorns, thyme, parsley stems)
- Apple cider vinegar (helps extract minerals from bones)
- Water (enough to cover the ingredients)

### The Process

1. Collect bones and vegetable scraps in a container in the freezer until you have enough for a batch. Chicken carcasses from roasted chicken are ideal. Beef and pork bones benefit from roasting first for deeper flavour.

2. Place the bones in a large pot. If using raw beef or pork bones, roast them in the oven at 400 degrees F (200 degrees C) for 30-45 minutes first. This caramelises the proteins and creates a richer, darker stock.

3. Cover with cold water, leaving an inch or two of headspace.

4. Add 1-2 tablespoons of apple cider vinegar. Let sit for 30 minutes -- the acid helps draw minerals out of the bones.

5. Bring to a boil, then reduce to a bare simmer. A rolling boil will emulsify the fat into the stock, making it cloudy. A gentle simmer keeps the stock clear.

6. Simmer for the appropriate time:
   - Fish stock: 30-60 minutes
   - Chicken stock: 4-6 hours (or up to 12 for maximum extraction)
   - Beef or pork stock: 12-24 hours
   - The longer the simmer, the more nutrients are extracted

7. Skim any foam or scum that rises to the surface during the first hour of simmering. This is coagulated protein and impurities that affect the flavour.

8. Add vegetables and herbs during the last hour of cooking. Adding them earlier results in mushy, overcooked vegetables that contribute little.

9. Strain the stock through a fine mesh strainer or cheesecloth.

10. Let the stock cool, then refrigerate. The fat will rise to the top and solidify, forming a seal that extends the stock's shelf life. Remove the fat before using (or save it for cooking).

### Storage

- Refrigerated stock lasts 4-5 days
- Frozen stock lasts 6 months or more
- Pressure-canned stock lasts for years
- Reduced stock ( simmered until concentrated) freezes in smaller containers and takes less space

## Specialised Stocks

### Chicken Stock

Chicken stock is the most commonly made and most versatile. After roasting a chicken, the carcass (with any remaining meat, skin, and fat) goes directly into the stock pot. Chicken feet, if available, add significant gelatin. Necks and backs from butchering are excellent stock material.

### Beef Stock

Beef stock benefits from roasted bones and long simmering. Knuckle bones, marrow bones, and oxtail all contribute different qualities. Beef stock is the foundation for French onion soup, beef stews, and rich gravies.

### Fish Stock

Fish stock is made from fish heads, bones, and trimmings. It simmers for a much shorter time (30-60 minutes) because the delicate flavours can become bitter with extended cooking. Fish stock is the base for chowders, bouillabaisse, and seafood risottos.

### Vegetable Stock

Vegetable stock uses no animal products at all. It is made by simmering vegetable scraps, herbs, and sometimes mushrooms or seaweed. While it lacks the gelatin of bone-based stocks, it is a flavourful base for vegetarian soups and sauces.

## Bone Broth for Health

The long-simmered bone broth that has become popular in health circles is essentially the same as traditional stock, with these specific practices:

### Key Factors

1. Quality of bones: Use bones from pasture-raised, grass-fed animals. The nutritional quality of the broth depends on the quality of the bones.

2. Acid addition: Apple cider vinegar (1-2 tablespoons per pot) is essential for extracting minerals from the bones. The acid breaks down the bone matrix and releases calcium, magnesium, and other minerals into the broth.

3. Long simmering: 12-24 hours for chicken, 24-48 hours for beef. The extended time maximises extraction of collagen, gelatin, and minerals.

4. Adding joints and feet: Chicken feet, beef knuckles, and other joint-rich bones contribute the most gelatin. Gelatin gives the broth its characteristic thick, jelly-like consistency when cooled.

5. Consuming regularly: Bone broth is most beneficial when consumed daily or several times per week.

### Nutritional Benefits

Bone broth provides:
- Collagen and gelatin for joint, skin, and gut health
- Easily absorbable minerals (calcium, magnesium, phosphorus)
- Glycine, an amino acid that supports sleep quality and immune function
- Glucosamine and chondroitin, compounds that support joint health
- A warming, easily digested food for recovery from illness

## Clarified Fat and Bullion Cubes

The Faires guide describes a method for making bullion-style cubes from rendered fat:

When making clarified fat, cut up onions, carrots, leeks, turnips, herbs, and spices, and add salt and pepper to the fat. Once it is liquefied, pour it through cheesecloth and allow it to cool. The flavoured, clarified fat can be used directly in cooking or cut into cubes for convenient portioning.

This technique combines the principles of stock-making with fat rendering, creating a concentrated flavour base that stores at room temperature for years.

## Integration with the Permaculture System

Bone broth and stock-making fit naturally into the permaculture kitchen:

- Bones from [[faires-rabbit-and-small-poultry-keeping]], [[faires-duck-and-goose-keeping]], and larger livestock are never wasted
- Vegetable scraps from garden harvesting and meal preparation become stock ingredients
- The [[natural-farm-composting-vermicomposting]] system receives the spent bones and vegetables after broth-making (they are partially broken down and decompose faster)
- Gelatin-rich stock thickens soups and sauces without the need for flour or cornstarch thickeners
- Stock-making is a low-energy cooking activity -- once brought to a simmer, it can be maintained on a woodstove or the lowest heat setting
- The practice of saving scraps and bones for stock embodies the principle of producing no waste

## Organ Meat Broths

Adding organ meats to broth is an excellent way to incorporate these nutrient-dense foods into the diet. Liver, heart, and kidney can be simmered along with bones to create especially rich broths. Alternatively, organ meats can be cooked separately and the cooking liquid saved as a fortified broth base.
