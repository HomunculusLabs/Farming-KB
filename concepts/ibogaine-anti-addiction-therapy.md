# Ibogaine Anti-Addiction Therapy

Ibogaine is an [[aminopropyl-silica-gel-chromatography-indole-alkaloid-purification]] derived from the [[iboga-root-bark-preparation]] [[tabernanthe-iboga]]. It has attracted significant attention for its reported
ability to interrupt opioid dependence, reduce withdrawal symptoms, and
facilitate long-term abstinence from addictive substances. Despite promising
anecdotal and observational evidence, ibogaine remains controversial due to

ibogaine were first described in Western scientific literature [[allegro-plants-and-drugs-in-the-ancient-world]]
1960s by Howard Lotsof, a heroin-dependent young man who observed that a
single ibogaine experience eliminated his cravings and withdrawal symptoms.
Lotsof subsequently dedicated his life to advocating for ibogaine research
and treatment access.

## Pharmacological Profile

Ibogaine has an exceptionally complex pharmacology, interacting with multiple
receptor systems simultaneously. It is a serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonist, a
NMDA receptor antagonist, a kappa opioid receptor agonist, and a sodium
channel blocker. It also affects dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine
transporters, and interacts with several other receptor types including
acetylcholine receptors [[allegro-heavenly-twins-and-the-mushroom-hermaphrodite]] sigma-2 receptor.

This polypharmacological profile distinguishes ibogaine from other
psychedelics and may contribute to its unique anti-addictive effects. The
NMDA receptor antagonism, in particular, shares a mechanism with ketamine's
rapid antidepressant action and may contribute to neuroplastic changes that
underlie ibogaine's therapeutic effects.

## Active Metabolite: Noribogaine

Ibogaine is metabolized in the liver primarily by CYP2D6 to noribogaine
(12-hydroxyibogamine), which may contribute significantly to its therapeutic
effects. Noribogaine has a much longer half-life than ibogaine itself,
persisting in the body for days to weeks after administration. It retains
activity at serotonin transporters and opioid receptors, suggesting that
noribogaine may mediate the sustained anti-craving effects observed after
ibogaine treatment.

The CYP2D6 polymorphism has important clinical implications for ibogaine
therapy. Individuals who are poor CYP2D6 metabolizers may experience higher
and more prolonged ibogaine levels, potentially increasing the risk of adverse
effects. Genetic testing for CYP2D6 status is recommended prior to ibogaine
treatment in clinics that offer such screening.

## Mechanism of Anti-Addictive Action

The mechanism by which ibogaine interrupts addiction is not fully understood
but likely involves multiple converging pathways. The kappa opioid receptor
agonism may regulate the dysphoric and stress-related components of withdrawal
and craving. NMDA receptor antagonism may facilitate the resetting of
maladaptive neural circuits that underlie addictive behavior.

Ibogaine also produces a profound [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]] lasting 12 to 36
hours, during which users frequently report vivid autobiographical memories
and emotional processing of traumatic experiences. This psychological
component may contribute to therapeutic outcomes by facilitating insight into
the root causes of addictive behavior and promoting motivation for change.

## Safety and Cardiac Risk

The most significant safety concern with ibogaine is its potential to cause
cardiac arrhythmias, specifically QT interval prolongation and torsades de
pointes. Ibogaine blocks the hERG potassium channel, which can lead to
life-threatening cardiac events, particularly in individuals with pre-existing
cardiac conditions or electrolyte imbalances.

Several deaths have been reported in association with ibogaine treatment,
primarily attributed to cardiac complications. This risk profile has led
regulatory agencies in many countries to prohibit ibogaine treatment outside
of approved research settings. Clinics that offer ibogaine therapy typically
require comprehensive cardiac screening including ECG, echocardiogram, and
blood work prior to treatment.
