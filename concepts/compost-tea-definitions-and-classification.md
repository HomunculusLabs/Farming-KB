## Overview

Compost tea is broadly defined as a water extract of compost that is brewed — typically with nutrients added to enhance beneficial microbial growth. However, the term has been applied inconsistently over time to a wide range of preparations with very different properties. Elaine Ingham"s work with the [[soil-foodweb-institute]] (SFI) provides a rigorous taxonomy that distinguishes the various production methods, clarifies what qualifies as true compost tea, and highlights the dangers of misapplied terminology.

## Four Production Methods

### Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT)

This is the most useful tea for improving plant-growing conditions. AACT is produced by extracting organisms from compost into water while actively maintaining dissolved oxygen levels above 6 ppm throughout the brewing process. The active aeration ensures that aerobic bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes survive and multiply. AACT is fundamentally a **preventative** — not a pesticide. When a vineyard or field has been in serious disease mode for years, weekly applications may be required to establish the beneficial organisms, help them survive, grow, and out-compete the disease community.

### Fermentative Compost Tea (FCT)

FCT selects for growth of facultative [[ingham-anaerobic-organisms-role-soil]] through a fermentative process. These teas can suppress certain diseases through antibiotic-inhibitory interactions — a mechanism fundamentally different from the competitive exclusion used by aerobic teas. While FCT has specific applications, it does not provide the full range of benefits associated with aerobic methods.

### Long-Brewing Compost Tea (LBCT)

LBCT starts aerobic but passes through an anaerobic phase before returning to aerobic conditions by the end of the brewing process. This method can produce variable results depending on the duration and severity of the anaerobic phase. The transition through [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] may kill sensitive beneficial organisms, limiting the tea's effectiveness.

### Not-Aerobic Compost Tea (NACT)

In NACT, anaerobic bacteria grow for the majority of the brewing process, producing toxic materials that can prevent growth of particular organisms. These teas operate through toxic inhibition rather than competitive exclusion and may pose risks to human health. NACT products could be considered pesticides and should be registered with the EPA.

## Formal Definitions

### Compost Tea

A water extract of **compost** (not manure, unless fully composted) that is brewed with foods selecting for beneficial organism growth. To retain all organism groups present in the source compost, the tea must remain aerobic throughout brewing.

### Plant Tea

A water extract of plant materials (no compost) brewed for some period, with or without added nutrients for enhancing beneficial organism life. Named after the plant material used (e.g., "marigold tea").

### Manure Tea

Made with manure. Important: if material has been fully composted, it becomes compost tea. Partly composted material is not finished compost and should not be treated as such.

### Compost Extract

Compost extracts lack the brewing period — organisms have not been given time to wake up or receive additional encouragement. They contain a subset of the compost biology but lack the population growth achieved through brewing.

### Compost Leachate

A passive process where water moves through compost. Unlike tea and extracts, leachate does not involve physical extraction of organisms from compost. It primarily contains soluble nutrients, enzymes, hormones, and humics. While growth responses can occur from these soluble materials, leachates lack the complete benefits of AACT.

### Sun Tea

Compost placed in water and exposed to sunlight. UV radiation does not penetrate water or plastic beyond a few micrometers. Laboratory experiments confirmed that even a simple plastic lid prevents UV-mutagenic effects on bacteria, so any covering protects organisms from UV damage (though not from desiccation).

## Criteria for True Compost Tea

### Required Components

True compost tea must contain the major constituents of compost. If the liquid produced lacks these components, it should not be called compost tea. Specifically:

- **Soluble nutrients** must be present in the liquid (though nutrients alone are insufficient)
- **Beneficial organisms** must be present — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes
- **Aerobic conditions** must be maintained throughout brewing

### Human Pathogen Safety

True compost does not contain human pathogens. Multiple mechanisms contribute to pathogen elimination:

- High temperatures during composting
- Passage through earthworm guts
- Contact with worm surfaces
- Competition with aerobic organisms in compost and tea
- Consumption by predators

There is no evidence that antibiotic production or toxic chemical production are significant mechanisms for pathogen reduction in real-world conditions. The key requirement is maintaining aerobic conditions so organisms remain active and perform their functions.

### When Tea Fails to Qualify

If the liquid goes anaerobic and beneficial fungi, protozoa, and nematodes are killed, the resulting liquid **cannot** be called compost tea. Without these organisms, the biological benefits of compost are lost. Such liquids should be termed "anaerobic bacterial tea" or "putrefying organic matter tea."

An interesting edge case involves fermentative conditions where [[bionutrients-lab-lactic-acid-bacteria-serum]] or fermentative yeasts dominate and put aerobic organisms to sleep without killing them. If the aerobic organisms can be resuscitated and recovered, the material may still qualify as compost tea — defined by what organisms are present and recoverable.

### Risks of Anaerobic Conditions

Allowing tea to go anaerobic can support growth of human pathogens including:

- *Clostridium* species
- *Escherichia coli*
- *Salmonella*
- *Shigella*

Even if conditions are subsequently returned to aerobic, the beneficial community never fully recovers its original diversity. This represents an unacceptable risk for agricultural and horticultural applications.

## Distinguishing Compost from Composted Manure

A critical distinction in compost tea production is the source material. If something has been properly composted, it is compost — regardless of whether the [[shirota-4-hydroxyindole-acetyl-protection-psilocin-synthesis-starting-material]] was manure, food waste, or yard debris. However, there is a threshold where one can determine whether composting has proceeded far enough to call the material compost rather than its original form. Partly composted material is not finished compost and should not be used as such. The maturity of the compost directly affects the quality and safety of the resulting tea.

## The Role of Food Resources

When no food resources are added to a compost tea brew, active aeration may not be strictly required if the source compost contains sufficient food resources to activate the biology. However, oxygen levels must still be monitored. The question is whether the compost contained enough food to activate the biology and create enough microbial growth demand to potentially drop oxygen below survival thresholds. In practice, most brewers add food resources (molasses, kelp, fish hydrolysate, humic acids) to ensure robust microbial proliferation during the brewing period.

## Understanding the Biological Context

The habitat we create through our growing practices needs to select for beneficial organisms and not diseases. In natural systems, pathogens are out-competed and eliminated all the time by appropriate biological processes. These systems have worked successfully since biology first appeared on the planet. The goal of AACT is to replicate and accelerate these natural competitive dynamics in agricultural and horticultural settings.

The plant itself plays an essential role — it must be healthy enough to produce the root exudates ("cakes and cookies") that support the beneficial microbial community. Without a healthy plant contributing to the soil food web, even the best compost tea cannot establish lasting beneficial populations.

## Key Principles

1. **Brewing matters** — passive extraction (leachate), active brewing (tea), and [[jadam-anaerobic-fermentation]] produce fundamentally different products with different organism profiles and different effects
2. **Oxygen is non-negotiable** — aerobic conditions are essential for maintaining the full complement of beneficial organisms
3. **Definitions have practical consequences** — calling an anaerobic product "compost tea" creates false expectations and may expose users to health risks
4. **The biology defines the product** — compost tea should be defined by what organisms it contains, not just by how it was made
5. **Source compost quality determines tea quality** — only properly finished, aerobic compost should be used as the starting material for brewing
6. **Food resources drive the brew** — the type and amount of food added during brewing shapes which organisms proliferate, allowing teas to be tailored toward bacterial or fungal dominance depending on crop needs
7. **Recovery from anaerobic events is incomplete** — even if oxygen is restored, the original diversity of beneficial organisms cannot be fully recovered after an anaerobic event
