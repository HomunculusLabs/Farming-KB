## Culturing Indigenous Microorganisms: Techniques and Methods

Indigenous microorganism (IMO) culturing is a set of low-cost, locally adaptable biotechnology
practices for harvesting and propagating beneficial microbes from natural environments. These
techniques originate from Korean [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] (LAB)

Lactic acid bacteria are among the most versatile and widely applied microorganisms in natural
farming. They serve functions spanning animal husbandry, aquaculture, crop production, and
food safety.

### Applications

In **natural piggery systems**, LAB are introduced into a litter substrate composed of compost,
soil, and sawdust. The bacteria colonize the bedding and continuously decompose manure and
organic waste, eliminating the need for routine cleaning. The litter remains dry and
odor-free because the microbes rapidly metabolize ammonia and volatile fatty acids that would
otherwise produce noxious smells.

In **aquaculture**, LAB preparations are added to fish ponds and shrimp tanks to control
ammonia levels and facilitate denitrification. The bacteria convert toxic ammonium compounds
into less harmful nitrogenous forms, improving water quality without chemical treatments.

As a **foliar spray**, diluted LAB solution aids in the decomposition of organic residue on leaf
surfaces, improving nutrient uptake and reducing fungal pathogen pressure through competitive
exclusion. LAB also produce enzymes and natural antibiotics during fermentation, contributing
suppressive effects against *Salmonella* and *Escherichia coli* in both animal and plant systems.

### Culture Method

The standard protocol for isolating lactic acid bacteria proceeds in two fermentation phases:

1. **Rice wash fermentation**: Collect the milky water from washing raw rice and fill a container
   to no more than 25–50% capacity, leaving a 50–75% air gap to support aerobic microbial
   activity. Cover loosely and ferment for 5–7 days at [[mushroom-growing-temperature-and-moisture]]), the rice will
have colonized with fungi, yeasts, and bacteria from the forest soil.

An alternative approach involves collecting litter, humus, and leaf mold directly from the site
and spreading it over cooked rice in an open tray. Once colonization is visible (typically
evidenced by fungal growth, color changes, and a characteristic earthy smell), add crude sugar
or molasses at a ratio of one-third the volume of the colonized rice. Ferment the mixture for
one additional week, then dilute 1:20 with chlorine-free water for field application.

## Bamboo Microorganisms

Bamboo groves represent another rich source of beneficial microorganisms. Bamboo roots exude
sugary substances into the surrounding soil, attracting and sustaining dense populations of
fungi and bacteria adapted to decomposing lignocellulosic material. The collection method
mirrors the forest technique: a container of cooked rice is buried in bamboo litter at the base
of a living bamboo clump. After colonization (2–10 days), the rice is harvested, mixed with
crude sugar, fermented for one week, and diluted for use. Bamboo-sourced microbes are
particularly valued for fermenting animal feeds due to their efficiency in breaking down fibrous
plant material.

## Plant-Specific Microorganisms

To capture microbes that have a symbiotic relationship with a particular crop species, select a
healthy, vigorous specimen of the target plant. Cut the plant at its base and immediately place
an open container of cooked rice upside down over the cut stump. The sap and cellular fluids
released from the cut attract the plant's associated microorganisms into the rice. After 2–3
days of exposure, the rice is collected and cultured using the standard sugar addition and
fermentation procedure.

## Rhizobium Nitrogen-Fixing Bacteria

Leguminous plants form root nodules inhabited by *Rhizobium* species, which fix atmospheric
nitrogen into ammonia accessible to the host plant. To propagate these bacteria, pull legume
plants at their flowering or early fruiting stage when nodule formation is at its peak. Collect
the soil adhering to the roots along with the nodules themselves. Mix this material with crude
sugar at an equal ratio by weight or volume. The sugar serves as a carbon source, stimulating
rapid rhizobial proliferation. The resulting inoculant can be applied to the next batch of
legume seeds before planting, enhancing nodulation and nitrogen fixation in the subsequent crop.

## BIM Formulation

Beneficial Indigenous Microorganism (BIM) preparations combine multiple microbial cultures into a
single composite inoculant. A standard BIM formulation consists of approximately 50% lactic acid
bacteria stock blended with 50% other cultured microbes, which may include forest, bamboo, and
plant-specific harvests. Greater microbial diversity in the formulation generally correlates with
broader functional capabilities and more resilient soil ecology. Practical guidelines include
using pure lactobacillus preparations for animal applications and bamboo-derived microbes for
fermented animal feed production, while mixed BIM formulations are preferred for soil and crop
applications.

## Feeding Microbes

Cultured microorganisms require carbohydrate and sugar substrates as their principal food source
to remain active after application. In [[bionutrients-fermented-plant-extracts]] (FPJ), ginger-garlic nutrient preparations, brown rice vinegar, and
[[bokashi-composting-and-effective-microorganisms]] (EM), typically
contain a defined consortium of lactic acid bacteria, photosynthetic bacteria, yeasts,
actinomycetes, and fermenting fungi in standardized proportions. While effective, these products
carry recurring purchase costs and may contain strains not optimally adapted to local conditions.
Indigenous [[forest-bamboo-plant-specific-microorganism-culturing]] achieves a functionally similar result by locally sourcing
microbes already adapted to the regional climate and soil ecology, at negligible material cost.
The trade-off is reduced standardization and consistency compared to laboratory-produced
products, though practitioners compensate by maintaining diverse BIM formulations that broaden
the functional range of the inoculant.
## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
