## Desert Reafforestation and Arid Land Agriculture

Desert reafforestation requires strategies derived from studying how desert peoples have traditionally managed their environments. Mollison's approach is based on fieldwork at Aboriginal settlements in central Australia (Ernabella, Papunya) and draws on techniques from oasis dwellers like the Papago Indians of Tucson.

## The "Dead Centre" Is a Myth

"First, I must state that in my opinion, based on real examples sighted, that the 'dead centre' is a myth. Not only will many important vegetables and tree crops grow in deserts, but the native vegetation, where not overburnt or overgrazed, is, in itself, a great resource."

Key facts:
- Water lies close underground in many places
- Mulch material is abundant as plants and leaves
- Growth in desert soil is phenomenal if water is available
- Modern [[drip-irrigation]] plus mulch will grow any domestic crop

## Broad Reafforestation Strategies

The broad strategies start from focal points and expand outward:
- **Upstream**: Secure headwaters and catchments
- **Upwind**: Establish vegetation that generates moisture downwind
- **From oases**: Expand from existing water sources

"In many areas, run-off from bare or rocky areas increases effective precipitation, so that small areas of a few acres to fifty acres may be selected where good underground or runoff water is available for gardens."

## Water Sources for Desert Gardens

- Rock-holes
- Small dams
- Rock seepage
- Underground water in soaks or sandy river beds
- Bores, wells, windmills
- Tank-water from roof catchment
- Runoff from domed rock, piedmont slopes, bare rocky ground

## Home Garden Strategy for Desert Settlements

### Location
Around the house (wiltja), in pest-protected, fenced, guarded areas where feral herbivores have least effect, rabbits are kept at bay by dogs, and organic wastes accumulate.

### The Dual Aim
1. Use available resources (wastewater, organic waste) productively
2. Alter house climate while doing so

### Water Conservation
"Trickle irrigation plus mulch is the key to [[water-conservation]], the reduction of salt and carbonate accumulation, and the buffering of pH values, as [[humic-acids-soil-biology-ingham]] tend to offset the effect of highly alkaline soil. Ploughing only increases alkalinity to intolerable levels."

### Mulch Materials
- Mulga (Acacia aneura)
- Tamarisk
- Desert oaks or casuarinas
- Tall cultivated bamboo
- Grain or sugar cane wastes
- Paulownia
- [[acacia-albida]]
- Leucaena

### Wastewater Integration
- Shower, toilet, and roof water available for gardens
- Washing-up, shower, bath water containing soap led under mulch is "a benefit, not a nuisance"
- Pebble mounds with showers overhead provide water treatment and garden moisture
- "Many showers, so placed, make [[vegetable-garden-watering-systems-spacing]] an automatic process"

## House Climate Modification

### Deciduous Vine Trellis to the North
- Grape vine or trees (Acacia albida) to the north provide summer shade, winter sun
- Evergreen vine trellis as arbour to the south (Tecoma vine)
- Vine trellis over wiltja roof, ivy or trellis on house walls

### Wind Protection
- Tall tamarisk, white cedar, and giant bamboo screen cold SE winds and provide mulch
- Light-foliaged Paulownia or A. albida to the north shade house and crops

### Cool Arbours
In the cool arbours, strawberries, mint, blackcurrant, gooseberry, and soft herbs grow in deep mulch.

### Soil-Covered Roofs
A small or strongly-constructed roof can be soil-covered, mulched, and planted to ice-plants, cacti, succulents, and hardy desert species. These yield cool air in summer and act as external insulation for winter cold.

## Runoff and Erosion Control

### Net and Pan System
The "net and pan" planting pattern is effective control in overgrazed, eroded, mined, or bulldozed sites:
- If tyres are available, "pans" can be made from these, filled with mulch
- [[diversion-drains-and-spreader-banks]] led in above the tread level
- Logs staked cross-slope on slight downhill grade make water zig-zag across erosion face
- Even small logs and branches pegged across erosion channels build up silt and leaves
- Fibrous-rooted hardy species (willow, ti-tree, acacia) planted beside barriers act as permanent silt traps

### What to Avoid
Massive contour trenches allow little soil absorption, create dry strips, and expose subsoil. "Such heavy-handed approaches need massive machinery, and achieve little in the way of water control and soil improvement, compared with planned chiselling and planting."

### Selected Site Enclosures
Areas of high natural runoff (base of domed rock, piedmont at valley mouths, rock seepage areas, old sheep pens) repay fencing. Evenari and Yeomans recommend runoff-to-garden ratios of 16:1 or 20:1.

## Termite-Proofing Timbers

Bamboo, mulga, and eucalypt should be butt-soaked in "Tanalith" (copper-chrome-arsenic salts) before use. In hot weather, a three-day soak suffices. Treated timber is safe to handle but should never be burnt (fumes and residues are toxic).

## Feral Herbivore Management

At Ernabella: 6,000 cattle and 20,000–30,000 brumbies on land supporting only 1,700 humans. These animals:
- Eat out traditional Aboriginal wild-food plants (60% extinct)
- Damage young trees and break branches on old trees
- Cause bare sand to blow on [[dune-country-stabilisation]]
- Foul and break down river-beds and waterholes

### Management Options
- After December or in dry periods, feral species easily trapped on waterholes using trap-yards and swing gates
- Potential products: dried meats, hides, leather, horn, blood and bone, selected export camels, selected horses
- Approved trailer-mounted processing units
- Automatic trapping at tanks and waterholes has been perfected
- Alternatives: mass burials in areas to be planted

### Preferred Species
"Smaller 'softer' species such as poultry, wallaby, emu, and euro or kangaroo are to be preferred." Small meats need no freezing (eaten at one sitting) and cause little environmental damage.

## Native Seed and Berry Species

Native plants used as food:
- **Panicum decompositum** (Native millet / Kalta kalta)
- **Eragrostis eriopoda** (Wangana)
- **[[portulaca-oleracea]]** (Wakati)
- **Themeda australis** (Kangaroo grass)
- **Owenia reticulata** (Emu berry)
- **Chenopodium rhadinostachyum**
- **Paspalidium jubiflorum**
- **Acacia aneura** (Mulga) and many other acacia species

## Aquatic Species for Desert

As dams, tanks, and lagoons develop:
- Native wild rice
- Water lily (Nymphea gigantea): stalks, tubers, seeds
- Nardoo (fern), native arrowroot
- Trapa or Eleocharis (water chestnuts)
- Lotus
- Sweet rush: shoots and bulbs
- Trapa reduces summer evaporation from open tanks or dams

## Nutrition Priorities

Dr. Archie Kalikorinus (Aboriginal Medical Centre, Sydney): "Improving nutrition and hygiene at camps and in outstations would be 'better than all the medical services' for health."

High vitamin C content in fruit is a prime aim, especially for women before and during pregnancy. Recommended plants above frost level: paw-paw, mango, tomato, peppers, acerola ([[acerola-barbados-cherry-permaculture-guide]]), parsley, and any green leaf crop.
