# Equatorial House Design Principles

Designing for the equatorial tropics inverts temperate-climate assumptions. The primary
challenge is not retaining heat but dissipating it, while managing intense rainfall,
seasonal hunger gaps, and cyclonic storms. [[bill-mollison]]'s *Permaculture: A Designers'
Manual* establishes a coherent design logic: orient to wind, shade everything, detach
heat sources, and integrate the house within a productive tropical garden system.

## Site Selection

### Wind Orientation, Not Solar Orientation

In equatorial latitudes the sun passes nearly overhead year-round, so solar orientation
is far less critical. Instead, **the house is oriented to prevailing winds**.
Cross-ventilation is the primary cooling strategy — every design decision flows from
maximising airflow through the living space.

### Induced Ventilation Through Vegetation

Siting the house within or beside a palm grove, or beneath trees permeable to wind at
ground level, creates induced ventilation as breezes are channelled through the canopy.
Palm trunks can be pruned upward to increase ground-level airflow. The goal is a site
both shaded and well-ventilated — the foundation of thermal comfort in the humid tropics.

### Shaded Valley Sites

Shaded valley sites are greatly preferred because they aid both cooling and shelter.
Valley bottoms receive cool air drainage at night, and surrounding ridges and tree cover
reduce direct solar exposure. Floors must be assessed for flood risk and stable soils
resistant to mudflow.

### Hazard Sheltering

The site must be sheltered from hurricanes, tsunami, and vulcanism:

- **Stable soils** resistant to mudflow and landslip
- **Elevation** above tsunami inundation and storm-surge lines
- **Distance** from active volcanic zones and lahar paths
- **Windward protection** from bamboo groves or forest edges that absorb storm energy

## House Design Essentials

### Walls and External Surfaces

Walls should be **white or reflective** and overshaded by wide eaves, palms, or trees.
External wall material must be **light and permeable to wind** — woven matting, bamboo
lattice, and mosquito screens are appropriate. Heavy external thermal mass stores
daytime heat and re-radiates it at night, worsening comfort.

### Thermal Mass Placement

Mass **belongs inside** the rooms, not in the outer shell. Internal mass should be
smooth and white-painted (e.g., polished concrete floors, interior masonry benches) to
stabilise indoor temperature without absorbing solar radiation directly. The principle:
**light construction on outer walls, mass internal to rooms**.

### Ventilation Architecture

Vertical louvres and adjustable window shutters control cross-ventilation, directing
breezes through the space while excluding rain during squalls. Doors and windows should
align to create unobstructed air paths from windward to leeward sides.

### Roof Systems

The roof is critical to tropical cooling:

- **Vented sheet metal roof** with a thin ceiling beneath creates a convective air space
- **Soffit lining** of permeable netting allows air to enter the roof cavity at the eaves
- **High roof vents** (ridge vents or gable-end louvres) exhaust hot air upward

This stack-effect ventilation draws cool air through the living space and exhausts
heated air through the roof, significantly reducing indoor temperatures.

### Detached Heat Sources

Cooking and other heat-generating activities must be **detached from the main living
structure**. Outdoor kitchens are the norm, removing the largest internal heat source
and reducing fire risk in dry periods.

## Hurricane-Resistant Design

In cyclone-prone regions, additional structural measures are essential:

- A **strong central core or refuge room** of heavy masonry for shelter during storms
- **Earth-bank shelters** for additional protection
- **Deep ground anchors** and **strapped timbers** to resist uplift forces
- **Bamboo groves planted to windward** — bamboo bends without breaking, absorbing
  enormous wind energy and protecting the house behind it
- Oversize swales and earthworks to manage storm-surge and floodwater

## The Tropical Home Garden

The equatorial house sits within a **tropical home garden** supplying food, fuel,
medicine, structural materials, and microclimate moderation.

### Seasonal Rhythm

The wet season is the **"hungry gap"** — humidity and pest pressure peak while many
crops are waterlogged. The dry season is more productive. Garden design must plan for
the hungry gap with stored foods, perennials fruiting through wet months, and
quick-growing vegetables started as rains begin.

### Tree Crop Species

- **Fruits:** mango, papaya, sapote, banana, coconut, breadfruit, avocado, pineapple,
  durian, limes, cashew, macadamia
- **Palms for oil and food:** oil palm, jelly palm, *Bactris*, *Mauritia*, salak palm,
  doum palm
- **Nitrogen-fixing legumes:** *Inga*, *Gliricidia*, *Leucaena*, *Cajanus*
- **Structural timber:** bamboo, balsa, teak, palms, mahogany, rattans

### Vegetables, Pest Management, and Domestic Animals

- **Root crops:** yam, sweet potato; **leaf/fruit vegetables:** amaranth, chilies, peppers; **legumes:** lima bean, velvet bean, *Dolichos* bean
- **Biological pest control:** guinea-fowl, chickens, geckoes, wolf spiders
- **Interplanted repellents:** marigolds, neem, pyrethrum
- **Phosphate-fixing interplants:** *Banksia*, *Casuarina*, *Gigasperma calaspora*,
  *Pultenea*
- Integrated small-stock (pigeons, bees, guinea-fowl, pheasant, bantams, guinea-pigs,
  small pigs, waterfowl) provide protein, pest control, and manure

### Garden Infrastructure

- **Mound beds** for drainage in heavy wet-season rains
- **Mulch and mulch-tree species** essential for building topsoil in rapid-decomposition tropical conditions
- **Specialist crops** where climate and market allow: rubber, betel-nut, chalmougra oil, chicle, essential oils, medicinals

### Design Criteria for the Tropical Garden

1. **Full nutrition** — calories, protein, vitamins, minerals from diverse sources
2. **[[water-conservation]]** — swales, mulch, shade canopy to retain soil moisture
3. **Cultural preferences** — species and layouts matching local food traditions
4. **Climate resilience** — hurricane protection, flood management, drought tolerance
5. **Protein sources** — from legumes, small livestock, and tree nuts
6. **Basic staples** — reliable carbohydrates for year-round food security
7. **Fresh vegetables and fruits** — daily nutrition from the garden
8. **Fuels, medicinals, and flowers** — full household needs met on-site

## Synthesis

The equatorial house is not a building on a cleared lot — it is an **integrated system**
where structure, vegetation, airflow, and food production work together. Cooled by wind
and shade, protected from storms by earthworks and bamboo, fed by the surrounding
garden, and enriched by the waste streams of its inhabitants, Mollison's principles
treat the house as one element in a living landscape, not an object separate from it.
