# Cannabis Watering Schedule and Frequency

Proper watering is fundamental to successful cannabis cultivation, yet it is one of the most common areas where new growers make mistakes. Both overwatering and underwatering can stress plants, reduce growth rates, and create conditions favorable to pests and disease. Developing an effective watering schedule requires understanding the interplay between plant size, pot size, growing medium, environmental conditions, and the plant's stage of growth.

[[cannabis-growing-environment-setup]]

## Basic Watering Principles

Cannabis plants prefer a wet-dry cycle rather than constantly moist conditions. The roots need periods of relative dryness to access oxygen, which is essential for healthy root function. When soil remains constantly waterlogged, roots are deprived of oxygen, leading to root rot, fungal growth, and stunted development.

The fundamental principle is to water thoroughly when the plant needs it and then allow the growing medium to dry partially before watering again. This cycle of saturation and partial drying promotes vigorous root growth as roots reach outward seeking moisture.

A useful approach is the finger test: inserting a finger one to two inches into the soil. If the soil feels moist at that depth, watering should be delayed. If the soil feels dry, it is time to water. This simple method works well for container-grown plants in soil.

## The Lift Test for Container Plants

One of the most reliable methods for judging when to water container-grown cannabis is the lift test. When a pot has just been watered thoroughly, it is noticeably heavier than when the growing medium has dried out. By lifting the pot regularly and noting the weight difference, growers develop an intuitive sense of when water is needed.

This method becomes more accurate with practice. A grower who consistently lifts their pots will quickly learn to distinguish the weight of a well-watered pot from one that is approaching dryness. The lift test is particularly useful because it accounts for the specific pot size, medium composition, and plant water consumption in that individual container.

For very large containers, the weight difference between wet and dry may be difficult to judge by hand. In these cases, a moisture meter can supplement the lift test, though mechanical meters are not always accurate and should be used as a secondary reference rather than a primary guide.

## Recommended Watering Schedule

A practical schedule for cannabis in standard containers involves a pattern of watering followed by drying. Day A involves watering, Day B allows the medium to dry, Day C allows continued drying, and Day D involves checking the soil moisture and watering if needed.

This schedule is approximate and depends heavily on pot size, plant size, temperature, humidity, and the water-retaining properties of the growing medium. Large plants in small pots during hot conditions may need daily watering, while small plants in large pots during cool conditions may only need water once per week.

The key is flexibility and observation. Rather than following a rigid calendar schedule, the grower should assess the actual moisture conditions of the growing medium each time before watering.

During vegetative growth under 24-hour lighting, water consumption is at its highest because the plant is actively growing and transpiring continuously. When plants transition to flowering with a 12-hour dark period, water consumption decreases somewhat because metabolic activity slows during the dark hours.

## Signs of Overwatering

Overwatering is one of the most common mistakes in cannabis cultivation, and it can be more damaging than occasional underwatering. Overwatered plants show several characteristic symptoms.

The most visible sign is drooping or wilting leaves that curl downward, resembling claws. This symptom is confusing because underwatering also causes wilting, but the difference lies in the soil moisture. If the soil is wet and the leaves are drooping, the plant is overwatered. If the soil is dry and the leaves are drooping, the plant needs water.

Other signs of overwatering include yellowing leaves, particularly lower leaves, a general lack of vigor despite adequate nutrition, slowed growth rates, and in severe cases, a foul smell from the growing medium as anaerobic bacteria multiply in waterlogged conditions.

Chronic overwatering promotes fungal gnats and root rot pathogens. The surface of the growing medium may develop algae or mold growth. In hydroponic systems, overwatering manifests as constantly submerged root zones without adequate air exposure.

## Signs of Underwatering

Underwatering produces wilting but the leaves typically curl upward and inward rather than drooping downward. The growing medium will be visibly dry and may pull away from the edges of the container. The pot will feel noticeably light when lifted.

Severely underwatered plants develop dry, crispy leaf tips and edges. Growth slows dramatically as the plant conserves resources. In extreme cases, leaves become brittle and may break when handled.

Recovery from underwatering is generally faster and more complete than recovery from overwatering, provided the dry period was not extreme enough to cause permanent root damage. Watering thoroughly and allowing the plant to rehydrate over several hours usually restores turgidity.

## Watering Techniques

When watering, the goal is to thoroughly saturate the growing medium while ensuring good drainage. Water should be applied slowly enough to be absorbed rather than running off the surface or channeling down the sides of the pot.

Water until approximately 10-20% of the water applied runs out of the drainage holes. This runoff carries away excess salts and waste products that accumulate in the growing medium over time. Collecting and measuring this runoff allows the grower to test its pH and nutrient content, providing valuable diagnostic information.

Water temperature matters. Cold water can shock roots, especially in soil grows. Room temperature or slightly warm water (65-75 degrees Fahrenheit) is ideal. Avoid using hot water, which can damage root tissue and kill beneficial soil organisms.

## Environmental Factors Affecting Watering Needs

Temperature directly affects transpiration rates. Higher temperatures cause plants to lose water faster through their leaves, increasing watering frequency. Grow rooms with intense lighting, particularly air-cooled or water-cooled systems that generate heat, may require more frequent watering than cooler environments.

Humidity affects the rate of water loss. In low humidity environments, plants transpire more rapidly and need more frequent watering. In high humidity, water loss slows and overwatering becomes a greater risk. The ideal relative humidity for cannabis is around 40-60%, depending on growth stage.

Air movement from fans increases transpiration and evaporation from the growing medium. While air circulation is essential for plant health, strong fans directed at the soil surface can accelerate drying and increase watering needs.

The growth stage affects water consumption. Plants in rapid vegetative growth consume more water than plants in early flowering. During late flowering, as the plant directs energy toward bud maturation and leaf senescence increases, water consumption may decrease slightly.

## Water Quality Considerations

The quality of water used for irrigation affects plant health and nutrient availability. Tap water from municipal supplies often contains chlorine or chloramine, which can harm beneficial soil microorganisms. Allowing tap water to sit in an open container for 24 hours allows chlorine to off-gas, though chloramine is more stable and may require treatment.

Water pH should be adjusted before application. For soil grows, water pH should be between 6.0 and 7.0. For hydroponic and soilless systems, the target is 5.5-6.5. Using pH-adjusted water prevents gradual pH drift in the growing medium that can cause nutrient lockout.

Hard water with high mineral content can cause gradual buildup of calcium and magnesium carbonates in the growing medium, raising pH and potentially causing nutrient imbalances over time. Soft water or reverse-osmosis water may lack sufficient calcium and magnesium, requiring supplementation.
