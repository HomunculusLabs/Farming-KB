# Holmgren Emergence, Gender Balance, and Long-Term Cycles

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: David Holmgren, *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002)

## Emergence

One of the key ideas from systems theory is "emergence" -- self-organisation within complex systems results in activity, structures, and behaviours that emerge from within the system but transform it or produce something completely new. This is analogous to the Reorganisation phase of Holling's ecosystem model, or the creation of new species in biological evolution.

### Example: Personal Computer Networks
The global network of personal computers arose out of the IT industry quite unpredicted by mainstream players. Most experts predicted a world controlled by hierarchical mainframe computers. Corporations and governments were strongly committed to centralised technology but had to adapt fast to the anarchic emergence of the networked world.

The advocates for the networked world were small, fringe actors with apparently little power. The phrase "having a life of its own" applies. Many see global computer networks as the beginning of a global human consciousness, a "hive mind" with either utopian or sinister connotations.

### Emergent Possibilities
Emergent possibilities are a natural outcome of the extraordinarily fast co-evolutionary growth of human systems based on fossil fuels. Information technology and genetic engineering show potential to overturn many assumptions.

As evidence mounts for the energy peak and rapid descent, the particular future is filled with uncertainty and emergent possibilities. Even the grand assumption that energy descent is inevitable may be mistaken -- some combination of computer networks, human culture, and biotechnology might create a runaway world of accelerating growth (at least for another few hundred years).

## Gender Balance and Sustainability

### Rhythmic vs Episodic Change
Industrial culture is biased toward episodic change; sustainable culture is biased toward rhythmic change. The classical view of history as a series of episodic crises with "great men" as leading actors ignores the less dramatic, rhythmic, cyclical nature of ordinary life which is the greater part of human experience.

Permaculture emphasises working with rhythmic cycles of change in nature, rather than excessive reliance on episodic intervention. This view of nature is more in tune with feminine rather than masculine culture.

### The Pulsing Model and Masculine/Feminine
The pulsing ecosystem model shows how episodic, even chaotic, change becomes integrated into ecological systems. This type of change is a two-edged sword: it can lead to either a spiral of degradation or to novel and often unintended transformative change.

Rather than rejecting episodic change as destructive, Holmgren shows how to use it creatively. In terms of gender cultures, this means beginning to see the limitations of patriarchal culture while recognising that masculine ways of action are potent agents of change that have their place.

### Traditional Domestic Life as Foundation
"It is the patterns of traditional life focused on the home and a domestic connection to nature, the cycles of the seasons, and even the mundane, supposedly boring aspects of childcare and education, housework and building maintenance, plant and animal husbandry, community support and maintenance, which must dominate any notions of sustainable culture."

### Ivan Illich on Patriarchy
Illich argued that industrialisation has transformed patriarchy into a "genderless" culture that has absorbed both men and women but leaves men structurally dominant. Despite feminist achievements, modern industrial culture has condensed around masculine-origin ways of thinking, doing, and being.

The bias toward seeing all systems as passive and inactive until a powerful actor pushes them somewhere is one of the most important of these inherited assumptions.

### Ambiguous Complementarity
Holmgren suggests society will eventually evolve a new structure of "ambiguous complementarity" between genders. Before this happens in enduring form, restoring asymmetrical balances is a task for all.

Vandana Shiva identified the cultures of women, indigenous, and peasant peoples as having a common invisibility that is consumed by the dominant culture.

## Long-Term Thinking and Large-Scale Cycles

### Nested Cycles
Making sense of our era demands getting past the notion of change driven by an inexorable arrow of progress. Nature and humanity are governed by cycles, both large and small, nested within each other like Russian nesting dolls and overlapping as Venn diagrams.

### Pulsing Patterns of Life on Earth
- Vast periods of tens of millions of years: land masses worn down, shallow seas, uniformly mild climate, low biodiversity, little change
- The last 1.6 million years (Quaternary): tectonic uplift, ice caps, varied climates, ice ages and interglacials, massive extinctions and prolific speciation
- Much of the wondrous biological and geographic diversity we accept as "natural" only exists in these periods of great change
- "It is unlikely that a species such as Homo sapiens could emerge, or even survive, the long, slow, stable periods that dominate the earth's geological history"

### Ice Ages and Interglacials
Ice ages lasting hundreds of thousands of years = the stable norm of the Quaternary. Interglacials (~10,000 years) = the explosive pulse of life based on new fertility and benign climates.

Within the current benign and remarkably stable interglacial, humanity developed agriculture, civilisation, and global industrial culture -- all dependent on the fragile stability of the "interglacial paradise."

### The Hindu Cultural Cycle
The ancient Hindu cosmology describes a 60,000-year cultural cycle:
- Dominated by a long matriarchy phase (three great enduring ages, ~54,438 years)
- Collapses in a vortex of chaos
- Patriarchy phase (Kali Yuga, Age of Conflict) lasts only 6,000 years
- By Hindu reckoning, over 440 years of chaotic change remain before the new matriarchal age emerges

### Holmgren's Conclusion
"Permaculture is a dynamic interplay between two phases: on the one hand, sustaining life within the cycle of the seasons, and on the other, conceptual abstraction and emotional intensity of creativity and design."

We must prepare children and descendants for uncertainty and rapid change, while instilling appreciation of enduring rhythms, long-term thinking, persistent ethical behaviour, and the importance of the simple, ordinary, even mundane aspects of life.

## Gender and Cultural Balance

Holmgren draws on Riane Eisler's partnership/dominator model to explore how gender balance relates to sustainability. Dominator cultures (hierarchical, male-dominated, war-oriented) tend toward exploitative resource use, while partnership cultures (egalitarian, collaborative) tend toward stewardship. He suggests that the energy descent transition requires a cultural shift toward partnership values. See [[permaculture-ethics]] for the ethical foundations.

## Long-Term Historical Cycles

Drawing on thinkers like Oswald Spengler and Arnold Toynbee, Holmgren explores civilisational cycles of growth, maturity, and decline. Industrial civilisation's trajectory mirrors patterns seen in previous civilisations: expansion driven by energy surpluses, increasing complexity, and eventual contraction when energy returns decline. Permaculture, in this framing, is both a practical response to energy descent and a cultural framework for navigating civilisational transition.

## The Role of Information

In the context of energy descent, information becomes increasingly valuable relative to material resources. Holmgren notes that permaculture design knowledge, ecological literacy, and practical skills are forms of wealth that appreciate rather than depreciate. The transmission of this knowledge through education, demonstration, and practice represents a critical strategy for building resilient communities. See [[homestead-education-resources]].

# Holmgren: Cellular Design and Permaculture Scale

updated: 2026-04-18

The principle of small and slow solutions ([[holmgren-small-slow-solutions-detailed]]) has deep energetic and systemic roots. Holmgren draws on natural systems to show why cellular design is fundamental to sustainable human organisation.

## Cellular Design in Nature

Cells, as explored in the pattern-to-details principle ([[holmgren-top-down-thinking-bottom-up-action]]), provide one of the most fundamental patterns in natural and sustainable design. Small cells, usually with a concentrated nucleus, replicate to create large systems. Most basic functions of living organisms operate at the cellular level, where relatively simple and very reliable processes with a long evolutionary history operate at the smallest possible scale.

Each cell in the organism is as autonomous as possible within the constraints of the larger organ and organism. Cells have their own optimum size beyond which they do not grow. Where growth occurs to meet requirements of the larger system, division into two similar-sized cells is the result.

Cellular design in nature suggests that functions are best dealt with at the smallest workable scale, and that replication and diversification are the mechanisms for growth to support larger-scale functions. Although excessive growth in individual cells is generally impossible, uncontrolled overgrowth of cells by replication does occur; it is generally a sign of large-scale systemic disorder, which in medicine is called cancer.

Despite the almost universal nature of this pattern in nature, in human society we have come to regard individual growth and replication as symbols of what is good and successful.

## Energetic Limits of Scale

A given energy supply can support a large mass moving slowly, or a small mass moving fast, but not both. If energy availability rises, systems can grow in size and increase in speed. If energy availability diminishes, systems must shrink, or slow down, or do both.

A speedboat is small, fast, manoeuvrable. An ocean liner is large, slow, unwieldy. An ocean liner designed to have the speed and manoeuvrability of a speedboat would require energy out of all proportion to the advantage. The liner has a very large, slow-turning engine with a very long service life; the speedboat has a high-revving, two-stroke engine needing regular tuning with limited service life.

Similarly, in the natural world, algae, bacteria and other micro-organisms come and go quickly, while large ones like forest trees and elephants are long-lived and reproduce slowly or infrequently. Large, expensive public buildings should be designed to last a long time, while small shelters may be temporary and built accordingly.

## Human Scale as Yardstick

Human scale and capacity should be the yardstick for a humane, democratic and sustainable society. Whenever we do anything of a self-reliant nature -- growing food, fixing a broken appliance, maintaining our health -- we are making very powerful and effective use of this principle. Whenever we purchase from small, local businesses or contribute to local community and environmental issues, we are also applying this principle.

## Speed and Perception

The speed of movement of materials and people between systems should be minimised. A reduction in speed is a reduction in total movement, increasing the energy available for the system's self-reliance and autonomy.

Speed, especially of personal movement, generates high levels of stimulation that drown out the subtle and the quiet. When we drive somewhere new, we are stimulated by the landscape. When we travel the same route regularly, we notice small changes but generally lose interest and become bored. However, if we ride a bicycle or walk over the same route, our eyes, ears, skin and noses are opened to a new world of subtle stimulation that the enclosure and speed of the car keeps from us.

## Permaculture Scale: Garden Agriculture

Permaculture has been closely associated, in fact synonymous in many people's minds, with food gardening. Holmgren prefers the term "garden agriculture" and has argued for it to be taken seriously as a form of agriculture. Food gardens are the smallest-scale and potentially most productive of all agricultural systems.

Garden agriculture can be seen as the cellular unit of a new agricultural system. Individual gardens replicate and diversify to create productive landscapes at neighbourhood and community scales. The key is that each unit remains functional and productive even without connection to larger systems.

## The Snail as Icon

The spiral house of the snail is small enough to be carried on its back and yet capable of incremental growth. With its lubricated foot, the snail easily and deliberately traverses any terrain. Although the bane of gardeners, the snail is an appropriate icon for small scale and slow speed.

## Application to Community and Economy

At the community level, cellular design suggests that neighbourhood-scale organisations and economies are more resilient than city-wide or national ones. [[bioregional-organization]] naturally emerges from cellular thinking -- each community cell is as autonomous as possible while still being connected to the larger organism of society.
