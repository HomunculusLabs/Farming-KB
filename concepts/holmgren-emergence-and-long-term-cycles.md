## Long-Term Historical Cycles

Drawing on thinkers like Oswald Spengler and Arnold Toynbee, Holmgren explores civilisational cycles of growth, maturity, and decline. Industrial civilisation's trajectory mirrors patterns seen in previous civilisations: expansion driven by energy surpluses, increasing complexity, and eventual contraction when energy returns decline. Permaculture, in this framing, is both a practical response to energy descent and a cultural framework for navigating civilisational transition.

## The Role of Information

In the context of energy descent, information becomes increasingly valuable relative to material resources. Holmgren notes that permaculture design knowledge, ecological literacy, and practical skills are forms of wealth that appreciate rather than depreciate. The transmission of this knowledge through education, demonstration, and practice represents a critical strategy for building resilient communities. # Holmgren: Cellular Design and Permaculture Scale

updated: 2026-04-18

The principle of small and slow solutions ([[holmgren-top-down-thinking-bottom-up-action]]), provide one of the most fundamental patterns in natural and sustainable design. Small cells, usually with a concentrated nucleus, replicate to create large systems. Most basic functions of living organisms operate at the cellular level, where relatively simple and very reliable processes with a long evolutionary history operate at the smallest possible scale.

Each cell in the organism is as autonomous as possible within the constraints of the larger organ and organism. Cells have their own optimum size beyond which they do not grow. Where growth occurs to meet requirements of the larger system, division into two similar-sized cells is the result.

Cellular design in nature suggests that functions are best dealt with at the smallest workable scale, and that replication and diversification are the mechanisms for growth to support larger-scale functions. Although excessive growth in individual cells is generally impossible, uncontrolled overgrowth of cells by replication does occur; it is generally a sign of large-scale systemic disorder, which in medicine is called cancer.

Despite the almost universal nature of this pattern in nature, in human society we have come to regard individual growth and replication as symbols of what is good and successful.

## Energetic Limits of Scale

A given energy supply can support a large mass moving slowly, or a small mass moving fast, but not both. If energy availability rises, systems can grow in size and increase in speed. If energy availability diminishes, systems must shrink, or slow down, or do both.

A speedboat is small, fast, manoeuvrable. An ocean liner is large, slow, unwieldy. An ocean liner designed to have the speed and manoeuvrability of a speedboat would require energy out of all proportion to the advantage. The liner has a very large, slow-turning engine with a very long service life; the speedboat has a high-revving, two-stroke engine needing regular tuning with limited service life.

Similarly, in the natural world, algae, bacteria and other micro-organisms come and go quickly, while large ones like forest trees and elephants are long-lived and reproduce slowly or infrequently. Large, expensive public buildings should be designed to last a long time, while small shelters may be temporary and built accordingly.

## Human Scale as Yardstick

Human scale and capacity should be the yardstick for a humane, democratic and sustainable society. Whenever we do anything of a self-reliant nature -- growing food, fixing a broken appliance, maintaining our health -- we are making very powerful and effective use of this principle. Whenever we purchase from small, local businesses or contribute to local community and environmental issues, we are also applying this principle.

## Speed and Perception

The speed of movement of materials and people between systems should be minimised. A reduction in speed is a reduction in total movement, increasing the energy available for the system's self-reliance and autonomy.

Speed, especially of personal movement, generates high levels of stimulation that drown out the subtle and the quiet. When we drive somewhere new, we are stimulated by the landscape. When we travel the same route regularly, we notice small changes but generally lose interest and become bored. However, if we ride a bicycle or walk over the same route, our eyes, ears, skin and noses are opened to a new world of subtle stimulation that the enclosure and speed of the car keeps from us.

## Permaculture Scale: Garden Agriculture

Permaculture has been closely associated, in fact synonymous in many people's minds, with food gardening. Holmgren prefers the term "garden agriculture" and has argued for it to be taken seriously as a form of agriculture. Food gardens are the smallest-scale and potentially most productive of all agricultural systems.

Garden agriculture can be seen as the cellular unit of a new agricultural system. Individual gardens replicate and diversify to create productive landscapes at neighbourhood and community scales. The key is that each unit remains functional and productive even without connection to larger systems.

## The Snail as Icon

The spiral house of the snail is small enough to be carried on its back and yet capable of incremental growth. With its lubricated foot, the snail easily and deliberately traverses any terrain. Although the bane of gardeners, the snail is an appropriate icon for small scale and slow speed.

## Application to Community and Economy

At the community level, cellular design suggests that neighbourhood-scale organisations and economies are more resilient than city-wide or national ones. [[bioregional-organization]] naturally emerges from cellular thinking -- each community cell is as autonomous as possible while still being connected to the larger organism of society.
