## The Problem of Airborne Contaminants

Understanding the scale of the contamination challenge helps explain why laboratory design matters. "Clean country air" contains approximately one million particles greater than 0.3 microns per cubic foot. A room with a cigarette being smoked contains more than one hundred million particles in the same air space. A properly designed sterile laboratory, by contrast, has less than one hundred particles per cubic foot.

Most spores contaminating mushroom cultures are between 0.5 and 20 microns in diameter. Particles greater than 10 microns fall out of the air due to gravity, but smaller particles — including bacterial endospores around 0.4 microns — remain suspended. Viruses at 0.05 microns are usually attached to larger particles such as fungal spores. This broad assortment of airborne debris poses the greatest danger to mushroom culture work.

## Three Levels of Laboratory Design

Stamets describes three tiers of sterile workspace, each providing progressively better protection against contamination.

### Level 1: Still Air Chamber (Glove Box)

A glove box is an airtight chamber that provides a semi-sterile still air environment in which transfer operations can be performed. It is the least expensive option and can be constructed from materials found at any hardware store.

The basic design consists of a transparent box (acrylic or polycarbonate) with two arm holes fitted with sealed sleeves. The operator's hands enter through the sleeves while the box remains otherwise closed. Before each use, the interior is wiped down with 70% isopropyl alcohol or 10% bleach solution.

Advantages include low cost, simplicity, and portability. A glove box can be set up on any table and stored when not in use. One builder mounted a short-wave ultraviolet light inside a glove box for additional disinfection between uses.

Limitations include restricted working space, discomfort during extended use, and the fact that the environment is "still" rather than actively filtered. Contaminants that enter the box remain there. Despite these limitations, many successful cultivators rely entirely on glove boxes for their mushroom cultivation sterile laboratory design.

### Level 2: Disinfected Room

A small storage room or closet can be converted into a workable inoculation chamber. The room is thoroughly cleaned, sealed as much as possible, and disinfected before each use. Chemical cleaners, aerosol disinfectants, and UV lights are used to reduce airborne contaminants.

Stamets describes an "aerodisinfector" — a device that vaporizes triethylene glycol through a heated wick to reduce airborne contamination. Triethylene glycol is finer and more volatile than mineral oil, and effectively settles airborne spores.

This approach requires more dedicated space but allows larger-scale work than a glove box. The key limitation is that chemical and UV disinfection leave "shadows" — areas where contaminants survive because the disinfecting agent does not reach them.

### Level 3: Laminar Flow Hood (HEPA System)

The gold standard for sterile work is the laminar flow hood, which uses High Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filters. These filters screen out all particulates exceeding 0.3 microns with 99.97% efficiency. HEPA filters are built into the hood so that filtered air flows in a laminar (parallel, non-turbulent) stream across the work surface.

HEPA filters operate in environments up to 80% humidity without disintegration. Special "water-resistant" filters are available for higher-humidity applications. Some professional sterile laboratories have entire walls or ceilings constructed of HEPA filters through which pressurized, filtered air is forced, creating a whole-room clean environment.

For the typical cultivator, a benchtop laminar flow hood provides an excellent workspace. The [[mushroom-contaminant-identification-key]] of contaminants prevents their spread.

## Cost Considerations

The cost range for sterile laboratory equipment spans several orders of magnitude.

A basic glove box can be built for under $50 using a clear plastic storage container, silicone sealant, and arm-length rubber gloves. It provides adequate protection for small-scale home cultivation.

A DIY laminar flow hood costs $200-500, depending on the HEPA filter size and blower quality. This investment pays for itself quickly in reduced contamination losses and expanded cultivation capability.
