# Holmgren Energy Descent Planning

Energy descent planning is the application of permaculture design principles to the practical challenge of adapting human systems to declining energy availability. Holmgren positions permaculture not as a lifestyle choice but as the design system for navigating the inevitable transition from fossil-fuel-powered civilisation to a low-energy future.

## The Energy Descent Reality

Once we accept the reality and magnitude of energy descent, we begin to ask what "sustainability" might actually mean in practice. If high-energy society cannot be sustained beyond the fossil fuel pulse, then our task shifts from sustaining the unsustainable to adapting to decline with grace and ethics. See [[holmgren-beyond-sustainability]].

Permaculture uses the conditions of energy descent to consciously design our pathway through it. This contrasts with both the techno-optimist position (which denies the need for fundamental change) and the survivalist position (which abandons the possibility of civilised adaptation). Permaculture offers a middle path: realistic about the scale of change ahead, but optimistic about the human capacity for creative adaptation.

The rate of change during energy descent will not be uniform. There will be periods of rapid contraction punctuated by plateaus of relative stability. As the rate of change slows, more enduring and sustainable adaptations become possible. Planning must account for this pulsing dynamic, as discussed in [[holmgren-ecological-succession-and-pulsing]].

## Planning from the Household Upward

Holmgren emphasises bottom-up redesign processes, starting with the individual and household as drivers for change at the market, community, and cultural level. This is not withdrawal from the world but the most effective leverage point for systemic change.

The [[holmgren-household-economy-and-self-reliance]] is the primary site of energy descent planning because:

- It is the scale at which individuals have the most direct control and influence
- Household-level changes aggregate into significant collective impact
- It builds practical skills and knowledge transferable to community-scale projects
- It demonstrates viable alternatives that can inspire broader adoption
- It provides immediate tangible benefits that reinforce continued engagement

As we reduce our dependence on the global economy and replace it with household and local economies, we reduce the demand that drives current inequities. Thus "look after yourself first" is not an invitation to greed but a challenge to grow up through self-reliance and personal responsibility.

## Key Planning Principles

Energy descent planning draws on all twelve [[holmgren-twelve-design-principles-overview]] but several are especially critical:

### Maximising Energy Capture and Storage

During energy descent, systems that efficiently catch and store energy become increasingly valuable. This includes [[holmgren-built-environment-energy-store]], biological systems (trees, soils, water), and social systems (community networks, knowledge, skills). The concept of "next best use" catches energy at each step in a cascading system, as discussed in [[holmgren-catch-and-store-energy-detailed]].

### Reducing Scale and Speed

[[permaculture-small-and-slow-solutions]] become more appropriate as energy availability declines. Large, fast, centralised systems depend on concentrated energy sources that will not be available in descent. Distributed, appropriate-scale systems are more resilient and require less energy to maintain. This connects to the beam balance image used to think about appropriate scales in design.

The principle of scale reduction applies across all domains. In food production, this means shifting from thousand-acre monocultures to diverse smallholdings and home gardens. In energy systems, it means distributed solar, wind, and biomass generation rather than centralised fossil fuel or nuclear power stations. In transport, it means prioritising walking, cycling, and local production over global supply chains. In governance, it means devolving decision-making to the most local scale capable of handling the issue effectively. At each level, the reduced scale demands less energy for maintenance and is more resilient to disruption.

### Building Redundancy and Diversity

[[holmgren-permaculture-diversity-principle]] reduces vulnerability to disruption. Energy descent planning involves maintaining multiple pathways for meeting basic needs, rather than depending on single solutions. This includes diverse food sources, energy systems, economic networks, and social structures.

### Adaptive Capacity Over Efficiency

In an energy growth phase, maximising efficiency is a rational strategy. In descent, the capacity to adapt to changing conditions becomes more important than peak efficiency. This means maintaining flexibility, skills, and social capital even when they appear redundant. See [[holmgren-permaculture-change-principle]].

## Bioregional Adaptation

Energy descent planning must be bioregionally specific. What works in one climate, soil type, and cultural context will not necessarily transfer. bioregionalism-in-permaculture|Bioregional organisation becomes increasingly important as global supply chains weaken and local self-reliance grows more critical.

The [[holmgren-zones-and-sectors-design-framework]] provides the spatial organising tools for bioregional adaptation, while [[holmgren-pattern-thinking-and-pattern-language]] provides the conceptual framework for understanding how local systems connect to larger-scale patterns.

Bioregional planning requires detailed understanding of local carrying capacity: how much food, water, fuel, and fiber a given landscape can sustainably produce. This understanding must account for climate variability, soil limitations, water availability, and existing land use patterns. Holmgren emphasises that realistic bioregional assessments are essential for avoiding the trap of techno-optimism — the belief that innovation will overcome fundamental biophysical limits. Honest assessment of what a landscape can actually support, combined with permaculture design to maximise that support, forms the foundation of credible energy descent planning.

## Food System Transformation

The food system is perhaps the most critical domain for energy descent planning because it represents one of the largest single uses of fossil energy in modern societies. Holmgren estimates that the average calorie of food consumed in industrial nations requires approximately ten calories of fossil fuel energy to produce, process, transport, and prepare.

Energy descent planning for food systems involves reversing this ratio by transitioning to low-input, locally adapted food production. Key strategies include: replacing annual grain monocultures with diverse perennial polycultures where climate allows; integrating livestock into mixed farming systems that cycle nutrients on-farm; developing local food processing and storage capacity to reduce dependence on refrigerated supply chains; and rebuilding the knowledge base of seasonal eating, food preservation, and seed saving that sustained human populations before the fossil fuel era. The [[faires-hugelkultur-and-forest-garden-strategy]] and other perennial-based food systems are particularly relevant because they require minimal energy inputs once established while producing diverse yields over decades.

## Cultural Dimensions

Much of the need to design for energy descent is informed by ethical principles that have been obscured by the energy wealth of recent centuries. Rapid energy descent means that individual behaviour today may be more significant for long-term outcomes than at any previous point in history.

[[holmgren-energy-descent-culture-adaptation]] to energy descent involves not just technical changes but fundamental shifts in values, expectations, and ways of thinking. The [[holmgren-attitudes-energy-descent]] include humility, patience, creativity, and willingness to learn from traditional cultures that maintained sustainable practices for millennia. Beliefs and values that developed with a rising energy base are likely to be dysfunctional -- even destructive -- in a world of limited and declining energy.

## Myth of the Steady State

A common misconception about energy descent is that it leads to a static, unchanging society once the transition is complete. Holmgren explicitly rejects this. Low-energy societies can be dynamic, innovative, and culturally rich — medieval Europe, traditional Japan, and many indigenous societies demonstrate that energy constraint does not imply stagnation. The difference is the kind of change that is possible: cultural, artistic, ecological, and social innovation can flourish within energy limits, while the scale of material transformation is necessarily bounded.

This distinction matters because it frames energy descent not as a return to primitive conditions but as a shift in the basis of civilisation. The knowledge, technologies, and cultural achievements of the high-energy era do not all disappear — many are preserved and adapted to lower-energy contexts. Solar photovoltaics, radio communication, bicycle transportation, and digital information storage are all technologies that can function at relatively low energy cost and can serve descent-era civilisation effectively. The task is discernment: separating energy-dependent innovations from those that provide lasting value.

## Economic Restructuring

Energy descent requires economic restructuring from globalised consumer capitalism to localised productive economies. This involves:

- Rebuilding household and local economies to reduce dependence on global systems
- Developing [[holmgren-lets-systems-and-alternative-economics]] that function with less energy throughput
- Redirecting investment from speculative financial assets to productive natural and social capital
- Creating livelihoods based on provision of real needs rather than consumer demand
- Building local currencies, barter networks, and mutual credit systems that maintain economic exchange even when national currencies falter
- Transitioning from debt-based money systems that require perpetual growth to equity-based systems compatible with a steady-state or contracting economy

Those working to combine traditional skills and knowledge with the latest understanding of ecological systems are central to this transition. They provide the models and the skills that will be needed as energy descent accelerates. Holmgren notes that the informal economy — household production, gift exchange, community volunteering, barter — already constitutes a significant share of total economic activity even in wealthy nations, but is invisible to conventional economic metrics. During energy descent, the informal economy becomes the primary economic sphere, and the skills of household self-reliance become the most valuable economic assets.

## The Retrofit Imperative

Rather than building new systems from scratch, much energy descent planning involves retrofitting existing settlements, buildings, and infrastructure. [[holmgren-suburban-edges-retrofit-design]] is particularly important given the vast investment already embedded in suburban development patterns.

The retrofit approach connects to [[holmgren-problems-of-success-and-energy-descent]] -- the challenge of adapting systems that were designed for energy abundance to function under energy scarcity. This is fundamentally different from, and more complex than, designing new systems for low-energy conditions. The concept of "next best use" applies here: at each step of the transition, existing structures and systems should be redirected to their most productive low-energy function. This cascading approach ensures that nothing is wasted and that each stage of the transition builds on what already exists rather than requiring wholesale demolition and reconstruction.

## Scenario Planning and the EDAP Process

Holmgren advocates for community-scale scenario planning as a practical tool for energy descent preparation. The Energy Descent Action Plan (EDAP) process, pioneered by the Transition Towns movement which drew heavily on Holmgren's thinking, involves communities collaboratively envisioning how their locality might function under varying degrees of energy reduction over a 15-20 year timeframe.

An EDAP typically includes three scenarios: a business-as-usual trajectory (continued energy growth, albeit with diminishing returns), a planned descent pathway (intentional, proactive adaptation), and an unmanaged collapse scenario (rapid, unplanned contraction). By working through these scenarios in detail — mapping local food systems, transport networks, energy infrastructure, water supply, health services, and economic flows — communities can identify vulnerabilities and leverage points for intervention. The planning process itself builds social cohesion and shared understanding, which are among the most critical resources during any period of rapid change.

## Historical and Contemporary Precedents

Energy descent is not merely theoretical. Several historical examples demonstrate how societies have adapted to reduced energy availability. Cuba's "Special Period" following the collapse of Soviet oil subsidies in the early 1990s forced a rapid transition from industrial, fossil-fuel-intensive agriculture to organic, localized food production. Urban agriculture exploded in Havana and other cities, with oxen replacing tractors and biological pest control replacing chemical inputs. While the transition was driven by crisis rather than design, it demonstrates that rapid adaptation is possible when necessity demands it.

The difference between Cuba's involuntary transition and the permaculture approach is intentionality. Cuba adapted under extreme duress with significant social hardship. Energy descent planning, as Holmgren conceives it, aims to begin the transition while there are still sufficient resources to manage it creatively. The goal is to reach a stable, low-energy state that supports human flourishing rather than merely survival.

## Energy Descent and Education

A critical but often overlooked dimension of energy descent planning is education and skill transfer. Holmgren emphasises that the knowledge and skills needed for low-energy living are fundamentally different from those taught in conventional education systems. Practical skills in food production, building, repair, water management, and community organisation become essential, while many professional specialisations developed during the era of cheap energy become less relevant.

Permaculture design education serves as a bridge between the current high-energy culture and the competencies needed for descent. The [[holmgren-twelve-design-principles-overview]] function as a transferable thinking framework applicable across all domains of life. Teaching these principles — through design courses, demonstration sites, and community projects — builds the cognitive infrastructure for adaptation that is at least as important as physical infrastructure.
