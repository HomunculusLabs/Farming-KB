# Permaculture Self-Audit and Personal Design

The permaculture self-audit is a process of systematically examining one's own needs, wants, habits, inputs, and outputs in the same way a designer analyses the elements of a garden or farm system. Developed by david holmgren within the framework of [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]], the self-audit is presented not as a confession of sins but as an ecological observation exercise designed to help people better understand the real structure of their existence.

## Why Self-Audit Matters

To an amazing degree, people are driven by processes they are barely aware of or think very little about. In teaching [[permaculture-education-and-design-course]], Holmgren and colleagues found that basic personal habits and behaviours are some of the most difficult and sensitive to deal with, and yet they are at the heart of the ecologically dysfunctional nature of modern society.

For example, daily bathing or showering is now regarded by many people in affluent countries as an unquestioned, non-negotiable part of personal hygiene. The consequent escalation in demand for energy, water, and access to bathrooms is a significant factor driving activity in the wider economy. Questioning such habits reveals how deeply embedded patterns of consumption have become invisible to critical examination.

## The Self-Audit Process

The self-audit process follows a structured sequence of steps analogous to the analysis of any permaculture design element. First, list or brainstorm all your needs, wants, addictions, abilities, liabilities, and responsibilities. Second, consider all the influences and connections between these elements. Third, map the material and energy flows and your personal movement patterns. Fourth, take responsibility without guilt or blame on others. Fifth, look for the easiest opportunities for reducing dependence, minimising harm, and improving quality of life. Sixth, make small changes and review the audit regularly.

This process mirrors the way a permaculture designer considers the needs and yields of a species as part of the garden design process. The self-audit applies exactly the same analytical framework to the human element in the system. It is top-down thinking implicit in "thinking globally, acting locally."

## Needs Versus Wants Versus Addictions

One key insight from the self-audit process is the degree to which fundamental material needs may be glossed over or taken for granted, while emotional, intellectual, and spiritual needs are highlighted. Many of these non-material needs are gained through, or in association with, material consumption, but they can be met in much simpler ways.

This emphasises the degree to which family upbringing, peer pressure, ceaseless advertising, and propaganda have linked emotional, intellectual, and even spiritual needs to consumerism. Every cultural and spiritual tradition in the world gives us the opposite message: that fulfilment comes not from acquisition but from connection, relationship, and meaningful activity.

## Addiction as a Design Problem

Holmgren describes permaculture, only partly tongue-in-cheek, as "aid for addicts." He uses the growing awareness of the structure and processes of pharmacological addiction to understand the widespread addictions in modern life to shopping, television and other media, driving cars, and other forces of unsustainability.

The term "hedonic treadmill" refers to the phenomenon in affluent societies where continuous increases in material wealth are now needed to maintain societal happiness measures, but overall improvements in happiness do not seem achievable. Holmgren argues we should call a spade a spade and recognise that this is addiction by another name.

Suggestions that the majority of the population may be addicted to using their cars are often dismissed on the basis that there is no comparison to the emotional and physical dependence or level of harm suffered by a drug addict. The answer is at two levels. First, a complete social and environmental audit of car transport can show the damage is deep and long-term. Second, the only way to test the emotional and physical dependence of commuters on driving is to withdraw the daily "hit." The irrational anger of people unable to get petrol during strikes is an indication of that addiction.

## Systemic Addiction

Beyond personal addictions, systemic addiction can be identified within organisations and institutions. A well-recognised example is the dependence of governments on revenue from gambling as well as alcohol and tobacco taxes. Any discussion about transitions to organic and low-input methods of broadacre cropping is pointless unless we acknowledge that current high-yield agriculture is addicted to inputs which maintain those yields, even if they are at the same time destroying the future productive base.

Understanding the structure of these addictive relationships does not provide instant answers, but it can help us see that irrationality is a greater driver in the design of our unsustainable society than scientific rationality, and allow us to design better adaptive strategies.

## Lessons from Addiction Recovery

In dealing with personal addictions or designing solutions for more complex large-scale ones, the lessons from dealing with drug addiction are useful and transferable. First, acknowledge our own addictive behaviours and admit that they are barriers to a better life and a more sustainable world. Second, recognise the emotional and other benefits or yields we get from the addiction. Third, avoid guilt and blame of others, including our parents. Fourth, disconnect from reinforcing relationships with addicts who are unwilling to acknowledge or deal with the particular addiction. Fifth, connect with ex-addicts who understand and form self-help groups of addicts willing to change.

## Self-Reliance as Political Action

Taking personal responsibility naturally moves us to be more self-reliant and less dependent on centralised sources of needs and responsibility. In the process, we discover that governments and corporations, while preaching self-reliance, actually need our dependence. This need at the centres of power has become so great that a slackening in the frenzy of consumption is called a "consumer strike."

Environmental groups have found that focused selective boycotts of corporations can have dramatic impact and force some beneficial and visible changes. Self-reliance tends to work as a more generalised and invisible consumer boycott, undermining the market share and psychosocial dominance of the centralised and large-scale economies that support and maintain addictive and dysfunctional behaviour.

At the same time, self-reliance tends to foster and stimulate new local forms of economic activity. Home food production, for example, has tended to foster rather than compete with small commercial organic growers serving local markets.

## Connection to Wholistic Thinking

The self-audit process is facilitated by learning to think wholistically. Taking responsibility for the consequences of our personal habits and behaviours is not simply an individual moral act. It is part of the systems thinking that is central to design for [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]]. Learning to think wholistically requires a willingness to see ourselves as elements within larger systems whose health depends on the health of all their parts.

The self-audit is thus not merely a personal practice but a design tool that connects individual behaviour to systemic outcomes. It bridges the gap between the personal and the political, the household and the bioregion, the individual garden and the global food system.

## Self-Audit in Permaculture Design Courses

In permaculture design courses, the self-audit exercise often proves to be one of the most transformative experiences for participants. Many students arrive expecting to learn about gardening techniques and land design, only to discover that the most challenging design work is the redesign of their own lives and habits. The self-audit creates a framework for this personal redesign that is rigorous without being judgmental.

The process of mapping one's own inputs and outputs, dependencies and contributions, wants and genuine needs, provides a concrete foundation for the abstract principles of permaculture. Students who complete a thorough self-audit are better equipped to apply permaculture thinking to landscape and community design because they have first applied it to themselves.

## Incremental Change and Regular Review

The self-audit is not a one-time exercise but an ongoing practice. Making small changes and reviewing the audit regularly ensures that the process remains practical and adaptive. This mirrors the [[permaculture-design-principles]] and the recognition that lasting change comes from incremental adjustment rather than radical upheaval.

As conditions change, as energy descent progresses, and as personal circumstances evolve, the self-audit provides a structured way to reassess priorities and identify new opportunities for reducing dependence and improving quality of life. It is a living document of one's relationship to the systems that sustain us.
