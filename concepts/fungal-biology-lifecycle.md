# Fungal Biology and Lifecycle

Fungi are heterotrophic organisms in Kingdom Fungi, distinct from plants and animals. They lack chlorophyll, have cell walls of chitin, and obtain nutrients by extracellular digestion. They are fundamental decomposers, plant symbionts, and pathogens in every terrestrial ecosystem.

## The Fungal Lifecycle (Basidiomycota)

### 1. Spore
- Haploid (1N), uninucleate reproductive unit; analogous to plant seeds
- Dispersed by wind, water, insects, or animals
- Extremely resilient; spore walls are among the hardest organic substances known
- Can remain viable for years in dormant state

### 2. Germination
- Spore hydrates and sends out a germ tube that develops into hyphae
- Some species germinate immediately (oysters); others need drying-then-rehydration (shiitake)
- Germination begins in the dimpled depression on the spore surface

### 3. Monokaryon (Primary Mycelium)
- Hyphae from a single spore are mononucleate and haploid
- Can grow vegetatively but cannot form fruiting bodies
- Each spore has a "sex" -- must mate with compatible mycelium

### 4. Dikaryon (Secondary Mycelium)
- Plasmogamy: hyphae from two compatible mating types fuse
- Resulting mycelium is dikaryotic (each cell has two compatible nuclei, n+n)
- Clamp connections form at septa to ensure each new cell receives both nuclei
- This is the dominant, long-lived vegetative phase -- the "mushroom plant"
- Mycelia from mated spores grow far faster than single-spore cultures

### 5. Fruiting Body (Basidiocarp)
- Triggered by environmental cues (see below)
- Hyphal knots form, develop into primordia, then pinheads, then mature mushrooms
- Cap, stem, gills, and veil are all differentiated mycelial tissue
- Basidia on gill surfaces undergo karyogamy (nuclear fusion) then meiosis to produce spores

### 6. Spore Release
- Spores are forcibly discharged from basidia (ballistospore discharge)
- Active discharge can propel spores several millimeters into air currents
- A single mushroom can release billions of spores over its lifetime
- After sporulation, the fruiting body decays rapidly

### Spore Ballistics and Aerodynamics (Money, 2002)
Nicholas Money's research in Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard reveals the sophisticated physics of spore discharge:
- **Buller's drop**: A fluid droplet (Buller's drop) forms at the base of each basidiospore seconds before discharge; surface tension of the merging drop provides the launching force
- **Spore velocity**: Basidiospores are launched at initial velocities of 0.1-1.8 m/s, sufficient to clear the gills and enter air currents
- **Stem function**: The stem lifts the cap above the ground-level boundary layer of still air (~few cm deep), placing spores where wind can carry them
- **Cap aerodynamics**: Mushroom caps act like aircraft wings — airflow splits at the leading edge, creating lift at high wind speeds (which can wrench fruiting bodies from soil). The pressure differential slows air immediately beneath the gills, allowing spores to fall through calm air and reducing blowback onto the cap
- **Campanulate caps**: Bell-shaped caps (e.g., *Conocybe*, *Psilocybe semilanceata*) are especially effective at slowing airflow beneath fertile tissue, common in open meadow species
- **Gasteromycete evolution**: Puffballs, earth-stars, and false truffles lost the water-dependent catapult mechanism, enabling colonization of drier habitats (sandy soils, deserts). False truffles evolved from boletes when genes for fruiting body emergence mutated, keeping mushrooms underground — spores dispersed by rodents attracted to the flesh
- **Fungal fossils**: Basidiomycete hyphae dated to Permian (290 mya); exquisitely preserved mushrooms in Cretaceous amber (90+ mya) show catapult spore mechanism already present alongside dinosaurs

## Spore Dispersal Mechanisms

- **Ballistospory**: active forcible discharge; basidiospores are shot into air
- **Wind**: primary long-distance dispersal vector
- **Water**: rain splashes spread spores short distances
- **Animals**: insects, mammals, and birds carry spores on bodies or in gut
- **Gravity**: puffballs and earthstars release spores clouds when impacted

## Mating Types

- **Heterothallic**: requires two compatible mating types (bipolar or tetrapolar). Most cultivated species are heterothallic with thousands of mating types determined by incompatibility genes
- **Homothallic**: single spore is self-fertile; can complete lifecycle alone. Less common among cultivated species
- Bipolar: one incompatibility locus (two mating types). Tetrapolar: two loci (A and B), creating hundreds of mating types

## Fruiting Triggers

### Light
- Blue wavelengths (370-460 nm) most effective for basidiocarp initiation (Badham, 1980)
- As little as 0.0025 seconds of light can trigger initiation in P. cubensis
- Saturation occurs at approximately 0.345 x 10^4 ergs/cm^2 at 460 nm
- Green and red wavelengths (>510 nm) are ineffective
- Light is also required for normal cap development and spore production

### Temperature Shift
- A 5-10F drop from colonization temperature triggers pinning
- Each species has optimal fruiting temperature; shiitake fruits at 10-18C, oyster at 15-22C
- Temperature fluctuation should be minimized during fruiting to avoid condensation

### CO2 Drop
- High CO2 during colonization promotes vegetative growth
- Lowering CO2 below 800-1000 ppm triggers primordia formation
- Continued high CO2 causes elongated stems and suppressed cap development

### Humidity
- 90-95% relative humidity needed for primordia to form and survive
- Evaporation from the substrate surface is itself a pinning trigger

## Tropisms in Fungi

### Gravitropism (Geotropism)
- Stipes exhibit negative geotropism -- grow upward against gravity
- Geotropic response in P. cubensis is linked to spore production; sporeless mutants do not show recurvation (Badham, 1982)
- When rotated horizontally under constant light, growth becomes horizontal

### Phototropism
- Stipes bend toward light (positive phototropism) during early development
- Cap opens toward light for optimal spore dispersal
- Light inhibits stipe elongation while promoting cap expansion

### Anemotropism (Wind Response)
- Stipes grow into wind flow during early development (before Stage 2)
- This positions the cap for optimal spore dispersal
- Mechanism may involve differential evaporative demand across the stipe

## Fungal Cell Biology

- **Hyphae**: tubular filamentous cells; typically 2-10 micrometers in diameter
- **Septa**: cross-walls dividing hyphae; pores allow cytoplasmic streaming
- **Mycelium**: collective mass of hyphae; can cover acres in nature
- **Cell wall**: primarily chitin and glucans (not cellulose like plants)
- **Extracellular digestion**: secrete enzymes into substrate, absorb dissolved nutrients
- **Woronin bodies**: specialized organelles plugging septal pores to maintain compartmentalization after damage

## Asexual vs Sexual Reproduction

- **Sexual**: meiosis produces genetically diverse spores; basidiospores in Basidiomycota, ascospores in Ascomycota
- **Asexual**: mitotic spores (conidia) or fragmentation; clones the parent genotype; important for rapid colonization
- Many fungi can switch between modes depending on environmental conditions
