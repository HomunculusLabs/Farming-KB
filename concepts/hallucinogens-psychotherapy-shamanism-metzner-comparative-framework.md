# Hallucinogens in Psychotherapy and Shamanism — A Comparative Framework

Ralph Metzner's 1998 paper "Hallucinogenic Drugs and Plants in Psychotherapy and Shamanism" (published in the *Journal of Psychoactive Drugs*) provides the most systematic comparison of Western psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy with indigenous shamanic healing practices. Metzner identifies four paradigms of hallucinogen use and highlights two radical differences between shamanic and Western worldviews.

## Four Paradigms of Hallucinogen Use

### 1. Western Psychedelic Psychotherapy

Two sub-models emerged in the 1950s–1960s:

**Psycholytic Therapy** (European model):
- Developed by Czech psychiatrist Stanislav Grof and others
- Series of sessions at gradually increasing doses
- Freudian analytic framework — LSD loosens defenses to reveal unconscious dynamics
- Grof discovered that beyond childhood issues, patients relive **perinatal matrices** (birth trauma)
- Term "psycholytic" = "psychic loosening or opening" (*seelische Auflockerung*)

**Psychedelic Therapy** (Anglo-American model):
- One or few high-dose sessions
- Coined by Humphrey Osmond and Aldous Huxley
- Originally used for alcoholism treatment (simulating "bottoming out" experience)
- Vivid hallucinatory imagery leading to insight and transformation

**Key shared principles:**
1. Profoundly expanded state of consciousness — goes beyond therapeutic insight into questioning fundamental reality concepts
2. **[[set-and-setting]]** are the primary determinants; the drug is a catalyst (Leary, Litwin & Metzner 1963)
3. Two metaphors: **amplifier** (nonspecific psychic amplifier) and **microscope** (opens hidden realms of mind to direct observation)
4. Therapist's personal experience with psychedelics is essential — without it, communication with the patient in an altered state is severely limited
5. Access to transcendent, [[metzner-transpersonal-dimensions-psychedelic-therapy]] of consciousness (Grof's "holotropic" states)

### 2. Shamanic Rituals of Healing and Divination

Characteristics that distinguish shamanic from Western approaches:

1. **Minimal talking** during the ceremony (except preparation/integration phases)
2. **Singing/chanting** is essential — rapid rhythmic beat similar to shamanic drumming
3. **Darkness or low light** — facilitates emergence of visions (exception: peyote around fire)
4. **Shaman takes the medicine** to "see" the cause of illness (not the patient)

**Common shamanic healing patterns:**
- Extraction of toxin (sorcerous implant)
- Retrieval of split-off soul fragment
- Dismemberment and reconstitution

**Shamanic divination** = equivalent of Western diagnosis, but accessed through the "intelligence associated with the plant medicine" — indigenous healers call entheogenic plants **"plant teachers"**

### 3. Syncretic Folk Religious Ceremonies

Large-group ceremonial forms bridging shamanism and organized religion:

- **[[native-american-church]] (NAC):** Peyote ceremony in tipi, all-night, roadman presiding, constant peyote songs. Legal protection for members with 25%+ Indian ancestry
- **[[brazilian-ayahuasca-churches]]:** Santo Daime, União do Vegetal (UDV), Barquinha — founded by rubber tappers in 1950s, now thousands of members. Long-term use associated with reduced addiction and no adverse health effects (Grob et al. 1996)
- **Bwiti Cult (Gabon/Zaire):** Iboga/Tabernanthe iboga ceremonies with death-rebirth initiation. Ibogaine shows specific action on cocaine receptor sites (Lotsof 1995)

### 4. Hybrid Shamanic-Therapeutic Rituals

Metzner's "[[metzner-neoshamanic-medicine-circles]]" blend Western and indigenous approaches:

- Circle structure with altar or fire
- Experienced elder/guide with assistants
- Low light / eye-shades
- Music: drumming, rattling, singing
- Invocation of four directions and elements
- Talking/singing staff ("council" format)
- Pre-ceremony intention sharing and post-ceremony integration

## Two Radical Differences

Metzner identifies two points where shamanic worldviews diverge fundamentally from Western science:

1. **Multiple realities/worlds** exist and can be explored in expanded states of consciousness ("nonordinary reality," "spirit world," "dreamtime," "upper/lower world")
2. **Spirit beings** encountered in dreams and visions are as real as physical organisms — they function as allies, power animals, guardians, and helpers
