## Fungal Sexual Pheromones and Mating Communication

Fungi communicate with one another through [[bloomfield-fungal-reproduction-and-chemical-communication]] in the [[bloomfield-pythium-oomycete-water-molds]] produce eggs in sacs called oogonia that swell at the tips of
hyphal branches. Male structures called antheridia develop as slender offshoots from
hyphae, attach to the oogonia surface, penetrate the eggs, and inject sperm. The
pheromones coordinating this process are antheridiol, released by the female to
attract males, and oogoniol, released by males and recognized by females. The chemical
structures of both compounds are very similar to human sex hormones, including
estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone. All are ring-shaped lipids called steroids,
employed for relaying messages between cells.

## The Mating Sequence

The female initiates the process by releasing antheridiol from her mycelium. If a male
is growing sufficiently close, he responds by producing antheridia. As these grow
toward the antheridiol source, they release oogoniol, which the female recognizes.
Sensing the success of her seductive behavior, the female forms oogonia and secretes
more antheridiol to inflame every male in the neighborhood. She soon becomes entangled
in a cage of male sex branches that clamber around until they pinpoint the source of
the pheromone. Once contact is made, the tips of the antheridia flatten against the
curved oogonium surface, stimulating egg formation. Finally, stumpy hyphae protrude
from each antheridium, penetrate the egg chamber, and inject sperm nuclei.
