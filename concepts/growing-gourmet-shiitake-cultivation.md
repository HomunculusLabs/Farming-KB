# Shiitake Cultivation

Shiitake (Lentinula edodes) is one of the most cultivated mushrooms in the world, with a history stretching back to the Sung Dynasty in China (960-1127 AD). Dr. Mimura's work in 1904 established modern shiitake cultivation techniques. Paul Stamets' treatment in Growing Gourmet and Medicinal Mushrooms focuses on indoor cultivation on supplemented sawdust, complementing traditional log-based methods.

## History and Significance

Shiitake has been cultivated for over a thousand years, making it one of the oldest deliberately grown mushrooms. The Sung Dynasty developed the original method of inoculating logs with shiitake spore water. Modern indoor cultivation on supplemented sawdust blocks has dramatically increased yields and shortened production cycles compared to traditional log cultivation, which typically takes 6-18 months from inoculation to first harvest.

## Mycelial Characteristics

Shiitake mycelium on agar is typically whitish and cottony, gradually thickening and becoming more tenacious as it matures. A distinctive feature of shiitake mycelium is browning, which is strain-specific in its onset and intensity. Some strains brown within days of reaching the edge of a petri dish, while others remain white for weeks. This browning is a natural maturation process and not a sign of contamination.

The mycelium forms a dense, rhizomorphic network on grain spawn. Recovery time from inoculation (leap-off) typically occurs within 2-3 days at 75F (24C), which is considered good performance. Shiitake mycelium produces a strong, tenacious hold on supplemented sawdust substrates, binding the block into a firm mass after colonization.

## Indoor Cultivation on Supplemented Sawdust

Indoor shiitake cultivation uses supplemented hardwood sawdust blocks, typically containing 20% rice bran or wheat bran by dry weight. Red alder (Alnus rubra) is described as an ideal base wood for sawdust, though many hardwoods including oak, beech, and maple are suitable. Blocks are typically 5 lbs of moistened sawdust/bran mix at 60-70% moisture content, packed into autoclavable polypropylene bags.

### Spawn Rate and Colonization

Approximately 100-150g of rye grain spawn is used per block. Colonization takes approximately 10-20 days at incubation temperatures of 75-80F (24-27C). After full colonization, blocks are moved to the growing room, where they may require an additional 30 days before fruiting conditions trigger mushroom formation.

### Fruiting Parameters

Shiitake requires distinct environmental triggers to initiate fruiting. Most strains require a cold shock, where the core temperature of the substrate is dropped below 60-65F (16-18C) for several days. Once primordia form, temperatures can be elevated to the 70-80F (21-27C) range for fruitbody development. Light is essential for proper cap development; shiitake demonstrates strong photosensitivity.

## Contamination Management

Shiitake blocks are susceptible to green mold (Trichoderma species), particularly during the fruiting phase. Stamets recommends a strategy of humidity fluctuation between 70-100% relative humidity to control green mold outbreaks. Additionally, contaminated blocks can be treated by washing with a forceful water spray, which physically removes the mold colony before it can establish itself firmly on the block surface.

### Humidity Management

The recommended approach involves alternating periods of high humidity (95-100%) for primordia formation with lower humidity (70-80%) periods that suppress mold growth. This cycling strategy takes advantage of shiitake's tolerance for brief humidity drops while creating unfavorable conditions for competitors.

## Substrate Preferences

Shiitake is a primary decomposer of hardwoods. Red alder is identified as particularly well-suited, often called a "weed tree" in the Pacific Northwest. The addition of 5% gypsum (calcium sulfate) to the sawdust/bran mix helps maintain kernel separation after sterilization and provides calcium and sulfur for mushroom metabolism. Supplementation with rice bran at 20% of dry weight significantly increases biological efficiency over plain sawdust.

## Yield and Harvest

Mushrooms should be picked before the caps fully expand and the gills are exposed. Properly managed indoor blocks can produce multiple flushes over a cropping cycle of several weeks to months. The form of product sold to market is primarily fresh mushrooms, though drying is also common. Shiitake is one of the best mushrooms for drying, as the drying process actually enhances flavor through the development of guanylic acid.

## Nutritional and Medicinal Properties

Shiitake contains significant amounts of lentinan, a beta-glucan polysaccharide with demonstrated immunomodulatory and anti-tumor properties. The mushroom is rich in B vitamins, ergosterol (provitamin D2), and contains all essential amino acids. It has been the subject of extensive clinical research, particularly in Japan, where lentinan is approved as an adjunct cancer therapy.

## Comparison with Log Cultivation

Indoor sawdust cultivation offers faster time-to-harvest (2-3 months vs. 6-18 months on logs), higher biological efficiency, and more consistent environmental control. However, many cultivators and consumers prefer the flavor and appearance of log-grown shiitake, which tend to have thicker caps and more concentrated flavor. Log-grown shiitake typically commands premium prices in specialty markets.
