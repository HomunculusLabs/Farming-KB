# Ready-to-Till Test

The ready-to-till test is a simple hands-on method for determining whether
solomon [[solomon-soil-testing-and-interpretation-low-input]] ready to till soil moisture test levels are appropriate for working the ground. Developed
and popularized by [[steve-solomon]] in *Gardening When It Counts* (2005), this
test is especially critical for anyone gardening in [[clay-soil-management-solomon]] soils,
where the consequences of tilling at the wrong moisture level are severe
and long-lasting.

## Performing the Test

The test itself takes only a moment and requires no tools:

1. Reach down into the soil to the depth you intend to work (typically
   the top 6–8 inches for most [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]] operations).
2. Take a generous handful of soil.
3. Compress it firmly in your hand, squeezing it into a ball roughly the
   size of a golf ball.
4. Press firmly on the ball with your thumb.

The way the ball responds to your thumb tells you everything you need to
know about whether the soil is ready.

## Reading the Results

### Soil Is Just Right to Work

If the ball crumbles apart easily under your thumb pressure, the soil is at
the ideal moisture content for working. This is the condition every gardener
hopes to find. The soil will break apart into small, friable pieces rather
than forming hard clods or turning to paste.

### Soil Is Too Wet

If the ball feels plastic or sticky and your thumb makes a visible dent in
a gooey surface, the soil is too wet to work. **Stop and wait.** Do not be
tempted to proceed — the damage done by working wet soil, particularly
[[clay-soil-management-solomon]], is severe and can ruin a seedbed for the entire season.

### Soil Will Not Form a Ball

If the soil refuses to hold together as a ball at all, one of two
conditions is likely:

- **The soil is too dry.** It needs rain or irrigation before it can be
  worked effectively.
- **The soil contains little or no clay.** Sandy and clayless soils
  simply cannot be molded into a ball regardless of their moisture level.

## Clayless Soils: A Special Case

Sandy and otherwise clayless soils behave very differently from clay soils
when it comes to workability. Because they lack the binding particles that
create clods, clayless soils can be worked at virtually any moisture level
without risk of forming hard, damaging lumps. Whether wet or dry, these
soils will not turn against you.

However, this freedom comes with a trade-off. Clayless soils do not hold
moisture well. Gardens built on purely sandy ground can become droughty
unless supplemented with regular irrigation or unless there happens to be a
moisture-holding subsoil layer not far below the surface. In that
exceptional case, deep-rooted crops can tap into the reserved water below,
partially compensating for the soil's poor surface-level retention.

## Spring vs. Autumn Timing

The ready-to-till test matters most in spring, when proper solomon ready to till soil moisture test
must be achieved before planting can begin. Working soil too early in spring
is one of the most common gardening mistakes.

In autumn, the stakes are lower. If you are doing end-of-season [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]]
and the soil is very slightly too moist, making a few clods is acceptable
because winter freeze-thaw cycles and precipitation will break them down
naturally before spring planting returns.

## The Dangers of Working Wet Clay Soil

Working [[clay-soil-management-solomon]] when it is too wet causes a cascade of problems:

- **Instant clod formation.** The soil instantly forms rock-hard clods
  that resist all attempts to break them apart afterward.
- **Dust on drying.** When those clods eventually dry out, they can
  crumble into fine dust that is easily blown or washed away.
- **Airless goo after rain.** When rain hits worked clay that has been
  allowed to dry and crack, it slumps into an airless, structureless goo
  where seeds will rot and roots cannot grow.

There is typically only a short window during the drying cycle of clay soil
when it reaches the proper consistency for forming a good seedbed. Missing
this window means waiting for another drying cycle — which may not come
before critical planting dates have passed.

## Hand-Digging vs. Rototilling

Solomon expresses a clear preference for hand-digging over rototilling,
particularly for the home food gardener. While hand-digging is slower, it
requires no gasoline, no engine oil, and no ongoing maintenance. Solomon
argues that with good technique and sharp tools, hand-digging actually
takes little more physical effort than operating a rototiller — and the
results are often superior, especially in clay soils where a tiller can
easily over-work the ground and destroy soil structure.

## Key Takeaways

- The ready-to-till test is essential whenever working [[clay-soil-management-solomon]].
- A crumbling ball means go; a plastic, dented ball means wait.
- [[clay-soil-management-solomon]] workability is the single biggest timing constraint for
  spring planting — getting it wrong can cost you the season.
- Clayless soils are forgiving and can be worked at any moisture level,
  but they demand irrigation or deep subsoil moisture to avoid drought
  stress.
- In autumn, err slightly on the wet side is acceptable; in spring,
  patience is critical.
- Hand-digging offers a low-maintenance alternative to mechanical
  [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]] with comparable effort and often better results.
