# Cider Making Guide

Hard cider is one of the most accessible and rewarding homestead fermentation projects. If you have apple trees, you have the primary ingredient. Hard cider has a long history in rural homesteading and is experiencing a major renaissance. The process is simpler than [[beer-brewing-basics]] or [[wine-making-from-grapes]] but produces a beverage of equal complexity and character.

## Apple Varieties for Cider

The best cider is made from a blend of apple types. Single-variety ciders can be excellent, but blending gives you control over sweetness, acidity, tannin, and flavor.

### Categories

**Sweets (provide sugar/alcohol)**
- Golden Delicious, Gala, Fuji, Jonagold, Mutsu
- High sugar, low acid, low tannin
- Use at 30-50% of the blend

**Sharps (provide acidity)**
- Granny Smith, Bramley's Seedling, Rhode Island Greening, Stayman
- High acid, moderate sugar
- Use at 10-30% of the blend

**Bittersweets (provide tannin and body)**
- Dabinett, Kingston Black, Yarlington Mill, Harry Masters Jersey, Chisel Jersey
- These are true cider apples -- high tannin, high sugar, low acid
- Use at 20-40% of the blend
- Hard to find commercially -- worth planting if serious about cider

**Bittersharps (provide tannin and acidity)**
- Foxwhelp, Brown Snout, Stoke Red
- High tannin, high acid
- Use at 10-20% of the blend
- Also rare -- look for them at specialty nurseries

### Homestead Blending Strategy
Most homesteaders have dessert apples, not true cider apples. You can still make excellent cider:

- Blend sweet apples with tart apples for balance
- Add crabapples for tannin (use sparingly -- 5-10% of the blend)
- If all your apples are sweet, add acid (tartaric or malic acid) to the juice
- A simple blend of 60% sweet, 30% tart, 10% crabapple works well

### Sourcing Apples
- Pick your own or harvest from existing trees
- Scavenge from abandoned orchards (with permission)
- Buy from local orchards -- many press and sell fresh juice
- Cider apples are available from specialty nurseries for planting
- Avoid supermarket apples -- often treated with post-harvest chemicals that inhibit fermentation

## Equipment

### Pressing
- Fruit crusher/scratter: breaks apples before pressing. Hand-crank models ($80-200) or build your own.
- Cider press: basket press is traditional and effective. Plans available for DIY builds ($50-150 in materials). Commercial presses: $200-800.
- Alternative: use a juicer for very small batches (1-2 gallons)

### Fermentation
- Fermenter: food-grade bucket (6.5 gallon) or glass carboy (5 gallon)
- Airlock and stopper
- Siphon and tubing
- Hydrometer and test jar
- Thermometer
- Sanitizer (Star San)
- Bottles: beer bottles with caps, wine bottles with corks, or swing-top bottles
- Capper or corker

## The Cider Making Process

### Step 1: Preparing the Apples
- Wash apples thoroughly (do not peel or core -- wild yeasts on the skin contribute character)
- Remove rotten apples and any with significant mold
- Crush or scratter the apples to a pulp consistency (like chunky applesauce)
- Press immediately after crushing to minimize oxidation

### Step 2: Pressing
- Load the press with pomace (crushed apples)
- Apply pressure gradually and evenly
- Collect the juice in a sanitized container
- Press yields approximately 1 gallon of juice per 15-20 pounds of apples
- Let the juice settle for 1-2 hours in a cool place, then siphon off the clear juice (leaving sediment behind)

### Step 3: Testing the Juice
- Measure specific gravity with a hydrometer. Target: 1.045-1.065 (SG) for a standard cider (5-8% ABV).
  - Below 1.045: the cider will be thin and low-alcohol. Add sugar or apple juice concentrate to raise SG.
  - Above 1.065: will produce a strong cider. Dilute with water or accept a higher-ABV product.
- Measure pH if possible. Target: 3.2-3.8. Below 3.2 is too sour; above 3.8 risks bacterial spoilage.
- Measure titratable acidity if possible. Target: 0.45-0.75%.

### Step 4: Choosing Your Yeast Approach

**Wild Fermentation (Traditional)**
- Use no added yeast -- rely on wild yeasts naturally present on the apples and in the environment
- Results in complex, funky, terroir-driven cider
- Less predictable -- can produce off-flavors or fail entirely
- More consistent with unwashed, organic apples from old orchards
- Fermentation may take longer and produce lower alcohol

**Cultured Yeast (Reliable)**
- Pitch a specific commercial yeast strain
- More predictable, cleaner fermentation
- Recommended for beginners
- Popular strains:
  - Nottingham Ale: clean, neutral, reliable. Good all-purpose cider yeast.
  - Lalvin 71B: fruity, enhances apple character. Good for sweet cider.
  - Safale US-05: clean, American-style. Produces dry cider.
  - Mangrove Jack's M02: specifically selected for cider. Enhances fruit aromatics.
  - Lalvin EC-1118 (Champagne): very strong, ferments to complete dryness. Good for sparkling cider.

### Step 5: Primary Fermentation
- Transfer juice to the fermenter, leaving 2-3 inches of headspace
- If using cultured yeast: pitch according to package instructions. Optionally add yeast nutrient (1 tsp per gallon).
- If wild fermenting: simply cover the fermenter with a cloth or loose lid for the first 48 hours, then fit an airlock.
- Ferment at 60-70F for 2-4 weeks
- Active fermentation (bubbling) should begin within 24-72 hours
- Fermentation is complete when bubbling stops and gravity is stable (typically 0.995-1.005)

### Step 6: Racking
- Siphon the cider off the sediment (lees) into a clean, sanitized vessel
- This clarifies the cider and prevents off-flavors from sitting on dead yeast
- Rack again after another 1-2 months if sediment accumulates
- Add potassium metabisulfite (1/4 tsp per 5 gallons) at each racking to prevent oxidation

### Step 7: Backsweetening (Optional)
If you want sweet or semi-sweet cider, you must stabilize before adding sugar:

1. Add potassium metabisulfite (1/4 tsp per 5 gallons) and potassium sorbate (1/2 tsp per 5 gallons)
2. Wait 24-48 hours for the sulfite and sorbate to take effect
3. Sweeten to taste with: fresh apple juice, apple juice concentrate, sugar, or honey
4. For still cider: bottle and serve
5. For sparkling cider: add priming sugar (3/4 cup per 5 gallons) and bottle in beer bottles with caps

### Step 8: Bottling
- Still cider: bottle in wine bottles with corks or swing-top bottles. Age 1-3 months.
- Sparkling cider: bottle in thick beer bottles with caps. Prime with sugar as for beer. Age 2-4 weeks for carbonation.
- Condition at room temperature, then store cool. Serve chilled.

## Aging

- Young cider (1-3 months): fresh, bright apple character, simple but pleasant
- Aged cider (6-12 months): more complex, mellow, developed flavors
- Extended aging (1-3+ years): gains depth and complexity. Higher-tannin ciders benefit most from aging.
- Store at 50-60F, on their side if corked, away from light

## Advanced Techniques

### Bottle Conditioning
Prime with sugar and yeast for natural carbonation in the bottle. Use strong bottles ( Belgian-style or champagne bottles) and add extra sugar (1 cup per 5 gallons) for higher carbonation levels. Age 2-6 weeks before serving.

### Keeving (Traditional French Method)
A complex process that creates naturally sweet, sparkling cider by retaining nutrients that promote a slow, incomplete fermentation. Involves calcium chloride addition, cold settling, and careful racking. Produces the finest traditional ciders but requires experience.

### Barrel Aging
Age cider in oak barrels (5-50 gallon). Adds vanilla, spice, and tannin complexity. New barrels impart more oak character; used bourbon or wine barrels add unique flavors. Age 1-6 months depending on the barrel and desired intensity.

## Common Problems

- Cider tastes like vinegar: acetic acid bacteria from excessive air exposure. Prevent with full vessels and proper sulfiting.
- Cloudy cider: pectin haze from insufficient pectin enzyme. Add pectic enzyme before fermentation.
- Rotten egg smell: hydrogen sulfide from stressed yeast. Add yeast nutrient and aerate.
- Cider won't clear: time is the best solution. Rack again. Fining agents (gelatin, sparkolloid) can speed clearing.
- Exploding bottles: fermentation not complete when bottled. Always confirm fermentation is finished (stable gravity) before bottling.
