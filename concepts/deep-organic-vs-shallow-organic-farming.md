# Deep-Organic vs Shallow-Organic Farming

## The Living Soil as Foundation

The concept of deep-organic farming rests on the recognition that soil is not an inert substance but a living ecosystem teeming with organisms whose life processes make plant nutrients accessible. A teaspoon of fertile soil contains far more than a million microscopic organisms—numbers now considered vastly underestimated. These creatures—bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, arthropods, and more—are the true workers of soil fertility. Their food is organic matter, and their metabolic activities create the conditions under which plants thrive.

## Chemical vs Natural Approaches to Soil Fertility

The chemical approach bypasses the soil ecosystem entirely, applying factory-created soluble fertilizers to feed plants directly with preprocessed nutrients. The natural approach adds organic matter to nourish the soil organisms, which in turn make nutrients available to plants. The distinction is fundamental: feeding the soil versus feeding the plants. But "feeding the soil" is really about feeding the soil creatures. Substituting a few soluble elements for a whole living system is like thinking an intravenous needle could deliver a delicious meal.

## Origins of Organic Farming

In the mid-1930s, organic farming arose from renewed recognition of soil organic matter's vital importance. Farmers observed undesirable changes in their soil and diminished crop and livestock health following the shift to chemical farming in the twentieth century. Their improvements over pre-chemical practices included more successful composting, symbiotically designed crop rotations, more effective green manuring, better management of plant residues, and adding mineral nutrients in their most natural form. The organic pioneers understood that the farm is not a factory but a human-managed microcosm of the natural world, where soil fertility is maintained through biological recycling of all plant and animal residues.

## The Battle for Legitimacy

The history of organic farming is a story of natural truths flourishing against corporate and industrial opposition. When Eliot Coleman started in 1965, the forces against organic farming included the USDA with its enormous budget, all land-grant universities, extension services, every feed and seed store, and the massive agrochemical industry. On the organic side were a few old-time farmers who never adopted chemicals and idealistic young newcomers.

The 1977 Washington University study by Barry Commoner's group provided the first major evidence: in side-by-side paired comparisons, Midwestern organic farmers were just as successful as their chemical-using neighbors. Earlier positive studies from France and the Netherlands had been dismissed by USDA officials. Even the USDA's own 1938 Yearbook (*Soils and Men*) and 1957 Yearbook (*Soil*) read like organic farming textbooks, yet by the mid-1960s these ideas were ridiculed and chemical farming was labeled "conventional."

## Deep-Organic Farming Defined

Deep-organic farmers, beyond rejecting agricultural chemicals, actively seek better ways to farm by mimicking Nature's elegant soil-plant economy. Their practices include:

- Using freely available natural soil foods from deep-rooting legumes, green manures, and composts
- Correcting the causes of infertility by establishing vigorous soil life
- Understanding that pest problems arise from plant stress
- Managing soil tilth, nutrient balance, organic matter, drainage, airflow, crop rotations, and varietal selection to reduce plant stress
- Freeing themselves from dependence on purchased fertilizers and pest-control products

The goal of deep-organic farming is to grow the most nutritious food possible while respecting the primacy of a healthy planet. This approach is threatening to the industrial agricultural establishment because it cannot be easily quantified, controlled, or profited from by outsiders.

## Shallow-Organic Farming Defined

Shallow-organic farmers reject agricultural chemicals but then look for quick-fix inputs. They use bagged or bottled organic fertilizers that temporarily treat symptoms of infertility, and they treat pest symptoms with the latest natural organic weapons. In doing so, they remain dependent on the industrial supply network—the same middlemen and fertilizer suppliers that chemical farmers rely on. The goal of shallow-organic farming is merely to follow approved guidelines and respect the primacy of international commerce. The industrial establishment views shallow-organic farming as an acceptable variation of chemical agribusiness since it remains easy to quantify, control, and profit from.

## The Fundamental Difference in Worldview

The distinction between deep and shallow organic is ultimately a difference in life views. The shallow view regards the natural world as consisting of mostly inadequate, usually malevolent systems that must be battled or modified. The deep-organic view understands that the natural world consists of elegant, impeccably designed, smoothly functioning systems that must be studied and nurtured.

Deep-organic farmers recognize that the living systems of truly fertile soil contain yet-undiscovered benefits for plants, livestock, and humans—benefits we may not even know how to test for because we are unaware of their mechanisms. They experience these benefits daily in the improved vigor of their crops and livestock, what scientists disparagingly call "anecdotal evidence."

## The Threat of Co-Option

Since the 1930s, organic farming has followed the traditional three-step progression of any idea challenging orthodoxy: dismissal, decades of contestation, and finally attempted takeover. The USDA moved to define and regulate "organic" standards, while ignoring the organic goal in favor of regulating the organic method. [[lady-eve-balfour]] warned in 1977: "The techniques of organic farming cannot be imprisoned in a rigid set of rules. They depend essentially on the attitude of the farmer."

## In Defense of Farmer's Instinct

Coleman uses a musical analogy: hearing a great artist perform without a sound system is like fresh food from a deep-organic grower—the unfiltered, direct experience is qualitatively different and nourishing in ways that mediated versions cannot replicate. Children's honest taste buds provide evidence: children notorious for hating vegetables specifically request the carrots and salad greens from deep-organic farms, and these vegetables become the trading currency of choice in school lunch boxes. This practical, experiential evidence, while dismissed by reductionist science, represents the accumulated wisdom of farmers working directly with living systems.

## The Rock Climber's Perspective

Coleson frames the farmer's mindset through his rock climbing experience. The scientist stands far from the cliff, speculating on coefficients of friction and surface fracturing. The merchandiser speculates on products to palliate the impossible. The climber—the farmer—stands next to the problem, studying and planning how to solve it. A problem is something to be solved, not studied to death or marketed. Where you stand and what your goals are determine what you see and what you do. Organic farmers' success threatens the scientific and commercial establishment because it demonstrates their redundancy—proving that imagination and determination, working with natural processes, can achieve what billions in research and products claimed was impossible.
