## Symbiotic Fungi — Lichen Symbiosis Overview

Lichens are mutualistic symbiotic associations between an alga and a fungus in which the two organisms jointly form a thallus that is distinct from either partner. The fungal partner is known as the mycobiont and the algal partner as the phycobiont. Both partners benefit from this symbiotic association, making lichens one of the oldest and most ecologically important examples of [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]]. However, if the lichen contains an additional partner — namely a green alga — the blue-green alga becomes relieved of its photosynthetic function and is geared to fix nitrogen exclusively. This is evidenced by the fact that in the presence of a green alga in a three-member association, the blue-green alga produces more heterocysts, which are the specialised sites of nitrogen fixation.

## Nature of the Mutualistic Relationship

The fungus derives nutrition from the alga, receiving photosynthetically produced carbohydrates. In turn, the alga is protected by the fungus, which provides shelter, water retention, mineral nutrients, and a favourable microenvironment. This mutual benefit characterises the lichen as a true mutualism within the broader spectrum of symbiotic relationships that also includes commensalism and parasitism.

The term symbiosis was first used in 1879 by Heinrich Anton de Bary, who defined it as "the living together of unlike organisms." Lichens represent a classic example of this definition, where the integrated thallus is morphologically and physiologically distinct from either partner alone.

## Growth Characteristics

Lichens are extremely slow-growing organisms, showing approximately 1 mm of growth per year. Despite this slow growth rate, they are highly tolerant to xeric (dry) conditions such as desiccation and heat. Consequently, one can find lichens growing on tree trunks, rocks, bare soil, and exposed surfaces across diverse environments — from Arctic tundra to tropical forests, from coastal zones to alpine habitats.

## Environmental Sensitivity

While highly tolerant to desiccation and temperature extremes, lichens are very sensitive to atmospheric pollutants. They are rarely seen near human habitations where automobile fumes and factory effluents prevail. This sensitivity to air quality makes lichens important bioindicators of environmental health and atmospheric purity.

## Broader Context of Fungal-Algal Symbiosis

Lichen symbiosis is part of a broader pattern of fungal associations with photosynthetic organisms found across the plant kingdom.

### Bryophyte Symbiosis

Liverworts such as *Anthoceros*, *Notothylas*, and *Blassia* possess blue-green algae (*Nostoc*) as symbiotic partners in mucilage cavities on the ventral side of the thallus. The mucilage dries out as the thallus matures, creating air-filled chambers invaded by Nostoc. Within the cavity, the liverwort produces branched hypha-like filaments interspersed with the algal colony. Photosynthate is transported to Nostoc by the bryophyte, while fixed nitrogen is transferred from Nostoc to the bryophyte (Rashid 1998). Nostoc in symbiosis produces more heterocysts than in the free-living condition, indicating active nitrogen fixation.

### Pteridophyte Symbiosis

The floating fern *Azolla* harbours filaments of *Anabaena* within ovoid cavities inside its leaves, forming a nitrogen-fixing symbiotic relationship. When free-living, *Anabaena* develops only 5-10% of cells into heterocysts; in symbiosis this increases to 25-30%.
