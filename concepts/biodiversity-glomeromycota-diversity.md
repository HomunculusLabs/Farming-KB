## Reproduction and Life History

Most evidence, including genetic analysis, indicates that AM fungi reproduce
clonally via mitospores formed in soil and in roots. Although one species,
*Gigaspora decipiens*, has been claimed to have a sexual reproductive phase,
this remains controversial. In these asexual organisms, genetic variation
within and between species is expected as a natural response to local and
regional selection pressures.

Infectivity of different fungal propagules varies among fungi at the subordinal
level. Mycorrhizal roots, detached hyphae, and spores of fungi in the
suborder Glomineae generally are highly infective, whereas auxiliary cells and
detached hyphae of fungi in the suborder Gigasporineae are not. Sporulation
generally commences between 4 and 8 weeks after onset of colonization and
plateaus in 12 to 16 weeks in greenhouse pot cultures.

## Ecological Significance

The major constraint on understanding the diversity and ecology of AM fungi is
the inability to culture them apart from their plant hosts. The plant provides
carbon to the fungus largely via an arbuscule-plant cell plasmalemma interface.
External fungal hyphae improve phosphorus acquisition by the plant in soils
with low phosphorus levels.

In soils where phosphorus levels exceed host requirements, the AM symbiosis may
become inhibitory or even pathogenic. In nature, [[biodiversity-fungi-soil-fungal-communities|biodiversity of fungi in soil ecosystems]].
