# Semi-Wild Vegetable Growing Techniques

In Chapter 4 of [[fukuoka-clay-pellet-desert-greening]] for detailed pellet-making methods.

For vegetables with poor germination such as spinach, carrot, and burdock: "A simple and effective solution is to coat the seeds with a mixture of clay and wood ashes or to sow them enclosed in [[fukuoka-aerial-seeding-clay-pellets-desert-revegetation-technique]]."

## Leguminous Vegetables

Include leguminous vegetables in seeds sown among weeds from spring to early summer. The best choices are "asparagus bean, cowpea, and mung bean" because they are "inexpensive and high-yielding." These contribute nitrogen to the soil while producing food.

## Weak Vegetables: Tomatoes and Eggplants

Tomatoes and eggplants "tend to become overwhelmed at first by weeds." The safest approach: raise young plants from [[vegetable-seed-and-transplant-guide]] them into a cover of clover and weeds. Critical technique: allow them to grow as bushes rather than training to single stems. "If the stem is allowed to creep along the ground, this will drop roots along its entire length from which many new stems will emerge and bear fruit."

## Perennial Potatoes

Once planted in the orchard, potatoes "will grow each year from the same spot, crawling vigorously along the ground to lengths of five feet or more and never giving in to weeds." If small potatoes are dug for food and some tubers always left behind, "there will never be any want of seed potatoes."

## Climbing Vegetables

**Gourd family** (bottle gourd, chayote) grow on sloping land and climb tree trunks. "A single hill of overwintered chayote will spread out over a 100-square-yard area and bear 600 fruit."

**Cucumbers, melons, squash, watermelons** should be trailing varieties. They need weed protection at the seedling stage but become strong once established. "Scattering bamboo stalks with the tops remaining or even firewood will give the vines something to grasp onto."

## Root Crops at the Orchard Edge

"Yam and sweet potato grow well at the foot of the orchard shelterbelt. These are especially enjoyable because the vines climb trees and produce fairly large tubers." Fukuoka was experimenting with growing sweet potato vines over the winter "to achieve large harvests" in cold climates.

## Giant Vegetables from Natural Cross-Pollination

When vegetables are allowed to cross-pollinate naturally near their original plants, "first-generation hybrids with different taste and appearance" result, "often absurdly large": giant daikon, huge turnips, giant Chinese cabbages, crosses between black mustard and Indian mustard, Chinese mustard and Indian mustard. "A garden of surprises."

In poor shallow soil, "daikon and turnips look ready to roll downhill, carrots and burdock short and thick and sinewy with many root hairs -- but strong pungent flavor makes them the very best."

## Self-Seeding Hardy Vegetables

Once planted, these vegetables take hold and persist indefinitely:
- Garlic
- Scallion
- Leek
- Honewort
- Dropwort
- Shepherd's-purse

## Things to Watch Out For

The method is "intended primarily for use in orchards, on earthen levees, and on fallow fields." One must accept the possibility of lower yields per unit area compared to intensive cultivation. However, "semi-wild vegetables have a pungent aroma and good body" and are "without question the most healthy and nutritious food man can eat."

Soil improvement is straightforward: "seeding clover together with the vegetables, and applying chicken droppings, manure, and well-rotted human waste." The types and amount of natural weed growth on the land reveal soil fertility and problems.

## Chickens in the Orchard

"Try raising vegetables as the undergrowth in an orchard and letting native fowl loose in the orchard. The birds will feed on the insects and their droppings will nourish the fruit trees. This is one perfect example of [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] at work."
