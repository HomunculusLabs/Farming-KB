# Dryland Building and Settlements

Dryland environments present unique challenges for human settlement, including extreme temperature fluctuations, water scarcity, high evaporation rates, and often poor or saline soils. Permaculture approaches to dryland building and settlement draw heavily on traditional techniques refined over centuries by indigenous peoples, combined with modern innovations in passive solar design and earth-sheltered construction.

## Earth-Sheltered and Underground Construction

Underground and earth-sheltered structures are among the most effective strategies for dryland habitation. Traditional earthed-up barns in high cold desert regions (such as near Aspen, Colorado) provide both winter hay storage and livestock shelter, with snow itself serving as insulation. In Fresno, California, underground orchards have been hand-dug to create shaded pits containing fig, grape, citrus, and pomegranate — a system covering 2.8 hectares where roots in pits stay cool, are easily mulched, and the ground surface can be funnel-shaped to catch and direct water to plant containers.

Looking up from these underground spaces, the sky is covered by the canopy of productive fruit trees, while the earth mass above moderates temperature extremes below. This principle — using the thermal mass of earth to buffer diurnal temperature swings — is fundamental to dryland architecture.

## Passive Cooling and Shading

In hot arid regions, cooling strategies are essential. At St. Theresa Mission in Central Australia, house verandahs are screened with vigorous climbing plants such as Bryona (a cucurbit), Lab-lab bean, and tomato vines. These living screens cool west-facing and sunward walls, sustained by waste wash-water and grey water — turning a waste product into a productive cooling system.

At Village Homes in Davis, California, earth-sheltered houses combine solar hot water, turf roofs, and slab floors for effective comfort control. Turf and living roofs provide active cooling through transpiration and shade, a technique also demonstrated on houses where ice plants and herbs serve the same function.

## Traditional Dryland Agriculture Infrastructure

The Canary Islands provide exceptional examples of dryland cultivation techniques refined over centuries. The deep mulch technique using volcanic cinder (arendo, a deep cinder mulch approximately 0.3 m deep) enables apple and pear production in areas receiving mainly night fog and condensation — virtually no rainfall. This system produces fruit, potato, and other crops by capturing atmospheric moisture.

Opuntia (prickly pear) serves multiple functions in Canarian dryland systems: fruit production (tuna), vegetable food, cattle forage, living hedge, and substrate for the cochineal scale insect used locally as a food colorant. Many desert Acacia species provide abundant poultry seed, as do Lycium and Solanum species, demonstrating the importance of multi-functional plant selection in water-limited environments.

## Nebka Formation and Desert Mound Building

Gidgee (Acacia cambagei) in Central Australian deserts creates natural desert mounds called nebkas — structures approximately 1.5 meters high and 12 meters across. Many sessile or weeping trees in deserts induce dust accretion in this way. Acacia species in the Kalahari perform the same function, providing deep absorbent soils and mulch for vines, other trees, and burrowing animals.

This natural process can be deliberately harnessed: by planting appropriate tree species, designers can create fertile microsites in otherwise barren desert landscapes, with the trees themselves building soil through wind-blown dust capture and leaf litter deposition.

## Biodome and Controlled Environment Agriculture

The Windstar Foundation's experimental Biodome in Old Snowmass, Colorado demonstrates year-round food production at 7,500 feet elevation in a high, cold, dryland environment where the natural growing season averages only 60 days. The domed greenhouse uses solar electricity, heating ponds, and integrates pest control through frogs and finches. Tilapia and hydroponic vegetables are grown in the pond systems, creating a nearly closed-loop food production system.

## Salting and Interceptor Banks

Dryland salinity is a major problem in regions like Western Australia, where deforestation, grazing, and wheat cultivation have caused widespread soil collapse. Visible indicators include collapsed soil at seepage lines and the presence of sea barley grass (Hordeum maritimum).

Interceptor banks are a key remediation strategy. A farmer develops these banks to divert surface water and throughflow off fields to natural valleys, isolating soil blocks for rain leaching and preventing a cascade effect of salty overland flow or throughflow moving downhill. Classic interceptor banks end on the streamline, are constructed with subsoil rammed on the downhill wall, and are typically 1.5 meters deep and 4 meters wide, spaced no more than 100 meters apart (or 1 meter vertical separation on slopes).

Where interceptor banks have been installed, soil collapse by flooding is prevented — evidenced by rushes growing on the upslope side and dry, cemented subsoils below the bank showing no throughflow or rising salted groundwater. Spike rush (Juncus) serves as an indicator species of imminent soil collapse, surface flooding, and anaerobic conditions.

## Windbreak and Protective Hedging

In desert environments, protective barriers serve multiple functions. Ocotillo is cultivated as a living fence in Papago gardens near Tucson, Arizona — it can be live-set to grow gated, and is widely used as reinforcement for mud wall construction. Its spiny nature provides effective protection while also serving as a useful construction material.

## Key Design Principles

- **Earth as thermal mass**: Use underground or earth-bermed construction to moderate temperatures
- **Living shade screens**: Climbing plants on walls provide evaporative cooling and food production
- **Grey water recycling**: Direct household wastewater to productive plantings
- **Atmospheric moisture harvesting**: Deep mulch systems can capture fog and condensation
- **Multi-functional plants**: Every species should serve multiple purposes (food, forage, shelter, soil building)
- **Nebka creation**: Harness wind-deposited soil around plantings to build fertility
- **Salinity management**: Use interceptor banks and careful water management to prevent salt accumulation
- **Species selection**: Choose plants adapted to arid conditions that provide multiple yields
