# Humus, Humic Acid, and Fulvic Acid Distinctions

The terms humus, humic acid, and fulvic acid are frequently used interchangeably in agriculture, but they represent distinct components of soil organic matter with different properties, formation pathways, and functions. Understanding these distinctions is essential for making informed decisions about soil amendments and compost tea production.

## Humus: The Complete System

Humus is the broadest category, encompassing the entire living and dead organic matter system in soil. It includes:

- **Living organisms**: Bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, earthworms, and plant roots
- **Simple organic matter**: Simple sugars, amino acids, proteins, simple carbohydrates, lipids, and thousands of other simple compounds
- **Intermediate organic matter**: Polysaccharides (multiple simple sugars bound together in increasingly complex, branched forms), amino-sugars, hormones, fats, and lipopolysaccharides
- **Fulvic acids**: Intermediate molecular weight fractions formed by the binding and branching of simpler compounds
- **Humic acids**: The most complex tier, formed when all of the above are bound together in very complex tertiary or three-dimensional binding complexes

Humus is therefore not a single substance but an entire ecosystem of organic matter at various stages of decomposition and complexity. The living component is critical: humus is not just dead carbon but a biologically active system.

## Humic Acid: Fungal Products

Humic acid sits at the most complex end of the organic matter spectrum. Key characteristics:

- Formed from the binding of simpler organic compounds into three-dimensional complexes
- Very high molecular weight
- Difficult to analyze chemically due to structural complexity
- Poorly soluble in water under acidic conditions
- Soluble in alkaline solutions
- Dark brown to black in color

### Biological Production

The production of humic acid is primarily a fungal process, though it requires the full soil foodweb:

- **Fungi** are the primary producers of humic acid. They create the complex "waste products" that form the backbone of humic acid through their enzymatic breakdown of lignin and cellulose
- **Earthworms** assist by processing organic material through their digestive systems, creating conditions favorable for fungal humification
- **Microarthropods** help fungi maintain dominance over bacterial processes in the soil environment
- **Bacteria** initiate the decomposition process by consuming simple sugars and rapid-growth food resources

Real humic acid production requires ALL of these organisms to be present. If the soil foodweb is incomplete, humic acid production is impaired. This is why simply adding humic acid products to soil without addressing the underlying biology is often ineffective: without the organisms to maintain the system, added humic acids are not replenished.

## Fulvic Acid: Intermediate Products

Fulvic acids occupy the middle ground in the organic matter complexity spectrum:

- Lower molecular weight than humic acids
- Soluble in both acidic and alkaline conditions
- Yellow to yellow-brown in color
- More chemically reactive than humic acids
- Formed as intermediate decomposition products on the pathway to humic acid

Fulvic acids are produced during the decomposition process by both bacteria and fungi, though the more complex fractions require fungal activity. They represent partially broken-down organic matter that has not yet been fully humified.

## The Bacteria-Then-Fungi Decomposition Sequence

Organic matter decomposition follows a predictable succession:

1. **Bacteria start the process**: They consume the easy-to-use sugars, simple proteins, and rapid-growth food resources. This is the initial, fastest phase of decomposition.

2. **Fungi take over**: They produce enzymes capable of breaking down lignin and cellulose, the tough structural compounds in plant material. Fungi come along and begin making the complex "waste products" that become humic acid.

3. **Fungi produce humic acid**: The fungal processing of complex plant compounds generates the three-dimensional binding complexes that constitute humic acid.

4. **Bacteria finish**: After fungi have done the heavy lifting, bacteria complete the job, further processing material into stable humus forms.

The key insight is that fungi are the bottleneck in humus production. If fungal biomass is low, humus formation is slow regardless of how much organic matter is added. The practical implication is that favoring fungi from the start of composting (rather than the typical bacterial-dominant approach) can dramatically speed up humification.

## Compost Tea Extraction of Humics

Extracting humic substances from compost requires more than passive leaching:

- **Simple water extraction or leaching** pulls out only some soluble nutrients and fails to extract the organisms
- **Active brewing in a good compost tea machine** with proper aeration extracts both organisms and the full range of humic substances
- The mixing action and bubble movement through compost are necessary to pull humics, fulvics, and soluble nutrients into solution
- Aeration must be serious enough to get organisms and soluble nutrients into solution

The tea then increases populations of beneficial organisms by maintaining conditions that allow them to grow to high numbers, delivering both the humic substances and the organisms needed to continue producing them in soil.

## Humic Acids as Fungal Foods

Humic acids serve an important dual role: they are both a product of fungal activity and a food source for fungi. When added to soil or compost tea:

- They feed beneficial fungi directly
- They support mycorrhizal colonization (VAM and ectomycorrhizal fungi)
- They help build macroaggregates in soil structure
- They contribute to nutrient retention and chelation

Liquid humic acid products are generally more effective than dry formulations because dry products take too long for organisms to solubilize during a typical compost tea brew cycle. Products that have been tested and shown to benefit fungi include Hydrahume AN, Eco-Nutrient kelp and humic acid blends, Horizon Ag humic acid, and Nutri-tech soluble forms.

## Self-Production vs. Commercial Products

The ideal approach is for growers to produce their own humic acids through biological composting:

- Fungi in thermal compost or worm compost naturally produce fulvic and humic materials
- These can be extracted through compost tea brewing and applied to crops
- The extracted tea contains both the humic substances and the organisms that produce them

Commercial humic acid products serve as a bridge for systems that have not yet built sufficient fungal biomass to produce their own humics. However, without establishing the fungal community that produces humic acids, dependence on external products continues indefinitely.

## The Clopyralid Caveat

A critical practical note: there are no known bacteria or fungi that decompose clopyralid, a persistent herbicide commonly found in grass clippings and hay. Compost made from clopyralid-contaminated material will carry the herbicide through the composting process and into humic acid products. This means that not all compost or commercial humic acid products are free of contaminants. Source material quality directly affects product quality.

## Charcoal, Leonardite, and Peat

Charcoal is related to leonardite and peat but is too carbonized to function as a fungal food. What charcoal does provide is physical fluff and aeration, which beneficial fungi need. Leonardite and peat are oxidized forms of ancient plant material that contain some humic substances, but their biological activity depends on whether beneficial organisms are present to interact with them.
