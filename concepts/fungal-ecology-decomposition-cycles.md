# Fungal Ecology — Decomposition Cycles
How fungi drive decomposition, nutrient cycling, and soil formation in ecosystems. Based on [[paul-stamets]]' *Mycelium Running* (2005). See also [[fungal-ecology]], [[fungal-decomposition]], [[fungal-ecosystem-roles]], and [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]].
## The Decomposition Succession
Stamets describes decomposition as a wave-like succession of organisms, each preparing the substrate for the next:
### Stage 1: Bacterial Colonization (Days)
- Bacteria are the first colonizers of fresh organic material
- Rapid population growth on simple sugars and amino acids
- Generate heat (thermophilic bacteria reach 130-160°F in compost piles)
- Create the moist, nutrient-rich environment that fungi need
### Stage 2: Fungal Colonization (Weeks)
- After initial bacterial breakdown, fungi colonize the partially decomposed material
- Saprophytic fungi specialize in breaking down complex plant polymers (cellulose, lignin, hemicellulose)
- White-rot fungi produce lignin peroxidases and manganese peroxidases to degrade lignin
- Brown-rot fungi use Fenton chemistry to depolymerize cellulose
- Mycelial networks spread through the substrate, secreting extracellular enzymes
### Stage 3: Invertebrate Processing (Weeks-Months)
- Fungal mycelium attracts and supports invertebrate decomposers
- Springtails (Collembola), mites, millipedes, and worms feed on fungal mycelium and spores
- Invertebrate grazing stimulates fungal growth (similar to grass grazing stimulating root growth)
- Castings and frass further break down organic material
- Invertebrates create channels for air and water movement through decomposing material
### Stage 4: Plant Recolonization (Months-Years)
- As decomposition progresses, nutrients become available for plant uptake
- Mycorrhizal fungi form partnerships with colonizing plant roots
- Endophytic fungi colonize plant tissues, providing disease resistance
- The cycle begins again as plants produce new organic material
## White Rot vs. Brown Rot
### White Rot Fungi
- Can degrade ALL components of wood, including lignin
- Produce extracellular enzymes: lignin peroxidase (LiP), manganese peroxidase (MnP), laccase
- Leave wood bleached or white because they remove the brown lignin
- Key species: Phanerochaete chrysosporium, Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus ostreatus
- **Critical for mycoremediation**: Their non-specific enzyme systems also degrade pollutants (PCBs, PAHs, petroleum)
### Brown Rot Fungi
- Degrade cellulose and hemicellulose but NOT lignin
- Use a different mechanism: generate hydroxyl radicals via Fenton chemistry (iron + H2O2)
- Leave wood brown and cubical because lignin remains
- Key species: Serpula lacrymans (dry rot), Postia placenta, Gloeophyllum trabeum
- Important in forest ecosystems for creating humus-rich brown rot residue
## Carbon Cycling
### Fungal Carbon Pathways
1. **Uptake**: Mycelium absorbs simple carbon compounds (sugars, amino acids) from substrate
2. **Respiration**: ~50% of absorbed carbon released as CO2 through respiration
3. **Biomass**: ~20% incorporated into mycelial tissue
4. **Exudates**: ~10-30% released as extracellular compounds (enzymes, organic acids)
5. **Storage**: Some carbon stored as glycogen, lipids, or in specialized structures (sclerotia, truffles)
### Carbon Sequestration
- Fungi play a critical role in long-term carbon storage
- **Glomalin**: Glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi; persists in soil for decades; binds soil aggregates and stores significant carbon
- **Humic substances**: Fungal decomposition products (humic and fulvic acids) are stable, long-lived carbon compounds
- **Ectomycorrhizal contribution**: ECM fungi contribute significantly to soil carbon through necromass (dead mycelium)
- **Deep soil carbon**: Mycorrhizal networks can transport carbon deep into soil profiles where it is protected from decomposition
## Soil Building
### How Fungi Create Soil
Stamets emphasizes that fungi are primary soil builders:
1. **Rock dissolution**: Fungal hyphae and associated organic acids dissolve minerals from rock particles, making nutrients available
2. **Aggregate formation**: Mycelium physically binds soil particles together; glomalin acts as biological glue
3. **Humus creation**: Partial decomposition of organic matter creates stable humus
4. **Channel creation**: Hyphal growth creates micropores for air and water movement
5. **Water retention**: Mycelial networks improve soil water-holding capacity
6. **Nutrient cycling**: Fungi decompose organic matter and make nutrients available to plants
### Forest Floor Layers
| Layer | Description | Dominant Organisms |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| Oi (litter) | Fresh leaves, twigs, recently fallen material | Bacteria, early fungi |
| Oe (fragmented) | Partially decomposed organic matter | White-rot fungi, actinomycetes |
| Oa (humus) | Well-decomposed organic matter, dark color | Brown-rot fungi, humus-feeding invertebrates |
| A (topsoil) | Mineral soil mixed with organic matter | Mycorrhizal fungi, bacteria, plant roots |
| E (eluviated) | Leached mineral layer | Limited biological activity |
| B (subsoil) | Accumulated minerals and clays | Deep mycorrhizal networks |
## Nutrient Cycling Roles
### Nitrogen
- Fungi decompose nitrogen-rich organic compounds (proteins, chitin, nucleic acids)
- Saprophytic fungi release ammonium (NH4+) through mineralization
- Mycorrhizal fungi transport nitrogen from soil to plant roots
- Some fungi fix atmospheric nitrogen in partnership with bacteria
- **Fungal:bacterial ratio**: Fungal-dominated soils have slower nitrogen cycling (more stable), bacterial-dominated soils have faster cycling (more available N)
### Phosphorus
- Fungi are critical for phosphorus availability in most ecosystems
- Mycorrhizal fungi solubilize phosphate minerals using organic acids
- Hyphal networks access phosphorus beyond the root depletion zone
- Phosphorus transported back to host plants in exchange for carbon
- Saprophytic fungi release phosphorus from organic compounds during decomposition
### Other Nutrients
- **Potassium**: Fungi release K from mineral structures through acidification
- **Calcium**: Oxalic acid from fungi solubilizes calcium compounds
- **Micronutrients**: Mycorrhizal fungi improve uptake of zinc, copper, iron, manganese
## Disturbance and Recovery
### How Disturbance Affects Fungal Ecology
| Disturbance Type | Effect on Fungi | Recovery Time |
|-----------------|----------------|---------------|
| Tilling | Destroys mycorrhizal networks, breaks hyphal connections | 1-3 years |
| Clear-cutting | Removes host trees, eliminates ECM fungi | 5-20 years |
| Fire | Kills surface fungi; some species survive as sclerotia | 1-10 years |
| Pesticides | Reduces fungal diversity, especially beneficial species | Variable |
| Compaction | Reduces air spaces, limits hyphal growth | 1-5 years |
| Chemical fertilization | Shifts balance from fungal to bacterial dominance | 1-3 years |
### Fungal Recovery Strategies
- **Spore bank**: Many fungi persist as spores in soil, germinating when conditions improve
- **Sclerotia**: Hardened fungal masses (e.g., Morchella sclerotia) survive adverse conditions
- **Mycorrhizal legacy**: Some mycorrhizal connections survive disturbance through surviving roots or root fragments
- **Pioneer species**: Fast-colonizing fungi (many saprophytes) quickly recolonize disturbed areas
- **Inoculation**: Active mycorrhizal inoculation can dramatically accelerate recovery (see [[mycoforestry-forest-restoration]])
## Fungal Bacterial Partnerships
Stamets emphasizes the interdependence of fungi and bacteria:
- **Bacteria on hyphae**: Bacterial biofilms on hyphal surfaces benefit from fungal exudates
- **Enzyme synergy**: Bacterial and fungal enzymes work together to degrade complex compounds
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Nitrogen-fixing bacteria associate with fungal hyphae
- **Nutrient exchange**: Fungi concentrate nutrients that bacteria need; bacteria produce compounds fungi use
- **Disease suppression**: Fungal-bacterial consortia suppress plant pathogens more effectively than either alone
