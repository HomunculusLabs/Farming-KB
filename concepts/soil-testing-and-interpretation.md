# Soil Testing and Interpretation

updated: 2026-04-18

[[soil-testing]] is the single most important step a gardener or farmer can take before applying any fertilizer. Without knowing what nutrients are present and what is missing, applications are guesswork -- wasteful at best and environmentally damaging at worst.

## The Von Liebig-Howard Regulation

The modern concept of fertilizers rests on Justus [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]], and the fourteen mineral nutrients that it contained are returned to the soil.

Farmers break the law when they take manures off the property and when they sell the livestock that ate the plants growing on the farm. Gardeners break it when they harvest crops, rake leaves, or pull weeds -- the removed nutrients are gone from the system. Lowenfels calls this intersection the "Von Liebig-Howard Regulation": when conditions are ideal, breaking the Law of Return is what creates the need to fertilize.

Many plants in a yard -- ornamental trees, shrubs, and most perennials -- really don't need additional input of nutrients and never will. They feed themselves. As long as flowers are not harvested, most perennials don't need fertilizer, either. Plants only need fertilizing when their soils become deficient in one or more of the fourteen mineral nutrients, regardless of what advertising says.

## Finding a Laboratory

Choose a local soil testing laboratory when possible, as they will have experience with regional soil types and appropriate corrective inputs. Government agencies and university agricultural extensions in most countries maintain soil laboratories. Universities are often preferable because they are not in the business of selling nutrient products, providing more straightforward test results.

Request sample reports from several laboratories to find one whose format you can understand and interpret. Home testing kits are available, but most only test for nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium -- not the other eleven essential nutrients -- so they are of limited value. Similarly, home pH tests are not always reliable.

Consistency between tests matters more than any single result. Stick with one laboratory so you can compare results year over year and track trends.

## Soil Sampling Technique

A soil test is only as good as the samples. Follow these principles:

- Use a plastic container and a wooden or plastic tool. Brass, bronze, and galvanized tools and containers should not be used because they can release copper or zinc into the samples. Because copper and zinc are reactive cations, they can distort other nutrient calculations as well.
- For biological tests, sterilize all containers and tools. Do not touch the soil with bare hands. If you need to clean soil of sticks and stones, run the sample over with a rolling pin while in a plastic bag.
- For gardens, sample to a depth of 6 inches (15 cm). For shrubs and trees, sample 4 to 6 inches deep under the drip line. For lawns, 4 inches from the base of the plant down.
- Take multiple samples from each distinct area (vegetable beds, annual flower beds, lawns, orchards) and mix them together for a single test per area.
- Also sample areas that are problematic and those doing exceptionally well, for comparison.

## Follow Through and Frequency

Most important, all good soil test reports come with specific suggestions for correcting any nutrient deficiencies or excesses. This is what makes the tests worth their minor cost. Make sure your laboratory knows the size of your garden, so recommendations are scaled appropriately. Also ensure the laboratory understands you are an organic gardener and will provide recommendations using [[hamilton-organic-fertilizers-and-plant-nutrition]]. Online organic fertilizer calculators (such as those from Oregon State University or Purdue University) can convert conventional recommendations.

The first test establishes a baseline. A second test later in the first season or at the beginning of the next season allows you to see trends. For most home gardeners using organic practices, after the initial two tests, there is no reason to test very often if you have been implementing recommendations. Consider a third check-up for annual and vegetable garden soils 2 to 3 years after your last test. Lawns can go 5 to 7 years. Soil around trees, shrubs, and perennials needs testing only if problems develop.

Discuss with your laboratory when appropriate follow-up tests should be taken. You want to monitor trends, and sometimes these take a while to show up. Work with your laboratory.

The real test, of course, is how the plants are doing. But it makes sense to test soils while things are working well, to ensure that no essential nutrient is close to becoming that Von Liebig limiter.

## Interpreting Nutrient Levels

Understanding what soil test numbers actually mean requires knowing the reporting system used by the laboratory. Most labs report nutrient levels in parts per million (ppm), but the sufficiency ranges considered optimal vary between laboratories and regions. A phosphorus level of 30 ppm might be considered adequate in one region but deficient in another, depending on soil type, climate, and crop requirements. Laboratories typically provide sufficiency ranges alongside reported values, making interpretation straightforward for the specific crops and conditions relevant to that region. However, gardeners growing unusual crops or using non-standard methods may need to research appropriate nutrient levels independently.

## The Phosphorus Problem

Phosphorus is one of the most commonly over-applied nutrients in home gardens. Unlike nitrogen, which is mobile and leaches readily, phosphorus is relatively immobile in soil and accumulates with repeated applications. Many garden soils test high or very high in phosphorus because years of compost and fertilizer applications have built up excess levels. High phosphorus can actually harm mycorrhizal associations, because plants reduce their investment in fungal symbionts when phosphorus is readily available. This mycorrhizal suppression in turn reduces the plant's ability to access other nutrients and water. For soils testing high in phosphorus, the appropriate response is to reduce or eliminate phosphorus inputs rather than adding more, even if plants show symptoms that might be attributed to [[query-how-to-fix-phosphorus-deficiency-in-cannabis]].

## Micronutrient Considerations

The fourteen essential mineral nutrients include several micronutrients (iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum, chlorine, and nickel) that are required in small quantities but can cause significant problems when deficient or excessive. Micronutrient deficiencies are often overlooked because their symptoms can resemble other problems including disease, drought stress, or pest damage. Iron deficiency, for example, causes chlorosis (yellowing) of young leaves that can be mistaken for nitrogen deficiency. Boron deficiency affects root tips and flowering. Zinc deficiency causes shortened internodes and small leaves. Most general soil tests include at least iron, manganese, zinc, and copper, but gardeners growing specific crops known to be sensitive to particular micronutrients may need to request additional tests.

## Soil Testing for Contaminants

Beyond nutrient analysis, soil testing can detect contamination by heavy metals, petroleum hydrocarbons, pesticides, and other pollutants. This is particularly important for urban and suburban gardens where soil may have been affected by historical industrial activity, lead paint from old buildings, or vehicle emissions along busy roads. Lead is the most common urban soil contaminant and is of special concern because it persists indefinitely and can be taken up by leafy vegetables and root crops. If contaminant testing reveals elevated levels, remediation strategies include adding phosphate amendments to immobilize lead, installing raised beds with imported soil, or choosing crops that do not accumulate the specific contaminant of concern.

## Building a Soil Testing History

The real value of soil testing emerges over time as results accumulate into a management history. Comparing results from year to year reveals trends that single tests cannot show. Rising organic matter levels confirm that management practices are [[building-soil]] health. Declining potassium levels might indicate that harvest removals are exceeding inputs. Changes in pH can reveal the effects of acidifying fertilizers or alkaline irrigation water. A well-maintained soil testing record, combined with notes on management practices, crop performance, and weather conditions, becomes an increasingly valuable decision-support tool as the data accumulates. Many experienced gardeners maintain soil testing records spanning decades, providing an invaluable resource for understanding long-term soil dynamics.

## Soil Testing Frequency

The appropriate frequency of soil testing depends on the intensity of land use and the management changes being implemented. For established perennial plantings with stable management, testing every 3-5 years is generally sufficient. For annual vegetable gardens where nutrients are removed with each harvest, testing every 1-2 years provides better guidance for fertilization decisions. After major management changes such as establishing a new garden, beginning a cover cropping program, or applying significant amendments, a follow-up test after one year helps assess the impact of the changes. Lowenfels recommends testing at the same time each year to make comparisons meaningful, as nutrient levels and pH can vary seasonally.
