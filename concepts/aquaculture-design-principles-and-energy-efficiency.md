## Aquaculture Design Principles and Energy Efficiency

Aquaculture, when properly designed within [[permaculture]] systems, offers one of the most productive and energy-efficient forms of food production available. [[bill-mollison]] places aquaculture alongside forestry as a stable, long-term occupation of responsible societies — both systems capable of significantly reducing the land area currently devoted to pastoralism and monoculture. Unlike its historical predecessors in large-scale grain farming, aquaculture reaches its greatest potential not as a high-energy monoculture, but as a diversified, community-integrated polyculture.

### The Case for Aquaculture

There are compelling reasons to develop aquaculture systems, particularly in areas vulnerable to famine or flood. Ponding, water harvesting, and diversion serve dual purposes: they aid food production generally while reducing the extremes of drought and flood, even in semi-arid regions. The transformation of terraced rice paddies to fish production across Indonesia illustrates a dramatic shift from starch to protein production, with significant gains in nutritional yield and reductions in fuel needed for food preparation.

Aquaculture is most enjoyable, convivial, and socially valuable when encountered as community taro-terrace culture. It becomes least desirable at industrial scale — large intensive prawn or catfish farms represent the same problematic monoculture thinking applied to water. The permaculture approach stresses sensible yield and integrated procedure over the "maximum yield of one species" mindset.

### Multi-Functional Pond Design

A well-designed pond serves far more purposes than fish production alone. A pond can function simultaneously as:

- A **mirror** reflecting light and sky
- A **heat store** moderating local microclimate
- A **run-off area** capturing and filtering water
- A **pollutant cleanser** processing waste through biological filtration
- A **transport system** enabling low-energy movement of goods
- A **fire barrier** providing protective breaks
- A **recreation asset** offering aesthetic and leisure value
- An **energy storage** medium capturing solar thermal energy
- An **irrigation accessory** supplying water to adjacent crops

This multi-functionality is intrinsically productive, and it presents numerous opportunities for aesthetic and functional placements of trees, plants, buildings, jetties, rafts, boats, and habitat structures for birds, wildlife, beavers, turtles, and other organisms among fringing vegetation, logs, stones, and hummocks.

### Energy Cost Analysis

The energy economics of aquaculture are extremely favorable compared to terrestrial protein production and wild-caught fisheries. J.E. Bardach identified food, fertiliser, and fuels for water pumping as the three main recurrent fish culture costs, with species selection being critical to economic viability. Well over 90% of production costs fall within these three factors.

Production cost comparisons reveal dramatic differences:

| System | MJ/kg | kcal/oz |
|--------|-------|---------|
| Wild-caught lobster | 32 | 21,801 |
| Wild-caught shrimp | 250 | 16,953 |
| Intensive silo rearing of perch | 224 | 15,167 |
| Pond catfish (USA) | 58 | 3,941 |
| Pond polyculture (carp, mullet, [[tilapia]], Israel) | 0.027 | 184 |
| Sewage/stream culture of carp in cages | <0.004 | 8 |

The most productive system — sewage or stream culture of carp in fast-flowing water without biocidal pollutants — achieves production costs orders of magnitude lower than wild-caught or intensive systems. This explains why distant-water fishing is collapsing in oil-conserving countries and why aquaculture is positioned to eclipse terrestrial protein production.

### Feed Conversion Efficiency

Fish demonstrate superior feed conversion ratios compared to terrestrial livestock:

- **Fish (catfish, carp, mullet):** 1.5–2.3 kg of invertebrate food per 1 kg of flesh
- **Chickens:** approximately 3.5:1 ratio
- **Beef, mutton, pork:** double to triple the feed cost of fish

No terrestrial species matches the inherent conversion efficiency of common aquaculture fish. As fossil fuel energy becomes increasingly problematic in terms of both cost and pollutants, these efficiency differences will drive a significant shift toward aquatic protein production.

### Stocking Rates and Carrying Capacity

Understanding stocking rates is fundamental to productive pond management. Conventional guidelines suggest stocking approximately 2 cm of fish per gallon (4 litres) for aquariums, or one 8 cm fish per square metre of water surface or linear metre of pond edge for fingerling production.

These figures reflect the pond's **carrying capacity** — the volume, area, or edge length that supplies sufficient oxygen and nutrients. Overstocking causes fish to cease growing or die, producing many small fish rather than fewer market-sized ones. Understocking, which Mollison identifies as the most common error on farms, results in excessive waste of productive capacity.

A baseline productivity figure of 200 kg of fish per hectare per year is considered modest. This can be achieved as 200 fish of 1 kg each, 400 fish of 500 g, 800 fish of 250 g, or 1,000 fish of 125 g. Approximately 300 g is generally accepted as the minimum pan-fish size, though smaller fish can be dried or processed into paste.

### Raising Carrying Capacity

Several strategies can lift the carrying capacity of ponds beyond baseline levels:

- **Aeration** — supplying additional oxygen through stirring, fountains, flowforms, or compressed air
- **Manuring and fertilisation** — providing additional nutrients to stimulate the food chain
- **Edge maximisation** — crenellating pond edges to increase the productive shoreline interface
- **Polyculture design** — selecting species that occupy different ecological niches within the pond

### Design Philosophy

The permaculture designer approaches aquaculture with the same methodologies applied to any designed system. One holds a central idea or species and examines how many designed features connect to and from it, nesting as many benefits within the system as possible. Wild ducks, for instance, do not annoy catfish and contribute phosphatic fertilisers — there is no reason to deprive them of islands, shallows, or nest boxes.

The innovative farmer is challenged to design for nutrients, plant control, multiple use, and thus higher yields. Skills in pond management, species integration, and water quality regulation are hard-won through experience. The recommended approach is to start small and build on successes, planning better strategies at each stage.
