# Wild Yeast Bread Baking

Wild yeast bread baking uses naturally occurring yeast and bacteria to leaven bread, producing superior flavor, texture, and keeping quality compared to commercial yeast. The sourdough tradition connects modern bakers to thousands of years of bread-making.

## Wild Yeast Capture

Wild yeast (primarily Saccharomyces exiguus and Candida milleri) lives on grains, fruits, and in the air. Combined with Lactobacillus bacteria, these organisms form a stable symbiotic culture -- a sourdough starter.

### Creating a Starter from Scratch

Day 1:
- 50g whole wheat or rye flour + 50g non-chlorinated water (room temp, 70-80F)
- Mix in a glass jar, cover loosely (cloth lid or lid with holes)
- Stir vigorously to incorporate air

Days 2-5:
- Discard all but 25g of starter
- Feed 50g flour + 50g water
- Stir, cover, leave at room temperature
- Expect bubbles by day 3-5; activity may stall around day 3-4 (normal)

Days 6-14:
- Continue daily feeding once activity is consistent (bubbles within 4-6 hours, doubling in volume)
- Switch to every-12-hour feedings as starter strengthens
- Starter is mature when it reliably doubles within 4-6 hours of feeding

### Alternative Capture Methods

- [[hamilton-organic-fruit-growing]] method: add a few organic grapes or unwashed berries to the initial mix
- Rye flour method: rye ferments faster due to higher enzyme and mineral content
- Whole [[fukuoka-barley-winter-grain-method]]: whole wheat or whole rye has more wild organisms than white flour

## Sourdough Maintenance

### Daily Feeding (Room Temperature)
- Keep at room temperature if baking daily
- Feed ratio: 1:2:2 (starter:flour:water by weight)
- Example: 25g starter + 50g flour + 50g water
- Feed when starter has peaked and begun to fall (not before)

### Weekly Maintenance (Refrigerator)
- Store in refrigerator after a peak feeding
- Feed once a week: discard most, feed 1:3:3 ratio, leave at room temp 2-4 hours, return to fridge
- For baking: remove from fridge, feed, wait until doubled (may take 2 feedings)

### Flour Selection for Feeding
- Rye flour creates the most active fermentation
- Bread flour (12-14% protein) gives best oven spring
- Whole wheat adds flavor and microbial diversity
- A mix of 50% bread flour / 50% whole wheat works well for most bakers
- Avoid all-purpose flour for feedings (lower [[solomon-manure-quality-variable-nutrient-content-horse-steer-chicken]])

## Basic Sourdough Bread Recipe

### Ingredients (two 500g loaves)
- 700g bread flour (or 600g bread flour + 100g whole wheat)
- 450g water (68-72% hydration)
- 200g active starter (100% hydration)
- 18g salt (2.5% of flour weight)

### Method
1. Autolyse: mix flour and water, rest 30-60 minutes (develops gluten without kneading)
2. Add starter and salt, mix until incorporated
3. Bulk fermentation: 4-6 hours at room temperature (75-80F ideal)
   - Perform stretch-and-folds every 30 minutes for first 2 hours (4 sets)
4. Bench rest: 20-30 minutes
5. Shape: pre-shape into round, bench rest 20 min, final shape (boule or batard)
6. Cold proof: refrigerate in banneton (proofing basket) for 12-16 hours
7. Bake: preheat Dutch oven to 500F
   - Score dough with lame or sharp knife
   - Bake covered 20 minutes at 500F
   - Remove lid, bake 20-25 minutes at 450F until dark caramel crust
8. Cool completely on wire rack (at least 1 hour -- crumb is still cooking)

## Regional Grain Varieties

Using locally grown grains connects bread to terroir and supports regional grain economies.

### Heritage Wheat Varieties
- Red Fife: Canadian heritage wheat, nutty and complex flavor, moderate protein (12-13%)
- Turkey Red: hard red winter wheat brought to Kansas in 1870s, excellent for bread
- Sonora: soft white wheat, low protein (9-10%), best for tortillas and flatbreads
- Emmer: [[quinoa-and-ancient-grain-cultivation]], 14-16% protein, nutty, requires adjustment to hydration
- Einkorn: oldest cultivated wheat, lower gluten, requires gentle handling
- Spelt: related to wheat but distinct species, nutty flavor, some gluten-sensitive people tolerate it

### Rye Varieties
- Danko Rye: Polish variety, excellent for sourdough, deep flavor
- Balbo Rye: heritage variety, good for both bread and whiskey
- Open-pollinated rye: best for homestead growing, adaptable to poor soils

### Growing Grain on the Homestead
- Wheat: 50-80 bushels/acre, plant fall (winter wheat) or spring
- Rye: most cold-tolerant grain, excellent for marginal soils, 40-70 bushels/acre
- Oats: good for [[coleman-cover-crop-rotation-and-soil-health]], 60-100 bushels/acre
- Requires: small-scale thresher or hand methods (flail, winnowing)
- Storage: whole grains store for years in cool, dry conditions

## Hydration Guide

| Hydration % | Dough Feel | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| 60-65% | Firm, easy to handle | Pan loaves, beginners |
| 66-72% | Moderate tackiness | Standard artisan loaves |
| 73-80% | Sticky, shaggy | Ciabatta, focaccia |
| 80%+ | Very sticky, spread | High-hydration rustic loaves |

## Troubleshooting

- Flat, dense loaf: under-fermented (extend bulk time), weak starter (more feedings), or under-kneaded
- Too sour: over-fermented during bulk or cold proof (reduce time), or starter is too acidic (adjust feeding ratio)
- Gummy crumb: under-baked (increase bake time or temp), or too much whole grain (reduce to 20-30%)
- No oven spring: weak starter, over-proofed, or scoring too shallow
- Starter not rising: temperature too cold, chlorinated water, or dead flour (try organic)

## Equipment

- Banneton (proofing basket) with linen liner
- Dutch oven or covered baker (for steam)
- Bench scraper
- Digital kitchen scale (essential -- volume measurements are unreliable)
- Lame (scoring blade)
- Proofing container with lid
