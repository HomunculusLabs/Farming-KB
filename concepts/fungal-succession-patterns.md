## Human Disturbance Effects on Fungal Succession

Human activities profoundly alter fungal successional patterns. Logging, agriculture, urbanization, and fire suppression each reset or redirect successional trajectories. Clear-cutting removes late-successional wood substrata and associated fungal species, while creating pulses of fresh woody debris that initiate new successional sequences. Agricultural conversion eliminates the complex age structure of natural forests, reducing the diversity of successional stages available to fungi. Fire suppression changes the frequency and intensity of disturbance, favoring some successional pathways over others. Understanding how human disturbance affects fungal succession is critical for conservation and for predicting ecosystem responses to global change. The loss of old-growth forests is particularly concerning, as [[fungal-inventory-methods]] must account for successional variation by sampling across multiple substrate ages, decay stages, and habitat types. Single-timepoint surveys inevitably underestimate diversity because they capture only a snapshot of a continuously changing community. The need for multiple seasonal samples, multi-year monitoring, and inclusion of diverse microhabitats makes comprehensive fungal inventory logistically demanding but scientifically essential. Integrating successional perspectives into inventory design improves both species richness estimates and understanding of ecosystem function in [[fungal-community-ecology]] and the resilience of decomposer systems to disturbance.

## Non-Wood Substrata: Soil, Dung, and Leaf Litter

While wood decomposition succession has received the most attention, fungal succession also occurs on other substrata. On soil surfaces, fungal communities change with depth, organic matter content, and root activity. In dung, the classic coprophilous succession from Zygomycota through Ascomycota to Basidiomycota has been documented across many herbivore species and environmental conditions. On leaf litter, succession progresses from freshly fallen leaves dominated by endophytic species to heavily decomposed material colonized by specialized litter decomposers. Each substratum type supports its own characteristic successional sequence, and the diversity of substrata within a landscape contributes to overall fungal diversity by providing multiple independent successional pathways running in parallel.
