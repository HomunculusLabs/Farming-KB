# Cannabis and Hypertension

## Overview

Approximately 24% of U.S. adults have high blood pressure or hypertension.
Orthodox medicine has no cure for hypertension, nor has it yet to fully
understand its genesis. Hypertension contributes to blindness, kidney
failure, heart attacks, strokes, and brain aneurysms. Before examining
high blood pressure, it helps to understand what blood pressure actually
is. Two basic factors determine blood pressure: the amount of blood inside
the body and the size of the blood vessels. Smooth muscles lining the
larger blood vessels regulate blood pressure by expansion or contraction.
Blood pressure naturally changes many times during the day in response to
different stimuli such as emotions, temperature, or level of physical
activity.

Blood pressure is a necessity of life. However, sustained high blood
pressure can be serious in both chronic and acute forms, contributing to
heart attacks, strokes, and many other serious medical conditions. Modern
medicine has identified several observable mental-emotional and physical
co-factors including family history, obesity, poor diet, smoking, alcohol
abuse, physical inactivity, arteriosclerosis, age, chronic stress,
depression, anxiety, insulin resistance, and diabetes. According to
orthodox medicine, "normal" pressure is 120/80 mm Hg, though optimal
pressure is not clearly defined. The risk of coronary vascular disease,
beginning at 115/75 mm Hg, doubles with each increment of 20/10 mm Hg.

Many patients with chronic but mild or even moderate hypertension
demonstrate no signs or symptoms until many years later. When symptoms do
appear, they may include dizziness, headaches, blurred vision, nausea, or
vomiting. Orthodox treatment is limited to lifelong pharmaceutical
management and intervention during acute phases.

## Cannabis and Hypertension

Cannabis has been a folk remedy for hypertension for as long as people and
the plant have coexisted. The presence of endocannabinoid receptors called
non-CB1 and non-CB2 in the cells that line arterial blood vessels strongly
suggests the system's involvement in balancing and maintaining a healthy
blood pressure. CB1 and CB2 receptors have been examined and measured in
patients with hypertension.

Researchers from the Glaucoma Clinic at Howard University Hospital in
Washington, DC (1976), were the first to look at the relationship between
blood pressure and THC. Results revealed that patients responded to the
inhalation of THC with an increase in heart rate followed by a substantial
drop in blood pressure (both systolic and diastolic) as well as a drop in
intraocular pressure. Scientists noted that the increase in heart rate
allowed the body to maintain adequate perfusion (cardiac output) while
lowering both blood and intraocular pressures in a parallel fashion,
potentially good news for patients with [[blesching-cannabis-cardiovascular-health]].

An animal trial from the University of Nottingham Medical School (2009)
discovered that intravenously administered anandamide (a naturally
occurring cannabinoid often referred to as the body's own THC) lowered
blood pressure in hypertensive rats but did not affect rats with normal
blood pressure. Anandamide achieved the pressure-lowering effects through
increased vasodilatation. Both studies indicated that THC and anandamide
mutually lowered blood pressure without compromising overall perfusion or
triggering commonly associated side effects.

### Strain-Specific Considerations

Anandamide, WIN55,212-2, and THC have each been tested in the context of
hypertension, with each achieving a drop in blood pressure via vasodilation.
Anandamide and THC bind with CB1 and CB2 relatively equally, while
WIN55,212-2 has a higher affinity for CB2. Cannabinoid research supports
the implication of both CB1 and CB2 in activating vasodilation.

- Sativas and indicas contain cannabinoids that bind with CB1 and CB2
- Indicas and indica-heavy strains present lower THC:CBD ratios, favoring
  CB2 expression
- Raw, fresh leaf or juice contains non-psychoactive CBD acid, also
  favoring CB2 activation
- Plants containing significant amounts of dietary cannabinoid (E)-BCP
  can further stimulate non-psychoactive CB2 activation

### Mind-Body Medicine and Hypertension

The physicians Grace and Graham at the New York Hospital-Cornell Medical
Center (1952) were the first to study a link between attitude and
hypertension, writing: "Arterial hypertension occurred when an individual
felt that he must constantly be prepared to meet all possible threats."

A CDC study (2000) on 3,310 initially normotensive persons over 22 years
confirmed anxiety and depression as predictive signs for future development
of hypertension. A JAMA-published study (2003) on 3,308 adults concluded
that tendencies for impatience and hostility suggested future risk for
developing hypertension. A University of Texas study (2006) found subjects
were less likely to be hypertensive if they identified with feeling good
about themselves, feeling happy, and feeling hopeful about the future.

Preventive measures including dietary changes, stress reduction, exercise,
quitting smoking, and reducing heavy alcohol use have all proven to
significantly assist in reducing hypertension. However, release from
chronically constricting emotions such as fears, anxieties, worries, and
suppressed anger may be a more lasting solution as root causes are
identified and transcended.

### Supporting Foods

- **Bush Tea (Rooibos):** Bronchodilator and antispasmodic with
  blood pressure-lowering properties via potassium (ATP) channel activation
- **Cacao:** Dark chocolate consumption may have protective impact on heart
  and vascular illnesses
- **Caraway:** Strong diuretic properties similar to Lasix and
  Hydrochlorothiazide
- **Garlic:** Contains allicin with potent antioxidant properties; odorless
  garlic powder prevents thrombus (clot) formation

  CB2 sites, found in [[comparison-black-pepper-vs-cardamom-tropical-spice-cultivation]], cinnamon, rosemary, basil, oregano,
