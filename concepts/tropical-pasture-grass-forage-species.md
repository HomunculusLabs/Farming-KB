# Tropical Pasture Grass and Forage Species

Tropical pastures form the foundation of livestock enterprises in warm climates. Selecting appropriate grass and forage species requires understanding rainfall zones, soil types, [[desert-temperature-regimes-and-soil-chemistry]], and integration with tree crop systems. A mixture of legumes with selected grass species plus storable forage is ideal for the tropics.

## Grassland and Range Management Strategies

Four key management strategies underpin productive tropical grazing systems:

1. **Addition of forage species** to grasslands — grass legumes or trees providing foliage, food sugars, seed carbohydrates, or fruits
2. **Environmental changes** — water storage, soil structure improvement, irrigation, windbreaks, shelter, key fertiliser or trace elements
3. **Conservation of forage** — rotational rests from grazing, hay and silage production, protein/urea supplements and molasses in drought, stocking below worst-case capacity
4. **Stock management** — planned buying and selling aligned with seasons, timing calving to spring/early summer, selling or drying off in autumn

## Tropical Pasture Grass Species

### Guinea Grass (*Panicum maximum*)
A bunch grass for warm areas receiving 90–300 cm of rainfall. [[drought-resistant-gardening-techniques]] but yields best in humid areas. Frost sensitive. Shade tolerant, often yielding well under thin-crowned tree crops. Growth is maintained during cloudy summer-autumn regimes, a valuable characteristic. Grazed down to 15–20 cm and combines well with twining legumes that climb on its stiff stems. Suited to [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]] across 12–18 fields, interspersed with sugar-pod trees and tree fruit forages. Drilled to 6 mm depth to establish. A first choice for sub-tropic and tropic pastures.

### Kikuyu Grass (*Pennisetum clandestinum*)
Cold tolerant, grown from cool to tropical areas. Valuable for cooler uplands, thinner soils, and good autumn growth when nitrogen-fixing trees (Acacia, Leucaena, Prosopis, Albizia) are established. Prefers light soils and red loams. Excellent for water spillways [[mollison-permaculture-two-arid-water-harvesting-and-erosion-control]]. Few legumes tolerate the tight sward, making nitrogen-fixing trees essential. Desmodium, Glycine, and white clover can be sod-drilled in autumn-cut areas.

### Makarikari Grass (*Panicum coloratum*)
Bunch and spreading types for 40–90 cm rainfall. Tolerates alluvial fans, flood plains, black clay soils, red earths, and even semi-caked salted soils. Needs a year after seeding to establish, suiting rotational systems. Lucerne interplant can succeed in irrigated areas. Valuable for winter-green feed and drought resistance. Suits long-rotation grazing in open savannah of Acacia and sugar-pod trees.

### Para Grass (*Brachiaria mutica*)
Suited to warm areas of low frost intensity, valuable for swampy soils, soaks, dams, and waterholes. Provides good soil structure through fibrous shallow roots. Can be grown with forage legumes such as phasey bean, greenleaf Desmodium, and Centro. Ideal as a fenced-out drought reserve. Can reach 4 m in one summer. Should not be planted where clogging of channels could be a problem.

### Sorghums (*Sorghum almum*, Silk Sorghum, *S. halapense*)
Annual, biennial, or persistent from seed. Most useful as broadcast-sown pioneers in slashed mulch at 50–90 cm rainfall. Applications include:
- Pioneers with perennial grasses
- Mulch in orchard strips
- Emergency dry-season fodder
- Garden mulch source
- Birdseed production
- Weed control

Easily grazed out, so they serve best as early establishment aids and can be surface-sown.

### Elephant/Banna Grass (*Pennisetum purpureum*)
Best on deep alluvial or coarse flats above 110 cm (alluvium) or 90 cm (coastal) rainfall. Reaches 2.5–4.5 m high, grazed to 1 m or cut to 15 cm for forage and mulch. Needs vigorous legume interplant (Leucaena or forage legumes such as Calopogon, Centrosema, Glycine in high-rainfall tropics). Can carry 7 milk cows per hectare if cut and hand-fed with Leucaena and sugar pods.

## Establishing Perennial Grass Swards

Creating permanent pasture on weedy or eroded areas is a one-to-three year process:

1. **Land-forming** — choose swaling, interception banks, or pitting; establish dams for irrigation above good soil types
2. **Pioneer sowing** — sow molasses grass or Sorghum almum mixed with sawdust into slashed weeds, or drill selected grass and legumes after slashing
3. **Transition** — burn molasses grass or drill selected perennials and broadcast Sorghum almum
4. **Tree legume planting** — concurrently with earth-forming, plant leguminous tree strips along swales at 30–100 m spacing; allow 2–3 years with light grazing to year 3
5. **Managed grazing** — commence rotational grazing and drill or broadcast forage legumes into established grasses (15–18 fields necessary for rotation)
6. **Rocky knolls** — leguminous tree pioneers followed by kikuyu sward; early furrows of banna grass provide erosion and wind control at 30 m spacing

## Tropical Forage Shrubs and Trees

### Desmanthus virgatus
Shrub to 3 m resembling Leucaena, tolerant of heavy cutting and browsing. Vigorous with prolific seeding. Should be kept on range rather than in field crop due to seeding habit. Yields 7–70 t/ha/year.

### Desmodium Species
- **D. discolor** — browse shrub to 3 m, yields ~30 t/ha/year green fodder, sown as strips in rangeland prior to rain, compatible with maize
- **D. distortum** — perennial to 2 m, good on acid soils (2–7 t/ha/year)
- **D. gyroides** — shrub to 4 m, tolerates wet tropical sites, cut for forage
- **D. nicaraguense** — excellent forage across wide soil range, pioneer in grassland

### Other Key Forage Species
- **Tagasaste** (*Chaemocytisus palmensis*) — tolerant and hardy, used in New Zealand dry areas for cut forage, pioneer, mulch, and nurse crop
- **Honey Locust** (*[[gleditsia-triacanthos]]*) — heavy pod loads in dry subtropics, frost-hardy, thornless forms available
- **Kiawe** (*Prosopis pallida*) — staple pod forage on dry savannah, dense wood, excellent firewood and termite-resistant posts

## Forage Legumes

### Calapo (*Calopogonium mucunoides*)
Short-lived twining perennial used as a pioneer on burnt or slashed weed areas to smother weeds before permanent systems are established. Suited to low-frost coastal areas above 125 cm rainfall. High seedling vigour, moderately shade tolerant, reseeds freely.

### Centro (*Centrosema pubescens*)
Twining perennial for pastures and grain crops, preferring more than 125 cm rain in warm tropical climates. Excellent cut forage and soil-builder, tolerant of wide soil range, acid soils, short flooding, and some frost. Ideal companion for guinea grass, banna, pangola, and para grasses. Climbs to 14 m, so not suited to short crops or small trees. Seed may need hot water treatment and inoculation.

### Glycine (*Neonotonia wightii*)
Slender, twining perennial with deep roots that cycle phosphates from deep soil layers. Resists drought but is affected by frosts. Useful in cool subtropics and tropics at 80–180 cm rainfall. Good winter growth in pastures with main growth in summer. Often fenced out in late summer as a winter reserve. Does best on well-drained deep red soils but also tolerates yellow clays and black cracking soils.

### Lucerne (*Medicago sativa*)
Grown from cool temperate to tropics, usually as pure sward cut to baled hay but also in well-managed pasture under rotation. Deep rooted, growing from 55 cm rainfall and up. Combines well with makarikari and sorghum. Regular resting is essential. Cut for hay just before flowering. Reseeded by chisel seeding. Inoculation essential, lime pelleting essential in acid soils. Silage with molasses is now popular.

### Siratro (*Macroptilium atropurpureum*)
Perennial creeping legume with good root system, suited to warm areas of 75 cm or more rain (ideally 90–110 cm). Poor soil tolerant with excellent nitrogen contribution to grasses. Ideal for rotational grazing, readily established, resistant to nematodes. Forms the basis of many excellent tropical pastures.

### Stylo (*Stylosanthes guianensis*)
Perennial pasture legume of warm areas, 90–400 cm rain. Good pioneer of poor acid soils, poor drainage, sands, rocky soils, and hillsides. Combined with low grasses such as signal grass and pangola. Excellent mulch in tree systems, can be cut to silage. Some shrubby types are excellent cassava or banana interplants.

## Integration with Tree Systems

Most grazing systems can extend under palms, between large tree crops, or as a complex with belts of forage tree legumes yielding fodder, fruit, pods, or large seed for food concentrates. Each soil type, location, rainfall area, slope type, and main crop needs individual assessment and planning. Soils under slashed pastures develop excellent structure with erosion effectively stopped.
