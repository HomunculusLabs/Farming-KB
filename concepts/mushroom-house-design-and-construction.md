# Mushroom House Design and Construction

## Overview

The design and construction of [[mushroom-growing-facilities]] represents a critical intersection of [[mycology]], engineering, and practical agriculture. Whether built as dedicated mushroom houses, adapted from existing cellars and caves, or constructed as simple temporary shelters, the growing facility must provide precise control over temperature, moisture, ventilation, and light — the environmental factors that determine the success or failure of a mushroom crop.

## Environmental Requirements

The fundamental principle underlying all [[mushroom-house-design]] is that the main factors to be regulated are temperature and moisture. Any situation in which these conditions may be controlled to accord with the requirements of the mushroom should prove satisfactory. In practice, the design must also address ventilation, structural durability in a perpetually moist environment, efficient use of space, and convenient access for bed preparation, maintenance, and harvesting.

The optimal temperature range for mushroom fruiting is approximately 48° to 56°F (about 9° to 13°C).

## Types of Growing Facilities in the United States

### Dedicated Mushroom Houses

Near Philadelphia, Chicago, Boston, and other production centers, mushrooms were grown wholly in special mushroom houses. Unquestionably, the major part of the American mushroom crop was grown in this way. These purpose-built structures offered the greatest control over environmental conditions and allowed the most efficient use of space through tiered bed systems.

### Underground Facilities

Some commercial growers in Minnesota, Ohio, Indiana, New York, and Missouri secured underground quarries, caves, or storage cellars of considerable extent. Such places were excellent when readily accessible, provided there was no danger from flooding or from seepage water. Natural caves in the limestone sections of Missouri, Kentucky, Indiana, Ohio, Virginia, and Arkansas had all been used successfully.

### Cellars and Adapted Spaces

Many antiquated beer storage cellars of limited capacity were in use in various cities. Abandoned coal mines were generally unsuitable as usually left, though hill coal mines in some sections were considered potentially viable if properly adapted.

## Mushroom House Construction

### Permanent Structures

As a permanent investment, the house should be built with due consideration for convenience, durability, and economy.

**Perfect drainage** is a factor of the first importance. Obtaining adequate drainage may be complicated by the fact that, for convenience in heating and for more uniform temperature, it is often desirable to construct the house over an excavation several feet below the natural surface. This semi-underground construction provides natural insulation but creates significant drainage challenges.

**Insulation and wall construction** — For some time, the custom in building better-class mushroom houses was to make the walls double, packing fresh shavings between the inner and outer walls. More expensive houses might be made of hollow tile or concrete.

### The Moisture Problem

The interior of a mushroom house always maintains a moist atmosphere, and the contact of this moisture-laden air with cold exterior walls produces constant precipitation of water — "sweating" or condensation. This condensation can lead to water dripping onto the beds, promoting bacterial and mold growth.

A sufficiently sloping roof provided with an air space between the ceiling and roof was recommended to correct this difficulty. The air space acts as a buffer zone, preventing the cold exterior roof surface from directly cooling the warm, moist interior air.

### Ventilation Design

Proper ventilation is essential. It is usually advisable to place ventilators in the roof, as the purpose of air exchange is then accomplished with less inconvenience from direct drafts. Roof ventilators allow warm, moist, carbon dioxide-rich air to rise and escape naturally, while fresh air enters through lower openings.

## Interior Layout and Bed Systems

### Tiered Bed Construction

The greatest economy is attained by building houses high enough to accommodate beds in tiers of from two to five levels. This vertical stacking dramatically increases the growing area per unit of floor space and per unit of building cost.

**Spacing requirements** — About 2.5 to 3 feet of free space between tiers is necessary, since the bed is at least 8 to 10 inches deep. Somewhat more than 3 feet in total height must be allowed for each tier.

### Structural Supports

The construction of supports for tiered bed systems requires particular attention. The upright supports for beds in tiers of four or five should be scantling not less than 2×6 inches, placed about 4 feet apart.

Experienced commercial growers adopted the use of large gas piping for bed supports, offering two advantages:

1. **Great permanency** — steel pipe does not rot, warp, or deteriorate in the moist environment
2. **Sanitary perfection** — smooth metal surfaces do not harbor pests, diseases, or residual organic matter

### Bed Widths and Aisle Space

Wall beds should seldom be more than 3 feet across. Beds approachable from either side may be 6 feet across. Ample space should be left for aisles, with 3 feet being usually sufficient. One or more wider aisles may be employed in very large houses.

## Heating Considerations

Since winter is the important season for mushroom work, heating is essential for any above-ground facility. The heating system must maintain the desired temperature range without creating hot spots or dry conditions. Semi-underground construction assists with heating by reducing heat loss through the floor and lower walls.

## Temporary and Economical Structures

For temporary use, a mushroom house may consist of a very simple boarded shed or unused barn with a heater. Many commercial mushroom houses that paid well were not particularly elaborate. Key requirements include weather-tight construction, adequate ventilation, a heating system, structural soundness, and easy access.

## Open Air Culture

In Southern England, simple culture frames of the usual type are employed, except that no glass is used, and they are covered with sheet iron or boards, upon which a layer of straw is placed if necessary. Fresh manure may be used under the compost to force early growth.

Open air culture for mushrooms cannot be regarded as having great possibilities of success in most regions. The variation in daily temperature is considerable, drying winds are not infrequent, and it is difficult to regulate moisture. In Southern England, successful open air beds were seen during late autumn and winter.

There are sections of California where open-air culture might be profitably practised. Both *[[agaricus-campestris]]* and *A. arvensis* are found in parts of California during winter months. The mean monthly temperature of San Francisco is normally from 50° to 60°F, and the daily range is not pronounced. A mean temperature rather below than above the requirements is desirable, say 48° to 56°F.

Inquiries about spawning pastures and lawns to establish natural cultures have not yielded reliable results. No permanent success was achieved, and the failures were under a variety of conditions. English correspondents give a similar version. It is therefore not to be depended upon, though spreading spawn about lawns or pastures involves so little labor that it may be worth the experiment when spawn is available.
