## Phytocannabinoids in Common Spices

## Overview

Cannabinoid compounds are not exclusive to the cannabis plant. (E)-beta-caryophyllene ((E)-BCP) is a [[mycorrhizal-networks-common-mycelial-network]] culinary spices and food plants. This sesquiterpene selectively activates CB2 receptors in the endocannabinoid system, providing anti-inflammatory and therapeutic benefits without any psychoactive effect. Regular consumption of these spices may contribute to baseline endocannabinoid system health and offers an accessible pathway to cannabinoid-based wellness for those who do not use cannabis.

## (E)-Beta-Caryophyllene: The Dietary Cannabinoid

(E)-beta-caryophyllene ((E)-BCP) was identified as a functional CB2 receptor agonist in 2008. Unlike THC, which binds both CB1 and CB2 receptors and produces psychoactive effects, (E)-BCP selectively targets CB2 receptors, which are concentrated in immune cells and peripheral tissues. This selectivity means that (E)-BCP can provide the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory benefits associated with CB2 activation without any intoxication or impairment.

## Spices and Their (E)-BCP Content

### Black Pepper (Piper nigrum) — 7-19%

Black pepper is one of the most widely consumed spices globally and a significant dietary source of (E)-BCP, with tested samples yielding between 7% and 19% (E)-beta-caryophyllene content. Black pepper also contains piperine, which enhances the bioavailability of many nutrients and pharmaceuticals.

Documented medicinal benefits include antibacterial properties, protective effects against colon cancer, and significant antioxidant activity. The combination of (E)-BCP with piperine may create synergistic therapeutic effects, enhancing the anti-inflammatory and bioavailability-enhancing properties of both compounds.

### Clove (Syzygium aromaticum) — approximately 4%

Clove contains approximately 4% (E)-beta-caryophyllene. Despite its lower (E)-BCP content relative to other spices on this list, clove is used in larger quantities in many cuisines and traditional medicine systems, making it a meaningful dietary contributor.

The essential oil of clove has been extensively studied and shown to work as an analgesic (particularly for dental pain, which is clove's most famous traditional use), anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, antifungal, antiviral (active against Herpes simplex I and II and hepatitis C), antibacterial (including against several multi-drug-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis strains), anticancer, cancer protective (skin and lung), antidiabetic, and insect repellent. Clove also contains aphrodisiac properties, and clove-based creams have been found effective in treating chronic anal fissures.

### Sri Lanka Cinnamon (Cinnamomum zeylanicum) — 7-11%

True cinnamon (Ceylon cinnamon) from Sri Lanka contains 7-11% (E)-beta-caryophyllene. This is distinct from cassia cinnamon, which is more commonly found in grocery stores but has different chemical properties.

Scientifically supported medicinal uses include improved fat and sugar metabolism (making it relevant for diabetes management), potential treatment for high blood pressure, antioxidant properties, broad-spectrum antibiotic properties, and the ability to cure mite infestations in animals. Cinnamon's effects on glucose metabolism may work synergistically with the CB2-mediated anti-inflammatory effects of (E)-BCP.

### Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis) — 1-8%

Two different samples of rosemary showed (E)-beta-caryophyllene content ranging from approximately 1% to 8%, depending on growing conditions, variety, and processing. Rosemary is a Mediterranean herb widely used in cooking and traditional medicine.

Medicinal uses supported by scientific studies include antimicrobial activity against bacteria and fungi, treatment of dyspepsia (digestive complaints), promotion of circulation, radioprotective effects, potential prevention of skin cancer tumors and other tumors, anti-inflammatory properties, and potential in [[blesching-cannabis-neurodegenerative-diseases]]. Rosemary's association with memory enhancement ("rosemary for remembrance") may relate to its neuroprotective properties.

### Basil (Ocimum spp.) — 5-14%

Multiple basil species contain (E)-beta-caryophyllene. African basil (Ocimum micranthum) typically contains approximately 14%, while common basil (Ocimum gratissimum) measured at 5%. The widely used sweet basil (Ocimum basilicum) has similar constituents.

Medicinal properties of Ocimum basilicum include antimicrobial activity against giardia, assistance with dyspepsia and high blood pressure (diuretic effect), potential effectiveness against cholera-induced diarrhea, antiviral activity against herpes virus I and II, adenoviruses, hepatitis B virus, and RNA viruses (coxsackievirus B1 and enterovirus 71), antibacterial effects for middle-ear infections, and anti-inflammatory properties. Basil has also shown protective gastrointestinal abilities and antibacterial properties against E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus.

## Mechanism of Action: CB2-Selective Activation

The CB2-selective nature of (E)-beta-caryophyllene is therapeutically significant because it allows for targeted anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects without central nervous system involvement. When (E)-BCP binds to CB2 receptors on immune cells:

- Pro-inflammatory cytokine production (TNF-alpha, IL-1 beta, IL-6) is reduced
- Anti-inflammatory cytokine release may be enhanced
- Immune cell migration and activation are modulated
- Oxidative stress pathways are downregulated
- NF-kB signaling, a master regulator of inflammation, is inhibited
