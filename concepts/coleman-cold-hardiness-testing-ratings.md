# Cold Hardiness Testing and Ratings

Understanding plant cold hardiness is essential for planning a
successful [[coleman-winter-harvest-philosophy]]. Eliot Coleman has spent decades observing
how different crops and varieties respond to freezing temperatures
inside unheated high tunnels. His ratings system helps growers
select the right crops for their conditions and anticipate how
plants will perform during extreme cold events.

## Principles of Cold Hardiness

Cold hardiness is not a fixed property but a dynamic condition that
changes based on several factors:
- **Acclimation:** Plants gradually increase their cold tolerance
  when exposed to progressively lower temperatures over weeks.
  A sudden hard freeze can damage plants that would survive the
  same temperature if properly acclimated.
- **Plant maturity:** Established plants with well-developed root
  systems are significantly hardier than young seedlings. This is
  why timing of the [[coleman-winter-harvest-planting-schedule]]
  is so critical.
- **Genetics:** Species and varieties differ enormously in their
  inherent cold tolerance. Selecting the right genetics is the
  first and most important decision a winter grower makes.
- **Growing conditions:** Well-nourished plants in healthy soil
  with adequate moisture are more cold-tolerant than stressed or
  nutrient-deficient plants.
- **Duration of exposure:** Brief temperature dips are more
  survivable than prolonged cold periods, even at the same minimum
  temperature.

## The Acclimation Process

Coleman describes acclimation as a two-stage process. In the first
stage, triggered by temperatures between 35 and 50 degrees Fahrenheit,
plants begin accumulating sugars and other cryoprotective compounds.
In the second stage, triggered by temperatures below freezing, plants
undergo more profound biochemical changes including membrane lipid
modification and the production of antifreeze proteins.

Full acclimation typically requires 2 to 4 weeks of progressively
colder temperatures. Plants that are actively growing in warm
conditions cannot instantly switch to a cold-hardy state. This is
why Coleman recommends that winter crops be established well before
the first hard freeze, allowing natural acclimation to occur.

## Killing Temperatures for Common Winter Crops

Based on Coleman's observations and published research, the following
approximate killing temperatures apply to fully acclimated plants
grown in protected culture with row covers:

**Extremely hardy (survive below 10 degrees Fahrenheit):**
- Mache: 5 degrees F or lower
- Claytonia: 5 degrees F or lower
- Minutina: 5 degrees F or lower
- Spinach (acclimated): approximately 10 degrees F
- Kale (Siberian types): approximately 5 degrees F
- Scallions ([[coleman-cold-hardy-varieties]]): approximately 10 degrees F

**Very hardy (survive 10 to 20 degrees Fahrenheit):**
- Tatsoi: approximately 15 degrees F
- Mizuna: approximately 15 degrees F
- Arugula (wild type): approximately 15 degrees F
- Turnip greens: approximately 15 degrees F
- Carrots (in ground): approximately 15 degrees F
- Leeks: approximately 15 degrees F

**Moderately hardy (survive 20 to 28 degrees Fahrenheit):**
- Lettuce (cold-hardy varieties): approximately 24 degrees F
- Endive: approximately 22 degrees F
- Escarole: approximately 22 degrees F
- Radishes: approximately 26 degrees F
- Arugula (cultivated type): approximately 25 degrees F
- Swiss chard: approximately 25 degrees F

These temperatures assume plants are under double protection (high
tunnel plus row cover) and fully acclimated. Open-field survival
temperatures are typically 5 to 10 degrees lower for each category.

## Variety Differences in Cold Tolerance

Within each species, significant variation exists between varieties.
Coleman has identified particularly cold-tolerant selections:
- **Spinach:** 'Giant Winter' and 'Viroflay' are among the most
  cold-hardy. 'Tyee' shows good bolt resistance in addition to
  cold tolerance.
- **Kale:** 'Red Russian' (Siberian type) and 'White Russian' are
  significantly hardier than curly kales. 'Nero di Toscana' (dinosaur
  kale) also performs well in cold conditions.
- **Lettuce:** 'Winter Density,' 'Rouge d'Hiver,' and 'North Pole'
  are bred for cold conditions. Butterhead types tend to be hardier
  than crisphead varieties.
- **Mache:** Large-seeded varieties like 'Vit' are more vigorous and
  slightly hardier than small-seeded types.
- **Arugula:** Wild arugula (Diplotaxis tenuifolia, 'Sylvetta') is
  substantially more cold-tolerant than standard arugula.

## Testing Cold Hardiness On-Farm

Coleman encourages growers to conduct their own cold hardiness
testing. This involves planting a range of varieties and observing
their performance through the winter. Simple methods include:
- **Tagged observation plants:** Mark specific plants of each variety
  and photograph them before and after cold events.
- **Minimum-maximum thermometers:** Place thermometers at crop level
  inside tunnels to record actual temperatures experienced.
- **Taste and texture evaluation:** Assess eating quality after
  freezing. Some varieties survive but become tough or bitter.
- **Yield recording:** Compare total harvested weight by variety
  across the winter season.

Over several seasons, this data reveals which varieties perform best
in a specific microclimate. Local conditions — elevation, wind
exposure, proximity to water bodies, and snow cover — all influence
actual cold hardiness performance.

## Microclimate Effects

The protected environment of a high tunnel creates a microclimate that
is significantly milder than ambient conditions. Coleman's research
shows that the combination of an unheated high tunnel with a row
cover inside can maintain temperatures 15 to 30 degrees above outside
minimums, depending on conditions. This effectively shifts the growing
zone by several hardiness zones.

Within a single high tunnel, microclimates vary. Areas near the end
walls experience more temperature fluctuation. The center of the
tunnel, away from doors and vents, maintains the most stable
temperature. Soil temperature also varies, with [[holzer-raised-beds-systems]] cooling
faster than ground-level soil.
