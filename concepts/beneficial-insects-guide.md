# Beneficial Insects Guide

Beneficial insects are predatory, parasitic, or pollinating insects that provide essential services in the garden and farm. They control pest populations, pollinate crops, and contribute to a balanced ecosystem. Attracting and conserving beneficial insects is a cornerstone of [[integrated-pest-management]] (IPM) and organic growing.

## Predatory Insects

### Ladybugs (Ladybird Beetles, Coccinellidae)

Both adults and larvae are voracious predators of aphids, mealybugs, scale insects, and soft-bodied pests. A single ladybug can consume 50-60 aphids per day; larvae consume up to 1,000 aphids during their development.

**Species to Know:**
- **Convergent ladybug (Hippodamia convergens)**: Most common released species. Native to North America. Migratory — many released ladybugs fly away within days.
- **Multicolored Asian lady beetle (Harmonia axyridis)**: Very effective predator but can become a nuisance indoors in fall. Not native; may outcompete native species.

**How to Attract and Retain:**
- Release ladybugs in the evening (they fly away during the heat of day)
- Water the release area before release
- Place them at the base of aphid-infested plants
- Plant umbelliferous flowers (dill, fennel, yarrow) for adult nectar feeding
- Provide shelter: leaf litter, straw mulch, insect hotels
- Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides that kill ladybugs along with pests

**Purchasing and Releasing:**
- Available from biological supply companies (1,500-2,000 per package)
- Store in refrigerator (not freezer) until release
- Best released in spring when aphids first appear
- Pre-water the garden; ladybugs need moisture to survive
- Release in small batches over several days rather than all at once

### Lacewings (Chrysopidae)

Lacewing larvae (called "aphid lions") are among the most effective generalist predators in the garden. They consume aphids, thrips, whiteflies, mealybugs, small caterpillars, and insect eggs.

**Life Cycle:**
- Adults lay eggs on thin stalks (to protect them from predation by their own larvae)
- Larvae are active predators for 2-3 weeks, consuming 100-600 aphids each
- Adults feed on nectar, pollen, and honeydew — not pests
- Multiple generations per year (2-4 in temperate climates)

**How to Attract:**
- Plant dill, angelica, coreopsis, cosmos, and sunflowers for adult nectar
- Avoid frequent tilling (destroys overwintering pupae)
- Provide leaf litter and mulch for overwintering
- See [[insectary-plantings]] for comprehensive plant lists

**Purchasing:**
- Lacewing eggs are available from suppliers; more cost-effective than ladybugs
- Release eggs or larvae in the garden near pest outbreaks
- Larvae are cannibalistic — distribute eggs evenly rather than in clumps

### Ground Beetles (Carabidae)

Large, fast-running beetles that hunt at night. They prey on slugs, snails, cutworms, root maggots, codling moth larvae, and other soil-dwelling pests.

**How to Attract:**
- Provide permanent ground cover and mulch (their habitat)
- Create beetle banks: raised strips of perennial grasses and wildflowers at field margins
- Avoid bare soil — ground beetles need cover to survive
- Do not use broad-spectrum soil insecticides
- Log and stone piles provide overwintering habitat

### Minute Pirate Bugs (Anthocoridae)

Tiny (1/16 inch) but extremely effective predators of thrips, spider mites, aphids, whiteflies, and small caterpillar eggs. They are among the first predators to arrive at pest outbreaks.

**How to Attract:**
- Plant alfalfa, buckwheat, clover, and small-flowered plants
- They are attracted by the presence of prey — if pests are present, pirate bugs usually follow
- Common in corn, alfalfa, and strawberry fields

### predatory Mites (Phytoseiidae)

Microscopic predators of spider mites, thrips, and other small arthropods. Essential for greenhouse and indoor growing where spider mites are a chronic problem.

**Key Species:**
- **Phytoseiulus persimilis**: Specialized [[spider-mite]] predator. Consumes 5-20 spider mites per day. Prefers humid conditions (60-90% RH).
- **Neoseiulus californicus**: More generalist; tolerates wider temperature and humidity ranges. Survives on pollen when prey is scarce.
- **Amblyseius cucumeris**: Primary thrips predator. Also feeds on spider mites.

**How to Attract and Maintain:**
- Maintain moderate humidity (difficult in dry climates — use mulch and ground covers)
- Avoid broad-spectrum miticides
- Provide pollen sources (many predatory mites can survive on pollen when prey is scarce)
- Purchase and release for greenhouse and indoor infestations

## Parasitic Insects

### Parasitic Wasps (Hymenoptera: various families)

A large group of tiny wasps that lay their eggs inside or on pest insects. The wasp larvae consume the pest from the inside, eventually killing it. Most parasitic wasps are extremely small (1-2 mm) and harmless to humans.

**Key Species:**
- **Aphidius colemani**: Parasitizes aphids. The aphid swells into a "mummy" (brown, hardened shell) before the wasp emerges. Extremely effective in greenhouses and gardens.
- **Trichogramma spp.**: Egg parasitoids that lay their eggs inside the eggs of moths and butterflies (including cabbage loopers, codling moths, and corn earworms). Prevent caterpillars before they hatch.
- **Encarsia formosa**: Parasitizes whiteflies. The whitefly nymph turns black when parasitized. Essential for greenhouse whitefly control.
- **Braconid wasps**: Parasitize tomato hornworms, cabbage worms, and other caterpillars. Visible as white silk cocoons on the backs of parasitized caterpillars. Leave parasitized caterpillars alone — the wasps will emerge and continue the cycle.

**How to Attract:**
- Plant umbelliferous flowers (dill, fennel, parsley, cilantro, yarrow, Queen Anne's lace) — the shallow flowers provide nectar for adult wasps
- Plant composite flowers (cosmos, zinnias, sunflowers, asters) for additional nectar sources
- Avoid insecticides — parasitic wasps are extremely sensitive to even organic sprays
- See [[insectary-plantings]] for detailed plant recommendations

**Purchasing:**
- Trichogramma wasps are available as eggs on cards; hang cards in the garden when target moths are laying eggs
- Aphidius and Encarsia available from biological suppliers for greenhouse use
- Release timing must match the pest's life cycle for effectiveness

### Tachinid Flies (Tachinidae)

Large, hairy flies that parasitize caterpillars, beetles, and bugs. Females lay eggs on or in pest insects. Larvae consume the host from within.

- **Hosts**: cabbage looper, imported cabbageworm, Japanese beetle, squash bug, stink bug
- **How to attract**: Plant dill, fennel, buckwheat, and clover for adult nectar
- **Identification**: Resemble houseflies but with bristly bodies. Look for small white eggs glued to the backs of caterpillars.

## Pollinators

### Honeybees (Apis mellifera)

The most well-known pollinators, essential for many fruit and vegetable crops. Managed in hives by beekeepers.

**Crops dependent on honeybee pollination**: Apples, pears, almonds, melons, squash, cucumbers, berries, clover

### Native Bees

Over 4,000 species of native bees in North America. Many are more efficient pollinators than honeybees for specific crops.

- **Bumblebees (Bombus spp.)**: Large, fuzzy bees that fly in cooler temperatures and shake flowers ("buzz pollination") to release pollen. Essential for tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, and blueberries. Nest in small colonies in the ground or in abandoned rodent burrows.
- **Mason bees (Osmia spp.)**: Solitary bees that nest in hollow stems or holes. Extremely efficient pollinators — 250 mason bees can pollinate as many apples as 20,000 honeybees. Provide mason bee houses (blocks with 5/16 inch holes).
- **Sweat bees (Halictidae)**: Small, often metallic-colored bees attracted to human sweat. Excellent generalist pollinators.
- **Leafcutter bees (Megachile spp.)**: Solitary bees that cut circular pieces from leaves to line their nests. Excellent alfalfa pollinators.

### How to Attract Pollinators

- **Provide continuous bloom**: Plant flowers that bloom from early spring through late fall
- **Plant native species**: Native bees co-evolved with native plants and are most attracted to them
