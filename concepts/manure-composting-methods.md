# Manure Composting Methods

If you have access to livestock manure — whether from your own animals, a neighbor's farm, or a feedlot — you have one of the most valuable materials for compost production. Different types of manure require different handling, but all share the same fundamental principles: proper C:N balancing, adequate moisture, sufficient soil for nitrate capture, and careful [[cervantes-reservoir-temperature-management]].

## General Principles

### Raw Manure Safety

Do NOT incorporate raw manure into soil before starting a [[vegetable-crop-demand-classification-fertility-matching-solomon]] whose edible parts will contact the soil. Some manures are contaminated with antibiotics, hormones, and pesticides. Certified organic growers are not allowed to harvest a crop unless more than 120 days have elapsed between putting the manure into soil and harvest.

### Solids and Liquids

Animal wastes come in two parts — solid and liquid (urine). They are equally valuable, which is unfortunate because the urine usually goes to waste. If animals are penned on bedding that soaks up urine, be sure to compost that bedding along with the solids.

## Ruminant Manure (Horse, Cow, Sheep)

### Characteristics

- C:N of solid fraction varies with what the animals are fed
- Could range from 15:1 to 30:1 depending on bedding and diet
- May contain valuable minerals in addition to nitrogen and potassium
- Urine-soaked [[fukuoka-straw-winter-grain-straw-mulch]]: C:N ~30:1
- Urine/straw and solids mixed in roughly equal quantities: excellent compost base

### Composting Method

1. Mix solids with roughly equal quantities of dried vegetation (corn stalks, spoiled hay, leaves, garden waste)
2. You can use up to half again more volume of dry matter than manure
3. Add soil to make up 5-10% of total starting volume
4. Adjust moisture to nicely damp
5. Compost and turn as usual

### Expected Results

Starting C:N close to 30:1 should yield compost adequate for medium-demand vegetables. This was the classic "hot bed" method of old market-gardening books — called "hot" because it heated the soil for early crops.

### Manure Without Bedding

If there is no bedding straw:
- Mix solids with an equal quantity of dried vegetation
- If you have neither vegetation nor bedding, mix manure with about 1/4 soil by starting volume
- It will still heat quite a bit
- If it gets too hot to insert your hand or smells like ammonia, break it down and mix in more soil
- Turn every two weeks
- Because ingredients started with a much lower C:N, this compost will be more potent than vegetation-based compost — possibly good enough for high-demand vegetables

### Sawdust Warning

If the urine has soaked into sawdust, forget about making compost that will grow veggies unless you live where the soil stays warm most of the year. Sawdust has a C:N of 500:1. The resulting compost will have such a high C:N that it ties up nitrogen in the soil for a very long time.

## Poultry and Rabbit Manure

### Characteristics

- Free-ranging chickens supplement grain with insects and tender green vegetation, producing highly potent manure
- C:N is well under 10:1 — extremely nitrogen-rich
- Rabbit manure is similarly potent
- Poultry manure contains lots of phosphorus (chickens eat phosphorus-rich seeds)

### Composting Method

Compost as you would other manure, with two critical differences:

1. **C:N adjustment**: Mix with enough vegetation to bring the starting C:N to about 20:1 (not 30:1 as with ruminant manure)
2. **Soil is essential**: Even more important to include soil when composting strong manure — the soil captures the ammonia that would otherwise escape from such nitrogen-rich material

### Temperature Management

Beware of overheating! With such low C:N material, the heap can easily exceed 155°F (68°C), causing massive nitrogen loss. If you can't insert your hand into the heap and keep it there for a short time, it must be cooled:
- Turn the heap
- Mix more soil into it as you turn
- Use a probe thermometer — don't let the core exceed 140°F (60°C)

### Rabbit Manure Management

If you keep rabbits:
- Spread a thick layer of grain straw under the cages to soak up urine
- When you start smelling ammonia around the cages, it's time to rake up the whole accumulation
- Compost with soil and spread new straw to start the next batch

### Expected Results

Properly composted poultry or rabbit manure will end up with a C:N of about 10:1 and contain at least 3% nitrogen — good enough for high-demand vegetables.

## Commercial Chicken Manure Products

Many [[solomon-garden-centers-seedling-traps]] sell bagged [[chicken-manure]] compost. These are usually made by blending chicken manure with sawdust. After a brief initial heating, the material cools and sits while the sawdust slowly decomposes. The product is then dried and sacked or pelletized.

### The Problem

The sawdust raises the C:N significantly. The bag analysis may show reasonable NPK, but the sawdust's decomposition process in the soil will tie up some of the nitrates in the chicken manure.

### The Verdict

These products work okay, but only if you use larger quantities than would be indicated by the lab analysis. Measured by result for dollar spent, you'll get better results using seedmeal, feathermeal, fishmeal, or rankage. And of course, the best results come from using COF.

## Obtaining Manure

### Feedlot Manure

Well-rotted feedlot manure can be obtained relatively cheaply. One dump-truck load can supply an average home garden's [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] deficit for about two years. Check with local farmers, feedlots, or livestock operations.

### Freshness Matters

The fresher the manure, the more nitrogen it retains. Manure that has been sitting in piles for months has already lost much of its nitrogen to the atmosphere as ammonia gas.

### Transport

If you don't have a pickup truck, you'll need to find someone willing to haul for you. The cost of manure itself is usually very low — transportation is the main expense.
