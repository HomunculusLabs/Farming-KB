# Double-Digging Bed Preparation Techniques

## Why Rototilling Falls Short

[[steve-solomon]] is sharply critical of rototilling as a primary bed preparation
method. The fundamental problem is depth: a walk-behind tiller loosens soil only
about five inches (12 cm) deep, while even a heavy tractor-mounted rotary
cultivator manages perhaps seven inches (18 cm). This is simply not enough for
most vegetables, whose root systems ideally extend several feet downward.

Worse, when soil contains significant clay, the tines create a compacted "plow
pan" -- a polished, airless layer at the maximum depth of the tines. This plow
pan acts as an effective barrier to root penetration, restricting the entire
root system to the shallow layer above it. Solomon notes that a plow pan created
by repeated moldboard plowing can persist for half a century. The only way to
eliminate it is to dig through it with a shovel or fork.

## The Double-Digging Process

After eliminating sod (by mowing closely, removing clippings, and spreading soil
amendments), Solomon describes a systematic hand-digging approach:

**First pass:** Dig up chunks of sod, turning them on their sides or upside down
to damage and kill the grass. This damages the root systems sufficiently that
much of the sod will die.

**Second pass (one week later):** Just before the grass begins growing again,
turn the chunks a second time, cutting through them while reducing their size.
This second turn kills most remaining grass and blends in amendments more
effectively. You will dig deeper, quicker, and with much less effort than the
first pass.

**Third pass (another week later):** By now enough sod has decomposed that you
will have fine, crumbly soil nearly to the depth of the blade. Any preexisting
plow pan will be gone.

## Practical Tips for Efficient Digging

Solomon, who was 63 when he wrote the book with 33 years of gardening
experience, offers these practical observations:

- Divide the garden into beds of 100 square feet (10 square meters) and dig one
bed at a time. Mark out strips about 5 feet (1.5 m) wide and 25 feet (8 m) long.
- The bigger the bite you try to take, the harder you will work. Be methodical
and precise; nibble off bits of a size and weight you can comfortably handle.  -
After turning new sod for about 90 minutes, he needs rest. That 90 minutes
covers about 125 square feet (11.5 square meters) of new sod.  - Working six
days a week for about 90 minutes a day, he can prepare a large garden in less
than a month.

## Autumn Versus Spring Digging

Autumn digging offers several advantages over spring:

1. The soil has all winter to settle and develop structure before planting.  2.
Resistant clumps of living sod can be pulled out during the spring re-dig and
tossed on the compost heap.  3. Weed seeds that sprout in autumn and early
spring are killed wholesale when you dig again.  4. Amendments incorporated in
autumn have time to begin breaking down and integrating with the soil.

## When NOT to Double-Dig

Solomon disagrees with the intensive gardening orthodoxy that all beds must be
double-dug to 24 inches (60 cm). He argues that loosening 12 inches is plenty
for most purposes. The second foot produces little additional benefit in
exchange for enormous effort. Over time, the second foot naturally becomes
looser as earthworms transport organic matter downward and as plant nutrients
leach into the subsoil, chemically loosening clay through a process called
"flocculation."

For clay soils specifically, Solomon warns against the common advice to remedy
them by mixing in huge amounts of organic matter. This is expensive, the organic
matter decomposes rapidly and requires annual replacement, and the result is a
soil that "will never grow vegetables as well as a light soil will -- never."
More critically, excessive organic matter can unbalance the soil's mineral
content, harming the nutritional quality of the food produced.

## The Efficient Alternative for Established Gardens

For gardens where the sod has already been broken (by prior tilling or digging),
restoring a bed or wide row for replanting takes Solomon only about 30 minutes
of moderate effort per 100 square feet. This ongoing maintenance is far easier
than initial breaking and is one of his strongest arguments against owning a
rototiller.
