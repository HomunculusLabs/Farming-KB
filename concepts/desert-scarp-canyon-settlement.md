## Desert Scarp and Canyon Settlement

## Overview

In arid landscapes, the most critical factor for human settlement is not food but water. [[bill-mollison]]'s analysis of desert settlement focuses on the geological features that make habitation possible — particularly scarps, box canyons, wadis, and pediments — and the [[condensation-strategies-desert-agriculture]] peoples have developed over millennia to locate, collect, and conserve water in these environments.

## Desert Erosion Geology

The classic arid landscape presents two distinct erosion levels that form the primary topographic features:

### The Scarp System
- A **receding scarp** travels geologically backwards over time
- There is a sharp **down-drop** at the scarp edge, followed by a back slope leading to the pediment
- Scarps consist of fairly soft rock capped by a hard laterite layer (aluminum oxides, ferric oxides, iron crust) approximately 6 inches thick
- This laterite cap covers desert uplands and buttes across vast areas
- Buttes — residual bits of scarp left behind as the scarp retreats — may rise 40 to 400 feet
- The Grand Canyon, a mile deep, presents an extreme example of this profile

### The Pediment
The pediment is the sloping plain at the base of the scarp. It connects to the [[wadi]] floor and provides the transitional zone between cliff and desert plain. Weakness lines in the rock create drainage patterns that cut back from the scarp front, eventually detaching residuals that crumble and distribute across the plain.

## Optimal Dwelling Placement

### The Notch at the Scarp Base
Water falling from the top of the scarp hits the pediment and splashes, cutting a **notch** at the base. This notch is a key water collection point. In [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-arid-regions]] worldwide — Anatolia, Turkey, southern Iran, Egypt, the American Southwest — people have carved dwellings into these soft scarps using primitive tools.

### Thermal Advantages of Cliff Dwellings
Once you are approximately 16 feet into rock, temperature variation becomes practically nil. The dwelling in the desert properly sits under the cliff:

- **Hot deserts**: Always on the shade side of the cliff
- **Cold deserts**: Situated where summer sun misses the dwelling but winter sun enters
- Gutters can be cut along the cliff top to redirect rain runoff away from dwelling entrances

### Box Canyon Villages
The most satisfactory place to locate a village is in **box canyons**, up within the cliffs. Advantages include:

- A single fence across the canyon entrance keeps out large feral animals (camels, wild horses)
- Abrupt cliffs rise on all sides, providing natural fortification
- Box canyons may be 300-400 yards wide
- Multiple smaller box canyons may branch off the main one
- A trickle of water often flows at the bottom where pediments meet in a "V" shape

## Water Finding and Collection

### Water on the Scarp
Contrary to intuition, water in the desert is often found **up on the scarp**, just before the drop-off point. Where streams during rain fall over the scarp edge, they swirl and create water holes. Following seed-eating birds (doves, pigeons) can lead to these water sources.

### Sand Water Storage
Sand stores water at approximately 50% of its bulk. A tank filled with sand effectively stores water without evaporation and protects it from animals. Aborigines use stones in pits with clay basins underneath to collect moisture through condensation.

### Bore Water Quality
Boring close to the pediment yields the best water:
- Near the scarp: 200-300 parts per million of salt (low, non-detectable)
- One mile off the scarp: may reach 1,100 parts per million (unusable)
- Modern windmills near the scarp can remove the threat of absolute water shortage

### Rain Patterns
Desert rainfall is characterized by extreme irregularity:
- Rain typically falls in sudden, heavy downpours
- A desert with a "10-inch average" may receive 27 inches in one year after three years of no rain, including 4 inches in a single day
- When rain pours off escarpments, water surges 3-6 feet high through constricted wadis
- This water picks up enormous loads of sand and silt before dispersing on the desert floor
- Soils remain saturated for long periods after initial soak — damp conditions may persist for a couple of years

## Desert Agriculture Potential

Given water, the growth potential in deserts is enormous:

- Soils are **unexploited** — fresh mineral, every sort of mineral in abundance
- Growth response is very fast — fruiting grapes from cuttings within 15 months
- Suitable crops include **citrus, grapes, apricots, pistachios, almonds**, and melon crops
- All deserts have natural melons (e.g., the Australian [[paddy-melon-mollison]])
- Most normal vegetable crops can grow in the desert
- Dates grow in soaked desert sands where soils remain saturated after rain

### The Date-Melon-Goat-Coffee Ecology
In the North African desert, a complete six-species ecology has sustained human populations for thousands of years:

- **Dates** — long-storage staple food
- **Melons** — fresh food source
- **Goats** — consume melons, dates, and desert scrub; produce milk, cheese, and dung fuel
- **Coffee** — beverage crop
- The system requires only fermentation and cheese-making knowledge
- Everything needed is present within this ecology

## Desert Food Resources

### Seed Production
Deserts produce enormous quantities of seed during rest periods. The desert goes to rest for years as seed and capsules, with a huge production that supports seed-eating birds (pigeons, doves, quail).

### Tubers
Enormous tubers grow from desert legumes, some weighing 300-400 pounds. These remain dormant for up to seven years, then push up and spread 200 yards of green growth after rain, producing abundant seed before dying back six feet under the sand. Australian aborigines locate these through what Mollison describes as "psychic divination."

### Other Resources
- **Dried foods**: Dates, dried apricots, desert nuts are mainstays
- **Pinion pines**: In the American Southwest, a family can gather 60 bushels of pine nuts per day in a good year
- **Insects**: Enormously common in deserts, both nocturnal and diurnal
- **Reptiles**: The desert equivalent of coastal fish — large and plentiful where shade is available

## Dwellings and Shelter

### Rock-Carved Dwellings
Desert scarps are carved into dwellings in many cultures:
- [[yage-common-themes-south-american-indians]] of the Southwest have long inhabited scarp niches
- Anatolian, Iranian, and Egyptian desert peoples carve into soft rock that hardens on air exposure
- Primitive tools suffice for hewing reasonable dwellings

### Drying Rooms
On the opposite scarp, catching the hot sun, drying rooms can be cut into rock. Dates, apricots, and desert nuts desiccate very quickly in these locations, providing long-storage food security.

## Feral Animals and Defense

Desert settlements face threats from feral animals, particularly in Australia:

- **Camels**: Thousands of wild camels descended from animals brought by Afghan cameleers; bull camels are dangerous
- A single fence across a box canyon entrance provides effective defense
- The canyon's natural cliffs provide additional protection

## Key Design Principles for Arid Settlement

1. **Locate dwellings under cliffs** — 16 feet into rock eliminates temperature variation
2. **Position on the correct solar aspect** — shade side in hot deserts, winter-sun side in cold deserts
3. **Live in box canyons** — natural fortification with single-point defense
4. **Collect water at the scarp top** — before the drop-off, where swirling creates natural catchments
5. **Store water in sand** — prevents evaporation and protects from animals
6. **Bore near the pediment** — closest to fresh water with lowest salinity
7. **Design for three-year drought cycles** — storage systems must sustain settlement through extended dry periods
8. **Use the complete [[dryland-strategies-and-desert-ecology]]** — integrate plants, animals, and [[yield-extension-and-storage-strategies]] into a self-sustaining system
