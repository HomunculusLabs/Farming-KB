## Fall and Winter Planting Schedules

Planning fall and winter planting schedules is one of the most critical and nuanced aspects of year-round vegetable production. [[eliot-coleman]]"s system relies on precise timing across multiple growing environments — outdoor fields, early greenhouses, later greenhouses, and cool greenhouses — to ensure continuous harvests through the coldest months.

## The Four Planting Sites

Coleman's [[four-season-farm]] employs four distinct planting zones for fall and winter harvesting:

1. **Outdoor fields** — Harvested until early November, these receive the earliest sowings and serve as the primary production zone during milder fall weather.
2. **Early greenhouses** — Sowings begin shortly after outdoor dates, providing protected growing conditions as temperatures drop.
3. **Later greenhouses** — These receive sowings roughly two weeks after early houses, extending the harvest window further into winter.
4. **Cool greenhouses** — The latest sowings, often a month or more after outdoor dates, are intended for the coldest period when minimal heat or passive protection sustains growth.

This staggered approach across environments creates a continuous supply chain rather than a single glut followed by scarcity.

## Scheduling Principles

### Sunlight as the Primary Factor

The fundamental driver of winter harvest scheduling is not temperature but **daylight**. As days shorten past roughly 10 hours (around November through mid-February in Coleman's Maine latitude of 44°N), plant growth slows dramatically regardless of temperature. The goal is to have crops reach near-maturity by the time the [[persephone-period]] (days under 10 hours) begins. During this period, plants essentially sit in a state of suspended animation — they don't grow, but they don't die either, and can be harvested as needed.

### Succession Sowing

Rather than sowing each crop once, Coleman recommends **frequent succession sowings** — sometimes weekly or biweekly — across all four sites. This buffers against germination failures, weather damage, and varying growth rates. Dates marked in bold in his system indicate sowings planned for multiple harvests.

### The Best Advice

Coleman is candid about the limitations of prescriptive dates: *"We are not entirely satisfied with the precision of these dates yet and will be adding more dates as we fine-tune our production in the years to come. No dates are given for crops where we are not sure of our recommendations. The best advice is to sow often and keep notes."*

This underscores that planting schedules are inherently local and must be adapted through careful record-keeping.

## Sample Crop Schedules

### Arugula

Arugula is one of the most cold-hardy salad greens and tolerates repeated cutting. Coleman"s schedule reflects its versatility:

| Site | Sowing Dates |
|------|-------------|
| Outdoor | September 4, 8, 12, 16 |
| Early House | September 22, 24, 26, 28, 30, October 2 |
| Cool House | October 20, 25, 30, November 4, 15, December 10 |

The wide sowing window — spanning from early September through early December in protected environments — ensures continuous availability. Late cool-house sowings are remarkably late but succeed because arugula grows quickly and can be harvested small.

### Lettuce

Lettuce requires slightly earlier timing than arugula due to its sensitivity to freezing:

| Site | Sowing Dates |
|------|-------------|
| Outdoor | August 29, September 2, 4, 6 |
| Early House | September 13, 15, 17 |
| Late House | September 19, 22, 25, 28; October 2, 6, 9, 12 |
| Cool House | October 24, 30; November 5, 8, 11 |

The late house receives the most intensive succession, with sowings roughly every 3 days for a month. This reflects the importance of lettuce in winter salad mix and its vulnerability to bolting or freezing if timing is off.

### Carrots

Carrots require early sowing to develop adequate root size before growth slows:

| Site | Sowing Dates |
|------|-------------|
| Outdoor | August 10, 16 |
| Late House | August 20 |
| Cool House | October 6, 12, 16, November 5 |

The remarkably late cool-house dates for carrots reflect the use of specific cold-tolerant varieties and the protected environment. "Sweet winter" or "candy" carrots develop higher sugar content after frost exposure.

### Mâche (Corn Salad)

Mâche is perhaps the quintessential winter crop — extremely cold-hardy and adapted to short-day conditions:

| Site | Sowing Dates |
|------|-------------|
| Outdoor | August 26, 30 |
| Early House | September 2, 6 |
| Late House | September 10 |
| Cool House | October 1, 5, November 30 |

### Spinach

Spinach thrives in cold conditions and is a cornerstone of winter production:

| Site | Sowing Dates |
|------|-------------|
| Outdoor | September 8, 12 |
| Early House | September 24, 28, October 2 |
| Late House | October 6, 9, 12 |
| Cool House | October 24, 30 |

## Adapting Schedules to Your Location

### Latitude Adjustments

Coleman"s farm is at approximately 44°N latitude. The timing of the Persephone period (10-hour day) varies by latitude:

- **Further south** (35°N): Persephone period is shorter and later; sowings can be later
- **Further north** (50°N+): Persephone period is longer and earlier; sowings must be earlier

The key is to determine your local 10-hour-day dates and work backward from those to plan maturity.

### Climate Zone Considerations

USDA hardiness zones provide a rough guide for outdoor growing, but greenhouse production modifies these zones significantly. A zone 5 grower with a cold house can produce as if in zone 7 or warmer.

### Microclimate Factors

Local conditions — elevation, proximity to water bodies, wind exposure, and urban heat islands — all affect actual frost dates and growing seasons. Coleman emphasizes the importance of **local observation and record-keeping** over any published schedule.

## Historical Reading on Intensive Cultivation

Coleman's planting philosophy draws on a rich tradition of intensive vegetable cultivation, particularly the French *maraîcher* (market gardener) system. Key historical references include:

- **Aguatias (1913)** — Identified "manure, water, and labor" as the backbone of intensive culture
- **Henderson"s *Gardening for Profit* (1867)** — The first American book on market gardening
- **Smith's *French Gardening* (1909)** — Clear presentation of intensive production systems funded by [[joseph-fels]]
- **Macself's *French Intensive Gardening* (1932)** — Hard data with excellent illustrations from a competent practitioner
- **Kropotkin's *Fields, Factories and Workshops* (1901)** — Perceptive observations called "one of the canonical texts of the ecological tradition" by *The Ecologist*

These works demonstrate that the principles of year-round vegetable production — [[succession-planting]], protected cultivation, intensive soil management — have been refined over more than a century of practice.

## Key Takeaways

- **Daylight, not temperature, governs winter growing** — plan sowings so crops reach maturity before the 10-hour-day threshold
- **Sow often and keep notes** — precision schedules require local adaptation through experience
- **Stagger across environments** — multiple planting sites create resilience and continuous supply
- **No single date is universal** — all schedules must be adapted to latitude, microclimate, and individual growing conditions
- **The tradition is centuries deep** — modern winter harvesting builds on intensive cultivation knowledge developed by French market gardeners in the 19th century
