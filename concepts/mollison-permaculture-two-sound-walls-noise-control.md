## Solutions: Earthworks, Vegetation, and Insulation

Earthworks, vegetation, or insulation all help with noise control. The design of the highways, where earth spoil is too often neatly levelled out, so permitting noise to flow uninterrupted, is a major factor. Well-placed embankments of earthed-over tyres plus good house insulation, as per Section 6.1 (the reactive house), is the answer to intolerable noise levels.

### Earth Berms and Banks

Earth embankments are among the most effective noise barriers available. They combine mass (essential for blocking low-frequency sound) with absorption (for reducing reflected sound). The key principles:

- Height matters: a berm should be tall enough to break the line of sight between noise source and receiver
- Width matters: wider berms absorb more sound through the earth mass
- Vegetated berms perform better than bare earth because the vegetation absorbs and scatters sound
- Slope: a berm with the steeper face towards the noise source is more effective

### Tyre Walls

Earthed-over tyre walls are a permaculture favourite for noise barriers. They combine:

- Mass from the earth fill (blocking sound transmission)
- Absorption from the irregular rubber surface
- Recycling of waste materials
- Ease of construction on steep or difficult sites
- Integration with planting — the tyres can be planted with trailing and climbing species

### Dense Vegetation Screens

While forest alone is relatively inefficient at noise reduction (100 m for 6-7 dB), dense vegetation in combination with earthworks and structural barriers is very effective. The best approach is layered:

1. A solid earth berm or wall as the primary barrier
2. Dense evergreen shrubs planted on and around the berm
3. Tall trees behind the berm to catch sound that rises above it
4. Ground-cover plants to prevent sound reflection off hard ground surfaces

### Structural Design for Noise

For the building itself, noise control aligns closely with thermal efficiency:

- **Double glazing**: Essential for both noise and thermal control
- **Draught-proofing**: Gaps around doors and windows transmit both cold air and noise
- **Massive walls**: Stone, earthbrick, rammed earth, and other high-mass walls provide both thermal mass and sound insulation
- **Insulated cavity walls**: The air space in cavity walls, especially when filled with insulation, creates an effective sound barrier
- **Solid floor construction**: Concrete slabs and earth floors transmit less noise than suspended timber floors

## Design Integration

Noise control should be considered early in the site planning process. Key strategies:

1. **Locate sensitive areas away from noise sources**: Place sleeping areas, meditation spaces, and healing spaces (hospitals, aged care) on the quiet side of the site
2. **Use existing topography**: Natural ridges and hollows can be enhanced as noise barriers
3. **Buffer zones**: Designate zones of increasing density between noise sources and occupied areas
4. **Permeable barriers**: Windbreaks and shelterbelts that are also permeable can reduce noise while maintaining airflow for ventilation
5. **Water features**: The sound of flowing water can mask undesirable noise — a permaculture technique that serves multiple functions
