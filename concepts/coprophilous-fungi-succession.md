## Coprophilous Fungi — Succession and Ecological Dynamics ## Overview Coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungi encompass species from multiple fungal groups, including **Myxomycetes, Zygomycetes, Ascomycetes, and Basidiomycetes**. Of the approximately 700 described myxomycete species, roughly **12 are exclusively coprophilous**. Dung represents a nutrient-rich, temporally dynamic substrate that supports a distinctive fungal community with well-documented successional patterns. The study of coprophilous fungi is central to [[psilocybe-global-biodiversity-hotspots]] gradients. This pattern has implications for paleoecological studies that use Sporormiella spores as proxies for megaherbivore presence.

## Spore Dispersal and Germination

### Digestive Tract Requirement

Spores of many coprophilous taxa germinate **ONLY after passage through the mammalian digestive tract**. This remarkable adaptation ensures that spores are deposited directly onto fresh dung, the optimal substrate for growth. The specific physiological trigger may involve changes in spore wall permeability, pH exposure, or enzymatic activation during gut transit.

### Dispersal Mechanisms

- **Forceful discharge** — many ascomycetes actively eject spores, which then adhere to vegetation above the dung
- **Gelatinous sheaths** — spore coatings that facilitate adhesion to plant surfaces
- **Insects and mites** — important secondary dispersal agents that carry spores between dung pats and onto herbage

## Competition and Defense

### Hyphal Interference

Competition through **hyphal interference** is widespread among coprophilous fungi. Many species produce diffusible metabolites that inhibit or kill competing fungi, creating zones of inhibition on the substrate.

### Antibiotic Production

Some coprophilous fungi produce **diffusible antibiotics** that suppress competitors. This competitive arena makes dung a productive source of novel antifungal natural products (Gloer 1995, 1997), with implications for pharmaceutical discovery.

### Physical Defenses

- *Chaetomium* species possess **ornamented perithecial hairs** that serve as defensive structures
- **Melanized pigments** in spores and hyphae provide protection against UV radiation during exposure on dung surfaces

## Collection Methods

### Substrate Selection

Optimal dung for collection:

- Source: **herbivores** — cattle, horses, bison, rabbits, deer
- Age: **several days old** with a crusted exterior
- The crust indicates initial colonization has begun but succession is still active

### Isolation and Culture

- **DRBC medium** (Dichloran Rose Bengal Chloramphenicol) restricts fast-growing Zygomycetes, allowing slower ascomycetes and basidiomycetes to be recovered
- **Warcup dilution technique** — serial dilution of dung suspensions plated onto selective media provides quantitative species counts and facilitates isolation of individual taxa

## Ecological Significance

Coprophilous fungi are key agents of **nutrient recycling** in grassland and pastoral ecosystems. They accelerate the decomposition of herbivore dung, releasing nitrogen and phosphorus for plant uptake.
