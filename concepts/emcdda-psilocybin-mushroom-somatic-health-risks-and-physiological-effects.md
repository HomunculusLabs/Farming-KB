# EMCDDA Psilocybin Mushroom Somatic Health Risks and Physiological Effects (2006)

## Overview

The EMCDDA's 2006 case study on hallucinogenic mushrooms examined both the
psychological and somatic (physical) health risks associated with their use.
While the psychological effects received greater attention in both research and
media coverage, the somatic health risks represented a distinct category of
concern with specific clinical implications for emergency medical response and
harm reduction messaging.

## Acute Physiological Effects

Psilocybin-containing mushrooms produce a range of acute physiological
effects that accompany their primary psychoactive action. These somatic
effects are generally transient and resolve without medical intervention,
but they can be distressing for users, particularly those who are
inexperienced or who consume unexpectedly potent specimens.

### Nausea and Gastrointestinal Distress
Nausea is one of the most commonly reported somatic effects of
[[health-risks-and-harm-reduction-for-hallucinogenic-mushroom-use]]. The EMCDDA noted that gastrointestinal
discomfort, including nausea, abdominal cramping, and in some cases
vomiting, was frequently reported by users. This effect was attributed
both to the pharmacological action of psilocybin on serotonin receptors
in the gut (the enteric nervous system contains a large proportion of the
body's serotonin receptors) and to the direct irritant effects of
consuming raw fungal material.

Brewing mushrooms into tea was identified as a common harm reduction
strategy to reduce nausea. The tea preparation method potentially reduces
the amount of insoluble fungal material that must be consumed, though it
does not eliminate the serotonergic effects of psilocybin on the gut.
Some users reported that the nausea was most pronounced during the onset
phase of the experience and typically subsided as the psychoactive effects
peaked.

### Cardiovascular Effects
Psilocybin mushrooms produce measurable cardiovascular effects, including
increased heart rate and elevated blood pressure. The EMCDDA noted that
these effects were generally modest in healthy individuals but could be
clinically significant in patients with pre-existing cardiovascular
conditions. The stimulant-like cardiovascular response is mediated through
the serotonergic and sympathetic nervous system activation produced by
psilocybin's action as a 5-HT2A receptor agonist.

For individuals with hypertension, cardiac arrhythmias, or other
cardiovascular disease, the cardiovascular effects of psilocybin could
theoretically pose a risk of adverse cardiac events. The EMCDDA case
study identified this as an area requiring further clinical investigation,
as the existing evidence base was limited to case reports and small-scale
studies rather than systematic epidemiological data.

### Pupil Dilation and Visual Disturbances
Mydriasis (pupil dilation) is a consistent and reliable physiological
marker of psilocybin intoxication. The EMCDDA noted that dilated pupils
were among the most recognizable signs of recent mushroom use. This effect
is directly mediated by psilocybin's serotonergic action and typically
persists for the duration of the psychoactive experience. Increased
sensitivity to light (photophobia) was also reported as an accompanying
effect.

### Thermoregulation
Alterations in body temperature perception were reported, though true
hyperthermia or hypothermia was rare. Some users experienced flushing or
feeling unusually warm or cold. These effects were generally mild and did
not typically require medical intervention. However, the EMCDDA noted that
the combination of altered temperature perception with vigorous physical
activity (such as dancing in club environments) could potentially lead to
dangerous overheating.

### Other Somatic Symptoms
Additional physiological effects reported in the EMCDDA case study
included dizziness, headache, muscle weakness or tension, tremor, and
increased perspiration. These effects were typically transient and resolved
spontaneously. Loss of appetite during the experience was commonly
reported, and some users experienced a metallic taste or dry mouth.

## Misidentification and Toxic Mushroom Poisoning

One of the most serious somatic health risks associated with hallucinogenic
mushroom use was not caused by psilocybin itself but by the misidentification
of toxic species. The EMCDDA highlighted that nearly all psilocybin-containing
mushrooms were small, brown, or tan, and could easily be mistaken for
non-psychoactive, inedible, or poisonous species found in similar habitats.

### Fatal Lookalikes
The primary distinguishing feature of most psilocybin mushrooms was their
tendency to bruise blue when handled, a reaction caused by the oxidation of
psilocin. However, reliance on this single identification characteristic
was dangerous, as some toxic species could appear similar to novices. The
EMCDDA emphasized that [[emcdda-wild-mushroom-foraging-misidentification-hazards]] carried inherent risks and
that misidentification could lead to potentially fatal poisoning from species
such as [[galerina-marginata]], which contains the same amatoxins as the death
cap (Amanita phalloides).

### Amanita muscaria as a Legal Alternative
Following legal restrictions on psilocybin mushrooms, some retailers began
marketing Amanita muscaria (fly agaric) as a legal alternative. The EMCDDA
flagged this as a significant health concern, as Amanita muscaria contains
ibotenic acid and muscimol, compounds with a completely different
pharmacological profile from psilocybin and carrying substantial toxicity
risks. Furthermore, closely related Amanita species are highly toxic and
could cause fatal poisoning, making accurate identification critical for
anyone consuming wild-harvested Amanita specimens.

## Emergency Medical Presentations

The EMCDDA collected data on emergency medical presentations related to
hallucinogenic mushroom use through [[hallucinogenic-mushroom-telephone-helplines-and-emergency-response]] hospital
reports. The most common reasons for emergency presentation were acute
psychological distress (panic attacks, anxiety, paranoid ideation) rather
than somatic complaints. However, when somatic effects prompted emergency
presentation, the most frequent complaints were severe nausea and vomiting,
chest pain or palpitations, and symptoms consistent with toxic mushroom
poisoning in cases of misidentification.

The case study noted that the management of acute mushroom intoxication
was primarily supportive. For psilocybin-containing mushrooms, treatment
focused on reassurance, monitoring of vital signs, and management of
agitation or anxiety with benzodiazepines when necessary. For cases of
toxic mushroom misidentification, more intensive medical intervention
including gastrointestinal decontamination and monitoring for hepatic
toxicity might be required.

## Risk Assessment Context

The EMCDDA placed the somatic health risks of psilocybin mushrooms in the
context of overall drug harm assessment. While the acute toxicity of
psilocybin was considered low compared to many other recreational drugs,
the unpredictable potency of [[cotter-mushroom-products-and-value-added-applications]] the risk of
misidentification represented genuine health concerns that justified
public health monitoring and risk reduction efforts. The case study
concluded that accurate information about product identification, dosing,
and potential somatic effects was essential for reducing harm.
