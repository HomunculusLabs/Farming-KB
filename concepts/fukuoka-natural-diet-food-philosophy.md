# Natural Diet and Food Philosophy

Fukuoka's food philosophy is inseparable from his farming philosophy. He argues
that agriculture arose from human cravings over food, and that farming methods
have had to adapt to changes in the human diet. Unless the diet is basically
sound, agriculture too cannot be normal. The failure of modern agriculture has
its roots in abnormal dietary practices and the low level of basic awareness
people have regarding diet.

## The Diet of Non-Discrimination

Fukuoka's thinking on natural diet parallels his thinking on natural farming.
Natural farming consists of adapting to true nature — nature understood with
non-discriminating knowledge. In the same way, a true natural diet is a way of
eating where one feeds randomly with an undiscriminating attitude on food taken
from the wild, crops grown by natural farming, and fish and shellfish caught
using natural methods.

One must abandon an artificial diet designed on the basis of discriminating
scientific knowledge and, gradually liberating oneself from philosophical
constraints, ultimately deny and transcend these. Natural diet and natural
farming are not separate ideas, but united intimately as one whole. They are one
too with natural fishing and animal husbandry. Man's food, clothing, shelter,
and spiritual existence must all be blended with nature in perfect harmony.

## Man Feeds with His Head

Animals are born with an instinctive ability to distinguish between what they can
and cannot eat, and partake fully of nature's plentiful stores. In man, however,
the stage of infancy during which he feeds instinctively is short. Once he
becomes familiar with his surroundings, he makes judgments and feeds selectively
according to impulse and fancy. Man is an animal that feeds with his head rather
than his mouth.

Scientifically, foods are characterized as sweet, sour, bitter, hot, tasty,
nutritious. But what is sweet is not always sweet, nor tasty always tasty. Man's
sense of taste and values change constantly with time and circumstances. When
full, the most delectable food is unpalatable; when hungry, the most awful-tasting
food is delicious. Nothing tastes good to a sick man and nothing is nutritious to
one who is not healthy.

The food that animals eat by instinct constitutes a complete diet, but man, with
his reliance on discriminating knowledge, has lost sight of what a complete diet
is. His efforts to analyze food — calling this a nutrient, that a calorie, and
trying to combine everything into a complete diet — lead only to fragmentation
and greater contradiction.

## Eating with the Seasons

Plants and animals live in accordance with the seasons, and Fukuoka advocates a
diet that follows this natural rhythm. The food that farmers and fishermen have
taken locally for thousands of years is a splendid example of natural diet in
accordance with the laws of nature.

The seven herbs of spring — Japanese parsley, shepherd's purse, cudweed,
chickweed, bee nettle, wild turnip, and wild radish — emerge early in the new
year from the slumbering earth. As the farmer enjoys these flavors, nature
provides shellfish as a complementary food. Spring brings bamboo shoots with
rockfish, red sea bream at barley harvest time, Spanish mackerel sashimi.

Each season offers its foods at precisely the right time. All fruits ripen at the
right time of year, and that is when they are most delicious and most needed by
the body. Summer provides melon, milk, and honey in the shade. Autumn brings
cereal grains, soybeans, chestnuts, and matsutake mushrooms. The ripened grains
of rice, having fully absorbed the yang of summer, provide calories for winter.
Barley, slightly more yin than rice, suits the palate as appetite lags under
summer heat.

## Food Color and Flavor as Indicators

Fukuoka arranges foods according to the colors of the four alternating seasons.
Summer is hot and yang (red and orange), spring is represented by brown and
yellow, fall by green and blue, and winter by indigo and purple. The diet
maintains a balance between yin and yang and the color arrangement is harmonic:
in summer (yang) one should eat yin foods, and in winter (yin) one should eat
yang foods.

Foods are represented by different colors: vegetables are green, seaweed is blue,
cereal grains are yellow, and meat is red. Meat is yang and vegetables yin, with
grains in between. Because man is an omnivorous animal that is yang, the
principle says that when grains — which are intermediate — are eaten as the
staple, yin vegetables should be consumed and meat should be avoided.

However, too much concern over whether something is yin or yang, acidic or
alkaline, leads right back into the realm of science and discriminating knowledge.
Those who have succeeded in fully renouncing the self are able to receive the
full blessings of nature without such calculations.

## Critique of Modern Food Quality

Fukuoka is sharply critical of the decline in modern food quality. He describes
how livestock farming is no longer a form of agriculture practiced in nature:
unfertilized battery chickens are machines for hatching factory-made eggs, while
hogs and cows are factory-produced meat and milk-fabricating machines. These
products could not possibly be wholesome.

Food today is thought of less as something that supports life than as something
to please the human palate and titillate the senses. The food industry has
produced endless varieties of canned, bottled, frozen, and instant foods packed
with additives for tickling the palate. This "instant" food has deviated far from
its original goal of nourishment.

The campaign for "tasty rice" illustrates the problem. Fukuoka argues that the
difference in taste between varieties is very small, and that tasty rice cannot
always be produced from a tasty variety. Depending on land, method of cultivation,
and weather, poor-tasting varieties may approach tasty ones in flavor, while tasty
rice, when hit by bad weather, is often less palatable. The chances of rice
retaining its inherent properties from field to table are less than one in several
hundred. The campaign for tasty rice has placed a great burden on the farmer and
forced the housewife to buy high-priced rice. The only one likely to benefit is
the merchant.

## Energy-Wasteful Agriculture

Natural farming produces 200,000 kilocalories of energy per man-day of labor
without outside inputs — about 100 times the daily intake of a farmer on a
natural diet. Ten times as much energy was expended in traditional farming with
horses and oxen, doubled again with small-scale mechanization, and doubled yet
again with large-scale mechanization. This geometric progression has produced the
energy-intensive, wasteful agricultural methods of today.

## The Nature of Food and the Nature of the Person

Fukuoka's deepest insight connects the nature of food to the nature of the person
eating it. Tastiness exists in the minds of people who believe something to taste
good. The absence of delicacies does not prevent the feeling of deliciousness from
arising. People who do not consume delicacies may not experience ecstasy at the
dinner table as often, but this does not mean they are unhappy. Quite the contrary.

The Japanese diet has undergone rapid development, but has this really been for
the good? The deterioration of food quality mirrors the deterioration of the
relationship between man and nature. A complete natural diet arises of itself
without man needing to know anything — as long as he accepts and lives in
accordance with the natural order.
