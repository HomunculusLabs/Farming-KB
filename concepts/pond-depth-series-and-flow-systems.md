The arrangement of ponds in series, their depth, and the flow of water between them profoundly affect total aquaculture productivity. Depth determines which species can be supported and what biological processes occur. Flow configuration determines whether ponds function as isolated units, parallel systems sharing a water source, or cascading series where the output of one pond feeds the next. Understanding these design variables allows the aquaculturist to create systems that maximise yield while minimising external inputs.

## Orders of Depth

Depth has its own orders and effects on pond productivity, operating quite differently from surface area or volume. Mere films of water or wet mud patches suffice to grow most productive green salad and root crops, allow bees to safely drink, revive frogs, and breed a few mosquitoes. Soil at field capacity is effectively a pond in hiding, supporting vast underground biological activity that connects to surface water systems.

From two to six centimetres depth, smaller floating plants take root and form mats, while scuds and small crustaceans flourish in the warm, well-oxygenated shallow water. From ten to 100 centimetres, true floating and anchored plants can separate out their niches, and almost any fish species covered by the water can be supported. Even in rich algal ponds, some light will penetrate to this depth, supporting photosynthesis throughout the water column.

The maximum commonly used depth for fish culture is two metres. At this depth, no unstable thermoclines develop to create sudden overturns of oxygen and temperature. The ponds are easily wind-aerated and yet deep enough to buffer air temperature changes, providing a stable environment for fish growth. Depths below two metres are used for specific purposes: deeper water provides refuge from high surface temperatures in summer and from ice in winter.

From two to fifteen metres, lakes of clear water are potentially productive with well-defined thermal stratification. Below fifteen metres, less biological cycling takes place, and still less in the cold, deep, sometimes anaerobic depths of V-shaped lakes with leaf-fall on the margins. Depths of four to five metres serve a limited use as fish refuges from temperature extremes. Caves under water serve the same purpose, and it is common to find trout sheltering in drainage pipes under dam walls on hot clear days.

Depth and plants together serve as a set of breeding refuges for the food organisms of omnivores and carnivores. The configuration of levels at different depths may have a profound effect on the total yield or availability of such food organisms. Shallow shelves with emergent vegetation produce scuds, amphipods, and insect larvae that feed small fish. Deeper zones support pelagic plankton and open-water forage species. The interplay between depth zones creates the habitat diversity that productive polycultures require.

Thermoclines may develop above fifteen metres depth and are fairly typical of lakes subject to sudden seasonal temperature changes. Overturns typically occur in autumn and spring. While in summer the surface waters remain consistently warmer than those at depth, in winter surface ice may form, then warmer water is found at depth, and finally ice forms deep in the lake where very cold streams pool up and freeze. Fish may live in a sort of icy sandwich, or tucked away in modest caves or hollows in shallower water.

## Ponds in Series and Flow Configurations

### Isolated Ponds

Isolated ponds are familiar to every aquarium keeper. New stock, diseased stock, trial polycultures, and insurance against general loss may all dictate a set of still-water ponds in quite separate situations not sharing any flow of water. A set of tanks above grade, or still-ponds filled one at a time, can be effectively isolated. Even if treated identically, isolated ponds will develop differently, reflecting the sensitivity of water systems to small initial differences that amplify over time. Water systems are more connected within themselves and convey small differences more rapidly than land systems.

### Parallel Systems

Ponds in parallel are perhaps the most common fish-culture arrangement, analogous to irrigation bays on land. They possess a head canal for inflow, individual flow-through to each pond, and a tail canal for drainage that also handles surplus water during rains. They are effectively isolated unless a disease, pollutant, or qualitative change occurs in the common water supply, when all may fail together unless flow regimes are staggered so that some flow while others are still.

The parallel series is ideal for valley floor ponds where water at head has been diverted along contour, for single feeder pipes, and for narrow tidal benches. The basic disadvantage is that one pond cannot feed another — a controlled trophic ladder cannot be established where food is cultured in one pond and allowed to flow down to higher trophic levels in the next.

### Consecutive Series

Consecutive series can be arranged in trophic levels, with orders of ponds breeding forage fish or shrimp spilling over to ponds containing carnivorous fish. Marshy ponds of tadpoles, scuds, and Daphnia can flow into ponds of omnivores, creating a self-feeding cascade that requires no external food inputs. Primary sewage lagoons (anaerobic) can feed secondary lagoons rich in insect fauna and zooplankton (facultative ponds), which in turn cascade into aerobic ponds of useful fish, mussels, shrimp, or green vegetable crop.

Small ponds about one-seventh or one-ninth the size of the next deal with the anaerobic phase and are best constructed as tunnels or canals from which methane gas can be collected, adding an energy yield to the system. A very small hatchery can supply a larger fry pool, which in turn supplies a fingerling tank, which provides stock for large ponds. The orders of size here are more like one-tenth or one-twentieth of the next, and their aeration, structure, and construction will differ accordingly. Eels and trout are reared in such increasing size sequences.

Consecutive series has an essential function in terms of size orders, but may make little sense for a set of equal-sized ponds of the same function. Consecutive series may also be forced by valley configuration or other site limitations, and then the risks of change in water quality compel close monitoring of each pond in the chain.

### Evolutionary Pond Systems

Every old fish culture system or terrace complex has an intricate set of ponds and flows. Elements are added as needed, as money and time permit, as new information and needs arise, or as new species are incorporated. Although complex, these systems work well and are comfortable to work with. Successful modifications are preserved, mistakes rectified, and catastrophes remedied. However, many such systems are never intended for easy reading, and a novice inheriting one might spend weeks working out how to control and manage the system. All are quite unique, and often subtle in operation, with complex water control.

In contrast, the flatland rice farmer who converts to catfish may evolve a simple, standardised, rectilinear farm visible from an airliner as a network of precise regularity imposed on the landscape. Anyone can understand it and the system is probably easy to control, but it costs in food inputs and tends toward monocultural simplicity that sacrifices the resilience and productivity of diverse polyculture systems.

## Design Considerations

factors including climate, water availability, and local
regulations. Integration with existing water management
systems maximizes resource efficiency and output.
