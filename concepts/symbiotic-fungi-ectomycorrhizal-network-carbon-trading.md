## Symbiotic Fungi — Ectomycorrhizal Network Carbon Trading

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) and [[dighton-mycorrhizal-networks-carbon-transfer]] (CMNs) represent one of the most significant but cryptic components of terrestrial ecosystem functioning, mediating resource exchange that shapes plant communities and [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], but the same carbon-for-nutrients exchange principle applies to [[mycelial-network-structure]] parameters include:
- **Hyphal density:** 2.9-6.8 mm per mm squared (varies by host plant)
- **Branching:** 8.6-9.7 per cm of hypha
- **Anastomosis frequency:** 75-78% of hyphal contacts
- **Viability:** networks show 100% viability after 7 days of growth

## Carbon Allocation Dynamics

### Tracking Carbon with Stable Isotopes

13C-labelled [[plantago-lanceolata]]* appeared in extraradical AM fungal hyphae within a few days and remained largely there for at least 30 days
- Higher 13C enrichment in lipids of intraradical mycelia than in plant root lipids shows that lipid metabolism is more active in the intraradical mycelia
- 13C enrichment showed a gradual release of carbon from roots to [[lowenfels-rhizosphere-bacteria-plant-interaction]], but at a much lower rate than direct transfer to AM fungi

Timing is critical in labelling experiments because carbon is continually respired. A large proportion of AM fungal carbon is contained in lipids in vesicles inside plant roots and in spores on the external mycelium, where they accumulate and may be stored for long periods.

### Carbon Transfer Between Plants

Carbon transfer between plants linked by CMNs has been demonstrated using stable carbon isotopes. Studies with C3 and C4 plants linked by CMNs have quantified interplant carbon movement. The CMNs thus serve as conduits for both carbon and nutrient movement between plants, affecting the carbon economy of entire plant communities.

## Nitrogen Transfer Through CMNs

Nitrogen movement from one plant (donor) to another (receiver) through mycorrhizal hyphae is of fundamental importance in both agricultural and natural ecosystems. This transfer is not restricted to mass flow and diffusion through soil pathways.

### Transfer Patterns

- **Unidirectional (one-way):** N transfer from N2-fixing mycorrhizal plants to non-N2-fixing mycorrhizal plants via CMNs (Newman 1988; He et al. 2003)
- **Bidirectional (two-way):** N transfer in both directions between N2-fixing and non-N2-fixing plants (Johansen and Jensen 1996; He et al. 2004, 2005)

The N2-fixing plant is not always the N-donor. Net N gains through CMNs in recipient plants may play beneficial roles in affecting their fitness.

### Quantification Methods

N transfer has been quantified in three ways:
- **%N transfer** — proportion of total enriched N found in receiver
- **Amount of N transferred** — mg per plant
- **%NDFT** — percentage of receiver nitrogen derived from transfer

15N enrichment methods use 15N-enriched inorganic chemicals or organic compounds (up to 99.90 atom % 15N) applied to donor plant tissues, growth media, or soils. Experimental designs use four reciprocal pairings to distinguish N transfer between soil and mycorrhizal pathways.

### Experimental Design

To distinguish mycorrhizal-mediated transfer from soil-pathway transfer:
- Root systems are separated by 25-37 micron nylon or metal mesh allowing only hyphal connections
- Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) hydrophobic membranes have also been used
