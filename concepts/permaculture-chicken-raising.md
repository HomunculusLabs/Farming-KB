# Permaculture Chicken Raising

updated: 2026-04-18

Chickens are one of the most productive livestock animals in [[permaculture-design]], providing meat, eggs, feathers, fertilizer, pest control, and weed control. The goal is to feed chickens from [[permaculture-zone-planning]] and profit from them in [[permaculture-zone-planning]].

## Placement and Housing

Chickens live in a coop on the edge of Zone 1, with an attached pen running along the border of the Zone 1 gardens and Zone 2 orchard. The pen should have:
- A second pen for chicks, surrounded by spiny shrubs for hawk protection
- Mulched ground with straw, corn stalks, sawdust, or bark
- Wire mesh around trees holding mulch in
- Fruit trees, grains, corn, sunflowers, and greens planted in the run
- Divided into several pens planted in succession for rotation
- Logs on the ground that can be flipped to reveal pill bugs and worms
- Fencing at least five feet high

Chickens cannot be let into mulched gardens (Zones 1-3) as they destroy mulch. They can forage in unmulched orchards: only bantams or very small chickens when the orchard is young, up to 50-100 chickens per acre in mature orchards or pasture.

### Chicken Tractor
For urban dwellers, chickens can live in a chicken tractor or ark -- a small movable house (3-8 chickens) with a pen about twice the coop size. Each chicken needs 3 square feet total. The floor is open so birds can eat and manure falls to the ground. Common designs include A-frame and rectangular models.

### Coop Design
- Minimum 3 square feet per chicken; 7x7 feet manages 16 chickens
- Set on stilts so manure drops through slats into a swale or wheelbarrow
- Roosts: long poles 18 inches from wall, low enough to fly up to
- Nesting boxes: 12x12 inches, one box per two hens, can be attached outside with lid access
- Chicken door: 12 inches high in the peak of the roof (deters predators, provides ventilation)
- Ladder to door: 5 feet wide with rungs 3 feet apart (difficult for foxes)

## Forage Plants

Plant these in chicken pens and pastures: alfalfa, amaranth, autumn olive, barley, buckwheat, chickweed, chicory, clover, comfrey, corn, cucumber, currant, dandelion, elderberry, fava beans, fennel, fruit and nut trees, honey locust, oak, oats, quinoa, Russian olive, rye, Siberian pea shrub, stinging nettle, sunflower, Swiss chard, vetch, wheat.

## Health

- Disinfect coop before introducing new chickens
- Clean bedding weekly, full disinfection yearly
- Fresh water always available
- Separate old and young birds
- Crushed shells daily for minerals
- Grit supply prevents worms and parasites
- Adequate space and nutrition prevents cannibalism
- Vitamin D needed; cod liver oil in northern winters

## Breeds

Two categories: light and heavy.
- Light breeds: fly short distances, aren't good mothers, less hardy, but excellent foragers that rarely need supplementary food
- Heavy breeds: don't fly, better mothers, lay brown eggs, hardier, lay longer in season, but not as good at foraging

Orpington is recommended for beginners -- fairly laid back, easy to handle, good dual-purpose breed.

## Egg Production

Chickens produce about one egg per day (300 per year). They molt once a year (usually fall) and stop laying. Molting is triggered by shorter days; a light on a timer maintains production. A chicken stops laying when old -- comb, vents, and wattle become shrunken and pale.

## Breeding

Keep a rooster (not possible in the city). Heritage breeds are more likely to get broody. To encourage broodiness:
- Build a private wooden nest (15x15x15 inches with roof)
- Use a dummy egg to trick the hen
- Don't disturb the nest; incubation takes 21 days
- After hatching, move chicks to a brooder and slip them back under the hen the first night
- Keep the family away from other chickens

### Brooder
A box with a red heat lamp and thermometer. Start at 95F, decrease 5F per week until 70F at 6 weeks. Use burlap/cloth rags for bedding the first week, then shredded newspaper or wood shavings. Chicks need 1 gallon water per 50 chicks, and small grit sprinkled on feed. Transfer to outside chick house at 4 weeks, remove the box at 6 weeks, integrate with flock at 10-12 weeks.
