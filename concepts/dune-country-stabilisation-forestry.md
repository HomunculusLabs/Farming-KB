### Pitting

Wherever rain flows or falls occasionally over bare sandy ground, pitting is an excellent device. Large discs with one-quarter section cut off (or a small disc fixed to a wheel) are drawn cross-country, and seed and fertiliser spread soon after. Seed, fertiliser, and sand blow into the pits, which respond very well to subsequent rain. Many hectares of these pits have grown well near Alice Springs in Central Australia.

### Disc Specifications

Machine blades for pitting use trailed discs cut to create pits. Discs can have 1/4, 1/2, or 3/4 of their perimeter removed, creating varying pit sizes for different conditions.

## Stabilisation Techniques

### Brush Fences

Severe dune blowouts on coasts need intensive treatment:
- Hand planting of quickset grasses such as **Ammophila spp.**
- Pelleted moth bean seed
- **Brush fences in 7-metre squares** for early stabilisation

**Fence construction:**
- Stakes 2 metres long, driven 1 metre deep
- Numerous bushy weaves between stakes
- Must have wind gaps of less than 50% to drop sand out
- Firm posts driven every 3-4 metres
- Combined with planting sequences for permanence

### Performance Data

Brush fences provide measurable benefits:
- **Temperature reduction**: 12°C or more in the shade of fences
- **Moisture retention**: 1.8-3.0% less moisture loss than open areas

### Chinese Approach

In China, rice-straw mats are used as fencing or surface stabilisers, and advanced trees in baskets are planted (basket and all) in the matting shelter. These pioneers give shelter and mulch for later forestry and crop establishment.

### Chemical and Physical Stabilisation

Water creates bonds between sand particles that require much higher wind speeds to move sand. Salt (especially where dew is present, as salt is hygroscopic or water-absorbing) has the same effect. Other approaches include:
- Tars, oils, or glues such as latex
- Lichen, bacterial, fungal, and algal mats that form naturally
- Salt crusts that prevent dune formation in some deserts

These delicate desert crusts are critical to stability. When hoofed animals, fast vehicles, or agriculture are introduced, wind erosion may quickly follow.

## Tree Planting Depth

Lethal soil temperatures are rarely formed below 15 centimetres in dunes. Careful placement of trees in holes this deep or deeper, stabilised from collapse by a woven basket or cardboard collar, prevents the new seedling from being "cooked" until it can cast its own shade.

### Fertiliser Placement

Fertiliser should be thinly placed at planting time, as rapid water infiltration in rains can carry it below root reach. After the tree has spread a root web, more fertiliser can be added and will be taken up by the plant. Any plants pioneering the dune must be able to stand stem burial until the area is stabilised.

## Nutrient Requirements

All plants in dunes benefit from nitrogenous fertiliser, phosphate, and trace elements. Old dunes are usually deficient in plant nutrients. Mill wastes (bark, sawdust) placed in hollows are very beneficial and have helped establish Acacia sophorae forests in coastal dunes in Tasmania.

## Long-term Protection

Once stabilised, dunes must be protected from:
- Recreation vehicles (devastation)
- Heavy browsing
- Badly aligned roads
- Sand mining to windward

Failure to protect stabilised dunes results in rapid reactivation and loss of the established system.
