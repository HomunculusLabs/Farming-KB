# Fungal Diversity and Fire Ecology

Fire plays a profound role in shaping fungal communities. Phoenicoid fungi are a specialized group that fruit exclusively on heat-treated substrata, and fire-mediated changes in soil chemistry, vegetation, and microclimate dramatically alter fungal assemblages.

[[fire-associated-fungi]] [[the-soil-food-web]] [[fungal-communities-extreme-environments]]

## Phoenicoid Fungi

Phoenicoid fungi are primarily ascomycetes and basidiomycetes that fruit on soil, plant material, burned wood following a forest fire, prairie fire, or fireplace fire, and on steam-sterilized soil in greenhouses. The terms pyrophilous, anthracophilous, and carbonicolous also refer to fungi that fruit on heat-treated substrata.

Although a burned habitat is not a stressed environment in the classical sense, forest fires usually lead to a 3-5 unit increase in pH because of ash deposition. The plant community and depth of burn play major roles in determining which fungi will appear following fire.

Heterothallic Neurospora species are common on charred vegetation in moist tropics and subtropics. In temperate forests, operculate discomycetes (Pezizales) and a few agarics dominate. In prairie ecosystems, pyrenomycetes are the characteristic post-fire fungi.

## Successional Timing After Fire

Dix and Webster (1995) assigned phoenicoid fungi from forested sites to four groups based on timing of fruiting following fire. Fruiting times vary depending on when a burn occurs and the duration of favorable conditions.

Group 1 species appear as early as 7 weeks but no later than 80 weeks after a burn, including Pyronema spp. and Trichophacea abundans. Group 2 includes a heterogeneous assemblage beginning to fruit 10-15 weeks after a burn, continuing up to 190 weeks, including Ascobolous carbonarius, Peziza trachycarpa, and Rhizinia undulata.

Group 3 species appear 20-50 weeks after fire and continue for 130-200 weeks, including Peziza endocarpoides and Trichophaea hemisphaerioides. Group 4 taxa do not generally appear until 50 weeks after a burn and may continue for 150 weeks, including Myxomphalia maura and Neotiella hetieri.

## Volcanic Eruptions and Fire

Volcanic eruptions with subsequent deposition of hot ash also lead to abundant fruiting of phoenicoid fungi. Six weeks after the eruption of Mount St. Helens in Washington State, apothecia of Anthracobia melaloma and perithecia of Gelasinospora reticulospora appeared within the tephra.

One year after the eruption, large discomycetes (Peziza species, Rhizinia undulata, Trichophaea hemisphaerioides) and agarics (Coprinus plicatilis, Pholiota carbonaria, Psathyrella carbonicola, Schizophyllum commune) were common.

## Mechanisms of Fire Response

Soil heating may stimulate some species to fruit, whereas other fungi may respond to changes in soil pH following fire. Sporocarp production by several phoenicoid fungi in undisturbed forest soils can be stimulated by application of lime, mimicking the pH increase caused by ash deposition.

The factors accounting for the presence of phoenicoid fungi in a particular location may vary even among species occupying similar habitats. This complexity makes generalizations about fire-fungal interactions difficult.

## Sampling Fire-Adapted Fungi

When designing sampling schedules for phoenicoid fungi, it is important to know how long after a burn different species fruit and how long they will continue to produce sporocarps. The initiation and duration of fruiting vary depending on the time of year a burn occurs and the duration of favorable conditions.

Repeated visits to burned sites over multiple years are necessary to capture the full complement of fire-associated species, as the four successional groups span up to 190 weeks.

## Fire in Grassland Ecosystems

In prairie ecosystems, pyrenomycetes dominate the post-fire fungal community, reflecting the different vegetation structure and fire regime compared to forests. Wicklow (1973, 1975) documented characteristic assemblages of phoenicoid fungi in prairie fire regimes.

Grassland fires tend to be more frequent and surface-level than forest fires, selecting for fungi adapted to rapid colonization of charred herbaceous material.

## Ecological Implications

Fire creates unique ecological opportunities for fungi by eliminating competitors, releasing nutrients, modifying substrate chemistry, and creating bare soil surfaces for colonization. The predictable response of phoenicoid fungi to fire suggests co-evolutionary relationships between fire regimes and fungal life histories.

Changes in fire frequency and intensity due to land management or climate change may therefore have significant consequences for fungal community composition and diversity.

## Soil Heating Mechanisms

The mechanisms triggering phoenicoid fungal fruiting are not fully understood. Soil heating during fire may directly stimulate dormant fungal propagules, break chemical inhibitors, or modify substrate chemistry to favor germination. The observation that lime application can stimulate fruiting of some phoenicoid species in undisturbed forest soils supports the pH-change hypothesis.

The depth of burn plays a major role in determining post-fire fungal communities. Surface fires and ground fires produce different heating patterns and therefore select for different fungal assemblages.

## Fire Management and Fungal Conservation

Prescribed burning practices affect fungal communities differently depending on fire intensity, frequency, and season. Low-intensity surface fires that preserve the organic soil horizon maintain much of the soil fungal community, while intense crown fires that consume the organic layer can devastate soil fungi for years. The recovery of post-fire fungal communities has implications for forest regeneration — mycorrhizal fungi are essential for tree seedling establishment, and their loss after severe fire can delay or alter successional trajectories. In regions where fire suppression has been the norm, reintroducing prescribed fire may actually benefit fungal diversity by creating the disturbed habitats that phoenicoid and early-successional species require. Fire managers are increasingly considering fungal community responses in burn planning, recognizing that [[fungal-community-assembly]] processes are integral to post-fire ecosystem recovery.

## Fungi as Fire Indicators

Certain fungal species serve as reliable indicators of fire history and severity. The presence and abundance of phoenicoid fungi can reveal the timing and intensity of historical fires, even decades after the event. Pyrophilous fungi such as Pyronema omphalodes and Geopyxis carbonaria are among the first organisms to colonize recently burned soil, making them useful bioindicators for monitoring post-fire ecosystem recovery. Their fruiting patterns provide a temporal record of fire disturbance that complements tree-ring and charcoal analysis in reconstructing fire regimes.
