## Fungal Diversity and Fire Ecology Fire plays a profound role in shaping fungal communities. Phoenicoid fungi are a specialized group that fruit exclusively on heat-treated substrata, and fire-mediated changes in soil chemistry, vegetation, and microclimate dramatically alter fungal assemblages. [[prairie-ecology|prairie ecosystems]], pyrenomycetes are the characteristic post-fire fungi.

## Successional Timing After Fire

Dix and Webster (1995) assigned phoenicoid fungi from forested sites to four groups based on timing of fruiting following fire. Fruiting times vary depending on when a burn occurs and the duration of favorable conditions.

Group 1 species appear as early as 7 weeks but no later than 80 weeks after a burn, including Pyronema spp. and Trichophacea abundans. Group 2 includes a heterogeneous assemblage beginning to fruit 10-15 weeks after a burn, continuing up to 190 weeks, including Ascobolous carbonarius, Peziza trachycarpa, and Rhizinia undulata.

Group 3 species appear 20-50 weeks after fire and continue for 130-200 weeks, including Peziza endocarpoides and Trichophaea hemisphaerioides. Group 4 taxa do not generally appear until 50 weeks after a burn and may continue for 150 weeks, including Myxomphalia maura and Neotiella hetieri.

## Volcanic Eruptions and Fire

Volcanic eruptions with subsequent deposition of hot ash also lead to abundant fruiting of phoenicoid fungi. Six weeks after the eruption of Mount St. Helens in Washington State, apothecia of Anthracobia melaloma and perithecia of Gelasinospora reticulospora appeared within the tephra.

One year after the eruption, large discomycetes (Peziza species, Rhizinia undulata, Trichophaea hemisphaerioides) and agarics (Coprinus plicatilis, Pholiota carbonaria, Psathyrella carbonicola, Schizophyllum commune) were common.

## Sampling Fire-Adapted Fungi

When designing sampling schedules for phoenicoid fungi, it is important to know how long after a burn different species fruit and how long they will continue to produce sporocarps. The initiation and duration of fruiting vary depending on the time of year a burn occurs and the duration of favorable conditions.

Repeated visits to burned sites over multiple years are necessary to capture the full complement of fire-associated species, as the four successional groups span up to 190 weeks.

The depth of burn plays a major role in determining post-fire fungal communities. Surface fires and ground fires produce different heating patterns and therefore select for different fungal assemblages.
