# Precipitation Types — Dew, Fog, and Condensation

Precipitation encompasses far more than rainfall alone. In [[permaculture-design]], understanding the full spectrum of water inputs — including condensation, dew, and fog — is critical for site planning, especially in arid and semi-arid regions where these supplementary sources can make the difference between a productive system and a failed one. Water promises to be the main limiting factor for survival and growth, and the major future expense of food gardens and agriculture.

## Two Basic Precipitation Inputs

There are two fundamental inputs to precipitation on any site:

1. **Water falling from clouds** — rainfall, snow, and hail descending from the atmosphere.
2. **Condensation** — water condensed or trapped from sometimes clear air or fogs by cool surfaces.

While rainfall is the only reliable and widespread measure we possess, condensation from fog and dew can be of critical importance on seaward slopes and at higher altitudes, particularly in cloud forests. The only reliable and widespread measures we possess are of "rainfall" — world rainfall averages about 86 cm (34 inches).

## Rainfall Thresholds and Aridity

- **Semi-arid:** 50 cm (20 inches) or less mean annual rainfall
- **Arid / desert:** 25 cm (10 inches) or less mean annual rainfall

However, seasonal or relative aridity can occur in any climate due to long-term cycles, jetstream fluctuations, or oceanic current changes. Local deforestation on a broad or even local scale can also reduce precipitation. People live and garden in average annual rainfalls of 10 cm (4 inches) or less, managing to both exist and produce crops. Exotic water enters dry regions as rivers and underground aquifers, enabling a great variety of local coping strategies.

## Distribution Over Averages

Rainfall averages are at best a very general guide to precipitation effects in the garden or orchard. A much safer strategy is to ensure that both the species chosen and water strategies developed yield some production even in "drier than usual" conditions. After all, a fish population out of water for an hour is as dead as if year-long drought were in effect.

Far more useful than averages is the **expected distribution of rainfall** (including extremes such as 100-year flood records) and data on the **intensity of rains**. These factors are limiting influences on the size of road culverts, dam spillways, and the storage capacity needed to see us over dry periods. Flooding histories of sites and districts indicate the real limits to the placement of plant systems, fences, and buildings — attention to flood records avoids future costs and disappointment. If flood data is omitted, life itself can be at risk in intense periods of rain.

## Light-Precipitation Balance

As precipitation rises, available light decreases. In extremely cloudy industrial or fog-bound humid climates, light becomes the limiting factor for some plants to ripen or even flower. At the dry end of the rainfall spectrum (around 50 cm or 20 inches mean rainfall), sun is plentiful and evaporation in excess of precipitation becomes the limiting factor. That factor determines arid-land storage strategies, just as the depth of seasonally frozen soils determines water reticulation strategies in cold climates.

## Types of Rainfall

Rainfall is classified by the process causing it:

- **Orographic:** The cooling of air as it rises over mountains or hills, producing rainfall on windward slopes and rain shadows on leeward sides.
- **Cyclonic / Frontal:** The overriding of cool and warm air masses from the polar circulation system, producing the characteristic warm and cold-front rains of mid-latitudes.
- **Convective:** Columns of hot air rising from deserts or oceans into cooler air aloft, responsible for the mosaic of rainfall patterns across desert regions.

Cloud bands bring rains on the east-northeast sides of high pressure cells, and on the south side of low pressure cells. Earth spin produces a drag effect, and this pattern affects climate to approximately 25° latitude, when cyclones feed the system.

## Dew

Dew is a common result of clear nights, rapid radiation loss, and a moist air mass over coasts and hills. It occurs more frequently in clear-sky deserts than in cloudy areas, which is counterintuitive but important for dryland design. A slight wind speed of 1–5 km/h assists the quantity deposited, while both still air and strong winds reduce dewfall. Intensity of deposition is greatest 3–100 cm above ground level — the highest deposition occurs over areas of dry ground (which chills less quickly at the surface), while lower deposition occurs over wet earth.

### Dew vs. Guttation vs. Distillation

Not to be confused with dew (a radiation heat loss effect from earth with clear night skies) is the moisture found on leaves above warm damp ground on cloudy nights. This is either:

- **Guttation:** Water exuded from the leaves themselves; droplets cling to the tips of leaves.
- **Distillation:** Rising ground vapour condensing on leaf surfaces; represents no net gain to total precipitation.

Dew, by contrast, clings to the whole leaf area and represents a genuine addition to site moisture.

### Dew Harvesting in Deserts

Only in deserts is the 4–5 cm (1–2 inches) of dew per year of any real significance in precipitation. Dew in deserts can be regarded as an accessory to, rather than a replacement for, trickle irrigation. Practical dew capture strategies include:

- **Stone piles:** Loosely stacked stones less than 1 m high, where low night winds cool rock surfaces and dew accumulates to dampen the ground below. In the Negev desert, each mound of stones may suffice to water one tree.
- **Radiation traps:** Very large structures such as those on Lanzarote in the Canary Islands, where cold air sinks in cinder-covered pits, condenses on cinders, and may grow one grape vine in each hole.
- **Metallic mesh fences:** Erected 1 m (3 feet) high, used as initial condensers in deserts. Shrubs are grown along the fence drip-line, and the fence is moved on after plants are established. In Morocco, such fences are proposed for deforested coastal areas.

The most efficient dew collectors are **free-standing shrubs of about 1–2 m (3–6 feet) in height**. Groups or solid stands of plants and grasses do less well in trapping dew, and this may help explain the discrete spacing of desert plants — perhaps 40% more dew is trapped on scattered shrubs than would be caught in still air or on closed vegetation canopies.

## Fog

Fog forms where warm water or the vapour of warm rain evaporates into cool air, or where cold ground chills an airstream and condenses the moisture. Chang (1968) identifies three types:

1. **Radiation ground fog:** Forms on clear nights when hollows and plateaus cool rapidly. Fog often forms in much the same pattern as winter frosts.
2. **Advection fog:** Where cold offshore currents condense the moisture in warm sea airstreams. These are the coastal and offshore fogs that plague many coasts such as Newfoundland and parts of northwest Europe.
3. **Upslope / orographic fog:** Where warm, humid airstreams are carried up hill slopes and condense as the air cools with altitude.

### Fog Drip Quantities

Unlike dew, fog can provide enormous quantities of moisture. Chang gives figures of 329 cm (128 inches) for [[table-mountain]], South Africa, and 127 cm (50 inches) for Lanai, Hawaii, from fog drip alone. In such areas, even field crops may thrive without irrigation. Typically, bare rock and new soil surfaces on sea-facing slopes are colonised with lichens and mosses, while rainforest develops on richer soils.

Much of New Zealand experiences upslope fog precipitation, and unless burnt or cleared to tussock grasslands, dense forests will develop. The irregular canopy of such forests serves as excellent fog condensers. Even with no visible fog, trees will condense considerable moisture on sea-facing slopes with night winds moving in off warm seas over the land, encountering the cool leaf laminae of forests.

In the very humid air of fog forests, giant trees may accumulate so much moisture that more large limbs fall in still air than in high winds (which tend to snap dry branches rather than living limbs). Almost permanent condensation fogs clothe the tops of high oceanic islands, and hanging mosses and epiphytes rapidly develop there, as they do at the base of waterfalls, for the same reasons — free moisture particles in the air.

## Integration in Permaculture Design

Any strategy that generates, conserves, or stores water is critical to the permaculture design approach. Fog and dew harvesting should be considered as supplementary water sources, particularly in marginal climates, integrated through strategic plant placement, stone mulching, and wind-permeable condenser structures. Species selection should ensure yield even under drier-than-usual conditions, and water storage should be sized for distribution extremes rather than averages.
