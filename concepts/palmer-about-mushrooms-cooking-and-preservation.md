# Cooking and Preservation

## Palmer's Fundamental Cooking Philosophy

Palmer strongly objects to disguising the natural flavor of mushrooms with heavy seasoning. He writes: "Spice, hot herbs, garlic, and kindred condiments are added so freely that the basis might as well be any other edible. The original substance is entirely disguised."

His core principle is that the cook thereby "disguises the very alarm which Nature has placed at the gateway" -- meaning that any acrid or harmful quality that would warn the eater is masked by fat, spice, or compounding with other food. "Let the mushroom absorb fat enough to cover this, or disguise the taste either by spice or by mixing one variety with another, and you may easily eat enough of a poisonous fungus to cause death."

He specifically condemns the practices of mixing mushrooms with gravies, frying in batter, simmering in fat, and seasoning with black pepper or parsley -- all of which destroy the delicate natural flavor and, more dangerously, mask the warning signs of harmful species.

## The Two Proper Cooking Methods

Palmer states: "There are but two ways in which it is proper to cook mushrooms."

### 1. Broiling (the preferred method for most species)
- Use a fine-wire gridiron.
- Sprinkle with salt and (if the species is well known as edible) red pepper.
- Butter as the fire browns them.
- Best for: the elm-tree mushroom, the honey-colored mushroom, and any others tasting raw of grass or trees. These are "only good broiled."

### 2. Stewing in Milk (like an oyster stew)
- Stew exactly as you would make an oyster stew.
- Suitable for: the meadow mushroom, horse-mushroom, and Coprinus -- these are "excellent cooked either way."

## Cooking as a Safety Test

Palmer advances the theory that "no fungus which, unspiced, being plainly cooked with dry heat, commends itself to the taste, can be, in the least, dangerous to human life." When he tests a new species:
- He broils it with the addition of salt and butter (no pepper).
- He eats a small piece on an empty stomach.
- Non-edible fungi typically disclose their character over the charcoal: "nauseous slime weeps from the stem, a grassy and disagreeable odor arises as it heats, or, on being tasted, there is no desire to take another mouthful."

**Important caveat**: This taste test is reliable for Russule, Boleti, and Clavariae, but "utterly worthless, even illusive, when applied to the deadly Amanite," which has no unusual taste even when raw or cooked. The Amanita "passes harmlessly through the stomach mixed with the other food."

## What NOT to Do

- Do NOT boil mushrooms like cabbage for an hour and pour away the water. Palmer sent delicate mushrooms to a leading hotel with proper stewing instructions; the chef "boiled them like cabbage for an hour, poured away the water, then stewed the debris, and sent them in, proudly consoling himself that he had saved a half dozen lives by extracting all the poison." Palmer calls this "a more convincing proof of ignorance in high places cannot be given."
- Do NOT cook mushrooms that are not young and perfectly fresh. Mushrooms in decomposition generate hydrogen gas in quantities sufficient to cause nausea and vomiting.
- Do NOT eat mushrooms that have been forced with potash or other chemicals -- they "cook like pieces of soap" and the liquor "resembled strong lye."

## The Effect of Cooking Heat

- The honey-colored mushroom (Agaricus melleus) is acrid and possibly noxious raw, but heat kills the virus, making it safe and delicious. Paulet reported a dog died from it; yet it "continues to be a favorite article of diet" in Europe.
- The acrid principle in the Lactarii (milk mushrooms) is subdued by heat; in most cases, salt and water also neutralize it, "leaving the substance of the mushroom unhurt and good for food."
- The Russula family's acrid varieties are similarly rendered harmless by cooking.
- The elm-tree mushroom, honey-colored mushroom, and others tasting raw of grass or trees are "only good broiled" -- the dry heat of broiling is essential for palatability.

## The Taste Perception of Mushrooms

Palmer argues that a taste for mushrooms does NOT need to be acquired, contrary to popular belief. He points to children as proof: "We may assume that children have unperverted appetites. With rare exceptions they reject spices, or any food of peculiar flavor." Yet children universally enjoy properly prepared mushrooms.

An infant of eighteen months "manifested great delight at the taste" of fairy-ring champignons. A twenty-one-month-old pointed at elm-tree mushrooms and exclaimed "Fish! Fish!" -- Palmer's friends had always called it "the tree-fish" because of its fish-like aroma when cooking.

## The Nature of Mushrooms as Food

### Flavor and Character
- Mushrooms occupy a place between animal and vegetable food, approximating the former more than the latter.
- Neither their odor when cooking nor the gravy they make resembles any form of vegetable food.
- This is natural, since the plant absorbs oxygen from the air after the manner of animals.
- "No better substitute for meat than fungus can be found."
- The elm-tree mushroom has "a fresh, tonic smell, and is much like fish broiled or fried."

### Nutritional and Digestive Qualities
- "During the season I eat them three times a day; sometimes of a half dozen kinds at a meal. I never enjoy better digestion than during toadstool time."
- They furnish a natural alkali which in some systems is greatly needed.
- Most kinds decay like meat from decomposition and maggots -- further evidence of their animal-like nature.
- Among nations where food is less abundant, the annual mushroom harvest "which requires no care is a great blessing." The Chinese, Russians, Germans, French, and Italians use them very extensively.
- The Lactarii (milk mushrooms) have been prescribed with success in phthisis (tuberculosis), even in advanced stages.

## Drying and Preservation for Winter

### Drying
- Palmer dries his superfluous summer stock for winter use.
- The Chinese make dried fungus a very general article of diet -- Palmer speaks from personal acquaintance with their habits, acquired in San Francisco, Honolulu, and Hong Kong.
- The fairy-ring champignon ([[marasmius-oreades]]) is particularly well suited: "it can be kept any length of time by drying; it expands again the moment it is put in water."

### Canned Mushrooms
- French mushrooms sold in cans are the common variety in the button form and are perfectly safe, though cases of nettle-rash have resulted from their use.
- Delicious Boleti have been imported stamped "Cepes a l'Huile" and "Cepes au naturel" -- "a great addition to any table."

### Oil and Vinegar Preservation
- Italians eat many dangerous fungi with impunity because "when fresh, their properties are changed by sweet-oil; preserved, they are neutralized by pickle."
- Either sweet-oil or vinegar renders harmless the peculiar alkali to which mushrooms owe their noxious qualities.
- However, timing is critical: vinegar applied BEFORE eating (on the plate) can neutralize Amanitine, but vinegar administered AFTER ingestion worsens absorption of the poison.

## Palmer's First-Aid Remedy for Non-Fatal Poisoning

For mushroom poisoning NOT caused by Amanitine (i.e., simple gastrointestinal disturbance from acrid, decomposed, or indigestible species):

- **One wineglass each of whiskey and sweet-oil, mixed** (equal parts).
- The whiskey may be omitted, or vinegar substituted for spirits, but sweet-oil should be taken liberally.
- This remedy should be used "in all cases rather than the use of emetics."
- Palmer was once poisoned by common mushrooms contaminated by decomposing Coprinus deliquescens in the same shipping box; symptoms included "dizziness, nausea, purging, perspiration, with alternate cold spells" -- all within thirty minutes. "Two wineglasses of whiskey and sweet-oil (equal parts) neutralized the poison, and in a few hours I was no worse for the experience."
- He later gave the same remedy to a man who arrived at his office "in a state of hysteria" after four diners at his table all fell ill. "He was given some whiskey and sweet-oil, and told that he had not eaten anything fatally poisonous. The next day he brought in the variety I suspected."

## Mushroom Culture and Commercial Value

- Cultivated mushrooms commanded $1-2 per pound in Palmer's time, owing to the difficulty and uncertainty of cultivation.
- In countries where food is less abundant, the annual mushroom harvest "which requires no care is a great blessing."
- Street sweepings spread loosely over soil will, as they gradually disintegrate over time, produce a fine crop of edible mushrooms -- the spores having been swallowed by horses and passed in their manure, undergoing whatever change is necessary in the intestinal canal to continue reproductive work.
- Palmer's chart of edible and poisonous mushrooms, published with L. Prang and Co., was "the first attempt in America to popularize the fungi" -- condensing matter and illustrations into twelve colored plates enabling any person with no botanical knowledge to "distinguish delicious food from deadly poison."
- One enthusiastic student sent twenty-five copies of the chart to as many different schools -- "an example which in this the day of object-teaching is surely worthy of imitation on the part of those in charge of the education of the young."

## Palmer on Mushrooms and Health

Palmer's personal testimony makes a compelling case for mushrooms as healthful food: "During the season I eat them three times a day; sometimes of a half dozen kinds at a meal. I never enjoy better digestion than during toadstool time." This was not casual indulgence but the result of two decades of careful testing and identification.

He attributes much of the public fear of mushrooms to the fact that newspaper reports of poisoning never identify the species involved. "It would be of great practical value if, whenever possible, this information were given to the public, and fresh samples of the injurious fungus sent to some mycologist for positive identification." Many reported cases of "toadstool poisoning" were likely caused by over-seasoning, decomposition, or improper cooking rather than any inherent poison in the mushroom.

Palmer notes that "there is nothing more healthful for both mind and body than the pursuit of any out-door science" -- mushroom foraging being an ideal combination of physical exercise, botanical study, and culinary reward. He expresses a "personal affection for these little companions of so many otherwise solitary rambles," calling them "curious creatures" surrounded by "a holy mystery."
