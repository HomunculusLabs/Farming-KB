### Canned Mushrooms
- French mushrooms sold in cans are the common variety in the button form and are perfectly safe, though cases of nettle-rash have resulted from their use.
- Delicious Boleti have been imported stamped "Cepes a l'Huile" and "Cepes au naturel" -- "a great addition to any table."

### Oil and Vinegar Preservation
- Italians eat many dangerous fungi with impunity because "when fresh, their properties are changed by sweet-oil; preserved, they are neutralized by pickle."
- Either sweet-oil or vinegar renders harmless the peculiar alkali to which mushrooms owe their noxious qualities.
- However, timing is critical: vinegar applied BEFORE eating (on the plate) can neutralize Amanitine, but vinegar administered AFTER ingestion worsens absorption of the poison.

## Palmer's First-Aid Remedy for Non-Fatal Poisoning

For mushroom poisoning NOT caused by Amanitine (i.e., simple gastrointestinal disturbance from acrid, decomposed, or indigestible species):

- **One wineglass each of whiskey and sweet-oil, mixed** (equal parts).
- The whiskey may be omitted, or vinegar substituted for spirits, but sweet-oil should be taken liberally.
- This remedy should be used "in all cases rather than the use of emetics."
- Palmer was once poisoned by common mushrooms contaminated by decomposing Coprinus deliquescens in the same shipping box; symptoms included "dizziness, nausea, purging, perspiration, with alternate cold spells" -- all within thirty minutes. "Two wineglasses of whiskey and sweet-oil (equal parts) neutralized the poison, and in a few hours I was no worse for the experience."
- He later gave the same remedy to a man who arrived at his office "in a state of hysteria" after four diners at his table all fell ill. "He was given some whiskey and sweet-oil, and told that he had not eaten anything fatally poisonous. The next day he brought in the variety I suspected."

## Mushroom Culture and Commercial Value

- Cultivated mushrooms commanded $1-2 per pound in Palmer's time, owing to the difficulty and uncertainty of cultivation.
- In countries where food is less abundant, the annual mushroom harvest "which requires no care is a great blessing."
- Street sweepings spread loosely over soil will, as they gradually disintegrate over time, produce a fine crop of edible mushrooms -- the spores having been swallowed by horses and passed in their manure, undergoing whatever change is necessary in the intestinal canal to continue reproductive work.
- Palmer's chart of edible and poisonous mushrooms, published with L. Prang and Co., was "the first attempt in America to popularize the fungi" -- condensing matter and illustrations into twelve colored plates enabling any person with no botanical knowledge to "distinguish delicious food from deadly poison."
- One enthusiastic student sent twenty-five copies of the chart to as many different schools -- "an example which in this the day of object-teaching is surely worthy of imitation on the part of those in charge of the education of the young."

## Palmer on Mushrooms and Health

Palmer's personal testimony makes a compelling case for mushrooms as healthful food: "During the season I eat them three times a day; sometimes of a half dozen kinds at a meal. I never enjoy better digestion than during toadstool time." This was not casual indulgence but the result of two decades of careful testing and identification.

He attributes much of the public fear of mushrooms to the fact that newspaper reports of poisoning never identify the species involved. "It would be of great practical value if, whenever possible, this information were given to the public, and fresh samples of the injurious fungus sent to some mycologist for positive identification." Many reported cases of "toadstool poisoning" were likely caused by over-seasoning, decomposition, or improper cooking rather than any inherent poison in the mushroom.

Palmer notes that "there is nothing more healthful for both mind and body than the pursuit of any out-door science" -- mushroom foraging being an ideal combination of physical exercise, botanical study, and culinary reward. He expresses a "personal affection for these little companions of so many otherwise solitary rambles," calling them "curious creatures" surrounded by "a holy mystery."
