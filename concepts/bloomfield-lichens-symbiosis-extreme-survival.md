Lichens are composite organisms formed from the symbiotic partnership
between fungi and photosynthetic algae or cyanobacteria. They represent
one of the most successful [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]], and from bare rock
to tree bark. Despite their ecological importance, they remain

## The Symbiotic Partnership

Lichens are fundamentally fungal organisms whose exposure to air and
sunlight is made possible by their partnership with photosynthetic
microorganisms. The fungal partner (mycobiont) provides structure,
moisture retention, and protection, while the algal or cyanobacterial
partner (photobiont) produces sugars through photosynthesis. Cells of
the photobiont occupy a discrete layer just beneath the lichen's
surface, where they receive sufficient light for photosynthesis but
are shielded by the fungus from damaging ultraviolet radiation.

When the fungal partner reproduces by discharging spores, it leaves
the photobiont behind. This means the fungus must capture a compatible
photosynthetic partner, typically one of only 100 or so species of
algae and bacteria found in lichens, each time it founds a new colony.
