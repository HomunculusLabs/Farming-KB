# Energy Conversion Efficiency Comparison

Understanding the conversion efficiency of various energy devices and systems is essential for making informed decisions about village-scale energy production. Mollison compiled comparative data from multiple sources to help [[permaculture]] designers evaluate technologies not just by their mechanical efficiency, but by their appropriateness, pollution profile, social acceptability, and local maintainability.

## Selection Criteria Beyond Efficiency

Mechanical efficiency is perhaps the least important concept for people when evaluating energy technologies. A technology is relevant only if:

1. **The process is non-polluting**, or appropriate to the place
2. **The technology is socially acceptable** and locally benign
3. **The cost is affordable** and amortizes within 10 years
4. **The technology can be locally produced and maintained**

If any technology destroys the basic quality or quantity of soil, water, or clean air, it must be rejected—regardless of its efficiency. This is what Mollison calls the **"economics of extinction."** For this reason, fission processes with radioactive by-products, coal as conventionally used, and mineral petroleum beyond initial or transition use are excluded.

## Biomass and Wood Combustion

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest | Average | Highest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cow dung burnt as fuel | Dung → water heat | — | 7% | — |
| Open fires | Wood → room heat | — | 15% | — |
| Kenyan bell-bottom cookstove | Wood → cooking heat | — | 30% | — |
| Well-made slow combustion stove | Wood → hot water, cooking, space heat | — | — | 80% |
| Wood burnt to generate power | Wood → electricity | — | — | 35% |

**Key insight**: Open fires are extremely wasteful of wood at only ~15% efficiency. Well-made slow combustion stoves achieve 80% but may pollute air. Cow dung burnt directly is very poor (7%) compared to biogas conversion. Converting wood to electricity loses significant energy compared to direct thermal use.

## Biogas and Methane

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest | Average | Highest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cow dung → methane | Biogas conversion | — | — | 55% |
| Heat cells | Heat rating → gas BTU | — | — | 93% |

**Key insight**: Converting cow dung to methane yields useful fertilizer residue while achieving 55% efficiency—far superior to burning dung directly at 7%. The fertilizer by-product makes biogas doubly valuable.

## Water Power

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest | Average | Highest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Undershot straight-paddle wheels | Water flow → shaft HP | 18% | 18% | 20% |
| Undershot Poncelot (curved vane) | Stream flow → shaft HP | — | — | 60% |
| Breast water wheels (curved vanes) | Water power → shaft HP | — | — | 75% |
| Well-designed overshot water wheels | Stream flow → shaft HP | — | — | 90% |
| Propelior turbines (hydraulic) | Stream flow → shaft HP | — | — | 81% |
| Rotary hydraulic turbines | Water at head → shaft HP | — | — | 80% |
| [[pelton-wheel]] / Turgo impulse | Falling water → shaft HP | — | — | 92–93% |

**Key insight**: Water power spans an enormous efficiency range depending on the technology chosen. Overshot water wheels achieve 90% while basic undershot paddle wheels manage only 18–20%. Pelton and Turgo impulse wheels are the most efficient at 92–93%.

## Engine and Generator Technologies

| Technology | Conversion | Lowest | Average | Highest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Typical diesel engine | Fuel → horsepower | — | — | 35% |
| Turbo-charged diesel engine | Fuel → horsepower | — | — | 40% |
| Adiabatic (cool) diesel | Fuel → horsepower | — | — | 50% |
| Gas turbines | Gas → electricity | — | — | 30% |
| Gas fuel cells | Gas → electricity | — | — | 65% |
| Diesel co-generation | Diesel → heat (60%) + electricity (30%) | — | — | 90% |

**Key insight**: Diesel co-generation—where waste heat is used constructively—achieves an effective 90% efficiency by utilizing both heat and electricity outputs. This is far superior to using diesel for electricity alone (30%) or mechanical power alone (35%).

## Amplified Efficiency Technologies

| Technology | Mechanism | Efficiency Gain |
|---|---|---|
| Heat pumps | Extracting low-grade heat to useful space heat/cooling | 350% (×3.5 to 7) |
| Compressed air heaters | Expanding cool compressed air before engine use | 500–600% (×5.5 to 6) |

**Key insight**: Heat pumps and compressed air pre-heating achieve "amplified" efficiencies exceeding 100% because they concentrate and upgrade diffuse energy (ambient heat, compressed air [[phase-change-materials-thermal-energy-storage]]) rather than converting fuel directly. These technologies effectively multiply the usefulness of energy inputs.

## Practical Implications for Village Design

### Prioritize Direct Thermal Use Over Electrical Conversion

Converting energy to electricity and then back to heat is wasteful. Direct [[solar-water-heating]] (30–80%) far outperforms solar-electric-then-electric-heating chains. Similarly, wood combustion for direct heat (80%) vastly outperforms wood-to-electricity (35%).

### Co-Generation Is Essential

Whenever engines or generators are used, the waste heat must be captured and used. Diesel co-generation achieves 90% effective utilization versus 30% for electricity-only generation.

### Choose Appropriate Water Power Technology

The difference between undershot paddle wheels (18%) and overshot wheels or Pelton turbines (90–93%) is enormous. Investment in proper water power technology pays enormous dividends in [[holmgren-biomass-fuels-and-energy-yield-ratios]].

### Biogas Over Direct Combustion

Cow dung biogas (55% + fertilizer) [[overnight-soak-versus-direct-hydration-grass-seed-substrate]] burning (7%) demonstrates the critical importance of conversion technology selection.

### Heat Pumps Offer Remarkable Returns

Where ambient heat or waste heat is available, heat pumps can deliver 350% effective efficiency—making them extremely attractive for space heating and cooling applications.

### Site-Specific Selection

No single technology is universally best. The appropriate choice depends on available resources (sun, water, wind, biomass), climate, capital, and local manufacturing capability. A diverse portfolio of energy sources—wind, tide, river, solar, and methane—used where each is most appropriate, provides the most resilient village energy system.
