# Soil Microbiology in Natural Farming

## The Living Soil

Fukuoka understood that healthy soil is alive with microbial communities. "One gram of soil on my farm contains about 100 million nitrogen-fixing bacteria and other soil-enriching microbes." These microorganisms are not merely inhabitants of soil — they are its creators and maintainers. Fukuoka considered such soil "the spark that restores the deserts."

The sheer density of microbial life — hundreds of millions of organisms per gram — supports the [[soil-food-web-structure]] and drives the [[fukuoka-natural-fertility-cycle]] that sustains crop growth without chemical inputs.

Among the microorganisms are bacteria, fungi (including true fungi and molds), slime molds, and yeasts. Predator-prey relationships exist within this vast group: fungi that wrap mycelia about their prey and kill by dissolution, bacteria that secrete substances to kill fungi, bacteriophages that kill bacteria, and viruses that kill both bacteria and fungi. Some viruses kill other viruses. This internal warfare keeps populations in check without any external management.

Thanks to these microbes, the earth's surface is always clean and beautiful. "If animals died and the carcasses just remained there without decomposing, a couple of days is all it would take to make the world an intolerable place to be."

## The Microbial Succession

Soil decomposition follows a natural succession: bacteria decompose organic matter, producing organic acids that inhibit further bacterial growth. As acidity rises, molds begin to flourish. Eventually the molds are replaced by mushrooms and other fungi that break down cellulose and lignin.

At each stage, different organisms perform different roles in an intricate, self-regulating web. This mirrors the broader principles of [[succession-in-permaculture]], where ecological communities develop through predictable stages toward maturity.

## The Natural Order of Decomposition

Fukuoka documented the full succession of decomposition on a single piece of rice straw dropped onto the earth. The straw draws flies and other small insects that lay eggs, from which maggots soon emerge. Before this, rice blast disease, leaf blotch, and rot-causing fungi already present on the rice leaves spread rapidly over the straw, but spider mites are soon crawling over this fungal growth.

Next, different microbes proliferate at once — yeasts, blue mold, bread mold, and trichoderma fungi — destroying the pathogens and beginning to decompose the straw. Nematodes feed on the fungi, bacteria feed on nematodes, mites consume the bacteria, and predaceous mites feed on those mites. Spiders, ground beetles, earwigs, mole crickets, and slugs join the community, mingling and living in the straw as it undergoes a succession of "tenants."

Once fibrin-decomposing fungi run out of food, lipoid- and lignin-decomposing bacteria take over, feeding on the fungi and their scraps. Parasitism and cannibalism set in among aerobic bacteria, which are gradually replaced by anaerobic bacteria. Lactic acid bacteria round off the process with lactic acid fermentation, at which point all trace of the straw disappears.

## Why Composting Is Unnecessary

While at the Kochi Prefecture Agricultural Testing Center, Fukuoka searched for beneficial bacteria to prepare compost from straw and brush. He collected refuse from garbage sinkholes, cattle, hog, chicken, rabbit, and sheep droppings. From these he isolated and cultured microorganisms, obtaining samples of many different bacteria, fungi, slime molds, and yeasts. He inoculated samples into straw and observed the rotting rates.

Later he realized the experiment was worthless. "Rather than going to all the trouble of isolating beneficial microbes and inoculating straw with this 'fermentation promoter,' all I had to do was scatter a handful of chicken droppings or clumps of soil over the straw. Not only was this the quickest way, it also gave the most completely rotted compost."

Fukuoka saw modern [[composting-materials-alternative]] methods as unnecessary interference. Nature's own methods are "far better" than any human-managed process. The [[worm-ecology-soil-building]] that develops naturally in undisturbed soil accomplishes what composting aims for, without the labor. "There is no call for making a lot of fuss over 'microbial' and 'enzymatic' farming methods."

## The Balance of Forces

"Hundreds of millions of microorganisms crowd each gram of soil. Bacteria are present in this soil, but so are other bacteria that kill these bacteria, and yet other bacteria that kill the killer bacteria. The soil contains bacteria harmful to man, but also many that are harmless or even beneficial."

This self-regulating balance is destroyed by chemical inputs. Spraying herbicides kills weeds but also acts as a bactericide and fungicide, eliminating putrefactive fungi on fallen leaves, devastating earthworm and ground beetle habitats, and destroying microorganisms in the ground. The [[composting-and-soil-biology]] depends on this balance remaining intact.

## Self-Governed Providence

"No species of bird flying in the air should become extinct. The earthworms that burrow through the soil must not vanish. Nor should the mice and spiders proliferate too greatly. If one type of fungus thrives just a little too well, this throws everything out of balance."

Tens of millions of species live on in perfect harmony without increase or decrease; they are born and they die unseen by man. Fukuoka called this "natural, self-governed providence" — a mystery that science cannot explain but that natural farming relies upon completely. The equilibrium between production and consumption has carried on for untold thousands and millions of years. "The mastery of the conductor performing this drama of natural transformation at once casual and intense is truly something to behold."

## Why Pesticides Destroy Soil Health

Fukuoka observed that treating soil with chloropicrin temporarily alleviates bacterial soft rot but the disease breaks out worse two years later — the germicide killed the moderating bacteria while leaving the pathogen. "Is it really possible to restore the balance of nature by spraying an array of bactericides and fungicides into a soil populated with such a large variety of microbes?" The answer is emphatically no. See [[fukuoka-pest-ecology]] for the broader implications.

## Pesticides and Microbial Mutation

Fukuoka argued that pesticides trigger the emergence of new resistant pathogens and mutants, not merely the selection of existing resistant strains. A single bacterium like Escherichia coli could, if its self-governing mechanisms ceased for just several days, leave a mass of progeny equivalent to the total mass of all living organisms on earth.

The botrytis case study illustrates this danger. After postwar pesticide spraying in citrus orchards triggered botrytis outbreaks in loquat groves, the fungus was found to form sclerotia containing eight genetically distinct spores — capable of producing more variants than even the tetrapolar shiitake fungus. "New strains of advanced animals and plants do not arise easily, but the chances of this happening in lower bacteria and fungi are very great and can lead to frightening consequences."

Fukuoka feared far more from bacteria, fungi, and viruses than from insects. He warned that scientists who investigate only toxicity in the human body while ignoring effects on soil and airborne microbes are "just sowing the seeds for future pollution."

## The Pine Rot Case Study

Fukuoka's investigation of pine rot revealed a cascade: soil acidification kills matsutake mushrooms (symbionts on pine roots), which allows parasitic molds to attack, followed by nematode infestation. "The first telltale sign of a global change in weather patterns will probably appear in microorganisms."

He viewed the loss of the matsutake as "a solemn warning that something is amiss in the world of soil microbes." Atmospheric pollution killing off the microbes that attack botrytis fungi may have triggered a chain reaction: gray mold explosion, nematode surge, dead pine trees, and destruction of the matsutake fungus.

## Microorganisms as Nature's Tillers

"Microorganisms and small animals act as nature's tillers." When roots die, humus increases, soil microbes proliferate and enrich the soil, earthworms appear, and moles follow. The soil works itself — plowing by humans actually interferes with these natural processes by breaking apart fungal networks and disrupting the soil's biological structure.

## The Knowledge Problem

Science examines microorganisms in flasks and test tubes, but "the soil that he knows is a discreet, isolated part of a whole. The only complete and whole soil is natural soil before it is broken down and analyzed." Soil science's approach of dividing soil into nitrogen, potassium, phosphorus, calcium, and manganese misses the living system entirely.

"If man jumps onto center stage and begins giving out directions like a know-nothing director, the play will be ruined. When something goes wrong in nature, the biosystem changes course. Unlike in a factory where the damage may consist of only a broken gear, in nature a disruption gives rise to an unending series of repercussions."
