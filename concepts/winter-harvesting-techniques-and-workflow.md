# Winter Harvesting Techniques and Workflow

Harvesting vegetables from unheated greenhouses in cold climates presents unique challenges that differ significantly from summer operations. Efficient techniques, proper tools, and careful workflow management are essential for maintaining productivity and product quality during the winter months.

## Harvest Schedule

[[four-season-farm]] harvests on **Mondays and Thursdays**, delivering to local stores and restaurants either late those afternoons or early the following mornings. In cold-climate areas with unheated greenhouses, a flexible schedule is essential:

- After below-zero nights, harvesting must wait until interior air temperature rises above **32°F (0°C)**.
- A typical midwinter harvest window runs from **10:00 AM to 3:00 PM**. Before 10:00 AM everything is still thawing; after 3:00 PM it begins freezing again.
- On bright sunny days, the window is wider and greenhouse temperatures can be quite pleasant.
- Having at least one **[[cool-greenhouse]]** (as opposed to cold/unheated) provides scheduling flexibility: harvest salad crops there in early morning, move to cold houses midday, then return to the cool house for radishes and turnips in late afternoon.

## Staying Warm While Working

### Backup Heating

A propane-fired salamander heater is kept in reserve for rare occasions when cold, snowy, and cloudy conditions prevent the sun from warming unheated houses above freezing. On average, this heater is used only **one harvest day per winter**. It has never been run solely to warm workers.

### Clothing and Hand Protection

- A **vest** (rather than a parka) is preferred for freedom of movement while harvesting.
- **Neoprene gloves** (cold-weather kayaking type) are available but even the thinnest are awkward for picking.
- Common practice: wear one neoprene glove on the knife hand, leave the other hand bare.
- Soak the bare hand in warm water every time full buckets are returned to the packing area.

### Cutting Tools

Small, sharp **Victorinox knives** (7 inches overall, 3-inch blade) are used for cutting, sharpened frequently. Cut leaves are placed in food-grade five-gallon buckets.

### Re-covering After Harvest

Inner covers (row-cover material) must be replaced immediately after harvesting. Once temperatures drop below freezing, the lightweight row-cover fabric freezes to itself and becomes impossible to place back over supporting wickets without tearing. PVA covers (discussed elsewhere) are stronger and less problematic, but replacing covers promptly remains best practice regardless of material.

## Washing and Packing

### Cleanliness Standards

Since all products are sold as fresh, raw foods, meticulous cleanliness is maintained:

- The washing and packing area is scrubbed with hot water after each harvest.
- All water comes from a drilled well tested annually.
- Hand washing is mandatory at all stages.
- Hats or hairnets are worn during washing and packing.

### Washing Procedure for Salad Mixes

Each salad ingredient is washed **separately** before mixing. This makes it easier to spot bad leaves or detritus when viewing only one shape and color of leaf at a time.

### The "Pac Man" Mixer

The salad mixer is a custom-built device:

- A barrel 48 inches long and 36 inches in diameter, made of wood slats with plywood ends.
- Mounted horizontally in a frame with a turning handle at one end.
- Divided into two halves hinged on the long side, opening like a clamshell.
- Holds up to 80 pounds of washed salad ingredients.
- After filling, the top half is closed and latched, then turned **ten slow revolutions** in each direction (clockwise and counterclockwise).
- The tumbling action gently raises and drops ingredients, producing thorough mixing without bruising.

## Working Efficiently

### Infrastructure Improvements

Small infrastructure changes can yield large efficiency gains. For example, installing larger-diameter pipes for sink filling and draining, plus raising water pressure, **cut sink turnover time by 75%**. The goal is continuous sink use—one person begins washing as soon as the first buckets arrive from the field.

### Harvesting Speed and Technique

Efficient harvesting requires quick hands, a quick mind, and a focused attitude. Key training points for new employees:

- Keep the harvest container as close as possible to the harvesting hand to **minimize hand travel**. On average, **40% of a harvester's time is spent moving the hand to the container**.
- Hold multiple cut leaves in the hand before dropping them—more leaves per hand motion means greater efficiency.
- Demonstrate specific skills such as harvesting spinach leaf by leaf.
- Employees who work slowly may need multiple reminders; harvesting alongside them to demonstrate pace differential is one approach.

### The Importance of Planting Schedules

The most critical skill for a four-season grower is maintaining a rigorous planting calendar:

- Keep careful notes on **seed-to-harvest days** throughout the year.
- Prepare a **day-by-day planting calendar** of dates for each crop.
- Since you cannot harvest what you haven't planted, the planting schedule must be as much a part of daily life as the harvest schedule.
- As Coleman quotes from Andrew Marvell: "But at my back I always hear / Time's wingéd chariot hurrying near." The next harvest follows quickly after the previous one, and products cannot be created instantly but arise from work begun months before.

## Small-Scale Harvesting Equipment Development

Hand-cutting mesclun with knives cannot compete on price with motorized harvesters used by large-scale producers. To address this, several approaches are being developed:

- **Greens Harvester** (Johnny's Selected Seeds): A human-powered harvester consisting of a cloth basket collector mounted behind a bow-saw frame holding a scalloped knife blade.
- **Battery-powered hand harvester**: Under development, the ideal tool would be light enough for one-hand use, adjustable for different cutting heights, wide enough to cover half a 30-inch bed, and reasonably priced.

Such tools would greatly benefit small growers and help close the efficiency gap with industrial-scale operations.
