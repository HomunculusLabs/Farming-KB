# Overgrazing and the Time Dimension

Overgrazing is one of the most misunderstood concepts in rangeland
management. Allan Savory's work builds on the insights of French
agronomist Andre Voisin, who demonstrated that overgrazing is not a
function of animal numbers but of the duration plants are exposed to
grazing. This time-based understanding is fundamental to [[hamilton-garden-planning-and-site-assessment]].

## Voisin's Revolutionary Insight

Andre Voisin's research in France in the 1950s revealed that overgrazing
occurs when plants are grazed before they have recovered from a previous
grazing. This is fundamentally a matter of time, not numbers. The same
number of animals can overgraze a pasture by staying too long, or can
avoid overgrazing by moving frequently.

The Niemöller analogy, popular in South Africa, helps explain the
concept: "The cows are not grazing. They are merely in the pasture. Only
the individual grass plants are being grazed. One cannot graze grasses
twice without ill effect; but a herd can graze grasses a thousand times
if each plant has a chance to recover."

## The Two Faces of Overgrazing

There are two distinct forms of overgrazing, both driven by timing:

**1. Plants grazed too soon after a previous grazing.** When a plant is
grazed, it uses stored energy to regrow. If grazed again before fully
recovering, it must draw on energy reserves that have not been replenished.
Repeated exposure gradually depletes the root system and the plant
declines or dies.

**2. Plants grazed during the dormant season (on perennial grasses).**
When a perennial grass is dormant and not growing, grazing removes leaf
area without the plant's ability to recover. In severe cases, plants may
die from exposure after leaf removal, though the primary effect is
reduced vigor and slower recovery when the growing season resumes.

## Recovery Periods: The Critical Variable

Recovery periods are determined by the speed at which a plant grows, which
in turn depends on several factors:
- **Moisture**: Adequate soil moisture accelerates recovery
- **Temperature**: Growth rates increase with favorable temperatures
- **Time of year**: Season affects both moisture and temperature
- **Soil health**: Good [[holmgren-mineral-balance-soil-fertility]]
  and [[savory-water-cycle-management]] speed
  recovery
- **Plant vigor**: Healthy plants recover faster than stressed ones
- **Degree of defoliation**: Heavier grazing requires longer recovery

Recovery can take as little as fifteen days in wet, warm conditions, or
extend to over 150 days during drought or winter. In [[savory-holistic-resource-management-brittle-vs-nonbrittle-environments]], recovery periods are
generally longer and more variable than in nonbrittle areas.

## Paddock Calculations

Savory demonstrates the practical implications with paddock calculations.
In a twenty-paddock system with one herd, each paddock receives an
average grazing period of about 25 days during a 500-day total
growing season. If a plant needs 60 days to recover, and the next
grazing comes at around 475 days, the plant has 415 days of rest. This
appears generous, but a critical problem arises.

The 500-day total is only an average. Within any paddock, different
plants begin growing at different times. A plant that begins growing on
day one receives 415 days of recovery. But a plant that begins growing
on day 475 receives only about minus 25 days of recovery: it is grazed
almost immediately after it starts growing.

The practical solution is to monitor base grasses and adjust paddock
use to ensure that the most recently grazed plants have adequate
recovery before being re-exposed. This monitoring is a core part of
[[bioremediation-monitoring-and-assessment]].

## The Perennial Grass Benchmark

Since plants cannot be individually marked in extensive operations, the
perennial grass component provides a practical benchmark. If the manager
can manage the grazing time to suit the perennial grass recovery, then
most other plants, which have shorter or similar recovery needs, will be
adequately protected. This does not eliminate all problems but provides
a workable standard for large-scale management.

## Overgrazing vs Overstocking

A critical distinction exists between overgrazing and overstocking:
- **Overgrazing** is a function of time: plants exposed to grazing
  without sufficient recovery
- **Overstocking** is a function of numbers: too many animals for the
  land to support over time

It is possible to overgraze without overstocking, and to overstock
without overgrazing. Many ranches are simultaneously overstocked and
overgrazed, but some are overstocked while individual plants receive
adequate recovery due to frequent movement. The key to holistic
management is addressing both, but primarily through time control.

## Overgrazing in Brittle vs Nonbrittle Environments

In [[savory-holistic-resource-management-brittle-vs-nonbrittle-environments]], the
constant humidity keeps soil surfaces moist and alive, enabling seeds to
sprout and plants to establish even when individual plants die from
overgrazing. The damage is buffered by the environment's inherent
biological activity.

In [[savory-holistic-resource-management-brittle-vs-nonbrittle-environments]], dead
plants leave bare ground that tends to remain bare because soil surfaces
dry out and lose biological activity. Overgrazing in brittle environments
is far more destructive because the environment cannot easily replace
lost plants. This is why the conventional approach of simply reducing
stock numbers often fails in brittle areas: it addresses overstocking
but does nothing to solve the time problem.

## Animal Behavior and Overgrazing

The way animals graze also affects overgrazing. Cattle in a large area
with ample forage typically wander widely, sampling many plants lightly
and returning to favorites more frequently. This creates a pattern where
the most palatable plants are grazed repeatedly while less palatable
ones are ignored. The resulting uneven utilization looks like
overgrazing of preferred species even when total numbers are low.

High stock density, achieved by confining animals to smaller areas for
short periods, forces more even utilization of available forage. When
combined with adequate recovery periods, this produces better results
than low-density continuous grazing.
