# Access Roads, Pathways, and Track Design

Access design is one of the first and most consequential decisions in any
permaculture layout. Mollison treats roads, tracks, and pathways not merely as
transportation infrastructure but as primary elements that shape water flow,
determine land use patterns, create or destroy microclimates, and define the
framework within which all other elements are arranged. Poorly placed roads are
among the most common and costly [[mollison-type-one-errors-in-design]] in land design. Access design integrates closely with
[[mollison-designers-road-water-harvesting-and-integration]],
[[mollison-settlement-siting-on-slopes-and-landscape-profile]],
and [[mollison-zone-planning-frequency-distance-and-placement]].

## Principles of Access Design

Mollison establishes several core principles that govern all access design:

- **Access comes first**: Before placing houses, gardens, dams, or forests,
  establish the access network. Roads are permanent, expensive to modify, and
  profoundly influence the function of everything they connect. The
  [[mollison-pdc-design-report-methodology]] sequence always includes
  access as an early step.
- **Minimum necessary**: Create the least amount of road surface necessary
  to serve the site's functions. Every metre of road represents compacted,
  non-productive land that sheds water and disrupts soil biology.
- **Multi-functional**: Roads should serve multiple purposes: access, water
  harvesting, fire breaks, property boundaries, and wind orientation. A
  road that only provides access is an under-designed element.
- **Follow natural contours**: Roads placed on the contour or at gentle
  angles to it minimise cut-and-fill, reduce erosion risk, and can harvest
  water effectively. Steep road grades (exceeding 10-15%) are expensive to
  construct, dangerous to use, and cause severe erosion.
- **Connect, don't divide**: Access should connect elements of the design
  without dividing productive areas into fragments too small for practical
  management. Consider the movement of people, animals, machinery, water,
  and wildlife.

## Road Types and Hierarchy

Mollison defines a hierarchy of access types, each with distinct design
criteria aligned with the [[mollison-zone-planning-frequency-distance-and-placement]] framework:

### Primary Access Roads
- **Function**: Connect the property to the public road network and provide
  access for heavy vehicles, machinery, and emergency services.
- **Zone**: Typically Zone 4-5, forming the backbone of the property's
  infrastructure.
- **Width**: 4-6 metres for two-way traffic; 3-4 metres for one-way with
  passing bays.
- **Design speed**: 30-50 km/h maximum for rural properties.
- **Grade**: Maximum 10% (1:10); preferably 5-8%. Steeper grades require
  extensive drainage and surface treatment.
- **Alignment**: Follow ridgelines where possible to minimise drainage
  requirements and avoid water concentration. On slopes, follow the contour
  at a gentle angle (1-3% fall across the road for drainage).
- **Surface**: Gravel or compacted earth with appropriate drainage. Concrete
  or asphalt only where absolutely necessary for traffic volume.
- **Curve radius**: Minimum 10-15 metres for vehicle turning; 20-30 metres
  for machinery and trucks.

### Secondary Roads and Farm Tracks
- **Function**: Provide access between major zones, connect fields and
  production areas to the primary road, and support [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-3-farm-forestry]] operations.
- **Zone**: Zone 2-4, connecting production areas.
- **Width**: 2.5-4 metres for vehicles; 1.5-2 metres for tractor and
  equipment access only.
- **Grade**: Maximum 15% with careful drainage; 8-10% preferred.
- **Surface**: Gravel, compacted earth, or grass track depending on traffic
  volume and soil type.

### Garden Paths and Foot Tracks
- **Function**: Provide pedestrian access for [[mollison-zone-one-garden-design]] management, harvesting, and daily movement.
- **Zone**: Zone 1-2.
- **Width**: 0.6-1.2 metres for single pedestrian; 1.5 metres for wheelbarrow
  and cart access.
- **Surface**: Stepping stones, gravel, wood chips, compacted earth, or
  living paths of low-growing herbs (thyme, chamomile, pennyroyal).
- **Design**: Curved paths are preferred over straight paths; they slow
  movement, create visual interest, and reduce erosion velocity on slopes.
  See [[mollison-designers-herb-spiral-design-construction]] for
  compact spiral pathway concepts.

### Animal Tracks and Laneways
- **Function**: Facilitate rotational movement of livestock between paddocks,
  provide access to water, shelter, and milking or handling facilities.
- **Design**: 2-4 metres wide; fenced on both sides; gentle grades; surface
  of compacted earth or gravel in high-traffic areas.
- **Integration**: Animal laneways double as fire breaks, windbreaks (when
  planted with hedgerow species), and contour water harvesting structures.
  See [[mollison-designers-woven-fence-hedge-live-barrier]] design.

## Water Harvesting from Roads

One of the most important multi-functions of road design is water harvesting.
Roads are effectively linear catchment surfaces that can be designed to direct
runoff to productive use. See [[mollison-designers-road-water-harvesting-and-integration]]:

- **Crown camber**: Roads should be crowned (higher in the centre) or
  sloped to one side to direct rainfall to specific collection points rather
  than allowing uncontrolled runoff down both sides.
- **Catch drains**: Shallow drains along the uphill side of contour roads
  intercept overland flow and direct it to storage or infiltration points.
  These drains can connect to [[mollison-pdc-swale-design-water-harvesting]] for
  distribution across slopes.
- **Spreader banks**: At intervals along the road, water is released from
  the roadside drain into adjoining land through level spreader banks that
  distribute flow evenly across the slope rather than concentrating it.
- **Pond recharge**: Road drains can be directed to fill dams and ponds,
  supplementing natural catchment. Even small road surfaces on large
  properties can contribute significant water volumes.
- **Ford crossings**: Where tracks cross watercourses, ford designs that
  incorporate rock weirs can slow water flow and create small sediment
  traps while allowing vehicle passage.

## Road Construction on Slopes

Road construction on sloping land requires careful earthworks design to
minimise environmental impact and long-term maintenance:

### Cut and Fill Balance
- The ideal road profile balances cut (material removed from the uphill
  side) with fill (material placed on the downhill side), minimising
  earthworks import or export.
- Cut slopes should be stabilised immediately with grass seeding or native
  plantings. See [[mollison-designers-post-earthworks-planting-and-slope-stabilisation]].
- Fill material should be compacted in layers, with the steepest edge
  protected by rock armouring or vegetated reinforcement.
