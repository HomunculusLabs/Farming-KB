# Wine Making From Grapes

Making wine from grapes on the homestead connects you to one of humanity's oldest agricultural traditions. Whether you grow your own grapes, source from local vineyards, or buy in bulk, the principles of winemaking are accessible to anyone with attention to detail and patience. (see [[pickle-making]]).

## Grape Selection

### Wine Grape Varieties by Type

**Red Wine Grapes**
- Cabernet Sauvignon: full-bodied, high tannin, blackcurrant flavors. Requires long growing season. (see [[smoking-meat-and-fish]]).
- Merlot: medium-bodied, softer tannin, plum and cherry. Easier to grow than Cabernet.
- Pinot Noir: light-bodied, earthy, red berry. Difficult to grow but rewarding.
- Syrah/Shiraz: full-bodied, spicy, dark fruit. Heat-tolerant.
- Zinfandel: bold, jammy, peppery. Very productive vine.
- Concord/Niagara: North American labrusca varieties. Foxy flavor, disease-resistant, cold-hardy. Good for sweet wines and jelly.

**White Wine Grapes**
- Chardonnay: full-bodied, buttery, apple. Adaptable to many climates.
- Sauvignon Blanc: crisp, herbaceous, citrus. High acidity.
- Riesling: aromatic, high acid, ranges from dry to sweet. Cold-hardy.
- Gewurztraminer: highly aromatic, lychee and spice. Needs cool climate.
- Muscat: floral, grapey, aromatic. Makes both dry and sweet wines.
- Seyval Blanc: French-American hybrid. Disease-resistant, cold-hardy. Good beginner grape.

### Choosing for Your Climate
- Cold climates (zone 4-5): Marquette, Frontenac, La Crescent, St. Croix, Brianna
- Moderate climates (zone 6-7): Cabernet Franc, Merlot, Chardonnay, Riesling, Vidal Blanc
- Warm climates (zone 8+): Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Zinfandel, Sangiovese, Muscat

### Sourcing Grapes
- Grow your own: requires 50-100 vines for meaningful wine production (approximately 15-20 pounds of grapes per gallon of wine)
- Local vineyards: many sell grapes by the pound or in bulk at harvest time
- Wine grape suppliers: ship refrigerated grapes from premium growing regions
- Fresh juice: many suppliers sell fresh-pressed grape juice in season (simplifies the process significantly)

## Equipment

### Essential Equipment
- Primary fermenter (food-grade bucket or open-top vessel, 7-10 gallon)
- Secondary fermenter (glass carboy, 5-6 gallon)
- Airlock and stopper
- Crush/destemmer: hand-crank or motorized
- Press: basket press (small scale) or bladder press (larger scale)
- Hydrometer and test jar
- Thermometer
- Siphon and tubing
- Sanitizer (Star San or potassium metabisulfite solution)
- Wine bottles, corks, and corker
- pH meter or test strips
- Titration kit for testing acid

### Nice-to-Have Equipment
- Refractometer (measures sugar in the vineyard)
- Oak barrels or oak chips for aging
- Degassing wand
- Floor corker (much easier than hand corkers)
- Wine thief (for sampling)

## The Winemaking Process

### Step 1: Testing and Adjusting Must
Before fermentation, test and adjust the grape juice (must):

- Sugar (Brix/SG): measure with hydrometer. Target: 21-25 Brix (1.090-1.110 SG). If low, add sugar (chaptalization). If high, dilute with water.
- Acid (TA): measure by titration. Target: 0.60-0.80% for reds, 0.65-0.85% for whites. If low, add tartaric acid. If high, blend with low-acid juice or cold stabilize.
- pH: target 3.2-3.6 for reds, 3.0-3.4 for whites. Affects taste, stability, and sulfite effectiveness.

### Step 2: Crushing and Destemming
- Remove grapes from stems (stems add harsh, green tannins)
- Crush grapes gently to break skins without crushing seeds (seed crush releases bitter tannins)
- For white wine: crush and press immediately (ferment juice only, no skins)
- For red wine: ferment on the skins to extract color, tannin, and flavor

### Step 3: Primary Fermentation
- For reds: add yeast (Lalvin RC212, BM45, or ICV-D25 are popular red wine yeasts) to the crushed grapes in an open or loosely covered vessel
- For whites: add yeast (Lalvin D47, EC-1118, or QA23) to the pressed juice in a closed vessel with airlock
- Fermentation temperature: 70-85F for reds, 55-65F for whites
- Add yeast nutrient (Fermaid-O or Fermaid-K) in staggered doses
- Primary fermentation lasts 5-14 days
- For reds: punch down the cap (submerge the floating skin cap into the juice) 2-3 times daily for even extraction
- Fermentation is complete when Brix reads 0 (or below) and gravity is stable

### Step 4: Pressing (Red Wine Only)
- Once primary fermentation is complete, press the fermented must to separate wine from skins and seeds
- Basket press: load the pomace (skins/seeds) into the basket and apply pressure. Gentle, good for small batches.
- Bladder press: inflate a bladder inside a cylinder of pomace. More efficient and even.
- Collect press wine separately from free-run wine -- press wine is more tannic and can be blended or kept separate

### Step 5: Secondary Fermentation (Aging)
- Rack (siphon) the wine into a clean glass carboy fitted with an airlock
- This is the bulk aging phase where wine clarifies and flavors develop
- Rack every 2-3 months to leave sediment behind
- Add sulfite (potassium metabisulfite) at each racking to prevent oxidation and microbial spoilage: 1/4 teaspoon per 5 gallons
- Age for 6-24 months depending on the wine style

### Step 6: Finishing
- Cold stabilization: chill wine to near freezing for 2-4 weeks. Causes tartrate crystals to precipitate (prevents crystals in the bottle).
- Degassing: stir vigorously or use a vacuum pump to remove dissolved CO2
- Clarification: most wines clear naturally with time. If needed, use fining agents (bentonite for proteins, sparkolloid for general clarification)
- Final adjustments: taste and adjust acid, sweetness (backsweeten after stabilizing), or tannin to preference

### Step 7: Bottling
- Sanitize bottles, corks, and all equipment
- Siphon wine into bottles, leaving 1/2 inch of headspace
- Cork immediately
- Store bottles on their sides (keeps corks moist) at 55-65F
- Red wine: drink after 6-12 months of bottle aging, improves for 3-10 years
- White wine: drink after 3-6 months, best within 1-3 years

## Oak Aging

Oak adds vanilla, spice, toast, and tannin complexity:

- Oak barrels: 5-50 gallon. Best flavor integration but expensive ($300-800+). Toast level (light, medium, heavy) affects flavor.
- Oak chips: inexpensive, add directly to the carboy. More aggressive extraction. Remove after 2-4 weeks. Medium toast is most versatile.
- Oak cubes: slower, more subtle extraction than chips. 4-8 weeks.
- Oak spirals: longest extraction time, most barrel-like character. 6-12 weeks.

## Sanitation

The most critical aspect of winemaking. Every surface that contacts wine must be sanitized:

- Star San: no-rinse sanitizer. Mix per instructions. Effective for 24 hours.
- Potassium metabisulfite solution: 1 tablespoon per gallon of water. Rinse with this solution before use.
- Clean first (remove visible residue), then sanitize.

## Common Problems

- Volatile acidity (vinegar smell): caused by acetic acid bacteria. Prevent with proper sulfiting and full vessels.
- Oxidation (brown color, flat taste): exposure to air. Minimize headspace, use sulfite.
- Hydrogen sulfide (rotten egg smell): yeast nutrient deficiency during fermentation.
- Refermentation in bottle: residual sugar ferments. Stabilize with potassium sorbate and sulfite before bottling sweet wines.
- Cork taint (musty, wet cardboard): contaminated cork. Buy quality corks, store properly.
