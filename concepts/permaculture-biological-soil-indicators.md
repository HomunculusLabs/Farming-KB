## 1. Soil Depth

Soil depth can be reliably assessed by comparing a single tree species across multiple sites.

Largest specimens of species such as Acacia, Prosopis, or honey locust invariably grow on the deepest soils. The same species will appear dwarfish on shallow soils of the same geological derivation.

Walking a ridgeline or transect and noting size variation within one species provides an immediate depth map without excavation. Understanding soil depth is critical for tree planting strategies, rootstock selection, and alexander shulgin placement.

## 2. Water Reserves

Deep-rooted trees serve as natural indicators of subsurface water availability. Species such as nut trees and Aleurites (candlenut) occur naturally only in sites that are well-drained yet water-conserving.

These trees often reveal hidden water-lines not associated with surface valleys and frequently stand directly over springs or aquifer discharge zones. In sandy soils, a wide variety of deep-rooted shrubs and trees indicates a clay base located one to two metres below the surface, a pattern common on desert borders.

Tree stem size also communicates water availability: large tree stems indicate well-watered sites, while small stems of the same species point to drier conditions.

## 6. Drainage

Soil drainage conditions are indicated by characteristic plant assemblages. Mosses, sundews, and fine-leaved heaths indicate poorly-drained soils where water sits at or near the surface for extended periods.

Chestnut trees, which require approximately two metres of well-drained soil, serve as indicators of good drainage when found thriving in a landscape. Drainage indicators are essential for planning earthworks and selecting appropriate species for specific moisture regimes.

## 7. Mineral Deposits

Perhaps the most remarkable category of biological indicators involves plants that reveal subsurface mineral deposits. Research by Davidov (Sputnik, 1979) documented plant systems growing directly over mineral deposits in Russia, including lead and copper-molybdate.

Leaf litter and humus from birch, cherry, honeysuckle, St John's Wort, wormwood, juniper, and heather have been found to reveal mineral lodes including tungsten and tin when analysed.

Specific indicator plants are associated with particular minerals:

Rue of Violets indicates zinc; Catchfly indicates cobalt.

Aster and Sneezeweed indicate selenium; Milk vetch indicates selenium and uranium.

Russian thistle indicates boron; Alyssum indicates nickel.

Honeysuckle indicates silver and gold; Horsetail indicates gold and silica.

This knowledge has practical implications for mine waste rehabilitation and for selecting plants that accumulate trace minerals for [[fungal-mineral-weathering]] and [[permaculture-soil-strategies]] and guide the establishment of tree clumps following natural fertility indicators. ## Practical Application
