## Mycorrhizae and Plant Succession

Mycorrhizal fungi play a pivotal role in plant community succession, influencing which species establish, persist, and ultimately dominate during both primary and secondary succession. By mediating nutrient availability and facilitating plant establishment on bare or disturbed ground, mycorrhizae act as key regulators of plant community assembly and ecosystem development.

## Nutrient Availability as a Successional Driver

Pedersen and Sylvia (1996) identified nutrient availability as one of the major determinants of success for early colonizing plant species during seral succession. The ability of plants to form mycorrhizal associations directly enhances their capacity to sequester nutrients from limited resources, conferring a significant competitive advantage in nutrient-poor successional habitats.

## Mycorrhizal Spore Dispersal by Animals

### Vectors of Inoculum
Small mammals are critical vectors for mycorrhizal fungal inoculum during succession. Key examples include:

- **Rodents** (*Microtus oregoni*, *Tamiasciurus douglasi*): Disperse spores of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi like *Glomus macrocarpus* and hypogeous ectomycorrhizal fungi like *Hymenogaster* (Trappe and Maser, 1976)
- **Tassel-eared squirrels** (*Scurius aberti*): Various ectomycorrhizal spores survive gut passage and form functional mycorrhizae with ponderosa pine (Kotter and Farentinos, 1984)
- **Pika, voles, chipmunks, marmots, deer, and mountain goats**: All contribute spore deposition on the Lyman Glacier forefront (Cazares and Trappe, 1994)

Cazares and Trappe (1994) demonstrated that mycophagy of both hypogeous and epigeous mycorrhizal fungi results in viable spore deposition in feces, providing essential inoculum for plant colonization.

### Patchy Inoculum Distribution
Spore dispersal by animals is rarely random. Small mammals defecate in middens and deposit more spores at active feeding sites, creating a patchy distribution of mycorrhizal inoculum potential. Allen and MacMahon (1985) documented that during primary colonization, mycorrhizal inoculum potential can vary from none to abundant in locations only centimeters apart.

## Case Studies in Primary Succession

### Mount St. Helens
M.F. Allen (1991) found that vegetation colonizing Mount St. Helens after the volcanic eruption consisted entirely of mycorrhizal species, including both arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) and ectomycorrhizal (ECM) forms. The total dependence on mycorrhizal species reflected the extreme nutrient poverty of the volcanic substrate.

### Galapagos Islands
In contrast to Mount St. Helens, studies on the Galapagos (Schmidt and Scow; Hendrix and Smith, cited in Allen, 1991) found a mixture of arbuscular mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal plants. The distribution of mycorrhizal associations correlated with soil fertility:
- **Nonmycorrhizal plants**: Established in more fertile, lowland soils
- **Mycorrhizal plants**: Established in poorer, rocky soils

### Lyman Glacier Forefront
The Lyman Glacier forefront provides a well-studied model of primary succession where mycorrhizal fungi play crucial roles:

#### Safe Sites for Establishment
Jumpponen et al. (1999) identified "safe sites" on glacial outwash—concave surfaces of coarse rocky particles that were ideal for trapping tree seeds and protecting them from desiccation. These microsites also served as foci for small mammal foraging, concentrating mycorrhizal spore-laden feces and further enhancing seedling survival.

#### Mycorrhizal Effects on Early Colonizers
The dark-septate mycorrhizal fungus *Phialocephala fortinii* significantly enhanced growth of lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*), an early colonizer, but only when nitrogen was added. However, total plant phosphorus was significantly enhanced by mycorrhizal colonization even without added nitrogen (Jumpponen et al., 1998):

| Treatment | Plant Dry Weight (mg) | Total N (% dry wt.) | Total P (% dry wt.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| No N, No Myco, No OM | 52.9 | 0.69 | 0.074 |
| No N, Plus Myco, Plus OM | 43.1 | 0.62 | 0.100 |
| +N, No Myco, No OM | 81.7 | 1.41 | 0.072 |
| +N, Plus Myco, Plus OM | 146.2 | 2.11 | 0.128 |

#### Ectomycorrhizal Species Succession
Jumpponen et al. (1999, 2002) documented succession of ectomycorrhizal species during primary succession on the Lyman Glacier forefront:
- **68 ectomycorrhizal species** belonging to **25 genera** were identified
- **No single species** occurred across all three successional sites
- **Maximum ECM diversity** occurred where tree canopies began to overlap

This pattern corresponds with findings from other studies (Dighton et al., 1986; Last et al., 1987; Visser, 1995) where diversity peaks at canopy closure, potentially related to both phosphorus limitation (Dighton and Harrison, 1990) and increasing proportions of nutrients in organic forms.

### Mt. Koma, Japan
Titus and Tsuyuzaki (2002) studied primary succession on volcanic substrates and found that mycorrhizal colonization patterns were species-dependent:
- *Agrostis scabra*: No effect of microsite on AM colonization
- *Campanula lasiocarpa*: Higher AM colonization near rock surfaces
- *Salix reinii*: Heavily ectomycorrhizal across all sites

This suggests that the successional models proposed by Allen and Allen (1990) depend on both environmental factors and plant species identity.

## Mycorrhizal Succession Patterns

### Ectomycorrhizal Succession
Ectomycorrhizal communities show clear successional patterns during primary succession:
1. Early-stage colonizers establish on nutrient-poor substrates
2. Diversity increases with canopy development and nutrient immobilization
3. Functional diversity increases to exploit mixed inorganic and organic nutrient sources

Conn and Dighton (2000) validated the hypothesis that increased ECM diversity allows greater functional expression. Where phosphorus was immobilized during early litter decomposition, the ECM community on pine seedlings contained a greater proportion of acid phosphatase-producing types.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Succession
Succession of AM fungi on herbaceous plant roots is less obvious than for ECM fungi. Hart et al. (2001) proposed two competing hypotheses:

1. **Driver hypothesis**: Mycorrhizal fungi are the driving force—differences in colonization and persistence potential among fungal species determine plant community composition
2. **Passenger hypothesis**: Mycorrhizal communities are determined by the plant community and environmental conditions, with fungal species changes tracking shifts in the plant assemblage

## The Importance of Mycelial Networks

### Maintaining Connectivity
Amaranthus and Perry (1989) demonstrated the critical importance of maintaining a continuous mycelial network during ecosystem recovery:
- **Partially cleared sites** (retaining mycorrhizal roots on remaining trees): ~90% survival of newly planted Douglas fir
- **Totally cleared areas**: Only 50% survival after 2 years

The existing mycelial network provides:
- Carbon and nutrient exchange between connected plants
- Access to larger nutrient and carbon pools for new recruits
- Greater ecosystem stability
- Maintenance of plant species composition following disturbance

This synergistic activity between surviving mature plants and new recruits allows for ecosystem resilience and continuity.

### Allen and Allen (1990) Hypothesis
Allen and Allen (1990) hypothesized patterns of mycorrhizal dependence in developing ecosystems relative to nutrient and water availability. M.F. Allen (1991) suggested that mycorrhizae increase plant species diversity during colonization by creating patchy inoculum distributions that allow both mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal species to establish.

## Saprotrophic Fungi in Plant Establishment

Even saprotrophic (decay) fungi can influence plant succession. Osonubi et al. (1990) showed that inoculating seeds of the pulpwood tree *Gmelina arborea* with the fungus *Chaetomium bostrychodes* significantly improved seed germination. The mechanism likely involves fungal enzyme production assisting in seed stratification or replacing the scarification process that would normally occur naturally.

## Microbial Community Shifts During Succession

During plant succession on glacial till, microbial communities undergo a fundamental transition:
- **Early stages**: Bacterial-dominated communities with high carbon respiration rates
- **Later stages**: Fungal-dominated communities with net carbon accumulation

Ohtonen et al. (1999) showed that this shift in carbon-use efficiency indicates fungi serve as a stabilizing force in developing ecosystems, facilitating net carbon fixation into biomass.

## Ecosystem Services of Fungi in Succession

Fungi provide multiple ecosystem services relevant to plant succession and community regulation:

| Ecosystem Service | Fungal Functional Group |
|---|---|
| Rock dissolution | Lichens, Saprotrophs, Mycorrhizae |
| Particle binding | Saprotrophs, Mycorrhizae |
| Organic residue decomposition | Saprotrophs, Ericoid and ECM |
| Nutrient mineralization | Saprotrophs, Ericoid and ECM |
| Soil aggregate stability | Saprotrophs, Arbuscular Mycorrhizae |
| Direct primary production | Lichens |
| Nutrient accessibility | Mycorrhizae |
| Plant defense against pathogens | Mycorrhizae, Endophytes, Saprotrophs |
| Plant defense against herbivory | Endophytes |
| Plant-plant interaction regulation | Mycorrhizae, Pathogens |
| Carbon sequestration and storage | Mycorrhizae, Saprotrophs |
| Modification of pollutants | Saprotrophs, Mycorrhizae |
