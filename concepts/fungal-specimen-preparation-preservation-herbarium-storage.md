# Fungal Specimen Preparation, Preservation, and Herbarium Storage

Proper specimen [[mushroom-spore-printing-syringe-preparation-and-preservation]] are fundamental
to [[biodiversity-fungal-biodiversity-estimation-methods]] studies and taxonomy.

Without carefully preserved voucher specimens, species
identifications cannot be verified by other researchers.

The large edited volume "[[biodiversity-of-fungi-biodiversity-patterns-ecosystems]]" provides
comprehensive protocols for preparing and maintaining fungal
specimens in [[biodiversity-fungal-herbarium-collections]].

## Drying Methods

Fungal specimens are primarily preserved by drying, which
must be prompt and complete to prevent degradation.

Three main drying methods are used in mycological practice.

Air drying is the most common method, used for sporocarpic
specimens and cultures grown on [[query-how-to-make-agar-plates-for-mushroom-cultivation]].

Specimens are placed on drying racks or in food dehydrators
with low heat to accelerate moisture removal.

[[aminopropyl-silica-gel-chromatography-indole-alkaloid-purification]] desiccation involves placing specimens and field
labels in sealed containers with silica crystals.

This method is suitable only for small, easily desiccated
specimens that would collapse or distort with air drying.

Freeze-drying (lyophilisation) freezes the specimen first,
then vaporises moisture under vacuum.

Freeze-dried specimens maintain their original shapes and are
good for display, but they become brittle and absorb moisture
readily over time.

After 5 to 6 years in storage, cells of freeze-dried
ascomycete and basidiomycete specimens often collapse.

## Group-Specific Preservation

Different fungal groups require tailored approaches.

Plant-pathogenic fungi on leaves are pressed in botanical
presses with corrugated aluminium spacers for [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]].

Lichens are air-dried over gentle heat at approximately
42 degrees Celsius; brittle lichens on soil are dried with
substrata glued to cards.

Myxomycetes are air-dried upright and stored in small tins
with substrata glued to removable paper inserts.

## Liquid Preservation

Delicate fungi such as Phalloid and Clathroid species can be
fixed and stored in liquid preservatives.

Specimens are submerged for 2 to 7 days then transferred
to storage solution.

Common fixatives include 70 percent ethanol, the Kew
Mixture, and the Copenhagen Solution.

Both recommended mixtures contain glycerol to prevent
specimen hardening.

Chemical fixation alters DNA, making liquid-preserved
specimens unsuitable for molecular studies.

Warm-air drying is preferred when both morphological
examination and DNA analysis are anticipated.

## Field Labels and Documentation

Every specimen must be accompanied by a field label containing
essential information.

Required data includes: tentative identification, collector
name and number, date, precise locality with coordinates and
elevation, and habitat and substratum descriptions.
