## Zoning and Placement Principles

### The Zone System in Practice

Zone 1 typically extends 5 to 30 meters from the house, depending on the
size of the property and the intensity of management. Within Zone 1, the
most intensively managed elements such as salad greens, culinary herbs,
seedling propagation, and daily kitchen waste composting are positioned
immediately adjacent to the kitchen door. Slightly farther out, main
vegetable beds, soft fruit, and small poultry systems occupy the middle
ring. The outer edge of Zone 1 transitions to [[mollison-designers-windbreak-design-species]] around the
house perimeter further refine the microclimate.

## Garden Design Elements

### Raised Beds and Intensive Cropping

Raised beds are the standard production unit in Zone 1 gardens. Mollison
recommends beds 1 to 1.5 meters wide (reachable from both sides without
stepping on the soil) and of variable length, with permanent paths between.
Raised beds improve drainage, warm earlier in spring, reduce soil compaction,
and allow intensive planting at closer spacings than flat ground. The deep,
loose soil of well-maintained raised beds supports high yields per unit
area, often 4 to 10 times the yield of conventional row cropping. Bed
preparation with [[mollison-designers-roof-water-catchment]] for irrigation
supply and with household [[graywater-systems]] for supplemental water.

## Domestic Resource Cycling

### Kitchen Waste and Composting

The daily flow of kitchen scraps, garden waste, and household organic
materials is a valuable nutrient stream that should be cycled back into
the garden as close to the source as possible. Mollison recommends a
three-bin composting system positioned conveniently between the kitchen
and the garden beds, allowing easy deposit of scraps and easy access to
finished compost. Worm farms (vermicompost) are ideal for processing
kitchen waste in small spaces and producing high-quality liquid fertilizer
for garden beds. The integration of composting into the daily household
routine ensures that nutrients are retained on site rather than exported
as waste.

### Greywater Reuse

Household greywater from showers, baths, and laundry can be directed to
garden irrigation through simple gravity-fed systems. Mollison describes
mulch-filled greywater trenches that receive and infiltrate greywater while
supporting productive plantings of banana, papaya, comfrey, and canna lily.
Greywater systems must be designed to prevent waterlogging and ensure that
all water is absorbed within the property boundary. This local water reuse
connects directly to [[water-management]] strategy.

### Tool Storage and Work Areas

A well-organized tool shed and potting area within Zone 1 reduces the time
and effort required for garden maintenance. Mollison recommends positioning
the tool shed centrally within the garden area, accessible from all bed
locations without backtracking. A covered work area for seedling
propagation, potting, and processing harvests should be adjacent to the
tool shed and close to a water supply. These support elements, while not
productive themselves, dramatically increase the efficiency of garden
management.

## Summary

Zone 1 connects to the rest of the permaculture system through multiple
pathways. Excess production feeds into preservation systems. Poultry from Zone
1 runs provide manure for garden fertility and pest control. Compost and
greywater from the house nourish the garden, which produces food and medicine
for the household. The efficient cycling of energy, water, nutrients, and
materials between house and garden exemplifies the permaculture principle of
closing loops. Zone 1 is where the designer's attention to detail has the
greatest impact on daily quality of life and household self-reliance. When
properly designed, Zone 1 maximizes food production while minimizing
maintenance effort, creating a living environment that is both productive
and pleasurable.
