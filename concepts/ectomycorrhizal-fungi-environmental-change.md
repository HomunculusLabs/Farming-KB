## Ectomycorrhizal Fungi and Environmental Change

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi form mutualistic symbioses with the roots of many forest trees and are critically important mediators of ecosystem responses to environmental change. As key players in nutrient cycling, carbon allocation, and plant community dynamics, ECM fungi are both affected by and modulate the impacts of atmospheric pollution, climate change, elevated CO₂, nitrogen deposition, and other anthropogenic disturbances on forest ecosystems.

## Elevated CO₂ and ECM Fungal Responses

Rising atmospheric CO₂ concentrations directly affect the carbon economy of the mycorrhizal symbiosis. When plants are grown under elevated CO₂, they typically increase photosynthetic carbon fixation, and a significant proportion of the extra carbon is allocated belowground to roots and their mycorrhizal fungal symbionts.

Gorisen and Kuyper (2000) demonstrated fungal species-specific responses of ectomycorrhizal Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*) to elevated CO₂, finding that not all ECM species respond equally to increased carbon supply. This differential response could drive shifts in ECM community composition under future atmospheric conditions. O'Neill et al. (1987) observed increased mycorrhizal colonization and seedling growth in loblolly pine and white oak under enriched CO₂ atmospheres.

Klironomos et al. (1997) studied soil fungal-arthropod responses to trembling aspen (*Populus tremuloides*) grown under enriched atmospheric CO₂ under field conditions, finding complex multi-trophic interactions. Klironomos et al. (1998) further showed interspecific differences in the response of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] to big sagebrush (*Artemisia tridentata*) grown under elevated CO₂.

Rygiewicz et al. (2000) investigated the morphotype community structure of ectomycorrhizas on Douglas-fir seedlings grown under elevated atmospheric CO₂ and temperature, finding that the combination of warming and CO₂ enrichment produced different community responses than either factor alone.

Hungate et al. (2000) studied soil microbiota in two annual grasslands, finding responses to elevated atmospheric CO₂ that were mediated by changes in plant inputs and soil moisture. Dhillion et al. (1996) assessed the impact of elevated CO₂ on soil microbial activity in a Mediterranean model ecosystem.

## Carbon Allocation and Mycorrhizal Function

The carbon cost of maintaining mycorrhizal symbioses is significant but generally outweighed by the nutritional benefits to the host plant. Douds et al. (1998) measured the carbon cost of the fungal symbiont relative to net leaf phosphorus accumulation in a split-root VA mycorrhizal symbiosis, providing quantitative data on the carbon-phosphorus trade-off.

Wang et al. (1989) used real-time dynamic measurements with ¹¹CO₂ to compare carbon partitioning patterns of mycorrhizal versus non-mycorrhizal plants. Finlay and Read (1986a, 1986b, 1986c) conducted landmark studies on the structure and function of the vegetative mycelium of ectomycorrhizal plants, demonstrating carbon translocation between plants interconnected by a common mycelium and the uptake and distribution of phosphorus by mycelial strands.

Simard et al. (1997a, 1997b) demonstrated reciprocal transfer of carbon isotopes between ectomycorrhizal birch and Douglas-fir, providing evidence for net carbon transfer between ectomycorrhizal tree species in the field through [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]]. This finding has profound implications for understanding belowground competition and facilitation in mixed-species forests.

Seegmüller and Rennenberg (1994) studied the interactive effects of mycorrhization and elevated carbon dioxide on growth of young pedunculate oak (*Quercus robur*), finding synergistic benefits. Staddon et al. (1999) examined the effects of mycorrhizal colonization and elevated atmospheric CO₂ on carbon fixation and below-ground carbon partitioning in ribwort plantain (*Plantago lanceolata*).

## Litter Quality and Decomposition

Environmental changes that alter plant tissue chemistry have cascading effects on decomposition processes mediated by fungi. Cotrufo et al. (1998) reviewed the relationship between increased atmospheric CO₂ and litter quality, noting that elevated CO₂ often reduces leaf nitrogen content, potentially slowing decomposition rates. Cotrufo and Ineson (2000) specifically asked whether elevated atmospheric CO₂ concentrations affect wood decomposition, finding that changes in wood chemistry under elevated CO₂ can alter decay rates.

De Angelis et al. (2000) studied litter quality and decomposition in a CO₂-enriched Mediterranean forest ecosystem, finding complex interactions between CO₂ effects on litter chemistry and decomposition dynamics. Conn and Dighton (2000) demonstrated that litter quality influences decomposition, ectomycorrhizal community structure, and mycorrhizal root surface acid phosphatase activity, linking aboveground litter inputs to belowground fungal community responses.

Hättenschwiler et al. (1999) investigated quality, decomposition, and isopod consumption of tree litter produced under elevated CO₂, finding that litter from CO₂-enriched environments was less palatable to detritivores. Dighton et al. (2000) studied the determinants of leaf litter patchiness in mixed-species New Jersey pine barrens forest and its possible influence on soil biota, connecting aboveground litter distribution patterns to belowground fungal activity.

## Nitrogen Cycling and ECM Fungi

Ectomycorrhizal fungi play critical roles in forest nitrogen cycling, both through direct uptake of inorganic nitrogen and through access to organic nitrogen sources. Dighton (1991) reviewed the acquisition of nutrients from organic resources by mycorrhizal autotrophic plants. Gundersen et al. (1998) synthesized NITREX data on the impact of nitrogen deposition on nitrogen cycling in forests, showing that ECM fungi are sensitive to even moderate increases in nitrogen availability.

Harrison et al. (1995) determined the critical load of nitrogen for Sitka spruce forests in Wales, finding that nutrient limitations involving mycorrhizal function play a key role in defining ecosystem sensitivity. Nylund (1989) provocatively suggested that classical theories about the relationship between nitrogen, carbohydrate availability, and ectomycorrhiza were crumbling, reflecting the growing understanding of the complexity of the nitrogen-mycorrhiza relationship.

Wallander et al. (1999) investigated relationships between fungal uptake of ammonium, fungal growth, and nitrogen availability in ectomycorrhizal Scots pine seedlings. Kären and Nylund (1997) studied the effects of ammonium sulphate on the community structure and biomass of ectomycorrhizal fungi in a Norway spruce stand in southwestern Sweden, finding significant community shifts under nitrogen enrichment.

## ECM Fungi in Boreal and Temperate Forest Dynamics

The long-term continuity of ECM fungal communities in forest ecosystems is an important consideration for understanding their responses to environmental change. Jonsson et al. (1999a) studied continuity of ectomycorrhizal fungi in self-regulating boreal Scots pine forests by comparing mycobiont diversity on seedlings and mature trees. Jonsson et al. (1999b) described ectomycorrhizal fungal communities in late-successional Swedish boreal forests and their composition following wildfire, showing that fire can dramatically reshape ECM communities.

Chapela et al. (2001) found that ectomycorrhizal fungi introduced with exotic pine plantations induce soil carbon depletion, highlighting the potential for novel ECM associations to alter ecosystem carbon dynamics in non-native forest plantings.

## Soil Respiration and Carbon Balance

Root and mycorrhizal respiration constitute a significant portion of total soil respiration, linking belowground fungal activity to ecosystem-level carbon balance. Bowden et al. (1993) partitioned contributions of aboveground litter, belowground litter, and root respiration to total soil respiration in a temperate mixed hardwood forest. Vogt (1991) reviewed carbon budgets of temperate forest ecosystems, emphasizing the importance of belowground allocation.

Edwards and Harris (1977) measured carbon cycling in a mixed deciduous forest floor, providing foundational data. Hendricks et al. (1993) assessed the role of fine roots in carbon and nutrient cycling, noting that mycorrhizal roots represent a particularly dynamic carbon pool. Gaudinski et al. (2001) used radiocarbon to measure the age of fine-root carbon in three eastern US forests.

Bonan (1993) studied physiological controls of the carbon balance of boreal forest ecosystems, and Bonan and Van Cleve (1992) investigated soil temperature, nitrogen mineralization, and carbon source-sink relationships in boreal forests. McMurtrie et al. (2000) modeled the effects of elevated CO₂ on forest growth and carbon storage, including consequences of changes in litter quality and root exudation.

## Belowground Responses to Global Change

Vogt et al. (1993) proposed belowground responses as indicators of environmental change, arguing that root and mycorrhizal systems provide early warning signals of ecosystem stress. Jastrow and Miller (2000) studied long-term effects of elevated atmospheric CO₂ on belowground biomass and transformations to soil organic matter in grassland, finding significant increases in soil carbon storage mediated by mycorrhizal fungi and other soil organisms.

Zak et al. (2000) reviewed elevated atmospheric CO₂, fine roots, and the response of soil microorganisms, proposing hypotheses about how increased root inputs under elevated CO₂ might stimulate soil microbial activity and alter nutrient cycling. O'Neill (1994) reviewed responses of soil biota to elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide more broadly.

Treseder and Allen (2000) proposed that mycorrhizal fungi have a potential role in soil carbon storage under elevated CO₂ and nitrogen deposition, as increased fungal biomass and the production of recalcitrant fungal compounds (like glomalin described by Wright and Upadhyaya, 1996) could contribute to long-term carbon sequestration.
