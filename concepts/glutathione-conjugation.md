## Overview

Glutathione conjugation is a major phase II detoxification pathway in which reduced glutathione, usually
abbreviated GSH, is attached to electrophilic xenobiotics, endogenous reactive intermediates, or oxidized
biomolecules. The reaction often converts a chemically dangerous molecule into a more water-soluble thioether
that can be transported, processed, and excreted.

The pathway is central to toxicology because many harmful chemicals are not toxic until metabolic activation
creates an electrophile. A conjugation reaction can intercept that electrophile before it binds DNA, proteins,

## Chemical Basis

Glutathione is a tripeptide composed of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine. The cysteine thiol is the chemically
important group. At physiological pH most thiols are protonated, but enzymes can stabilize the thiolate form,
making sulfur a much stronger nucleophile.

The substrate side of the reaction is usually electrophilic. Common targets include epoxides, quinones, alpha
beta unsaturated carbonyls, alkyl halides, sulfonates, nitroaromatics, and reactive metabolites formed by
oxidation. The reaction creates a carbon sulfur bond and decreases the substrate's tendency to attack
biological macromolecules.

Not all conjugation is simply detoxifying. Some GSH conjugates can be processed to reactive cysteine
conjugates, especially in kidney tissue. This dual nature is why toxicologists treat the pathway as a
disposition system rather than a guarantee of safety.

## Enzymes

Glutathione S-transferases, or GSTs, catalyze many GSH [[microwave-sterilization-dose-response-microbial-kill-curves]]. Drugs that form quinone imines, epoxides, or Michael acceptors often depend on GSH
availability for safe handling. Depletion of cellular GSH can therefore increase toxicity without changing the
parent drug dose.

A classic pattern is bioactivation followed by conjugation. Oxidative metabolism creates an electrophilic
metabolite; GSH conjugation competes with protein binding; and excretion removes the conjugate. The balance
among these steps helps determine whether exposure is tolerated or injurious.

Drug developers watch GSH trapping assays because formation of a GSH adduct can signal reactive metabolite
risk. The finding is not automatically disqualifying, but it prompts closer study of covalent binding, dose,
exposure margins, species differences, and clinical monitoring strategies.
