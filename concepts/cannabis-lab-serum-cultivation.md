# Cannabis LAB Serum Cultivation

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) serum represents a practical, low-cost biological amendment that cannabis cultivators can produce at home to potentially enhance plant health, terpene production, and [[query-how-do-you-make-effective-microorganisms-em-for-agriculture]] (EM) and Korean Natural Farming, LAB serum involves fermenting a simple mixture of rice, milk, and molasses to produce a probiotic culture rich in beneficial lactic acid bacteria.

## Origins in Effective Microorganisms

The concept of using cultured beneficial microorganisms in agriculture was formalized by Teruo Higa through his development of Effective Microorganisms (EM/EM1). Higa's original formulation described an inoculant of multiple species of beneficial microorganisms providing a wide variety of functions, including suppression of plant pathogens and diseases, conservation of energy in plants, solubilization of soil minerals, and maintenance of soil microbial-ecological balance, photosynthetic efficiency, and [[query-how-does-no-till-cannabis-cultivation-work]] follows a similar philosophy but focuses specifically on culturing lactic acid bacteria through a simplified fermentation process that can be performed by individual growers.

## Evidence from Agricultural Research

Research on greenhouse tomatoes has demonstrated measurable benefits from biological amendments containing lactic acid bacteria. When tomatoes were grown with a blend of humic fertilizer from vermicompost and bio-fertilizer containing Lactobacillus casei, Lactobacillus lactis, Rhodopseudomonas palustris, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, total tomato yield increased by 19 to 21 percent after soil application and by 13 to 14 percent after foliar application of the bio-fertilizer and humic fertilizer, respectively.

Lactic acid bacteria have documented antifungal activity beyond their role as bio-fertilizers. Research by Magnusson, Strom, Roos, Sjogren, and Schnurer demonstrated broad and complex antifungal activity among environmental isolates of lactic acid bacteria, suggesting potential applications in suppressing fungal pathogens that affect cannabis.

## LAB Serum Production Method

The production of homemade LAB serum requires only basic ingredients: fresh rice, water, milk, molasses, a glass jar, and a one-way fermentation valve. The process occurs in two distinct fermentation phases.

In the first phase, rice is soaked in a jar with water and shaken vigorously. The rice is then strained out, and the now milky rice water is kept. This water is a carbohydrate-rich solution resulting from the washing of rice starches. The jar is covered loosely and left in a dark cupboard for several weeks. When the solution forms a skin on top and separates into three visible layers, it is ready. The middle layer is recovered using a siphon.

In the second phase, the extracted rice wash solution is mixed with ten parts milk. The jar is sealed with a one-way fermentation valve. Over 7 to 8 days of fermentation, the dropping pH causes excess milk fats and carbohydrates to coagulate into a curd. The liquid below the curd is a probiotic whey containing the cultured lactic acid bacteria. This liquid is strained and mixed with an equal volume of molasses (1:1 ratio) to stabilize the serum by providing a food source for the bacteria.

## Application to Cannabis

The stabilized LAB serum can be applied to cannabis plants through soil drenching or foliar spraying. For soil application, the serum is diluted with water and applied to the root zone, where the lactic acid bacteria colonize the rhizosphere and contribute to the soil [[cannabis-terpene-profiles-sativa]].

## The Curd Byproduct

The curd that forms during the milk fermentation phase is a probiotic-rich material with its own uses. It can be blended into a soil surface tea (SST), incorporated into compost piles to accelerate decomposition, or used as a supplement in other fermentation-based farming inputs. While not the primary product of LAB serum production, the curd represents a valuable secondary output that should not be wasted.

Foliar application delivers the bacteria directly to the phyllosphere (leaf surface), where they can help suppress foliar pathogens such as powdery mildew and botrytis. The lactic acid bacteria compete with pathogenic organisms for leaf surface resources and may produce antimicrobial compounds that inhibit pathogen colonization. Foliar sprays are typically applied in the early morning or evening to avoid the rapid drying that occurs in direct sunlight.

Some cultivators alternate between soil and foliar applications, applying soil drenches during the vegetative stage and switching to foliar sprays during early flowering when soil moisture needs to be carefully managed. The dilution ratio for application varies depending on the stage of the plant and the method of application, with typical dilutions ranging from 1:20 to 1:100 (serum to water).

## Quality Control and Troubleshooting

Successful LAB serum production is indicated by a clean, sour aroma similar to yogurt or sauerkraut. A putrid, rotten, or sulfurous smell indicates that undesirable organisms have contaminated the fermentation, and the batch should be discarded. The one-way fermentation valve is critical for maintaining the anaerobic conditions that favor lactic acid bacteria over spoilage organisms.

Visual inspection of the fermentation is also informative. The three-layer separation during the first phase (rice wash fermentation) should show a clear middle layer between a starchy top layer and a settled bottom layer. During the milk fermentation phase, the curd should form a distinct, clean separation from the liquid whey below. Cloudy or discolored whey may indicate contamination.

Consistency in production can be improved by using the same type of rice and milk for each batch, maintaining consistent temperature during fermentation, and using a sanitized jar and valve for each new batch. Over time, experienced cultivators develop an intuitive sense for the correct progression of the fermentation based on smell, appearance, and timing.

## Stabilization and Storage

The addition of molasses to the final LAB serum is a critical step for long-term viability of the bacterial culture. The sugars in molasses provide a food source that keeps the lactic acid bacteria alive during storage. Without this food source, the bacteria would gradually die off and the serum would lose its effectiveness.

The stabilized serum should be stored in a cool, dark location. Refrigeration extends the shelf life significantly, potentially preserving viable bacteria for several months. At room temperature, the serum remains effective for several weeks. Freezing is possible but may reduce viability for some bacterial strains. Before each use, the serum should be checked for signs of spoilage including off-smells, discoloration, or gas production in the storage container.
