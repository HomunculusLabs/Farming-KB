# Puff Balls (Lycoperdaceae)

The Lycoperdaceae, commonly known as puff balls, are a
distinctive family of fleshy fungi within the order
Basidiomycetes. They are so different in general form and
structure from the families of gilled and pored fungi
that they may be identified with comparative ease, making
them among the safest wild fungi for amateur collectors.

## Morphological Characteristics

Puff balls are commonly more or less spheroidal or ovate
in form. When young they are white and solid throughout.
Within the walls of the solid sporophore, practically all
of the tissues differentiate at maturity into a mass of
countless, dry, colored [[spore]]s, together with some spongy
remains of the hyphal tissues. Some smaller species
develop a mouth or opening at the apex at maturity, while
most larger species liberate spores only through general
breaking of the sporophore wall. The distinguishing
structural feature is the internal spore-forming habit.

## Edibility

When young, and practically up to the time of attaining
full size, nearly all puff balls possess white flesh. So
long as the inner tissue is white, the puff balls are
recommended for the table. When past their prime, or when
attacked by insects and bacteria, the flesh becomes
yellowish, purplish, or otherwise discolored, and in this
condition no species is wholesome. White flesh may be
considered an all-sufficient indication of edibility, and
this is so unmistakable that one may collect and eat puff
balls at will. By the simple rule of using no form with
colored flesh, no mistake is possible.

## Principal Edible Species

### Calvatia cyathiforme

The more important of the two common edible species from
the standpoint of constant appearance and wide
distribution in the Eastern and Central States is
*Calvatia cyathiforme*. Its habitat is much the same as
that of *Agaricus campestris*, and pastures usually bring
it forth in quantity when the autumn is cool and moist.
This species is more nearly broadly pear-shaped than
spherical, usually two to five inches in diameter. It is
clear white and smooth when young but becomes pink, brown,
or purple-brown when older. As the plant dries, the outer
wall peels off in areas, leaving a saucer-like sterile
basal part from which the species name is derived.

### Calvatia gigantea

*C. gigantea*, the giant puff ball, is the largest of all
the fleshy fungi, often measuring 12 to 18 inches in
diameter, with specimens over two feet reported. The
sporophores are vertically compressed when more than ten
inches in lateral diameter, giving a strongly oblate
spheroidal shape. It is widely distributed throughout the
United States in gardens, pastures, fields, and
occasionally open woods. The texture is soft and the flesh
pure white. The large size commends it particularly for
broiling in thick slices. As it approaches maturity the
flesh becomes yellowish or olive green.

## Cultivation Potential

The mycelium of *C. gigantea* grows readily when cultures
are made by the tissue-fragment method, with profuse
growth upon decayed leaves or soil. Pure cultures were
made by Duggar in 1902. However, no sufficient attempts
have been made to cultivate this species commercially.
It does not grow so readily as *Agaricus campestris* in
freshly prepared compost. A well-rotted compost mixed
with soil and well compressed might be a suitable
starting point for cultivation trials.

## Spore Discharge Mechanism

The spore discharge mechanism of puff balls is
distinctive. In smaller species, spores are liberated
through a definite opening (ostiole) at the apex of the
sporophore. Raindrops striking the mature puff ball
create a puff of spores that escapes through this
opening, giving the fungi their common name. In larger
species such as *Calvatia gigantea*, the outer wall
ruptures irregularly, releasing spores to be
distributed by wind. A single large giant puff ball
may contain an estimated seven trillion spores. The
spores are typically brown, olive-brown, or purplish in
mass deposit, and vary in size among species.

## Other Species of Note

*Bovista plumbea*, the lead puff ball, is a smaller
species found on lawns and pastures. It is attached to
the ground by a cord-like mycelial strand and is
readily distinguished by its lead-colored outer
surface when mature. *Lycoperdon perlatum*, the
gem-studded puff ball, is covered with small warts or
granules and is common in woods and open areas during
late summer and autumn. Both species are edible when
young and white within.

## Distinction from Earth Stars

The earth stars (*Geastrum*) are relatives of the puff
balls that deserve mention. In these fungi, the outer
wall splits radially at maturity and recurves, forming
a star-like base upon which the inner spore-containing
body sits. While earth stars are not poisonous, they
are generally too tough and small to be of culinary
interest. Their striking appearance makes them
favorites among nature photographers and amateur
mycologists.

## Importance for Amateur Collectors

Aside from wild *Agaricus campestris* and the morels,
there are probably no other species of fleshy fungi so
generally collected and eaten as the two common puff
balls. While the edible qualities may not equal those of
many agarics, they are sufficiently attractive in flavor
to meet with general favor. More important for amateur
collectors is the fact that puff balls cannot possibly be
mistaken for poisonous species of mushrooms, making them
an ideal introduction to wild fungus collecting.
