# Palm Clump [[palm-planting-patterns-and-mulch-systems|Patterns]]

Palm clump planting is a [[permaculture-design|permaculture design]] strategy that arranges palms (especially
coconuts) in circular groups rather than traditional linear grid plantations. Drawn from
Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, the pattern integrates palm
polycultures into village-scale zoning while improving mulch efficiency, ground-space
utilisation, and intercropping potential.

## Village Zonation Around Palm Polycultures

Mollison defines a concentric zonation model for villages built around palm polycultures:

| Zone | Designation | Function |
|------|-------------|----------|
| A | Livestock | Immediate village surrounds for small stock |
| B | Palm polyculture | Core productive zone of mixed palms and intercrops |
| C | Forage | Grazing and fodder for larger animals |
| D | Padi crop | Rice paddy production |
| E | Header dam & aquaculture | Water storage and fish production |
| F | Commercial crops | Market crops grown within palm systems |
| G1–G3 | Wild gathering | Progressive reduction in cultivated species; fuelwoods and nursery trees |

This creates a graduated transition from intensive human-managed systems outward to
semi-wild gathering zones, ensuring every ring of the landscape is productive.

## Land Patterning Prerequisites

Cross-slope hollows to hold mulch and capture runoff should precede all palm and tree
planting. Broad swales provide dual-purpose access routes during harvest and distribute
water across the site, retaining organic matter on-site rather than losing it to erosion.

## Clump vs Linear/Grid Planting

### The Traditional Grid Approach

Conventional plantations use square grids, typically 6 × 6 m for coconuts on new sites.
Practical limitations include: mulch distributed thinly across the plantation (husks
left at only one tree in 10–30 due to labour costs); only ~30% of ground area freed
for understorey use; replanting disturbs long linear rows; and harvest access compacts
soil throughout the grid.

### The Clump (Circle) Approach

Ten to twelve coconuts planted in a tight circle, each only a few feet apart, quickly
adopt a **divergent growth habit** — fronds spread outward from the clump centre rather
than competing vertically. Nut production compares favourably with grid patterns, and
nuts drop cleanly to the ground within the circle for easy collection.

## Benefits of Clump Planting

**Mulching efficiency** — the most significant practical advantage. Coconuts mulched
with their own fronds and husks show better growth and bearing. In clumps, all husks
are gathered and processed in one place; 10–12 palms generate ~0.5 m deep mulch for
the inner circle. By contrast, in conventional plantations natural fall line-mulches
only one in 8–12 rows (~2 m wide × 0.5 m high beds).

**Ground space freed** — clump patterning frees up to 60% of ground area vs 30% for
linear planting. This lightly shaded ground is available for intercropping, grazing,
or fallow recovery.

**Trellis support** — outward-spreading fronds form natural trellises for vanilla,
black pepper, and other vine crops, adding productive layers without infrastructure.

**Watering efficiency** — irrigation is targeted to discrete circles rather than spread
across an entire grid.

**Replanting ease** — wide spacing between clumps allows replanting in discrete sets
of 10–12 palms without the gross linear disturbance of row-replacement in grid systems.

**Run-off harvesting** — circular geometry is ideal for circular swales or coconut-
circle pits that capture and infiltrate water directly around each clump.

## Spacing Rules

The fundamental rule: **space at twice the frond length for that site, plus two feet.**
Sites with longer fronds (more vigorous growth, better soils, higher rainfall) require
wider spacing. On new sites, coconuts are normally planted at 6 × 6 m in grids; clump
spacing is measured from clump centre to clump centre.

Standard intercrop spacings within palm systems:

| Crop | Spacing |
|------|---------|
| Cacao | 2 × 3 m |
| Banana | 3 × 3 m |
| Cassava | 1 × 1 m |
| Velvet beans | 0.5 × 0.5 m |
| Maize | 1 × 0.5 m (in rows) |
| Citrus | 9 × 9 m |

These spacings fit within the light and root zones available beneath and between clumps.

## Access Roads and Harvest Routes

Sensible roading or grassed access ways are essential for gathering heavy palm crops.
Access routes should align with broad swales for dual-purpose water management and
harvest logistics. Grassed tracks between clumps reduce soil compaction. Routes must
accommodate carts or vehicles for transporting harvested nuts from clump collection
points to processing areas. In clump systems, access is concentrated between widely
spaced clumps, reducing total disturbed area compared to linear plantations where
access cuts across every row.

## Mulch Management: Clump vs Linear Systems

In conventional plantations, natural frond and husk fall provides inconsistent mulch
coverage, and labour costs make comprehensive redistribution impractical. Clump
planting concentrates all organic fall into a manageable area. Ten to twelve palms
shedding into a single circle produce deep, continuous mulch that suppresses weeds,
retains soil moisture, feeds palms directly, and eliminates the labour bottleneck of
distribution.

## Ensuring Mulch Sources for the Whole System

Layout design should guarantee mulch sources for both the palm system and short-term
intercrops. Clumps should be positioned so frond fall naturally covers intercrop zones.
Dedicated mulch-producing species should be integrated into the polyculture, and cross-
slope hollows used to trap wind-blown organic matter.

## Banana + Avocado: The Standard Interplant

The combination of banana and avocado has become the standard interplant within palm
clump systems. At harvest, banana plants with their full root mass provide approximately
**25 tonnes per hectare of organic matter**. Avocado adds long-term canopy diversity
and additional fruit production. Together they supply continuous mulch material for the
palm clump and surrounding intercrops.

Small tree legumes further bolster the organic matter budget:
- **Cassia** — fast-growing, coppices readily.
- **Calliandra** — high leaf biomass, good forage.
- **Leucaena** — nitrogen-fixing, deep-rooted, palatable to livestock.

These legumes fill gaps between clumps, fix atmospheric nitrogen, and provide prunings
that supplement the palm's own mulch production.
