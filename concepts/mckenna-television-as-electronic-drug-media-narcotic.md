## Heroin and Television Compared

McKenna drew explicit parallels between heroin addiction and television
dependency. Both substances induce a passive, trance-like state. Both
provide an experience of simulated reality that substitutes for
engagement with the actual world. Both create tolerance, requiring
escalating doses to achieve the same effect. Both produce withdrawal
symptoms when withheld. And both serve to pacify populations that might
otherwise question the conditions of their existence.

The comparison was not merely rhetorical. McKenna pointed to neurological
research suggesting that television viewing produces brainwave patterns
characteristic of a hypnotic trance — alpha waves dominate while beta
waves associated with active critical thinking are suppressed. The
viewer is not thinking; he is being thought.

## The Hidden Persuader and Fascist Potential

Following the lead of media critics like Wilson Bryan Key and the
tradition begun by Vance Packard's *The Hidden Persuaders*, McKenna
warned that television's true function is not entertainment or
information but the engineering of consent. Advertising, news framing,
and narrative conventions all operate below the threshold of conscious
awareness to shape desires, opinions, and behaviors.

McKenna saw in this a genuine fascist potential. When any single medium
monopolizes the flow of information and images to a population, and
when that medium operates primarily through emotional manipulation
rather than rational discourse, the conditions exist for authoritarian
control without the need for overt coercion. The population
self-disciplines through internalized values that have been implanted
rather than chosen.

## Philip K. Dick and the Construction of Reality

McKenna referenced Philip K. Dick's novel *The Man in the High Castle*
as a literary exploration of the theme that reality itself can be
manufactured and imposed. In Dick's novel, an alternative history in
which the Axis powers won World War II is maintained through the
controlled flow of information and imagery. The characters inhabit a
manufactured reality that they accept as natural because they have no
access to alternative narratives.

For McKenna, this was not science fiction but description. The
television-saturated society is one in which the boundaries of
acceptable thought are set not by reason or evidence but by the
structural limitations of the dominant medium and the economic
interests that control it. Reality, as experienced through television,
is a construction — and like all constructions, it serves the interests
of its constructors.

## Suppression of Psychedelic Experience

McKenna contrasted the cultural acceptance of television with the
systematic suppression of psychedelic experience. Psychedelics, which
dissolve conditioned patterns of thought and open awareness to
dimensions of experience normally filtered out by the nervous system,
are criminalized and pathologized. Television, which reinforces
conditioned patterns and narrows awareness, is celebrated and
ubiquitous.

This inversion, McKenna argued, reveals the true priorities of
industrial civilization. A society that drugs its children with
television from infancy while imprisoning those who explore
consciousness through plants is not acting from concern for health or
safety. It is acting to preserve a particular model of consciousness —
one that is compatible with consumerism, social conformity, and the
acceptance of authority.

## The Archaic Revival as Antidote

McKenna's proposed solution was not the reform of television but the
revival of what he called the "archaic" mode of consciousness — a
direct, embodied, participatory relationship with nature and with the
living world that preceded the rise of literacy, urbanization, and
electronic media. Psychedelic plants, in his view, were the original
