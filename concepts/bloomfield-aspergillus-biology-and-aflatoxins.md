Aspergillus is one of the most ubiquitous and economically significant
genera of fungi on Earth. Found virtually everywhere on the planet, these
ascomycetes play dual roles as beneficial organisms essential to food
production and as dangerous pathogens that contaminate food supplies
with potent carcinogens. Nicholas P. Money explores the fascinating
biology of Aspergillus in "Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard."

## Isolation and Ubiquity

It is a remarkably simple matter to isolate an Aspergillus from the
atmosphere. Opening a Petri dish anywhere outside the sterile air of an
operating room for a few seconds, closing the lid, and leaving it for a
day or so will likely produce the conidium-producing stalks of an
Aspergillus. If the first experiment fails, the fungus is certain to
appear on the second or third attempt. Once isolated, the culture can
be maintained indefinitely by seeding fresh agar medium with spores
from an older plate. The fact that Aspergillus strains can be isolated
anywhere on the planet is a testament to the effectiveness of their
asexual reproductive mechanism.

## Structure of Conidium Production

The spore-producing stalk of an Aspergillus is a hypha that emerges
from its food source and grows into the air. Once the stalk has
elongated, its tip swells and an array of specialized cells called
phialides arise on the rounded head. Phialides are shaped like bowling
pins. The nucleus inside the phialide divides continuously, and with
each duplication, one of the daughter nuclei moves to the tip of the
cell, where it is packaged into a spore and extruded like a blob of
toothpaste. This process repeats every few minutes so that each
phialide gives rise to a chain of spherical conidia.

Penicillium species, famous for their gift of [[aspergillus-flavus]] and Aspergillus parasiticus
are the two principal species responsible for aflatoxin contamination.
The link between liver cancer in humans and the consumption of
contaminated peanut products and meat is due to the consumption of
aflatoxin-tainted food. Aflatoxins have been detected in human milk,
indicating that nursing infants can be exposed through breastfeeding.

Research on aflatoxins received an unexpected boost from the turkey
industry. In 1960, the poultry industry suffered catastrophic losses
when "Turkey X disease" killed over 100,000 turkeys in England. It was
discovered that the birds had been fed aflatoxin-contaminated peanut
meal. Investigation revealed that Aspergillus flavus had colonized the
peanut meal during storage, producing lethal concentrations of
aflatoxins. This incident galvanized research into mycotoxins and food
safety.

## Toxicity and Risk Assessment

If rats are fed a dose of aflatoxins equivalent to the maximum exposure
encountered by humans, they develop liver tumors at an alarming rate.
However, extrapolation from rodents to humans is problematic. The
carcinogenic potency of aflatoxins in rats is tenfold lower than
forecast by the diseased rodents when applied to humans. Rats develop
liver cancer with the characteristic swollen belly and wasting seen in
human victims of the disease. The question of whether the presence or
absence of aflatoxins in animal feed may explain why the poultry
industry has yet to embrace fungal food remains unresolved.

## Beneficial Roles: Soy Sauce and Antibiotics

On a brighter note, soy sauce is produced when a pair of Aspergillus
species are allowed to ferment cooked soybeans. The fermentation process
breaks down proteins and carbohydrates in the soybeans, producing the
complex flavors characteristic of soy sauce. This application
demonstrates the long history of beneficial human-fungal interactions
through fermented foods.

Penicillium species, close relatives of Aspergillus, manufacture
antibiotics. By secreting antibiotics, some fungi benefit by removing
bacterial competitors and absorb nutrients from their leaking cells.
The discovery of penicillin from Penicillium notatum by Alexander
Fleming revolutionized medicine and demonstrated the profound
importance of fungal secondary metabolites to human welfare.

## Pathogenic Potential

Some species of Aspergillus can cause allergies, colonize the lungs,
and initiate invasive infections in patients with weakened immune
defenses. Aspergillosis is a serious concern for immunocompromised
patients, particularly those undergoing organ transplantation or
chemotherapy. The conidia of [[bloomfield-fungal-pleomorphy-anamorph-teleomorph]] means that the same
genome can produce radically different structures depending on whether
mating occurs.

The ability of Aspergillus to switch between sexual and asexual
reproduction provides evolutionary flexibility. The asexual conidial
stage allows rapid colonization of new substrates, while the sexual
stage generates genetic diversity through recombination. This dual
strategy helps explain the extraordinary ecological success and global
distribution of these remarkable fungi.
