# Entheogenic Syncretic Churches of Brazil

The syncretic religious movements of Brazil represent a unique phenomenon in the
history of entheogenic practice: the transformation of indigenous Amazonian
plant medicine ceremonies into organized, legally recognized churches open to
the general public. Three principal Brazilian churches, the Santo Daime, the
Uniao do Vegetal (UDV), and the Barquinia, use ayahuasca as their central
sacrament, while the Bwiti cult of Central Africa provides a parallel example
with the iboga plant. Together, these movements demonstrate how entheogenic
practices can evolve from [[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]] contexts into broad-based religious
institutions.

## Origins and Founding Visions

All three [[metzner-barquinha-umbanda-and-afro-brazilian-ayahuasca-syncretism]] churches were founded in the 1950s by rubber
tappers working in the Amazon region who came into contact with indigenous or
mestizo ayahuasca practitioners. Each founder reported a significant visionary
experience that instructed them to organize a church using the brew as the
principal sacrament.

This pattern of founding vision mirrors similar origins in other entheogenic
religious movements worldwide. The use of peyote by the Native American Church
in the United States, the Bwiti cult's adoption of iboga in Africa, and various
syncretic traditions throughout history all share the characteristic of a
revelatory experience that establishes the sacramental framework. In each case,
the original shamanic context of healing and divination is transformed into a
communal religious practice.

Members of the Brazilian churches come from all walks of life in both urban and
rural environments. Each has by now several thousand members in Brazil, and two
of the churches have established significant satellite centers in [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]
and Europe. The churches are officially recognized and the use of ayahuasca is
legal in Brazil within that framework.

## Categorizing the Ceremonial Forms

In his comparative framework, Metzner distinguishes four paradigms for the use
of hallucinogenic substances. The first is Western [[metzner-psychedelic-psychotherapy-clinical-history]],
where the drug amplifies internal self-analysis. The second is traditional
shamanic healing and divination, where the healer takes the medicine to see the
causes of illness. The third is syncretic folk religious ceremonies, which form
the primary subject of this article. The fourth is hybrid shamanic-therapeutic
rituals that blend elements of the first two traditions.

The syncretic churches occupy a distinct position on this spectrum. In shamanic
practice, the emphasis is on healing and divination conducted in small groups of
six to twelve participants, or sometimes just with one or two afflicted
individuals. In the syncretic churches, the focus shifts toward group worship,
celebration, singing, and prayer in larger gatherings of twenty to several
hundred participants. There is very little or no discussion or sharing of
visions or insights, as there would be in shamanic healing or divination
contexts.

## The Santo Daime

The Santo Daime was founded by a Brazilian of African descent, which is
reflected in the ceremonial structure. The church's rituals involve the singing
of hymns by the entire congregation, led by a small group of women singers. This
participatory musical element creates a communal experience distinct from the
more passive reception typical of Western religious services.

Some Santo Daime ceremonies also incorporate dancing in simple rhythmic, swaying
steps. Metzner notes that this character is somewhat reminiscent of African-
American gospel services, suggesting the African cultural influence of the
founder is expressed through the ceremonial form itself. The combination of
congregational hymn singing, rhythmic movement, and the shared experience of the
ayahuasca sacrament creates a powerful sense of community bonding.

Typically, ceremonies are held weekly in specially built temples and may range
in size from twenty or thirty to several hundred participants. The Santo Daime
has expanded beyond Brazil to establish satellite centers internationally, and
has pursued legal recognition in several countries.

## The Uniao do Vegetal (UDV)

The UDV is the largest of the Brazilian ayahuasca churches and maintains the
most formally structured ceremonies. Participants sit in rows in straight-backed
chairs during ceremonies, listening to sermons and songs delivered by maestres
who sit around a central table. The arrangement is more formal than that of the
Santo Daime, with a clearer hierarchical structure of religious authority.

The ceremony format includes a question-and-answer period, providing a
structured opportunity for members to seek guidance. Sometimes testimonials of
life transformations are offered by longer-term members, reminiscent of
Alcoholics Anonymous confessions. This element of personal testimony and public
sharing of transformation narratives serves both as a community-building
practice and as a means of reinforcing the perceived benefits of church
membership.

The UDV has been the most active in seeking legal protection in the United
States. Its American branch pursued federal litigation that resulted in a
preliminary injunction barring the US Department of Justice from enforcing drug
laws against the church.

## The Barquinia

Less well known outside of Brazil than the Santo Daime or UDV, the Barquinia
church has incorporated elements of Umbanda, an Afro-Brazilian religious
movement that involves contact with deceased ancestors and deities known as
orixas. This incorporation of African spiritual traditions alongside Amazonian
ayahuasca practice and Christian elements creates a particularly rich syncretic
blend.

Barquinia ceremonies feature both singing and dancing, and the contact with
orixas adds a dimension of ancestor veneration and spirit communication that is
less prominent in the other two churches. The Barquinia demonstrates the
flexibility of the syncretic framework: diverse spiritual traditions can be
woven together with the ayahuasca sacrament as the unifying element.

## The Bwiti Cult: An African Parallel

The Bwiti cult among the Fang people in Gabon and Zaire provides a parallel
example of [[metzner-syncretic-entheogenic-churches-brazil-bwiti]] religion outside the Americas, using iboga
rather than ayahuasca as the sacrament. Originally, the Fang, who are village
dwellers, said they learned about the iboga brew from the Pygmies, the deep
forest dwellers.

Bwiti initiation involves a powerful death-rebirth experience in which the
initiate's "head is opened up," after which they are able to converse with
ancestor spirits who can guide them in their lives. The Bwiti ceremonies are
held in temples, with an altar and officiating priests, where initiate men and
women daubed with white mud sit and sing in lengthy ceremonies. Often a mirror
is placed in the assembly room in which initiates may "see" their ancestral
spirits.

The use of iboga exists in both shamanic and syncretic religious ceremonial
forms, paralleling the dual existence of peyote use in both Huichol shamanic
practice and the organized Native American Church. This pattern suggests that
entheogenic plant medicines naturally lend themselves to both intimate healing
contexts and larger communal religious frameworks.
