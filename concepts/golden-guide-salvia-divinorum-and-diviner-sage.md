# Salvia Divinorum (Hojas de la Pastora)

Salvia divinorum, of Mexico, is the only one of 700 species of Salvia known
to be used as a hallucinogen. Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca employ the leaves as
a divinatory narcotic, hence divinorum ("of the diviners"). The Mazatecs call
the plant hojas de la Pastora in Spanish and shka-Pastora in their native
tongue, both names meaning "leaves of the Shepherdess." The leaves are chewed
fresh, or the plants are ground on a metate, then diluted with water and
filtered for drinking.

## Botanical Characteristics

The plant is not known in the wild and rarely, if ever, develops from seed.
The Mazatecs plant this mint vegetatively in remote mountain ravines, and
most families use it as a drug when the [[golden-guide-morning-glory-and-ololiuqui]]

## Connection to Aztec Pipilzintzintli

It is commonly believed that Salvia divinorum may be the pipilzintzintli
of the ancient Aztecs. The Aztec name pipilzintzintli has been translated
variously as "the noble little plant" or "the most noble little prince of
plants." Spanish chroniclers mentioned this plant in their descriptions of
Aztec religious practices, noting its use in divination and healing
ceremonies. However, the identification of pipilzintzintli with Salvia
divinorum remains uncertain, as the Aztec descriptions are not specific
enough to confirm the botanical identity with certainty.

The fact that Salvia divinorum is not known to grow in the wild and rarely
sets seed suggests that it has been cultivated by humans for a very long
time, possibly since pre-Columbian times. This characteristic is consistent
with the hypothesis that the plant has been maintained in cultivation by
indigenous peoples since the Aztec era or earlier, passed down through
generations of healers as a sacred medicinal plant.
