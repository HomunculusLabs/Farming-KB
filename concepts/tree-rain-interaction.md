# How Trees Interact with Rain

## The Destructive Impact of Rain on Bare Soil

Rain falling on bare soil or thinly spaced cultivated crops carries a devastating impact. Raindrop impact can typically remove **80 tonnes of soil per hectare**, or up to **1,000 tonnes in extreme downpours**. When we bare the soil, we lose the earth itself.

Water runoff and pan evaporation — estimated at **80–90% of all rain falling on Australia** — carries nutrients and silt to the sea or to inland basins. As land is cleared, runoff initially increases, filling dams quickly and pleasing people. But the dams will silt up, rivers will eventually cease to flow, and clearing forests results in **flood and drought** rather than a long-regulated and steady supply of clean water.

## The Forest Rain Interaction Process

When rain falls on a forest, a complex and beneficial process begins:

### 1. Canopy Shelter and Impact Nullification

The tree canopy **shelters and nullifies the impact effect of raindrops**, reducing rain to a thin mist below the canopy, even in the most torrential showers. There is slight measurable silt loss from mature forests, which is exceeded by the soil creation activity of the forest itself.

### 2. Interception

If rain is light, little penetrates beyond the canopy. A film of water spreads across leaves and stems, trapped there by surface tension. Tree cells absorb what they need, and the remainder evaporates to air. Where no rain penetrates the canopy, this is termed **"total interception."**

**Interception** is the amount of rainfall caught in the crown — the most important primary effect of trees or forests on rain.

#### Factors Influencing Interception

- **Crown thickness** — thicker canopies intercept more
- **Crown density** — denser foliage captures more water
- **Season** — deciduous trees in leafless state intercept less
- **Intensity of rain** — light rain is more readily intercepted
- **Evaporation after rain** — sunny conditions allow rapid re-evaporation

#### Interception Ranges

| Condition | Interception Level |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Least interception (thinned/deciduous forest, winter rain, heavy showers, cloudy) | ~10% of rainfall |
| Average interception | 10–15% of rainfall |
| Maximum interception (dense evergreen, light summer rain, sunny) | Up to 100% |

### 3. Throughfall

When more rain falls, or heavy rains impact the trees, water drifts as mists or droplets to earth. This water is called **throughfall**. Throughfall depends on rain intensity — there is little interception effect in heavy downpours. As an average figure, throughfall is approximately **85%** of humid conditions.

#### Throughfall as Nutrient-Rich Water

Throughfall is no longer just rainwater — it contains many plant cells and nutrients, making it a much richer brew than rainwater. Dissolved salts, organic content, dust, and plant exudates are included:

> "The results show that rain washes large amounts of potassium and smaller amounts of nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium, and magnesium from the canopies to the surface soil. Litter adds organic matter, and is a rich source of calcium and nitrogen and a moderately rich source of magnesium and potassium."
> — Murray & Mitchell, *Red Gum and the Nutrient Balance*

### 4. Specialized Water Conduits and Storage

Trees often provide special receptors, conduits, and storage systems for throughfall water. The random fall of rain is **converted into well-directed patterns of flow** that serve the needs and growth of the forest:

- **Palm and plantain stem bases** hold water as aerial ponds, often rich in algae and mosquitoes
- **Epiphytes** absorb many times their bulk of water
- **Flanged roots** of Terminalia trees and figs collect and direct water
- **Insloping branches** and **fissured bark** direct water to tap roots
- **Spider webs** catch their share of throughfall
- **Stem mosses** absorb large quantities
- **Fungi** soak up what they need
- **Weeping branches** of some species direct throughfall to fibrous peripheral roots

## The Nutrient Cycle Through Canopy

The throughfall mechanism creates a critical nutrient cycling pathway in the forest:

1. **Rain enters** the canopy from above
2. **Dust and atmospheric nutrients** are washed from leaf surfaces
3. **Plant exudates** — dissolved minerals, organic compounds — are added
4. **Enriched water** passes to the forest floor as throughfall
5. **Litter layer** receives this nutrient-rich water, adding its own organic matter
6. **Soil organisms** process the litter, making nutrients available to roots
7. **Root uptake** returns nutrients to the tree
8. **Transpiration** returns water to the atmosphere for the next cycle

This closed-loop system means that the forest is largely self-sustaining in nutrients, with additional inputs from windborne dust, bird deposits, nitrogen fixation, and mineral weathering by roots.

## Design Implications

### For Water Management

- **Preserve existing forest canopy** to maximize interception and reduce soil erosion
- **Design agroforestry systems** that mimic the layered canopy structure of natural forests
- **Use edge plantings** of dense evergreens to maximize water capture from passing rain
- **Plan for throughfall distribution** when designing tree systems for crops or gardens below

### For Nutrient Management

- **Understand that throughfall is fertilizer** — the water reaching the ground under trees is enriched with potassium, nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium, and magnesium
- **Design plant guilds** that take advantage of throughfall nutrient distribution
- **Maintain litter layers** under trees — they are the receiving end of this nutrient stream and the source of calcium and nitrogen
- **Consider epiphytes and mosses** as part of the water management system in humid climates

### For Erosion Control

- **Never leave soil bare** — the impact of rain on bare soil is devastating
- **Use canopy layers** to break raindrop energy before it reaches the soil surface
- **Design for interception** in high-rainfall areas to reduce peak runoff
- **Plant dense, multi-layered systems** rather than monocultures for maximum rain interaction benefit

## Comparison: Forest vs. Bare Ground

| Factor | Forest | Bare Ground |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Soil loss from rain | Negligible (soil creation exceeds loss) | Up to 1,000 t/ha in extreme events |
| Water runoff | Minimal; most water infiltrates | 80–90% of rain lost to runoff/evaporation |
| Rain impact on soil | Nullified by canopy | Full destructive force of raindrops |
| Water quality | Enriched with nutrients through throughfall | Carries away topsoil and nutrients |
| Long-term water supply | Steady, regulated streamflow | Flood and drought cycle |
