# Permaculture Zone Analysis

Zone analysis is a core planning tool in [[permaculture-design]] that organizes elements of a design based on
their frequency of use and the distance from the central
hub (typically the home or dwelling). Developed by Bill
Mollison, zones are an abstract but practical way to deal
with distances from the core of a design, ensuring that
high-maintenance elements are placed close at hand while
low-maintenance elements are positioned farther away.

## Understanding Zones

Zones are not literally concentric circles. They are a
convenient abstract representation of how frequently
different areas are visited. The fundamental principle is
simple: that which is farthest should require the least
number of visits. Elements that need daily attention
belong close to the house; elements needing only annual
attention can be placed at the periphery.

Mollison emphasized that zones are not bounded. They
represent relative distance and frequency of interaction,
not fixed boundaries. A zone one area doesn't really
extend more than 20 to 30 feet from the foundation of
the house, and not from all of the foundation.

## Zone One: The Intensive Garden

Zone one is the area of daily presence, around the
entries to the house and along frequently traveled
pathways. It contains high-energy, high-production units:

- Soft herbs and salad greens picked daily
- Frequent-harvest vegetables like broccoli and beans
- Small ponds for watercress and frog habitat
- Composting areas close to the kitchen
- Chickens, ducklings, and small animals needing care

Zone one should be fully mulched with no bare soil, well
bordered against weeds, and protected from browsing
animals. The key principle: if you can see it from your
window and walk to it in slippers, you will actually
harvest it.

## Zone Two: Main Crop Gardens

Zone two contains crops harvested less frequently. These
may include:

- Main crop vegetables (tomatoes, peppers, squash)
- Bramble fruits like raspberries
- Asparagus and other perennial vegetables
- Orchard trees needing regular attention
- Chickens and larger poultry

Zone two may be row-cropped and need not be fully mulched
if free mulch is not available. It is visited several
times per week rather than daily.

## Zone Three: Field Crops

Zone three contains broad-acre crops and orchards visited
only occasionally during the growing season. These include
grain crops, large orchards, pastured poultry, and
livestock grazing systems. Minimal management with
periodic attention.

## Zone Four: Forage and Timber

Zone four is for forage systems, timber production, and
large-scale stock management. It is visited perhaps a few
times per year for harvesting firewood, gathering forage,
or checking livestock.

## Zone Five: Wilderness

Zone five is the unmanaged wilderness area, left entirely
to natural processes. It serves as a wildlife corridor,
genetic reservoir, and reference ecosystem. Even small
properties benefit from dedicating some area to wild
nature.

## Designing with Zones

Effective zone analysis considers:
- The actual patterns of human movement on site
- Age and physical ability of the residents
- Available time for garden management
- Changes in capacity over time (permaculture designs
  adjust to your age; yield increases while digging
  decreases)
- Access paths that connect zones efficiently

## Common Mistakes

Mollison identified a critical error: placing the garden
too far from the house. Many people build a house, then
hunt for a garden site, ending up with a garden hidden
behind the house where it gets neglected. Plants that
are not under your eye and not along your daily paths
simply do not get harvested.

## Connecting Zones to Sectors

Zone analysis works in tandem with [[permaculture-sector-analysis]]. While zones organize elements by distance
and frequency of use, sectors map external energies such
as sun, wind, fire risk, and water flow. Together, they
provide a complete picture for intelligent placement of
every element in the design.
