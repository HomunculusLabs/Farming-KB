# Cannabinoid Concentration Correlation with [[cannabis-endorhiza-microbiome-structure]]

## Overview

One of the most intriguing findings of the Winston et al. (2014) study was the significant
correlation between cannabinoid concentration and composition and the structure of the
Cannabis [[core-endorhiza-bacterial-community-composition-cannabis]]. However, this correlation is confounded by the
simultaneous influence of [[soil-physicochemical-properties-cannabis-microbiome-assembly-winston]] on both cannabinoid production
and [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]], making it difficult to establish a direct causal
relationship between the plant's [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] and its root microbiome.

## The Cannabinoid-Microbiome Correlation

### Mantel Test Results
Cannabinoid data from the second experiment — including delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol
(THC) and other cannabinoid measurements — was correlated with endorhiza community
composition using Mantel tests. The analysis revealed a statistically significant
correlation between cannabinoid profiles and [[weighted-unifrac-strain-clustering-cannabis-endorhiza-community-structure]] for
unweighted analysis (r-stat: 0.863, p-value = 0.001). This suggests that the types
of bacteria present in the root interior are associated with the cannabinoid profile
of the plant.

### The Confounding Problem: Soil-Driven Cannabinoid Variation
The critical caveat is that plants from one of the two soil types in the second
experiment had both higher THC composition and higher THC concentration than plants
from the other soil type. THC variables were also significantly correlated with the
soil edaphic variables (pH, nitrogen, carbon, salinity, water content). This creates
a confounding triangle:
- Soil type affects cannabinoid production (known agronomic factor)
- Soil type affects microbial community structure (strongly demonstrated in the study)
- Cannabinoids and microbiome appear correlated, but both are driven by soil

As the authors note, "any association between microbiota and THC is very hard to
disassociate from soil physicochemical variables." This means the observed correlation
between cannabinoids and the endorhiza microbiome may be an indirect effect of shared
soil influence rather than a direct plant-microbe interaction mediated by cannabinoids.

## Potential Mechanisms for Direct Cannabinoid-Microbiome Interactions

Despite the confounding issue, several plausible mechanisms could explain a direct
relationship between cannabinoid production and endorhiza community structure:

### Root Exudate Modulation
Cannabinoids and their precursors may be exuded into the rhizosphere through root
secretions, where they could directly influence microbial community assembly. Root
exudates are a primary driver of rhizosphere microbiome composition, and the specific
chemical profile of exudates varies among plant species and cultivars. If Cannabis
cultivars produce different blends of cannabinoid-related compounds in their root
exudates, this could select for different microbial communities.

### Antimicrobial Properties of Cannabinoids
Cannabinoids, particularly THC and cannabidiol (CBD), have demonstrated antimicrobial
properties against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Differential
cannabinoid concentrations in root tissues could create selective pressure favouring
cannabinoid-resistant bacterial strains, thereby shaping the endorhiza community.
Bacteria that can tolerate or metabolize cannabinoids would have a competitive advantage
in the root interior of high-THC cultivars.

### Metabolic Cross-Feeding
Some endorhiza bacteria may metabolize cannabinoid precursors or degradation products
as carbon or nitrogen sources. If different cultivars produce different profiles of
secondary metabolites, this could support different metabolic niches within the root
interior. The presence of Methylophilus specifically enriched in Bookoo Kush suggests
that certain cultivars may create unique metabolic environments that support specialist
bacteria.

### Plant Immune System Mediation
Cannabinoid biosynthesis shares precursors with other secondary metabolic pathways
that are involved in plant defence signalling. Cultivars with different cannabinoid
profiles may have different basal immune activation states, which could in turn affect
which bacteria are permitted to colonize the root interior. A highly defended root
environment might exclude more bacterial taxa, resulting in a less diverse but more
specialized endorhiza community.
