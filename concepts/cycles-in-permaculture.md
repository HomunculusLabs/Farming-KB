# Cycles in Permaculture

Cycles are fundamental organizing principles in [[bill-mollison]]
describes a cycle as **"a niche in time"** — a recurring event or phenomenon that
represents a diversion from the inevitable trend of entropy. Living systems
capture, transform, and redirect energy and materials through repeated loops,
creating the conditions for sustained productivity and increasing complexity.

## Life as the Engine of Cycling

Living organisms occupy a special role in cycling materials. In a mature tropical
forest, nearly all material nutrients are held and cycled within living forms —
from canopy to forest floor and back again. Life does not merely participate in
cycles; it actively constructs and accelerates them.

### Geese as a Model Cycle

Mollison uses geese as a vivid illustration of a functional nutrient cycle:

- Geese graze on grasses, converting plant material into flesh and energy
- Digestive systems process and concentrate nutrients
- Defecation returns enriched waste to the soil surface
- Fungi, bacteria, and grass roots work on the waste material
- [[composting]], succession, polyculture grazing)

The balance between these determines the **number of opportunities** in any
system. **Biocides** destroy the organisms responsible for [[decomposition]] and
nutrient transfer, collapsing cycling opportunity entirely.

## People and Cycles

Human beings are themselves cycling agents, exchanging air, soils, foods,
minerals, and pathogens with their environment. Mollison extends the computing
maxim — **"garbage in, garbage out"** — to people: the quality of inputs
directly shapes the quality of outputs.

### Creating Local Ecology

Like wombats burrowing and modifying their landscape, people can deliberately
create their own local ecology. Waste products — food scraps, manure, greywater
— can be designed to build soil rather than pollute.

## Dislocation from Cycles

Mollison contrasts tribal peoples, who remain tied to specific soils and
landscapes, with modern people who have been forcibly dislocated from their
ecological contexts. This dislocation manifests through:

- **Travel** — rapid movement causes stress, spreads pathogens, and introduces
  species to new environments where they may become invasive
- **Post-invasion evolution** — introduced species assemble into novel
  ecological communities with entirely new potentials

## Nutrient Cycling in Different Climates

### Tropical Systems

Nutrient cycling in the tropics is essentially **continuous** — warmth and
moisture allow decomposition and growth year-round. Interruptions come from
drought, cold events, nutrient-poor parent materials, and human interference.

### Cycle Schedules

Cycles operate across vastly different timescales:

- **Day/night** — frequent, driving photosynthesis, respiration, and behavior
- **Seasonal** — annual, governing growth, reproduction, and dormancy
- **Sunspot cycles** — long-term (~11 years), influencing climate and ecology

### Aboriginal Time Maps

Aboriginal peoples read cycle-indicators — flowering times, animal migrations,
star positions — as **time maps** coordinating human activity with ecological
process. This deep attunement is a model for [[permaculture]] design: observe and
work with existing temporal patterns rather than imposing arbitrary schedules.

## Philosophical Dimensions

Mollison embeds a philosophical vision within his discussion of cycles:

- We literally **contain molecules of Einstein** — every atom in our bodies has
  been part of countless other living beings and geological processes
- We are **components of all things**, interconnected across time and space
- The distinction between a **bishop and a goose** is, in material terms,
  superficial — both participate in the same cycling systems

This reinforces the permaculture ethic of recognizing our embeddedness in
ecological systems and designing accordingly.

## Key Principles

- Maximize the number of regenerative cycles in any design
- Design for nutrient capture and reuse at every opportunity
- Avoid interventions (especially biocides) that collapse cycling organisms
- Use animals as designed nutrient concentrators
- Learn from indigenous cycle-reading and time-mapping practices
- Recognize that human health depends on the quality of cycles we participate in
