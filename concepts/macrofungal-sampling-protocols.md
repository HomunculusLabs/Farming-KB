# Macrofungal Sampling Protocols and Collection Methods

## Preliminary Research

Before initiating a survey or monitoring program of any group of
organisms, an investigator should carry out some preliminary background
research. Essential materials include maps of the area and descriptions
of its climate, geology, and vegetation. Learning to recognize the
woody plant species and major plant associations likely to be
encountered is particularly important. Knowledge of the anticipated
species diversity and distributions of the macrofungi is also very
helpful.

## Factors Influencing Species Richness

### Seasonality and Annual Variation

Within a geographic region, fruiting is influenced by elevation and
latitude and their effects on temperature and precipitation. Different
species exhibit different fruiting phenologies, which vary from year to
year. Maximum richness of fruiting species occurs only during brief
periods and differs among years. Only 5% to 20% of the ectomycorrhizal
species encountered at sites in Olympic National Park fruited in 2
successive years.

### Temperature and Precipitation

Fungi fruit when temperatures are above freezing and moisture is
available. Temperature also has a major impact on macrofungi fruiting,
an effect that may not be limited to the fruiting season. Warm summers
increased production of mycorrhizal sporocarps in spruce forests but
had no effect on sporocarp production in pine forests. Excess moisture
also can prevent fruiting in some species.

### Long-Term Studies

The challenge of measuring species diversity of macrofungi increases
in years when many species fruit simultaneously. Some species may fruit
only 1 year out of 4 or more, underscoring the need for long-term (at
least 5-year, preferably 10-year) studies. Species richness estimators
did not stabilize during a 21-year study of fungal fruiting phenology
in Switzerland. See [[fungal-biodiversity-forest-ecosystems]] for analysis.

## Vegetation Effects

The type of vegetation in an area affects the species richness and
composition of macrofungi. Grasslands, deserts, forests, tundra, and
other habitats all have characteristic species. Plant species
composition also influences the number and species of macrofungi
because plants constitute the habitat and energy source for most fungi.
Vegetation zones or plant associations are useful criteria to use when
dividing a landscape for sampling.

## Geographic Considerations

Mycologists with broad geographic experience were surveyed to determine
what factors most strongly affect diversity. For the fungi treated in
this chapter, diversity of habitats rather than geography was believed
to have the strongest influence on fungal species richness. In much of
Europe, tree diversity is low, and Europe was ranked lowest in fungal
diversity when compared with other regions, although it has been
collected most intensively.

## Succession

Succession of macrofungi must be considered from several perspectives.
There are successions of sporocarp production on particular substrata,
although all species may be present from the beginning. Herbivore dung,
for example, has characteristic fungi that fruit consecutively over
time. Ectomycorrhizal fungi on host root systems also exhibit
succession: some species fruit in very young stands of trees, some are
restricted to older stands, and some occur in stands of all ages.

## Collection Methods

### Plot Design

The investigator must choose where, when, and how to collect data.
Field studies are particularly sensitive to timing and location of
observations. If a measure of species richness is the goal, then
baseline data for a site may involve intense collecting over a period
of several days when sporocarp production is high. Ideally, one samples
fleshy macrofungi every 1-2 weeks throughout the fruiting season.

### Documentation

Thorough documentation of each specimen is essential. This includes
recording the date, location (with coordinates and elevation), habitat
description, associated plant species, substratum, and observable
features of the sporocarp. Color photographs should be taken in the
field. Notes on weather conditions preceding the collection are also
valuable. Herbarium specimens must be prepared carefully to preserve
diagnostic characters.

### Preservation

Sporocarps should be preserved promptly after collection. Methods
include air-drying, desiccant drying, or freezing. Drying should be
done at moderate temperatures to prevent degradation of sporocarp DNA.
Preserved specimens serve as vouchers for future reference and
molecular analysis. All specimens must be labeled with unique accession
numbers and linked to field data records.

## Equipment

Essential field equipment includes collection bags or boxes, a pocket
knife, hand lens, camera, GPS unit, field notebook, measuring tape,
and appropriate clothing. For more detailed documentation, a
temperature probe and soil moisture meter may be useful. Specimen
labels should be pre-printed or prepared in the field with indelible
ink. Aluminum foil is useful for wrapping delicate specimens.

## Taxonomic Resources

Identification of macrofungi requires access to appropriate taxonomic
resources. For basidiomycetes, monographic treatments and regional
floras are essential. For ascomycetes, fewer comprehensive field
guides exist. Modern identification increasingly relies on molecular
techniques, especially for sterile or morphologically ambiguous
specimens. Herbarium collections provide critical comparative material
for identification of newly collected specimens.
