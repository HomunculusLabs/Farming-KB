## Mycorrhizal Types and Associations

## Overview

Mycorrhizae are symbiotic associations between fungi and plant roots that are among the most widespread and ecologically important mutualisms on Earth. Approximately **95% of vascular plants** form [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] (Brundrett 1991), making them a nearly universal feature of terrestrial plant ecology. These associations play critical roles [[comparison-soil-protozoa-vs-nematodes-in-nutrient-cycling]], primary production, soil formation, and [[biodiversity-specialization-ecosystem-stability]].

The seven recognized [[mycorrhizal-types]] — arbuscular, ecto-, ectendo-, ericoid, arbutoid, orchidaceous, and monotropoid — differ fundamentally in their anatomy, host specificity, [[fungal-taxonomy]], and ecological function. Understanding these distinctions is essential for interpreting belowground processes in any ecosystem.

## Ecosystem Significance

### Contribution to Net Primary Production

Mycorrhizae make substantial contributions to ecosystem productivity. Vogt et al. (1982) estimated that mycorrhizal tissues account for approximately **14–15% of net primary production**. These figures highlight that belowground carbon allocation to mycorrhizal fungi represents one of the largest single fluxes in forest carbon budgets, rivaling or exceeding aboveground litterfall in many systems. The contribution rises considerably when mycorrhizal biomass is combined with fine root allocation: up to **45% in young stands** and **75% in mature forest stands**. These values underscore the enormous carbon and biomass investment that ecosystems allocate to maintaining belowground fungal symbioses.

### Functional Role in Ecosystem Development

Pankow et al. (1991) proposed that mycorrhizae are most important during the **protective and maintenance stages** of ecosystem development, rather than in early productive stages. In this framework, mycorrhizal fungi function as key regulators of **nutrient cycling**, mediating the transfer of nutrients from decomposing organic matter back to living plants. This positions mycorrhizae as central nodes in the decomposition-mineralization-uptake pathway.

## Major Mycorrhizal Types

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae (AM)

Arbuscular mycorrhizae are formed by fungi in the **Glomeromycota** (formerly classified as zygomycetes) and are the most common mycorrhizal type worldwide.

The term "arbuscular" derives from the Latin *arbusculum* (small tree), referring to the tree-like branching of the arbuscule within the host cell. AM fungi are obligate biotrophs — they cannot complete their life cycle without a host plant — and are among the oldest known symbiotic fungi, with fossil evidence dating back over 450 million years to the earliest land plants.

**Host range:** Herbaceous plants, grasses, and tropical trees. AM associations are formed with a very broad range of plant species but involve relatively few fungal species — a striking asymmetry in specificity.

**[[metzner-shamanic-ceremony-versus-psychotherapy-structural-features]]:**
- **Arbuscules** — highly branched hyphal structures that form within root cortical cells, serving as the primary site of nutrient exchange between fungus and plant
- **Vesicles** — swollen hyphal compartments used for lipid storage, typically found in the root cortex
- Extensive extraradical hyphal networks extending into the surrounding soil

**Identification:** Fungal species are primarily identified by **spore anatomy**, as the internal structures within roots offer limited diagnostic value for distinguishing among AM fungal taxa.

### Ectomycorrhizae (ECM)

Ectomycorrhizae are formed predominantly by fungi in the **Basidiomycota** and **Ascomycota**, with some zygomycete representatives.

ECM associations are the dominant mycorrhizal type in many temperate and boreal forest ecosystems, where they can form extensive belowground networks — sometimes called "[[common-mycorrhizal-networks]]" (CMNs) — that connect multiple individual trees. These networks facilitate carbon and nutrient transfer between trees and have been implicated in forest regeneration, seedling establishment, and interplant signaling.

**Host range:** Primarily coniferous and deciduous trees in temperate and boreal forests. Unlike AM, ECM associations involve a relatively narrow range of plant host species but encompass an enormous diversity of fungal taxa — the inverse specificity pattern compared to AM.

**Structural features:**
- **Sheath (mantle)** — a dense layer of fungal tissue that encases the root surface, physically separating the root from the soil
- **Hartig net** — a network of hyphae that penetrates between cortical cells (but remains outside the endodermis), forming the main interface for nutrient exchange
- Extraradical hyphae and often conspicuous fruiting bodies (mushrooms)

**Identification:** Morphological identification relies on characteristics of the sheath structure, branching patterns, and rhizomorph morphology (Agerer 1987–1999 provides comprehensive morphological atlases).

### Ectendomycorrhizae

Ectendomycorrhizae exhibit a combination of both **ectomycorrhizal and [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]]** characteristics (Laiho & Mikola 1964). They possess a fungal sheath on the root surface and a Hartig net, but also penetrate cortical cells in a manner somewhat analogous to arbuscule formation. This intermediate morphology places them at the boundary between the two dominant mycorrhizal categories.

### Ericoid Mycorrhizae

Ericoid mycorrhizae are an ecologically specialized type restricted to the **Ericaceae family** — plants such as heaths, heathers, and blueberries that are typically sclerophyllous evergreens growing in **nitrogen- and phosphorus-poor habitats** (bogs, heathlands, acidic soils).

**Structural features:**
- **Hyphal coils** within cortical cells (distinct from the tree-like arbuscules of AM)
- No external sheath or Hartig net

**Fungal partners:** Only a few fungal genera form ericoid associations, most notably *Hymenoscyphus* and *Oidiodendron*. Despite this limited taxonomic diversity, ericoid mycorrhizae are critical for plant survival in some of the most nutrient-impoverished environments on Earth.

### Arbutoid Mycorrhizae

Arbutoid mycorrhizae are found in plants of the genus **Arbutus** (and a few related genera in the Ericaceae subfamily Arbutoideae). Structurally, they form **hyphal coils** within cortical cells but also possess a fungal sheath, showing affinities with both ericoid and ectomycorrhizal types.

### Orchidaceous Mycorrhizae

Orchidaceous mycorrhizae represent an **obligate association** — the fungal partner is essential for the plant's survival. The fungus (typically *Rhizoctonia*) is **transported within the seed** and is required for seed germination and early seedling establishment. Orchid seeds contain minimal endosperm and cannot germinate without fungal carbohydrate supply, making this one of the most dependent plant-fungal mutualisms known.

This extreme dependency has profound ecological and evolutionary implications. Many orchids remain dependent on fungal associates even as adults, and some achlorophyllous (non-photosynthetic) orchid species are entirely mycoheterotrophic throughout their lives, obtaining all carbon from fungal partners who themselves are often connected to surrounding trees via ECM or AM networks.

## Mycorrhizal Ecosystem Services

Mycorrhizal fungi provide a wide array of ecosystem services that extend far beyond individual plant nutrition:

| Ecosystem Service | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Soil formation** | Rock dissolution through organic acid secretion; physical binding of soil particles by hyphal networks |
| **Soil fertility** | Decomposition of organic matter; mineralization of nutrients (especially N and P) |
| **Soil stability** | Aggregation of soil particles; prevention of erosion through hyphal and rhizomorph networks |
| **Primary production** | Direct contribution to NPP; enhanced nutrient access (especially P); increased plant yield |
| **Defense** | Protection against soil-borne pathogens; deterrence of herbivory |
| **Plant community structure** | Influencing competitive hierarchies and species coexistence through differential mycorrhizal benefits |
| **Secondary production** | Supporting soil food webs (fungivorous microfauna, invertebrates) |
| **Pollutant modification** | Degradation or sequestration of organic and inorganic contaminants |
| **Carbon sequestration** | Contribution to stable soil carbon pools through recalcitrant fungal biomass and glomalin production |

## Comparative Summary

The diversity of mycorrhizal types reflects adaptations to different environmental conditions and host plant lineages. A useful general pattern is the inverse relationship between **host plant breadth** and **fungal taxonomic diversity**: AM associations cover the most plant species with the fewest fungi, while ECM associations cover fewer plant species but involve a spectacular diversity of fungal taxa. Ericoid and orchidaceous types occupy specialized ecological niches with narrow host ranges and limited fungal partners.

| Feature | AM | ECM | Ericoid | Orchidaceous |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fungal phyla** | Glomeromycota | Basidiomycota, Ascomycota | Ascomycota | Basidiomycota |
| **Host breadth** | Very broad | Narrow (trees) | Ericaceae only | Orchidaceae only |
| **Fungal diversity** | Low | Very high | Low | Moderate |
| **Key structure** | Arbuscules + vesicles | Sheath + Hartig net | Hyphal coils | Hyphal coils |
| **Cell penetration** | Yes (arbuscules) | No (intercellular Hartig net) | Yes (coils) | Yes (coils) |
| **Obligate?** | Mostly facultative | Mostly facultative | Facultative | Obligate |
