# Substrate Contamination

Contamination is the presence of unwanted organisms in a mushroom substrate
that compete with or parasitize the cultivated [[mycelium]]. It is the primary
cause of crop failure in [[mushroom-cultivation-fruiting-chambers]], and managing it is the
central challenge of every cultivation operation, from home growers to
commercial facilities.

## Categories of Contaminants

### Mold Contaminants

Molds are the most visible and common competitors in mushroom substrates:

- **Trichoderma** (green mold) — the most aggressive competitor in
  mushroom cultivation. Rapidly colonizes substrates, outcompeting the
  intended mycelium. Spores are ubiquitous in indoor environments. Favored by
  high humidity, poor ventilation, and excess supplementation. Infected blocks
  show dense green patches that spread quickly. Often introduced via
  insufficiently pasteurized substrate or contaminated grain spawn.
- **Aspergillus** — produces yellow, green, or black colonies depending on
  species. *A. niger* (black) and *A. flavus* (yellow-green) are most common
  in cultivation. Some species produce mycotoxins. Thrives in warm
  conditions (25-35°C).
- **Penicillium** — blue-green mold that grows slowly but steadily.
  Often indicates pH is too low or substrate is too acidic.
- **Neurospora** (pink mold) — produces bright orange-pink, rapidly
  expanding colonies. Extremely prolific spore production. Common in tropical
  cultivation. Can spread through an entire facility within days if not
  contained. Substrate should be discarded immediately.
- **Mucor / Rhizopus** (pin mold) — gray, fuzzy growth that can overrun
  substrates. Often indicates anaerobic pockets or excessively wet substrate.

### Bacterial Contaminants

Bacteria thrive in wet, anaerobic conditions:

- **Bacterial blotch** (*Pseudomonas tolaasii*) — causes brown, sunken
  lesions on mushroom caps during fruiting. Spread by splashing water.
  Reduced by lowering surface humidity and improving air circulation.
- **Bacterial wet bubble** — slimy, foul-smelling substrate conditions.
  Caused by over-wet substrate (>70% moisture) or poor drainage.
- **Endospore-forming bacteria** — survive pasteurization temperatures.
  Common in straw that was baled wet or stored improperly.

### Yeast Contaminants

Yeasts produce a powdery or slimy layer on substrate surfaces. They compete
for nutrients but rarely kill established mycelium. Often indicate excess
moisture or insufficient gas exchange during colonization.

### Insect Pests

While not "contamination" in the microbiological sense, pests often introduce
or exacerbate contamination:

- **Fungus gnats** — larvae feed on mycelium and create entry wounds for
  bacterial infection. Adults vector mold spores between containers.
- **Phorid flies** — similar to fungus gnats but smaller and more
  difficult to exclude. Particularly problematic in commercial operations.
- **Mites** — microscopic arthropods that feed on both mycelium and mold.
  Can transport Trichoderma spores between blocks.

## Sources of Contamination

Understanding where contaminants originate is essential for prevention:

1. **Raw materials** — straw, sawdust, and grain may carry mold spores,
  bacteria, or pest eggs. Proper [[oyster-mushroom-contamination-prevention]]
  mitigates this.
2. **Spawn** — contaminated grain spawn is the most efficient vector because
  it spreads the contaminant throughout the substrate during inoculation.
  Always inspect spawn before use.
3. **Air** — airborne spores settle on exposed substrate surfaces. This is
  why sealing containers during [[mycelial-colonization]] and using filtered
   air during transfers is critical.
4. **Water** — standing water, dirty humidifiers, and contaminated misting
  systems can introduce bacteria and mold spores to the fruiting environment.
5. **Tools and surfaces** — unsterilized tools, gloves, and work surfaces
  transfer organisms between batches. Regular cleaning with 70% isopropyl
  alcohol or 10% bleach solutions is standard practice.
6. **The operator** — clothing, hair, and respiration carry spores. Face
  masks and clean garments reduce this vector, especially during inoculation.

## Prevention Strategy

Contamination management follows the hierarchy of controls:

### Elimination
- Source clean, high-quality raw materials
- Use proven, vigorously growing spawn
- Pasteurize or sterilize substrates thoroughly

### Barrier Controls
- Work in a clean area (still air box for hobbyists; laminar flow hood for
  commercial spawn production)
- Seal containers immediately after inoculation
- Use HEPA-filtered intake air in grow rooms
- Install insect screens on all vents and openings

### Process Controls
- Maintain proper substrate moisture (60-65%)
- Keep colonization temperature in the optimal range for the species
- Monitor CO₂ and humidity to avoid conditions that favor competitors
- Implement strict hygiene protocols between batches
- Quarantine and discard contaminated blocks immediately — never open them
  in the grow room

### Biological Controls
- Some growers use beneficial bacteria (*Bacillus subtilis*) as a substrate
  supplement to suppress Trichoderma colonization
- Proper pasteurization preserves thermophilic bacteria that naturally
  inhibit competitor organisms

## Decision Framework: Save or Discard

When contamination is detected:

- **Small, isolated mold spot** on the surface of an otherwise healthy block
  during colonization: cut away the affected area and apply salt paste. Risky
  but sometimes successful.
- **Trichoderma or Neurospora** anywhere in the grow room: discard the block
  immediately in a sealed bag. Do not open — the spore release will
  contaminate future batches.
- **Bacterial blotch** on fruiting mushrooms: harvest affected mushrooms,
  reduce humidity, improve airflow. Substrate may continue producing.
- **Widespread contamination** (>25% of block surface): discard. The energy
  cost of trying to salvage a compromised block exceeds the value of any
  potential harvest.
