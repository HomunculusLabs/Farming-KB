## The Fivefold Water-Saving Strategy

Hemenway outlines five factors that determine a successful water
harvesting system:

1. **Rainfall patterns**: How much rain falls, and when. Rain that
   reliably replenishes storage every few weeks dictates a different
   approach than regions with regular two-month droughts
2. **Water consumption**: Can be greatly reduced through drip
   irrigation, watering only when needed, and planting drought-adapted
   species
3. **Catchment area**: Roof area, pavement, and other hard surfaces can
   all serve as catchment. Some creative gardeners even divert street
   gutter water into swales to fill ponds and irrigate trees
4. **Storage capacity**: Budget, space, and aesthetics determine
   whether tanks (expensive but compact) or ponds (attractive but larger)
   are most appropriate
5. **Placement relative to catchment**: Using gravity whenever possible
   eliminates the need for pumps. Storage placed higher than the garden
   allows gravity-powered irrigation

Note that all factors except rainfall are controllable, giving the
gardener considerable leverage in designing an effective system.

## Calculating Roof Collection

To estimate how much water your roof will collect:

- A = area of roof (ground footprint, not total sloping area)
- R = rainfall in inches per year
- Calculate: (A times R) divided by 12 = cubic feet of water per year
- Multiply by 7.5 to convert to gallons

Example: a 30-by-36-foot roof (1,080 square feet) in a region with
35 inches of annual rainfall collects about 23,625 gallons per year.

## Pond Design Principles

Ponds are a particularly appealing way to store water. Beyond practical
benefits, the sight of sparkling water edged by lush greenery on a hot
day seems to drop the temperature by ten degrees.

### The Secret of Depth

The secret of storing water in a pond is depth. By digging a 2-by-12-foot
garden pond 4 feet deep instead of the usual 2, you can store over 4,000
gallons of water. This provides a substantial irrigation reservoir while
maintaining enough water for aquatic plants and fish. A practical
approach is to maintain one pond strictly for irrigation and a second,
smaller one for fish and ornamental plants.

### Tom Ward's Integrated System

Forestry consultant and permaculturist Tom Ward built a 3,000-gallon
pond on the uphill side of his vegetable garden in Ashland, Oregon,
demonstrating integrated water design. The pond is fed by downspouts
from neighboring houses. A swale runs alongside the pond, and overflow
dribbles over a rock waterfall into the swale. The water is captured by
the level swale and sinks into the soil, creating a subsurface irrigation
system. The expanding lens of subterranean wetness moves downhill toward
the vegetable garden like a slow underground tide. At the swale's outlet
end, blueberry bushes capture any remaining water.

This system integrates pond, swale, garden, and berry bushes into a
healthy, smoothly functioning whole that substantially reduces reliance
on municipal water. It exemplifies how [[hemenway-edge-effect-in-garden-design]], where
maximizing edge increases habitat diversity and productive planting area.

## Greywater Integration

Greywater from sinks, showers, and laundry is nearly pure but unfit for
direct human reuse. Plants and soil organisms will gratefully accept the
watery part and eagerly consume the dissolved contents as food. Reusing
greywater closes a loop in the household ecosystem, creating a tight
cycle that uses far less energy than the conventional linear flow of
clean water in and dirty water out to a sewage system.

## Swales and Contour

Swales are shallow ditches dug on contour to catch and hold rainwater,
allowing it to sink into the soil rather than running off. In
[[hemenway-zone-and-sector-planning]], swales are typically placed on
contour in outer zones to passively irrigate food forests and orchards.
They connect to the broader water-harvesting strategy, working alongside
roof catchment, ponds, and greywater systems to create a garden where
drought and flood are the exceptions rather than the rule.

In a well-designed garden, perfectly watered plants, neither parched
nor drowned, are the natural state. A healthy garden tunes its
relationship with water toward harmony, using rich soil, ponds, wetlands,
and the intelligence of the gardener to survive both drying and drenching.
