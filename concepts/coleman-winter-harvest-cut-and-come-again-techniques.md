# Winter Harvest Techniques and Cut-and-Come-Again Methods

## Overview

The winter harvest system developed by Eliot Coleman at Four Season Farm in Harborside, Maine, relies heavily on cut-and-come-again harvesting as the primary method for maintaining continuous production through the coldest months. This technique transforms cold-hardy leaf crops from single-harvest vegetables into renewable salad factories that can be harvested repeatedly throughout the winter. The method is central to the economic viability of unheated winter greenhouse production because it maximizes yield per square foot while minimizing the labor and resources needed for repeated planting cycles.

## The Cut-and-Come-Again Principle

Cut-and-come-again harvesting involves cutting the above-ground portion of leafy greens at a height of 1-2 inches above the soil surface, leaving the growing crown and root system intact. The plant then regrows from the remaining crown, producing a second (and often third, fourth, or fifth) flush of leaves. This approach works because:

- The root system remains undisturbed and fully functional
- The crown contains meristematic tissue capable of producing new leaves
- Cold-hardy species have evolved to survive repeated defoliation from herbivory and weather
- The protected greenhouse environment prevents desiccation that would kill cut plants outdoors

Coleman describes this as the backbone of midwinter production: "In midwinter the vigorous regrowth on cut-and-come-again crops provides the harvest while late-fall-and-winter-sown crops slowly reach productive size."

## Species Selection for Cut-and-Come-Again

Not all crops respond equally well to repeated harvesting. Coleman identifies the best performers:

**Top performers** (most regrowth cycles):
- **Mache (corn salad)**: Extremely cold-tolerant; regrows slowly but reliably through multiple cuts
- **Claytonia (miner's lettuce)**: Prolific regrower; can be cut 4-6 times per season
- **Spinach**: Excellent regrowth in cold conditions; best when harvested at baby leaf stage
- **Mizuna**: Japanese mustard green; regrows rapidly with good flavor
- **Tatsoi**: Similar to mizuna with spoon-shaped leaves; 3-4 cuts per plant
- **Arugula**: Spicy green that regrows well if not cut too low
- **Lettuce (leaf types)**: Loose-leaf and oak-leaf varieties work best; cut above growing point

**Moderate performers** (2-3 cuts):
- **Chard**: Regrows from the base; older leaves become tough if left too long
- **Mustard greens**: Good regrowth but can become overly spicy in cold
- **Pak choi**: Cut individual leaves rather than whole heads for best results
- **Kale**: Regrows from leaf axils; best when outer leaves are harvested individually
- **Scallions**: Cut above the white base; regrows 2-3 times

**Poor performers** (single harvest or limited regrowth):
- **Radicchio**: Forms a tight head; does not regrow after cutting
- **Endive/Escarole**: Better as a cut-once crop
- **Beet greens**: Can be cut once; regrowth is slow and of lower quality

## Harvest Timing and Frequency

The timing of cut-and-come-again harvests is critical during winter months:

**Growth rate considerations**: In the depths of winter (December-January at Four Season Farm), growth rates slow dramatically due to short day length. Coleman notes that when day length drops below 10 hours, plant growth essentially pauses. During this period, harvests must be spaced further apart—typically 4-6 weeks between cuts—because the plants simply cannot regrow quickly enough for more frequent cutting.

**Seasonal harvest frequency**:
- October-November: Every 2-3 weeks (plants still growing actively)
- December-January: Every 4-6 weeks (growth minimal due to short days)
- February-March: Every 2-3 weeks (day length increasing, growth accelerating)
- April-May: Every 1-2 weeks (rapid spring growth)

**Cutting height**: Always leave at least 1 inch of leaf material above the growing point. Cutting too low kills the crown meristem and prevents regrowth. Coleman recommends cutting at approximately 1.5 inches for most species.

**Time of day**: Harvest in the late morning after frost has lifted from the inner row-cover layer. Leaves harvested while frozen will wilt upon thawing. The double-layer protection system (greenhouse plus inner row cover) prevents the plants from freezing solid, but early morning temperatures under the inner layer may still be near freezing.

## The Baby Leaf Strategy

Coleman strongly advocates harvesting at the baby leaf stage for winter salad production. Baby leaf crops offer several advantages:

- **Faster turnaround**: Baby leaves reach harvestable size in 3-4 weeks rather than the 6-8 weeks needed for full-size plants
- **Better flavor**: Young leaves are more tender and less bitter than mature leaves
- **Higher quality**: No tough stems or fibrous midribs
- **More cuts per planting**: Smaller plants allocate more energy to regrowth
- **Space efficiency**: Baby leaf crops can be planted at higher density

The baby leaf approach is fundamental to Coleman's winter salad mix production, which combines multiple species for flavor, texture, and visual appeal.

## Coleman's Winter Salad Mix

The cut-and-come-again system supports a diverse salad mix harvested from multiple beds:

**Base greens** (mild flavor, bulk):
- Spinach, lettuce, mache, claytonia

**Accent greens** (spicy/peppery):
- Arugula, mustard, mizuna, tatsoi

**Visual accents** (color and texture):
- Red chard, beet greens, radicchio spears, purple mustard

Coleman aims for never leaving a greenhouse bed unplanted. Within 24 hours of final harvest from a bed, the residues are removed, the soil is re-prepared, and new crops are planted. This intensive rotation maximizes the productive capacity of each square foot.

## Knife Technique and Harvest Quality

Proper cutting technique affects both regrowth and product quality:

- Use a sharp harvest knife or scissors; dull blades crush tissue and slow regrowth
- Cut cleanly across the bed in a single pass; avoid ragged or angled cuts
- Harvest into clean containers; avoid piling leaves too deep which causes heating and wilting
- Move cut leaves immediately to cold storage; even in winter, direct sun exposure after cutting causes rapid deterioration
- Leave no cut leaf debris on the bed surface; remove all trimmings to prevent disease and pest harborage

## Post-Harvest Handling

Winter-harvested greens require careful handling despite the cold ambient temperatures:

- **Cooling**: Move to cold storage immediately; target 34-36°F (1-2°C)
- **Moisture**: Light misting before storage prevents desiccation without promoting rot
- **Packaging**: Use ventilated containers or bags to allow gas exchange
- **Shelf life**: Properly handled winter greens last 7-14 days in cold storage, often longer than summer-harvested greens because the cold-season crops have naturally thicker cell walls and higher sugar content

## Economics of Cut-and-Come-Again

The cut-and-come-again method significantly improves the economics of winter greenhouse production:

- **Reduced replanting costs**: Each planting produces multiple harvests, reducing seed, soil amendment, and labor costs
- **Higher revenue per bed**: Multiple harvests from the same square footage over the winter season
- **Labor efficiency**: Harvesting existing plants is faster than preparing beds and transplanting new crops
- **Extended season**: Cut-and-come-again bridges the gap between fall plantings and spring crops

Coleman estimates that cut-and-come-again crops provide 60-70% of the total winter harvest revenue at Four Season Farm, making it the single most important production technique for winter profitability.

## Common Problems and Solutions

**Slow regrowth**: Usually caused by cutting too low, insufficient light, or nutrient depletion. Solutions include raising cutting height, adding row covers for warmth, and applying dilute liquid organic fertilizer.

**Declining quality**: Flavor and texture decline with successive cuts. After 3-4 cuts, most crops should be rotated out and replanted.

**Disease buildup**: Repeated cutting creates wounds susceptible to fungal infection. Ensure good air circulation, remove all crop debris, and rotate plant families between beds.

**Bolting**: Some species (arugula, mustard) may bolt in late winter as day length increases. Harvest completely and replant with heat-tolerant varieties.
