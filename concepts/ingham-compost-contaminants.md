# Compost Contaminants - Herbicide Persistence

Extracted from Elaine Ingham's AACT Field Guide, covering herbicide
contamination issues that affect compost quality and
[[ingham-aact-disease-suppression]] effectiveness.

## The Clopyralid Problem

Clopyralid is the most significant herbicide contamination concern for
compost. SFI has no knowledge of any bacteria or fungi that decompose
clopyralid. Which is to say, they don't know about them -- they may be
out there. But clearly those bacteria and fungi do not occur routinely on
grass clippings.

**Practical implication:** Do NOT use clopyralid-treated material in
compost. This is explicitly stated as a rule for compost ingredient
selection: "leaves, tree debris, grass clippings (no clopyralid treated
material however!)."

## Hierarchy of Compost Contaminant Problems

From most to least problematic:

1. **Strongylid medicine (de-wormer)** -- the biggest problem in
   manure-based composts
2. **Herbicide residues** (clopyralid, aminopyralid, and related
   picolinic acid herbicides) -- no known biological decomposition
3. **Insecticide residues** -- can be decomposed by some soil organisms
4. **Fungicide residues** -- particularly damaging since they kill the
   fungi you are trying to grow
5. **Copper and sulfur** -- sulfur is a very potent fungicide; copper
   is more toxic to bacteria than fungi

## Antibiotics in Compost

Antibiotics are generally easy to deal with in compost, as long as you
don't overwhelm the system with high concentrations. The aerobic biology
in good compost can decompose most antibiotic residues.

## How to Know There Is a Problem

The way to know there is a contaminant problem is when temperature
fails to reach proper levels in compost, or when the biology does not
establish properly after application. Contaminants suppress the very
organisms you need for decomposition and disease suppression.

## E. coli and Human Pathogens

While not herbicide contaminants, E. coli and human pathogens are
another class of compost contaminant. Let manure with high numbers of
E. coli and good numbers of beneficial organisms sit in a pile for a few
months, and the E. coli will be outcompeted by the beneficial organisms.

**Prevention:** Use compost that was properly brought to temperature for
the required time with adequate oxygen. When proper composting is
performed, no matter how much molasses you put into compost tea, the tea
will not contain E. coli.

**Remediation if poor compost:** Garlic juice has been shown to knock out
E. coli in tea. Citric acid at low concentrations (1-2 drops in a 50
gallon tea maker) can reduce E. coli and other human pathogens. Alaska
Humus can be used to inoculate questionable compost.

## Compost Longevity

If you keep organisms in warm conditions with good moisture (50%), and
don't let contaminants get into it, compost should remain good for up to
2 years. Add foods to the compost, and you keep the organisms happy and
growing. Getting some good fungi growing is also important for longevity.

## Chlordane and Persistent Pesticides

While some organisms can chew up chlordane, they are not always found in
compost starting materials. This is an older persistent pesticide that
may still be present in some soils.

## Grass Clipping Warning

Grass clippings are the most common vector for clopyralid contamination.
Lawn care companies frequently use picolinic acid herbicides that
persist through composting. Always verify the source of grass clippings
before adding them to compost piles.

## Impact on AACT

Contaminated compost produces contaminated tea. If the starting compost
contains herbicide residues, the AACT will carry those residues to your
plants and soil, defeating the purpose of biological management. Always
test compost from unknown sources before use.

## Key Takeaways

- Clopyralid has no known biological decomposition pathway in compost
- Never use clopyralid-treated material in compost
- Grass clippings are the most common contamination vector
- De-wormer residues in manure are the biggest compost contaminant problem
- Temperature failure in composting may indicate contaminant presence
- Properly composted material at correct temperature/time eliminates E. coli
- Compost kept at 50% moisture, warm, uncontaminated lasts up to 2 years
- Garlic juice and citric acid can remediate E. coli in tea from poor compost
