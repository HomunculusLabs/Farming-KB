## Pitting

Wherever rain flows or falls occasionally over bare sandy ground, pitting is an excellent technique. A set of large discs with one-quarter section cut off, or a small disc fixed to a wheel, is drawn cross-country. Seed and fertiliser are spread soon after. Seed, fertiliser, and sand blow into the pits, which respond very well to subsequent rain. Many hectares of these pits have grown well near Alice Springs in Central Australia.

## Intensive Dune Stabilisation

Severe dune "blowouts" on coasts need more intensive treatment:

- **Hand planting of quickset grasses**: Such as *Ammophila* spp.
- **Pelleted moth bean seed**: Broadcast in treated areas.
- **Brush fences in 7-metre squares**: Built from stakes 2 metres long driven 1 metre deep, with numerous bushy weaves between. Pits of seed and *Acacia victoriae* may succeed after rain.

### Brush Fence Specifications

- Height: 0.9-1.0 metres
- Spacing: 7 metres apart in parallel rows across the main wind direction
- Wind gaps: Less than 50% to drop sand out
- Posts: Firm, driven every 3-4 metres

Temperature reductions of 12°C have been measured in the shade of such fences, with moisture loss 1.8-3.0% less than open areas. These fences are precursors to the planting of hardy perennials adapted to dune conditions.

## Chinese Approach to Dune Stabilisation

In China, where they take trees seriously, rice-straw mats are used as fencing or surface stabilisers. Advanced trees in baskets are planted (basket and all) in the matting shelter. These pioneer trees give shelter and mulch for later forestry and crop establishment.

## Planting Techniques

Since lethal soil temperatures are rarely formed below 15 cm in dunes, careful placement of trees in holes this deep or deeper — stabilised from collapse by a woven basket or cardboard collar — will prevent the seedling from being "cooked" until it can cast its own shade.

Any fertiliser should be thinly placed at planting time, as rapid infiltration of water in rains can carry it below root reach. After the tree has spread a root web, more fertiliser can be added and will be taken up by the plant. All pioneering plants on dunes must be able to stand stem burial until the area is stabilised.

## Nutrient Requirements

All plants in dunes benefit from nitrogenous fertiliser, phosphate, and trace elements, as old dunes are usually deficient in plant nutrients. Sawdust and mill wastes (bark, sawdust) are very beneficial in hollows — for example, mill wastes have helped establish an *Acacia sophorae* forest in coastal dunes in Tasmania.

## Long-Term Dune Protection

Once stabilised, dunes must be protected from several threats:

- **Recreation vehicles**: Devastation by off-road vehicles destroys years of stabilisation work.
- **Heavy browsing**: Uncontrolled grazing by livestock or wild animals.
- **Badly aligned roads**: Roads that funnel wind or disrupt natural drainage patterns.
- **Sand mining to windward**: Removal of sand upwind exposes stabilised areas to fresh sand movement.

## Surface Stabilisation Mechanisms

Apart from vegetation and pebble stabilisation, any "cementing" system helps. Water creates bonds between particles that require much higher wind speeds to move the sand. Salt (especially where dew is present, as salt is hygroscopic or water-absorbing) similarly binds particles. Chemical stabilisers include tars, oils, or glues such as latex.

Natural stabilisers include [[lichens]], bacteria, fungi, and algal mats, as well as salt crusts. These often delicate desert crusts are critical to stability — when hoofed animals, fast vehicles, or agriculture are introduced to deserts, wind erosion may quickly follow the destruction of these crusts. In urgent cases, stabilisation can be effected by spraying tar oils, laying pebble beds, or building brush fences combined with planting sequences for permanence.
