# Hallucinogenic Plants — World Survey

Based on [[richard-evans-schultes]]'s Golden Guide to Hallucinogenic Plants (1976). A survey of psychoactive plant use across cultures, organized by geography and function.

## The New World vs Old World Disparity

The most striking finding: the New World (Americas) has 90+ hallucinogenic species in active cultural use, while the Old World has only 15-20 — many abandoned in antiquity. This cannot be explained by botanical differences. Old World plants like Cannabis and Amanita muscaria were once widely used but fell into disuse, while New World peoples maintained and elaborated hallucinogenic traditions.

See [[hallucinogenic-plants-new-world]] and [[hallucinogenic-plants-old-world]] for species profiles.

## Geographic Distribution

### South America (Amazon Basin) — The Richest Hallucinogenic Region
- **Ayahuasca** (Banisteriopsis caapi + DMT admixtures) — the most sophisticated pharmacological preparation known
- **Virola** snuffs — the most recently discovered hallucinogenic complex (Schultes, 1954)
- **Yopo/Vilca** (Anadenanthera) snuffs — widespread from Orinoco to southern Peru
- **Brugmansia/Datura** — tree daturas and herbaceous daturas used in Andean and Amazonian cultures
- **Kahei** (Aporosa spp.) — snuff used in the Amazon
- **Parica** ( Mimosa spp. or related genera) — snuff preparations

### Mesoamerica — The Mushroom and Cactus Center
- **Teonanacatl** — psilocybin mushrooms (Psilocybe mexicana and allies) used by Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Chatino peoples
- **Peyote** (Lophophora williamsii) — central to Huichol, Tarahumara, Cora ceremonies
- **Ololiuqui** (Turbina corymbosa) and **Tlitliltzin** (Ipomoea violacea) — morning glory seeds
- **Sinicuichi** (Heimia salicifolia) — auditory hallucinogen
- **Salvia divinorum** — Mazatec divinatory herb
- **Datura** species — multiple species used from pre-Columbian times
- **Cacao** (Theobroma cacao) — mild stimulant with possible psychoactive admixtures in ceremonial use

### North America
- **Peyote** — spread northward into Plains Indian culture via the Native American Church
- **Datura stramonium/inoxia** — used by southwestern and Plains tribes
- **Sophora secundiflora** (mescal bean) — pre-peyote ceremonial use in the Southwest
- **Amanita muscaria** — used by Mazatec in Oaxaca (independent of Siberian use)

### Africa
- **Iboga** (Tabernanthe iboga) — Bwiti religion of Gabon/Congo
- **Cannabis** — introduced by Arab traders; widespread across sub-Saharan Africa
- **Morning glories** (Ipomoea tricolor) — West African ethnomedical use
- **Alchornea floribunda** — iboga substitute used in some regions

### Asia
- **Cannabis** — ancient use from China (2700 BCE) through India (Soma debate), Middle East, Southeast Asia
- **Amanita muscaria** — Siberian shamanic use among Koryak, Kamchadal, Chukchi
- **Datura metel** — Ayurvedic medicine and Hindu ritual (India)
- **Betel nut** (Areca catechu) — most widely used psychoactive after tobacco, alcohol, caffeine
- **Nutmeg** (Myristica fragrans) — spice with hallucinogenic properties at high doses
- **Ergot** (Claviceps purpurea) — not used deliberately, but source of ergot alkaloids (LSD precursor)
- **Fly agaric** — also found in Japan and Himalayan regions

### Oceania
- **Kava** (Piper methysticum) — central to Pacific Island social and ceremonial life
- **Betel nut** — widespread in Melanesia, Micronesia

### Europe
- **Amanita muscaria** — possible Viking/Celtic use (speculative)
- **Solanaceous plants** — belladonna, henbane, mandrake in medieval witchcraft and medicine
- **Ergot** — accidental ergotism ("St. Anthony's Fire") may have influenced European history (Salem witch trials hypothesis)
- **Cannabis** — medieval medicinal use; largely abandoned
- **Water lilies** (Nymphaea) — possibly used in ancient Egypt

## Cultural Functions of Hallucinogenic Plants

### 1. Divination and Prophecy
The most widespread use across cultures. Shamans and priests enter altered states to:
- Diagnose illness
- Locate lost objects or people
- Predict future events
- Communicate with spirits
- Find game animals

**Examples**: Ayahuasca ceremonies (Amazon), Salvia divinorum divination (Mazatec), San Pedro mesadas (Peru), Iboga Bwiti initiation (Gabon)

### 2. Healing and Medicine
Hallucinogenic plants are often the most valued medicines in Indigenous pharmacopoeias. Aboriginal peoples attribute sickness to spiritual causes; plants that allow contact with the spirit world are considered the most powerful medicines.

**Examples**: Ayahuasca as "the purge" (Amazonian healing), peyote curing ceremonies (Huichol), kava for anxiety (Pacific)

### 3. Religious and Ceremonial Use
- **Mesoamerican mushroom cult** — teonanacatl as sacrament; the "flesh of the gods"
- **Peyote pilgrimage** — Huichol annual journey to Wirikuta to harvest peyote
- **Bwiti initiation** — massive iboga doses in Gabonese rites of passage
- **Kava ceremonies** — formal political and social rituals throughout Oceania

### 4. Initiation and Rites of Passage
- **Bwiti** — iboga root bark consumption during multi-day initiation
- **Amazonian tribal initiation** — ayahuasca or snuff use to mark adulthood
- **Huichol peyote hunt** — as both annual renewal and individual rite of passage

### 5. Recreation and Social Bonding
- **Kava** — social beverage throughout Pacific Islands
- **Betel nut** — social chewing throughout South/Southeast Asia
- **Cannabis** — widespread recreational use
- **Virola snuff** — shared communal experience among Amazonian tribes

### 6. Sorcery and Warfare
- **Datura/Brugmansia** — used in Chilean and Colombian sorcery
- **Latua pubiflora** — Mapuche use to drive victims insane
- **Anadenanthera snuff** — sometimes used before battle (Orinoco tribes)

## Pharmacological Sophistication

Schultes emphasizes that Indigenous peoples achieved extraordinary pharmacological understanding:

1. **Ayahuasca**: Combining MAO inhibitor (Banisteriopsis caapi — harmine/harmaline) with DMT source (Psychotria viridis or Diplopterys cabrerana) — a biochemical synergy not understood by Western science until the 20th century

2. **Admixture plants**: Many ayahuasca preparations include 15-20 additional plants to modify, intensify, or lengthen effects — a sophisticated polypharmacy

3. **Alkaline activation**: Yopo and Vilca snuffs are prepared by mixing seeds with alkaline ash (lime or shell), which converts the alkaloids to free base form for nasal absorption — exactly what modern chemistry would recommend

4. **Preparation techniques**: San Pedro cactus is boiled for hours; Virola bark is beaten and boiled; morning glory seeds are ground and soaked — all techniques that optimize alkaloid extraction

5. **Subsidiary plants**: Schultes notes that "many hallucinogenic preparations call for subsidiary plants added to alter, increase, or lengthen the effects" — demonstrating deep empirical knowledge

## The "Model Psychosis" Concept

The Golden Guide discusses the historical framing of hallucinogens as producing "model psychoses" — a Western psychiatric concept that Schultes critiques. Indigenous users do not experience hallucinogens as "psychotic episodes" but as meaningful, structured experiences with cultural significance. The "set and setting" concept (later formalized by [[timothy-leary]]) is implicit in the ceremonial frameworks that surround hallucinogen use in traditional societies.

## Conservation and Cultural Survival

Many hallucinogenic plant species face threats:
- **Peyote**: Overharvesting, habitat destruction in south Texas; slow-growing cactus
- **Ayahuasca vines**: Increasing commercial demand threatens wild populations
- **Salvia divinorum**: Extremely limited natural range in Sierra Mazateca
- **Psilocybe species**: Habitat destruction in tropical and subtropical regions
