# Rust Fungi (Urediniomycetes)

## Overview

Rust fungi (Class **Urediniomycetes**, Phylum **Basidiomycota**) comprise the largest and most ubiquitous group of obligately biotrophic fungi on vascular plants. The name "rust" is descriptive — often one or more spore stages are rusty orange to brownish because of the color of spores emerging en masse from pustules on the host. Most rust pustules measure a few millimeters or less in diameter, although rusts associated with stem cankers on trees may sporulate in contiguous areas more than a meter long.

## Taxonomy and Diversity

An estimated **168 rust genera** and approximately **7,000 species** are currently accepted, more than half of which belong to the genus *Puccinia*. According to Hiratsuka and Sato (1982), the majority of species in temperate regions of the northern hemisphere, Australia, and New Zealand have been well catalogued, but many new genera and species are still expected from tropical and subtropical regions such as South America, Africa, and Southeastern Asia.

Farr and colleagues (1989) listed 53 rust genera from North America with approximately 925 different host-fungus associations. Of those, 47% are *Puccinia* species and 18% (168) are from subtropical to tropical localities. South American records include 23 rust genera and 218 host-fungus associations, with about half again being *Puccinia* species. Tropical American rust records number only about one-third of those from North America, likely reflecting insufficient study rather than true diversity.

Buriticá and Hennen (1983) collected **100 species of rusts** in a single 40-hectare tract in the neotropics, 20% of which were new taxa. About one-third of the plant species in the area had their own unique rust species. Intensive studies will likely show that the diversity of rust species in the tropics is higher than in temperate regions. Many of the rust genera known from South America are exclusively tropical (e.g., the majority of the Raveneliaceae), and according to Oberwinkler (1993), several rusts with unique characteristics and distinct taxonomic features are known from warmer regions.

## Host Range

Potential hosts come from a broad range of vascular plant families, including **ferns, gymnosperms, monocots, and dicots**. No rusts have been reported from mosses or liverworts, but related small genera of heterobasidiomycetes (*Iola*, *Platycarpa*, *Ptechetelium*) are parasites of mosses and ferns, with the latter two genera restricted to the tropics and warranting further study. A particular spore stage of a rust will often have a narrow range of hosts, sometimes limited to one or two congeneric plant species.

## Life Cycle and Spore Stages

Rust fungi are highly specialized parasites with several unique features. A single species may produce up to **five morphologically and cytologically distinct spore-producing structures** in successive stages of reproduction:

1. **Spermagonia** — the first recognizable stage, producing spermatia and receptive hyphae
2. **Aecia** — cup-shaped structures producing aeciospores, often on different hosts
3. **Uredinia** — producing urediniospores (the repeating stage that drives epidemics)
4. **Telia** — producing teliospores (often thick-walled overwintering structures)
5. **Basidia** — producing basidiospores from germinated teliospores

The various spore types usually are produced at different times, although they sometimes may be found simultaneously on the same host. Some rust species require several seasons and **two unrelated species of host plants**, often in different families or orders, to complete their life cycles. In such cases, a spore stage produced on one host is only capable of infecting the other (alternate) host. This phenomenon of **heteroecism** (alternation between two unrelated hosts) adds considerable complexity to the study and management of rust diseases.

**Microcyclic rusts** — species with reduced life cycles — are more common in cool temperate to subarctic regions, where a short growing season selects for rusts that can survive perennially on the telial host (Savile 1972, 1976). The telia of microcyclic rusts simulate the habit of the parental macrocyclic forms and occur on the host plants of the aecial stage of the latter. This can create confusion during identification unless distinctive spermagonia, paraphyses, or urediniospores are present.

## Infection and Symptoms

Unlike other plant pathogens, rusts usually infect **healthy, vigorously growing plants**, so if infections are small and limited to certain plant parts (such as foliage), they may be difficult to detect. Perennial, systemic infections may cause deformities such as witches' brooms, cankers, or galls. Plants with severe rust infections may appear stunted, chlorotic (yellowed), or otherwise discolored. Heavy uredinial sporulation may turn the affected plant part orange. Rusts sporulate on leaves, shoots, fruits, and woody stems.

In temperate regions, aecial stages are generally most prevalent in the spring, with uredinial states developing midseason. Telia develop in the fall, usually overwintering in dead host tissue. In tropical regions, teliospores may develop and immediately germinate at all times of the year, and thick spore walls and pigmentation — identifying features for temperate rusts — are far less common. Unless distinctive spermagonia, paraphyses, or urediniospores are present in collected material, a tropical rust may be difficult to identify (Savile 1980).

Both new and old infected tissues should be collected in case spore stages of early and advanced infections differ. This is particularly important with rusts that produce all five spore stages on a single host (autoecious rusts).

## Obligate Biotrophy

Rust fungi are unable to grow saprobically in nature and thus are found only in environments suitable for their hosts. Their relationships with their hosts, on which they are completely dependent and with which they have coevolved, are highly specialized (Laundon 1973; Savile 1976). They cannot be cultured in vitro except under very specialized conditions. This obligate biotrophic lifestyle means that rusts cannot survive independently of their hosts, making host plant distribution a primary determinant of rust distribution.

## Economic Importance

Although rust infections are not generally fatal to plants, they may severely limit growth and fruiting ability. Notoriously damaging rusts include:

- **Wheat stem rust** (*Puccinia graminis*) — historically one of the most devastating diseases of cereal crops worldwide
- **Coffee rust** (*Hemileia vastatrix*) — a major threat to coffee production in tropical regions
- **White pine blister rust** (*Cronartium ribicola*) — a destructive disease of five-needled pines requiring an alternate host in the genus *Ribes*

## Identification

Identification of a rust species frequently begins with **host identification**, so healthy, uninfected host foliage and reproductive structures should be collected along with the rust. There are numerous regional indices and taxonomic monographs. Major references include:

- Arthur (1934) and Cummins (1962, 1971, 1978) for North America
- Ziller (1974) for western Canadian tree rusts
- León-Gallegos and Cummins (1981) for Mexico
- Jørstad (1956, 1959) for South America
- Cummins (1950) and Ito (1950) for Chinese and Japanese species
- Doidge (1939), Eboh (1986), and Gjaerum (1986) for African rusts
- Gäumann (1959) and Wilson and Henderson (1966) for Europe
- Kuprevich and Tranzschel´ (1957) and Savulescu (1953) for the former Soviet Union

Hiratsuka and Sato (1982) provided an extensive listing of regional rust literature including many additional references. Correct identification often requires examination of multiple spore stages, which means collecting at different times of year or from different host plants when the rust is known to be heteroecious. Microscopic examination of spore morphology, wall ornamentation, and germ pore characteristics is typically necessary for species-level determination.

## Facultative Biotrophs vs. Obligate Biotrophs

It is important to distinguish rust fungi (obligate biotrophs) from facultatively biotrophic fungi. Obligate biotrophs produce either intercellular or extracellular mycelia, often with specialized feeding structures called haustoria that penetrate and extract nutrients from the host plant. They require a living host, are unable to live saprobically in nature, and rarely are cultured in vitro. Many are host-specific to certain plant families, often making it necessary to identify the host plant to identify the fungus.

Facultative biotrophs, by contrast, may also cause diseases in living plants but generally do not fruit on living tissues. Sporulation occurs either after the plant dies or in islands of dead tissue surrounded by living cells. Because they can grow and sporulate after the plant is dead, they often are cultured easily in vitro. Under field conditions, symptoms produced by facultative biotrophs can be confused with symptoms and signs of obligate biotrophic fungi on living tissues.

## Epiphytic and Saprotrophic Fungi on Living Plants

Saprotrophs on living plants are, primarily, epiphytic fungi that fruit on but do not penetrate living plant surfaces such as leaves. Many obtain nutrients from extracellular plant exudates or from the excretions of plant-feeding insects, such as aphids. These fungi are distinct from parasitic species and require different collection techniques — they often need to be encouraged to fruit after collection to facilitate identification.

## Ecological Role

Pathogenic fungi like rusts have direct and indirect effects on their environments. They can influence the structure and stand composition of vegetation in plant communities and ecosystems (Harper 1990). They affect a plant's ability to compete for limited resources by reducing growth rates and increasing susceptibility to other pests. They affect genetic diversity of hosts by selecting for disease resistance, and they act as natural thinning and diversifying agents in dense clusters of seedlings. Seed and seedling pathogens can select for seed dispersal patterns (Augspurger 1983; Burdon et al. 1990). Augspurger (1983) noted that survival of seeds from the tropical tree *Platypodium elegans* increased with distance from an adult tree, as distantly dispersed seeds were more likely to encounter light gaps where they would be at lower risk from damping-off diseases.

The striking manifestations of fungal diseases encountered in the field may in fact be rare events limited to species in which the host and pathogen have not coevolved. Harper (1990) speculated that certain host populations could become extinct after being challenged by a pathogen for the first time, stating that if a host plant lacks the genetic variance to respond to selection, it may become extinct with no hint of why this occurred.

## Molecular and Genetic Studies

Modern molecular methods have expanded our ability to study rust fungi beyond traditional morphology-based taxonomy. Techniques such as RFLP (Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism) analysis, PCR amplification of specific gene regions, and DNA sequencing allow researchers to discriminate between rust varieties, study geographic distributions, and determine if mutational changes have occurred in specific sequences.

For RFLP studies, restriction enzyme products are separated by agarose gel electrophoresis, stained with ethidium bromide, and analyzed with UV light. Sequences of genes can be used to design specific sets of restriction enzymes to analyze genetic variation. If mutations are known to occur in specific sequences, allele-specific PCR primers can be designed to amplify 200–300 bp regions for genetic analysis. The amplified DNA can then be used for SSCP analysis (Single-Strand Conformation Polymorphism), RFLP, or direct sequence analysis.

These molecular approaches have proven particularly valuable for studying rust populations in agricultural systems, where understanding genetic diversity and pathogen migration patterns is critical for disease management and breeding for resistance. They also allow researchers to detect cryptic species that may be morphologically similar but genetically distinct, contributing to more accurate estimates of rust species diversity worldwide.

Electron microscopy has also been used to study the physical structure of rust fungal DNA. The mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) of rust fungi can be extracted and examined using transmission electron microscopy. All three forms of DNA — covalently closed circular (ccc), replicative intermediate, and linear — can be observed with this method. A grid is placed in 95% ethanol for 10 seconds, shadowed with platinum/carbon coating, and examined. Internal standards such as the plasmid pBR322 can be used for calibration. Contour length measurements of mtDNA range from 6–25 mm and generally agree with size estimates obtained from electrophoresis and restriction enzyme mapping.

## Collecting Rust Specimens

Proper collection technique is essential for accurate rust identification. The following guidelines should be followed:

- **Collect both healthy and infected tissue** — healthy foliage and reproductive structures are needed for host identification
- **Collect at multiple times** — different spore stages may be present at different times of year
- **Note the host carefully** — many rusts are host-specific, and incorrect host identification will lead to incorrect rust identification
- **Preserve specimens properly** — dried herbarium specimens should include both the rust structures and host material
- **Document collection data** — location, date, habitat, and host information should be recorded
- **Use paper envelopes** — not plastic bags, which promote mold growth on collected specimens

When collecting in tropical regions, special care should be taken because thick spore walls and wall pigmentation — key identifying features for temperate rusts — are far less common, making identification more challenging without mature sporulating structures.
