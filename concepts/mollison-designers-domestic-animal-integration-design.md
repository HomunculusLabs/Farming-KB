# Domestic Animal Integration Design

Bill Mollison's Permaculture: A Designers Manual devotes considerable
attention to the integration of domestic animals into designed
ecosystems. Rather than treating animals as isolated production units,
permaculture designs them as functional elements that cycle nutrients,
control pests, prepare ground for planting, and produce food, fiber,
and fertility within diverse polycultural systems.

## Design Principles for Animal Integration

Mollison establishes several key principles for incorporating animals
into permaculture designs:

- Every animal should perform multiple functions (yield, manure, pest
  control, soil aeration, weed management)
- Animal systems should be positioned to minimize energy inputs
  (feeding, watering, moving) and maximize beneficial outputs
- Animal numbers should match the carrying capacity of the system to
  prevent overgrazing and soil degradation
- Animal housing should integrate with other system elements (greenhouses,
  composting areas, [[permaculture-zone-one-design]] gardens)
- Animals should cycle between zones and functions seasonally

## Chicken Integration

Chickens are perhaps the most versatile small animal in permaculture
design. Mollison describes multiple integration strategies:

- **Chicken tractors:** Mobile coops without floors that are moved
  across garden beds or pasture. Chickens scratch, weed, eat pests
  and weed seeds, and deposit manure in place. After 1-2 weeks of
  chicken occupation, the area is ready for planting. This eliminates
  the need for mechanical tillage and external fertilizer.
- **Deep litter systems:** Chickens are housed on thick beds of carbon
  material (straw, leaves, wood chips). Their scratching and manure
  create compost over 6-12 months, producing rich material for garden
  beds. Mollison connects this to [[compost-building-guide]] and [[soil-building-permaculture]]
  strategies.
- **Orchard poultry:** Chickens ranging under fruit trees control
  codling moth larvae (which pupate in soil), consume fallen fruit
  (reducing disease carryover), and fertilize the trees. Fencing
  must protect trees from bark damage when chickens are young.
- **Dual-purpose breeds:** Mollison favors heritage breeds that
  produce both eggs and meat, are good foragers, and brood their own
  chicks, reducing system dependence on purchased stock and feed.

## Duck Integration

Ducks offer advantages over chickens in wet environments and for
specific pest control functions:

- Ducks are superior slug and snail predators, making them ideal
  for [[permaculture-zone-one-design]] gardens and [[food-forest-design]] understories
  Muscovy and Khaki Campbell ducks are particularly effective
- Duck manure is higher in nitrogen than chicken manure and does
  not require composting before application (unlike chicken manure)
- Ducks forage effectively in aquatic systems, controlling mosquito
  larvae and algae in [[aquaculture-in-permaculture]] ponds and rice paddies
- Duck eggs are larger and richer than chicken eggs; duck meat is
  a high-quality protein source

Mollison recommends combining ducks with rice paddies, aquaculture
ponds, and wetland edge systems for maximum integration.

## Rabbit Integration

Rabbits are efficient converters of forage to protein and produce
valuable manure. Mollison describes their role in permaculture systems:

- Rabbits can be raised in colony systems (warrens) or in elevated
  hutches. Colony systems require deep litter and predator protection
- Rabbit manure is a "cool" manure that can be applied directly to
  gardens without composting, making it immediately available for
  [[permaculture-zone-one-design]] vegetable production
- Rabbits eat a wide variety of forage plants including tree leaves
  (mulberry, moringa), comfrey, alfalfa, and vegetable trimmings,
  converting garden waste into protein
- Rabbit fur provides additional yield and is a marketable product
- Rabbit systems require minimal space and are suitable for urban and
  suburban [[permaculture-design]] sites

## Goat Integration

Goats serve as browsers and brush controllers in permaculture systems:

- Goats preferentially eat woody plants, thistles, brambles, and
  broadleaf weeds, making them excellent for [[mollison-designers-house-site-preparation-and-drainage-design]]
  and pasture improvement
- Goat manure enriches soil with readily available nutrients
- Goat milk provides dairy production on land unsuitable for cows
- Goats can be integrated with tree crop systems (silvopasture),
  controlling understory growth without damaging mature trees if
  properly managed
- Mollison warns that goats can be destructive to young trees and
  gardens if not properly contained; rotational grazing and
  protective fencing are essential

## Pig Integration

Pigs are powerful soil builders and nutrient cyclers:

- Pigs root and till soil, breaking compacted ground and incorporating
  organic matter. Mollison describes using pigs for land clearing
  and [[mollison-designers-house-site-preparation-and-drainage-design]] before planting
- Pig manure is high in nutrients and, when composted with carbon
  material, produces excellent fertilizer
- Pigs can be run through orchards after harvest to clean up fallen
  fruit (reducing pest carryover) and fertilize the soil
- Pigs convert food waste, crop residues, and forage into high-quality
  meat efficiently
- Pig tractor systems (movable paddocks) distribute manure and
  tillage across the landscape without permanent damage to any area

## Manure Cycling Systems

Central to animal integration is the cycling of manure through the
system. Mollison describes several strategies:

- **Deep bedding composting:** Animals on thick carbon beds produce
  compost in situ
- **Anaerobic digestion:** Biogas from animal manure produces cooking
  fuel and nutrient-rich effluent
- **Worm farming (vermiculture):** Animal manure feeds worm populations
  that produce castings for garden use
- **Aquaculture integration:** Animal manure (particularly duck)
  fertilizes pond systems, supporting fish and aquatic plant production

## Forage Design

Designing diverse forage systems reduces and eventually eliminates
dependence on purchased feed. Mollison advocates planting:

- Tree lucerne (tagasaste), carob, honey locust, and mulberry as
  fodder trees
