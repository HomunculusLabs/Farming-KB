# Tissue Culture and Spore Germination Techniques Two primary methods exist for starting mushroom cultures: tissue culture (cloning from a living mushroom) and multispore germination (from spores). Each method has distinct advantages and characteristics that influence strain genetics, vigor, and consistency. ## Tissue Culture: Cloning Mushrooms Tissue culture is an assured method of preserving the exact genetic character of a living mushroom. In tissue culture a living specimen is cloned, whereas in multispore culture new strains are created from genetic recombination. ### Timing and Specimen Selection Tissue cultures must be taken from mushrooms within 24 to 48 hours of being picked. If specimens are several days old, too dry, or too mature, a pure culture will be difficult to isolate. The best locations for excising clean tissue include: - The cap interior - The upper region of the stem - The area where the gill plate joins the underside of the cap Some mushrooms have a thick cuticle overlaying the cap that can be peeled back to access the underlying flesh. ### Procedure 1. Wipe the mushroom surface with an alcohol-soaked cotton swab, removing dirt and damaged external tissue 2. Break the mushroom cap or stem, exposing interior hyphae 3. Flame a scalpel until red-hot and cool it in a media-filled petri dish 4. Cut into the flesh, removing a small fragment of tissue 5. Transfer the tissue fragment to the center of a nutrient-filled petri dish as quickly as possible, minimizing air exposure 6. Repeat into at least three, preferably five more dishes 7. Label each dish with species, date, culture type (tissue), and agar medium ### Contamination Expectations An overall contamination rate of 10% is tolerable for most cultivators. In primary cultures from wild specimens, 25% contamination is not unusual. The most commonly encountered contaminants in tissue culture are bacteria. If sporulating mold colonies develop adjacent to the growing mycelium, the culture should be promptly isolated through successive transfers. ## [[mushroom-cultivator-strain-selection]]

## Agar Media Formulations

The choice of agar medium significantly influences culture
growth rate, morphology, and contamination resistance:
- **Malt Extract Agar (MEA)** — the standard all-purpose
  medium. Two percent malt extract provides balanced
  nutrition for most mushroom species. Simple to prepare,
  reliable, and well-suited to both tissue culture and
  spore germination across a wide range of gourmet and
  medicinal mushroom species commonly cultivated.
- **Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA)** — slightly richer than MEA
  due to added potato starch. Produces denser mycelial
  growth but can encourage bacterial contaminants in
  specimens that are not thoroughly surface-sterilized
  before transfer to the culture medium.
- **Wood-based agar** — supplemented with sawdust or wood
  extract for wood-decomposing species. Mimics natural
  substrate conditions and can improve subsequent
  colonization performance when cultures are transferred
  to sawdust-based fruiting substrates later in the
  cultivation process.
- **Antibiotic-amended agar** — gentamicin or penicillin
  can be added to suppress bacterial contamination in
  difficult specimens. However, antibiotics may also
  inhibit some fungal growth and should be used only when
  bacterial contamination is a recurring problem that
  cannot be solved through improved sterile technique.

## Spore Germination Techniques

Multispore germination creates new genetic combinations,
producing diverse strains from a single spore print:
- **Spore print method** — a spore print is scraped lightly
  with a flame-sterilized scalpel and the spores are
  streaked across the agar surface. This distributes
  spores so individual germination events can be observed
  and the fastest-growing sectors selected for isolation.
- **Spore syringe method** — spores suspended in sterile
  water are deposited in small drops on the agar surface.
  This allows precise volume control and is the standard
  method for species with very fine or lightweight spores
  that are difficult to handle as dry prints.
- **Hydration treatment** — some species benefit from a
  24-48 hour soak in sterile water or dilute hydrogen
  peroxide before plating. This treatment softens spore
  walls and improves germination rates significantly for
  species with thick-walled or dormant spore structures.
- **Temperature optimization** — germination temperature
  varies by species. Most temperate species germinate at
  20-25°C while tropical species may require 28-32°C.
  Maintaining consistent temperature during the
  germination window improves success rates markedly.

## Strain Isolation and Selection

After germination, individual strains must be isolated and
evaluated for desirable cultivation characteristics:
- **Sector isolation** — fast-growing rhizomorphic sectors
  are cut from the leading edge of germination plates and
  transferred to fresh agar. Rhizomorphic growth typically
  indicates stronger, more vigorous strains suitable for
  cultivation on prepared fruiting substrates.
- **Multiple plate technique** — germinating spores across
  many plates increases the genetic pool available for
  selection. Stamets recommends starting with at least
  ten plates per spore print to ensure adequate genetic
  diversity for strain selection.
- **Genetic stabilization** — isolated strains may need
  several transfer generations to stabilize growth
  characteristics before performance testing. Each
  transfer to fresh agar allows the cultivator to observe
  consistency of growth rate, morphology, and sectoring
  patterns that predict fruiting reliability.
