# Tissue Culture and Spore Germination Techniques

Two primary methods exist for starting mushroom cultures: tissue
culture (cloning from a living mushroom) and multispore germination
(from spores). Each method has distinct advantages and
characteristics that influence strain genetics, vigor, and
consistency.

## Tissue Culture: Cloning Mushrooms

Tissue culture is an assured method of preserving the exact genetic
character of a living mushroom. In tissue culture a living specimen
is cloned, whereas in multispore culture new strains are created from
genetic recombination.

### Timing and Specimen Selection
Tissue cultures must be taken from mushrooms within 24 to 48 hours
of being picked. If specimens are several days old, too dry, or too
mature, a pure culture will be difficult to isolate. The best
locations for excising clean tissue include:
- The cap interior
- The upper region of the stem
- The area where the gill plate joins the underside of the cap

Some mushrooms have a thick cuticle overlaying the cap that can be
peeled back to access the underlying flesh.

### Procedure
1. Wipe the mushroom surface with an alcohol-soaked cotton swab,
   removing dirt and damaged external tissue
2. Break the mushroom cap or stem, exposing interior hyphae
3. Flame a scalpel until red-hot and cool it in a media-filled petri
   dish
4. Cut into the flesh, removing a small fragment of tissue
5. Transfer the tissue fragment to the center of a nutrient-filled
   petri dish as quickly as possible, minimizing air exposure
6. Repeat into at least three, preferably five more dishes
7. Label each dish with species, date, culture type (tissue), and
   agar medium

### Contamination Expectations
An overall contamination rate of 10% is tolerable for most cultivators.
In primary cultures from wild specimens, 25% contamination is not
unusual. The most commonly encountered contaminants in tissue culture
are bacteria. If sporulating mold colonies develop adjacent to the
growing mycelium, the culture should be promptly isolated through
successive transfers.

## [[spore-print-collection]]

A nearly contaminant-free spore print can be obtained by choosing
a healthy young specimen with the partial veil intact. The veil seals
the gills from the outside, creating a semi-sterile chamber. By
carefully removing the veil tissue under aseptic conditions, clean
spores are obtained.

Spores can be collected on typing paper, sterile petri dishes, or
glass microscope slides. If glass slides are used, the spores can be
sandwiched between two pieces and taped along the edges. A spore
print carelessly taken or stored can easily become contaminated.

## Spore Germination Techniques

### Direct Transfer Method
1. Sterilize an inoculating loop or scalpel over flame for 5-10
   seconds until red-hot
2. Cool the tip by inserting it into sterile media in a petri dish
3. Scrape some spores off the print
4. Streak the tip across the agar surface
5. Inoculate at least three dishes to improve germination chances

### Free-Fall Method
Scrape the spore print above an opened petri dish, allowing spores
to free-fall onto the medium. Mycelium started from spores in this
manner is called a multispore culture.

### Hydration Method for Old Spores
When first produced, spores are moist, inflated cells with a
relatively high germination rate. As they dry and collapse, germination
becomes difficult. The probability of germinating dehydrated spores
increases by:
1. Sterilizing an eye dropper or syringe and a water-filled test tube
   at 15 psi for 30 minutes
2. Touching spores onto a scalpel and inserting into sterile water
3. Sealing tightly and letting stand for 6-12 hours
4. Drawing up the spore solution and inoculating plates with one or
   two drops

## Mycelial Characteristics After Germination

Spore germination and initial contamination should be evident in
three to seven days. Germinating spores appear as thread-like strands
of cells emanating from a central point, initially grayish and diffuse,
becoming whitish as hyphae divide and spread.

Most species (Agaricus, Coprinus, Lentinus, Panaeolus, Psilocybe)
have grayish to whitish mycelia. Exceptions include Lepista nuda with
remarkable purplish blue mycelium and Psilocybe tampanensis with
multi-colored brownish hues. Growth type varies: aerial (often from
high humidity) or appressed (from dry conditions), cottony or
rhizomorphic.

## Multispore Culture Considerations

Multispore germination creates many strains simultaneously, some
incompatible with others. This mixture can limit total yields, with
less productive strains inhibiting more productive ones. Strains from
domesticated parents tend to resemble their fruiting behavior, while
cultures from wild specimens may fruit poorly under artificial
conditions.

### Monokaryons and Dikaryons
A network from a single spore is a monokaryon, generally incapable of
producing fertile mushrooms. When two compatible monokaryons mate,
cytoplasmic and genetic material are exchanged, creating a dikaryon
that can produce fertile offspring. Branching between different
dikaryotic strains is called anastomosis.

### Single Spore Isolation
By diluting spores in sterile water and further diluting into larger
volumes, cultivators can isolate individual monokaryons and institute
controlled mating schedules for developing high-yielding strains. For
every hundred spores, only one to five germinate on average.

### Symbiotic Germination Aids
Some mushrooms resist artificial spore germination. Nils Fries (1979)
discovered that adding activated charcoal and the red yeast
Rhodotorula glutinis to media stimulated germination in Cantharellus
cibarius, which had previously resisted all efforts. Activated
charcoal is recommended for any mushroom whose spores do not easily
germinate. See [[mushroom-cultivator-strain-selection]]
for evaluating resulting cultures.
