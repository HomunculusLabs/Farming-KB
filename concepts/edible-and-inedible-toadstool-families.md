## The Five Families of Toadstools

McIlvaine identifies five families commonly encountered by foragers, each
distinguished by the structure of its [[spore]]-bearing surface beneath the cap.

### 1. Agarics (Agaricini) — Gill-Bearing Fungi

The largest family of edible fungi, with more members than all other eaten
families combined. Characterized by gills (thin, radiating plaits) beneath the
cap. Includes both the common meadow mushroom (*A. campestris*) and the
lethal *Amanita* genus.

### 2. Polyporei and Boleti — Tube and Pore Fungi

These have tubes or sponge-like pore surfaces beneath the cap instead of gills.
Many Boleti are described as "luscious" and are favorites among mycophagists,
especially in Europe. In America they have been rarely consumed. Several
varieties were long considered non-edible, but careful experimenters have
progressively removed species from the blacklist.

### 3. Hydnei — Spine or Tooth Fungi

Distinguished by spike-like projections on the underside of the cap. One of
the safest families: nearly all are edible, and none are known to contain
poisonous principles.

### 4. Clavaria — Club and Coral Fungi

Club-shaped or branching coral-like forms bearing thin spore-producing
membranes on upper surfaces. Nearly all are edible and easy to identify.

### 5. Lycoperdons — Puffballs

Among the most recognizable fungi. Young puffballs with pure white flesh are
excellent, tender, and nutritious. As they mature the flesh turns yellow and
becomes bitter. The *Scleroderma* group ("earthballs") has dark flesh inside,
is coarse and uninviting, and its edibility has not been determined.

## The Boleti Color-Change Myth vs. Reality

A persistent myth holds that any boletus that changes color when cut is
poisonous. McIlvaine addresses this directly:

- **Noxious Boleti** are bitter and change color (to red or blue) when cut.
- **Color change alone does not indicate poison.** It must be accompanied by
  a distinctly bitter taste to signal danger.
- Many edible Boleti also exhibit color change on exposure to air, leading to
  unwarranted fear and blacklisting of perfectly safe species.

The practical rule: taste a small piece (spit it out). If bitter, discard.
If the [[boletus-species-alarming-color-changes]] but the flesh is mild, it is likely safe. No recorded
death from boletus poisoning exists in the historical record.

## The Amanita Genus — Identification Features

The *Amanita* genus is the most dangerous group of mushrooms. Of approximately
30 members, at least 8 are edible but at least 5 contain a deadly alkaloid
responsible for most fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide.

### Key Features of Amanitas

**Gills:** White (except *A. caesaria*, which has yellow gills). Free from the stem.

**Cap:** Shows remnants of the universal veil as warts, scurf, or scales
(easily removable). Cap color varies widely:

- Bright scarlet — *A. muscarius* ([[fly-agaric]])
- Lemon-yellow — *A. mappa*
- Satiny white — *A. vernus*, *A. phalloides* ([[amanita-phalloides-death-cap]])

**Stem:** Tall, free from gills, with a ring near the top and a swollen bulbous base.

**Volva:** The base is covered by a cup-like sheath (volva), the remains of
the universal veil at ground level. The poisonous Amanita *invariably springs
from the volva*. This is the single most important diagnostic feature. Flesh
is white and tasteless when young.

## Amanita vs. Common Mushroom — Critical Differences

Distinguishing deadly Amanitas from the harmless common mushroom is essential
for any forager:

| Feature | Common Mushroom | Poisonous Amanita |
|---|---|---|
| **Volva (cup at base)** | Never present | Always present |
| **Gill color** | Pink to purple | White (except *A. caesaria*) |
| **Stem height** | Shorter | Taller relative to cap |
| **Cap scales/warts** | Absent | Present (veil remnants) |
| **Ring on stem** | Variable, often inconspicuous | Usually prominent |

The single most reliable feature is the **volva**. If a mushroom has a
cup-like sheath at the base, treat it as potentially deadly. The common
meadow mushroom never possesses a volva — making this the key field mark.

## Palmer's Remedy for Non-Fatal Toadstool Upset

For mild gastrointestinal distress from consuming unfamiliar or slightly
noxious species (non-Amanita only), Julius A. Palmer recommends:

> **Sweet-oil and whiskey in equal proportions.**

This applies *only* to non-Amanita poisoning. [[amanita-alkaloid-medicinal-uses-pilocarpine-treatment]] poisoning
requires immediate emergency medical attention — no home remedy is adequate
for [[bloomfield-amatoxin-poisoning-mechanism]].

## Summary of Edibility by Family

| Family | Spore Surface | Edibility | Danger Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[agaricini]] | Gills/plaits | Mixed — choice edibles and deadly Amanitas | Very high |
| Polyporei/Boleti | Tubes/pores | Mostly edible, some bitter | Low |
| Hydnei | Spines/teeth | Nearly all edible | Negligible |
| Clavaria | External membranes | Nearly all edible | Negligible |
| Lycoperdons | Internal (puffballs) | White-fleshed excellent; dark uncertain | Low |
