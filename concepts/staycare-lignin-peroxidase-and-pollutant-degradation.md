# Lignin Peroxidase and Pollutant Degradation

Lignin peroxidase (LiP) is a water-soluble, glycosylated extracellular
enzyme secreted by white rot fungi. Like manganese peroxidase (MnP), LiP
is dependent on hydrogen peroxide for catalysis. LiP is unique among
fungal peroxidases in its ability to produce radical cations from the
one-electron oxidation of non-phenolic aromatic compounds, making it a
powerful agent for the degradation of recalcitrant environmental pollutants
including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, chlorinated phenols, and
nitroaromatic compounds.

## Discovery and Significance

LiP was the first enzyme shown to be capable of depolymerizing synthetic
lignin in vitro. Isolated initially from [[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]] by
Tien and Kirk (1984), LiP is a haem-containing peroxidase that operates at
an optimum pH below 3.0. The enzyme shows signs of instability under
certain conditions but is protected by veratryl alcohol (VA), a secondary
metabolite produced by many white rot fungi that enhances LiP activity by
protecting it from inactivation by excess H2O2.

## Catalytic Mechanism

LiP has the same heme and similar active site residues as MnP and follows
the same general catalytic cycle. Initial oxidation with H2O2 yields the
two-electron oxidized enzyme (Compound I) containing Fe(IV) and a porphyrin
cation radical. Two steps of single-electron reduction restore the enzyme
to the native state. Compound II can also react with H2O2 to form the
catalytically inactive Compound III, depending on the relative
concentrations of H2O2 to reducing substrate. Compound III may either be
destroyed by further reaction with H2O2 or slowly revert to the native
state.

## Redox Potential and Substrate Specificity

Redox potential is a primary determinant of whether an aromatic compound
is a substrate for LiP. Strong electron-withdrawing groups such as alpha-
carbonyl or nitro groups on the benzene ring tend to deactivate aromatic
nuclei, whereas alkoxy groups tend to activate them. LiP appears relatively
non-specific towards the nature of the reductant and accepts electrons from
a broad range of substrates including sulfides. Small dimethoxylated
non-phenolic aromatics such as VA and 1,4-dimethoxybenzene (DMB) serve as
crucial substrates for LiP.

## Redox Mediation by Veratryl Alcohol

VA is a fungal metabolite produced simultaneously with LiP. The products
of VA oxidation are radical cations that act as one-electron oxidants
themselves, creating reactive radical cations in secondary substrates by
charge transfer. When secondary substrates are phenylpropanoid structures
typifying lignin, carbon-carbon and C-O-ether side chain cleavage reactions
of the radical cations lead to lignin depolymerization. The primary
oxidant (VA radical) is restored following oxidation of the secondary
substrate, creating a catalytic redox shuttle.

The LiP-VA redox mediator system has been documented for a broad range of
pollutant compounds. These include benzo[a]pyrene oxidation, lignin
depolymerization, and aromatic ring-opening and ring hydroxylation
reactions. This mediator system effectively extends both the substrate
range and redox capacity of LiP beyond its direct catalytic reach.

## Degradation of Chlorinated Phenols

LiP from P. chrysosporium catalyzes the peroxidative dechlorination of
polychlorinated phenols to the corresponding 1,4-benzoquinones with
concomitant release of inorganic chloride. The reaction mechanism involves
two sequential one-electron oxidations of the chlorophenol to yield a
cyclohexadienone cation, followed by nucleophilic attack by water and
elimination of chloride. For example, 2,4,6-trichlorophenol is oxidized
to 2,6-dichloro-1,4-benzoquinone, and pentachlorophenol is converted to
tetrachloro-1,4-benzoquinone.

## Role in Chlorophenoxyacetic Acid Degradation

Biodegradation of herbicides such as 2,4,5-T by P. chrysosporium proceeds
through chlorinated phenolic intermediates whose further transformation is
catalyzed by LiP and other ligninolytic enzymes. Interestingly, 2,4-D
degradation has been demonstrated under conditions that suppress LiP and
MnP synthesis, and effective degradation by peroxidase-negative mutants
has been observed, indicating that non-ligninolytic pathways also contribute
to herbicide degradation.

## Inhibition by Pollutant Metabolites

A significant challenge in using LiP for bioremediation is the inhibition
of the enzyme by certain pollutant metabolites. During TNT degradation by
P. chrysosporium, LiP activity disappears when TNT is added to ligninolytic
cultures. This inhibition is caused not by TNT itself but by intermediate
metabolites such as 2-hydroxylamino-4,6-dinitrotoluene and 4-hydroxylamino-
2,6-dinitrotoluene. Amino-dinitrotoluene congeners are not inhibitory to
LiP, suggesting that hydroxylamino intermediates are the actual inhibitors.

## Isozyme Diversity

LiP comprises a family of isozymes whose major forms have been
crystallized, their genes identified, and site-directed mutagenesis programs
initiated. This isozyme diversity allows different strains to produce
distinct enzyme profiles optimized for different environmental conditions
and pollutant substrates. The genetic manipulation of LiP isozymes offers
potential for engineering fungi with enhanced bioremediation capabilities.

## Enzyme Localization in Vivo

Immunogold-cytochemical labelling studies using electron microscopy have
shown that LiP molecules are too large to penetrate intact secondary wood
cell walls and remain close to the surface of fungal hyphae or adhere to
the inner surface of the wood cell wall. Degradation of lignocellulose
occurs by surface interaction between cell wall and enzymes, but initiation
of decay can occur at a distance from the hyphae through diffusible small
molecular mass molecules.

## Synergy with Manganese Peroxidase and Laccase

LiP, MnP, and laccase form the triad of ligninolytic enzymes that white rot
fungi deploy for pollutant degradation. While LiP is uniquely capable of
oxidizing non-phenolic aromatics with high redox potentials, MnP extends
degradation reach through diffusible Mn(III) chelates, and laccase handles
phenolic substrates. Together they provide complementary pathways for
attacking the diverse chemical structures found in environmental pollutants.
The combined action of all three enzymes produces more complete
mineralization than any single enzyme acting alone.
