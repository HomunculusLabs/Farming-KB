# Fukuoka on Education and Modern Schooling

In [[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]], [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] offers pointed observations about the failures of modern education and proposes alternatives rooted in direct experience of nature. His critique extends from conventional classrooms to universities and connects to his broader [[fukuoka-road-critique-of-modern-science]].

## No Natural Children Remain

Fukuoka laments that "there just are no natural children around any longer who live according to their instincts." He links this to the broader destruction of nature: with nature ruined, "natural people, natural farming, and natural diet are all receding and vanishing at an accelerating speed." The education system, in his view, accelerates this disconnection rather than correcting it.

## The Loss of Innocent Perception

Education, as Fukuoka sees it, actively destroys the child's innate connection to nature. "The flower perceived innocently is itself divine nature, but when examined with the intellect, this is transformed into the cold flower of reason." Learning about nature through textbooks and analysis "distances one from nature" rather than bringing one closer. The child who would naturally "frolic innocently with this wonderful sprite, this angel inhabiting the fields" is instead taught to dissect and categorize.

## The Innocence of Children and Natural Learning

Fukuoka's chapter "The Innocence of Children" offers his most concrete vision for how children naturally learn. He recounts a visit to his farm by "a group of forty or fifty kindergarteners" who "ran about, playing and filling the orchard with their laughter." They found seeds on the large, thick-stemmed burdock growing there and "had fun throwing these onto the clothes of other children." Fukuoka saw the deeper meaning: "just as the birds and wind carry off maple seed, the playing of these children was helping to sow the burdock seed. They had become a part of that natural vegetable garden."

He draws the radical conclusion that "if you try throwing weak, spiritless youths into the hills to fend for themselves, they'll manage quite well. This means that they know how to live." An adult cannot practice natural farming "without distinguishing between clover and weeds," but "young people grow and search for food in the midst of nature as they find it." The implication is that children possess an innate wisdom about living that schooling suppresses and that direct immersion in nature would restore.

Fukuoka argues that "scientific knowledge is unnecessary" for this kind of learning, "but only while natural farming remains immature and confined within the realm of scientific agriculture." When a "grander, more 'macro' form of natural farming is established, anything at all — wood sorrel included — will be allowed." The goal is education that frees children to find their place within nature's wholeness rather than training them to manipulate its parts.

## Children's Natural Curiosity vs. Schooling's Suppression

Fukuoka's African experiences, documented in [[fukuoka-road-africa-greening-desert]], provide a striking example of children's natural curiosity. When he gave Somali refugee children seeds, they "shrugged their shoulders, saying 'Wangaranai' (I don't understand)." But when he gestured how to plant, they responded immediately. Two or three days later, twenty children came running: "Kai, kai" (Come, come), beckoning him to see the daikon, onion, and cucumber seedlings sprouting at the river's edge. He hadn't expected "such an easy success."

No classroom instruction was needed. The children learned by doing, guided by their own curiosity and the immediate feedback of the natural world. This stands in sharp contrast to what Fukuoka saw as the deadening effect of formal schooling, where children are "weighed down with petty, microcosmic scientific knowledge" and "lose the freedom to acquire with their own hands macrocosmic wisdom." When we "weigh the child down with petty, microcosmic scientific knowledge, he loses the freedom to acquire with his own hands macrocosmic wisdom."

## Japanese Schools "Decaying"

During his American travels, Fukuoka met two Japanese sisters of junior high school age at a Korean Zen temple in New York. They were growing tomatoes using natural farming methods. When he talked with them, he learned "that their mother, who lives in Kobe, had sent them here, saying that Japanese high schools are decaying." Fukuoka was "astounded and impressed" — "the simple, rustic life that Japanese people have forgotten is still to be found in America's countryside."

This anecdote reveals Fukuoka's view that the rot of modern education is not confined to any one country but is part of the broader civilizational disease he diagnoses throughout [[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]].

## Michio Kushi's Educational Vision

Fukuoka documents the efforts of Michio Kushi, leader of the macrobiotic movement, who "claimed that conventional schools today are no good" and was "working to establish an international college based on principles of natural order in order to revolutionize education." Kushi had "institutes in several countries teaching macrobiotic cooking, philosophy, and medicine, as well as spiritual development and the reconstruction of society in a more peaceful direction." He had purchased a former Franciscan monastery for the school, with the word "nature" in its name.

## The International Nature University

In Paris, Fukuoka encountered Dr. Norman William, an American Indian chief's son practicing natural farming in the center of Paris. William was establishing the "International Nature University" on a site near the Palais d'Elysée — a wooded area with trees over two yards in diameter, dark rich soil, and twenty acres of virgin forest. Fukuoka endorsed the location, noting that in Japan, "people often build shrines and temples where the trees are large and the soil is black."

Fukuoka was told that the university "will be built by twelve holy men." In a ceremony that "had all the charm of a fairy tale," William had him touch a constellation pole in an Indian tepee, declaring: "Now you are a founder. Whenever you visit, you will stay here and give lectures or do whatever you please." William, who had influence at UNESCO, envisioned a school where students would learn through direct engagement with nature rather than classroom abstraction.

Kushi too asked Fukuoka to join his effort to create "a totally new type of university." Fukuoka replied: "A Japanese farmer doesn't have that kind of time to spare. But I'll be happy to give whatever advice I can on farming." Yet he noted with hope that "new higher institutes of learning critical of today's universities may soon emerge around the world."

## Real Education Comes from Nature, Not Classrooms

Fukuoka's core educational philosophy is that real learning happens through immersion in nature, not through classroom instruction. He writes: "If children are allowed to play freely in a field that transcends science, they will develop natural methods of farming by themselves." The kindergarten children who played in his orchard, scattering burdock seeds as they played, were learning more about natural agriculture than any textbook could teach.

This philosophy connects to his broader [[fukuoka-road-observation-natures-patterns]] approach: the most important knowledge is not analytical but participatory. Children who grow food, observe insects, and feel the soil develop an understanding that no amount of scientific instruction can replicate. Fukuoka's vision for the International Nature University was essentially this principle institutionalized — a place where students would learn farming by farming, learn ecology by living within an ecosystem, and learn philosophy by experiencing the wholeness of nature directly.

## Agricultural Research as Failed Education

Fukuoka reserves particular scorn for agricultural universities and research stations. When he told researchers to scatter straw uncut on fields, "this was laughed off by the folks at the Ehime Prefecture facility as just too crude." It took nine years for them to accept the method. "Government research centers and universities don't do studies of this sort. They work only in the opposite direction."

The failure of agricultural education is symptomatic of a deeper problem: institutions train specialists who can only see nature through the lens of their particular discipline. A plant pathologist sees pathogens; a fertilizer expert sees nutrient deficiencies; an entomologist sees pests. None sees the whole. This fragmented education produces fragmented knowledge, which leads to the "incoherent chaos" Fukuoka describes in his [[fukuoka-road-critique-of-modern-science]].

## Learning from Insects

In one of his most subversive educational anecdotes, Fukuoka describes discovering that insects in his fields were creating new varieties of rice — performing cross-pollination more skillfully than he could with scissors and pincers. College students who visited dismissed this discovery "as having little value" because "man is much better at creating new varieties than the insects." Fukuoka's response: "Why, that's incredible. Man should never have gone to all that trouble." This encapsulates his view that the most important lessons come not from classrooms but from observing nature directly.

## Education and the Critique of Modern Civilization

Fukuoka's educational critique is inseparable from his broader civilizational diagnosis. Modern education transmits the scientific worldview that splits God, nature, and man into separate domains. It trains children to see the flower as an object of analysis rather than "divine nature." It produces the specialists who build irrigation canals in Somalia that crumble, the agronomists who prescribe chemical fertilizers for dying soil, the geneticists who look for life in DNA.

The alternative is not anti-intellectualism but a different kind of intelligence — one that begins from wholeness and innocence rather than fragmentation and cleverness. As he puts it in [[fukuoka-road-do-nothing-farming]], "all is unnecessary" when one sees from the standpoint of God. Education should cultivate this sight, not bury it under "petty, microcosmic scientific knowledge."

## Computers Replacing Teachers

Fukuoka warns that "an age in which parents and teachers worry over the ideologically prejudiced education of children is still preferable" to what is coming: "the handling of all problems and concerns by computers and robots in place of humans." He fears the day when "stacks of data processed and output by computers will be transmitted directly into the human brain, which will have become a mere data receiving unit."

## The Pit of Knowledge

Fukuoka's most sustained critique of formal education comes in a section he calls "The Pit of Knowledge." He asks: "What sort of thing does a college do? Properly speaking, the goal of a college should be to create people who are not lost, to create sages — people without doubts or illusions. That is why students learn things at school. Long ago, I think the purpose of study was to create individuals who have no need to know anything. But colleges today are different. Everything has been broken down into disciplines, discrete fields of specialized learning have been created in which the more one studies, the less one comes to know the world at large."

He illustrates the problem with the study of rice: "Everything is broken up into discrete domains and studied." Plant pathologists examine one spot on a leaf, entomologists study insects, fertilizer experts look at soil chemistry. "As this underground pit of knowledge is enlarged, the darkness of the unknown surrounding it grows. One bit of knowledge creates two new queries in our minds. To say 'I understand' is to not understand."

## Students at the University of California, Davis

At the agricultural division of UC Davis, one of the foremost agricultural science programs in the U.S., Fukuoka met with students running a farm aimed at natural farming. The students asked provocative questions: "You talk of speaking with the crops, but do you mean by this that the eggplant is unhappy?" Another asked: "Isn't it true that the closer one approaches to nature and natural farming, the less this works to the benefit of mankind?"

Fukuoka's response was characteristically direct: "If you want to know whether the eggplant is happy or sad, ask the eggplant. But you ought to be worrying about yourselves rather than the eggplant." He added: "If people try to grow crops with their minds, they end up as mere farmers. But if they look on with detachment, they are able to observe the entire universe through the crops."

## Learning Under Trees, Not Fluorescent Lights

Fukuoka contrasted academic learning with natural learning: "You listen to your teachers speak about nature in lecture halls under fluorescent lighting. That's why you find the lectures dull. When a man and a woman sit together under the cool shade of a tree on a sunny day and talk, that alone is enjoyable, isn't it?" For Fukuoka, the proper education happens not in classrooms but through direct, innocent contact with nature — the kind of learning that happens when one simply observes a rice plant growing under the sun without breaking it into parts.

## The True Purpose of Education

For Fukuoka, the ultimate purpose of education should be to produce people who can live without needing to know anything — who trust nature rather than their own intellectual accumulation. The university that understands a single rice plant in its wholeness has no need for a discipline devoted to rice. "Once we are able to look at the rice and understand it, then a discipline devoted to rice is no longer necessary." This radical vision of education — one that dissolves its own disciplines — is the logical extension of do-nothing farming applied to learning itself.
