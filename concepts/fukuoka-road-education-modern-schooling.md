## Agricultural Research as Failed Education

Fukuoka reserves particular scorn for agricultural universities and research stations. When he told researchers to scatter straw uncut on fields, "this was laughed off by the folks at the Ehime Prefecture facility as just too crude." It took nine years for them to accept the method. "Government research centers and universities don"t do studies of this sort. They work only in the opposite direction."

The failure of agricultural education is symptomatic of a deeper problem: institutions train specialists who can only see nature through the lens of their particular discipline. A plant pathologist sees pathogens; a fertilizer expert sees [[fukuoka-road-do-nothing-farming]], "all is unnecessary" when one sees from the standpoint of God. Education should cultivate this sight, not bury it under "petty, microcosmic scientific knowledge."

## Computers Replacing Teachers

Fukuoka warns that "an age in which parents and teachers worry over the ideologically prejudiced education of children is still preferable" to what is coming: "the handling of all problems and concerns by computers and robots in place of humans." He fears the day when "stacks of data processed and output by computers will be transmitted directly into the human brain, which will have become a mere data receiving unit."

## The Pit of Knowledge

Fukuoka"s most sustained critique of formal education comes in a section he calls "The Pit of Knowledge." He asks: "What sort of thing does a college do? Properly speaking, the goal of a college should be to create people who are not lost, to create sages — people without doubts or illusions. That is why students learn things at school. Long ago, I think the purpose of study was to create individuals who have no need to know anything. But colleges today are different. Everything has been broken down into disciplines, discrete fields of specialized learning have been created in which the more one studies, the less one comes to know the world at large."

He illustrates the problem with the study of rice: "Everything is broken up into discrete domains and studied." Plant pathologists examine one spot on a leaf, entomologists study insects, fertilizer experts look at soil chemistry. "As this underground pit of knowledge is enlarged, the darkness of the unknown surrounding it grows. One bit of knowledge creates two new queries in our minds. To say "I understand' is to not understand."

## Students at the University of California, Davis

At the agricultural division of UC Davis, one of the foremost agricultural science programs in the U.S., Fukuoka met with students running a farm aimed at natural farming. The students asked provocative questions: "You talk of speaking with the crops, but do you mean by this that the eggplant is unhappy?" Another asked: "Isn"t it true that the closer one approaches to nature and natural farming, the less this works to the benefit of mankind?"

Fukuoka"s response was characteristically direct: "If you want to know whether the eggplant is happy or sad, ask the eggplant. But you ought to be worrying about yourselves rather than the eggplant." He added: "If people try to grow crops with their minds, they end up as mere farmers. But if they look on with detachment, they are able to observe the entire universe through the crops."

## Learning Under Trees, Not Fluorescent Lights

Fukuoka contrasted academic learning with natural learning: "You listen to your teachers speak about nature in lecture halls under fluorescent lighting. That"s why you find the lectures dull. When a man and a woman sit together under the cool shade of a tree on a sunny day and talk, that alone is enjoyable, isn't it?" For Fukuoka, the proper education happens not in classrooms but through direct, innocent contact with nature — the kind of learning that happens when one simply observes a rice plant growing under the sun without breaking it into parts.

## The True Purpose of Education

For Fukuoka, the ultimate purpose of education should be to produce people who can live without needing to know anything — who trust nature rather than their own intellectual accumulation. The university that understands a single rice plant in its wholeness has no need for a discipline devoted to rice. "Once we are able to look at the rice and understand it, then a discipline devoted to rice is no longer necessary." This radical vision of education — one that dissolves its own disciplines — is the logical extension of do-nothing farming applied to learning itself.
