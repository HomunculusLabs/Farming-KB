## The Mislabeled Seedling Problem

Unless a seedling is a visibly distinct variety (like red cabbage vs. green),
the buyer cannot tell if the label is correct. Several ethical temptations
face seedling sellers:

- **Pretty plastic labels** with pictures cost as much as or more than the seed
  itself, creating economic incentive to reuse labels
- **Seed shortages** may tempt vendors to substitute a similar variety and
  mislabel it
- **Tomatoes** are one species that is not likely to be mislabeled because
  customers remember flavors and will notice differences
- **Green bell peppers and eggplant** are entirely different — the garden
  center operator may not be able to get enough of a home-garden variety and
  may fill trays with a canning or processing variety instead

## Pot-Bound Seedlings: How to Identify

Seedling raisers face a narrow window: plants go from too young to ethically
sell to almost too old to ethically sell in under ten days. The profit motive
encourages keeping seedlings looking good past their prime.

### Too Young

Root system has not filled the pot; the soil ball crumbles during
transplanting. Excessive damage to delicate root hairs means the seedling
will not grow well for a week to ten days.

### Just Right

Roots have extended to the extremities of the container, soil ball holds
together during transplanting. The seedling can grow immediately.

### Pot-Bound (Too Old)

Roots have overfilled the pot, wrapping around and around inside. Even with
adequate watering, these seedlings wilt easily until new roots grow into
soil. Leaves hardly grow for one to two weeks after transplanting.

### Detection Method

Lift the seedling by the stem between thumb and forefinger, gently tap the
side of the pot, and see if any root tips are visible at the bottom. If the
root-to-top ratio looks unbalanced, the seedling is pot-bound.
