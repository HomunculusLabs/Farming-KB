## Permaculture in Humid Landscapes: Keypoint and Keyline Design

In Pamphlet II of the [[permaculture-design]] Course, [[bill-mollison]] turns from the global environmental crisis to practical landscape design, beginning with humid landscapes — those receiving more than 30 inches of annual rainfall. The central thesis is deceptively simple: the storage of water on the landscape. The critical observation is that modern civilization, particularly in America, is failing to do this. Mollison"s approach, building on P.A. Yeomans' keyline concepts, provides a systematic method for capturing, storing, and distributing water across agricultural and forested land using the land's own topography.

## The Humid Landscape Profile

In humid landscapes that are not extremely young (volcanic or newly faulted), the terrain develops softly rounded outlines over time. When walking up a valley or along a ridge, the hills display a characteristic rounded "S" shaped profile. This profile is the key to understanding water movement and the basis for all [[keyline-design]].

The transition point where the landscape changes from convex (bulging outward) to concave (curving inward) is a critical location called the **keypoint**. This point marks where the hillside"s shape shifts from shedding water (convex) to collecting and slowing it (concave). It is the highest point in the valley profile where water can be economically stored using mechanical tools. Above the keypoint, slopes are too steep for conventional earthworks; below it, the land flattens sufficiently to allow dam construction and water distribution.

## Reading Water Flow on the Landscape

Understanding how individual raindrops travel across the landscape is essential to keyline design. Each raindrop, wherever it falls, takes the shortest path across the landscape from where it lands to where it reaches the river line — the lowest point of the valley. This shortest path crosses contour lines at right angles, because that represents the steepest descent between two elevations.

As rain falls on hilltops, it runs off. The water follows these cross-contour paths, gathering in small channels that merge into creeks, which join the main valley flow. Once water enters the river valley, it is committed to flowing to the sea — it has left the usable landscape. The designer's task is to intercept and store this water before it reaches the valley floor.

## Locating the Keypoint

The keypoint can be identified either through field observation or from a contour map. On a map, it appears where contour lines begin to spread apart — indicating the transition from steep to flatter ground. In the field, it is where the valley head, where the small creeks originate, begins to level out. At this point, the keyline starts to fall from approximately 1:1,000 to 1:2,000 below contour — a very gentle gradient.

## The Keyline System: Treating the Landscape as a Roof and Tank

Having identified the keypoint, Mollison's approach treats the entire landscape as if it were a roof (the hillside) draining into a tank (the keypoint dam). The system works as follows:

### The Diversion Drain
Starting at the keypoint, a gently falling drain (or "gutter") is constructed around the hill. This drain follows the contour with an extremely slight fall — one foot of drop per 2,000 feet of length. The drain intercepts all runoff from above the keypoint, collecting water that would otherwise flow directly down the watercourses to the valley floor.

### The Keypoint Dam
At the keypoint itself, a small dam is constructed. This is the highest point in the valley profile where dam construction is economically feasible. The dam gathers all the water from the top of the hill down to that point. While not the most economical dam per unit of water stored (it requires substantial earthwork), the keypoint dam is strategically invaluable because it operates at the highest usable elevation, enabling gravity-fed distribution to all lower areas.

The dam can be made as large as resources allow. Once filled, it provides a year-round water supply that can be released through the dam wall by siphon or lock-pipe into the contour drain system.

### Irrigation Channels
From the keypoint, irrigation channels extend along the contour to any point below. These channels range in slope from 1:200 to 1:2000. Water from the keypoint dam can be directed through these channels to irrigate pasture, crops, or forest systems anywhere on the lower landscape — all by gravity, without pumps.

### Flow Control
Mollison described a simple flow control mechanism: a sheet of canvas or plastic fastened like a flag to a light plastic pipe, with a chain attached to the other end serving as a weight. By pegging the flag down within the drain, water can be held back until sufficient depth is achieved, then released by lifting the flag. This allows precise control of irrigation flow without valves or mechanical infrastructure.

## Design Implications: Forestry Above, Irrigation Below

The keypoint naturally divides the landscape into two management zones. Above the keypoint, where slopes are steep and erosion risk is high, forestry is the appropriate land use. Trees stabilize the soil, slow runoff, and increase infiltration. Below the keypoint, where the land flattens and water can be distributed by gravity, irrigated pasture and crops are appropriate. The keypoint dam provides the water supply; the contour channels provide the distribution network.

This zoning principle — forests on steep upper slopes, intensive production on flatter lower land with irrigation — is one of the most fundamental and widely applied patterns in permaculture landscape design.

## The Broader Keyline Philosophy

Mollison"s treatment of keyline builds on P.A. Yeomans' original keyline plan (detailed in *Water for Every Farm*) but adapts it within the broader [[permaculture-design-framework]]. Several key principles emerge:

1. **Work with gravity, not against it**: All water movement in a keyline system is gravity-fed. The designer's role is to position storages and channels so that water moves where it is needed without mechanical energy input.

2. **Store water high, use it low**: The strategic position of the keypoint dam — at the highest feasible point — maximizes the area that can be served by gravity flow. Water stored low in the valley serves only the valley floor; water stored at the keypoint serves the entire lower landscape.

3. **Interrupt the path to the sea**: Every drop of water that reaches the valley floor is committed to flowing to the ocean. The earlier water is intercepted and stored, the more uses it can serve before leaving the system.

4. **Start from the highest intervention point**: The general principle that "the closer to the source we can intervene, the greater the network we can establish" applies to all resources, not just water. Nutrients, seeds, and information all follow similar logic.

5. **Cascade resources through multiple uses**: Water captured at the keypoint can sequentially serve domestic use, animal watering, irrigation, aquaculture, and wildlife habitat before it continues downslope. Each use adds value without consuming the resource.

## Practical Considerations

Mollison noted that the keyline system requires careful surveying and earthwork, but the investment pays dividends for decades or centuries. The dams, drains, and channels are permanent infrastructure that, once built, operate without ongoing energy input. The system is scalable — applicable to small homesteads of a few acres or large properties of thousands of acres.

The contour map is the designer's primary tool. By studying where contours spread (keypoint locations), where they converge (water concentration points), and where they run parallel (potential irrigation channels), the entire water management plan for a property can be developed before any earth is moved.

This approach stands in stark contrast to conventional water engineering, which typically dams the lowest point in the valley — creating a reservoir that is difficult to distribute from and that serves only a fraction of the landscape. The keyline principle of storing high and distributing wide represents one of permaculture's most practical and immediately applicable design insights.
