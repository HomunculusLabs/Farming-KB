## Holmgren Design Thinking Guidelines

## The Two-Way Process of Observation

Observation and interaction involve a two-way process between the designer and
the system being designed. Because of the bias built into our culture by Cartesian
dualism, we need constant reminding that observation influences reality. The maxim
"everything works both ways" is a foundation for design thinking that avoids the
pitfalls of both naive objectivity and pure subjectivity.

## Core Design Maxims

Holmgren provides a set of specific guidelines that help prevent designers from
falling back into dualistic thinking:

- **All observations are relative** — observation reflects internal state as much
  as external reality. Ethics and ideology act as filters determining what we see.
  It is better to be clear about assumptions than to pretend to objectivity.
- **Top-down thinking, bottom-up action** — step back to see connections and
  contexts that reveal a subject as part of larger systems, while acting at
  leverage points where small-scale interventions can influence larger systems.
  This counters the prevailing mode of top-down governmental and corporate action.
- **The landscape is the textbook** — all the knowledge needed to create and
  manage low-energy human support systems can come from working with nature.
  All senses provide valuable information: smelling or tasting soil reveals
  biological balance invisible to the eye.
- **Failure is useful so long as we learn** — the [[holmgren-action-learning-loop]] allows
  starting from limited knowledge and broadening perspective incrementally.
  Don't assume you caused any success; conduct small trials and consider
  alternative explanations.
- **Elegant solutions are simple, even invisible** — enormous complexity often
  indicates poor design. Self-organizing living systems may do most of the work.
  Effective systems work so well we don't notice them until they fail.
- **Make the smallest intervention necessary** — large-scale interventions are
  likely to do more harm than good and require large energy inputs. The Bradley
  method of bush regeneration exemplifies this: careful observation and minimal
  disturbance rather than aggressive herbicide application.
- **Avoid too much of a good thing** — the temptation to amplify successful
  actions is universal but often destructive. Overuse of fertilizer, over-
  consumption of sugar, fat, and protein — all reflect the same pattern of
  exceeding natural limits in conditions of surplus.
- **The problem is the solution** — things we view negatively may have positive
  aspects more important than the negative perception. Weeds may be environmental
  indicators, soil repair agents, or undervalued resources.
- **Recognise and break out of design cul-de-sacs** — proven solutions become
  entrenched through tradition and institutions. Rapid environmental change can
  transform optimum solutions into dead ends with no way forward.

## The Problem Is the Solution

This is perhaps the most distinctive and counterintuitive of Holmgren's maxims.
It expresses the idea that things are not always as they seem — what we view as
negatives may harbor positive aspects that compensate for or exceed the perceived
problem.

### Weeds as Surplus

Pests can be viewed as surpluses of nature that need to be used rather than
destroyed. Weeds may function as:

- **[[fungi-as-environmental-indicators-and-biosensors]]** signaling the need for management change
- **Soil repair agents** fixing damage caused by previous practices
- **Undervalued resources** with economic or cultural uses we fail to recognize

### Finding Solutions Where Problems Are Extreme

The best solutions to problems are often found where the problem is most severe.
Co-evolution over time develops the most effective responses in extreme
conditions. Where a problem is less severe, people often ignore it or develop
solutions that use more effort and resources than necessary.

Holmgren's example: the Amalfi coast of Italy has developed creative solutions
for very steep land that would not emerge in gentler terrain. Conversely,
Mediterranean regions have poorly developed [[comparison-drip-irrigation-vs-rainwater-harvesting]] compared to
Australia because abundant permanent water sources reduced the incentive.

### Open Inquiry Over Urgent Solutions

An open, inquiring attitude to problems is almost always more fruitful than an
urgent demand for solutions. The latter is often driven by fear and unquestioned
consensus about the nature of the problem. The principle connects to obtaining a
yield and creatively using change — problems reframed as surplus become
resources.

## The Action Learning Loop

The action learning loop provides a simple process for progressive design
improvement: observe, design, implement, observe results, adjust, repeat. This
allows starting from wherever you are, no matter how little knowledge you have,
and progressively broadening your perspective through incremental adjustment.

When working with complex natural systems, it is essential to remember that we
don't understand all factors and that [[fukuoka-nature-no-cause-and-effect]] are often a loop or web
rather than a linear chain. Small trials with careful observation of multiple
possible causes for success or failure produce better outcomes than large
interventions based on assumed understanding.

## Design Cul-de-Sacs

In nature and human behavior, proven solutions tend to become entrenched while
recent innovations are easily swept away. This conservatism is functional when
environmental change is slow — internal novelty is more often harmful than
useful. But rapid change in environmental conditions can transform the "optimum
solution" into a design cul-de-sac with no way forward and no going back.

The frog-in-boiling-water metaphor illustrates this: gradual change prevents
recognition of danger until it is too late. [[energy-descent-action-planning]] demands that we
anticipate which current solutions will become cul-de-sacs and actively develop
alternatives before crisis forces the transition.
