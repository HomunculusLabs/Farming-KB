# Neurotropic Fungi in Oceania

Oceania (Australia, New Zealand, and Pacific islands) has significant diversity of
[[neurotropic-fungi-world-checklist]], with particularly high species richness in
eastern Australia and New Zealand. These regions show remarkable biogeographic connections to
species found in the Americas and Southeast Asia.

## Australia

### Eastern Australia
Eastern Australia has the highest documented neurotropic fungal diversity on the continent:
- **Psilocybe subaeruginosa** - The most common and well-known Australian neurotropic species. Found
  in subtropical and temperate forests, particularly on woodchips and in forest litter
- **P. cubensis** - Common in tropical and subtropical regions, growing on bovine dung
- **P. cyanescens** - Known from disturbed habitats
- **P. aucklandii** - Closely related to P. zapotecorum from Mexico and South America (both in
  Section Zapotecorum), demonstrating a remarkable trans-Pacific biogeographic connection
- **Gymnopilus purpuratus** - Widespread
- **Panaeolina foenisecii**, **Panaeolus** species - Common in pastures
- **Gerronema fibula** - Disjunct distribution: also found in Malaysia, New Guinea, Solomon Islands,
  South America, and Europe
- **Amanita muscaria** - Introduced with exotic pine plantations; now naturalized in eucalyptus
  and pine forests
- **Hypholoma** species documented

### Western and Central Australia
- Very poorly surveyed for neurotropic fungi
- Harsh arid conditions limit fungal diversity in interior regions

### Tasmania
- **P. semilanceata** - Present in temperate pastures, representing the southern extent of this
  widespread species

## New Zealand

New Zealand has a surprisingly rich neurotropic mycota:
- **Psilocybe aucklandii** - Closely related to P. zapotecorum (Mexico/South America); both belong
  to Section Zapotecorum. This relationship was documented by Allen, Merlin & Jansen (1991)
- **P. makarorae** - Closely related to P. mexicana (Mexico/Guatemala) and P. samuiensis (Thailand),
  all belonging to Section Mexicanae. Another striking trans-Pacific connection
- **P. semilanceata** - Abundant in New Zealand pastures
- **P. cubensis** - Present in tropical northern areas
- **Copelandia cyanescens** - Documented
- **Panaeolus subbalteatus** - Present
- **Amanita muscaria** - Commonly found in association with introduced Pinus species
- **Claviceps** species on pasture grasses
- **Hypholoma** species

## Pacific Islands

### New Caledonia
- **Hypholoma neocaledonica** - An endemic species (Guzman, 1979, 1980, 1983)

### Solomon Islands
- **Gerronema fibula** - Documented by Corner (1994)

### Samoa
- **Copelandia cyanescens** - Used traditionally; documented by Cox (1981)

### Hawaiian Islands
- No native Psilocybe species have been recorded, despite the Hawaiian Archipelago's tropical
  climate
- **Copelandia cyanescens** grows at high elevations (up to 3,000 m on Maui)
- **C. anomala**, **C. bispora**, **C. cambodginiensis**, **C. tropicalis** all documented
- **Panaeolus subbalteatus**, **P. sphinctrinus** present
- **Amanita muscaria** - Present
- See Allen (1998), "Magic Mushrooms of the Hawaiian Islands"

### Macquarie Island (Subantarctic)
- **Panaeolus mollearinus** - The southernmost record of a neurotropic fungus (Singer, 1960)

## Biogeographic Significance

The Oceania region provides crucial evidence for understanding the evolutionary history of
neurotropic fungi:

1. **Trans-Pacific connections:** The relationships between P. aucklandii/P. zapotecorum and
   P. makarorae/P. mexicana/P. samuiensis suggest ancient Gondwanan connections or dispersal
   routes linking South America, Southeast Asia, and Australasia

2. **High diversity despite limited survey:** Australia and New Zealand have been less
   mycologically explored than Europe but show comparable or higher species richness, supporting
   the southern hemisphere origin hypothesis

3. **Endemic species:** Several species (P. aucklandii, P. makarorae, H. neocaledonica) are
   restricted to Oceania, suggesting long-term evolution in isolation
