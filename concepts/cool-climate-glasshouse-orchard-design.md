## Cool Climate Glasshouse and Orchard Design

Cool temperate humid climates are the regions where greenhouses are most commonly used — for speciality crop or aquatic species production, for house heating, and for winter production of vegetable and fruit crops. Modern glasshouses are becoming very sophisticated with respect to heat energy conservation and heat absorption, as **70–90% of the cost of crop** can be attributed to artificial heating. This chapter covers the design principles for efficient glasshouses and the fundamentals of cool-climate orchard systems.

## Glasshouse Design Principles

### Insulation Requirements

Insulation is essential at multiple levels of the glasshouse structure:

**Ground insulation**: The ground below the greenhouse must be insulated, usually as a trench about 1 m deep provided with panel insulation, or with straw or sawdust-filled plastic bags. Without ground insulation, stored heat escapes rapidly into the surrounding cold earth.

**Structural insulation**: The endwalls, poleward walls, and poleward slope of the roof should all be solid insulated walls. Only the sunward roof slope and wall need glazing, and even these can be air-insulated using:
- Trapped bubble insulation as standard plastic sheets
- Air maintained at pressure between double plastic walls
- Insulated shutters at night to cover the glazed areas

### Internal Heat Sources

Inside the greenhouse, where the whole earth floor is insulated from the cold earth outside, several additional heat masses can be provided:

- **Water storage**: Plastic or metal tanks or drums of water (some can serve as fish ponds)
- **Active heat from animals**: Areas below plant benches can house rabbits, guinea pigs, poultry, or small [[permaculture-domestic-animals]] at night, providing considerable winter heat
- **[[permaculture]] strategies for the humid landscape.

### Integration with Water and Landscape

Orchards in humid cool climates benefit from:

- **Keyline [[humus]] through groundcover and mulch, and mineral correction

### Successional Planting with Brambles

As discussed in berry production, fruit trees can be established within blackberry clumps. Apple, fig, quince, pear, and plum trees (ungrafted whips) planted inside the edge of blackberry brambles grow and fruit in 4–6 years. Cattle attracted by fallen fruit trample out the blackberries, creating a self-managing succession from bramble thicket to productive orchard. After 7+ years, the mature trees are sufficiently hardy to withstand browsing by hoofed animals, completing the transition from protected nursery to open orchard.

### Animal Integration

Orchards in cool climates are ideal for integration with livestock:

- **Poultry** can control insect pests and contribute fertility through droppings
- **Cattle and sheep** can graze the orchard floor, managing groundcover and fallen fruit
- **Pigs** can be used for site preparation, rooting out brambles and preparing ground for new plantings
- **Geese** can maintain grass between trees and contribute to weed control

### Orchard Layout Considerations

The design of orchards should account for:

- **Contour planting** on slopes to [[query-how-do-i-garden-on-a-slope-and-prevent-erosion]] and facilitate irrigation
- **Species selection** based on frost-hardiness and chilling-hour requirements
- **Pollination partners** planted in appropriate ratios and proximities
- **Access lanes** for harvesting, pruning, and animal management
- **Shelter belts** of mixed species to reduce wind speed over the orchard floor

## The Design Integration Principle

Both glasshouse and orchard systems in cool humid climates demonstrate the permaculture principle that **every element should perform multiple functions**:

- A greenhouse provides not only crop production but also house heating, animal shelter, compost processing, and water storage
- An orchard provides fruit, livestock forage, wind protection, soil building, [[water-management]], and [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]]
- Water serves irrigation, domestic use, energy, aquaculture, nutrient transport, and fire control simultaneously

The cool humid climate, despite its challenges of cold, wind, and seasonal limitation, offers abundant rainfall, fertile glacial soils, and natural forest productivity. By designing glasshouses and orchards as integrated components of a whole landscape system — rather than isolated production units — the permaculture designer can create resilient, energy-efficient food systems that leverage the strengths of this climate while mitigating its constraints.
