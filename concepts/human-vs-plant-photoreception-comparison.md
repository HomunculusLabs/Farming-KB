## Human Photoreception

Human vision relies on a sophisticated system of photoreceptor cells located in the retina at
the back of the eyeball. The retina contains approximately 125 million rod cells and 6 million
cone cells packed into an area roughly the size of a passport photo — equivalent to a digital
camera with approximately 130 megapixel resolution.

- **Rods**: These photoreceptors are sensitive to all [[blesching-cannabis-improved-night-vision]]. They contain the photopigment rhodopsin,
which absorbs light across the visible spectrum.

- **Cones**: These photoreceptors come in three types, each containing a different photopsin
protein tuned to absorb different wavelengths of light — short (blue), medium (green), and
long (red). Cones enable color vision but require brighter light levels to function, which
is why human color perception diminishes in low light.

- **Processing**: When a rod or cone absorbs a photon of light, its photopigment undergoes a
chemical change that triggers an electrical signal. This signal is transmitted through the
optic nerve to the brain, where signals from millions of photoreceptors are processed into a
coherent visual image representing the position, shape, brightness, and color of objects in
space.

Plants detect light through a completely different system of photoreceptor proteins that are

## Key Differences

**Image formation vs. environmental monitoring**: The most fundamental difference is that
human photoreception creates images — two-dimensional representations of the external world.
Plant photoreception does not form images. Instead, plants monitor aggregate light conditions
including direction, intensity, color, and duration. A plant knows if light is coming from
the left or right, but it does not "see" objects the way humans do.

**Centralized vs. distributed**: Human photoreception is centralized in the eyes, with
information processed by the brain. Plant photoreception is distributed across multiple
tissues — leaves detect photoperiod, shoot tips detect light direction, and various tissues
contain different combinations of photoreceptors for different functions.

**Spectral range**: Both humans and plants detect light in the visible spectrum, but plants
also respond to ultraviolet and far-red wavelengths that humans cannot perceive. Plants can
detect the UV light that causes sunburn and the far-red light present at dusk, extending
their sensory range beyond human capability.
