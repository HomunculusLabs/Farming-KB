# PF Tek Brown Rice Vermiculite Cultivation Method

The "PF TEK" (Psilocybe Fanaticus Technique) is the most widely used method for amateur [[grass-seed-cultivation-of-psilocybe-mushrooms]] cubensis and related species. Developed by Robert McPherson (alias "Psilocybe Fanaticus" or "PF") in the early 1990s, it was revolutionary in its simplicity: it required no pressure cooker, no flow hood, and no specialized equipment beyond items available at any grocery or garden store. The method remains the standard entry point for home cultivation and the foundation upon which more advanced techniques are built.

## Overview and Principles

The PF TEK is based on a simple premise: create a sterile, nutritious substrate in small containers, inoculate it with mushroom spores, allow the mycelium to colonize fully, then introduce fruiting conditions. The key innovations that made this accessible were:

1. **Brown rice flour + [[pf-tek-brown-rice-vermiculite-substrate]]** — a simple, effective, and contamination-resistant medium.
2. **Half-pint canning jars** as the cultivation vessel — the tapered shape allows the colonized cake to slide out easily.
3. **Steam sterilization** instead of pressure cooking — a boiling water bath achieves sufficient sterility for the relatively clean BRF substrate.
4. **Dry vermiculite barrier layer** — a filter that allows gas exchange while preventing airborne contamination.
5. **Spore [[mushroom-spore-syringe-inoculation]]** — a simple, low-contamination method of introducing the mushroom culture.

## Materials Required

### Substrate Ingredients
- **Brown rice** — standard long-grain brown rice from any grocery store. Must be brown (unpolished), not white rice, as the bran contains [[teaming-with-nutrients-essential-plant-nutrients]] for mycelium growth.
- **Vermiculite** — coarse grade preferred. Available at garden centers. This expanded mica mineral provides water retention, aeration, and structure. Medium or coarse grade is preferred; fine grade compacts too much and restricts gas exchange.

### Equipment
- **Wide-mouth half-pint (8 oz / 250 ml) canning jars** — the wide mouth is essential; regular-mouth jars have shoulders that trap the substrate cake. Kerr and Ball brands are standard.
- **Canning jar lids** — with the rubberized sealing ring.
- **Hammer and small nail or punch** — for making inoculation holes in the lids.
- **Mixing bowl** — large enough to prepare substrate for all jars.
- **Measuring cups and spoons**
- **Large cooking pot with tight-fitting lid** — for steam sterilization. Must be tall enough to elevate jars above the water line.
- **Aluminum foil** — to cover jar lids during sterilization and incubation.
- **Spore syringe** — containing Psilocybe cubensis spores in sterile water.
- **Alcohol lamp or lighter** — for flame sterilization of the needle between inoculations.
- **Isopropyl alcohol (70%)** — for [[challenge-microorganisms-microwave-surface-sterilization]].
- **Rubbing alcohol or Lysol** — for workspace disinfection.
- **Transparent storage container or aquarium** — for the fruiting chamber (terrarium).
- **Perlite** — for humidity control in the fruiting chamber.

## Substrate Preparation

### The Basic Recipe

For each half-pint jar:
- 1/2 cup vermiculite
- 1/4 cup brown rice flour
- 1/4 cup water (distilled or spring water preferred)

This ratio produces a substrate with approximately 60% moisture content by weight, which is optimal for P. cubensis mycelium. The vermiculite provides structure and water retention while the brown rice flour provides nutrition. The relatively low nutrient density is intentional — it favors mushroom mycelium over competing contaminant organisms.

### Mixing Procedure

1. Place the measured vermiculite in the mixing bowl.
2. Add the water and mix thoroughly. The vermiculite should absorb all water — it should be damp but not dripping. Squeeze out excess if necessary.
3. Gradually add the brown rice flour while mixing. Distribute the flour evenly throughout the damp vermiculite. The mixture should be fluffy and crumbly, not muddy or compacted.
4. Fill each jar with the substrate mixture. Fill to approximately 1/2 inch (1.25 cm) below the rim. Do not pack the substrate down — it should remain loose for proper colonization.
5. Wipe the inside rim of each jar clean with a paper towel or cloth. No substrate should remain above the level where the dry vermiculite barrier will sit.

### The Dry Vermiculite Barrier

This is a critical innovation of the PF TEK:

1. Fill the top 1/2 inch of each jar with dry, clean vermiculite. This creates a contamination barrier that:
   - Allows gas exchange (oxygen in, CO2 out) through the vermiculite's porous structure.
   - Prevents airborne contaminant spores from reaching the nutritious substrate.
   - Acts as an indicator — if the dry layer becomes wet or discolored, contamination has likely breached the barrier.

### Lid Preparation

1. Using a hammer and nail (or a punch tool), make 4 holes in each canning jar lid. Space them evenly around the perimeter, about halfway between the center and the edge.
2. The holes should be approximately 1/16 inch (1.5 mm) in diameter — large enough for the syringe needle but small enough to limit contamination.
3. Place the lid (rubber side down, facing up) on the jar and screw on the metal band. Tighten firmly but do not overtighten.
4. Cover each lid with a square of aluminum foil, crimping it tightly around the jar neck. This foil protects the lid during sterilization and serves as an additional contamination barrier.

## Steam Sterilization

### Setup

1. Place a washcloth, jar rings, or a rack at the bottom of the large pot to elevate the jars above the direct heat.
2. Add water to the pot — approximately 1-2 inches deep. The water level should be below the bottom of the jars.
3. Place the jars in the pot. They should not be touching each other or the pot walls. If stacking is necessary, use a layer of foil between layers.
4. Put the lid on the pot.

### Sterilization Cycle

1. Bring the pot to a rolling boil over high heat.
2. Once boiling, reduce heat to maintain a steady steam.
3. Steam for 60-90 minutes. A full 90 minutes provides greater margin of safety, especially at higher altitudes where water boils at a lower temperature.
4. After the cycle, turn off the heat but do not remove the lid. Allow the pot to cool slowly — rapid cooling can create a vacuum that pulls contaminated air through the inoculation holes.
5. Wait until the jars are completely cool to the touch (room temperature, approximately 20-25°C / 68-77°F). This takes 4-8 hours. Inoculating while jars are still warm will kill the spores.

### Why Steam Instead of Pressure Cooking

The PF TEK substrate is relatively clean and nutrient-poor compared to grain-based substrates. Brown rice flour contains fewer readily available simple sugars than whole grains, making it less attractive to bacterial contamination. Combined with the dry vermiculite barrier, a 90-minute steam treatment achieves adequate sterility for this specific substrate. Pressure cooking (15 PSI for 60 minutes) provides additional safety margin and is recommended if available, but is not strictly necessary for the basic PF TEK.

## Inoculation

### Workspace Preparation

1. Choose a small, enclosed room (bathroom is ideal). Close windows and turn off fans and air conditioning to minimize air currents.
2. Clean all surfaces with 70% isopropyl alcohol or a disinfectant spray.
3. Some practitioners shower and wear freshly laundered clothing before inoculation.
4. Have the alcohol lamp, spore syringe, and jars arranged within easy reach.

### Inoculation Procedure

1. Remove the foil from each jar. Do not remove the lids.
2. Shake the spore syringe vigorously to distribute spores evenly. If the syringe is new, flame-sterilize the needle until it glows red, then allow it to cool for 15-30 seconds. An alcohol wipe is an alternative to flaming between jars.
3. Insert the needle through one of the 4 holes in the lid, angled toward the side of the jar. The goal is to deposit spores between the glass and the substrate, where they will slide down along the inner wall.
4. Depress the plunger to inject approximately 1/4 cc (ml) of spore solution per hole. Each jar receives a total of approximately 1 cc across 4 injection points.
5. Withdraw the needle and repeat for the remaining 3 holes.
6. Flame-sterilize the needle or wipe with alcohol between jars.
7. Replace the foil covers on the inoculated jars.

### Tips for Success

- Work quickly but carefully. Minimize the time each jar is open to the air.
- Inject at an angle so the spore solution runs down the inside glass surface — this provides maximum surface area for spore germination.
- Do not inject directly into the center of the substrate; the surface is more favorable for germination.
- A standard 10 cc spore syringe is sufficient for approximately 10 jars.

## Colonization (Incubation)

### Conditions

- **Temperature:** 75-81°F (24-27°C). P. cubensis mycelium grows fastest in this range. Higher temperatures increase contamination risk; lower temperatures slow growth significantly.
- **Light:** Not required during colonization. In fact, darkness is slightly preferred, though ambient room light is harmless.
- **Air exchange:** The dry vermiculite barrier and lid holes provide sufficient gas exchange. Do not loosen lids or remove foil during colonization.
- **Duration:** Full colonization of a half-pint jar typically takes 14-28 days. [[factors-affecting-mycoremediation-efficiency]] speed include spore viability, strain genetics, temperature, and substrate moisture.

### Monitoring

- White, cottony growth should be visible at inoculation points within 3-7 days.
- Growth spreads radially from each inoculation point.
- After 10-14 days, growth from different points should merge.
- Full colonization is indicated by the entire substrate being white and consolidated. The bottom of the jar is typically the last area to colonize.
- Wait 5-7 days after apparent full colonization before moving to fruiting. This "consolidation period" allows the mycelium to strengthen and fully digest the substrate.

### Troubleshooting Colonization

- **No growth after 7-10 days:** Spores may be non-viable, temperature too low, or substrate too wet/dry.
- **Slow growth:** Temperature may be below optimal range, or substrate may be compacted.
- **Growth stops before full colonization:** Possible contamination (bacteria competing for resources) or substrate dried out.
- **Green, black, pink, or off-white growth:** Contamination. Do not open contaminated jars — dispose of them unopened.
