# The Myth of Glaukos — Honey, Blue Staining, and Mushroom Preservation

## Overview

The myth of Glaukos, son of King Minos and the Moon Goddess Pasiphae, is one of the most suggestive yet overlooked narratives in classical mythology for understanding the possible role of psychoactive mushrooms in [[mckenna-food-gods-minoan-crete-mushroom-religion|Minoan culture]]. Preserved in the works of Apollodorus and Hygeinus, with fragmented versions in earlier writers and dramatic treatments by Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides, the story encodes what Terence McKenna identifies as "all the motifs of a barely remembered mushroom cult." The myth's prehistoric origins and its fascination for the great dramatists suggest it preserved cultural memory reaching back thousands of years before the classical period.

## The Myth

The story follows Apollodorus's version. While Glaukos, the son of Minos and Pasiphae, was still a small child, he died from falling into a pithos (a large storage jar) filled with honey while pursuing a rat or fly — the manuscripts are uncertain. Upon his disappearance, Minos made many attempts to find him and finally consulted diviners, who answered that Minos had among his herds a cow of three different colors, and that the man who could offer the best simile for this phenomenon would also know how to restore the boy to life.

The diviners gathered for this task, and Polyidos, son of Koiranos, compared the cow's colors to the fruit of the bramble. Compelled to search for the boy, he eventually found him through his powers of divination. But Minos next insisted that Polyidos must restore the boy to life. He was therefore shut up in a tomb with the dead body.

While in this great perplexity, Polyidos saw a snake approach the corpse. Fearing for his own life should any harm befall the body, he threw a stone at the serpent and killed it. Then a second snake crept forth, and when it saw its mate lying dead, it disappeared, only to return with an herb which it placed on the dead snake, immediately restoring it to life. Polyidos took the same herb and applied it to the body of Glaukos, thereby raising him from the dead.

Minos would not allow Polyidos to depart until he had taught Glaukos the art of divination. Under compulsion, Polyidos instructed the youth. But when Polyidos was about to sail away, he bade Glaukos spit into his mouth. Glaukos did so, and thereby unwittingly lost the power of divination.

## Decoding the Symbolism

McKenna performs a detailed symbolic analysis of the myth's elements:

### The Name Glaukos

The entry point into the myth's intention is the meaning of the name itself. Glaukos simply means "blue-gray." This is significant because the flesh of [[stropharia-cubensis]] and other psilocybin mushrooms has the property of staining a bluish color when bruised or broken. This blue staining is an enzymatic reaction and a fairly reliable indicator of the presence of psilocybin.

Glaukos, "the blue-gray one," preserved in a jar of honey, seems symbolic of the mushroom itself — a psilocybin mushroom preserved in honey, awaiting its time of resurrection.

### Honey and Mushroom Preservation

The antiseptic properties of honey have made it a preferred medium among many peoples for preserving delicate foods. In Mexico, honey has long been used to preserve psilocybin-containing mushrooms — a practice McKenna observed directly during his ethnobotanical work with the Mazatec. Wasson noted the frequent allusions to honey in connection with Soma in the Rig Veda, rejecting the notion that mead could have been the basis of Soma: "Honey, madhu, is mentioned frequently in the Rig Veda but mead never. Honey is cited for its sweetness and also is often applied as a metaphor of enhancement to Soma."

The fact that Glaukos fell into a honey pot — whose shape suggests the bucket-shaped graves of the Natufians — and was preserved there until his resurrection is "highly suggestive." Herodotus mentions that the Babylonians preserved their dead in honey, and the use of large storage vessels (pithoi) for burying the dead was widespread in the Bronze Age Aegean.

### The Cattle Motif

The motif of cattle appears in the bizarre section concerning the three-colored cow and the need to demonstrate linguistic facility before being able to find the lost child. This echoes the central importance of cattle in the [[mckenna-food-gods-soma-vedic-enigma|Soma cult]] and the [[mckenna-food-gods-stoned-ape-hypothesis|relationship between cattle, dung, and psilocybin mushrooms]].

### The Serpent and Herbal Knowledge

The serpent, familiar from the Genesis story of Eden, appears once again with accurate and secret information concerning plants — especially plants that confer immortality. The pattern of a serpent possessing botanical knowledge of life-giving or consciousness-altering plants is one of the most persistent motifs in world mythology, connecting the Minoan, Judeo-Christian, and Mesoamerican traditions.

### Polyidos and the Elusive Vision

Polyidos, "the man-who-has-many-ideas," functions as the shaman figure who uses knowledge gained from the serpent to restore Glaukos to life. He shares his shamanic understanding with the boy, but later "all the information leaves Glaukos and returns to his departing teacher." McKenna suggests this may refer to "the elusive nature of the visions glimpsed during mushroom intoxication" — the way that psychedelic insight, though transformative, cannot be fully retained in ordinary consciousness.

## Parallels with New World Mushroom Cults

The pattern of associating psychoactive mushrooms with small children appears throughout Mesoamerican mushroom cults. In their range across Mesoamerica, the psychoactive mushrooms are thought of as small children — "los ninos, 'the dear sweet little ones,'" as Maria Sabina, the mushroom shamaness of Huautla de Jimenez, called them.

McKenna identifies this as "an instance of the motif of the alchemical children, the elfin denizens of some nearby magical continuum, accessed through psilocybin." The parallel between the Greek Glaukos preserved in honey and the Mesoamerican "little ones" who speak through the mushroom eater suggests a deep structural similarity in how mushroom-using cultures conceptualize their relationship with the entheogenic experience.

## Connection to Minoan Opium Use

The myth of Glaukos exists within a broader context of Minoan interest in psychoactive substances. Kerenyi and other scholars have established that the late Mycenaean-Minoan civilization was "more fascinated by opium than by psychedelic plants." This suggests a historical progression: originally, visionary experiences were obtained through mushroom use (the Glaukos myth's deepest layer), later supplemented or replaced by opium as ecological conditions changed and the original mushroom knowledge faded.

## The Pithos and Bronze Age Burial Practices

The honey-filled pithos into which Glaukos fell has additional cultural resonance. Large storage jars were used throughout the Bronze Age Aegean for burial of the dead. The association between death, preservation in honey, and resurrection in the myth may reflect actual Minoan funerary practices involving honey-preserved bodies and the hope for an afterlife — practices that paralleled those of the Babylonians and other Near Eastern cultures.

## Significance for Minoan Mushroom Use

McKenna acknowledges that "we may never know with certainty the role that hallucinogenic fungi and plants had in the Minoan world," given that much can change over four thousand years. However, the myth of Glaukos, with its dense concentration of mushroom-cult motifs — death and rebirth, cattle, serpents with herbal knowledge, blue-gray coloration, and preservation in honey — represents strong circumstantial evidence that the memory of mushroom use survived into the classical period, encoded in mythological language that preserved truth even as it obscured fact.
