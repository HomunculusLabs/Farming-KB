## What Is Natto

Natto is a traditional Japanese fermented [[soybean]] food known for its distinctive sticky, stringy texture and strong aroma. It has been consumed in Japan for over a thousand years, particularly in the eastern Kanto region. Natto is made by inoculating steamed soybeans with [[bacillus-subtilis]] var. natto (formerly Bacillus natto), a soil bacterium that thrives at high temperatures and produces the characteristic viscosity and flavor through its fermentation activity.

## Bacillus Subtilis Natto

The natto bacterium is a subspecies of Bacillus subtilis, a common soil-dwelling bacterium. Key properties:

- Gram-positive, spore-forming bacterium
- Survives boiling temperatures (spores remain viable at 100C / 212F)
- Optimal growth temperature: 100-106F (38-41C)
- Produces poly-gamma-glutamic acid (gamma-PGA), which creates the sticky, stringy texture
- Produces nattokinase, an [[enzyme]] with fibrinolytic (blood-thinning) properties
- Produces vitamin K2 (menaquinone-7), important for bone and cardiovascular health
- Aerobic — requires oxygen during fermentation

## Soybean Selection and Preparation

### Bean Variety

- Small soybeans are traditional — they have a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio, promoting better fermentation
- Standard dried soybeans work fine for home preparation
- Organic, non-GMO soybeans are preferred for quality and health
- Black soybean natto (kuromame natto) is a specialty variation with a sweeter flavor

### Soaking

1. Rinse soybeans and remove any debris
2. Soak in plenty of water for 12-18 hours (overnight) at room temperature
3. Beans will roughly triple in volume
4. Small beans: 8-12 hours may suffice
5. In winter, soak longer; in summer, shorter (and change water midway)

### Cooking / Steaming

Natto requires very soft, well-cooked beans:

Pressure cooker method (recommended):
- Drain soaked beans, add fresh water to cover
- Cook at pressure for 45-60 minutes
- Beans should crush easily between your fingers; they should be almost falling apart
- Excess softness is better than undercooking — the beans need to be easily penetrated by bacteria

Boiling method:
- Boil for 3-4 hours, adding water as needed to keep beans submerged
- Check frequently — beans must be very soft

### Drying

After cooking:
1. Drain all cooking water thoroughly
2. Spread beans on a clean towel or tray
3. Surface-dry for 30-60 minutes
4. Beans should be dry to the touch but still moist internally
5. Excess surface moisture promotes unwanted bacterial growth

## Inoculation

### Natto Starter Options

- Commercial natto starter (Bacillus subtilis natto spores) — most reliable, available from Japanese suppliers and online
- Store-bought natto — use 1-2 tablespoons of fresh natto as a starter; mash and mix with a small amount of sterile water to create a slurry
- Traditional method: wrap steamed beans in rice straw (warazuri) — Bacillus subtilis naturally colonizes rice straw

### Inoculation Process

1. Ensure beans are below 115F (46C) but still warm — the bacteria need warmth to activate
2. Dissolve natto starter spores in a small amount of sterile water (follow package instructions, typically 1/2 teaspoon spores in 1 tablespoon water)
3. If using store-bought natto as starter: mash 1-2 tablespoons natto with 1-2 tablespoons sterile water
4. Pour the inoculant over the warm beans and mix thoroughly
5. Every bean should be coated — this ensures even fermentation

## Fermentation

### Temperature and Duration

- Temperature: 100-106F (38-41C), ideally 104F (40C)
- Duration: 20-24 hours
- Aeration: the beans need some oxygen — do not seal containers airtight

### Containers and Setup

1. Spread inoculated beans in a thin layer (1/2 to 1 inch deep)
2. Suitable containers:
   - Shallow glass or ceramic dishes
   - Foil trays
   - Traditional natto boxes (cedar boxes with paper wrapping)
   - Canning jar rings with plastic wrap stretched across (placed bean-side down)
3. Cover loosely with plastic wrap, cheesecloth, or paper towels
4. Place a wet paper towel or damp cloth nearby to maintain humidity (prevents beans from drying out)
5. Incubate at target temperature

### Incubation Methods

- Yogurt maker or yogurt incubator (if temperature can reach 100F+)
- Dehydrator set to 100-105F with trays removed
- Oven with the light on (check temperature — may not reach target)
- Heating pad set to low under an insulated box
- Seedling heat mat in a closed container with a thermometer

### Checking Progress

- After 12-18 hours, beans may begin to develop a white film or frosty appearance (bacterial colonies)
- After 20-24 hours, a noticeable ammonia-like aroma should be present
- The characteristic stickiness develops most strongly after the beans are cooled

## Post-Fermentation

### Aging in the Refrigerator

The stringy texture and full flavor of natto develop during a 24-48 hour refrigeration period after fermentation:

1. Remove beans from the incubator
2. Let cool to room temperature (15-30 minutes)
3. Cover and refrigerate for 24-48 hours
4. During this time, the Bacillus bacteria continue to slowly develop the sticky gamma-PGA
5. After aging, natto is ready to eat

### Storing

- Refrigerated: 5-7 days in an airtight container
- Frozen: up to 3 months; thaw in refrigerator before eating
- Texture and flavor improve slightly with 1-2 days of refrigeration after the initial ferment

## Eating Natto

Traditional serving:
- Stir vigorously with chopsticks until the beans become very sticky and stringy
- Add soy sauce, mustard (karashi), and chopped green onions
- Serve over steamed rice (natto gohan)
- Add raw egg (tamago kake gohan style) for extra richness

Other uses:
- Natto sushi rolls (natto maki)
- Mixed with okra or kimchi for extra texture
- Natto spaghetti (natto pasta)
- On toast or crackers
- Blended into smoothies (for the adventurous)
