# Westerners' Single-Minded Dedication to Zen and Natural Diet

## Overview

In *The Road Back to Nature*, Masanobu Fukuoka draws a striking contrast between Western and
Japanese [[weil-cultural-attitudes-toward-altered-states]] adopting alternative lifestyles such as Zen meditation, natural diet,
and [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]]. He observes that Westerners who commit to these practices do so with a
single-mindedness and sincerity that the Japanese have largely lost. While the Japanese
approach these traditions with intellectual curiosity and halfhearted compromise, Westerners
embrace them fully even while acknowledging their own lack of understanding.

## Descartes and the Western Philosophical Crisis

Fukuoka frames the cultural shift he observes within the broader context of Western
philosophy. He quotes Descartes' famous proposition — "I think, therefore I am" — as the
starting point for the Western worldview that has led to environmental crisis.

In this philosophical framework, nature exists only because the thinking self exists to
perceive it. Nature is an object to be used and refashioned according to human will.

This anthropocentric philosophy produced the sciences that have allowed Western civilization
to control the world and other races. But by the 1980s, Fukuoka observed that Westerners had
begun to realize something was fundamentally wrong with this approach.

They could see the fallacies of [[fukuoka-descartes-western-philosophy-nature]] but did not know which direction to turn. They had started to question the road taken by Christianity and to notice new possibilities in [[fukuoka-western-eastern-philosophy-agriculture]] and Buddhism.

## The Japanese Paradox: Catching Up on a Road to Nowhere

The Japanese, meanwhile, had taken the opposite trajectory. Impressed by Western military and
economic conquest, they had spent the postwar decades eagerly Westernizing their diet, their
clothing, their agriculture, and their thinking. They had become clever, their diet rich, and
their physical stature larger.

But Westerners saw things differently. As Fukuoka reports: "All the Japanese have done is to
travel in thirty or forty years the road that took us a century or two to build. Perhaps they
have caught up and even passed us. But we don't envy them for it because that road leads
nowhere."

The Westerners' insight was that the Japanese had merely imitated the surface of Western
civilization without understanding its deeper crisis. The Japanese had gained material wealth
but lost their simplicity, their traditional wisdom, and their connection to nature.

## Westerners at the Zen Temple

Fukuoka found particularly telling evidence for this cultural contrast in the behavior of
Westerners who came to Japan to practice Zen meditation. Many young foreigners who visited
Fukuoka's farm had first stopped by a Zen temple such as Eihei-ji or practiced zazen
(sitting meditation) elsewhere.

When asked about their experience, they were able to clearly and unequivocally say: "I don't
understand." This admission of ignorance was, in Fukuoka's view, a sign of wisdom.

The Westerners recognized the vastness of what they did not know and were not ashamed to
acknowledge it. They continued practicing not because they believed they had achieved
enlightenment but precisely because they recognized they had not.

By contrast, Japanese people who tried zazen would quickly put on a knowing air and claim to
understand Zen. They would arrive at a point of intellectual satisfaction and stop meditating.
The Westerners, unable to achieve such easy satisfaction, kept going.

As Fukuoka describes their attitude: "Nothing came of it and I don't understand it all, but
since I do see that I've been traveling the wrong road up until now, I guess that, for better
or worse, all I can do is to go on meditating."

## The Natural Diet Contrast

The same pattern appeared in dietary habits. Westerners who had concluded that the Western
diet was harmful would switch to an Eastern or natural diet and stick to it rigorously. If
they were on a [[cho-brown-rice-vinegar-preparation]] diet, they maintained it strictly. If they had decided not to eat
fish, they would not touch even a single small dried sardine.

The Japanese, by contrast, tried a natural diet only when their health deteriorated, and as
soon as their condition improved slightly, they returned to their former diet. They drifted
from right to left and back again, perpetually unable to commit fully to any path.

Fukuoka characterized the Japanese as fundamentally halfhearted, always reasoning that if
approach A did not work, they would try approach B, and when both showed advantages and
disadvantages, they would compromise on a third road that satisfied neither.

## The Power of Simplicity

Fukuoka saw in Westerners' single-mindedness a quality he called "simplicity of heart." This
simplicity allowed them to pursue Eastern practices knowing full well that what they were
doing was little more than imitation. They did not need to understand Zen intellectually to
practice it. They did not need to grasp the philosophical foundations of natural farming to try
it. They simply chose a path and followed it.

This simplicity, Fukuoka believed, was what ultimately counted. The ability to commit fully
to a course of action, even in the face of uncertainty, was more valuable than sophisticated
understanding combined with halfhearted practice.

## Body and Mind Must Agree

A key insight from Fukuoka's observation is that genuine commitment requires the alignment of
body and mind. He described visitors to his mountain farm who thought they wanted to become
farmers but had not truly made up their minds. They talked about how life in the cities was
meaningless, but they were merely trying farm life as an experiment.

The only people who were able to live for any length of time on the mountain were those who
were sick or in total despair — people for whom there was no alternative. People dedicated to
a natural diet or vegetarianism, who found the wild greens growing near the huts delicious,
also adapted well.

But those who came with their "city mind" to enjoy a bit of nature and get a nice tan, who
thought of returning home while working in the field — their bodies were present but their
minds remained in Tokyo.

## The Reversal of Cultural Exchange

Perhaps the most profound aspect of Fukuoka's observation was the reversal it implied in the
traditional direction of cultural influence. For centuries, knowledge had flowed from East to
West, from West to East, but always in the direction of modernization and material progress.

Now, Fukuoka witnessed Westerners mining the forgotten wisdom of Eastern philosophy not as an
academic exercise but as a practical guide for living. Although they might have little more
than a cursory understanding of Buddhist thought, as they pursued a vegetarian diet and
meditated, their bodies came to resemble those of Oriental people and their behavior became
like that of the Japanese of old.

They were, in Fukuoka's view, living a Buddhist life even while claiming not to understand
Buddhist philosophy.

## Implications for Natural Farming

This cultural comparison had direct implications for the spread of natural farming. Fukuoka
noted that practically no one in Japan was practicing natural farming, while abroad the
number of practitioners was increasing rapidly.

He attributed this to the Western capacity for making decisions and following through on them.
Westerners who encountered natural farming tended to commit to it fully, while Japanese
farmers were more likely to try it partially and abandon it when it proved inconvenient.
