## Inocybe aeruginascens — Urban Psilocybin Mushroom

*[[inocybe-aeruginascens]]* is a psilocybin-containing mushroom belonging to the genus *Inocybe* (family Cortinariaceae), a group better known for producing the toxic compound **muscarine** in many of its approximately 160 European species. The [[discovery-of-psilocybin-mushrooms-by-western-science]] in *I. aeruginascens* was significant because it represented the first confirmation of the alkaloid in the *Inocybe* genus and, at the time, the family Cortinariaceae — challenging the hypothesis that psilocybin was a useful chemotaxonomic marker limited to specific fungal families.

## Discovery and Taxonomy

- **1965:** First discovered by I. Ferencz in Ócsa, Hungary (county of Pest), where fruiting bodies did not match any known *Inocybe* species
- **1968:** Formally described as a new species by Babos — *Inocybe aeruginascens* Babos
- **1975:** First collected in Germany (Berlin-Köpenick and Berlin-Baumschulenweg districts)
- **1980s:** Rapidly expanded across the Brandenburg region of Germany, reaching Rostock on the Baltic coast by 1989
- **1980:** Also found in Holland (1980) and the Rhône Valley of Switzerland (1984)

The Czech mycologist Herink claimed to have discovered the species during the 1930s and deposited herbarium samples, but these were never confirmed by biochemical analysis.

## The Critical Safety Issue

*Inocybe aeruginascens* poses a **serious identification hazard** because it grows in the same habitats as the edible **[[fairy-ring]] mushroom** (*[[marasmius-oreades]]*) and belongs to a genus that includes potentially lethal species containing muscarine. Multiple accidental intoxications have resulted from misidentification:

- **1977, Hohen-Neuendorf:** 3 people
- **1980, Teltow:** 2 people
- **1980, Potsdam:** 1 person (misidentified as fairy ring mushroom; father and son both affected)
- **1984, Oranienburg:** 4 people
- **1984, Magdeburg:** 2 people
- **1985, Woltersdorf:** 3 people
- **1986, Potsdam:** 7 people (an extended family)
- **1970, Budapest:** 1 person

In all cases, symptoms subsided after several hours with no lasting side effects. Notably, despite the unfavorable psychological "set" of believing oneself to be poisoned by unknown mushrooms, all cases reportedly induced **euphoria** — a remarkable feature that distinguishes *I. aeruginascens* from most other [[ceremonial-shamanic-use-psychoactive-mushrooms]] in involuntary intoxication contexts.

## Habitat — Distinctly Urban

The most unusual feature of *I. aeruginascens* is its **strict association with human-developed areas**. Unlike all other European psilocybin-containing species, it grows almost exclusively in:

- Parks and gardens
- Tree-lined sidewalks (paved margin areas, sometimes on bare sandy soil)
- Edges of trails in residential areas
- Village and town centers
- Near the roots of deciduous trees (*Populus*, *Tilia*, *Quercus*, *Betula*)
- Suburban residential areas

It does NOT grow in forests, pastures, or natural grasslands — environments where other European [[psilocybin-mushrooms]] thrive. Its prime locations are in the middle of villages and towns, where it grows much like other *Inocybe* species but is easily mistaken for fairy ring mushrooms.

This urban habitat preference may explain its rapid expansion during the 1980s — landscaping, park maintenance, and urban tree planting all create suitable conditions.

## Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

Unlike the saprotrophic *Psilocybe* and *Panaeolus* species, *Inocybe aeruginascens* is a **mycorrhizal** mushroom — it requires a symbiotic relationship with certain tree species to fruit. Fruiting occurs at the time of greatest biochemical activity of the partner trees, in their root areas.

- **Season:** Late May through October, with peak fruiting in May and June
- **[[cold-shock-fruiting-trigger-species-specificity-psilocybe-panaeolus]]:** Extended rainfall
- **Ecology:** Grows in the root zones of various deciduous trees

## Morphology

### Macroscopic Features

The species is easily confused with other "LBMs" (Little Brown Mushrooms) and particularly with fairy ring mushrooms (*Marasmius oreades*).

**Key distinguishing characters:**

- **Greenish to bluish discoloration** — especially apparent when mushrooms are stored overnight (particularly in the refrigerator). This is the most reliable field character for differentiation from other *Inocybe* species during the May–June fruiting period.
- **Fibrehead** appearance (typical of the *Inocybe* genus)
- **Scaly cap** with fibrillose surface
- **Spores:** Angular to ellipsoid, typical of the genus

Unlike thin-fleshed *Psilocybe* species, *I. aeruginascens* frequently suffers from **fly larvae infestations** in older colonies. The resulting lesions turn greenish-blue — a feature that can be confused with the bluing reaction but is actually insect damage.

## Alkaloid Content

*Inocybe aeruginascens* contains psilocybin at levels comparable to *[[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]]*:

| Location | Year Found | Year Analyzed | Psilocybin (% dry) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potsdam | 1984 | 1984 | 0.38 |
| Potsdam | 1983 | 1984 | 0.34 |
| Potsdam | 1982 | 1984 | 0.33 |
| Berlin | 1975 | 1985 | 0.11 |
| Hungary | 1967 | 1985 | 0.22 |

|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.110 | 0.43 | 0.15 |

| 5 | 0.298 | 0.16 | 0.20 |

**Notable characteristics:**

- Psilocybin levels are fairly constant across locations and years
- Alkaloid content of stored fruiting bodies declines only slightly over time
- **Baeocystin** is present at levels comparable to psilocybin — this ratio differs markedly from *Psilocybe* species, which contain 3–5× more psilocybin than baeocystin
- **No muscarine** was detected in any specimen, despite the presence of muscarine in at least 40 other *Inocybe* species

## Aeruginascin — A Novel Alkaloid

Gartz identified a previously unknown alkaloid that he named **aeruginascin**, found only in *Inocybe aeruginascens*. Its molecular structure is similar to psilocybin and baeocystin. It is:

- Soluble only in polar solvents (water, methanol, acetic acid)
- Present at concentrations comparable to psilocybin and baeocystin
- Species-specific — its presence serves as a chromatographic "fingerprint" for identifying *I. aeruginascens*

The role of aeruginascin in the mushroom's psychoactive effects is unknown, but Gartz hypothesizes it may contribute to the consistent euphoria reported in involuntary intoxication cases, even under psychologically unfavorable conditions.

## Psychoactive Effects

### Involuntary Intoxication Reports

Symptoms from documented cases include:

- Colorful illusions and hallucinations (woven patterns appearing as crimson stripes)
- Spatial illusions with striking feelings of weightlessness
- Magnification of distances
- Depersonalization ("standing beside himself as an observer")
- Numbness (paraesthesia) spreading across the body
- Motor disturbances affecting speech
- Hot flashes, nausea without vomiting
- Strongly dilated pupils
- A "marked sense of intoxication" described as "mostly amusing"

### Controlled Self-Experiment

A mycologist consumed 2.4 g of dried mushrooms:

- **30 minutes:** Comfortable feeling of loss of gravity, no other somatic effects
- Abstract hallucinations developed slowly — bursting colors and lights
- **Complete loss of gravity** followed by vivid sensation of the soul in flight, coupled with euphoria
- Visions of strange patterns and formations through a window into a dark forest landscape — "deeply impressive" and seeming to "impart an inkling of eternity"
- **Duration:** 4 hours total, effects dissipated without dysphoria or somatic side effects
- **Notably gradual onset** compared to *P. semilanceata* (where onset included heavy flow of tears and initial anxiety)

## Comparison with Psilocybe semilanceata

In a control experiment using 0.8 g dried *P. semilanceata*:

- **Onset:** Much more sudden, including heavy flow of tears and initial anxiety
- **Imagery:** Underwater streamers, aesthetically enhanced visions
- **Contrast:** *I. aeruginascens* effects were more gradual, gentler, and euphoric

The gradual onset of *I. aeruginascens* effects may be due to slow release and resorption of alkaloids from the organic matrix of the mushroom tissue — a hypothesis requiring further investigation.
