## Desert Revegetation Through Natural Farming

Masanobu Fukuoka proposed that deserts could be revegetated by abandoning
scientific approaches and instead scattering seed-bearing clay pellets over
barren land. His method relies on nature's inherent capacity for recovery
rather than human engineering, large-scale irrigation, or chemical inputs.

## The Core Insight: Rain From the Ground

Fukuoka challenged the conventional understanding that deserts form because
insufficient rain falls from above. Standing in an American desert, he
experienced a reversal of causality: rain does not fall from the heavens;
rather, it issues forth from the ground. Vegetation creates the conditions
for its own sustenance through transpiration, moisture retention, and the
microclimates it generates.

When vegetation disappears, the cycle breaks. Bare soil absorbs and
reradiates heat, preventing condensation. Organic matter that once held
moisture like a sponge is lost to wind erosion. Building dams and
irrigation canals addresses symptoms rather than causes.

The true task is restoring the vegetation that once generated rainfall
patterns. Fukuoka argued that scientific study of why deserts spread would
take too long and produce an infinite regress of causes and effects.

## Clay Pellet Technology

Fukuoka's practical method involves coating seeds in clay to create
protective pellets. The seeds of green manure trees such as black wattle
are mixed with clover, alfalfa, bur clover, grain seeds, and vegetable
seeds. This mixture is first coated with a layer of soil, then with an
outer layer of clay.

The resulting pellets contain microorganisms from the soil layer. These
microbes are essential to Fukuoka's approach. He reported that one gram
of soil from his farm contained approximately 100 million nitrogen-fixing
bacteria and other soil-enriching microbes.

## How the Pellets Work

Once scattered over deserts and savannahs, the hard clay coating protects
seeds from predation by birds and rodents. The seeds remain dormant until
