## Plants as Heaters and Seed Germination Factors

The relationship between plants and thermal conditions extends far beyond passive responses to temperature. Certain plants actively generate heat, while all seeds have specific thermal and light requirements for successful germination. Understanding these mechanisms allows [[philodendron-selloum]]* store fats that are metabolically "burnt" to create heat, causing flowers to heat up significantly. *Philodendron selloum* may register **46 degrees C (115 degrees F)** when surrounding air is only **4 degrees C (39 degrees F)** — a temperature differential of 42 degrees C. Crocuses can heat up to **15 degrees C (27 degrees F)** above ambient air temperature.

### Skunk Cabbage (*Symplocarpus foetidus*)

[[skunk-cabbage]] is perhaps the most extraordinary thermogenic plant. It generates **20-25 degrees C (36-45 degrees F)** of extra heat above ambient conditions, and this heat serves multiple functions:

1. **Snow melting:** The heat melts a hole in spring snow, protecting blooms from cold
2. **Plant incubator:** Creates a warm microclimate that supports the rest of the plant's early growth
3. **Insect attraction:** Odorous scents are scattered by the warmth to attract pollinating flies
4. **Wind-powered pollination:** The shape of the first leaf creates a contained vortex from wind that carries pollen down to unpollinated lower flowers, achieving fertilisation in cold winds without insects

### Design Applications of Thermogenic Plants

Understorey clumps of thermogenic species can assist frost-tender, fly-pollinated, or heat-starved plants in cold climates. Tall interplant systems may similarly assist general heat requirements for ground crops. However, many of these "heater" plants produce unpleasant odours and should be used with caution in residential settings.

## Seed Germination and Temperature

The effect of soil temperature alone on germination of a wide range of vegetable seeds is profound. Between **0-38 degrees C (32-100 degrees F)**, the time to germinate (in days) can be reduced to **one-tenth or one-fourth** of that in cold soils simply by increasing soil temperatures.

### Critical Temperature Thresholds

- **Below 10 degrees C (50 degrees F):** Many cucurbits, beans, and subtropical species will not germinate
- **10-15 degrees C (50-59 degrees F):** Minimum range for most temperate vegetable seeds
- **15-20 degrees C (59-68 degrees F):** Nearly all vegetable seeds will germinate successfully
- **Above 24 degrees C (74 degrees F):** Celery and similar species refuse to germinate
- **Above 36 degrees C (97 degrees F):** Plants cannot survive transplant shock from nursery stock

### Practical Soil Warming Strategies

- **Black or clear plastic mulch** in the field before planting raises soil temperature
- **Greenhouses** provide controlled temperature environments
- **Exposing bare earth to the sun** can provide sufficient warming in mild conditions
- **Measurement:** A simple thermometer inserted **2.5 cm (1 inch)** into soil, or a dedicated soil thermometer, suffices for monitoring

The practical message is clear: do not plant until soil reaches at least 10 degrees C (50 degrees F), or actively warm the soil before planting.

## Light Requirements for Germination

Beyond temperature, light itself serves as a germination trigger for many species. The relationship between light and seed germination varies dramatically:

### Light-Requiring Seeds
- **Carrots** need a definite quantity of light and are usually surface-planted
- Surface-scattering is the standard technique, or seeds can be soaked overnight then exposed to a day under a low-wattage light bulb before planting and light covering
- **Critical detail:** Light-triggered seeds only respond if they have been wetted first

### Dark-Germinating Seeds
- Larger seeds usually accept burial and germinate successfully in darkness
- Some weed seeds and desert species can germinate from deep burial

### Flash-Triggered Germination
- A few weed species, such as wild tobacco (*Nicotiana* spp.), require only a **mere flash of light** — as brief as turning over a clod of soil — to initiate germination
- This extraordinary sensitivity explains why soil disturbance can trigger sudden weed outbreaks

## Stratification and Vernalisation

Cold-area seeds, particularly tree and berry seeds from boreal or cold regions, require exposure to prolonged cold before germination. This process is called **stratification** (or **vernalisation**) and reproduces the natural conditions seeds experience at the litter level in cold forests or marshes.

### The Cold Requirement

Seeds need exposure to approximately **0-5 degrees C (32-40 degrees F)** for the period from autumn to spring — the natural winter cycle in their native range.

### Practical Stratification Methods

- **Refrigerator storage:** Apple seeds in sand, chestnuts in peat, stored in a refrigerator and potted out as they shoot
- **Aquatic seeds:** Wild rice and other "soft" aquatic seeds stored in open ponds or under water in a refrigerator
- **Natural cold sites:** In warm climates, stratification can be achieved by keeping seeds in sand or peat (or water for aquatics) in cold shaded valleys or under open cool trellis
- Seeds should be checked in late winter and spring for signs of germination

### Heat Treatment — The Opposite of Stratification

Many tree legume seeds require the opposite treatment — exposure to heat. Methods include:
- Heating in an oven at **95 degrees C (200 degrees F)** for 10-20 minutes
- Pouring very hot (near-boiling) water over seeds
- Burning seeds in a light straw fire

These treatments break physical dormancy mechanisms (hard seed coats) that prevent water uptake and germination.

## Digestive Treatment of Seeds

Many experienced gardeners feed seeds to themselves (in sandwiches) or to animals (chickens, cattle), collect the manure, make a slurry, and sow seeds of tomatoes, berries, and tree legumes. The passage through a digestive system provides a compounded treatment:

- **Chemical:** Acid and alkali exposure
- **Thermal:** Hot and cold cycling
- **Mechanical:** Cracking in teeth or bird crops
- **Packaging:** Seeds are deposited in manure, a medium to which many species are specifically adapted

This mimics the natural seed dispersal mechanisms that co-evolved between plants and herbivores.

## Photoperiod and Flowering

Day length (more precisely, night length) varies with latitude, and flowering plants are adapted to bloom and set seed in response to specific photoperiods. This has important implications for plant selection and variety choice.

### Day-Length Categories

- **Day-neutral plants:** Flower regardless of day length if other factors (temperature, nutrition, water) are satisfactory
- **Short-day plants:** Require shorter day lengths (or longer nights) to flower — typically tropical and subtropical species
- **Long-day plants:** Require longer day lengths to flower — typically temperate species

### Practical Exploitation of Photoperiod Responses

Photoperiod sensitivity can be deliberately exploited:
- Transferring tropical (short-day) corn to a temperate (long-day) hot-summer climate produces excellent green-leaf fodder crops without seed production
- Tobacco moved from temperate to cool areas produces leaf rather than seed
- Some decorative foliage plants maintain vegetative growth under non-native day lengths

In New Guinea highlands (short days), cabbages from long-day climates may never flower, instead reaching 1-3 metres in height with leaves plucked at regular intervals for vegetable fodder.

### Latitude and Day-Length Characteristics

- **Low latitudes (0-30 degrees):** Tropical climates with colder mountain regions; approximately equal days and nights year-round
- **Mid latitudes (30-50 degrees):** Cool to temperate climates with boreal mountain regions; long summer days and short winter days
- **High latitudes (>50 degrees):** Very long summer days, very short winter days; good diffuse light radiation throughout the growing season, but no plant growth in winter

### Implications for Seed Saving

Photoperiod sensitivity is the primary reason for choosing varieties from local growers or selecting for flowering in new introductions. A **local seed source** is essential for all crops wanted in seed, as varieties adapted to different latitudes may fail to flower or produce seed when grown outside their photoperiod range.
