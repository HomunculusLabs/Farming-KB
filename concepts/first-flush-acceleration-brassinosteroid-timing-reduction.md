# First Flush Acceleration by Brassinosteroid Treatment

## Overview

First flush acceleration refers to the significant reduction in time to first fruiting observed when the brassinosteroid 22S,23S-homobrassinolide is added to fungal cultivation substrates. In the original Gartz (1990) study on *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]*, brassinosteroid-treated cultures produced their first sporocarps 1 to 2 weeks earlier than untreated controls, representing a meaningful reduction in total cultivation cycle time. This effect was demonstrated on dung/grain substrate at a concentration of 10⁻² ppm.

## Mechanism

The acceleration of first flush timing is attributed to the growth-promoting properties of brassinosteroids acting on fungal mycelium. Brassinosteroids are known to stimulate both cell elongation and cell division in plants, and evidence from the Gartz study suggests similar effects occur in fungi:

- **Cell division stimulation**: Brassinosteroids increase the rate of hyphal branching and extension, allowing the [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]] to colonize the substrate more rapidly and reach full maturity sooner. On malt agar, the study observed mycelial growth occurring "two to three times faster" than in controls.
- **Metabolic priming**: The steroid appears to shift the mycelium into a reproductive state more quickly, possibly by modulating gene expression related to fruiting body initiation. The earlier transition from vegetative to reproductive growth is the direct cause of the accelerated first flush.
- **Substrate utilization**: Faster colonization may also reflect improved enzymatic activity for substrate degradation, making nutrients available sooner for fruiting. The KH₂PO₄ supplementation in the dung/grain substrate provides additional phosphate for ATP-driven metabolic processes.
- **Stress reduction**: Brassinosteroids are known to have anti-stress effects in plants. If similar protective effects occur in fungi, the treated mycelium may experience less metabolic stress during colonization, allowing it to reach reproductive maturity more efficiently.

## Quantitative Results from Gartz (1990)

The study used dung/grain substrate inoculated with *Psilocybe cubensis* mycelium and compared brassinosteroid-treated cultures against untreated controls. Each condition was run for ten replicates to ensure statistical reliability:

| Parameter | Control | Brassinosteroid-Treated |
|-----------|---------|------------------------|
| Time to first flush | 4–5 weeks | 3–3.5 weeks |
| First flush yield (mushrooms) | 1–3 per flush | 4–7 per flush |
| Total dry mass | 2.3–2.9 g / 10 g substrate | 3.4–3.9 g / 10 g substrate |
| Incomplete fruiting bodies | Normally present | Almost completely suppressed |
| Annulus (ring) on stems | Present | Absent |

The brassinosteroid concentration used was 10⁻² ppm (0.01 ppm), applied at [[cotter-compost-substrate-preparation-manure-mushroom-cultivation]]. The very low effective concentration suggests that brassinosteroids are extremely potent in fungal systems, similar to their nanomolar-level activity in plants.

## Dual Substrate Evidence

The acceleration was observed in two different cultivation systems, strengthening the evidence for a genuine brassinosteroid effect:

1. **Malt agar (6%)**: Mycelial growth occurred 2–3× faster than controls. This demonstrated that brassinosteroids accelerate the vegetative growth phase directly, independent of substrate complexity.
2. **Dung/grain substrate**: First flush appeared 1–2 weeks earlier, with 4–7 mushrooms versus 1–3 in controls. This demonstrated that the growth acceleration translates into meaningful fruiting body production improvements.

The fact that the effect was observed on both a simple (agar) and complex (dung/grain) substrate suggests that brassinosteroids act directly on fungal physiology rather than through substrate-mediated effects.

## Implications for Cultivation

The reduction of 1 to 2 weeks in time to first harvest has practical implications for both commercial and research cultivation:

1. **Faster turnaround**: Shorter cultivation cycles mean more production cycles per year in commercial settings. Over a year, this could increase total annual output by 25–30% for a given cultivation space.
2. **Reduced contamination window**: Less time between inoculation and fruiting means less opportunity for competing organisms to establish. Contamination risk is time-dependent, so faster cycles inherently reduce losses.
3. **Resource efficiency**: Faster substrate colonization and fruiting mean energy and space are utilized more efficiently. Incubation space (often temperature-controlled) is freed sooner.
4. **Flush count advantage**: When the first flush occurs earlier, subsequent flushes are also shifted earlier, allowing the same total number of flushes in less calendar time.
5. **Predictability**: More rapid and synchronous fruiting simplifies harvest scheduling and labor allocation.

## Substrate Considerations

The first flush acceleration was observed on dung/grain substrate. On malt agar, the effect manifested as accelerated mycelial growth rather than fruiting body production, since agar plates are not typically used for fruiting. The dung/grain substrate is relevant because:

- Horse dung and rice grain provide a nutritionally rich, complex substrate that supports full fruiting body development.
- The addition of KH₂PO₄ (5 g/L) provides essential phosphate for metabolic processes including energy transfer and nucleic acid synthesis.
- The combination of a nitrogen-rich grain component with carbon-rich dung creates balanced nutrition for fungal growth and reproduction.
- The complex substrate provides a realistic cultivation environment that validates the brassinosteroid effect under practical conditions.

## Species Specificity

The first flush acceleration has only been documented in *Psilocybe cubensis* in peer-reviewed literature. Whether the same effect occurs in other cultivated species remains an open question. The study noted that *P. cubensis* was chosen for its "high stability" as a strain, suggesting that strain selection may also influence the magnitude of the response.

Brassinosteroids have been tested on higher plants across Angiospermae, Gymnospermae, and green algae with generally positive growth effects, but the fungal kingdom remains largely unexplored in this regard. About 30 brassinosteroid members have been detected across a wide variety of higher plants. The results with *P. cubensis* suggest that fungal responsiveness to brassinosteroids may be a general phenomenon worthy of investigation across commercially important species including *Pleurotus*, *Ganoderma*, and *Agaricus*.

## Dose-Response Considerations

The single concentration tested (10⁻² ppm) makes it impossible to construct a full dose-response curve. Key questions that remain include:

- Whether lower concentrations (e.g., 10⁻³ or 10⁻⁴ ppm) still produce measurable acceleration
- Whether higher concentrations (e.g., 10⁻¹ or 1 ppm) produce diminishing returns or toxicity
- Whether the optimal concentration differs between substrate types (agar vs. grain vs. compost)
- Whether the timing of application (at inoculation vs. at full colonization vs. at pinning) affects results
- Whether repeated applications across flushes provide cumulative benefits

The very low concentration used (0.01 ppm) is notable because it suggests that brassinosteroids are extremely potent in fungal systems, similar to their activity in plants where they are active at nanomolar concentrations. This potency means that treatment cost per unit of substrate would be very low if commercial application were pursued.

## Relationship to Dry Mass Enhancement

First flush acceleration and dry mass enhancement appear to be correlated effects of the same treatment. Cultures that fruited earlier also produced significantly more dry mass (3.4–3.9 g vs 2.3–2.9 g per 10 g of substrate, a 48–70% increase). This suggests that brassinosteroid treatment does not simply speed up the clock but actually improves the overall efficiency of the fungal life cycle, resulting in both faster and more productive fruiting.

The suppression of incomplete fruiting bodies further supports this efficiency hypothesis. Energy that would have been wasted on malformed, non-viable mushrooms is instead redirected into fewer, larger, healthy fruiting bodies. This dual effect — faster development and more efficient resource allocation — makes brassinosteroid treatment particularly attractive for cultivation optimization.

## Morphological Side Effects

An interesting and unexpected observation was that brassinosteroid-treated fruiting bodies "lacked the typical membranous annulus on the stems." The annulus (partial veil remnant) is a characteristic taxonomic feature of *Psilocybe cubensis*. Its absence suggests that brassinosteroids may alter developmental gene expression in ways that affect morphological differentiation, not just growth rate. This [[annulus-loss-morphological-effect-brassinosteroid-treatment]], while not directly relevant to cultivation yield, provides additional evidence that brassinosteroids interact with fundamental developmental pathways in fungi.
