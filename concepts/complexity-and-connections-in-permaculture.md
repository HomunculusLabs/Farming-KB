## Complexity and Connections in Permaculture

**Complexity** in ecological systems refers not to mere diversity of species but to the number and quality of functional connections between elements. Bill Mollison argues that the nature and value of connections — not the number of elements alone — determines whether a system is productive, stable, and sustainable.

## The Mathematics of Complexity

As Waddington (1977) observed, in systems where elements interact, complexity rises roughly as the **square of the number of elements**. "Two's company, three's a crowd... and five or six is getting to be a shambles!"

This combinatorial explosion means that even modest increases in species diversity can dramatically increase the number of possible functional connections. A chicken, for example, connects to garden waste, insect populations, soil fertility, egg production, weed control, and dozens of other system components simultaneously.

## Designing with Incomplete Knowledge

Permaculture design must proceed with incomplete knowledge. We cannot know everything about how a chicken interacts with its environment — the chicken is "busy making connections itself, about which we could not know and, of course, for which we could not design."

The permaculture approach is to:
- **Design for essentials** based on what we understand
- **Trust the chicken** (and every other living element) to attend to details we cannot anticipate
- **Check yields** at later stages to verify the system is functioning
- **Observe health and behaviour** as indicators of system integrity

This humility contrasts sharply with the industrial approach of controlling every variable, which creates fragile, energy-dependent systems.

## The Nature of Connections

In nature, connections cannot be made as easily as fixing a wire or pipe. We do not "connect" the legume to the orange tree, the chicken to the seed, or the hedgerow to the wind. Instead, we must:

1. **Understand how components function** in their own right
2. **Place them where we trust they will work** together
3. **Allow them to create additional connections** beyond our design intent

Living elements do not confine their functions to our design concepts — they always do more than we plan for.

## Benign vs. Destructive Complexity

Not all complexity is beneficial. Mollison distinguishes between:

- **Benign complexity** — cooperative organisms working together productively, where each element's outputs become another's inputs
- **Competitive or inharmonious complexity** — introductions or migrations that increase complexity but reduce stability with respect to desirable local yield

The question is always one of **matching needs with products** and assigning proper values to connections. A well-connected system where every output finds a useful input is complex in the best sense.

## Simple Associations, Complex Animal Communities

Very simple plant associations can support remarkably productive and complex animal communities. Mangrove swamps exemplify this: a single mangrove species stand may appear botanically simple, but mobile species working at different stages of leaf decomposition create a layered, productive ecology. Each decomposer species feeds others in turn.

Mobile species occupy a great variety of niches within one tree or swamp stand — from underground to canopy, across schedules from low to high tide. The simplicity of the plant community belies the richness of the animal community it supports.

## Old vs. Young Deserts

Time enables complexity to develop. Old deserts like Central Australia may exhibit some 3,000 species of woody plants, while recently desertified areas like southwest Asia may have as few as 150 plants surviving recent changes from forest.

Permaculture designers can act as **agents of constructive change**, bringing species from the world's arid lands to assist local recolonisation and pioneering natural reafforestation. The tragedy of modern agriculture is destroying invaluable species complexes to grow a single crop like wheat, "laying waste to the future."

## Island Evolution and Unique Functions

Evolving complex species assemblies in isolated sites (like the Galapagos Islands) may depend more on a **species-swarm** arising from pioneer or survivor species than on invaders adapting from borderlands. Only when many niches are empty can a species differentiate and survive without competition — hence the dodo and Darwin's finches.

Unique island species often provide functions not easily found in continental ecologies. This principle applies to permaculture: unique or uncommon species may provide functions that common species cannot.

## Order and Chaos

Order and disorder arise from **actual ground conditions**, not abstract energy theory:

- A jumble of diverse elements is disordered
- An element running wild or in destructive mode is disordered
- Too few or too many forced connections lead to disorder

**Order is found in things working beneficially together.** It is not the forced condition of neatness, tidiness, and straightness — all of which are, in energy terms, disordered. True order may lie in apparent confusion; the acid test is whether the system produces energy to or beyond consumption.

The seemingly wild and naturally functioning garden of a New Guinea villager is beautifully ordered and in harmony, while the clipped lawns and pruned roses of the suburban estate represent nature in wild disarray — maintained only by massive energy inputs.

### Principle of Order

> Order and harmony produce energy for other uses. Disorder consumes energy to no useful end. Neatness, tidiness, uniformity, and straightness signify an energy-maintained disorder in natural systems.

## Permitted vs. Forced Functions

All key living elements may supply many functions, but **forcing too many work functions on an element causes it to collapse**. One cannot reasonably expect a cow to give milk, raise a calf, forage its own food, plough, haul water, and tread a corn mill simultaneously.

Forcing functions is very different from placing an element where its **natural behaviours** permit benefits to other parts of the system. A correctly placed tree or chicken experiences no more stress than normal — and if needed elements are placed close by, it may experience less stress than normal. The chicken may be busy, but not overworked.

### Principle of Stress and Harmony

> Placing elements where their natural behaviours benefit the system is the design approach that permits components to provide many functions without forced stress.

## Design Implications

For permaculture designers, the complexity principle suggests:

- **Focus on the quality of connections**, not just the number of species
- **Design for known essentials**, trust living elements to handle the rest
- **Value benign complexity** — cooperative, mutually beneficial relationships
- **Avoid forced functions** — place elements where their natural behaviours create benefits
- **Accept apparent messiness** if the system is productive and self-maintaining
- **Introduce unique species** when they fill functions unavailable from common species
- **Think in terms of webs and cycles**, not hierarchies
