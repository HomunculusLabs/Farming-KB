# Permaculture Access and Circulation Design

Access design -- the planning of roads, pathways, tracks, and circulation patterns -- is a critical but often overlooked aspect of permaculture design. Bill Mollison treats access as a primary design element because it determines how people, animals, water, and materials move across the landscape. Poor access design creates erosion, wastes energy, and makes the site difficult to maintain.

## Principles of Access Design

### Minimum Length, Maximum Function

Mollison advocates for the shortest possible paths that serve the most functions. Every pathway should be justified by its use. Unnecessary paths compact soil, waste space, and create maintenance burdens.

### Follow the Contour

On sloped land, roads and main pathways should follow the contour wherever possible. Mollison explicitly recommends using the keyline system as the road system. Contour roads:

- Do not channel water downhill (preventing erosion)
- Provide easy access across the slope at a constant elevation
- Double as water distribution channels when designed appropriately
- Create level terraces for planting alongside

### Multiple Functions

Every path and road should serve more than one purpose:
- Access routes that also function as [[swale-construction-guide|swales]] for water harvesting
- Pathways that define garden bed shapes (keyhole beds, mandala gardens)
- Roads that double as firebreaks
- Paths that channel foot traffic away from sensitive areas

### Connect Elements Logically

Circulation should connect frequently used elements with the shortest, most direct routes:
- Kitchen to herb garden to compost to chicken coop
- House to workshop to garden to orchard
- Grazing routes from shelter to pasture to water

## Types of Access

### Primary Access (Vehicular)

- Main driveway or farm road
- Designed for regular vehicle use
- Should connect the public road to the house and major work areas
- Minimum width: 3-4 meters (10-13 feet) for two-way traffic
- Follow contour on slopes; use gentle grades (maximum 10-15% for regular use)
- Locate to allow good drainage and avoid erosion
- Consider winter access, snow removal, and maintenance requirements

### Secondary Access (Light Vehicle/Equipment)

- Farm tracks, orchard lanes, equipment paths
- Width: 2-3 meters (6-10 feet)
- May be seasonal or occasional use
- Can be grassed or mulched rather than paved
- Follow contour for water management

### Tertiary Access (Pedestrian)

- Garden paths, footpaths, walking trails
- Width: 0.6-1.2 meters (2-4 feet) for single file; 1.5 meters for two people
- Materials: gravel, wood chips, stepping stones, mulch, compacted earth
- Can be informal and changeable
- Should be comfortable to walk (not too steep, good traction)

### Specialized Access

- Animal tracks: Routes for rotational grazing, chicken tractors, duck runs
- Water channels: Routes for irrigation water, greywater flow
- Fire access: Roads and cleared paths for firefighting equipment
- Emergency egress: Secondary exits from the property

## Design Considerations

### Slope and Grade

- Maximum comfortable walking grade: 10% (1 meter rise per 10 meters distance)
- Maximum grade for light vehicles: 15%
- Steps or switchbacks needed above 15% for pedestrian paths
- Roads steeper than 20% are difficult for most vehicles
- Always provide drainage on sloped roads (crown the road surface, add drainage dips)

### Surface Materials

Choose based on use, climate, and resources:
- **Compact earth**: Simplest; good for low-traffic tertiary paths
- **Gravel**: Good drainage, durable for secondary access
- **Wood chips**: Soft, attractive, decompose over time (good for garden paths)
- **Stepping stones**: Low impact, attractive, good for informal paths
- **Concrete or pavers**: Durable, permanent, high embodied energy
- **Grass**: Lowest maintenance but can be slippery; good for occasional-use secondary paths

### Gate and Transition Points

- Place gates at zone boundaries and property entrances
- Design transitions between different pathway types (e.g., driveway to garden path)
- Include gathering spaces at junctions and destinations
- Consider wheelchair and mobility access requirements

### Integration With Water Systems

Mollison emphasizes the connection between roads and water:
- Roads on contour naturally collect and direct water
- Drainage from roads should feed into swales, ponds, or gardens (not into waterways)
- Road crossings of waterways need proper culverts or bridges
- Drainage dips on roads slow water and prevent erosion

### Integration With Zone Planning

- Zone 0 to Zone 1: Wide, paved, well-lit, direct path
- Zone 1 paths: Frequent, comfortable, close to house
- Zone 2 paths: Wider spacing, less formal
- Zone 3 paths: Vehicle access, functional rather than aesthetic
- Zone 4 paths: Minimal, for occasional access (harvest, firewood)
- Zone 5 paths: Minimal or none; access for observation only

## Common Mistakes

- Placing the house at the property edge (exposed to road noise, wind, fire)
- Running roads directly downhill (creates erosion channels)
- Making paths too wide (wastes productive space)
- Using impermeable surfaces that create runoff
- Designing access for vehicles when pedestrian access would suffice
- Failing to plan for maintenance (weeds, erosion, surface degradation)
