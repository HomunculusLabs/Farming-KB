## Ubiquity of Endophytes

Fungal surveys during the past 20 years have demonstrated that endophytic colonization of land plants by fungi is ubiquitous. Endophytes are known from plants growing in tropical, temperate, and boreal forests; from herbaceous plants in various habitats, including extreme arctic, alpine, and xeric environments; and from mesic temperate and tropical forests.

Endophytic fungi occur in mosses and hepatics, ferns and fern allies, numerous angiosperms and gymnosperms including tropical palms, broad-leaved trees, estuarine plants, diverse herbaceous annuals, and many deciduous and evergreen perennials.

## Species Richness

The species diversity of the internal mycobiota is relatively high, both within and among individual host species. A single conifer needle may harbor several dozen species. Heisteria concinna leaves in Panama supported 242 endophytic species.

Detailed investigations of the internal mycobiota of plants frequently uncover novel taxa and reveal new distributions of known species. Investigation of even well-characterized, economically important plants for endophytic fungi frequently yields novel taxa.

In tropical forests, species richness may be considerably higher than in temperate forests. Arnold and colleagues (2000) documented exceptionally high diversity in tropical hosts.

## Tissue Specificity

Endophytes show distinct tissue-specific distribution patterns. Assemblages of endophytic species in leaves differ from those in twigs of Acer macrophyllum, Alnus rubra, and Quercus petraea. Outer bark communities differ from xylem communities in Alnus, Castanea sativa, Quercus ilex, Salix fragilis, and Quercus robur.

Generally, the diversity of species colonizing inner bark is less than that of outer bark. Outer bark communities include many generalist species, while inner bark colonists tend to exhibit greater host specificity. Colonists of inner bark (phellophytes) include Tryblidiopsis pinastri and Phialocephala scopiformis.

## Spatial and Temporal Distribution

Endophyte species composition varies within individual leaves, with consistent differences between leaf blade and petiole. Bills and Polishook (1991, 1992) noted differences in species richness among sites but a core group of taxa recovered in relatively constant proportions from all sites for each host species.

Species richness increases at a constant rate with sampling intensity, eventually becoming asymptotic. Variation in species assemblages on the same host at different sites is usually attributable to recovery of incidental species with disjunct distributions.

## Effect of Tissue Age

A consistent trend confirmed for foliar and stem endophytes is that overall infection frequencies increase with the age of host organs or tissues. This is best observed from evergreen plants but is also apparent in deciduous trees and annuals.

Infection densities of Rhabdocline parkeri in Douglas fir epidermis increase at a constant rate with needle age, caused by repeated infection events rather than extended colonization from prior sites. Age of foliage strongly influences fungal colonization frequencies in the tropical palm Euterpe oleracea.

## Bark Endophyte Succession

Barklund and Kowalski (1996) noted a sequential pattern of bark endophyte species composition in Norway spruce internodes. Tryblidiopsis pinastri occurred in greater abundance in upper crowns and was dominant in younger internodes, whereas Phialocephala scopiformis, Mollisia cinerea, Tapesia livido-fusca, and Geniculosporellum serpens were more prominent in lower branches and older internodes.

Species evenness among age classes was high, indicating a well-spread dominance rather than age-related species succession.

## Grass Endophytes

Systemic clavicipitaceous grass endophytes represent a specialized category distinct from nonsystemic endophytes. Taxonomically these are primarily Neotyphodium anamorphs of Balansiae (Clavicipitaceae), colonizing leaf, culm, and root tissues of cool-season grasses and transmitted in host seeds.

These symptomless endophytes of Lolium, Festuca, and other pooid grasses are interspecific hybrid strains derived from Epichloe species that cause partial or complete host sterility (choke disease).

## Ecological Roles

Endophytic fungi have been described as mutualists protecting grasses and conifers against insect herbivory. Many produce biologically active secondary metabolites. Fisher and colleagues (1984b) reported antibacterial or antifungal activity for more than 30% of endophytic isolates from ericaceous plants.

Endophytic species of the Xylariaceae frequently produce compounds with high biological activity, including cytochalasins and indole diterpenes. Although diverse endophytes produce toxins in culture, such compounds have been difficult to detect in host tissue.

## Detection Challenges

Endophytes typically are present as internal, unseen, microscopic hyphae; their presence is revealed externally only when they sporulate, usually a seasonal and ephemeral event. Many endophytes are highly host- or tissue-specific.

Conventional methods for sampling fungi are inadequate for accurately enumerating microfungi. Detection and quantification generally require selective isolation procedures. Fungi that neither grow nor sporulate in culture must be detected by comparisons of ribosomal DNA gene sequences.
