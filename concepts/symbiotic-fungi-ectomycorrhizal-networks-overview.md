# Ectomycorrhizal Networks Overview

Source: Symbiotic Fungi (Varma and Kharkwal, eds.), Chapters 1, 5, 10

## Introduction

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) associations represent one of the most widespread and ecologically
significant types of fungal symbiosis, particularly dominant in temperate and boreal forest
ecosystems. The term mycorrhiza (fungus-root) was first applied by German forest pathologist
A.B. Frank in 1885. An estimated 95% of all plant species belong to genera that characteristically
form mycorrhizae.

In ectomycorrhizal symbiosis, fungal hyphae surround root cells without penetrating them,
forming a characteristic sheath (mantle) and an intercellular interface known as the Hartig net.
This arrangement provides a large surface area for nutrient interchange between host and fungus.

## Structural Features

In the ectomycorrhiza, the fungus does not penetrate the host cells but forms a sheath around
the roots and traverses only the intercellular spaces of the cortical layers, forming the Hartig
net. A mantle of hyphae covers the root surface, and mycelium extends from the mantle into the
soil.

Key structural characteristics:

- Most ectomycorrhizal fungi are basidiomycetes, though ascomycetes are also involved
- Host plant symbionts include both gymnosperms and angiosperms
- Families commonly forming ECM: Salicaceae (willow), Betulaceae (birch), Fagaceae (beech),
  Pinaceae (pine)
- ECM associations may make trees more resistant to cold and dry conditions
- Mycorrhizal root systems increase absorptive area 10-1,000 times compared to non-mycorrhizal roots

## Nutrient Exchange Mechanisms

The ECM symbiosis is characterized by bi-directional movement of nutrients: carbon flows from
the host plant to the fungus, while inorganic nutrients -- particularly nitrogen and phosphorus --
move from the fungus to the plant.

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are able to absorb and transfer all of the 15 major macro- and micronutrients
necessary for plant growth. They release powerful chemicals into the soil that dissolve
hard-to-capture nutrients such as phosphorous, iron, and other tightly bound soil nutrients.
This extraction process is particularly important in plant nutrition, explaining why
non-mycorrhizal plants require high levels of fertility.

## Ion Flux Measurement at ECM Roots

Non-invasive ion-selective microelectrodes enable net flux measurements at the surface of
undisturbed ectomycorrhizal roots. The technique measures ion gradients (following Fick's
law of diffusion) at the root surface.

Applications include studying:

- Effects of gene mutation or ecotype differences
- Fungal strain variation in nutrient uptake capacity
- Responses to biotic factors (phytotoxins, myc factors)
- Responses to abiotic factors (temperature, osmotic stress)
- Cation measurements: K+, H+, NH4+, Ca2+
- Anion measurements: NO3-

The method uses Nernst equation principles, where voltage differences across ion-selective
membranes correspond logarithmically to ion concentration ratios, with an ideal slope of
59 mV per decade change in activity of a monovalent ion at 25 degrees C. Microelectrodes
are made from borosilicate glass capillaries, internally silanized, filled at the tip with
an ion-selective cocktail containing the ionophore, and backfilled with saline solution.

Detection limits range from 1-10 micromolar. Measurements require a Faraday cage on an
antivibration table to reduce background noise at pico-Amp current levels.

## Types of Mycorrhizae

Current taxonomy recognizes seven types based on morphology, anatomy, and taxonomy:

1. **Ectomycorrhiza** -- hyphae surround but do not penetrate root cells; common in temperate
   trees
2. **Endomycorrhiza (arbuscular)** -- hyphae penetrate cells forming vesicles and arbuscules;
   most widespread type
3. **Ericoid** -- found in Ericaceae; fungal symbiont penetrates cortical cells; each cell
   infected individually from outer wall
4. **Arbutoid** -- features of both ECM and endomycorrhizae; intracellular penetration with
   sheath and Hartig net; dolipore septum present
5. **Monotropoid** -- associated with achlorophyllous Monotropaceae; distinctive peg-like
   intracellular penetration
6. **Ect-endomycorrhiza** -- formed with Pinaceae; Hartig net but little or no sheath; formed
   by E-strain fungi (imperfect ascomycetes)
7. **Orchidaceous** -- endomycorrhizal type with hyphal coils within cells; orchid seeds
   germinate only in presence of suitable fungus

## Ericoid Mycorrhizae

In the Ericaceae (heather family), ectomycorrhizal hyphae form a web surrounding the roots.
These ericoid mycorrhizae are endomycorrhizae in the general sense since the fungal symbiont
penetrates and establishes within cortical cells. Each cortical cell is infected from the outer
cortical wall; lateral spread from cell to cell does not occur.

Infected cells appear fully packed with fungal hyphae. The host cell dies as the association
disintegrates, restricting the functional life of these epidermal cells to the period prior
to breakdown. This contrasts with arbuscular mycorrhizae where the host cell typically
remains viable.

## Carbon Allocation in ECM Systems

Symbiotic mycorrhizal fungi play a pivotal role in biological interactions and biogeochemical
cycles. The carbon they obtain from their photosynthetic plant hosts is allocated through the
mycorrhizal mycelium to the soil ecosystem. Beyond interactions with host plant roots, the
mycelia interact with organic and inorganic substrates and different organisms including
bacteria, other fungi, soil micro- and mesofauna, and roots of secondary host plants.

Plants may divert up to 80% or more of net fixed energy to below-ground processes. This is
not energy lost to the plant; soil organisms in the root zone greatly influence plant
establishment through effects on nutrient cycling, pathogens, soil aeration, and water uptake.

## Interactions with Soil Organisms

Mycorrhizal fungi interact with a wide assortment of organisms in the rhizosphere. Specific
bacteria stimulate ECM formation in conifer nurseries and are called mycorrhization helper
bacteria (MHB). In certain cases these bacteria eliminate the need for soil fumigation.

Mycorrhizal fungi also interact with root pathogens that parasitize feeder roots. Proposed
protective mechanisms include:

- Development of a mechanical barrier (especially the ECM mantle)
- Production of antibiotic compounds that suppress pathogens
- Competition for nutrients, including siderophore production
- Induction of generalized host defense mechanisms

## [[mycorrhizal-diversity-assessment]]

Mycorrhizal diversity can be assessed through classical fungal processing (field notes,
specimen preservation, herbarium facilities), isolation from soils and sporophores, and
modern molecular methods including PCR and DNA sequencing. Molecular identification has
revolutionized fungal systematics and ecology.

Direct application of molecular methods to environmental material can detect many more,
often different, sets of fungal taxa than specimen-based methods. Public culture collections
include ATCC, CBS, BCCM-MUCL, DSMZ, INVAM, and IFO.
