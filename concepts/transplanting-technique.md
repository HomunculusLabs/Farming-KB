## Transplanting Technique

Proper transplanting technique can make the difference between seedlings that stall for weeks and seedlings that take off immediately. The key is creating a seamless connection between the seedling"s root ball and the surrounding garden soil, eliminating the [[query-how-do-i-prevent-and-manage-transplant-shock-in-garden-plants]] that sets most garden center purchases back by days or even weeks.

## The Goal: Zero Setback

The ideal transplant experiences no detectable setback — it begins growing immediately in its new location. This is achievable with proper technique, even though most gardeners accept that transplants will "wilt a bit" before recovering. That wilting period represents lost growing time that can be avoided entirely.

## Preparing the Planting Hole

### Location: Use Hills for Large Transplants

For large transplants like tomatoes, peppers, and eggplants, the best approach is to plant into a prepared hill:

1. **Prepare the hill** using standard hill-making technique — dig about 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) in diameter, incorporating compost or manure and COF
2. **Scoop a small hole** in the center of the prepared spot

### Hole Dimensions

The hole should be:
- **About twice the volume** of the seedling's root ball
- **Deep enough** that when the seedling is placed in and the hole is refilled, the soil line falls just below the seedling's first two leaves (true leaves, not cotyledons)

### Fertilizer Placement

When digging the hole, blend the COF or compost into the soil so that most of the amendment ends up **below** the seedling. This encourages roots to grow downward into the fertile zone rather than staying near the surface.

## The Muddy Slurry Technique

This is the critical step that eliminates transplant shock:

### Materials Needed

- One bucket of full-strength [[comfrey-tea-liquid-fertilizer]] (or compost/manure tea)
- One quart jar or tin can
- Loose soil for refilling

### Step-by-Step Procedure

1. **Mix a bucketful of full-strength liquid fertilizer** or use compost/manure tea for fertigation
2. **Scoop out a quart (liter)** of the fertigation liquid
3. **Pour it into the planting hole** — gently enough not to wash soil away from the sides, but rapidly enough to fill the hole before it soaks in
4. **Immediately slide the seedling's root ball into the hole** — minimizing handling damage to roots and avoiding breaking off soil from the root ball
5. **Quickly push loose soil back in around the root ball** — creating a muddy slurry
6. **Settle the slurry** into all the nooks and crannies of the root ball

### Why This Works

The muddy slurry creates a tight connection between the seedling's roots and the surrounding soil. There are no air gaps, no dry zones, and no abrupt transitions between [[solomon-potting-mix-and-seedling-soil-recipe]] garden soil. The roots can immediately begin drawing moisture and nutrients from the surrounding soil.

### Results

If done properly:
- The seedling will **not need watering any more often** than anything else in the garden
- It will grow **really fast** — often visibly from day one
- There is no wilting period, no recovery time, no lost growing days

## Hardening Off Before Transplanting

### The Hardening-Off Process

Seedlings raised indoors need gradual exposure to outdoor conditions:

1. **Begin** when seedlings have one true leaf
2. **Take them outside** during the day when weather is pleasant
3. **Gradually increase exposure** over 1-2 weeks
4. **Start with sheltered, partly shaded locations** and move to full sun
5. **Bring them in at night** initially, then leave out on mild nights
6. **Extend outdoor time progressively** until they are out full-time

### Signs of Proper Hardening

- Thicker stems
- Darker green leaves
- [[fukuoka-ideal-rice-plant-morphology-compact-growth-high-yield]] (not leggy)
- Slight reduction in leaf size compared to greenhouse-grown plants

### What Happens Without Hardening Off

Unhardened seedlings face cascading shocks when planted directly from indoor conditions:

- **Temperature shock**: Even mild outdoor temperatures stress plants accustomed to constant warmth
- **Wind shock**: Leaves and stems that never experienced wind get bruised or torn
- **Sun shock**: Full sunlight after filtered greenhouse light causes rapid wilting as the root system cannot keep up with transpiration
- **Combined effect**: The seedling may take 1-2 weeks to recover, completely negating the head start that transplanting was supposed to provide

## When to Transplant

### Timing by Crop

- **Tomatoes**: About 6 weeks after sowing, when nighttime temperatures stay above 50°F (10°C)
- **Peppers and eggplants**: After tomatoes — they need even warmer conditions
- **Cucurbits (zucchini, squash, melons)**: Only 3-4 weeks after sowing; they grow fast and should not be held too long
- **Cool-season crops** (if using transplants): As soon as the ground can be worked

### Timing by Weather

- **[[soil-temperature]]**: Most warm-season transplants need soil above 60°F (15°C) to grow
- **Air temperature**: No benefit to putting warm-season crops out before the last frost date unless using protection
- **Overcast days**: Ideal for transplanting — reduced sun stress
- **Cool, calm evenings**: Best time of day to set out transplants

## Direct Seeding vs. Transplanting: A Cost-Benefit Analysis

### When Transplanting Is Worth It

- **Tomatoes, peppers, eggplants**: In almost all climates, the extended harvest season justifies the effort
- **Melons and winter squash**: In short-season areas only
- **Extending the season**: Getting the first ripe tomato or pepper before summer ends is a worthy ambition

### When Direct Seeding Is Better

- **Root vegetables** (carrots, beets, radishes, turnips, parsnips): Transplanting damages the taproot
- **Corn**: Root system is badly shocked by transplanting
- **Peas and beans**: Germinate quickly from seed and resent root disturbance
- **Lettuce and most greens**: Direct seeding produces better results with less effort
- **[[cabbage-family-pests]]**: Direct seeding with [[coleman-succession-planting]] provides continuous harvest without the all-at-once problem of purchased transplants

### The Succession Planting Alternative

Instead of buying six cabbage seedlings that all mature simultaneously, consider direct-seeding four plants every two weeks. This produces a continuous supply of two cabbages per week throughout the season — far more useful than a one-time glut.
