## Turkey Tail (Trametes versicolor) PSK and PSP Clinical Trials

## Overview

Trametes versicolor, commonly known as turkey tail, is a bracket fungus found worldwide on dead logs. While not edible due to its tough texture, hot water extracts have been used in traditional Chinese medicine for centuries. Modern research has produced two critically important compounds from this mushroom: **PSK (polysaccharide-K, "Krestin")** and **PSP (polysaccharide-peptide)**, both derived from deep-tank fermentation of mycelial cultures.

PSK was first isolated in Japan in the late 1960s; PSP was isolated in China around 1983. Both are protein-bound polysaccharides with remarkable anticancer properties and minimal side-effects. By 1987, PSK ("Krestin") accounted for more than 25% of total national expenditure for anti-cancer agents in Japan.

## Chemical Characteristics

PSK and PSP are closely related polysaccharopeptides but differ in composition:
- **PSK** contains fucose in its structure
- **PSP** lacks fucose but contains arabinose and rhamnose instead
- Both have molecular weights of approximately 100 kDa
- Both are water-soluble protein-bound polysaccharides
- Key active constituents include beta-glucan-proteins with antitumour, antiviral, and immunomodulating properties

## Mechanisms of Action

PSK acts through both direct and indirect pathways:
- **Direct effects**: Cytostatic and cytotoxic activity against tumour cells
- **Indirect effects**: Boosts cellular immunity through T-cell activation
- **Antiviral activity**: Stimulates interferon production
- PSK and PSP are potent immunostimulators with specific activity for T-cells and natural killer cells

## PSK Clinical Trials

### Gastric Cancer

PSK has been used as immunotherapy for more gastric cancer patients than any other cancer type. Key trials include:

- **Kaibara et al. (1976)**: Stage IV patients received PSK at 3g/day post-surgery combined with Mitomycin C and 5-FU chemotherapy (n=66). Two-year survival rate was more than double compared to historical controls.
- **Fujimoto et al. (1979)**: Larger prospective study (n=230) confirmed improved survival with PSK adjunct therapy.
- **Hattori et al. (1979)** (n=110) and **Kodama et al. (1982)** (n=450): Demonstrated PSK protection against immunosuppression normally associated with surgery and long-term chemotherapy.
- **Kondo and Torisu (1985)**: One of the few double-blind randomised controlled trials (n=144) found significant increases in disease-free and overall survival, plus enhanced immune function measured by delayed-type hypersensitivity skin tests and neutrophil chemotaxis.
- **Nakazato et al. (1994)**: Phase III two-arm trial by the Study Group of Immunochemotherapy with PSK for Gastric Cancer of Japan showed combining PSK with conventional chemotherapy significantly improved disease-free and overall survival.

Dendritic cell infiltration of gastric tumours prior to surgery appears to predict PSK response -- patients with limited dendritic cell infiltration benefited more from PSK immunotherapy.

### Other Cancers

- **Oesophageal cancer**: Non-controlled retrospective analysis (n=133) showed improvements in one-year and two-year survival with combined radiation, chemotherapy, and PSK immunotherapy. PSK improved overall survival in patients with elevated pre-operative alpha1-anti-chymotrypsin or sialic acid levels.
- **Nasopharyngeal carcinoma**: Small-scale trial in Taiwan showed small but significant impact on five-year survival with PSK adjunct therapy.
- **[[medicinal-mushrooms-biological-response-modifiers]] modifier, improving immunological status after surgery, radiotherapy, and/or chemotherapy.

In a double-blind Phase II trial with nearly 300 gastric, oesophageal, and lung cancer patients, PSP was compared against shark liver oil (batyl alcohol) as adjunct to conventional therapy. PSP was effective for 82% of patients versus 48% for the control group, with improvements in clinical symptoms, blood profiles, immune indices, and Karnovsky performance status.

### Phase III

A Phase III trial (n=650) in Shanghai hospitals with 189 randomised to PSP versus placebo and 461 unblinded showed PSP improved disease-free survival of gastric, oesophageal, and non-small-cell lung cancers while reducing side-effects of conventional treatments. PSP demonstrated a protective effect on immunological functions, qualifying it as a clinical biological response modifier.

PSP combined with IL-2 showed dramatic anti-tumour effects (Mao et al., 1998), suggesting lower IL-2 doses could be used clinically with reduced side-effects. PSP with radiotherapy induced significant increases in apoptotic cells at 24 hours compared to radiation alone, indicating its antitumour mechanism may involve DNA damage and apoptosis induction.

## Safety Profile

Both PSK and PSP have remarkably few side-effects. Common adverse reactions of radiotherapy and chemotherapy (haematopoietic toxicity) are substantially ameliorated by PSP. In Phase I human clinical tests, these beta-glucans demonstrate remarkably few side-effects, and no maximum tolerated dose was reached.
