## Herb Spiral Microclimate Design

The herb spiral is one of the most iconic and immediately practical designs in permaculture. It combines a two-dimensional pattern (a spiral) with a three-dimensional one (a mound of soil) to form a beautiful, space-saving living sculpture that generates multiple microclimates from a single garden feature.

## What It Is

An herb spiral begins as a mound of good soil about 3 feet high and 5 feet across. To turn this mound into a spiral, fist- to head-sized rocks are placed in a spiral pattern that winds from the bottom inward to the top, leaving about a foot of soil between the tiers of the rock spiral. The design coils roughly 20 to 30 linear feet of row into a compact roundish pattern about 5 feet across.

## The Problem It Solves

Imagine planting an [[cotter-growing-space-design-fruiting-rooms]]. A rectangular [[query-best-mediterranean-herbs-for-beginner-herb-garden]] like oregano, rosemary, and thyme
- **North-facing slope**: Cooler and moister -- suited to parsley, chives, and cilantro
- **East-facing side**: Gets morning sun, dries earlier in the day -- good for coriander and plants that bolt in afternoon heat
- **West-facing side**: Gets afternoon sun -- suited to heat-loving varieties
- **Top of the spiral**: Driest and warmest -- for drought-tolerant herbs
- **Bottom of the spiral**: Wettest and coolest -- for moisture-loving plants

The south-facing slope will be hotter than the north. The east-facing side, which gets morning sun, will dry out earlier in the day than the west one. The soil at the bottom will stay wetter than that at the top. Each herb can be located in its preferred environment, all accessible from a single standing position.

## Space Efficiency

Beyond microclimates, the herb spiral is a masterclass in space efficiency. It coils what would be 20 to 30 linear feet of pathside plants into a 5-foot-diameter circle. All herbs can grow right outside the door, using only the path space necessary to walk around the spiral. Mounding up the soil means central herbs can be reached without bending over very far.

## Construction Tips

- **Save on topsoil**: Place a few rocks or a heap of subsoil at the base of the mound, then build over that
- **Irrigation**: Run plastic tubing (1/4 or 1/2 inch) inside the mound, emerging from the top, and attach a mini-sprinkler
- **Water feature**: Consider sinking a small basin or tiny pond (1 to 3 feet across) at the bottom of the spiral for watercress, waterchestnuts, or other edible pond plants
- **Aesthetic appeal**: Built with attractive stone, an herb spiral can serve as an eye-catching central feature of any garden
- **Stone selection**: Fist- to head-sized rocks work well; larger stones at the base provide structural stability

## Suggested Plant Placement

Numbered from bottom to top, a typical spiral might include:

- **1 (bottom)**: Feverfew, watercress (if pond present), nasturtiums
- **2-3**: Calendula, coriander (east-facing, protected from afternoon heat)
- **4-5**: Parsley, chives (north-facing, cooler and moister)
- **6-7**: Fennel, yarrow
- **8-10**: Sage, echinacea, chamomile (west-facing)
- **11-14 (top/sunny south)**: Thyme, oregano, dill, rosemary (hottest, driest)

**Top / sunny south side**: Rosemary, thyme, oregano, lavender
**East side**: Coriander, chamomile
**North side / cooler**: Parsley, chives, mint (contained)
**West side**: Sage, echinacea
**Bottom**: Watercress, nasturtiums
