# Mineral Transformations and Biogeochemical Cycles by Fungi

## Overview

Chapter 5 of *Fungi in the Environment*, by Gadd, Burford, Fomina, and Melville, presents a geomycological perspective on how fungi transform minerals and drive biogeochemical cycles. Fungi are not merely decomposers of organic matter — they are active geological agents that weather rocks, solubilize and precipitate minerals, and mediate the cycling of essential elements including carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, iron, and manganese.

## Geomycology Defined

### What Is Geomycology?

Geomycology is the study of fungal interactions with minerals and geological substrates. It encompasses:

- **Bioweathering**: The breakdown of rocks and minerals by fungal activity
- **Biomineralization**: The formation of minerals by fungal metabolic processes
- **Bioaccumulation**: The concentration of metals and minerals within fungal biomass
- **Biotransformation**: The chemical alteration of mineral species by fungi

Fungi are uniquely suited to geomycological roles because their filamentous growth habit allows them to penetrate rock crevices, their exudates include powerful chelating agents and organic acids, and their biomass provides vast surface area for mineral interactions.

### Fungi as Dominant Soil Biomass

In many soils, fungi comprise the largest pool of living biomass, exceeding bacteria, other microorganisms, and invertebrates. Their filamentous growth habit and exopolymer production contribute to soil structure maintenance. This biomass dominance means fungal metabolic activities have outsized effects on mineral cycling relative to their proportion of total soil mass.

## Mechanisms of Mineral Transformation

### Organic Acid Production and Chelation

Fungi excrete low-molecular-weight organic acids that are primary agents of mineral dissolution:

- **Oxalic acid**: Forms soluble complexes with calcium, iron, and aluminum; [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]] crystals can deposit extensively on hyphae and rhizomorphs
- **Citric acid**: Effective at solubilizing phosphate from insoluble mineral forms
- **Gluconic acid**: Important in phosphate solubilization by some fungal species
- **Other acids**: Malic, fumaric, succinic, and tartaric acids contribute to mineral weathering

The chelating action of these acids strips metal cations from mineral lattices, destabilizing crystal structures and releasing bound nutrients. This is particularly important for phosphorus, which in many soils is locked in insoluble mineral forms.

### Extracellular Enzymatic Action

Fungi produce [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]] that attack mineral-organic complexes:

- **Phosphatases**: Release phosphate from organic esters and mineral-bound organic phosphorus
- **Oxidoreductases**: Transform manganese and iron between oxidation states, affecting their solubility and bioavailability
- **Laccases**: Oxidize [[ingham-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds]] and contribute to lignin and humic substance degradation

### Redox Transformations

Fungi can change the oxidation state of metals, dramatically altering their solubility:

- **Iron reduction**: Reduces Fe(III) to more soluble Fe(II), increasing iron availability
- **Manganese oxidation**: Oxidizes Mn(II) to Mn(III/IV) oxides, which can precipitate and coat fungal structures — these redox transformations affect both metal availability and organic pollutant degradation

### Biomineralization

Fungi actively precipitate minerals through metabolic processes:

- **Calcium oxalate**: The most common fungal biomineral; functions in calcium regulation, metal detoxification, and possibly [[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]]
- **Metal carbonates and phosphates**: Precipitated through fungal [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]], often as detoxification mechanisms
- **Iron and manganese oxides**: Formed through fungal redox activity, contributing to soil mineralogy

## Elemental Cycles Mediated by Fungi

### Carbon Cycle

Fungi are central to the terrestrial carbon cycle:

- **Decomposition**: The primary agents of lignocellulose decomposition, especially white-rot and brown-rot Basidiomycota
- **Carbon sequestration**: Mycorrhizal fungi contribute significantly to stable soil carbon pools through recalcitrant compounds like glomalin and melanin
- **Soil respiration**: Fungal respiration accounts for a substantial fraction of total soil CO₂ efflux
- **Priming effects**: Fungal colonization of organic matter can accelerate or decelerate decomposition of existing soil organic matter

### Nitrogen Cycle

Fungal contributions to nitrogen cycling include:

- **Mineralization**: Converting organic nitrogen to ammonium through protease and deaminase activity
- **Immobilization**: Incorporating inorganic nitrogen into fungal biomass
- **Nitrification and denitrification**: Some fungi contribute to these processes, though bacteria dominate
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Limited evidence for fungal N₂ fixation, though some endophytic fungi may fix nitrogen

### Phosphorus Cycle

Fungi are particularly important in phosphorus cycling:

- **Mineral phosphate solubilization**: Organic acids dissolve insoluble phosphate minerals (apatite, variscite)
- **Organic phosphorus mineralization**: Phosphatases release phosphate from organic compounds
- **Mycorrhizal phosphorus transport**: Arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal fungi transport phosphate to host plants in exchange for carbon
- **Phosphorus storage**: Fungi accumulate polyphosphate granules as a phosphorus reserve

### Metal Cycles

Fungi transform and mobilize metals through:

- **Solubilization**: Organic acid and siderophore production release metals from minerals
- **Accumulation**: Biosorption and bioaccumulation concentrate metals in fungal biomass
- **Precipitation**: Metal immobilization through mineral formation
- **Volatilization**: Some fungi can methylate toxic metals (mercury, tin, arsenic), converting them to volatile forms

## Fungal Rock Weathering

### Lichen-Mediated Weathering

Lichens (symbiotic associations of fungi with photobionts) are among the most effective biological weathering agents:

- Produce oxalic acid and other acids at the rock-lichen interface
- Physical penetration of hyphae into rock crevices exerts mechanical force
- Create distinct weathering zones (lichen zone, transition zone, unweathered rock)
- Contribute significantly to early soil formation on bare rock surfaces

### Mycorrhizal Weathering

Ectomycorrhizal fungi directly weather minerals in the rhizosphere:

- Hyphae penetrate mineral grains and excrete organic acids at the hypha-mineral interface
- Release calcium, magnesium, potassium, and phosphorus from silicate minerals
- This "ectomycorrhizal weathering" is a major source of plant-available nutrients in forest ecosystems

## Environmental Applications

### Bioremediation

Fungal mineral transformations have practical applications:

- **Metal-contaminated sites**: Fungi can immobilize toxic metals through precipitation and biosorption
- **Radionuclide containment**: Fungal biomass can accumulate uranium, cesium, and strontium
- **Acid mine drainage**: Fungal carbonate precipitation can neutralize acidity and immobilize metals

### Soil Formation and Fertility

Understanding fungal mineral transformations informs:

- **Soil development**: Fungi accelerate primary succession on bare rock
- **Nutrient availability**: Fungal solubilization makes essential nutrients plant-available
- **Soil structure**: Fungal exopolymers and hyphae stabilize soil aggregates
