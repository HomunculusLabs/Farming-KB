# Permaculture Community-Scale Design

Community-scale permaculture design applies the same principles used for individual properties to villages, neighborhoods, and bioregional systems. Both Holmgren and Mollison argue that permaculture's relevance extends far beyond backyard gardens to encompass the full range of human settlement patterns and social organization.

## From Household to Community

Holmgren identifies a natural progression in permaculture practice from household self-reliance to community self-reliance. The household garden is the starting point -- a manageable scale where individuals can learn [[permaculture-design]] through direct experience. As skills and confidence grow, the focus expands to neighborhood and community systems.

Community-scale design requires attention to patterns of social organization as much as physical design. Holmgren emphasizes that intentional communities need diversity in age, interests, and livelihoods in order to provide a basis for economic exchange, allow for the emergence of natural authority on different issues, and foster recognition that commonality and difference exist in dynamic tension.

Mollison stresses that site designing needs "a multi-disciplinary and bio-social approach that takes into account the effects the environment has on its intended occupants." The permaculture approach contrasts sharply with conventional design: permaculture is stimulated by perceived social problems and governed by values of energy conservation and self-reliance, whereas conventional design is driven by profit motives with little consultation. Permaculture design leads to dynamic, healthy areas with empowered residents; conventional design often leads to dislocated populations and systemic powerlessness.

## Bioregional Organization

Mollison and Holmgren both advocate for bioregional organization -- human communities organized around natural watershed and ecosystem boundaries rather than political ones. Mollison defines a bioregional association as "an association of the residents of a natural and identifiable region," defined by watershed, tribal or language boundaries, town boundaries, or some combination of these factors. The acid test: "it is recognised as such by its inhabitants."

Bioregional thinking connects [[permaculture-design]] to the larger landscape: catchment management, regional food systems, local energy generation, and watershed-scale [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]]. Bioregional sectors that apply at the property level also operate at the regional level, including regional climate patterns, seasonal energy flows, and relationships between upland catchments and lowland water systems.

### Scale and Structure

Ideally, a bioregion encompasses 7,000 to 40,000 people. Of these, perhaps only a hundred will be initially interested in any regional association. The work of the bioregional group is to "assess the natural, technical, service, and financial resources of the region, and to identify areas where leakage of resources (water, soil, money, talent) leave the region." This assessment quickly points to local self-reliance strategies.

Mollison envisions regional associations formed around specific domains: consumer-producer and soil societies for [[permaculture-soil-strategies]], owner-builder associations for shelter, appropriate technology groups for energy, and "earthbank" associations for finance. A regional office with 4-6 consultants serves as a land access centre, leasehold register, self-funding scheme hub, and trade coordination centre between regions.

Tribal maps often defined bioregions precisely: totemic clans claimed territories bounded by the natural ranges of particular plants and animals, limited by topographic and climatic factors. Mollison expands the familiar maxim beyond "think globally and act locally" to "act and think globally and locally." Bioregions need both periodic federal congresses and occasional global congresses, with intermarriage, mutual trade, skills exchange, and educational exchange enriching all participating regions.

### Resource Index Categories

Mollison compiled a comprehensive resource index for bioregions covering: food and food support systems; shelter and buildings; livelihoods and support services; information, media, and communication; community and security; social life; health services; future trends; and transport services. Each category includes practical resources essential to the functioning of a small region -- people, skills, machinery, biological products -- that assist resource conservation, regional cash flow, employment, and community security.

## Land Access and Common Resources

A critical challenge for community-scale permaculture is access to land. Mollison describes multiple strategies:

- **Allotment systems** (Britain): Public posting of people who want land and people who have land, facilitating matches without cash rental
- **Farm-link programs** (Australia): Connecting urban households with rural producers for guaranteed crop purchase and labor exchange
- **WWOOF**: Working weekends on organic farms, where visitors pay to learn and work
- **Community land trusts**: Shared ownership models that separate land ownership from building ownership
- **Neglected urban land**: Working with local councils to convert abandoned lots to productive gardens

## Cooperative Economics

Holmgren argues that local economic self-reliance is a core permaculture strategy. Local Exchange Trading Systems (LETS), cooperatives, barter networks, and direct producer-consumer relationships all reduce dependence on the fossil-fuel economy. The key principle is that money spent locally circulates many more times than money spent in the global economy, creating more local economic activity per unit of expenditure. [[permaculture-currency-and-economics]] explores these alternative financial systems in depth.

## The Village Scale

Both authors point to the village as an optimal scale for human settlement. Villages allow for local food production, walking-scale transport, face-to-face governance, and strong community bonds. Holmgren notes that hamlet and village density patterns (low to medium) are more sustainable than either urban high-rise or remote rural isolation. The [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]] scenario implies a gradual re-concentration of population into smaller, more densely networked settlements. [[permaculture-village-design]] and [[permaculture-settlement-design]] explore these patterns further.

## Intentional Communities

Holmgren draws on extensive experience with intentional communities to identify key success factors: shared values balanced with personal autonomy, economic interdependence, clear governance structures, and sufficient diversity to prevent groupthink. He warns that communities formed purely around ideology tend to be fragile, while those formed around practical shared activities (food production, building, child-rearing) tend to be more resilient.

## Community Design Methodology

The process of designing a community-scale permaculture system follows a structured methodology that parallels individual site design but operates at a larger geographic and social scale. Mollison outlines a series of steps that begin with broad regional assessment and progressively narrow to specific site plans:

1. **Regional mapping**: Identify climate zones, watershed boundaries, geology, existing vegetation, and settlement patterns across the bioregion. This mapping exercise, described in [[permaculture-bioregional-organization]], reveals the natural structure that community design should follow rather than override.

2. **Resource and needs analysis**: Catalog what the region produces (food, timber, water, skills) and what it consumes. The gap between production and consumption reveals import dependencies and opportunities for local self-reliance. Mollison's resource index categories provide the framework for this assessment.

3. **Sector analysis at community scale**: Just as individual properties have sector analyses for sun, wind, fire, and wildlife, communities need sector analyses for regional climate patterns, flood zones, wildfire risk corridors, and major transportation routes. These large-scale sectors determine where different types of development should and should not occur.

4. **Infrastructure planning**: Design water supply, energy systems, waste cycling, and transportation networks to serve the community as an integrated whole. This is where permaculture community design diverges most sharply from conventional planning: rather than treating each infrastructure system independently, the permaculture approach seeks to connect outputs of one system to inputs of another. Wastewater becomes irrigation; organic waste becomes compost; building placement optimizes passive solar gain.

## Social Structures and Governance

Holmgren's experience with intentional communities led him to emphasize that social design is at least as important as physical design at the community scale. Key principles include:

**Consensus and consent-based governance**: While consensus decision-making can be slow, Holmgren argues that it produces decisions that everyone is committed to implementing, reducing the friction that plagues majority-rule systems. For larger communities, sociocracy (dynamic governance) offers a structured alternative that combines consent-based decision-making with delegated authority circles. The [[holmgren-community-design-and-intentional-communities]] page explores these governance models in detail.

**Conflict resolution mechanisms**: Every community experiences conflict. Permaculture communities benefit from establishing conflict resolution processes before conflicts arise — mediation protocols, clear communication guidelines, and agreed-upon consequences for boundary violations. Communities that lack these structures tend to either fragment or develop informal power hierarchies that undermine their egalitarian ideals.

**Economic diversity**: Holmgren warns against communities that depend on a single economic activity. Diversity in livelihoods — some members farming, others teaching, others doing trades or remote work — provides resilience against economic shocks and reduces interpersonal competition for limited roles. The [[community-economics-in-permaculture]] framework outlines strategies for building diverse local economies.

## Education and Knowledge Transfer

Community-scale permaculture depends on effective knowledge transfer. Mollison's Permaculture Design Certificate (PDC) course was originally conceived as a tool for community empowerment — a 72-hour intensive that gives participants enough design literacy to begin transforming their own properties and communities. The global spread of PDC courses, now taught in over 100 countries, represents one of the most successful examples of decentralized environmental education. Beyond formal courses, community-scale knowledge transfer occurs through demonstration sites, skill-sharing workshops, mentorship programs, and the kind of informal learning that happens when people work side by side on shared projects.

The [[faires-permaculture-community-and-village-design]] approach emphasizes that the most effective community designs are those that community members co-create through participatory processes, rather than designs imposed by outside experts. When people participate in the design of their own communities, they develop ownership and commitment that top-down planning cannot achieve. This participatory approach also ensures that local knowledge — about microclimates, soil conditions, community dynamics, and cultural preferences — is incorporated into the final design.

## Case Studies in Community Permaculture

Several established communities demonstrate permaculture principles at the community scale. Crystal Waters Ecovillage in Queensland, Australia (designed by Max Lindegger and Robert Tap in the 1980s following Mollison's principles) houses over 200 residents on 640 acres, with 80% of the land designated as common conservation area. Individual residential lots average 1-2 acres, with food production integrated into residential landscaping. The village demonstrates that community-scale permaculture can achieve both ecological restoration and comfortable human habitation simultaneously.

The Findhorn Foundation community in Scotland, while not originally designed as a permaculture project, has evolved many permaculture-compatible features over its 60-year history, including ecological building, local food production, wind energy generation, and a community currency system. Its example shows that permaculture principles can be applied incrementally to existing communities as well as to new developments.
