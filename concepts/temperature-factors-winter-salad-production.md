# Temperature Factors for Winter Salad Production

Successful winter salad production in protected cultivation depends on understanding how temperature dynamics affect leafy crops. [[eliot-coleman]] identifies specific temperature thresholds and four critical temperature factors that determine crop quality, survival, and harvestability during the coldest months.

## Critical Temperature Thresholds

The following temperature benchmarks define the operating range for winter salad crops in unheated or minimally heated greenhouse environments:

| Temperature | Significance |
|---|---|
| **115°F (46°C)** | Upper lethal limit — plants die |
| **95°F (35°C)** | Maximum photosynthesis rate — too hot if sustained |
| **75°F (24°C)** | Optimum photosynthesis and plant growth |
| **55°F (13°C)** | Average daytime greenhouse temperature during the coldest winter months in unheated structures |
| **35°F (2°C)** | Ideal minimum greenhouse air temperature — no freezing of crops |
| **15°F (-9°C)** | Lower limit for quality harvest of most winter salad crops |
| **0°F (-18°C)** | Extreme cold threshold — crops require heavy protection |

These thresholds explain why unheated greenhouses work for winter production: even when outside temperatures drop well below freezing, the greenhouse effect combined with row cover layers maintains temperatures above the critical 35°F minimum inside the growing zone.

## The Four Temperature Factors

Coleman emphasizes that it is not simply the absolute minimum temperature that determines crop success, but rather the interaction of four distinct factors:

### 1. Rate of Temperature Drop
How quickly the temperature falls is critical. Gradual cooling allows plants to undergo physiological changes that increase cold tolerance — a process known as cold acclimation or hardening off. Rapid temperature drops (such as those from a sudden cold front with high winds) can cause cellular damage before the plant's protective mechanisms can activate.

**Practical implications:**
- Successive layers of protection (greenhouse → inner layer → row cover) create a stepped temperature gradient that slows the rate of cooling
- Closing structures before expected cold events gives the system time to stabilize
- Windbreaks around greenhouses reduce the rate of heat loss

### 2. Depth of Temperature Drop
The magnitude of temperature change from daytime high to nighttime low within the protected growing environment determines how much stress the crop experiences. A greenhouse that drops from 55°F to 35°F presents far less stress than one dropping from 55°F to 15°F.

**Practical implications:**
- Thermal mass (water barrels, soil mass) helps moderate temperature swings
- Multiple protection layers reduce the depth of temperature drop at the crop level
- Monitoring minimum temperatures at crop height (not just at the greenhouse thermostat) is essential

### 3. Duration of Temperature Drop
How long temperatures remain below critical thresholds matters as much as the minimum reached. A brief dip to 20°F may cause no damage, while sustained temperatures at 25°F for 48 hours could be harmful. Prolonged cold stress depletes the plant's carbohydrate reserves and can lead to progressive tissue damage.

**Practical implications:**
- Short cold snaps are far less damaging than extended cold periods
- Sunny days between cold nights allow plants to recover and rebuild carbohydrate reserves
- Choosing crop planting dates to avoid the absolute coldest period of winter for harvest improves outcomes

### 4. Frequency of Temperature Drop
Repeated freeze-thaw cycles or frequent cold events are more damaging than a single sustained cold period. Each cold event requires the plant to expend energy on stress responses, and frequent cycling prevents full recovery between events.

**Practical implications:**
- Consistently cool but stable conditions are preferable to wildly fluctuating ones
- Greenhouse thermal management should aim for stability, not just warmth
- Inner row covers that are removed and replaced daily can inadvertently increase freeze-thaw cycling

## How Protection Layers Modify Temperature Dynamics

Each layer of protection modifies all four temperature factors:

| Factor | Effect of Adding a Protection Layer |
|---|---|
| **Rate of drop** | Slows cooling — creates thermal buffer between layers |
| **Depth of drop** | Reduces the magnitude — each layer captures ~5-10°F of warmth |
| **Duration of drop** | Shortens time below critical thresholds — thermal mass in soil releases heat longer |
| **Frequency of drop** | Stabilizes temperatures — reduces oscillation from outside weather |

A typical setup in Coleman's Maine operation uses two layers of protection inside the greenhouse: a [[floating-row-cover]] directly over the crops, and sometimes an additional layer. This multi-layer approach can maintain crop-level temperatures 20-30°F above outside air temperatures.

## Summer Temperature Considerations

The same greenhouse structures used for winter production face the opposite challenge in summer. Coleman provides temperature maps showing summer heat extremes, noting that:

- Hotter summers require better greenhouse ventilation systems
- Growers in hot climates may choose to close the greenhouse during peak summer weeks and **solarize the soil** — using the greenhouse's trapped heat to kill soil pathogens and weed seeds
- Summer crops like tomatoes, cucumbers, and peppers need adequate airflow to prevent disease and heat stress

## Monitoring and Management

For serious winter harvest operations, temperature monitoring should include:

1. **Outside air temperature** — for reference and planning
2. **Greenhouse air temperature** — at plant height, not just at the ridge
3. **Under-row-cover temperature** — the actual microclimate the crop experiences
4. **Soil temperature** — affects root activity and nutrient availability

Simple min/max thermometers placed at each level provide the data needed to understand how the protection system performs and when additional layers (or layer removal) are warranted.

## Crop-Specific Temperature Tolerance

Different winter salad crops have varying tolerances within these temperature parameters:

- **Most cold-hardy** (survive to 10°F or below with protection): Mache, claytonia, spinach, minutina
- **Moderately cold-hardy** (survive to 15°F with protection): Tatsoi, scallions, leeks, turnips
- **Less cold-hardy** (survive to 20-25°F with protection): Lettuce, endive, radicchio, Asian greens
- **Least cold-hardy** (need above 25°F): Arugula, beet greens, chard

Understanding these four temperature factors allows growers to make informed decisions about when to add or remove protection layers, when to harvest, and which crops to prioritize for the coldest periods.
