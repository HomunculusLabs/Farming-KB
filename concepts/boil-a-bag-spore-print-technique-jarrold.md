# Boil-a-Bag Spore Print Technique

## Overview

The spore print technique described by D.H. Jarrold is a critical component of the complete boil-a-bag [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] cycle. After successfully [[falconer-growing-mushrooms-in-sheds]], the cultivator must harvest spore prints to produce the inoculum for future crops. This creates a self-sustaining cultivation loop — from spore print to mycelium to mushroom and back to spore print — that eliminates the need to purchase new spores for each grow.

The method emphasizes sterility throughout every step, as contamination during spore printing will carry through to the next generation of grows and potentially ruin entire batches of inoculated substrate.

## Equipment Required

The following equipment is needed for the Jarrold spore print method:

- **Small clean glass jars** with metal screw lids
- **Metal baking tray** (for sterilization in the oven)
- **Tweezers** (metal, for flame sterilization)
- **Scissors** (metal, for cutting mushroom caps)
- **Methylated spirits burner** (or alcohol lamp)
- **Protective breathing mask** or clean handkerchief
- **Oven** (for jar sterilization)

## Step 1: Jar Sterilization

Proper sterilization of the collection jars is the foundation of successful spore printing. Any contamination introduced at this stage will compromise the entire spore print.

### Oven Sterilization Method

1. Take the lids off the clean glass jars and lean them alongside the jars on a metal baking tray
2. Place the tray under the grill (broiler) of the oven
3. Sterilize at **half the maximum oven heat** for 30 minutes
4. After 30 minutes, allow the jars to cool inside the oven

### Sealing the Sterile Jars

1. Once cooled, carefully transfer to the growing area while maintaining sterility
2. Go into the grill and place a lid loosely on each jar — do not tighten yet
3. Remove the tray from the grill and tighten the lids firmly onto each jar
4. The jars are now sealed sterile containers ready for spore collection

**Critical note:** The lids must be loose during cooling to allow any residual moisture to escape. Tightening the lids while the jars are still hot can create condensation inside, which promotes bacterial growth and mold contamination.

## Step 2: Safety Precautions

Before proceeding to the actual spore print collection, Jarrold emphasizes an important safety measure:

> **WARNING: Wear a protective breathing mask or a clean handkerchief covering your nose and mouth for this part of spore print taking. This is not for your safety — it is so that your breath does not directly infect the spore solution.**

Human breath carries bacteria, mold spores, and other microorganisms that can contaminate the sterile collection environment. By wearing a mask, the cultivator prevents exhaled contaminants from reaching the spore print.

## Step 3: Collecting the Spore Print

With sterile jars prepared and safety measures in place, the actual spore print collection can begin.

### Preparation

1. Take the sterilized jars, tweezers, scissors, and methylated spirits burner to the [[duggar-mushroom-growing-history-and-methods]] tank ([[aquarium-humidity-chamber-mushroom-fruiting]])
2. Ensure the working area is as clean and draft-free as possible
3. Put on your breathing mask before opening any jars

### Cap Removal

1. Select a mature mushroom with an open cap that has visible spore-bearing gills
2. Get one of the sterilized jars and unscrew its lid — but leave it sitting loosely on top, do not remove it entirely
3. This technique minimizes the time the jar is exposed to open air

### Sterilizing Tools

1. Flame sterilize the tweezers using the methylated spirits burner
2. Flame sterilize the scissors using the same burner
3. Allow the tools to cool briefly — hot tools can damage the mushroom tissue

### Harvesting the Cap

1. Gently push the flame-sterilized tweezers into the top of a spore-loaded mushroom cap
2. Cut the cap from the stem with the sterilized scissors
3. Carefully place the cap **gills-down** into the sterile jar
4. **Immediately** replace the lid — but do not tighten it all the way

**Critical note:** The lid must not be tightened fully because any moisture present in the jar or on the spores needs to evaporate naturally. If the lid is sealed tight while moisture is present, the damp environment will encourage [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] contamination of the spore print.

## Step 4: Spore Deposition Period

The spore-laden cap needs time to release its spores onto the bottom of the jar:

- **Minimum time:** At least 24 hours with the cap gills-down in the loosely lidded jar
- **Ideal conditions:** A clean, draft-free area at room temperature
- **What happens:** The mushroom cap gradually releases millions of spores from its gills, which settle on the bottom of the jar forming a visible print

After the 24-hour deposition period, the spore print should be clearly visible as a pattern on the bottom of the jar. The color and pattern of the print vary by species but are typically dark (purple-brown for Psilocybe species).

## Step 5: Drying and Final Sealing

After the spores have been deposited, the caps must be removed and the spores must be dried before long-term storage.

### Cap Removal

1. Using flame-sterilized tweezers, carefully remove the mushroom caps from the jars
2. Dispose of the spent caps appropriately
3. Work quickly to minimize exposure time

### Drying Period

1. Leave the jars (now containing only the spore print, without caps) standing in a **clean, draft-free area**
2. Allow the spores to lose their moisture for a further 24 hours
3. The spores must be thoroughly dry before long-term storage

### Final Sealing

1. Once the spores are completely dry, firmly tighten the lids onto each jar
2. The jars are now ready for long-term storage
3. Label each jar with the species, date, and any relevant collection information

Properly dried and sealed spore prints can remain viable for months or even years when stored in a cool, dark place. Some cultivators store them in the refrigerator for extended viability.

## The Complete Cycle

Jarrold describes the completion of this process as "The Circle Is Now Complete." This refers to the self-sustaining nature of the cultivation method:

1. **Start** with a spore print in a jar
2. **Create** a spore syringe from the print
3. **Inoculate** sterile straw substrate bags
4. **Grow** mycelium through the substrate
5. **Fruit** mushrooms in the humidity chamber
6. **Harvest** mature mushrooms
7. **Print** spores from harvested caps
8. **Return** to step 1

This closed-loop cycle means that a single successful spore print can theoretically sustain an unlimited number of future grows, making the initial investment in spores or prints the only ongoing cost of the cultivation method.

## Contamination Risks

The spore print stage is particularly vulnerable to contamination because:

- **Environmental exposure:** The process involves working with open jars in a non-sterile environment
- **[[mushroom-casing-moisture-management]]:** Improper drying can lead to bacterial growth in the stored spores
- **Human contamination:** Breath, skin contact, and clothing can introduce contaminants
- **Cross-contamination:** Multiple species being printed in the same area can contaminate each other
