# Polyethylene Sheeting Fruiting Bins for Small-Scale Mushroom Cultivation

## Overview

A simple and effective approach to mushroom fruiting chambers uses **clear plastic bins covered with polyethylene sheeting** rather than elaborate setups with humidifiers, cool-mist devices, and automated environmental controls. This method relies on evaporation from the casing surface itself to maintain adequate humidity within the chamber, making it both economical and low-maintenance.

## Design and Construction

### Container Selection

The fruiting containers are **clear plastic bins** that are stackable, providing excellent space efficiency. The clear walls allow ambient light to reach the developing mushrooms from all sides, promoting even growth and reducing the tendency of mushrooms to grow toward a single light source.

Key container requirements:

- **Clear transparent walls** — allows light penetration for normal fruiting morphogenesis
- **Rigid construction** — maintains shape when stacked and provides structural support for the polyethylene cover
- **Smooth interior surfaces** — facilitates cleaning between uses and reduces contamination harboring points
- **Appropriate size** — must accommodate five cased trays (approximately 1 litre each) with space between them for air circulation

### Air Exchange System

Small holes are **melted into the sides** of the bins to provide passive air exchange. This is typically done with a heated nail or metal rod, creating clean-edged circular openings. The number and size of holes depends on the bin dimensions, but the principle is to provide enough exchange to prevent CO₂ buildup without creating excessive drying.

These holes can be covered with **mesh or filter material** to prevent the entry of fungus gnats and other small flying insects. Fungus gnats are a common pest in mushroom cultivation and can introduce contamination through their bodies and feces.

### Polyethylene Cover

The bins are covered with **polyethylene sheeting** rather than rigid lids. This flexible cover has several advantages over solid lids:

- **Passive humidity regulation** — condensation forms on the underside of the polyethylene and drips back into the bin, maintaining high humidity without active humidification
- **Gas permeability** — polyethylene is somewhat permeable to gases, allowing slow CO₂ escape and O₂ ingress even through the sheeting itself
- **Easy access** — the sheeting can be lifted for misting and harvesting without removing the entire cover
- **Visual inspection** — the sheeting can be lifted momentarily to check on fruiting progress without disrupting the environment

The sheeting should be large enough to drape over the entire top of the bin with some overhang on the sides, ensuring a good seal against the bin rim.

## Humidity Dynamics

The core principle of this system is that **evaporation from the casing surface is sufficient** to maintain proper humidity levels inside the bin. When five cased trays are placed inside one bin, the combined surface area of moist casing soil provides substantial evaporative output.

This works because:

1. The casing soil is maintained at near field capacity by daily misting
2. The enclosed space of the bin traps moisture that evaporates from the casing
3. The polyethylene cover prevents rapid moisture loss to the ambient air
4. Condensation on the bin walls and cover recycles moisture back into the environment

No humidifier is needed because the biological system itself — the wet casing soil — acts as the humidity source. This is a self-regulating system: when humidity is high, evaporation slows; when humidity drops (after opening the bin), evaporation increases from the moist casing surface.

## Misting Regimen

The casings are **misted each day** to prevent the casing surface from drying out. This is critical because:

- Mycelium at the casing surface desiccates quickly when exposed to moving air
- Pin formation (primordia initiation) requires sustained high surface humidity
- Developing mushrooms pull moisture from the casing soil, accelerating surface drying

After a flush is picked, watering is **increased** because the maturing mushrooms have pulled substantial moisture from the casing soil during their growth cycle. Replenishing this moisture is essential for supporting the next flush.

The source document notes that developing an intuitive feel for watering is more valuable than following rigid schedules. Factors like ambient humidity, temperature, the species being grown, and the casing soil's water-holding capacity all influence the optimal misting frequency and volume.

## Advantages Over Elaborate Setups

| Feature | Polyethylene Bin System | Elaborate System |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Very low (bins + sheeting) | High (humidifiers, controllers, tubing) |
| Maintenance | Daily misting only | Daily cleaning of devices, refilling water |
| Failure points | Almost none | Pump failures, clogging, power outages |
| Space efficiency | Stackable bins | Bulky equipment + bins |
| Contamination risk | Low (no standing water) | Higher (humidifier reservoirs can harbor bacteria) |
| Skill required | Beginner-friendly | Requires technical knowledge |

## Species Compatibility

This system has been successfully used with:

- **Psilocybe cubensis** — including strains like Ecuador that are noted as easy fruiting varieties
- **Psilocybe mexicana** — produces mushrooms from cased trays
- **Psilocybe tampanensis** — produces mushrooms from cased trays
- **Panaeolus subbalteatus** — noted to fruit in this system

The source specifically notes that Psilocybe mexicana, P. tampanensis, and Panaeolus subbalteatus do not benefit from cold-shocking, while harder-to-fruit strains of other species may require a refrigeration step before being placed in the bins.

## Stacking and Space Efficiency

One of the primary advantages of this system is that the bins are **stackable**. In a small growing space, multiple bins can be arranged vertically, each containing five cased trays. This vertical arrangement makes very efficient use of limited space, which is especially valuable for cultivators working in closets, cabinets, or other confined areas.

When stacking, ensure that:

- The weight of upper bins does not crush the polyethylene covers of lower bins
- Air exchange holes on the sides remain unobstructed
- Adequate ambient light reaches all levels of the stack
- There is enough space between stacks for the cultivator to access bins for misting and harvesting

## Limitations

This system works well for small to medium-scale cultivation but may not scale effectively to large operations where dozens or hundreds of trays need simultaneous fruiting. In such cases, automated environmental control becomes more practical.

Additionally, the system relies on the cultivator's diligence in daily misting. Missing even a few days of misting can cause the casing to dry out, leading to reduced or failed flushes.

## Cleaning and Reuse

Between crops, the bins and polyethylene sheeting should be thoroughly cleaned to prevent carryover of mold spores or bacterial contaminants. A recommended cleaning protocol:

1. **Remove all spent substrate** — scrape out any remaining casing soil or mycelial debris
2. **Wash with soap and water** — use a mild dish soap and warm water to remove organic residue
3. **Bleach rinse** — soak or spray with a 10% bleach solution (1 part household bleach to 9 parts water) for 10 minutes
4. **Rinse thoroughly** — bleach residue can harm mycelium; rinse completely with clean water
5. **Air dry** — allow bins to dry completely before the next use

The polyethylene sheeting can be replaced between crops for maximum hygiene, or cleaned alongside the bins if it is still in good condition. Sheet that has become torn, cloudy, or difficult to clean should be discarded and replaced.

## Comparison With Shotgun Fruiting Chamber

The polyethylene bin system is conceptually similar to the popular "shotgun fruiting chamber" (SGFC) but differs in several key ways:

- **No perlite layer** — the SGFC uses a base layer of wet perlite for humidity, while the polyethylene bin relies on evaporation from cased trays
- **Passive airflow only** — the SGFC relies on natural convection through numerous holes, while the polyethylene bin has fewer holes and depends more on the sealed environment
- **Tray-based** — the polyethylene bin holds cased trays that can be removed individually, while the SGFC typically holds cakes or trays directly
- **Stackable** — the rigid bin design allows vertical stacking; SGFCs are typically not stackable

Both systems share the philosophy of simplicity and low cost, making them accessible to novice cultivators.

## Seasonal Considerations

The polyethylene bin system is more sensitive to ambient environmental conditions than automated systems. Seasonal changes can affect performance:

- **Winter** — low ambient humidity means faster evaporation from the casing surface and more frequent misting is required. Indoor heating can also dry the air significantly.
- **Summer** — high ambient humidity can reduce the need for misting but may increase condensation inside the bin, raising the risk of bacterial blotch on mushroom caps.
- **Spring and autumn** — moderate ambient conditions are generally ideal, requiring standard daily misting.

Cultivators should adjust their misting frequency seasonally rather than maintaining a fixed schedule year-round.
