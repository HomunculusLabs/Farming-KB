## Overview

Ayahuasca occupies a unique position in Peruvian society where it functions simultaneously as a sacred indigenous medicine, a popular cultural practice among mestizos, and an officially promoted tourist attraction. Unlike the United States and many Western nations where ayahuasca exists in a legal gray area or is explicitly prohibited, Peru has integrated the brew into its national cultural identity and [[weil-cultural-attitudes-toward-altered-states]] different psychoactive plants. Ayahuasca, however, occupies a largely unambiguous positive space in Peruvian culture.

## The Isolated Shoemaker Case

The general acceptance of ayahuasca in Peru has not been absolute. A notable exception was the imprisonment of American ayahuasquero Alan Shoemaker for distributing ayahuasca. However, this appears to have been an isolated incident rather than indicative of a broader policy shift. The circumstances surrounding Shoemaker's case were unusual and did not lead to systematic enforcement against ayahuasca practitioners.

This isolated enforcement action may have been driven by factors other than the substance itself — possibly related to Shoemaker's status as a foreign national operating within Peru, or to specific local circumstances rather than national policy.

## Christian Missionary Coexistence

Pentecostal, Adventist, and to a lesser extent Catholic missionaries have gained many converts in indigenous communities throughout the Peruvian Amazon. While these Christian denominations publicly disavow shamanism and ayahuasca use, the reality is more nuanced. Some converts continue to privately consume ayahuasca and maintain other native religious practices alongside their Christian faith.

This syncretic coexistence reflects the deep cultural roots of ayahuasca in Amazonian communities. Even when indigenous people adopt new religious frameworks, the practical and spiritual benefits attributed to ayahuasca — healing, vision, community bonding — prove difficult to fully abandon. The persistence of private ayahuasca use among Christian converts suggests that the brew's [[shipibo-san-francisco-ceremonial-center-ucayali]] reflects the deep Shipibo tradition of ayahuasca use. Shipibo communities have a particularly rich ayahuasca culture, known for their distinctive icaros (ceremonial songs) and their tradition of visionary art inspired by ayahuasca experiences.

In San Francisco, there are at least a couple of ayahuasqueros in almost every Shipibo family, and many of these practitioners are willing to host ceremonies for both other Peruvians and foreign visitors. This density of practitioners makes San Francisco a significant destination for tourists seeking an immersive indigenous ayahuasca experience.

### Yarina and Pucallpa

Yarina, a town near the city of Pucallpa in the Ucayali region, serves as another important node in the ayahuasca tourism network. Pucallpa is the second-largest city in the Peruvian Amazon after Iquitos, and the surrounding region has a long history of both indigenous and mestizo ayahuasca use.
