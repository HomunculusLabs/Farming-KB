# Peruvian Government Ayahuasca Festivals and Presidential Ceremony Participation

## Overview

Ayahuasca occupies a unique position in Peruvian society where it functions simultaneously as a sacred indigenous medicine, a popular cultural practice among mestizos, and an officially promoted tourist attraction. Unlike the United States and many Western nations where ayahuasca exists in a legal gray area or is explicitly prohibited, Peru has integrated the brew into its national cultural identity and tourism infrastructure at multiple levels of government.

## Government-Sponsored Ayahuasca Festivals

The Peruvian government tourist agencies actively sponsor ayahuasca festivals as cultural events. This represents a remarkable contrast to drug policies in most other nations, where psychoactive substances are typically subject to prohibition and suppression. In Peru, ayahuasca is treated as a cultural heritage asset worthy of promotion and celebration.

These government-sponsored festivals serve multiple functions:

- **Cultural preservation**: They provide a platform for indigenous and mestizo ayahuasca traditions to be practiced openly and transmitted to new generations.
- **Tourism promotion**: Ayahuasca has become a significant draw for international tourists visiting Peru, contributing to the local economy in the Amazonian regions where these festivals take place.
- **National identity**: The festivals reinforce ayahuasca as a distinctively Peruvian cultural practice, distinguishing Peru from neighboring South American nations.

## Open Market Sales

The brew and its raw materials — primarily Banisteriopsis caapi (the ayahuasca vine) and Psychotria viridis (the chacruna leaf containing DMT) — are openly sold in markets throughout Peru. This open commercial availability reflects the cultural acceptance of ayahuasca and stands in stark contrast to the underground market dynamics that characterize psychoactive substance distribution in most Western countries.

The raw materials are available to both local practitioners who prepare the brew for traditional ceremonies and to tourists who may wish to prepare their own. This accessibility is rooted in centuries of indigenous use and has been maintained despite increasing international attention.

## Presidential Participation: Alejandro Toledo

Perhaps the most striking example of Peru's official embrace of ayahuasca was the participation of then-President Alejandro Toledo in an ayahuasca ceremony. The involvement of a sitting head of state in an ayahuasca ritual sent a powerful symbolic message about the cultural legitimacy of the practice.

President Toledo's participation was notable for several reasons:

- **Official endorsement at the highest level**: No other national leader had publicly participated in an ayahuasca ceremony at the time, making Toledo's involvement unprecedented.
- **Cultural legitimacy**: The president's engagement signaled to both domestic and international audiences that ayahuasca was a respected and valued aspect of Peruvian heritage.
- **Contrast with US drug policy**: While the United States pursued aggressive drug interdiction in South America, Peru's own president was publicly participating in a psychoactive plant ceremony.

## Contrast with the Drug War

Peru's relationship with ayahuasca exists alongside a vigorous drug war focused primarily on cocaine. The country's drug policy reveals a striking duality:

- **Cocaine and coca**: Peru is a major coca producer, and cocaine trafficking remains a serious law enforcement priority. Cocaine traffickers have been known to close the only road connecting Lima to several provinces for weeks at a time, abetted by corrupt politicians and military officials.
- **Ayahuasca**: Despite its psychoactive properties, ayahuasca is regarded as an herbal tonic rather than an illegal drug. The distinction between "drug" and "medicine" in Peruvian culture maps onto a distinction between substances associated with exploitation and addiction (cocaine) versus those associated with healing and tradition (ayahuasca).

Television shows in Peru portray marijuana as a killer weed, yet hemp leaf patches adorn the clothing of many adolescents — illustrating the complex and sometimes contradictory attitudes toward different psychoactive plants. Ayahuasca, however, occupies a largely unambiguous positive space in Peruvian culture.

## The Isolated Shoemaker Case

The general acceptance of ayahuasca in Peru has not been absolute. A notable exception was the imprisonment of American ayahuasquero Alan Shoemaker for distributing ayahuasca. However, this appears to have been an isolated incident rather than indicative of a broader policy shift. The circumstances surrounding Shoemaker's case were unusual and did not lead to systematic enforcement against ayahuasca practitioners.

This isolated enforcement action may have been driven by factors other than the substance itself — possibly related to Shoemaker's status as a foreign national operating within Peru, or to specific local circumstances rather than national policy.

## Christian Missionary Coexistence

Pentecostal, Adventist, and to a lesser extent Catholic missionaries have gained many converts in indigenous communities throughout the Peruvian Amazon. While these Christian denominations publicly disavow shamanism and ayahuasca use, the reality is more nuanced. Some converts continue to privately consume ayahuasca and maintain other native religious practices alongside their Christian faith.

This syncretic coexistence reflects the deep cultural roots of ayahuasca in Amazonian communities. Even when indigenous people adopt new religious frameworks, the practical and spiritual benefits attributed to ayahuasca — healing, vision, community bonding — prove difficult to fully abandon. The persistence of private ayahuasca use among Christian converts suggests that the brew's cultural significance extends beyond any single religious framework.

## Implications for Ayahuasca Tourism

The government's open promotion of ayahuasca creates a unique environment for tourists. Unlike underground psychedelic scenes in Western countries, ayahuasca tourism in Peru operates with a degree of official sanction and infrastructure support. Government tourist agencies actively market ayahuasca experiences as part of the broader Peruvian tourism offering.

However, this official embrace also contributes to the commercialization of what was traditionally a sacred and communal practice. When a practice becomes a government-promoted tourist attraction, the dynamics between practitioners and participants inevitably shift. The festivals, while preserving cultural traditions in one sense, also transform them into performances for external consumption.

The tension between cultural preservation and commercial exploitation is a central challenge for Peruvian ayahuasca culture as it navigates increasing international interest and government involvement.

## Tourism Infrastructure and Regional Hubs

The integration of ayahuasca into Peru's tourism economy has led to the development of specific regional hubs that cater to both domestic and international visitors. These hubs vary in character, from indigenous towns with deep ceremonial traditions to purpose-built retreat centers.

### Iquitos

For the past decade leading up to the early 2000s, Iquitos had been the primary destination for ayahuasca tourists in Peru. As the largest city in the Peruvian Amazon, Iquitos offers a range of ayahuasca experiences from budget-friendly ceremonies with local practitioners to expensive American-led retreats with luxury accommodations. The city's accessibility by air and its established tourist infrastructure made it the default entry point for most Western visitors seeking ayahuasca.

### San Francisco (Shipibo Town)

The Shipibo town of San Francisco, reachable by taxi from Yarina, declared itself the "Centro Ceremonial del Ayahuasca" with an enormous sign at the town entrance. The town's identification as a ceremonial center reflects the deep Shipibo tradition of ayahuasca use. Shipibo communities have a particularly rich ayahuasca culture, known for their distinctive icaros (ceremonial songs) and their tradition of visionary art inspired by ayahuasca experiences.

In San Francisco, there are at least a couple of ayahuasqueros in almost every Shipibo family, and many of these practitioners are willing to host ceremonies for both other Peruvians and foreign visitors. This density of practitioners makes San Francisco a significant destination for tourists seeking an immersive indigenous ayahuasca experience.

### Yarina and Pucallpa

Yarina, a town near the city of Pucallpa in the Ucayali region, serves as another important node in the ayahuasca tourism network. Pucallpa is the second-largest city in the Peruvian Amazon after Iquitos, and the surrounding region has a long history of both indigenous and mestizo ayahuasca use. The Ucayali region was the site of R. Stuart's observations during his cactus identification research project.

## International Context

Peru's official embrace of ayahuasca is part of a broader pattern across South America, though each country approaches the practice differently:

- **Brazil**: Ayahuasca is legal for religious use within formally recognized churches such as Santo Daime and União do Vegetal (UDV). The Brazilian government has granted these churches constitutional protection for their ceremonial use of ayahuasca.
- **Colombia**: Indigenous communities have legal protections for their traditional use of ayahuasca, and the substance is available in markets in Amazonian regions.
- **Ecuador**: Ayahuasca use is tolerated in indigenous communities and has become a growing tourist attraction.

Peru's approach is distinctive in that the government actively promotes ayahuasca as a tourist attraction rather than merely tolerating or protecting its use. This proactive stance reflects both the deep cultural roots of the practice and the economic value it generates for Amazonian communities.

## Economic Dimensions

The economic impact of government-supported ayahuasca tourism extends beyond the immediate ceremonial fees. Practitioners earn significantly more from foreign tourists than from local clients. One practitioner in the Yarina area was reported to charge thirty dollars for giving ayahuasca to a tourist, while the going rate for a Peruvian was about two dollars. Another enterprising shaman earned a couple hundred dollars per month catering to tourists while his neighbors could not afford a thirty-cent taxi ride.

This economic disparity has significant social implications within Amazonian communities. Ayahuasca tourism can create economic winners and losers, potentially straining community relationships. The government's promotion of ayahuasca tourism, while generating revenue, may inadvertently accelerate these economic inequalities.

## Tourism Safety and Medical Considerations

The open commercial environment for ayahuasca in Peru means that tourists have relatively easy access to ceremonies, but this accessibility comes with risks. Most shamans are unfamiliar with Western pharmaceuticals, making it the tourist's responsibility to be aware that ayahuasca can have adverse interactions with various prescription medicines, particularly those used to treat AIDS, depression, and psychiatric disorders.

At least one documented case involved an AIDS patient who died during a ceremony in Bolivia — a death that was particularly notable because the patient was sufficiently healthy to dance at the beginning of the service. This case underscores the importance of medical screening and awareness of drug interactions, particularly in an environment where ayahuasca is readily available and regulation is minimal.

The combination of open market availability, government tourism promotion, and limited medical oversight creates a situation where tourists bear primary responsibility for their own safety. While the cultural acceptance of ayahuasca in Peru makes it relatively easy to find and participate in ceremonies, the lack of standardized screening procedures or practitioner certification means that quality and safety vary widely.
