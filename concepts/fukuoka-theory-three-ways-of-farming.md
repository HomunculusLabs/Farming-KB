# Fukuoka's Three Ways of Farming: Mahayana, Hinayana, and Scientific

[[masanobu-fukuoka]] divided farming into three fundamental categories based on
the relationship between human action and natural forces. This philosophical
framework, rooted in Buddhist terminology, reveals the core assumptions
underlying different approaches to agriculture and their relative merits.

## The Three Categories

Fukuoka arranged the three ways of farming along a spectrum from absolute to
relative:

- **Absolute World:** Mahayana natural farming (pure natural farming)
- **Relative World:** Hinayana natural farming (organic, idealistic farming)
  and scientific farming (scientific agriculture)

Mahayana and scientific farming exist on entirely different planes. Hinayana
and scientific farming occupy the same dimension and can be directly compared.

## Mahayana Natural Farming

Mahayana natural farming represents the highest ideal: when the human spirit
and human life blend completely with the natural order. Man lives freely as an
integral part of the natural world, subsisting on its bounty without
purposeful effort. This way of farming transcends time and space, reaching the
zenith of understanding and enlightenment.

Fukuoka compares this relationship to an ideal marriage where partners realize
a perfect life without asking for, giving, or receiving anything of each
other. It is the very embodiment of life in accordance with nature. Those who
live such a life are hermits and wise men. Mahayana farming is nature itself,
superior in every respect to all other ways of farming.

### Philosophical Superiority

Scientific farming is the sum of knowledge and forces extracted from nature by
the human intellect, amounting to finite human knowledge. No matter how one
totals it, human knowledge remains a tiny, closely circumscribed fraction of
the infinitude of the natural world. In contrast to the vast, boundless,
perfect knowledge and power of nature, the finite knowledge of man is always
limited to small pockets of time and space. As imperfection can never equal
perfection, scientific farming must always yield to Mahayana natural farming.

## Hinayana Natural Farming

Hinayana natural farming arises when man earnestly seeks entry to the realm
of Mahayana farming. Desirous of nature's true blessings and bounty, he
prepares himself to receive them. This is the road leading directly to
complete enlightenment, but it falls short of that perfect state. The
relationship to nature is like a lover who yearns for his beloved but has not
realized full union.

Hinayana farming belongs in the same world of relativity as scientific
farming. Both are derived from nature as verified with discriminating
knowledge. The fundamental difference is one of direction: Hinayana farming
attempts to cast off human knowledge and action to make the greatest possible
use of pure natural forces, whereas scientific farming adds human knowledge
to nature's powers in an effort to establish a superior method.

### The Sword-Fighting Analogy

Borrowing from the art of swordfighting, Hinayana natural farming is the
one-sword school directed toward the center, while scientific farming is the
two-sword school directed outward. Mahayana natural farming is the unmoving
no-sword school, comparison with which is impossible.

Scientific farming increases the number of swords at its disposal. Natural
farming tries to obtain the best results while rendering all means useless,
reducing the number of swords (Hinayana) or doing entirely without them
(Mahayana). The philosophical conviction is that if man genuinely approaches
nature, even abandoning all deeds and actions, nature will take each over and
perform them for him.

## Scientific Farming

Scientific farming exists in a state of contradiction: man is basically
estranged from nature, living in an artificial world, yet longing to return.
Scientific agriculture wanders blindly, now calling upon nature's blessings,
now rejecting them in favor of human knowledge. The lover here cannot decide
whose hand to ask in marriage and, agonizing over indecision, imprudently
courts multiple suitors heedless of social proprieties.

### When Scientific Farming Excels

Scientific methods have the upper hand only when growing produce in unnatural
environments: accelerated growth, cramped plots, hothouses, and hotbeds.
Through adroit management, yields increase and out-of-season produce satisfies
consumer cravings using high technology, chemical fertilizers, and pest
control agents. Yet this is only because natural farming cannot be practiced
under such conditions. Even under ideal conditions, scientific farming does
not produce more at lower cost than natural farming.

### When Both Ways Are Equally Effective

Under nearly natural conditions such as field cropping or summer grazing,
natural farming will never produce results inferior to scientific agriculture
because it takes full advantage of nature's forces. Man imitates nature but
cannot produce anything from scratch. He is a student of nature; nature is
the teacher. Were the teacher to use its full powers, the student would lose.

### The Distortion of Scientific Results

The achievements of scientific farming may excel in a restricted sense but are
invariably inferior in all other ways. When scientific methods grow a fruit
tree for large fruit, all efforts concentrate on that end. Yet what is
produced is deformed fruit: always large, even unnaturally so, but with grave
flaws. The unnaturalness of scientific farming leads directly to
incompleteness; its results are always distorted and of only local utility.

The irregular shape of scientific farming represents distortions and
imperfections arising from narrow research findings. This contrasts with the
perfect circle of nature toward which Hinayana natural farming aspires.

## Yield Comparisons

Fukuoka summarizes the yield relationships:

1. Scientific farming excels only under unnatural, man-made conditions where
   natural farming cannot be practiced
2. Under conditions approaching nature, Hinayana natural farming yields
   results at least as good as or better than scientific farming
3. In holistic terms, Mahayana natural farming is always superior to
   scientific farming
