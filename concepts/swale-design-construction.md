# Swale Design and Construction

Swales are shallow, level-bottomed ditches excavated on contour — lines of equal elevation across a slope. They capture surface water runoff, slow its movement, and allow it to infiltrate into the soil. Swales are one of the most effective tools in the permaculture toolkit for rehydrating landscapes, preventing erosion, and establishing trees and perennial plantings on slopes.

## Swale Types

### Contour Swale

The standard permaculture swale. Dug exactly on contour with a level bottom. Water spreads evenly along the entire length. Used for tree planting, slope stabilization, and broad-scale hydration.

### Diversion Swale

Dug with a slight grade (0.5-2% slope) to move water from one area to another. Not truly on contour. Used to redirect water away from buildings, roads, or erosion-prone areas toward storage or planting zones. See [[earthworks-for-water-management]].

### Infiltration Swale

A shallow, wide swale filled with gravel or coarse organic material. Designed for maximum infiltration in a small area. Commonly used in urban settings or along building foundations connected to [[rainwater-harvesting-systems]].

### Net and Pan Swale

A series of small, interconnected swales forming a net-like pattern across a slope. Used in arid and semi-arid regions to capture every possible drop of rainfall. Labor-intensive but extremely effective for desert restoration.

### Berm "n Basin (Boomerang Swale)

Crescent-shaped swales on gentle slopes, typically used around individual trees or planting areas. The berm (mound) is on the downhill side, and the basin (depression) captures water. Simple to construct with hand tools.

## Sizing Formula

Proper sizing prevents swale failure from overflow or insufficient capture.

### Basic Sizing Calculation

The swale volume must be able to hold the expected runoff from a design storm (typically a 10-year, 24-hour rainfall event).

**Runoff Volume Formula:**

    V = R × C × A × 10,000 / 1000

Where:
- V = runoff volume in cubic meters (m³)
- R = rainfall depth in mm (for the design storm)
- C = runoff coefficient (0.1 for forested, 0.3 for pasture, 0.6 for bare soil)
- A = catchment area in hectares

**Swale Volume Capacity:**

    V_swale = W × D × L

Where:
- W = bottom width of swale in meters
- D = average depth of swale in meters
- L = length of swale in meters

The swale volume should be 1.2 to 1.5 times the expected runoff volume to provide a safety margin.

### Rule-of-Thumb Sizing

For most homestead-scale applications on moderate slopes:

| Slope | Swale Spacing | Swale Width | Swale Depth |
|-------|--------------|-------------|-------------|
| Gentle (3-5%) | 15-25 m apart | 0.5-1.0 m | 0.2-0.3 m |
| Moderate (5-10%) | 8-15 m apart | 0.5-1.0 m | 0.2-0.4 m |
| Steep (10-20%) | 5-10 m apart | 0.5-1.0 m | 0.3-0.5 m |

Spacing decreases on steeper slopes because the catchment area per swale is smaller and water moves faster.

### Berm Sizing

The berm (mound on the downhill side) should be:
- Width: 1.5-2 times the swale width
- Height: equal to or slightly less than swale depth
- Slope: 1:3 or flatter on both sides to prevent slumping

## Construction Steps

### Step 1: Design and Planning

1. Observe the site during and after rainfall to understand water flow patterns
2. Identify the keypoint(s) — the highest points where water naturally concentrates (see [[keyline-design]])
3. Determine the design storm rainfall for your area (consult local weather data)
4. Calculate catchment area, runoff volume, and required swale dimensions
5. Plan swale spacing and layout on a topographic map or by flagging in the field
6. Mark tree planting positions on the berm

### Step 2: Marking the Contour

1. Use an A-frame level, bunyip (water level), laser level, or dumpy level to find the contour line
2. Place stakes or flags every 2-3 meters along the contour
3. Walk the line and verify it feels level — small adjustments may be needed for natural variation
4. On very long swales (over 50 meters), allow for a very slight (0.1-0.5%) grade to prevent ponding in low spots

### Step 3: Excavation

**Hand Digging (for swales up to 30 meters)**
1. Dig along the marked contour, piling excavated soil on the downhill side to form the berm
2. Maintain a flat, level bottom as you dig
3. Check level frequently with a straight board and spirit level
4. Compact the berm by tamping or walking on it
5. Shape the berm with a slight crown (higher in the middle) for stability

**Machine Digging (for larger swales)**
1. Hire an excavator or use a tractor with a front-end loader and box blade
2. The operator must understand the concept of "on contour" — supervise closely
3. Dig in passes, checking level after each pass
4. Use the excavated material to build the berm on the downhill side
5. Finish the bottom by hand or with a box blade for a smooth, level surface

### Step 4: Spillway Construction

Every swale needs a safe overflow point to prevent catastrophic failure during extreme rainfall:

1. Identify the highest point of the swale bottom (naturally or by design)
2. At this point, lower the berm to create a spillway
3. The spillway should be 0.3-0.5 m wide, armored with rocks or grass
4. The spillway directs overflow to the next swale below or to a safe discharge point
5. Never allow water to overflow the berm at an unarmored point — this causes erosion and potential swale failure

### Step 5: Stabilization

1. Plant the berm immediately with a cover crop (see [[cover-cropping-guide]]) to prevent erosion
2. Mulch the berm heavily with straw or wood chips
3. Plant trees and perennials on the berm (see below)
4. Line the swale bottom with gravel or wood chips if water velocity will be high
5. In erosion-prone areas, place rocks or logs along the swale bottom to dissipate energy

## Planting on Swales

Swales create ideal planting conditions — the berm is well-drained and moisture-retentive, and the swale bottom provides a moist microclimate.

### Tree Planting

- Plant trees on the top of the berm, not in the swale bottom (root rot risk)
- Space trees 3-6 meters apart depending on mature size
- Use deep-rooted trees on the uphill side and shallow-rooted on the downhill side
- Fruit trees, nut trees, and nitrogen-fixing trees all thrive on swale berms
- See [[grafting-fruit-trees]] for establishing fruit tree orchards on swales
