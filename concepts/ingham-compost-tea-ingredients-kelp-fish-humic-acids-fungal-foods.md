# AACT Ingredient Classification: Bacterial Foods, Fungal Foods, and Minerals

## Overview

Ingham's Field Guide devotes extensive discussion to the classification of compost tea
ingredients by the organism groups they selectively promote. Understanding which foods
grow bacteria vs. fungi is essential for producing teas targeted to specific soil or plant
needs. The wrong ingredient balance can produce anaerobic conditions, favor pathogens,
or fail to establish desired organism groups.

## The Inoculum Principle

AACT brewing extracts organisms from compost and then grows them during the brewing
period. The key insight: "We don't need huge amounts of compost because we just need to
get an inoculum of the good guys. Then we want to grow the organisms we've extracted."

Compost-to-water ratios are non-linear (more water = proportionally less compost needed):

| Water Volume | Compost Needed |
|-------------|----------------|
| 5 gallons   | ~1 pound       |
| 25 gallons  | ~7 pounds      |
| 50 gallons  | ~10 pounds     |
| 100 gallons | ~12 pounds     |
| 500 gallons | ~15–20 pounds  |

Machine design matters—poorly aerated machines require more compost to compensate for
organism losses during brewing.

## Bacterial Foods

### Simple Sugars

Simple sugars selectively feed bacteria and are the primary tool for boosting bacterial
biomass in tea:

- **Molasses**: The most commonly used bacterial food. Contains diverse sugars (not just
  sucrose) plus humic material. Blackstrap, unsulfured molasses preferred.
  - Rate: 0.1–0.5% of water volume (0.5–2.5 gallons per 500 gallons)
  - Higher concentrations (3–5%) enhance fungal growth and improve organism adhesion
    to leaf surfaces
  - At very high levels, can drive tea anaerobic—monitor carefully
- **Fruit juice**: Provides diverse sugar profile; useful alternative to molasses
- **Honey**: Helps bacteria, NOT fungi—contains no fungal food components

### Critical Warning: Sugar and Anaerobiosis

"If you add enough sugar, the soil in these areas will go anaerobic as the bacteria grow
and use up the oxygen." Bacteria consume oxygen as they metabolize simple sugars. Excess
sugar → bacterial bloom → oxygen depletion → anaerobic conditions → pathogen growth.

The gradient matters: too little sugar means organisms lack food to grow; too much means
anaerobic conditions. The right amount depends on existing soil biology, temperature,
moisture, and compaction.

### What NOT to Use

- **White sugar**: Too limited—just sucrose, no diversity benefit
- **Milk, yogurt, whey**: Risky—can drive tea anaerobic in warm conditions

## Fungal Foods

### Why Fungi Need Different Foods

Fungi require more complex carbon sources than bacteria. While molasses contains SOME
fungal food, it is not ideal for maximizing [[bioremediation-fungal-biomass-biosorbent-material]]. To grow primarily fungal teas,
you need materials specifically geared to fungi.

### Primary Fungal Foods

- **Humic acids**: Made by enzymatic hydrolysis of leonardite or hot water extraction.
  Provides thousands of different humic acid compounds—far more than commercial products
  claiming "three humic acids." Rate: 1–4 oz (50–200 ml) per 50-gallon brew.
- **Fish hydrolysate**: Contains enzymatically hydrolyzed bone, cartilage, and scale
  material. Preferred: Organic Gem, Neptune's Harvest. Rate: 2–10 oz per 50-gal brew.
  Beware: hydrolysate from anaerobic scraps can introduce problems.
- **Oatmeal**: Effective fungal food; part of the "humic acid, oatmeal mix" Ingham
  recommends for fungal-dominant teas
- **Cornmeal, corn bran, wheat bran**: Complex carbohydrates that favor fungal growth

### Kelp: Dual-Role Ingredient

Kelp serves two functions in AACT:

1. **Fungal surface area**: Fungi use kelp particles as substrate for hyphal growth
   (½ lb per 500 gallons provides sufficient surface area)
2. **Plant micronutrients**: Kelp supplies [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-trace-minerals]]; amount should relate to plant
   nutrient needs, not just organism growth

Too much kelp can kill plants through excess nitrogen. Too little means missing essential
micronutrients, especially in leached soils.

### Fungal Compost Requirements

To make effective fungal tea, you need fungal compost first:

- Above 150 µg fungal biomass (preferably 300+ µg)
- Above 10 µg active fungi
- Good bacterial, protozoan, and nematode numbers
- If the compost lacks fungi, no amount of fungal food in the tea will compensate

## Minerals

- **Rock dust**: Added for plant nutrient needs (amount depends on crop requirements).
  Contains [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]] minerals.
- **Greensand**: Potassium source plus trace minerals
- **Rock phosphate**: Phosphorus source for plant nutrition
- **Lignite**: Only useful if adequate soil biology exists to break it down. Without the
  right organisms, lignite remains inert.

## Compost Base: Aerobic Thermal Compost + Worm Castings

The recommended compost base is a mix of good aerobic thermal compost and aerobic worm
castings in approximately 50:50 or 25:75 ratio. Ingham specifically warns: "DO NOT USE
CEDAR GROVE PRODUCTS, THEY DO NOT MAKE COMPOST."

## Bacterial vs. Fungal Tea Decision Framework

| Condition | Tea Type | Key Ingredients |
|-----------|----------|-----------------|
| Annual vegetables, disturbed soil | Bacterial-dominant | Molasses, simple sugars |
| Perennials, shrubs, trees | Fungal-dominant | Humic acid, fish hydrolysate, oatmeal |
| [[aact-disease-suppression-mechanisms-ingham]] (foliar) | Balanced, fungi ≥ threshold | Molasses + humic acid + kelp |
| Root rot prevention | Fungal-dominant | Humic acid, fish hydrolysate |
| Soil structure building | Fungal-dominant | Humic acid, kelp, fungal compost |

### Fungal Biomass Threshold

Disease suppression requires minimum fungal levels. "There needs to be bacteria even in
fungal tea, but fungi need to be at a minimum level to see the disease suppression we
would all like to have." Some bacterial-only diseases exist (black spot, Taphrina), but
most foliar diseases require the fungal component when conditions are favorable for
pathogen growth.
