# Far-Red Light and the Phytochrome Molecular Switch Mechanism

## Overview

The discovery that far-red light can reverse the effect of red light on plant
flowering revealed the existence of a molecular switch that controls
photoperiodic responses. Harry Borthwick and colleagues at the USDA
demonstrated this far-red reversal effect in the early 1950s, and by the
early 1960s, Warren L. Butler and colleagues identified a single
photoreceptor, phytochrome, responsible for both red and far-red responses.
This light-activated switch mechanism allows plants to measure the duration
of continuous darkness with remarkable precision.

## The Far-Red Reversal Effect

Borthwick's team made the surprising discovery that far-red light, with
wavelengths slightly longer than bright red and barely visible at dusk,
could completely cancel the effect of red light on flowering. When irises,
which normally do not flower during long nights, received a brief pulse of
red light in the middle of the night, they produced flowers. But if far-red
light was applied immediately after the red pulse, the plants behaved as if
they had never seen the red light at all and did not flower. This reversal
was repeatable: red followed by far-red produced no [[phytochrome-red-far-red-light-switch]] that can
be toggled repeatedly without degradation of the photoreceptor.

## Red Versus Far-Red Spectral Properties

Red light has wavelengths shorter than approximately 700 nanometers, while
far-red extends from approximately 700 to 750 nanometers. The distinction
between these two spectral bands is ecologically meaningful because
sunlight contains abundant red light but reduced far-red. However, at dawn
and dusk, atmospheric scattering preferentially filters out red light while
allowing more far-red to pass through, creating the characteristic red-to-
far-red ratio shift that signals twilight to plants. This natural spectral
gradient provides the environmental signal that phytochrome has evolved to
detect.
