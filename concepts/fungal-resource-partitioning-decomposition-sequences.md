# Fungal Resource Partitioning and Decomposition Sequences

Fungi function as primary, secondary, and tertiary decomposers in the
often complex process of degrading mollison [[mollison-designers-humus-building-and-soil-organic-matter]]. The sequence of
fungal species appearing during [[biodiversity-of-fungi-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]] and connects
to the broader biodiversity-fungi-soil-ecosystems, see that page. The decomposition sequence follows a general
pattern:

### Primary Colonizers

Primary colonizers are typically fast-growing, ruderal species that
rapidly exploit readily available soluble carbohydrates, simple sugars,
and amino acids. These R-selected fungi have high reproductive capacity
and short life spans. In coprophilous systems, Zygomycetes often fill
this role, appearing within 5-14 days. In leaf litter, species of
Cladosporium, Aureobasidium, and other common phylloplane fungi may
serve as primary colonizers.

### Secondary Decomposers

Secondary decomposers specialize in the breakdown of more complex
polysaccharides, particularly cellulose and hemicellulose. These fungi
produce a suite of cellulolytic and hemicellulolytic enzymes. In dung
systems, Ascomycetes typically dominate this stage. In leaf litter,
numerous hyphomycetes and discomycetes specialize in cellulose
degradation.

### Tertiary Decomposers

Tertiary decomposers are primarily lignin-degrading basidiomycetes
that attack the recalcitrant aromatic polymers remaining after
cellulose and hemicellulose have been depleted. These fungi are
typically slow-growing, stress-tolerant species with high enzymatic
competence. Wood-decomposing basidiomycetes such as those in the
Polyporales and Agaricales are characteristic tertiary decomposers.

## Resource Partitioning Strategies

### Enzymatic Specialization

Different fungal species produce different complements of
[[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]], allowing them to exploit different chemical
components of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]:
- **Cellulases**: Hydrolyze cellulose to glucose
- **Hemicellulases**: Degrade hemicellulose polymers
- **Lignin peroxidases and manganese peroxidases**: Oxidatively
  degrade lignin
- **Laccases**: Oxidize [[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]]
- **Pectinases**: [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]] pectin in middle lamellae
- **Cutinases**: Degrade cutin in leaf surfaces
- **Chitinases**: [[query-how-do-white-rot-fungi-break-down-lignin]] chitin in fungal cell walls and insect
  exoskeletons

### Substrate Specificity

Studies of [[aact-forest-litter-fungi-mycorrhizal-ingham]] have revealed patterns of [[fungal-endemism-host-specificity]].
Bills and Polishook (1996) investigated the complementarity of fungal
species isolated from litter of two plant species growing together in
a forest in Puerto Rico, addressing the question of specificity and
ubiquity of microfungi on different host plants. The overlap of
species between host-specific litter samples was only 15-28%,
suggesting considerable substrate partitioning.

The diversity of microfungi associated with litter of particular plant
species has been studied extensively. Kirk (1981, 1982, 1984) identified
137 species of microfungi from 44 collections of leaf litter of Laurus
nobilis over 12 years. No species was found in more than 75% of the
samples, 35% of species were found only once, and a majority (64%) was
found in less than 9% of the samples, strongly suggesting that many
species of microfungi are rare.
