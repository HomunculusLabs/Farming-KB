# Oyster Mushroom Species Guide

Oyster mushrooms (genus Pleurotus) are among the most widely [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]] worldwide, valued for their fast growth, broad substrate tolerance, excellent culinary qualities, and ease of cultivation. The genus contains over 30 described species, but five are most commonly grown commercially and by home cultivators.

## Genus Overview

Pleurotus species are white-rot fungi that decompose both cellulose and lignin, giving them an unusually broad substrate range. They are found worldwide on dead and dying hardwood trees. Most species are saprotrophic, though some can be weak parasites on stressed trees. Oyster mushrooms are aggressive colonizers, making them forgiving for beginners but also competitive contaminants in mushroom farms growing other species.

## Pleurotus ostreatus (Pearl/Tree Oyster)

The most widely cultivated and commercially available oyster mushroom. Extremely versatile and forgiving for beginners. Naturally found across temperate North America, Europe, and Asia on dead hardwood.

Best substrates: straw, hardwood sawdust, coffee grounds, paper/cardboard, agricultural waste. Grows well on virtually any lignocellulosic material. See [[shiitake-cultivation-guide]] for sawdust block [[amanita-muscaria-preparation-methods]] that also apply to oysters.

Multiple commercial strains available varying in color (white, gray, blue-gray), cold tolerance, and yield.

## Pleurotus pulmonarius (Phoenix/Indian Oyster)

Very similar to P. ostreatus but distinguished by warmer temperature preference, faster colonization, and slightly thinner flesh. Often confused with P. ostreatus in the wild.

Excellent for summer cultivation in warm climates. The "Phoenix" name refers to its ability to fruit prolifically after rain events. Often outcompetes P. ostreatus in warmer environments.

## Pleurotus citrinopileatus (Golden Oyster)

Native to subtropical and tropical hardwood forests of East Asia. Strikingly bright yellow fruiting bodies make it popular for farmers markets and visual appeal.

More demanding than other oyster species. Requires higher temperatures and humidity. Has a shorter shelf life and the bright yellow color fades rapidly after harvest. Flavors described as similar to cashews. Some growers report allergic reactions to the spores — wear a mask during harvest.

## Pleurotus djamor (Pink Oyster)

A tropical species with vivid pink coloration that fades to orange-tan with age. Native to subtropical and tropical regions worldwide.

The most heat-tolerant of the common oyster species but also the most perishable — fruiting bodies deteriorate within 24-48 hours at room temperature. Best consumed immediately after harvest or sold same-day. The pink color is temperature-sensitive: cooler growing conditions intensify the color.

Excellent choice for tropical [[query-how-to-grow-rare-tropical-fruits-in-marginal-and-subtropical-climates]]. Does not tolerate cold temperatures at all.

## Pleurotus eryngii (King Oyster)

The largest and most commercially valuable oyster mushroom. Native to the Mediterranean, North Africa, and Middle East. Distinctive thick, central stem and small cap — the reverse morphology of other oyster species.

The stem is the primary edible portion, with a texture remarkably similar to scallops or abalone when sliced and seared. Premium product in Japanese, Korean, and Chinese markets.

More challenging to cultivate than other Pleurotus species:

- Requires cooler temperatures
- Slower colonization (3-4 weeks)
- More sensitive to contamination
- Requires careful humidity management
- Benefits from higher supplementation (25-30% bran)

King Oyster benefits from a "cut and flip" technique where the bag is opened and the block is flipped upside down to produce a single large, stem-dominant mushroom.

## General Cultivation Notes

### Substrate Preparation

All Pleurotus species share similar substrate preparation:

- Pasteurization is sufficient (not full sterilization) for straw-based substrates
- Hot water pasteurization: 160-180 F for 60-90 minutes
- Lime bath: 24-hour soak in pH 12 hydrated lime water
- Sawdust-based substrates should be sterilized at 15 PSI for 2 hours

### Fruiting Conditions (All Species)

- Humidity: 85-95% (critical for all oyster species)
- Light: 500-1000 lux indirect light
- Fresh air exchange: essential; oysters need more FAE than most species
- CO2: below 600 ppm for normal morphology; higher CO2 produces stem elongation

### Common Issues

- Contamination by Trichoderma: most common problem; improve pasteurization
- Dried-out fruiting bodies: increase humidity
- Long stems, small caps: increase fresh air exchange (lower CO2)
- Bacterial blotch: reduce humidity, improve air circulation
- Mite infestations: maintain clean growing environment
