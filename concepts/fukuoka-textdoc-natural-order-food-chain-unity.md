## Overview

In the opening section of "The Road Man Must Follow," Masanobu Fukuoka
presents his vision of the natural order as a unified, harmonious whole
rather than a competitive struggle for survival. He describes the
incredibly complex web of predator-prey relationships among animals,
plants, and microorganisms, arguing that these interconnections should be
understood as neither competitive nor cooperative, but as one and the
same. This page explores Fukuoka's ecological philosophy as articulated
in the textdoc source.

## The Community of Organic Interrelationships

Fukuoka begins by stating that organisms of all manner and form inhabit
the earth's surface. Broadly divided into animals, plants, and
microorganisms, they differ from each other but are united in a single
community of organic interrelationships. Man characterizes these
relationships either as a competitive struggle for dominance and survival
or as cooperation and mutual benefit. From an absolute perspective,
however, Fukuoka argues these are neither competitive nor cooperative,
but one and the same. All living things belong to an endless food chain;
all live by feeding on something and die at the hand of something else.
This is the proper order of living nature.

## The Cycles of Matter and Energy

Matter and energy on the earth's surface are in a constant state of flux,
passing through continuous cycles without birth or death. Plants growing
on the earth are fed upon by bird and beast. Some animals become prey
to others, while others succumb to disease or age. Their wastes and
remains are broken down by microorganisms which proliferate and die,
returning nutrients to the earth that are taken up once more by plants.
Fukuoka presents this as the true image of the universe: not a
battlefield but a grand, self-sustaining cycle.

## Microbial Predator-Prey Relationships

Among the microorganisms, Fukuoka describes an intricate web of
predator-prey relationships. There are bacteria, fungi (including true
fungi and molds), slime molds, and yeasts. Fungi wrap mycelia about
their prey and kill by dissolution. Bacteria secrete substances that
kill fungi. Bacteriophages kill bacteria. Viruses kill both bacteria
and fungi, and some viruses kill other viruses. There are also viruses,
bacteria, and fungi that parasitize and kill plants and animals. This
microscopic warfare operates continuously at scales invisible to the
human eye, maintaining balance in ways that man can barely comprehend.

## The Vertebrate and Invertebrate Food Web

Fukuoka traces a remarkably detailed food chain among larger organisms.
Spiders kill rice borers and leafhoppers which feed on rice. Mites kill
the spiders. Predaceous mites feed on those mites. Ladybugs feed on
predaceous mites. Earwigs feed on ladybugs. Cricket moles and centipedes
eat earwig eggs. Swallows feed on centipedes. Snakes eat small birds.
Kites and dogs kill snakes. At every level, bacteria and viruses attack
these birds, beasts, and insects. Amoebae and nematodes feed on the
bacteria. Nematode remains are consumed by earthworms, which are
relished by moles. Weasels feed on moles, and microorganisms break down
the weasel's carcass, providing nutrients for plants.

## The Family Metaphor

The natural ecosystem is therefore an incredibly complex array of
interdependently linked organisms, none of which live separate from the
rest, none of which simply die and are done with. Fukuoka insists this
must not be seen as a world of intense competition for survival or of
the strong eating the weak, but as a united family of many members that
live together in a single harmony. This metaphor of family unity is
central to his philosophy and distinguishes his view from both
Darwinian competition-based ecology and naive cooperation-based
perspectives.

## The Mystery of Self-Governed Providence

Fukuoka poses a profound question: what is the mechanism by which all
organisms propagate in just measure, becoming neither too numerous nor
too scarce? No species of bird should become extinct. Earthworms must
not vanish. Mice and spiders must not proliferate too greatly. If one
type of fungus thrives just a little too well, everything is thrown out
of balance. Tens of millions of species live on in perfect harmony
without increase or decrease; they are born and die unseen by man. The
mastery of the conductor performing this drama of natural transformation,
at once casual and intense, is truly something to behold.

## Man as the Sole Heretic

Despite nature's perfect self-regulation, there is one who disrupts the
natural order: man. Man is the sole heretic in the natural order. Only
he acts as he pleases. Instead of burying his corpses in the earth where
microbes can process them, he douses them with heavy fuel oil and
cremates them. Fukuoka contrasts human refuse processing plants, which
he calls child's play, with the infinitely elaborate methods of treating
garbage used by nature. Human society can barely handle kitchen waste,
while nature works on a vastly greater scale with perfect efficiency.

## Implications for Agriculture

The practical implication of this ecological vision is that farmers
should not interfere with natural balance. When something goes wrong in
nature, the biosystem changes course. Unlike a factory where damage may
consist of a broken gear, in nature a disruption gives rise to an
unending series of repercussions. When man jumps onto center stage and
begins giving directions like a know-nothing director, the play is
ruined. This principle underlies all of Fukuoka's farming practices:
minimal intervention allows nature's self-governing mechanisms to
maintain balance far more effectively than human management.
