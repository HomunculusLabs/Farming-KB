## Farm Strategies and Yield Extension in Permaculture

## Water Storage Strategies (12-20% of Landscape)

Dedicating 12-20% of the landscape to water storage creates multiple compounding benefits:

1. **Direct product increase**: Water is more productive per unit area than land; fish are more efficient at food conversion than cattle, so ponds produce more animal protein per hectare than pasture.

2. **Product increase on remaining land**: Irrigation from stored water plus nutrient-rich water (e.g., fish manure-enriched) increases crop production on the surrounding land.

3. **System interaction**: Ducks on water increase yields in and around ponds through pest control, weed control, and manuring.

4. **Microclimatic buffering**: Water bodies moderate temperature extremes, reducing frost damage and heat stress for nearby plantings.

## Land Forming Strategies

Land forming—reshaping topography to optimize water distribution—produces multiple benefits:

1. **Product increase** due to even irrigation with no dry areas or waterlogging
2. **Land stability** through reduction of soil loss from water run-off or salting
3. **Energy savings** as gravity flow replaces pumped water (site-dependent)
4. **Water recycling** becomes possible through controlled distribution

## Soil Development Strategies

Deep soil development (20-30% of landscape dedicated to soil building) yields:

1. **Product increase** due to deeper root penetration into well-structured soils
2. **Water infiltration** with zero run-off through improved absorption capacity
3. **Microclimate buffering** of soil [[mushroom-growing-temperature-and-moisture]]
4. **Nutrient supply** from enhanced biological activity in deep, well-structured soils

## Forestry and Tree Systems (20-30% of Landscape)

Dedicated forestry and tree systems provide extensive benefits beyond timber:

1. **Shelter effects**: Increased plant yields, animal protein, and microclimate buffering both above and below ground
2. **Increased [[fukuoka-textdoc-land-requirements-per-diet-type-carrying-capacity]]** through shrub and tree fodder systems
3. **Nutrient recycling** via leguminous trees and deep-rooted mineral cyclers
4. **Intrinsic products**: Nectar for honey, seeds, firewood from fallen timber
5. **Biological control**: Insect and bird habitat providing natural pest predation
6. **[[permaculture-designers-manual-wildlife-corridors]]**: Connected habitat patches supporting biodiversity

### Tree-Based Production Benefits
- **Precipitation increase** due to night condensation and improved water penetration
- **Perennial superiority**: Perennials outperform annuals in bulk production, energy savings, and length of productive yield
- **Cross-wind rainfall**: Trees positioned across prevailing winds can increase local rainfall
- **Drought-proof stockfeed**: Selected self-forage browse provides medicinal qualities and resilience
- **On-farm timber**: Durable timber for fence posts and construction reduces purchased inputs
- **Reduced carcass loss**: Shelter reduces animal losses from shivering, sweating, and exposure
- **Increased crop production** in sheltered areas
- **Increased carcass weight**: Shaded pasture allows cattle to graze all day in hot weather instead of sheltering from the sun
- **Reduced evaporation**: Less wind over water surfaces from tree shelter

## Market and Processing Strategies

Raw product yields can be multiplied through processing and marketing strategies:

1. **Specialty market selection**: Fresh herbs near restaurants, unusual varieties for ethnic markets
2. **Direct marketing**: Self-pick operations, mail order, direct dispatch, roadside sales—eliminating middlemen
3. **Value-added processing**: Converting seed to oil, fruit to preserves, crude eucalyptus oil to fractions
4. **On-farm fuel production**: Processing fuels on the farm saves money and enables surplus fuel sale

## Social and Financial Strategies

The social dimension of farm strategy is critical for economic sustainability:

1. **Farm-link strategy**: Urban groups contract to buy specific produce, providing market stability
2. **Educational income**: Field days and educational courses generate supplementary revenue
3. **Agri-tourism**: Guest houses and holiday farms provide income from urban visitors
4. **Direct investment**: City people invest directly in specific farms
5. **Local financial systems**: Credit unions and banks recycle money locally
6. **Equipment pooling**: Vehicle and implement pools with neighbours save approximately 90% capital
7. **Labour exchange**: Neighbours share labour during peak periods
8. **Producer cooperatives**: Joint marketing and processing increases bargaining power

## No-Tillage Strategies

Low or no-tillage farming saves multiple resources simultaneously:
- **Energy** in [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]] operations
- **Soil** structure and biology preserved
- **Water** retained through reduced evaporation
- **Time** between crops reduced, enabling more production per year

## Case Study: Norm Sims Farm

From an interview with farmer [[norm-sims]] (Weekly Times, 5 Jan 1983), specific savings from applied site strategies:

On land-forming: "We expect to double production over the next few years, using half the irrigation water" (four times benefit); "Salinity is reduced." In severe drought: "Pasture production has never looked better and water is available." "It took us six days to irrigate what we now do in two" and "Rather than restricting watering intervals we are restricting the area" (aiming to milk 185 cows on 24 hectares).

On grazing rotation and electric fencing: "26 paddocks are grazed in a 21-day rotation" (average field of 1.6 hectares each with a trough water-point for cattle).

Strategies in use:
- Laser levelling of fields for even irrigation
- Water reticulation
- Water storage and recycling
- 21-day grazing rotation
- Central access road
- Concentrated rations crop grown on-farm
- Pasture area reduced to give best watering regime

## The Design vs. Variety Debate

It is obvious from the evidence that primary increases in crop yield do not come primarily from varietal selections—a fiction promoted by agricultural companies, seed patent holders, agricultural researchers, and extension officers. Yield increases come from **attention to site design and development**, followed by wise enterprise selection to suit the (modified) site, concurrently with a marketing and processing strategy.

These are often permanent or durable strategies, so it is not in the commercial interest of input suppliers to encourage them, as the continuous benefit accrues to the farmer alone and the role of middlemen and traders is reduced. In the Western world, the 4-6% of people in essential production are effectively enslaved, while the remaining 96% derive secondary or tertiary benefits without adequate return to primary producers.

## Extending Yields Over Time

The concentration of yields into one short period is a fiscal, not an environmental or subsistence strategy. It has resulted in a "feast and famine" regime in markets and fields, with consequent high storage costs. The permaculture aim is to disperse food yield over time so that many products are available at any season:

- **Variety selection**: Early, mid, and late season varieties
- **Microclimate manipulation**: Planting the same variety in early or late-ripening situations
- **Extended-season varieties**: Selecting cultivars that yield over a long period
- **Increased diversity**: Leaf, fruit, seed, and root products from the same system
- **Self-storing species**: Tubers, hard seeds, fuelwood, and rhizomes cropped on demand
- **[[biodiversity-of-fungi-fungal-culture-preservation-techniques]]**: Preserving, drying, pitting, and cool storage
- **Regional trade**: Utilizing land at different altitudes or latitudes

## Yields and Storage

How yields endure is important, for there are unlimited opportunities to use durable yields in terms of season or lifetime. Food can be stored for days, weeks, or years through preservation strategies. Water not open to evaporation and pollution, or with natural cleansing organisms, will keep indefinitely. Shelters may outlast the forests that build them, or can be made of living or durable materials such as ivy, concrete, or stone.

Energy alone is difficult to store. Batteries leak or decay, heat escapes, and insulation breaks down. Only living things, like forests, increase their energy store. Because of seasonal or diurnal cycles, designers should pay close attention to storage strategies.

## Cultural Impediments to Yield

[[weil-cultural-attitudes-toward-altered-states]] significantly limit the utilization of available yields. In affluent suburban societies, particularly in North America, an almost total zoophobia—fear of anything that moves—has developed. It is very difficult, almost taboo, to talk of using rabbits, quail, pigs, poultry, or cows in city farms or urban gardens in the United States, despite these being commonplace city farm animals in England and ordinary village animals in Asia.

When useful animals are effectively abolished from cities, the ecological niche is filled by less desirable species: pigeons forage the streets, gulls defecate in reservoirs, large dogs tip over garbage bins, and rats proliferate. The refusal to integrate productive animals into human settlements creates the very pest problems that justify the exclusion—a self-reinforcing cycle of dysfunction.
