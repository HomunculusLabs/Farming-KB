# Pollinator Conservation

## Overview

Pollinators—bees, butterflies, moths, flies, beetles, wasps, and hummingbirds—are essential for ecosystem function and food production. Approximately 75% of flowering plants and 35% of global food crops depend on animal pollination. Pollinator populations are declining worldwide due to habitat loss, pesticide exposure, disease, and climate change. Creating and maintaining [[pollinator-habitat]] is one of the most impactful conservation actions a homesteader can take. This connects to [[native-plant-gardening|gardening]], conservation, and [[invasive-species-management]] practices.

## Native Bee Decline

### Scope of the Crisis
Native bee populations are declining across North America:

- The Rusty Patched Bumblebee was listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act in 2017
- Several other bumblebee species have experienced range contractions of 50-90%
- Over 25% of native bee species are at risk of extinction
- Overall pollinator abundance has declined by approximately 40% in many regions over the past two decades

### Causes of Decline

#### Habitat Loss and Fragmentation
- Conversion of natural areas to agriculture and development eliminates nesting and foraging habitat
- Large monoculture fields create "pollinator deserts" with no food outside the brief blooming period of the crop
- Fragmentation isolates pollinator populations, reducing genetic diversity and recolonization potential

#### Pesticide Exposure
- **Neonicotinoids**: Systemic insecticides that persist in pollen and nectar at sub-lethal levels; impair bee navigation, memory, and reproduction; found in 90%+ of agricultural samples tested
- **Broad-spectrum insecticides**: Kill non-target pollinators alongside pests
- **Fungicides**: Some fungicides interact synergistically with insecticides, increasing toxicity
- **Herbicides**: Eliminate floral resources that pollinators depend on

#### Disease and Parasites
- **Varroa mite**: Devastates managed honeybee colonies; may also affect some native bees
- **Nosema**: Gut parasites that reduce bee energy and lifespan
- **Deformed wing virus**: Spread by Varroa mites; causes wing deformities and colony collapse
- **Chalkbrood**: Fungal disease affecting solitary and social bees

#### Climate Change
- Phenological mismatch: flowers bloom earlier due to warming, but bees emerge at their historical timing, missing peak nectar
- Range shifts: some bee species cannot migrate fast enough to track shifting climate zones
- Extreme weather: droughts, heat waves, and late frosts reduce floral resources and nest survival

## Native Bee Diversity

### Types of Native Bees
North America has approximately 4,000 native bee species:

- **Bumblebees (Bombus spp.)**: Large, fuzzy, social bees; excellent pollinators of tomatoes, peppers, and blueberries due to buzz pollination; approximately 50 species in North America
- **Ground-nesting bees (70% of native species)**: Andrena, Colletes, Halictus, and others nest in bare, well-drained soil; include mining bees, sweat bees, and cellophane bees
- **Cavity-nesting bees**: Mason bees (Osmia), leafcutter bees (Megachile), and carpenter bees nest in hollow stems, dead wood, or existing cavities
- **Cellophane bees (Colletidae)**: Line their nests with a cellophane-like secretion; among the earliest spring-emerging bees

### Why Native Bees Matter
Native bees are often more efficient pollinators than honeybees:

- Many native bees are active earlier in the season and in cooler temperatures than honeybees
- Bumblebees perform buzz pollination (sonication) that honeybees cannot, essential for tomatoes, eggplants, and blueberries
- Squash bees (Peponapis) specialize on cucurbit flowers and are more efficient squash pollinators than honeybees
- Native bees visit more flowers per minute than honeybees in many crop systems
- Diverse bee communities provide pollination insurance—if one species fails, others fill the gap

## Habitat Creation

### Floral Resources
Provide continuous bloom from early spring through late fall:

#### Spring Bloomers (March-May)
- Willow (Salix spp.): Early pollen and nectar source; supports emerging queen bumblebees
- Red maple ([[acer-rubrum]]): Early-blooming tree; important for early-emerging bees
- Serviceberry (Amelanchier spp.): White flowers; bee favorite
- Creeping phlox (Phlox subulata): Ground cover; early nectar source
- Wild geranium ([[geranium-maculatum]]): Woodland native; excellent for shade gardens
- Bloodroot ([[sanguinaria-canadensis]]): Early spring ephemeral; native bee resource
- Pussy willow ([[salix-discolor]]): Gray catkins provide early pollen

#### Summer Bloomers (June-August)
- Milkweeds (Asclepias spp.): Monarch butterfly host plant; excellent bee resource
- Purple coneflower ([[echinacea-purpurea]]): Long bloom period; attracts diverse pollinators
- Wild bergamot ([[monarda-fistulosa]]): Long bloom; bee and hummingbird plant
- Black-eyed Susan (Rudbeckia hirta): Easy to grow; self-seeds readily
- Anise hyssop (Agastache foeniculum): Anise-scented; extremely attractive to bees
- Joe-pye weed (Eutrochium purpureum): Tall, showy; butterfly and bee magnet
- Buttonbush (Cephalanthus occidentalis): Wetland shrub; pollinator favorite

#### Fall Bloomers (September-November)
- New England aster (Symphyotrichum novae-angliae): Critical late-season nectar source
- Goldenrod (Solidago spp.): Major fall pollen and nectar source; not allergenic
- Blue mistflower (Conoclinium coelestinum): Late-fall bloomer; butterfly and bee plant
- Aromatic aster (Symphyotrichum oblongifolium): Very late bloomer; drought-tolerant
- Plains coreopsis (Coreopsis tinctoria): Extended bloom through fall
- Boneset (Eupatorium perfoliatum): Late summer-fall; white flowers; medicinal

### Nesting Habitat

#### Ground-Nesting Bee Habitat
Approximately 70% of native bees nest in the ground:

- **Bare soil patches**: Leave areas of bare, well-drained, south-facing soil; avoid heavy mulch over nesting areas
- **Sandy or loamy soil**: Many ground-nesters prefer sandy or well-drained soil
- **Slopes**: South- and east-facing slopes warm earliest in spring; preferred by emerging bees
- **Minimal disturbance**: Avoid tilling, compacting, or heavily mulching ground-nesting areas

#### Cavity-Nesting Bee Habitat
About 30% of native bees nest in above-ground cavities:

- **Dead wood**: Retain standing dead trees (snags) and fallen logs for wood-nesting bees
- **Hollow stems**: Leave perennial plant stems standing through winter; mason bees and leafcutter bees use pithy stems (raspberry, elderberry, sumac)
- **Nesting blocks**: Drill holes (4-8mm diameter, 4-6 inches deep) into untreated wood blocks; hang in a sheltered, south-facing location
- **Bee hotels**: Commercial or homemade structures with tubes, stems, and drilled blocks; must be cleaned or replaced annually to prevent disease buildup

#### Bumblebee Nesting
Bumblebees nest in small cavities near the ground:

- Abandoned rodent burrows are preferred nest sites
- Compost piles, brush piles, and dense grass tussocks provide potential nest sites
- Leave some areas of unmowed, undisturbed vegetation for bumblebee nesting

## Pesticide Reduction

### Integrated Pest Management (IPM)
See [[integrated-pest-management|pest management]] for detailed IPM strategies. Key principles for pollinator protection:

- **Monitor before treating**: Identify pests accurately; only treat when economic thresholds are exceeded
- **Use targeted products**: Choose products that minimize non-target impacts
- **Time applications carefully**: Apply pesticides in the evening when bees are not foraging; avoid application during bloom
- **Avoid systemic insecticides**: Neonicotinoids and other systemic products contaminate pollen and nectar for months after application
- **Spot treat**: Treat only affected plants rather than blanket-spraying entire areas

### Organic Alternatives
- **Insecticidal soap**: Effective against soft-bodied insects; minimal residual toxicity
- **Neem oil**: Broad-spectrum but degrades quickly; avoid spraying on flowers
- **Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt)**: Targeted to specific insect groups; relatively safe for bees
- **Kaolin clay**: Particle film that deters pests without toxicity
- **Horticultural oil**: Smothering agent; apply when bees are not active
- **Biological controls**: Predatory insects, nematodes, and fungal pathogens for specific pests

## Nest Site Protection

### Overwintering Habitat
Many pollinators overwinter in or on the ground:

- Leave fallen leaves in garden beds; many butterflies and moths overwinter as pupae in leaf litter
- Leave perennial stems standing until spring; cavity-nesting bees overwinter in stems
