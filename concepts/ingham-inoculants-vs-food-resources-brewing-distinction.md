# Inoculants vs Food Resources in AACT Brewing

## Overview

A critical distinction in Dr. Elaine Ingham's compost tea methodology is the
difference between inoculants and food resources. Many novice compost tea
brewers confuse these two categories, leading to brews that are rich in
microbial food but poor in microbial diversity. Understanding this distinction
is essential for producing effective [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] compost tea (AACT).

## What Is an Inoculant?

An inoculant is the source of living microorganisms — the "seed" of the compost
tea. The inoculant introduces the diverse community of bacteria, fungi,
protozoa, and nematodes that will multiply during the brewing process and
colonize the soil or leaf surfaces where the tea is applied.

### Compost as the Primary Inoculant

The quality of compost used as inoculant is the single most important factor
determining tea quality. The compost must contain:

- **High bacterial biomass**: At least 100-300 micrograms per gram, representing
  diverse beneficial species.
- **Adequate fungal biomass**: At least 10-100 micrograms per gram for
  general-purpose teas, higher for fungal-dominant teas.
- **[[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]]**: Higher-level organisms that serve as nutrient
  cyclers in the soil food web.
- **No pathogens**: Compost must be properly cured and tested. Ingham
  recommends [[thermal-composting-vs-aact]] above 131°F (55°C) for three days minimum.

### Inoculant Quantity

Typical rates are 1-5 pounds of compost per 50 gallons of tea. More compost
does not necessarily produce better tea; the limiting factor is usually the
food supply during brewing rather than initial inoculant density.

### Alternative Inoculants

- **Vermicompost**: Higher bacterial diversity than thermal compost but lower
  fungal biomass. Good for bacterial-dominant teas.
- **Forest soil**: Useful for fungal-dominant teas, as forests maintain high
  fungal-to-bacterial ratios. Use cautiously due to potential pathogens.
- **Commercial microbial preparations**: Products containing Trichoderma,
  Bacillus, or [[pgpr-mycorrhizal-inoculants-microplant-establishment]] can supplement but not replace compost.

## What Are Food Resources?

Food resources are materials added to feed the microorganisms introduced by the
inoculant. Without adequate food, microorganisms cannot reproduce to sufficient
densities during the 24-hour brewing cycle.

### Bacterial Foods

Simple carbon compounds supporting rapid bacterial reproduction:

- **Molasses**: The most common bacterial food. [[ingham-blackstrap-molasses-compost-tea-concentration-osmotic-effects]] preferred
  for higher mineral content. Typical rate: 1-4 tablespoons per 5 gallons.
- **Fish hydrolysate**: Cold-processed, enzyme-digested fish provides amino
  acids and proteins feeding both bacteria and fungi.
- **Alfalfa meal**: Source of proteins, vitamins, and growth factors supporting
  diverse [[cannabis-endorhiza-bacterial-communities]].

### Fungal Foods

More complex carbon compounds supporting hyphal growth:

- **Humic acids**: Serve as both food and habitat for fungi; chelate minerals
  for fungal metabolism.
- **Rock dust**: Minerals that fungi solubilize through acid secretion; physical
  structure provides hyphal attachment sites.
- **Kelp meal**: Trace minerals particularly support fungal enzyme production.
- **Chitin**: Selectively feeds beneficial fungi and actinobacteria while
  suppressing certain pathogens.

### The Critical Balance

Adding food without inoculant produces a sterile nutrient solution rather than
biologically active tea. Adding inoculant without food produces tea with
limited microbial multiplication. Both components are essential.
