# Trees in Permaculture Design

Trees are the cornerstone species of the ecology. While the
micro-organisms within the soil provide the nutrient cycle which
supports and builds trees, it is the trees which hold in place the
very ecology that creates the life-giving soil. Some consider trees to
be the dominant life form on the planet, and their argument has merit:
the ecology could survive without humans, but neither humans nor the
ecology could survive without trees.

## The Ecological Power of Trees

We all know how trees are constructed: roots, trunk, branches, and
leaves. But what makes trees such ecological powerhouses that they
can shape the planetary atmosphere, create rain, and cause the
evolution of animals across eons? Trees with their branches, leaves,
and roots block wind, create a shade zone under their canopy, clean
water, hold soil and prevent erosion, increase oxygen concentration
around them, and attract animals.

Put all of these effects together and each individual tree creates its
own microclimate. The microclimate of each tree is perfect for
engendering the forces that create healthy soil around the tree. As
discussed in the section on soil, healthy soil is the ecological
engine which creates the biosphere. One of the main ways healthy soil
creates the biosphere is by creating more trees, which in turn create
more healthy soil, on and on in a self-reinforcing cycle.

By growing close together the healthy microclimates of trees overlap,
interact, and resonate, creating an even healthier microclimate that
eventually gives rise to forests. Trees are one of the greatest
examples of stacking of functions in permaculture design one could
find, performing all of these ecological services simultaneously.

## Coniferous and Deciduous Trees

There are basically two types of trees: coniferous and deciduous.
Coniferous trees are pine trees, also called evergreens. They stay
green all year round through summer, fall, and winter. They reproduce
by the use of pine cones and pollen. Instead of leaves their canopies
are made of needles. Because they are evergreen and their canopy is
always full, they provide a consistent windbreak and shade screen
year-round.

Deciduous trees have canopies made of leaves which turn colors and
drop off as the seasons change. They reproduce by pollinated flowers
and then fruits or nuts which drop at certain times, often carried
off by animals to spread offspring over a greater area. Because
deciduous trees drop their leaves, their profile as a windbreak and
shade screen changes with the seasons. During winter months they let
more wind through, let more sunlight through, and transpire less
moisture. This seasonal variation makes deciduous trees valuable for
passive solar design, providing summer shade while allowing winter
sunlight to reach structures.

## Food Production from Trees

In addition to their microclimate effects, trees provide food in the
form of fruits and nuts. However some trees also have edible leaves,
flowers, and even bark. When looking for trees that provide foodstuffs
it is important to open the mind to greater possibilities than the
usual apples, pears, oranges, and such. The full range of edible tree
products is far broader than most people realize.

## Trees in Site Design

Trees can be planted in such a way that their microclimates help
temper the climates around structures, providing shade and windbreaks.
They are the lynch pins of food forests and can be used in areas where
rough soil makes gardening difficult, as trees help prepare the soil
over time. When choosing trees, keep in mind that trees grow. Many a
building foundation has been destroyed because large trees were planted
too close. Choose trees that provide the functions you need and are
native to your site area. The best way to research is to walk through
local woods and forests with keen observation skills.

## Buildings Like Trees

Architect William McDonough went beyond just the concept of using
trees in design and uses trees as his inspiration for design. He
coined the concept of buildings like trees, not in the sense of form
with trunks and leafy canopies, but rather in terms of function. Look
at all that trees do, and imagine if our buildings did the same. What
would the modern world be like if buildings were powered by the Sun,
created healthy microclimates, created healthy soil, cleaned water,
improved air quality, produced food, and provided natural habitat for
diverse species?

This concept aligns with ecological design principles where the
ecological impact and function guide the style and aesthetic of the
design, rather than the client or architect deciding the style
regardless of ecological impact. To make a beautiful permaculture
design, one need only design it to its maximum ecological potential,
pay attention to the quality of the craftsmanship, and the beauty will
present itself.

## Key Principles

- Trees are the cornerstone species: they hold the ecology in place,
  create healthy soil microclimates, and drive the nutrient cycle
  that supports all other life
- Every tree creates its own microclimate: shade, wind reduction,
  water cleaning, soil building, oxygen production, and animal
  attraction all happen simultaneously
- Choose native species: trees that are native to your site area
  provide the functions you need while being adapted to local
  conditions and supporting local ecology
- Account for growth: always research the mature size of trees before
  planting to avoid foundation damage, roof impacts, and branch
  conflicts with structures and power lines
- Deciduous trees provide seasonal variation: shade in summer and
  sunlight penetration in winter make them ideal for passive solar
  integration with buildings

## Applications

- Planting deciduous trees on the south side of structures for
  summer shade and winter solar access in passive solar design
- Using evergreen conifers as year-round windbreaks to protect
  buildings and gardens from prevailing winds
- Selecting native fruit and nut trees that provide food while
  supporting local wildlife and ecological networks
- Placing trees in areas with poor soil to gradually improve soil
  conditions through root activity and leaf litter
- Designing tree plantings that overlap their microclimates to
  create the conditions for forest succession and food forest
  development
