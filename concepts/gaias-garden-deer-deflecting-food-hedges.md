# Deer-Deflecting Food Hedges

In Gaia's Garden, Toby Hemenway demonstrates how a deer problem can be
transformed from a frustrating pest issue into a design opportunity by
creating a deer-deflecting food hedge -- sometimes called a "fedge." This
multifunctional planting both protects the garden from browsing deer and
produces food for people and wildlife, embodying the permaculture principle
that every element should serve multiple functions.

## The Problem: Deer as Garden Invaders

Deer are a major challenge for many gardeners, capable of devouring almost
any unprotected plant. When deer establish regular trails into a yard, they
can quickly destroy years of careful planting. Hemenway observed deer wearing
a well-worn path into his yard from the southwest, making it clear where
defensive action was needed.

## The Fedge Concept

Rather than building an expensive fence, Hemenway placed a dense hedge along
the deer's entry route to deflect them toward less valuable plantings. The
hedge was designed with a dual-face approach:

- **Outside face (deer side):** Planted with wildlife species that deer find
  palatable, providing a food source that satisfies their appetite before
  they reach more valuable garden plants
- **Inside face (house side):** Grafted with domestic fruit varieties,
  providing human food that deer cannot easily reach

This arrangement means the deer browse the outer plants while the inner
plants produce fruit for the gardener. The hedge acts as both a physical
barrier and a sacrificial food zone.

## Species for the Deer-Facing Side

Hemenway selected tough, wildlife-friendly shrubs for the outer face of his
deer-deflecting hedge:

- Manchurian plum (Prunus manshurica)
- Nanking cherry (Prunus tomentosa)
- Wild roses (Rosa spp.)
- Manchurian apricot (Armeniaca mandshurica)
- Buffaloberry (Shepherdia argentea)
- Osage orange (Maclura pomifera)
- Gooseberry (Ribes spp.)
- Currant (Ribes spp.)
- Siberian pea shrub (Caragana arborascens)

These species are vigorous, thorny in some cases, and tolerant of browsing.
They form a dense barrier that deer are reluctant to push through.

## Species for the House-Facing Side

On the protected inner face, Hemenway grafted domestic fruit varieties onto
the wild rootstock:

- Domestic plum cultivars grafted onto wild plum rootstock
- Asian plum varieties grafted onto shrubby apricot stock
- Edible cultivars grafted onto wild cherries
- Berry bushes tucked among the shrubs

This approach uses the wild plants as rootstock and wind protection while
harvesting improved fruit varieties from the sheltered side.

## Soil-Building Integration

Beyond deer defense and food production, Hemenway integrated
[[gaias-garden-nitrogen-fixers-guild-design]] species
directly into the hedge:

- Siberian pea shrub and buffaloberry add nitrogen to the soil through
  their root-associated bacteria
- Deep-rooted species such as chicory, yarrow, and daikon radish pull
  nutrients from subsoil and deposit them on the surface at leaf fall,
  functioning as [[gaias-garden-nutrient-accumulators-dynamic-accumulators]]
- These [[gaias-garden-spike-roots-soil-conditioning]]
  naturally build soil fertility beneath the hedge

## Water Conservation Within the Hedge

Hemenway also addressed water needs by incorporating
[[gaias-garden-mulch-plants-ecological-garden]]:

- Comfrey and cardoon (a thick-leaved artichoke relative) produce abundant
  leaves that can be slashed periodically and left on the ground
- This creates a self-renewing [[gaias-garden-living-mulches-ground-covers]] that holds moisture in the soil
- In southern Oregon's ninety-day dry season, these mulch plants saved
  significant irrigation water
- The result was plump, well-hydrated fruit even during drought periods

## Design Principles Applied

The deer-deflecting food hedge illustrates several key ecological design
principles from [[ecological-gardening-principles]]:

- **Multiple functions:** One hedge provides deer defense, food production,
  nitrogen fixation, nutrient accumulation, mulch generation, and water
  conservation
- **Each function supported by many elements:** Deer defense comes from
  thorny branches, dense growth, and the sacrificial outer face
- **Using biological resources:** The hedge uses living plants instead of
  manufactured fencing materials
- **Turning problems into solutions:** The deer problem became the impetus
  for creating one of the most productive and multifunctional elements in
  the garden

## Connection to Hedgerow Design

The deer-deflecting food hedge is a specialized form of the broader
[[gaias-garden-hedgerows-and-windbreaks]] concept. While traditional
hedgerows focus primarily on windbreak functions, the fedge adds wildlife
feeding, deer deflection, and intensive food production. Both approaches
demonstrate how [[gaias-garden-stacking-functions-detailed]] into a
single planting creates more resilient and productive gardens.

## Practical Tips

- Observe deer movement patterns before placing the hedge; position it
  across their most-used entry routes
- Make the hedge at least 4-6 feet tall and densely planted to be an
  effective barrier
- Include thorny species on the deer side to further discourage penetration
- Plan for the mature width of all species to avoid overcrowding
- Grafting domestic varieties onto wild rootstock is an advanced technique;
  beginners can simply plant domestic fruit shrubs on the protected side
- Combine the fedge with a [[gaias-garden-swales-and-berms-construction]]
  on the downhill side to capture water for the hedge
