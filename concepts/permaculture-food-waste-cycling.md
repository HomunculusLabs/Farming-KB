# Permaculture Food Waste Cycling

Food waste cycling transforms organic waste streams into valuable resources — compost, animal feed, soil amendments, and biogas. In permaculture, "produce no waste" means every output becomes an input for another element. Food waste cycling closes loops between household, garden, and animal systems, reducing external inputs and building soil fertility.

## The Waste Hierarchy

1. **Reduce** — minimize waste through better planning, preservation, and storage
2. **Feed to animals** — direct conversion of food scraps to eggs, meat, and manure
3. **Compost** — biological decomposition into stable humus
4. **Vermicompost** — worm-processed compost, higher in nutrients
5. **Bokashi** — anaerobic fermentation for including meat and dairy
6. **Biogas** — anaerobic digestion producing methane for cooking/heating

## Compost Systems

### Thermophilic Composting
Hot composting achieves temperatures of 130-160°F (55-70°C), killing weed seeds, pathogens, and breaking down material quickly (weeks to months).

**Process:**
- Layer carbon-rich (browns) and nitrogen-rich (greens) materials at roughly 30:1 C:N ratio
- Build piles to minimum 3x3x3 feet (1 cubic meter) for heat retention
- Maintain moisture at 50-60% (like a wrung-out sponge)
- Turn when temperature peaks and begins to drop (every 3-7 days)
- Compost is finished when temperature stabilizes at ambient, material is dark and crumbly

**Carbon sources (browns):** dry leaves, straw, wood chips, cardboard, paper, sawdust
**Nitrogen sources (greens):** kitchen scraps, green plant material, manure, coffee grounds

### Cold Composting
Simple stacking of materials without active management. Slower (6-12 months) but requires less labor. Does not reliably kill weed seeds or pathogens. Suitable for non-critical applications like mulching perennial beds.

### Sheet Composting (Sheet Mulching)
Layer organic materials directly on the soil surface where you want to garden:
1. Mow or cut existing vegetation to ground level
2. Lay down cardboard or newspaper (5-10 sheets thick, overlapping)
3. Add compost or manure layer (2-4 inches)
4. Add browns (leaves, straw) (4-6 inches)
5. Add more greens if available (2-4 inches)
6. Top with mulch (straw, wood chips)
7. Plant directly into the mulch by pulling it aside and adding compost pockets

This method builds soil in place and eliminates the need to transport and spread compost.

## Black Soldier Fly Larvae (BSFL)

Black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) larvae are one of the most efficient biological waste processors available. They can convert food waste (including meat and dairy) into high-quality animal feed at remarkable rates.

### BSFL System Design
- A container with drainage (bucket, bin, or custom unit)
- Food waste added daily to the top
- Larvae self-harvest by migrating upward or into collection containers when ready to pupate
- Residual material (frass) is an excellent soil amendment

### Advantages
- Process waste 2-5 times faster than conventional composting
- Larvae are 40-45% protein, 30-35% fat — excellent poultry and fish feed
- Can handle meat, dairy, and oily foods that traditional composting cannot
- Self-separating harvest — larvae migrate when ready
- Minimal odor when properly managed
- Adult flies do not bite, sting, or spread disease

### Integration
- Feed BSFL to chickens, ducks, or fish in [[permaculture-animal-integration]] systems
- Can reduce poultry feed costs by 30-50% with sufficient waste input
- Frass (larvae castings) adds directly to compost or garden beds
- Systems can be scaled from small countertop units to commercial operations

## Poultry Feed from Waste

Beyond BSFL, several food waste streams can directly feed poultry:

### Kitchen Scraps
Most vegetable and fruit scraps are excellent chicken and duck feed:
- Vegetable peels and trimmings
- Fruit waste (cores, peels, bruised fruit)
- Bread, rice, pasta (in moderation)
- Eggshells (crushed for calcium)
- Dairy products (small amounts for ducks; avoid for chickens)
- Meat scraps (BSFL processing preferred for chickens)

### Garden Waste
- Thinnings from vegetable gardens
- Excess or overripe produce
- Weeds (before seed set)
- [[permaculture-guild-design]] plants like comfrey cut for chicken feed

### Sprouted Grains and Fodder
Sprouting grains increases protein, vitamin, and enzyme content:
- Soak grains (wheat, barley, oats, sunflower seeds) for 8-12 hours
- Drain and rinse daily
- Feed at 3-7 days of growth as fodder

## Bokashi Fermentation

Bokashi is an anaerobic fermentation process originating from Japan that pre-digests organic waste using effective microorganisms (EM).

### Process
1. Add food waste (including meat, dairy, cooked foods) to a sealed bokashi bucket
2. Sprinkle bokashi bran (inoculated with EM) over each layer
3. Press down to exclude air, seal the bucket
4. Drain liquid (bokashi tea) every few days — dilute 1:100 as plant fertilizer
5. After 2-4 weeks, the fermented material is ready for burial or composting
6. Bury in garden soil or add to compost pile; soil organisms finish the breakdown in 2-4 weeks

### Advantages
- Processes all food types including meat, dairy, and cooked foods
- No odors when properly managed (fermentation smell, not rot)
- Fast processing (2-4 weeks in bucket)
- Works indoors or in small spaces
- Bokashi tea is a potent liquid fertilizer
- Fermented material adds beneficial microorganisms to soil

## Closing the Loop

A complete food waste cycling system integrates multiple methods:

1. Kitchen scraps sorted by type
2. Meat/dairy/oils to bokashi or BSFL
3. Vegetable scraps to compost or direct poultry feed
4. Garden waste to compost, mulch, or poultry forage
5. Compost and frass to garden beds
6. Bokashi tea as liquid fertilizer
7. Poultry manure and bedding back to compost
8. Eggs and produce from garden and animals feed back to the household

This creates a nearly closed nutrient cycle, dramatically reducing external inputs and waste sent to landfill.
