# Starting a New Garden: Killing Sod and Preparing Soil

Converting a grass-covered area into productive [[aphid-management-vegetable-garden-solomon]] beds is one of the
most physically demanding but foundational tasks in food gardening. [[steve-solomon]]
covers two primary methods — **rotary cultivation** and **hand-digging** — each with
specific techniques, timing considerations, and realistic expectations about the
effort involved.

## The Ready-to-Till Moisture Test

Before any tilling or digging, the soil must be at the right moisture content.
Working soil that is too wet destroys structure; working soil that is too dry
wastes energy. The ready-to-till test is a simple hands-on check:

1. **Grab a handful of soil** from a few inches below the surface.
2. **Squeeze it into a ball** in your fist.
3. **Press the ball with your thumb.**

**Interpreting the results:**

| Result | Meaning | Action |
|--------|---------|--------|
| Ball **crumbles** when pressed | Soil moisture is ideal — ready to till | Proceed |
| Ball is **gooey or sticks** to your thumb | Too wet — soil will form hard clods later | Wait for drainage/drying |
| Soil **won't form a ball at all** | No clay content — won't form problematic clods | Can till at wider moisture range |

This test matters because tilling wet clay-loam soils creates large, rock-hard
clods that persist through the entire [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] resist breaking down
with subsequent cultivation passes.

## Killing Sod by Rotary Cultivation

Rotary cultivation uses a powered [[rototiller]] to kill existing grass and begin
incorporating it into the soil. This is faster than hand-digging but requires
careful technique to be effective.

### Equipment Selection: Front-Tine Only

- Use a **front-tine rototiller** exclusively for sod-killing work.
- **Never use a rear-tine tiller** for this purpose — rear-tine machines
  bury the grass too deeply, where it can survive and regenerate.

### The Shallow-First-Pass Technique

The core principle is to start shallow and gradually deepen across multiple
sessions:

1. **Set tines to ~1 inch depth** for the first pass.
2. **Work a single row repeatedly** — go back and forth over the same strip
   multiple times before moving laterally to the next row. Do not try to cover
   the whole area in one shallow pass.
3. **Wait 3–7 days** after the first session. This waiting period is critical:
   it allows the severed grass roots and crowns to dry out and die in the sun
   and air. Tilling again too soon simply re-buries living grass.
4. **Make a second pass** at the same or slightly greater depth after the wait.
5. **Cross-till** by running the machine perpendicular to the first direction
   (e.g., if the first session was east-west, the second should be north-south).
6. **Repeat the cycle** of tilling and waiting until the sod is fully destroyed
   and incorporated.

### The 5-Inch Depth Limit

Walk-behind tillers of any type — front-tine or rear-tine — only effectively
loosen soil to approximately **5 inches deep**, regardless of how deeply the
tines are set. This is a hard physical limit of the machines. Deeper tine
settings mostly cause the tiller to bounce or walk forward without penetrating
further.

## Plow Pans: The Hidden Problem

On land that was previously farmed (even decades ago), a **[[plow-pan]]** may exist
at approximately **7 inches below the surface**. Plow pans are compacted layers
of soil created by the repeated passage of moldboard plow shares at a consistent
depth. Key characteristics:

- They persist for **decades** after the land has been abandoned from
  conventional agriculture.
- They are **extremely compacted** — far denser than the soil above or below.
- They **block root penetration**, confining vegetable roots to the top 5–7
  inches and severely limiting [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] and [[ericoid-mycorrhizae-organic-nutrient-access]].
- Walk-behind tillers **cannot break through** a plow pan — the 5-inch depth
  limit means they never reach it.

Plow pans must be broken up by **deep digging ([[double-digging]])** or subsoiling
equipment. This is one of the strongest arguments for thorough hand-digging of
new garden beds, especially on former farmland.

## Hand-Digging Sod

Hand-digging is slower but gives the gardener complete control over depth and
soil handling. It is the only practical way to address plow pans and break up
compaction below the tiller's reach.

### The Chunk-and-Flip Technique

The basic method for hand-removing sod:

1. **Drive the spade vertically** through the sod and into the soil below.
2. **Lift the chunk** of sod and soil together.
3. **Turn it completely upside down**, grass-side down, and drop it back in
   place.

This buries the grass where it cannot photosynthesize and will decompose. The
soil that was beneath the grass is now on the surface, ready for further
refinement.

### Dividing the Garden into Manageable Beds

- Split the total garden area into **100 square foot beds** (e.g., 5 feet wide
  by 20 feet long, or 4 × 25 foot strips).
- This division makes the work psychologically manageable and allows you to
  track progress.
- **Work one bed at a time**, completing each before moving to the next.

### Nibbling: The Systematic Approach

Rather than trying to dig an entire bed-width in each spade stroke:

- **Nibble small bits** — advance the spade just one blade-width at a time
  along the row.
- Work **systematically across the bed**, establishing a rhythm of lift-flip-drop
  and moving forward steadily.
- This approach is far less exhausting than attempting large, ambitious digs
  and produces more consistent results.

## Timing: Autumn vs. Spring

**[[solomon-autumn-digging-new-garden-bed-preparation]] is strongly preferred** over spring for starting a new garden:

- Any weed seeds that germinate after the initial digging are **killed by
  winter frost** before they can establish.
- The soil settles and mellows over winter, producing a finer seedbed by
  spring.
- [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] (inverted sod) begins decomposing during the off-season.
- Spring work is reduced to light raking and planting rather than heavy sod
  removal.

Spring digging is acceptable but means the gardener must contend with actively
growing weeds alongside the vegetable crop, and the soil may not be as well
structured for early plantings.
