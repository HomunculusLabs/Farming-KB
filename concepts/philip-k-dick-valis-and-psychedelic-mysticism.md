# Philip K. Dick, VALIS, and Psychedelic Mysticism

Philip K. Dick (1928-1982) is best known through the films Blade Runner and Total Recall, which were based on his writings.

Less well known is the fact that Dick spent the last decade of his life struggling to come to terms with a series of visionary mystical experiences that fundamentally transformed his understanding of reality.

These experiences, though not directly induced by psychedelic substances, share remarkable features with the [[psilocybin-and-mystical-experience]] and provide important philosophical material for understanding the nature of the Other encountered in altered states of consciousness.

## The Visionary Experiences of 1974

In February and March of 1974, Philip K. Dick underwent a series of what he called theophanies, direct encounters with what he perceived as a vast cosmic intelligence.

The experiences began when a mysterious woman delivered a prescription painkiller to his door.

Dick noticed a gleaming gold fish pendant she wore, which he described as seeming to emit a pink beam of light that transmitted information directly into his consciousness.

This initial experience triggered a cascade of further visionary episodes lasting approximately two months, during which Dick felt as if a cosmic intelligence was communicating with him directly, as if a deity were online and divulging secret information.

The impact was so profound that Dick chose to incorporate the thematic content of these experiences into a number of novels as well as an eight-thousand-page exegesis in which he attempted to make philosophical sense of what had happened to him.

## VALIS: Vast Active Living Intelligence System

Dick's most sustained exploration of his visionary experiences appears in his novel VALIS (1981), an acronym for Vast Active Living Intelligence System.

VALIS is described as a hidden entity of immense power and sentience that is in the process of infiltrating our reality by establishing communications with certain individuals.

These communications are experienced as theophany, moments of overwhelming revelation in which the nature of reality is suddenly and dramatically disclosed.

In the novel, VALIS is essentially outside of our dimension but able to penetrate our world, revealing itself through mystical experience and through the unlikely juxtaposition of meaningfully related events, what the psychologist Carl Jung called synchronicity.

## The Ubik Connection

Dick's earlier novel Ubik (1969) provides another important resource for understanding his views on the nature of reality and consciousness.

In Ubik, the main characters are killed in an explosion at the beginning of the story and then placed in a collective suspended animation machine that keeps some of their brain processes functioning.

In this half-life state, the characters experience a simulated reality so convincing that they fail to realize they are no longer in the real world.

The novel explores what happens when someone outside of this simulated reality attempts to communicate with those inside it.

Communication from the outside manifests as eerie sequences of synchronistic events within the simulation: significant messages appear on washroom mirrors, turn up on matchbook labels, and interrupt television shows.

This narrative device captures Dick's view of how a transcendent intelligence might communicate with beings embedded within the reality process: not through dramatic supernatural intervention but through subtle, meaningful coincidences that gradually reveal the presence of a deeper order.

## Three Options for the Other

Powell uses Dick's fiction as a springboard for considering three possible explanations for the intelligent Other encountered in psychedelic and mystical states.

The first option is that the intelligence exists outside of the normal dimensions of reality, like a programmer standing outside a computer system.

This corresponds to Dick's VALIS concept, in which the cosmic intelligence is located in some other dimension but can penetrate our world.

The second option is that the Other represents some extremely advanced extraterrestrial intelligence existing elsewhere in the Universe.

This corresponds to SETI-type scenarios in which an advanced civilization communicates across vast distances of space.

The third option, which Powell favors, is that the Universe is organism-like, such that the intelligence exists throughout Nature itself.

In this view, the Other is not outside the system but is immanent within it, an inherent property of the informational fabric of reality that becomes perceptible when consciousness is sufficiently expanded.

## Temporal Lobe Epilepsy and the Reductionist Trap

Since Dick's death, it has been speculated that he suffered from temporal lobe epilepsy, a brain disorder that can produce hallucinatory experiences resembling mystical states.

Powell argues that this diagnosis, even if accurate, does not explain away the reality of Dick's experiences.

To label an experience in order to explain it away is to avoid the very real nature of the mystical experience however it should arise.

We should not be surprised if unusual neuronal activity is concurrent with a mystical experience, for altered forms of awareness demand altered brain processes.

Such changes in brain state can be achieved through many different means, whether through [[psilocybin-compound-profile]], endogenous DMT, meditation, fasting, or spontaneous epileptic disturbances.

The phenomenological content of the experience, its meaning, coherence, and transformative power, cannot be reduced to the neurological conditions that made it possible, any more than the meaning of a book can be reduced to the chemistry of the ink and paper on which it is printed.

## The Infinite Regress Problem

One of the strongest arguments against the outside-the-system model of the Other is the infinite regress problem.

If the cosmic intelligence exists outside of our reality, then what lies outside of it?

And what lies outside of that?

The chain of outsides can be continued indefinitely, rendering the theory ungrounded and unverifiable.

Powell argues that this dilemma leads to the conclusion that the Other must be found within the Universal Computation rather than outside it.

If we restrict ourselves to this one Universe, then our theoretical model is constrained and bounded, making it more amenable to a single holistic explanation.

This does not deny the reality of Dick's VALIS but rather locates it within our reality, as an inherent property of the informational fabric of Nature that becomes accessible to consciousness under appropriate conditions.

## Legacy and Relevance

Dick's later work remains deeply relevant to contemporary discussions about the nature of consciousness, reality, and the [[psilocybin-and-mystical-experience]].

His fiction demonstrates that the questions raised by mystical and psychedelic experience cannot be contained within conventional scientific materialism, yet also resist easy accommodation within traditional religious frameworks.

The experience of reality as fundamentally strange, permeable, and alive with meaning that characterizes both Dick's fiction and the psychedelic state points toward a understanding of consciousness and reality that our current conceptual frameworks are still struggling to articulate.
