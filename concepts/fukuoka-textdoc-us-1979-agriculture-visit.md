## Desertification Through Livestock Grazing

Because meat is the food staple in America, agriculture is dominated by livestock
farming. Fukuoka observed that grazing had destroyed the ecology of natural
grasses, devastating the land. He watched this process unfolding with his own
eyes and could hardly believe what he saw. Land that has lost its fertility
becomes barren of nature's strength, which accounts for the development of a
modern agriculture totally reliant on petroleum energy.

Fukuoka's observation connected the American experience to a global pattern: the
expansion of livestock grazing inevitably leads to soil degradation, which in turn
requires ever greater energy inputs to maintain agricultural productivity. This
creates a negative feedback loop in which the land's productive capacity steadily
declines while the energy required to extract crops from it increases.

## The Cycle of Large-Scale Mechanization

The low productivity of degraded land drives farmers to expand their operations.
Large operations require mechanization with machinery of increasing size. Fukuoka
called this machinery "big iron" and noted that it breaks down the structure of
the soil, setting up a negative cycle. Agriculture that ignores the forces of
nature and relies solely on the human intellect and human effort is ultimately
unprofitable. It was inevitable, in Fukuoka's view, that crops produced with the
help of petroleum would be transformed into a strategic commodity for securing
cheap oil.

He noted the absurdity of this arrangement: the farmer becomes a subcontractor to
the oil industry, and control over his livelihood passes into the hands of the
industrialist and businessman. The merchant has the last say over the farmer's
right to loss or gain, life or death. This is not a system that serves either the
farmer or the consumer, but only the middlemen and energy producers.

## Fragility of Commercial Monoculture

To illustrate just how fragile [[night-break-photoperiodism-manipulation-commercial-agriculture]] is with its large-scale,
subcontractor-type monoculture farming, Fukuoka pointed to the comparison between
American and Japanese farm economics. U.S. farmers working 500 to 700 acres have
smaller net incomes than Japanese farmers on 3 to 5 acres. Despite the enormous
scale of American operations, the costs of mechanization, chemical inputs, and
petroleum energy consume virtually all of the revenue generated by the crops.

This observation directly contradicts the common assumption that larger farms are
inherently more efficient or more economically stable. Fukuoka saw that the
apparent efficiency of large-scale [[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]], the cost of producing rice is almost the
same as the cost of wheat production, and both can be produced more cheaply this
way than buying imported grain. The mechanism by which the market price of rice
is set has nothing whatsoever to do with farmers or the actual cost of production.
The retail price of farm produce is high in Japan because the costs of
distribution are too high—five times those in the United States and twice as high
as in West Germany.

## Philosophical Roots of Agricultural Destruction

Fukuoka concluded that the faults of modern farming were rooted in the basic
illusions of Western philosophy that support the foundations of scientific
agriculture. He saw that mistaken ideology had led man astray in how he lived his
life and secured his essentials of food, clothing, and shelter. He noted that
confusion over food had bred confusion over farming, which had destroyed nature.
And he understood also that the destruction of nature had enfeebled man and
thrown the world into disarray.

This diagnosis is central to Fukuoka's worldview. He did not see the problems of
modern agriculture as primarily technical or economic, but as philosophical. The
Western belief in a man-nature dichotomy, in which man stands apart from and
opposes nature, leads inevitably to an agriculture based on conquest and
control. The Eastern view, in which man seeks to become one with nature, leads
instead to an agriculture based on cooperation and humility.

## Comparison with Japanese Agriculture

Fukuoka drew a direct connection between the destruction he witnessed in America
and the trajectory of Japanese agriculture. In Japan, the transition to
petroleum-based agriculture had already transformed farming from an activity
conducted under the open skies into the manufacture of petroleum-derived foods.
The farmer had become a seller of false goods called "nutritional food."

Statistics told Fukuoka that the average American farmer feeds one hundred people
and the average Japanese farmer only ten, but he argued that Japanese farmers
actually have a higher productivity than American farmers. The difference only
appears to favor American farmers because they farm under much better conditions
in terms of land area, while Japanese farmers work smaller plots more
intensively. The comparison, in his view, was misleading because it measured
output per farmer rather than output per unit of energy input or per unit of
land degradation.

## The Illusion of Agricultural Progress

Fukuoka's U.S. visit reinforced his belief that what is called agricultural
progress is actually a march toward destruction. He had predicted forty years
earlier that the age of centrifugal expansion fed by the growing material desires
of man, the era of rampant modern science, would soon pass and be replaced by a
period of contraction and convergence as man sought to improve his spiritual life.
By 1979, he acknowledged that he had been wrong—the expansion had continued
unabated.

He noted that even [[coleman-deep-organic-farming]], which had come into its own with the pollution
problem, only served as a temporary stopgap, a brief respite. Being essentially a
rehashing of the animal-based [[jadam-nutrient-cycling-traditional-farming-mineral-science]] of the past, and being part and
parcel [[fukuoka-critique-of-scientific-agriculture]] to begin with, organic farming would eventually
be swallowed whole and assimilated by [[critique-of-scientific-agriculture]]. The only true
alternative, in Fukuoka's view, was the complete reorientation of agriculture
toward [[beneficial-indigenous-microorganisms-natural-farming]] based on the philosophy of Mu.

## Global Significance

The experience of visiting the United States solidified Fukuoka's determination
to spread natural farming beyond Japan. The errors he identified in American
agriculture—dependence on petroleum energy, soil destruction through overgrazing
and mechanization, the illusion of cheap food produced at vast scale—were the
same errors being replicated throughout the developed and developing world.
