# Fungal Enzyme Systems for Bioremediation

The biochemical machinery that enables fungi to break down some of the most recalcitrant molecules on Earth. From [[paul-stamets]]" *Mycelium Running* (2005).

## Lignin: Nature's Most Recalcitrant Molecule

Wood's main structural fiber, lignin, is one of the most resistant molecules manufactured by nature. Its long-chained carbon-hydrogen design rebuffs most enzymes. However, mycelial enzymes are uniquely equipped to degrade lignin into shorter-chained molecules.

This same enzymatic versatility allows fungi to degrade man-made compounds specifically designed to resist biological breakdown — including wood preservatives, pesticides, and chemical warfare agents.

## The Three Major Enzyme Systems

### 1. Lignin Peroxidase (LiP)

- **Produced by**: White-rot fungi (primarily Phanerochaete chrysosporium)
- **Mechanism**: Generates highly reactive free radicals that attack aromatic rings
- **Targets**: Lignin, PAHs, PCBs, dioxins, some pesticides
- **Key feature**: Can cleave carbon-carbon bonds in aromatic structures
- **Patent**: Aust & Bumpus, U.S. Patent 4,891,320 (1990)

### 2. Manganese Peroxidase (MnP)

- **Produced by**: White-rot fungi (Phanerochaete sordida, Phanerochaete chrysosporium, many others)
- **Mechanism**: Oxidizes Mn(II) to Mn(III), creating a powerful diffusible oxidant
- **Targets**: Lignin, phenolic compounds, petrochemicals, dyes
- **Key feature**: Mn(III) chelates can diffuse away from the mycelium, extending the oxidative reach
- **Patent**: Aust et al., multiple patents (1995)

### 3. Laccase

- **Produced by**: White-rot fungi (Trametes versicolor, Pleurotus spp.) and many other fungi
- **Mechanism**: Four-copper oxidase that reduces oxygen to water while oxidizing phenolic substrates
- **Targets**: Phenolic compounds, PAHs, dyes, pesticides
- **Key feature**: Does not require hydrogen peroxide (unlike LiP and MnP)
- **Also produced by**: Mushroom mycelium as a natural defense — peroxidases that break down plant fibers AND kill foreign spores

## Additional Enzymes

### Quinone Reductases
- **Produced by**: Brown-rot fungi (Gloeophyllum trabeum)
- **Targets**: Wood preservatives, quinone-based compounds
- **Key feature**: Powerful enough to consume many commercial wood preservatives

### Organomercury Lyases
- **Produced by**: Some fungi (species not fully specified)
- **Targets**: Organomercury compounds
- **Mechanism**: Breaks down organomercury into HgO via mercuric reductase
- **Key feature**: Once freed, mercury can bond with selenium for sequestration

### Chitin-Dissolving Enzymes
- **Produced by**: Entomopathogenic fungi (Metarhizium, Beauveria, Cordyceps)
- **Targets**: Insect exoskeletons
- **Mechanism**: Dissolves chitin in insect cuticle, allowing hyphal penetration
- **Application**: [[mycopesticides]]

### Cellulases
- **Produced by**: Nearly all wood-decomposing fungi
- **Targets**: Cellulose (the most abundant organic polymer on Earth)
- **Mechanism**: Breaks beta-1,4-glycosidic bonds in cellulose chains
- **Role in remediation**: Degrades cellulosic contaminants, releases bound pollutants

## Enzymatic Nonspecificity: The Key to Bioremediation

The critical insight from [[fungal-ecology]]: fungal enzymes are not highly specific like pharmaceutical drugs. They are "promiscuous catalysts" — the same enzymes that evolved to decompose lignin can also oxidize a wide range of synthetic compounds:

- Lignin peroxidase → breaks down aromatic rings in both lignin and PAHs/PCBs
- Manganese peroxidase → oxidizes both natural phenolics and synthetic phenolic compounds
- Laccase → degrades both natural and synthetic dyes, pesticides, and phenols

This nonspecificity is what makes fungi such powerful bioremediators.

## Toxin Classification by Fungal Degradation

Stamets provides a chart matching mushroom species to toxin classes:

### Classes of Toxins Degradeable by Fungi

1. **Pentachlorophenol (PCP)** and other chlorophenols — wood preservatives
2. **Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs)** — industrial coolants/insulators
3. **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)** — petroleum byproducts
4. **BTEX** — benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene — petroleum components
5. **Organophosphates** — pesticides, chemical warfare agents
6. **PAHs in creosote** — railroad ties, utility poles
7. **Heavy metals** — via bioaccumulation, not enzymatic degradation
8. **E. coli and other bacteria** — via antibacterial compounds

## Strain Training for Targeted Remediation

A technique developed by [[paul-stamets]] and tested at Battelle:

1. Start with a library of aggressive mushroom strains (Stamets provided 26 strains to Battelle)
2. Introduce nonnutritive agar with a sample of the target substrate (wood or toxin)
3. Select strains that show faster growth and diverging fans of running mycelium
4. Incrementally replace natural nutrients with the target toxin
5. Gradually increase toxin concentration while decreasing other food sources
6. Continue until the toxin becomes the sole source of nutrition
7. The adapted strain's enzymatic pathways have been redirected to metabolize the toxin

This technique successfully produced strains capable of neutralizing surrogates of sarin, soman, and VX nerve agents.

## The Fungal-Bacterial Partnership

[[fungal-decomposition]] works best as a tandem approach:

- **Fungi** break down large, recalcitrant molecules (high molecular weight) using extracellular enzymes
- **Bacteria** feast on the smaller fragments produced by fungal activity
- **Mycelium** provides a nurturing food source for toxin-tolerant bacteria
- **Bacteria** are better at degrading toxins with smaller molecular weights
- **Together**: They constitute the biological pumps of the carbon-nitrogen cycle

Mycelium fosters specific microbial communities through its antibiotic secretions. The resident bacteria it harbors can stifle growth of competing bacteria, creating a synergistic partnership.
