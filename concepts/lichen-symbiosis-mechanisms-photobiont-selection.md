# Lichen Symbiosis Mechanisms and Photobiont Selection

Lichens are classic examples of mutualistic symbiosis between fungi (mycobionts) and photosynthetic partners (photobionts). Unlike most other fungal-plant symbioses, lichen associations result in the formation of a unique thallus structure that is morphologically, physiologically, and ecologically distinct from either partner alone. The mechanisms governing partner selection, thallus development, and mutual benefit have been the subject of intensive study.

## The Symbiotic Partnership

A lichen is an association between an alga and a fungus in which the two organisms jointly form a thallus distinct from either partner:

- **Mycobiont (fungal partner)**: The fungus typically forms the majority of the lichen's bulk and determines the lichen's external morphology. Lichen fungi are typically members of the Ascomycota (ascolichens), and rarely the Basidiomycota (basidiolichens). Neither ascolichens nor basidiolichens form monophyletic lineages within their respective phyla but form several major lichen-forming groups (Lutzoni et al. 2004).

- **Photobiont (photosynthetic partner)**: The photobiont provides carbon through photosynthesis. It may be a green alga (Chlorophyta), a yellow-green alga (Xanthophyta), or a cyanobacterium (blue-green alga). Common photobiont genera include Trebouxia, Trentepohlia, and Nostoc.

- **Cyanobacterial dual function**: When the photobiont is a cyanobacterium (e.g., Anabaena, Nostoc), it performs both photosynthesis and nitrogen fixation, providing the lichen with both carbon and fixed nitrogen. In three-member associations containing both a green alga and a cyanobacterium, the cyanobacterium produces more heterocysts (nitrogen-fixing cells) than in the free-living condition, indicating specialization toward nitrogen fixation.

## Photobiont Selection Mechanisms

The process by which lichen fungi select and maintain their photobiont partners involves several levels of specificity:

- **Specificity spectrum**: Some lichen fungi are highly specific, consistently associating with a single photobiont species or even a specific genotype. Others are generalists, capable of forming associations with multiple photobiont species. The degree of specificity varies among lichen taxa and may reflect different evolutionary strategies.

- **Pre-contact recognition**: Fungal hyphae may detect photobiont-specific chemical signals before physical contact, though the molecular mechanisms of this recognition are not fully characterized.

- **Compatibility testing**: Upon contact, the fungus assesses photobiont compatibility through surface interactions that may involve lectin-carbohydrate binding or other molecular recognition systems. Incompatible photobionts are rejected before full thallus development.

- **Post-contact selection**: Within a developing thallus, the fungus may selectively retain compatible photobiont cells while eliminating incompatible ones, ensuring that only functional partnerships are maintained.

## Comparison with Geosiphon: An Endocytobiotic Analog

The comparison between lichen symbiosis and the Geosiphon-Nostoc symbiosis illuminates the range of fungal-algal partnership strategies:

- **Lichens**: The photobiont lives outside the fungal cell; contact occurs between the two partners. Vegetative reproduction occurs through soredia and isidia that spread both partners together. Lichens are robust toward dehydration and resistant to high temperatures and high light irradiance.

- **Geosiphon**: Represents an endocytobiotic consortium with the photobiont living inside the fungal cell. Vegetative propagation of the entire symbiotic system does not exist. Geosiphon does not survive water loss and is very sensitive to high temperature and grows only in moderate light (Kluge and Schubler 2002).

## Thallus Organization and Metabolite Exchange

The lichen thallus is organized to optimize metabolite exchange between partners:

- **Mycobiont dominance**: The fungal partner typically constitutes 80-90% of the thallus biomass, creating a physical structure that protects the photobiont while maximizing light capture and gas exchange.

- **Carbon flow**: The photobiont transfers 20-80% of its photosynthetically fixed carbon to the mycobiont as sugar alcohols (mainly ribitol in green algae, glucose in cyanobacteria). The fungus converts these to mannitol and other storage compounds.

- **Water and mineral relations**: The mycobiont's hyphal structure creates a highly efficient water-harvesting system, absorbing atmospheric moisture and dissolved minerals. The poikilohydric nature of lichens allows them to survive extreme desiccation.

- **Growth rate**: Lichens are extremely slow-growing, showing about 1 mm of radial growth per year, but are highly tolerant to xeric conditions such as desiccation and heat.

## Sensitivity to Environmental Pollution

Lichens are among the most sensitive organisms to atmospheric pollution, particularly sulfur dioxide and heavy metals:

- **Bioindicators**: The absence of lichens near human habitations where automobile fumes and factory effluents prevail has made them important bioindicators of air quality.

- **Pollution mechanisms**: Sulfur dioxide disrupts photosynthesis in the photobiont, while heavy metals accumulate in the thallus and interfere with cellular processes.

- **Nitrogen sensitivity**: Paradoxically, some lichens are sensitive to elevated nitrogen deposition, which can disrupt the carefully balanced nutrient exchange between partners.

## Evolution of Fungal-Algal Mutualism

The repeated independent evolution of lichen symbiosis across multiple fungal lineages suggests that the partnership confers strong selective advantages:

- **Multiple origins**: Lichen-forming ability has evolved independently at least 10-15 times within the Ascomycota and multiple times within the Basidiomycota, indicating that the fungal-algal partnership is an evolutionarily accessible strategy.

- **Ancient origin**: Fossil evidence from the Rhynie Chert (Early Devonian, ~400 million years ago) suggests that lichen-like associations were among the earliest terrestrial symbioses.

- **Genomic insights**: The first sequenced genome of Laccaria bicolor (Martin et al. 2008) and subsequent fungal genomes have revealed genes involved in symbiotic communication that may have ancient origins predating the evolution of lichen symbiosis.

## Lichen Growth Forms and Ecological Adaptation

Lichen growth forms reflect adaptations to different environmental conditions. Crustose lichens grow tightly adhered to the substrate, making them resistant to desiccation and physical disturbance. Foliose lichens have leaf-like lobes that increase photosynthetic surface area but are more vulnerable to mechanical damage. Fruticose lichens are shrub-like with three-dimensional branching that maximizes light capture and moisture interception.

Each growth form represents a different trade-off between productivity, stress tolerance, and dispersal ability. The mycobiont controls growth form development, with the same fungal species producing consistent morphology regardless of which photobiont it associates with.

## Secondary Metabolites in Lichen Ecology

Lichens produce a remarkable diversity of secondary metabolites (often called "lichen acids") that serve multiple ecological functions. These compounds include depsides, depsidones, dibenzofurans, and usnic acid, many of which are found almost exclusively in lichens. Functions include:

- **Anti-herbivore defense**: Many lichen acids are bitter or toxic to invertebrate and vertebrate herbivores.

- **Antimicrobial activity**: Lichen acids inhibit bacterial and fungal growth, protecting the lichen thallus from pathogen attack.

- **Metal chelation**: Some lichen compounds chelate metal ions, potentially playing a role in mineral nutrition and metal tolerance.

- **UV protection**: Pigmented lichen compounds absorb ultraviolet radiation, protecting both the photobiont and the mycobiont from DNA damage.

- **Water retention**: Hydrophobic compounds on the thallus surface influence water absorption and retention patterns.

## Comparison with Other Fungal Symbioses

Lichen symbiosis represents one of several major [[fungal-symbiosis-types]] in nature. Unlike [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] that form associations with plant roots for nutrient exchange, or [[endophytic-fungi]] that inhabit plant tissues internally, lichen fungi create an entirely new organismal structure with their photosynthetic partners. The study of lichen symbiosis mechanisms contributes to our broader understanding of [[fungal-symbiosis-with-plants-beyond-mycorrhizae]].
