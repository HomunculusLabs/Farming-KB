## Lichen Symbiosis Mechanisms and Photobiont Selection

Lichens are classic examples of mutualistic symbiosis between fungi
(mycobionts) and photosynthetic partners (photobionts). Unlike most other
fungal-plant symbioses, lichen associations result in the formation of a
unique thallus structure that is morphologically, physiologically, and
ecologically distinct from either partner alone. The mechanisms governing
partner selection, thallus development, and mutual benefit have been the
subject of intensive study.

## The Symbiotic Partnership

A lichen is an association between an alga and a fungus in which the two
organisms jointly form a thallus distinct from either partner:

- **Mycobiont (fungal partner)**: The fungus typically forms the majority of
  the lichen's bulk and determines the lichen's external morphology. Lichen
  fungi are typically members of the Ascomycota (ascolichens), and rarely the
  Basidiomycota (basidiolichens). Neither ascolichens nor basidiolichens form
  monophyletic lineages within their respective phyla but form several major
  lichen-forming groups (Lutzoni et al. 2004).

- **Photobiont (photosynthetic partner)**: The photobiont provides carbon
  through photosynthesis. It may be a green alga (Chlorophyta), a yellow-green
  alga (Xanthophyta), or a cyanobacterium (blue-green alga). Common
  photobiont genera include Trebouxia, Trentepohlia, and Nostoc.

- **Cyanobacterial dual function**: When the photobiont is a cyanobacterium
  (e.g., Anabaena, Nostoc), it performs both photosynthesis and nitrogen
  fixation, providing the lichen with both carbon and fixed nitrogen. In
  three-member associations containing both a green alga and a cyanobacterium,
  the cyanobacterium produces more heterocysts (nitrogen-fixing cells) than
  in the free-living condition, indicating specialization toward nitrogen
  fixation.

## Photobiont Selection Mechanisms

The process by which lichen fungi select and maintain their photobiont
partners involves several levels of specificity:

- **Specificity spectrum**: Some lichen fungi are highly specific, consistently
  associating with a single photobiont species or even a specific genotype.
  Others are generalists, capable of forming associations with multiple
  photobiont species. The degree of specificity varies among lichen taxa and
  may reflect different evolutionary strategies.

- **Pre-contact recognition**: [[query-how-do-plants-use-chemical-signals-to-defend-against-insects]] before physical contact, though the molecular mechanisms of
  this recognition are not fully characterized.

- **Compatibility testing**: Upon contact, the fungus assesses photobiont
  compatibility through surface interactions that may involve lectin-
  carbohydrate binding or other molecular recognition systems. Incompatible
  photobionts are rejected before full thallus development.

- **Post-contact selection**: Within a developing thallus, the fungus may
  selectively retain compatible photobiont cells while eliminating
  incompatible ones, ensuring that only functional partnerships are maintained.
