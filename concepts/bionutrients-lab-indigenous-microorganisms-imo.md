# Bionutrients Lab — Indigenous Microorganisms (IMO)

**Updated:** 2026-04-24

Indigenous Microorganisms (IMO) are beneficial microorganisms collected from local environments — primarily forests, bamboo groves, and specific plant rhizospheres — and cultured for use in agriculture. The core principle is that locally adapted microorganisms are more effective than imported or commercial cultures because they are already suited to local soil, climate, and ecological conditions. As described by Gil Carandang, IMO forms the foundation of the Beneficial Indigenous Microorganism (BIM) formulation alongside lactic acid bacteria.

## Why Indigenous Microorganisms

The philosophy behind IMO is rooted in self-reliance and ecological adaptation:

- Starter cultures of beneficial microorganisms have been available commercially, but they remain purchased inputs under someone else's control
- Local, indigenous microorganisms are already adapted to the specific environment
- Farmers can collect, culture, and multiply these microbes themselves, eliminating dependence on purchased biological inputs
- Greater microbial diversity from multiple local sources creates more resilient soil ecologies
- The goal is to "recover the spirit of farmers" and their "independent authority" over farming techniques (per Dr. Han Kyu Cho)

## Forest Beneficial Microorganisms

The primary method for collecting diverse, aged beneficial microorganisms is from local forest environments.

### Site Selection

1. Find a healthy, old, robust tree in your local forest
2. Check the humus litter around the tree
3. The tree should have accumulated deep humus, litter, compost — at least 2 feet to 1 yard deep
4. Through observation, deep humus indicates high soil fertility and microbial biodiversity

### Collection Method

1. Cook rice and place it in a flat container with lid (e.g., plastic lunch box)
2. Add about 1 inch of cooked rice, allowing air space in the container
3. A larger surface area traps more microorganisms
4. Cover container with metal netting or equivalent to protect from animals (rats, etc.)
5. Bury the container in the litter/humus of the forest around the selected tree
6. Leave for 2-10 days (time varies with temperature)
7. Dig up container — white and other color molds on the rice indicate microbial contamination (infection)
8. The cooked rice is now infected with microorganisms from the local forest

### Culturing

1. Add 1/3 amount of crude sugar or molasses to the infected cooked rice
2. After one week, the concoction will look like sticky, liquidy rice
3. Add equal amount of crude sugar or molasses for storage (arrests microbial activities)
4. Store in a cooler area

### Application

1. Dilute serum with 20 parts water
2. Strain and put in a container
3. This diluted form is the basic forest microorganism preparation

### Alternative Collection Method

- Get litter and humus from the forest floor
- Spread sparingly on top of cooked rice in the container
- Forest leaf molds can also be used
- Follow the same culturing procedure

## Bamboo Microorganisms

Bamboo attracts powerful beneficial microorganisms because bamboo roots exude sugary substances that draw in diverse microbial communities.

### Collection Method

1. Follow the same cooked rice container technique
2. Bury the container in bamboo plant litter instead of forest humus
3. Same culturing and application procedures apply

### Specific Use

- Bamboo microorganisms are specifically used for fermenting feeds fed to animals
- The unique microbial profile from bamboo environments is valued for feed fermentation

## Plant-Specific Microorganisms

Beneficial microorganisms from specific target crops can be trapped and cultured to support those same crops.

### Method

1. Select healthy, vigorous plants of the crop you want to grow
2. Cut the plants
3. Place an inverted container with cooked rice over the cut plant
4. Beneficial microorganisms specific to that plant will be attracted to the rice
5. Follow standard culturing procedure

### Example Applications

- Rice microorganisms: Collected from healthy, vigorous rice plants, used to support rice cultivation
- Any crop can have its specific microorganisms cultured this way
- The same procedure works for any plant of choice

## Rhizobium Nitrogen-Fixing Bacteria

While not IMO in the strict sense, rhizobium culture follows a similar self-reliant philosophy:

1. Observe nodules on legume roots (created by bacteria fixing nitrogen)
2. Pull out legume plants at flowering/fruiting stage (maximum rhizobium activity)
3. Collect soil containing the leguminous bacteria
4. Mix with equal ratio of crude sugar
5. Rhizobium bacteria proliferate feeding on the sugar
6. Use to inoculate next batch of legume seeds

## BIM Formulation

The complete Beneficial Indigenous Microorganism (BIM) recipe combines all collected microbes:

- **50% lactic acid bacteria** (the dominant component)
- **Remaining 50% distributed among:**
  - Forest microorganisms
  - Bamboo microorganisms
  - Plant-specific microorganisms
  - Other collected microbial cultures

The more diverse the microbial sources, the better. The 50% LAB ratio is maintained consistently. For plants, the full BIM blend is used. For animals, pure lacto bacilli is used alone. Bamboo microorganisms are reserved for fermenting animal feeds.

## Theory of Microbial Farming

Natural farmers of the East focus on microbial farming as their primary approach to soil fertility, in contrast to Western biodynamic and organic farming which emphasizes composts, nutrients, cover crops, and rotations. The key insight is:

- Living soil is the alpha and omega of soil fertility
- What makes soil alive is the existence of life forms, especially microorganisms
- Without microorganisms, soil fertility and health cannot be created or sustained
- Sustainable agriculture depends on soil fertility, which is determined by diversity and balance of microbial ecologies
- BIM + bionutrients = complete system for establishing healthy, fertile, living soil

When sprayed on plants, IMO increases phyllosphere bacteria that process organic matter on leaf surfaces, making nutrients available to plants. The proliferation of beneficial microorganisms combined with bionutrients can arrest pest and disease problems by restoring ecological balance — the oriental medicine principle that disease results from imbalance and disharmony.
