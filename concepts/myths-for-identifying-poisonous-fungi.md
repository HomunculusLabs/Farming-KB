## Myths for Identifying Poisonous Fungi

The identification of poisonous fungi has long been plagued by folk beliefs, rule-of-thumb methods, and false assumptions. John Ramsbottom, in his 1945 work *Mushrooms and Toadstools*, systematically dismantled these myths, demonstrating that none of the commonly cited tests for distinguishing edible from poisonous species is reliable. The following is a detailed examination of each major myth and the scientific reality behind it.

## The Colour Myth

One of the most persistent beliefs is that colour serves as a warning signal for toxicity. Bright colours are most often supposed to indicate danger, but in practice, all colours except white, cream, and pale yellow arouse suspicion among the uninformed.

### Reality

Colour is of no significance whatsoever in determining whether a fungus is poisonous. Its importance lies solely in being one of the main characters that distinguish one species from another. Edible species span the full spectrum of fungal coloration, as do poisonous ones. Colour is a taxonomic character — useful for identification once the edibility of that species is already established — but it carries no intrinsic signal of danger.

## The "Looks Poisonous" Fallacy

People frequently assert that certain toadstools "look poisonous," a judgement usually based on colour but sometimes on shape or structure. The Field Mushroom (*Psalliota campestris*) is presumably taken as the model against which all other species are judged, so the more a fungus differs from this pattern, the more it is thought to deviate from perfection as food.

### The Doctrine of Signatures

The ancient *doctrine of signatures* still exerts its influence: a fungus with a fancied resemblance to something unpleasant is assumed to be itself objectionable. Whatever the shape or structure of a toadstool, whatever it reminds one of, these associations are not of the slightest value in determining whether it is wholesome.

## The Smell Myth

Many fungi have characteristic smells which, to an experienced collector, serve as an aid to identification. However, there is an enormous range of odours among toadstools:

- **Common mushroom smell** — the benchmark, derived from the Field Mushroom
- **Meal-like odours** — fresh meal and rancid meal
- **Sweet scents** — anise, jasmine, hyacinth, musk
- **Fruity scents** — various fruits
- **Pungent odours** — garlic, radish
- **Unusual odours** — fish, crab, goat-moth, coal-gas, ammonia, prussic acid, acetylene
- **Offensive odours** — bed-bugs, and occasionally stronger descriptions

Smells are difficult to define. People differ in their sensitivity, their criteria of association, and even dried specimens may retain their special odour for many years. Some require a good sniff; others are noticeable anywhere in the neighbourhood; occasionally it is necessary to bruise or cut a specimen for the odour to become manifest.

### Reality

It might be thought that smell would give a clue to edibility, but this is not so. None of the British poisonous species has an unpleasant smell, and the deadly poisonous species of *Amanita* are completely odourless.

## Colour Change When Cut

It has been a common belief from earliest times that if the flesh of a fungus changes colour when cut or broken, it is poisonous. Some species of *Boletus* (e.g., *B. luridus*) almost instantly become a vivid blue when broken. Other fungi become reddish, yellowish, or blackish, and the "milk" of some *Lactarius* species gradually changes colour.

### The Chemistry Behind Colour Change

The change of colour is purely a chemical phenomenon. Certain substances called **chromogens** (e.g., boletol in *Boletus* spp.; tyrosin in *Russula* spp.) occur in the flesh together with **oxidizing enzymes** (lactase, tyrosinase). These enzymes act upon the chromogens in the presence of oxygen and moisture, and the chromogens acquire a new, sometimes vivid, colour. On continued contact with air, the flesh may undergo a series of colour changes due to gradual alteration in the chromogen.

### Reality

None of these colour changes is of any significance in denoting the presence of poisonous substances. The bright blues may be disconcerting when preparing a fungus for cooking, but they are not danger signals. The Field Mushroom itself changes colour slightly (flesh becoming reddish), and the Horse Mushroom becomes yellowish. Other edible mushrooms show even stronger coloration — red in *Psalliota haemorrhoidaria*, yellow in the Yellow Staining Mushroom (*P. xanthoderma*).

## The "Edible Fungi Peel" Myth

Perhaps the most widely current and dangerous belief is that edible fungi "peel." This relic of antiquity is relied on by many to distinguish the Field Mushroom from all other species.

### The Structural Basis

Whether the skin of the cap can be removed depends upon a structural character of the epidermis:

- **Two distinct layers** — if the epidermis is formed of two different kinds of tissue, the outer skin can be peeled away
- **Single layer** — if the epidermis is in one layer, it is too closely applied to the underlying tissue to come away without tearing

Even members of the same genus may differ in this respect: *[[russula-emetica]]* has a separable pellicle, whereas *R. drimeia* does not.

### Reality

Whether or not a fungus will peel is a character of a species, but it gives no clue to edibility. The Field Mushroom peels — but so does the Death Cap (*Amanita phalloides*). A failure to realise this tragic fact led to two deaths at Ipswich in 1944.

## The Silver Spoon Test

Almost equally current is the similarly antique belief that a poisonous fungus is revealed during cooking if a silver spoon is turned black. Silver is blackened by sulphides, but it is presumably not affected by any fungus — certainly not by *Amanita phalloides*.

### Origins of the Belief

The origin likely connects with the idea held by many ancient authors that fungi have a natural affinity for poisonous substances and readily absorb them. Specimens which grew near serpent's holes, rusty nails, rotten rags, or even on trees bearing noxious fruits, were thought to be rendered harmful through imbibing deleterious substances and vapours. A poisonous fungus when cooked would apparently release these, which were attracted to the silver and discoloured it.

### Surviving Practices

In southern Italy, where the harmful effect of neighbouring metals on a fungus is still believed in, it is customary to put a piece of metal in the water in which fungi are cooked, with the notion that any poisonous substance attaches itself to the metal. Variants replace the silver spoon with onion, parsley, or other vegetables — similarly worthless as tests.

## The Manured Ground Myth

Connected with the ideas above is the belief that fungi are harmful if they grow in highly manured ground. This persists regardless of the fact that manure is the normal habitat of the form of the Field Mushroom that gave rise to all [[classification-of-cultivated-mushroom-species]] races. Several other edible fungi also favour a rich substratum. Habitat richness bears no relation to toxicity.

## Taste as a Guide

The taste of some fungi is intensely acrid, that of others very bitter, whereas the majority are mild. Taste, like colour and smell, is a specific character — so much so that in the large genus *Russula*, some classifications make taste a primary division. Though it is unwise to eat an acrid species uncooked, taste is no real guide to safety.

### Reality

None of the really dangerous fungi has a sharp taste. The most deadly mushrooms are mild and unremarkable on the palate.

## The Animal Nibbling Test

No reliance should be placed on the observation that a species is nibbled by some animal or other. The physiological differences between animals and humans make this test entirely unreliable.

### Examples

- **Rabbits** are not usually affected by *Amanita phalloides*, simply because their stomach contents are able to neutralise the poison
- **Slugs** have so different a mode of food assimilation from humans that it is absurd to rely on them as indicators — they thrive on the deadly poisonous species of *Amanita*

## Habitat Myths

All remaining tests are similarly useless:

- Edible fungi supposedly grow in **fields** and not in **woods**
- Edible fungi supposedly grow in **grass** and not **on or under trees**
- Edible fungi supposedly occur **early in the season**

None of these generalisations holds. [[classification-of-edible-and-poisonous-mushroom-families]] species are found across all habitats, substrates, and seasonal timings. These distinctions are entirely without diagnostic value.

## The Only Reliable Method

To know whether a species is edible or poisonous, it is necessary to be able to **recognise it** and to **learn its reputation** from the accumulated experience of predecessors. No rule-of-thumb method is safe. There are many toadstools which are perfectly wholesome and readily distinguished; indiscriminate eating of others may render the consumer "meet for repentance or beyond it."

## The Devastating Conclusion

The ultimate irony — and the most urgent warning — is this: by every one of the usual tests, the deadly poisonous *Amanita phalloides*, *Amanita verna*, and *Amanita virosa* would be considered perfectly safe, whereas the Field Mushroom — one of the most valued edible species — would give rise to doubts. This inversion of the folk tests is precisely why they are not merely useless but actively dangerous, and why species-level identification remains the only trustworthy approach to fungal foraging.
