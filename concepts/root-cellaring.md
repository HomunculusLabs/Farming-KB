## Root Cellaring

Root cellaring is the practice of storing fresh vegetables and fruit in living condition for many months without any processing or energy input beyond what the earth provides. In cold-winter climates, it's possible to have the makings for a fresh salad in the cellar throughout the winter — eating bins of [[growing-root-vegetables-guide]], your own cabbage, Brussels sprouts still on the stalk in midwinter, or sprouting your own Belgian endive (an expensive delicacy).

## The Fundamental Principle

To keep a body in robust health you must feed it a sizeable amount of fresh food, preferably raw. The quality of canned and frozen foods has been massively reduced, and the same is almost equally true of dried foods, especially if they were blanched during processing. Root cellaring preserves food in its most nutritious state.

## Types of Cellars

### Basement Cellars

Most homes are still equipped for root cellaring because most of them have a basement. However, it may be necessary to wall off and highly insulate a part of the basement to create a winter food-storage cellar.

### Outside Cellars

If you don't have a suitable basement:
- Dig a cellar outside (though this must be done when the ground isn't frozen — during heavy snows it's too late)
- Requires no permits nor adherence to any prescribed construction standards (other than the obvious requirement that it not collapse while you are inside it)
- Old non-working chest freezers may be recycled into small root-storage compartments

## Storage Conditions by Crop Type

### Root Vegetables (Carrots, Beets, Parsnips, Turnips, Rutabagas, Potatoes)

- **Container**: Slatted crates or bins
- **Moisture**: If the vegetable tends to dry out (like carrots or beets), pack in damp sand, sawdust, or leaves
- **Temperature**: 32-40°F (0-4°C) for most roots
- **Humidity**: High (85-95%)
- **Potatoes**: Ideal at 40°F (4°C) — colder than that, some varieties become sweet as starch converts to sugar. Warmer, they sprout

### Cabbage Family (Cabbage, Brussels Sprouts)

- **Method**: Transplant into the cellar with roots in moist soil
- **Timing**: Bring in just before it gets too cold outside — after some frosts but before hard freezing begins
- **Preparation**: Dig up, shake earth from roots, remove some outer leaves, transplant into cellar beds
- **Brussels sprouts**: Store still on the stalk
- **Cabbage**: Heads blanch during storage, becoming milder and more tender

### Earth-Bed Method

Leafy crops like cabbages and heads of endive can be stored in earth beds:
1. Dig them up just before hard freezing
2. Shake earth from roots, remove some outer leaves
3. Transplant into the cellar with roots in moist soil
4. There is no reason why such earth beds could not be constructed on shelves, although traditionally cellar floors were bare earth

## Making a Root Cellar Work

### Temperature Management

- A few old woollen blankets help stabilize temperature
- Monitor regularly — adjust ventilation as needed
- The key is to maintain cool but not freezing temperatures

### Humidity Management

- Root vegetables need high humidity — pack in damp material
- Potatoes need moderate humidity — don't pack in wet material
- Cabbage family needs moist soil for roots

### Ventilation

- Some [[growing-gourmet-growing-room-ventilation-air-exchange]] is necessary to [[query-how-do-i-prevent-mold-and-mildew-in-my-cannabis-grow]] gas buildup
- But too much ventilation dries out the stored crops
- Balance is key — most cellars need occasional monitoring

## Seed Saving and Root Cellaring

### Biennial Crops

The only way people in snow country can make seed for most biennial crops is to overwinter the roots in a cellar and then plant them back outside in spring. This was done extensively (and commercially to a lesser extent) in the United States before the seed industry developed.

### The Process

1. In late autumn, dig up the best specimens of biennial plants (kale, cabbage, Brussels sprouts, onions, beets, carrots, etc.)
2. Select the healthiest, truest-to-type plants
3. Store in the root cellar in moist soil or damp sand
4. In spring, transplant back outside
5. The plants will flower and set seed

### Practical Tip

When storing plants for seed saving:
- Choose the largest, healthiest roots
- Mark them clearly so they don't get eaten
- Maintain slightly warmer conditions than for eating crops (40-45°F / 4-7°C)
- Check periodically for rot — remove any diseased specimens immediately

## Climate Limitations

Root cellaring is not applicable everywhere:
- In tropical [[query-how-to-grow-rare-tropical-fruits-in-marginal-and-subtropical-climates]], the ground doesn't get cold enough
- In extremely cold climates, maintaining above-freezing temperatures requires insulation
- In arid climates, maintaining humidity is the challenge

### Where Cellaring Isn't Needed

In climates where vegetables grow lushly 12 months a year (tropical and subtropical), cellaring is unnecessary. An unheated outbuilding is plenty good enough for temporary storage.

### The Middle Ground

[[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]] with mild winters:
- Greens may overwinter in their growing beds with only a bit of soil mounded over the roots against shallow freeze
- Root vegetables can be stored in uninsulated sheds with straw covering
- Simpler methods work for shorter storage periods

## Recommended Reading

*Root Cellaring* by Mike and Nancy Bubel is an excellent and comprehensive book about the subject, covering construction methods, crop-by-crop storage requirements, and troubleshooting. It's the definitive reference for anyone serious about food preservation without processing.
