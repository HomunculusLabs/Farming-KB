## Berry Fruit Production in Cool Humid Climates

No region so suits berry production as the cool humid climates. Berries occupy ecological niches from high montane to seashore sites, and natural stands fill forest clearings, edge roadways, and paths. They serve as food for birds, foxes, rodents, and people. **Biological pest control** within cages is beneficial: tree frogs, small lizards, and insectivorous birds (such as quail) can be introduced for pest control. Cage walls may support trellis of bramble fruits (youngberry, boysenberry). Maintenance consists primarily of pruning and adding mulch as needed to maintain soil health.

### Field Culture with Hawk Kites

In field conditions, losses to birds are unavoidable but may be minimised using **tethered predator hawk kites** above the crop at critical ripening periods. These kites imitate local hawks or eagles and are very effective bird repellents. They should be removed after the crop is picked so that birds do not become accustomed to them.

### Self-Pick Farm Design

Since much of the berry crop cost is in picking, it is preferable to plant for **self-pick sales** where farms are within 15–20 km of town markets. For self-pick operations:

- Berries (currant, gooseberry, raspberry, strawberry) are best planted in **double rows on contour mounds** 1–1.5 m high
- **Grassed paths** and wider spacing of mounds or ridges than in cage culture allow pickers easy access
- Self-pick farms need **parking and weigh-out centres, buckets for pickers, and produce tubs** for crop

## Rampant Blackberry Control

Blackberry can become an aggressive invasive, but several non-chemical methods are effective, scaled to the area requiring control:

### Small Areas (up to 0.1–0.25 ha)

Individual plants can be eliminated using a **strong rubber-backed carpet, tearproof fibre, or tough plastic**. Clumps are covered and weighted for 7–10 days, when leaves have rotted and branches blanched. The clump is then uncovered and the roots dug out.

### Medium Areas (0.1–2.0 ha)

Medium areas can be completely controlled by:

1. **Erecting permanent electric fencing** to reduce the area to 0.4 ha (1 acre) lots
2. **Releasing 20 pigs per lot**, followed by 12 goats on rotation
3. Over 2–4 years, the pigs eat the roots and the goats consume any regrowth

This control is permanent if:
- The areas are subsequently grazed by sheep or goats (even geese help)
- The area is planted to thickish forest
- The area is used for hay and regularly cut

### Large Areas in Steep Gullies

Two methods have proven effective for large-scale control:

**Cattle trampling**: Cattle are fed in winter with untied bales of fodder thrown into the blackberry gullies. They trample and destroy the vines while "sowing" grass. Such gullies need reafforestation later. This method was used successfully by Lance Jones of Tasmania over many acres.

**Fruit tree succession**: Apple, fig, quince, pear, and plum trees (ungrafted whips) are planted inside the edge of blackberry clumps, about 12 metres in from the edge. These grow and fruit in 4–6 years. Cattle come in to eat the fallen fruit and trample out the blackberries. Used by [[coprosma-repens-entity]], pine, or cypress hedges.

## Integrated Design Perspective

Berry fruits exemplify the [[permaculture]] principle of **integrating multiple functions**. A berry planting simultaneously provides food production, wildlife habitat, wind protection, nursery conditions for tree seedlings, livestock exclusion, and soil stabilisation on slopes. The cage culture system further demonstrates how intensification — through physical protection, biological pest control, and polyculture — can multiply yields by orders of magnitude while maintaining organic principles. The key insight is that berries are not merely a crop but a **successional element** that bridges the gap between open land and mature forest, making them indispensable in any cool-climate permaculture design.
