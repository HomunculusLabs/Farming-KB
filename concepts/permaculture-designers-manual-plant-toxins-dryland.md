# Plant Toxins and Dryland Hazards

> From: Bill Mollison, *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (1988), Chapters 8, 9, 10, 11

Understanding plant toxins and environmental hazards is essential for permaculture designers working in any climate, but particularly in drylands and degraded landscapes. Mollison stresses that the health of plants, animals, and the environment of soils and buildings are all dependent on the balance of elements, radioactive substances, radiation, and water quality.

## Nitrate Accumulation

Nitrogen is a major plant nutrient and part of all amino acids, chlorophyll, and enzymes. Plants absorb nitrogen in the form of nitrates or ammonium and convert this to ammonia to create proteins. In water or with bacteria, nitrogen forms nitrates which at low levels are beneficial, but at high levels (more than 80 ppm) are lethal to young animals and children.

Mouth bacteria and sewage bacteria convert nitrates to nitrites, hence nitrosamines, which are cancer-associated. Nitrates are building up in all soil waters below agricultural land and occur naturally in many desert bore waters. Designers should take the following precautions:

- Fertilise plants in the garden at minimum nitrogen levels; do not use heavy doses of nitrogen fertiliser, but rather a modest legume interplant.
- Check water levels for human health, especially in deserts.
- Constantly measure nitrate levels in leaves, water, and soil.
- Never inflict high nitrate levels on people with malnutrition -- they can be killed by excess nitrate in their food.

Accepted European (EEC) standards are 50 mg/l in water (60-80 ppm). These levels are being exceeded where nitrate fertilisers and even natural manures are spread on soils. In humid areas, 70% of human intake is from vegetables and 21% from water.

Some nitrite/nitrate levels (ppm): 0-50 is safe for human consumption; 500 for spinach grown from compost; 2,000-3,000 for chemical fertiliser-grown spinach.

Too much animal manure also releases surplus nitrogen. Reduce cattle and run them on pastures, not on feedlots. Dispose of sewage in forests to aid tree growth.

## Boron Toxicity

Surplus boron can cause anaemia in people. In soils, 0.75 ppm is ideal, but 1.0 ppm can be toxic to plants. It is likely to be at toxic levels in dry years where soils have been derived from marine sediments. Boron seems essential to sugar transport in plants, pollen formation (hence fertility), and cell wall structure (like calcium).

Borax is poisonous to seed and insects, used with dilute honey as an ant and cockroach poison. Sea sediments and the sea contain high boron levels.

## Fluorine Toxicity

Although beneficial in small amounts as in seaweed fertiliser, fluorine is a serious poison at high levels. It is commonly found in bore water or in downwind pollution plumes from metal processing works (zinc, aluminium, copper), causing bone deformation in animals, stunted growth, and serious metabolic disturbance. Check water, especially bore water, for high levels and use tanks for drinking water. Fluorine is useful at very low trace levels only. Surplus can cause anaemia; high levels are found in marine phosphate deposits, hence fluorine can be high in fertilisers derived from these.

## Copper and Selenium Issues

While copper deficiency is rare or absent in adults, copper toxicity is of more concern, especially where copper sulphate is used to clear algae from drinking water, and from foods or water standing in or boiled in copper utensils and pipes.

Selenium can cause "thrift" in animals in many countries where a severe dietary deficiency occurs. Five mg/year is sufficient for sheep; more can cause toxicity. Seaweeds may supply trace elements safely.

## Persistent Biocides

If you buy or inherit an old orchard, canefield, or plantation (banana, pineapple, cotton, tobacco) it is unlikely that any animal product (milk, eggs, meat) will be free of high levels of biocides. The only choice is to go into forestry and produce non-food crops until other methods are developed. Always test vegetables for residual toxic materials.

## Groundwater Contamination

Groundwater can contain 80-90 biocidal substances below farms, including those derived from fertilisers, sprays, and fuels. Near industrial waste dumps, dioxins, radioactives, and heavy metal wastes (cadmium, chromium, mercury) can be added. Never use untested wells or bore waters for any purpose. Drink tank water and harvest surface run-off for gardens. Several decades may be needed to clear most aquifers of pollutants.

## Dryland-Specific Toxicity Risks

In dryland environments, toxicity risks are amplified by concentration effects:

- Salt accumulation from irrigation concentrates trace elements to toxic levels.
- Nitrate build-up in bore waters occurs naturally in many desert areas.
- Marine-derived sediments release boron to toxic levels in dry years.
- Deforestation and overgrazing concentrate minerals in surface soils.
- Industrial pollutants accumulate in endorheic (closed) drainage basins with no outlet to the sea.
- Evaporation in high temperatures draws soluble toxins toward the soil surface.

## Designer Responsibilities

Mollison is clear: we must not add to existing pollution. Avoid all biocides, high levels of nitrates, and watch on-farm disposal of oils and fuels. There is a complex and constant interaction between food, soil, trace elements, pH, biocides, and fertilisers. Heavy use of macronutrient fertilisers can cause deficiency of micronutrients.
