These three types of [[permaculture-water-harvesting]] system. While swales hold water and dams store it, diversion drains, interceptor drains, and spreader banks **move, control, and distribute** water across the landscape. Together, they enable the [[oyster-mushroom-pests-sciarid-cecid-phorid-fly-mite-integrated-management]] of floodwaters, irrigation, wildfire control, and [[irrigation-systems]], sand beds, or swales for absorption. Unlike swales, diversion drains are **built to flow** after rain — they convey water from overland flow or feeder streams to destinations where it can be stored or used.

### Design and Construction

- **Slope**: As little as **1:6000** in fine sands — extremely gentle gradients are sufficient
- **Dam crests and irrigation drains** require equally careful placement and surveying
- Even without a stream intake, diversion drains gather water from overland flow in as little as **1–1.5 cm (1/2 inch) of rain over 24 hours**
- Isolated dams are normally fitted with diversion drains even in quite dry country

### Integration with Keyline Systems

Diversion drains are the **essential connectors** of dam series built on Keyline systems. The overflow of one dam enters the feeder channel of the next, creating cascading storage systems across the landscape.

### Low Walls as Mini-Swales

If low earth walls are raised across the flow channels of larger diversion drains, they function as a **series of mini-swales** for specific [[query-what-are-the-different-fruit-tree-training-systems-and-which-should-i-use|tree]] sites, while surplus water flows on to downstream storages.

### Applications

- Leading water from rock faces to underground cave storages or open tanks (as practiced in the Canary Islands)
- Connecting roof tank overflow to swales and gardens
- Collecting overland flow from roads and paved surfaces
- Feeding irrigation systems from dam overflows

## Spill Gates and Flow Control

### Types of Spill Gates

**Fixed spill gates**: Concrete slide holders and aprons — permanent structures for controlled release.

**Moveable spill gates ("flags")**: Plastic sheets weighted with a chain sewn into the foot, supported across the drain by a light pipe sleeved into the top. These are temporary, portable structures.

### Operational Capabilities

- Slide gates can open onto ridge lines, allowing water to spread downhill
- Plastic flags can be placed and removed at any point along a drain
- **One or two people can water 200–240 ha (400–600 acres) in a morning** using flag systems

### Wildfire Control

Spill gates serve as effective **wildfire control systems** in forested areas. For sophisticated wildfire or irrigation control, both slide gates and dam-base gate valves can be remotely operated by:

- Radio signal and storage batteries
- Buried electrical conduits powering small motors or hydraulic slides
- Infrared sensors and automatic spill-gates for complete automated wildfire control

Such systems remove risk for firefighters and allow forests to regenerate in semi-arid areas.

## Interceptor Drains

### Function

Interceptor drains (designed by [[harry-whittington-mollison]] of West Australia) operate in the **opposite sense to diversion drains**. They are specifically designed to **prevent overland water flow and waterlogging**, which collapses dryland valley or downslope soils in a desert soil catena.

### Key Differences from Swales and Diversion Drains

Interceptor drains differ from both swales and diversion drains in their construction:

- The **subsoil layers are rammed** (by bulldozer blade) hard against the downhill bank
- This effectively **prevents or impedes water seepage** through the downhill wall
- They are always **1.5–2.5 m (6–8 feet) deep**
- They are **carefully spaced** for systematic coverage

### Functions

- **Totally intercept overland flow** and direct it to streams or valley run-offs
- **Stop salt water seepages** in shallow sand seams
- **Isolate large blocks of soil** from waterlogging and salt seepage
- **Cut off seepage from salt lakes** and divert early (salted) overland flow around saltpans, letting later fresh floodwater fill the pans

### Desertification Prevention

Interceptor drains effectively cut up **incipient or degraded croplands** subject to desertification into blocks of 100 m (330 feet) to a maximum of 300 m (985 feet) wide. Each block is isolated from flooding and the salty "cascade" flow from uphill. After this preparation, trees can be planted in **previously desertified soils**.

### Deeper Salt Seams

Where deeper sand seams carrying salty water are located, these can be **trenched out and stopped** with a vertical plastic barrier, backed by compacted clay on the uphill side.

### Slope Specifications

Interceptor drains are made to flow at **1:600 to 1:1500** cross-slope, providing effective [[water-management]] while maintaining the structural integrity of the interception.

## Spreader Banks

### Function

Spreader drains or banks are designed to **spill a thin sheet of surplus (overflow) water down a broad grassy slope** for irrigation or, in deserts, to prevent channel scour and gullying.

### Design

- Take overflow from swales and dams
- May be **tens or hundreds of metres** long
- The **lower side is dead level** and compacted or even concreted
- Water enters from a dam, swale, or minor stream
- Water **leaves as a thin sheet flow** downslope
- **Spoil is piled uphill**, preferably in mounds, or removed to allow downhill overland flow to enter the spreader ditch

### Irrigation Bay Systems

For irrigation areas in flatlands, the bank is often pierced by a series of **dead-level pipe outlets**, each feeding an irrigation bay (itself planed flat), confined by low side walls called **steering banks**. At the lower end of each bay, a **tail drain** leads off excess water to a stream or secondary storage dam.

**Terminology:**

- **Head race**: The uphill or primary feeder drain with cross-slides that block flow, causing levelled pipes to flood into bays
- **Steering banks**: Low side walls confining water to irrigation bays
- **Tail drain**: Surplus water drain at the downstream end of each bay
- **Bay length**: Should not exceed 100 m to prevent water waste through evaporation

In older, established spreader banks, the whole lower lip may be concreted to give a permanent level spill immune to breakage by cattle or vehicles.

### Spoil Management

Spoil lines uphill from spreader banks are best piled in mounds or at least broken by **frequent openings** to allow downhill overland flow to enter the drain without carrying silt loads.

## Integrated Water Management

In any landscape, a subtle and well-planned combination of these earthworks — **dams, drains, spreader banks, swales, and appropriate pipes, gate-valves, spill gates, flags, and culverts** — will:

- **Harvest** surplus flood or overland flow waters
- **Store** water for dry periods
- **Use** water productively across the landscape
- **Flush out salts** from soils
- **Spread water evenly** over crops
- **Put out wildfires** through sheet irrigation
- **Modestly irrigate** land without expensive pumped systems

The key is to first decide on, then carefully survey all systems, and above all consider the **long-term effects on the immediate landscape and soils**.

## Rehabilitative vs. Exploitative Use

All earthworks can be classified by their long-term impact:

**Rehabilitative and remedial** — when they replace salted and eroded lands with perennial browse or forests

**Damaging and exploitative** — when used to:
- Irrigate high water-demand crops (like lucerne/alfalfa) in drylands
