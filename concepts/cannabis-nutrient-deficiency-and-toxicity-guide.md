# Cannabis Nutrient Deficiency and Toxicity Guide

Diagnosing nutrient problems in cannabis requires distinguishing between true [[hamilton-nutrient-deficiencies-and-treatment]], chemical burns from over-fertilization, pH-related lockout, and pest or disease damage. The key diagnostic difference is that a chemical burn looks like damage that starts at the leaf tips and moves inward with crispy, flaky residue, while a nutrient deficiency appears as cellular collapse with discoloration but without the burnt appearance.

See [[cannabis-nutrient-recipes]] for feeding solutions, and [[cannabis-environment-control]] for pH management details.

The location of symptoms on the plant provides critical diagnostic information. Problems affecting only the lower portion and mid-section indicate mobile nutrient deficiencies (the plant is pulling nutrients from older leaves to supply new growth). Problems affecting only the top of the plant and new growth tips indicate immobile nutrient deficiencies. Problems covering the entire plant suggest a systemic issue.

## Nitrogen (N) Deficiency

Nitrogen deficiency is the most common nutrient problem in cannabis. During vegetative growth, leaves turn uniformly yellow, starting from the lowest leaves and progressing upward. During flowering, nitrogen deficiency manifests as stunted growth with yellowing leaves. If the plant appears to be actively dying during flowering with severe yellowing, nitrogen is still needed. If the plant looks otherwise healthy but has dark green or reddish-yellow discoloration during flowering, this is a [[query-how-to-fix-phosphorus-deficiency-in-cannabis]], not nitrogen.

Nitrogen is highly mobile in the plant, which is why symptoms appear first in the oldest (lowest) leaves. A mild nitrogen deficiency during late flowering is normal and even desirable, as it encourages the plant to use up stored nitrogen and focus energy on bud production.

## Phosphorus (P) Deficiency

Phosphorus deficiency during flowering presents as stunted growth with leaves that look reddish, dark green, or yellowish-green but are not dying. The plant may appear otherwise healthy but fails to develop bud mass properly. Phosphorus is critical for flower development and energy transfer within the plant.

Phosphorus deficiency can be difficult to distinguish from normal late-flowering coloration. The key difference is that deficient plants show stunted bud development alongside the discoloration, while normally maturing plants continue to produce healthy bud mass despite leaf color changes.

## Magnesium (Mg) Deficiency

[[query-how-to-fix-magnesium-deficiency-in-cannabis]] causes leaves to curl upward, twist, and turn yellow while the leaf veins remain green. This interveinal chlorosis is the hallmark symptom. Before attributing curling yellow leaves to magnesium deficiency, verify that the lights are not burning the foliage and that the grow chamber has adequate air circulation.

Epsom salts (magnesium sulfate) are the standard remedy. Add one-quarter to one-third tablespoon of Epsom salts per 3 gallons of water and apply as a foliar spray or soil drench. Results should be visible within a few days as new growth emerges green and healthy.

## Potassium (K) Deficiency

[[knf-potassium-deficiency-and-excess]] causes the tips of leaves to turn brown and curl slightly. The browning typically starts at the leaf edges and tips and progresses inward. Potassium is important for water regulation, enzyme activation, and overall plant vigor. Deficiency is more common during heavy flowering when potassium demand is highest.

## Iron (Fe) Deficiency

Iron deficiency presents as yellow leaves with distinctly green veins, a condition called interveinal chlorosis. This is similar in appearance to magnesium deficiency but differs in that iron deficiency typically affects the newest growth first (upper leaves), while magnesium deficiency affects older growth first (lower leaves).

Iron deficiency is often caused by pH being too high rather than an actual absence of iron in the medium. Correcting pH is often sufficient to resolve the symptom. If the medium pH is correct, a chelated iron supplement can be applied.

## Manganese (Mn) Deficiency

[[query-how-to-fix-manganese-deficiency-in-cannabis]] causes yellowing at the base of leaves while the tips remain unaffected and healthy-looking. This distinguishes it from other nutrient deficiencies that typically affect tips first. Manganese plays a role in photosynthesis and enzyme function.

## Chemical Burn (Nutrient Toxicity)

Chemical burn is the most common nutrient problem for new growers. It results from over-feeding and presents as damage that starts at the leaf tips and slowly moves toward the center, leaving behind crispy, flaky matter that can be rubbed off between the fingers. The pattern resembles the burn on a half-smoked joint, with the tip being the most severely damaged.

The plant distributes nutrients to the lower leaves first and works upward, so chemical burn damage typically starts at the bottom and progresses up the plant over several days. Unlike nutrient deficiency symptoms, which show cellular collapse and discoloration, chemical burn has a distinctly charred, crispy texture.

## Nutrient Lockout

Nutrient lockout occurs when the plant cannot access one or more nutrients despite their presence in the growing medium. This can be caused by incorrect pH, chemical reactions in the medium that produce toxic substances blocking root uptake, or incompatible nutrient combinations that create insoluble compounds.

pH problems are the most common cause of lockout. In soil, cannabis prefers a pH of 6.0-7.0. In hydroponics, the optimal range is 5.2-6.3. Outside these ranges, specific nutrients become unavailable to the plant even if they are abundant in the medium.

The solution to persistent lockout is to flush the growing medium with plain, pH-balanced water and then resume feeding with a fresh, properly balanced nutrient solution. If lockout persists after flushing, transplanting into fresh medium is the most reliable solution.

## Root-Bound (Pot-Bound) Plants

When a plant outgrows its container, the root mass fills the entire pot and begins circling. This creates stress that produces symptoms resembling nutrient deficiency, including yellowing leaves and stunted growth. The only cure is transplantation into a larger container with fresh medium. Root-bound plants should be transplanted before flowering begins, as the rapid growth of the flowering period will compound root stress.

## Bad Genetics

Some nutrient-like symptoms are caused by poor genetics rather than actual nutritional problems. Mutations, warping, flowering disorders, and general weakness in the genetic line can cause leaves to yellow, curl, or develop spots even when nutrients are perfectly balanced. If nutrient problems persist despite correct feeding and pH management, bad genetics may be the cause. The only solution is to obtain higher-quality genetics from a reputable source.

## Calcium (Ca) Deficiency

[[knf-calcium-deficiency-symptoms]] causes new growth to appear distorted, curled, or crinkled. Leaf tips may hook downward and the plant may show signs of rot at the root zone. Calcium is immobile in the plant, so symptoms appear on new growth first. Calcium plays a critical role in cell wall structure and root development.

Calcium deficiency is often related to growing medium pH rather than actual calcium absence. In soilless and hydroponic systems, calcium is typically supplied through cal-mag supplements. Ensuring adequate calcium also helps prevent bud rot during late flowering.

## Sulfur (S) Deficiency

Sulfur deficiency causes newer leaves to turn uniformly yellow, similar to nitrogen deficiency but appearing on the upper growth rather than lower leaves. The yellowing may be accompanied by slightly stunted growth. Sulfur is relatively immobile in the plant.

## Over-Watering Symptoms

Over-watering produces symptoms that mimic nutrient deficiency: drooping, yellowing leaves that look wilted despite wet soil. The key diagnostic sign is that the leaves feel limp and heavy rather than dry and crispy. Over-watering also deprives roots of oxygen, leading to root rot and further nutrient uptake problems. Allow the medium to dry between waterings and ensure containers have adequate drainage.

## Troubleshooting Summary

Always check pH first. Then identify whether symptoms are on lower leaves (mobile nutrients), upper leaves (immobile nutrients), or the entire plant (systemic issues). Distinguish between burnt crispy tissue (toxicity) and soft discolored tissue (deficiency). When in doubt, flush the medium and start with fresh nutrients at reduced strength before escalating to stronger solutions.

## Merged: Cannabis Nutrient Flush Before Harvest

Updated: 2026-04-18

Flushing nutrients from cannabis plants in the final days before harvest is a critical technique that improves the taste, smoothness, and quality of the final cured product. The goal is to completely rid the plant of nitrogen and other residual chemicals.

## Why Flush

- Residual nitrogen and nutrients in the plant tissue create a harsh, chemical taste when smoked

- Flushing forces the plant to use up stored nutrients, resulting in cleaner-burning, better-tasting bud

- Makes the bud easier and more predictable to cure

- Reduces the risk of nutrient-related health issues for medical users

## When to Flush

Begin flushing 7 days before the planned harvest date. Some growers flush for up to 14 days for a more thorough cleanse. The optimal flush duration depends on the growing medium — soil holds more residual nutrients than hydroponic media, so soil growers may benefit from a longer flush period of 10-14 days, while hydroponic growers can often achieve a thorough flush in 5-7 days.

## How to Flush

1. **Stop all nutrient feeding** — give only plain water (pH-adjusted)

2. **For soil growers**: Water heavily to leach residual nutrients from the soil, then water with plain water only

3. **For hydroponic growers**: Drain the reservoir, refill with plain pH-adjusted water (no nutrients)

4. **Monitor the plant**: Leaf tips will begin to yellow and show signs of nitrogen deficiency — this is expected and desired

## Flushing for Different Growing Systems

Soil growers should flush with 3 times the container volume of water to thoroughly leach accumulated salts. For example, a 5-gallon pot needs approximately 15 gallons of flush water. Coco coir growers follow a similar protocol. Deep water culture (DWC) growers should drain and refill the reservoir with plain pH-adjusted water. Drip and ebb-and-flow systems benefit from multiple reservoir changes during the flush period.

## Signs the Flush Is Working

- Leaf tips yellowing and curling (nitrogen deficiency)

- Older fan leaves may yellow and drop off

- Overall plant appearance declines — this is normal

- Bud resin production continues even as leaves decline
