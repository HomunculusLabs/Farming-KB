# Water Harvesting in Arid Lands

## Overview

Water is the dominant design theme for arid landscapes. The quantity of fresh water (less than 700 ppm salt) is the final arbiter of successful settlement and sustainable agriculture in drylands. To obtain and retain fresh water, designers must intercept it before it washes salt from soils, mixes with deeper saline groundwaters, or runs off roof areas and rocks. This requires a comprehensive understanding of rainwater conservation, tank storage, [[swale]] systems, and water spreading techniques.

## Design Aims for Water in Arid Landscapes

The fundamental aims of water harvesting in drylands can be summarised as:

- **Store fresh water** for cooking and drinking
- **Divert sheet flow and waste water** to gardens
- **Infiltrate water into soils** and produce plant growth on site
- **Give rainwater run-off time** to soak into the landscape where people live

## Rainwater Conservation

### Two Basic Strategies

1. **Household tanks, cisterns, and sealed wells** to capture roof water from individual buildings
2. **Large public sealed and roofed reservoirs** to hold runoff from public buildings, paved areas, and roads

### Domestic Tank Systems

Every family roof should catch its own drinking water in tanks of 20,000–50,000 litres (5,000–12,000 gallons), depending on family size and regularity of general supply. Only 1 cm of rain produces 1,000 litres for a roof of 100 square metres, or 100,000 litres per hectare of sealed runoff.

#### Tank Construction Materials

Tanks can be built from a wide variety of materials:

- Concrete poured in central vertical sliding forms
- Brick-lined, domed, and rendered in sandy soils
- "Stack sack" (plastic tubes of 1:9 cement:sand, pricked and soaked)
- Plastic-lined holes with stack sack walls
- Cement plaster on wire-netting base
- Excavated in rock
- Galvanised steel, fibreglass, plastic, or plastic resins

#### Water Quality in Tanks

It is beneficial to place limestone, dolomite, or marble gravel (or mollusc shells) in a bag in every tank to:

- Offset acidity
- Fix metallic salts
- "Harden" the water

Alkaline or neutral water is healthier to drink, associated with fewer heart attacks and reduced dietary metals.

### Common Myths About Tank Water

1. **"It's stagnant water"** — untrue; tank water tastes fresh for years and remains clear and clean
2. **"It breeds mosquitoes"** — not true of covered tanks; mosquitoes can be totally excluded, and in open tanks, mosquito-eating fish can be stocked
3. **"It contains dust from the roof"** — true, but dust settles as biological sediment and keeps the water clear

## Swales

### Definition and Function

A swale is a dead-level hollow built on contour (although the base need not be level) which creates temporary pools. Swales are ripped, gravelled, sanded, or planted at the base to assist rapid water infiltration. They are the cheapest water storages for trees, serving as large compacted collection areas alongside roads.

### Design Requirements

Swales must be large enough to handle:

- All tank or storage overflow
- Pavement runoff
- Additional harvested and diverted overland water flow from outside the settlement

They may need a capacity of 200% of village runoff, which then infiltrates locally as soil water storage to 3–5 m depth.

### Benefits of Swales

- **Reduced infrastructure cost**: Eliminates need for stormwater drains, gutters, and kerbing
- **Tree production**: Grows shade trees for pavements, courtyards, and parking areas
- **Material supply**: Provides dirt spoil for road-beds, sand for construction, and topsoil/loam/clay for gardens
- **Rapid infiltration**: Many swales infiltrate water in 2–48 hours, providing ample tree irrigation after establishment

### Case Study: Village Homes, Davis, CA

Village Homes is a fully swaled development that grows its trees on harvested water, planted in or beside the swales. After a few rains, ample soil water is available for tree growth.

### Domestic Swales for Roof Drip

Small stone-filled domestic swales provide a shaded soil-water source for vine trellises. Neat swales for roof drip can be made from rock-shingle-filled hollows or channels with vertical concrete edges, left unsealed below to allow infiltration.

## Water Conservation in Buildings

### Rationing Approaches

1. **Fixed household ration**: Fitting float valves to all home roof tanks (of fixed dimension) and pumping them full only once per week
2. **Metered use with sliding scale**: Strict metering with increasing cost for excess use, making waste more expensive than conservation

### Indoor and Outdoor Conservation

The most effective water savings come from:

- **Gardens**: Planting shrubberies instead of lawns, using sub-surface trickle irrigation, mulch, and shade (saves ~50% of total water use)
- **Toilets**: Feeding hand-basin and shower water to low-head, low-flush cisterns (saves ~40% of remaining use)
- **Greywater reuse**: Bathwater diverted to pre-soak troughs for clothes, laundry water to gardens
- **Fixtures**: Water-saving shower heads and timer taps (saves 14–20% of shower water)
- **Kitchen**: Sink water diverted to garden use

### Simple Systems

Much simpler but hygienic systems use "rope and pulley" showers, basins for hands and laundry under lean-tos, and earth-closet toilets. These use a fraction of the water caught on roof areas.

## Water Harvesting on Open Sites

### Earth Tanks in Clay Soils

Where good clays are found, earth tanks can be made in the ground. Essentials include:

- Tanks should be deep and narrow
- They should intercept a runoff area
- They should ideally be roofed to prevent evaporation

### Sealed Runoff Surfaces

A variety of deliberately sealed or compacted runoff surfaces have been constructed:

- **Ground-level surfaces**: Bitumen-sealed and fenced areas, roaded catchments of 300 m long by 10 m wide with side slopes of 1:20 and drains at 1:100–1:200
- **Stabilisation**: Using gums, light cement mix, or well-compacted clay soils
- **Sheet metal roofing**: Up to 600 square metres feeding 100,000-litre tanks
- **Clay spread-banks**: Square or circular banks sloping to a central cistern, with topsoil removed and excavated material rolled down to create runoff area

### Rock Slab Catchment

The cheapest "roof" is locating near a bare rock slab in granite, or a natural sealed cap on hills, with a gutter (rock-cut or cemented) directing water to storage cisterns or tanks.

### Tank Siting

- Sites chosen above houses or settlements
- Accessory windmills in foothill settlements (or solar-powered pumps) lift clean groundwater to tanks during high demand
- On flat plains, tanks raised on stands with windmill supply — a very reliable and widely-used system
- Large garages, factories, recreation halls, and shops provide ample roof area for catchment

## Creating Oasis Systems

By instituting a system of cisterns, tanks, and swales, any desert settlement (wherever average rainfall exceeds 30 cm) can create for itself a reserve of soil and roof water that is, in effect, an oasis. The proportion of water supplied to swales by local runoff versus widespread interceptor banks changes as rainfall decreases. Below 30 cm, more extensive harvesting of landscape runoff must be made and directed to village swales to support large trees.
