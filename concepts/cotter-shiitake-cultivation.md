# [[mushroom-training-shiitake-cultivation]]

Shiitake ([[lentinula-edodes]]) is one of the most popular and commercially valuable cultivated mushrooms worldwide. Known for its rich umami flavor and medicinal properties, shiitake can be grown outdoors on logs or indoors on supplemented sawdust. From Cotter's "Organic Mushroom Farming and Mycoremediation."

## Species Overview

- Scientific name: Lentinula edodes
- Difficulty: Indoor 3 / Outdoor 1
- One of the most recognized and marketable mushrooms globally
- Fruiting temperatures: 50-75°F (10-24°C) depending on strain
- Prefers hardwood trees, especially oak, but also maple, beech, birch, and ironwood
- Strain-specific wood preferences: each strain may prefer its own unique wood chemistry

## Mycelium and Spawn Characteristics

- White, cottony mycelium that becomes brown with age
- Exudes a light yellow metabolite on overcolonized substrates
- Slower growing than oyster mushroom mycelium
- Fruity, sweet smell when healthy
- Commercial spawn available as cereal grain, supplemented sawdust, and wooden dowels (plug spawn)
- Grain spawn preferred for indoor cultivation; sawdust or plug spawn for outdoor

## Strain Selection

Shiitake strains are categorized by fruiting temperature windows:

- **Cold-weather strains**: Fruit at 50-65°F (10-18°C); typically produce in spring and fall
- **Warm-weather strains**: Fruit at 60-80°F (16-27°C); fruit during summer months
- **Wide-range strains**: Adapt to broader temperature windows
- Wild strains can be cloned from mature specimens and tested for fruiting capabilities
- Local strains adapted to regional conditions generally perform best
- Rotating strains with different temperature windows maximizes harvest periods year-round

## Outdoor Log Cultivation

### Log Selection and Preparation

- Use fresh hardwood logs, cut within one month of inoculation
- Oak is preferred, but maple, beech, birch, and ironwood also work well
- Log diameter: 4-8 inches is ideal; larger logs take longer to colonize but produce longer
- Length: 3-4 feet for easy handling
- Avoid logs with signs of other fungal colonization
- Inoculate immediately after cutting when possible

### Inoculation with Plug Spawn

1. Drill holes 1-1.5 inches deep using a 5/16-inch bit
2. Space holes in a diamond pattern, 6 inches apart along the log, 2 inches between rows
3. Insert plug spawn into each hole; tap flush with a hammer
4. Seal each hole with food-grade wax (cheese wax or beeswax) to prevent drying
5. Also wax the cut ends of the logs
6. Stack logs in a shaded area with good air circulation

### Inoculation with Sawdust Spawn

1. Drill holes slightly larger than for plug spawn (to accommodate loose sawdust)
2. Use an inoculation tool to pack sawdust spawn into each hole
3. Seal with wax as with plug spawn
4. Sawdust spawn colonizes faster than plug spawn due to more inoculation points

### Log Management

- Stack logs in a shaded location (under trees or behind a building)
- Keep logs off the ground (on pallets, rocks, or a log rack) to prevent contamination from soil organisms
- Maintain moisture: water during dry periods, or rely on natural rainfall in humid climates
- Avoid direct sunlight which dries the logs
- Full colonization takes 6-18 months depending on log size, species, and climate
- Logs can produce mushrooms for 3-6 years

### Forcing Fruiting

To stimulate a flush of mushrooms on colonized logs:

1. Soak logs in cold water (ice water works well) for 12-24 hours
2. Remove logs and stack in a fruiting position
3. Mushrooms should appear in 7-14 days
4. Allow logs to rest for 6-8 weeks before forcing another flush
5. Approximately 2-4 flushes per year is sustainable

## Indoor Sawdust Cultivation

### [[substrate-preparation]]

1. Mix hardwood sawdust with 5% wheat or rice bran (dry weight supplement)
2. Hydrate to field capacity
3. Sterilize in autoclave bags at 15 PSI for 2 hours
4. Cool to below 90°F (32°C) before inoculation

### Inoculation and Incubation

1. Inoculate with grain or sawdust spawn (5-10% by weight)
2. Seal bags and incubate at 77°F (25°C) in darkness
3. Incubation period: 30-60 days until full colonization
4. Mycelium turns brown as it reaches maturity (browning phase)

### Fruiting Initiation

1. Cold shock: refrigerate bags at 35-40°F (2-4°C) for 2-3 days
2. Remove bags and cut slits or holes for fruiting
3. Maintain temperature at 55-65°F (13-18°C)
4. Humidity: 85-95%
5. Light: Indirect or diffuse
6. Fresh air exchange is critical for proper cap development
7. First mushrooms appear 7-14 days after cold shock

### Expected Yields

- Indoor: Approximately 1 pound of mushrooms per 5 pounds of supplemented sawdust
- 2-3 flushes, 3-4 weeks apart
- Most growers plan for only 1-2 flushes indoors before dumping blocks
- Spent blocks can be buried outdoors for additional future fruiting

## Harvesting

- Harvest when the cap is 70-80% expanded, before the veil breaks fully
- Cut at the base with a sharp knife or twist gently to remove
- Trim the base where substrate is attached
- Shiitakes store better when harvested slightly immature
- Avoid harvesting during wet conditions to reduce storage problems

## Storage

- Fresh: 2-3 weeks at 38-42°F (3-6°C)
- Drying: Excellent; dried shiitake is a major commercial product
- Reconstitutes well; drying actually concentrates flavor (umami compounds)
- Can be powdered for use as a seasoning or supplement
- Freeze-drying preserves the best quality for medicinal use

## Marketing

- One of the most recognizable and marketable mushrooms
- Strong consumer demand; sells itself in most markets
- Dried shiitake commands premium prices and has long shelf life
- Fresh: $8-15 per pound depending on quality and market
- Dried: $20-40 per pound
- Promote dual value as food and medicine
- The name "shiitake" is well-established; no need for alternative marketing names
