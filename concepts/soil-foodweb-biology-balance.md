## Soil Foodweb Biology and Balance

The soil foodweb is the complex community of organisms living in soil
that interact with each other and with plants in interconnected ways.
Dr. [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], cycle nutrients, fix atmospheric
nitrogen, and create soil structure through their biological activities.
The foodweb is the living engine that drives soil fertility without
the need for synthetic chemical inputs.

When the foodweb is functioning properly, plants receive balanced
nutrition delivered at the right time and in the right form. This
biological approach to fertility is fundamentally different from
conventional chemical management, which bypasses the biology entirely.

## Bacterial vs. Fungal Dominated Soils

Different plant communities have evolved to thrive with different soil
biology ratios. Annual crops, grasses, and brassicas generally prefer
bacterial-dominated soils where fast-growing bacteria rapidly cycle
nutrients needed by these quick-maturing plants.

Perennial plants, shrubs, and trees typically require fungal-dominated
soils for optimal health. Fungi decompose more recalcitrant organic
materials including lignin and cellulose, providing a slower, steadier
[[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] inputs. Restoring
fungal populations is often the first priority when rebuilding degraded
soil foodwebs for perennial or orchard systems.

## Protozoa and Their Critical Roles

Protozoa — including flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates — are the
primary grazers of soil bacteria and essential components of the
[[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] process. As they consume bacteria, they excrete excess
nitrogen in ammonium form, making it directly available to plants.

Flagellates are the smallest protozoa and most numerous in soil. They
thrive in water-filled pore spaces and are sensitive to soil compaction.
Amoebae are larger and can move through soil pores to find bacterial
prey. Ciliates are the least common and indicate soil disturbance.

This protozoan grazing process is called the microbial loop and is
central to how soil biology feeds plants. Without protozoa, nutrients
locked in bacterial biomass would remain unavailable to plant roots,
and the cycling process would slow dramatically.

## Nematode Diversity and Function

Nematodes occupy multiple functional roles within the soil foodweb.
Bacterial-feeding nematodes consume bacteria and release nitrogen in
plant-available forms, functioning similarly to protozoa but at larger
scale and through the soil profile.
