# Solar Radiation, Albedo, and Heat

## Overview

Incoming solar radiation is the fundamental energy source driving all life on Earth and all atmospheric circulation. Understanding how radiation interacts with the atmosphere, earth surfaces, and living organisms is essential for [[photoperiodism]], fruit colouration |

| 574-700 nm | Yellow/Orange/Red | Visible spectrum; used in photosynthesis |
| Above 700 nm | Far red/Infrared | Absorbed and transpired into heat by plants; no strong growth effects |

### Ultraviolet Concerns

Ozone in the upper atmosphere absorbs much of the damaging ultraviolet light. However, ozone layer destruction has increased UV incidence at Earth's surface, raising concerns about skin cancer rates, damage to plant tissues, and effects on plankton and oceanic food chains.

### Carbon Dioxide and Warming

Carbon dioxide, currently 3-4% of the atmosphere, is expected to rise to 6% and cause approximately 3°C (5.5°F) warming of the Earth. This process appears already to be affecting world climate as a warming trend and will cause sea level changes through thermal expansion of ocean water and ice melting.

## Major Effects of Radiation

### Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis in plants is the basis of all life on Earth. Plant growth relies on two narrow bands of light in the visible spectrum and is damaged by extreme ultraviolet or excessive heat wavelengths.

### Temperature Effects

Radiation absorption and re-emission creates temperature differences that drive weather, influence building comfort, and determine growing seasons. Much used in passive solar house design.

### Flowering and Germination

Radiation triggers flowering and germination in many plant species through photoperiodism (day-length effects). This is of basic importance to the spread and success of specific plant groups.

## Plant [[photosynthetic-efficiency]]:

- **Solar range adaptation**: Silvery or shiny leaves in high-heat environments; red leaves where more of the green spectrum is absorbed and less heat is needed
- **Leaf orientation**: Leaves may turn edge-on when light and heat levels get too high to reduce absorption
- **Canopy adjustment**: Leaves greatly enlarge surface area under shady canopies; trees develop larger leaves at lower canopy layers
- **Approximately 10%** of light penetrates or is transmitted by foliage, though the canopy of rainforests in very humid areas may permit only 0.01% of light to pass through to the forest floor

## Colour, Reflection, and Surface Properties

### Leaf Colour

Plants reflect green/blue wavelengths while absorbing red and blue for photosynthesis. Flowers reflect a wider spectrum, becoming conspicuous in the landscape to attract pollinators.

### Bark and Surface Variation

Plants exhibit bark surfaces ranging from almost white to almost black:

- **Dark/black bark**: Good absorbers and heat radiators
- **White/light bark**: Good reflectors

Leaf surfaces vary from hard and shiny to soft, rough, and hairy:

- **Waxy leaf surfaces**: Found in coastal or cold areas and some understory plants; provide greater reflection regardless of colour
- **Woolly leaf surfaces**: Found in deserts and at high altitudes; trap boundary-layer air for insulation

## Albedo and Absorption

### Definition

The albedo is the ratio of reflected light to received light. It determines how natural and designed surfaces behave with respect to incoming radiation:

- Light reflected goes back into the atmosphere or is absorbed by nearby surfaces and structures (such as greenhouses)
- Light absorbed is converted into long-wave radiation and re-emitted as heat

### Soil Heat Dynamics

Soils and similar dense materials absorb heat from daytime radiation to a depth of approximately 51 cm (20 inches). This absorption takes time, so soil heat build-up lags a few hours behind hourly air temperatures. Re-radiation also takes time, so absorbing surfaces lose heat slowly, lagging behind air temperatures. The result is that the lowest soil temperatures occur just after dawn.

### Frost Formation

Radiation loss at night produces frost in conditions of still air, particularly:

- In hollows where cold air pools
- On flats and in large clearings of 9-30 m (30-100 feet) across or more within forests
- On high plateaus where rapid radiative cooling occurs

Some frost (**advection frost**) flows as cold air downhill along slopes and valleys to pool in flat areas. Dense autumn fogs often indicate the extent of winter frosts and are clearly visible from high vantage points — a useful observational technique for frost mapping.

## The Fate of Incoming Waves

When radiation encounters an object or substance, it may be:

1. **Reflected**: Turned away almost unchanged, as light off a flat mirror or white wall
2. **Refracted**: Sharply bent or curved, as light in water or sea waves around a headland
3. **Absorbed**: Converted from short wavelengths to long wavelengths (light to heat). All absorbed light is eventually emitted as heat
4. **Transmitted**: Passed through the object, as light through glass or water

Different substances are transparent to different wavelengths due to their molecular structure. By choosing materials, colours, or shapes of fabricated or natural components, designers can manipulate energy on site — redirecting, converting, or passing on incoming energy.

## The Earth as a Black Body

The Earth acts like a black body, accepting short wavelengths from the sun and emitting long wavelengths after absorption. Curiously, snow is also a black body in terms of heat radiation — it absorbs and re-emits heat efficiently despite appearing white to visible light. Black objects such as crows or charcoal can become effective reflectors if their shiny surfaces are adjusted to reflect radiation at certain angles.

## Heat Storage

It is difficult to store heat for long periods in field conditions, although it can be achieved in:

- **Insulated water masses** — water has high thermal mass and stores heat effectively
- **Solid materials** such as stone and earth — absorb and release heat slowly
