# Mycology as a Field of Study

## Overview

In the late 19th century, [[mycology]] — the study of fungi — was one of the most neglected branches [[fukuoka-illusions-of-natural-science]]. Palmer's 1879 lectures and writings reveal a field shrouded in mystery, tradition, and general ignorance, yet offering rich rewards for the patient student. His observations about the state of mycological knowledge remain historically significant for understanding how the science developed.

## The State of Mycological Knowledge in 1879

### A Virgin Field for Explorers
Palmer estimated that there were probably not twelve men in America who could tell the average person much more about mushrooms and toadstools than they already knew. This profound ignorance, combined with the inherent mystery of the subject, was precisely what drew him into the field. He described it as a "virgin sea for explorers" — a realm where fundamental questions remained unanswered and where even basic taxonomy was incomplete.

### Contrast with Other Sciences
Palmer drew a striking contrast between mycology and other natural sciences. In any large city bookstore, one could buy a treatise explaining geology, botany, or astronomy in familiar terms. These sciences had been simplified, bound to natural laws, and made to reveal their secrets even to children. Not so with mycology. The subject remained wrapped in "a labyrinth of tradition, error, and uncertainty," and even among those who had removed these elements, confusion and doubt persisted.

## The Fundamental Challenge of Fungal Identification

### No Systematic Path
The most frustrating aspect of mycology, in Palmer's view, was that it could not be learned systematically. In many sciences, one step leads logically to the next, and a given plan produces a certain result. Mycology defies this pattern entirely. Palmer compared fungi to the Chinese language: each character means something, but you cannot reason from one to define the position or meaning of the next.

### Each Species Must Be Learned Individually
With roughly one thousand species of good-sized fungi — many with distinctions so slight that an amateur would not notice them — there is no shortcut to competence. Each species must be identified separately, its habits, place of growth, taste, and qualities learned through direct experience. The microscope reveals structural differences even between the nearest allies, and the most careful student finds that skill cannot be imparted to those outside the field.

### The Limits of Description
Palmer acknowledged the inherent inadequacy of words for conveying fungal knowledge. Every little fungus crushed beneath one's tread, every mould creeping through cheese, every fine-spun web over stale bread has its own distinct structure. No amount of verbal description can substitute for [[fukuoka-direct-observation-method]] and handling of specimens.

## The Mushroom-Toadstool Myth and Public Education

### The False Dichotomy
The most common question Palmer received in his walks was: "Please show me how I can tell a mushroom from a toadstool." When he answered that he knew of no such rule, questioners believed him "a harmless lunatic." People invariably claimed to know someone — a French cook, an old gardener, a quoted authority — who possessed an infallible rule. The truth, Palmer insisted, was that every mushroom is a toadstool and every toadstool is a mushroom.

### The Single-Species Illusion
Palmer explained the persistence of this myth through what might be called the "single-species illusion." Out of approximately one thousand good-sized fungi, the average person who claims to distinguish [[distinguishing-mushrooms-from-toadstools]] can infallibly recognize only one variety: the common pasture mushroom (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*). The other nine hundred and ninety-nine species are all "toadstools" to them. They know that a certain fungus with a white top, pink underneath, delicious fragrance, and pleasant taste can be found in a certain pasture at a certain time. Everything else is classified as dangerous.

### Why This One Species Dominates
The common mushroom's dominance in the popular imagination is partly due to its unique cultivability. It is the only species of fungus ever propagated artificially, and so far as was known in Palmer's time, the only one susceptible of cultivation. It follows horses through every climate and can be raised from horse manure. The gardener takes spawn (a mass of white threads analogous to roots), plants it in a bed of loam and rotted [[compost]], and produces a crop in six weeks. Hence, it became "the mushroom" of the civilized world.

## The Hobby and Its Value

### The Philosophy of Amateur Study
Palmer opened his lectures with a philosophical reflection on the value of having a hobby. A gentleman who had seen much of life told him: "No man in this world can be happy without a hobby." Palmer expanded on this: the person who passes through life without being an enthusiastic student of something loses more than they can appreciate. Nature is generous, the field is the world, and no person has the right to say their study is important and another's is useless.

### Practical Benefits
Beyond intellectual satisfaction, Palmer believed mycology had practical value. He estimated that tons of rich, wholesome food rot yearly in American woods and pastures because of general ignorance about fungi. While mushrooms were considered valueless in America where food was abundant and cheap, other nations — the Chinese, Russians, Germans, French, and Italians — used them extensively.

### Culinary Position
Palmer placed mushrooms in a unique culinary position, between animal and vegetable food, approximating the former more than the latter. Most kinds decay like meat through [[decomposition]] and maggots, rather than wilting like plants. They grow quicker than other vegetables, sometimes pushing above the soil in a single night, though most development is subterranean and requires days or even weeks.

## What Can Be Achieved

Despite the challenges, Palmer believed that a person of ordinary tact and perceptive faculties could be easily taught to identify twenty to thirty different varieties of delicious and wholesome mushrooms. The critical first lesson is learning to recognize the *Amanita* family — the one group to which deaths by poison can be distinctly traced — and reject all its species. Beyond that, safe groups like puff-balls and Boleti provide a foundation, and individual species within the Agaricini can be learned gradually.

Mushrooms appear in the same spots year after year, so the gatherer can develop regular haunts where favorite varieties are found with certainty. For Palmer, no more useful nor pleasanter object for a country walk could be found.
