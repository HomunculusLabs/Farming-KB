# Seed Germination and Stratification

## Overview

Successful [[plant-propagation]] depends on understanding the environmental triggers that cause seeds to germinate. Three primary factors govern germination: soil temperature, light exposure, and cold treatment (stratification). Each seed species has evolved specific requirements that must be met before the embryo will break dormancy. Mastering these factors allows growers to optimize planting times, improve germination rates, and successfully propagate species from diverse climates.

## Soil Temperature and Germination

### The Temperature Range

Most vegetable seeds germinate within the range of 0-38 deg C (32-100 deg F). Within this range, increasing soil temperature can reduce germination time to one-tenth or one-fourth of the time required in cold soils. The relationship between temperature and germination speed is dramatic — a few degrees of warming can mean the difference between seeds emerging in 3 days versus 3 weeks.

### The Practical Temperature Window

While the full range extends from 0-38 deg C, the practical germination window for most vegetable crops is 10-24 deg C (50-74 deg F). Almost all vegetable seeds will germinate reliably in soils at 15-20 deg C (59-68 deg F), making this the safe target range for general planting.

### Lower Limits

Many cucurbits (squash, melons, cucumbers), beans, and subtropical species refuse to germinate below 10 deg C (50 deg F). Planting these species too early in cold spring soils results in seed rot rather than germination. The solution is to wait until soils reach 10 deg C or to warm the soil using:

- **Greenhouses or cold frames**: Enclosed structures trap solar heat
- **Clear plastic mulch**: Transmits light and traps heat in the soil beneath
- **Black plastic mulch**: Absorbs radiation and transfers heat to soil
- **Exposing bare earth**: Simply removing mulch or vegetation to allow direct solar warming

### Upper Limits

Some species have upper temperature limits beyond which germination ceases. Celery, for example, will not germinate above 24 deg C (74 deg F). This is critical for summer planting in hot climates, where celery may need to be started in shaded areas or during cooler seasons.

### Measuring Soil Temperature

A simple thermometer inserted 2.5 cm (1 inch) into the soil is sufficient for monitoring. Dedicated soil thermometers are available commercially. For crop-specific requirements, reference works like Knott's Handbook for Vegetable Growers (Maynard and Lorenz, 1980) provide detailed tabulations.

## Light and Germination

### Light-Requiring Seeds

Some seeds require exposure to light as a germination trigger. Carrots are the classic example — they need a definite quantity of light and are typically surface-planted or only lightly covered. Strategies for light-requiring seeds include:

- **Surface scattering**: Broadcast seeds on the soil surface without covering
- **Pre-soaking and light exposure**: Soak seeds overnight, then expose them to a day under a low-wattage bulb or in open sunlight before planting and covering lightly
- **Critical detail**: Seeds only respond to light exposure if they have been wetted first

### Dark-Germinating Seeds

Larger seeds generally accept burial and germinate in darkness. Some weed species and desert seeds have evolved to germinate when deeply buried, giving them access to more stable moisture conditions deep in the soil profile.

### Flash Germination Triggers

Some species respond to extremely brief light exposure. Wild tobacco seeds, for example, can be triggered to germinate by a mere flash of light — such as occurs when a clod of soil is turned over during cultivation. This adaptation ensures the seed germinates when it is brought near the surface where seedlings can emerge successfully.

## Stratification (Vernalization)

### What is Stratification?

Stratification is the exposure of seeds to cold, moist conditions that mimic the natural winter experience of seeds lying on the forest floor or in marsh litter. Many seeds from cold-climate trees, shrubs, and perennials will not germinate without this cold treatment. The process breaks chemical dormancy mechanisms that prevent germination during unfavorable seasons.

### The Cold Treatment

Seeds from boreal or cold-area species (especially trees and berries) need exposure to temperatures of approximately 0-5 deg C (32-40 deg F) for an extended period, typically corresponding to the autumn-to-spring interval in their native climate. This replicates conditions at the litter level in cold forests or marshes.

### Methods for Stratification

**Refrigerator method (most reliable for small quantities):**
- Store seeds in damp sand, peat moss, or vermiculite
- Place in a sealed container or plastic bag in the refrigerator
- Check periodically for signs of germination in late winter and spring
- Pot out seeds as they begin to sprout

**Natural cold spots:**
- Bury seed containers in cold, shaded valleys
- Place under open, cool trellis structures in warm climates
- Store in unheated sheds or cold frames

**Aquatic seeds:**
- Wild rice and other "soft" aquatic seeds are stored in open ponds
- Alternatively, keep submerged in water in an ordinary refrigerator

### Heat Treatment: The Opposite of Stratification

Many tree legume seeds have hard, impermeable seed coats that require heat treatment rather than cold. Methods include:

- **Oven treatment**: Heat at 95 deg C (200 deg F) for 10-20 minutes
- **Hot water soak**: Pour near-boiling water over seeds and let them soak as the water cools
- **Light straw fire**: Briefly burn seeds in a light straw fire to scarify the seed coat

These treatments crack or weaken the hard seed coat, allowing water penetration that triggers germination.

## Biological Seed Treatment

### Passage Through Digestive Systems

Many traditional gardeners use a biological approach to seed preparation. Seeds are fed to animals (chickens, cattle) or consumed by people (in sandwiches), and the manure is collected, made into a slurry, and sown. This method works for tomatoes, berries, and tree legumes, among others.

The digestive passage provides a compound treatment:
- **Acid/alkali exposure**: Stomach acids weaken seed coats
- **Temperature variation**: Body heat followed by cooler external temperatures
- **Mechanical scarification**: Chewing (for humans) or grinding in bird crops cracks seed coats
- **Nutrient packaging**: Seeds are deposited in manure rich in organic matter and moisture

Many wild seeds are specifically adapted to this process — some germinate only after passing through the gut of a specific animal.

## Flowering and Day Length

### Photoperiodism

Flowering plants are adapted to bloom and set seed in response to specific day lengths (more precisely, night lengths). This adaptation ensures flowering occurs during favorable seasonal conditions. There are three categories:

- **Day-neutral plants**: Flower whenever other conditions (temperature, moisture, maturity) are satisfactory, regardless of day length
- **Short-day plants**: Require days shorter than a critical length to flower. Tropical species are typically short-day plants
- **Long-day plants**: Require days longer than a critical length to flower. Temperate species are typically long-day plants

### Practical Applications

Day-length sensitivity can be exploited for production:
- **Foliage crops**: Transferring tropical short-day corn to temperate long-day climates produces excellent green-leaf fodder without flowering. Similarly, tobacco moved to cool areas produces leaf rather than going to seed
- **Ornamental foliage**: Some decorative plants produce better foliage when prevented from flowering by day-length manipulation

### The Importance of Local Seed Sources

Day-length requirements are the reason for choosing varieties from local growers and selecting for flowering when introducing new species. Plants moved to different latitudes may never flower, produce seed, or may behave unpredictably. In New Guinea highlands (short days), cabbages from long-day climates may never flower and can reach 1-3 meters in height, with leaves harvested repeatedly for fodder.

### Latitude and Day Length

- **Low latitudes (0-30 deg)**: Tropical climates with cold mountain regions; roughly equal days and nights year-round
- **Mid latitudes (30-50 deg)**: Cool to temperate climates with boreal mountain regions; long summer days and short winter days
- **High latitudes (>50 deg)**: Very long summer days, short or no winter daylight; dramatic seasonal variation

## Key Principles

1. **Most vegetables germinate best at 15-20 deg C (59-68 deg F)** — this is the safe target range
2. **Know the lower and upper limits** for each species — planting outside these ranges wastes seed
3. **Warm the soil before early planting** — clear or black plastic mulch, greenhouses, or bare earth exposure
4. **Light-requiring seeds** (carrots, many small seeds) must be surface-planted or pre-soaked and light-exposed while wet
5. **Cold-climate tree and berry seeds need stratification** at 0-5 deg C for weeks or months
6. **Hard-coated legume seeds need heat treatment** — hot water, oven, or light fire
7. **Digestive passage** is a traditional and effective compound seed treatment
8. **Day length controls flowering** — always source seed locally or select for local photoperiod adaptation
9. **A simple soil thermometer at 2.5 cm depth** is the most important germination tool
