Fungi produce a diverse array of extracellular fungal enzymes systems that
enable them to decompose complex plant polymers including lignin, cellulose,
hemicellulose, and pectin, with the specific enzymes deployed determined by
substrate composition and the resource quality of the material being colonized.

## Resource Quality as a Determinant of Decomposition

The rate at which a resource is decomposed is dependent on its chemical composition
(Heal and Dighton, 1985; Heal et al., 1997), [[cannabinoid-microbiome-correlation-confounded-edaphic-factors]] including available
moisture and temperature, and the colonization of the resource by appropriate
saprotrophic organisms (Cooke and Rayner, 1984).

The input of different types of plant litters, varying in chemical composition
and hence resource quality, varies substantially with ecosystem type
(Dickinson and Pugh, 1974; Cadish and Giller, 1997). The general consensus is
that the c:n ratio and lignin:nitrogen ratios can be used as determinants
of the resistance of resources to decomposition and ultimate mineralization of
nutrients (Melillo et al., 1982).

Where the c:n ratio or lignin:N ratios are high, there are reduced rates of
decomposition rate, compared to resources containing lower ratios. High
c:n ratio means decomposer organisms must immobilize nitrogen from the
environment to support their growth, slowing net nutrient mineralization.

## The (Lignin+Polyphenol):N Ratio

Other secondary chemicals produced by plants, particularly polyphenols and tannins,
also inhibit decomposition rates (Harborne, 1997). These compounds can directly
inhibit microbial enzyme activity and bind to proteins, making nitrogen less
available to decomposer organisms.

Vanlauwe et al. (1997) showed that there often was little negative relationship
between nitrogen mineralization from leaf litter and either the polyphenol:N
or lignin:N ratio individually. Both rates of decomposition (mass loss) and
nitrogen mineralization rates are, however, strongly correlated to the combined
(lignin+polyphenol):N ratio.

Vanlauwe et al. (1997) provided regression analysis showing the comparative
predictive power of various resource quality indices on decomposition rate
and nitrogen release from leaf litter:

- **C:N ratio**: regression coefficient of 0.74 for litter mass loss (significant
  at α = 0.05), 0.61 for N mineralization
- **Lignin:N ratio**: regression coefficient of 0.68 for litter mass loss, 0.42
  for N mineralization
- **Polyphenol:N ratio**: regression coefficient of 0.54 for litter mass loss,
  0.76 for N mineralization (significant at α = 0.05)
- **(Lignin+polyphenol):N ratio**: regression coefficient of 0.77 for litter
  mass loss, 0.68 for N mineralization (significant for both at α = 0.05)

The combined (lignin+polyphenol):N ratio was the strongest overall predictor,
capturing both the structural resistance of lignin and the chemical inhibition
by polyphenols, normalized to nitrogen available to decomposer organisms.

## Fungal Enzyme Systems for Lignin Degradation

[[lignin-degradation]] is the most recalcitrant plant polymer and requires a highly specialized
suite of fungal enzymes for its degradation. The enzyme system includes:
lignin peroxidase, [[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]], glucose oxidase, cellobiose oxidase,
arylalcohol oxidase, glyoxaloxidase, and laccases (Sinsabaugh and Liptak, 1997).

[[staycare-lignin-peroxidase-and-pollutant-degradation]] manganese peroxidase are heme-containing enzymes requiring
[[cervantes-hydrogen-peroxide-sterilization]] as co-substrate to generate reactive oxidant species capable of
cleaving stable carbon-carbon and ether bonds in lignin. Laccases are
copper-containing oxidases using molecular oxygen directly. Supporting enzymes
such as glucose oxidase generate the hydrogen peroxide needed by peroxidases.

## Fungal Enzyme Systems for Cellulose Degradation

[[fungal-cellulose-degradation]] degradation is accomplished by three main classes of fungal enzymes: exo-1,4-β-glucanase, endo-1,4-β-glucanase, and 1,4-β-glucosidases
(Sinsabaugh and Liptak, 1997). Endoglucanases cleave internal β-1,4-glycosidic
bonds within the cellulose chain. Exoglucanases processively release cellobiose
units from chain ends. β-glucosidases hydrolyze cellobiose to glucose, completing
the conversion of insoluble cellulose to metabolically usable sugar.

## Fungal Enzyme Systems for Hemicellulose Degradation

[[Hemicellulose]] requires an even more diverse array of fungal enzymes than
cellulose due to its heterogeneous and branched structure. The major enzyme
systems identified by Sinsabaugh and Liptak (1997) include:

- Endo-1,4-β-xylanases and endo-1,4-β-mannases for backbone hydrolysis
- 1,4-β-xylosidases and 1,4-β-D-mannosidases for monosaccharide release
- 1,4-β-glucosidases, α-L-arabinosidases, and α-glucuronidases for side chains
- α-galactosidases for galactose side group removal
- Acetylxylan esterases and acetyl galactoglucomannan esterases for deacetylation

The structural complexity of hemicellulose—including xylans, mannans,
glucomannans, and mixed polymers with diverse side-chain substitutions—necessitates
this broad enzymatic repertoire. No single fungal species typically produces the
full complement of hemicellulase enzymes, contributing to the need for fungal
succession during decomposition of hemicellulose-rich substrates.

## Fungal Enzyme Systems for Pectin Degradation

[[Pectin]] is degraded by polygalacturonases, endo-1,4-α-polygalacturonase,
exo-1,4-α-polygalacturonase, pectinlyases (pectate lyases), and pectinesterases
(Sinsabaugh and Liptak, 1997). Pectinesterases first remove methyl ester groups,
converting pectin to pectate. Polygalacturonases hydrolyze the α-1,4-galacturonan
backbone, while pectinlyases cleave via β-elimination. Combined action facilitates
access to other cell wall components including cellulose and hemicellulose.

## Substrate Specialization and Fungal Succession

Not all materials can be utilized by all fungal species due to variability in
chemical composition of plant remains. Decomposition is fundamentally a product
of enzyme activity dependent on the specific substrates of the resource. The
ability of different species to produce specific enzymes dictates in part the
succession of fungi as they colonize resources.

Early colonizers produce enzymes for readily available sugars, while later
successional species produce ligninolytic enzymes for recalcitrant material.
Additional factors include growth rates, antibiotic [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]], and
environmental constraints (Cook and Rayner, 1984; Lockwood, 1992; Wicklow, 1992;
Frankland 1992, 1998).

Linkins et al. (1984) discussed [[factors-affecting-mycoremediation-efficiency]] extracellular cellulase
activity, particularly the positive influence of temperature and the
cellulose:lignin ratio. [[fungal-cellulose-degradation]] becomes unavailable when the
cellulose:lignin ratio declines below 0.5.

## Nutrient Control Over Enzyme Expression

Sinsabaugh et al. (1993) studied extracellular enzymes in wood decomposition,
most derived from fungal activity. Using standardized wood, lignocellulase
production did not differ between temperate forest locations. However, nitrogen
immobilization ranged from 43 to 139 mg g⁻¹ wood and phosphorus immobilization
from 2.2 to 4.4 mg g⁻¹ at 80% mass loss.

Decomposition is governed by fungal enzymes production, regulated by nitrogen
and phosphorus availability. Where nutrients are less available, fungi expend
greater energy producing enzymes to sequester nutrients from organic sources.
This suggests large edaphic control over enzyme expression, closely related to
inorganic N and P supplies in soil water.

## Modeling and Functional Diversity

Sinsabaugh et al. (1993) developed a model containing both microbial and soil
nutrient controls over fungal enzymes expression, enabling hypothesis
development for decomposition and nutrient-cycling experiments.

Zak, citing Gochenaur (1975), described greater enzymatic diversity in desert
fungal communities than in less xeric ecosystems, suggesting fungi from arid
environments may have evolved greater functional diversity to cope with extreme
spatial and temporal heterogeneity than fungi from mesic environments.
