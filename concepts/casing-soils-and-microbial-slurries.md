## Casing Soils and Microbial Slurries

Casing soils are non-nutritive (or minimally nutritive) layers applied to the surface of fully colonized substrates to create a microenvironment that stimulates fruiting in certain mushroom species. For many species, particularly those that grow on composted manure, grasses, and agricultural wastes in the wild, casing is not merely beneficial but essential — the interaction between [[mycelium]] and specific [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] in the casing layer is the trigger that initiates fruitbody formation.

## Purpose and Function of Casing

Casing soils serve multiple critical functions:

- **Moisture retention** — primordia develop in a high-humidity environment protected from desiccation
- **[[holzer-climate-zone-microclimate-creation]]** — the interface between dense substrate and loose casing provides the conditions where primordia form
- **Microbial interaction** — many species require contact with specific soil microbes before committing energy to a fruiting cycle
- **Physical structure** — ridges and surface irregularities create protected hollows where primordia can develop and swell with water

When mycelium rises from the relatively dense substrate into the less dense casing, it becomes **rhizomorphic** — bundling into rope-like strands that channel resources upward. Upon reaching the surface, growth shifts horizontally to form a dense mat of cross-connections, which is where fruiting initiates.

## Casing Soil Composition

Common casing materials include:

| Material | Role |
|---|---|
| **Peat moss** | Base medium with high water retention |
| **Coconut coir** | Sustainable alternative to peat with good aeration |
| **Vermiculite** | Improves aeration and moisture retention |
| **Perlite** | Enhances drainage and aeration |
| **Sand** | Adds structure and weight |
| **Lime** | Adjusts pH upward |
| **[[gypsum]]** | Provides calcium and improves structure |

### Ideal Properties

- **High water retention** with good aeration
- **pH neutral to slightly acidic** — matching the native environment of the target species
- **Loose structure** with minute air pockets (not packed tight)
- **Level surface** that has been raked or dibbled to create a series of ridges

### Moisture Testing

The squeeze test determines correct moisture:
- **Steady stream of water** → too wet
- **A few drops** → perfect
- **No water drips** → too dry

Note that some materials (especially coir) are hydrophobic at first and may take time to fully absorb water. Patience during initial wetting is important.

### Depth

As a basic rule, apply casing **1 inch deep for every 6–8 inches of colonized substrate** beneath.

## Which Species Need Casing

Casing is generally reserved for **terrestrial mushrooms** — species that in the wild grow on composted manure, grasses, and agricultural wastes. Most wood-decomposing mushrooms (shiitakes, oysters) do not require microbial interactions to trigger fruiting and typically do not need casing.

Species that require or benefit from casing include:

- *Agaricus* spp. (white button, portabella, almond portabella)
- *Lepiota* spp. (parasol)
- *Stropharia* spp. ([[king-stropharia]])
- *Clitocybe* spp. (blewits)
- *Morchella* spp. (morels)

## Pasteurization of Casing Soil

Casing soils should not contain mycoparasites such as *Trichoderma* spp. or other pathogens. Most growers **pasteurize** their [[casing-soil]] rather than sterilize it:

- **Low-heat pasteurization:** 130–145°F (54–63°C) for **6–8 hours** in a steam chamber
- This preserves heat-tolerant beneficial microbes while killing competitive pathogens
- Monitor temperature periodically to stay within the window

**Sterilization** is generally avoided because it destroys all microbial life. Agaricus growers have discovered that sterilized casing soils produce **zero yields** — the microbial component is essential. However, if you plan to reintroduce a specific [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]], sterilization followed by deliberate inoculation is appropriate.

### Modern Commercial Casings

[[history-of-commercial-mushroom-cultivation]] operations increasingly supplement casing soils with:
- **Time-release fertilizers**
- **Supplements high in fat and protein** as yield boosters

These go beyond traditional non-nutritive casings to actively support fruiting performance.

## Microbial Slurries: The Key to Fruiting

Many mushroom species will remain in the colonization stage indefinitely until they encounter compatible microbial partners. The mechanism is not fully understood but may involve:

- **[[query-how-do-plants-use-chemical-signals-to-defend-against-insects]]** from specific bacteria or actinomycetes
- **Physical interaction** with microbial biofilms on soil particles
- **Molecular products** — it may not be the organism itself but a specific chemical input or removal that triggers fruiting

### Collecting Microbial Source Material

The best source of compatible microbes is **soil from the stem base** of a wild specimen or recently harvested [[classification-of-cultivated-mushroom-species]]. The soil at the attachment point contains the right combination of microbes embedded in or adhering to mycelial hyphae. If no fresh mushroom is available, native soil from near where the species fruits naturally is a reasonable alternative — each gram of soil can harbor more microbial cells than there are species of life on the planet.

### Progressive Dilutions

Soil samples are prepared as a series of progressive dilutions to isolate the specific microbial population that triggers fruiting. The concept is to "chase" the right microbes by diluting until only the critical organisms remain:

1. Harvest fresh mushroom with stem base intact, including anchoring mycelium and adhering soil
2. Place sample in 250 ml sterilized distilled water (label as sample 0)
3. Shake vigorously to break apart mycelium and soil particles; settle 10 minutes
4. Fill twelve sterile jars/tubes with 9 ml sterile distilled water each (labeled 1–12)
5. Transfer 1 ml from sample 0 to tube 1 (now 10 ml total) — shake vigorously, settle
6. Transfer 1 ml from tube 1 to tube 2 — repeat through tube 12
7. Each transfer dilutes the microbial population by 10×

Theoretically, by tube 12 the dilution approaches a single individual cell — hence "[[dilution-to-extinction]]."

## Casing Dilution Assay

The dilution assay determines which microbial population level triggers fruiting, using replicates and controls for scientific rigor.

### Setup Requirements

- **12 dilution samples** × 3 replicates = 36 test containers
- **6 controls:** 3 with untreated casing + 3 with no casing at all
- **Total: 42 containers** of colonized growing medium in identical containers
- All containers should be colonized with pure mycelium in the lab
- Quart mason jars filled halfway with colonized medium work well — they leave room for 1 inch of casing plus 2–3 inches of headspace

### Procedure

**Step 1 — Prepare casing soil:** Mix ~1 gallon of moist casing soil, autoclave or steam for one hour, cool in a clean environment.

**Step 2 — Prepare test containers:** Use identical containers with identical weights of colonized medium, pre-fitted with breathing filters.

**Step 3 — Apply casing:** Cover all but 3 containers with identical weight/volume of sterilized casing. Work quickly to minimize contamination risk.

**Step 4 — Label:** Number 36 jars as dilutions 1–12 in groups of three (D1-1, D1-2, D1-3... D12-1, D12-2, D12-3). Label 6 controls (C1-1 through C1-3 for cased controls; C2-1 through C2-3 for uncased controls).

**Step 5 — Apply dilutions:** Add 1 ml of each dilution to its three jars using a sterile pipette. Apply sterile water (not dilutions) to controls. Use fresh pipette tips for each transfer.

**Step 6 — Seal and incubate:** Fit jars with foam plugs or cotton fill covered loosely with foil. Transfer to a temperature-appropriate incubation area in darkness (lab incubator or Styrofoam cooler to minimize fluctuations).

**Step 7 — Daily observation:** Check each group for signs of hyphae streaming upward into the casing. Note morphological features (strand-like, rhizomorphic, threading, knotting). Photograph and circle growing masses on clear jars. Record all observations in a lab notebook.

**Step 8 — Trigger fruiting:** After a few weeks of casing colonization, transfer jars to a fruiting area that is a few degrees cooler with diffuse natural or fluorescent light.

### Interpreting Results

The critical data point is the **dilution at which fruiting begins** — specifically, the lowest dilution that produces fruiting, adjacent to the highest dilution that does not. This identifies the microbial population threshold that triggers the fruiting response.

Controls serve to eliminate abiotic factors (chemical or physical properties of the casing soil itself) as the cause of fruiting.
