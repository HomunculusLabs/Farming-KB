# Permaculture Design Methodology

updated: 2026-04-18

Permaculture design methodology is a systematic approach to assembling conceptual, material, and strategic components in a pattern which functions to benefit life in all its forms. As presented in Chapter 3 of Mollison's Designers' Manual, the methodology integrates fabricated, natural, spatial, temporal, social, and ethical parts to achieve a whole, concentrating not on components themselves but on the relationships between them.

## The Design Definition

Permaculture design is a system of assembling conceptual, material, and strategic components in a pattern which functions to benefit life in all its forms. It seeks to provide a sustainable and secure place for living things on this earth.

The prime directive for functional design: every component of a design should function in many ways; every essential function should be supported by many components.

## Distinguishing Design from Components

Mollison differentiates design from its component parts:

- TECHNIQUE is one-dimensional -- how we do something. Almost all pre-1950 gardening books were books on technique alone; design was largely overlooked.
- STRATEGY adds the dimension of time to technique, expanding conceptual dimensions. Any planting calendar is a strategic guide. Strategy is the use of technique to achieve a future goal.
- MATERIALS are glass, mud, wood. ASSEMBLIES are the putting together of technologies, buildings, plants, and animals.

Design is a continuous process, guided by information and skills derived from earlier observations. All designs containing life forms undergo long-term change.

## Eight Design Methods

1. ANALYSIS -- Design by listing characteristics of components. List inputs (needs), outputs (products), behaviors, and intrinsic properties of each element. Then find beneficial connections between components so each serves the needs and accepts the products of others. The goal: zero pollution (unused outputs) and zero extra work (unmet inputs).

2. OBSERVATION -- Design by expanding on direct observation of a site. No complex of map overlays or computer data will ever supplant field observation for dependability. Use child-like, thematic, instrumental, or experiential approaches. Record value-free notes, develop speculations, test hypotheses, and evolve design strategies.

3. DEDUCTION FROM NATURE -- Design by adopting lessons from natural systems. Imitate both structure and process. Study how water runs, where trees grow naturally, how species propagate themselves. Set up self-generating systems: headwater plantations for downstream propagation, bird perches for pioneer seeding, nest boxes for pest-control predators.

4. OPTIONS AND DECISIONS -- Design as a selection of pathways based on decisions. At each stage, multiple options exist; the designer selects based on energy efficiency, client needs, and site realities.

5. DATA OVERLAY -- Design by map overlays. Multiple transparent maps (soils, water, vegetation, slope, access) are overlaid to reveal optimal placement patterns.

6. RANDOM ASSEMBLY -- Design by assessing results of random assemblies. Components are randomly placed and their interactions evaluated; beneficial assemblies are retained.

7. FLOW DIAGRAMS -- Design for workplaces. Energy and material flows through a system are mapped to identify inefficiencies and reconnections.

8. ZONE AND SECTOR ANALYSIS -- Design by application of a master pattern. Zones conserve on-site energy (people, wastes, fuels) by placing frequently-used components near the center. Sectors manage incoming wild energies (sun, wind, fire, water, wildlife) from outside.

## The Analysis Method in Detail

The analysis method operates without site experience -- its strength and weakness. For each component, list:
- PRODUCTS: materials, behaviors, derived products
- NEEDS: inputs required for full function
- INTRINSICS: breed characteristics, tolerances, climate responses

Key principle: a pollutant is an output not used by any other component; extra work is the result of an input not provided by another component. Connect components to eliminate both.

Example: chickens placed in an orchard provide pest control and manure (outputs serving orchard needs) while receiving forage and shelter (orchard serving chicken needs). The chicken-house can heat and be heated by a glasshouse; both receive heat from a chimney.

## Information as Resource

Resources are practical and useful energy storages; information is only a potential resource until put to use. Never confuse assembling information with making a real resource difference. This is the academic fallacy.

## Incremental Design

Most effective site planning results from incremental adaptation of proven designs -- fine-tuning successful patterns for specific climate and purpose. Known effective design units have been subjected to long tests and evolved from trials to reliable standards.

## The Golden Rule

Develop the nearest area first, get it under control, then expand the perimeter. Any soil, with effort and compost from recycled wastes, will grow a good garden -- stay close to home.

## The Eight Methods in Practice

Mollison's eight design methods are not mutually exclusive but
complementary tools that can be combined depending on site conditions and
designer preference. The methods are: Analysis (systematic site assessment
and element placement), Overview (holistic pattern reading), Design by
Deduction (applying known principles to site conditions), Lateral Thinking
(breaking assumptions to find novel solutions), Flow Diagrams (mapping
energy and material flows), Random Assembly (generating unexpected
combinations for evaluation), Data Overlay (layering maps of different
factors), and Incremental Design (starting small and adapting). Most
experienced designers develop a personal synthesis that draws on several
methods.

The Analysis method remains the most widely taught and practiced approach.
It begins with a thorough site inventory — mapping climate, soil, water,
vegetation, existing structures, and human factors — then overlays desired
elements and uses logical placement based on needs and yields. Zones
(distance-from-house frequency analysis) and sectors (incoming energies
like sun, wind, fire, and views) are the primary organizing frameworks.
The result is a site plan that minimizes energy waste by placing
frequently-used elements close to the point of use and locating elements
to capture or deflect incoming energies as appropriate.
