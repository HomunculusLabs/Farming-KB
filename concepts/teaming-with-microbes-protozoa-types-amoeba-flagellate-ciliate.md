Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic organisms that serve as crucial
intermediate consumers in the [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa]] by
releasing ammonium as waste after digesting bacteria.

### Flagellates

Flagellates are the smallest and often the first to arrive at sites of
bacterial abundance. They are propelled by one or two whip-like
flagella that function like tiny propellers. Their small size allows
them to navigate through the tiniest soil pores where larger
organisms cannot reach, giving them access to bacteria growing in
confined spaces.

A few flagellates, like Euglena (the classic freshwater flagellate of
pond water), are autotrophs and produce their own food via
photosynthesis. Most soil flagellates, however, are heterotrophic,
obtaining nutrients by consuming bacteria and other microorganisms.

Flagellates play an important role in [[root-exudates]].

### Ciliates

Ciliates are typically larger than flagellates but smaller than amoebae.
They are covered with rows of hair-like cilia that beat in coordinated
waves, resembling the oars of a Roman galley. These cilia serve dual
functions: they propel the organism through soil water and create
water currents that sweep bacteria toward the ciliate's mouth region
for ingestion. The familiar paramecium is a ciliate protozoan.

Ciliates require more oxygen than other protozoan types and are
therefore more sensitive to soil compaction and poor aeration. They
are the first to disappear when soil becomes anaerobic. Some ciliates
live in sand and engage in a primitive form of bacterial farming,
maintaining bacterial colonies as a food source.

Ciliates also form symbiotic relationships with bacteria. Some harbor
methane-generating bacteria internally, contributing to methane
production through anaerobic respiration within their bodies.

## Succession Patterns

When a bacterial food source becomes available in soil, protozoa
arrive in a characteristic progression:

1. Flagellates arrive first, exploiting their small size to access
   bacteria in confined spaces
2. Ciliates follow as bacterial populations expand
3. Amoebae arrive last, moving slowly through the soil matrix

As bacterial populations are consumed, the larger ciliates and
amoebae begin eating smaller ciliates and flagellates. This
intraspecific predation reduces pressure on bacteria, allowing their
populations to stabilize. This self-regulating mechanism maintains
[[teaming-with-microbes-classic-soil-science]]
moisture -- specifically the hydroscopic water films on soil particle
surfaces -- for movement, feeding, and reproduction. When soil dries,
most protozoa enter a dormant cyst stage. Survival time in cyst form
varies by species, with some capable of withstanding dry periods of
several years. This dormancy mechanism ensures protozoa survive
periods of drought and can resume mineralization activity when
conditions improve.
