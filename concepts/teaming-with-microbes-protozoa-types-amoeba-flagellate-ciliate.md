### Ciliates

Ciliates are typically larger than flagellates but smaller than amoebae.
They are covered with rows of hair-like cilia that beat in coordinated
waves, resembling the oars of a Roman galley. These cilia serve dual
functions: they propel the organism through soil water and create
water currents that sweep bacteria toward the ciliate's mouth region
for ingestion. The familiar paramecium is a ciliate protozoan.

Ciliates require more oxygen than other protozoan types and are
therefore more sensitive to soil compaction and poor aeration. They
are the first to disappear when soil becomes anaerobic. Some ciliates
live in sand and engage in a primitive form of bacterial farming,
maintaining bacterial colonies as a food source.

Ciliates also form symbiotic relationships with bacteria. Some harbor
methane-generating bacteria internally, contributing to methane
production through anaerobic respiration within their bodies.

## Succession Patterns

When a bacterial food source becomes available in soil, protozoa
arrive in a characteristic progression:

1. Flagellates arrive first, exploiting their small size to access
   bacteria in confined spaces
2. Ciliates follow as bacterial populations expand
3. Amoebae arrive last, moving slowly through the soil matrix
