## Rangeland Ecology and Multispecies Grazing

Rangeland ecology demonstrates that natural, polycultural systems of herbivory far
outperform cultivated monocultures of livestock, both in total productivity and in
long-term land health. Understanding the relationships between grazing animals,
vegetation, soil, and water is essential for sustainable land management.

## Sod Seeding and Pasture Renovation

[[sod-seeding]] — the direct introduction of new species into existing pasture without
full cultivation — is a key technique for pasture improvement. Minimal tillage with
tines or chisel points creates shallow furrows into which seed, trace elements, major
nutrients, and water-retaining gels are trickled. This approach prevents the spread of
rushes (*[[juncus]]*) and soil acidification that commonly results from ploughing marshy
coastal lands.

Typical seed mixes for sod seeding include:
- **Clovers** — selected for site, pH, and drainage conditions
- **Spikeroots** — [[dandelion]], plantain, and chicory for deep nutrient cycling
- **Woody browse and medicinals** — wormwood, [[tagasaste]], *Elaeagnus*, pines, willows,
  and poplars
- **Grasses** — matched to specific site and soil factors

Root cuttings of [[comfrey]], willow, and poplar can also be set out in deep-ripped
furrows, either as a pasture browse combination or as pioneers for farm forestry. In
coastal pasture establishment, clover at 0.7 kg/ha is sod-seeded after a slash of
brush and rushes and an autumn burn, with approximately one tonne of dolomite per year
applied to correct acidity.

## Rangeland Productivity: Natural vs. Cultivated Systems

Brown (1972) estimated the standing crop of large herbivores on the African savannah
at approximately **500 kg/ha** — roughly equal to the productivity of improved pastures
in humid temperate areas. However, the savannah achieves this without external fossil
energy inputs, by hosting diverse guilds of:

- **5 species of browsers**
- **11 species of grazers**
- **4 species that alternate between grazing and browsing**

This figure excludes the far greater biomass of smaller mammals, birds, insects,
burrowers, reptiles, and plant products, making it a minimal yield estimate for a
natural system. No known cultivated livestock system can surpass this productivity
without external inputs, nor can similar yields be achieved without stressing soils,
plants, or animals.

Additional yield data from degraded systems shows that rabbit clearance operations
(Tasmania, 1954) yielded approximately **1,100 rabbits per acre**, equivalent to close
to **700 kg/ha**. Such pastures also contain enormous numbers of insect larvae in
excess of this figure, suggesting that total protein yield from polycultural systems
is vastly underappreciated.

## The Stock Unit System

The stock unit is a useful concept for assessing carrying capacity. The standard unit
is a European cow and calf at approximately **450 kg liveweight**, with equivalents:

| Stock Unit Equivalents | Count |
|---|---|
| African cows | 2 |
| European sheep | 5 |
| African sheep | 20 |
| Thompson's gazelles | 25 |
| Elephants (per 10 SU) | 1 |
| Black rhino (per 10 SU) | 4 |
| Zebras (per 10 SU) | 15 |

In terms of browse-year equivalents, **one European cow browse year** equals:
- 266 blacktailed jackrabbits
- 164 antelope jackrabbits
- 18 kangaroos
- 6 sheep
- 385 ground squirrels

Kangaroos carry **58% assimilable protein** compared to only **35% for cattle**, making
them far more productive from a protein-production standpoint when well-managed.

## Range Plant Categories and Animal Diets

Range plants fall into five broad groups:

1. **Forbs** — annual and perennial herbs and bulbous species
2. **Grasses** — and other monocotyledons, annual and perennial
3. **Trees and shrubs** — browsed by specialist species
4. **Mosses and lichens** — consumed by small herbivores and cold-area species
5. **Fungi and saprophytes** — utilized by specialized feeders

Animals balance their diets very differently. Elk consume approximately 65% browse
(maximum 85%), 5% grasses, and 3% mosses, while antelope take 74% browse, 4%
grasses, and 22% forbs. These proportions shift dynamically with seasons, making a
variety of animal species essential for full forage utilization.

## Range Utilization Efficiency

Different species exploit range differently. Deer may commonly utilize **92% of a
range area**, while cattle exploit only **52%**. Deer also convert food to flesh at
efficiencies 2–3 times greater than cattle, depending on species.

Water dependency varies enormously: cattle can be expected to range only **3–7 km from
water**, whereas deer, kangaroos, goats, and small antelope can exist without free
surface water except in extreme drought conditions.

## Soil and Hydrological Impacts

### Infiltration Rates
In the first 30 minutes of rainfall, vegetative cover dramatically affects infiltration:

| Vegetative Cover | Rainfall Infiltration |
|---|---|
| 5% | 1.2% |
| 15% | 1.3% |
| 25% | 1.7% |
| 35% | 2.4% |

### Trampling and Soil Compaction
After only **9 years of stocking**, Stoddart (1955) reported:
- **44% reduction** in soil pore space in the top 2 inches
- **60% reduction** in the lower 24-inch soil layer

This corresponds to up to **five times less water** absorbed by degraded rangelands.

### Runoff and Erosion

| Vegetation Type | Runoff (% of rain) | Soil Eroded (t/ha) |
|---|---|---|
| Wheat grass (good climax) | 0.4 | 0.001 |
| Lupin and needle grass (early depletion) | 49.9 | 0.963 |
| Annual brome grass | 25.5 | 0.425 |
| Annual weed species | 60.8 | 3.094 |

A total of **65–70% plant cover** is needed to completely prevent erosion. Runoff
water from rangelands may carry **6–10% silt by volume**, with organic matter losses
measured at 500 tonnes in flows of 17,500 tonnes per hour.

## Integrated Insect Management

Unharvested species or predator removal leads to overpopulation and range damage,
which directly causes insect plagues. Canadian workers found a direct correlation
between range depletion and grasshopper outbreaks. Infested rangelands can lose **67%
of their browse** to grasshoppers, plague locusts, or Mormon crickets.
