# Permaculture Green Manure Crops

Green manure crops are plants grown specifically to be incorporated into the soil while still green, adding organic matter, nitrogen, and other nutrients. They are a cornerstone of [[soil-building-permaculture]] and [[permaculture-crop-rotation]], improving soil structure, fertility, and biological activity without importing external inputs.

## What Is Green Manure

Green manure is the practice of growing plants and then ploughing or digging them into the soil while they are still green and succulent. As the green material decomposes, it releases nitrogen, organic matter, and minerals that feed soil organisms and improve soil structure.

Green manure crops serve several critical functions: they add organic matter to the soil, fix atmospheric nitrogen (in the case of leguminous species), prevent soil erosion, suppress weeds, break pest and disease cycles, improve soil structure through root penetration, and bring up nutrients from deep soil layers.

This practice connects directly to [[permaculture-soil-strategies]] and the broader goal of closing nutrient loops within [[permaculture-design]] systems.

## Leguminous Green Manures

Leguminous green manures are the most commonly used because of their ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiotic bacteria in root nodules. When the green material is incorporated into the soil, this fixed nitrogen becomes available to subsequent crops.

Key leguminous green manure species include clovers (white, red, crimson), alfalfa (lucerne), vetch (common and hairy), field peas, broad beans, cowpeas, lupins, fenugreek, and lentils. These species connect to [[permaculture-nitrogen-fixing-trees-guide]] concepts but at an annual or herbaceous scale.

## Non-Leguminous Green Manures

Non-leguminous green manures do not fix nitrogen but contribute large amounts of organic matter and biomass. They are particularly useful for improving soil structure and adding carbon to the soil.

Common non-leguminous green manures include buckwheat (fast-growing, good for phosphorus mobilisation), rye (deep-rooted, excellent for soil structure), oats (quick-growing, good for weed suppression), mustard (biofumigant properties, suppresses soil-borne diseases), and phacelia (excellent bee forage, fast-growing).

Mustard is particularly interesting because it contains glucosinolates that, when broken down by soil microbes, release compounds toxic to certain soil-borne pests and diseases. This biofumigation effect is a form of natural pest management within [[permaculture-companion-planting]].

## Black Wattle in Rotation

Black wattle (Acacia mearnsii) demonstrates green manure principles at a tree-crop scale. Rotations with black wattle give twice the yield because of the green manure the trees provide. As a fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing tree to 25-28 meters, it produces abundant leaf litter and woody biomass.

When incorporated into farming rotations, black wattle trees can be harvested for firewood or tannin bark while their leaf litter and root nodules have already enriched the soil for subsequent crops. This tree-scale green manuring connects to [[permaculture-forestry-systems]] and [[permaculture-tree-crop-systems]].

## Integration with Tree Lupin

Tree lupin (Lupinus arboreus) is a nitrogen-fixing pioneer that can be cut hard to supply mulch rich in nitrogen. When slashed and left on the soil surface (rather than incorporated), it functions as a green manure through the [[chop-and-drop-mulching]] approach. Its fast growth and high nitrogen content make it an effective green manure for establishing new planting areas.

## Cover Crop Functions

Green manure crops often serve simultaneously as cover crops, protecting bare soil from erosion, preventing nutrient leaching, and suppressing weed growth during fallow periods between main crops. The distinction between green manure and cover crop is often blurred in practice, as the same species can serve both functions.

In [[permaculture-succession]], cover crops play a role similar to pioneer species, occupying bare ground and preparing conditions for more demanding crops. This succession-mimicking approach is fundamental to [[permaculture-design-process]].

## Green Manure in Permaculture Plant Selection

In the plant selection guide, green manure crops appear in several contexts. Vegetables or flowers can be grown, or alternatively a green manure crop could be grown to prepare the soil for future plantings. Some plants are ploughed into the soil as a green manure and are said to repel nematodes, combining green manure functions with pest management.

The choice of green manure depends on the specific soil needs: nitrogen-fixing legumes for low-nitrogen soils, deep-rooted species like rye for compacted soils, fast-growing species like buckwheat for quick cover, and biofumigant species like mustard for disease suppression.

## Timing and Management

Green manure crops should be incorporated into the soil when they are still young and succulent, before they become woody. At this stage they decompose quickly and release nutrients readily. If left too long, the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio increases and decomposition slows, potentially temporarily tying up nitrogen.

For permaculture systems that minimise soil disturbance, green manure can be managed as a chop-and-drop mulch rather than being dug in. The green material is cut at the base and left on the soil surface, where it decomposes and feeds soil organisms while protecting the soil surface. This no-till approach aligns with [[permaculture-earthworks]] principles of minimal soil disturbance.

## Seasonal Strategies

Different green manure species suit different seasons. Winter green manures like crimson clover, winter rye, and hairy vetch protect soil through cold months and add nitrogen for spring plantings. Summer green manures like buckwheat, cowpeas, and sudangrass grow quickly in warm weather and can produce multiple crops in a single season.

Selecting green manures for each season ensures continuous soil protection and nutrient cycling throughout the year, connecting to [[permaculture-cycles-and-niches-in-time]] principles of temporal design.

## Green Manure in Holzer Systems

In Sepp Holzer's approach, green manure functions are often served by the natural plant communities that develop on his raised beds and terraces. The diverse mixture of plants that colonises Holzer's beds includes nitrogen fixers, deep-rooted accumulators, and biomass producers that together perform green manure functions without deliberate sowing of cover crop mixes.

This wild, self-seeding approach to green manure is less predictable than sown cover crops but requires far less labour and management. It relies on having a diverse seed bank in the soil and creating conditions where useful plants can establish naturally. This connects to [[sepp-holzer-method]] of working with natural processes rather than controlling them.

For farmers who need more predictable results, especially in the early stages of converting to permaculture methods, deliberate green manure sowing provides a reliable bridge between conventional and ecological approaches. Over time, as soil health improves and diversity increases, the need for sown green manures may decrease.

## Green Manure in Rotation Design

When designing crop rotations, green manure phases should be included at regular intervals. A typical rotation might alternate between food crops and green manure crops, with the green manure phase rebuilding soil fertility depleted by the food crop phase.

The length of the green manure phase depends on the condition of the soil and the demands of the subsequent crop. Severely depleted soils may need a full season of green manure, while healthy soils may only need a short catch crop between main plantings. This rotational thinking connects to [[permaculture-crop-rotation]] and [[permaculture-design]].
