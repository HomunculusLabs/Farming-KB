# Albert Hofmann and the Eleusinian Potion Analysis

Albert Hofmann, the Swiss chemist renowned for his discovery of [[plutarch-and-cicero-on-the-eleusinian-mysteries]]. His contribution to *The Road to Eleusis* (1978) is
the second of three chapters, titled "A Challenging Question and My Answer."
It addresses directly whether ancient Greeks could have isolated an
hallucinogen from ergot.

## The Question from Wasson

In July 1975, R. Gordon Wasson visited Hofmann at his home in Danbury and
posed the question: whether Early Man in ancient Greece could have hit on a
method to isolate an hallucinogen from ergot that would have given him an
experience comparable to LSD or [[claviceps-purpurea]] (Fr.)
Tul., a parasitic fungus on rye, barley, wheat, and certain wild grasses.
Other species include C. paspali, C. nigricans, and C. glabra, each
parasitical to various grasses. Ergot is not of uniform chemical composition:
it occurs in biological or chemical races differing mainly in their
alkaloidal constituents.

More than thirty alkaloids have been isolated from ergot. They fall into two
broad groups: the water-insoluble ergotamine and ergotoxine type, and the
water-soluble alkaloids including ergonovine (ergometrine), lysergic acid
amide, and lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide. The pharmacological distinction
is critical. The water-soluble alkaloids are psychoactive; the insoluble
ones are not.

Medicinally the most useful alkaloids include ergotamine (isolated by A.
Stoll in 1918, used in Cafergot for migraine), Hydergine (for geriatric
disorders), and ergonovine (the specific uterotonic principle, used in
obstetrics). Hofmann himself played a central role in developing many of
these: he prepared ergonovine in 1937, discovered LSD in 1943, and later
isolated psilocybin and psilocin from the Mexican [[mexican-sacred-mushrooms-indigenous-use-and-ethnobotany]].

## The Ololiuhqui Connection

Hofmann's interest in hallucinogenic agents, originating from his work with
LSD, brought him into contact with Wasson. From Roger Heim, who had studied
Wasson's sacred mushrooms, Hofmann received samples for [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]].
With his assistant Hans Tscherter he succeeded in isolating the
hallucinogenic principles, which he named psilocybin and psilocin.

Inspired by this success, Hofmann turned to ololiuhqui, another
psychotropic Mesoamerican plant. Analysis of the [[golden-guide-morning-glory-and-ololiuqui]] seeds
([[ipomoea-violacea]]) yielded an unexpected result: the
active principles were familiar ergot alkaloids. The main components were
lysergic acid amide and lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide, both water-soluble,
closely related to LSD. Another constituent was ergonovine itself.

This was a stunning finding: the ancient Mexican drugs contained as their
psychoactive principles some of the very same alkaloids found in ergot.

## Water-Soluble Alkaloids in Mediterranean Ergot

The psychoactive property of these simple lysergic acid amides is well
established. A further finding proved of utmost importance: the main
constituents of the [[sacred-mexican-morning-glories-ololiuqui-and-tlitliltzin]] glory seeds are also the main alkaloids
in ergot growing on the wild grass Paspalum distichum, which grows commonly
all around the Mediterranean basin and is often infected with Claviceps
paspali.

Within the various kinds of ergot produced by the genus Claviceps and its
many hosts, types do exist that contain hallucinogenic alkaloids, the same
alkaloids as in the Mexican hallucinogenic morning-glories. These alkaloids
are soluble in water, in contrast to the non-hallucinogenic medicinally
useful alkaloids. With the techniques available in antiquity, it was
therefore easy to prepare a hallucinogenic extract from suitable kinds of
ergot.

Hofmann analyzed ergot of wheat and ergot of barley and found basically the
same alkaloids as ergot of rye: alkaloids of the ergotamine and ergotoxine
group, ergonovine, and sometimes traces of lysergic acid amide. Ergonovine
and lysergic acid amide, both psychoactive, are soluble in water whereas
the other alkaloids are not.

## Hofmann's Self-Experiment with Ergonovine

To test the psychoactive potential of ergonovine directly, Hofmann performed
a self-experiment on 1 April 1976. He ingested 2.0 mg ergonovine
hydrogenmaleinate (1.5 mg ergonovine base) in a glass of water. His log:

- 13:00 -- slight nausea, same effect as experienced in LSD or psilocybin
  experiments. Tired, need to lie down. With eyes closed, colored figures.
- 13:30 -- trees in the nearby forest seem to live, branches moving in a
  threatening way.
- 14:30 -- strong desire to dream, unable to do systematic work, afflicted
  by mollusk-like forms and feelings.
- 16:00 -- motives and colors clearer but bearing hidden dangers.
- 17:00 -- after a short sleep, awoke by a kind of inner explosion of all
  the senses.
- 18:00 -- forced to become active by an unexpected visit, but lived more
  in inner than outer world throughout the evening.
- 22:00 -- all effects worn off, normal feeling.

This proved that ergonovine possesses psychotropic, mood-changing, slightly
hallucinogenic activity when taken at doses comparable to effective doses of
lysergic acid amide. Its potency is about one-twentieth that of LSD and
about five times that of psilocybin.

## Darnel (Lolium temulentum) and Ergot

Finally, Hofmann discussed an ergot parasitical to darnel (Lolium
temulentum), a weed that plagues grain crops, called aira in Greek and
lolium in Latin. Its French name ivraie and German Taumellolch both point
to folk belief in its psychotropic activity. Analysis showed that the plant
itself contains no alkaloids, but Lolium species are notoriously prey to
the Claviceps fungus. Samples of ergot grown on L. temulentum and L.
perenne showed large variation but some contained substantial amounts of
ergonovine together with ergotamine-group alkaloids.

## Conclusion

Hofmann's answer to Wasson's question was unequivocal: yes, Early Man in
ancient Greece could have arrived at an hallucinogen from ergot. He might
have done this from ergot growing on wheat or barley. An easier way would
have been to use ergot growing on Paspalum distichum, which contains only
hallucinogenic alkaloids. This conclusion rests on the assumption that the
herbalists of ancient Greece were as intelligent and resourceful as the
herbalists of pre-Conquest Mexico.
