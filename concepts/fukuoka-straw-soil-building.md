## Soil Building Without Compost or Fertilizer

## The Soil Works Itself

The soil lives of its own accord and plows itself. It needs no help from man.
Farmers often talk of "taming the soil" and of a field becoming "mature," but why
is it that trees in mountain forests grow to such magnificent heights without the
benefit of hoe or fertilizer, while the farmer's fields can grow only puny
crops? Has the farmer ever given any careful thought to what plowing is?

In nature, the very grasses and trees, and the earthworm-ecology|earthworms and moles in the
ground, have acted the part of plow horse and oxen, completely rearranging and
renewing the soil. What can be more desirable to the farmer than being able to
work the fields without pulling a plow or swinging a hoe? Let the grasses plow
the topsoil and the trees work the deeper layers.

Farmers usually plow the soil to a depth of about four to eight inches, whereas

year. This is absolutely essential for microorganisms to multiply in the earth,
for the soil to grow more fertile, and for the roots of large trees to
penetrate deep into the ground. Working the soil with plow and hoe actually
interferes with these processes. If man leaves the soil to itself, the forces
of nature will enrich and loosen.

Applying [[cervantes-fertilizer-application-strain-feeding]] and weeding
bring a halt to the normal aggregation and enrichment of topsoil. Clearing new
land for agriculture by pulling up trees and bushes robs the deeper layers of
the soil of a source of [[jeavons-green-manures-cover-crops]]. This enables soil in the field, garden, and hillside
orchard to become naturally enriched. It is far wiser to plant green manure
trees and encourage the soil within the orchard to enrich naturally than to
apply fertilizer.

## The Ground Cover System

### Ladino Clover

Fukuoka planted the seeds of thirty legumes, crucifers, and grasses
throughout his orchard and came to the general conclusion that he should grow
a weed cover using ladino clover as the primary crop. Its features are
remarkable: it eliminates weeds in two to three years, improves soil down to
a depth of sixteen to eighteen inches, seed does not have to be sown again
for six to eight years, does not compete strongly with fruit trees for
fertilizer or moisture, grows back easily after being cut, and does not hinder
farming operations.

### Alfalfa for Deep Soil Conditioning

In warm regions where clover tends to lose its vigor in the summer, and in
cold, dry areas, mixed seeding with alfalfa is desirable. Alfalfa is very
deep-rooted, sending roots down to depths of six feet or more. This makes it
ideal for improving the deeper soil strata. A hardy perennial, it is resistant
to droughty and cold conditions as well as to high temperatures.

### Bur Clover

Bur clover, useful in controlling spring weeds, withers in the summer but
grows back again in the fall and suppresses winter weeds as well. A useful

organic matter. Its leaves decompose and add nutrients to the soil surface.
