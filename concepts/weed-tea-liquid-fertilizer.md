# Weed Tea and General Plant Extract Liquid Fertilizer

## Overview

Weed tea, also called general plant extract fertilizer or green manure tea, is a liquid [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] that transforms "waste" plants into a valuable garden resource.

## Nutrient Release Through Fermentation

The fermentation process that converts plant material into liquid fertilizer involves several simultaneous biological processes:

1. **Leaching**: Water-soluble nutrients (nitrates, potassium ions, simple sugars, amino acids) dissolve directly from plant tissues into the water within hours of submersion
2. **Enzymatic breakdown**: Plant cell walls containing cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin are broken down by cellulolytic and pectinolytic enzymes produced by naturally occurring bacteria and fungi. This releases bound nutrients from structural tissues
3. **Microbial decomposition**: Aerobic bacteria (Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and numerous other genera) colonize the plant material and metabolize [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]], and humic precursors that provide both immediate and sustained nutritional benefits when applied to plants and soil.

## Suitable Plant Materials

Nearly any non-woody green plant material can be used for weed tea. Some materials produce better results than others:

**Excellent materials (high [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-trace-minerals]]
- Green prunings from herbs: mint, lemon balm, borage, chamomile

**Acceptable materials (moderate nutrient content):**
- Mixed weed piles containing a variety of species
- Vegetable garden clean-up debris (non-diseased)
- Clover and other legume green matter
- Young tree leaves (before they toughen)

**Materials to avoid:**
- Woody stems and branches (decompose too slowly)
- Diseased plant material (pathogens may survive fermentation and spread)
- Seed-bearing weeds (seeds may survive and germinate when the tea is applied)
- Plants treated with systemic herbicides or pesticides (residues persist in the tea)
- Poisonous plants (ricinus/castor bean, oleander, nightshade family berries) — while unlikely to affect crops through soil application, they pose handling risks
- Eucalyptus and walnut leaves (contain allelopathic compounds that may harm other plants)

## Preparation Methods

### Basic Aerobic Weed Tea

1. Collect approximately 1 kg of fresh green plant material
2. Chop or crush the material to increase surface area for microbial colonization and [[ingham-blackstrap-molasses-compost-tea-concentration-osmotic-effects]] per 10 liters of water
2. The molasses feeds rapid bacterial reproduction, producing a more biologically active tea
3. Fermentation time is reduced to 1-2 weeks
4. The resulting tea has higher [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]:

- **Spring**: Spring weeds (chickweed, bittercress, dead nettle) tend to be high in nitrogen and moisture, producing nitrogen-dominant tea ideal for seedlings and leafy crops
- **Early summer**: Mixed annual weeds at peak growth contain the broadest nutrient spectrum. This is the optimal period for weed tea production
- **Late summer**: Weeds begin to toughen and may contain more fiber and less soluble nitrogen. Tea quality declines but remains useful. Add molasses to boost microbial activity
- **Autumn**: Use garden clean-up debris (spent annuals, fallen leaves from nutrient-accumulating plants). The tea will be lower in nitrogen but still provides potassium and trace minerals for autumn-planted crops
- **Winter**: Limited fresh material available. Use stored dried weeds, or make tea from winter-active weeds (chickweed in mild climates). Fermentation is slower in cold temperatures
