# Weed Tea and General Plant Extract Liquid Fertilizer

## Overview

Weed tea, also called general plant extract fertilizer or green manure tea, is a liquid [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-solomon]] produced by fermenting any available green plant material in water. Unlike comfrey tea or nettle tea, which use specific plants with known nutrient profiles, weed tea is a versatile, improvisational method that turns garden weeds, prunings, crop residues, and volunteer plants into usable liquid fertilizer. The concept is simple: any green, actively growing plant contains nutrients in its tissues, and those nutrients can be extracted into water through fermentation. While less predictable in composition than targeted teas, weed tea is a zero-cost, zero-waste approach to [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] that transforms "waste" plants into a valuable garden resource.

## Nutrient Release Through Fermentation

The fermentation process that converts plant material into liquid fertilizer involves several simultaneous biological processes:

1. **Leaching**: Water-soluble nutrients (nitrates, potassium ions, simple sugars, amino acids) dissolve directly from plant tissues into the water within hours of submersion
2. **Enzymatic breakdown**: Plant cell walls containing cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin are broken down by cellulolytic and pectinolytic enzymes produced by naturally occurring bacteria and fungi. This releases bound nutrients from structural tissues
3. **Microbial decomposition**: Aerobic bacteria (Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and numerous other genera) colonize the plant material and metabolize [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]], converting complex plant molecules into simpler, more soluble forms
4. **Mineralization**: Microbial metabolism converts organic nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur into plant-available inorganic forms (ammonium, phosphate, sulfate) through mineralization processes
5. **Chelation**: Organic acids produced during fermentation (citric, malic, oxalic, succinic acids) chelate mineral cations, keeping them in solution and improving plant uptake

The resulting liquid contains a complex mixture of soluble nutrients, organic acids, enzymes, [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]], and humic precursors that provide both immediate and sustained nutritional benefits when applied to plants and soil.

## Suitable Plant Materials

Nearly any non-woody green plant material can be used for weed tea. Some materials produce better results than others:

**Excellent materials (high [[solomon-manure-quality-variable-nutrient-content-horse-steer-chicken]]):**
- Annual weeds in active growth (especially those growing in fertile soil): chickweed, lamb's quarters, pigweed, purslane, dandelion leaves
- Green crop residues: pea and bean vines (after harvest), spent brassica plants, bolted lettuce
- Lawn clippings (from unsprayed lawns): surprisingly nitrogen-rich due to continuous cutting and regrowth
- Aquatic plants: duckweed, water hyacinth, pond algae — naturally high in nitrogen and [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-trace-minerals]]
- Green prunings from herbs: mint, lemon balm, borage, chamomile

**Acceptable materials (moderate nutrient content):**
- Mixed weed piles containing a variety of species
- Vegetable garden clean-up debris (non-diseased)
- Clover and other legume green matter
- Young tree leaves (before they toughen)

**Materials to avoid:**
- Woody stems and branches (decompose too slowly)
- Diseased plant material (pathogens may survive fermentation and spread)
- Seed-bearing weeds (seeds may survive and germinate when the tea is applied)
- Plants treated with systemic herbicides or pesticides (residues persist in the tea)
- Poisonous plants (ricinus/castor bean, oleander, nightshade family berries) — while unlikely to affect crops through soil application, they pose handling risks
- Eucalyptus and walnut leaves (contain allelopathic compounds that may harm other plants)

## Preparation Methods

### Basic Aerobic Weed Tea

1. Collect approximately 1 kg of fresh green plant material
2. Chop or crush the material to increase surface area for microbial colonization and [[green-cannabis-soil-flushing-nutrient-leaching]]. Smaller pieces ferment faster and more completely
3. Place in a non-metallic container (10-20 liter bucket or barrel)
4. Fill with rainwater or dechlorinated tap water to cover the plant material by 5-10 cm
5. Stir vigorously once or twice daily to maintain aerobic conditions and distribute microbial activity
6. After 2-4 weeks, the liquid darkens and develops an earthy, fermented odor
7. Strain through a coarse mesh or cheesecloth
8. Dilute 1:10 with water for soil application, or 1:15-1:20 for foliar spray
9. Add the spent solids to the compost pile — they are partially decomposed and will finish quickly

### Weed Tea with Molasses (Enhanced Microbial Method)

Adding a carbohydrate source accelerates fermentation and boosts microbial activity:

1. Follow the basic recipe but add 2-3 tablespoons of unsulfured [[ingham-blackstrap-molasses-compost-tea-concentration-osmotic-effects]] per 10 liters of water
2. The molasses feeds rapid bacterial reproduction, producing a more biologically active tea
3. Fermentation time is reduced to 1-2 weeks
4. The resulting tea has higher [[soil-microbial-biomass-measurement-ecological-significance]] and more organic acids
5. This enhanced tea is particularly effective as a soil drench to boost soil biological activity

### Thermo-Compost Weed Tea (Quick Extraction)

For rapid nutrient extraction without long fermentation:

1. Place chopped weed material in a heat-tolerant container
2. Pour boiling water over the material (1:5 ratio of plant material to water by volume)
3. Cover and let steep for 24-48 hours
4. Strain and dilute 1:5 to 1:10 with water
5. This method kills most microorganisms but extracts the bulk of soluble nutrients quickly
6. Use within 24 hours — the product is not stable for storage

## Seasonal Considerations

The composition of weed tea varies significantly throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]:

- **Spring**: Spring weeds (chickweed, bittercress, dead nettle) tend to be high in nitrogen and moisture, producing nitrogen-dominant tea ideal for seedlings and leafy crops
- **Early summer**: Mixed annual weeds at peak growth contain the broadest nutrient spectrum. This is the optimal period for weed tea production
- **Late summer**: Weeds begin to toughen and may contain more fiber and less soluble nitrogen. Tea quality declines but remains useful. Add molasses to boost microbial activity
- **Autumn**: Use garden clean-up debris (spent annuals, fallen leaves from nutrient-accumulating plants). The tea will be lower in nitrogen but still provides potassium and trace minerals for autumn-planted crops
- **Winter**: Limited fresh material available. Use stored dried weeds, or make tea from winter-active weeds (chickweed in mild climates). Fermentation is slower in cold temperatures
