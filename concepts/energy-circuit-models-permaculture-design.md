## The Basic Energy Circuit

The fundamental energy circuit (Figure 7 in Holmgren's book) shows an ecosystem as a system bounded by an environment, with energy sources flowing in, being captured and transformed by primary producers, moving through consumer levels, and ultimately being lost as heat. The key features are:

- **Energy sources**: External inputs (sun, rain, minerals) entering the system boundary
- **Primary producers**: Green plants that capture solar energy through photosynthesis
- **Consumers**: Animals and other organisms that feed on producers and each other
- **Energy storages**: Accumulations of energy in various forms (biomass, soil organic matter, water)
- **Heat sink**: Inevitable energy dissipation losses represented by an electrical earth symbol
- **Feedback loops**: High-quality energy fed back to stimulate inflows

## Key Patterns in Energy Circuits

Several universal patterns emerge from energy circuit analysis:

1. **Reducing energy and mass along the food chain**: Each trophic level receives less total energy and mass than the level below it
2. **Increasing power and value per unit**: Energy becomes more concentrated and capable of driving a wider range of processes as it moves up the chain
3. **Essential dissipation losses**: Some energy is always lost to the environment at every transformation step
4. **Feedback from storages**: Stored energy (biomass, organic matter) feeds back to help capture more energy — a positive feedback loop that accelerates system productivity

The Australian example Holmgren gives traces a simple food chain: Sun, rain, and minerals feed a eucalypt tree, which feeds a caterpillar, which feeds a bird, which feeds a goanna. At each step, total energy decreases but the energy quality (capacity to do work per unit) increases.

## Entropy and Self-Organisation

Throughout the universe, energy spreads from centres of concentration to vacant regions, tending to remain dispersed. High-quality energy degrades into lower-quality forms, reducing its capacity to do "work." This tendency toward disorder and eventual death is called entropy, and it affects every living and non-living system.

However, self-organising systems — primarily living ones — can capture and transform a limited proportion of absorbed energy. This energy is held in storages of varying form and durability for self-maintenance, growth, and capture of more energy. The stored energy is generally higher in quality than the source from which it was derived and is thus capable of driving a wider range of processes than the original source energy.

Living systems that optimise energy transformation and storage efficiency tend to prevail through evolution. This is the fundamental design imperative behind all permaculture strategies. Design by nature is not simply a metaphor but a result of the forces of self-organisation observable everywhere in the living and wider universe.

## The Distinction Between Sources and Storages

In energy circuit modelling, the distinction between sources and storages is context-dependent: what is a storage for one element is a source for another. Humans are not confined to a fixed ecological niche, so what functions as a source in one situation becomes a storage in another.

Despite this fluidity, the distinction is useful for explaining permaculture design strategies. Holmgren groups landscape-level energy storages into four key categories:

1. **Water**: Stored in vegetation canopies, soil profiles, aquifers, wetlands, and constructed dams
2. **Living soil**: Humus and organic matter that hold mineral nutrients, water, and carbon
3. **Trees**: Long-lived woody biomass accumulating carbon for decades or centuries
4. **Seed**: Genetic and energetic reserves for future propagation

## Catchment Models: From Plumbing to Ecology

Holmgren presents three models of river and catchment systems, originally developed by Tane (1996), that illustrate the shift from engineering to ecological thinking:

**Plumbing drainage model**: The old paradigm of draining water away fast. Large dams low in catchments, regulated flows too fast and constant for river health. Now largely discredited as destructive of natural resources. This model was depicted by the Murray Darling Basin Commission in its system description for the managed flow of the Murray River.

**Hydrographic model**: Recognition of the sinuous, complex nature of natural river floodplains, which slow and divert water flow optimally for maintaining natural resources. This model values seasonal variations and pulses of natural flow over the constant regulated flows of the engineering approach.

**Ecographic model**: The latest understanding, incorporating rivers and floodplains as highly productive ecosystems constantly renewing and rebuilding themselves in response to catchment change. Tane sees Aboriginal "abstract" paintings of these water-dominated landscapes as graphical descriptions of physical and biological resource patterns as an integrated whole.

## The Plumbing Model in Detail

The old plumbing drainage model treats rivers as pipes. Water is a nuisance to be removed as quickly as possible. Large reservoirs are built low in catchments, and flows are regulated to be as constant as possible. This maximises water extraction for human use but destroys the seasonal variations and pulses that riverine ecosystems evolved to depend upon.

The storages in this model are too large and too low in the landscape. The regulated flows are too fast and too constant for the sustenance of river health and productivity. Over time, this approach leads to declining water quality, loss of biodiversity, salinisation, and the eventual collapse of the very productivity it was designed to support.

## Water Storage Patterns in Nature

Natural water storage follows a pattern from ephemeral to permanent as water moves under the influence of gravity:

- **Vegetation canopies**: Hold substantial quantities of rainwater in tissues and trapped moisture
- **Forest litter and mulch**: Act as an open sponge, absorbing and holding water
- **Topsoil humus**: Provides a relatively stable moisture supply for plants
- **Deep subsoils**: Especially clay, provide very stable moisture though harder to access
- **Springs and soaks**: Sustain base flows between run-off events
- **Lakes and reservoirs**: Most common in geologically young landscapes
- **Wetlands and swamps**: Temporary storages and filters, called "kidneys of catchment landscapes"

## Nutrient Storage in Landscapes

The evolution of ecosystems and catchment landscapes in response to limited availability of mineral nutrients is more difficult to understand than water. Mineral nutrients are largely invisible, and they control the productivity of every ecosystem in subtle but important ways. Ecosystems develop against a geochemical background of nutrient imbalance and deficiency, seeking to overcome these through mechanisms that mine bedrock and catch nutrients leaking from adjacent systems.

## Applications in Design

Energy circuit thinking has practical design implications:

- **Locate storages strategically**: Position water, nutrient, and carbon storages where they can capture the most flow and serve the most functions
- **Maximise feedback**: Design systems where stored energy actively contributes to capturing more energy (e.g., shade trees that mulch and protect soil, increasing water-holding capacity)
- **Minimise dissipation**: Reduce energy losses through careful placement, stacking of functions, and seasonal timing
- **Value high-quality energy**: Recognise that energy concentrated by biological processes (seeds, compost, fertile soil) is more valuable per unit than diffuse sources

## From EMERGY to Real Wealth

All biological and mineral resources can be considered as embodied energy. The tools, infrastructure, and technology needed to support human society derive from primary energy sources collected from the natural environment. The more useful and durable forms into which we transform those sources are stores of high-quality energy — or, in Holmgren's phrase, "real wealth."

This energetic view challenges conventional economic notions of value. Because of a major disconnection between biophysical sciences and social sciences, the energetic view has little impact on normal understandings of wealth. Understanding of these energy laws was fundamental to the development of the permaculture concept itself.

## Energy Circuit Language in Practice

The Odum energy circuit language uses standardised symbols that can be combined to model systems of any complexity. Key symbols include:

- **Circle with arrow**: Energy source
- **Tank-shaped symbol**: Energy storage
- **Hexagon**: Consumer or transformer
- **Arrow with wavy line**: Feedback pathway
- **Electrical earth symbol**: Heat sink (energy dissipation)

These symbols allow designers to map the flows and storages in any system — a garden, a farm, a household, a watershed, or an economy. The resulting diagrams reveal leverage points, bottlenecks, and opportunities for intervention that may not be visible from a verbal description alone.

## Limitations and Critiques

While powerful, energy circuit models have limitations. They can oversimplify complex ecological relationships by reducing everything to energy flows. Cultural, spiritual, and aesthetic values are difficult to represent in energetic terms. The models also require data that may be unavailable for many systems, especially in developing countries.

Despite these limitations, the framework provides an invaluable check on assumptions. When a proposed design shows poor energy capture or excessive dissipation in the circuit model, it is likely to fail in practice regardless of how attractive it may appear on other criteria.
