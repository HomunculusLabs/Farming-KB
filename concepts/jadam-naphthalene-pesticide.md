Grain storage operators placed naphthalene crystals or mothballs among stored wheat, corn, and other cereals to prevent infestation by weevils (Sitophilus spp.), grain borers (Rhyzopertha dominica), and Indian meal moths (Plodia interpunctella). The vapor pressure of naphthalene at room temperature is sufficient to maintain insecticidal concentrations within enclosed storage spaces for several months, making it an effective passive protection method.
In horticultural applications, naphthalene-based soil treatments were used experimentally in the mid-twentieth century for nematode suppression. Research demonstrated that naphthalene applied to soil at appropriate rates could reduce populations of root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) by interfering with their chemoreception and movement through the soil matrix. However, the development of more effective synthetic nematicides such as methyl bromide and later organophosphate compounds largely displaced naphthalene from mainstream agricultural use. ## Mode of Action and Toxicological Profile
The insecticidal action of naphthalene operates through multiple biochemical pathways. When insects are exposed to naphthalene vapour, the compound is absorbed through the spiracles (the respiratory openings in the insect exoskeleton) and enters the tracheal system. Once inside the insect body, naphthalene is metabolised by cytochrome P450 enzymes into naphthalene-1,2-epoxide and other reactive intermediates. These metabolites cause oxidative damage to cellular membranes and disrupt normal metabolic function. Additionally, naphthalene acts as a neurotoxin by interfering with octopamine signalling — a neurotransmitter system that is present in invertebrates but absent in vertebrates, which partly explains the selective toxicity of naphthalene toward insects.
The repellent effect of naphthalene is mediated primarily through olfactory receptor neurons. Many insects possess specialised chemoreceptors that detect naphthalene at extremely low concentrations (parts per billion in air). Activation of these receptors triggers avoidance behaviour, causing insects to flee the treated area rather than remaining to feed or oviposit. This repellent mechanism is particularly valuable in stored product protection because it prevents initial infestation rather than requiring control of an established population.

## Environmental Fate and Persistence
Naphthalene released into the environment undergoes both biodegradation and photodegradation. In aerobic soils, naphthalene is broken down by bacteria such as Pseudomonas putida and various Mycobacterium species that use naphthalene as a carbon source. The half-life of naphthalene in soil ranges from a few days in warm, moist conditions with active microbial populations to several weeks in cold or dry soils. In water, photolysis by ultraviolet light is the primary degradation pathway, with a half-life of approximately 1-3 days in surface waters exposed to direct sunlight.
These degradation characteristics are relevant to JADAM practitioners because they determine how long naphthalene remains active after application and what the residual environmental impact might be. For greenhouse and storage applications, the slow degradation rate in enclosed spaces is an advantage, as it extends the period of pest protection. For field applications, the relatively rapid degradation in open environments means that naphthalene residues are unlikely to persist long enough to contaminate soil or water significantly, though JADAM still recommends avoiding application near water sources and sensitive habitats.
