# Naphthalene Pesticide (JADAM Method)

## Overview

Naphthalene pesticide is one of Youngsang Cho's documented inventions within the JADAM organic farming system. It is listed among the major inventions in JADAM organic farming alongside the wetting agent, sulfur, liquid fertilizer, microorganism solution, and all numbered pesticide recipes. Naphthalene (C10H8) is a naturally occurring aromatic hydrocarbon with well-known insecticidal and repellent properties that has been used for pest control for over a century.

## What is Naphthalene

Naphthalene is a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon with the following characteristics:

- A white crystalline solid with a characteristic mothball odor
- Found naturally in coal tar and petroleum deposits
- Has been used for centuries as a moth repellent in stored clothing
- Acts as both a contact insecticide and a volatile repellent
- Volatilizes at room temperature, creating a gaseous barrier against insects
- Molecular formula C10H8, consisting of two fused benzene rings
- Moderately soluble in organic solvents but poorly soluble in water

The approach is consistent with JADAM's broader philosophy of repurposing inexpensive, widely available industrial chemicals for agricultural use at a fraction of commercial pesticide costs.

## Potential Applications

Based on naphthalene's known properties and the JADAM framework, the method likely targets:

- Stored grain protection against weevils and moths
- Soil pest repellent when placed in planting areas before seeding
- Greenhouse pest control through vapor action in enclosed spaces
- Storage facility pest management for harvested crops
- Root-knot nematode suppression in treated soil zones

## How Naphthalene Works as a Pesticide

Naphthalene exerts its pesticidal effect through several mechanisms:

1. **Vapor toxicity**: As it sublimates, naphthalene gas is toxic to insects at sufficient concentrations, damaging respiratory and nervous systems
2. **Repellency**: The strong odor deters egg-laying and feeding by many insect pests
3. **Desiccation**: Contact with naphthalene crystals can disrupt insect cuticular wax layers

These properties make it particularly suited for enclosed spaces (greenhouses, storage bins) where vapor concentrations can build up effectively.

## Safety Considerations

- Naphthalene is toxic if ingested or inhaled in large quantities
- Use in well-ventilated areas to avoid respiratory irritation
- Keep away from food and water sources during application
- Use appropriate personal protective equipment when handling crystals
- Follow all local regulations regarding naphthalene use in agriculture
- Not recommended for direct food crop application near harvest
- Avoid use in areas where children or pets may have access

## Historical Use of Naphthalene in Agriculture
Naphthalene has a long history of use as a pesticide and repellent extending well before the development of JADAM's method. In the early twentieth century, naphthalene was widely employed in granary and storage facility management throughout Europe and North America. Grain storage operators placed naphthalene crystals or mothballs among stored wheat, corn, and other cereals to prevent infestation by weevils (Sitophilus spp.), grain borers (Rhyzopertha dominica), and Indian meal moths (Plodia interpunctella). The vapor pressure of naphthalene at room temperature is sufficient to maintain insecticidal concentrations within enclosed storage spaces for several months, making it an effective passive protection method.
In horticultural applications, naphthalene-based soil treatments were used experimentally in the mid-twentieth century for nematode suppression. Research demonstrated that naphthalene applied to soil at appropriate rates could reduce populations of root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) by interfering with their chemoreception and movement through the soil matrix. However, the development of more effective synthetic nematicides such as methyl bromide and later organophosphate compounds largely displaced naphthalene from mainstream agricultural use. JADAM's revival of naphthalene for agricultural purposes reflects a broader return to simpler, less toxic, and more affordable pest management strategies.

## Mode of Action and Toxicological Profile
The insecticidal action of naphthalene operates through multiple biochemical pathways. When insects are exposed to naphthalene vapour, the compound is absorbed through the spiracles (the respiratory openings in the insect exoskeleton) and enters the tracheal system. Once inside the insect body, naphthalene is metabolised by cytochrome P450 enzymes into naphthalene-1,2-epoxide and other reactive intermediates. These metabolites cause oxidative damage to cellular membranes and disrupt normal metabolic function. Additionally, naphthalene acts as a neurotoxin by interfering with octopamine signalling — a neurotransmitter system that is present in invertebrates but absent in vertebrates, which partly explains the selective toxicity of naphthalene toward insects.
The repellent effect of naphthalene is mediated primarily through olfactory receptor neurons. Many insects possess specialised chemoreceptors that detect naphthalene at extremely low concentrations (parts per billion in air). Activation of these receptors triggers avoidance behaviour, causing insects to flee the treated area rather than remaining to feed or oviposit. This repellent mechanism is particularly valuable in stored product protection because it prevents initial infestation rather than requiring control of an established population.

## Cost Advantages in the JADAM Framework
Naphthalene is remarkably inexpensive compared to commercial agricultural pesticides. Industrial-grade naphthalene is produced in large quantities as a byproduct of coal tar distillation and petroleum refining, making it one of the cheapest aromatic hydrocarbons available commercially. In the [[ultra-low-cost-jadam-microorganism-solution-and-soil-foundation]], this accessibility is a significant advantage. A kilogram of naphthalene typically costs a fraction of the price of even the least expensive commercial insecticide, and the effective dose rates are low enough that a small quantity can treat a large area.
This cost advantage aligns with JADAM's core mission of making effective agriculture accessible to smallholders and subsistence farmers who cannot afford commercial pesticide inputs. When combined with the [[ultra-low-jadam-wetting-agent-jwa]] — which itself costs pennies per litre to produce — the overall pest management cost can be reduced to a negligible fraction of conventional approaches. Youngsang Cho has repeatedly emphasised that the goal of JADAM is not to eliminate purchased inputs entirely but to reduce them to levels that are economically sustainable for farmers operating on thin margins.

## Integration with Other JADAM Pest Control Methods
The naphthalene pesticide occupies a specific niche within the broader JADAM pest management system. For foliar spraying during the growing season, the [[jadam-natural-pesticide-recipes]] provide targeted control of specific pest groups with minimal non-target effects. The naphthalene method, by contrast, is most suited for pre-planting soil treatment, storage protection, and greenhouse fumigation — situations where a vapour-phase pesticide is more practical than a liquid spray.
In greenhouse applications, naphthalene can be used in combination with the [[jadam-herbal-smoker]] for comprehensive pest management. The herbal smoker produces insecticidal smoke from burning plant materials, providing rapid knockdown of flying and crawling insects, while naphthalene crystals placed in corners and under benches provide residual repellent protection between smoking sessions. This layered approach — combining immediate knockdown with residual protection — is a hallmark of JADAM integrated pest management and mirrors the multi-tactic strategies used in conventional greenhouse production, but without synthetic chemical inputs.

## Environmental Fate and Persistence
Naphthalene released into the environment undergoes both biodegradation and photodegradation. In aerobic soils, naphthalene is broken down by bacteria such as Pseudomonas putida and various Mycobacterium species that use naphthalene as a carbon source. The half-life of naphthalene in soil ranges from a few days in warm, moist conditions with active microbial populations to several weeks in cold or dry soils. In water, photolysis by ultraviolet light is the primary degradation pathway, with a half-life of approximately 1-3 days in surface waters exposed to direct sunlight.
These degradation characteristics are relevant to JADAM practitioners because they determine how long naphthalene remains active after application and what the residual environmental impact might be. For greenhouse and storage applications, the slow degradation rate in enclosed spaces is an advantage, as it extends the period of pest protection. For field applications, the relatively rapid degradation in open environments means that naphthalene residues are unlikely to persist long enough to contaminate soil or water significantly, though JADAM still recommends avoiding application near water sources and sensitive habitats. The [[jadam-concentration-guidelines]] developed by JADAM take these environmental factors into account, recommending application rates that are effective against target pests while minimising non-target environmental exposure.

## Naphthalene in Stored Product Integrated Pest Management
Stored product pest management represents perhaps the most practical application of naphthalene in the JADAM system. Post-harvest losses to insects can range from 10-30% in tropical and subtropical regions, representing a devastating economic burden for smallholder farmers who lack access to modern storage facilities. JADAM's naphthalene method offers a low-cost alternative to commercial fumigants such as phosphine (produced from aluminium phosphide tablets) which, while effective, are expensive, highly toxic to handle, and increasingly regulated due to safety concerns.
For grain storage, JADAM recommends placing naphthalene crystals in permeable cloth bags at multiple locations within the storage container — at the surface, at mid-depth, and at the bottom. This distributed placement ensures that vapour concentrations are maintained throughout the grain mass rather than being concentrated only in the headspace above the grain. The storage container should be as airtight as possible to prevent vapour loss, and the naphthalene should be replaced every 2-3 months as the crystals gradually sublimate. For seed grain intended for planting, JADAM recommends removing the naphthalene at least two weeks before sowing and airing the seed in sunlight to ensure that no residual vapour affects germination rates.
The integration of naphthalene with other JADAM storage protection methods — including the [[jadam-herbal-smoker]] for storage facility disinfection and diatomaceous earth as a physical barrier against crawling insects — creates a comprehensive stored product protection programme that approaches the effectiveness of commercial systems at a fraction of the cost. This integrated approach is particularly valuable for community seed banks and farmer-managed grain reserves, where the goal is to preserve genetic diversity and food security without depending on expensive commercial inputs.

## Regulatory Status and International Considerations
The regulatory status of naphthalene for agricultural use varies significantly between countries. In many jurisdictions, naphthalene is registered as a household pesticide (primarily for moth control) but is not specifically registered for agricultural crop protection. This regulatory gap means that JADAM practitioners who use naphthalene in agricultural settings may be operating in a legal grey area, depending on local pesticide regulations. JADAM emphasises that practitioners should familiarise themselves with local regulations before using naphthalene in any agricultural context and should consider the [[jadam-concentration-guidelines]] as maximum recommended doses rather than minimum effective ones.
In the European Union, naphthalene is classified as a substance of very high concern (SVHC) under the REACH regulation due to its persistence, bioaccumulation potential, and toxicity to aquatic organisms. This classification restricts its use in commercial agricultural products but does not necessarily prohibit its use by individual growers for their own purposes. In the United States, naphthalene is registered with the EPA as a pesticide for moth control and is available over the counter, but its use on food crops is restricted. In many developing countries, naphthalene is widely available with minimal regulatory restrictions, making it an accessible option for the smallholder farmers who are JADAM's primary audience.
The contrast between the regulatory treatment of naphthalene and that of synthetic agricultural pesticides illustrates a broader issue in pesticide regulation. Many synthetic pesticides that are approved for agricultural use have toxicity profiles and environmental persistence characteristics that are comparable to or worse than those of naphthalene, but they benefit from the regulatory frameworks designed for commercial pesticide registration. Naphthalene, as an older substance that predates modern pesticide registration systems, falls outside these frameworks and is therefore subject to inconsistent and often poorly defined regulatory treatment. JADAM's approach of using naphthalene at minimal effective doses, in combination with other low-impact methods, represents a pragmatic response to this regulatory ambiguity.

## Dosage Guidelines and Practical Tips
For practitioners using naphthalene in the JADAM framework, several practical guidelines help ensure safe and effective application. For greenhouse fumigation, JADAM recommends placing 50-100 grams of naphthalene crystals in shallow, open containers distributed throughout the greenhouse space at a rate of approximately one container per 10 square metres of floor area. The greenhouse should be sealed (closing all doors, windows, and ventilation openings) for 24-48 hours, then thoroughly ventilated before re-entry. This treatment can be repeated at monthly intervals during the winter months when greenhouse pest pressure is highest.
For soil treatment before planting, naphthalene crystals can be scattered on the soil surface at a rate of approximately 5-10 grams per square metre and then lightly incorporated into the top 2-3 centimetres of soil using a rake. The treated area should then be covered with clear plastic sheeting for 7-10 days to trap the naphthalene vapour in the soil zone. After removal of the plastic, the soil should be allowed to aerate for at least one week before planting. This method is particularly effective against soil-dwelling pests such as wireworms, cutworms, and root-knot nematodes, and it complements the [[jadam-soil-foundation-work]] that JADAM recommends as the foundation of all crop production.
Personal protective equipment should always be worn when handling naphthalene crystals. At minimum, nitrile gloves and a dust mask should be used to prevent skin contact and inhalation of naphthalene dust. Eye protection is recommended when handling large quantities. Naphthalene should be stored in airtight containers in a cool, dry, well-ventilated location away from food, feed, and water sources. The [[jadam-concentration-cautions]] provide additional safety guidance specific to the Korean regulatory context but the general principles — use the minimum effective dose, maximise ventilation, and avoid contact with food crops near harvest — apply universally.
