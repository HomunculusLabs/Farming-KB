## Aquaculture Water Quality

Water quality is the single most critical factor in aquaculture success, affecting every aspect of fish health, growth, reproduction, and survival. The key parameters include dissolved oxygen, temperature, pH, salinity, flow, and nutrient levels—each interacting with the others in complex ways.

## Dissolved Oxygen

### Critical Thresholds

All life processes and [[decomposition]] in aerated waters consume oxygen. The generally satisfactory level for ponds is **5 parts per million (ppm)**. At 1 ppm, most fish species die, with only a select group of adapted air-breathers surviving in stagnant, weedy tropical still-ponds.

### Air-Breathing Fish Species

Several fish species possess specialized adaptations for surviving low-oxygen conditions:

- **Snakeheads** (*Ophiocephalus*): Possess air-breathing organs
- **Singhi** (*Heteropneustes*): Catfish with accessory respiratory structures
- **Walking catfish** (*[[clarias]]*): Has labyrinth organs near the gills for atmospheric oxygen extraction
- **Mahi** (*Notopterus*): Air-breathing freshwater fish
- **Climbing perch** (*Anabas*): Can breathe atmospheric air through specialized organs

These species enable productive aquaculture even in oxygen-depleted waters. Derelict swampy ponds can yield 2,400 kg protein per hectare per year when stocked with a polyculture of three or more air-breathing species.

### Oxygen Dynamics

Less oxygen dissolves in warm water than in cold water, and aquatic plants that produce oxygen by day consume it at night. This creates a dangerous situation in healthy, weed-filled shallow ponds: severe oxygen deficiency can develop on warm summer nights. For ponds holding valuable fish stocks, both waste reduction and automatic aeration at critically low levels are necessary.

Oxygen supply becomes a limiting factor at fish stock densities above approximately 5,000 kg/ha, at which point supplemental aeration via air bubbles is required.

### Aeration Methods

Several approaches to pond aeration are available:

- **Commercial aerators**: Many models available, some solar-powered
- **Gravity-fed fountains and showers**: Effective where water can be led from a 2 m or greater head
- **Flowforms**: Sculpted water channels that create vortical flow patterns, oxygenating water as it passes through
- **Cone aerators (trompe)**: Simple devices that entrain air into flowing water
- **Rippled falls and weirs**: Natural-looking aeration in stream beds

A significant energy saving is achieved by aerating only part of the pond during critical periods—fish will naturally gather ("kettle") in the oxygenated zone. For eels, the aerated section is designed with a narrow pass to the main pond to prevent the oxygenated water from escaping.

### Combining Feeding and Aeration

It is often ideal to combine the feeding station with the oxygenated area so that food wastes are also oxidized, simplifying waste control. Fish naturally congregate where food is provided, so positioning the aerator at the feeding point ensures maximum benefit.

## Water Classification

Waters are classified by their oxygen status:

### Aerobic
Oxygen is well-supplied by wind overturn, turbulent flow, aerators, or rapids, with a light load of decaying organic material. These conditions support the widest range of fish species.

### Facultative
Common in swamps and weed-clogged ponds, and secondary treatment sewage ponds. The surface may be aerated, but sediment collects in cold periods and the pond base becomes anaerobic. Sulphur-fixing bacteria decompose the sediments in these zones.

### Anaerobic
Found in primary sewage ponds and over-fertilized shallows with low oxygen status (1 ppm or less). Only the most tolerant species survive these conditions.

## Temperature

### The 21°C Divide

At approximately 21°C (70°F), a categorical difference separates **coldwater fish** (for which such temperatures are lethal or debilitating) from **tropical fish** (which suffer cold stress or will not breed below this point). A few species like *[[gambusia]]* (mosquito fish) are temperate-tolerant or eurythermal, surviving across a range of 1–36°C.

Optimum feeding and spawning temperatures:
- **Coldwater fish**: 15–18°C (rainbow trout die if kept above 14°C for extended periods)
- **Tropical fish**: 20–25°C ([[tilapia]] die below 12°C)

### Temperature Management Strategies

Higher temperatures stimulate plant growth, algae production, and general biological turnover. Maintaining optimal winter temperatures can extend growing periods by 30–40% and increase yields 2–5 times:

- **Solar ponds**: Heated water bodies that transfer heat to adjacent ponds
- **Waste heat**: Utilizing heated water from power stations, salt works, or food processing plants
- **Glazed canals and refuges**: Glass or plastic covers over waterways to retain heat
- **Raft columns**: Insulated floating structures as heat refuges
- **Deep kettles**: 5 m or deeper pockets in shallow ponds where fish can shelter from cold
- **Earth-covered pipes**: Subsurface heat exchange systems
- **[[thermosiphon]] collectors**: Passive solar heating using buried pipes under dark surfaces (bitumen roads, cement, rocks)
- **Glasshouses**: Enclosing small ponds for winter production
- **Compost heat**: Utilizing decomposition heat from compost piles near ponds

Pond water pumped over a dark roof, rocks, or cement areas extracts solar heat at close to 100% efficiency. Heat pumps from canals can shorten fish or plant maturation times to approximately one-third of those in cool, open pond areas.

## Acidity-Alkalinity (pH)

### Problematic Soils

Ponds in areas of peats, mangroves, cordgrass flats, samphires, and water derived from heaths, granites, or siliceous soils can be very acidic (pH 4.0 or less). The acids involved include humic acids, tannins, and minor organic acids. Hydrogen sulphide may also be released by ponded peats or swamps, creating sulphuric acid.

Remediation methods include peat stripping, mounding, fresh-water or rain leaching, and liming. Below peats, especially in basaltic soils, bluish clays produced under reducing conditions may occur.

### Ideal pH Range

Pond pH is ideally **6.0–8.0** or even up to 8.5. Very few fish grow well below these levels; notable exceptions include salmonids, which are also one of the few satisfactory fish in monocultures. Lethal limits for most fish are pH 3.7 (acid) and 10.5 (alkaline).

### Creating a pH Mosaic

In practice, a pH mosaic across a series of ponds is desirable. Many valuable food organisms prefer soft (acid) water, while fish, molluscs, and freshwater lobsters and prawns prefer hard (alkaline) waters. Crushed dolomite, marble chips, hard limestone gravels, and oyster or mussel shells provide slow-release calcium in tanks, small ponds, and canal systems.

For soft-water species, organic acids (hydrochloric, humic, or phosphoric) are used, or peat/moss filtration systems create the desired acidity. The interaction between pH, respiration, and plant density is complex: pH may rise at night or in clogged coastal waters with Chara and other algal forms, or fall as peats form and anaerobic conditions develop.

## Salinity

### Salinity Classification

| Classification | Salinity (ppt) | Notes |
|---------------|----------------|-------|
| Fresh water | <1 | Very little dissolved salt |
| Coastal lagoons | 7–9 | Upper limit for frog breeding; safe for human consumption |
| Brackish (detectable) | 11–12 | Estuarine conditions; oysters begin appearing |
| Marine organisms zone | ~27 | Many shellfish and marine organisms |
| Open sea | 33–35 | Full marine salinity |
| Hypersaline | 40–60 | Few organisms spawn above 40–50 ppt |

### Species Tolerance

Fish that live their entire lives in freshwater rarely do well above 8 ppt. However, many fish and shrimp migrate between fresh and salt water—some spawning in rivers (trout), some in the sea (eels). These **euryhaline** species, including many mullet, salmonids, some oysters and shrimp, tolerate a wide salinity range. Saltwater fish rarely tolerate salinity below 28 ppt.

### Estuarine Gradients

In estuaries, a **halocline** (salt-fresh interface) can develop, with denser salt water at depth or pushing upstream as a tidal wedge below fresh water. Marine forms live at depth while freshwater fish inhabit the surface, sometimes for many miles inland. Extreme temperature and salinity changes occur with tidal cycles: a mud-living shellfish may experience fresh water at 4°C at low tide and seawater at 20°C at high tide.

### Salinity Management

In pond culture, salinity is controlled through flood by-pass canals, sea weirs, and locks. A salinity mosaic can be established to mix species as diverse as carp and mullet. Brief immersion in salt water is a traditional remedy for reducing fungal disease in freshwater fish.

The hazard of marine pond culture in tropical regions is torrential rain, which can dilute seawater enough to kill lobsters, prawns, or milkfish in marine impoundments—even with flood diversion drains.

## Flow

Even modest flow stimulates gaseous exchange and photosynthesis, promoting plant growth up to the point where mechanical damage by turbulence occurs. Flow in food-rich waters increases yields far beyond what is possible in still ponds, though most streams lack heavy natural food yields unless fertilized or manured.

The design balance is to maintain sufficient flow for aeration and vigorous plant growth while restricting flow enough to prevent rapid leaching of soluble nutrients and calcium. For this reason, flow is often piped or channeled to fish and terrace areas rather than working directly in the stream, where floods can cause fish losses, structural damage, and fertilizer washout.

A practical technique is holding back flow for a few days to allow fertilizer uptake, then resuming flow for aeration.
