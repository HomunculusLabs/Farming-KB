# Site Resources and Access Placement

In [[permaculture-design]], the identification of site resources and the strategic placement of access infrastructure form the foundational moves from which all other design decisions flow. [[bill-mollison]] emphasizes that before any planting, building, or earthwork, the designer must systematically catalog every resource on and around the site and then establish access as the very first design act. The quality of these early decisions determines whether a site becomes a thriving, energy-storing system or a source of perpetual expense and conflict.

The designer's own senses are the best compound tool for this initial assessment: judging wind, temperature, evaporation, and slope through direct observation. Experience is the second best tool. Only after these should the designer apply [[maps]], photography, and other instruments.

## Categories of Site Resources

### Intrinsic Resources

Resources intrinsic to the site fall into several distinct classes:

- **Earth resources**: Soil types, clay deposits, mineral deposits, stone, and topographic features. A client may sit on a literal gold mine or a valuable clay seam without knowing it. The designer's job includes identifying these and advising the client to secure mineral rights before others stake claims. On one documented case, a client discovered both a gold mine and another mineral deposit on property he thought contained only an old hole in the ground—previous leases had lapsed, leaving the resources available for exploitation.

- **Plant and biological resources**: Existing forests, biomass, crops, animal forage, and insect populations. Forest management is a critical resource consideration. A fire sweeping through unmanaged forest destroys hundreds of tons of biomass. Managed forests, by contrast, can be thinned, swaled, and directed toward producing far more biomass than they would at a natural "crowded standstill" with only slight accretion value. Removing dead wood manually does not drop the soil twelve inches the way a catastrophic fire will. Active forest management—swaleing, encouraging new growth—channels far more energy through the forest system than passive neglect.

- **Energy resources**: Wind, water power, wood, oil, and gas. Mollison warns that wind power is increasingly being classified alongside mining resources by governments and multinational corporations. If a client has a good wind site, they should stake it out immediately and secure exploitation permits. The cost of staking a claim is minimal; the consequence of inaction is losing control of a valuable resource to an outside entity. The same diligence applies to clay deposits and any other exploitable energy or mineral resource.

- **Water as a salable mineral**: Clean water is a marketable commodity. Clients with good water sources may be able to sell it—after analyzing quality. However, in regions with acid rain, selling water means selling acid. Clients should also secure water rights on their own hillside before being bypassed by external claims. Coastal clients may need special rights to collect seaweeds, driftwood, or shells.

### Extrinsic Resources

A category frequently missed by consultants: resources that lie in the district rather than on the site itself. These include:

- **Market resources**: Demand for products the site could supply, such as soil conditioners, tanks, or [[permaculture]] establishment equipment. The designer should identify unfulfilled district demands. As an example, Mollison notes that if a region is short of tanks, anyone selling them next year could do well—even through simple pamphlet distribution rather than holding inventory.
- **Waste products**: District wastes that could serve as inputs for the client's systems, creating beneficial cycles between the site and its surrounding community.
- **Unfulfilled demands**: Gaps in the local economy that the client could fill, ideally securing distributor or manufacturing rights rather than merely acting as an agent.

Securing these extrinsic connections, Mollison states, can earn the designer's fee before any design work begins. The designer serves as a resource detective, increasing the number of ways in which the site stores energy and builds soil fertility rather than depleting them. If the client won't cooperate with these strategies, Mollison dryly suggests pointing them toward the agriculture department or forestry commissioner instead—[[bill-mollison]] work on an ethical basis and will not simply instruct a client to cut their forest down.

### Client Resource Rights

The designer must investigate what permits, licenses, and rights the client needs to exploit on-site resources. This includes wind power permits, clay mining rights, water rights, and collection rights for coastal clients. Mollison advises engaging a good lawyer to check for covenants before the client purchases land. Assembling the full resource inventory—earth, biological, energy, and social resources that may require rights—is a core professional responsibility. In some jurisdictions, failing to stake a claim on wind or mineral resources means losing them to corporations who can legally stake them out from under an unsuspecting landowner.

## Access Road Design

### Placing Access: The First Design Move

**Placing access is the first move in design.** This cannot be overstated. Everything else—house sites, water systems, agricultural zones—fits into the framework established by the access network. A misplaced road costs more over time than almost anything else on the site, including the house itself. The designer must pay much attention to access; it is there that the client can be saved the most money.

### Keyline Principles for Road Placement

Access roads designed according to keyline principles minimize maintenance and long-term cost. Specific guidelines include:

- **Run gently across slope** with turns placed on plateaus rather than on steep grades.
- **Follow ridge tops**: In rough country, the center of ridges is often the only viable road location. Ridge-top roads drain naturally and avoid the erosion problems of valley roads.
- **Valley roads** along rivers exist but require high maintenance and should be avoided where possible.
- **Finish access with an upgrade to the dwelling**, even if the road must drop slightly to achieve this. A descending approach carries water around the house—a persistent nuisance—and eliminates the possibility of a gravity roll-start when a car battery is flat.
- **Sun exposure matters**: In winter climates, siting the access road in the sun reduces shoveling. Windbreak plantings can stop snow drifting on the road. An access road can serve multiple functions simultaneously while still protecting against fire.

The designer must also determine who maintains existing access roads, whether a new road will be publicly maintained, and explain the reasoning behind every placement decision to the client. A road built according to keyline principles, even with an extra mile of initial travel, can save thousands of dollars over its lifetime through reduced maintenance alone.

### Community and Multi-Dwelling Access

When designing for groups or land trusts, the designer must insist that house sites be professionally selected and connected by a single access system. Mollison provides a vivid example: a hippie community bought a long valley with public road access. Every member either headed for the ridge or scattered down into the bush, creating a succession of roads running up and down slope. They should have built on mid-slope positions with a single access road only a third as long and indefinitely sustainable. All those roads began gulling out almost immediately. As Mollison puts it: "You don't have the right to do that to land. Nobody has the right to do that to land."

## House Site Selection

### Criteria

After access, house site selection is the next critical decision. Key criteria include:

- **Thermal position**: The site must receive adequate sun and be sheltered from cold winds. Sixty percent of energy efficiency is lost through exposure; solar collectors drop to 40% efficiency in high winds.
- **Avoid fire tunnels**: Valleys funnel wind and fire. A house on a ridge within a valley catches everything the valley channels.
- **Ridge positioning**: The ideal site is just below a ridge top, not on it. The occupant loses almost nothing in view but gains enormous thermal and wind protection. Mollison notes that bare ridge-top houses change hands every four years because nobody can stand the misery and energy expense. A happy place, by contrast, may change hands only three or four times in 200 years.
- **Appalachian sites**: A minor ridge between two major ridges, backing into the mountains while facing the sun, is an ideal configuration seen repeatedly in traditional Appalachian settlements. This "saddle" position between two higher ridges provides shelter from cross-winds while maintaining solar access and views.

The absence of trees around a house site is another indicator of poor siting. As a rule, the fewer trees around a dwelling, the more frequently it resells. Lack of trees is miserable for both cattle and people—a well-sited, well-treed homestead may change hands only three or four times in two centuries.

### Type-One Errors

Mollison identifies two catastrophic siting errors—type-one errors—that designers must absolutely avoid:

1. **Siting on ridges**: Exposes occupants to fire, cold, and relentless wind. These sites are also vulnerable to mud flows and volcanic hazards. The designer should maintain a checklist of natural catastrophe risks when writing reports. Beyond physical danger, ridge-top living is miserable: bare ridge-top houses change hands every four years because nobody can tolerate the energy expense and exposure.
2. **Siting in the bush**: Placing a house in a small clearing within forest or scrub creates immediate and irreversible conflict between the occupants and local wildlife. From the moment people arrive, animals race for their gardens and food stores. The designer has an ethical obligation to prevent this outcome by either choosing a different site type or providing complete exclusion (fencing, glasshouses).

## The "Redneck Conversion" Problem

The bush-siting error leads to what Mollison calls the "redneck conversion"—one of the most memorable and ethically charged concepts [[ecological-succession-in-permaculture-design]]. Permaculture clients are typically gentle, conservation-minded people who love wildlife. When placed in the bush, they become targets for every local animal seeking food. The result is a cycle of killing: woodchucks, wallabies, possums, even prized trees like white pines that attract wildlife and prevent food production within 40 feet. The site gradually begins to look like "a bit of penicillin in the middle of bacteria"—everything around the clearing gets murdered.

The client develops a guilt complex, heads back to town, and eventually abandons the site to someone else willing to carry out the ongoing destruction. Mollison is blunt: the designer who causes this has committed a serious error. What turns a gentle vegetarian into someone who beats possums with a club is constant, unavoidable killing—and the designer created the conditions for it. This is not merely an inconvenience; it is an ethical failure that violates the permaculture ethic of care for all living systems.

### Retrofit Design vs. New Design

Designers may find themselves in one of two positions when beginning a project, each presenting distinct challenges and opportunities:

- **Retrofit design**: Some infrastructure already exists and must be worked around. The most difficult and valuable retrofit work is often around dwellings, where most energy is burned. Mollison suggests that for a farm client, starting around the house—crawling under foundations, auditing energy use—can save far more money than any farm-level intervention. Retrofit is inherently harder because the most damaging decisions have already been made and locked in by existing construction. The designer must work within constraints imposed by prior mistakes, which is why retrofit jobs demand even more creativity than greenfield designs.
- **New design**: Nothing has yet happened. This is, Mollison says, "a very happy state of affairs" because the designer has freedom to place access and structures optimally from the start. The designer should advise the client to begin making their own plans and setting priorities during this phase, while the professional analysis is being prepared.

## Designing for Fully Woodded Sites

When a client owns completely forested land, the designer should first have a long discussion about alternatives to clearing. The instinct to carve out garden space from forest is strong but often misguided.

- Can the client buy certain foods from neighbors instead?
- Can income be generated from the forest to purchase food?
- If clearing is necessary, can it be concentrated in a very tight area rather than spread across the property?

Each of these questions probes whether extensive clearing is truly needed or whether a more intensive, compact approach would serve the client better while preserving the forest as a productive biological resource.

Mollison proposes a proven alternative: a large attached glasshouse combined with a fully enclosed and electric-fenced one-eighth acre. Australian conservation areas have demonstrated that all food needs can be met from an eighth of an acre under intensive cultivation. In one such area, all 385 titles are owned by groups with a single conservation aim, each limiting themselves to one-eighth of an acre. The result is a gigantic wildlife area supporting thousands of kangaroos, wallabies, and possums. The remaining land stays as [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] with zero conflict between occupants and animals. Much food can be produced under glass, and there are no worries about bears, raccoons, or other raiding animals. This tight situation may not cost more than an extensive one, and it eliminates the redneck conversion problem entirely. If clearing is ultimately chosen, the designer must ensure that all cleared material is used to the highest advantage.

## Water, Access, and House Sites as a Single Entity

The culminating principle of this design phase is to lay out water systems, access, and house sites **as a single integrated entity**. These three elements are not independent decisions but components of one system. As designer Dan Hemenway notes, it is useful to first identify water resources and flows, then to site access, dwellings, and land uses in relationship to those flows. If this integration is done correctly, Mollison assures the designer, everything else will fit into place naturally and there is no need to worry much about the details. The client may then abandon some of their original priorities in favor of new enterprises revealed by the resource analysis.

A critical practical note: with rich clients who own bulldozers, the designer must be extremely careful about when and how information is shared. Revealing a concept like a diversion drain before the design is complete can result in the client immediately executing it badly—charging uphill at 25 degrees with heavy machinery.

Mollison advises keeping quiet until the full report is ready, then presenting it properly. Premature revelation of partial design concepts leads to expensive, irreversible mistakes on the ground.

## Summary of Design Sequence

The following sequence captures Mollison's recommended order of operations for establishing a permaculture design:

1. Set [[holzer-high-beds-property-boundaries]] and experience the site directly using all senses.
2. Catalog all resources: earth, plant, biological, energy, water, and extrinsic.
3. Secure client rights to all exploitable resources.
4. Place access according to keyline principles—this is the first design move.
5. Select house sites using thermal, fire, and wind criteria.
6. Integrate water systems with access and house sites as a unified entity.
7. Review the client's priorities against the resource analysis and suggest modifications.
8. Lay out zones and detailed land uses within the established framework.

This sequence ensures that the most expensive and irreversible decisions—roads, houses, water infrastructure—are made first and made correctly, with all subsequent design layers building on a sound foundation. Mollison's overarching ethic is clear: work to increase the ways in which a site stores energy, build soil fertility, and create conditions where people can live happily on their land without being forced into destructive conflicts with the natural world. If a designer makes a client happy while doing this, the work has succeeded.
