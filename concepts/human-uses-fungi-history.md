# Human Uses of Fungi Throughout History

The relationship between humans and fungi spans
millennia, encompassing food production, medicine,
industry, biotechnology, and spiritual practices
across every human culture. Fungi have shaped
human civilization in ways that are often
underappreciated, from the leavening of bread to
the production of life-saving antibiotics. The
extraordinary metabolic versatility of the fungal
kingdom has provided humans with an immense
toolkit of biological processes and products.

## Food and Fermentation

### Bread and Baking
The use of Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's
yeast) for bread leavening dates back at least
5,000 years to ancient Egypt. Yeast fermentation
produces carbon dioxide, which creates the
characteristic air pockets in leavened bread.
Sourdough traditions use wild yeast communities
(primarily Saccharomyces exiguus and Candida
milleri) in symbiosis with lactic acid bacteria
(Lactobacillus species), producing complex flavors
through mixed fermentation that cannot be
replicated with commercial yeast alone.

### Alcoholic Beverages
Fermented beverages represent one of the oldest
biotechnological applications of fungi. Beer
production uses Saccharomyces cerevisiae (ale
yeasts) or Saccharomyces pastorianus (lager
yeasts) to ferment malt sugars into ethanol and
carbon dioxide. Wine production relies on both
inoculated strains and wild yeasts present on
grape skins. Asian rice wines and spirits use
Aspergillus oryzae (koji mold) for starch
saccharification before yeast fermentation
converts the released sugars into alcohol.

### Fermented Foods Beyond Alcohol
A vast array of fermented foods depend on fungal
metabolism for their characteristic flavors and
textures. Soy sauce uses Aspergillus sojae and A.
oryzae to hydrolyze soy proteins and carbohydrates
during fermentation. Miso is a fermented soybean
paste using A. oryzae. Tempeh uses Rhizopus
oligosporus to ferment soybeans into a nutritious
cake. Cheese production employs Penicillium
roqueforti (blue cheese) and P. camemberti
(Camembert and Brie) for ripening and flavor
development.

### Edible Mushrooms
Mushroom cultivation is one of the most
significant applications of fungal biology for
food production. Global mushroom production
exceeds 40 million metric tons annually, dominated
by Agaricus bisporus (button, cremini, portobello)
and Pleurotus species (oyster mushrooms). Shiitake
(Lentinula edodes) is the second most cultivated
species globally. Wild mushroom foraging remains
economically and culturally important in many
regions, particularly for premium species like
truffles (Tuber spp.), porcini (Boletus edulis),
and matsutake (Tricholoma matsutake).

## Medicine

### Antibiotics
The discovery of penicillin from Penicillium
notatum by Alexander Fleming in 1928
revolutionized medicine and inaugurated the
antibiotic era. Penicillin and its semisynthetic
derivatives have saved millions of lives from
bacterial infections. Other fungal-derived
antibiotics include cephalosporins (from
Acremonium chrysogenum), fusidic acid (from
Fusidium coccineum), and griseofulvin (from
Penicillium griseofulvum).

### Immunosuppressants and Statins
Cyclosporine A, isolated from the mold
Tolypocladium inflatum, enabled modern organ
transplantation by preventing graft rejection. The
cholesterol-lowering drugs lovastatin (from
Aspergillus terreus) and compactin (from
Penicillium citrinum) and their derivatives are
among the most widely prescribed pharmaceuticals
globally.

## Industrial Applications

### Enzymes
Fungi are prolific producers of industrial enzymes
used in diverse applications. Cellulases and
hemicellulases are used in biofuel production and
textile processing. Proteases serve as detergent
additives and food processing aids. Amylases are
essential for starch processing, brewing, and
baking. Laccases and peroxidases from white-rot
fungi are used in bioremediation, textile dye
decolorization, and biobleaching of paper pulp.

### Organic Acids and Bioremediation
Fungal fermentation produces citric acid
(Aspergillus niger) at industrial scale exceeding
2 million metric tons annually. Fungi are
increasingly used for environmental cleanup:
white-rot fungi degrade polycyclic aromatic
hydrocarbons, polychlorinated biphenyls, and
synthetic dyes. Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.)
can break down petroleum hydrocarbons and have
been used to clean contaminated sites.

## Biotechnology and Research

Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's yeast) and
Schizosaccharomyces pombe (fission yeast) are
fundamental model organisms in molecular biology.
S. cerevisiae was the first eukaryotic organism
with a fully sequenced genome (1996). Fungal
expression systems (Pichia pastoris, Aspergillus
niger, Trichoderma reesei) are used for industrial
production of recombinant proteins including
enzymes, antibodies, vaccines, and therapeutic
proteins for human medicine.
