## Taxonomy

- Formerly Agaricus blazei (Heinemann)
- Now believed synonymous with Agaricus blazei ss. Heinemann but NOT Agaricus blazei ss. Murrill
- Closest relative: A. subrufescens (slender but tasty, differs in spore size: 7.5-10μm vs 4-5μm)
- DNA tests will further illuminate lineages

## Description

- **Cap:** 7-25 cm broad, convex to hemispheric, eventually flattening
- **Cap surface:** White, smooth, covered with brownish fibrillose patches
- **Partial veil:** Membranous, cottony, typically forming median ring
- **Gills:** Pallid at first, gray, then chocolate brown at maturity
- **Stem:** Cylindrical, solid, tall, whitish, smooth; thick flesh
- **Staining:** Bright yellow when freshly cut; browning reaction after harvest
- **Spore deposit:** Dark chocolate brown, 5.7-7 by 3.8-4.6 μm
- **Scent:** Strong almond, especially during cooking
- **Growth:** Singly or in clusters from dense rhizomorphs

## Habitat and Cultivation

- **Natural habitat:** Soils with plentiful lignin-rich debris, mixed woods, composted soils, forest edges
- **Distribution:** Sal Hose do Rio Preto district (NW of Sao Paulo, Brazil); fields and mountainous regions
- **Type of rot:** Mottled white rot
- **Temperature:** 70-95°F (summer mushroom in northern latitudes)
- **Companion plants:** Kale, zucchini, squash, potatoes, melons, and other leafy greens
- **Cultivation:** Mounds of leached cow manure or compost, inoculated and planted with cover crops

One of the most protein-rich of all cultivated mushrooms (33-48% protein by dry weight).

## Medicinal Properties

### Immunomodulation and Cancer
- Produces beta-1,6-glucans and beta-1,3-glucans — polysaccharides studied for immunopotentiating properties
- Beta-glucan levels up to 14% reported (Stamets' analysis: 9% beta-glucans, ~27% total polysaccharides)
- Unique beta-glucans promote natural killer cells and are selectively cytotoxic to tumor cells
- Numerous analyses isolating tumoricidal and immunomodulatory constituents (Kujimiya et al. 1998; Ito et al. 1997; Kob et al. 1994)
- Cultured mycelium produces antitumor compounds (Mizuno et al. 1999)
- Compounds specifically increase apoptosis in cancerous cells but NOT in healthy cells
- Triggers immune response against tumors

### Antibacterial
- Yellowish metabolite exuded by mycelium has bactericidal properties

### Heavy Metal Accumulation
- Absorbs cadmium, copper, lead, and mercury (Stijve et al. 2003)
- Chinese-grown specimens had significantly more cadmium than US-grown (Stijve 2003)

## Mycorestoration Potential

- Excellent candidate for recycling manure wastes (cows, chickens, horses)
- Consumer of E. coli and other coliforms
- Works well in warmer climates for outdoor cultivation
- Mushroom beds can double as mycofiltration buffers around point-source pollution centers (dairies, chicken farms, slaughterhouses)

## Culinary Notes

- Powerful almond flavor easily overpowers other foods
- Best prepared separately: basted in olive oil, cooked with rosemary and onions
- Good thinly sliced in soups
- Best treated as a delicacy, not a daily consumable
- Dried mushrooms can have terrible taste — market fresh for culinary use
- Dried forms usually powdered for capsules, tablets, or extracts

## Controversy

- Center of commercial controversy fueled by competition between Brazilian producers, and Chinese vs Japanese growers
- Ikekawa (2003) questioned veracity of Mizuno's seminal medicinal property research
- Subsequent research has confirmed medically significant anticancer constituents and immune enhancement
