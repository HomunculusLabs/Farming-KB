## Fungi in Grassland and Agricultural Ecosystems (Dighton)

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: John Dighton, *Fungi in [[comparison-phosphorus-solubilizing-microorganisms-vs-mycorrhizal-phosphorus-acquisition]]** — AM hyphae extend beyond the root depletion zone to access phosphorus from soil volumes unavailable to roots alone
- **Soil aggregation** — AM hyphae and glomalin production stabilize soil aggregates, critical for grassland soil structure and resistance to erosion
- **Plant community interactions** — AM networks can mediate competition between grass species, influencing [[singh-mycelial-mats-and-biological-soil-crusts]] in desert grasslands of Utah and Wyoming. These communities include:
- Dark septate fungi
- Melanized hyphomycetes
- Yeast-like fungi

Soil crust fungal communities contribute to [[fungal-interactions-with-microbes]] soil aggregates, with mycorrhizal fungi providing both enmeshment of particles and production of sticky extracellular materials.

## Agriculture and Mycorrhizal Function

### Mycorrhizal Benefits in Cropping Systems
Fitter (1985, 1991) noted that while the nutritional benefit of mycorrhizae in natural ecosystems is not always obvious, a clear nutritional benefit accrues in agricultural contexts. This is because:
- Agricultural soils are often disturbed, reducing mycorrhizal inoculum
- High-input fertilization can reduce mycorrhizal dependency
- Crop rotation and tillage affect mycorrhizal community continuity

### Collembola Grazing in Agroecosystems
Warnock et al. (1982) demonstrated that collembolan grazing on AM extraradical hyphae significantly reduces the efficiency of [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]] and no-till systems generally maintain higher [[comparison-no-till-vs-conventional-tillage]] systems.

## Nutrient Cycling in Agroecosystems

### Decomposition of Crop Residues
Post-harvest crop residue decomposition follows the same principles as natural litter decomposition, but with important differences:
- Residues are often concentrated in uniform layers rather than distributed as in natural systems
- C:N ratios of crop residues vary widely (straw is very high, legume residues are low)
- Agricultural management (tillage, irrigation, fertilization) alters decomposition conditions

### Nitrogen Dynamics
During decomposition of high-C:N crop residues (e.g., cereal straw), nitrogen is immobilized by decomposer fungi, potentially creating nitrogen deficiency for the following crop. This immobilization period can last weeks to months depending on residue quality, temperature, and moisture.

### Soil Fungal:Bacterial Ratios
Agricultural intensification typically shifts soil microbial communities toward bacterial dominance:
- Conventional tillage disrupts fungal hyphal networks
- High nitrogen inputs favor bacterial growth over fungal
- Monoculture reduces the diversity of organic substrates available for fungal decomposers

This shift has implications for soil structure, nutrient retention, and disease suppression, as fungal-dominated systems tend to be more stable and retain nutrients more effectively.
