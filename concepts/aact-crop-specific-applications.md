## AACT Crop-Specific Applications

[[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) can be applied to virtually any crop, but the biology requirements, application rates, timing, and techniques vary significantly between crop types. Understanding the specific needs of each crop system ensures that AACT delivers maximum benefit.

## General Principles

Before diving into crop-specific recommendations, several universal principles apply:

1. **Match biology to the plant's successional stage:** Annuals and early-successional plants prefer bacterial-dominated soils. Perennials, shrubs, and trees prefer fungal-dominated soils.
2. **Apply at the right time:** Early-season applications establish protective biology before pathogen pressure builds. Pre-bloom applications are particularly important for fruit crops.
3. **Soil health first:** AACT cannot compensate for severely degraded soil structure, compaction, or chemical contamination. Address fundamental soil problems alongside biological amendments.
4. **Observe and adapt:** Every site is unique. Monitor results and adjust application rates, timing, and biology targets accordingly.
5. **AACT is not a magic cure-all:** It is one tool in a holistic soil and plant health management system. It works best when combined with proper tillage (or no-till), cover cropping, organic matter management, and balanced nutrition.

## Vineyard Applications

Vineyards are among the most common and well-documented AACT application sites. Grapevines are perennial crops that evolved in fungal-dominated forest edge ecosystems, making them particularly responsive to fungal-rich compost teas.

### Application Strategy

- **Spring (bud break to bloom):** Apply fungal-dominated AACT as a soil drench around the root zone to establish mycorrhizal associations and suppress soil-borne pathogens.
- **Pre-bloom foliar:** 1–2 foliar applications before flowering to establish protective phyllosphere biology.
- **Post-bloom through veraison:** Regular foliar applications every 10–14 days for disease prevention.
- **Pre-harvest:** Final foliar applications to protect ripening fruit.
- **Post-harvest:** Soil drench to support root biology going into dormancy.

### Vineyard-Specific Considerations

- **Powdery mildew suppression:** Fungal-dominated teas on foliage compete with powdery mildew for leaf surface space.
- **Botrytis prevention:** Complete leaf coverage is essential; botrytis colonizes any unprotected tissue.
- **No spraying during blossom:** Avoid spraying during bloom to prevent interference with pollination and fruit set.
- **Copper and sulfur management:** Many vineyards use copper and sulfur sprays. These materials are biocidal and will kill AACT organisms. If transitioning to biological management, reduce copper/sulfur programs gradually while increasing AACT applications. Allow at least 7–10 days between copper/sulfur sprays and AACT applications.
- **Kaligreen (potassium bicarbonate):** Less damaging to biology than copper/sulfur but still has some antimicrobial effect.

### Fermentation and Wine Quality

Some vineyard managers report improved fermentation characteristics and wine quality from biologically managed vineyards, attributed to healthier fruit with more diverse native yeast populations on grape surfaces.

## Orchard Applications

Fruit trees (apples, pears, peaches, cherries, etc.) are perennial, fungal-dominated crops requiring robust mycorrhizal networks and diverse soil biology.

### Application Strategy

- **Dormant season:** Soil drench around the drip line to inoculate root zones.
- **Green tip to bloom:** Foliar applications to protect emerging foliage and blossoms.
- **Fruit development:** Regular foliar applications every 10–14 days.
- **Post-harvest:** Soil application to support root biology during senescence.

### Orchard-Specific Considerations

- **Apple scab:** Early-season foliar AACT can help suppress apple scab by occupying infection sites before the pathogen.
- **Fire blight:** Bacterial-dominated teas during bloom may compete with *Erwinia amylovora*, though research is ongoing.
- **Codling moth:** Indirect benefits through improved tree health and natural enemy support.
- **Soil building under trees:** Long-term AACT applications build soil organic matter and aggregate structure, improving water retention and root exploration.

## Vegetable and Flower Applications

Vegetables and annual flowers are mostly early-successional plants that thrive in bacterial-dominated soils. AACT programs for these crops focus on rapid establishment of protective biology and nutrient cycling.

### Application Strategy

- **Pre-planting:** Soil drench or injection to establish biology before transplanting.
- **At transplanting:** Drench root balls or dip transplants in AACT before planting.
- **Vegetative growth:** Foliar applications every 7–14 days.
- **Pre-flowering:** Critical application window for disease prevention on fruiting vegetables.
- **Fruiting:** Continue foliar applications through harvest.

### Crop-Specific Notes

- **Tomatoes:** Benefit from both bacterial and fungal teas. Foliar applications help suppress early blight and septoria leaf spot. Soil applications support mycorrhizal associations that improve phosphorus uptake.
- **Potatoes:** Susceptible to soil-borne diseases including scab and late blight. Pre-plant soil drenches and in-furrow applications are important. Non-host cover crops combined with AACT help suppress nematode populations.
- **Strawberries:** Highly responsive to AACT. Both soil and foliar applications improve fruit quality and suppress botrytis and powdery mildew. Multiple small applications are more effective than fewer large ones.

## Row Crop Applications

Large-scale commodity crops (wheat, corn, soybeans, rice) present unique challenges for AACT application due to acreage, equipment limitations, and economics.

### Wheat Applications

- **Fall or spring soil application:** Can be applied with or near fertilizer bands.
- **Foliar at tillering and boot stage:** In-furrow or aerial application where equipment allows.
- **Focus on root zone biology:** Mycorrhizal associations in wheat improve nutrient uptake efficiency and drought tolerance.

### Rice Applications

- **Flooded conditions create unique challenges:** AACT organisms must survive in anaerobic or fluctuating oxygen environments.
- **Apply to nursery beds:** Treating seedling trays before transplanting establishes biology early.
- **Water management:** Coordinate AACT applications with drain periods when aerobic soil conditions exist.

## Cane Fruit Applications

Raspberries, blackberries, and other cane fruits are perennial but have relatively shallow root systems. They benefit from:

- **High-frequency, low-volume applications** due to shallow rooting.
- **Both soil and foliar programs** for comprehensive protection.
- **Post-harvest care:** Soil biology support during the critical fall root-building period.

## Cover Crops and Soil Building

Cover crops and AACT work synergistically:

- **Cover crops feed soil biology:** Their root exudates and residue provide food for the organisms introduced by AACT.
- **AACT accelerates cover crop decomposition:** When cover crops are terminated, AACT applications speed breakdown and nutrient release.
- **Together they build soil structure:** The combination produces more stable aggregates, better water infiltration, and deeper root channels.

## When AACT Doesn't Work

When results are disappointing, common causes include:

1. **Poor compost quality:** The starting material lacked adequate beneficial biology.
2. **Anaerobic brewing:** Tea went anaerobic, killing beneficials and producing phytotoxins.
3. **Incompatible chemicals:** Pesticide, herbicide, or fertilizer residues killed applied organisms.
4. **Compacted soil:** Organisms cannot survive or move in severely compacted soil.
5. **Excessive tillage:** Disrupts established fungal networks faster than they can rebuild.
6. **Insufficient application frequency:** One or two applications cannot overcome years of biological degradation.
7. **Environmental stress:** Extreme drought, heat, or cold limit organism survival and plant response.

## Application Timing with Tillage

Tillage disrupts fungal networks and soil structure. After tillage, apply AACT to re-establish biology as quickly as possible. Reduced tillage or no-till systems preserve established biology and amplify AACT benefits over time.
