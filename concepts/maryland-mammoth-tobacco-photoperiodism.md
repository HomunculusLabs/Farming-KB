## Garner and Allard's Investigation

The breakthrough came when Garner and Allard at the USDA Bureau of Plant
Industry began systematic experiments with Maryland Mammoth in 1918. They
noted that plants grown outdoors during summer remained vegetative
indefinitely, while those brought into the greenhouse in autumn eventually
flowered. To test whether day length was the controlling factor, they
constructed light-tight frames and covered plants for a portion of each
day during summer, artificially creating short-day conditions. Under these
treatments, Maryland Mammoth plants promptly initiated flowering. In the
reciprocal experiment, extending the natural short winter days with
supplemental electric light prevented flowering entirely. These experiments
provided the first conclusive evidence that the duration of daily light
exposure controlled the floral transition, and Maryland Mammoth was
classified as a short-day plant.

## Short-Day Plant Physiology

Maryland Mammoth is a quintessential short-day plant: it requires a dark
period longer than a critical threshold (approximately 12-14 hours) in
order to flower. During summer, when nights are shorter than this critical
duration, the plant's photoperiodic sensing mechanism — mediated by the
photoreceptor phytochrome — detects the insufficient dark period and
maintains the plant in vegetative growth mode. As autumn progresses and
nights lengthen beyond the critical threshold, the phytochrome system
signals that conditions are appropriate for flowering, and the plant
transitions to reproductive development. The molecular basis involves the
production of a floral stimulus (originally termed "florigen") in the
leaves under inductive photoperiods, which is then transported to the shoot
apex where it activates the floral meristem identity genes.

## Role in the Discovery of Photoperiodism

Maryland Mammoth tobacco served as the key experimental organism in the
discovery of photoperiodism. Without this unusual variety, the connection
between day [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] might not have been recognized for many
more years. The dramatic nature of Maryland Mammoth"s response — growing to
enormous size without flowering, then flowering promptly when day length
was manipulated — made it an ideal subject for experimentation. Garner and
Allard's 1920 publication, "Effect of the Relative Length of Day and Night
and Other Factors of the Environment on Growth and Reproduction in Plants,"
is one of the most cited papers in plant physiology and established the
concept of photoperiodism that remains central to the field today. The
paper documented responses in Maryland Mammoth and numerous other species,
establishing the general applicability of the photoperiodic concept.
