# Guerilla Growing

Guerilla growing, a term coined in the early 1970s, is the clandestine cultivation of
cannabis in remote outdoor locations. It requires strategy, time, and often physical
prowess. Depending on location and local laws, guerilla growing in remote areas may be
the only option for cultivators who cannot grow at home.

## Site Selection

Location and security are the primary concerns for a guerilla grower. Choose a location
with limited public access. Check regulations for hunting and recreation, and consider who
might use the area: hunters, hikers, mushroom foragers, other growers, dirt-bikers, and
scouts. Select a remote site unlikely to be visited casually.

## Vegetation Cover

Look for sites with big green stands of existing vegetation. Cannabis is a vigorous plant
with a large root system, and a flowering female will stand out if surrounding vegetation
dies back before harvest. Stands of thorny blackberry bushes, ferns, and meadow grass are
good options because they provide natural camouflage and deter casual visitors. Stinging
nettles also camouflage cannabis well and their painful sting deters curious visitors.

## Sunlight Requirements

Plants need a minimum of five to six hours of direct sunshine per day. Scout potential
sites during winter when foliage is absent to accurately assess sun exposure. The angle
of the sun changes through the seasons -- a site that receives full sun in summer may be
shaded in spring or autumn. Remember that the sun takes a higher path in spring and
summer. Rocky terrain, hillside terraces, and grasslands all receive good amounts of
sunlight.

## Water Access

Ample water is an important factor for site selection. If rainfall is unreliable, locate
the garden near a water source that does not dry up in summer. Hauling water to a remote
site is labor-intensive and increases the risk of detection. Exclusive access by boat can
reduce discovery risk, but ensure plants are not visible from the waterway. Many people
use waterways and explore land bordering rivers, so choose bank locations carefully.

## Soil Preparation

Prepare the marijuana patch up to six months before planting. Remove competing vegetation
in the fall for a spring garden. Clear patches to allow sufficient sunshine, cut back
roots of competing plants, and till planting holes two to three feet square. Allow
amended soil to sit for a month or more before planting. Proper planning and preparation
are important since remote locations are hard to visit regularly.

A complete low-maintenance setup is the goal for most guerilla growers. Loosen the soil,
amend it, and throw in a handful of polymers to retain moisture. A thick layer of mulch
early in the year will attract water, keep the soil cool, and prevent evaporation.

## Strain Selection for Guerilla Grows

Choose strains suited to the local climate and growing season. Fast-finishing indica-
dominant hybrids (6-8 weeks flowering) are preferred in temperate climates to ensure
harvest before frost. [[autoflowering-cannabis]] varieties are ideal for guerilla grows
because they complete their life cycle regardless of photoperiod, allowing planting
throughout the season.

## Planting and Maintenance

Start clones in rockwool, Jiffy, or soilless grow cubes for three weeks, then
transplant into four-inch pots of organic soil mix. Grow under an HID or compact
fluorescent lamp for two weeks. Harden-off before moving outside into the garden. Bury
clones deep in the ground to promote a deep root system that will not require a lot of
additional water. Some growers transplant by removing the first few sets of leaves and
burying the root ball deeper with only six inches of foliage left above ground. Roots
will grow along the underground stem in a few weeks, creating more self-sufficient
plants.

## Security Strategies

- Visit the site at irregular times to avoid establishing a pattern
- Use circuitous routes to approach the site
- Avoid leaving trails or signs of activity
- Cover the bottom of your shoes with duct tape to camouflage footprints
- Plant indicator crops (tomatoes) at home to monitor conditions remotely
- Consider planting several small plots rather than one large garden
- Disguise plants among tall companion vegetation
- Park vehicles in a discreet place away from the trailhead
- Paint containers black, dark green, or camouflage
- Be prepared with a believable cover story and props (camera, fishing pole, etc.)
- Visit late in the afternoon when most visitors are likely gone
- Plant 'Ducks Foot' varieties to fool casual observers into believing it is not
  cannabis

## Pest Prevention

Pest prevention is crucial for guerilla crops where regular monitoring is difficult.
Pre-treat soil with beneficial nematodes and diatomaceous earth. Use companion planting
to deter pests. Physical barriers like chicken wire can protect young plants from
animals. It is easier to keep pests from attacking plants in the first place than to try
and do damage control later.

## Harvest Considerations

Harvest before cold, damp autumn weather sets in -- this weather causes bud mold
(botrytis) and powdery mildew. Plan the harvest timing carefully. Strains that finish
flowering before surrounding vegetation dies back are less conspicuous. If the guerilla
garden is on public land, there is risk of discovery or theft as plants approach
maturity. Some guerilla growers harvest slightly early to reduce this risk. Limit
potential discovery by harvesting at night.

## Tools and Supplies

The High Lifter water pump is noted as an efficient option for guerilla growers who need
to pump water from nearby sources. Battery-powered tools, lightweight supplies, and
camouflage gear make remote visits more efficient. Electric scissors (like Bonsai
electric scissors) cut trimming time significantly for outdoor harvests. Take a sharp
pocket knife and a backpack to haul your crop incognito. If harvesting more than one
variety, put them in separate bags.

## Risks

- Discovery by hikers, hunters, or law enforcement
- Theft by other growers or passersby
- Animal damage (deer, rabbits, rodents)
- Weather extremes (drought, flood, early frost)
- Difficulty providing consistent care
- Physical demands of accessing remote sites
