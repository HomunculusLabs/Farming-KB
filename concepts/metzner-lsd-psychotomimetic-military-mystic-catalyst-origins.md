# LSD from Psychotomimetic to Mystic Catalyst: Military and Therapeutic Origins

## Overview

Ralph Metzner's 1998 review of hallucinogens in [[hallucinogenic-drugs-in-psychotherapy-and-shamanism]]
opened with an account of LSD's dual origins as both a military weapon and a
mystical catalyst. When [[albert-hofmann]] first discovered the mind-altering
properties of LSD at the height of World War II in a Swiss pharmaceutical lab,
the compound was initially characterized as "psychotomimetic" (mimicking
psychosis) and "psycholytic" (dissolving psychic defenses). These two
characterizations defined the first decade of LSD research and established a
tension between control and liberation that has persisted throughout the history
of [[maps-multidisciplinary-association-for-psychedelic-studies]].

## The Sandoz Discovery and "Seelische Auflockerung"

The first research papers from Sandoz Laboratories, where Hofmann synthesized
LSD and accidentally discovered its properties in 1943, described the compound
as bringing about "psychic loosening or opening" (*seelische Auflockerung*).
This was the psycholytic concept—the idea that LSD could dissolve the defenses
of the ego, making unconscious material accessible to therapeutic exploration.

The psycholytic concept became the dominant model for LSD-assisted psychotherapy
in Europe. Neurotic patients suffering from anxiety, depression, or obsessive-
compulsive disorders were given LSD in a series of sessions at gradually
increasing doses while undergoing standard analytic interactions using a Freudian
perspective. The rationale was that through psycholysis—the loosening of
defenses—the patient would become more vividly aware of previously unconscious
emotional dynamics and reaction patterns, leading to resolution of inner
conflicts.

## The Military and Espionage Interest

The prospect of unhinging the mind from its normal parameters "intrigued the
military and espionage agencies of both superpowers, especially the Americans."
Considerable research effort and expense was devoted for about ten years to
determining the most effective surreptitious delivery systems to unsuspecting
enemy soldiers, agents, or leaders, for maximum confusion, disorientation, or
embarrassment.

The CIA's MK-Ultra program, Operation Artichoke, and related projects
represented the most extensive investigation of LSD as a weapon. The program
included involuntary dosing of unwitting subjects, including CIA employees,
military personnel, and members of the general public. The program remained
classified until 1975, when it was exposed by the Church Committee.

Metzner noted the irony that LSD's capacity to "tap into the hidden mystical
potentials of the human mind" was precisely what "ruined its applicability as a
weapon of war." Rather than making subjects predictably submissive to mind-
control programming, LSD had "the unnerving propensity to suspend the existing
mental programming and thereby release one into awesome worlds of cosmic
consciousness." The military was not prepared for soldiers or espionage agents
to turn into mystics.

## The Psycholytic Model in Europe

The psycholytic approach became the dominant model for LSD-assisted psychotherapy
in Europe, particularly in the United Kingdom, Czechoslovakia, and West Germany.
Practitioners working within this model administered LSD in a series of sessions
at gradually increasing doses, typically ranging from 25 to 400 micrograms, over
a period of weeks or months.

The Czech psychiatrist [[stanislav-grof]], working within the psycholytic model,
made the startling discovery that in a series involving increasing doses there
could be an even deeper psychic opening—to birth and pre-birth memories. After
resolving conflicts stemming from Freudian dynamics of early childhood, patients
would relive the significant sensory-emotional features of their birth
experience—patterns Grof named "[[metzner-grof-perinatal-matrices-and-transpersonal-realms]]."

This discovery extended the psycholytic model beyond Freudian psychoanalysis
into the territory of what would later be called transpersonal psychology. The
[[metzner-grof-perinatal-matrices-psychedelic-therapy]] suggested that consciousness extends beyond individual
biography to include the birth experience itself, and potentially the entire
history of human evolution.

## The Psychedelic Model in America

More or less simultaneously with the psycholytic approach in Europe, the
psychedelic model became the preferred approach in Anglo-American psychological
circles. The English psychiatrist Humphrey Osmond, working in Canada with Abram
Hoffer on the treatment of alcoholism with LSD, introduced the term
"psychedelic" in an exchange of letters with Aldous Huxley.
