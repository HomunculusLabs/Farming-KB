## Fungal Appressorium and Infection Structures

Fungal infection structures are specialized hyphal morphologies that
enable [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] to force
penetration pegs through host surfaces. These structures represent a
remarkable convergence of mechanical force generation, biochemical
modification, and developmental regulation.

## Appressorium Formation and Development

The formation of an appressorium begins when a fungal spore lands on a
suitable host surface. In the [[fungi-in-the-environment-rice-blast-magnaporthe-grisea]]*,
three-celled conidia dispersed by wind, dew, or rain splash attach to
the hydrophobic rice leaf surface using adhesive material released from
the conidial apex. Within an hour, a germ tube 15 to 30 micrometers in
length develops. After approximately four hours, the germ tube hooks,
changes direction, swells, and flattens against the leaf surface,
marking the beginning of differentiation into a specialized infection
structure called the appressorium.

As the appressorium develops, an inner layer of melanin and an outer
layer of chitin are deposited in its cell wall. These layers are
critical for the appressorium to generate up to 8 MPa of pressure, a
remarkable feat exceeding the pressure in an automobile tire. The
appressorium produces a penetration peg in a small melanin-free region
in contact with the leaf surface. The enormous turgor pressure within
the appressorium enables the penetration peg to pierce the tough plant
epidermis.

turgor, representing one of the highest pressures generated by any
living cell. The glycerol is synthesized from lipid reserves within
the conidium and concentrated within the appressorium through the
