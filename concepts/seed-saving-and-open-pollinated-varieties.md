# [[seed-saving-germination-and-sowing-techniques|Seed]] Saving and [[open-pollinated-vs-hybrid-seed-varieties|Open]]-Pollinated Varieties

## Overview

[[seed-quality-and-selection|Seed]] saving and the use of open-pollinated (OP) varieties form a cornerstone of [[steve-solomon]]'s approach in *[[gardening-when-it-counts]]*. Rather than depending on commercial seed companies for each season's planting, Solomon advocates for gardeners to learn the skills of seed selection, production, and preservation. This not only reduces costs but develops locally adapted varieties that perform better over time and maintains genetic diversity in the food supply.

## Understanding Seed Types

### Open-Pollinated (OP) Varieties

Open-pollinated varieties are genetically stable populations that reproduce true-to-type when allowed to pollinate naturally among themselves. Key characteristics:

- Seeds saved from OP plants will produce offspring similar to the parent generation
- OP varieties have been developed through generations of selection by farmers and gardeners
- They exhibit natural genetic variation within defined parameters, providing resilience
- Solomon provides growing instructions for OP varieties (page 113, 245) and specifically references OP [[growing-chinese-cabbage]] (page 316), corn (pages 275, 276, 277), and brassicas

### Hybrid Varieties

Hybrid varieties are produced by crossing two distinct inbred parent lines:

- **F1 hybrids** (first generation) exhibit hybrid vigor (heterosis): uniform growth, high yield, and often disease resistance
- Seeds saved from F1 hybrids do **not** breed true — the F2 generation segregates into a range of types, most of which are inferior to the F1
- **History of hybrids** (pages 141-143): Solomon provides a critical [[lemon-cannabis-alchemy-historical-perspective-and-references]] on how hybrid varieties displaced OP varieties in commercial agriculture, emphasizing what was lost in the transition
- **Hybrid corn** (pages 275, 276): Widely adopted but requires purchasing new seed each year
- **Hybrid cucurbits** (pages 267, 269): Common in commercial seed catalogs
- **Hybrid tomatoes** (page 264): Popular but often bred for shipping durability at the expense of flavor and nutritional quality
- **Super-hybrids** (page 130): An extreme form that may be marketed as advanced but often represents even further narrowing of genetic diversity

### The Case for Open-Pollinated Varieties

Solomon argues that OP varieties offer several advantages over hybrids for the home gardener:

1. **Seed saving**: OP varieties allow gardeners to produce their own seed, eliminating dependence on commercial suppliers
2. **Local adaptation**: Over generations of seed saving in a specific location, OP varieties naturally select for traits suited to local climate, soil, and growing conditions
3. **Genetic diversity**: Each OP variety contains a range of genetic material, providing resilience against pests, diseases, and weather extremes
4. **Nutritional quality**: Solomon cites research showing that vegetables from OP varieties often have higher nutritional content than modern hybrids (page 141-143)
5. **Flavor**: Many gardeners find OP varieties have superior flavor compared to hybrids bred for commercial traits

## Growing Your Own Seed (Pages 134-138, 138-141)

### Why Save Your Own Seed

Solomon identifies several compelling reasons for home seed production:

- **Cost savings**: Seeds are a recurring expense; producing your own eliminates this cost
- **Quality control**: You can select for the traits that matter most in your garden
- **Security**: Seed supply disruptions (whether from market forces or other causes) don't affect gardeners who maintain their own seed stocks
- **Adaptation**: Each generation of seed saving in your specific conditions produces plants better adapted to those conditions

### Basic Principles of Seed Saving

**Breeding plants and seeds** (pages 15, 136-137, 138-141) requires understanding several key concepts:

- **Isolation distance**: Many vegetables cross-pollinate with other varieties of the same species. To maintain varietal purity, different varieties must be separated by sufficient distance (which varies by crop) or flower at different times.
- **Self-pollinating vs. cross-pollinating**: Some crops (tomatoes, lettuce, peas, beans) naturally self-pollinate and require minimal isolation. Others (corn, brassicas, cucurbits, carrots) cross-pollinate readily and need careful management.
- **Self-sterile varieties** (page 140): Some varieties require cross-pollination with another plant to set viable seed, even within a self-pollinating species.
- **Population size**: Maintaining adequate genetic diversity requires saving seed from a minimum number of plants (typically 20-50 for most vegetables, more for outbreeding species).
- **[[cervantes-hybrid-vigor-inbreeding-depression]]** (page 136): Repeatedly saving seed from too few plants causes gradual decline in vigor and yield.

### Seed Production for Specific Crops

**Hybrid seed growing** (pages 138-141, 298): While Solomon generally advocates for OP varieties, he provides practical guidance for those who want to produce F1 hybrid seed, explaining the process of maintaining parent lines and performing controlled crosses.

**Brassicas** (pages 243-248, 298-299): Many brassicas are biennials that require overwintering before producing seed. They also cross-pollinate readily within species, requiring careful isolation.

**Corn** (pages 274-277): Wind-pollinated with heavy pollen, requiring large isolation distances (typically 1/4 mile or more) between varieties. Corn is one of the most challenging crops for home seed savers.

**Cucurbits** (pages 266-270): Insect-pollinated with complex crossing relationships between species. Solomon notes that wild cucurbits (page 129) can also cross with cultivated varieties.

**Legumes** (pages 277-284): Mostly self-pollinating, making them among the easiest crops for seed saving. Inoculants (page 277) may be needed for optimal nitrogen fixation but are separate from seed production concerns.

**Tomatoes** (pages 261-265): Naturally self-pollinating with minimal crossing, making them excellent candidates for beginning seed savers.

## The Seed Business (Pages 97-104)

### How Commercial Seed Is Produced

Solomon provides an insider's view of the commercial seed industry:

- **Commercial seed production** (pages 97-104): Seeds are grown under controlled conditions, often in specific regions chosen for ideal climate, and processed using sophisticated equipment
- **[[seed-germination-standards-and-testing]]** (pages 101-104, 107, 108): Federal regulations require minimum germination rates for commercially sold seed, but these standards don't guarantee seed quality beyond basic viability
- **Field germination vs. [[solomon-seed-sprouting-conditions-laboratory-germination-technique]]**: Lab conditions (optimal temperature, moisture, sterility) typically produce higher germination rates than real-world garden conditions

### Ethical Guidelines for Seed Companies

Solomon proposes ethical guidelines for seed companies (pages 106-107):

- Transparent labeling of variety type (OP vs. hybrid)
- Accurate representation of germination rates and seed quality
- Honest descriptions of variety characteristics
- Responsiveness to customer feedback about variety performance

## Seed Trials (Pages 107-110)

### Running Your Own Trials

Solomon strongly advocates for gardeners to conduct their own seed trials (pages 2, 107-110):

- **Purpose**: Identify which varieties actually perform best in your specific conditions, rather than relying on catalog descriptions
- **Method**: Grow multiple varieties of the same crop side by side under identical conditions, evaluate performance
- **Factors to evaluate**: Germination rate, growth vigor, pest and disease resistance, yield, flavor, storage quality
- **Long-term value**: Trial results become more valuable over years as you build a database of variety performance in your garden

### Seed Trials and Pest Resistance

Seed trials are particularly important for identifying pest-resistant varieties (pages 109, 217-218). A variety that performs poorly in a trial may not be a bad variety — it may simply be poorly suited to your conditions. The goal is to identify the varieties that naturally resist the [[mushroom-pests-and-diseases]] present in your garden.

## Seed Storage (Pages 132-134)

Proper seed storage is essential for maintaining viability:

- **Conditions**: Cool, dry, dark storage maximizes seed longevity
- **Containers**: Airtight containers with desiccant packets protect against moisture
- **Refrigeration**: For long-term storage, refrigeration or freezing extends seed life significantly
- **Viability testing**: Before planting, test germination by sprouting a sample on moist paper towels

## Seed-Related Techniques

### Chitting (Presprouting) (Pages 124-127, 252, 267)

Chitting is the practice of presprouting seeds before planting:

- **Purpose**: Ensures that only viable seeds are planted, reduces time to emergence, and allows precise placement
