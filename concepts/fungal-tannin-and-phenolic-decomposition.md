Tannins and phenolic compounds are among the most chemically
recalcitrant components of plant litter, and their decomposition by
fungi represents a critical bottleneck in nutrient cycling and carbon
turnover in terrestrial ecosystems. Fungi possess specialized enzyme
systems for degrading these compounds, though the process is inherently
slow and significantly influences the overall rate of litter
decomposition.
## Chemistry of Tannins and Phenolics
Tannins are water-soluble polyphenolic compounds with molecular weights
ranging from 500 to 3000 Daltons. They are broadly classified into two
categories: hydrolyzable tannins (composed of gallic or ellagic acid
units linked to a sugar core) and condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins,
composed of flavanol units linked by carbon-carbon bonds). Condensed
tannins are generally more recalcitrant to microbial degradation than
hydrolyzable tannins. Plant phenolics encompass a broader range of
compounds including simple phenolic acids (caffeic acid, ferulic acid),
flavonoids, lignin monomers, and complex polyphenolic polymers. These
compounds serve multiple functions in plants including defense against
herbivores and pathogens, UV protection, and structural support.
## Inhibition of Decomposition
Tannins and phenolics significantly inhibit decomposition rates by soil
microorganisms through multiple mechanisms. They can directly inhibit
microbial enzymes by binding to protein active sites or denaturing
enzyme proteins through tanning reactions. They can precipitate
proteins, making organic nitrogen unavailable to decomposers. They can
chelate metal ions required as enzyme cofactors. They can also bind to
cell walls and membranes, interfering with nutrient uptake. Vanlauwe et
al. (1997) demonstrated that both decomposition and
[[fungal-manganese-peroxidase-remediation]] and lignin peroxidase,
contribute through oxidative mechanisms. These are primarily produced by
white-rot basidiomycetes.
## Pathogen Effects on Phenolic Content
Fungal pathogens can influence phenolic content of plant litter through
their effects on living plant tissues. Pathogens induce higher levels of
plant-defense chemicals, particularly polyphenols and tannins, in
infected tissues. This pathogen-induced increase in phenolic content has
cascading effects on decomposition. Higher phenolic content reduces
palatability to soil fauna and increases the C:N ratio of leaf
material, reducing the ability of saprotrophic and mycorrhizal fungi to
decompose litter. With sustained high pathogen levels, this positive
feedback mechanism could reduce soil fertility at local and regional
levels, influencing below-ground
[[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]] and soil microbial
community composition.
