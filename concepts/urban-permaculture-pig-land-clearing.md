# Pig Land Clearing

Pigs offer a remarkably effective biological solution for clearing stubborn weed infestations and preparing land for orchard planting. Rather than relying on heavy machinery or chemical herbicides, permaculture systems leverage pigs' natural rooting behavior to transform dense weed mats into workable soil. This approach aligns with the broader principle of using animals as partners in [[permaculture-animal-integration]] and [[hemenway-zone-and-sector-planning]].

## Junkus Weed and Mechanical Difficulty

Junkus (Juncus) is a thick, rhizomatous weed that spreads aggressively through pasture land. It forms dense mats of roots and runners that are extremely resistant to mechanical removal. Tractors and mowers struggle to cut through it effectively, and tilling often breaks the rhizomes into pieces that simply regrow. For farmers looking to establish new orchard sites on junkus-infested land, conventional clearing methods are both expensive and largely ineffective.

Rhizomatous weeds like junkus are particularly challenging because their underground storage organs allow them to survive disturbance. Each piece of broken rhizome can generate a new plant, meaning that mechanical tilling can actually spread the infestation rather than control it. Chemical herbicides may kill the tops but rarely reach the full depth of the root system, and repeated applications contaminate soil and water.

## The Pig Solution

Pigs love to dig with their snouts, rooting up whatever they can find. When fenced into an area dominated by junkus, they systematically work through the soil, tearing up rhizomes and eating roots and tubers. The longer they remain in the area, the more thoroughly the soil is cleared.

The process works because pigs are highly motivated foragers that will dig through tough root masses all day long. They consume the rhizomes, eliminating the regrowth mechanism. Their rooting aerates compacted soil and breaks up hardpans. Their manure adds organic matter and fertility to the freshly turned soil. They accomplish all of this without diesel fuel, mechanical wear, or soil compaction from heavy equipment.

Pigs are among the most intelligent domesticated animals, and they thrive when given complex environments to explore. A junkus-infested paddock is not a problem for a pig -- it is an adventure playground and a buffet. This natural enthusiasm for rooting makes them ideal partners in land preparation.

## Integrated Benefits

The pig land-clearing system creates multiple simultaneous benefits:

1. **Feed recycling** -- pigs are fed kitchen and farm garbage, converting waste into meat and soil fertility
2. **Land preparation** -- the rooted-up soil is ready for orchard planting without additional tilling
3. **Soil improvement** -- pig manure and aeration create favorable conditions for newly planted fruit and nut trees
4. **Animal welfare** -- pigs engage in natural behaviors (rooting, foraging, exploring) rather than being confined to concrete pens
5. **Food production** -- the pigs themselves become a protein source for the farm community

This is a perfect example of the permaculture principle that "every element should perform multiple functions." The pigs are not just land-clearing tools -- they are waste recyclers, soil builders, and food producers simultaneously.

## Planning the Rotation

Effective pig clearing requires careful planning:

- **Fencing** -- electric fencing or sturdy panels contain pigs in the target area; portable electric fence allows easy movement between paddocks
- **Duration** -- leave pigs long enough to thoroughly work the soil but not so long they compact it or create excessive erosion; typically 2-4 weeks per paddock depending on density
- **Water** -- provide wallows and drinking water; pigs need access to mud for thermoregulation and skin health
- **Shelter** -- shade structures protect pigs from sun and rain; simple A-frame shelters work well and can be moved with the pigs
- **Follow-up** -- once pigs are moved out, plant ground covers immediately to prevent any surviving junkus from reestablishing
- **Stocking density** -- too few pigs and the job takes too long; too many and they compact the soil. Start with 4-6 pigs per acre and adjust based on results.

## Preparing for Orchard Planting

After the pigs have cleared the junkus, the site is ready for orchard establishment. The soil is loose, aerated, and enriched with manure. The process from this point typically follows this sequence:

1. **Assess soil** -- test pH, nutrient levels, and drainage patterns across the cleared area
2. **Plant cover crops** -- nitrogen-fixing cover crops (clover, vetch, field peas) build additional soil before fruit and nut trees go in
3. **Sheet mulch** -- where cover crops are not feasible, sheet mulch with cardboard, compost, and straw to suppress any surviving weeds
4. **Layout design** -- mark tree planting spots considering mature canopy size, sun angles, and water flow
5. **Plant fruit and nut trees** -- bare-root or container trees go into the prepared, fertile soil
6. **Establish understory** -- plant guild species around each tree for pest control, nutrient cycling, and mulch production

This sequence -- pig clearing, cover cropping, then tree planting -- mimics natural succession while dramatically speeding up the timeline. What might take decades of natural succession can be accomplished in a single growing season with pig power. The soil building benefits connect to [[nutrient-retention-soil-food-web]] principles.

## Urban Applications

While full-scale pig clearing is impractical in most urban settings, the principle translates to smaller systems that achieve similar results:

- **Chicken tractors** -- mobile chicken coops (called "tractors") can clear garden beds of weeds and pests while fertilizing the soil. Chickens scratch up the top few inches of soil, eat weed seeds and insect pests, and deposit manure. Move the tractor every few days for thorough coverage.
- **Duck paddocks** -- ducks are excellent for clearing slug and snail infestations. Confine them to a garden bed for a few days and they will consume virtually every slug and snail in the area.
- **Rabbit trenches** -- rabbits can be housed over compost piles or garden beds, where their manure and urine directly fertilize the soil below.
- **Guinea pig rotations** -- in some urban permaculture systems, guinea pigs are rotated through raised beds for weed control and fertilization.

## Other Animal-Based Clearing Methods

Beyond pigs, other animals contribute to land clearing and preparation:

- **Goats** -- the most versatile browsers, capable of clearing brush, woody weeds, and invasive species. Goats will eat plants that other livestock refuse, including blackberry brambles, poison ivy, and multiflora rose.
- **Sheep** -- less destructive rooting than pigs but effective for grazing down grass and herbaceous weeds. Their lighter weight makes them suitable for more fragile soils.
- **Cattle** -- heavy grazing can reduce weed competition in pasture, though cattle are less selective than goats or sheep.

## Ethics and Considerations

The permaculture approach treats pigs as partners in land management, not just meat animals. They receive quality of life -- space to root, forage, wallow, and socialize -- while performing valuable ecological work. The system honors the animal by integrating its natural behaviors into the farm's productive cycles. This ethical dimension connects to [[permaculture-introduction-and-ethics]] and [[urban-permaculture-guilds-plant-stacking]].

Key ethical considerations:

- Provide adequate space, shelter, and social companionship (pigs are highly social)
- Ensure access to clean water and wallows at all times
- Protect from predators and extreme weather
- Work with the pigs' natural instincts rather than against them
- Consider the full lifecycle -- from land clearing to food production to soil building

## Comparing Methods

A comparison of land clearing approaches:

| Method | Cost | Soil Impact | Speed | Side Benefits |
|--------|------|-------------|-------|---------------|
| Chemical herbicide | High | Negative (kills soil life) | Moderate | None |
| Mechanical tilling | Medium | Negative (compaction, breaks soil structure) | Fast | None |
| Pig clearing | Low (feed cost only) | Positive (aeration, manure) | Moderate | Meat, soil building |
| Sheet mulching | Low | Positive (adds organic matter) | Slow (months) | Weed suppression, moisture retention |

The pig approach stands out for its positive soil impact and multiple yields. While slower than mechanical tilling for the initial clearing, it produces a better soil environment for the subsequent orchard planting.
