# [[permaculture-ethics]] and Principles

Permaculture is a design framework for creating sustainable human settlements and agricultural systems. It was co-originated by [[bill-mollison]] and [[david-holmgren]] in the 1970s, emerging from a synthesis of ecological knowledge, traditional land-use practices, and systems thinking. The word itself is a contraction of "permanent agriculture" and "permanent culture," reflecting its ambition to design enduring, regenerative ways of living.

At the heart of permaculture lie three core ethics and twelve design principles. The ethics provide the foundational "why" — the moral reasoning that guides all permaculture work. The principles, articulated most completely by David Holmgren, provide the "how" — a practical design toolkit for applying those ethics on the ground. Together they form a coherent framework that can be adapted to any climate, culture, or scale of project.

It is important to understand that not every principle applies equally to every project. Think of the twelve principles as a menu of design tools rather than a rigid checklist. A given site or situation may call for heavy emphasis on some principles and little or none on others. Good [[permaculture-design]] emerges from thoughtful observation and the discerning application of these tools in context.

Permaculture has grown far beyond its Australian origins and is now practiced on every inhabited continent. Its influence can be seen in urban farms, rural homesteads, community resilience projects, and even in organizational design and digital systems. Despite this diversity of application, the three ethics and twelve principles remain the common thread that unites all permaculture practice.

The framework is intentionally open and adaptable. It draws from ecology, indigenous land management, [[appropriate-technology]], and community organizing without being confined to any single discipline. This breadth is one of permaculture's greatest strengths, allowing it to address complex, interconnected problems that single-issue approaches cannot.

## The Three Core Ethics

### Earth Care

Earth Care is the first and foundational ethic of permaculture. It calls for an altruistic commitment to the health of all living systems — soil, water, air, plants, animals, and the complex ecological relationships that bind them together. This is not passive stewardship but an active, deliberate practice of regenerating damaged ecosystems and protecting intact ones.

In practice, Earth Care means designing systems that rebuild soil fertility, clean water, increase biodiversity, and sequester carbon. It means treating the natural world not as a resource to be extracted but as a living community to which we belong. Every design decision — from choosing perennial crops over annuals to composting organic waste — is evaluated against its impact on ecological health.

Earth Care also carries a long-term perspective. A healthy ecosystem provides for generation after generation, whereas degraded landscapes collapse under short-term exploitation. Permaculture designers aim to leave every site better than they found it, creating positive ecological legacies that outlast any individual project.

The ethic of Earth Care extends to the global scale as well. Climate change, biodiversity loss, and pollution are planetary challenges that demand systemic responses. While permaculture begins at the local level — with a garden, a farm, a watershed — its cumulative impact contributes to the broader healing of the biosphere.

### People Care

People Care recognizes that human beings are part of ecological systems, not separate from them. It insists that people are not expendable and that any truly sustainable design must address human needs for food, shelter, health, education, community, and meaningful work. Compassion and mutual aid are central to this ethic.

People Care in permaculture extends beyond basic survival to encompass quality of life. It asks whether a design supports social connection, reduces drudgery, and empowers people to meet their own needs. Community gardens, cooperative housing, and local economic networks are all expressions of People Care in action.

This ethic also demands attention to equity and inclusion. Permaculture systems should be accessible to people of all backgrounds and abilities. When People Care is neglected, even ecologically sound designs can become tools of privilege, serving a fortunate few while leaving others behind.

Self-care is also a legitimate expression of People Care. Burnout undermines the long-term effectiveness of any permaculture practitioner. Designing systems that are manageable and enjoyable to maintain ensures that people can continue their work for years and decades rather than burning out after a single season.

### Fair Share

Fair Share is sometimes called "return of surplus" or "limit consumption and share the surplus." It completes the ethical triad by addressing the distribution of resources and the dangers of unchecked accumulation. The principle holds that there should be enough for all, and that abundance should be shared rather than hoarded.

In a world of finite resources, Fair Share challenges the growth-at-all-costs paradigm. It asks designers to consider limits — how much land, energy, and material is truly needed, and what to do with the surplus once basic needs are met. Sharing surplus strengthens community resilience and reduces the ecological footprint of individual households.

Fair Share also has a temporal dimension. It asks us to consider the needs of future generations and to design systems that do not compromise the ability of others — including other species — to meet their needs. This is closely aligned with the concept of intergenerational equity found in many indigenous traditions.

This ethic naturally constrains consumption and encourages generosity. When a household produces more food than it needs, the surplus is preserved, shared, or sold locally. When a community harvests excess timber, it is used for communal projects. Fair Share turns surplus from a driver of waste into a driver of connection and reciprocity.

## David Holmgren's Twelve Design Principles

### 1. Observe and Interact

Good design begins with careful observation. Before making any changes, spend time studying the site — its patterns of sun, wind, water, soil, and existing life. Observation reveals opportunities and constraints that would be missed by rushing to implement a plan. Interaction means engaging with the system as you observe it, testing assumptions and refining your understanding through direct experience.

Holmgren recommends observing a site for at least a full year before making significant changes. This allows the designer to witness seasonal cycles — frosts, floods, droughts, migrations — that may not be apparent from a single visit. Patience in observation prevents costly mistakes and reveals patterns that no amount of planning can substitute for.

### 2. Catch and Store Energy

Every sustainable system captures and holds onto the energy and resources that flow through it. This might mean harvesting rainwater, [[building-soil]] organic matter, planting windbreaks, or preserving seasonal harvests. The goal is to reduce dependence on external inputs by cycling energy and materials within the system as many times as possible.

Storing energy is especially important in the context of fossil fuel decline and climate instability. Systems that have built-in reserves — of water, food, seeds, and fertility — are far more resilient than those that depend on just-in-time deliveries from distant supply chains. The more energy a system can capture and retain, the less vulnerable it is to external disruption.

### 3. Obtain a Yield

A system that does not produce a useful yield is not truly sustainable, no matter how well-intentioned it may be. Every element in a design should provide for at least one human need, whether food, fiber, fuel, medicine, or habitat. Yield is not limited to material goods — it also includes non-tangible returns like beauty, education, and community cohesion.

The principle of obtaining a yield also serves as a practical check on idealism. A beautiful [[food-forest]] that produces nothing edible is not a functioning permaculture system. Designers must ensure that their systems are productive enough to justify the time, energy, and resources invested in them.

### 4. Apply Self-Regulation and Accept Feedback

Healthy systems regulate themselves through feedback loops. Permaculture designers build feedback mechanisms into their systems and remain open to critical information, even when it is uncomfortable. This principle also applies personally: designers must be willing to admit mistakes, abandon failing strategies, and adapt to changing conditions.

Self-regulation also means knowing when to stop. A healthy system does not grow without limit — it reaches a dynamic equilibrium where growth gives way to stability and maintenance. Permaculture designers resist the temptation to endlessly expand and instead focus on optimizing and deepening the systems they have already built.

### 5. [[use-and-value-renewable-resources]] and Services

Whenever possible, choose resources that can be replenished over human timescales — timber, solar energy, biological nitrogen fixation — over non-renewable alternatives. This principle also reminds us to value the services that nature provides for free, such as pest control by predatory insects or water purification by wetlands, rather than replacing them with energy-intensive technological substitutes.

The distinction between a resource and a service is important. A resource is a material stock that can be harvested and used. A service is an ongoing natural process that benefits the system without being consumed. Valuing services means designing with ecological processes rather than against them, letting nature do the work wherever possible.

### 6. Produce No Waste

Waste is simply a resource in the wrong place. Permaculture design seeks to close loops so that the output of one element becomes the input for another. Composting food scraps, using greywater for irrigation, and designing for deconstruction rather than demolition are all expressions of this principle. The aim is a system where nothing is discarded and everything has value.

### 7. Design from Patterns to Details

Start with the big picture — the broad patterns of landscape, climate, and social context — before filling in the details. A watershed pattern informs water management; a sun pattern informs planting zones; a community pattern informs shared spaces. By designing from patterns down, individual details fit into a coherent whole rather than becoming isolated, disconnected elements.

Patterns exist at every scale, from the spiral of a nautilus shell to the branching of a river system to the social networks of a neighborhood. Learning to recognize these patterns — in nature, in culture, and in data — is one of the most powerful skills a permaculture designer can develop. Patterns are the bridge between observation and design.

### 8. Integrate Rather Than Segregate

Relationships between elements are more productive than the elements themselves in isolation. Planting guilds of mutually supportive species, stacking functions in multi-use structures, and creating gathering spaces that encourage social interaction all embody this principle. Integration reduces the need for external inputs by harnessing the synergies that emerge when elements work together.

The permaculture concept of "each element performs multiple functions" is the flip side of this principle. An apple tree provides fruit, shade, mulch, pollen, and a trellis for climbing vines. By seeking these multi-functional relationships, designers create dense, interdependent systems that are far more productive than collections of single-purpose elements.

### 9. Use Small and Slow Solutions

Small-scale, incremental change is more manageable, more adaptable, and less risky than large, rapid interventions. Slow solutions give systems time to stabilize and feedback loops time to develop. This principle cautions against the hubris of grand schemes and encourages designers to start small, learn from results, and scale up gradually as confidence builds.

The proverb "slow is smooth, smooth is fast" captures the spirit of this principle well. Rushed implementations often fail and require costly rework, while patient, incremental approaches compound into transformative change over time. Small and slow is ultimately faster than fast and broken.

### 10. Use and Value Diversity

Diversity provides resilience. A diverse polyculture is less vulnerable to pest outbreaks and market fluctuations than a monoculture. Similarly, diverse communities, economies, and skill sets are more adaptable to disruption. Permaculture designers actively cultivate diversity at every level — biological, social, and economic — as a hedge against uncertainty and collapse.

Diversity also enriches human experience. A diverse landscape is more beautiful, more interesting, and more educationally rich than a uniform one. A diverse community is more creative and more just. Valuing diversity is not just a pragmatic strategy — it is a recognition of the intrinsic worth of variety and difference.

### 11. Use Edges and Value the Marginal

The edge — where one ecosystem meets another, where forest meets field, or where water meets land — is where the greatest diversity and productivity occur. Permaculture designers seek to maximize productive edges through techniques like keyline design, pond edges, and mixed borders. This principle also has a social dimension: marginalized people and overlooked spaces often hold untapped potential.

A pond edge, for example, supports more species per square meter than either the open water or the dry land beside it. A hedgerow between a field and a forest serves as a wildlife corridor, a windbreak, a source of forage, and a microclimate modifier all at once. Edges are where the action is, and good design takes advantage of that fact.

### 12. Creatively Use and Respond to Change

Change is inevitable and can be a source of opportunity rather than a threat. Disturbance — whether from fire, flood, economic disruption, or social upheaval — opens niches for innovation and renewal. Permaculture designers build flexibility into their systems so they can absorb shocks and reorganize around new conditions rather than breaking under the strain.

Succession is a natural model for this principle. After a disturbance, ecosystems do not return to their previous state — they move forward through a series of successional stages toward a new equilibrium. Permaculture designers can work with succession rather than against it, planting pioneer species that prepare the ground for later, more complex communities.

## Putting It All Together

The ethics and principles of permaculture are not abstract philosophy — they are practical tools for designing the world we want to live in. The three ethics remind us to keep our work grounded in care, compassion, and justice. The twelve principles give us concrete strategies for turning those values into action. Used together, they offer a pathway toward systems that are ecologically regenerative, socially just, and economically viable over the long term.

Learning permaculture is a lifelong process of observation, experimentation, and refinement. The ethics provide a constant compass, and the principles provide an evolving toolkit. As conditions change — as climates shift, as communities grow, as new knowledge emerges — the framework adapts, remaining as relevant in the future as it was when Mollison and Holmgren first articulated it decades ago.
