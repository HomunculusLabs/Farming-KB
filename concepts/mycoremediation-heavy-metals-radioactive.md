# Mycoremediation of Heavy Metals and Radioactive Elements

Using mushrooms to bioaccumulate, sequester, and remove heavy metals (arsenic, cadmium, lead, mercury, copper) and radioactive elements (cesium-134, cesium-137) from contaminated environments. From [[paul-stamets]]' *Mycelium Running* (2005).

## Two Distinct Mechanisms

Unlike organic pollutants which fungi enzymatically decompose, heavy metals cannot be broken down. Fungi deal with metals through two different mechanisms:

### 1. Bioaccumulation / Hyperaccumulation (Living Mycelium)

Mushrooms selectively absorb and concentrate metals at levels 1 to 10,000 times above background environmental concentrations. The mushroom becomes a "portable cellular vessel" for the toxin — when harvested, ambient contamination levels decrease.

### 2. Biosorption (Dead Mycelium)

Even dead fungal biomass can bind metals through ion exchange, complexation, and precipitation on cell wall surfaces. This is the one exception to the rule that mycelium must be alive for mycoremediation.

## Chernobyl: Mushrooms as Radioactive Bioaccumulators

After the April 26, 1986 Chernobyl reactor meltdown:

- Nearby residents absorbed radiation levels 100x greater than Hiroshima survivors at similar proximity
- German health officials discovered people showed increased whole-body radioactivity after a single meal of wild mushrooms
- Nearly all radioactive cesium in Swedish mushrooms is traceable to Chernobyl
- French officials confiscated Bulgarian mushrooms containing 4x permitted cesium levels
- Japan confiscated Italian porcini (Boletus edulis) for high cesium levels
- Moscow officials passed Geiger counters over all marketplace foods
- A single jar of dried mushrooms emitted radiation 20x the admissible level
- Mushrooms top the list of all foods identified as radioactive bioaccumulators

## Case Study: Vashon Island, Washington

In 2003, Stamets visited Vashon Island to address residents concerned about environmental health:

- Decades of airborne pollutants from Tacoma's Asarco smelter contaminated the island with arsenic, cadmium, and mercury
- For the first time, Stamets advised people NOT to eat wild mushrooms near their homes
- Recommended having mushrooms analyzed first ($35-$150 per test from ATSDR/CDC) to identify which species were accumulating metals
- Community was receptive but lacked a manual for mycorestoration — this was one motivation for writing Mycelium Running

## Mercury Biosorption: Arica et al. (2003)

A breakthrough study using turkey tail (Trametes versicolor) and phoenix oyster (Pleurotus pulmonarius var. 'sajor-caju'):

- **Method**: Combined mycelium with small beads (essentially composed of the mycelia) and alginic acid
- **Result**: Removed 97% of mercuric ions from water
- **Dead mycelium test**: Even dead mycelium removed 73-81% of mercuric ions — physical features of cellular architecture bonded with mercuric ions
- **Mechanism**: Live mycelium produces organomercury lyases (enzymes that break down organomercury into HgO via mercuric reductase); freed mercury bonds with selenium

## Strategic Harvesting for Remediation

A proposed strategy for long-term, incremental heavy metal cleanup:

1. Grow mycorrhizal or saprophytic mushrooms through contaminated mulch
2. Selectively harvest each year during fruiting season
3. Reduce background contamination level with each harvest
4. Take metal-laden mushrooms to toxic waste sites for burial, storage, or incineration
5. Residual metals can be resold to metal recycling industry

### Safety Calculation

To determine heavy metal exposure from mushrooms:
- Take mg of heavy metals per kg (dry weight) of mushroom
- Divide by body weight
- Compare to daily allowable limit from ATSDR (http://www.atsdr.cdc.gov)

## Enzymatic Metal Precipitation

Using various reducing enzymes, fungi can precipitate many metals around their mycelia:
- Silver (Ag)
- Selenium (Se)
- Tellurite (Te)
- Cadmium (Cd)
- Lead (Pb)
- Mercury (Hg)

Some fungi produce organomercury lyases that break down organomercury into HgO by mercuric reductase. Once freed, mercury can bond with selenium — another metal some mushrooms concentrate through "upchanneling."

## Key Principles

- Mushrooms forming fungi have a decided advantage: the compacted hyphae of the mushroom body make it easy to collect bioconcentrated toxins in solid form
- The toxins move into a portable vessel: a mushroom that, when removed, reduces ambient toxin levels
- Some species show metal specificity — favoring particular heavy metals over others
- About 2 dozen species are currently known as hyperaccumulators
- Knowing where your mushrooms come from is critical — all the more reason to support organic growers
