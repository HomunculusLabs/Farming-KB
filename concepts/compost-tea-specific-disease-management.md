## Compost Tea for Specific Disease Management

Compost tea (AACT) has been applied against a wide range of plant diseases and pests, with varying degrees of documented success. The effectiveness depends critically on whether the tea contains the right balance of organisms — particularly the bacterial-to-fungal ratio — for the specific pathogen being targeted.

## Fungal Pathogens

### Black Spot (Diplocarpon rosae)

Black spot is one of the easier diseases to control with compost tea. The black coloration is caused by spores of the fungus; the initial infection appears as vague gray hyphal growth on the leaf surface. As hyphae mature, spores form and the characteristic black spots develop and enlarge.

The key protective mechanism is **surface coverage** — if beneficial organisms already occupy the leaf surface, the black-spot fungus cannot push them out because its enzymatic ability is extremely weak. It cannot outcompete any other organism for space. This makes it vulnerable to any well-made compost tea.

The reason black spot is so prevalent is that conventional management practices continuously destroy the biology on leaf surfaces. Getting the organisms back and then ceasing to kill them is the solution. Neighbors" practices can also undermine efforts by destroying healthy leaf-surface biology through drift of fungicides and other chemicals.

### Powdery Mildew

Powdery mildew has been controlled on vines (3+ months of documented success) and strawberries (4+ months) using AACT. Unlike black spot, mildew is not considered easy to control and requires more attention to tea composition.

The SARE grant vineyard trials demonstrated that adequate **fungal biomass** on leaf surfaces was essential for protection. Late in the season, when fungal biomass declined even as bacterial biomass remained high, protection failed. This finding is critical: bacterial dominance alone is insufficient for mildew — the fungal component must be maintained throughout the season.

Roger Hanson at Paso Robles Vineyard achieved two successful seasons controlling mildew and other pathogens, reducing sulfur and chemical sprays from 10–14 per season down to just one application. This represents a dramatic reduction in chemical inputs while maintaining disease control.

### Fusarium (Including Snow Mold)

Fusarium appears to be preventable with a **bacterial tea alone** — the full organism complement is not required. This makes it one of the most straightforward targets for [[compost-tea-application-methods]] and one of the easiest diseases to demonstrate compost tea efficacy.

### Rhizoctonia

Some Rhizoctonia species can be prevented with bacterial tea, but not all species. Certain Rhizoctonia strains require the **full bacterial plus fungal tea** to achieve suppression, indicating species-specific sensitivity to different [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]]. Before targeting Rhizoctonia, identify the specific species present to determine the tea type needed.

### Anthracnose

Anthracnose location determines the tea type needed:
- **Crown, root, or soil anthracnose**: Requires a strong fungal component in the tea.
- **Foliar anthracnose**: Can be prevented with bacterial tea alone.

This distinction is important for recipe development — the same disease organism requires different approaches depending on where it is attacking the plant.

### Cedar Apple Rust and General Rust

AACT with a strong fungal component stops cedar apple rust. Work on wheat in Nebraska showed excellent rust control results. These diseases require adequate fungal biomass in the tea for consistent suppression, making [[ingham-fungal-food-selection]] in the tea recipe critical.

### Late Blight and Fireblight

Extensive experience exists with late blight suppression through compost tea. Fungi are needed to suppress fireblight. Anecdotal reports from several estates in New York suggest fireblight was removed through AACT programs (contact Paul Wagner and James Sottilo at soilfoodwebny@aol.com for documented results).

A multi-year scientific study on mildew control in vineyards was underway at the time of writing, with one more year of data collection before publication. This study was testing application frequency and concentration optimization.

### Verticillium Wilt

Verticillium prevention requires comprehensive coverage across all plant surfaces and the orchard floor: leaves, blossoms, bark, and downed leaves must be covered at least 70% with both bacteria and fungi (fungi must be at least 5% of that 70%). Applications should begin two weeks before bud break. Fall spraying of the orchard floor and downed leaves is the most effective timing because it removes overwintering sites for the pathogen.

### Peach Leaf Curl (Taphrina)

Leaf curl caused by Taphrina is effectively controlled when the tea contains beneficial fungi growing actively. The protocol requires multiple applications across seasons:

1. **Fall**: Foliar spray before leaf drop to establish healthy organisms and promote decomposition.
2. **Fall**: Soil drench to decompose leaves instead of allowing pathogens to overwinter on fallen debris.
3. **Spring**: Soil drench again before growth begins.
4. **Pre-budbreak**: Foliar AACT two weeks before budbreak to protect emerging tissue.
5. **Season**: Minimum monthly applications, or every 10–14 days depending on disease severity.

A single spray is never adequate — the organisms must be established and maintained over time. Compost tea is not a pesticide and should not be discussed as equivalent to chemical control methods.

### Brown Rot (Cherries)

No replicated data exists, but AACT has shown good results against brown rot. The tea should be made as fungal as possible for best results.

## Insect Pest Management

### Scale Insects

Using tea with excellent fungal biomass, scale on rhododendrons and apples has been eliminated. This appears to be a fungal-biomass-dependent effect, suggesting other scale-infested plants may respond similarly, though data is limited to these two genera so far.

### Spider Mites

Evidence is entirely anecdotal but remarkably consistent — nearly everyone using AACT reports that foliar insects "go away." However, rigorous scientific studies with entomologists are still needed to document and quantify this effect with controlled experiments.

### Root Grubs and Wireworms

AACT typically contains fungi parasitic on insect larvae, particularly **Beauveria** species. Beauveria may be available as a direct inoculum and can be added to AACT so it is active and ready to attack root grubs and wireworms on contact in the soil. Adding paper or cardboard to compost provides food sources that encourage these insect-attacking fungi to proliferate.

### Tent Caterpillars

[[bacillus-thuringiensis]] (Bt) must be ingested to work, but getting it inside tent structures is difficult. A high-bacterial brew may also work by causing digestive disruption when ingested. However, bacteria do not generally attack insect cuticles — for that, **Beauveria** or **Entomophaga** (insect-attacking fungi) are needed. Including paper or cardboard in compost, along with organic matter containing natural sources of these fungi, ensures their presence in the tea.

### Fruit Fly Larvae

Beauveria does not penetrate into the maggot inside the fruit, so soil application timing is critical. Apply Beauveria-containing tea as a soil drench in fall (150 L/HA) so the fungus has all winter to consume larval stages in the soil, then again in spring before emergence. Foliar sprays with bacteria that repel adult flies complement the soil program.

## Hydroponics Applications

Compost tea has been applied successfully in hydroponic systems to address algal problems and some root diseases. The approach involves adding enough tea to the water column to restore predator populations ([[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]]) so that normal nutrient cycling returns. Work on water "mold" problems (oomycetes) was ongoing. The key challenge in hydroponics is maintaining aerobic conditions when adding organic matter to a water-based system.

## Key Principle: Biology Over Chemistry

Across all these diseases and pests, the consistent theme is that compost tea works by establishing the right biological community rather than through chemical toxicity. The specific organism mix needed varies by target:

- Some diseases require **bacterial dominance** (Fusarium, foliar anthracnose)
- Some require **fungal dominance** (crown anthracnose, cedar apple rust, peach leaf curl)
- Most require **both bacteria and fungi** at adequate levels
- A few require specific **biocontrol organisms** (Beauveria for insect larvae)

Understanding the biology of the target pathogen or pest determines the tea recipe needed for success. When the full foodweb is present with adequate coverage, [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]] is the natural outcome.
