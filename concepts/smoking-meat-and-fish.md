## Overview
Smoking is one of the oldest food preservation methods, using wood smoke to dry, flavor, and preserve meat and fish. Smoke deposits antimicrobial compounds on the food surface, while the drying process reduces moisture to levels that inhibit bacterial growth. Two primary methods exist: cold smoking (preservation focus, 68-86F) and hot smoking (cooking focus, 140-180F). Both can be done with simple equipment on a homestead.
## Cold Smoking vs Hot Smoking
### Cold Smoking
- Temperature: 68-86F (20-30C)
- Purpose: Preservation and flavor — does not cook the food
- Duration: 12 hours to several weeks depending on the product
- Must be cured (salted or brined) before cold smoking for safety
- Produces deeply smoked, dry products that store without refrigeration
- Examples: smoked salmon, bacon, ham, salami, jerky, smoked cheese
- Requires a separate smoke source from the smoking chamber (fire is outside, smoke is piped in)
### Hot Smoking
- Temperature: 140-180F (60-82C)
- Purpose: Cooking and flavor — food is fully cooked during smoking
- Duration: 1-6 hours
- Curing optional but recommended for flavor and safety
- Produces moist, cooked products that require refrigeration
- Examples: smoked chicken, ribs, brisket, fish fillets, trout
- Fire and food are in the same chamber or adjacent with direct heat transfer
### Warm Smoking (Intermediate)
- Temperature: 86-140F (30-60C)
- Bridges the gap between cold and hot smoking
- Used for products that benefit from longer smoking at moderate temperatures
- Examples: kielbasa, some sausages, smoked duck
## Brining and Curing
### Dry Cure
A mixture of salt, sugar, and optional spices rubbed directly onto the meat surface:
- Salt concentration: 2-3% of meat weight for mild cure, 3-4% for preservation cure
- Sugar: 1-2% of meat weight (balances salt flavor, aids browning)
- Prague Powder #1 (pink curing salt): 0.25% of meat weight for nitrite protection against botulism during cold smoking
- Apply cure, place in non-reactive container (glass, stainless, food-grade plastic), refrigerate
- Time: 1 day per 2 pounds of meat thickness (e.g., a 4-inch thick pork belly cures for 2 days)
- Flip and redistribute cure daily
### Wet Brine
Salt and sugar dissolved in water, with meat fully submerged:
- Basic brine: 1 cup kosher salt + 1/2 cup sugar per gallon of water
- Prague Powder #1: 1 teaspoon per 5 pounds of meat
- Submerge meat, weight down to keep fully covered
- Refrigerate for 12-48 hours depending on thickness
- Rinse before smoking to remove surface salt
### Why Curing Matters for Cold Smoking
Cold smoking temperatures (68-86F) are in the bacterial danger zone (40-140F). Without curing:
- Surface bacteria can multiply during the extended smoking time
- Clostridium botulinum spores could potentially germinate in the anaerobic interior of large pieces of meat
- Salt and nitrites in the cure inhibit bacterial growth during the process
## Wood Selection
### Best Smoking Woods
| Wood | Flavor Profile | Best With |
|------|---------------|-----------|
| Hickory | Strong, pungent, bacon-like | Pork, beef, ribs |
| Oak | Medium, versatile, balanced | Beef, pork, fish, poultry |
| Apple | Mild, sweet, fruity | Poultry, pork, fish, game birds |
| Cherry | Mild, sweet, slightly tart | Poultry, pork, duck, lamb |
| Maple | Mild, sweet | Pork, poultry, vegetables |
| Alder | Very mild, delicate | Salmon, trout, other fish |
| Mesquite | Very strong, earthy | Beef, brisket (use sparingly) |
| Pecan | Rich, nutty, medium | Poultry, pork, cheese |
| Peach | Mild, sweet | Poultry, pork |
### Woods to Avoid
- Pine, fir, spruce, cedar — Resinous, produce harsh bitter smoke and can cause illness
- Treated or painted wood — Toxic chemicals
- Plywood, particle board — Glues and adhesives are toxic when burned
- Locust, osage orange — Extremely dense and harsh
- Moldy wood — Can produce off-flavors and [[mycotoxins]]
### Wood Preparation
- Use seasoned wood (dried 6-12 months) — moisture content around 20%
- Soak wood chunks in water for 30-60 minutes before use for longer, cooler smoke
- For chips: scatter a handful at a time over hot coals
- For chunks: 2-4 fist-sized chunks per smoking session
- For logs/splits: use in offset smokers, add one every 45-60 minutes
- Never use more wood than needed — excessive smoke creates bitter, acrid flavors
### Smoker Types
**Offset smoker:** Classic design with separate firebox. Excellent for long sessions. Good for hot and cold smoking.
**Vertical bullet smoker:** Cylindrical, fire at bottom, water pan in middle. Efficient, beginner-friendly, good for hot smoking.
**Cabinet smoker:** Insulated box with electric/gas heat and wood chip tray. Precise temperature control, easy to use.
**DIY options:** 55-gallon drum ($100), cinder block stack (permanent), or small smokehouse with separate fire pit and underground pipe (ideal for cold smoking).
## Smoking Process
### Hot Smoking Step-by-Step
1. Cure or brine the meat (optional for hot smoking but recommended)
2. Rinse, dry, and air-dry 1-2 hours to form a pellicle (tacky surface that absorbs smoke)
3. Preheat smoker to target temperature (140F initially, increasing to 160-180F)
4. Add wood, place meat on racks skin/fat side up
5. Smoke for recommended time, checking internal temperature with a meat thermometer:
   - Poultry: 165F, Pork: 145F (3-min rest), Fish: 145F, Brisket: 203F
6. Rest 10-30 minutes before slicing
### Cold Smoking Step-by-Step
1. Cure with dry cure or brine including curing salts (essential for safety)
2. Rinse and air-dry 12-24 hours to form a pellicle
3. Smoke at 68-86F for 12 hours to several weeks
4. Monitor temperature — if ambient exceeds 90F, move product to cooler location
5. After smoking, air-dry to reduce moisture to 25-35% for preservation
6. Refrigerate or freeze after smoking
## Food Safety for Smoked Products
### Cold Smoking Risks
Cold smoking is riskier because food spends extended time in the danger zone:
- Always cure with salt and nitrites before cold smoking
- Monitor temperature; if it exceeds 90F, move product to cooler location
- After smoking, products should lose 20-25% of original weight through moisture loss
- Cold-smoked fish: 2-3 weeks refrigerated, 3-6 months frozen
- Cold-smoked meats: 1-2 weeks refrigerated, 4-6 months frozen
### Hot Smoking Safety
- Use thermometer to verify safe internal temperatures
- Refrigerate within 2 hours, consume within 3-4 days or freeze up to 6 months
### Botulism Risk
- Low oxygen + moderate temperatures + moisture creates botulism potential
- Curing salts (Prague Powder #1) are the primary defense
- Never cold-smoke uncured meat for extended periods
- Discard any product with off-odor, slime, mold, or unusual color
## Storage Guidelines
- Hot-smoked fish: 3-4 days refrigerated, 2-3 months frozen
- Cold-smoked salmon: 2-3 weeks refrigerated, 3-6 months frozen
- Hot-smoked chicken: 3-4 days refrigerated, 4 months frozen
- Bacon (cold smoked): 1-2 weeks refrigerated, 6 months frozen
- Smoked jerky: 2-3 weeks refrigerated, 6 months frozen
