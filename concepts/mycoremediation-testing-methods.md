# Mycoremediation Testing Methods

Low-tech biological assays provide accessible, cost-effective ways to evaluate whether [[mycoremediation]] and [[mycofiltration]] efforts have meaningfully reduced contaminant toxicity. While they cannot identify specific degradation products or confirm complete mineralization, bioassays offer an integrative measure of residual biological harm that chemical analysis alone may miss. The three core assays described here—bean germination, earthworm survival, and bacterial contamination—form a practical triad for field-level assessment.

These methods are intentionally designed to be deployable without sophisticated laboratory equipment, making them suitable for community-scale remediation projects, [[citizen-science-fungal-biodiversity-discovery]] programs, and initial screening before committing to expensive professional analysis. Each assay targets a different trophic level and biological endpoint, providing complementary information about the overall safety of treated materials.

## Bean Germination Assay

The bean test is a broad-spectrum phytotoxicity screen. Seeds of a fast-germinating legume (e.g., mung beans or common bush beans) are exposed to serial dilutions of the treated water, soil extract, or substrate leachate to determine whether residual contaminants inhibit germination and early seedling vigor. Beans are an excellent choice because they germinate reliably within 3–5 days, are sensitive to a wide range of phytotoxins, and their root development is easy to measure quantitatively.

### Procedure

1. **Prepare sample extract**: Collect a representative sample of the mycofiltered water or a soil/slurry extract. If testing solid substrate, mix a measured mass (e.g., 100 g dry weight) with deionized water at a 1:5 ratio, shake for 30 minutes, and allow solids to settle. Filter or decant the supernatant.

2. **Set up serial dilutions**: Prepare 12 dilution wells or containers. The first well receives undiluted sample (100% concentration). Each subsequent well is diluted by half (e.g., 50%, 25%, 12.5%, continuing to roughly 0.05% in the 12th well). Use a neutral control well filled with deionized or distilled water. The 12-dilution series provides a wide dynamic range, allowing detection of both high and low residual toxicity.

3. **Plant seeds**: Place 5–10 pre-soaked bean seeds on filter paper or sterile cotton in each container. Add enough of the corresponding dilution to keep the medium moist but not submerged. Cover loosely to retain humidity while permitting gas exchange. Pre-soaking seeds for 4–8 hours in distilled water improves uniformity of germination timing.

4. **Incubate**: Maintain at room temperature (20–25 °C) with indirect light. Check daily for 5–7 days, replenishing moisture as needed with the appropriate dilution. Avoid direct sunlight, which can cause temperature fluctuations and accelerate evaporation.

5. **Record observations**: For each dilution, track germination rate (percentage of seeds sprouted), radicle length, hypocotyl length, and any visible abnormalities (discoloration, lesions, stunting). Calculate an EC50 value—the dilution at which 50% of seeds fail to germinate or show significant impairment—by plotting germination or root length against concentration.

6. **Document photographically**: Photograph each dilution tray at the final time point. Visual documentation provides a clear record that is useful for comparison across sampling dates and for communicating results to stakeholders who may not interpret numerical data easily.

### Interpretation

A low EC50 (i.e., toxicity persists even at high dilution) indicates the mycofiltration process did not sufficiently reduce phytotoxic compounds. A high EC50, approaching the control, suggests effective detoxification. Comparing pre- and post-treatment EC50 values provides a direct measure of remediation efficiency. Root elongation is generally the most sensitive endpoint; even when germination percentage appears normal, shortened or deformed roots can reveal sublethal contamination.

## Eisenia fetida Earthworm Assay

The earthworm assay evaluates soil toxicity using red wigglers (*[[eisenia-fetida]]*), a standard bioindicator organism in ecotoxicology. This 14-day survival test is sensitive to a wide range of contaminants including heavy metals, pesticides, [[mycoremediation-petroleum-hydrocarbons]], and [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]] hydrocarbons. *Eisenia fetida* is preferred because of its wide availability, ease of culturing, and established sensitivity profiles in the ecotoxicological literature.

### Procedure

1. **Prepare test soil**: Homogenize the treated soil or substrate. Moisture-adjust to approximately 60–80% of water-holding capacity. Fill replicate containers (typically 500 g soil per container, 4–5 replicates per treatment group). Include a negative control using uncontaminated soil of similar texture and organic matter content. Containers should be transparent or translucent to allow observation without opening.

2. **Acclimate worms**: Obtain healthy adult *Eisenia fetida* (clitellate, 300–500 mg each) from a verified supplier or existing culture. Rinse briefly in deionized water and blot dry. Allow worms to void gut contents on moist filter paper for 24 hours if precise mass measurements are needed.

3. **Introduce worms**: Place 10 worms into each test container. Cover containers with perforated lids to maintain humidity while allowing air exchange. Keep in a climate-controlled environment at 20 °C ± 2 °C with a 12:12 light-dark cycle. Avoid stacking containers to ensure uniform conditions.

4. **Monitor over 14 days**: Check containers on days 1, 4, 7, 10, and 14. Record mortality (worms unresponsive to gentle prodding), behavioral changes (surface emergence, curling, excess mucus production), and mass change at the end of the test period. Remove dead worms at each check to prevent [[decomposition]] from confounding results.

5. **Calculate survival and sublethal endpoints**: Report percent survival per treatment. The LC50 (concentration causing 50% mortality) can be estimated if multiple concentration levels are tested. Note sublethal effects such as mass loss exceeding 10%, which may indicate chronic toxicity even when survival is high.

### Interpretation

Survival rates at or above 80% in treated soil, with no significant mass loss relative to the control, indicate the substrate is likely safe for planting or land application. Consistent mortality or sublethal stress signals that residual toxicity remains and further remediation or stabilization is necessary. If worms attempt to escape the treated soil (climbing container walls), this avoidance behavior is itself a meaningful toxicity signal, as earthworms actively avoid chemically hostile substrates.

## Bacterial Contamination Assays

Monitoring bacterial contaminants—particularly total heterotrophic counts, coliforms, and potential pathogens—is essential when mycoremediation targets sewage, agricultural runoff, or mixed waste streams. Three complementary methods provide a practical assessment ranging from rapid qualitative screening to quantitative enumeration.

### Gram Stain for Rapid Morphological Screening

Prepare a heat-fixed smear of the sample (or an enrichment culture) on a glass slide. Apply crystal violet for 30 seconds, rinse, apply Gram's iodine for 30 seconds, rinse, decolorize briefly with ethanol or acetone (2–3 seconds), rinse, and counterstain with safranin for 30 seconds. Gram-positive organisms appear purple; gram-negative organisms appear pink/red. This rapid differential stain helps characterize the dominant bacterial community and can flag the presence of enteric gram-negative rods, which are of concern in fecal contamination scenarios. A trained eye can distinguish cocci, bacilli, and spiral forms within minutes, providing early directional information about the [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]].

### Dilution-to-Extinction

Prepare a 10-fold serial dilution series of the liquid sample in sterile saline or broth (e.g., 10⁻¹ through 10⁻⁸). Inoculate liquid growth medium (nutrient broth or a selective medium) with 1 mL from each dilution. Incubate at the appropriate temperature for 24–48 hours. The most dilute tube showing visible growth indicates the approximate order of magnitude of the viable cell count. This method is useful for quick, equipment-free estimates of bacterial load and requires only test tubes, a pipette (or even a loop), and an incubator or warm location.

### Spread Plating for Quantitative Counts

Spread 0.1 mL aliquots from selected dilutions (typically 10⁻⁴ through 10⁻⁷) onto solid agar plates (nutrient agar for total heterotrophs; MacConkey agar for gram-negative enteric bacteria; EMB or Endo agar for coliforms). Incubate plates inverted at 35–37 °C for 24–48 hours. Count colonies on plates yielding 30–300 colonies and back-calculate to colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL) of the original sample. For coliform confirmation, transfer lactose-fermenting colonies to Brilliant Green Lactose Bile Broth and check for gas production at 44.5 °C (fecal coliforms). Plate counts provide the most reliable quantitative data and are the standard method for regulatory compliance testing.

## Professional Laboratory Follow-Up

Bioassays answer the question "Is it still toxic?" but they cannot answer "What is it now?" or "Is it completely gone?" Mycoremediation frequently transforms rather than mineralizes contaminants, producing metabolites that may be less toxic, equally toxic, or in rare cases more toxic than the parent compound. Professional analytical chemistry—typically gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS), liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), or inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) for metals—is required to:

- Confirm whether the original contaminant has been degraded below regulatory thresholds
- Identify any transformation products that may have formed
- Distinguish between true biodegradation (mineralization to CO₂, H₂O, and biomass) and biotransformation (conversion to a different chemical form)
- Satisfy regulatory reporting requirements for contaminated site closure
- Assess whether residual metal concentrations have been immobilized into less bioavailable forms or remain in mobile, toxic species

Send paired samples (pre-treatment and post-treatment) to an accredited environmental laboratory. Request analysis for the known contaminants of concern as well as a broad-spectrum scan for unexpected metabolites. Pair the analytical results with your bioassay data to build a complete remediation efficacy profile. The combination of biological toxicity data and chemical speciation data provides the strongest basis for decisions about substrate reuse, disposal, or further treatment.

## Evaluating Mycofiltration Efficiency with Bioassays

To use these assays as a practical evaluation framework for mycofiltration installations:

1. **Establish baselines**: Run all three assays on untreated influent before mycofiltration begins. Document EC50, earthworm survival, and bacterial counts as reference points. Without baselines, it is impossible to quantify improvement or compare the performance of different mycelial species or substrate configurations.

2. **Sample at the outlet**: Collect treated effluent or substrate samples at defined intervals (e.g., weekly for the first month, then monthly). Run the same assays and compare against baselines. Maintain consistent [[lichen-biodiversity-sampling-protocols-data-analysis]] to ensure comparability across time points.

3. **Track trends over time**: Mycofiltration efficiency typically improves as the mycelial mat matures and the associated microbial community establishes. Plot bioassay results over time to visualize this maturation curve. Expect variable results in the first few weeks as the system establishes biological equilibrium.

4. **Set practical benchmarks**: Define pass/fail criteria appropriate to your context—for example, bean germination above 80% at full strength, earthworm survival above 80% over 14 days, and coliform counts below 200 CFU/100 mL for non-potable reuse. Benchmarks should reflect the intended end use of the treated material.

5. **Combine with simple chemistry**: Supplement bioassays with basic field tests (pH, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, turbidity) to capture gross water quality parameters that influence organism health and assay reliability. A sudden pH shift, for example, could cause assay failure unrelated to the contaminants of interest.

6. **Escalate when bioassays fail**: If any bioassay indicates persistent toxicity after a reasonable remediation period (typically 4–8 weeks of active mycofiltration), send samples for professional analysis before making decisions about substrate disposal, land application, or system redesign.

These low-tech assays democratize remediation monitoring, enabling community-scale projects to generate meaningful efficacy data without expensive instrumentation. They are not a substitute for professional analysis, but they are a powerful complement—detecting biological harm that targeted chemical testing may overlook, and providing an integrative snapshot of ecosystem-level safety.

## Quality Control and Common Pitfalls

Reliable bioassay results depend on consistent methodology and awareness of potential confounding factors:

- **Always run controls**: Every assay batch must include a negative control (clean water or uncontaminated soil) and, where possible, a positive control (a sample with known toxicity) to verify that the assay organisms are responding as expected. A negative control that shows poor germination or worm mortality indicates a procedural problem rather than sample toxicity.

- **Avoid cross-contamination**: Use separate tools for each dilution level. Flame-sterilize loops between plates. Change gloves between handling raw and treated samples.

- **Account for sample matrix effects**: Highly colored or turbid samples can interfere with visual assessments. Clarify extracts by settling or gentle centrifugation before use in the bean assay. High organic matter in soil samples can cause false negatives in the worm assay by binding contaminants and reducing bioavailability—this is a realistic effect worth measuring, not a flaw to eliminate.

- **Temperature matters**: All three assays are temperature-sensitive. Germination rates shift dramatically outside the 20–25 °C window. Worm metabolism and bacterial growth rates are equally temperature-dependent. Record [[jadam-ambient-temperature-principle]] alongside assay results and, if possible, use a temperature-controlled space.

- **Replication is essential**: A single bean seed or a single worm is anecdotal. Minimum replication of 5 seeds per dilution and 10 worms per container provides statistical confidence. Where resources allow, run duplicate containers per treatment group.

- **Blind scoring reduces bias**: If the expected outcome is known, have an uninformed assistant score germination or worm mortality to prevent unconscious bias from inflating positive results.

## Integration with Mycofiltration Design

These bioassays are most valuable when integrated into the mycofiltration system from the design stage, not retrofitted after installation. Consider the following design principles:

- **Sample ports**: Build sampling points into the filtration bed at the inlet, midpoint, and outlet to track contaminant reduction spatially as well as temporally. This helps identify channeling or dead zones within the substrate.

- **Parallel test beds**: If evaluating multiple fungal species or substrate formulations, run identical small-scale test beds side by side and compare bioassay results to select the best performer before scaling up.

- **Pre-treatment baselines**: Characterize the influent contaminant profile with bioassays before selecting a fungal species. Different fungi target different compound classes; knowing what is actually present in your waste stream allows a more informed species selection.

- **Long-term monitoring plan**: Mycofiltration beds have a finite service life as the substrate clogs, the mycelium senesces, or adsorption capacity is exhausted. Plan to run bioassays at regular intervals over the entire expected lifespan of the installation to detect performance decline before it becomes a compliance or safety issue.
