# Erosion and Land Deterioration

In [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]],
erosion is viewed not as an isolated problem but as a symptom of
dysfunction in the [[savory-ecosystem-foundation-blocks]]. Savory argues that the standard approach to erosion
control, which typically involves mechanical earthworks and structural
interventions, addresses symptoms rather than causes. True erosion control
requires restoring the biological processes that maintain soil stability.

## Erosion as Symptom, Not Cause

The conventional approach to erosion has been to build check dams,
contour banks, and other structures to slow water flow and catch sediment.
While these measures may provide temporary relief, they do not address the
underlying cause. Savory observed that erosion continued to advance on
ranches that had received extensive mechanical erosion control treatments
because the biological health of the land continued to decline.

Erosion is the result of an ineffective [[savory-water-cycle-management]], which in turn results from poor soil cover, capped surfaces,
and inadequate plant communities. As long as these root causes persist,
erosion will continue regardless of structural interventions.

## Types of Erosion

Two primary types of erosion affect managed lands:

Water erosion occurs when rainfall cannot infiltrate the soil and instead
runs off across the surface. The runoff carries soil particles, carving
gullies and removing topsoil. Water erosion is most severe on bare, capped
ground where raindrop impact has destroyed crumb structure.

Wind erosion occurs on bare, dry soil surfaces where no vegetation or
litter anchors the soil. Wind lifts and transports fine particles, reducing
soil fertility and damaging nearby vegetation through abrasion. Wind
erosion is particularly severe in arid [[savory-brittle-environments]].

## The Erosion-Desertification Connection

Erosion and [[savory-perennial-grass-and-desertification]] are
intimately linked. As erosion removes topsoil, plant growth declines,
which reduces soil cover, which accelerates further erosion. This positive
feedback loop can transform productive grassland into desert within
decades.

Savory notes that accelerated erosion is destroying millions of years of
biological capital. The soil that erodes from a hillside took centuries to
develop through the slow processes of weathering, organic accumulation, and
biological activity. Once gone, it cannot be replaced within any meaningful
human timescale.

## Conventional Erosion Control

Standard erosion control measures include building earthworks across
gullies and arroyos, constructing contour banks, planting trees, and
seeding bare areas. While these interventions can slow erosion temporarily,
they fail to address why erosion is occurring in the first place.

The problem with mechanical solutions is that they treat the symptom
without removing the cause. Gullies form because water is not infiltrating
the soil, which happens because the soil surface is capped and bare, which
happens because the biological community has deteriorated. Building a dam
in the gully does nothing to restore the soil surface or the plant
community.

## The Holistic Approach to Erosion

The holistic approach addresses the biological causes of erosion. Since
erosion results from poor [[savory-water-cycle-management]]
effectiveness, the solution lies in restoring soil cover, breaking surface
capping, and reestablishing healthy plant communities. In brittle
environments, this requires [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] to break capped surfaces and create conditions for plant
establishment.

Erosion gullies whose steep banks grant no foothold to plants can be
treated by concentrating livestock in the gully. The animals break down the
steep banks and create conditions for natural succession to heal them. This
high animal impact, while curing the gully, also tends to correct the water
cycle that caused the damage in the first place.

## Fire and Erosion

[[savory-fire-as-management-tool]] is a major contributor to erosion
because it exposes soil surfaces. The immediate effect of burning is bare
ground, and the time required to rebuild soil cover depends on the
brittleness of the environment. In brittle environments, fire followed by
rest can set off a spiral of exposure, capping, erosion, and plant loss
that persists for decades.

Savory observed that erosion for every bushel of corn produced is a
standard consequence of mainstream agriculture. The constant exposure of
soil through tillage, combined with the removal of plant cover, creates
conditions for both water and wind erosion that no structural intervention
can fully address.

## Riparian Erosion

Stream banks and riparian areas are particularly susceptible to erosion.
Where livestock concentrate at water points without planned movement,
trailing creates paths that wash out during rains. Steep stream banks
erode when vegetation is lost.

However, properly managed livestock can cure the very erosion they might
cause. Very high [[savory-animal-impact-and-herd-effect]] for
very short periods can promote well-vegetated stream banks. The key is the
difference between prolonged, one-way trailing damage and the beneficial
effect of milling excited animals for very short periods.

## The Role of Soil Cover

Soil cover is the primary defense against erosion. Cover comes in two
forms: erect growing plants that intercept rainfall and reduce raindrop
energy, and dead prone plant material that slows water flow across the
land. Without either form of cover, erosion is inevitable whenever rain
falls or wind blows.

Maintaining soil cover requires addressing all four ecosystem foundation
blocks simultaneously. Healthy plant communities produce the litter and
root systems that protect soil. Effective water and mineral cycles support
plant growth. Energy flow provides the biological activity that maintains
soil structure.

## Monitoring Erosion

[[savory-land-monitoring]] should track erosion indicators
including gully formation, soil deposition patterns, exposed roots, pedestal
plants, rill formation, and changes in stream bank stability. Photographic
records at fixed points provide the most accessible means of tracking
erosion trends over time.
