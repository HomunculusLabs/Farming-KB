## Pest Control and Companion Planting Roles

Mollison emphasizes that essential oil plants serve critical pest management functions within permaculture systems. Many aromatic species produce volatile compounds that deter insect pests, reduce fungal pathogens on neighbouring crops, and attract beneficial predatory insects. Rosemary and lavender planted as garden hedges repel cabbage moths and carrot flies while attracting pollinators. Citronella, rich in citronellal and geraniol, provides effective mosquito and biting-insect repellence in tropical permaculture zones — Mollison recommends mass-planting it around living areas and animal housing.

In companion planting systems, aromatic herbs create protective microclimates. Basil interplanted with tomatoes deters whitefly and aphids; mint near brassicas repels flea beetles; and thyme among cabbage family crops confuses diamondback moths. These interactions align with the permaculture principle of multiple functions — each plant serves at least three roles including pest deterrence, culinary or medicinal use, and aesthetic or habitat value.

## Income Generation and Economic Considerations

Essential oil crops represent one of the highest value-per-hectare options in permaculture design. Mollison positions them as ideal for smallholders because they require relatively little land to produce a meaningful income. A single hectare of lavender can yield 10-15 kg of essential oil annually, with wholesale prices varying widely by species and quality. Rosemary oil commands premium prices for both aromatherapy and culinary markets.

The key economic advantage noted in the Designers Manual is that essential oils are stable, concentrated products that store well without refrigeration, making them far more practical for remote producers than fresh herbs or perishable crops. Processing can be done on-farm using simple steam distillation equipment, and the concentrated oil is easy to transport to market. Mollison advocates for cooperative processing facilities where several smallholders share distillation equipment, reducing individual capital costs while enabling production at commercially viable scale.

## Cultivation Requirements by Climate Zone

**Temperate zones:** Lavender, rosemary, and peppermint are the primary essential oil crops. Lavender requires well-drained alkaline soil (pH 6.5-7.5) and full sun; it is extremely drought-tolerant once established but vulnerable to root rot in waterlogged conditions. Rosemary prefers sandy, poor soils and tolerates coastal salt spray. Both are propagated from semi-hardwood cuttings.

**Subtropical zones:** Lemongrass, geranium, and tea tree (Melaleuca) expand the species range. Lemongrass is propagated by dividing root clumps and thrives in the nutrient-rich environments of [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]] integration. Vetiver's deep root system (documented to 3-4 metres) provides erosion control alongside oil production. Patchouli prefers shaded understory conditions and humid environments, making it ideal for planting beneath taller canopy species.

**Arid zones:** Mollison notes that some aromatic species tolerate arid conditions remarkably well. Rosemary, sage, and wormwood (Artemisia absinthium) produce more concentrated essential oils under water stress, making them particularly valuable for dryland permaculture systems where few other high-value crops survive.

## Species Not Referenced Explicitly by Mollison but Aligned with His Principles

Several essential oil species widely adopted in permaculture design complement Mollison's framework. Clary sage (Salvia sclarea) fixes nitrogen through rhizobial associations while producing high-value oil for perfumery. Helichrysum (Helichrysum italicum) thrives on poor, rocky Mediterranean soils and produces an oil prized for skin repair. These species exemplify the permaculture strategy of selecting plants that yield in marginal conditions while performing ecosystem services.

## Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling

Timing of harvest significantly affects essential oil yield and quality. Most aromatic plants reach peak oil concentration just before or during flowering, when volatile compounds are at their highest concentration. Mollison notes that morning harvest, after dew has dried but before the heat of the day causes volatile loss, produces the best results. For tree-based oils like eucalyptus, younger leaves typically contain higher concentrations of essential oil than mature foliage.

Post-harvest handling is critical for leaf and flower crops. Material should be distilled as quickly as possible after harvest — wilting reduces oil content. If distillation must be delayed, plant material should be dried in shade with good airflow rather than in direct sun, which volatilizes the most valuable aromatic compounds. For root-based oils such as vetiver, roots are typically harvested at 18-24 months, washed, chopped, and dried before distillation.
