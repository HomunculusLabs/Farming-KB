# Open Systems, Entropy, and Life Complexity

## Thermodynamics and Living Systems

The material world can be predictably measured by applications of the [[laws-of-thermodynamics]], but these laws relate primarily to non-living or experimentally "closed systems." The concept of entropy is not necessarily applicable to those living, open earth systems with which we are involved and in which we are immersed. Such laws are more useful in finding an effective path through material technologies than through a life-complexed world. The key word in open systems is **exchange**.

For example, on the local level, cities appear to be "open," but as they return little energy to the systems that supply them, and pass on their wastes as pollutants to the sea, they are not in exchange but in a localized one-way trade with respect to their food resource. All cities break the basic "law of return."

## Life Systems as Organizers of Complexity

Life systems constantly organize and create complex storages from diffuse energy and materials, accumulating, decomposing, building, and transforming them for further use. Designers can use these effects by finding pathways or routes by which life systems convert diffuse materials into those of most use.

For example, if we have a "waste" such as manure, we can leave it on a field. Although this is of productive use, we have only achieved one function. Alternatively, we can route it through a series of transformations that give us a variety of resources:

1. **Fermentation and distillation** to alcohol
2. **Biogas digestion** where anaerobic organisms convert it to methane for cooking, heating, or vehicle fuel
3. **Liquid effluent** sent to fields for irrigation
4. **Solid sludge** fed to worms, which convert it to rich horticultural soil
5. **Worms themselves** used to feed fish or poultry

This cascading approach to waste is fundamental to permaculture design: every output becomes an input to another process, multiplying the utility derived from a single resource.

## Birch's Six Principles of Natural Systems

Paul Birch articulated six foundational principles that describe how natural systems function:

### 1. Nothing in Nature Grows Forever
There is a constant cycle of decay and rebirth. No organism or system expands indefinitely; growth is balanced by decomposition and recycling. This principle reminds designers that sustainable systems must include returns and recycling, not just extraction and expansion.

### 2. Continuation of Life Depends on Biogeochemical Cycles
The maintenance of global biogeochemical cycles of essential elements—particularly carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, and phosphorus—is essential. Designers must cycle these and other minor nutrients to stimulate growth, and keep the atmosphere and waters of earth unpolluted. This is why composting, mulching, and nutrient cycling are core permaculture practices.

### 3. Extinction Risk Is Greatest at High and Low Densities
The probability of extinction of populations or a species is greatest when density is very high or very low. Both crowding and too few individuals may result in reaching thresholds of survival. This has direct implications for planting densities, stocking rates, and the maintenance of genetic diversity in designed systems.

### 4. Survival Depends on One or Two Key Factors
The chance that species have to survive and reproduce is dependent primarily upon one or two key factors in the complex web of relations of the organism to its environment. If designers can determine what these critical factors are, they can exclude limiting factors by design (e.g., frost) and increase others (e.g., shelter, nest sites).

### 5. Our Ability to Change the Earth Outpaces Our Foresight
Our ability to change the face of the earth increases at a faster rate than our ability to foresee the consequence of such change. Hence the folly of destroying life systems for short-term profit. This principle argues for precaution, for small and slow solutions, and for designs that work with natural processes rather than against them.

### 6. Living Organisms Are Both Means and Ends
In addition to their instrumental value to humans and other living organisms, living organisms have intrinsic worth. This is the life ethic thesis so often missing from otherwise ethical systems. It provides the philosophical foundation for Earth Care as a permaculture ethic.

## Practical Design Considerations

These principles translate into several practical design considerations:

- **Durability**: The systems we construct should last as long as possible, and take least maintenance.
- **Self-sufficiency**: These systems, fueled by the sun, should produce not only their own needs, but the needs of the people creating or controlling them. They are sustainable because they sustain both themselves and those who construct them.
- **Energy return**: We can use energy to construct these systems, providing that in their lifetime, they store or conserve more energy than we use to construct them or to maintain them.

## The Designer's Work: Catching and Storing Energy

The designer's work is to set up useful energy storages in a landscape or building. Such storages, available for increasing yields, are called resources. This involves proceeding from an initial state (energy diffuse and uncaught) to a designed state (energy stored in useful forms). The transformation from State A to State B is the essence of permaculture design.

## Open vs. Closed Systems in Practice

A critical distinction must be made between truly open systems (which exchange energy and materials freely) and pseudo-open systems like modern cities, which import vast quantities of resources but export only wastes. A well-designed permaculture site functions as a true open system:

- Energy enters freely (sun, wind, rain)
- Materials cycle internally (compost, mulch, animal manures)
- Surpluses are exported or traded (yield)
- Wastes are absorbed and transformed (no pollution)

The difference between a city and a permaculture site is not one of openness or closure, but of the quality and direction of exchange. Cities practice one-way extraction; permaculture practices reciprocal exchange where outputs feed back into the system.

## Implications for Modern Civilization

The failure of modern civilization to operate as a true open system is evident in declining soil fertility, polluted waterways, depleted aquifers, and accumulating wastes. These are symptoms of systems that take but do not return, that consume but do not recycle. Permaculture design, by contrast, models itself on the most successful open system known: the mature forest ecosystem, which captures, stores, cycles, and diversifies energy and materials with remarkable efficiency and resilience.
