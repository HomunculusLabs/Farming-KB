---# Subcutaneous and Dematiaceous Fungal Pathogens

Subcutaneous fungal pathogens are distinguished from systemic and
opportunistic pathogens by their ability to cause disease in an
immunologically normal host following traumatic implantation into the
dermal layers of the skin. Unlike opportunistic pathogens that require
immunocompromised hosts, these fungi are capable of establishing
infection through direct inoculation, typically via splinter injuries,
thorn pricks, or other penetrating wounds. The clinical conditions they
cause include chromoblastomycosis, phaeohyphomycosis, mycetoma, and
sporotrichosis.

## Clinical Categories

### Chromoblastomycosis

Chromoblastomycosis is a chronic subcutaneous mycosis characterized by
the presence in tissue of sclerotic cells (also called Medlar bodies or
copper pennies), which are thick-walled, darkly pigmented, septate
cells that divide by binary fission. The disease typically affects the
lower extremities and presents as verrucous or tumorous skin lesions
that may persist for years or decades. The causative agents are primarily
dematiaceous (dark-pigmented) fungi found in soil and on plant material.

### Phaeohyphomycosis

Phaeohyphomycosis refers to infections caused by [[fungal-hyphae]]) within abscesses,
sinus tracts, and swollen tissue. Eumycetoma is caused by true fungi,
as opposed to actinomycetoma caused by filamentous bacteria. The disease
is most common in tropical and subtropical regions, particularly among
people who walk barefoot.
