# Fungal Roles in Soil Food Webs

## Overview

Fungi [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]] occupy multiple trophic positions within soil food webs, serving simultaneously as decomposers (processing dead organic matter), mutualists (trading nutrients with plants), pathogens (attacking living tissue), and prey (supporting fungal-feeding organisms). This multifunctional role makes fungi central hubs in soil food web structure and energy flow.

## Fungal Energy Channels

Soil food webs are organized into two primary energy channels:
- **Fungal-based channel**: Energy flows predominantly through fungal decomposition of organic matter, with fungal biomass supporting fungivorous microarthropods and nematodes
- **Bacterial-based channel**: Energy flows through bacterial processing of labile organic matter, supporting bacterivorous organisms

The fungal channel predominates in:
- Acidic soils (pH < 5.5)
- Forest ecosystems with high C:N ratio litter
- Systems with recalcitrant organic matter
- Dry or drought-prone environments

The bacterial channel predominates in:
- Neutral to alkaline soils
- Grasslands with labile root inputs
- Systems with high-quality organic matter
- Moist environments

## Fungal Feeding by Soil Fauna

Fungal biomass is consumed by a specialized fauna:
- **Collembola (springtails)**: Major fungivores that selectively graze fungal hyphae and spores. Selective grazing can shift fungal community composition toward less palatable species.
- **Oribatid mites**: Both fungivorous and detritivorous; important regulators of fungal biomass
- **Fungivorous nematodes**: Aphelenchoides, Aphelenchus, and Ditylenchus species feed on fungal hyphae
- **Testate amoebae**: Some species feed on fungal spores and hyphae

## Top-Down Regulation

Grazing pressure regulates fungal biomass and activity:
- Exclusion of fungiverous microarthropods increases fungal immobilization of nitrogen and phosphorus into surface litters
- Moderate grazing stimulates fungal growth (compensatory growth response) and accelerates nutrient mineralization
- Heavy grazing reduces fungal biomass and shifts decomposition pathways toward bacterial dominance
- Selective grazing by collembola can alter the outcome of fungal competition, affecting which species dominate in the community

## Bottom-Up Regulation

Resource quality controls fungal community composition and function:
- High C:N ratio resources favor fungi over bacteria (due to lower nitrogen requirements per unit carbon assimilated)
- Lignin-rich resources selectively favor white-rot basidiomycetes
- Simple sugar availability favors opportunistic "sugar fungi"
- Phosphorus limitation increases fungal phosphatase production

## Mycorrhizal Fungi in Food Webs

Mycorrhizal fungi complicate food web analysis because they derive carbon from living plants rather than dead organic matter:
- ECM and AM hyphae are consumed by fungivorous fauna, making plant-derived carbon available to higher trophic levels
- Mycorrhizal fruiting bodies are important food resources for mammals (rodents, deer, primates), insects (diptera, coleoptera), and other invertebrates
- Sporocarps of hypogeous (below-ground) fungi are dispersed by mammals (squirrels, voles), creating a mutualistic dispersal relationship
- Kotter and Farentinos (1984) showed tassel-eared squirrels act as spore dispersal agents for hypogeous mycorrhizal fungi

## Fungal Pathways and Nutrient Cycling

Fungal-mediated food web pathways influence nutrient cycling:
- The "fungal shunt" accelerates decomposition by processing recalcitrant organic matter that bacteria cannot degrade
- Fungal biomass has higher C:N ratio (~10-15:1) than bacterial biomass (~4-6:1), resulting in slower nutrient turnover but greater carbon storage
- Mycelial translocation connects spatially separated nutrient pools
- Fungal residues ([[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]], chitin, melanin) contribute to stable soil organic matter

## Trophic Interactions and Stability

The multi-channel structure of fungal and bacterial energy pathways contributes to soil food web stability:
- Complementarity between channels maintains ecosystem function when one channel is disrupted
- Diversity within the fungal channel (multiple fungal species performing different functions) provides functional redundancy
- Cross-connections between channels (e.g., fungivores that also consume bacteria) create network resilience
