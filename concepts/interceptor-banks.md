# Interceptor Banks

Interceptor banks are deep contour earthworks designed to halt the process of [[desertification]] and dryland salinity.

## The Problem They Solve

### Limitations of Traditional Contour Banks

Earlier approaches used shallow (0.5 m deep) contour banks to slow soil losses on slopes. These failed to address the fundamental problems because:

- They had little effect on wind erosion, gully formation, or tree death
- Salt eventually caused contour banks to collapse
- Run-off floods overrode them in heavy rains
- They did not stop the throughflow or seepage that drives [[soil-collapse]]

### The WIS.ALT. Insight

The WIS.ALT. approach recognises that while salt problems first appear in low flat areas and valleys, the water causing the problem cascades downslope from hilltops. Therefore, the solution must tackle the problem at its source — the upper slopes and ridgelines.

## Construction of Interceptor Banks

### Positioning

Interceptor banks begin 1–3 metres below ridgelines and plateaus. This positioning intercepts water before it can gain destructive momentum flowing downhill.

### Dimensions

- **Depth**: 2 metres (compared to 0.5 m for traditional contour banks)
- **Width**: 3–5 metres
- **Combined structure**: A deep ditch with a compacted bank on the downslope side

### Construction Steps

1. **Topsoil removal** — push off any topsoil to preserve it
2. **Subsoil breaking** — dig 1–2 metres down with a bulldozer blade to break up the concreted subsoil; ripping is often necessary to fracture the cemented B-horizon
3. **Bank compaction** — the excavated clayey and stony material is rammed firmly against the downhill bank of the ditch to create a sealed barrier
4. **Grading** — the downhill bank slopes at grades of 1:3000 toward natural stream beds, while upper banks are made level as swales

The combination of ditch and compacted bank forms the interceptor bank. The sealed downslope bank is critical — it prevents all intercepted water from continuing downslope.

## How They Work

### Stopping Overland Flow and Seepage

When properly constructed, interceptor banks:

- **Stop all high run-off** — overland flow from rain is captured in the ditch
- **Stop throughflow** — the slower seepage of water through the topsoil, which perches on the compacted B-horizon as a general seepage line, is also intercepted
- **Prevent downslope flooding** — no flooding of lower soil profiles occurs once interception is complete
- **Carry water to streams** — both salted and fresh water are directed via the graded banks to natural waterways and ultimately to the sea

### Spacing

Interceptor banks are placed every 3 metres of vertical height downhill:

- **Steep slopes (10–15°+)**: Banks form a continuous series of ditches from ridge to foothills, piped or stone-filled at intervals for machinery crossings
- **Moderate slopes (5°)**: Maximum spacing of 300 metres between ditches; ditches end at (or start at) and spill into natural waterways
- **Spacing calculation**: Carefully designed to totally intercept any water that would otherwise flow or seep downhill

### Blocking Deep Seepages

In addition to surface flow interception, interceptor systems address deeper water movement:

- **Shallow aquifers** (less than 2 m depth) from old sand seams or permeable rock strata are dug out to base and clay-filled or blocked with vertical plastic barriers
- **Deep sand seams** (to 6 m depth) are excavated by backhoe and blocked by vertical plastic sheets buried in clay backfill
- **[[singh-detection-methods-degrading-fungi-environment]]**: Sand seams can be found by backhoe digging, satellite radar, or dowsing
- **Result**: Seepage effectively comes to a standstill

### Managing Sandy Areas

Deep sands and old dunes (up to 93% silica sand grains) do not collapse but act as freshwater "tanks" that seep slowly from their base:

- [[diversion-drains-interceptor-drains-spreader-banks]] downslope from sandy areas remain water-filled with fresh water year-round
- These permanent water channels attract waterfowl, small fish, and water plants
- Salt lakes above valley farms can have early wet-season floods diverted around them, with only later fresh run-off directed to fill them
- This makes salt lakes suitable for fish and waterfowl habitat

## Observed Effects and Benefits

### Immediate Effects (Within Construction)

- Wind erosion ceases on construction
- Flooding during rains stops
- Crops regain health as fertiliser is no longer washed away
- Dying trees begin to recover
- New trees often appear spontaneously on the banks soon after construction

### Medium-Term Effects (2–3 Years)

- Grasses begin to regenerate in the strips between banks
- Dying trees regain health
- Collapsed soil areas begin to recover
- Water becomes available to stock and wildlife as waterholes where no freshwater previously existed at the head of catchments

### Long-Term Effects (Up to 10 Years)

- Full soil recovery and gully elimination with careful husbandry
- Tree-lines 5–8 trees wide below each bank stop the drying-out effect of desert winds and eliminate wind erosion on fields
- The uphill side of ditches is left free of trees to allow for maintenance access

## Economic Returns

Farmers in Western Australia estimate that in 5–7 years, cash returns from increased livestock and crop yields pay for all the earthworks. This makes interceptor banks not only ecologically sound but economically viable.

## Application to Different Regions

### Victoria and Inland Australia

In Victoria, the primary salting mechanism is rising groundwater from deforested aquifers rather than soil collapse. Some deep sand aquifers contain very salty water (30,000–42,000 ppm), and where these surface, concentrations of 180,000 ppm are possible in lagoons or creek seepages. Here:

- Interceptor banks have not been widely trialled
- The primary solution is restoration of forests to hills and plains
- Tile drains and pumping can reduce wetland salting but are insufficient for broad [[dryland-salting-mollison]]

### Where Interceptor Banks Are Most Appropriate

Interceptor banks are most effective in soils with:

- Obvious cemented B-horizons
- Rainy-season waterlogging
- Both the B-horizon and underlying strata can be quite dry
- Evidence of soil collapse (mottled soils, iron concretions, sunken areas)

### Where Tree Planting May Be More Appropriate

Tree planting alone may be sufficient where:

- Deep permeable surface soils or strata exist
- No cemented sub-surface layer occurs
- Groundwaters have not reached within 2 metres of the surface

## Critical Principle

A fundamental principle is that **until interceptor banks have been made in collapsed soils, tree death will continue and new trees will live only until they are a year or two into growth — after that, they waterlog and die.** The interception of water flow must precede or accompany revegetation efforts.

## Diagnostic Method

The best way to determine local conditions and choose the correct strategy is by **digging backhoe pits** and observing how they fill with water:

- **Water welling up from deep seepages**: Indicates rising groundwater — interceptor banks needed
- **Water trickling down from shallow seepage above the B-horizon**: Indicates throughflow — interceptor banks will capture this
- **Dry pits with no water movement**: May indicate tree planting alone could be effective

This simple diagnostic determines whether corrective strategies should use trees alone, interceptor banks plus trees, or a combination of approaches.
