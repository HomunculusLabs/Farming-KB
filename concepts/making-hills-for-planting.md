## Making Hills for Planting

Hills are concentrated zones of highly fertile soil where individual plants or small clusters are grown. The technique of planting in hills is one of the oldest horticultural methods — it was the standard approach used by [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] gardeners for centuries. Hills remain one of the most efficient ways to grow large, nutrient-demanding plants, especially when resources or time are limited.

## What Is a Hill?

A hill is not necessarily a raised mound (though it often is). Agronomically, a "hill" is a localized zone of concentrated fertility where seeds are planted. The concept is identical to what agronomists call "banding" — placing extra fertilizer in a concentrated zone so that young plant roots immediately encounter nutrient-rich soil, giving them a fast start.

## Why Hills Work

Plants started in hills get a fast start because of the high fertility created immediately below where the seeds are sown. The principle is simple: rather than trying to make the entire garden soil equally fertile (which requires enormous amounts of amendment), you concentrate your limited resources into small zones where they have the greatest impact.

Hills are especially frugal because it would not be practical to turn the entire garden into soil that meets the high nutritional demands of crops like [[winter-squash-guide]] or corn.

## Standard Hill Dimensions and Spacing

### For Large Plants (Squash, Melons, Tomatoes, Sweet Corn)

- **Hill diameter**: 18-24 inches (45-60 cm) when first prepared; may extend to 30-36 inches (75-90 cm) as the season progresses
- **Hill spacing**: Usually 4 feet (120 cm) from center to center
- **Dry climate spacing**: Can increase to 5-6 feet (150-180 cm) or even 8-10 feet (2.5-3 m) where rainfall is very low
- **Smaller plants on raised beds**: Use about half as much fertility and dig a smaller mound, approximately 10 inches (25 cm) in diameter
- **Medium-demand plants on raised beds**: Space hills 24 x 30 inches (60 x 75 cm) or 24 x 24 inches

### Traditional Native American Layout

The traditional Native American approach used hills on 4-foot (120 cm) centers across the garden area, which remains an excellent spacing standard for many large crops.

## How to Make Hills

### Standard Method

1. **Prepare the area**: First prepare the whole area or at least the part where medium-demand plants will grow. Spread lime, compost or manure, and COF over the whole area.

2. **Mark hill positions**: Mark out hills on the desired spacing pattern.

3. **Add extra fertility**: At each hill location, place a shovelful or two of low-potency manure or compost in a small pile. For extra nutrition, add a pint or two (0.5-1 liter) of [[chicken-manure]] or COF.

4. **Dig and blend**: Dig about 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) in diameter, blending the amendments in by going down the full depth of the shovel blade. Work up a circular spot, mixing everything thoroughly.

5. **Form the mound**: You should end up with a circular mound of highly fertile soil. If there is not much of a visible mound when done, take soil from the surrounding area and toss it on top.

### Survival Gardening Method (Last-Minute Garden)

When spring planting time has arrived but nothing has been dug and there is only sod:

1. **Mark out hills** on 4-foot (120 cm) centers
2. **Dig an 18-inch-diameter (45 cm) spot** at each proposed hill center — a person can dig many of these in one day
3. **Find fertilizer**: Be creative — fish, road kill, cow pats, compost, whatever is available
4. **Incorporate fertilizer deep**: Place fertilizer in the center of the hill and sow seeds around the outside (so seedlings are not too close to decomposing material)
5. **Give each hill a second digging**: Remove surviving sod clumps, shake off soil, and toss roots aside to shrivel in the sun
6. **Sow seeds and plant starts** in the fertilized hills

### Progressive Expansion (Survival Method)

After sowing, while waiting for seeds to germinate:

1. **Kill grass between hills** by spading up the surrounding ground
2. **Make more hills** for autumn-harvested crops
3. **Collect more fertilizer** and dig it in ever-widening circles around existing hills
4. **Keep expanding ahead of growing plants** so you do not disturb their expanding root systems

By mid-season, you will have dug and fertilized the entire plot.

## Growing Potatoes in Hills

Potatoes have a specialized hilling method:

1. **Dig rows** about 12 inches (30 cm) wide with row centers 3-4 feet (90-120 cm) apart, adjusted for anticipated rainfall
2. **Fertilize the rows** as best you can, then plant seed potatoes
3. **Before vines emerge**, turn over a strip about 6-12 inches (15-30 cm) to each side of the row
4. **Fertilize the expanded strip** if possible, creating an 18-24 inch (45-60 cm) wide growing zone
5. **Gradually hill up**: As vines grow, pull soil and weeds up against them with a hoe, gradually building up the hills throughout the season
6. **Result**: By season's end, you will have hilled up the entire potato bed, producing a surprisingly large crop from what was originally sod and weeds

## The Efficiency of Hills

The hill method is remarkably efficient because:

- **Minimal amendment needed**: Fertility is concentrated, not spread thin
- **Fast results**: Plants get immediate access to nutrients
- **Scalable**: Start with as few hills as you have time for, expand progressively
- **Works on sod**: No need to fully prepare the garden before planting
- **Ancient and proven**: This method fed civilizations for thousands of years before the concept of prepared garden beds existed
