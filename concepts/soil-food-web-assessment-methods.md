# Soil Food Web Assessment Methods

Updated: 2026-04-18

Dr. Elaine Ingham's soil food web assessment approach provides a systematic method for evaluating the biological health of soil. The assessment determines whether the right organisms in the right biomass are present for the specific plants being grown.

## The Soil Food Web Concept

The soil food web is the community of organisms living in soil -- bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms. These organisms perform essential functions: nutrient cycling, disease suppression, soil structure building, and decomposition. When the food web is balanced and complete, plants receive adequate nutrition without chemical fertilizers, and diseases and pests are naturally controlled.

## When to Assess

Soil food web assessment should be performed:

- Before starting a compost tea program (baseline)
- After 4-5 years of compost tea application (to confirm soil health recovery)
- Annually to track progress and adjust management
- When disease or pest problems arise unexpectedly
- When changing crop types (annual vs perennial systems have different biological requirements)

## Laboratory Analysis (SFI Method)

The Soil Foodweb Inc. (SFI) laboratory analysis provides quantitative data on soil biology:

Sample types available:
- Soil sample (S-3 test through International Ag Lab for nutrients; separate SFI test for biology at $252 -- includes active and total bacterial biomass, fungal biomass, protozoa, and nematodes)
- Compost sample (to verify compost quality before tea brewing)
- Compost tea sample (to verify tea biology before application)
- Leaf surface sample (to assess foliar biology and disease protection)

Sampling protocol:
1. Call the lab before taking samples to follow their recommended protocol
2. Take soil samples from the root zone (typically 0-6 inches depth)
3. Include lists of current practices and future goals with samples
4. Send samples overnight with appropriate documentation
5. Have nutrient analysis results faxed to SFI along with biological samples

## Interpreting Results: Annual vs Perennial Systems

The key interpretation is whether the soil biology matches the plant type:

Annual plants (vegetables, grasses, brassicas, row crops):
- Prefer bacterial-dominated soils
- Bacterial biomass should exceed fungal biomass
- High bacterial activity provides rapid nutrient cycling for fast growth
- Protozoa and bacterial-feeding nematodes cycle nitrogen

Perennial plants (trees, shrubs, vines, pastures):
- Prefer fungal-dominated soils
- Fungal biomass should equal or exceed bacterial biomass
- Fungi provide slow-release nutrient cycling suited to woody growth
- Mycorrhizal associations are critical for nutrient uptake
- Fungal hyphae build soil structure through aggregate formation

## Key Indicators to Assess

1. Active vs total bacterial biomass: The ratio indicates how much of the bacterial population is metabolically active. Low activity relative to total biomass may indicate nutrient limitation or stress.

2. Active vs total fungal biomass: Same principle for fungi. Low fungal activity may indicate inadequate fungal food sources.

3. Bacterial to fungal ratio: Determines whether soil is suited to annual or perennial plants. A ratio greater than 1 favors annuals; less than 1 favors perennials.

4. Protozoa numbers: Adequate protozoa are essential for cycling nitrogen from bacteria to plant-available forms. Flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates each occupy different soil pore sizes.

5. Nematode community: The types and numbers of nematodes indicate soil health. Beneficial bacterial-feeding and fungal-feeding nematodes cycle nutrients. Root-feeding nematodes indicate problems. Predatory nematodes indicate a mature, balanced food web.

6. Mycorrhizal colonization: For perennial plants, assess the percentage of root length colonized by mycorrhizal fungi. Low colonization indicates need for inoculation.

## Water Usage as a Biological Indicator

Ingham provides a practical field indicator: as soil microbiology rebuilds health, water usage decreases. In the first year, typically 30% water use decrease is noticed. In the second and third years, up to 50-60% decrease. After 4-5 years when soil is healthy, compost tea usage decreases to seasonal or during pest or disease alerts only.

## Chemical Analysis Integration

Biological assessment should always be paired with chemical/nutrient analysis. The two provide complementary information: chemistry tells you what nutrients are present, biology tells you whether those nutrients are available to plants. A soil may have adequate total nutrients but poor availability due to biological deficiency.

## On-Site Microscope Assessment

Ingham recommends developing on-site microscope assessment capability. A compound microscope at 400x magnification allows qualitative assessment of bacterial and fungal biomass, protozoa, and nematodes. This provides rapid feedback without waiting for lab results, enabling same-day management decisions.
