# Soil Food Web Assessment Methods

## Overview

Assessing [[the-soil-food-web]] is the essential first step in understanding the biological health of any soil system. As described in Chapter 13 of *[[teaming-with-microbes-actinomycetes]]* by Lowenfels and Lewis, a soil census reveals which organisms are present, which are absent, and whether the existing biological community is functioning as it should. Without this baseline knowledge, any attempt to improve soil health is essentially guesswork.

The assessment methods range from simple visual surveys accessible to any gardener to laboratory analyses requiring professional expertise. Together, they provide a comprehensive picture of soil biology at every scale — from visible macroarthropods down to microscopic bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes.

## Why Soil Food Webs Vary by Location

No two areas in a landscape support identical soil food webs. Several factors drive this variation:

- **Plant exudate specificity.** Different plant species release different mixtures of root exudates, which in turn attract different communities of bacteria and fungi. Because the base of the food web differs, the organisms at higher trophic levels — protozoa, nematodes, arthropods — also differ. A tomato plant's root zone supports a different biological community than an oak tree's.
- **Chemical inputs.** Areas that have been treated with commercial [[teaming-with-nutrients-natural-vs-synthetic-fertilizers]] consistently exhibit reduced soil life. High concentrations of soluble salts and ammonium nitrogen suppress fungal growth and can harm sensitive microbial populations.
- **Physical disturbance.** Compacted soils and those subjected to rototilling show markedly fewer fungi and earthworms. Fungal hyphae are physically broken by tilling, and compaction eliminates the air spaces earthworms need to move through soil.
- **Land use history.** A vegetable garden, a lawn, and the root zone of an established shrub each carry a distinct biological signature shaped by years of differing management practices.

Because of this variability, each distinct area of a property should be censused separately. A vegetable garden bed, a lawn, the drip line of an ornamental tree, and a perennial border each warrant their own assessment.

## Census Method 1: Direct Visual Survey (Shovel and Tarp)

The simplest and most intuitive assessment method involves physically digging into the soil and examining the organisms present. This method reveals macrofauna — organisms large enough to see with the naked eye.

### Procedure

1. Mark out a 12-inch square on the soil surface.
2. Dig a hole to a depth of approximately 12 inches, placing all excavated soil onto a clean tarp.
3. Gently break apart the soil and sift through it by hand.
4. Observe and tally all visible organisms.

### What to Look For

- **Earthworms and worm castings.** The presence of earthworms is an excellent indicator. Where worms thrive, the conditions also support bacteria, protozoa, and nematodes, even if those are not directly visible. Worm castings on the soil surface are further evidence of active biological processing.
- **Macroarthropods and other invertebrates.** Millipedes, centipedes, beetles, spiders, springtails, slugs, and snails are all members of a healthy [[soil-food-web]]. Their presence indicates that the lower tiers of the web — bacteria, fungi, and their consumers — are producing enough energy to sustain higher-level predators and detritivores.

### Reference Organism Densities

Typical visible organism counts per square foot vary by ecosystem type:

| Ecosystem | Arthropods | Earthworms |
|-----------|-----------|------------|
| Vegetable garden | 500 – 2,000 | 5 – 30 |
| Prairie / grassland | 10,000 – 25,000 | 10 – 50 |
| Forest | Similar to prairie | Similar to prairie |

Gardens typically show lower numbers than undisturbed natural systems, but consistent presence of diverse macrofauna is a positive sign regardless of absolute counts.

## Census Method 2: Pitfall Traps

Pitfall traps capture ground-dwelling macroarthropods and gastropods that actively move across the soil surface. They are particularly effective for organisms that may be missed during a static shovel survey.

### Procedure

1. Dig a small hole and sink a quart-sized container (such as a deli cup or jar) into the ground so its rim is exactly level with the soil surface.
2. Add a preservative to the container — antifreeze or mothballs work well — to kill and preserve captured organisms and to prevent them from climbing out.
3. Provide a rain cover, such as a small umbrella or inverted plate supported above the trap, to prevent the preservative from being diluted or the trap from overflowing.
4. Leave the trap in place for several days to a week.
5. Retrieve the container and examine its contents.

### Interpretation

Pitfall traps capture a range of surface-active organisms including beetles, spiders, centipedes, millipedes, slugs, and snails. A productive trap with multiple species suggests a healthy, active soil food web. Traps that remain completely empty are a strong warning sign that the surface-level food web has been depleted and requires restoration through improved soil management practices.

Multiple traps placed in different zones provide a more complete picture than a single trap.

## Census Method 3: Berlese Funnel (Microarthropod Extraction)

The Berlese funnel is a time-tested apparatus for extracting microarthropods — organisms too small to detect with the naked eye but critically important to soil decomposition [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]]. It was invented by the Italian entomologist **Giovanni Berlese (1863–1927)**.

### Construction (DIY)

A serviceable Berlese funnel can be built from common materials:

1. Cut the bottom off a 1-liter plastic bottle to create an open-ended funnel shape.
2. Insert a piece of window screening with 1.5–3 mm openings into the bottle to act as a retaining mesh — this holds the soil sample while allowing small organisms to pass through.
3. Position the mouth of the modified bottle into a quart-sized collection container.

### Procedure

1. Collect a soil sample consisting of the **top 8 inches of soil** including the surface duff layer (leaf litter, organic debris).
2. Place the sample into the upper portion of the funnel, resting on the screen.
3. Optionally add a small amount of antifreeze or ethyl alcohol to the collection container below to kill and preserve organisms as they fall through.
4. Apply a heat source — a 40–60 watt incandescent light bulb positioned about 6 inches above the sample — to gradually dry the soil from the top. As the soil dries and warms, microarthropods migrate downward and fall through the screen into the collection container.
5. Leave the apparatus running for **3 to 7 days**.
6. An alternative to the heat source is placing mothballs on top of the soil sample; the chemical irritant drives organisms downward through the same principle.

### Examination and Interpretation

The collected organisms should be examined with a magnifying glass or a MacroScope. Common finds include:

- **Mites** (Acari)
- **Springtails** (Collembola)
- **Beetles** (Coleoptera) and their larval stages
- Other small arthropods

A **diverse catch** — multiple species across different groups — is the strongest positive indicator. Diversity signals that the soil food web is intact and functioning at multiple trophic levels. A catch dominated by a single organism type, or very low total numbers, is cause for concern and suggests the need for biological remediation.

## Census Method 4: Nematode Extraction

Nematodes occupy a pivotal position in the soil food web as consumers of bacteria, fungi, and each other. Assessing nematode populations provides insight into the balance of bacterial and fungal energy channels within the soil.

### Procedure

1. Construct a funnel fitted with surgical tubing at its narrow end, clamped shut.
2. Mix a soil sample with **dechlorinated water** to form a thick mud or slurry.
3. Pour the mixture into the funnel.
4. Allow the setup to stand for approximately **24 hours**.
5. Over time, active nematodes migrate downward through the sediment and accumulate in the tubing below the clamp.
6. Release the clamp and collect the concentrated nematode suspension into a dish.
7. Examine the concentrate under a microscope.

### Interpretation

Nematode identification requires some training, as the same general body plan encompasses bacterial feeders, fungal feeders, predatory nematodes, and plant-parasitic nematodes. A healthy soil should contain a mix of these functional groups, with bacterial-feeding and fungal-feeding nematodes being the most abundant. The ratio of fungal-feeding to bacterial-feeding nematodes provides an indirect measure of the soil's fungal-to-bacterial energy balance.

## Professional Laboratory Testing

While the four census methods above are accessible to gardeners and land managers, they cannot quantify the microscopic populations that form the foundation of the soil food web — bacteria, fungi, and protozoa — with any precision. For a definitive assessment, professional laboratory testing is recommended.

### What Professional Labs Test

- **Total and active bacterial biomass**
- **Total and active fungal biomass**
- **Protozoan populations** (flagellates, amoebae, ciliates)
- **Nematode community analysis** (identification to genus and functional group)
- **Fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratio (F:B ratio)**

### The F:B Ratio

The **fungal-to-bacterial ratio** is one of the most important metrics in [[soil-food-web-assessment]]. Different plant communities and successional stages favor different F:B ratios:

- **Early successional systems** (disturbed soils, annual weeds, most vegetable crops) tend toward **bacterial dominance** (lower F:B ratio, typically 0.1:1 to 1:1).
- **Late successional systems** (forests, perennial grasslands, mature shrubs) tend toward **fungal dominance** (higher F:B ratio, typically 5:1 to 100:1 or more).
- **Most garden soils** fall in between, with an F:B ratio in the range of 1:1 to 5:1 being desirable for mixed vegetable and ornamental plantings.

Knowing the F:B ratio allows managers to adjust their practices — adding fungal foods (e.g., woody mulches, humic acids) to raise the ratio, or bacterial foods (e.g., compost tea, molasses) to lower it — to match the needs of the plants being grown.

### Limitations of Traditional Soil Tests

Conventional agricultural soil tests report NPK (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) levels, pH, and sometimes micronutrient concentrations. While these chemical metrics are useful for certain purposes, they are **not sufficient** for assessing soil food web health. A soil can have adequate NPK values and still be biologically dead. The soil food web is what makes nutrients available to plants in the first place; without it, added fertilizers may leach, volatilize, or become locked in unavailable forms.
