# Seed Storage and Preservation

The difference between buying a small packet of seed every year and buying a larger quantity that lasts several years is mostly a matter of proper storage. Understanding how temperature, humidity, and seed moisture interact to determine storage life allows gardeners to save substantial money on seed purchases while maintaining high germination rates.

## The Economics of Seed Storage

When you account for seeds that don't sprout and for thinning, you often need far more seed than you might expect. Buying bulk seed rather than small packets can save 60-70% or more. The key to making bulk purchases economical is understanding how to preserve germination over multiple years.

### Example: Kohlrabi

Johnny's [[johnnys-selected-seeds]] sells a mini-packet of kohlrabi (~45 seeds) for $2.85. A thousand seeds of the same variety costs $7.60. If you purchased 1,000 seeds in mini-packets, you'd need eight or nine at $23.75 total — versus $7.60 in bulk. Even if you only use half of 1,000 seeds before they lose viability, it is still cheaper than purchasing three mini-packets containing only 360 seeds.

## Why Seed Dies in Storage

### Food Reserve Depletion

Every seed contains an embryo sleeping within a store of food. The dormant embryo consumes some of its food reserves just to stay alive. If the seed is stored too long, there won't be enough reserves remaining to allow the embryo to fully develop when it finally sprouts. The seedling will run out of food and die before it gets established.

### Starvation After Emergence

A seedling that starts with insufficient reserves may emerge with two small leaves, sit there stunned, and then fall over and die. This might seem like disease killed it, but it was actually death by starvation — the first true leaf is also produced with food from the seed, and if those reserves are depleted, the seedling cannot continue.

### The Temperature-Moisture Relationship

Seed storage life follows a precise mathematical relationship:

- For every 10°F **increase** in temperature combined with a 1% increase in seed moisture content, the storage life of the seed is **cut in half**
- For every 10°F **decrease** in temperature combined with a 1% decrease in seed moisture, the storage life of the seed **doubles**

### Standard Conditions

Standard conditions are assumed to be 70°F (21°C) with enough humidity that the seed stabilizes at 13% moisture by weight — which is what usually happens in a [[permaculture-designers-manual-temperate-climate-design]].

### Practical Examples

| Storage Condition | Moisture | Temperature | Storage Life vs. Standard |
|------------------|----------|-------------|--------------------------|
| Standard | 13% | 70°F (21°C) | 1× (baseline) |
| Cool + dry | 12% | 60°F (15.5°C) | ~4× longer |
| Warm + humid | 14% | 80°F (26.5°C) | ~0.25× (quarter life) |
| Climate-controlled | 10% | 50°F (10°C) | ~16× longer |
| Worst case (greenhouse) | 15%+ | 85°F+ | < 1 year |

When the author ran [[territorial-seed-company]], he built a climate-controlled storeroom holding seeds at 50°F (10°C) with 50% relative humidity, so seeds dried to about 10% moisture — seeds lasted roughly four times longer than under standard conditions. Johnny's and Park [[seed-company-supplier-reference]] also do this.

## How to Store Seeds at Home

### The Silica Gel Method

1. **Get silica gel crystals** from a hobby/craft store (about $1 per pound/0.5 kg)
2. **Understand the color indicator**: Silica gel is blue when dry, turns pink when saturated with moisture
3. **Reactivate spent gel**: Spread on a baking pan, bake at 230°F (110°C) for a few hours until they turn deep blue again. Do not exceed 212°F (100°C) or they may melt.

### The Storage Container

1. Get a large airtight container — a one-gallon (4-liter) plastic storage box that seals nearly airtight works well
2. For each half gallon of seed-storage volume, put in about one cup (250 mL) of silica gel
3. Keep the crystals in a cloth sachet or small, clean, open-topped container so you can monitor their color
4. If the gel turns pink, remove it, reactivate it, cool it off, and return it to the container

### The Cool Storage Location

The second requirement is cool and stable temperature:

- **Best**: Refrigerator — claim half a shelf (about one-eighth of the space). This is what the author does.
- **Good**: Root cellar
- **Acceptable**: Unheated room during winter and the coolest place you can find in summer (under the house, basement)
- **Avoid**: Greenhouses, garages, sheds, or anywhere with temperature fluctuations

### What NOT to Do

- Don't store seeds in a [[cardboard-box-inoculation-hood-draft-prevention-mycology]] in a steamy greenhouse — they might last less than a year
- Don't leave seed packets in a hot car, on a windowsill, or in direct sunlight
- Don't store seeds where humidity fluctuates wildly

## Uncertainty About Purchased Seed

There is always more uncertainty about the storage life of seeds you purchased than those you grew yourself. The seed you buy is new to you, but:
- For how many years, and under what [[spawn-storage-conditions]], has it been sitting on some seed-warehouse shelf?
- How much of its potential storage life is left?
- How good was that seed from the start? Weather may not have cooperated — seed may have failed to fully mature, been rained on while drying, or been grown on infertile soil

When you grow your own seed, you know a lot more about its history and storage conditions.

## Testing Stored Seed

Before planting a large area with stored seed, test a sample:

1. Count out 10-20 seeds
2. Place on a damp paper towel in a plastic bag
3. Keep at 70-80°F (21-27°C)
4. Check after the species' expected germination days
5. Calculate percentage and decide whether to sow normally, overseed, or replace

## Species Differences in Storage Life

Some species maintain viability much longer than others:

- **Long-lived** (5+ years properly stored): Tomato, pepper, some squash, melon, cucumber
- **Moderate** (3-4 years): Bean, pea, most brassicas, corn
- **Short-lived** (1-2 years): Onion, parsnip, celery, some lettuce varieties
