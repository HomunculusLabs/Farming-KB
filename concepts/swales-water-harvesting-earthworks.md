# Swales

Swales are long, level excavations built on contour that capture, hold, and infiltrate overland water flow into the soil profile. They are among the most important and versatile water-harvesting earthworks in [[permaculture-design]], applicable across climates from arid to humid, on steep slopes and flatlands alike, in both urban and rural settings.

## What Swales Are

A swale is a **large hollow or broad drain** designed to first pool, then absorb all surplus water flow. Unlike drains or channels, swales are not intended or permitted to convey water — their sole function is to **hold water** on the landscape long enough for it to infiltrate into the soil and recharge groundwater.

Swales can vary greatly in form:

- **Small ridges** in gardens
- **Rock-piles** across slopes
- **Deliberately-excavated hollows** in flatlands and low-slope landscapes
- **Broad, shallow basins** spanning hillside contours

## Construction Principles

### Contour Alignment

Swales are **always built on contour or dead-level survey lines**. This is fundamental — any slope in a swale defeats its purpose by causing water to flow rather than pool and infiltrate.

### Uncompacted Construction

Unlike dams, swale banks and bases are **never compacted or sealed**. Instead, the swale soils can be:

- Gravelled — to improve infiltration in heavy soils
- Ripped — to break up compacted subsoil layers
- Loosened — to increase permeability
- Dressed with gypsum — to improve soil structure in clay soils

### Adaptive Sizing

Swale depth and width should be varied to match the local infiltration rate:

- **Wider and shallower swales** — for sandy soils (fast infiltration)
- **Narrower and deeper swales** — for clay-fraction soils (slow infiltration)

Swales should be designed to **absorb or infiltrate all water caught within 3 hours to 3 days**.

## The Essential Role of Trees

Trees are **not optional** in swale systems — they are the essential component. Without trees, several problems arise:

- **Soil waterlogging** occurs when water infiltrates but has no biological pump to remove it
- **Local rainfall deficits** develop from lack of evapotranspiration of stored water
- **Salt concentration** can occur in arid areas when water evaporates without transpiration

### Tree Integration

Trees should ideally not exceed in width the **total crown spread of the fringing trees** planted on swale banks. The benefits of trees in swale systems include:

- **Overshading and cooling** of swale soils, reducing evaporation and dissolved salt concentration
- **Root systems** that create channels for deep water penetration
- **Evapotranspiration** that pumps stored water back into the atmosphere as humidity
- **Humus accumulation** from leaf litter that improves soil water-holding capacity over time
- **Mulch production** that protects soil surface and feeds soil biology

### Establishment Timeline

1. After initial rains soak in about 1 meter of water, trees are **seeded or planted** on either bank or side slopes (may take two wet periods for establishment)
2. **3–5 years** for tree belts to overshade the swale base and begin humus accumulation from leaf tissue
3. Humus also accumulates from **wash-down and wind movement** from bare or uphill areas
4. Absorption efficiency **increases with age** due to tree root development and humus effects

## Climate Applications

### Arid and Semi-Arid Areas

In arid areas, it is **imperative** to plant trees on swales. Without trees, salt concentration and soil collapse occur downhill. Swales in Australian drylands have consistently grown larger and healthier trees than open plantings. In very dry areas, the inter-swale space may be fairly bare, existing mainly to run water into swales.

### Sub-Humid Areas

Swales are widely used and highly effective. The inter-swale space (3–18 m or 12–60 feet, depending on rainfall) is typically planted with hardy or mulch-producing species.

### Humid Areas

Natural swales in humid forests (e.g., Tasmania) generate much larger trees, provide level access ways, and support thick humus and specialized plants. **Orchids, fungi, and ginseng** do particularly well in swale floors. In humid areas, rainfall may exceed 127 cm (50 inches) and inter-swale spacing narrows to as little as 3 m (12 feet).

## Urban and Suburban Applications

The Village Homes development in Davis, California (designed by Michael Corbett) is a landmark example of swale integration in suburban design:

- Swales accept **all road and excess roof run-off**
- Hundreds of **productive trees** are supported throughout the settlement
- Water penetrated soils to **6 m (19 feet) deep** after a few years
- Swales were **self-shaded after 3–4 years** of tree growth

Every sub-humid and arid townscape can benefit from fitting all roads and paved areas with swales. Benefits include:

- **Energy gain** from tree shade on pavement, reducing urban heat islands
- **Reduced roading and water use costs**
- **Production of fuel, mulch, and food** from swale-planted trees
- Tank overflow and greywater can be directed to swales (avoiding boron detergents)

## Swale Features and Enhancements

### Specialized Planting Zones

- **Chestnuts and citrus** may need to be planted on nearby spoil banks for adequate root drainage in fast-absorbing swales
- **Mulch pits** and manure-filled pits can be dug in swales for heavy nutrient feeders (yams, bananas)
- **Sealed water reserves** using oil drums, plastic liners, or tyre ponds can hold water for young plants, planted with lotus, kangkong, watercress, or Chinese water chestnut

### Access Design

Keep swale width to match the **tractor, donkey cart, foot track, or wheelbarrow access width** planned for the site. Sections can be widened at regular intervals for plant assemblies, ponds, mulch pits, and higher-water-need trees, keeping access open throughout.

### Wind Protection

- Lower slope swales can be planted mainly to **Casuarina or leguminous trees** to prevent upslope winds
- **Ridges** should always have windbreak and condensation plants of hardy, useful species (Casuarina, Acacia, Leucaena, silky oak, pine, cypress)
- Windbreaks can occupy every **sixth to tenth swale** where wind is limiting
- Plant windbreaks on the **downslope side** to allow mulch collection in the swale base

### Silt Management

Silt from swales is periodically **graded out or shovelled up to tree lines** on the downslope side. Swales can be widened over time to create terrace systems.

## Inter-Swale Spacing

The distance between swales depends on rainfall:

| Rainfall | Inter-Swale Space |
|----------|-------------------|
| > 127 cm (50 in) | 3 m (12 ft) |
| ~50 cm (20 in) | ~9 m (30 ft) |
| < 25 cm (10 in) | 18 m (60 ft) |

## Swale Longevity

All swales are **temporary features** — they are precursors to forest rehabilitation. As tree systems mature, they supplant the swale's function. Eventually, normal forest processes take over, and the swale becomes a subtle topographic feature supporting specialized understory ecology.
