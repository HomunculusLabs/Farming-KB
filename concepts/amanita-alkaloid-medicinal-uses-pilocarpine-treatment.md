# Amanita Alkaloid Medicinal Uses and Pilocarpine Treatment

The study of [[amanita]] alkaloids—particularly amanitine, the toxic principle of
*[[amanita-phalloides]]*—represents one of the most significant intersections of
mycology, toxicology, and 19th-century experimental medicine. [[julius-auboineau-palmer]]
Palmer, drawing on the work of French researchers Cordier and Sicard, documented
both the perilous nature of [[amanitine-poisoning-and-absorption-mechanisms]] the first promising
experimental treatments using [[pilocarpine]] and atropine.

## Proposed Medicinal Applications

### Cordier's Opium Substitution Hypothesis

Cordier proposed that the narcotic properties of *[[amanita-phalloides-death-cap]]* might
serve as a substitute for opium in medical practice. His reasoning was grounded
in practical pharmacoeconomics: [[query-how-to-identify-and-avoid-the-death-cap-amanita-phalloides]] was abundantly available in wooded
districts, required no cultivation, and its psychoactive properties—though
primarily toxic at standard doses—suggested potential for controlled therapeutic
use. This proposal, while never adopted into mainstream medicine, reflects the
19th-century tendency to investigate toxic fungal alkaloids for pharmaceutical
potential.

### Lactarius Species in Tuberculosis Treatment

Acrid milk mushrooms of the genus *Lactarius* were reported to have been
prescribed with success in the treatment of phthisis (pulmonary tuberculosis),
even in advanced stages of the disease—a significant claim given the limited
therapeutic options in the pre-antibiotic era. The acrid principles of Lactarius
species, while irritating in raw form, were apparently processed to yield
therapeutic compounds.

## The Nature and Danger of Amanita Alkaloid

### Amanitine as the Best-Defined Fungal Toxin

Authorities of the period recognized the Amanita alkaloid as the best-defined
of all fungal toxic properties. Amanitine was understood to be a potent
alkaloidal poison absorbed directly through the intestinal vessels. Unlike acrid
or indigestible mushroom constituents that irritate the gastrointestinal tract,
amanitine enters the bloodstream and acts systemically, making it far more
dangerous and difficult to treat.

### Delayed Onset and Extreme Potency

Cordier and Sicard documented that symptoms of amanitine poisoning typically
appear between 4 and 12 or more hours after ingestion. This extended latency
is particularly insidious: victims may believe they have eaten safely before
severe symptoms begin. Cases were recorded in which individuals died from
consuming only a single piece of *Amanita* stem—a testament to the toxin's
extraordinary potency.

### Routes of Absorption

Palmer emphasized in his 1878 *Le Moniteur* article that amanitine absorption
is not limited to ingestion. He documented absorption through skin contact
(notably in the hollow of the hand) and through the lungs. Personal experiments
confirmed these additional routes, expanding understanding of how fungal
alkaloids enter the body and underscoring the danger of handling toxic Amanita
species without ingestion.

## Inadequacy of Standard Treatments

The standard medical advice for suspected [[amanita-toxins-and-mushroom-poisoning]] was administration
of emetics and purgatives. While appropriate for acrid or indigestible elements
remaining in the gastrointestinal tract, they were wholly inadequate for
absorbed alkaloidal poisons like amanitine. As Palmer noted, once the toxin had
been absorbed by the intestinal vessels, removing every vestige from stomach and
bowels might not save the patient. The standard approach addressed the wrong
problem entirely.

## Sicard's Pilocarpine Experiment

### The Control: Standard Treatment Failure

Sicard conducted a landmark experiment using a dog poisoned with Amanita toxin.
Under the standard protocol, the dog was given mineral turpentine to provoke
vomiting and buckthorn syrup as a purgative. These measures evacuated the
gastrointestinal contents but failed to address the absorbed toxin. When it
became clear the poison had entered the dog's system, the animal was, under
conventional thinking, "allowed to die"—standard methods offered no further
recourse.

### The Pilocarpine Intervention

Sicard then attempted an entirely novel approach on a second poisoned dog:
1. **Sub-cutaneous injection of 2mg nitrate of pilocarpine** — a potent
   sudorific agent known to stimulate sweating and glandular secretion.
2. **A second injection near the heart** — to accelerate distribution of the
   antidotal agent through the circulatory system.
3. **Friction with straw** — to further stimulate circulation and cutaneous
   activity.
4. **Nitrate of potash in marshmallow solution** — serving as a diuretic and
   soothing mucilaginous preparation.

### Results

Under this regimen, the dog's pupils—dilated and unresponsive as a hallmark of
amanitine poisoning—became sensitive and contracted. The poison's progression
was arrested. The dog survived, though it never fully returned to normal health.
Despite the incomplete recovery, the experiment demonstrated for the first time
that absorbed amanitine could be actively counteracted through chemical
intervention rather than gastrointestinal evacuation alone.

## Atropine as an Antidote

Palmer further identified a remedy in the alkaloids of the Solanaceae family,
particularly **atropine** administered by hypodermic injection. Atropine, a
parasympatholytic compound from *[[atropa-belladonna]]*, acts as a physiological
antagonist to many effects of muscarinic alkaloids. Its use in Amanita
poisoning represented another departure from the purely evacuative approach,
targeting the toxin's pharmacological action rather than physical removal.

## Clinical Diagnosis

### The Critical Time Window

Palmer articulated a key diagnostic principle: a person presenting with
cholera-like symptoms (profuse watery diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal cramps,
dehydration) between 6 and 18 hours after eating mushrooms is almost certainly
under Amanita alkaloid influence. This temporal criterion was essential for
distinguishing amanitine poisoning from other forms of food poisoning.

### Prognostic Significance of Symptom Onset

A shorter interval between the mushroom meal and symptom onset generally
indicates a less favorable prognosis. Rapid onset suggests a large absorbed dose
or heightened susceptibility. Conversely, a longer latency period, while
allowing potential intervention, was associated with severe poisoning because the
toxin had more time to be fully absorbed and distributed before clinical signs
prompted treatment.

## Historical Significance

The work of Cordier, Sicard, and Palmer represents an important chapter in the
history of toxicology and experimental pharmacology. Their investigations moved
the medical understanding of mushroom poisoning beyond gastrointestinal
decontamination toward systemic pharmacological intervention—foreshadowing
modern approaches to alkaloid poisoning. Sicard's pilocarpine experiment
demonstrated that absorbed fungal toxins could be chemically antagonized, a
concept central to the treatment of many classes of poisoning.
