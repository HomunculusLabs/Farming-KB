# Design for Children and Education Gardens

Designing [[forage-and-fodder-trees-permaculture-systems]] for children requires attention to safety,
accessibility, sensory engagement, and educational value. Mollison believed
that children learn most effectively through direct interaction with living
systems, and that school and [[gaias-garden-creating-community-gardens]] are powerful vehicles for
transmitting ecological literacy. An education garden must function as both a
productive growing system and a rich learning environment.

## Principles of Children's Garden Design

Children's gardens should prioritise hands-on interaction over observation.
Every element should be accessible at child height and scale. Pathways must be
wide enough for group movement and wheelchair access. Raised beds at 40 to 60
centimetres allow young children to reach the centre without stepping into the
bed. Plantings should be dense, tactile, and responsive to encourage repeated
engagement throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

The garden layout should create distinct zones for different activities: a
food production area, a sensory exploration area, a wildlife habitat area,
a gathering and teaching space, and a play area integrated with productive
plantings. Clear boundaries and defined spaces give children a sense of
ownership and security within each zone.

## Safe Plant Selection

All plants in children's gardens must be non-toxic. Common garden plants that
should be avoided include: foxglove, lantana, oleander, datura, castor oil
plant, nightshade (unripe), and many ornamental bulbs. Edible species are
preferred because they carry no toxicity risk and teach children that food
comes from gardens. Suitable edible plants include: strawberries, cherry
tomatoes, peas, beans, sunflowers, pumpkins, radishes, herbs (mint, basil,
rosemary), and edible flowers (nasturtium, calendula, viola, borage).

Thorny plants (raspberries, bougainvillea) should be placed away from high-
traffic areas. Trees with heavy branch drop (eucalyptus, melaleuca) should be
positioned away from play zones. All mulch materials should be non-splintering
and free of contaminants.

## Sensory Garden Elements

Sensory gardens engage all five senses and are particularly valuable for
younger children and those with sensory processing differences.

**Sight:** Brightly coloured flowers (sunflowers, zinnias, marigolds), varied
leaf shapes and colours (red basil, silver sage, purple orach), and plants
that move in the wind (grasses, poppies). Colour-themed beds can teach plant
identification and seasonal change.

**Smell:** Fragrant herbs (lavender, rosemary, thyme, mint, lemon balm),
scented flowers (jasmine, honeysuckle, sweet pea), and aromatic leaves
(pineapple sage, geranium). Herbs release fragrance when brushed or crushed,
encouraging tactile engagement.

**Touch:** Textured leaves (lamb's ear, fuzzy sage), smooth stones, rough
bark, soft moss, and varied mulch materials. Tactile plants invite handling
and teach children to observe closely.

**Taste:** Edible flowers, herbs, and vegetables that can be picked and eaten
directly. Cherry tomatoes, strawberries, peas, and beans provide instant
gratification. A designated tasting area with clean produce builds confidence
in food growing.

**Sound:** Bamboo wind chimes, rustling grasses, water features, seed pods
that rattle, and spaces designed for listening to birds and insects.

## Child-Scale Tools and Infrastructure

Tools must be sized for children's hands and strength. Short-handled trowels,
rakes, and hoes allow proper technique development. Lightweight watering cans
with rosettes prevent soil erosion. Bucket and wheelbarrow stations at child
height encourage independence. A potting bench at 60 to 70 centimetres allows
seed sowing and transplanting without adult assistance.

Infrastructure includes: raised beds with wide tops for sitting, a shaded
gathering area with seating for a class group, a tool storage shed accessible
to children, compost bins with removable fronts for turning, a weather
station for recording observations, and a washing station for post-garden
cleanup. Signage should combine words with pictures for pre-readers.

## Curriculum Integration

Education gardens support learning across multiple subjects. Mathematics
through measuring beds, calculating seed spacing, recording growth rates, and
harvest weights. Science through [[soil-testing]], plant life cycles, insect
observation, and weather monitoring. Language through garden journals,
observation writing, and [[cannabis-male-plant-identification]]. Art through nature drawing,
seed mosaics, and flower pressing. History through heritage food plants and
traditional growing methods.

Mollison emphasises that the garden should teach systems thinking: the
connections between soil, water, plants, insects, and food production.
Seasonal cycles, decomposition, nutrient cycling, and energy flow become
tangible concepts when experienced directly.

## Playground Integration with Food Production

Productive plantings can be woven into playground design. Fruit trees provide
shade over play areas. Pumpkin patches become autumn mazes. Bean teepees
create living play structures. Sandpits bordered with strawberry runners
combine play with production. A small orchard can function as both a food
source and an adventure landscape.

Chicken coops and rabbit hutches near play areas give children responsibility
for animal care and direct observation of nutrient cycling. Compost systems
connected to garden beds demonstrate decomposition visually. Worm farms with
transparent sections allow observation of soil organisms at work.

## Animal Systems for Children

Small livestock in education gardens teaches responsibility, animal husbandry,
and the role of animals in productive systems. Chickens are the most common
choice: they are hardy, productive, and engaging for children. A small flock
of three to five hens provides eggs, manure for compost, insect control, and
endless observation opportunities. Rabbit systems, bee hives (with appropriate
safety measures), and fish in small ponds extend the range of animal systems
that children can manage.

Safety around animals requires supervision, proper fencing, and clear
protocols for feeding and handling. Children should learn to approach animals
calmly, recognise animal body language, and understand basic hygiene after
contact. These lessons in animal care carry over into broader ecological
literacy and respect for living systems.
