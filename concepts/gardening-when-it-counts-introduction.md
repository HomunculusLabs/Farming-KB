## Gardening When It Counts — Introduction and Philosophy

## Overview

*[[gardening-when-it-counts]]: [[growing-food-in-hard-times]]* by Steve Solomon is a comprehensive guide to vegetable gardening written from the perspective of someone who has spent decades growing food through both lean and prosperous times. Published in 2005 by New Society Publishers as part of their "Books for Wiser Living from Mother Earth News" series, the book advocates for practical, low-input gardening methods that produce real calories and nutrition rather than mere horticultural entertainment.

## The Author"s Background

Steve Solomon has been growing the majority of his household's food for decades, 12 months per year. By the time of writing, he was 63 years old and had retired to Tasmania — a temperate South Pacific island with a climate similar to Oregon's. His experience spans both the lean years of the 1970s (high inflation and unemployment) and the fat years of the 1980s through the early 2000s. Crucially, he continued gardening through both, suggesting that food self-reliance is not merely an economic necessity but a way of life.

## The Central Thesis

Solomon's core argument is that most home vegetable gardening — as commonly practiced — is **inefficient** and would not sustain a household in hard times. The popular "intensive" backyard gardening methods (widely promoted in gardening magazines and television shows) produce attractive results on small plots but require:

- High inputs of water
- High inputs of fertilizer and soil amendments
- Significant labor per unit of food produced
- Ideal conditions that may not persist during economic or climatic disruption

Solomon advocates instead for gardening methods that are:
- **Calorie-efficient**: Producing maximum food energy per unit of effort and input
- **Water-efficient**: Minimizing irrigation dependence
- **Low-input**: Reducing reliance on purchased fertilizers, pesticides, and soil amendments
- **Resilient**: Capable of producing food even when conditions are less than ideal
- **Scalable**: Suitable for garden sizes ranging from small backyard plots to larger homesteads

## Context: Lean Times and Fat Times

### The 1970s — Lean Years

During the 1970s, inflation and unemployment were high. These were years when many people turned to vegetable gardening out of economic necessity. The "back-to-the-land" movement and interest in self-sufficiency were driven by real economic pressure.

### The 1980s-2000s — Fat Years

Good economic times returned in the 1980s and continued through the first half of the new millennium. During easy times, people went to restaurants and took summer vacations. Solomon notes that he continued gardening through these prosperous decades — maintaining his skills and food production capacity regardless of economic conditions.

### The Implication

Solomon"s implicit argument is that gardening skills should be maintained during good times so they are available when needed. A gardener who only starts during a crisis faces a steep learning curve at precisely the worst moment. The book is addressed to "Mr. and Ms. Everybody Else" — people who rely on conventional wisdom for gardening guidance, which Solomon suggests is often wrong.

## Book Structure

The book is organized into chapters covering the complete cycle of food gardening:

|---------|-------|
| 1 | Introduction — getting land, becoming a vegetable gardener |
| 2 | Basics — what is a vegetable, helping plants grow, increasing soil fertility |
| 3 | Tools and tasks |

## Key Themes Preview

### Water Efficiency

A significant portion of the book addresses water use. Solomon presents four different plant spacing systems and discusses how wider spacing can dramatically reduce or eliminate the need for irrigation. This is particularly relevant for gardeners in water-scarce regions or those preparing for drought conditions.

### Soil Fertility Without Store-Bought Inputs

The book explores methods for increasing soil fertility without relying on purchased fertilizers. This includes composting (at multiple quality levels), green manures, cover crops, and even humanure (human waste composting). The goal is closed-loop fertility that does not depend on external inputs.

### Seed Sovereignty

Chapter 5 on seeds covers the commercial seed business, how to evaluate seed suppliers, techniques for successful germination, strategies for saving on seed purchases, and the ultimate self-reliance step of growing your own seed. This connects to broader themes of food security and independence from commercial seed companies.

### Practical Crop Selection

Rather than listing every possible vegetable, Solomon categorizes crops by difficulty:

- **Easiest crops**: High-reward, low-effort vegetables suitable for beginners
- **Harder crops**: Vegetables that require more skill or attention
- **Difficult vegetables**: Crops that are challenging to grow well and may not be worth the effort for gardeners focused on calorie production

### Realistic Expectations

Throughout, Solomon emphasizes producing **real food** — staple calories and essential nutrition — rather than treating the garden as a hobby that produces small quantities of expensive gourmet vegetables. This "gardening when it counts" philosophy means every square foot of garden space and every hour of labor should contribute meaningfully to household food security.

## Publication Context

The book was published by New Society Publishers, whose mission is to "publish books that contribute in fundamental ways to building an ecologically sustainable and just society." It was printed on 100% post-consumer recycled paper, processed chlorine-free, from ancient-forest-free sources — reflecting the publisher"s commitment to minimizing environmental impact.

The collaboration with Mother Earth News — "North America"s Original Guide to Living Wisely" for over 30 years — places the book within a tradition of practical self-reliance literature aimed at people with "a passion for self-reliance and a desire to live in harmony with nature."
