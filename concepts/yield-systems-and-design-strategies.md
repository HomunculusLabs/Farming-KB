# Yield Systems and Design Strategies

## Overview

Yield is the central purpose of any designed system in [[compost]] heat, surplus forage).

Energy yield is the foundation of system self-reliance. A system that generates more energy than it consumes is regenerative; one that runs a deficit is dependent on external inputs.

### Impalpable Yields

Not all valuable yields are material. Impalpable yields include:

- **Health** — improved physical and mental well-being of system inhabitants.
- **Nutrition** — the quality and diversity of food produced, beyond mere caloric quantity.
- **Security** — resilience against shocks, whether climate events, economic disruption, or supply chain failures.
- **Social context** — strengthened community bonds, cultural continuity, educational value, and aesthetic enrichment.

These yields are often the most significant in human terms, yet the hardest to measure. A well-designed system optimizes for impalpable yields alongside tangible ones.

## System Yield: A Comprehensive Definition

The formal definition of system yield is the **sum total of surplus energy produced, stored, conserved, reused, or converted** by the system. This definition is deliberately broad:

- It includes energy at every stage — from initial production through storage and reuse.
- It accounts for conserved energy (what is not wasted) as much as generated energy.
- It encompasses converted energy — energy transformed from one form to another while retaining utility.

This holistic view prevents the narrow mistake of counting only harvested products and ignoring the system's full energetic contribution.

## The Role of Life in Yield

**Living things are the only effective intervening systems** for generating yield. Abiotic processes — sunlight falling on bare rock, water running through a pipe — are passive and linear. Only living organisms can:

- Capture energy and convert it into complex, usable forms.
- Create feedback loops that regulate and stabilize system flows.
- Adapt and evolve in response to changing conditions.
- Generate complexity and diversity from simple inputs.

The designer's task is to assemble and support living communities that perform these functions. The richer and more interconnected the biological community, the greater and more reliable the yield.

## Strategies That Create Yield

Mollison organizes yield-generating strategies into six categories, each addressing a different dimension of design:

### Functional Strategies

These strategies focus on what the system *does* — the roles and functions performed by its elements:

- **Niche creation** — designing specific roles and conditions so that every element has a productive place and no niche goes unfilled.
- **Soil rehabilitation** — restoring degraded soils through biological means ([[mycorrhizal-inoculation]]) to rebuild the foundation of productivity.
- **Water diversion and management** — directing water to where it is most needed, slowing its passage through the landscape, and maximizing absorption.
- **Integrating structures** — embedding built elements (buildings, fences, paths) into the living system so they serve multiple functions.
- **Low-maintenance species selection** — choosing plants and animals that require minimal external input once established.
- **Beneficial guilds** — assembling groups of species that support each other through nutrient sharing, pest control, microclimate modification, and other mutual benefits.

### Spatial Strategies

These strategies address *where* elements are placed in relation to each other:

- **[[annidation]] (nesting)** — arranging elements in nested hierarchies, like a forest with canopy, understory, shrub, herb, and ground layers, each occupying its own vertical niche.
- **Tessellation (mosaic)** — creating a patchwork of interlocking elements across the landscape, like a mosaic, where each patch supports its neighbors.
- **Edge harmonics** — maximizing and managing the interfaces between different elements or communities, since edges are where diversity and interaction are greatest.
- **Zone, sector, and slope strategies** — organizing placement by frequency of use (zones), by directional influences like sun and wind (sectors), and by elevation and water flow (slope).

### Sequential Strategies

These strategies govern *when* activities and yields occur through time:

- **Interplanting** — growing multiple species together so that their productive phases overlap and fill temporal gaps.
- **Cyclic frequency** — timing interventions (planting, pruning, harvest, grazing) to align with natural rhythms and maximize productive cycles.
- **Browsing sequences** — managing animal movement through a landscape so that each area receives the right level of disturbance and recovery at the right time.

### Conservation Strategies

Conservation strategies focus on retaining value within the system:

- **Recycling at the highest level** — returning waste products to their most productive use rather than discarding or downcycling them. [[composting]] manure back into gardens, using greywater for irrigation, and feeding food scraps to poultry are all examples.

The key principle is to prevent energy and nutrients from leaving the system before they have been fully utilized.

### Cultural Strategies

These strategies address the human dimension of design:

- **Removing barriers** — identifying and eliminating social, economic, or psychological obstacles that prevent people from engaging productively with the system.
- **Expanding choices** — increasing the range of options available to people within the system, empowering them to adapt and innovate.

Cultural strategies recognize that people are integral to [[permaculture]] systems, not external managers.

### Administrative Strategies

These strategies operate at the institutional and organizational level:

- **Socio-legal reform** — changing laws, regulations, and social norms to remove obstacles to sustainable practice and create supportive frameworks.
- **Cooperative endeavors** — forming partnerships, cooperatives, and community organizations that pool resources, knowledge, and labor.
- **Financial recycling** — keeping capital circulating within local economies and systems rather than extracting it to distant centers.

## The Designer's Limitation

A critical insight runs through all of Mollison's work on yield: **yield is not a fixed property of a site or system.** It is limited primarily by the designer's comprehension of the system and their ability to orchestrate its components. The same piece of land can yield vastly different results depending on the skill and understanding of the person designing it.

This means that the most important investment in any permaculture project is the development of the designer's knowledge, observation skills, and creative capacity. Every system contains more potential yield than is currently being realized — the gap between actual and potential yield is the measure of the designer's room for growth.
