# McKenna on Shamanic Identification Signs and Training

## Overview

In his 1983 Esalen lecture on [[terence-mckenna]] offered detailed observations about how shamans are identified
in traditional societies, what qualities distinguish them, and how their
training differs from Western models of professional development. These
reflections draw on McKenna's extensive travels among indigenous peoples and
his direct experience with shamanic practitioners in the Amazon and elsewhere.

## The Tragedy of Cultural Amnesia

McKenna opened his discussion of shamanism by stating what he called "the
tragedy of our cultural situation": Western civilization has no living shamanic
tradition. This is not a matter of losing rituals or ceremonies — shamanism, in
McKenna's view, is primarily a set of techniques rather than rituals. It is a
body of practical knowledge about navigating non-ordinary states of
consciousness that has been developed over millennia, making it possible —
though perhaps not for everyone — to explore dimensions of experience that
remain inaccessible to the uninitiated.

## Signs of the Shamanic Calling

In archaic societies where shamanism functions as a thriving institution, the
signs of a potential shaman are fairly easy to recognize, though they may seem
unusual or alarming from a Western perspective. McKenna identified several key
indicators:

- **Oddness or uniqueness**: The individual stands out from the group in some
  marked way. This is not mere eccentricity but a qualitative difference in
  perception, sensitivity, or behavior that is recognized by the community as
  significant.

- **Epilepsy**: In many preliterate societies, epilepsy is often treated as a
  signature of shamanic potential rather than a disease. Those who have
  epileptic seizures and survive are brought to the attention of the shaman and
  trained in breathing techniques, plant usage, and other practices.

- **Survival of unusual ordeals**: People who are struck by lightning and live
  are thought to make excellent shamans. Similarly, those who nearly die of a
  disease and fight their way back to health after weeks in an indeterminate
  zone between life and death are considered to possess exceptional strength of
  soul.

- **Hypersensitivity to trance states**: Among aspiring shamans, there must be
  some demonstrable sign of inner strength or an unusual capacity for entering
  and navigating [[weil-the-natural-mind-altered-states-normalcy]] of consciousness.

## The Shaman's Social Position

McKenna emphasized that shamans occupy a paradoxical position in their
societies — simultaneously central and peripheral. The shaman sits at the
headman's side in council meetings and is consulted on matters of greatest
importance. Yet after the council, the shaman returns to a hut at the edge of
the village. They are not normally allowed to hold political power because
their role is considered sacred, and the sacred must remain separate from the
profane machinery of governance.

The shaman is called upon in crisis: someone dying or ill, a psychological
difficulty, a marital quarrel, a theft, or weather that must be predicted.
Their role is functional and situational rather than institutional or
bureaucratic. This peripheral centrality gives the shaman a unique vantage
point — close enough to power to influence it, far enough from it to maintain
independence.

## The Shaman as Phenomenologist

Based on his direct encounters with shamans [[fungi-around-the-world-species-distribution]], McKenna
challenged the anthropological stereotype of shamans as passive bearers of
tradition. He found that shamans are invariably sophisticated about what they
are doing. They understand plant chemistry at a practical level, yet they
describe energy fields as "spirits." McKenna suggested that the word "spirit"
as used by shamans functions as a technical gloss for a very complicated
concept, comparable to the way a quantum physicist might use the term "charm"
— a precise label for a phenomenon that resists description in ordinary
language.

The shaman is, in McKenna's view, "the true phenomenologist of this world" —
a practitioner of direct experiential investigation whose methodology, though
expressed in mythological rather than scientific language, produces reliable
knowledge about the properties of consciousness and the dimensions accessible
through altered states.

## Hereditary Shamanic Lines

McKenna raised the possibility that there may be hereditary shamanic family
lines, at least in the case of hallucinogen-using shamans. His reasoning was
biological: shamanic ability is determined to some degree by the number of
active receptor sites in the brain, which facilitates entry into these
experiences. If receptor density has a genetic component, then shamanic
aptitude could be inherited, and cultures that recognize and cultivate this
ability would naturally develop family lineages of practitioners.

However, McKenna noted that some people claim to have these experiences
naturally, without the use of plants, and he expressed being "underwhelmed by
the evidence that this is so." His standard for verification remained
practical: "What can you show me?"

## The Verification Standard

McKenna described his own process of engaging with claims of shamanic
knowledge. His persistent question — "What can you show me?" — reflects a
demand for demonstrable, experiential proof rather than theoretical claims or
lineage credentials. In the Amazon, this standard was met when informants led
him on a hike to collect ayahuasca vine, prepared the brew, and demonstrated
the reality of the experience directly.

This verification standard has implications for the transmission of shamanic
knowledge in general. It suggests that the authority of the shaman rests not
on social position, inherited status, or theoretical knowledge, but on the
demonstrable ability to produce and navigate the relevant states of
consciousness.

## Secrecy and Hidden Information Systems

McKenna noted that these shamanic information systems have not been well
studied, partly because they are deliberately kept secret. The transmission of
knowledge occurs through direct personal apprenticeship, often lasting years,
under the guidance of an experienced elder. What goes on in this training is
not fully known to outside observers, and the shamans themselves have reasons
— both practical and spiritual — for maintaining discretion about their methods.

McKenna also observed that shamanism is not a pleasant office in traditional
societies. The shaman's life involves confrontation with illness, death,
sorcery, and the most disturbing aspects of both the human and non-human
worlds. The role demands a psychological resilience that most people do not
possess.
