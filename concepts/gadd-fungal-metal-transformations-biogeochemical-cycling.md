## Fungal Metal Transformations and Biogeochemical Cycling

Fungi play a central role in the Earth's biogeochemical cycles — the processes by which elements move through the lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere. Through mechanisms collectively termed "geomycology," fungi transform rocks and minerals, mobilize and immobilize metals, and influence the availability of essential nutrients for all life. This chapter from Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer's *Fungi in the Environment* provides a comprehensive overview of fungal [[fungal-biogeochemical-mineral-transformations]].

## Rocks and Minerals as Nutrient Reservoirs

Rocks and minerals represent a vast reservoir of elements essential to life. Bulk biological metals — sodium (Na), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), and calcium (Ca) — are among the eight most abundant elements in the Earth's crust, collectively comprising 11.06% of crustal rock (Fraústo da Silva & Williams, 1993). Rocks also contain essential trace metals (Mn, Mo, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn) and phosphorus, all crucial for microbial and plant growth. ## Evolutionary Context

Fungi have been components of terrestrial microbial communities since the Ordovician period (480–460 million years before present). Their filamentous explorative growth habit, high surface-area-to-mass ratio, nutrient translocation ability, and hydrated mucilaginous sheaths have made fungal processes integral to biogeochemical change throughout geological time. [[fungal-community-profiling-rock-mineral-surfaces-gadd]]
- **Thigmotropism**: Directed growth toward grooves, ridges, and pores in solid material allows fungi to locate and exploit weakened sites
- **Mucilage lubrication**: Mucilaginous slime containing acidic and metal-chelating metabolites facilitates penetration

Indirect biomechanical effects include shrinking and swelling of hydrated mucilage, which can physically disrupt mineral structures.

### Biochemical Weathering ("Heterotrophic Leaching")

Biochemical processes are believed to be much more important than mechanical degradation. The three primary mechanisms are:

**1. Acidolysis (Proton-Promoted Dissolution)**
Fungi acidify their microenvironment through excretion of protons and organic acids, and through formation of carbonic acid from respiratory CO₂. Many fungi can excrete large quantities of protons, particularly when ammonium is the nitrogen source, dramatically lowering local pH and dissolving mineral matrices.

**2. Complexolysis (Ligand-Promoted Dissolution)**
Fungi excrete metal-complexing metabolites including:

- **Carboxylic acids**: Oxalic acid, citric acid — with strong chelating properties that aggressively attack mineral surfaces
- **Amino acids**: Organic nitrogen compounds that can bind and solubilize metal ions
- **Siderophores**: High-affinity iron-chelating compounds produced under iron-limited conditions
