# Fungal Metal Transformations and Biogeochemical Cycling

Fungi play a central role in the Earth's biogeochemical cycles — the processes by which elements move through the lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere. Through mechanisms collectively termed "geomycology," fungi transform rocks and minerals, mobilize and immobilize metals, and influence the availability of essential nutrients for all life. This chapter from Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer's *Fungi in the Environment* provides a comprehensive overview of fungal [[fungal-mineral-transformations-biogeochemical-cycles]] involvement in [[fungal-biogeochemical-mineral-transformations]].

## Rocks and Minerals as Nutrient Reservoirs

Rocks and minerals represent a vast reservoir of elements essential to life. Bulk biological metals — sodium (Na), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), and calcium (Ca) — are among the eight most abundant elements in the Earth's crust, collectively comprising 11.06% of crustal rock (Fraústo da Silva & Williams, 1993). Rocks also contain essential trace metals (Mn, Mo, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn) and phosphorus, all crucial for microbial and plant growth. (see [[gadd-mineral-transformations-biogeochemical-cycles-fungi]]).

These elements serve diverse biological functions:

- **Energy capture and macromolecular synthesis**: Carbon (C), nitrogen (N), hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), phosphorus (P), sulfur (S)
- **Information transmission**: Sodium, potassium, calcium
- **Catalysis**: Iron, copper, zinc, molybdenum
- **Electron transfer**: Iron
- **Structural building**: Calcium, phosphorus, silicon

The critical challenge is that these elements are locked in mineral [[fungal-mineral-transformations-biogeochemical-cycles]] forms that organisms cannot directly use. They must be released into bioavailable forms through weathering — the breakdown of rock substrates via physical, chemical, and biological processes.

## Fungi as Weathering Agents

While bacterial geomicrobiology has historically received more research attention, there is increasing evidence that fungi can dissolve minerals and mobilize metals more efficiently than bacteria across a wider range of pH values and redox conditions (Gu et al., 1998; Castro et al., 2000; Burford et al., 2003). (see [[gadd-fungal-metal-interactions]]).

Fungi are exceptionally well-suited as weathering agents due to several key adaptations:

- **Environmental resilience**: Highly resistant to metal toxicity, UV radiation, and desiccation
- **Metabolic versatility**: Can adopt diverse growth, metabolic, and morphological strategies
- **Chemical arsenal**: Exude protons and metal-complexing metabolites (organic acids, siderophores)
- **Symbiotic capability**: Form mutualistic associations with plants, algae, and cyanobacteria (mycorrhizae, lichens)
- **Filamentous growth**: Hyphal networks can explore large volumes of soil, increase or decrease surface area, and translocate nutrients over considerable distances
- **Mucilaginous sheaths**: Hydrated slime surrounding hyphae creates interconnected microenvironments for [[dighton-fungal-rock-weathering-mineral-dissolution]]

## Evolutionary Context

Fungi have been components of terrestrial microbial communities since the Ordovician period (480–460 million years before present). Their filamentous explorative growth habit, high surface-area-to-mass ratio, nutrient translocation ability, and hydrated mucilaginous sheaths have made fungal processes integral to biogeochemical change throughout geological time. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]], in particular, play a crucial role in cycling elements in soil ecosystems.

## Rock-Dwelling Fungi: Classification

Rock- and stone-dwelling microorganisms are classified by their relationship to the mineral surface:

- **Epilithic**: Occur on rock surfaces; often microcolonial black-coloured fungi forming spherical clusters of tightly packed cells with thick pigmented walls
- **Hypolithic**: Associated with pebbles on or near rock surfaces
- **Endolithic**: Inhabit the rock subsurface, forming distinct masses or brightly coloured layers
- **Chasmolithic**: Grow in pre-existing cracks and fissures, often visible from the surface
- **Cryptoendolithic**: Grow inside cavities among crystal grains, invisible from the surface
- **Euendolithic**: Specialized cryptoendoliths capable of actively boring into rock

Fungi have been found in limestone, soapstone, marble, granite, sandstone, andesite, basalt, gneiss, dolerite, amphibolite, and quartz — spanning virtually all rock types across all geographical and climatic zones.

## Mechanisms of Fungal Weathering

Fungal weathering operates through two synergistic categories of action:

### Biomechanical Weathering

Direct biomechanical degradation occurs through:

- **Hyphal penetration**: Fungal hyphae penetrate decayed rocks and tunnel into intact mineral matter along crystal planes, cleavages, cracks, and grain boundaries
- **Turgor pressure**: Osmotically generated pressure within hyphae exerts considerable mechanical force against [[fungal-community-profiling-rock-mineral-surfaces-gadd]]
- **Thigmotropism**: Directed growth toward grooves, ridges, and pores in solid material allows fungi to locate and exploit weakened sites
- **Mucilage lubrication**: Mucilaginous slime containing acidic and metal-chelating metabolites facilitates penetration

Indirect biomechanical effects include shrinking and swelling of hydrated mucilage, which can physically disrupt mineral structures.

### Biochemical Weathering ("Heterotrophic Leaching")

Biochemical processes are believed to be much more important than mechanical degradation. The three primary mechanisms are:

**1. Acidolysis (Proton-Promoted Dissolution)**
Fungi acidify their microenvironment through excretion of protons and organic acids, and through formation of carbonic acid from respiratory CO₂. Many fungi can excrete large quantities of protons, particularly when ammonium is the nitrogen source, dramatically lowering local pH and dissolving mineral matrices.

**2. Complexolysis (Ligand-Promoted Dissolution)**
Fungi excrete metal-complexing metabolites including:

- **Carboxylic acids**: Oxalic acid, citric acid — with strong chelating properties that aggressively attack mineral surfaces
- **Amino acids**: Organic nitrogen compounds that can bind and solubilize metal ions
- **Siderophores**: High-affinity iron-chelating compounds produced under iron-limited conditions
- **[[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]]**: Aromatic compounds with metal-binding capacity

Carboxylic acids provide both protons for acidolysis and chelating anions for complexolysis, making them particularly effective weathering agents. When a fungal culture excretes large amounts of a strong chelator like oxalate, the dissolution mechanism shifts from proton-promoted to ligand-promoted dissolution.
