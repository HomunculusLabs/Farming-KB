# History of Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy

## Overview

Psychedelic-assisted therapy has a complex history spanning more than seven decades — from early promise through prohibition, underground survival, and a modern renaissance driven by clinical evidence. This page traces that arc from the 1950s to the present.

## The First Wave (1950-1970)

### Discovery and Early Research

- **1943**: Albert Hofmann discovers LSD's psychoactive properties; see [[albert-hofmann]]
- **Late 1940s**: Early researchers begin exploring LSD's potential therapeutic applications
- **1950**: Humphry Osmond and John Smythies propose the adrenochrome hypothesis of schizophrenia; Osmond begins using LSD to model psychotic states
- **1951**: First published report on LSD therapy by Max Rinkel

### Alcoholism Treatment

- **1953**: Osmond and Abram Hoffer begin treating alcoholics with LSD at Saskatchewan Hospital in Canada
- Results were remarkably positive: approximately 50% of patients reported sustained sobriety after a single high-dose LSD session
- By 1966, over 1,000 papers on LSD therapy had been published, including six major studies on alcoholism
- The Minnesota model used "psychedelic" (high-dose, mystical) rather than "psycholytic" (low-dose, analytic) approaches

### Psycholytic Therapy

- Developed primarily in the UK and Europe
- Used lower, repeated doses of LSD or psilocybin in conjunction with psychoanalysis
- Practitioners included Ronald Sandison (UK), Hanscarl Leuner (Germany), and Stanislav Grof (Czechoslovakia/US)
- Focused on accessing unconscious material, processing trauma, and working through psychodynamic issues
- Sessions were conducted weekly over months; patients were encouraged to explore difficult emotions

### Psychedelic Therapy

- Developed primarily in North America
- Used single high doses intended to produce a mystical or transcendent experience
- Based on the premise that a transformative "peak experience" could catalyze lasting behavioral change
- Practitioners included Osmond, Hoffer, and later William Savage and others
- See [[terence-mckenna]] and [[timothy-leary]] for later advocates of the psychedelic experience model

### Expansion and Promise

- By the mid-1960s, LSD was being researched for: alcoholism, anxiety in terminal illness, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, autism, and personality disorders
- Over 40,000 patients received psychedelic therapy in clinical settings
- Major research centers existed at Harvard, Stanford, Maryland, UCLA, and in the UK, Canada, Germany, Czechoslovakia, and the Soviet Union
- The Spring Grove Hospital in Maryland conducted pioneering work with cancer patients, showing significant reduction in death anxiety

## Prohibition (1966-1990)

### Counterculture Backlash

- LSD escaped the laboratory and became associated with the counterculture movement of the 1960s
- See [[timothy-leary]] for the Harvard controversy and Leary's role in popularizing psychedelics
- Media sensationalism focused on "bad trips," psychotic episodes, and recreational misuse
- Government concern escalated amid the Vietnam War era and political unrest

### Legal Restrictions

- **1966**: LSD made illegal in California; federal scheduling followed under the Controlled Substances Act of 1970
- **1968**: Psilocybin and psilocin added to Schedule I
- **1971**: UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances internationalized prohibition
- Research funding was effectively eliminated; human research became nearly impossible to conduct

### The Research Freeze

- The last FDA-approved psychedelic study of the first wave ended in the early 1970s
- Most researchers abandoned the field; equipment was destroyed, data was lost
- A small number of therapists continued underground — most notably Leo Zeff, who trained dozens of underground psychedelic therapists from the 1970s until his death in 1988

## The Underground and Seeds of Revival (1970-2000)

### Underground Therapy

- Despite prohibition, a network of underground psychedelic therapists continued to work
- Leo Zeff ("the Secret Chief") developed protocols for MDMA and psychedelic therapy and trained practitioners across North America and Europe
- See [[alexander-shulgin]] and [[ann-shulgin]] for the Shulgins' role in exploring and documenting hundreds of psychoactive compounds

### MDMA Emergence

- **1976-1980**: MDMA begins to be used in therapy by underground practitioners
- **1985**: MDMA scheduled as Schedule I (Emergency Scheduling) despite objections from therapists
- This catalyzed the formation of **MAPS** (Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic Studies) in 1986 by Rick Doblin

### Early Academic Revival

- **1990**: Rick Strassman receives first FDA approval in decades for DMT research (published as "DMT: The Spirit Molecule")
- **1992**: FDA reopens the door to psychedelic research, approving a study of LSD for anxiety in advanced cancer patients (this study took years to launch)
- Grof's Holotropic Breathwork (non-drug technique) kept the therapeutic framework alive in legal contexts

## The Renaissance (2000-Present)

### MAPS and Clinical Research

- MAPS has been the primary driver of the modern psychedelic therapy revival
- **2000s**: First modern clinical trials of MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD
- **2006**: Roland Griffiths publishes landmark study on psilocybin and mystical experience at Johns Hopkins
- **2011**: First modern psilocybin study for cancer-related anxiety (at Johns Hopkins and NYU)

### Breakthrough Therapy Designations

- **2017**: FDA grants Breakthrough Therapy designation to MDMA for PTSD (MAPS-sponsored)
- **2018-2019**: FDA grants Breakthrough Therapy designation to psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression (Compass Pathways, Usona Institute)
- These designations accelerate the regulatory review process based on preliminary evidence of substantial improvement

### Current Clinical Trials (as of 2026)

- **MDMA for PTSD**: Phase 3 trials completed; New Drug Application submitted to FDA
- **Psilocybin for depression**: Multiple Phase 2 and Phase 3 trials ongoing globally
- **Psilocybin for end-of-life anxiety**: Positive Phase 2 results published
- **Psilocybin for substance use disorders**: Trials for alcohol use disorder, smoking cessation, and cocaine dependence underway
- **Ibogaine for opioid use disorder**: Early-stage clinical trials beginning
- **5-MeO-DMT**: Preliminary research at Johns Hopkins and elsewhere
- **Ketamine/esketamine**: Already FDA-approved (Spravato) for treatment-resistant depression, though not a classical psychedelic, it paved the regulatory path

### Global Expansion

- **Australia (2023)**: First country to reschedule psilocybin and MDMA for therapeutic use
- **Oregon (2020)**: First US state to legalize psilocybin-assisted therapy (Measure 109)
- **Colorado (2022)**: Second state to establish regulated psychedelic therapy program
- **Canada**: Special Access Program and growing number of clinical trials
- **UK, EU, Switzerland**: Active clinical trials and regulatory discussions
- **Israel, Japan, Netherlands**: Research programs established

## Key Figures in the Revival

- Rick Doblin (MAPS founder)
- Roland Griffiths (Johns Hopkins psilocybin research)
- David Nutt (UK psychopharmacology, drug policy reform)
- Robin Carhart-Harris (Imperial College London, psilocybin fMRI research)
- Charles Grob (UCLA, cancer anxiety studies)
- Anthony Bossis (NYU, psilocybin and mysticism)
- See [[john-halpern]] for work with peyote and psychedelics in psychiatric contexts

## Therapeutic Models

### Modern Protocols

Modern psychedelic-assisted therapy typically involves:

- **Preparation**: 1-3 therapy sessions to establish rapport, discuss intentions, and prepare for the experience
- **Medication session**: Single (or 2-3) sessions with the psychedelic substance in a comfortable, controlled setting
  - Trained therapists (typically 2) present throughout
  - Music is carefully curated; eyeshades are often used
  - Duration: 4-8 hours for psilocybin; 6-8 hours for MDMA
- **Integration**: Multiple follow-up therapy sessions to process the experience and implement insights
- **Set and setting**: Emphasized as critical to therapeutic outcome — see [[psychedelic-integration]]

## Challenges and Criticisms

- Equity and access: therapy may be very expensive; risk of creating a two-tier system
- Cultural appropriation: Indigenous knowledge underpinning psychedelic traditions is rarely credited or compensated
- Training standards: no consensus on therapist training requirements or certification
- Commercialization concerns: pharmaceutical industry involvement may prioritize profit over accessibility
- Safety: proper screening and preparation are essential; adverse events are possible
- Research limitations: many studies have small sample sizes and limited long-term follow-up
