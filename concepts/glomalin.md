# Glomalin and Soil Carbon

Glomalin is a glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) that plays a critical role in soil structure, carbon storage, and soil health. Discovered in 1996 by USDA soil scientist Sarah Wright, glomalin is now recognized as one of the most important compounds in terrestrial carbon cycling.

## What Is Glomalin

Glomalin is a sticky glycoprotein (part sugar, part protein) secreted by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. It serves multiple functions:
- **Structural:** Seals gaps in fungal cell walls and provides structural integrity to hyphae
- **Soil binding:** Its sticky nature glues soil particles together into water-stable aggregates
- **Carbon storage:** Extremely stable molecules with great longevity in soil
- **Nutrient cycling:** Contains two carbon sites along with nitrogen

## Carbon Contribution

Where [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are present, glomalin contributes **27-30% of the total soil carbon**. This is remarkable because:
- Glomalin molecules are extremely stable and long-lasting in soil
- They persist long after the fungi that produced them have died
- The carbon in glomalin far surpasses the amount present even in humic acids, despite humic acids weighing up to 20 times more
- Glomalin is produced continuously as AMF grow and die

## Soil Structure Benefits

Glomalin's sticky properties are essential for creating and maintaining soil structure:
- Binds individual soil grains into water-stable aggregates
- Creates pore spaces essential for air and water movement
- Improves soil aeration and drainage
- Reduces soil erosion by stabilizing aggregates against wind and water
- Helps form and maintain the crumb structure of healthy soil

## Relationship to Mycorrhizal Fungi

Glomalin is produced exclusively by fungi in the phylum Glomeromycota — the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. Key points:
- Production is continuous as fungal hyphae grow through soil
- The compound remains in soil long after fungal death
- Practices that support AMF (minimal tillage, low phosphorus fertilizer, living roots) increase glomalin production
- Practices that destroy AMF (heavy tillage, high phosphorus, bare fallow) reduce glomalin accumulation

## Agricultural Implications

Understanding glomalin has profound implications for farming and gardening:
- **Carbon farming:** Supporting mycorrhizal fungi is one of the most effective ways to sequester carbon in soil
- **Soil restoration:** Glomalin production helps rebuild degraded soils by creating structure
- **Erosion prevention:** Glomalin-stabilized aggregates resist water and wind erosion
- **Fertilizer reduction:** Soils rich in glomalin and AMF can access phosphorus and other nutrients more efficiently, reducing fertilizer needs

## Supporting Glomalin Production

See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for full practices. Key strategies:
- Practice [[no-till-farming]] to protect hyphal networks
- Keep living roots in soil year-round ([[cover-cropping]])
- Minimize phosphorus fertilizer applications
- Apply [[compost-tea-aact]] with fungal foods
- Use woody mulches to support fungal dominance
- Avoid fungicides entirely
