# Single Species vs. Whole Community in Soil Biology

## The Case for Whole Community Approach

We typically want a whole set of beneficial organisms, not just one
or two species. Well-made, local compost supplies tens of thousands
of species of bacteria. Correctly made aerobic compost has them all,
and not just bacteria, but fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. Thermal,
worm, and static compost can all have great communities of each kind
of organism. The whole [[soil-food-web]] is needed to prevent both the easy
and the harder-to-prevent diseases.

Bacteria alone can account for only a few of the preventative
mechanisms that work in soil. The rest of the food web is needed for
the remaining protective functions. See [[compost-biology-soil-food-web-inoculation]]
for more on inoculation approaches. Typically, you will see
improvement if you add any one or more species back into a really
sick soil. But you will not continue to see benefit over time. The
improvement stalls because you have to build the food web back all
the way to get to a place where no more pesticides or low to no
inorganic fertilizer is required.

## When Single Species Inocula Are Useful

Single species inocula can be useful if you know the precise thing
you want to control, know that you are buying the correct
environmental isolate that will grow in the conditions in your soil,
and know the temperature, moisture, and humidity are right for it
to grow. This approach can be extremely specific. You should also
know if you already have the beneficial organism in your soil,
compost, or compost tea, so you know if you need to add it.

Single species products that sit on the shelf for days or weeks
contain dormant organisms. A put-to-sleep tea is much better than
an inoculum containing just a few species of bacteria, but a few
bacterial species, especially very beneficial ones, is much better
than not having those species in your soil at all. It is all
relative.

## Limitations of Shelf-Stable Products

The point of compost tea is to have the bacteria, fungi, and protozoa
active when you add them to soil or plant surfaces. When organisms
are in a product that has to sit on the shelf for days, weeks, or
months, those organisms must be dormant, or the product would spoil.
See [[bacterial-vs-fungal-compost-teas]] for a comparison of tea types.
Therefore, no product that can sit for days or weeks can function as
a true compost tea. You can put compost tea to sleep, but in doing
so you lose perhaps 50% of the species of bacteria. Not 50% of the
individuals, but 50% of the species.

## Diversity as a Quality Indicator

As long as you have the diversity of organisms you need, then
addition of food to the soil works fine. Compost tea is a way to
deliver food and organisms to plant surfaces in a variety of
environments. Typically, the higher the total bacteria and total
fungi biomass, the better your diversity, though not a perfect
relationship.

With many soils, lack of diversity is an issue. Applying compost or
compost tea delivers an inoculum of organisms that can improve the
diversity of your soil. This will serve to build diversity over time.
When people are getting good biology in their applications, there are
no negative experiences. We have encountered cases where there was
no huge impact, but never a case where the tea did less than the
conventional system.

## Adding Inoculants to Tea

If you know you are missing a certain set of organisms, and that a
microbial inoculant contains the missing organisms, then adding that
inoculant would be a good idea. More likely to survive addition to
the tea than to the compost. But more likely to wake up dormant
organisms going into the compost than into the tea. The inoculant
approach can work, but testing is needed to verify survival.

## The 99.99% Problem with Plate Counts

Remember that 99.99% of the bacteria in soil, compost, or compost
tea will not grow on plates. So plate count methods miss most
organisms when they do counts. They also measure viability, not
activity, and we need active organisms in tea. Some beneficial
bacteria species encourage the growth of beneficial fungi which
occupy the space that disease fungi would otherwise occupy. This is
a very different mechanism from what you might think based on lab
culture methods. What happens on plates in incubators is very
different from what happens in real soil conditions.

## Building the Complete Food Web

Therefore, you need a compost tea that can give you the whole food
web, not just bacteria. A just-bacterial tea is not bad; it may not
have all the organisms we see are needed, but it has some benefit.
The key is understanding what your soil or plant needs and matching
the tea to those needs. Annual plants are generally bacterial, while
perennial plants are fungal. The relative requirements for the ratio
of fungi to bacteria vary along with successional stage. Matching
the tea biology to the plant needs is the key to success.

## The Ecosystem Approach

The whole food web approach recognizes that organisms function
within a community context. Bacteria and fungi are the primary
decomposers, breaking down organic matter and making nutrients
available. Protozoa and nematodes graze on bacteria and fungi,
releasing nutrients in plant-available forms through their waste.
Microarthropods, earthworms, and higher organisms further process
organic matter and create soil structure. Removing any level of
this web reduces the overall function of the system.

## Why Single Species Products Fall Short

Products that contain only one or a few species of microorganisms
cannot replicate the functions of a complete [[soil-food-web]]. A single
bacterial species may perform one function well, such as nitrogen
fixation or phosphate solubilization, but it cannot provide the
full range of nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and soil
building functions that a diverse community delivers. The
interactions between species in a complete community produce
emergent properties that no single organism can achieve. See also [[biofertilizers-and-soil-inoculants]].

## Compost Tea as Community Delivery

Compost tea, when properly brewed from high-quality compost,
delivers a diverse community of organisms rather than a single
species. This community includes bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and
sometimes nematodes, all of which have roles to play in soil and
plant health. The tea serves as an inoculum that introduces or
reinforces the complete food web, allowing the natural interactions
between organisms to develop and self-organize in the soil or on
plant surfaces.

## Monitoring Community Health

monitoring assesses the overall community structure. Direct
