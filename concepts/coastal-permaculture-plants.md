# Coastal Permaculture Plants

Coastal environments present some of the harshest growing conditions for plants: salt spray, strong winds, sandy nutrient-poor soils, and exposure to extreme weather. Despite these challenges, a diverse array of plant species have adapted to thrive right at the ocean's edge. These coastal plants are invaluable in permaculture design for stabilizing dunes, providing windbreaks, producing food, and creating resilient ecosystems in maritime zones.

## Coastal Ground Covers

Ground covers are the first line of defense against coastal erosion. They stabilize sand, build organic matter, and create the microclimates necessary for larger plants to establish.

### Carpobrotus spp (Pigface, Karkalla)

Native to southern Australia, pigface is one of the most effective coastal ground covers. The leaves are thick and fleshy, triangular in cross section, often with a reddish tinge. Purple flowers produce purplish-red fruits. Common on sand dunes, beaches, and headlands, with C. rossii and C. modestus extending well inland.

**Edible uses:** Considered one of Australia's tastiest fruits, with a flavour reminiscent of salty apples. Aboriginal peoples cooked the leaves to salt their meat, and the dried fruit was also eaten. In Queensland, the mucilaginous leaf juice treats midge bites and jellyfish stings. The related South African species contains mesembrine, a compound with mild physiological action.

**Medicinal:** Juice from both Australian and South African species is used traditionally to soothe burns and scalds. The plants build organic matter effectively in sand and can stabilize heavy soils such as dam walls.

### Tetragonia tetragonioides (Warrigal Cabbage, New Zealand Spinach)

A shrubby, creeping herb common to sheltered beaches, saltmarshes, arid woodlands, and plains extending into central Australia. Also native to the coast of New Zealand. This is the only plant native to Australia to be cultivated internationally as a vegetable.

**Culinary:** Leaves are cooked like spinach, and succulent young leaves can be eaten raw in salads. As a perennial leaf vegetable adapted to coastal conditions, it represents a valuable food plant for maritime permaculture systems.

### Arctostaphylos uva-ursi (Bearberry, Kinnikinik)

A trailing or creeping shrub (rarely exceeding 75 cm) of the northern temperate region, hardy to frost. The red berries are fairly bland and mealy, usually eaten cooked, preserved, or made into jams, jellies, marmalades, sauces, and pastes. The dried leaves are used as tea in some parts of Russia.

### Arctotis stoechadifolia (African Daisy)

A bushy, creeping perennial (to 25 cm) native to South Africa. It is half-hardy to frost and an extremely effective sand binder. In sand it builds organic matter well, survives hot dry summers, and has proven to stabilize heavy soils such as dam walls.

## Coastal Trees

### Avicennia marina (Grey Mangrove, White Mangrove)

The common mangrove tree of estuaries, river banks, and sheltered coasts of Australia. These remarkable trees take their oxygen from specialised roots (pneumatophores) that thrust up from the mud. The soil is typically soft, moist, saline, and has no profile.

**Edible:** The seed, after three separate boilings, looks and tastes similar to avocado. Golden-yellow flowers yield nectar producing a light, amber-coloured, pleasant honey from mid-autumn to early winter. Leaves are palatable to stock.

**Companions:** Casuarina glauca (swamp oak) on less inundated margins in the south; Ceriops tagal (small stilted mangrove) in the north. Tolerant of some frost.

**Timber:** Extremely tough and hard wood with conspicuous brown growth rings, density about 880 kg/m³. Used for knees and elbows in boat building.

### Elaeagnus angustifolia (Russian Olive, Bohemian Olive, Oleaster)

A deciduous small tree to 12 m, native from the Mediterranean through SW Asia. Extremely tough seaside plant, hardy to -34°C, growing right to the sea in France. Has a symbiotic relationship with Frankia spp nitrogen-fixing soil microbes.

**Edible:** Yellow, sweet berries eaten fresh or made into pies, sauces, jams, jellies, sherbets, and wines. Provides poultry forage in late summer.

**Functions:** Good pioneer species (but thorny), windbreak and hedge species, bird habitat, erosion control. Occurs naturally with Tamarix gallica. Prefers dry, well-drained, alkaline soils in sunny, open positions.

### Metrosideros tomentosa (New Zealand Christmas Tree)

A large tree (to 10 m), somewhat frost hardy. Commonly found growing with roots dangling into the sea. Hardy to salt spray.

### Other Coastal Trees

- **Betula populifolia** (grey birch) — To 9 m; thrives on sandy or swampy soil; extremely frost hardy; best planted in groves
- **Chrysobalanus icaco** (cocoa plum) — Extremely hardy shrub or tree to 12 m; branches creep in all directions; fruit eaten raw, stewed, dried, or in jellies; seed kernels roasted and eaten
- **Myoporum laetum** — Fast-growing evergreen to 6 m; very hardy in exposed places
- **Nyssa sylvatica** (black tupelo) — Frost-hardy tree to 30 m; edible fruit for preserves
- **Persea borbonica** (red bay) — Evergreen frost-hardy tree; leaves used as bay leaf substitute; relative of avocado
- **Populus tremula** (European aspen) — Small suckering tree; withstands full sea exposure; frost hardy and salt resistant
- **Salix caprea** (goat willow) — Coastal willow species
- **Sapindus saponaria** (soapberry) — Small evergreen tree to 8 m; grows where salt water overflows frequently
- **[[taxodium-distichum]]** (bald cypress) — Swamp tree of the Everglades; quite frost hardy

## Coastal Shrubs

- **Actinostrobus pyramidalis** (Swan River cypress pine) — Small ornamental conifer to 3 m; tolerant of salty land
- **Enchylaena tomentosa** (ruby saltbush) — Small shrub to 1 m; adapted to saline and marshy conditions throughout arid Australia
- **Baccharis halimifolia** (groundsel bush) — Frost-hardy deciduous shrub 1–3 m

## Permaculture Design Principles for Coastal Planting

Coastal permaculture design should consider:

1. **Wind exposure** — Use pioneer ground covers first, then establish windbreak hedges before planting trees
2. **Salt spray** — Select species with proven salt tolerance; position sensitive plants behind salt-tolerant barriers
3. **Sand stabilization** — Carpobrotus and Arctotis are primary sand binders for dune systems
4. **Food production** — Sea grape, warrigal cabbage, natal plum, and mangrove provide food in harsh coastal conditions
5. **Multi-functionality** — Choose plants that serve multiple roles (hedge + food, ground cover + medicine)
6. **Frost pockets** — Coastal areas can have unexpected frost; match species to local microclimate
7. **Soil building** — Sandy coastal soils need organic matter; use fleshy-leaved plants and nitrogen fixers
8. **Water management** — Fresh water may be limited; select drought-adapted species alongside salt-tolerant ones
