# Fractals and Branching Patterns in Nature

Fractals and branching patterns are among the most pervasive structures in the natural world. From the dendritic spread of tree roots underground to the intricate branching of a river delta seen from space, these self-similar patterns recur at every scale and across virtually every type of natural system. Understanding fractals gives [[permaculture]] designers a powerful framework for designing efficient pathways, distribution systems, and exchange networks.

## What Are Fractals?

The term **fractal** was coined by Benoit Mandelbrot (from the Latin *fractus*, meaning "shattered"). He assembled his own insights and the speculations of others to found a mathematics of fractals — a system evolving to make sense of irregular phenomena, just as Euclid did for regular and measurable forms.

Fractals are geometric shapes containing detailed structure at arbitrarily small scales, typically exhibiting self-similarity. They are generated by the repetition of relatively simple form-generators and are as common in nature as in abstractions.

### Examples of Fractal Forms

Fractal patterns appear across an extraordinary range of natural phenomena:

- **Impact shatter-zones** and explosion patterns
- **Clouds** and turbulent flow structures
- **Forked lightning** bolts
- **Neurone nets** and their signal patterns
- **Computer searching procedures** (binary trees)
- **Plant identification keys** (dichotomous branching)
- **Snowflakes** with their hexagonal symmetry
- **Tree branches and roots** — the quintessential fractal
- **Coastlines** and their intricate boundaries
- **Turbulence** in fluids and gases

### Fractals Within Regular Forms

A critical insight is that fractal patterns can underlie quite regular shapes in nature. In the permaculture tree model, fractal patterns (as branches and roots) are contained within a form comprehensible to Euclid — having straight axes, a plane, and regular curved lines drawn as arcs of perfect circles. Thus the apparent chaos of fractals can be seen to underlie regular (but never perfect) shapes, as branches underlie the crown canopy of a tree.

As Mandelbrot demonstrated, fractals have their own regular generators and evolutions — they are not truly chaotic but follow mathematical laws of their own.

## Tree Roots as Underground Fractals

Tree roots are, in fact, a slow shatter or explosion underground. One practical application of this insight: a proven way to plant an apple tree in very hard ground is to detonate a small plug of gelignite a foot or two below the surface. The roots will follow the shatter pattern and further elaborate it, because the shatter zone mimics the fractal branching pattern that roots naturally follow.

This reveals a profound connection between destructive processes (explosions, shattering) and constructive ones (root growth, nutrient gathering) — both follow fractal geometry.

## Scatters as Fractals

Scatters of objects may at first seem unrelated to either flow models or fractals, but fractal analysis is being used to describe:
- Scatters of tree clumps in grassland
- Lichen distribution on stones
- Growth points on the surface of spheroids created by branched phenomena (like the plan view of a tree crown)
- A curved section through cut or pruned branches below a crown, which appears as a scatter of points

## The Physics of Branching Conduits

### Why Branching Works

Fractal theory provides a way to measure, compute, and design for branched or scattered phenomena. But the physical advantages of developing ever-smaller conduits are equally important (Vogel, 1981):

**Large conduits** (main arteries, trunks, rivers):
- Useful for mass transport
- Laminar flow patterns within them
- Small surface area relative to volume
- Inefficient for diffusion of materials or conduction of heat across their walls

**Small conduits** (minor branches, capillaries, rills):
- Flow is slow, almost viscous in very small tubes
- Direction changes possible without turbulence or energy losses
- Walls can be permeable
- Efficient collection, exchange, and transfer of materials, heat, and light
- Many small conduits efficiently interpenetrate exchange media

### The Surface-to-Volume Advantage

Wherever there is a need to collect or distribute materials, or to trade both ways with media, branching is an effective response because it maximizes the surface area available for exchange. The key insight: **many small conduits are better than one large one** when exchange (not just transport) is the goal.

### The Cross-Sectional Area Principle

The high-pressure/low-flow nature of minor branches demands a very large total cross-sectional area relative to the main supply arteries. Small conduits may develop areas which in sum are **300 to 1,600 times** that of the supply artery. This principle is visible in human infrastructure: main roads are much less in total area than the foot tracks that lead off them.

### Biological Resilience

In organisms, multiple branches give the being a chance to:
- **Recover from injury** — damage to one branch does not destroy the whole system
- **Preserve information** — multiple pathways carry redundant information
- **Permit regrowth** — minor damage triggers regrowth from remaining branches

It is a foolproof system of interchange.

## Design Applications

### Garden Path Design

In permaculture garden design, branching principles apply directly:

- **Multiple paths over straight lines**: Speed is not essential in gardens; the goal is contact and exchange. Multiple winding paths bring the gardener into better contact with the garden, enabling collection and servicing.
- **Keyhole beds**: Cul-de-sac or keyhole-shaped beds (the same shape as sacs in lungs) create better mutual exchange between species and improve gardener access.
- **Convoluted paths**: These have the same effect as branching in organisms — they enhance nutrient trading and create microclimatic diversity.

### Enhancing Access

Multiple small paths enhance access to food systems, or any system where materials are both taken and given. The principle that small conduits should have 300-1,600× the total area of supply arteries suggests that garden footpaths should be far more extensive than main access routes.

### Exchange Surfaces

Another way to effect interchange is to elaborate on the walls of larger conduits by:
- Involutions (folding inward)
- Attached fins
- Irregular surfaces
- Creating spirals in fluids or gases by bending or spiralling the conduits themselves
- Inducing a larger surface of contact between transported material and exchange media

This is visible in biological systems: lungs, kidneys, and intestines all maximize internal surface area through folding, branching, and texturing.

## Branching as an Interaction

Branching in trees is as often a result of external forces (wind and salt pruning, secateurs, or insect attack) as it is a result of internal cell patterns. It is as much forced upon things as it is the "best thing to do." Therefore, the branched form must be seen as an **interaction between**:
- The organism or process itself
- The purpose it serves
- The external forces of the media in which it is immersed

This has design implications: branching patterns should be shaped by both the internal needs of the system and the external forces (wind, water, traffic, sunlight) acting upon it.

## Gathering and Dispersal

Along the streamlines of the tree model, fluids and gases pass in conduits or along "transmission cords." Food and signals are relayed to cells, and gases exchanged. Organs served by or serving these systems are either half-models of the tree (kidneys, lungs) or branching fractals (mesenteries).

No matter how long or complex conduits are, their contents ultimately diverge, escape, and disperse. At the intake, materials are gathered from dispersed sources. This **gathering and dispersal from both ends or margins of events** is the basic function of the tree-like forms that pervade living natural systems and phenomena such as rivers or lava flows.
