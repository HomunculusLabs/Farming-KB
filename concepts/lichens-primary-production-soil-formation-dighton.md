## Carbon Fixation and Primary Production

### Semi-arid Forests
Beymer and Klopatek (1991) measured approximately 28 kg C per hectare fixed by the lichen crust community in a pinyon pine and juniper forest in the Grand Canyon region. Using radioactive tracer techniques (14C), they estimated that 34-36% of this fixed carbon becomes incorporated into soil organic matter — a significant contribution to soil carbon pools in these water-limited ecosystems.

### Arctic and Subarctic Ecosystems
In the Norwegian high arctic, Cooper and Wookey (2001) measured growth rates of fruticose lichens (*Cetraria* spp., *Cladonia* spp., *Alectoria nigricans*) at 2.4 to 10.6 mg per gram per week, representing 2.5-11.2% of original biomass in one growing season (approximately 10 weeks). The arctic tumbleweed lichen *Masonhalea richardsonii* increased biomass by about 10% per year in Alaska (Peck et al., 2000).

These lichens are critical winter feed for reindeer and caribou. On Svalbard, lichen biomass can become severely depleted due to intense grazing pressure, low growth rates, and trampling damage from herbivores — creating a classic predator-prey dynamic between mammals and their fungal food source.

### Temperate Forest Canopies
Sillett et al. (2000) showed that colonization of experimental branches by arboreal lichens was highest in clear-cut and old-growth Douglas fir forests and lowest in young (10-year-old) forests. The lichen *Lobaria oregana* showed differential bark preferences depending on forest age — rough bark in old-growth, no preference in young forest, smooth bark in clear-cuts.

Palmqvist and Sundberg (2000) reported light use efficiency of 0.5-2% per dry weight across five macrolichen species, with a significant positive correlation between intercepted irradiance and growth when lichens were wet. Ground-inhabiting lichens produced 30-70 g/m2 compared to only 1-4 g/m2 for epiphytes in the same Swedish locality, attributed to better water-holding capacity and greater light levels at ground level.

### Tropical Ecosystems
In tropical environments, lichen productivity is limited by high dark respiration rates. Lange et al. (2000) found that within the genus *Leptogium*, 47-88% of carbon gained during photosynthesis was lost as respiration, severely limiting net carbon accumulation. This respiratory cost means that lichens are less significant primary producers in warm, humid tropical forests compared to temperate and polar ecosystems.

### Nitrogen Economy of Mat-Forming Lichens
Crittenden et al. (1994) showed that mat-forming lichens are severely nitrogen-limited in oligotrophic environments. There was a significant positive relationship between nitrogen availability and chitin content (a measure of fungal biomass), demonstrating that nitrogen limits the fungal component of the lichen thallus.

Crittenden (1989) reported that these lichens depend heavily on intercepting nitrogen in precipitation, with interception efficiency often approaching 100%:

| Lichen Species | NO3-N Retention (%) | NH4-N Retention (%) | N2 Fixation (mg/m2/82 days) |
|---------------|-------------------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| *Stereocaulon paschale* | 86-100 | 40-99 | 669 |
| *Cladonia stellaris* | 62-99 | 50-97 | 0 |

*Stereocaulon* both fixes atmospheric nitrogen AND intercepts deposited nitrogen, while *Cladonia* relies entirely on precipitation interception yet still accumulated 95 mg total biomass N from this source alone.

## Photoprotection

The fungal partner provides critical photoprotection for the photosynthetic apparatus. Solhaug and Gauslaa (1996) extracted the compound parietin from *Xanthoria parietina* using acetone and demonstrated that extracted lichens showed reduced photosynthetic oxygen production at high light intensities. This provided direct evidence that the fungal-produced blue-light filtering chemical protects the algal partner from photoinhibition — a clear example of mutualism within the [[comparison-lichen-symbiosis-vs-mycorrhizal-symbiosis]].

## Desert Carbon Sequestration

Lange et al. (1994) measured photosynthesis and water relations of lichen soil crusts in the coastal fog zone of the Namib Desert, demonstrating that even in extreme arid environments, lichens can contribute to carbon sequestration during brief periods of moisture availability from fog events. This finding expanded the known range of environments where lichens function as significant primary producers.

## Significance

Lichens demonstrate that the fungal kingdom plays a direct role in primary production, not merely in decomposition and nutrient cycling. Their role is most significant in:

- Extreme environments where vascular plants cannot survive (arctic tundra, hot deserts, alpine zones)
- Early successional stages of soil development on bare rock
- Forest canopies where they access leached nutrients unavailable to ground-rooted plants
- Dryland ecosystems where soil crusts prevent erosion and fix nitrogen
- Nutrient-poor ecosystems where nitrogen interception from precipitation is a critical input
