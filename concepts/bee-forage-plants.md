# Bee Forage Plants

Providing abundant, diverse forage is essential for honey bee health and
honey production. This guide covers the best nectar and pollen plants by
season, with regional considerations and planting strategies for the
homestead.

## Why Planting for Bees Matters

- Extends the foraging season beyond natural bloom periods
- Fills nutritional gaps (dearth periods) when colonies might otherwise starve
- Increases honey production and colony strength
- Supports native pollinators alongside managed honey bees
- Integrates with [[food-forest-design|polyculture design]] on the homestead

## Understanding Bee Nutrition

Bees need both nectar (carbohydrates) and pollen (protein, lipids, vitamins,
minerals). A healthy diet requires diversity — no single plant provides
complete nutrition.

- **Nectar plants** — provide energy; determine honey production and variety
- **Pollen plants** — provide protein for brood rearing; critical in spring
  and fall
- **Dual-purpose plants** — provide both; these are the most valuable

## Spring Forage (March - May)

Spring is the critical build-up period. Colonies need abundant pollen for
brood production and nectar for energy.

| Plant | Bloom Time | Resource | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maple (Acer spp.) | Feb-Apr | Pollen | Early tree pollen; critical for spring build-up |
| Dandelion | Apr-May | Both | Excellent early pollen; often undervalued |
| Crimson clover | Apr-Jun | Both | Annual; excellent cover crop |
| Fruit trees (apple, cherry, plum) | Mar-May | Both | High-value orchard forage |
| Willow (Salix spp.) | Mar-Apr | Pollen | Very early pollen source |
| Wild mustard | Apr-May | Both | Common weed; good early forage |
| Blueberry | Apr-May | Both | Excellent native shrub |
| Hawthorn | Apr-May | Both | Ornamental and wildlife tree |
| Autumn olive | Apr-May | Both | Invasive but prolific forage; consider native alternatives |
| Borage | May-Sep | Both | Continuous bloomer; exceptional bee plant |

## Summer Forage (June - August)

The main nectar flow period. Abundant nectar produces surplus honey.

| Plant | Bloom Time | Resource | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| White clover | May-Sep | Both | Primary summer honey source in many areas |
| Sweet clover (yellow/white) | Jun-Aug | Both | Biennial; one of the best honey plants |
| Basswood / Linden | Jun-Jul | Nectar | Premium honey tree; short but intense flow |
| Lavender | Jun-Aug | Both | Long bloom; drought tolerant once established |
| Sunflower | Jul-Sep | Both | Annual; excellent late pollen |
| Bee balm (Monarda) | Jul-Aug | Both | Native perennial; highly attractive |
| Thyme | Jun-Aug | Both | Drought tolerant; culinary herb |
| Russian sage | Jul-Oct | Nectar | Long bloom; very attractive to bees |
| Purple loosestrife | Jul-Sep | Both | Invasive; avoid planting but bees love it |
| Squash/pumpkin | Jun-Aug | Pollen | Heavy pollen producer; needs bee visits for fruit set |

## Fall Forage (September - November)

Fall forage is crucial for building winter stores and raising fat, healthy
winter bees.

| Plant | Bloom Time | Resource | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goldenrod (Solidago spp.) | Aug-Oct | Both | Primary fall honey source; produces strong honey |
| Aster (Symphyotrichum spp.) | Aug-Oct | Both | Pairs with goldenrod; critical fall pollen |
| Japanese knotweed | Aug-Oct | Nectar | Invasive; produces late fall honey |
| Ivy (Hedera helix) | Oct-Nov | Nectar | Very late; provides winter stores in mild climates |
| Autumn-blooming crocus | Sep-Oct | Both | Saffron crocus; pollen and nectar |
| Sedum (Autumn Joy) | Sep-Oct | Both | Ornamental; excellent late forage |
| Smartweed | Aug-Oct | Both | Common wetland plant; good pollen |

## Regional Considerations

### Northeast / Midwest
Focus on: maples, dandelion, clover, basswood, goldenrod, aster. The
typical season runs March through October with a potential summer dearth
in July-August.

### Southeast
Longer season (February - November). Key plants: tupelo, gallberry,
sourwood, palmetto, chinese tallowtree. Summer dearth is common — plant
for gap-filling.

### Pacific Northwest
Cool springs favor early bloomers. Key plants: bigleaf maple, blackberry,
fireweed, clover, snowberry. Plant for the July-September gap.

### Southwest / Arid Regions
Irrigation is often needed. Key plants: mesquite, creosote, catclaw,
desert willow. Drought-tolerant natives are essential.

## Planting Strategies

### Hedgerows and Windbreaks
Linear plantings of flowering trees and shrubs along property boundaries
provide continuous forage and habitat. See [[windbreak-and-shelterbelt-design]] principles for
design guidance. Good species: crabapple, hawthorn, elderberry, serviceberry.

### Cover Crops
Integrate bee forage into your cover cropping program. Crimson clover,
buckwheat, and phacelia serve as both soil builders and bee food. See
[[cover-cropping-guide]] for rotation planning.

### Meadow Planting
Convert unused areas to wildflower meadows using native seed mixes.
Mow once per year in late fall after plants have gone to seed.

### Orchards
Fruit trees provide excellent spring forage. Underplant with clover or
dutch white clover as a living mulch that also feeds bees.

## Trees as Bee Forage

Trees are the most efficient forage plants — a single mature tree provides
the nectar and pollen of acres of herbaceous plants. Key bee trees:

- **Tupelo** (Nyssa ogeche) — premium honey, southeastern US
- **Basswood** (Tilia americana) — excellent nectar, northern US
- **Black locust** (Robinia pseudoacacia) — prolific nectar, light honey
- **Sourwood** (Oxydendrum arboreum) — prized Appalachian honey
- **Eucalyptus** — prolific in mild climates; medicinal honey
- **Manuka** (Leptospermum scoparium) — New Zealand; see
  [[honey-varieties-guide]]

Planting trees is a long-term investment but yields the highest forage
return per acre.

## Avoiding Toxic Plants

Some plants produce nectar or pollen toxic to bees. Avoid planting or be
aware of:
- California buckeye (Aesculus californica) — toxic pollen causes brood
  death
- Rhododendron/azalea — grayanotoxin in nectar ("mad honey")
- Mountain laurel (Kalmia latifolia) — toxic nectar
- Yellow jessamine (Gelsemium sempervirens) — toxic nectar and pollen
