# Solanaceous Hallucinogens: Datura, Mandrake, and Henbane

The Solanaceae (nightshade family) contains some of the most potent and historically significant hallucinogenic plants known to humanity. Documented extensively in [[hallucinogens-and-shamanism-harner-anthology]], these plants have been used in shamanism, witchcraft, medicine, and warfare across both the Old and New Worlds for thousands of years.

## Datura (Jimson Weed, Devil's Apple)

Datura is a genus of herbaceous plants and shrubs found worldwide in tropical and temperate regions. Known by a profusion of evocative common names — Jimson weed (a corruption of "Jamestown weed"), devil's apple, thorn apple, mad apple, devil's weed, Gabriel's trumpet, angel's trumpet — Datura has been used widely and apparently from ancient times in shamanism, witchcraft, and the vision quest in Europe, Asia, Africa, and among American Indian tribes.

All parts of the Datura plant contain tropane alkaloids, with the highest concentrations typically found in the seeds and flowers. The effects of Datura intoxication are notoriously powerful and potentially lethal. Physical symptoms include dryness of the mouth and throat, dilated pupils, blurred vision, rapid heartbeat, difficulty urinating, and elevated body temperature.

The mental effects are characterized by a distinctive combination of delirium and hallucination. Unlike the classical psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, mescaline), Datura alkaloids typically produce true hallucinations — imagery that the user cannot distinguish from reality — along with amnesia for the experience, confusion, and disorientation. The experience is frequently described as terrifying rather than pleasurable.

Among the Jivaro of the Ecuadorian Amazon, Datura arborea (known as maikua) is considered even more powerful than natem (ayahuasca). Jivaro shamans use it to see the wakani birds that send magical darts into victims of witchcraft, and to engage in tsentsak (magical dart) duels with enemy shamans.

The name "Jimson weed" itself derives from the Jamestown, Virginia colony, where in 1676 British soldiers sent to suppress Bacon's Rebellion mistakenly ate Datura stramonium in a salad. For eleven days they were reported to have gone "mad," exhibiting "very Perverse" behavior including kissing and pawing one another and embracing the ground.

## Mandrake (Mandragora)

[[mandrake-mandragora-officinarum-in-european-folklore]], the true mandrake, is perhaps the most legendary of all magical plants in European tradition. Its forked root, which often resembles a human form, gave rise to an elaborate body of folklore. The plant was believed to emit a lethal scream when pulled from the earth, and various magical procedures were prescribed for its safe harvesting — including tying a dog to the root and then calling the dog away.

The root contains the tropane alkaloids hyoscyamine, scopolamine, and atropine. In classical antiquity, mandrake was used as an anaesthetic and sedative. Dioscorides prescribed it for pain relief, and it was administered in wine before surgery. Theophrastus warned that harvesting mandrake required specific ritual precautions.

In medieval and early modern European magic, mandrake root served as a powerful amulet and was a standard ingredient in witches' flying ointments. Its humanoid shape made it a natural focus for sympathetic magic, and small roots (called "puppets" or "mandrake babes") were carried as talismans for wealth, fertility, and protection.

The mandrake's association with fertility and sexuality is ancient and pervasive. The Old Testament contains two references to mandrakes (dudaim) as aphrodisiacs. In Genesis 30:14-16, Rachel bargains with Leah for mandrakes found in the field, hoping they will make her fertile.

## Henbane (Hyoscyamus)

Henbane (Hyoscyamus niger) is a biennial plant native to Europe and Asia that has been used since antiquity for its psychoactive properties. The Oracle of Delphi may have used henbane, and the plant is mentioned in the works of Dioscorides, Pliny, and other classical authors.

Gustav Schenk's self-experiment with henbane seed smoke produced a vivid account of the characteristic solanaceous experience: "My teeth were clenched, and a dizzy rage took possession of me. I know that I trembled with horror; but I also know that I was permeated by a peculiar sense of well-being connected with the crazy sensation that my feet were growing lighter, expanding and breaking loose from my body."

The sensation of body dissolution — feeling one's feet grow lighter, parts of the body becoming independent, the head growing larger — is typical of henbane intoxication. This was counterbalanced by "an intoxicating sensation of flying" and visions of "clouds, the lowering sky, herds of beasts, falling leaves which were quite unlike any ordinary leaves, billowing streamers of steam and rivers of molten metal."

## Belladonna (Deadly Nightshade)

Atropa belladonna is perhaps the most toxic member of the family. Its name derives from the Italian "bella donna" ([[fukuoka-scalpel-beautiful-woman-science-nature]]), because women historically used preparations from the plant to dilate their pupils — a practice considered aesthetically desirable. The plant contains atropine, scopolamine, and hyoscyamine.

Belladonna was a primary ingredient in witches' flying ointments. The physician Andrés Laguna in 1545 identified belladonna among the "herbs cold and soporiferous in the ultimate degree" that composed the ointment confiscated from accused witches.

In the Roman Empire, belladonna was used as a poison. Livia, wife of Augustus, was said to have used it to eliminate potential rivals. The name Atropa derives from Atropos, one of the three Fates in Greek mythology who cuts the thread of life.

## Pharmacology: The Tropane Alkaloids

The common pharmacological thread connecting these plants is the tropane alkaloid group: atropine, hyoscyamine, and scopolamine (also called hyoscine). These are anticholinergic compounds — they block the action of acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors in the nervous system.

This pharmacological mechanism produces the characteristic syndrome of solanaceous intoxication: dry mucous membranes, dilated pupils, tachycardia, urinary retention, elevated body temperature, and the distinctive mental state of delirium with true hallucinations. Unlike the serotonergic psychedelics, the anticholinergic hallucinogens impair rather than enhance cognitive function.

A critical pharmacological property is skin absorbability. Atropine can be absorbed through intact skin, which is why the solanaceous plants were effective as topical ointments — a fact that explains the historical use of "flying ointments" applied to the body rather than ingested.

The toxicity of the tropane alkaloids is significant. Fatal doses are not greatly above effective doses, and the narrow therapeutic window means that the difference between a visionary experience and death can be very small. This undoubtedly contributed to the aura of danger and power surrounding these plants in folk tradition.

## Old World and New World Parallels

The solanaceous hallucinogens are one of the few groups of psychoactive plants used in shamanic contexts in both hemispheres. Datura species are used by indigenous peoples throughout the Americas — from the Jivaro of Ecuador to the Zuni of the American Southwest — as well as in India, China, and Africa. This cross-cultural distribution may reflect the plants' global range and their distinctive pharmacological properties.

# Latua pubiflora — Tree of Sorcerers

Latua pubiflora, known locally as "latué" or "árbol de los brujos" ("sorcerers' tree"), is one of the most enigmatic psychoactive plants of South America. Used exclusively by the Mapuche Indian medicine men of Valdivia, Chile, this plant produces powerful deliriant effects including visual hallucinations, disorientation, and in some cases permanent insanity. It is the only species in its genus and is endemic to a narrow strip of coastal mountains in central Chile, making it one of the most geographically restricted psychoactive plants known.

## Botanical Description

Latua pubiflora is a shrub or small tree belonging to the nightshade family (Solanaceae), the same family that contains [[belladonna-atropa-belladonna-ethnobotany]], and [[tree-daturas-brugmansia-south-american-ethnobotany]]), though the two genera are distinct.
