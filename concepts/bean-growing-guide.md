## Overview

Beans are one of the most productive and versatile crops in the home garden. They fix nitrogen from the air through a symbiotic relationship with [[rhizobia]] bacteria, improving soil fertility while producing abundant harvests. This guide covers pole vs bush beans, inoculation, [[succession-planting]], trellis systems, and drying beans for storage.

## Pole vs Bush Beans

### Bush Beans
- Compact, determinate growth habit — 12-24 inches tall
- No trellis or support required
- All beans mature within a 2-3 week window
- Easier for large plantings and mechanized harvest
- Good for containers and raised beds
- Total yield per plant is lower than pole beans
- Varieties: Blue Lake Bush, Provider, Contender, Roma II, Dragon Tongue
- Maturity: 50-65 days from sowing

### Pole Beans
- Indeterminate, vining habit — 6-10+ feet tall
- Require trellis, teepee, or other support
- Produce continuously over 6-8+ weeks
- Higher total yield per square foot of garden space
- Easier to harvest (standing up vs bending over)
- Longer harvest season
- Varieties: Blue Lake Pole, Kentucky Wonder, Fortex, Rattlesnake, Scarlet Runner, Trail of Tears
- Maturity: 60-75 days to first harvest, then continuous

### Choosing Between Them
- Small gardens with limited space: pole beans (vertical growing)
- Large beds or field planting: bush beans (no infrastructure needed)
- Extended harvest desired: pole beans
- Quick crop for succession: bush beans (faster maturity)
- Many gardeners grow both types

## Inoculation

Bean inoculation introduces rhizobia bacteria to the soil, enabling nitrogen fixation.

### Why Inoculate
- Rhizobia bacteria form nodules on bean roots
- Inside these nodules, bacteria convert atmospheric nitrogen (N2) into plant-available forms
- This reduces or eliminates the need for nitrogen fertilizer
- Plants grow more vigorously, especially in soils without prior legume history
- Inoculated beans leave behind nitrogen-enriched soil for the next crop

### How to Inoculate
- Purchase legume inoculant specific for beans (Phaseolus spp.) — different than pea/[[soybean]] inoculant
- Inoculant is a peat-based powder containing live [[rhizobium]] bacteria
- Moisten seeds lightly, then roll in inoculant powder until coated
- Plant immediately — bacteria die quickly when exposed to sun and air
- Store unused inoculant in a cool, dark place; it loses viability over time
- Inoculant is inexpensive and widely available from seed suppliers

### When Inoculation Is Most Important
- First-time bean planting in a garden bed
- Soils that have not grown legumes in 3+ years
- Sandy or low-organic-matter soils with poor microbial life
- Less critical in established gardens with regular legume rotation

## Planting

### Soil Requirements
- Well-draining soil, pH 6.0-6.8
- Beans do not need rich soil — excess nitrogen reduces nodulation
- Avoid planting in cold, wet soil — seeds rot easily
- [[soil-temperature]] must be at least 60F for reliable germination (70F ideal)

### Sowing
- Direct sow only — beans dislike transplanting
- Plant 1-2 inches deep, 2-4 inches apart in rows
- Row spacing: 18-24 inches for bush beans, 30-36 inches for pole beans with trellis
- Germination: 5-10 days in warm soil
- For pole beans on trellis: sow 3-4 seeds at the base of each support

### Seed Treatment
- Do not pre-soak seeds — they crack easily when over-soaked
- Some gardeners dust with inoculant only
- Treat seeds with [[diatomaceous-earth]] if seed corn maggots are a known pest

## Succession Planting

Succession planting extends the bean harvest over many weeks.

### Schedule
- Sow first crop after last frost when soil reaches 60F
- Sow subsequent crops every 2-3 weeks through mid-summer
- Stop sowing about 10-12 weeks before first fall frost (beans need time to mature)
- In warm climates with a long season, 4-6 successions are possible

### Tips
- Track sowing dates in a garden journal
- Later successions may face more pest pressure (Mexican bean beetles peak in mid-summer)
- Bush beans are better for succession than pole beans (faster maturity, defined harvest window)
- For continuous pole bean harvest, stagger planting on different trellis sections

## Trellis Types for Pole Beans

### Teepee Trellis
- 3-8 bamboo poles or saplings lashed together at the top
- Simple, inexpensive, and classic
- Good for small gardens and children's gardens
- Space poles 6-8 inches apart at the base, forming a circle 3-4 feet in diameter
- Plant 3-4 seeds around each pole

### A-Frame Trellis
- Two panels of wire mesh or lattice hinged at the top
- Sturdy, reusable year after year
- Allows planting on both sides
- Angle provides good sun exposure

### T-String Trellis
- Posts with horizontal wire at top, strings running down to each plant
- Efficient for row plantings
- Common in market gardens
- Strings can be composted at end of season

### Livestock Panel Trellis
- Cattle panels (16 feet long, 50 inches tall) supported by T-posts
- Very sturdy and long-lasting
- Multiple beans per panel
- Can be arched to create a tunnel

### Corn Stalks (Three Sisters)
- Pole beans climbing corn stalks as part of the traditional Three Sisters planting
- Plant beans 2-3 weeks after corn to avoid competition
- See [[food-forest-design]] for [[companion-planting]] concepts

## Drying Beans

Drying beans are left on the plant until fully mature and dry, then shelled and stored.

### Growing Dry Beans
- Most pole and bush bean varieties can be dried, but some are specifically bred for it
- Dry bean varieties: Black Turtle, Pinto, Navy (Pea Bean), Cranberry, Jacob's Cattle, Good Mother Stallard, Red Kidney, Anasazi
- Allow pods to dry completely on the plant (brown, brittle, rattling)
- If frost threatens before pods are dry, pull entire plants and hang indoors to finish drying

### Harvesting and Processing
- Shell beans by hand or thresh by beating pods in a bag
- Winnow to separate chaff (pour between bowls in a light breeze)
- Spread beans on screens to dry further for 1-2 weeks
- Beans are dry enough for storage when a thumbnail cannot dent them
