# Greenhouse Tomato Vertical Growing

The [[coleman-four-season-farm]] Farm uses an intensive vertical growing system for
greenhouse tomatoes that keeps plants in vigorous production from early
spring through late fall. The system combines standard commercial
tomato trellis equipment with a lowering-and-layering technique that
allows indeterminate vines to continue producing far beyond what
fixed-height supports would permit.

## Trellis System

Tomatoes are supported by plastic twine that unrolls from small spools
attached to wire frames. These wire frames hang from horizontal support
wires running the length of the greenhouse. This is standard equipment
in commercial tomato houses worldwide. The horizontal support wires
are positioned ideally about 8 feet above the ground.

## Single-Stem Pruning

All tomatoes are pruned to a single stem by removing all suckers that
develop between the leaf branch and the main stem. Commercial tomato
trellis clips secure the stem to the twine, with a clip placed about
every 12 inches as the plant stem elongates. This regular clipping
keeps the plant upright and properly aligned with the twine.

### Fruit Cluster Management

- **Beefsteak varieties:** Fruit clusters are limited to four fruits
  by pruning off the extras.
- **Medium-sized varieties:** Clusters are limited to five fruits.
- **Cherry tomatoes:** As soon as a cluster begins ripening its first
  fruit, any further blossoms at the end of the cluster are pinched
  off to direct energy into ripening existing fruit.

## The Lowering-and-Layering Technique

As the plants' growing tips reach the support wire (about 8 feet
above ground), the following sequence begins:

1. **Harvest and clean:** The lowest fruit has been harvested and the
   lower branches are removed up to the height of the lowest fruit
   cluster.

2. **Unroll twine:** One or two turns of twine are unrolled from each
   spool, lowering the top of each plant about 12 inches.

3. **Slide horizontally:** The wire frame holding the spool is moved
   along the support wire, shifting the tops horizontally.

4. **Curve around corners:** At the end of the row, plants are curved
   around the corner and over to the partner row that moves
   horizontally in the opposite direction. The paired rows resemble
   circular trolleys for moving clothes in large dry-cleaning
   establishments.

5. **Repeat:** This lowering and sideways movement is repeated every
   time the tops grow back up to the height of the support wire.

The bare lower stems end up lying along the ground. Some growers use
low wickets to keep stems from contacting the soil, but the Four
Season Farm has found this unnecessary. The top 8 feet of each vine
keeps producing tomatoes throughout the season. In this way, a crop
set out early in spring stays in vigorous production until late fall.

## Managing Timing and Soil Temperature

The largest greenhouse used for tomatoes at the farm is occupied all
winter by a mid-September-sown unheated spinach crop, providing four
to five harvests before the end of March. This creates a timing
challenge: how to transition from winter spinach to summer tomatoes.

### Spinach-to-Tomato Transition

The spinach crop is pulled in late March or early April, just before
it begins to go to seed. Later-planted spinach in another house
ensures uninterrupted production through spring. After pulling
spinach, beds are immediately refertilized with an inch of compost,
and tomato plants are set out on April 7.

To warm the soil before tomatoes go in, the inner covers protecting
the spinach are pulled back every sunny day during March to allow
direct solar heating. Once spinach is removed, a propane heater runs
for a few days before transplanting to prevent night temperatures
from falling below 60 degrees Fahrenheit.

### Alternative: Extended [[coleman-soil-preparation-for-winter]]

In another greenhouse, the previous crop is cleared a month or more
before tomatoes are needed. Wickets and row-cover inner layers are
removed, soil is prepared, and a sheet of clear plastic is laid
directly on the soil. This is the most effective way to trap incoming
solar heat in the soil. It also stimulates weed seed germination,
and the weed seedlings are flamed off before transplanting.

Using clear plastic laid on the soil as an extra-warm inner layer,
soil temperature at the 4-inch depth can reach 65 degrees Fahrenheit
with only solar heat. However, the income from the preceding spinach
crop more than makes up for the slightly slower tomato start.

## Tradeoffs

These are the sorts of choices a multiple-crop grower must constantly
make. The grower must weigh the value of continued winter spinach
harvest against the benefit of earlier soil warming for tomatoes. At
the Four Season Farm, the income from spinach has consistently been
judged worth the tradeoff of a slightly later tomato start.
