## Pattern Classification and Unified Event Taxonomy

## Overview

In [[bill-mollison]] as part of his [[general-pattern-model-events]], provides designers with a systematic way to analyze, compare, and understand the essential characteristics of any phenomenon. By classifying events across multiple dimensions, we gain insight into their nature, stage of development, dimensionality, and spatial relationship to their environment.

## The Classification System

All events are susceptible to classification across four primary dimensions: Nature, Stage, Dimension, and Location. Together, these create a shorthand annotation system for describing any phenomenon.

### A. Nature (What Kind of Process?)

Events are classified by their fundamental character:

- **A1: Explosive, disintegration, erosion, impact** — Destructive or dissipative processes. Examples: a landslide, a falling bomb, soil erosion, an explosion
- **A2: Grow, integration, construction, translation** — Creative or constructive processes. Examples: a growing sapling, building a dam, the formation of a coral reef
- **A3: Conceptual, idea, creative thought, insight** — Cognitive or informational processes. Examples: a design concept, a scientific insight, a cultural innovation

This tripartite division mirrors fundamental thermodynamic and ecological principles: energy can be dissipated, concentrated, or reorganized as information.

### B. Stage (Where in the Life Cycle?)

Every event exists at a particular stage in its life cycle:

- **B1: Potential only** — The event exists as latent possibility. Examples: an ungerminated seed, an unexplored idea, an unexploded bomb
- **B2: In process of evolution** — The event is actively developing. Examples: a growing tree, an ongoing conversation, a developing weather system
- **B3: Completed** — Growth and expansion have ceased; the event is fully articulated. Examples: a mature forest, a completed building, a finished composition
- **B4: Decaying** — The event is disintegrating, being replaced, or invaded by new events. Examples: a rotting log, a declining civilization, a dying star

This stage classification recognizes that nothing is static—all phenomena are in transition between states, and the designer must identify the current stage to intervene appropriately.

### C. Dimension (How Many Dimensions?)

Events are classified by their dimensional complexity:

- **C1: One dimension** — Linear phenomena, curves. Examples: a river section, a shoreline, a timeline
- **C2: Two dimensions** — Surface phenomena, tessellations, dendrites. Examples: a leaf surface, a stream network from above, a mosaic pattern
- **C3: Three dimensions** — Solid phenomena, trees. Examples: a tree in full form, a mountain, a [[crystal-structure-and-crystal-defects]]
- **C4: Four dimensions** — Moving solid phenomena, including the time dimension. Examples: a growing tree, a flowing river, a migrating herd
- **C5: More dimensions** — Conceptual phenomena, models of particles or forces, states of energy. Examples: quantum states, economic models, ecological relationships

This classification reflects the idea that phenomena exist at different levels of dimensional complexity, and that adding dimensions (especially time) fundamentally changes the nature of what is being described.

### D. Location (Where in Space?)

Events are classified by their spatial relationship to their environment:

- **D1: Generating across equi-potential surfaces** — Events forming along surfaces of equal energy or potential. Examples: storms at sea, weather fronts, geological fault lines
- **D2: Within media** — Events occurring within a continuous medium. Examples: weather "frontal" systems, ocean currents, sound waves in air
- **D3: Through surfaces at 90° or so** — Events penetrating boundaries approximately perpendicularly. Examples: trees growing upward through the ground surface, roots penetrating downward, rain falling through the atmosphere
- **D4: Englobements** — Events that envelop or contain other events. Examples: some explosions, organisms (which contain their own internal processes), planetary atmospheres
- **D5: An idea** — Events located outside normal dimensions of space-time. Examples: mathematical concepts, [[holmgren-ethical-principles-care-earth-people-fair-shares]], design intentions

## Applied Classification: Examples

Using this system, any event can be given a compact annotation:

| Event | Classification | Interpretation |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| A sapling | A2, B2, C3, D3 | Constructive process, actively evolving, three-dimensional solid, growing through a surface |
| A falling bomb | A1, B1, C4, D2 | Explosive/dissipative process, at potential stage, four-dimensional (moving), within medium |
| A mature forest | A2, B3, C3, D4 | Constructive process, completed stage, three-dimensional, englobing/containing |
| A design concept | A3, B1, C5, D5 | Conceptual process, at potential stage, multi-dimensional, located outside space-time |

## The Purpose of Classification

This classification system serves several important functions for [[permaculture]] designers:

### 1. Pattern Recognition

By training ourselves to classify events along these dimensions, we develop the ability to quickly recognize the essential nature of any phenomenon and identify appropriate design responses.

### 2. Comparative Analysis

Events with similar classifications may share [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]]. A dendritic stream network (C2, A2, D1) and a tree root system share the same dimensional and locational characteristics, suggesting similar design approaches for both.

### 3. Intervention Timing

Understanding the stage (B1–B4) of a system tells us when and how to intervene. Working with a system at B2 (evolving) requires different strategies than one at B4 (decaying).

### 4. Cross-Disciplinary Connection

The same classification system applies across disciplines—biology, geology, hydrology, sociology, and design—revealing deep structural similarities between apparently unrelated phenomena.

### 5. Design Documentation

The compact notation allows designers to efficiently record and communicate the essential characteristics of elements in a design, creating a shared [[pattern-language-for-permaculture-design]] practitioners.

## Philosophical Foundation

This classification system rests on the [[permaculture-principle-1-observe-interact]] that all phenomena are expressions of a single underlying pattern. By providing a universal taxonomy, it supports the view that:

- All events are connected through shared structural principles
- Design knowledge from one domain transfers to others
- Understanding the classification of an event reveals its relationships to other events
- The designer's task is to work with these natural orders rather than impose artificial ones

The system is deliberately simple—five categories with a few subcategories each—yet capable of describing the essential characteristics of any phenomenon the designer encounters.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.
