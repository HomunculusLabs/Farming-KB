# Permaculture Urban Design

Urban permaculture applies ecological design principles to cities, towns, and densely populated areas. Despite limited space, urban environments offer unique opportunities: high population density means many potential collaborators within walking distance, vertical surfaces add growing area, and the urban heat island extends the growing season. The challenge is to maximize productivity, biodiversity, and community connection within constrained spaces.

## Balcony Gardens

Even a small balcony can produce a meaningful amount of food and serve as a habitat for pollinators.

### Design Principles
- **Vertical growing** — trellises, hanging baskets, wall-mounted planters, stackable containers
- **Microclimate awareness** — balconies have specific sun exposure, wind patterns, and temperature extremes
- **Water efficiency** — self-watering containers, drip irrigation from a single tap
- **Season extension** — cold frames, row covers, and shade cloth extend the growing season

### Productive Balcony Plants
- **Herbs** — basil, mint, rosemary, thyme, cilantro, parsley
- **Salad greens** — lettuce, spinach, arugula, Asian greens (cut-and-come-again harvesting)
- **Tomatoes and peppers** — compact or determinate varieties in 5+ gallon containers
- **Strawberries** — hanging baskets or stackable planters
- **Beans and peas** — climbing varieties on trellises against walls
- **Microgreens** — extremely productive per square foot, grown in shallow trays year-round

### Container Considerations
- Use containers with drainage holes; add a layer of gravel or perlite at the bottom
- Lightweight potting mixes reduce weight load on balconies
- Self-watering containers (sub-irrigated planters) reduce maintenance
- Mulch containers to retain moisture and reduce watering frequency

## Rooftop Farms

Rooftop agriculture transforms underutilized urban space into productive growing areas. Green roofs also provide insulation, stormwater management, and habitat.

### Structural Considerations
- **Load capacity** — consult a structural engineer; saturated soil and plant weight can exceed 100 lbs per square foot
- **Waterproofing** — a durable waterproof membrane is essential to prevent leaks
- **Wind exposure** — rooftops are windier than ground level; use windbreaks and secure containers
- **Access** — stairs, freight elevators, or hoist systems for moving materials and harvests

### Growing Systems
- **Container systems** — modular planters, fabric grow bags, or custom-built raised beds
- **Green roof systems** — extensive (shallow substrate, sedums, low maintenance) or intensive (deeper soil, food crops, higher maintenance)
- **Hydroponics** — water-based growing systems for high productivity per square foot
- **Aquaponics** — integrated fish and plant systems

### Yields
Well-managed rooftop farms can produce 5-15 pounds of vegetables per square foot per year. Even a modest 500 square foot rooftop can produce thousands of pounds of food annually.

## Community Gardens

Community gardens are shared growing spaces where individuals or groups cultivate plots of land, typically on vacant lots, park land, or institutional grounds.

### Organizational Models
- **Allotment model** — individual plots assigned to gardeners who manage their own space
- **Communal model** — shared garden managed collectively by a group
- **Hybrid model** — combination of individual plots and shared areas (orchard, herb garden, gathering space)

### Permaculture Features in Community Gardens
- Composting stations for organic waste from plots and surrounding community
- Rainwater harvesting from nearby buildings
- Fruit trees and berry bushes in shared areas
- Pollinator habitat (flower borders, insect hotels, native plant areas)
- Children's garden areas for education
- Tool libraries and shared infrastructure
- Social gathering spaces (seating areas, fire pits, meeting spaces)

### Challenges
- Land tenure insecurity — many community gardens occupy land that could be developed
- Soil contamination — urban soils may contain lead, arsenic, or other pollutants; test before planting
- Water access — some sites lack water connections; rainwater harvesting addresses this
- Social dynamics — conflict resolution and inclusive governance are ongoing needs

## Food Forests in Cities

Urban food forests are multi-layered perennial polycultures that produce food while providing ecosystem services in urban environments.

### Design Considerations
- **Species selection** — choose compact, disease-resistant varieties suited to urban conditions
- **Pollination** — ensure pollinator partners are planted together; urban environments may have limited pollinator populations
- **Access** — design paths for harvesting, maintenance, and public access
- **Safety** — avoid thorny or toxic plants in public spaces; consider visibility for security
- **Soil** — imported soil and compost may be needed on degraded urban sites
- **Canopy management** — maintain visibility and safety by keeping trees pruned to appropriate heights

### Notable Examples
- **Beacon Food Forest** (Seattle, WA) — 7-acre public food forest with edible plants, fruit trees, and community space
- **Jude's Urban Food Forest** — various models integrating food production into urban park design
- **Smaller-scale urban food forests** — backyard food forests, street tree corridors with edible understory, church and school grounds

## Vertical Growing

Vertical growing maximizes production per square foot by utilizing walls, fences, trellises, and stacked systems.

### Techniques
- **Trellising** — train climbing vegetables (beans, cucumbers, tomatoes, indeterminate varieties) on vertical supports
- **Living walls** — planted panels attached to walls, either hydroponic or soil-based
- **Window farming** — small-scale hydroponic systems in windows
- **Espalier** — training fruit trees flat against walls or fences
- **Stacked container gardens** — tiered planters, strawberry towers, potato boxes

### Benefits
- Dramatically increases growing area per ground square foot
- Improves air circulation (reduces disease pressure)
- Easier harvesting (less bending)
- Creates microclimates (warmth from sun-heated walls)
- Provides privacy screening and noise reduction

## Water Management in Urban Settings

Urban permaculture prioritizes capturing and cycling water on-site:
- Rain barrels and cisterns connected to downspouts
- Rain gardens in low spots to absorb runoff
- Greywater systems (where legally permitted) from sinks and showers
- Drip irrigation from harvested water
- Permeable surfaces (gravel, permeable pavers) to reduce runoff

See [[water-management]] for detailed water strategies.

## Urban Livestock

Small-scale animal keeping in cities is possible and increasingly legal:
- Chickens (2-6 hens) — most common urban livestock; check local ordinances
- Ducks — sometimes permitted where chickens are; need water access
- Bees — rooftop or backyard hives; provide pollination and honey
- Rabbits — quiet, space-efficient meat production
- Quail — very small space requirements; eggs and meat
