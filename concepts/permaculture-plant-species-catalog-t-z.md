## Permaculture Plant Species Catalog: T through Z

This catalog represents the final section of [[bill-mollison]]'s comprehensive plant index from *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, covering useful species from the letters T through Z. These species span food crops, nitrogen fixers, fiber plants, medicinal herbs, and ecological support species essential to [[permaculture-design]] systems.

## Timber and Forestry Species

### Teak (*Tectona grandis*)
Teak is one of the world's most valuable tropical hardwoods, prized for its durability, water resistance, and natural oils that resist insects and rot. In permaculture systems, teak serves as a long-term investment in forest systems, providing high-value timber while contributing to canopy cover and microclimate regulation. It is best suited to tropical and subtropical zones and requires well-drained soils.

### Tipuana Tipu (*Tipuana tipu*)
Also known as rosewood, Tipuana is a fast-growing shade tree native to South America. It is widely planted as an ornamental and street tree, producing abundant shade and fixing nitrogen through its leguminous root system. Its rapid growth makes it useful for establishing quick canopy in new permaculture systems.

### Yucca (*Yucca spp.*)
Yuccas are drought-tolerant succulents that serve multiple functions in dryland permaculture systems. They provide fiber for rope and basketry, edible flowers and fruits in some species, and serve as effective barrier plants due to their sharp leaf tips. They are exceptionally hardy and require minimal water once established.

## Food Crops

### Corn/Maize (*Zea mays*)
One of the world's three most important cereal crops, maize is a warm-season grass that produces grain, fodder, and stover (stalk residue). In permaculture, maize is often integrated into polyculture systems such as the Three Sisters (corn, beans, squash) where it provides structural support for climbing beans while the beans fix nitrogen and squash suppresses weeds. Maize is a heavy feeder and benefits from companion planting with legumes.

### Wheat (*Triticum spp.*, *T. aestivum*)
Wheat is the most widely grown cereal crop globally and a cool-season annual grass. In permaculture design, wheat can be integrated into rotation systems with legumes to maintain soil fertility. While large-scale monoculture wheat production is unsustainable, small-scale and no-till wheat production within diverse polycultures can be productive and soil-building.

### Cocoa (*Theobroma cacao*)
Cacao is an understory tropical tree that produces the beans from which chocolate and cocoa products are derived. It naturally grows beneath taller canopy trees, making it ideal for agroforestry systems and food forest designs. Cacao requires consistent moisture, good drainage, and partial shade — conditions that mature food forests naturally provide.

### Grape (*Vitis vinifera*)
The common grape vine is a deciduous climbing plant that produces fruit for fresh eating, drying (raisins), and winemaking. In permaculture systems, grapes can be trained along pergolas, fences, and building facades, providing both food production [[mollison-designers-solar-chimneys-and-passive-cooling-in-desert-housing]] through summer shade. Their deep root systems help break up subsoil layers.

### Mung Bean (*Vigna radiata*)
Mung beans are fast-growing warm-season legumes that produce edible seeds and sprouts. As nitrogen fixers, they [[query-how-does-rock-dust-improve-soil-fertility]] while providing a high-protein food crop. Their short growing season makes them excellent for catch cropping and filling gaps in seasonal planting schedules.

### Cowpea (*Vigna sinensis*)
Also known as black-eyed pea, cowpeas are drought-tolerant legumes that thrive in hot conditions. They serve dual purposes as food crops and [[green-manure-cover-crops]], producing edible pods and seeds while fixing atmospheric nitrogen. They are particularly valuable in tropical and subtropical systems for soil improvement between main crops.

### Fava/Broad Bean (*Vicia faba*)
Fava beans are cool-season legumes that are among the most effective nitrogen fixers, capable of fixing up to 200 kg of nitrogen per hectare. They produce edible beans, can be used as a cover crop, and their deep taproots help break up compacted soils. Fava beans are one of the few legumes that thrive in cool, wet conditions.

### Wild Rice (*Zizania lacustris*)
Wild rice is an aquatic grass native to North America that grows in shallow lakes and slow-moving streams. Unlike cultivated rice, it is a perennial that reseeds itself naturally. It provides high-value food for humans and wildlife, and its presence in water systems indicates good water quality. In permaculture aquaculture designs, wild rice can be integrated into pond edges and slow-water zones.

### Indian Water Chestnut (*Trapa spp. natans*)
Water chestnuts are aquatic plants that produce edible corms beneath the water surface. They are excellent additions to aquaculture and pond systems in permaculture designs, providing food production while filtering water and creating habitat for fish and other aquatic organisms.

## Leguminous and Nitrogen-Fixing Species

### Tephrosia (*Tephrosia candida*)
Tephrosia is a fast-growing leguminous shrub widely used in tropical permaculture systems as a green manure and mulch crop. Its leaves contain rotenone, a natural insecticide, making it useful as a biological pest control when incorporated into mulch or compost teas. It is commonly planted in alley cropping systems to improve soil fertility between rows of fruit or timber trees.

### Clover (*Trifolium spp.*)
Clovers are versatile leguminous ground covers that fix nitrogen, suppress weeds, and provide excellent forage for livestock and pollinators. White clover (*T. repens*) is one of the most widely used living mulch plants in permaculture, forming a dense mat beneath taller crops. Kenya white clover (*T. semipilosum*) is adapted to warmer tropical conditions.

### Fenugreek (*Trigonella foenum-graecum*)
Fenugreek is an annual legume cultivated for its aromatic seeds and edible leaves, used extensively in cooking and traditional medicine. As a nitrogen fixer, it contributes to soil fertility while providing a culinary crop. Its leaves can be harvested repeatedly as a cut-and-come-again green.

### Vetch (*Vicia spp.*)
Vetches are vigorous climbing or trailing legumes used extensively as [[cover-crops]]. They are among the most effective cool-season nitrogen fixers and are often planted in fallow periods to protect and enrich soil. Common vetch and hairy vetch are particularly valuable in temperate permaculture rotations.

## Pasture and Forage Grasses

### Kangaroo Grass (*Themeda australis*)
Kangaroo grass is a native Australian perennial tussock grass that was once the dominant grass species across much of the continent. It provides excellent forage for livestock, deep root systems that prevent erosion, and habitat for wildlife. In permaculture grazing systems, it serves as a drought-tolerant, low-maintenance pasture species that requires no fertilizer inputs once established.

### Vetiver Grass (*Vetiveria zizanoides*)
Vetiver grass is one of the most important soil conservation plants in permaculture. Its exceptionally deep root system (reaching 3-4 meters) binds soil on steep slopes and embankments, preventing erosion. Vetiver hedges planted along contours act as living barriers that slow water runoff, trap sediment, and filter pollutants from agricultural runoff. The roots also produce vetiver oil, used in perfumery and insect repellent.

## Medicinal and Culinary Herbs

### Thyme (*Thymus spp.*, *T. vulgaris*)
Thyme is a low-growing Mediterranean herb that serves multiple functions in permaculture systems. As a culinary herb, it is widely used in cooking. As a ground cover, its dense mat suppresses weeds and retains soil moisture. Its aromatic flowers are highly attractive to beneficial insects, particularly predatory wasps that control pest populations in nearby crops.

### Dandelion (*Taraxacum officinale*)
Often dismissed as a weed, dandelion is one of the most useful plants in permaculture. Its deep taproot breaks up compacted soils and mines minerals from deep subsoil layers, bringing them to the surface where they become available to other plants. All parts are edible — leaves for salads, flowers for wine and fritters, roots for coffee substitute and medicine. It is also an early nectar source for bees and other pollinators.

### Nettle (*Urtica dioica*)
Stinging nettle is a nutrient-dense perennial that indicates rich soil and serves many functions. It is one of the best [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]], concentrating nitrogen, iron, calcium, and other minerals in its leaves. Nettle tea is an excellent liquid fertilizer, and the plant supports over 40 species of insect, including many beneficial predatory species. Young leaves are edible when cooked and are among the most nutritious wild greens available.

### Ginger (*Zingiber officinale*)
Ginger is a tropical rhizomatous herb prized for its culinary and medicinal rhizomes. In permaculture food forests, ginger thrives in the understory layer beneath taller canopy species, requiring partial shade, consistent moisture, and rich, well-drained soil. It is an excellent high-value crop for diversified tropical food production systems.

### Violet (*Viola odorata*)
Sweet violet is a low-growing perennial that produces edible flowers and leaves with a delicate fragrance. It thrives in partial shade and moist conditions, making it ideal for the understory of food forests and as a living mulch beneath fruit trees. Violets bloom early in spring, providing critical early nectar for emerging pollinators.

## Ecological Support Species

### Tamarisk (*Tamarix apetala* + spp.)
Tamarisk is a drought-tolerant shrub or small tree that thrives in saline and alkaline soils where few other species survive. While it has become invasive in some arid regions due to its high water consumption, in controlled permaculture applications it can be useful for revegetating saline wastelands, providing windbreaks in desert-edge environments, and producing fuelwood where other trees cannot grow.

### Seagrass (*Zostera spp.*)
Seagrasses are submerged marine angiosperms that form extensive underwater meadows in shallow coastal waters. They are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth, sequestering carbon at rates up to 35 times faster than tropical rainforests. Seagrass beds stabilize coastlines, improve water quality, provide nursery habitat for commercially important fish and shellfish species, and are a critical component of healthy marine ecosystems referenced in permaculture coastal zone design.

### Blueberry and Cranberry (*Vaccinium spp.*)
Vaccinium species are acid-loving shrubs that produce nutrient-dense berries. Blueberries (*V. spp.*) and cranberries (*V. macrocarpon* + spp.) thrive in the acidic, organic-rich soils that permaculture systems can create through mulching with pine needles, peat, or other acidic amendments. They are excellent understory crops for food forest designs in temperate regions, providing high-value fruit while supporting wildlife habitat.

### Wandering Jew (*Tradescantia albiflora*)
Wandering Jew is a fast-spreading ground cover that is useful in permaculture systems for rapid site stabilization and erosion control on disturbed soils. Its ability to root from any stem node allows it to quickly cover bare ground, reducing weed establishment and protecting soil from erosion. It also serves as a nursery ground cover, preparing sites for more permanent planting.

### Bower Vine (*Tetragonia implexicona*)
Bower vine is a vigorous climbing plant useful in permaculture for creating living screens, providing shade on pergolas, and adding vertical green infrastructure to garden designs. As a climbing species, it occupies the vertical layer of a [[food-forest-design]] without competing for ground-level space with other crops.

## Design Considerations for Plant Selection

The species catalog in the Designers' Manual reflects Mollison's principle that a successful permaculture system requires a diverse palette of functional plants. Each species listed serves one or more of the following functions:

- **Food production** — direct human yields of fruit, grain, vegetables, nuts, or herbs
- **Soil building** — nitrogen fixation, dynamic accumulation of minerals, organic matter production
- **Erosion control** — deep root systems, ground cover, slope stabilization
- **Pest management** — attracting beneficial insects, producing natural insecticides, providing habitat for predators
- **Microclimate modification** — shade, windbreaks, humidity regulation
- **Water management** — filtering runoff, stabilizing waterways, reducing evaporation
- **Fiber and materials** — timber, rope, basketry, construction materials
- **Wildlife habitat** — food and shelter for birds, insects, and other organisms

Effective permaculture design selects species not in isolation but in functional guilds — groups of plants that support each other through nutrient cycling, pest protection, and microclimate creation. The goal is to maximize functional connections between elements, not simply to maximize the number of species.
