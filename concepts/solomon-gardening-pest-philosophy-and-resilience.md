## Gardening Pest Philosophy and Resilience

Before reaching for any pesticide, natural or synthetic, Steve Solomon asks gardeners to reconsider their fundamental relationship with insect pests and plant diseases. His approach, which he calls "gardening aikido," treats pest problems as diagnostic information rather than enemies to be destroyed. This philosophical framework has practical implications for how much time, money, and emotional energy a gardener invests in pest control.

## Gardening Aikido

Solomon and his wife Muriel apply a principle from their relationship to gardening: when something is irritating, the irritation is an opportunity to examine what within yourself might be contributing to the problem. Applied to pests, this means asking whether the struggling plant is simply not growing fast enough to overcome the problem. The question shifts from "how do I kill this pest?" to "what is making this plant vulnerable?"

## Accepting Weather-Related Losses

Even with ideal soil and perfect technique, some years the weather will defeat you. Extended cool, humid, damp conditions can trigger blight that blackens entire crops. No amount of intervention can prevent this. Solomon recalls one miserable summer when continuous cool, humid days caused late blight to blacken all of Cascadia"s tomato vines. The entire crop from Canada to northern California died. Such events are beyond the gardener's control.

Acceptance is not resignation; it is wisdom. A diverse garden that contains crops preferring both hotter and cooler conditions will always produce something, even in a bad year. Growing at least some really hardy vegetables almost certainly guarantees that something edible will survive even the worst weather.

## Urban Versus Rural Pest Pressure

Urban gardeners and those living in prosperous agricultural districts inevitably face more pest problems. Where the surrounding landscape is dominated by a single crop species, pests of that crop reach plague proportions. Where diverse wild habitats support stable predator populations, pest problems are naturally moderated. This is worth considering when choosing a homesite, though most gardeners do not have the luxury of choosing their location based on pest ecology.

The contrast between Solomon's experience in Oregon's Willamette Valley, where brassica pests were overwhelming due to intensive seed crop production, and his later experience in Tasmania, where the same pests were absent, illustrates how dramatically surrounding land use affects garden pest pressure. Gardeners in areas of high agricultural monoculture should expect more problems and plan accordingly with row covers, beneficial habitat, and extra plantings.
