# DMT-Containing Plants Growing in the United States

## Overview

N,N-Dimethyltryptamine (DMT) is a powerful hallucinogenic tryptamine
found in numerous plant species worldwide.
While DMT is most famously associated with the Amazonian brew ayahuasca,
several plant species containing appreciable amounts of DMT grow naturally
throughout the United States.
These include common grasses, legumes, and other widespread plants
found in lawns, fallow fields, and gardens across the country.
The easy access to these plants, combined with information online
about extraction methods, has raised public health and regulatory concerns.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

DMT is a tryptamine derivative with the molecular formula C12H16N2.
It acts primarily as a serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonist,
producing profound alterations in perception, mood, and cognition.
When smoked or vaporized, DMT produces extremely rapid onset,
with peak intoxication within 2-5 minutes resolving over 20-30 minutes.
This short duration led to it being called the "businessman's trip" in the 1960s.
Effective smoked doses range from 40-50 mg, with reports from 20 to 100 mg.

DMT is not orally active because monoamine oxidase (MAO) enzymes
in the gut and liver rapidly break it down.
For oral activity, DMT must be co-administered with a reversible MAO-A inhibitor,
as in ayahuasca.
The intravenous effective dosage range is 0.1-0.4 mg/kg;
0.6 mg/kg was found excessive in Strassman's research.
DMT is physiologically non-addictive and structurally similar to serotonin and psilocin.

## DMT Sources in the United States

### Phalaris Species (Canary Grass)

Perhaps the most ubiquitous DMT source in the US,
Phalaris species include Phalaris arundinacea (reed canary grass),
Phalaris tuberosa, and Phalaris aquatica.
These grasses are found throughout the entire United States
in fallow fields, cracks in sidewalks, lawns, pastures, and disturbed areas.
DMT content is highly variable depending on strain, growing conditions, and harvest time.
Some high-percentage strains can be obtained through specialty suppliers.

Research by Barker and Hovin (1974) confirmed that alkaloid concentration
in reed canary grass is a heritable trait,
suggesting selective breeding could produce strains
with consistently higher or lower alkaloid content.
Simple juice extraction methods can obtain appreciable amounts of DMT
from these common grasses.

### Desmanthus illinoensis (Prairie Bundleflower)

Desmanthus illinoensis is a prairie legume with wide distribution
across the American Midwest and Great Plains.
The root bark contains approximately 0.34% DMT by dry weight,
making it a relatively concentrated natural source.
It is a member of the Fabaceae (legume) family
and is sometimes grown as a forage crop or ornamental plant.
Unlike Phalaris grasses, Desmanthus concentrates its alkaloids
in the root system rather than above-ground foliage.

## Ayahuasca Analogs Using US Plants

### The MAOI Requirement

For DMT to be orally active, it must be combined with a reversible MAO-A inhibitor.
The traditional Amazonian preparation uses Banisteriopsis caapi,
but several alternatives grow within the United States.

### Peganum harmala (Syrian Rue)

Peganum harmala was introduced into the western United States
and has naturalized in the wild.
It contains 2-4% MAOI content by dry weight
as beta-carboline alkaloids (harmine and harmaline),
making it an effective substitute for Banisteriopsis caapi in analog preparations.
The seeds are the primary plant part used,
and Peganum harmala has become invasive in some western states.

### Passiflora incarnata (Passionflower)

Passiflora incarnata grows throughout the southeastern United States
and much of the Midwest.
It contains approximately 1% MAOI content
and bears an edible fruit (passionfruit).
While its MAOI content is lower than Peganum harmala,
it has been explored in non-traditional ayahuasca-like preparations.

### Non-Traditional Brews

Non-traditional boiled brews combining DMT-containing US plants
with locally available MAOI plants are not merely theoretical.
Recipes can be found online, and self-reports confirm
they produce experiences similar to traditional ayahuasca.
These carry the same risks, including dangerous interactions with SSRIs.

## Traditional Ayahuasca Context

Indigenous peoples of the Amazon have used DMT
for spiritual and medicinal purposes for thousands of years.
The traditional brew combines Psychotria viridis leaves
with the Banisteriopsis caapi vine.
An average 100 mL dose contains approximately 24 mg DMT,
107 mg tetrahydroharmine, 20 mg harmaline, and 170 mg harmine.
Oral ayahuasca produces a 3-4 hour intoxication
peaking within the first hour, with nausea and vomiting routinely following.
The beta-carboline alkaloids themselves
are also reported to have mild sedative and hallucinogenic properties.

## Religious and Legal Context

Several syncretic religions originating in Brazil use ayahuasca as a sacrament,
notably the Santo Daime and the Uniao do Vegetal (UDV).
The UDV has been in federal litigation in the US seeking protection,
achieving preliminary injunctions barring DEA enforcement.
DMT is a Schedule I controlled substance, but the plants themselves
are generally not scheduled, creating a complex legal situation.
The plants are legal to grow and possess,
but extracting DMT constitutes manufacture of a controlled substance.

## Safety Considerations

DMT itself is physiologically non-addictive and does not produce dependence.
However, the MAOIs used in orally active preparations carry significant risks,
particularly dangerous interactions with SSRIs leading to serotonin syndrome.
MAOIs also interact dangerously with tyramine-containing foods
and certain medications.
A pilot survey of UDV members in Brazil
found no adverse health consequences from regular ayahuasca use.
