# Open-Air Mushroom Culture and Compost Selection

## Overview

While most commercial mushroom production takes place in controlled environments such as caves, cellars, and purpose-built houses, open-air culture has been practiced with varying degrees of success in regions with mild climates. Simultaneously, the selection and preparation of compost — the nutrient substratum on which mushrooms grow — is perhaps the single most important factor in successful cultivation.

## Open-Air Culture

### English Experience

In Southern England, where winter temperature extremes are less severe, mushrooms have been grown successfully in modified hot beds or cold frames. Simple culture frames of the usual type are employed, but without glass. Instead, they are covered with sheet iron or boards, with a final layer of straw placed on top if necessary. Fresh manure may be placed under the compost to provide bottom heat and encourage early spawn growth. This method has often proven very successful in the English climate.

### Limitations

Open-air mushroom culture cannot be regarded as having great possibilities of success in most regions. Several fundamental difficulties exist:

- **Temperature variability**: Commercial mushroom growing demands a relatively uniform temperature, and daily temperature variation outdoors is usually considerable.
- **Drying winds**: Wind exposure can rapidly desiccate mushroom beds, disrupting the moisture balance essential for mycelial growth and fruiting.
- **Moisture regulation**: It is difficult to maintain the consistently high humidity and proper moisture content in the compost that indoor environments provide.

### Requirements for Success

Under the most favorable conditions, open-air beds would need to be deeper than those made indoors, with fresh manure at the bottom to maintain bottom heat. Mulching with clean straw is required, and cloth covers may be desirable. The specific covers needed would be determined by the daily range of temperature in the region.

### California Potential

Certain sections of California may offer suitable conditions for open-air mushroom culture. Both *[[agaricus-campestris]]* and *A. arvensis* are found naturally in parts of California during winter months. The mean monthly temperature of San Francisco and an important section of the state is normally 50 to 60 degrees F, with a relatively modest daily range. Based on English experience, a mean temperature slightly below the ideal — approximately 48 to 56 degrees F — is actually preferable. Open-air beds must, of course, be protected from drenching rains.


### Field and Pasture Spawning

Inquiries are frequently received about spawning pastures and lawns to establish natural cultures that would increase the "spontaneous" autumn supply. Experiments along these lines have generally shown no permanent success under a variety of conditions. Communication with persons reported successful typically reveals that results depend entirely on the season and are wholly inconsistent. English correspondents report similar findings for this natural or field culture approach. At present, it cannot be depended upon.

Nevertheless, the labor involved in spreading spawn about lawns or pastures is minimal, and in favorable seasons one may be well repaid for the effort. The same approach could be tried with other edible varieties suited to the conditions.



## Compost Selection



### Stable Manure as the Essential Substratum

There is no known substitute for stable manure as a compost for commercial mushroom production. Wherever *Agaricus campestris* and related species are cultivated, stable manure is the nutrient substratum employed. The best results are obtained only when manure is hauled fresh from the stables and permitted to undergo a specific type of fermentation.



### Quality Criteria

Only the highest quality manure should be selected for mushroom work:

**Bedding material**: Considerable bedding straw should be present with the manure. Good straw is not likely to be present in excessive quantities, but even a small amount of "weeds" (weed seeds mixed with bedding) is a nuisance. The straw of various grains possesses distinct advantages over other grasses, apparently due to a certain resistance to complete fermentation or decay, yielding a highly porous substratum that maintains excellent physical condition in the beds.

**Substitute bedding materials**: Sawdust and shavings are increasingly used as bedding substitutes as straw becomes more expensive and less available. These do not render the manure unfit for mushroom work, and compost containing these substances is extensively used. However, they may affect the physical properties of the finished compost.

**Materials to avoid**: Manure from veterinary hospitals or from stables that freely use disinfectants should be avoided. The chemicals in disinfectants may inhibit mycelial growth or introduce contaminants.



### Short and Trashy Compost

Compost made from refuse hay, weeds, or similar short-fiber bedding materials tends to be "short" and trashy after fermentation. Experience clearly indicates that such compost does not make the best substratum for mushrooms. It is probably less retentive of moisture and may lack the structural porosity needed for optimal [[mycelial-colonization]].



### Animal Diet Considerations

The diet of the animals producing the manure affects its quality for [[mushroom-cultivation]]:

- **Grass-fed animals**: Manure from animals fed largely on grass has not proven satisfactory in tests that have been conducted.
- **Leguminous fodders**: French experience rules out manure from stables employing chiefly leguminous fodders.
- **Grain-fed animals with rye straw**: The French ideal is manure from grain-fed animals bedded with rye straw, which produces compost of the highest quality for mushroom cultivation.



### The Growing Scarcity of Straw Manure

Unfortunately, it is becoming increasingly difficult in many cities to obtain straw manure, as animals are bedded less and less with straw. Sawdust and shavings, being cheaper substitutes, are now commonly used. This trend has implications for the long-term availability of ideal [[mushroom-compost]], though the industry has adapted to using available materials.
