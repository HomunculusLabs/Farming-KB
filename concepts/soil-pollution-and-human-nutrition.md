# Soil Pollution and Human Nutrition

The integrity of soils and waters is foundational to human health. In permaculture design, understanding the legacy of chemical pollution in agricultural landscapes and its direct consequences for nutrition is essential for making responsible land-use decisions. This concept page addresses soil rehabilitation timelines, major categories of soil and water pollutants, and the critical linkages between soil health, food quality, and human nutrition as outlined in Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*.

## Soil Rehabilitation Timelines

Soil recovery from damage or neglect follows predictable but often lengthy timescales depending on the severity and nature of the degradation:

- **Garden-scale rehabilitation**: 3–4 years of consistent organic management can restore a reasonably maintained garden soil to productive health.
- **Devastated soil landscapes**: Full recovery of badly degraded land requires 5–15 years of active rehabilitation work including cover cropping, mulching, and biological inoculation.
- **Chemically polluted soils**: Rehabilitation times are significantly longer and in many cases may be effectively impossible within a human lifetime. Persistent organic pollutants and heavy metals do not simply degrade or leach away — they must be managed through land-use change rather than "remediation" in the traditional sense.

These timelines underscore the permaculture principle of protracted and thoughtful observation: understanding a site's pollution history before committing to food-production goals is critical.

## Pollution of Soils and Waters

### Agricultural Chemical Legacy

Intensive monoculture systems — including orchards, sugarcane, pineapple, cotton, tobacco, and banana plantations — have historically relied on what Mollison describes as an "orgy of mineral additives, arsenicals, Aldrin, DDT, copper salts, and dioxins." The cumulative effect of decades of these applications has left vast areas of agricultural land contaminated with persistent organic pollutants (POPs) that resist biological breakdown and remain hazardous for decades after application ceases.

### Persistent Biocides: DDT, Aldrin, Dieldrin, BHC

Organochlorine insecticides are among the most problematic legacy contaminants in agriculture:

- **Long-term persistence**: Apple orchards in West Australia that ceased chemical use 18–20 years ago still produce unsafe levels of Aldrin and Dieldrin in free-range chicken eggs, demonstrating bioaccumulation through food chains.
- **Cross-contamination**: Attempts at prawn farming on former agricultural land failed due to residual Dieldrin in the soil, showing that chemical residues affect not just terrestrial food production but aquatic systems as well.
- **Land-use imperative**: Old orchards, cane fields, and plantation sites contaminated with persistent biocides must be directed to **forestry** rather than food production. Trees can serve as biological stabilisers and long-term sinks for contaminants without introducing them into human food chains.

### Lead Contamination in Urban Soils

Lead from historical car exhaust emissions and lead-based paint has accumulated to levels of up to **1,100 ppm** in urban soils — far exceeding safe thresholds for food production. Remediation strategies for lead-contaminated urban sites include:

- Constructing a base layer of **cracked bricks or gravel** to create a physical barrier between contaminated native soil and growing media.
- Building **raised beds at least 30 cm deep** filled with clean imported soil.
- Incorporating **40% or more organic compost** into growing media, which can help immobilise lead through chelation and reduce plant uptake.
- Conducting **leaf tissue analysis** regularly to monitor for lead uptake in food crops.
- Using **vinegar washes** on harvested leafy greens and root vegetables as a final precautionary measure.

### Heavy Metals in Pasture and Crop Systems

- **Cadmium and uranium**: Market gardens using phosphate fertilisers have contributed to cadmium and uranium accumulation in soils, posing long-term risks to food safety.
- **Copper toxicity**: Copper levels reaching 50 ppm in pasture soils become poisonous to sheep, causing toxaemic jaundice. This is particularly relevant where copper-based fungicides have been applied intensively.
- **Copper buffering**: Molybdenum, zinc, and sulphur in the soil can buffer copper uptake by plants and animals, providing a natural mechanism for managing copper toxicity in mixed pasture systems.

### Groundwater Pollution

Beneath conventional farms, **80–90 distinct biocidal substances** have been detected in groundwater. Industrial areas add further contamination including dioxins, radioactive materials, and heavy metals. The implications are severe: once groundwater is contaminated, purification is extraordinarily difficult and expensive. Mollison indicates that **rainwater harvesting and strict water conservation** are essential strategies for permaculture sites, reducing dependence on potentially compromised aquifer and municipal water supplies.

## Human Health and Nutrition

Mollison identifies four broad statements that frame the relationship between food systems and human nutritional health:

### 1. The Value of Dietary Diversity

A **mixed omnivorous diet** has been exhibited by most human groups throughout history. Excessive dietary simplicity — reliance on a narrow range of foods — carries significant nutritional dangers. Permaculture design inherently promotes diversity through polycultures, integrated food systems, and seasonal eating patterns that mirror natural ecological variety.

### 2. Malnutrition in Developed Countries

Paradoxically, affluent nations suffer widespread malnutrition driven by refined and processed foods, excessive animal fat consumption, and food additives. These dietary patterns contribute to degenerative diseases including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and various cancers. Permaculture addresses this by encouraging whole-food systems where nutrient-dense, freshly harvested produce forms the dietary foundation rather than industrially processed alternatives.

### 3. Nutritional Challenges in Famine-Affected Areas

In regions facing food scarcity, particular attention must be paid to adequate intake of **vitamins C, A, and B-complex**, as well as the minerals **iron and zinc**. Importantly, whole grains and pulses grown in **alkaline desert soils** may actively strip zinc from the body due to high phytate levels combined with soil chemistry, compounding deficiency risks in already vulnerable populations.

### 4. The Soil–Food–Nutrition Nexus

There exists a **complex interaction** between food quality, soil health, trace element availability, soil pH, biocide residues, and fertiliser practices. Heavy fertiliser use, particularly with synthetic NPK formulations, frequently causes **micronutrient deficiency** in crops by diluting trace mineral concentrations and disrupting soil biology responsible for mineral cycling. Healthy soils rich in organic matter and biological activity produce nutritionally superior food.

## Individual Variables in Nutrition

Human nutritional needs are not uniform. Significant individual differences exist in:

- **Metabolic function** — variations in how efficiently individuals absorb and utilise nutrients
- **Food allergies and sensitivities** — reactions to specific foods that vary widely between individuals
- **Body weight and composition** — caloric and nutrient requirements scale with body mass and activity level

Permaculture systems, by providing diverse food options, allow individuals to select diets suited to their particular needs rather than depending on standardised industrial food products.

## The Sane Procedure: A Permaculture Approach to Health

Mollison distils a sensible, integrated approach to health into the following principles:

- **Basic hygiene** — clean food handling, sanitary living conditions, and safe water access
- **Healthy plants and animals** — well-nourished, biocide-free food sources as the foundation of good nutrition
- **Avoidance of biocides** — both in agriculture and in domestic environments, reducing the body's toxic burden
- **Regular exercise** — physical activity integrated into daily life through productive land-based work
- **Clean water** — protected through rainwater harvesting, soil conservation, and pollution prevention
- **Cheerful disposition** — mental and emotional well-being supported by meaningful work, community, and connection to healthy ecosystems

These principles reflect the permaculture ethic of caring for people through systemic design that produces health as a natural outcome rather than requiring isolated interventions.

## Key Design Implications

- **Never assume land is clean** — investigate the full chemical history of any site before committing to food production.
- **Direct contaminated land to appropriate uses** — forestry, fibre production, or biofuel crops rather than food.
- **Build soil biology** as the primary mechanism for nutrient cycling and plant nutrition.
- **Harvest rainwater** and conserve water to reduce dependence on potentially polluted groundwater.
- **Design for dietary diversity** in all food systems, ensuring a wide range of nutrients is available locally.
- **Prioritise trace mineral availability** through mineral-rich compost, rock dust amendments, and diverse cropping.
