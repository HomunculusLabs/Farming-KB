## Rice Straw Mulch in Natural Farming

## Overview

Spreading rice straw might be considered rather unimportant, but it is fundamental to Fukuoka's method of growing rice and winter grain. It is connected with everything — fertility, germination, weeds, keeping away sparrows, and water management. In actual practice and in theory, the use of straw in farming is a crucial issue. "This is something that I cannot seem to get people to understand," Fukuoka wrote.

## Why Uncut Straw

**Germination**: When rice straw is spread over the field immediately after harvest, it creates a moist, protected microclimate that encourages the germination of the next crop's seeds. The straw shields the soil from direct sun and wind, preventing the surface from drying out.

**Weed suppression**: A thick layer of straw stops weed seeds in the soil from receiving the light they need to germinate. Meanwhile, the grain seeds — which are either sown in clay pellets before the rice harvest or broadcast directly — are already established or positioned to sprout ahead of the weeds.

**Bird deterrence**: Sparrows and other birds are kept away from the newly sown seeds by the layer of straw, which hides the seeds from sight and makes foraging difficult.

**Water management**: The straw layer helps retain soil moisture during dry periods and moderates temperature fluctuations. During the flooding period for rice, the straw helps maintain the water level and prevents excessive evaporation.

**Soil structure**: Over time, the decomposing straw improves soil structure by adding [[extending-growing-season]] to the next in Fukuoka's year-round farming system:

1. **October**: White clover and winter grain seeds are broadcast among the ripening rice stalks
2. **Harvest**: Rice is harvested; the sprouted clover and grain seeds are trampled by harvesters' feet but recover quickly
3. **Post-harvest**: All rice straw is scattered uncut over the field — this is the critical moment
4. **November-December**: Rice seeds in clay pellets are broadcast among the growing barley or rye
5. **Spring**: The straw has partially decomposed; chicken manure is spread to help decomposition continue
6. **May**: Winter grain is harvested; its straw is also scattered over the field
7. **Flooding**: Water stands in the field for a week or ten days, weakening weeds and clover while rice sprouts through the straw
8. **Summer**: Rice grows in the straw-mulched, clover-covered field until autumn harvest

Each cycle returns more organic matter to the soil than the crop removed, meaning fertility increases year by year without any external inputs.

## The Problem of Straw Removal

In conventional farming, straw is typically removed from the field after harvest and either burned or used for other purposes. This creates a fertility deficit that must be made up with [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]]. The straw is not waste — it is the field's future fertility. By returning every ounce of straw to the field, the farmer creates a closed nutrient loop that can sustain production indefinitely.

## Why People Resist

Despite its simplicity and effectiveness, Fukuoka found it extraordinarily difficult to convince other farmers to spread straw uncut over their fields. The reasons for resistance include:

- It looks messy and untidy compared to a clean, bare field
- Agricultural schools teach that straw should be chopped or removed
- It contradicts the aesthetic of industrial agriculture, which values uniformity
- Farmers fear that straw will harbor pests or diseases
- The practice is so simple that people cannot believe it works

Fukuoka spent thirty years developing his method, and the central insight — that uncut straw is the key — was one of the last things he discovered. Yet it is the simplest element of the entire system.

## The Deeper Significance

The straw mulch exemplifies a core principle [[natural-farming-methods-comparison]]. Even after years of experimental data showed that uncut straw produced the best results, the researchers continued to chop straw. Their training, their assumptions, and their professional identity were all invested in the idea that agriculture required technical intervention.

Fukuoka encountered this resistance throughout his career. Agricultural experts would visit his farm, see the results with their own eyes, acknowledge that the methods worked — and then return to their institutions and continue promoting chemical-based methods. The gap between seeing and believing, between evidence and practice, was enormous.
