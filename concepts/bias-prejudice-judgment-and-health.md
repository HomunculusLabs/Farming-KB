## Overview

Biases, prejudices, and judgments are classified in mind-body medicine as
unhealthy, emotionally charged habits that directly undermine physical
and psychological well-being. These automatic cognitive patterns
influence choices without the benefit of reason, logic, or critical
thinking.

Unlike discernment — defined as the ability to see and understand
situations clearly and intelligently — biases operate below conscious
awareness, fueling chronic fear, anger, anxiety, and defensiveness.

More than one hundred distinct cognitive biases have been documented,
all sharing the common feature of automatically directing behavior in
unhealthy ways.

## Negativity Bias and Optimistic Bias

Negativity bias is the tendency to more readily imagine things going
wrong than right. Its evolutionary roots lie in environments where
predator threats demanded heightened vigilance.

Unchecked in modern contexts, negativity bias fuels chronic fear states
with measurable impacts on physical health through persistent stress
hormone activation.

Optimistic bias operates in the opposite direction: people acknowledge
risks apply to others but not to themselves. A University of Maryland
study (2000) found most smokers acknowledged smoking causes death for
most people, yet doubted they personally would die from it even after
decades of use.

This disconnect demonstrates how optimistic bias overrides rational
risk assessment, with implications for treatment adherence and
preventive health behaviors.

## Discernment, Positivity Ratios, and Health

The distinction between bias and discernment is central to the
mind-body medicine framework. Bias operates automatically, narrowing
perception. Discernment involves intelligent exploration and a desire
to understand rather than judge.

Specific positivity ratios predict well-being. University of Washington
research (1998) showed couples resolving conflict succeed with a 5:1
positive-to-negative ratio. University of Michigan researchers found
individual flourishing requires a 3:1 ratio.

Even single emotional words affect physiology. A University of Gottingen
study (2009) found viewing words like "happy" or "brutal" directly
reduced or increased anxiety, suggesting that carefully chosen words
alone can diminish stress and enhance coping mechanisms.
