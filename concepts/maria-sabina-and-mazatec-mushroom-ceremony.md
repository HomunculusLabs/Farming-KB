# Maria Sabina and the Mazatec Mushroom Ceremony

The story of Maria Sabina (c. 1894-1985), a Mazatec curandera from Huautla de Jimenez, Oaxaca, Mexico, represents the pivotal moment when indigenous mushroom practices intersected with Western psychedelic culture.

## Background

The Mazatec mushroom tradition was one of the few surviving indigenous psilocybin mushroom practices in the Americas. The Spanish conquest had driven mushroom use underground, and by the 17th century, knowledge of teonanacatl ("God's flesh") had largely disappeared from the historical record. As Letcher documents, the cleric Jacinto de la Serna recorded one of the last colonial-era accounts of mushroom use in the 17th century, describing a man named Juan Chichiten who celebrated a saint's day with a mushroom vigil "after the manner of Communion."

## Rediscovery

The Western rediscovery began ironically with a scholarly denial. In 1915, American botanist William Safford published a paper arguing that Sahagun's teonanacatl had been misidentified and was actually peyote. This was challenged by Blas Pablo Reko in 1919, who wrote that teonanacatl "is actually as Sahagun says, a fungus which grows on dung-heaps and which is still used under the same old name by the Indians of the Sierra Juarez in Oaxaca in their religious feasts."

In 1936, Robert J. Weitlaner, a linguist living in Mexico City, conducted field research among the Mazatec in Huautla de Jimenez and discovered that mushrooms were indeed used in curative divinatory rites. Richard Evans Schultes joined Reko in 1938 and made the first formal identification: Panaeolus campanulatus var. sphinctrinus.

## Wasson's Visit (1955)

R. Gordon Wasson and his photographer Allan Richardson traveled to Huautla in June 1955, where they participated in a velada (night ceremony) conducted by Maria Sabina on June 29-30. Wasson consumed six pairs of the mushrooms and described visions of geometric patterns giving way to "architectural structures, with colonnades and architraves, patios of regal splendour." He felt himself a "disembodied eye hovering above strange, new landscapes" and sensed he understood "the true, awful meaning of the word 'ecstasy.'"

## Maria Sabina's Life

Letcher provides details from Sabina's autobiography. As a child, she learned of mushrooms from her curandero uncle Aurelio, who could "see" after eating them. She first ate mushrooms while tending her family's goats on the mountain. The mushrooms transported her to a world of temples and golden palaces, where they appeared as "clownlike dwarfs with trumpets, children that sang and danced, children of the flesh of flowers." The mushrooms spoke to her and offered help.

She conducted her first healing ceremony when she was only eight years old. Her definitive calling came when her sister Ana Maria became seriously ill. After eating more mushrooms, she entered deeper than ever before, where a mushroom spirit asked what she wished to become. She answered "a saint," which granted her access to great knowledge of cures.

By the time Wasson met her in 1955, she was a renowned curandera de los hongos, the highest quality, una Senora sin mancha, "a woman of rare moral and spiritual power."

## The Life Magazine Article (1957)

Wasson published his account in Life magazine on May 13, 1957, in an article captioned "Seeking the Magic Mushroom." The article coined the term "magic mushroom" and was read by millions. This publicity transformed Huautla into a destination for Western psychedelic seekers, which ultimately disrupted the indigenous mushroom tradition Sabina had preserved.

## Aftermath

The flood of Western visitors to Huautla following Wasson's publicity eventually led to the commercialization and degradation of the Mazatec mushroom tradition. Maria Sabina herself reportedly came to regret the publicity, feeling that the sacred nature of the veladas had been violated. Mexican authorities eventually arrested foreigners found with mushrooms.

## Scientific Follow-up

Wasson enlisted Roger Heim (1900-1979), Professor of Mycology at the Musee National d'Histoire Naturelle in Paris, who identified several species of Psilocybe, Conocybe, and other genera. Heim cultivated specimens and sent them to Albert Hofmann at Sandoz, who in 1958 synthesized psilocybin and psilocin. The team also identified Ololiuhqui as Turbina corymbosa (morning glory) and Salvia divinorum as a hallucinogenic mint used by Mazatec curanderos.
