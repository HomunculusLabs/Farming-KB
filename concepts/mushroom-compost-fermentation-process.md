# Mushroom Compost Fermentation Process

## Overview

The fermentation (or "composting") of stable manure is one of the most critical and technically demanding aspects of commercial [[mushroom-cultivation]]. The process transforms raw, fresh stable manure into a suitable growing medium for *[[agaricus-campestris]]* by subjecting it to controlled microbial decomposition. While the same end result may be obtained by a variety of methods, understanding the underlying principles is essential for intelligent compost management.

## Principles of Fermentation

### Why Fermentation is Necessary

The fundamental purpose of fermentation is **biological rather than chemical**. Fresh manure contains very readily soluble and relatively unstable organic products that become the seat of pronounced and abundant bacterial activity. In the presence of this rapid bacterial activity, the mycelium of the mushroom will not grow—and this is not merely due to the accompanying temperature increase, as demonstrated by the failure of mycelium to grow even when manure is present in such small quantities that no noticeable temperature rise occurs.

Chemically, fermentation is actually **more injurious than beneficial** to the manure. Grown in sterilized fresh manure in pure cultures, mushroom mycelium grows as rapidly and more profusely than in fermented manure similarly treated. This conclusively demonstrates that fermentation is not chemically essential. Instead, fermentation acts by:

1. **Removing or transforming rapidly fermentable products** that would otherwise support competitive bacterial growth
2. **Inducing changes in the insoluble material and straw** so that mushroom mycelium can grow slowly and compete with the microorganisms subsequently involved
3. **Creating conditions where common mould fungi do not develop profusely**, while facilitating bacterial and direct chemical action that favors [[mushroom-spawn]] over mould mycelia

### The Nutrient Question

The nutrients used by the mushroom include both gradually soluble organic products and necessary inorganic salts. The once-prevalent belief that the "ammonia" of the manure might constitute the only important fertilizer in mushroom culture is erroneous, though manure treated in a way that causes loss of much free ammonia is injured for manurial purposes.

## Practical Fermentation Methods

### Standard Procedure

Beginning with good materials, the essentials are:

1. **Adequate moisture at all times**
2. **Sufficient forking over (turning)** to effect equal fermentation and uniformity throughout, and sometimes to force a temperature decline

Fresh stable manure should be thrown into **piles not more than 4 feet high** and of any desired extent. It is unsatisfactory to make one great pyramid or many nondescript heaps. At the outset, the manure should be thoroughly wet throughout. Subsequently it must be maintained in a moist state and turned three or four times (or more frequently, depending on conditions).

### Timing and Temperature

Under ordinary circumstances, with well-moistened manure, proper fermentation may be achieved in **three weeks or somewhat less**, with turnings at intervals of three to five days. Key temperature milestones include:

- **140° to 150°F**: Attainment of this temperature between the first three turnings is advantageous
- **Above 125°F for several days at a time**: This represents the best type of fermentation, as demonstrated by repeated experiments
- **120° to 130°F**: The temperature to which the compost commonly declines when fermentation is approaching completion

### Factors Affecting Fermentation

Several variables influence the fermentation process:

- **Shavings or sawdust content**: May necessitate a longer fermentation interval; the longest fermentation period is required when there is much resin in the shavings
- **Moisture level**: If there is little moisture, the manure will "burn" easily and require a much longer fermentation period
- **Compression**: If the pile has been compressed by tramping or by long standing when more than 4 feet high, fermentation will be retarded
- **Scale**: The difficulty of fermentation increases with smaller quantities of compost, which dry out quickly and where a certain amount of burning is scarcely avoidable

### The Problem of "Burning"

"Burning" is indicative of fairly high temperature combined with rapid drying out. In a properly arranged compost pile, the greatest burning occurs just beneath the surface. The extent to which burning injures the compost remains somewhat uncertain, but certainly a reasonably good yield may be obtained from burned material. Special attention to moisture and compactness of the material is required to minimize burning.

### Signs of Sufficient Composting

Several unmistakable indicators signal that fermentation is complete:

1. **Loss of practically all objectionable odor**
2. **Oleaginous "feel"**—the compost develops a characteristic slightly oily texture
3. **Pliability**—the material is supple and workable rather than stiff or brittle
4. **Uniform brown color** of the straw

### Compost Under Cover

It is preferable to prepare compost under cover when feasible, particularly during mid-summer (when excessive heat and drying are risks) or during freezing weather (when cold can halt microbial activity). Manure that has been piled in a barn lot or otherwise subjected to leaching will have lost much of its value. The cardinal rule: **never use for compost anything but the best fresh stable manure**.

## Quantity Requirements

A full coal carload of manure will usually suffice for about **3,000 square feet** of beds (assuming beds of the usual depth). A carload is approximately 12 to 14 large wagon loads, with each two-horse manure wagon covering about 225 square feet. The average wagon body holds enough for perhaps 100 square feet. When estimating amounts needed, the age, moisture content, compactness of the product, and the amount of straw present must be considered—more straw means greater loss of bulk after composting.

## Large-Scale Grower's Method

Some large commercial growers use an abbreviated approach: manure is thrown into huge piles, moistened, and left with no further attention for two or three weeks while additional material is collected daily. This accumulated manure is then turned once or twice and made into beds. Fermentation continues after the beds are made, but because the beds are well compressed and moist, there are no disadvantageous consequences—provided spawning is delayed until there is no danger of overheating.

## Quick Method for Home Use

Where fermentation is not possible and mushrooms are desired in very small quantities for home consumption, a modified procedure may be employed: fresh manure is freed from long straw, mixed thoroughly with one-third as much light garden loam, moistened if required, and allowed to stand three or four days before preparing the bed. The material is spread in layers of about two inches deep, each layer firmly tamped down, until a bed eight inches deep is prepared.

## Substitutes for Stable Manure

Extensive experiments with alternative vegetable products—including decayed leaves, sawdust, and woods mould—demonstrated decisively that **none of these products approaches the value of stable manure** for mushroom growing. Even when equal quantities of manure were combined with sawdust or well-fermented deciduous leaves, mushroom size was reduced and yield was lessened. While sawdust and decaying leaves are undoubtedly improved by the addition of phosphates and nitrates, no combination of fertilizers has been able to bring these products to the point of being a worthy competitor of stable manure.

It is possible that certain forest species of *Agaricus*, notably *A. silvicola* and *A. placomyces*, might be more amenable to successful culture on such alternative substrata. Substitutes would be of particular value to those in towns who wish to grow mushrooms for home consumption but cannot ferment manure without objectionable consequences.
