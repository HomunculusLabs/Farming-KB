These notes distill key concepts from Bill Mollison's Permaculture Design Course (PDC) and [[patterns-in-nature]]

Permaculture design is pattern-based. Understanding natural patterns allows designers to create efficient, resilient systems.

### Key Patterns
- **Branching**: Trees, rivers, blood vessels, and root systems all follow branching patterns for efficient distribution. Use branching patterns for pathways, water systems, and plant layout.
- **Spirals**: Herb spirals maximize growing area and microclimate variety in a small space. Spiral patterns appear in shells, galaxies, and weather systems.
- **Waves and Lobes**: Edge effects create more interface area. Keyhole beds and lobe-shaped gardens maximize edge for planting.
- **Nets and Webs**: Food webs, social networks, and interconnected systems create resilience through multiple pathways.
- **Rings and Circles**: Mandala gardens, zone planning, and animal paddock rotation follow circular patterns.
- **Fractals**: Self-similar patterns at different scales. A branch looks like a miniature tree. A watershed contains smaller watersheds.

### Pattern Application
The design process flows from pattern to detail:
1. Identify the broadest patterns (climate, topography, water flow).
2. Design within these patterns (zones, sectors, water harvesting).
3. Fill in details (specific plant species, building materials).

## Climate and Bioregion Strategies

Permaculture design must be adapted to local climate and bioregion. Mollison emphasizes that design varies dramatically between climates.

### Temperate Climates
- Four seasons; cold winters limit growing season.
- Focus on frost protection, season extension, food preservation.
- Deciduous forests as the model ecosystem.
- Key strategies: greenhouse/hoophouse, root cellars, preserved foods.

### Tropical Climates
- Year-round growing season with wet and dry periods.
- Abundant biomass but rapid decomposition.
- Tropical forests and savannas as model ecosystems.
- Key strategies: mulching, polycultures, [[keyline-design]], drought-tolerant species, windbreaks.

### Cool/Cold Climates
- Short growing season, long cold periods.
- Focus on heat capture, insulation, food storage.
- Boreal forest as model ecosystem.
- Key strategies: thermal mass, passive solar, earth-sheltered structures.

## Design Methods

### The Design Process
Mollison outlines a systematic approach to permaculture design:

1. **Site Analysis**: Map the site thoroughly -- topography, water, soils, vegetation, existing structures, climate data, sectors.
2. **Client Interview**: Understand the needs, goals, resources, and constraints of the people involved.
3. **Concept Design**: Develop broad zone and sector plans based on observation and analysis.
4. **Detailed Design**: Specify elements, species, materials, and management plans.
5. **Implementation Plan**: Create a timeline and phasing strategy.
6. **Monitoring and Adjustment**: Observe results and adapt the design over time.

### Mapping and Overlay
The overlay method involves creating separate transparent maps for different elements (water, access, vegetation, structures, microclimates) and overlaying them to find optimal placement where multiple needs converge.

### Random Assembly
A creativity technique where elements and their functions are randomly paired to generate novel design solutions. Example: "chicken" + "greenhouse" leads to the chicken tractor inside a greenhouse concept (heat + CO2 + manure + pest control).

## [[graywater-systems]]**: Recycle household water for irrigation.
- **Rainwater harvesting**: Collect roof runoff in tanks and cisterns.

### Water Principle: Slow it, spread it, sink it.
The goal is to keep water on the land as long as possible, allowing it to infiltrate and recharge groundwater rather than running off.

## Soil in Permaculture

Mollison emphasizes that soil is the foundation of all terrestrial life. Permaculture aims to build soil rather than deplete it.

### Soil Building Strategies
- Composting and compost tea application
- Mulching with organic materials
- Cover cropping and green manures
- Minimal tillage (no-till or reduced tillage)
- Integrating animals for manure and soil disturbance
- Biochar incorporation

### [[woodlot-management]]**: Sustainable timber and firewood production.

## Animals in Permaculture

Animals are integral to permaculture systems, providing:
- Manure for fertility
- Pest control (chickens eat insects, ducks eat slugs)
- Soil cultivation (pigs root, chickens scratch)
- Weed management (goats graze, geese weed)
- Products (eggs, meat, milk, fiber)

### Integrated Animal Systems
- **Chicken tractors**: Mobile coops that concentrate chicken activity where needed.
- **Paddock rotation**: Moving grazing animals regularly to prevent overgrazing and build soil.
- **Aquaculture**: Integrating fish, ducks, and water plants in pond systems.

## Social Permaculture

Mollison and Holmgren both emphasize that permaculture is not just about agriculture -- it's about designing sustainable human culture:

- **Community economics**: Local trading systems, LETS (Local Exchange Trading Systems), community-supported agriculture.
- **Bioregionalism**: Organizing social and economic systems around natural boundaries.
- **[[community-land-trusts]], cooperatives, commons governance.
