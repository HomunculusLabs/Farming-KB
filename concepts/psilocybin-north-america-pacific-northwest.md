# Psychoactive Mushrooms of the Pacific Northwest

The Pacific Northwest of North America -- encompassing western Washington, British Columbia, and western Oregon -- is one of the world's richest regions for naturally occurring [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-psilocybin-and-psilocin]]-containing mushrooms. The region's mild, wet climate, extensive forests, and widespread use of bark mulch in landscaping have created ideal conditions for a diverse community of psychoactive fungal species.

## The Region

The Pacific Northwest's primary zone for psychoactive mushroom use stretches from Southern Oregon north through Washington State into British Columbia, Canada. This area lies between the Pacific Ocean and the Cascade Mountains. The region's reputation is so established that the city of Redmond, WA -- formerly known as the "bicycle capital" -- was renamed the "psilocybin capital" by several newspapers starting in 1978.

## Major Species

### Psilocybe semilanceata (Liberty Cap)

[[psilocybe-semilanceata]] is the most widely used species in the Pacific Northwest and has been since the late 1960s. Key facts:

- **Arrival:** Believed to have spread from Europe; was identified in Canada in 1966 and recognized as identical to European specimens
- **Habitat:** Pasture grasses, particularly in areas west of the Cascade Mountains
- **Alkaloid content:** Approximately 1% psilocybin of dry weight -- among the highest of any species
- **Reputation:** Known as one of the most potent species without generally causing unwanted physical side effects
- **Season:** Fall through early winter; fruiting ceases when temperatures drop below freezing
- **Slang names:** Liberty Cap (shared with European name)
- **Spread:** Habitat is expanding eastward into pasture lands beyond the Cascade Mountains

### Psilocybe stuntzii (Blue Veil)

[[psilocybe-stuntzii-profile]] is the second most important species in the region.

- **Discovery:** First found at the University of Washington, Seattle, in fall 1972
- **Habitat:** Bark mulch in parks, gardens, golf courses, football fields
- **Key feature:** Distinct ring on stem, unlike most other regional species
- **Bluing:** Strong and rapid blue staining reaction
- **Season:** Can fruit year-round under favorable conditions -- unique among regional species
- **Slang names:** Blue veil, Stuntzees

### Psilocybe cyanescens (Wavy Cap)

[[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]] is the third most significant species.

- **Habitat:** Wood chips, sawdust, decaying plant material in parks
- **Key feature:** Undulating, twisted caps (giving the "wavy" name)
- **Alkaloid content:** Very high; contains about twice the psilocybin of P. pelliculosa
- **Bluing:** Extremely strong blue staining, even in cool temperatures
- **Slang names:** Blue wavy, Cyan, Grandote
- **Outdoor cultivation:** Since the early 1980s, mycophiles have used "natural outdoor cultivation" -- selecting natural wood substrates striated with mycelia (rhizomorphs) that turn blue when handled, then transferring onto fresh wood chips or commercial mulch (not cedar). After several months of growth, the mycelia fruit during fall

### Panaeolus subbalteatus (Red Cap)

[[panaeolus-subbalteatus-profile]] is regionally important as a spring-fruiting species.

- **Habitat:** Fertilized fields, pastures, compost heaps
- **Season:** Spring through fall -- often the first species to appear each year
- **Slang names:** Red cap
- **User perception:** Considered slightly more poisonous than Psilocybe species, but still widely used
- **Alkaloid content:** Variable; dried mushrooms from the Pacific Northwest contain 0.16% - 0.65% psilocybin
- **Significance:** Important because it fruits in spring, months before the Psilocybe species appear in fall

### Psilocybe pelliculosa

- **Habitat:** Forests, wood chips, and sawdust
- **Appearance:** Extremely difficult to distinguish from P. semilanceata macroscopically
- **Difference from P. semilanceata:** Grows in forests on wood chips and sawdust rather than pasture grasses
- **Potency:** About 30-50% of the psilocybin content of P. cyanescens
- **Slang name:** Also called "Liberty Cap" (creating confusion with P. semilanceata)

### Psilocybe baeocystis

- **Habitat:** Inland areas of the Pacific Northwest; wood debris and lawns in parks
- **Notable:** Frequently found on school and university campuses
- **Reputation:** Considered to cause more physical problems than other species
- **Infamy:** The only known psilocybin-related fatality involved this species (a child in 1960; see [[psilocybin-toxicity-and-safety-profile]])
- **Caution:** The reputation for physical problems may be more psychological than pharmacological, rooted in the fatality association rather than actual chemical properties

## Usage Patterns and Culture

### Scale of Use

In 1977, J. Ott estimated that several tens of thousands of applied dosages of psychotropic mushroom material were harvested and used each year in the Pacific Northwest. By the early 1980s, prominent experts estimated that the number of hallucinogenic mushroom users in the United States outnumbered LSD users for the first time. The total number of magic mushroom consumers across North America is likely close to one million, quite possibly higher.

### Safety Record

Despite widespread usage in the Pacific Northwest, there have never been reports of fatalities or serious physical damage as a consequence of using Psilocybe or Panaeolus species. Local papers have merely mentioned occasional panic reactions that subsided as the acute effects wore off. This remarkable safety record spans decades of use by hundreds of thousands of people.

### The Bicycle Capital Becomes the Psilocybin Capital

The city of Redmond, Washington illustrates the cultural shift. Formerly known as the "bicycle capital," several newspapers renamed it the "psilocybin capital" starting in 1978, reflecting the community's association with mushroom use.

### Literary Influence

Tom Robbins's popular 1971 novel "Another Roadside Attraction" significantly contributed to the increasing popularity of Psilocybe semilanceata in the region from the late 1960s onward.

## Hawaii

Since the late 1960s, the usage of [[panaeolus-cyanescens-cultivation-guide]] and its closest relatives has become fashionable in Hawaii, even though the taxonomic differentiation of the Panaeolus species from each other is extremely difficult.

### Mushroom Trade Attempts

Several entrepreneurial attempts to commercialize Hawaiian mushrooms failed due to ignorance of preservation science:

1. **Dry ice freezing:** Mushrooms frozen with solid carbon dioxide for export to the Pacific Northwest and Southern California. The mushrooms turned to mushy pulp -- the entrepreneurs were unaware that psychoactive ingredients remain stable only when mushrooms are dried and stored below 50 C (122 F)
2. **Honey preservation:** Panaeolus species preserved in honey appeared on the black market in Hawaii and occasionally in North America during the early 1970s. Mushrooms could be preserved for only short periods this way

### "Hawaiian Strains" of Psilocybe cubensis

"Hawaiian strains" of [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] are frequently referenced, but the species is not native to the Hawaiian Islands. It should grow there only under conditions of artificial cultivation, and any spawn used must have been isolated from fruiting bodies originating elsewhere.

### Importation Patterns

Several cases demonstrate that psychoactive mushrooms arrived in Hawaii through imported materials:

- **Menton, France (1965):** Panaeolus cyanescens appeared on compost of straw and decayed horse dung from a race track used by horses from tropical countries. The species was likely imported.
- **Budapest Fairgrounds (early 1970s):** Panaeolus cyanescens was found next to pavilions from Thailand and Vietnam

## The Gulf States

Some areas along the U.S. Gulf states have acquired reputations similar to the Pacific Northwest as centers of psychoactive mushroom use, though with different species assemblages reflecting the subtropical climate.

## Comparison: Pacific Northwest vs. Europe

| Feature | Pacific Northwest | Europe |
|---|---|---|
| Dominant species | P. semilanceata | P. semilanceata |
| Secondary species | P. stuntzii, P. cyanescens | P. cyanescens, P. bohemica |
| Spring species | P. subbalteatus | Few options |
| Outdoor cultivation | Common (wood chips) | Rare |
| Cultural integration | High | Variable by country |
| Legal status | Illegal | Variable by country |
| User population | ~1 million (North America) | Hundreds of thousands |
