# Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) Overview

Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) is a liquid extract produced by aerating high-quality compost in water, typically for 24–48 hours. Developed and popularized by Dr. Elaine R. Ingham, AACT represents a biological approach to soil and plant health that focuses on restoring and maintaining the soil foodweb rather than relying on chemical inputs. The *Field Guide for Actively Aerated Compost Tea*, compiled by Dr. Carole Ann Rollins from Ingham's correspondence and research, serves as the primary reference for AACT practitioners.

## What is AACT?

Actively Aerated Compost Tea is created by placing mature, biologically diverse compost in water and actively maintaining dissolved oxygen levels through mechanical aeration. This process extracts and multiplies the beneficial microorganisms — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes — present in the compost. The resulting liquid contains a concentrated population of soil biology that can be applied to soils, plant surfaces, or growing media.

The key distinction of AACT is the active aeration component. Unlike passive extraction methods (compost extracts or leachates), AACT maintains aerobic conditions throughout the brewing process, ensuring that the beneficial aerobic organisms dominate while suppressing anaerobic pathogens. The aeration also stimulates microbial reproduction, increasing the total biomass of beneficial organisms in the tea.

## Distinction from Other Compost Tea Types

The field guide carefully distinguishes AACT from several related preparations:

- **Plant tea**: Made by steeping plant material in water. Contains plant nutrients and compounds but limited soil biology.

- **Manure tea**: Made from animal manures. Can contain beneficial biology but risks introducing pathogens if not properly composted first.

- **Compost extract**: A liquid extraction of compost without brewing or aeration. Contains the organisms present in the compost but does not increase their populations.

- **Compost leachate**: Liquid that drains from compost piles passively. Often anaerobic and may contain phytotoxic compounds.

- **Fermentative compost tea (FCT)**: Also called "compost tea" in older literature. Brewed without active aeration; may become anaerobic and produce different (sometimes beneficial, sometimes harmful) biology.

- **Long-brewing compost tea (LBCT)**: Extended brewing periods, typically 3–14 days. These develop different microbial communities than short-brew AACT.

- **Not-aerobic compost tea (NACT)**: Brewed under conditions where anaerobic organisms are intentionally or accidentally dominant.

Understanding these distinctions is critical because different tea types serve different purposes and carry different risks. AACT is preferred for most applications because it provides consistent, aerobic biology with minimal risk of pathogen contamination.

## The AACT Movement

The field guide emerged from the AACT Community — a network of practitioners, researchers, and consultants working to establish AACT as a legitimate agricultural practice. Dr. Ingham served as the primary mentor and knowledge source, answering questions through internet correspondence over several years. The guide was compiled as a way to preserve and disseminate this accumulated knowledge.

The movement is described as "a pioneering industry at the forefront of change with humility and respect for the Earth and all the natural resources." This framing positions AACT not as a commercial product but as a knowledge-based practice rooted in ecological understanding.

Dr. Ingham's credentials span multiple institutions: President of the Sustainable Studies Institute, President of the Soil Foodweb Institute, Director of Research at Soil Foodweb Inc., and Associate Professor in the Department of Forest Science at Oregon State University. This interdisciplinary background — bridging academic research, commercial consulting, and education — reflects the breadth of AACT's applications.

## Applications Across Industries

AACT has been applied across a wide range of agricultural and land management contexts:

- **Row crops and broadacre agriculture**: Rebuilding soil biology in degraded farmland
- **Orchards and vineyards**: Foliar and soil applications for disease suppression and nutrient cycling
- **Turf and turf management**: Golf courses, sports fields, and lawns
- **Nurseries and greenhouses**: Seedling inoculation and growing media enhancement
- **Landscaping and restoration**: Revegetation projects and erosion control
- **Composting operations**: Inoculating new compost piles to accelerate decomposition
- **Wastewater treatment**: Supporting biological treatment systems

The field guide includes application rate sheets for various industries, addressing a gap that practitioners faced when working with clients unfamiliar with AACT. Standardized application rates help ensure consistent results and build credibility.

## The Soil Foodweb Foundation

AACT is grounded in the soil foodweb concept — the understanding that soil health depends on a complex community of organisms interacting in food chains and nutrient cycles. The soil foodweb includes:

- **Bacteria**: Primary decomposers of simple organic compounds; dominate in disturbed or annual crop soils
- **Fungi**: Decomposers of complex organic matter; dominate in perennial systems and forests
- **Protozoa**: Consume bacteria, releasing plant-available nutrients through the microbial loop
- **Nematodes**: Both bacterial-feeding and fungal-feeding; regulate microbial populations and cycle nutrients
- **Microarthropods**: Larger organisms that fragment organic matter and prey on smaller organisms

The health of this foodweb determines soil structure, nutrient availability, disease suppression, and ultimately plant health. Chemical fertilizers and pesticides often damage the foodweb, creating dependency on further inputs. AACT aims to restore the foodweb so that natural processes can provide these services.

## Making the Shift to Sustainability

The field guide frames AACT as part of a broader shift from input-intensive agriculture to biologically-based management. Five key functions of a healthy soil foodweb are identified:

1. **Outcompete disease organisms**: A diverse and active microbial community occupies ecological niches that would otherwise be available to pathogens
2. **Retain nutrients**: Bacterial and fungal biomass holds nutrients in the soil, preventing leaching and making them available to plants
3. **Make nutrients available**: Through predation and decomposition, soil organisms convert organic nutrients into plant-available forms at rates plants require
4. **Decompose toxins**: Certain soil organisms can break down organic pollutants and allelopathic compounds
5. **Build soil structure**: Fungal hyphae and bacterial glues aggregate soil particles, improving water infiltration, root penetration, and gas exchange

## Testing and Monitoring

The field guide emphasizes the importance of testing both compost and finished tea to ensure biological quality. Two primary testing methods are discussed:

- **Plate counts**: Traditional microbiological technique where samples are spread on agar plates and colonies counted. This method significantly underestimates total populations because most soil organisms (90–99%) cannot be cultured on artificial media.

- **Direct microscopy**: Examining samples under a microscope to count organisms directly. This method provides more accurate population estimates and allows identification of organism types. Ingham strongly advocates for direct microscopy as the preferred testing method.

The field guide provides detailed protocols for both methods and includes comparison data showing why direct microscopy gives more reliable and actionable results for AACT production.

## Data Collection and Research

The field guide includes templates for a Data Collection Research Project designed to gather information about conventional practices across industries. By collecting data on current costs, inputs, and outcomes, practitioners can evaluate the economic case for switching to AACT-based management. This research approach reflects the movement's emphasis on evidence-based practice rather than ideological advocacy.
