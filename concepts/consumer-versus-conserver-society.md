# Consumer versus Conserver Society

Holmgren develops a foundational distinction between two fundamental modes of human organization: the consumer society and the conserver society. This framework underlies the entire argument of "Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability" and connects directly to [[permaculture-energy-descent-planning]]. The consumer/conserver distinction is not merely a lifestyle choice but a structural analysis of how societies organize energy flows, material cycles, and social relationships.

## Two Cultural Systems

Holmgren provides a systematic comparison between the two modes as sets of coherent system priorities:

| Characteristic | Industrial Culture | Sustainable Culture |
|---|---|---|
| Energy base | Non-renewable | Renewable |
| Material flows | Linear | Cyclical |
| Natural assets | Consumption | Storage |
| Organization | Centralised | Distributed Network |
| Scale | Large | Small |
| Movement | Fast | Slow |
| Feedback | Positive | Negative |
| Focus | Centre | Edge |
| Activity | Episodic change | Rhythmic stability |
| Thinking | Reductionist | Wholistic |
| Gender | Masculine | Feminine |

The dynamic balance between these polarised pairs runs through Holmgren's explanation of all permaculture principles. The limitation of this concept is that it suggests some stable state we might arrive at, when in reality a low-energy sustainable culture may be hundreds of years away. Paradoxically, it is easier to characterise that culture than to explain how we get there.

## Consumer Society

The consumer society is characterized by:
- **Linear resource flows**: Extract resources, manufacture products, use them, discard them
- **Growth dependency**: Economic health measured by increasing throughput of materials and energy
- **Specialization**: Narrow expertise, long supply chains, dependence on distant systems
- **Competition**: Individual and corporate competition as primary organizing principle
- **Fossil fuel dependence**: The entire system is powered by concentrated, one-time energy sources
- **Novelty**: Continuous creation of new products, fashions, and experiences to drive consumption
- **Positive feedback loops**: Growth generates more growth, consumption generates more consumption

Holmgren argues that consumer society is structurally incapable of sustainability because it depends on continuous expansion. When the energy source (fossil fuels) that enables this expansion peaks and declines, the consumer society faces a fundamental structural crisis -- not a temporary problem amenable to technical fixes.

## Conserver Society

The conserver society represents the alternative mode of organization:
- **Circular resource flows**: Materials cycle within the system; outputs become inputs
- **Steady-state economics**: Value is measured in quality of life and system health, not throughput
- **Generalization**: Broad skills, local self-reliance, short supply chains
- **Cooperation**: Mutual aid, shared resources, collective decision-making
- **Renewable energy dependence**: Powered by current solar income, not ancient capital
- **Durability**: Goods designed for longevity, repairability, and adaptability
- **Negative feedback loops**: Self-regulation prevents overshoot and maintains balance

Holmgren draws on the work of Ted Trainer and others who have analyzed the conserver society model. He notes that many elements of conserver society are already present in traditional cultures, in intentional communities, and in the frugal practices of older generations who lived through periods of scarcity.

## The Fossil Energy Pulse

Holmgren frames the consumer society as a pulse within the long arc of human history. The fossil fuel age is a brief, energetic burst -- like a forest fire -- that releases stored energy rapidly. Just as a fire converts forest biomass to ash and releases nutrients for new growth, the fossil fuel pulse has created enormous wealth, infrastructure, and knowledge but at the cost of natural capital depletion.

Today we have consumed the easy half of the total likely reserves of conventional oil, and the world's population has more than tripled since the beginning of the oil age. The rebuilding of social and cultural capital must occur within a context of declining net energy availability, and in ways that reinforce the simultaneous rebuilding of natural capital in our landscapes.

## The Energy Descent Transition

The transition from consumer to conserver society is not optional -- it is driven by the fundamental physics of energy descent. As net energy from fossil fuels declines, the complex systems that depend on high energy throughput will progressively fail. The question is not whether the transition will happen but how -- through planned, adaptive redesign (permaculture) or through chaotic collapse.

Holmgren maps this transition as a cultural shift across multiple domains:
- **Settlement patterns**: From dispersed, car-dependent suburbs to compact, walkable villages
- **Food systems**: From globalized, industrial agriculture to local, diverse, labor-intensive production
- **Economy**: From growth-based money systems to steady-state and gift economies
- **Governance**: From centralized, hierarchical institutions to decentralized, participatory structures
- **Technology**: From energy-intensive, specialized machines to low-energy, general-purpose tools
- **Culture**: From individualism and consumer identity to community solidarity and producer identity

## Socio-Political Cycles

Holmgren extends the consumer/conserver analysis into a pulsing model of socio-political change. The current phase of laissez-faire capitalism began with Thatcher and Reagan in the early 1980s, signalling the demise of Keynesian economics and social democratic politics that had prevailed since the Great Depression and World War II.

During the preceding decades, a mix of factors contributed to an accumulation of public infrastructure, education, health, and social wealth: the work ethic and culture of frugality, faith in societal structures, rising wages and working conditions, and strong public policy to control the worst excesses of capitalism.

By the 1970s, oil crises and other factors impacted the capacity to support continually rising living standards. The agents of the elites sold the message that public bureaucracy inhibited efficient market distribution. The result has been the consumption of public assets built up over previous decades -- physical infrastructure, institutional knowledge, shared scientific knowledge, and even the goodwill of non-government organisations.

## Permaculture as Conserver Design

Permaculture is, at its core, the design science of the conserver society. The [[permaculture-ethics]] of earth care, people care, and fair share are the ethical foundation of conservation. The [[permaculture-earthworks-guide]] are the design tools for building conserver systems. [[retrofitting-suburbs-permaculture]] is the practical strategy for making the transition in existing settlements.

Holmgren argues that the consumer/conserver distinction is more useful than the more common developed/developing or rich/poor dichotomies because it describes structural relationships rather than relative positions. A wealthy community with strong local food systems, renewable energy, and cooperative economics is more "conserver" than a poor community dependent on imported food, diesel generators, and extractive industries.

## The Problem Is the Solution

Holmgren applies the Permaculture aphorism "the problem is the solution" to the global crisis. Global capitalism has been like a fire converting green forests to ashes, but it has also released potential and information from cultural norms and institutions that were hopelessly inappropriate for a world of declining energy. The ashes of the consumed forest provide opportunities for the seeds of pioneering species to reform the forest in a way that better reflects large-scale realities.

Similarly, globalization provides opportunities for "social seeding" to create new bioregional cultures adapted to energetic realities. The cultural and ecological destruction created by global capitalism is providing new opportunities to set the parameters of very large-scale and long-term cultural systems adapted to declining energy.

## Beyond Sustainability

The term "beyond sustainability" in Holmgren's subtitle reflects his argument that sustainability -- maintaining current systems -- is impossible in a context of energy descent. For any human culture to be considered sustainable it must have the capacity to reproduce itself down the generations while providing human material needs without cataclysmic breakdown. If high-energy society is energetically impossible as anything more than a pulse in the long run of human history, then it cannot be sustainable.

The task is not to sustain the consumer society but to redesign for a conserver future. This requires letting go of attachments to growth-based prosperity and embracing a different kind of wealth: health, community, skills, ecological function, and resilience. Holmgren suggests we need to "get over our naive and simplistic notions of sustainability as a likely reality for ourselves or even our grandchildren and instead accept that our task is to use our familiarity with continuous change to adapt to energy descent."

## Developing a Post-Affluent Culture

In developing a post-affluent culture it is not necessary to denigrate what parents, grandparents or ancestors did as ignorant, shortsighted or anti-nature. Instead, Holmgren urges recognition that the ground on which we stand has been prepared for us by those who have gone before. A more positive view of generational change enables constructive action in the face of the immense opportunities from energy descent.
