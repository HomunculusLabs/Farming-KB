# Pest Management and IPM for Cannabis

## The IPM Hierarchy

[[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] follows a strict escalation protocol. Prevention and cleanliness sit at the top of the control hierarchy. The logical progression for pest [[vegetable-insect-and-disease-control]] begins with sanitation and escalates only as needed:

1. **Cleanliness** — the first and most important defense
2. **Manual removal** — smash pests and eggs between thumb and forefinger or two sponges
3. **Biological controls** — predators and beneficial organisms
4. **Physical barriers** — screens, traps, sticky traps
5. **Natural-organic sprays** — pyrethrum, neem, insecticidal soap
6. **Chemical controls** — harsh synthetics, last resort only

Any spray, no matter how benign, slows plant growth by clogging stomata with filmy residue. Avoid spraying within two weeks of harvest. Spray timing matters: apply soon after eggs hatch to catch young adults at their weakest stage. Lightweight horticultural oil spray works well alone or as an additive to kill larvae and eggs.

## Outdoor Pest Management

Properly grown outdoor cannabis has few pest problems. Low-tech, natural approaches work well for the pests that do appear.

### Large Chewing Insects

Caterpillars and snails can be hand-picked from foliage. Caterpillar populations can be reduced at the source by installing bat houses — resident bats eat moths and decrease chewing caterpillar numbers. Birds also consume caterpillars, aphids, and other insects. Attract birds with suet, bird houses, baths, and feeders, but cover tender seedlings and clones with wire or nylon mesh to protect them from birds too.

### Mammalian Pests

**Deer and elk** love newly formed cannabis growth and may destroy crops by trampling. A cage around plants is the best deterrent. Deer are repelled by blood meal (activated with water in cloth sacks), human hair (not your own — evidence risk), and scented soap. A proper deer fence is 8 feet tall with the top foot sloping outward at 45°. Electric fences and large dogs are also effective. Urine dispersed around the garden perimeter signals human presence.

**Gophers** tunnel underground and eat roots. Pocket gophers are controlled with traps, poison baits (with caution near edible crops), or by lining planting holes with wire mesh baskets.

**Moles and shrews** are actually beneficial — they eat slugs, insects, and larvae.

### Beneficial Predators

- **Ladybugs** and **praying mantis**: Available from nursery supply stores for aphid and general insect control
- **Barn owls**: Eat mice, gophers, and voles; hard to attract in urban areas
- **Frogs and toads**: Eat insects and slugs; frogs need water, toads are terrestrial
- **Snakes**: Eat gophers, squirrels, mice, moles, shrews — and frogs. Plan carefully before introducing predators that eat each other.
- **Marigolds** (Tagetes erecta and T. patula): Repel nematodes from soil for 2-3 years when planted and tilled under in infested areas. They do not affect above-ground insects.

## Indoor Pest Management

Indoor environments create ideal conditions for pest outbreaks: warm temperatures, high humidity, no natural wind, and the absence of natural predators. Prevention is far more effective than treatment.

### Spider Mites

The most common and destructive cannabis pest. Two-spotted spider mites (Tetranychus urticae) and russet mites are the primary species. Mites reproduce rapidly — populations can explode from a few individuals to thousands in two weeks. They thrive in hot, dry conditions with low humidity.

**Identification**: Look for stippling (small yellow/white dots) on leaf surfaces, fine webbing on undersides of leaves and between branches, and tiny moving dots visible with a magnifying glass.

**Control**:
- Increase humidity — mites struggle above 60% RH
- Introduce predatory mites (Phytoseiulus persimilis, Amblyseius californicus)
- Spray with neem oil or pyrethrum, targeting undersides of leaves
- Use lightweight horticultural oil to smother eggs and larvae
- Quarantine new clones and plants for at least one week before introducing to the garden

### Fungus Gnats

Small dark flies that hover around the soil surface. Their larvae feed on roots [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] in the growing medium, creating entry points for pathogens. Fungus gnats thrive in moist, organic-rich soils.

**Control**:
- Allow soil surface to dry between waterings
- Use yellow sticky traps to monitor and reduce adult populations
- Apply biological controls: [[bacillus-thuringiensis]] var. israelensis (Bti) products like Gnatrol or Vectobac
- Apply diatomaceous earth to soil surface as a physical barrier
- Reduce organic matter in hydroponic systems

### Thrips

Tiny, slender insects that rasp leaf surfaces and suck plant juices. They leave silver streaks and black fecal spots on leaves. Thrips are vectors for plant viruses.

**Control**:
- Blue sticky traps (thrips are attracted to blue more than yellow)
- Neem oil sprays
- Predatory mites (Amblyseius cucumeris)
- Minute pirate bugs (Orius insidiosus)

## Spray Reference

### Approved Organic Controls

| Product | Form | Target Pests |
|---------|------|-------------|
| Pyrethrins | Aerosol, liquid, wettable powder | Mites, aphids, whiteflies, thrips |
| Neem | Oil, emulsifiable liquid | Broad-spectrum insecticide, antifeedant |
| Insecticidal soap | Liquid | Aphids, mites, whiteflies, soft-bodied insects |
| Bacillus thuringiensis | Dust, granules, wettable powder | Caterpillars, fungus gnat larvae |
| Diatomaceous earth | Dust | Slugs, crawling insects, soft-bodied pests |
| Sulfur | Dust, wettable powder | Fungal diseases, mites |
| Copper sulfate | Dust, wettable powder | Fungal [[plant-viral-and-bacterial-diseases]] |
| Horticultural oil | Oil | Mites, eggs, larvae, scale |

### Neem Oil

Extracted from the Indian neem tree (Azadirachta indica), neem is both an antifeedant (insects stop eating) and a growth disruptor (interferes with molting). It controls aphids, thrips, whiteflies, fungus gnats, mealybugs, and leafminers. When added to the nutrient solution in hydroponics, neem enters plant tissue and becomes systemic. As a foliar spray, it acts as a contact insecticide and remains effective on foliage for extended periods.

### Application Guidelines

- Use only contact sprays approved for edible plants
- Avoid systemic chemical products
- Do not spray seedlings or unrooted cuttings
- Wait until cuttings are rooted and seedlings are at least one month old
- Apply sprays soon after egg hatch for maximum effectiveness
- Do not spray within two weeks of harvest
- Spreader-sticker products improve coverage and absorption but increase residue

## Prevention Protocol

The most effective pest management strategy prevents infestations from establishing:

1. **Quarantine** all new plants, clones, and equipment for 1-2 weeks
2. **Inspect** top and bottom of leaves weekly for stippling, webbing, and damage
3. **Maintain** environmental conditions outside pest comfort zones
4. **Clean** grow area between crops — remove all plant debris
5. **Filter** intake air to prevent pest entry
6. **Monitor** with yellow/blue sticky traps for early detection
7. **Avoid** bringing outdoor tools, clothing, or pets into the grow room
8. **Manage** humidity — keep RH between 40-50% to discourage mites while supporting plant growth
