## Direct Seeding Techniques

Methods for sowing seeds directly in the garden rather than starting them indoors and transplanting. Steve Solomon strongly prefers direct seeding for most vegetables, arguing that directly seeded plants often outgrow transplants because they never suffer root shock.

## Why Direct Seed

Solomon is deeply suspicious of garden center transplants. His reasons:

- **No root shock**: Direct-seeded plants never suffer transplant setback
- **Better root systems**: Roots develop naturally in their permanent position
- **Lower cost**: Avoids the expense of purchased seedlings
- **Greater reliability**: You control seed quality and variety selection
- **Hardier plants**: Plants that survive thinning competition are the most vigorous

Some species cannot be transplanted at all — beets, carrots, corn, and radishes usually fail to make useful roots after transplanting. Lettuce generally does better from direct seed.

## The Problem with Transplants

When transplanted, seedlings go into shock:
- Roots are damaged or constrained
- Leaves and stems get bruised by wind (if grown indoors without air movement)
- Unfiltered sun shocks greenhouse-grown plants
- Pot-bound seedlings won't grow for a week to ten days after setting out
- Seedlings that are too young to be sold may have their root ball crumble during transplanting, causing severe root hair damage

Even properly hardened-off transplants lose growing time. Solomon calculates that if you figure a ten-day growth loss from setting out transplants versus the seed not even germinating for a week after sowing, there is almost no gain from using transplants at all.

## Making Seeds Come Up

Solomon offers key advice for reliable germination:

### Seed Quality Matters

The seed business has been degraded by "WalMartization." Picture-packet seeds from discount retailers often:
- Meet only USDA minimum germination standards (as low as 50% for some species like leek)
- Come from "sweepings off the floor" — lightweight, immature, chaff-filled seed
- Are not regionally adapted to your climate
- May be mislabeled varieties

**Solution**: Buy from ethical mail-order seed companies that:
- Test germination twice yearly and maintain levels well above minimum
- Also supply the farmer/commercial trade (which demands high germination)
- Conduct their own variety trials
- Offer regionally appropriate selections

### Sowing Technique

1. **Prepare the bed**: Dig in complete organic fertilizer or compost/manure. Let the soil settle a few days to restore capillary connections.

2. **Sow at the right time**: The biggest single cause of pest trouble is sowing too early. In cold-damp conditions, seeds either rot or seedlings are weak and vulnerable.

3. **Plant thick, thin gradually**:
   - Sow 2-3 large seeds per station for every one final plant wanted
   - Sow up to 4-5 small seeds per station
   - For tiny, pricey seeds like celery, sow as many as 10 per spot
   - Thin gradually: remove weakest seedlings as true leaves develop

### The Thumbprint Method

For small seeds that grow large plants (cabbage, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower, Chinese cabbage, celery, celeriac, kale):

1. After preparing and settling the bed, place a thin layer of compost/manure on the surface and level it
2. With your thumb, press a small indentation about half an inch (1.25 cm) deep
3. Count 4-5 seeds into the bottom of the depression
4. Cover with a bit of loose soil (or compost for moisture-holding)
5. Trace a 6-inch-diameter (15 cm) circle in the soil around the seeds to mark the spot
6. Place stations on whatever final spacing you want (e.g., 24 x 24 inches for cabbage)

### Gradual Thinning

This is critical — Solomon calls it a "contractual agreement" between humans and vegetables:

1. **Immediately after emergence**: Thin any obvious overcrowding
2. **Within a week**: Thin to about 1 inch (2.5 cm) apart; for big seeds, to at least 1.5 inches (4 cm)
3. **Within two weeks of emergence**: The most vigorous individuals stand out. Remove weaker ones to give strong ones room.
4. **When best plant has three true leaves**: It should stand alone. Never allow seedlings to strongly compete with each other for light, water, and nutrients.

Watch for suspiciously vigorous "off-types" — intervariety crosses (like a cabbage-kale cross or zucchini-pumpkin cross) that will outgrow everything but produce disappointing results.

### Seed Storage for Reuse

Seed longevity depends on temperature and moisture:
- For every 10°F increase combined with 1% moisture increase, seed life is cut in half
- For every 10°F decrease combined with 1% moisture decrease, seed life doubles
- Standard conditions: 70°F (21°C) at 13% seed moisture content
- Store seeds in airtight containers with silica gel desiccant

To save money, buy larger quantities (1000+ seeds) from mail-order companies rather than multiple mini-packets. Even using only half the seeds over 3 years is cheaper than buying small packets.

## When Transplants Are Justified

Solomon acknowledges transplants make sense for:
- **Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant**: In short-season areas, essential for getting ripe fruit before frost
- **Heat-loving crops**: Where growing season is too short
- **Overwintered crops**: In mild winter maritime climates

For everything else, direct seed and accept that crops may start yielding a few weeks later — but will be healthier and more self-sufficient.
