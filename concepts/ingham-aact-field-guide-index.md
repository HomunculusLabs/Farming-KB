It is one of the most detailed practical references on compost tea available and serves as a primary source for the wiki's [[cervantes-compost-tea-organic-brewing]] topic cluster.

Ingham's approach emphasizes the [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] as the foundation of plant health. Her methodology differs from simple fermented manure teas by maintaining aerobic conditions throughout the brewing process, which selectively promotes beneficial bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes while suppressing anaerobic organisms. This distinction is central to her work and to the [[aact-definitions-and-types-ingham]] she defines.

### Core Scientific Principles

Ingham's compost tea methodology rests on several key scientific principles that distinguish it from simpler fermentation approaches. The first is the importance of maintaining dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L throughout the brewing process — below this threshold, anaerobic organisms begin to dominate, potentially producing phytotoxic compounds and human pathogens. The second principle is organism diversity: healthy compost tea should contain representatives from all four major groups of the [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. Each group performs distinct functions: bacteria decompose simple organic compounds and compete with pathogens on leaf surfaces; fungi decompose complex materials like lignin and cellulose while forming [[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]] associations with plant roots; protozoa (flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates) consume bacteria and release plant-available nitrogen through the microbial loop; and beneficial nematodes regulate bacterial and fungal populations while contributing additional nutrient cycling. The third principle is that the compost tea must be matched to the plant's needs — bacterial-dominant teas suit annual vegetables and grasses, while fungal-dominant teas suit perennials, trees, and shrubs. See [[bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]] for detailed guidance on balancing these communities and [[aact-compost-organism-levels]] for target organism densities.

### Key Differences From Other Compost Tea Methods

Ingham's actively aerated compost tea (AACT) differs significantly from other liquid organic amendments, and the distinctions matter for both efficacy and safety. Fermented compost tea (FCT), the simplest method, involves steeping compost in water for days to weeks without aeration. This process inevitably goes anaerobic, producing a tea dominated by anaerobic organisms that may include pathogens like Clostridium and E. coli variants. While FCT can contain nutrients leached from the compost, its biological value is unpredictable and potentially harmful. Compost extract, another distinct category, is made by vigorously agitating compost in water for a short period (minutes) without brewing. This extracts existing organisms from the compost without allowing them to multiply — useful when you need a quick application but don't have time to brew. Leachate is the liquid that drains from the bottom of a compost pile or worm bin — it can contain soluble nutrients but may also harbor pathogens if the composting process was incomplete. Plant tea is made by steeping plant material (comfrey, nettle, kelp) in water and is primarily a nutrient source rather than a biological inoculant. Only AACT maintains consistent aerobic conditions that allow beneficial organisms to multiply by 10,000 to 100,000 times during a 24-hour brew cycle while suppressing anaerobic organisms. See [[aact-definitions-and-types-ingham]] for complete definitions and [[aact-pathogen-safety-ingham]] for safety testing protocols.

### Compost Tea in Broader Soil Management

Ingham's compost tea system is designed as one component of a comprehensive soil management strategy rather than a standalone solution. In her framework, compost tea serves as a rapid inoculation tool that can quickly establish beneficial microbial populations on plant surfaces and in the root zone, but it works best when combined with practices that support long-term soil health. These supporting practices include minimizing tillage (which disrupts fungal networks), maintaining [[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] diversity through cover cropping and organic matter additions, avoiding synthetic fertilizers and pesticides that harm non-target soil organisms, and maintaining appropriate soil moisture and pH. Ingham's approach contrasts with conventional agriculture's reliance on chemical inputs by treating the soil as a living ecosystem that, when properly managed, can provide plants with all the nutrients they need while suppressing disease organisms. The compost tea acts as a "microbial transplant" — analogous to a probiotic for soil — that introduces or reinforces beneficial organism populations. Foliar applications of compost tea create a competitive biological barrier on leaf surfaces, occupying the ecological niches that pathogenic fungi and bacteria would otherwise colonize. Soil drenches of compost tea inoculate the rhizosphere with organisms that cycle nutrients, fix nitrogen, solubilize phosphorus, and protect roots from pathogenic organisms. The frequency of application depends on the system: high-intensity vegetable production may benefit from weekly foliar sprays during the growing season, while perennial systems like orchards may only need annual or semi-annual applications.
