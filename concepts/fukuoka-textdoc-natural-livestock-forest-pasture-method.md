## The Question of Efficiency

Is grazing, which allows one person to raise hundreds of head without
doing anything, really inefficient? Is it not, rather, the most efficient
form of production there is? The modern factory system concentrates
animals in confined spaces, requires massive inputs of processed feed,
antibiotics, and energy for climate control, while producing inferior
meat, eggs, and milk. By contrast, open grazing requires almost no
inputs beyond the land itself and produces food of far superior quality.

## Forest Grazing with Cattle

There is no need to worry that cows or other animals cannot be raised in
uncultivated pastures. They can be raised in mixed woods or even in
mountain forests planted with Japanese cypress or pine. Grasses and
underbrush have to be cut the first seven or eight years after planting
trees on a mountain, but the labor of cutting the brush can be eliminated
very nicely by raising cows. The grazing cattle may slightly damage a few
young saplings along a fixed path through the cypresses, but the planted
saplings will remain almost entirely unaffected.

This may seem hard to believe, but it is only natural when we recall that
animals in nature do not indiscriminately ravage anything unrelated to
what they eat. Obviously, a natural forest would be even more ideal than
a reforested area. The key insight is that animals naturally select their
food and movement patterns in ways that maintain rather than destroy the
ecosystem they inhabit.

## Goats and Sheep on Rough Terrain

Even fields covered with a thick growth of wild roses and creepers that
seem worthless for grazing can be used to raise goats and sheep, which
love to feed on these intractable shrubs and vines and could clean up the
undergrowth in the densest jungle. Different livestock species fill
different ecological niches, and the natural farmer exploits these
complementarities rather than forcing all animals into uniform
conditions.

## Mixed Species Polyculture

What happens when we let pigs, chickens, and rabbits graze together with
the cows in an orchard? The pigs like to root up the ground looking for
the insects and earthworms they are fond of in valleys and damp areas;
they are like small tractors that dig up the soil. Just sow some clover
and grain in the turned soil, and with the cow and pig droppings, you
should get a fine growth of pasturage. Once this pasture grass begins to
nourish, then you should be able to raise chickens, goats, and rabbits
in the same way.

This polyculture approach mimics the natural diversity of grazing
ecosystems, where multiple species coexist and contribute to soil health,
pest control, [[beneficial-insects-cannabis]] that help control the
tick population. The natural approach to parasite management is not to
eradicate parasites with chemicals but to create conditions in which
parasite populations are naturally regulated by their predators and by
the health of the host animals.
