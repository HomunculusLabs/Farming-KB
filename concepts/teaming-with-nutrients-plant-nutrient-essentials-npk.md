# Plant Nutrient Essentials: NPK
Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK) form the foundation of plant nutrition.
These three macronutrients are used by plants in the greatest quantities after
carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, and they are the elements represented on every
fertilizer label worldwide. Understanding their roles at the cellular level
allows gardeners to make informed decisions about fertilization rather than
relying on advertising or label pictures.

## The Law of the Minimum and the Rise of NPK

[[von-liebig-law-of-the-minimum-and-fertilizer-history]]
states that plant growth is limited by the least abundant essential mineral, no
matter how abundant the others are. The application of this law revealed that
nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) were used by plants in the
greatest quantities and became the focus of modern fertilization. Today every
fertilizer package carries three numbers representing the percentage by weight
of N, P2O5 (phosphorus pentoxide), and K2O (potassium oxide).
These label conventions are historical artifacts from the way chemists used
to measure phosphorus and potassium. To convert label phosphorus to actual
elemental P, multiply by 0.44; for potassium to actual K, multiply by 0.83.
Many gardeners think they only need to look for these three numbers to know
which fertilizer to use, but fourteen mineral nutrients are essential -- not
just three. Focusing solely on NPK can lead to deficiencies in calcium,
magnesium, sulfur, and the [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]]
if the [[soil-food-web]] is not functioning properly.
Laws require that the percentages of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium be
listed on fertilizer packages. This is why every fertilizer package carries the
nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium trilogy on its label. It is also why many people
don't consider compost or manures to be fertilizers -- these are instead
classified as soil amendments because in most cases the amounts of NPK vary
from batch to batch and are usually not measured for labeling purposes.

## Nitrogen (N)

Nitrogen is the backbone of amino acids and proteins, including all enzymes
required for cellular activity. It is also the base element for nucleotides
(DNA and RNA) and is an essential part of chlorophyll (C55H72MgN4O5) -- four
nitrogen atoms per molecule. Without nitrogen, there is no photosynthesis,
which explains why nitrogen-deficient lawns turn yellow.
Nitrogen is mobile in plants. When nitrogen is scarce, the plant robs it from
older cells to sustain new growth, so deficiency symptoms (yellowing/chlorosis)
appear first in older leaves. Most plants are 3-4% nitrogen by weight.

## Phosphorus (P)
Phosphorus is critical for energy metabolism as the base of ATP (adenosine
triphosphate). ATP has two bonds between three phosphorus atoms; breaking these
bonds releases energy for cellular processes, while making them stores energy.
Plant cells have enzymes that split phosphorus from ATP and rebind it to ADP.
Without phosphorus, there isn't energy to sustain growth.
Phosphorus is also a component of DNA and RNA, the molecules storing and
translating the genetic code. As part of phospholipids, it is a major component
of cell membranes -- the gate-keeping system controlling what enters every
cell. No new cells can be made at root or shoot apical meristems without
phosphorus.
Phosphorus is mobile in plants. Deficiency causes stunted growth and
bluish-green leaves (from sugar accumulation when energy is insufficient to
use them). Symptoms appear first in older leaves.

### Phosphorus in Soil

Phosphorus ultimately comes from weathering of apatite rock. It enters soils
in organic form (decaying matter) and inorganic form (adsorbed to clay and
organic matter). It is so tightly adsorbed that only about 1 lb per acre may
be dissolved and available to plants. The attraction is an ionic one, binding
the phosphorus in place, and as a result it does not move far in soil.
Because it moves so little in soil, roots must grow continuously to maintain
new contact, and 95% of plants rely on [[symbiotic-fungi-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-mechanisms]]
for phosphorus uptake. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are best known for freeing phosphorus,
which is so tightly bound to soil particles that little of it is readily
available to plants. Although phosphorus is probably the nutrient most moved by
them, nitrogen, copper, and zinc are also brought to the root by mycorrhizal
fungi.

### The Phosphorus Crisis

Global phosphorus supplies are declining precipitously. The Global Phosphorus
Research Initiative estimates mined supplies will be insufficient in 30-40
years, with 95% of remaining mineable phosphorus in Morocco, China, South
Africa, Jordan, and the US. Over 22 million tons are applied to fields
annually. Gardeners must adopt sustainable practices to avoid testing the Law
of the Minimum on this essential nutrient.

### Phosphorus and pH

When pH gets below 6, phosphorus in the form HPO4-2 becomes much less
available because at lower pH this anion bonds more strongly to the cations
of iron and aluminum. It is fixed, meaning it is not usable. If the pH gets
above 7, too much becomes fixed by reacting with calcium and forming calcium
phosphate. This renders phosphorus increasingly unavailable to plants in
sufficient quantities. The sweet spot for [[phosphorus-availability]] is the
narrow range around pH 6.0-6.5.

## Potassium (K)

Potassium is the only essential nutrient that is not a constituent of any
structural part of a plant. Its role is purely regulatory. As a positively
charged ion (K+), potassium is the key solute in the cell cytosol, where it
can exist in high concentrations without damage. Potassium ions play the key
role in water movement into and out of cells -- guard cells open and close
stomata based on potassium concentration differentials, regulating CO2 and
water levels for the entire plant.
Potassium also regulates more than 60 key enzymatic reactions, is crucial for
starch formation (storing photosynthetic sugar), and is essential for sugar
movement throughout the plant. Normally, concentrations of nutrients are
greater inside a root cell than outside in the soil. When there is a very low
concentration of potassium ions outside the cell, potassium can be actively
transported into the cell -- this says something about the importance of
potassium to the operation of the plant cell.
Potassium is mobile in plants. Deficiency appears first in older leaves as
dead spots (cells die when water/mineral balance cannot be maintained) and
wilting (stomata fail to operate properly). Potassium is held by negative
charges on clay and organic matter but not tightly adsorbed, so it remains
readily available. It is the 8th most abundant element (2.5% of Earth's
crust).
Potassium uptake is the nutrient most affected by compacted soils. A whopping
50 percent reduction can occur in compacted soils. Because potassium is
required for the regulation of carbon dioxide and water levels, which are both
important for photosynthesis, it is no wonder plants become stunted in
anaerobic soils.

## Calcium, Magnesium, and Sulfur

Beyond NPK, three additional macronutrients are used in large quantities.
While not part of the NPK label, deficiencies in any of these can be just as
limiting to plant growth.
