## Soil Food Web Overview

## Definition

A soil food web is a complex system of interconnected food chains linking all the
organisms that live in and on soil. Unlike a simple food chain (A eats B eats C), a
food web consists of many cross-linked chains, creating an intricate network of
predator-prey relationships. Each soil environment has a different set of organisms
and thus a different soil food web. The concept was popularized by Dr. Elaine
Ingham at Oregon State University, who wrote the foreword to "Teaming with
Microbes."

## The Web"s Members

A mere teaspoon of good garden soil contains staggering numbers of organisms: a
billion invisible bacteria, several yards of [[comparison-biointensive-vs-square-foot-gardening]]), centipedes, springtails, ants, slugs, beetle larvae, and
more. An acre of good garden soil contains 2,000 pounds of bacteria, 2,400 pounds
of fungi, 900 pounds each of earthworms, arthropods, and algae, and 133 pounds of
protozoa. All share a common denominator: every organism needs carbon-based
energy to survive.

## Plants Are in Control

The soil food web always starts with the plant. Plants produce chemicals called
exudates (carbohydrates and proteins) through their roots, which wake up, attract,
and grow specific beneficial bacteria and fungi in the rhizosphere -- a zone
extending about a millimeter from the root surface. Individual plants can control
the numbers and types of fungi and bacteria attracted by the exudates they
produce, changing these populations depending on their nutrient needs throughout

-- directly in the rhizosphere, right where plant roots can absorb them. Soil
