## Bee Range

Bees form a hive or clustered hive site and range over an area about 2.5 km radius. Commercial registered hive sites are thus sited 5 km apart in order to restrict disease transmission from hive site to hive site. As many as 100-150 hives are commonly grouped at one site, depending on the richness of the range resource.

## Hive Site Selection

The hive site should be:
- Sheltered from extremes of cold, wet, wind, and sun
- Not atypical of the climate of the range (or bees are tempted out in adverse weather and perish)
- Close to water (bees use large quantities to cool hives)
- Accessible for hive management and honey extraction

### Water Access
Access to water is most safely achieved by providing soaked mats or hessian at pond edges, so that bees cannot drown while drinking. Small ponds or troughs free of dragonflies give less losses than large ponds (where dragonflies are efficient predators).

## Foraging Behaviour

Bees prefer to fly 100 m or more to forage, and their flight assists in the evaporation of nectar to honey, so that forage species are planted this distance or more from hives. Bees more efficiently harvest clumped rather than scattered nectar sources, so hedgerows, fields, or clumps of preferred forage species are better than a scatter of the same species in a mosaic of individual plants.

### Cold Wind Protection
Cold winds most restrict foraging, so that hedgerow (even low hedgerow of 0.5-1 m) is essential cover, preferably leading from the hive site (bee village) to the forage. Such hedges can be made of rosemary, Acacia, or even soil ridges with catmint, capeweed, thyme, or field daisies.

## Bee Forage Species

### Early Pollen Sources
Willows (especially pussywillow), Acacias, pines, and vine crops yielding early pollen are very beneficial on range.

### Propolis Sources
Bees gather propolis (a hard waxy substance used to plug wind gaps or repaint hives) from Xanthorrhoeas, poplars, and pines. Very few sources of propolis are needed in the whole range.

### Forage Categories
It is best to have 35 main forage species over the whole season, as species such as clover and eucalypts may vary in yield from year to year.

#### Classic (Highly Preferred) Honeys
- Heather honeys: sage, thyme, catmint, rosemary
- Pine tree or basswood (Tilia spp.) honeys
- Marsh tupelo honeys (subtropics)
- Leatherwood (Eucryphia) honeys of Chile and Tasmania
- Citrus honeys

#### Bulk Honeys
- Clovers
- Eucalypts
- Acacias
- Mustard and buckwheat
- Blackberry, raspberry, apples (Rosaceae family)
- Mixed garden honey

## Pollen

Pollen species are selected for a good spread of yield over the season. Pollen traps are fitted to hives at periods of high yield. Hives should yield about the same weight of pollen as honey, and pollen provides a high-protein additive to flours or starchy foods.

Special pollens from some Acacia and goldenrod may cause asthma if inhaled by susceptible people. Eaten, these honeys or pollens act to prevent allergic reaction.

## Temperature Management

Bees ventilate hives as a "super organism," inhaling and exhaling in about a 1.2 minute rhythm. Hive temperatures are normally about 21 degrees C. In cold weather, hive insulation is important (in Denmark bees are over-wintered in thatched attic areas, or stored in insulated hive houses). In hot summers, hives need shade and good ventilation, or honey is lost as energy used in cooling (fanning) by the hive.

## Integrated Bee Farm Design

Having designed the physical outline of a bee range (which in truth resembles any well-sheltered farm), local lists of honey plants can be consulted for path-sides, road verges, hedgerow, and forest. The same area presents a unique opportunity to grow crops dependent on bee pollination: kiwifruit, bramble berries, smallfruit, mustards and Brassicas, clovers, apples or pome fruits, buckwheat, Acacia, stone pine.

Crops within a mile of hives will out-yield crops in bee-deficient areas by a factor of 3-10 times.
