# The Opium Wars and the Origins of Drug Diplomacy

In Food of the Gods, Terence McKenna devotes considerable attention to the Opium Wars of the nineteenth century as a paradigmatic example of how dominator cultures use psychoactive substances as instruments of political and economic control. The Opium Wars represent, in McKenna's analysis, the origin point of modern international drug diplomacy — a pattern of state-sponsored drug trafficking that has continued into the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.




## Origins of the Opium Trade

The prohibition of tobacco smoking in China by the last emperor of the Ming Dynasty (1628-1644) inadvertently led frustrated tobacco addicts to experiment with smoking opium. Before this time, the smoking of opium was not known anywhere. This illustrates a recurring pattern in drug policy: the suppression of one substance inevitably leads to involvement with another. By 1793, opium and tobacco were being routinely smoked together throughout China.

Beginning in 1729, the Chinese had strictly prohibited the importation and sale of opium. Despite these prohibitions, opium imports brought by the Portuguese from plantations in Goa continued to rise. By 1830 more than 25,000 chests of opium were being illegally imported into China annually, driven by English financial interests that felt threatened by the prohibitions.




## The First Opium War (1839-1842)

The incident that triggered open conflict was the destruction of 20,000 chests of opium by Chinese authorities. In 1838 the emperor Tao-Kwang sent Commissioner Lin to Canton to end the illegal opium trade. When official orders to remove the contraband were ignored, Commissioner Lin burned both the Chinese warehouses on land and the British ships awaiting unloading in the harbor. Chroniclers who witnessed the event recalled that the aroma was "incomparable."

The British response was decisive military action. In 1840 Chusan was captured, and the following year the British bombarded and destroyed forts on the Canton River. The local Chinese commander agreed to cede Hong Kong and pay an indemnity of six million Chinese silver dollars. The First Opium War established the fundamental pattern: military force deployed to protect the profits of an illegal drug trade.




## The Second Opium War and Legalization

Fifteen years later a second war broke out, also ending unsuccessfully for China. Shortly afterward the Treaty of Tientsin legalized the Chinese opium traffic. McKenna argues that this incident became the model for much larger forays into international drug trading by twentieth-century governments. It demonstrated clearly that the potential marketability of new drugs can and will overwhelm institutional forces that oppose them.

The pattern established by England's nineteenth-century opium diplomacy has been repeated, McKenna contends, in Central Intelligence Agency collusion in the international heroin and cocaine trade of the modern era. The Opium Wars established the template: identify a lucrative drug market, deploy state power to protect it, and criminalize domestic use while profiting from foreign sales.




## Opium in the Classical World

McKenna traces opium's history far deeper than the nineteenth century. The opium poppy (Papaver somniferum) has been in the physicians' armamentarium since at least 1600 B.C. An Egyptian medical treatise of that period prescribed opium for crying children, just as Victorian nannies dosed infants with opiate-laced Godfrey's Cordial.

In ancient Greece, the poppy was known as "the destroyer of grief." The Greeks consecrated it to Nyx (goddess of night), Morpheus (god of dreams), and Thanatos (god of death) — summarizing all its properties in the deities to whom it was offered. Early Minoan texts testify to extensive poppy cultivation on both Crete and Pylos during the Late Minoan period, where the poppy head served as an ideogram in financial tallies. The confusion of grain and poppy is understandable, as Demeter was the goddess of both.

The connection between opium and the [[mckenna-eleusinian-mysteries]] is also noted: iconographic confusion exists between the poppy flower and the pomegranate, both plants associated with the Mysteries. The transformation from mushroom-based ecstasy to opium-based intoxication may represent the late phase of Minoan shamanic spirituality, with the aniconic column symbolizing mushroom roots and the poppy representing the new botanical sacrament.




## Paracelsus and the Revival of Opium

After opium use nearly ceased in Europe during the medieval period, it was Paracelsus, the famed "father of chemotherapy," who revived interest in the substance. The sixteenth-century Swiss alchemist advocated and used opium on a lavish scale, claiming to possess a "secret remedy" called laudanum that was "superior to all other heroic remedies." His followers, including the alchemist van Helmont, became known as "Doctor Opiatus" — the first "croaker" or addiction specialist.




## De Quincey and the Literature of Opium

Thomas De Quincey, like Timothy Leary in the 1960s, was able to convey the visionary power of opium intoxication. In his Confessions of an English Opium-Eater, De Quincey almost single-handedly created the cultural image of opium intoxication and invented the form of the drug "confession" — the primary genre of subsequent drug literature. His descriptions of Piranesi's endless ascending staircases, drawn from opium dreams, remain among the most powerful evocations of altered consciousness in the English language.

The fifty years following De Quincey's Confessions saw a deep grappling with opium's impact on creativity. De Quincey pioneered the deliberate study, from within personal experience, of how dreams and visions are formed, how opium intensifies them, and how they are re-composed and used in conscious art. He learned his waking technique partly from observation of how the mind works in dreams and reveries under the influence of opium.




## The Hypocrisy of Drug Policy

McKenna uses the opium trade as a lens for understanding the broader hypocrisy of dominator drug policy. The same British establishment that used military force to open Chinese markets to opium simultaneously enacted draconian domestic laws against opium use at home. The drug was profitable when sold to foreigners but dangerous when consumed by British citizens.

This double standard extends to the modern era, where McKenna argues the [[mckenna-opium-tobacco-paradoxical-attitudes]] toward drugs cannot be explained by pharmacology or public health but only by politics and economics. The drugs most aggressively prohibited — psychedelics, cannabis, coca — are generally the least harmful, while the drugs most aggressively promoted — sugar, alcohol, tobacco, caffeine — are among the most harmful.
