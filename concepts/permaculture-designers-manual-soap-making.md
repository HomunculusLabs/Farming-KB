The manual also references soap production and its role in appropriate technology for self-reliant communities.

## Soap Composition and Soil Health

### Typical Detergent Analysis

A typical analysis of a powdered detergent or a soap could include:
- Sodium or potassium salts or polysulphates
- Silicates, sulphates or bicarbonates
- Borates
- Residual biocides (concentrated in animal fats) e.g. DDT, Dieldren, Hexachlor from dairy cattle
- Additives such as resins (hardeners), scents, dyes, and brighteners
- Faecal bacteria and viral or worm pathogens from washing

### Effects on Soil and Plants

The manual identifies several concerns with commercial detergents:

**Sodium and Potassium Salts**
- Can deflocculate soil clays at concentrations greater than 1,000 ppm
- Can damage plant leaves through direct contact
- Particularly problematic when the basic water supply is already saline

**Borates**
- At greater than 0.5 ppm, can create excessive boron concentration in soils
- Above 1.0 ppm, harmful to soil life and plants

**Biocides in Animal Fats**
- Residual biocides concentrated in animal fats used in soap manufacture
- These persist through wastewater treatment and affect soil biology
- Include DDT, Dieldren, Hexachlor from dairy cattle

## Permaculture Soap Recommendations

### Plain Soaps for Crops

The manual strongly advocates using plain soaps on crops wherever possible. More complex pollutants should be routed to tree systems (well-monitored), as woody perennials can cope better than garden vegetables and allow more time for decomposition of long-term pollutants.

### Special Soaps for Critical Areas

In critical areas, especially in arid or delicate environments:
- Create special soaps from unpolluted oils, potash or sodium
- These can be used safely on food crops irrigated with greywater
- Avoid all additives (scents, dyes, brighteners, resins)
- Test soil and plant response regularly

### Potash Soaps

The older potash soaps are preferred over modern detergents for garden use. Potash soaps:
- Break down more readily in soil systems
- Do not contain the problematic phosphates found in many detergents
- The phosphorus problem is in detergents, not in potash soaps
- Can be made locally from wood ashes and fats or oils

## Soap Making from Local Resources

### Potash from Wood Ashes

Wood ashes are a traditional source of potassium carbonate (potash), the alkali needed for soap making. The process involves:
1. Leaching wood ashes with water to extract potassium carbonate
2. Boiling the lye solution to concentrate it
3. Testing concentration with an egg or potato (float test)
4. Combining with fats or oils
5. Cooking until saponification is complete

### Fat and Oil Sources

In a permaculture system, fats and oils for soap making can come from:
- Animal fats (tallow, lard) from livestock
- Plant oils (olive, coconut, palm)
- Used cooking oil (filtered)
- Bee's wax additions for harder soap

## Soap and Water Quality

### Hard and Soft Water

The manual discusses the properties of alkali (soaps or carbonates) and their interaction with water hardness:
- Hard water contains calcium and magnesium ions
- These ions react with soap to form insoluble scum
- Water softeners exchange calcium and magnesium ions for sodium ions, allowing soaps to lather
- (Acid) waters treated differently; used with soap, they lather easily
- Washing with soaps and detergents is itself a form of water treatment

### Soap in Dryland Systems

Soap, especially in drylands, is noted as a product where citrus and grains in appropriate association can provide materials for local self-reliance. This connects soap production to broader dryland permaculture strategies.
