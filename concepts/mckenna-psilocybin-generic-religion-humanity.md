# McKenna on Psilocybin as the Generic Religion of Humanity

## Overview

A central thesis of *Food of the Gods* is that psilocybin-containing mushrooms constituted a "generic religion" — a universal, cross-cultural religious practice that preceded and gave rise to all later organized religions. McKenna argues that the ecstatic mushroom experience was not one religion among many but was the original religious impulse itself, the generative source from which all subsequent religious forms were derived. The suppression of this mushroom religion, in his view, represents the single most consequential event in human [[shroom-cultural-history-hippie-revolution-and-counterculture]].

## The Term "Generic Religion"

McKenna uses the term "generic religion" to denote a religious practice that is:

- **Pre-ethnic**: Arising before the differentiation of human populations into distinct ethnic and linguistic groups, and thus shared across all early human cultures
- **Plant-based**: Rooted in a specific psychoactive organism rather than in abstract theological propositions
- **Experiential rather than doctrinal**: Centered on direct, unmediated experience of the divine rather than on belief systems, moral codes, or institutional hierarchies
- **Self-evidencing**: Capable of producing genuine religious experiences — visions, feelings of unity, encounters with the sacred — that do not require faith or interpretation to validate

The mushroom religion was "generic" in the same sense that a generic drug is one that precedes all branded versions: it was the original, unbranded, universal form of human religiosity, from which all later "branded" religions — Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, Hinduism — were derived.

## Evidence from Comparative Religion

McKenna builds his case through the identification of mushroom-related motifs across multiple independent religious traditions:

### The Vedic Soma Cult

The Rig Veda's nearly 120 hymns to Soma constitute "the earliest scriptural writings" of the Indo-European peoples. Soma is described as "the pillar of the World," a plant whose consumption grants visions, immortality, and divine knowledge. The ecstatic language of the Soma hymns — "great exciters of exhilaration," "swift as thought," "all-pervading" — is consistent with psilocybin intoxication. McKenna argues that Soma's connection to cattle (Soma is compared to "a cow with milk to the calf") and its mountain origin point to a mushroom that grew in the dung of cattle on the Anatolian plateau.

### The Iranian Haoma

The pre-Zoroastrian religion of Iran used "Haoma," cognate with Soma, as its central sacrament. Zoroaster's attempted suppression of Haoma — calling it "the filthiness of this intoxicant" — represents one of the earliest recorded instances of the dominator culture's rejection of the mushroom religion. Yet Haoma survived even Zoroaster's ban, remaining "the central liturgical act of Zoroastrianism ever since that religion developed liturgical worship."

### The Eleusinian Mysteries

The Greek Eleusinian Mysteries, conducted for nearly two thousand years, centered on a potion — the *kykeon* — that induced visionary experiences. McKenna connects the Eleusinian tradition to the earlier Minoan mushroom religion and argues that the *kykeon* was originally or partly derived from ergotized grain containing psychoactive alkaloids related to the mushroom experience.

### Mesoamerican Mushroom Cults

The mushroom cults of Mesoamerica, documented by the Wassons among the Mazatec and still practiced today, represent the most direct surviving expression of the original mushroom religion. Maria Sabina's mushroom veladas, in which participants consume [[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]] in a ritual context, are essentially identical in structure and intention to the Soma ceremonies of ancient India — separated by thousands of years and thousands of miles but united by the same pharmacological agent and the same religious intent.

## The Universality Argument

McKenna's claim that psilocybin constituted a "generic" religion rests on the universality of mushroom-related symbolism across cultures that had no direct contact:

- **The serpent as botanical guardian**: Appears in Genesis, the Gilgamesh epic, Minoan Glaukos myth, Greek Asklepios, and Mesoamerican Quetzalcoatl
- **The mushroom as child**: Maria Sabina's "los ninos" parallels the Minoan Glaukos myth and the "alchemical children" of European folklore
- **The cattle-mushroom complex**: Sacred cattle appear in Vedic India, ancient Egypt, Minoan Crete, and African pastoral societies — all cultures where psilocybin mushrooms grow in cattle dung
- **The blue staining reaction**: The distinctive blue-black bruising of psilocybin mushrooms is reflected in the name Glaukos ("blue-gray") and may underlie the association of certain deities with blue or black coloring
- **The death-and-resurrection motif**: The mushroom's cycle of emergence, sporulation, and disappearance — and its capacity to "die" (be dried) and be "resurrected" (reconstituted) — is reflected in the dying-god myths of Osiris, Dionysus, Jesus, and Quetzalcoatl

McKenna argues that the probability of these motifs arising independently in unconnected cultures is vanishingly small. The more parsimonious explanation is that they share a common origin in a universal mushroom-using religious practice that was carried with migrating human populations from Africa into Asia, Europe, and the Americas.

### The Migration Vector

Human migration patterns from Africa support the universality argument. The "Out of Africa" migration that populated the rest of the world would have carried the mushroom religion along with it — not as a formal institution but as an embodied practice, embedded in the relationship between pastoral peoples and the mushrooms that grew in their cattle's dung. As populations diverged and lost contact, the mushroom religion diversified into the various forms documented in different cultures, but the core motifs — the serpent, the dying god, the sacred cattle, the visionary plant — remained recognizable across all of them.

## The Process of Suppression and Replacement

The transition from the generic mushroom religion to the specific "branded" religions of historical civilization followed a consistent pattern that McKenna traces through multiple cultures:

1. **Original practice**: Direct mushroom use produces ecstatic, boundary-dissolving religious experience
2. **Cultural elaboration**: The mushroom experience is elaborated into mythology, ritual, and social organization
3. **Ecological displacement**: Climate change, migration, or cultural change disrupts access to the mushroom
4. **Substitution**: Other psychoactive plants (opium, alcohol, ergot) are adopted as replacements
5. **Institutionalization**: The religious experience is codified into doctrine, priesthood, and hierarchy
6. **Suppression**: The original mushroom practice is forgotten, forbidden, or driven underground
7. **Amnesia**: The connection between the original mushroom religion and the later institutional religion is lost

This pattern is visible in the transition from Soma to Brahmanical Hinduism, from the Eleusinian Mysteries to Christianity, from Haoma to Zoroastrianism, and from Mesoamerican mushroom cults to Catholic syncretism.

## The Stropharia Cubensis Hypothesis

McKenna identifies *Stropharia cubensis* (now *Psilocybe cubensis*) as the most likely candidate for the original mushroom of the generic religion, for several reasons:

- **Coprophilic habit**: It grows on cattle dung, linking it to the cattle-mushroom complex that pervades ancient mythology
- **Tropical distribution**: Its natural range includes the African grasslands where human beings evolved and where McKenna places the origin of the mushroom-religion partnership
- **Consistent psychoactivity**: Unlike the unreliable *[[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]* (Wasson's Soma candidate), *Stropharia cubensis* produces reliable, dose-dependent visionary experiences
- **Cultural persistence**: It remains in active ceremonial use in Mesoamerica, the region nearest to its natural range that maintained the mushroom religion into historical times

### Ecological Requirements

*Stropharia cubensis* requires warm, moist grassland conditions — precisely the conditions that prevailed in the African tropical grasslands during the Pleistocene, when McKenna argues the original mushroom-human partnership formed. As global climate changed and these grasslands contracted, the mushroom's range shrank, and human populations that had relied on it for religious experience were forced to either migrate with the mushroom or find substitutes.

This ecological dependency explains, in McKenna's framework, why the mushroom religion persisted longest in tropical and subtropical regions (Mesoamerica, Southeast Asia, parts of Africa) and was earliest lost in temperate zones where the mushroom could not grow. The abandonment of the mystery was, at root, an ecological displacement — not a cultural choice but a consequence of climate change forcing human populations into environments where the sacred mushroom could no longer be found.

### Pharmacological Profile

McKenna contrasts *Stropharia cubensis* favorably with Wasson's *Amanita muscaria* hypothesis, noting that Wasson himself came to express "profound misgivings" about the fly-agaric identification. The active compounds in *Stropharia* — psilocybin and psilocin — are serotonin analogs that directly affect the brain's consciousness-generating systems, producing experiences qualitatively different from the deliriant effects of *Amanita muscaria*. The *Stropharia* experience is characterized by enhanced pattern recognition, synesthesia, visionary imagery, ego dissolution, and feelings of unity with nature — precisely the qualities described in the Soma hymns and attributed to the Eleusinian kykeon.

## Implications for Understanding Religion

If McKenna's thesis is correct, the history of religion is not a story of diverse cultures independently inventing spiritual practices but rather the story of a single, universal religious practice — the mushroom sacrament — being progressively forgotten, replaced, and institutionalized as human populations expanded, migrated, and differentiated. The great world religions are, in this view, diminished and distorted descendants of an original, direct, experiential communion with the sacred that required no intermediary, no hierarchy, and no doctrine — only the mushroom itself.

This framing has profound implications for contemporary debates about religious freedom, drug policy, and the nature of consciousness. If the original religious impulse was pharmacological rather than theological, then the prohibition of psychoactive plants is not merely a law enforcement issue but an act of religious suppression comparable to the destruction of temples, the burning of heretics, and the eradication of indigenous cultures.

### The Rediscovery Imperative

McKenna's argument leads to an explicit call for the rediscovery and reintegration of the mushroom sacrament into modern religious life. He frames this not as nostalgia for a lost past but as a practical necessity: the dominator culture that replaced the partnership model and its mushroom religion has produced ecological catastrophe, nuclear proliferation, and [[fukuoka-flavor-philosophy-spiritual-alienation-food]] on a planetary scale. The recovery of the original religious experience — through the responsible use of psilocybin — is, in McKenna's view, humanity's best hope for cultural renewal and ecological survival.

The "[[mckenna-archaic-revival-four-part-structure-paradise-paradise-lost-hell-regained]]" that McKenna advocates is precisely the revival of this generic religious impulse: the direct, experiential encounter with the sacred through the medium of the mushroom, stripped of institutional mediation and doctrinal overlay.
