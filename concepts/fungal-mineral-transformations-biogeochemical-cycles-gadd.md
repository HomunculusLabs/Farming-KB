# Fungal Mineral Transformations and Biogeochemical Cycling

## Overview

Fungi play fundamental but frequently overlooked roles in mineral
transformations and biogeochemical cycling. The discipline of geomycology
examines how fungi interact with rocks, minerals, and metals, acting as
agents of weathering, elemental cycling, and soil formation. Fungi can
dissolve minerals and mobilize metals at higher pH values and over a wider
redox range than bacteria, making them uniquely important geological agents
in terrestrial ecosystems.

## Geomycology: An Emerging Discipline

Geomycology is the study of fungal roles in geological processes, bridging
[[mycelial-networks]]. Fungi have been components of terrestrial
[[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] since the Ordovician period (480-460 million years
ago).

## Rock-Dwelling Fungi

Fungi colonize rocks through several ecological strategies. Epiliths grow
on rock surfaces (often as darkly pigmented microcolonial fungi with thick
walls); hypoliths are associated with the undersides of pebbles; endoliths
inhabit the rock subsurface; chasmoliths grow in pre-existing cracks and
fissures; cryptoendoliths grow inside cavities among crystal grains; and
euendoliths actively bore into submerged rock. Filamentous fungi across
zygomycetes, ascomycetes, and basidiomycetes occupy all these niches.

Fungi have been found on nearly all rock types including limestone, marble,
granite, sandstone, basalt, andesite, and gneiss. Lichen symbioses with
algae or cyanobacteria represent one of the most successful strategies for
fungal survival in extreme sub-aerial environments and are extremely
biogeochemically active. Oligotrophic rock-dwelling fungi scavenge nutrients
from air, rainwater, organic residues on [[fungal-community-profiling-rock-mineral-surfaces-gadd]], and waste products
of other microorganisms.

## Mechanisms of Fungal Weathering

Fungi weather minerals through two synergistic categories of action:
biomechanical and biochemical. Biochemical processes are generally considered
more important than mechanical degradation.

### Biomechanical Weathering

Direct biomechanical weathering occurs through hyphal penetration into rock
along crystal planes, cleavage, cracks, and grain boundaries. Hyphal turgor
pressure provides the mechanical force for penetration, facilitated by
thigmotropic growth (contact guidance toward grooves and pores) and
lubrication with mucilaginous slime containing acidic and metal-chelating
metabolites. Indirect biomechanical weathering occurs through shrinking and
swelling of hydrated mucilage produced by fungal colonies.

### Biochemical Weathering

The three main mechanisms of mineral solubilization are acidolysis
(proton-promoted dissolution), complexolysis (ligand-promoted dissolution),
and redoxolysis ([[fungal-metal-redox-transformations-detailed]]). Acidolysis occurs when fungi
acidify their microenvironment through proton excretion, organic acid
secretion, and carbonic acid formation from respiratory CO2. Complexolysis
involves the excretion of metal-complexing metabolites including carboxylic
acids (especially oxalic and citric acid), amino acids, siderophores, and
[[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]]. When fungi excrete large amounts of a strong chelator
like oxalate, the solubilization mechanism shifts from proton-promoted to
ligand-promoted dissolution.

### Carbonate Precipitation

Microbial carbonate precipitation coupled with silicate weathering provides
an important CO2 sink. Many near-surface limestones and calcic soil horizons
