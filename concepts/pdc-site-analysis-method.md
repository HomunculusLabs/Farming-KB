# Site Analysis Method

The analysis of a site is the systematic process of understanding all the forces, resources, and patterns that affect a design. It begins at the macroscale and works inward to smaller and smaller detail -- following the permaculture principle of designing from patterns to details.

## The Broad Scale Site

Before analyzing the specific site, understand its place in the broader context. Start globally and zoom in progressively:

### Scale Hierarchy
1. **Global**: What hemisphere? What continent?
2. **Country**: National climate patterns and regulations
3. **Region**: Regional ecology, bioregion, and watershed
4. **State/Province**: State-level climate data and regulations
5. **County**: County-level maps, zoning, and resources
6. **Local**: Nearby roads, towns, cities, trash dumps, forests, neighbors
7. **Site**: The specific property being designed

### Why Broad Scale Matters

A disciplined permaculture designer would do a full analysis at each step. While this may seem redundant, the data gathered at broader scales applies to dozens, if not hundreds, of future projects. For example, a full analysis of your county produces data usable for any future project in that county.

### Resource Acquisition

Large-scale maps of the watershed, county, and state can be acquired at your local County or City Office.

## Client Interview

Before beginning physical site analysis, conduct a thorough [[williams-pdc-client-interview-process]]. The interview establishes the human context for the design and determines what the analysis should prioritize.

## General Climate Data

Every permaculture analysis should include:
- **Solar altitude and azimuth**: Sun angles through the year for passive solar design
- **Prevailing seasonal winds**: Wind direction and intensity by season
- **Flood plains**: Whether the site is in a flood zone
- **Average winter and summer temperatures**: For plant selection and shelter design
- **Average annual rainfall**: For water system sizing and plant selection
- **Microclimates**: Identify and map any microclimates on the base map

## History of the Site

Permaculture Principle #1 calls for designing from patterns to details, which includes understanding the historical patterns of the site over time. Research should cover:

- Major climatic events (floods, fires, droughts, freezes)
- Human habitation history
- Historical plants and crops used on the site
- Design precedence from the past
- Previous land uses (agriculture, industry, mining)
- Indigenous land management practices

By putting the history together, you can tell the "story of the site" to gain an understanding of its patterns over time.

## Indicator Species

Observe the site for plant and animal indicator species. These reveal soil conditions, water table levels, frost patterns, and overall ecosystem health without needing expensive testing. For example:
- Certain weeds indicate compacted or acidic soil
- Specific birds indicate healthy insect populations
- Moss growth patterns reveal moisture and shade conditions

## Inventories

Inventories are lists of available resources that already exist on the site. Create comprehensive lists of:

- **Plants**: Existing vegetation, trees, crops, weeds
- **Animals**: Wildlife, livestock, insects, pollinators
- **Building materials**: Timber, stone, clay, sand, gravel
- **Salvageable materials**: Existing structures, fencing, pipes, containers
- **Water sources**: Springs, streams, wells, runoff patterns
- **People power**: Available labor, skills, community resources
- **Community resources**: Local organizations, markets, networks

Plus lists of any other resources relevant to the project.

## Observation and Interaction

Permaculture Principle #1 calls for observation and interaction. Many experienced designers recommend not implementing any design until you have observed the site for a full year to see how it acts in each season. The most rigorous recommend three years of observation, since the first year may be a fluke.

However, most people cannot wait that long. At minimum, the designer should observe the site as often as possible and under as many different conditions as possible:
- During the day, afternoon, evening, and morning
- During rain and after rain
- When dry
- In different seasons
- If possible, camp out on the site several times

### Interaction

Permaculture Principle #1 calls not only for observation but interaction:
- Do small projects to see what effect they have
- Go for long hikes across the property
- Test soil infiltration in different areas
- Observe how the site responds to stimulus

## Soil Samples

Take soil samples and test infiltration rates from around the site. For a systematic approach, create a grid and test at regular intervals. For a less formal approach, sample where you observe differences that may indicate varying conditions:
- Different types of grasses or ground cover
- Different soil colorations
- Along slopes (top, middle, bottom)

Clearly number samples and track their locations. Color in areas on the base map to denote soil types, and place sample numbers on the working copy.

## Surface Water Flow

Using the topographical information from the working base map, chart the flow of water across the site. This helps determine what earthworks will be needed to slow, spread, and sink the flow of water. The ultimate goal is to ensure that all rainwater falling on the site stays on the site and infiltrates into the ground, or that any water draining from the site is cleansed by bioswales and deposited into natural waterways.

## Zoning

After completing the sector analysis, draw [[zone-sector-analysis-guide]] onto the base map. Zones rank areas by frequency of human impact, from Zone 0 (permanent structures) through Zone 5 (wilderness).
