# Fungal Biomonitoring

Fungi serve as powerful bioindicators of environmental quality and ecosystem health. Their sensitivity to environmental conditions, their roles in nutrient cycling, and their diverse community structures make them valuable tools for monitoring pollution, climate change, and habitat disturbance. Fungal biomonitoring encompasses a range of approaches from traditional fruiting body surveys to advanced molecular techniques and stable isotope analysis.

## Stable Isotope Biomonitoring

Natural abundance stable isotope analysis (d13C, d15N) provides a non-destructive method for assessing fungal functional roles and ecosystem processes. The usefulness of these isotopes depends on small but measurable differences in the isotope ratios of 13C:12C and 15N:14N, which result from fractionations during chemical, physical, and biological processing. Isotope ratios are determined by isotope ratio mass spectroscopy and expressed relative to international reference standards (Taylor and Fransson, 2006).

### Nitrogen Isotopes (d15N)

- **d15N as a trophic indicator**: ECM fungi are consistently enriched in 15N compared to both host plants and saprotrophic fungi. The degree of 15N enrichment correlates with fungal trophic strategy -- mycoheterotrophic fungi (parasitic on mycorrhizal networks) show the highest enrichment. This is because there is small discrimination against the heavier isotope 15N during metabolic processing, leading to progressively higher d15N values at higher trophic levels
- **Ecosystem nitrogen cycling**: Natural abundance of 15N in host plants is closely linked to mycorrhizal association type (Michelsen et al., 1998; Hobbie et al., 2000). It indicates the importance of ECM fungi in nitrogen cycling and in supplying host plants with nitrogen derived from organic sources. Variations from standard ratios in biological material are small, with total ranges within ecosystems usually c. -10 per mil to +20 per mil (Handley and Raven, 1992)
- **Saprotrophic-mycorrhizal divide**: The difference between ECM and saprotrophic fungi in isotope data has been consistently documented. Fully mycoheterotrophic orchids show distinct 15N and 13C enrichment compared to autotrophic plants (Trudell et al., 2003), reflecting their exploitation of mycorrhizal networks

### Carbon Isotopes (d13C)

- **d13C for carbon source tracking**: Isotopic signatures distinguish fungi utilizing recent photosynthate (mycorrhizal) versus older soil carbon (saprotrophic). Canopy layer effects are detectable, with fungi in different vertical positions showing distinct d13C values. The d13C of plant material varies considerably, from -40 to -9 per mil, with much of this variation accounted for by differences between C3 and C4 plants
- **Single-spore analysis**: Remarkably, d13C has been determined in single spores of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (Gigaspora marginata). Using the widely different d13C of C3 and C4 plants, Nakano et al. (1999) showed that the d13C of spores decreased linearly from the C4 plant to the proximity of the C3 plant in dual cultures
- **Ecosystem-level assessments**: Paired d15N and d13C measurements across fungal species can reveal the structure and function of belowground food webs, the degree of mycorrhizal vs. saprotrophic dominance, and carbon residence times in different ecosystem pools

### Limitations and Future Directions

Key limitations of stable isotope biomonitoring include insufficient understanding of fractionation during uptake and metabolic processing, the difficulty of distinguishing nitrogen sources in fungi utilizing mixed substrates, and the fact that at most perhaps only 5% of environmental fungi can be cultivated (Hawksworth, 2001), limiting controlled studies. Future applications include investigating species or community succession in saprotrophic fungi, and determining the extent to which late colonizers utilize the mycelium of earlier colonizers versus woody components (Frankland, 1998).

## Molecular Community Monitoring

### DGGE/TGGE Fingerprinting

Denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE) and temperature gradient gel electrophoresis (TGGE) are electrophoretic fingerprinting techniques that exploit differences in sequence composition of the 18S rRNA gene. Usually, a portion of the 18S rRNA gene is amplified (with all fragments having the same size, usually less than 500 bp) from environmental DNA. Members of the community are separated by differences in melting behaviour caused by variation within the 18S sequence arrangement for each species. These techniques have been applied to:

- Decomposing leaf litter at different stages of decay (Nikolcheva et al., 2003, 2005)
- Coniferous forest soils under different management regimes (Cullings et al., 2005; Hernesmaa et al., 2005)
- Wheat rhizosphere fungal communities (Smit et al., 1999)
- Rock-dwelling fungal communities from sandstone, where profiles demonstrated considerable variations between samples from the same geographical origin

### RISA and ARISA

Ribosomal intergenic spacer analysis (RISA) is a valuable tool for monitoring the structure and dynamics of complex communities. The basis for this technique is gel separation of gene fragments by length differentiation of the ITS region of the rDNA. Automated ribosomal intergenic spacer analysis (ARISA) is a high-throughput, automated version using a fluorescently labelled primer to exploit length variations within the ITS region. Community complexity ranges from approximately 30 to over 200 fungal peaks per profile, highlighting the sensitivity of this approach.

A study utilizing RISA and ARISA on weathered pegmatitic granite explored mineralogical influences on indigenous fungal communities (Gleeson et al., 2005). Average sample ribotype numbers ranged from 25 on K-feldspar to 4 on quartz, demonstrating that distinct fungal populations inhabited rocks of particular mineralogical types, and that individual chemical elements can exert selective pressure on fungal community structure.

### T-RFLP

Terminal restriction fragment length polymorphism (T-RFLP) analyses whole microbial community nucleic acids. Like ARISA, it is capable of higher resolution compared with commonly used gel electrophoresis systems. T-RFLP combined with ascomycete-selective primers revealed the same predominant species in saltmarsh cordgrass communities as direct microscopy and ascospore capture (Buchan et al., 2002, 2003).

### Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST)

MLST provides population-level resolution for monitoring pathogen spread, identifying outbreak sources, and tracking genetic changes in fungal populations over time. Internet-accessible databases (mlst.org, multilocus.net) enable global comparison of sequence types.

## Lichens as Air Quality Indicators

Lichens are classic bioindicators of air quality because they absorb nutrients and pollutants directly from the atmosphere. An estimated 6% of Earth's land surface is covered by lichen-dominated vegetation (Haas and Purvis, 2006). Lichen community composition, species diversity, and thallus morphology respond to:

- **SO2 pollution**: Lichens are highly sensitive to sulphur dioxide. Epiphytic lichen surveys are standardized monitoring tools in many countries and have been used for decades to map atmospheric SO2 deposition patterns. The decline and recovery of lichen communities tracks industrial pollution history
- **Heavy metal deposition**: Lichens can accumulate metals such as lead, copper, and radionuclides (Purvis, 1996), forming a variety of metal-organic biominerals during growth on metal-rich substrates (Purvis and Halls, 1996). Lichens therefore serve as passive samplers for atmospheric heavy metal deposition
- **Nitrogen deposition**: Nitrogen pollution favors nitrophilous lichen species and causes shifts in community composition, providing an integrated measure of chronic nitrogen deposition
- **Acid rain**: Combined effects of acid deposition on lichen communities integrate multiple pollutant stresses

Lichens also serve as bioindicators in the tropics, where foliicolous lichens on tree leaves can be used as indicators of forest continuity and air quality (Lucking, 1997).

## Mycorrhizal Monitoring

Mycorrhizal community composition and function respond to multiple environmental pressures:

- **Soil pH changes**: Acidification from pollution shifts ECM species composition. Different EM fungal species show distinct pH preferences, and acidification can eliminate sensitive species while favoring acid-tolerant taxa
- **Heavy metal contamination**: Shifts in ECM species composition on contaminated soils, with metal-tolerant species becoming dominant. Metal-tolerant ectomycorrhizal strains (e.g., of Paxillus involutus) can protect host plants from excessive metal accumulation
- **Forest management practices**: Clear-cutting, prescribed burning, and other management activities alter mycorrhizal community structure. The EM fungal genus Piloderma and Hebeloma crustuliniforme have been used as indicators of forest soil conditions
- **Climate change**: Altered phenology and host-fungus synchrony affect mycorrhizal function. Carbon allocation within EM mycelium was significantly greater when colonizing potassium feldspar patches than quartz patches (Rosling et al., 2004b), suggesting that climate-driven changes in mineral weathering rates could cascade through mycorrhizal networks

## Spore Monitoring

Airborne spore sampling (using volumetric spore traps) provides data on:

- **Seasonal patterns of fungal activity**: Spore concentrations vary seasonally and diurnally, peaking during warm, dry conditions in many ecosystems
- **Allergen exposure assessment**: Spores of Alternaria, Cladosporium, Aspergillus, and Penicillium dominate outdoor air spora and contribute to allergic rhinitis, asthma, and hypersensitivity pneumonitis
- **Detection of pathogen spread**: Monitoring for Coccidioides spores in desert regions, and other pathogenic fungi in agricultural and clinical settings
- **Biosecurity monitoring**: Detection of invasive fungal species through airborne spore trapping networks

## Rock and Mineral Community Monitoring

Lithic fungal communities are ubiquitous in the geological environment and play important roles in nutrient, metal, and carbon cycling (Gadd, 2000; Nannipieri et al., 2003). Diverse microbial communities have been found living in pore spaces of exposed rock from the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica (Torre et al., 2003), extreme hot and cold deserts (Staley et al., 1982; Sterflinger, 2000), and in rock substrates buried several kilometres below the Earth's surface. Monitoring these communities through molecular methods provides insights into geomicrobiological processes and environmental change.

Microscopical analyses of phototrophic epilithic microbial communities from the Roman Catacombs found that the most frequently encountered fungal isolates were Sporotrichum and Aspergillus spp. (Albertano and Urzi, 1999). Cultivation-dependent studies on sandstone identified members of Pleospora, Acremonium, Engyodontium, Stemphylium, and Penicillium (Melville, Zhu, and Gadd, unpublished).

## Challenges and Limitations

A persistent challenge in fungal biomonitoring is the reliance on morphological and physiological identification of cultivable species, which will never elucidate the inherent complexities of community dynamics. The majority of environmental species are refractory to laboratory cultivation, including biotrophs (rust and smut fungi), many basidiomycetes, arbuscular endomycorrhizas (Glomerales), and rock-dwelling fungi with low metabolic activity (Thorn et al., 1996; Sterflinger et al., 1998). This has led to systematic underestimation of fungal community complexity and diversity. DNA-based characterization techniques are becoming increasingly important for addressing these limitations, although bias in nucleic acid extraction yields remains a problem because lysis efficiency may vary between different species and between spores and mycelia (Prosser, 2002).
