# Mushroom Tropisms

Tropisms are directional growth responses in organisms, where the direction of the stimulus determines the direction of growth. In mushrooms ([[singh-basidiomycete-fungi-in-petroleum-hydrocarbon-cleanup]]), tropisms are critical for orienting the mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis body for optimal spore dispersal. Unlike phototropism in plants—which is primarily mediated by auxin redistribution—fungal tropisms rely on fundamentally different hormonal and physical mechanisms.

## Types of Tropisms in Mushrooms

### Phototropism (Light Response)

Mushrooms exhibit complex responses to mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis that vary by developmental stage and species. At least four distinct photo-responses have been documented in mushroom-forming fungi (Eger-Hummel, 1980):

- **Initiation of mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis**: Light is required to trigger the formation of primordia in many species. In [[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]], [[light-basidiocarp-initiation-psilocybe-cubensis-research]] requires mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis exposure (Badham, 1980).
- **Stipe elongation**: Light exerts an inhibitory effect on stipe (stem) elongation in many species, causing mushrooms grown in darkness to develop elongated, etiolated stems.
- **Directional growth**: The direction of mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis can influence the curvature of the growing stipe, though this is often secondary to gravitropic or anemotropic responses.
- **Spore production**: Light is necessary for sporulation in P. cubensis; mushrooms kept in darkness produce few or no viable mushroom-spore-print-collection|spores.

Light appears to influence tropic responses through at least two mechanisms: by heating the mushroom surface and increasing [[evaporative-demand-hypothesis-mushroom-tropism]] on the illuminated side, and by triggering hormonal responses associated with sporulation and cap development.

### Geotropism (Gravity Response)

Gravitropism is one of the most studied tropisms in mushrooms. The key responses include:

- **Negative gravitropism**: The stipe grows upward (against mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis), ensuring the cap is elevated for spore release.
- **Stage-dependent switching**: In P. cubensis, the gravitropic response changes character as the mushroom matures. During early stages (Stage 1–2), the stipe elongates upward. At Stage 2, a negative geotropic curvature occurs in fertile basidiocarps, turning the pileus upward from any deviated position to orient the gills downward.
- **Connection to sporulation**: The negative geotropic response at maturity is closely linked to spore formation. Sporeless mutants of P. cubensis do not exhibit this gravitropic sensitivity, suggesting that the hormones or signals associated with spore production also regulate gravitropic bending.
- **Equalization by rotation**: When basidiocarps are rotated horizontally to equalize the gravitational stimulus under constant illumination, growth becomes horizontal and pileus expansion is also horizontal.

### Anemotropism (Wind Response)

Anemotropism—the growth response to wind or air flow—is a less commonly studied but important tropism in mushrooms:

- **Growth into the wind**: Primordia of P. cubensis placed in a wind tunnel direct stipe growth into the air flow during early development (Stages 1–2). This occurs regardless of mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis direction or fertility.
- **Wind speed and boundary layer**: The mechanism likely involves redistribution of the boundary layer around the stipe. The boundary layer is thicker on the leeward (downwind) side, creating a differential in evaporative conditions.
- **Adaptive significance**: Anemotropic growth may position the cap in the most favorable location for spore dispersal by growing toward areas of greater air movement.

## Hormonal Basis of Mushroom Tropisms

The hormonal control of tropisms in mushrooms is distinct from that of higher plants:

- **Mushroom-specific hormones**: Several researchers have postulated the existence of mushroom-specific hormones involved in tropic responses (Gruen, 1963; Hagimoto, 1963). These are not auxins, gibberellins, or other plant hormones, though some mushrooms do contain plant hormones.
- **Link to lamellae (gills)**: Decapitation experiments have linked tropism-regulating hormones to the gill tissue. Agar diffusates from the gills can partially replace the influence of the pileus on stipe growth direction (Hagimoto and Konishi, 1960).
- **Ethylene involvement**: In Phycomyces blakesleeanus, ethylene has been suggested as a mediator of the avoidance response to physical barriers (Russo et al., 1975). A similar mechanism may operate in mushroom-forming fungi.
- **No auxin regulation**: While higher plants use auxin redistribution as the primary mechanism for directional growth, no evidence suggests that auxins regulate mushroom tropisms, despite their presence in some fungal tissues (Konishi and Hagimoto, 1961).

## Comparison with Other Fungi

The zygomycete Phycomyces blakesleeanus has served as a [[canary-grass-phototropism-model-organism]] for studying fungal tropisms and shows both similarities and differences with mushroom-forming basidiomycetes:

| Feature | Phycomyces | Psilocybe cubensis |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|
| Anemotropism | Present | Present |
| Phototropism | Strong | Moderate |
| Gravitropism | Present | Stage-dependent |
| Sporangiophore/stipe | Single sporangiophore | Multiple basidiocarps |
| Avoidance response | Well-documented | Less studied |
| Water vapor role | Critical | Likely involved |

Phycomyces shows both anemotropism and an avoidance response (growing away from physical barriers), and water vapor has been demonstrated as critical to these responses (Johnson and Gamow, 1971). The stipe curvature mechanism in P. cubensis may be analogous to that in Phycomyces, where growth responds to both mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis and centrifugal force (Dennison, 1961).

## Evolutionary and Ecological Significance

Tropisms serve adaptive functions for mushrooms in their natural environments:

- **Spore dispersal optimization**: All tropic responses ultimately serve to position the cap and gills in the optimal orientation for spore release and dispersal by wind.
- **Habitat navigation**: For lignicolous (wood-dwelling) and coprophagous (dung-dwelling) species, the habitat is inherently unstable—mushrooms may grow on fallen logs, dung pats, or other substrates at odd angles. Gravitropism ensures that regardless of substrate orientation, the gills face downward for efficient spore release (Plunkett, 1961).
- **Spacing in clusters**: Directed growth toward evaporative demand (anemotropism) may increase spacing between stipes of cespitose (clustered) mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis bodies, reducing competition and improving air circulation around individual caps.
- **Temporal coordination**: The switch from wind-directed growth to mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis-directed growth at maturity ensures that early growth navigates the microenvironment while the final orientation prioritizes spore release geometry.

## The Boundary Layer Hypothesis

The leading explanation for anemotropic bending in mushrooms involves the boundary layer—a thin layer of relatively still air that clings to the surface of the stipe. When wind flows past the mushroom:

1. The boundary layer is thicker on the leeward (downwind) side than on the windward side.
2. This creates a differential in gas exchange: water vapor and other gases accumulate more on the leeward side.
3. The windward side experiences greater evaporative demand (drying effect).
4. Differential cell expansion rates result: cells on the leeward side, exposed to higher humidity and less evaporative stress, elongate more than windward cells.
5. The net effect is curvature toward the wind.

This hypothesis is supported by observations in Phycomyces, where water vapor has been demonstrated as critical to tropic responses (Johnson and Gamow, 1971). In P. cubensis, the evaporative demand is a function of at least four factors: wind velocity, humidity, mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis intensity, and temperature—all of which interact to determine the direction and degree of stipe curvature.

## Physical Force Hypothesis

An alternative explanation suggests that the mushroom responds directly to the mechanical force of wind. This is supported by studies on Phycomyces blakesleeanus showing that sporangiophore growth responds to mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis and centrifugal force (Dennison, 1961). Under this model, the physical pressure of air flow against one side of the stipe could compress cells on the windward side or stimulate differential growth. However, the boundary layer hypothesis is generally preferred because it better explains the interaction between wind, humidity, and mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis in determining growth direction.

## Interaction Between Tropisms

In natural environments, mushrooms rarely experience a single tropic stimulus in isolation. Multiple tropisms interact and sometimes compete:

- **Wind vs. mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis**: During early development, anemotropism can override gravitropism, causing the stipe to grow horizontally into the wind. At maturity, gravitropism associated with spore production becomes dominant.
- **Light vs. wind**: Under strong illumination, the mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis-induced evaporative demand can become the dominant factor, overriding the wind effect. In low-mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis conditions (approximately 1% of field intensity), wind effects dominate.
- **Stage-dependent hierarchy**: The hierarchy of tropic responses changes with developmental stage. Early stages prioritize substrate exploration and microenvironmental navigation, while later stages prioritize spore dispersal orientation.
- **Light requirement for geotropism**: In P. cubensis, the negative geotropic recurvation at Stage 2 requires some mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis exposure, but this may be indirect—mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis is needed for spore production, and spore production is linked to geotropic sensitivity.

## Experimental Methodology

Badham's 1982 study established important methodological approaches for studying mushroom tropisms:

- **Wind tunnel design**: A closed-circuit wind tunnel with controlled wind velocity (9 ± 3 cm/sec), humidity (94 ± 3% RH), temperature (24.5 ± 1.5°C), and mushroom-mushroom-fruiting-body-morphogenesis (0.002 cal/cm²/min) provided a controlled environment for observing directional growth.
- **Rotation experiments**: Rotating cultures at 3 rpm perpendicular to airflow distinguished between gravitropic and anemotropic responses. Rotating parallel to airflow confirmed the directional nature of growth.
- **Sterile mutant comparison**: Using sporeless mutants allowed researchers to separate the effects of sporulation from other tropic responses, revealing the connection between spore production and geotropic sensitivity.
- **Stage-based analysis**: Dividing development into four stages (primordial swelling, spherical pileus, open hemispherical pileus, plane pileus) enabled precise characterization of when tropic responses change.
