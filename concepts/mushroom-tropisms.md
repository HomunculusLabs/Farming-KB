### Geotropism

Geotropism is the response to gravitational force. In mushrooms, negative
geotropism (upward growth against gravity) is the most common form. This
response becomes pronounced during later developmental stages when the pileus
must orient horizontally for spore release. It is especially important for
lignicolous or coprophagous species on unstable substrates.

### Anemotropism

Anemotropism is a less-studied tropic response involving directional
growth in relation to air currents. In *Psilocybe cubensis*, the stipe grows
into the wind during early developmental stages, a response that has also
been observed in the zygomycete *Phycomyces blakesleeanus*. This wind-
directed growth may serve as a mechanism to position caps favorably for spore
dispersal or to increase spacing between stipes of cespitose (clustered)
fruiting bodies.

## Mechanisms of Tropic Response

### Evaporative Demand Hypothesis

One prominent explanation for stipe curvature in mushrooms involves the
concept of **evaporative demand**. This hypothesis proposes that differential
water loss across the stipe surface causes cells on one side to elongate at a
different rate than cells on the other, producing curvature. Evaporative
demand is a function of at least four environmental factors:

- Wind velocity
- Humidity
- Light intensity
- Temperature

When wind flows past a mushroom stipe, it creates a thicker boundary layer on
the leeward (downwind) side compared to the windward side. The windward side
experiences greater evaporative demand, potentially causing faster cell
elongation and bending toward the wind source. This mechanism may explain both
anemotropic and phototropic responses, since light can heat the mushroom
surface and increase evaporation rates.
