# Serial Sectioning Technique in Mycology: The Trow-Hartog Rivalry

One of the most bitter rivalries in the [[bloomfield-history-of-mycology-pioneers]] centered on
the technique of serial sectioning and its application to understanding
[[basidiomycete-mating-systems-sexual-reproduction]] in water molds.
The conflict between Marcus Hartog of Queen's College, Ireland, and
Professor Trow of University College, Cardiff, in the 1890s illustrates
how methodological disagreements can escalate into personal vendettas
that shape the trajectory of scientific inquiry.

## The Mystery of Oomycete Sex

At the close of the [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]], [[fungal-life-cycles-sexual-reproduction-metamorphosis]] in the
oomycetes (water molds) was considered one of the most important
botanical mysteries.
These [[aquatic-fungi-and-ingoldian-spores]] produced egg-like structures called oogonia alongside
male structures called antheridia, but the precise mechanism by which
fertilization occurred was hotly contested.
The difficulty lay in the microscopic scale of the organisms and the
rapidity with which the critical events of fertilization took place.
Sperm nuclei are produced inside the antheridium just before they are
propelled into the egg, making it almost impossible to obtain a specimen
in which fertilization was occurring at the moment it was preserved.

## Trow's Serial Sectioning Method

Trow developed an approach that was revolutionary for its time.
He pickled fungi in an acidic fixative solution, embedded them in
paraffin wax, and then cut shavings just a few thousandths of a
millimeter thick from the specimen using a microtome fitted with a
razor-sharp steel blade.
Before viewing, the slivers were stained with gentian violet, eosin,
and other dyes to enhance the contrast between different cell
components.

By studying successive slices from single oogonia, Trow was able to
reconstruct their three-dimensional structure.
This enabled him to determine the location of nuclei within intact
cells and to piece together a logical progression of events leading to
egg formation.
Using this method, Trow reported that water mold eggs were fertilized
by male antheridia — precisely the mechanism that later research would
confirm.

## Hartog's Rejection and Authority

Marcus Hartog considered himself an expert on oomycete reproduction.
He had been a student of Anton de Bary, "the founder of modern
mycology," at Strasbourg, and his credentials were formidable.
Despite this pedigree, Hartog clung to the older practice of examining
whole cells crushed on a microscope slide, a technique that could not
resolve the internal structures visible through serial sectioning.

Hartog was unmoved by Trow's evidence and invoked the authority of de
Bary, who had dismissed fertilization in water molds as an illusion
comparable "to the passage of spiritual mediums like the renowned
Mrs. Guppy through brick-walls and closed doors and windows."
Hartog published scathing critiques of Trow's work in the Annals of
Botany, expressing "regret that so careful a technique and such powers
of observation as the author's should have been overweighted with a
priori and obsolete views."

## Vindication

Within a few years of Hartog's attacks, Trow was completely vindicated
when other mycologists independently corroborated his version of events.
The serial sectioning technique was adopted more widely and became a
standard method for reconstructing the three-dimensional anatomy of
microscopic organisms.
Trow's demonstration that antheridia actually penetrated oogonia and
delivered sperm nuclei to eggs was confirmed by multiple independent
researchers using the same methods.

Hartog's resistance to the technique delayed acceptance of the correct
mechanism of oomycete fertilization, but the episode also demonstrated
the power of serial sectioning as a tool for biological investigation.
The technique would go on to become indispensable in neuroanatomy,
embryology, and plant pathology, where understanding the
three-dimensional relationships between microscopic structures was
essential.

## The Broader Significance of Serial Sectioning

The Trow-Hartog rivalry was not merely a personality conflict but a
clash between two fundamentally different approaches to microscopy.
Whole-mount preparation, Hartog's preferred method, preserved the
overall morphology of the specimen but sacrificed internal detail.
Serial sectioning, by contrast, destroyed the intact specimen but
revealed its internal architecture in exquisite detail.

The lesson of the episode is that methodological innovation in
microscopy often meets resistance from established practitioners who
have invested their careers in older techniques.
The serial sectioning method required specialized equipment (the
microtome), extensive technical skill, and enormous patience —
hundreds of sections had to be cut, mounted, stained, and examined
individually to reconstruct a single specimen.
But the results spoke for themselves, and the technique became one of
the foundational methods of modern cell biology.

## De Bary's Cautionary Legacy

The invocation of de Bary's authority by Hartog is a reminder that
even the greatest scientists can be wrong, and that their authority
can be misused to suppress valid findings.
De Bary's comparison of oomycete fertilization to spiritual mediumship
was a vivid metaphor but poor science.
His skepticism about fertilization in water molds was based on the
limitations of the whole-mount technique, not on any fundamental
understanding of the organisms.

The Hartog-Trow episode illustrates a recurring pattern in the history
of science: established authorities resist new methods and new findings
that challenge their worldview, only to be vindicated (or refuted) by
the accumulated weight of evidence.
In this case, the evidence came from the microtome and the serial
section, tools that revealed what the naked eye and the whole mount
could not.
