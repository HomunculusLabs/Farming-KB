# Darwin, Phytochrome, and Photoperiodism Discovery

The discovery that plants measure light through sophisticated photoreceptor
systems, culminating in the identification of phytochrome as the molecular
switch controlling flowering, represents one of the great detective stories in
plant biology. Beginning with Charles Darwin's deceptively simple experiments
on phototropism in 1880 and culminating in Warren Butler's demonstration of
phytochrome in the early 1960s, this line of research revealed that plants
possess a sensory apparatus far more nuanced than anyone had imagined. The
story spans genetics, photobiology, and agricultural science, demonstrating
how a simple observation about a tobacco plant's flowering habits could lead
to the discovery of a fundamental biological mechanism.

## Darwin's Phototropism Experiments

For the twenty years following the publication of *On the Origin of Species*,
Darwin and his son Francis conducted experiments on the effects of light on
plant growth, published in 1880 in *The Power of Movement in Plants*. Darwin
had observed that almost all plants bend toward lateral light, a behavior
called phototropism. In 1864, Julius von Sachs had discovered that blue light
is the primary color inducing phototropism, but the mechanism and location
of light perception remained unknown.

The Darwins designed a classic experiment using canary grass seedlings grown
in total darkness. They illuminated the plants with a gas lamp so dim they
could not see the seedlings themselves, yet after only three hours the plants
had curved toward the light. The bending always occurred about two centimeters
below the tip, suggesting that the light-sensing organ and the bending organ
were different parts of the plant.

To determine which part perceived the light, they tested five conditions: an
untreated seedling (which bent normally), a seedling with its tip removed
(which did not bend), a seedling with its tip covered by a lightproof cap
(which did not bend), a seedling with its tip covered by a clear glass cap
(which bent normally), and a seedling with its middle section covered by a
lightproof tube (which bent normally). This elegantly demonstrated that the
"eyes" of the plant were in the shoot tip, which perceives light and
transmits the bending signal to the stem below. They had proven rudimentary
sight in plants.

## Maryland Mammoth and the Discovery of Photoperiodism

In 1906, farmers in southern Maryland noticed a tobacco strain that grew to
four and a half meters, produced nearly a hundred leaves, and never stopped
growing until frost, yet rarely flowered. These valleys had been home to some
of America's greatest tobacco farms since the first settlers arrived from
Europe at the end of the seventeenth century. This "Maryland Mammoth" posed a
practical problem: no flowers meant no seed for the next crop.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of
Agriculture set out to determine why Maryland Mammoth did not know when to
stop making leaves and start making flowers and seeds instead. They planted
the Maryland Mammoth in pots and left one group outside in the fields. The
other group was put in the field during the day but moved to a dark shed every
afternoon.

Simply limiting the amount of light the plants saw was enough to cause
Maryland Mammoth to stop growing and start flowering. If Maryland Mammoth was
exposed to the long days of summer, it would keep growing leaves. But if it
experienced artificially shorter days, then it would flower. They named this
phenomenon photoperiodism, the first strong evidence that plants measure how
much light they receive. Other experiments revealed that chrysanthemums and
soybeans are "short-day" plants while irises and barley are "long-day"
plants.

## Plants Measure Night, Not Day

A key subsequent discovery was that plants measure not the length of the day
but the length of the continuous period of darkness. Scientists found they
could prevent short-day plants like soybeans from flowering by turning lights
on for only a few minutes in the middle of the night. Conversely, they could
induce long-day plants like irises to flower in midwinter with a brief
midnight flash of light.

This technique became commercially important for chrysanthemum growers, who
could suppress flowering until two weeks before Mother's Day by interrupting
the dark period. The flowers normally blossom in the autumn as the days get
shorter. Chrysanthemums grown in greenhouses can be kept from flowering by
turning on the lights for a few minutes at night throughout the autumn and
winter. Then two weeks before Mother's Day, the farmers stop turning on the
lights at night, and all the plants start to flower at once, ready for
harvest and shipping.

## Red and Far-Red: The Light-Activated Switch

Scientists discovered that only red light flashes during the night influenced
flowering. Blue or green flashes had no effect, but seconds of red light were
sufficient. Plants were differentiating between colors: they were using blue
light to know which direction to bend in and red light to measure the length
of the night.

Then researchers found that far-red light (wavelengths slightly longer than
red, most often seen at dusk) could cancel the effect of red light. A pulse
of red light induced flowering in irises; a subsequent pulse of far-red
light prevented it. Red light after far-red restored the flowering response.
The plant seemed to "remember" the last color it had seen. A few seconds of
either color was enough. It was like a light-activated switch: red light
turns on flowering, far-red light turns it off.

## Phytochrome: The Plant Color Receptor

By the early 1960s, Warren Butler and colleagues demonstrated that a single
photoreceptor was responsible for both the red and far-red effects. They named
it phytochrome, meaning "plant color." Phytochrome operates as a
light-activated molecular switch with two interconvertible forms. Red light
converts phytochrome to its active form, primed to receive far-red light.
Far-red light converts it back to its inactive form, primed to receive red
light.

Ecologically, this mechanism is elegant. In nature, the last light a plant
sees at the end of the day is far-red, and this signifies to the plant that
it should "turn off." In the morning, it sees red light and it wakes up. In
this way a plant measures how long ago it last saw red light and adjusts its
growth accordingly.

## The Leaf as the Eye for Photoperiodism

Surprisingly, the "eye" for photoperiodism is not in the plant tip, as
Darwin had shown for phototropism. If in the middle of the night one shines
a beam of light on different parts of the plant, one discovers that it is
sufficient to illuminate any single leaf in order to regulate flowering in
the entire plant. On the other hand, if all the leaves are pruned, leaving
only the stem and the apex, the plant is blind to any flashes of light, even
if the entire plant is illuminated.

If the phytochrome in a single leaf sees red light in the middle of the
night, it is as if the entire plant were illuminated. Phytochrome in the
leaves receives the light cues and initiates a mobile signal that propagates
throughout the plant and induces flowering. This separation of phototropism
(tip-based, blue-light driven) and photoperiodism (leaf-based, red/far-red
driven) demonstrates that plants have evolved multiple independent
light-sensing systems, each optimized for different ecological functions.
