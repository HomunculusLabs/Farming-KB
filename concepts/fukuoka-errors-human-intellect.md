## The Errors of the Human Intellect

From "The Natural Way of Farming" by Masanobu Fukuoka, Chapter 1 of "The [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]] developed early in the West as one branch of the natural sciences, which arose in Western learning as the study of matter. The natural sciences took a materialistic viewpoint that interpreted nature analytically and dialectically. This was a consequence of Western man's belief in a man-nature dichotomy.

In contrast to the Eastern view that man should seek to become one with nature, Western man used [[fukuoka-scalpel-beautiful-woman-science-nature]] and reveal a bloody mass of tissue. What a miserable, wasted effort.

This vivid and disturbing metaphor captures the essence of Fukuoka's epistemological critique. Dissection reveals structure but destroys life. The living whole possesses qualities -- beauty, harmony, self-organization -- that are not present in any of its parts.

A soil ecosystem teeming with bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and arthropods has properties of fertility, water retention, and [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]], and these emergent properties vanish, no matter how thoroughly you catalog the remaining components.

## The Maze of Relative Subjectivity

When people want to know what Okuninushi no Mikoto, the Shinto deity of agriculture, carries around in the huge sack on his shoulder, they immediately open the sack and thrust their hands in. They think that to understand the interior of the sack, they must know its contents.

Man jumps into that great sack called nature, and grabbing whatever he can, turns it over and examines it, asking himself what it is and how it works, and drawing his own conclusions about what purpose nature serves. But no matter how careful his observations and reasoning, each and every interpretation carries the risk of causing grievous error because man cannot know nature any more than he can know the uses for the objects in Okuninushi's sack.

The metaphor of Okuninushi's sack is particularly apt because it highlights the problem of purpose. When the scientist examines nature, he always asks "what is this for?" or "how can this be used?"

These questions presuppose that nature exists as a resource for human exploitation. But what if nature has no purpose -- or rather, what if its purpose is simply to be itself? The moment we ask "what is this soil good for?" we have already distorted our understanding of the soil.

We have replaced the experience of soil as a living mystery with the concept of soil as a substrate for production.

## The Problem of Incomplete Understanding

When man says that he is capable of knowing nature, to "know" does not mean to grasp and understand the true essence of nature. It means only that man knows that nature which he is able to know.

This distinction is crucial. The knowledge that science produces is not wrong in itself; it is simply incomplete in a way that cannot be remedied by more research. No amount of additional data will transform incomplete knowledge into complete knowledge, because completeness is not a function of quantity.

A jigsaw puzzle with 999 out of 1,000 pieces assembled does not reveal "99.9% of the picture." It reveals a picture that is fundamentally different from the completed puzzle. The missing piece may be the one that transforms a meaningless pattern into a recognizable face. In nature, the "missing pieces" are not peripheral details but the very connections that give the system its character.

Just as the world known to a frog in a well is not the entire world but only the world within that well, so the nature that man can perceive and know is only that nature which he has been able to grasp with his own hands and his own subjectivity. But of course, this is not true nature.

## The Alternative: Non-Discriminating Knowledge

If discriminating knowledge -- the analytical, reductionist mode of understanding -- leads us into error, what is the alternative? Fukuoka proposes what he calls "non-discriminating knowledge," a mode of awareness that apprehends the whole without breaking it into parts.

This is not intuition in the mystical sense, though it has mystical overtones. It is more like the knowledge that a skilled craftsperson has of their material -- a knowledge that is felt rather than thought, embodied rather than conceptualized.

The farmer with non-discriminating knowledge does not need to analyze the soil because he can feel its health under his feet. He does not need to identify pest species because he can sense when the ecosystem is out of balance. He does not need to measure nutrients because he can see in the color and vigor of his plants whether they are well-nourished.

This knowledge is not inferior to scientific knowledge; in the domain of farming, it is superior, because it deals with wholes rather than parts.
