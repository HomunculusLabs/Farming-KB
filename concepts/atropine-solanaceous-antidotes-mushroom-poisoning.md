# Atropine and Solanaceous Antidotes for Mushroom Poisoning

## Overview

The discovery that alkaloids from the nightshade family (Solanaceae) —
particularly atropine from belladonna — serve as effective antidotes to
Amanita mushroom poisoning represents one of the most important advances in
19th-century mycotoxicology. [[julius-auboineau-palmer]] documented the
convergent lines of evidence in *About Mushrooms* (1894), drawing on
experimental physiology, clinical observation, and his own published research.

## Dr. Isaac Ott's Frog Experiments

The experimental foundation came from Dr. [[isaac-ott]], Demonstrator of
Physiology at the University of Pennsylvania. Ott administered muscarine
subcutaneously to frogs with the sternum removed, allowing direct cardiac
observation. His first experiment proceeded as follows:

- **3:55 PM** — Heart beating at thirty-six beats per minute.
- **3:58 PM** — 0.005 grain muscarine injected subcutaneously.
- **4:00 PM** — Heart arrested in diastole; ventricle distended with blood
  and bulbous; pricking produced only contraction.
- **4:03 PM** — 0.01 grain atropine injected subcutaneously.
- **4:05 PM** — Heart spontaneously resumed at twenty-eight per minute;
  by 4:09 PM restored to thirty-six per minute. The frog fully recovered.

A second experiment on a different frog produced nearly identical results,
confirming the reproducibility of atropine's antagonistic action.

## Independent Clinical Corroboration

Palmer noted a striking example of simultaneous discovery. In April 1877 —
two years before Ott's published experiments — a New Jersey physician wrote
to Palmer describing the successful hypodermic use of belladonna on a patient
suffering from [[amanitine-toadstool-poisoning]]. The physician arrived at this treatment
simply by observing the patient's symptoms and recognizing their parallel to
those counteracted by belladonna alkaloids.

## Palmer's 1879 Publication in the *Moniteur Scientifique*

In January 1879, Palmer published in the *Moniteur Scientifique* of Paris on
the nature of Amanita poison and its treatment, asserting that atropine
administered subcutaneously was the specific remedy for poisoning by any
mushroom of the sub-genus *Amanita*. He also suggested the trial of nicotine
or tobacco for non-users. The article was copied and commented upon by
several American medical journals.

## Additional Solanaceous Antidotes

Evidence accumulated that multiple Solanaceae-derived alkaloids were
effective against Amanita poisoning:

- **Daturine and hyoscyamine** — Dr. T.F. Wood of North Carolina reported
  the successful administration of both agents in Amanita poisoning cases.
- **An Australian solanaceous plant** — The *Scientific American* (October 11)
  reported that a native Australian nightshade served as a valuable remedy.
- **Stramonium** — Professor Schiff of Italy advocated stramonium
  (*Datura stramonium*) for [[psilocybin-mushroom-poisoning-treatment]].

Palmer concluded that the convergence among independent researchers was
remarkable and unavoidable: the antidotes to mushroom poisoning must be
sought among the nightshades, botanically the *Solanaceae*.

## The Nature of the Poison: Muscarine and Amanitine

### Muscarine

The toxic alkaloid in *Amanita muscarius* was isolated by Schmiedeberg and
Koppe, who named it **muscarine** — a colorless alkaloid acting primarily
on the parasympathetic nervous system, causing cardiac arrest in diastole,
profuse salivation, perspiration, and gastrointestinal distress.

### Amanitine: A Half-Century of Nomenclatural Confusion

Palmer highlighted a serious nomenclatural problem. For fifty years,
**amanitine** had been correctly applied to the poisonous principle of
certain fungi. Letellier named it so because it was found most particularly
in the genus *Amanita*. Descourtilz confirmed this, and Boudier, in his
1864 Orfila prize essay, stated that "the amanitine of Dr. Letellier must
be considered the poisonous principle of the false orange mushroom."
Webster's Dictionary likewise defined amanitine as "the poisonous principle
of some fungi."

Dr. Ott, however, used amanitine for a *non-poisonous* base coexisting
with muscarine in *A. muscarius*. Palmer protested that applying a name
with fifty years of established meaning to a different substance could
only add to "the almost hopeless confusion already existing in this
branch of science."

## Dr. Ott's Conclusions and Palmer's Rebuttal

Ott published three conclusions alongside his experiments. Palmer contested the
first two:

1. The *Agaricus* comprises nearly one thousand varieties, the majority
   poisonous — Palmer countered that only ~700 Agaricini exist among ~1,000
   non-microscopic fungi, and the majority are non-esculent (tough, woody,
   bitter, slimy). Only four Amanita varieties cause fatal poisoning.
2. Edible varieties may become poisonous under unknown conditions — Palmer
   disputed this through five seasons of self-experimentation (see below).
3. Letellier's amanitine is found in some species; *A. muscarius* contains
   both muscarine and a non-poisonous base called amanitine.

## Palmer's Personal Experiments

The theory that edible mushrooms could become poisonous rested on the
observation that poisonous and non-poisonous fungi show nearly identical
[[guzman-magic-mushrooms-chemical-constituents]], differing only in alkaline quantity. Palmer tested
this over five seasons through rigorous self-experiments: eating every edible
variety from widely separated localities; propagating and mixing spawn to
produce new forms; and preparing mixed stews of unrelated species — each
verified safe in isolation — eaten in combination.

In no case did he experience harm. Palmer concluded: "You cannot make a
noxious dish out of innoxious elements. You cannot pluck food and poison
from the same plant."

## The Curse of Tradition in Mycology

Palmer closed with a sharp critique of his era's mycological knowledge.
"The curse of the [[neglected-science-of-mycology]] has been tradition," he wrote. "Men
have written on it without personal acquaintance with a single toadstool."
Leading magazines published articles by writers who condensed mycological
texts without understanding the subject, weaving in tests that were
"utterly absurd." Palmer called for systematic, original study — experiment
and analysis of non-microscopic fungi as esculents, poisons, or medicines —
declaring that "there is no field in nature that offers such opportunity
for new and important discoveries."

## Historical Significance

The convergence of Ott's experimental physiology, the New Jersey physician's
clinical empiricism, Palmer's 1879 publication, Wood's clinical reports, and
Schiff's pharmacological advocacy collectively established what remains a
foundational principle: atropine and related [[mckenna-witch-ointments-tropane-alkaloids-flying-salves]] are the
specific pharmacological antidotes to muscarine-type mushroom poisoning — a
principle that continues to inform emergency medicine protocols for *Amanita*
ingestion to the present day.
