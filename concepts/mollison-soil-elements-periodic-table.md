# Soil Elements and the Periodic Table in Permaculture

## Overview

Mollison presents a unique treatment of the periodic table of elements
from a permaculture perspective, examining each element for its role
in soils, plants, animals, and human health. This section (Chapter 8)
provides practical guidance for soil management, water quality, and
toxicity avoidance, connecting chemistry directly to design decisions.

## Key Elements and Their Roles

### Hydrogen (H)
- An extremely mobile and reactive gaseous element
- The number of free ions determines soil pH together with hydroxyl
  (OH) radicals in alkaline areas
- Some plants, especially algae and rushes, can transpire hydrogen
  and break down halogenated hydrocarbons used in pesticides and
  herbicides
- Combined with carbon as methane (CH4), emitted by decaying humus
  in anaerobic environments
- A sulphurous smell in subsoils is a guide to wet-season
  waterlogging

### Boron (B)
- A trace element necessary for brassicas and beets
- Many detergents contain boron, causing boron pollution in gardens
- Use soap instead, especially in drylands where citrus and grains
  can be boron-poisoned in dry seasons
- Borax at 160 g/ha used on beet and Brassica crops where soil
  levels are low
- Essential to sugar transport in plants, pollen formation, and
  cell wall structure
- Ideal at 0.75 ppm; 1.0 ppm can be toxic to plants

### Carbon (C)
- The basic building block of life forms with hydrogen and oxygen
- Added to soils as humus, compost, and [[mollison-mulching-and-soil-health]]
- 10-20% humus ensures good structure in both clays and sands
- More than 50% humus inhibits uptake of polluting heavy metals
- Carbon in soils is about 58% of organic matter (combustible)
- Carbon dioxide release from agriculture and forest felling creates
  the greenhouse effect

### Nitrogen (N)
- A major plant nutrient, 80% of air itself
- Legumes and many non-legumes (alder, Casuarina) fix nitrogen from
  air if molybdenum is present as a catalyst
- Nitrogen as a foliar spray of urea can increase rice grain
  protein content by 40%
- In water with bacteria, nitrogen forms nitrates: beneficial at low
  levels but lethal to young animals and children above 80 ppm
- Nitrates building up in all soils below agricultural land
- Compost-fed spinach contains 500 ppm nitrate vs 2,000-3,000 ppm in
  chemically fertilised spinach

### Oxygen (O)
- Necessary for respiration; soils need oxygen (air) for good
  structure with 12-30% soil bulk as pore space
- Aeration achieved using calcium, trees, worms, ripping, humus, or
  blowing air through sub-surface irrigation pipes
- Waterlogging reduces pore space and root oxygen

### Fluorine (F)
- Beneficial in small amounts as in seaweed fertiliser
- Serious poison at high levels, commonly found in bore water
- Causes bone deformation in animals, stunted growth, and metabolic
  disturbance
- Fluorine at 1 mg/l (1 ppm) is sufficient for normal growth
- High levels found in marine phosphate deposits, hence in
  fertilisers derived from these

### Sodium (Na)
- With chlorine, makes up common salt
- At high levels, causes collapse of soils and loss of pore space,
  displaces calcium and disperses colloids
- Real problems occur when soil salts are mobilised by irrigation,
  potash fertiliser, or soil flooding in dryland areas
- Salt in vegetable cooking water blocks uptake of some heavy metals
  on plant proteins

### Aluminium (Al)
- A "Jekyll and Hyde" element; forms a large part of soils and red
  clays
- Poisonous as aluminium sulphate, formed in acid rain or sulphur
  polluted soils
- Released when cooking onions in aluminium cookware
- Heavy aluminium silica uptake may assist development of senile
  dementia
- Adequate calcium provides partial defence
- Soluble aluminium tolerable at 0.1 to 1.0 ppm; above 15 ppm many
  plants die

### Phosphorus (P)
- An essential plant element, recycled by many trees
- Fixed by root associates of Casuarina, Pultenea, and Banksia
- Also recycled in pond mud, bones, and freshwater mussels
- Some oceanic guano deposits contaminated by cadmium, mercury,
  uranium (40 ppm), and fluorides
- Most western soils now over-supplied with phosphorus

## Practical Soil Management Implications

The element-by-element analysis leads to several key design principles:

1. Test water sources (especially bore water) for fluoride, boron,
   and nitrate levels
2. Avoid aluminium cookware for salty, acid, or sulphurous foods
3. Use calcium (lime) in adequate quantities in all gardens
4. Add humus to block heavy metal uptake (50% humus blocks lead)
5. Use legumes for nitrogen rather than chemical fertilisers
6. Avoid excessive animal manure which releases surplus nitrogen
7. Grow green manures from organic hedgerows for safe compost
   materials
8. Check rainwater tanks for adequate pH buffering (marble chips,
   sea shells)
