# Oyster Mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus)

The most versatile and widely used mushroom for both [[mycoremediation-basics]] and [[mushroom-cultivation-basics]]. From [[paul-stamets]]' *Mycelium Running* (2005).

## Taxonomy and Description

- **Order**: Agaricales
- **Family**: Pleurotaceae
- **Genus**: Pleurotus
- **Common names**: Oyster mushroom, pearl oyster
- **Distribution**: Found on virtually every continent; one of the most widespread saprophytic mushrooms in the world
- **Habitat**: Dead or dying hardwood trees and logs, especially oaks, beeches, and aspens

## Why Oyster Mushrooms Are Special

Stamets calls oyster mushrooms "the most versatile" for mycoremediation because they:
- Can break down more classes of toxins than any other single species
- Are found virtually worldwide — native strains available everywhere
- Are extremely aggressive colonizers — outcompete most contaminants
- Grow on the widest range of substrates — straw, sawdust, paper, cardboard, coffee grounds, agricultural waste
- Fruit prolifically — high yields with minimal input
- Are edible and delicious — dual-purpose (food + remediation)

## Mycoremediation Capabilities

### Toxins Degradeable by P. ostreatus

| Toxin Class | Specific Compounds | Mechanism |
|-------------|-------------------|-----------|
| PAHs | Naphthalene, anthracene, phenanthrene, benzo[a]pyrene | Ligninolytic enzymes |
| Petroleum | Crude oil, diesel, bunker C | Extracellular enzyme oxidation |
| Pentachlorophenol | Wood preservative PCP | Laccase, peroxidase |
| Bacteria | E. coli, coliforms | Antibacterial secretions |
| E. coli | Waterborne pathogen | Physical filtration + antibiosis |
| Organochlorines | DDT, dioxin-related | Laccase-mediated oxidation |
| Oils | Motor oil, cooking oil | Biosorption + enzymatic breakdown |

### Visual Bioindicator

When oyster mycelium absorbs oil:
1. Mycelium darkens with absorption
2. Metabolizes the oil over days to weeks
3. Turns white again after digestion
This darkening/whitening cycle serves as a visual bioindicator of remediation progress.

## Cultivation Parameters

### Substrates
- Straw (wheat, barley, rye, rice)
- Sawdust (hardwood preferred — alder, oak, beech)
- Coffee grounds
- Paper and cardboard
- Agricultural waste (corn stalks, banana leaves, bagasse)
- Wood chips
- Leached cow manure

### Environmental Parameters
- **Spawn run temperature**: 65-80°F (18-27°C)
- **Fruiting temperature**: 55-75°F (13-24°C) — varies by strain
- **Humidity**: 85-95% for fruiting
- **Light**: Indirect natural or fluorescent — needed for fruiting initiation
- **Fresh air exchange**: Critical during fruiting — high CO2 produces deformed mushrooms
- **pH**: 6.0-7.5

### Inoculation Rates
- **Straw**: 10-30% by mass
- **Wood chips**: 10-20%
- **Pasteurization**: 160°F for 1-2 hours (hot water submersion preferred)

### Yields
- **Biological efficiency**: 50-200% (weight of fresh mushrooms per dry substrate weight)
- **Time to first fruit**: 2-4 weeks after full colonization
- **Number of flushes**: 3-5, diminishing over time
- **Straw bags**: 1-2 lbs fresh mushrooms per 6-8 lb bag

## Spore Production

- Oyster mushrooms convert an estimated 50% of their mass into spores (possibly high)
- A single mushroom kit (~1 lb fresh = ~45g dry) could produce ~2.5g spores = ~2.5 billion spores
- Even at 10% of this estimate, that's 250 million spores — 25,000 meters laid end to end
- Perennial species like Ganoderma applanatum produce even more: ~5 trillion spores annually

## Cold Incubation Method

A technique from Stamets particularly suited to oyster mushrooms:

1. Place oyster spores on moist, untreated wheat straw
2. Incubate outdoors at 35-50°F (January in Pacific Northwest)
3. No pasteurization needed — cold temperatures suppress competitors
4. Mushrooms begin fruiting by late March
5. The exponential spiral of generation makes this method self-sustaining

## Spore-in-Oil Technique

Stamets' innovation for forest inoculation:
- Immerse spores in canola, corn, or safflower oil
- Use as chainsaw lubricant when cutting trees
- As chainsaw cuts, spore-infused oil distributes spores to fresh cut surfaces
- Oils help spores adhere and resist washing/blowing away
- Optimal concentration: ~100 million spores per liter of oil
- Tested on alder trees (Alnus rubra) ~6 inches diameter

## Role in Ecological Succession

Oyster mushrooms are primary decomposers — among the first organisms to colonize dead wood. They:
- Break down lignin and cellulose simultaneously
- Create conditions for secondary decomposers
- Set the stage for mycorrhizal fungi and eventually new plant growth
- Their spent substrate enriches soil for subsequent organisms
