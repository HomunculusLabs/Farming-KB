# Permaculture Definition and Philosophy

## The Preface and Movement History

The permaculture movement emerged from the ferment of the late 1960s, a period when many people recognized the failures of military adventurism, ruthless land exploitation, the arrogance of polluters, and general insensitivity to human and environmental needs. Between 1972 and 1974, [[bill-mollison]] and [[david-holmgren]] developed an interdisciplinary earth science — permaculture — with potential for positivistic, integrated, and global outreach.

By January 1981, the concept had matured sufficiently to be taught as an applied design system, when the first 26 students graduated from an intensive 140-hour lecture series. From that beginning, thousands of people have attended [[permaculture-design]] courses, workshops, lectures, and seminars. Graduates formed a loose global network, effectively acting in many countries. The movement has no central structure but maintains a strong sense of shared work. Everybody is free to act as an individual, form a small group, or work within any other organization.

Mollison emphasizes cooperation with many other groups of diverse beliefs and practices: the system includes good practices from many disciplines and systems and offers them as an integrated whole. The permaculture approach includes practices from organic agriculture, [[keyline-design]], indigenous land management, ecological science, [[appropriate-technology]], and community economics.

By 1984, it had become clear that many of the systems proposed a decade earlier did in fact constitute a sustainable earth care system. Almost all proposals had been tested and tried, and where skills and capital existed, people could make a living from products derived from stable landscapes. However, Mollison is careful to note that making a living is not the primary aim of permaculture. The priority order is:

1. **First**: Stabilise and care for land
2. **Second**: Serve household, regional, and local needs
3. **Third**: Produce a surplus for sale or exchange

## The Formal Definition

Permaculture (permanent agriculture) is defined as:

> The conscious design and maintenance of agriculturally productive ecosystems which have the diversity, stability, and resilience of natural ecosystems. It is the harmonious integration of landscape and people providing their food, energy, shelter, and other material and non-material needs in a sustainable way. Without permanent agriculture there is no possibility of a stable social order.

Permaculture design is further described as:

> A system of assembling conceptual, material, and strategic components in a pattern which functions to benefit life in all its forms.

The word "permaculture" was coined by Mollison. Its copyright is vested in the Permaculture Institutes and their College of Graduates, guarded for purposes of consistent education. The word can be used by anybody adhering to the ethics and principles, though teaching permaculture is restricted to graduates of a Permaculture Institute who adhere to agreed curricula.




## The Philosophy

The philosophy behind permaculture rests on several key principles:

1. **Working with, rather than against, nature**
2. **Protracted and thoughtful observation rather than protracted and thoughtless action**
3. **Looking at systems in all their functions, rather than asking only one yield of them**
4. **Allowing systems to demonstrate their own evolutions**

This philosophy is described as close to Taoism in its orientation — a way of being that flows with natural processes rather than imposing artificial patterns upon them. The fundamental question that shapes this philosophy can be asked in two ways:

- *"What can I get from this land, or person?"* — which leads to war and waste
- *"What does this person, or land, have to give if I cooperate with them?"* — which leads to peace and plenty

Mollison argues that most conflicts lie not in the answers to questions but in how the questions are asked. We should be alert to rephrase or refuse the "wrong" question. This reframing is central to permaculture thinking — the discipline of asking better questions about our relationship with natural systems.




## Hemispheric Conventions

An interesting practical feature of the manual is Mollison's system of hemispheric conventions. To make the text and figures useful in both hemispheres, he uses "sun-side" or "sunwards" and "shade-side" or "polewards" rather than south and north, and converts months to seasons. A sun symbol rather than a compass arrow indicates solar direction in figures. This reflects both the global ambition of permaculture and Mollison's characteristic humor (noting the problem of North Americans "wandering on the north face of their hills, looking for the sun, poised dangerously upside-down on the Earth").




## Self-Reliance and Cooperation

A recurring theme in the philosophy is the balance between self-reliance and cooperation. Mollison writes that the ethic can be coupled to a determination to make our own way: to be neither employers nor employees, landlords nor tenants, but to be self-reliant as individuals and to cooperate as groups.

Unity in people, he argues, comes from common adherence to a set of ethical principles, each person perhaps going their own way at their own pace and within the limits of their resources, yet all leading to the same goals — a living, complex, and sustainable earth. Those who agree on such ethics, philosophies, and goals form what Mollison calls a "global nation."




## The Book as a Teaching Resource

The manual was written specifically for teachers, students, and designers. It follows on and greatly enlarges upon the foundation texts. Each volume carries a surcharge paid by the publisher to the Permaculture Institute, which holds the funds in trust for tree-planting and periodically releases monies to selected groups active in permanent reafforestation. This mechanism reflects the practical application of permaculture ethics within the publishing process itself — an attempt to ensure that the creation of the book (which uses paper and resources) is offset by the planting of trees.




## Access to Information

The manual provides multiple access points: chapter and section contents, main subjects in the index, common and Latin names of plants in the appendix, and a glossary of terms. The International Permaculture Journal is referenced as an ongoing resource for themes, reviews, events, directories, and other useful data.

The movement's information-sharing ethic reflects a broader commitment to open knowledge — permaculture knowledge is meant to be practiced, shared, and improved upon by communities worldwide, not hoarded or commercialized.
