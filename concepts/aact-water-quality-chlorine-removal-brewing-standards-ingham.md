## Chlorine Removal Methods

### Citric Acid Neutralization

One practical approach described in Ingham's correspondence involves using citric acid to neutralize residual chlorine. Citric acid works by chemically reducing chlorine compounds through oxidation-reduction reactions.

The recommended approach is to test water with a simple chlorine test kit before each brew. If chlorine is detected at levels above 0.5 ppm, treatment is necessary. Citric acid should be added incrementally, with retesting after each addition, until chlorine levels drop below detectable limits.

The ascorbic acid (vitamin C) method is also effective — 1000 mg of ascorbic acid can neutralize approximately 1 ppm of chlorine in 100 gallons of water. Overcorrection with citric acid should be avoided, as excessive acidity can also harm beneficial organisms.

### Aeration and Off-Gassing

For free chlorine (not chloramines), vigorous aeration can reduce concentrations over 12-24 hours. Running the AACT brewer's air pump through clean water for a full day before adding compost allows much of the free chlorine to off-gas. This method is inexpensive but requires planning ahead and does not work against chloramines.

Brewers relying on off-gassing should verify that their municipality uses free chlorine rather than chloramines using the local water utility's annual water quality report.

### Activated Carbon Filtration

High-quality activated carbon filters can remove both chlorine and chloramines, though effectiveness depends on contact time and filter quality. Point-of-use carbon filters with sufficient capacity are recommended for AACT brewers. Carbon filters must be replaced according to manufacturer recommendations, as exhausted filters can release accumulated contaminants back into the water stream.

### Reverse Osmosis Water

Reverse osmosis (RO) water provides the cleanest baseline for AACT brewing. RO systems remove virtually all contaminants including chlorine, chloramines, heavy metals, dissolved minerals, and [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]] capacity — as temperature rises, the water's ability to hold oxygen decreases.

### Cold Water Problems

Below 55°F (13°C), bacterial reproduction slows dramatically, extending the time needed to reach adequate [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]] requires pre-warming before adding compost. Using warm (not hot) water from indoor sources or allowing water to reach ambient greenhouse temperatures helps maintain adequate microbial activity.

### Hot Water Problems

Above 85°F (29°C), certain pathogenic organisms may proliferate and dissolved oxygen capacity decreases significantly. Summer brewing presents specific challenges when water temperatures in outdoor tanks exceed 80°F, potentially causing brews to go anaerobic more quickly. Ingham recommends brewing in shaded locations, using insulated tanks, or brewing during cooler parts of the day. Some brewers add frozen water bottles to maintain optimal temperature ranges.

## Water pH for AACT Brewing

Source water pH should ideally be between 6.0 and 7.5 for AACT brewing. Most municipal water supplies fall within this range, but some groundwater sources may be alkaline (pH 7.5-8.5) or acidic (pH 5.5-6.0).

Extremely alkaline water can inhibit fungal growth, while very acidic water may favor acid-tolerant bacteria at the expense of beneficial fungal species. Ingham recommends testing source water pH before each brew and adjusting if necessary. Small amounts of citric acid can lower pH, while agricultural limestone or potassium bicarbonate can raise it. However, pH adjustments should be made before adding compost, as the compost itself acts as a buffer.
