# Return to Nature Philosophy

The title of Masanobu Fukuoka's second major work, "The Road Back to Nature," encapsulates his deepest conviction: that human civilization has wandered far from its natural origins and must find its way back. This [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming]] -- no tillage, no fertilizer, no pesticides, and no weeding -- are not merely agricultural techniques but a philosophy of non-intervention. "The trees and grasses release seeds that fall to the ground, there to germinate and grow into new plants. The seeds sown by nature are not so weak as to grow only in plowed fields. Plants have always grown by [[comparison-seed-starting-indoors-vs-direct-seeding-techniques]], without tillage."

He was candid about the limitations of even [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] as a movement: "Of course, in a sense, natural farming will never be perfected. It will not see general application in its true form, and will serve only as a brake to slow the mad onslaught of [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]]." This is not defeatism but realism -- the recognition that the return to nature must be a change of consciousness, not merely a change of technique.

## The Road Back in Practice

The road back to nature is not a return to primitive living. Fukuoka did not advocate abandoning all technology or civilization. Rather, he advocated a fundamental change in consciousness -- a recognition that nature is not an enemy to be conquered or a resource to be exploited but a teacher to be listened to and a community to which we belong.

In practical terms, the road back to nature means:

- Growing food by natural methods that work with nature rather than against it
- Eating locally grown, seasonal, whole foods
- Living simply and avoiding unnecessary consumption
- Developing a direct, personal relationship with the land
- Recognizing the limits of human knowledge and the dangers of intellectual arrogance

## Nature as Absolute Reality

For Fukuoka, the return to nature was ultimately a spiritual journey. He believed that nature is not just the physical world but the manifestation of an absolute reality that transcends human understanding. The [[fukuoka-western-eastern-philosophy-agriculture]] of Mu -- nothingness, the void -- points toward this reality. See also [[fukuoka-natures-self-organizing-principles]] is what humanity must learn from and return to.

Even the disposal of death demonstrates nature's perfect system. Countless microbes and small animals in the soil engage in an assembly-line operation of decomposition, returning every living thing to the earth. Man, by contrast, disrupts this cycle with cremation and burial practices that prevent nature from completing its work. The return to nature means trusting these ancient processes rather than replacing them with human inventions.

## Beyond Organic Farming

Fukuoka was clear that even organic farming, as commonly practiced, falls short of a true return to nature. "Even organic farming, which everyone is making such a big fuss over lately, is just another type of [[fukuoka-illusions-scientific-farming-centrifugal-centripetal-model]]. A lot of trouble is taken to move organic materials first here then there, to process and treat." The return to nature requires going beyond the mere substitution of organic inputs for chemical ones.
