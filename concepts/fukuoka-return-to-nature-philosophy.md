# Return to Nature Philosophy

The title of Masanobu Fukuoka's second major work, "The Road Back to Nature," encapsulates his deepest conviction: that human civilization has wandered far from its natural origins and must find its way back. This [[fukuoka-natural-diet-theory]] is not merely about farming but about the fundamental relationship between humanity and the natural world.

## The Road Back: A Personal Journey

Fukuoka described his own journey in the preface to "The Natural Way of Farming": "While still a youth, a certain turn of events set me out on the proud and lonely road back to nature. With sadness, though, I learned that one person cannot live alone. One either lives in association with people or in communion with nature. I found also, to my despair, that people were no longer truly human, and nature no longer truly natural."

These words capture the loneliness and difficulty of the path. Fukuoka spent fifty years wandering in search of nature, and even at the end of that long search, he acknowledged: "I have traveled a long way, yet as night falls there remains still a long way to go." This humility -- the recognition that the journey is never complete -- is itself a key aspect of the return to nature.

## The Original Unity

Fukuoka believed that human beings originally lived in harmony with nature. Before the development of agriculture, technology, and civilization, humans were part of the natural world, not separate from it. They ate what grew naturally in their environment, lived in simple dwellings, and did not attempt to control or improve upon nature.

This original state was not primitive or miserable, as modern people often assume. It was a state of genuine happiness and contentment. Fukuoka quoted Christ's saying: "Even Solomon, robed in his greatest splendor at the height of his glory, paled before a single lily in the fields. Ancient wisdom teaches us that, even without a single possession, we can be happy as long as there are flowers in the fields and birds singing."

## The Departure

The departure from nature began, in Fukuoka's view, with the development of human intellect and the desire to control nature. When people began to think of themselves as separate from and superior to nature, they began to manipulate their environment. Agriculture, in its conventional form, was one of the first expressions of this separation -- the idea that humans can and should "raise" crops rather than allowing crops to grow naturally.

As civilization developed, the separation deepened. Science, technology, industry, and urbanization all moved humanity further from its natural roots. Each advance seemed to bring benefits, but each also created new problems that required further advances to solve.

## The Consequences of Separation

The consequences of humanity's separation from nature are visible everywhere: environmental destruction, chronic disease, social fragmentation, and spiritual emptiness. Fukuoka saw these not as separate problems but as symptoms of a single underlying condition -- the loss of our connection to the natural world.

He was particularly critical of modern Japan, which he saw as rapidly abandoning its traditional relationship with nature in favor of Western-style materialism. "Japan today is rapidly being Americanized and plunging into a culture of decadence," he wrote. "The Japanese have come far, gaining in importance and prestige to the point where they lead the world in many ways. But to Westerners, the Japanese are merely retracing the route passed over earlier by the West."

## Natural Farming as the Path of Return

Natural farming is the practical expression of the return to nature. Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming-detailed]] -- no tillage, no fertilizer, no pesticides, and no weeding -- are not merely agricultural techniques but a philosophy of non-intervention. "The trees and grasses release seeds that fall to the ground, there to germinate and grow into new plants. The seeds sown by nature are not so weak as to grow only in plowed fields. Plants have always grown by direct seeding, without tillage."

He was candid about the limitations of even natural farming as a movement: "Of course, in a sense, natural farming will never be perfected. It will not see general application in its true form, and will serve only as a brake to slow the mad onslaught of scientific agriculture." This is not defeatism but realism -- the recognition that the return to nature must be a change of consciousness, not merely a change of technique.

## The Road Back in Practice

The road back to nature is not a return to primitive living. Fukuoka did not advocate abandoning all technology or civilization. Rather, he advocated a fundamental change in consciousness -- a recognition that nature is not an enemy to be conquered or a resource to be exploited but a teacher to be listened to and a community to which we belong.

In practical terms, the road back to nature means:

- Growing food by natural methods that work with nature rather than against it
- Eating locally grown, seasonal, whole foods
- Living simply and avoiding unnecessary consumption
- Developing a direct, personal relationship with the land
- Recognizing the limits of human knowledge and the dangers of intellectual arrogance

## Nature as Absolute Reality

For Fukuoka, the return to nature was ultimately a spiritual journey. He believed that nature is not just the physical world but the manifestation of an absolute reality that transcends human understanding. The Eastern philosophy of Mu -- nothingness, the void -- points toward this reality. See also [[fukuoka-mu-nothingness-philosophy]].

"According to Oriental philosophy, ours is not a world of relativity but an absolute, selfless world that transcends space and time," he wrote. "Oriental philosophy holds that the standpoint of absolute universality -- which may be called the standpoint of God -- known only to those who stand at the true void transcending space-time is capable of becoming the Great Way."

## The Urgency of Return

Fukuoka believed that the return to nature was not optional but urgently necessary. The destruction of the environment, the depletion of resources, and the proliferation of weapons of mass destruction all pointed toward an impending crisis. "Mankind has developed through the human intellect and now through that same faculty is in the process of collapsing," he warned.

The road back to nature is the only road forward. It is not a retreat but an advance -- a movement toward a deeper, more authentic way of being human.

## The Self-Organizing Natural World

Fukuoka saw in nature's self-organizing systems a model for human society. The natural world operates through incredibly complex food chains and ecological relationships that maintain perfect balance without any central authority. "Tens of millions of species live on in perfect harmony without increase or decrease; they are born and they die unseen by man." This [[fukuoka-natures-self-organizing-principles]] is what humanity must learn from and return to.

Even the disposal of death demonstrates nature's perfect system. Countless microbes and small animals in the soil engage in an assembly-line operation of decomposition, returning every living thing to the earth. Man, by contrast, disrupts this cycle with cremation and burial practices that prevent nature from completing its work. The return to nature means trusting these ancient processes rather than replacing them with human inventions.

## Beyond Organic Farming

Fukuoka was clear that even organic farming, as commonly practiced, falls short of a true return to nature. "Even organic farming, which everyone is making such a big fuss over lately, is just another type of scientific farming. A lot of trouble is taken to move organic materials first here then there, to process and treat." The return to nature requires going beyond the mere substitution of organic inputs for chemical ones. It requires a fundamental shift in the [[fukuoka-rice-and-barley-natural-farming]].

Only over the last fifty years or so have chemical fertilizers become thought of as indispensable. True, the ancient practice of using manure and compost does help speed crop growth, but this also depletes the land from which the organic material in the compost is taken. The [[fukuoka-compost-unnecessary-philosophy]] follows directly from this observation -- even well-meaning organic interventions can become forms of manipulation.

## The Illusion of Control

Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] rests on the observation that human attempts to control nature invariably produce the opposite of the intended result. Pesticides create resistant pests. Fertilizers degrade the soil they are meant to enrich. Irrigation causes salinization. Each intervention creates a new problem that requires yet another intervention, leading to an endless spiral of escalating control that ultimately destroys the very resource it seeks to manage.

The return to nature means abandoning this spiral of control and trusting in the inherent wisdom of natural processes. This is not passivity -- it is the most active form of intelligence, requiring deep observation, patience, and the courage to let go of the illusion that human knowledge can improve upon nature's designs.
