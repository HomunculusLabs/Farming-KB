# Fuelwood Forestry Systems

Fuelwood forestry systems are designed plantings of trees and shrubs managed
primarily for sustainable wood fuel production. In the Permaculture Designers
Manual, Mollison treats fuelwood as a fundamental need of any rural or
semi-rural settlement and designs forestry systems to supply it reliably from
on-site resources. Rather than relying on fossil fuels or external timber
sources, permaculture forestry integrates fuelwood production into the broader
landscape design, using coppice and pollard systems, nitrogen-fixing species,
and multi-strata plantings that also yield food, fodder, habitat, and soil
conservation benefits alongside their primary fuelwood function.

## Fuelwood Demand and Yield Calculations

### Estimating Household Demand

Mollison provides baseline figures for fuelwood consumption. A typical
household using wood for cooking and heating requires 3 to 5 cubic meters
of air-dried wood per year in temperate climates and 2 to 3 cubic meters in
subtropical and tropical climates where heating demands are lower. These
figures vary with stove efficiency, insulation quality of the dwelling, and
cooking practices. Improved woodstoves and rocket stoves can reduce
consumption by 40 to 60 percent compared to open fires, significantly
reducing the area required for fuelwood production.

### Tree Yield Estimation

Yield per hectare depends on species, climate, soil quality, and management
regime. Fast-growing species under intensive short-rotation coppice can
produce 10 to 20 cubic meters per hectare per year in favorable conditions.
Species such as Eucalyptus spp., Leucaena leucocephala, Casuarina spp., and
Grevillea robusta are among the highest producers in their respective
climates. Under less intensive management, yields of 5 to 10 cubic meters
per hectare per year are more typical. Mollison advises designing for the
lower end of yield estimates to ensure reliability, as actual yields are
affected by drought, pest damage, and management inconsistencies.

### Area Sizing

To calculate the required fuelwood plantation area, divide annual household
demand by the expected per-hectare yield. For a household needing 4 cubic
meters per year from a plantation yielding 8 cubic meters per hectare per
year, 0.5 hectares of managed fuelwood forest is required. Adding a 20
percent buffer for variability gives a target of 0.6 hectares. This area
can be distributed across multiple small plantings integrated with other
land uses rather than concentrated in a single block.

## Coppice Systems

### Principles of Coppicing

Coppicing is the practice of cutting trees near ground level and allowing
them to regrow from the stump (stool). Most broadleaf hardwoods and many
other species readily coppice, producing multiple new shoots that grow
rapidly in full sunlight. The cycle of cutting and regrowth can be repeated
indefinitely, making coppice one of the most sustainable methods of wood
production. Coppice stools can remain productive for centuries with proper
management, and the repeated cutting cycle actually extends the lifespan of
the tree beyond what a single trunk would achieve.

### Rotation Lengths

The coppice rotation (the interval between harvests) depends on the intended
product and species growth rate. Short rotations of 1 to 3 years produce
small-diameter material suitable for kindling, bean poles, and craft wood.
Medium rotations of 5 to 10 years produce poles for fencing, garden stakes,
and light construction. Long rotations of 15 to 25 years produce timber-
sized material suitable for construction and larger fuelwood pieces. Mollison
recommends designing a mixture of rotation lengths across the fuelwood area
to provide a continuous supply of different wood sizes throughout the year.

### Pollarding

Pollarding is similar to coppicing but the cut is made above the reach of
grazing animals, typically at 2 to 3 meters height. This allows the
production area to be simultaneously used for grazing by [[mollison-designers-free-range-poultry-systems]] or livestock. Pollarded trees produce high
fodder yields in addition to fuelwood and are well-suited to hedgerow and
alley cropping configurations. Species such as ash, elm, oak, willow, and
poplar respond well to pollarding.

## Species Selection for Fuelwood

### Fast-Growing Fuelwood Species

Species selection must match climate, soil, and management objectives.
Eucalyptus species are among the fastest-growing fuelwood trees in
subtropical and tropical zones, producing dense, hot-burning wood with
high energy content per unit volume. However, they can be water-demanding
and allelopathic, requiring careful placement. Leucaena leucocephala is
a nitrogen-fixing legume that coppices vigorously and provides both
fuelwood and high-protein fodder. Casuarina equisetifolia thrives on
sandy, infertile soils and produces excellent firewood.

### Temperate Fuelwood Species

In temperate climates, oak (Quercus spp.), ash (Fraxinus spp.), hawthorn
(Crataegus spp.), hazel (Corylus avellana), and willow (Salix spp.) are
traditional coppice species with proven fuelwood performance. Oak produces
the highest energy-density wood but grows more slowly. Willow and poplar
provide rapid biomass production on wet or marginal sites. Mollison
recommends mixed-species plantings that combine fast-growing pioneers for
early yield with longer-lived species for sustained production.

### Multi-Function Species

Fuelwood species should be selected for multiple functions wherever possible.
Nitrogen-fixing trees such as Acacia, Albizia, Robinia, and tagasaste
improve soil fertility while producing fuelwood. Fruit-bearing trees such as
mulberry, carob, and honey locust provide food alongside wood. Species with
good fodder value support integrated livestock-forestry systems. This multi-
function approach is central to [[permaculture-design]] and ensures that fuelwood
plantations contribute to the overall productivity and resilience of the
property.

## Integration with Water and Land Systems

Fuelwood forestry integrates closely with [[mollison-designers-keyline-land-shaping]] [[water-management]]. Trees planted on keyline contours benefit from
the improved soil moisture distribution and in turn stabilize slopes and
filter runoff. Riparian plantings of willow and poplar provide both fuelwood
and stream bank stabilization. Fuelwood plantations on the outer zones of
the property serve as [[mollison-designers-windbreak-design-species]] for
inner productive areas, performing the dual function of shelter and wood
production. The design should position fuelwood production in Zones 3 and 4,
where the area required for reliable supply can be accommodated without
competing with intensive food production in inner zones.

## Summary

Fuelwood forestry systems provide a sustainable, on-site supply of wood fuel
through coppice and pollard management of fast-growing tree species. Yield
calculations based on household demand and species productivity determine the
required plantation area. Multi-function species that fix nitrogen, provide
fodder, and contribute to soil conservation maximize the overall productivity
of the forestry system. Integrated with keyline water management and windbreak
design, fuelwood forestry becomes a reliable, self-renewing element of the
permaculture landscape.
