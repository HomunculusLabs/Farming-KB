# Somalia Refugee Camp Gardens and Desert Revegetation by Hand

Fukuoka's 1985-1986 mission to Somali refugee camps near Ethiopia
demonstrated that vegetable gardens could be established directly
in desert conditions with the simplest possible methods. His
experience revealed both the extraordinary potential for grassroots
revegetation and the political obstacles facing agricultural aid
in developing nations.

## Origin of the Mission

The trip came through an invitation from a UNEP Action to Combat
Desertification official who asked Fukuoka to come up with ideas for
greening deserts. Fukuoka had been thinking about desertification
for seven years. The natural way of farming he had practiced for
over forty years could serve as a means for stopping and revegetating
deserts and as a low-cost method of farming arid lands.

## No Agricultural Guidance Infrastructure

When Fukuoka attempted to carry out his plan, he discovered that
"nowhere to go" existed. Although aid to Africa in food, clothing,
and other articles was being offered in vast quantities, no
institution existed anywhere in the vast continent for providing
direct agricultural guidance to farmers and peasants.

No one, apparently, was working in that capacity at all. After
searching all over Tokyo, he learned that several young Japanese
volunteers were trying to set up a 75-acre farm near Ethiopian
refugee camps in a remote area of Somalia.

## Political Obstacles in Somalia

Fukuoka's proposal of peasant self-sufficiency through natural farming
ran directly counter to Somali government policy of encouraging cash
crop production on large plantations. He received threats: "Giving
seeds to nomadic people and telling them to become farmers will be a
blatant act of contempt." Donations aimed at self-sufficiency were
viewed as rebellion against national policy. Somehow, he departed.

## Supplies and Journey

Fukuoka traveled with 600 kilograms of daikon donated by the
Murata Nursery in Matsuyama, several hundred kilograms of
vegetable seeds from individual contributors, and several hundred
seedlings of Japanese fruit trees. He transferred through multiple
cities to Mogadishu, then boarded a small Cessna for the
backcountry.

## Aerial Observations

From the air, a splendid panorama of the vast savanna dotted with
thorny bushes opened up. Despite the appearance of aridity,
Fukuoka noted encouraging signs: at least one live spring existed
within any field of view; two rivers at least a thousand
kilometers long flow through the desert; and the desert sand
contained clay, which he considered cause for hope.

He told the young people of Somalia: "This earth is young that it
is sleeping. The land must be awakened from its slumber with
natural farming."

## Refugee Camp Conditions and Humor

Hundreds of thousands of refugees from Ethiopia were living in
temporary huts that were "little more than bird's nests made of thorny
branches." Food consisted entirely of wheat from the U.S., old rice
from Japan, and macaroni from Italy. There were no vegetables.

Yet Fukuoka found "healthful, bright-eyed children running about and
playing." Refugees had humorous perspectives on food aid: "America's
generosity is aimed at turning us into bread-eating people. As for
Italy, it figures that by giving us free macaroni for three years,
we'll turn into macaroni eaters," they laughed.

## Failed Foreign Aid Farms and Salt Buildup

Fukuoka observed multiple failed projects: a Russian-aided farm with
long irrigation canals left to crumble; a 500-hectare U.S.-built farm
cleared by bulldozers that looked "like the empty remains of an
abandoned airfield"; a Japanese volunteer farm that failed when
irrigation pump oil ran out; and a French farm that succeeded only
because large trees provided shade and Somalian farmers did the work.

The conventional method of leveling land with bulldozers and pumping
river water inevitably causes salt buildup, just as in California.
Fukuoka doubted such methods could be sustained for long.

## The Children's Garden Breakthrough

The breakthrough came through the simplest possible method.
Fukuoka gave seeds to curious children who thrust out their
hands. Gesticulating, he showed them how to make a furrow with a
stick on a sand dune, drop seeds in the furrow, and apply river
water for three days.

Two or three days later, about twenty children beckoned him to
follow them to the river's edge. There he saw seedlings of
daikon, onions, cucumbers, and other vegetables sprouting in
small round gardens a couple of yards in diameter.

## Rapid Spread

Word spread rapidly. Older youths began asking for seed. Within
five days, "tens and hundreds of women and old people gathered
around me." The two large bags of seed disappeared.

Together with children and forty or fifty adults, he scattered
seeds in clay pellets over rock-strewn savanna, mixed rice seed
with barnyard millet and proso millet, ran water over them, and
had everyone stamp down on the scattered seeds.

## Results After Departure and Plant Irrigation

Following his return to Japan, Fukuoka received encouraging reports:
large vegetables were being grown; little gardens had sprung up around
many huts; lemons, citrus, persimmon and pomegranate trees were growing
especially fast, some reaching five feet in half a year; and banana
and papaya saplings planted below acacias were growing well. In spring
1986, the Somali government recognized the benefits of home gardens.

Fukuoka also proposed "plant irrigation": instead of canals, create
a green belt of various tree types, using roots to draw river water
underground and deeply penetrate dry ground. He had gathered samples
of several plants in Australia that could help combat desertification
and remove salt from topsoil.
