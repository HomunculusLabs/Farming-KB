# Casing Soils and Microbial Fruiting Triggers

## Overview

Casing soils cover colonized [[mushroom-cultivation-bulk-substrates]] (straw, compost) to stimulate
mushrooms that benefit from a surface interface. They give primordia more time
to form in a high-humidity environment, help them avoid drying out, and can
provide [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] needed for fruiting. Casing is applied after
substrate colonization is complete to reduce competition from organisms.

Most wood-decomposing mushrooms (shiitakes, oysters) do not require microbial
interactions for fruiting, so casing is generally reserved for species that grow
on [[comparison-wood-chip-mulch-vs-leaf-mold-vs-composted-manure-soil-building]], grasses, and agricultural wastes -- primarily terrestrial
mushrooms in the wild.

## Casing Soil Composition

Common materials include [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-coco-coir-and-peat-moss]], coconut coir, vermiculite, perlite, sand,
lime, and gypsum. A high-quality casing soil has:

- High water retention
- Good aeration
- pH neutral to slightly acidic
- Loose structure with minute air pockets (not packed tight)

The casing depth should be proportional to biomass below: approximately 1 inch
deep for every 6-8 inches of colonized media. Rake or dibble the surface to
create ridges -- primordia form in the hollows between ridges, developing caps
and swelling with surrounding water.

### Moisture Testing

Squeeze a handful of wet casing: a few drops of water is perfect. A steady
stream means too wet; no drips means too dry. Allow time for media to absorb
water, as some materials (coir, peat) are hydrophobic at first. Too wet casing
suffocates aerobic mycelium and creates havens for competing molds and bacteria.
Too dry casing prevents primordia development.

### pH Considerations

Soil microbes operate within narrow pH windows. Test the pH of all soil or
media planned for casing to replicate the mushroom's preferred native
conditions. Inexpensive pH paper or a digital pH meter can be used.

### Pasteurizing Casing Soil

Many growers pasteurize casing soil using low heat (130-145 degrees F / 54-63
degrees C) for six to eight hours. This preserves heat-tolerant beneficial
microbes while preventing competitive pathogens. Sterilizing casing soil is not
recommended unless reintroducing specific microbial communities.

## Casing with Microbial Slurries

Many mushroom species require or benefit from certain microbial activity for
fruiting. The compatible microbes remain somewhat mysterious, but species known
to benefit include:

- Agaricus spp. (white button, portabella, almond portabella)
- Lepiota spp. (parasol)
- Stropharia spp. ([[cotter-king-stropharia-cultivation]])
- Clitocybe spp. (blewits)
- Morchella spp. (morels)

### The Key Discovery

Agaricus growers have found that [[sterilized-casing-soil-recipe-peat-vermiculite-limestone]] (all microbes
destroyed) results in zero yields, while pasteurized casing soil almost always
induces fruiting. The exact mechanism is still in question but must involve
chemical or physical aspects signaling the mycelium to devote energy to
fruiting. Cotter emphasizes that successful cultivation requires attention to
other kingdoms of life, not just the fungus itself.

### Collecting Microbial Samples

The best method: collect soil from the stem base of a wild or recently
harvested mushroom. The soil at the stem base contains the right combination of
microbes embedded in or adhering to the mycelial hyphae. If no mushrooms are
available, use native soil from near where the species fruits naturally.
