# Pond Edge Planting

From Permaculture: A Designers' Manual by Bill Mollison.

## Bank Stabilisers

The stabilisation of banks by grasses and clump plants helps considerably in reducing turbidity and algae in ponds. Trees are a danger on dam walls; if they fall (and they often do in those conditions) they take part of the wall with them. However, bamboos, ginger, sweet potato, pepino, and clump grasses assist bank stabilisation without this risk.

Trees at the base of walls are advantageous in shading, removing water, and reducing weeds. The key bank stabiliser species are:

- Juncus (rushes)
- Scirpus (bulrushes)
- Various grasses and clovers
- Phyla nodosa (Lippia)
- Bamboo clumps
- Pampas grass clumps

It is always an advantage to smooth-finish banks and surrounds so that a mower or scythe can be used until the selected plants take over.

## Surface Stabilisers

Pond surface stabilisers reduce light penetration and thus algae growth:

- Water lilies (Nymphaea)
- Azolla (a small floating fern that also fixes nitrogen)
- Water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes) -- productive but potentially invasive

## Wet Food Patches

In the domestic garden layout, the wet food patch (A) receives clean water and grows wet crops such as kangkong, watercress, Chinese water chestnut, and similar species. The bank is planted to dry staple foods -- yam, sweet potato, cassava -- which benefit somewhat from the wastewater. Some of this crop is under partial shade, which taro appreciates, as do some other sub-aquatics.

All tank overflow goes first to the wet garden bed, which is in part under the house trellis, in part in the open. This creates a productive edge between the aquatic and terrestrial zones.

## The "Grey" Water Patch

The grey water patch contains no direct food plants and no root crop, only vigorous, damp-tolerant, mulch-tolerant green forage crop:

- Comfrey
- Banana grass
- Lemongrass
- Legumes

These are regularly cut and removed either to the animal pens as green feed or to the garden beds. The area is sunken and surrounded by a bank on which can grow pigeon pea, papaya, banana, or all of these and more.

## Swale Ponds and Wet Zones

Swale sections can be over-deepened so that although the swale lip is surveyed level, its floor may rise and fall. Deepening is most effective in clay-fraction soils, and may result in shallow ponds for water-needy crops. Widening is most effective in sand or volcanic-fraction soils, and readily admits water to the ground table.

Two pit systems are useful in swales: one mulch and manure-filled for heavy nutrient feeders (yams, bananas, etc.) and the other to hold oil drums, plastic liners, or tyre ponds as a sealed water reserve for watering young plants. These can be planted with lotus, kangkong, watercress, Chinese water chestnut, or like crop.

Keeping the swale width to the access width planned, sections can be widened at regular intervals to take assemblies of plants, to dig ponds or mulch pits, and to plant trees of higher water need.

## Erosion Control on Steep Banks

Even small logs and branches, pegged across erosion channels, build up a layer-cake of silt and leaves, beside which can be planted willow, Acacia, or any other fibrous-rooted and hardy species, which then act as a permanent silt trap. Mulch behind logs and barriers quickly stabilises the seed bed for planting.

Small wire netting fences, with stone-weighted hay uphill, will trap silt and spread water, as will cross-swales of lemongrass or Vetiver grass.

On very steep slopes, there is often no recourse other than to plant pampas, bamboo, lemongrass, and root-mat pioneers and to make upslope plantings of chestnut, Acacia, carob, olive or other large species which will cascade seed downslope over time.

## The "Net and Pan" Pattern

The "net and pan" planting pattern is an effective control in overgrazed, eroded, mined or bulldozed sites. If tyres are available, the "pans" can be made from these, filled with mulch, and the diversion drains led in above the tread level. For people with access to logs, these can be staked cross-slope, on a slight downhill grade so that water is made to zig-zag across the erosion face, and hence absorb into the ground.

## Taro Terrace Bunds

In taro terrace systems, bunds planted to Dolichos hosei or Phyla nodosa (was Lippia nodosa) are useful nitrogenous mulch sources, and remain short and trimmed at the water level by Tilapia or grass carp. This demonstrates the productive integration of livestock with pond-edge plant management.
