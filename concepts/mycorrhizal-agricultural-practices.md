# Mycorrhizal Agricultural Practices

How farming and gardening practices affect mycorrhizal fungi — what helps, what hurts, and how to manage soils for maximum mycorrhizal benefit. Based on Jeff Lowenfels' *Teaming with Fungi*. See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for the biological overview and [[mycorrhizal-inoculum-production]] for making inoculum.

## Practices That Harm Mycorrhizae

### Tillage

Any surface soil disturbance — plowing, tilling, stock grazing — damages the belowground mycorrhizal network. Arbuscular mycorrhizal spores concentrate in the top 6-15 inches (15-38 cm) of soil, so tillage destroys or buries spores beyond root reach. Hyphae are severed, cutting nutrient transport.

**Fall tillage is especially damaging** — hyphae damaged in fall won't be available for cover crops or spring planting, reducing phosphorus availability until a new network forms.

### High Phosphorus Fertilizer

Excess phosphorus is the most common inhibitor of mycorrhizal formation. Many farmers saturate soil with synthetic phosphate to ensure availability, but this directly suppresses mycorrhizal spore germination and hyphal growth. **It can take decades for phosphorus levels to reduce enough for mycorrhizae to recover.**

Even organic farmers can create this problem: animal manures contain high phosphate salts (often >1000 ppm), and manure-amended soil can retain excess phosphorus for 100+ years.

**Practical threshold:** Most experts recommend <80 ppm phosphorus or <75 μg phosphorus per gram of soil for mycorrhizal establishment (figures are contested and vary by plant type).

### High Nitrogen

Excess nitrogen also suppresses mycorrhizal formation, though its effects are somewhat less persistent than phosphorus.

### Fungicides

Some fungicides are directly toxic to arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, attacking their unique cell walls or metabolic processes. Seed coatings and soil drenches are the most damaging. **Spraying leaves has far less impact than soil drenching.**

Notable exception: metalaxyl-based fungicides may actually stimulate some mycorrhizal formation, perhaps by eliminating competing fungi.

### Herbicides and Pesticides

Not directly toxic to mycorrhizae, but if they kill the host plant, spore production drops. Nonselective reach can impact soil food web organisms that support mycorrhizae. Some pesticides increase colonization indirectly by killing pests, producing healthier plants with more exudates.

### Field Burning

Spores die above 120°F (49°C). Post-harvest field burning creates enough heat to kill surface spores. Re-inoculation needed after burning.

### GM Crops with Bt

Bt toxin released into the rhizosphere can negatively impact helper organisms in the mycorrhizosphere and reduce mycorrhizal colonization. More study needed as GM crop varieties expand.

### Non-Host Crop Rotation

Planting non-mycorrhizal crops (brassicas, beets, buckwheat) deprives fungi of carbon. Without host roots, spore populations decline and re-establishment via inoculation can take up to 2 months. Some brassicas release allelopathic chemicals that prevent mycorrhizal spore germination — these persist in soil even after plants are removed.

### Fallowing

Leaving fields cropless eliminates the carbon source mycorrhizae need. If fallow is necessary, inoculate before the next growing season.

## Practices That Help Mycorrhizae

### Cover Cropping

Planting mycorrhizal host plants as cover crops maintains fungal populations during off-season. This is critical for short-season climates (e.g., Alaska) where re-establishment takes too long.

### Organic Amendments and Mulches

Soils on organically maintained farms carry the same arbuscular fungal colonization as nearby undisturbed land. Effective organic mulches: chicken litter, rice hulls, straw, sewage sludge. Each has unique impacts on colonization and reproduction rates. Compost and composted manures are compatible, but monitor phosphorus buildup from heavy manure use.

### Reduced Tillage

No-till and reduced-till systems preserve the mycorrhizal network. Hyphae remain intact and connected to host roots, maintaining continuous nutrient and water transport.

### Mycorrhizae-Host Crop Rotation

Rotate crops that form mycorrhizal associations (corn, legumes, potatoes, most vegetables) to maintain soil fungal populations. See [[non-mycorrhizal-plants]] for the list of plants that don't form associations.

### Appropriate Inoculation

When soil tests show low propagule counts, inoculate via:
- Seed coating
- Drip irrigation injection
- Liquid spray
- Rolled seed treatment
- Direct soil incorporation

### Low-Phosphorus Fertilizers

Choose fertilizers with P number of 6 or less. Compatible options: alfalfa meal (2-1-3), fish emulsion (5-2-2), soybean meal (7-2-1), compost tea. This ensures mycorrhizae can form while still providing nutrients.

### Ericaceous Plant Mycorrhizae

Blueberries, cranberries, heathers, and rhododendrons form ericoid mycorrhizae (not arbuscular). No commercial spore mix exists yet. **Unsterilized peat moss from previously colonized ericaceous plants** is the most effective inoculant — harvest from root zone in late summer/fall, including root fragments. Ten of 13 commercial peat brands tested at University of Vermont successfully established ericaceous mycorrhizae.

## Fertilizer Label Math

N-P-K numbers on fertilizer labels are misleading:
- N is actual nitrogen percentage
- P is phosphorus **pentoxide** (P₂O₅) — multiply by 0.44 for actual phosphorus
- K is potassium **oxide** (K₂O) — multiply by 0.83 for actual potassium

Example: A label showing 10-20-10 actually contains 10% N, 8.8% P, 8.3% K.
