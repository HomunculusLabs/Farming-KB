## Urban Food Marketing Strategies

The marketing of food produced through [[permaculture]] systems is as important as the production itself. [[bill-mollison]]’s [[permaculture-design]] Course devotes considerable attention to the diverse strategies available to small producers for selling their products directly, bypassing conventional wholesale and retail channels that typically capture the majority of food dollars.

## Farmers’ Markets

Farmers’ markets represent one of the most successful [[coleman-direct-marketing-and-csa-models]] channels for small-scale producers. In Tasmania, regional markets operate one day per week—either Saturday or Sunday—in cities and country towns. They serve as community gathering points where producers sell a remarkably broad range of goods: fresh produce, seedlings, small poultry and animals, eggs, old clothes, breads, baked goods, preserves, jams, fresh and dried herbs, soaps, shampoos, essential oils, rubbing oils, and massage oils. Musical bands often perform, and hot drink stalls provide additional income.

Individual stall operators at these markets typically earn between $300 and $400 per market day. Hot soup and strawberry-and-cream stalls alone have generated $600 in a single weekend. For many small farmers, one weekend of market income is sufficient to sustain their operation, allowing one partner to run the market stall while the other maintains outside employment or works the farm.

A critical success factor for farmers’ markets is producer control of market space. Wherever producers have failed to control market space, rentals have been increased until small farmers are priced out, transforming the market into a commercial operation featuring trucked-in goods rather than local production.

## Wayside Stalls

Wayside (roadside) stalls are the most effective mechanism for disposing of main crop surpluses—particularly bulk crops like pumpkins. These unmanned operations function on the honor system: produce is displayed in convenient $1, $2, and $5 lots with a slotted money box for payments.

An important behavioral observation: very few people steal the product, though some steal the money from the box. The closer a stall is located to town, the more likely the money box will be emptied. Despite these losses, the system works economically because the volume of product moved and the labor savings (no staffing required) outweigh the theft.

Wayside stalls can operate as shared enterprises among several producers, since they run all week and not every producer has access to a good traffic location. Joint leasing of a site allows multiple farmers to share the costs and benefits of a well-positioned stall.

## Door-to-Door Sales

Door-to-door selling in rural areas proves more lucrative than in urban settings because rural households purchase larger quantities per visit. Regular weekly appearances build strong customer relationships. Mollison’s personal experience from 1951 demonstrated that consistent, reliable service generates substantial income—thousands of dollars annually from a single acre of sweet corn and other vegetables.

The social dimension of door-to-door sales should not be underestimated. Regular visits generate cups of tea, bread and butter, and conversations that build the trust relationships essential for repeat business. Customers come to depend on seeing the vendor on specific days and want to know what will be available.

## Introducing New Crops

When introducing an unfamiliar crop to a market, the recommended strategy is to give the first lot away with recipes. Mollison’s experience with sweet corn in 1951—its first year of cultivation in Australia—illustrates this principle. Nobody recognized the crop, so it was wrapped in recipe sheets and given away in pairs of ears. When he returned with subsequent harvests, there was no difficulty selling all production, including quantities sufficient for customers to freeze.

The same approach worked with bread: giving away small loaves created demand for full-sized products. The principle is general: unfamiliar products require education, and free samples with preparation instructions are the most effective educational tool.

## Pricing Philosophy

Mollison recommends pricing below retail town prices for several reasons. Organic production typically costs less than [[microwave-sterilization-versus-conventional-methods-comparison]] (no purchased chemical inputs), so lower prices still maintain profitability. When people receive high-quality organic food at below-retail prices, they are more likely to become regular customers and buy in greater volume. If a producer earns more than $400 to $500 per week through direct marketing, Mollison considers them to be doing very well.

A note of caution: some marketers charge premium prices for organic products, which Mollison considers unfair precisely because production costs are lower. He advocates passing these savings to consumers to build market share and customer loyalty.

## Self-Pick Operations

Self-pick (pick-your-own) fruit operations require specific design considerations to be profitable:

### Plant Protection
Broad intercrop spacing is essential to prevent trampling. Mounded growing beds protect plants from foot traffic—strawberries mounded up and well-mulched with sawdust can withstand significant pedestrian traffic. [[query-how-do-i-grow-gooseberries-and-currants]] handle trampling better than raspberries or strawberries.

### The “Eat and Pay” Phenomenon
A key insight about self-pick operations is that consumers consistently eat more than they pay for. People typically pick six pints of strawberries but eat two in the field, paying for only four. Mollison notes: “You don’t take into account that they have eaten two.” Despite this uncounted consumption, returns remain high because the volume of paid product and the labor savings outweigh the losses.

### Crop Selection
Small fruits—gooseberries, strawberries, loganberries, [[query-how-to-grow-black-currants-at-home]], and red currants—are most suitable for self-pick. These crops tolerate handling better than delicate fruits and benefit from the mounded bed design that self-pick operations require.

## The City as Untapped Production Resource

Melbourne and San Francisco each contain approximately half a million citrus trees in private yards. Most of this fruit falls to the ground and rots. One entrepreneur surveyed Melbourne’s chestnut trees, identified several thousand productive specimens, offered owners wholesale prices for excess nuts, and built a $70,000 annual retail business from what was previously waste. This led to grafting operations, seed sales, and ultimately farm purchase—all from urban resources that no one else recognized as valuable.

An Adelaide operator runs 9,000 sheep as a suburban lawn-mowing service, renting sheep at $6 per week. This represents a dual income stream: the sheep owner earns rental income while providing fire hazard reduction at a cost comparable to one hour of human labor. The demand for this service exceeds supply.

## Cooperative Processing Infrastructure

Community-shared processing equipment multiplies the value of raw production. Mollison advocates for well-off individuals to purchase and rent out processing equipment within their districts. Key equipment includes grain grinders, olive presses, sunflower seed presses, [[comparison-steam-distillation-vs-co2-extraction-for-essential-oils]] equipment, and fermentation vats.

The Yugoslavian model of town-owned stills is particularly instructive: every small town hall houses a community distillation facility where residents bring fruit, receive proportional output, and the town council operates the equipment as a public service.

## Food Cooperatives

Food cooperatives, numbering approximately 40,000 in the United States alone, represent a significant direct-marketing channel. These typically involve 400 to 500 member families and have evolved from simple buying clubs to organizations that purchase and operate their own farms with hired managers.

## Warehouse Marketing Spaces

Converting old warehouses into marketing spaces provides affordable retail venues for multiple small producers. Weekend markets in these spaces generate $300 to $400 per stall. The key is securing space through purchase or long-term lease, then turning management over to producer marketing groups.

## Regional Self-Sufficiency Through Non-Competitive Specialization

The most sophisticated marketing strategy involves regional planning. Rather than having every farmer grow the same crops (leading to boom-bust cycles where all apple growers succeed or fail together), permaculture consultants work to identify what grows best on each property and encourage specialization. One farmer with suitable land produces grain for the entire district while others focus on vegetables, fruit, or livestock. Each specialist has a guaranteed local market, and the district achieves self-sufficiency without wasteful competition.

This approach is described as “the very opposite of what has been happening under the agricultural department,” which historically filled whole valleys with identical orchards.

## Consulting and Nursery Functions

Permaculture consultants should actively stimulate clients to establish nursery operations for specific species—bamboo, aquatics, quail, guinea fowl, pigeons, or soil conditioning tools like the Wallace plow. Once early clients establish these supply businesses, the consultant can recommend them to subsequent clients. Within 12 to 24 months of beginning consultancy work in an area, a complete local supplier network should be in place.

## Lessons from Mistakes

Mollison shares a significant personal error: planting 12 acres of market garden single-handedly without accounting for the labor of picking and harvesting. He never managed to see more than four acres of the planting, spending months trying to harvest what he had sown. This experience underscores a critical marketing principle: production capacity must account for harvest and distribution labor, not just planting and growing.
