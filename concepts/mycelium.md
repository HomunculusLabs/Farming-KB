# Mycelium

Mycelium is the vegetative body of a fungus, consisting of a mass of branching,
thread-like hyphae that form an extensive network through soil, wood, and other
substrates. Often described as the hidden "body" of the mushroom, mycelium is
the primary growth and feeding structure of fungi, while the mushroom itself is
merely the reproductive organ. The study of mycelium has revealed it to be one
of the most remarkable biological structures on Earth, functioning as both an
individual organism and a distributed intelligence system.

## Structure and Growth

Mycelium is composed of hyphae, individual tubular cells that grow by extending
at their tips. Each hypha is typically 2 to 10 micrometers in diameter and can
extend at rates of up to several millimeters per day under optimal conditions.
Hyphae are divided by septa (cross-walls) that contain pores allowing cytoplasmic
streaming and the transport of nutrients, organelles, and signaling molecules
between cells.

As hyphae branch and interweave, they form a dense network called a mycelial
mat. In some species, particularly wood-decay fungi, this mat can become
extremely dense and leathery. A single individual of Armillaria ostoyae (honey
fungus) in Oregon covers over 2,385 acres and is estimated to be between 2,400
and 8,650 years old, making it one of the largest and oldest organisms on Earth.

## Ecological Roles

Mycelium plays fundamental roles in virtually all terrestrial ecosystems:

**Decomposition**: Saprotrophic fungi break down dead organic matter, recycling
carbon, nitrogen, and other nutrients back into the ecosystem. Mycelium secretes
extracellular enzymes including cellulases, ligninases, and proteases that
decompose complex organic polymers that few other organisms can process. Without
fungal decomposition, the world's forests would be buried under mountains of
undecomposed plant material. See
[[mycelium-running-white-rot-brown-rot-fungi|rot fungi and wood decomposition]]
for details on decay mechanisms.

**[[bloomfield-mycorrhizal-symbiosis-and-fungal-ecology]]**: Approximately 90 percent of plant species form
mycorrhizal associations with fungi. In exchange for carbohydrates from
photosynthesis, the fungal mycelium extends the plant's root system dramatically,
providing access to water and minerals (particularly phosphorus) that would
otherwise be unavailable. See [[mycorrhiza|mycorrhizal associations]] for a
comprehensive overview.

**Soil Formation**: [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]] help bind soil particles together,
improving soil structure, water retention, and erosion resistance. Glomalin, a
glycoprotein produced by [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] fungi, is a major component of
soil organic matter and contributes significantly to carbon sequestration.

**Biological Networks**: Research by Suzanne Simard and others has demonstrated
that trees can share carbon, nutrients, and chemical alarm signals through common
[[dighton-mycorrhizal-networks-carbon-transfer]], leading to the concept of the "wood wide web." See
[[mycelium-as-natures-internet|mycelium as nature's internet]] for extended
discussion.

## Mycelium in Cultivation

In [[mushroom-cultivation|mushroom cultivation]], establishing a healthy,
vigorous mycelium culture is the critical first step. The process typically
begins with inoculation of a sterile substrate with fungal spores or living
tissue. As the mycelium colonizes the substrate, it digests available nutrients
and consolidates into a dense white network.

Cultivators assess mycelial health by its appearance: healthy mycelium is
typically bright white, fluffy, and grows rapidly with organized, rhizomorphic
(strand-like) structures. Contaminated cultures may show discoloration (green,
black, pink), unusual textures (slimy, powdery), or off-putting odors. The
distinction between healthy mycelium and common contaminants is a foundational
skill for any cultivator.

## Industrial Applications

Mycelium has attracted significant commercial interest for diverse applications:

- **Biodegradable packaging**: Companies like Ecovative Design grow mycelium on
  agricultural waste to produce foam-like materials that replace polystyrene.
- **[[natural-building-materials-guide]]**: Mycelium composites are being developed as insulation,
  structural panels, and even bricks for sustainable construction.
- **Textiles**: Mycelium-based leather alternatives are being produced for use in
  fashion and furniture.
- **Bioremediation**: Mycelium can break down or sequester environmental
  pollutants including petroleum hydrocarbons and heavy metals.
- **Food**: Mycelium is cultivated as a protein-rich food source and as a
  substrate for fermentation in products like tempeh and Quorn.

See [[mycelium-composites-materials|mycelium composites]] for materials science
details.

## Growth Patterns

Mycelium exhibits several distinct growth patterns:

- **Rhizomorphic growth**: Organized, strand-like growth indicating vigorous,
  healthy mycelium. Rhizomorphic growth is preferred by cultivators.
- **Cottony (tomentose) growth**: Fluffy, undifferentiated growth that may
  indicate less vigorous genetics or suboptimal conditions.
- **[[growing-gourmet-sclerotia-formation-biology-mushrooms]]**: Dense, hardened survival structures produced by
  species like Psilocybe mexicana and P. tampanensis.
