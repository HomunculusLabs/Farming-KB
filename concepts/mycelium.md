# Mycelium

Mycelium is the vegetative body of a fungus, consisting of a mass of branching,
thread-like hyphae that form an extensive network through soil, wood, and other
substrates. Often described as the hidden "body" of the mushroom, mycelium is
the primary growth and feeding structure of fungi, while the mushroom itself
is merely the reproductive organ. The study of mycelium has revealed it to be
one of the most remarkable biological structures on Earth, functioning as
both an individual organism and a distributed intelligence system.

## Structure and Growth

Mycelium is composed of hyphae, individual tubular cells that grow by
extending at their tips. Each hypha is typically 2 to 10 micrometers in
diameter and can extend at rates of up to several millimeters per day under
optimal conditions. Hyphae are divided into cells by septa (cross-walls) that
contain pores allowing cytoplasmic streaming and the transport of nutrients,
organelles, and signaling molecules between cells.

As hyphae branch and interweave, they form a dense network called a mycelial
mat. In some species, particularly wood-decay fungi, this mat can become
extremely dense and leathery, forming structures visible to the naked eye.
The total length of mycelial networks can be staggering: a single individual
of Armillaria ostoyae (honey fungus) in Oregon covers over 2,385 acres and
is estimated to be between 2,400 and 8,650 years old, making it one of the
largest and oldest organisms on Earth.

## Ecological Roles

Mycelium plays fundamental roles in virtually all terrestrial ecosystems:

**Decomposition**: Saprotrophic fungi break down dead organic matter, recycling
carbon, nitrogen, and other nutrients back into the ecosystem. Mycelium
secretes extracellular enzymes including cellulases, ligninases, and proteases
that decompose complex organic polymers that few other organisms can process.
Without fungal decomposition, the world's forests would be buried under
mountains of undecomposed plant material, and nutrient cycling would
effectively cease.

**Mycorrhizal Symbiosis**: Approximately 90 percent of plant species form
mycorrhizal associations with fungi. In exchange for carbohydrates produced
through photosynthesis, the fungal mycelium extends the plant's root system
dramatically, providing access to water and minerals (particularly phosphorus)
that would otherwise be unavailable. This symbiotic relationship is critical
for plant health and ecosystem productivity.

**Soil Formation**: Mycelial networks help bind soil particles together,
improving soil structure, water retention, and erosion resistance. Glomalin, a
glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, is a major component
of soil organic matter and contributes significantly to carbon sequestration.
Soils rich in mycelium tend to be more fertile and resilient.

**Biological Networks**: The mycelial network functions as an information and
resource distribution system within ecosystems. Research by Suzanne Simard and
others has demonstrated that trees can share carbon, nutrients, and chemical
alarm signals through common mycorrhizal networks, leading to the concept of
the "wood wide web." Mother trees may preferentially transfer resources to
their own offspring through these networks.

## Mycelium in Cultivation

In mushroom cultivation, establishing a healthy, vigorous mycelium culture is
the critical first step. The process typically begins with inoculation of a
sterile substrate with fungal spores or living tissue from a desired mushroom
strain. As the mycelium colonizes the substrate, it digests available
nutrients and consolidates into a dense white network. The quality and vigor
of the mycelium directly determines the success and yield of subsequent
fruiting.

Cultivators assess mycelial health by its appearance: healthy mycelium is
typically bright white, fluffy, and grows rapidly with organized, rhizomorphic
(strand-like) structures. Contaminated cultures may show discoloration
(green, black, pink), unusual textures (slimy, powdery), or off-putting
odors. The distinction between healthy mycelium and common contaminants is
a foundational skill for any cultivator.

## Industrial Applications

Mycelium has attracted significant commercial interest for diverse
applications:

- **Biodegradable packaging**: Companies like Ecovative Design grow mycelium
  on agricultural waste to produce foam-like materials that can replace
  polystyrene packaging and are fully compostable.
- **Building materials**: Mycelium composites are being developed as
  insulation, structural panels, and even bricks for sustainable
  construction, with properties comparable to conventional materials.
- **Textiles**: Mycelium-based leather alternatives are being produced for
  use in fashion and furniture, offering a sustainable alternative to
  animal leather.
- **Bioremediation**: Mycelium can break down or sequester environmental
  pollutants including petroleum hydrocarbons, heavy metals, and pesticides.
  The term "mycoremediation" describes this application.
- **Food**: Mycelium is cultivated as a protein-rich food source and as a
  substrate for fermentation in products like tempeh and Quorn.

## Growth Patterns

Mycelium exhibits several distinct growth patterns that reflect its
adaptability and intelligence:

- **Rhizomorphic growth**: Organized, strand-like growth that indicates
  vigorous, healthy mycelium seeking resources. Rhizomorphic growth is
  preferred by cultivators as it typically leads to more robust fruiting.
- **Cottony (tomentose) growth**: Fluffy, undifferentiated growth that may
  indicate less vigorous genetics or suboptimal growing conditions. While
  still capable of fruiting, cottony mycelium often produces lower yields.
- **Sclerotia formation**: Some species form dense, hardened masses of
  mycelium called sclerotia as survival structures. Psilocybe mexicana
  and P. tampanensis are notable for producing sclerotia containing
  psychoactive compounds.
