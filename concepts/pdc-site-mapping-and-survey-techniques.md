# PDC Site Mapping and Survey Techniques

Mapping and surveying form the visual language of [[permaculture-design]]. The Williams PDC teaches that a designer cannot create an effective design without first thoroughly mapping the existing conditions of a site. Mapping is not merely documentation -- it is a mode of thinking that reveals relationships, patterns, and design opportunities invisible to casual observation.

## The Base Map: Foundation of All Design Work

The base map is the map of the entire site within legal property limits. It serves as the canvas upon which all analytical data and design proposals are drawn. Williams provides specific guidance for creating and working with base maps.

### Sources for Base Maps

1. **County or city offices**: The primary recommended source. Local government offices maintain property maps that show legal boundaries, existing structures, and sometimes topographic contours.
2. **Google Earth**: When official maps are unavailable, Google Earth provides satellite imagery that can be used to trace property boundaries, identify existing features, and understand the broader site context.
3. **Professional surveyors**: For complex sites or when precise topographic data is needed, a professional land survey may be commissioned.

### Working with Base Maps

The original base map should be kept clean and set aside as reference. All analytical work happens on copies:

- **Tracing paper method**: Single sheet of tracing paper placed over the base map with all analytical data drawn on it. Multiple layers of tracing paper make it difficult to see the base map underneath, so a single comprehensive analytical sheet is recommended.
- **Transparency method**: Multiple transparent overlays, each containing related categories of data. One transparency might contain sectors and zones; another might show surface water flow and soil types. This method allows individual layers to be viewed independently or in combination.
- **Digital methods**: Computer programs can create layered digital base maps with toggleable layers, combining the flexibility of transparencies with the precision of digital tools.

### What to Include on Working Maps

The working base map should progressively accumulate:
- Property boundaries and legal dimensions
- Existing structures and their footprints
- Topographic contours (even approximate hand-contoured lines)
- Existing trees (with species and approximate canopy spread)
- Water features (streams, ponds, drainage, springs)
- Roads, paths, and access routes
- Utility lines and infrastructure
- Fence lines and property divisions
- North arrow and scale bar

## Topographic Survey Techniques

Understanding the shape of the land is essential for [[water-management]], earthworks design, and structure placement. Williams covers several approaches to topographic survey:

### Professional Topographic Survey

For significant earthworks or construction, a professional survey with contour intervals (typically 1-foot or 2-foot contours) provides the most reliable data. This is especially important for water-harvesting earthworks where grades must be precise.

### Approximate Contouring Methods

When professional surveys are not feasible, approximate contour lines can be established using:
- **A-frame level**: A simple homemade tool consisting of an A-shaped frame with a plumb bob. When the two legs sit at the same elevation, the plumb bob aligns with a marked center point. By walking the A-frame across a slope and marking points of equal elevation, contour lines can be mapped.
- **Water level**: A clear tube filled with water, where the water surface at both ends indicates the same elevation. Two people can quickly map contours by keeping the water level at the same height at both tube ends.
- **Laser level**: Modern laser levels can project a horizontal reference line across a site, allowing a single person to map contours by marking where the laser line intersects the ground surface.

### [[reading-the-landscape]]

Williams emphasizes that contour information is already written in the landscape for those who know how to read it:
- **Water flow patterns**: Water always flows perpendicular to contours, downhill along the steepest gradient. Existing erosion patterns, drainage channels, and sediment deposits reveal the topographic story.
- **Vegetation patterns**: Different plant communities often grow at different elevations and moisture levels. The boundary between a wet-loving plant community and a drought-tolerant one often follows a contour.
- **Soil color changes**: Variations in soil color across a slope can indicate changes in elevation, moisture retention, or soil composition.

## Soil Survey and Mapping

### Grid Sampling Method

The most systematic approach: establish a grid across the site and take soil samples at regular intervals (e.g., every 50 feet on a rectangular grid). Each sample is numbered and its location recorded on the base map.

### Opportunistic Sampling Method

Williams acknowledges that rigorous grid sampling may not always be feasible. The opportunistic approach takes samples wherever observable differences suggest varying conditions:
- Where two different grass species meet (may indicate soil type boundary)
- Where ground surface color changes are visible
- At the top, middle, and bottom of slopes
- Near existing trees versus open areas
- Where water tends to collect versus where it drains quickly

### Jar Test Soil Analysis

1. Fill a clear glass jar (mason jar works well) about half full with soil sample
2. Fill the rest with water
3. Close lid tightly and shake vigorously for several minutes
4. Let settle for 24-48 hours
5. Soil will settle in layers: sand on bottom (heaviest), silt in middle, clay on top (lightest)
6. Measure each layer with a ruler and calculate percentages
7. Optional organic matter layer may float on top

### Infiltration Rate Testing

Using the coffee-can infiltrometer:
1. Remove both ends of a metal coffee can
2. Drive the can approximately one inch into the soil surface
3. Fill a one-gallon container with water
4. Pour water slowly into the infiltrometer
5. Time how long for all water to infiltrate and disappear
6. Record results on the base map at each test location

### Mapping Soil Results

Soil survey results are mapped onto the working base map using:
- Color-coded areas for different soil types (sandy, loamy, clay-heavy)
- Numbered sample locations with corresponding data tables
- Infiltration rate values marked at each test point
- Lines indicating transitions between soil types

## Water Flow Mapping

Surface water flow mapping follows directly from topographic data:

1. Identify the highest and lowest points on the site
2. Draw arrows showing the direction water would flow during rainfall
3. Identify existing drainage channels, swales, and water courses
4. Note where water concentrates (potential erosion points)
5. Mark where water would naturally infiltrate versus where it would run off
6. Identify opportunities for water-harvesting earthworks
7. Check for existing drainage infrastructure (culverts, storm drains, French drains)

Williams emphasizes that the ultimate goal is to ensure all rainwater falling on the site stays on the site and infiltrates into the ground, or at minimum is cleansed before leaving the property.

## Sector Analysis Mapping

The sector analysis is presented as a circle overlaid on the base map, centered on Zone 0. Williams provides detailed guidance:

### Construction Method

1. Draw a large circle centered on the center of the site (Zone 0)
2. Create concentric rings equal to the number of sectors to be plotted
3. Starting from the center, draw angle lines out to the appropriate concentric ring for each sector
4. Recommended layering (inside to outside): summer sun, winter sun, prevailing winds, then remaining sectors
5. Color in the outermost arc of each sector first, then fill in wedges to avoid overlapping errors

### Standard Sectors to Map

- Fire hazard direction (essential on every design)
- Summer wind sector (~80 degrees wide)
- Winter wind sector (~80 degrees wide, different direction)
- Summer sun sector (solar azimuth at summer solstice)
- Winter sun sector (solar azimuth at winter solstice)
- Noise sectors (roads, industry, neighbors)
- Desirable views (to be preserved in design)
- Undesirable views (to be blocked in design)
- Pollution sources (chemical drift, light pollution)
- Wildlife corridors (animal movement routes)
- Privacy concerns (sight lines from neighbors)
