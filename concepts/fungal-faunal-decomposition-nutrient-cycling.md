# Fungal-Faunal Research in Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling

The research literature on fungal-faunal interactions in soil decomposition and nutrient cycling reveals a complex web of relationships between fungi, soil invertebrates, and ecosystem processes. The body of work cited across multiple studies demonstrates how grazing, comminution, [[spore-dispersal]], and competitive interactions between fungi and fauna shape the rates and pathways of nutrient cycling in terrestrial ecosystems.

## Soil Fauna as Fungal Grazers

A substantial body of research has documented the feeding preferences of soil microarthropods for specific fungal species. Parkinson et al. (1979) showed that collembolan grazing affected the rate of fungal colonization of leaf litter, establishing early evidence that fauna directly influence fungal community dynamics during decomposition. Reddy and Das (1983) further characterized microfungal food preferences of soil microarthropods in pine plantation ecosystems, revealing that these preferences are not random but reflect nutritional discrimination among available fungal taxa.

The collembolan *Onychiurus armatus* has been a model organism for studying fungal feeding preferences. Shaw (1985, 1988) demonstrated a consistent hierarchy in the fungal feeding preferences of *O. armatus*, showing that this collembolan selectively grazes certain fungal species over others. Newell (1984a, 1984b) extended this work by studying interactions between two decomposer basidiomycetes and *O. armatus* under Sitka spruce, demonstrating that selective grazing could alter both the distribution and abundance of competing fungi, with downstream effects on litter decomposition rates.

## Nematode-Fungal Interactions

Nematodes represent another important group of fungal grazers. Chen and Ferris (1999) investigated the effects of nematode grazing on nitrogen mineralization during [[fungal-decomposition]] of organic matter, finding that grazing altered nutrient cycling dynamics. Ruess and Dighton (1996) conducted cultural studies on soil nematodes and their fungal hosts, while Ruess et al. (2000) characterized food preferences of the fungal-feeding nematode *Aphelenchoides* species, demonstrating that nematode feeding could influence both fungal community composition and nutrient fluxes.

Sutherland and Fortin (1968) showed that the nematode *Aphelenchus avenae* could affect ectotrophic [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and the red pine mycorrhizal relationship, linking faunal grazing directly to mycorrhizal function and plant nutrition. Laakso and Setälä (1999) further explored population- and ecosystem-level effects of predation on microbial-feeding nematodes, revealing cascading trophic effects.

## Earthworms as Vectors of Fungal Propagules

Earthworms play a dual role in fungal-faunal interactions: as grazers on fungal mycelia and as important vectors for [[fungal-spore-dispersal]]. McGilveen and Cole (1976) documented spore dispersal of Endogonaceae ([[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]) by worms, ants, wasps, and birds, highlighting the multiple dispersal pathways available to mycorrhizal fungi. Reddell and Spain (1991) specifically identified earthworms as vectors of viable propagules of mycorrhizal fungi.

Cooke (1983) examined how fungi influence food selection by *Lumbricus terrestris*, while Moody et al. (1995, 1996) studied selective consumption of decomposing wheat straw by earthworms and the fate of fungal spores passing through earthworm guts. Görres et al. (2001) investigated soil micropore structure and carbon mineralization in earthworm burrows and casts, showing how earthworm engineering activity creates distinct microhabitats for fungal activity.

Gange (1993) demonstrated the translocation of mycorrhizal fungi by earthworms during early succession, establishing a mechanism by which earthworms could facilitate plant colonization of disturbed sites. Doube et al. (1994a, 1994b, 1995) explored interactions between earthworms, beneficial soil microorganisms, and root pathogens, proposing earthworms as a "delivery service" for biocontrol agents of root disease.

## Fungal Feeding by Mites and Insects

Oribatid mites are significant fungal feeders in soil. Mitchell and Parkinson (1976) documented fungal feeding by oribatid mites in aspen woodland soil. Shew and Beute (1979) found evidence for the involvement of soil-borne mites in *Pythium* pod rot of peanut, demonstrating how mite-fungal interactions can have important agricultural implications.

The interactions between insects and fungi span mutualism to pathogenesis. Bass and Cherrett (1996) described the fungus garden structure in leaf-cutting ants, while Cherrett et al. (1989) characterized the mutualism between leaf-cutting ants and their cultivated fungi. Wilding et al. (1989) provided a comprehensive treatment of insect-fungus interactions. Hanski (1989) reviewed fungivory as an ecological phenomenon linking fungi, insects, and ecosystem processes.

## Microbial Community Dynamics

The interactions between fauna and fungal communities have broader implications for microbial community structure. Visser (1985) reviewed the role of soil invertebrates in determining the composition of soil microbial communities. Coleman et al. (1990) studied seasonal and faunal effects on decomposition in semiarid prairie meadow and lodgepole pine forest. Nieminen and Setälä (2001) showed that bacteria and microbial-feeders modify the performance of decomposer fungi, demonstrating multi-trophic interactions.

Edwards (2000) reviewed soil invertebrate controls and microbial interactions in nutrient and organic matter dynamics in both natural and agroecosystems, while Anderson (2000) examined food web functioning and ecosystem processes, highlighting the challenges of scaling from small-scale interactions to ecosystem-level predictions.

## Implications for Ecosystem Processes

The collective body of research on fungal-faunal interactions demonstrates that:

1. **Selective grazing** by soil fauna can alter fungal community composition, which in turn affects decomposition rates and nutrient mineralization pathways
2. **Compensatory growth** of fungi in response to moderate grazing can stimulate fungal productivity and colonization of new resources
3. **Spore dispersal** by earthworms, ants, and other fauna is critical for maintaining mycorrhizal networks and fungal community connectivity
4. **Trophic cascades** originating from faunal grazing on fungi can propagate through soil food webs to affect plant nutrition and ecosystem productivity
5. **Scaling challenges** remain in extrapolating from controlled laboratory studies to field conditions, as the effects observed in contrived settings may not accurately represent natural interactions

## Key Methodological Approaches

The research in this area has employed diverse methodologies including controlled feeding preference assays, microcosm experiments, field observations of grazing behavior, spore viability testing after gut passage, and isotope tracing of nutrient flows. Each approach contributes complementary insights but also carries limitations that complicate integration into a unified understanding of fungal-faunal ecosystem roles.
