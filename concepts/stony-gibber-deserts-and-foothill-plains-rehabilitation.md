# Stony (Gibber) Deserts and Foothill Plains Rehabilitation

## Overview

Two major arid landform types — stony (gibber) deserts and lower foothill plains — present unique opportunities for rehabilitation through [[water-management]] and strategic earthworks. While both appear barren and unproductive, they harbour productive potential when appropriate techniques are applied. Gibber deserts can be transformed into tree-growing sites through windrow systems, while plains can be regenerated using circle-swales and shallow contour banks.

## Stony (Gibber) Deserts

### Formation

Vast areas of desert develop a stone layer (gibber pavement) over fairly well-structured soils. The stones were once part of a soil matrix but are revealed after centuries of wind erosion has removed finer material. Old stony deserts feature wind-faceted stones whose facets reveal the direction and intensity of local sandstorms and the saltation of sand grains.

### Stability Considerations

REG (gibber desert) is stabilised solely by its stone layer. Active dunes can also be stabilised by laying pebble beds over them. If the stone layer is disturbed, erosion recommences. This means:

- Windrowing stones for annual cropping will certainly increase erosion
- Windrowing in stages to plant trees at 10–30 m intervals is the sustainable approach
- Limited trials should precede broadscale windrow systems

### Windrow Systems

#### Creation

Wherever a road grader passes across gibber country, a windrow of stones results. Large sturdy side-rakes are now developed which will also windrow stones in fields or on gibber. These windrows present a major opportunity for rehabilitation.

#### Benefits of Stone Windrows

- **Water capture**: Water is impeded and largely absorbed below the stone piles
- **Dew formation**: Dew forms in the aerated heaps of stones
- **Nutrient accumulation**: Insects, reptiles, and birds leave manures and their remains in windrows
- **Seed beds**: The bare area between windrows provides greater runoff and a seed bed for smaller shrubs, legumes, and forbs
- **Tree establishment**: Windrows placed across slope every 10–20 m serve as excellent tree sites

#### Supplementary Techniques

- Ripping the pavement below or between windrows helps root penetration
- A small application of fertiliser greatly aids initial growth
- Swales can be made just upslope of windrows to maximise water capture

#### Natural Stone Windrows

Some natural stone windrows evolve below cliff shorelines on receding lakes. Below the stones, the soil is cool, moist, and well-structured due to insect occupancy. Many tree species thrive in these conditions.

#### Historical Precedent

It is thought that the Nabateans made windrows on steep slopes to increase runoff and lead water by hill-base channels to fields. However, good absorption also occurs in windrows over clay or clay-loam soils on gentle slopes, suggesting a broader applicability than runoff farming alone.

## Lower Foothills and Plains

### Understanding Subtle Slopes

Even flat land has some slope — not detectable by eye but revealed by rains and sheet flows of water. These extensive plains, often browsed to near extinction, can be regenerated or seeded by relatively simple earthworks.

### Circle-Swale Systems

#### Construction

Circle-swales are preferably made by tractor and tilt blade, or a road grader. Where remnant vegetation exists (saltbush, bluebush, *Acacia* species, or other shrubs), a circular ditch is driven around them, casting earth outward to create a "ditch and bank" landscape.

#### Design Specifications

- Circles can be 30–100 m across
- Smaller circles (20 m or less) may become partly over-shaded by trees
- Pelleted seed in ditches assists revegetation

#### Function

Seeds, leaf mulch, dry manure, and water all gather in the ditch. Where no mother plants exist, seed and fertiliser can first be broadcast to await wind and rain. A series of conjoined circles totally prevents runoff and traps seed and dust.

### Graded Contour Banks

On plains with falls of 10 m/km, a 30 cm contour wall will hold water up for several kilometres during heavy rain. This provides extensive subsurface soil moisture for vegetation establishment. Seed can be sown as soon as the ground is firm, or pelleted seed spread beforehand.

### Yeomans' Shallow Swales

A larger-scale approach uses 1–2 m banks thrown up by repeated side-casts, developed by P.A. Yeomans for semi-desert conditions. These back up water for miles, especially when sited to run from hill to hill through a pass between low ranges.

#### Features

- Water can be left to soak in, or spilled under slide-gate control into smaller walled fields
- Suitable for growing sunflower, millet, beans, or other short-term crops
- Trees and shrubs often establish well on the bunds
- Crops can be sown as seasonal lakes or pools dry up, following receding waters

#### Limitations

These systems suit fairly permeable soils but can develop salt flats in areas with clay soils and salty surface water. Careful assessment of soil type and water quality is essential before implementation.

## Integrated Biosocial Strategies

Effective rehabilitation of degraded drylands requires more than technical earthworks. Successful projects integrate several complementary strategies:

### Livestock Transition

- Replacing erosive grazing animals (goats, sheep) with non-erosive species (rabbits, pigs)
- Government revolving loans for purchasing alternative livestock
- Community cooperatives for collective marketing

### Cropping Transition

- Replacing export monocrops with diverse local food crops
- Integrating tree crops and vegetables
- Using pioneer legumes for soil improvement and erosion control

### Community Development

- Employment and education of local people in rehabilitation work
- Meaningful local work that relieves poverty while restoring land
- Integration of education with development activities

### Water Infrastructure

- Wells and galleries to tap groundwater
- Silt from erosion building productive terraces
- Copious spillways allowing flood flow over stabilised areas

## Design Principles for Gibber and Plain Rehabilitation

1. **Trial before scale**: Limited trials should always precede broadscale implementation
2. **Stage windrowing**: Trees are needed to prevent dust, so assess tree viability before committing to large windrow systems
3. **Work with existing vegetation**: Design around remnant plants rather than clearing them
4. **Address underlying causes**: Stocking rates, pest control, and land use practices must change alongside physical interventions
5. **Expect cascading benefits**: Water capture leads to soil development, which supports vegetation, which further improves water infiltration
