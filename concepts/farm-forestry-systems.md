# Farm Forestry Systems

Farm forestry integrates tree production into agricultural landscapes, combining ecological function with economic yield. As outlined in *[[agroforestry]] systems.
- **Fish breeding operations** — Supplying fingerlings for farm dams and aquaculture integrated with forestry.
- **Poultry species breeders** — Providing heritage and adapted poultry breeds for integrated free-range systems.
- **Bee fodder plant suppliers** — Distributing nectar and pollen-producing trees and shrubs to support apiary industries.
- **Hedgerow tree species suppliers** — Offering multi-purpose trees suited to windbreak, shelter, and boundary plantings.
- **Conservation and reforestation tree suppliers** — Providing native and adapted species for ecological restoration and long-term timber production.
- **Specialty nurseries** — Covering niche needs including medicinal herbs, salt-affected land species, smog-tolerant urban trees, culinary herbs, and plants producing natural food dyes.

## Essential Support Services

For farm forestry to scale beyond individual pioneer operations, a range of support services must develop alongside it. Mollison identifies three critical service categories:

1. **Combined plant nurseries** — Centralised facilities that aggregate stock from multiple specialists, making it easier for farmers to source diverse plant material from a single supplier.
2. **Processing and marketing systems** — Infrastructure for turning tree products (timber, fruit, nuts, oils) into marketable goods and connecting producers with buyers.
3. **Hire services** — Access to specialised planting, harvesting, and processing machinery without the capital burden of ownership, lowering the barrier to entry for small-scale growers.

## The Crop in Pasture System

The crop-in-pasture model is one of the most accessible farm forestry approaches, layering high-value trees into existing pastoral land without sacrificing grazing area. It is particularly well-suited to properties where farmers wish to diversify income without taking large areas out of agricultural production.

### Design Principles

- Trees are planted in widely spaced rows aligned to contour, preserving the majority of land for pasture production.
- Row spacing is generous enough to allow full sun penetration and easy machinery access between tree lines.
- High-value species are selected for timber, nut, or fodder production, ensuring each tree earns its place in the landscape.

### Integration Timeline

- **Years 1–3:** Trees establish and are vulnerable to stock damage. Grazing is deferred or carefully managed with electric fencing or tree guards.
- **Years 3–6:** As trees harden and develop sufficient height and bark, livestock are progressively introduced beneath the canopy.
- **Mature phase:** The system functions as a fully integrated silvopastoral enterprise, with animals cycling nutrients through fallen leaf litter and manure while harvesting pasture and any edible tree fodder.

### Suitable Forage Trees

Species such as poplar, fig, willow, chestnut, and oak provide edible foliage that supplements pasture, particularly during dry periods when ground-level feed is scarce. The deep root systems of these trees access subsoil moisture and minerals unavailable to shallow-rooted pasture species, effectively bringing nutrients up into the grazing cycle through leaf fall and animal consumption.

## Woodlot Systems

Woodlots are dedicated areas of intensive tree production managed on defined rotation cycles. They serve as the farm's primary source of timber and fuel products.

### Firewood Production

- Managed on short rotations of 2–7 years.
- Relies heavily on **coppice** systems where stumps regenerate new shoots after harvesting, eliminating replanting costs.
- Species are selected for rapid growth, high calorific value, and willingness to coppice repeatedly.

### Polewood Production

- Intermediate rotation lengths producing poles for fencing, construction, and industrial use.
- Products include fencing posts, building poles, woodchip for mulch, fibre for pulp, cellulose extraction, and essential oils.
- Often uses fast-growing species that produce straight, self-pruning stems.

### Long-Term Fine Timbers

- Rotation lengths of 40–100 years, producing premium hardwoods for furniture-making, decorative inlay, and architectural panelling.
- Species include black walnut, oak, rosewood, and redwoods — trees valued for density, grain character, and durability.
- Requires intergenerational planning but yields the highest per-unit economic returns.

## Hedgerow Forests

Hedgerow forests represent a polycultural approach to forestry that mimics natural woodland edge communities. Rather than planting a single species in rows, hedgerow systems incorporate 6–30 species in mixed plantings.

### Key Characteristics

- **Species diversity** creates resilience against pests, diseases, and climatic extremes that might devastate a monoculture.
- **Gather-and-select harvesting** — rather than clear-felling entire blocks, farmers selectively harvest individual stems as they mature, maintaining continuous canopy cover.
- **Multiple yield streams** from a single planting, including timber, fodder, fruit, nuts, medicines, and wildlife habitat.
- **Structural complexity** — The mixture of canopy heights, root depths, and growth rates means that no single pest or disease can easily propagate through the entire system.
- **Low establishment cost** — Hedgerows can be established along existing fence lines and property boundaries, requiring no additional land allocation.
- **Edge-optimised design** — Hedgerows maximise the productive edge between forest and open land, a core permaculture principle.

## Risks of Monoculture Forestry

Mollison strongly warns against monoculture tree plantations, identifying several systemic risks inherent in single-species forestry systems.

### Critical Risks

- **Glut conditions** — When many growers plant the same species for the same market, harvests coincide and oversupply crashes prices, destroying profitability.
- **Land ownership change** — Large monoculture plantations attract corporate buyers, leading to consolidation and displacement of family-scale farmers.
- **Catastrophic fire** — Uniform, single-species stands burn intensely and completely, with no species variation to slow fire spread or provide refugia for wildlife.
- **Displacement of bioregional needs** — Monocultures serve export or industrial markets while failing to produce the diverse range of products that local communities actually need — firewood, fencing, food, fodder, and medicines.
- **Ecological simplification** — Monocultures replace complex native ecosystems with biologically impoverished stands that support far fewer species and provide none of the ecosystem services of a diverse forest.
- **Economic fragility** — Dependency on a single species for income creates the same vulnerability that diversified farm forestry is designed to avoid.

## Design Implications for Permaculture

Farm forestry in a permaculture context means designing tree systems that are diverse, multi-layered, and responsive to local ecological and economic conditions. The emphasis is on polyculture over monoculture, selective harvest over clear-felling, and integration with existing farm enterprises rather than separation from them.

Effective [[farm-forestry-design]] begins with a thorough analysis of the site's climate, soils, water resources, and existing land use. From this foundation, the designer selects species and system types that match both the ecological capacity of the land and the economic needs of the farm family. Support services — nurseries, processing cooperatives, and shared machinery — form the enabling infrastructure that allows individual farmers to participate in forestry without bearing the full cost of specialised equipment and knowledge.

A well-designed farm forestry system produces fuel, timber, food, wildlife habitat, and soil stability simultaneously — turning trees from a commodity crop into the foundational infrastructure of a regenerative farm.
