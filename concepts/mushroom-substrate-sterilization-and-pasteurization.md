# Mushroom Substrate Sterilization and Pasteurization

Substrate sterilization and pasteurization are critical processes in mushroom-cultivation that eliminate or reduce competitor organisms from the growing
medium before inoculation. In the [[boil-a-bag-mushroom-cultivation-technique]]
described by D.H. Jarrold, sterilization is accomplished primarily through
pressure cooking, while the initial substrate preparation involves a hot water
soaking process that serves a pasteurizing function. Understanding the
distinction between these two approaches and their proper application is
essential for successful cultivation.

## Sterilization vs. Pasteurization

Sterilization is the complete elimination of all living organisms, including
bacteria, fungal spores, and viruses, from a material or surface. In mushroom
cultivation, true sterilization is typically achieved through pressure cooking at
15 psi (approximately 121 degrees C) for sustained periods. This level of
treatment is necessary when the substrate is highly nutritious, as any surviving
contaminant organisms would rapidly colonize the substrate and outcompete the
intended mushroom species.

Pasteurization, by contrast, involves heating the substrate to a temperature
sufficient to kill most harmful organisms (typically 60 to 80 degrees C) while
preserving some beneficial microorganisms. Pasteurization is commonly used for
bulk substrates that are less nutrient-dense, as the surviving beneficial
organisms can help protect against contamination by more aggressive competitors.
The hot water soaking of straw described by Jarrold functions as a form of
pasteurization, reducing the microbial load before the more intensive pressure
cooking sterilization step.

## Straw Substrate Preparation

The primary substrate in the boil-a-bag technique is chopped cereal straw, cut
to lengths of 3 to 10 cm. Pre-cut straw of appropriate size is available from
many pet stores. The straw serves as a carbon-rich base material that supports
mycelial growth while maintaining adequate porosity for gas exchange.

The straw is prepared by placing it in a watertight container and soaking it in
hot water enriched with a nutritional supplement. Jarrold recommends dissolving
approximately one heaped tablespoon of Marmite, Vegemite, or yeast extract in
boiling water, then adding approximately 6 litres of additional boiling water.
This nutrient-enriched water is poured over the straw, which is then weighed
down to ensure full submersion. The soaking process serves dual purposes: the
heat of the boiling water pasteurizes the straw by killing many potential
contaminants, while the yeast extract provides B vitamins and other nutrients
that accelerate spore germination and early mycelial growth.

## Soaking Duration and Drainage

The straw should soak for approximately one hour, after which the water level
should be checked to confirm it still covers the straw. Additional boiling water
may be added if necessary, and a second hour of soaking may be performed. After
soaking, the straw is turned out into a bathtub and left to drain thoroughly
for approximately one hour. Proper drainage is essential: substrate that is too
wet creates anaerobic conditions that favour bacterial contamination and inhibit
mycelial growth. If the straw remains excessively wet after draining, surplus
water can be removed by manually squeezing the straw.

## Nutritional Supplementation

After draining, brown rice flour is sprinkled evenly over the straw
(approximately 3 to 4 handfuls for enough straw to fill 6 bags) and thoroughly
mixed in by hand. The brown rice flour provides additional carbohydrates and
nutrients that support vigorous mycelial colonization. However, the addition of
nutrients increases the risk of contamination, which is why thorough
sterilization via pressure cooking is necessary after supplementation. Simpler
straw-only substrates may sometimes be pasteurized rather than fully sterilized,
but the addition of brown rice flour generally necessitates the more aggressive
sterilization approach.

## Pressure Cooker Sterilization

Once the substrate is prepared, packed into boil-a-bags, and fitted with filter
cans, the bags are sterilized in a pressure cooker at 15 psi for 60 minutes.
Jarrold suggests that it may be preferable to conduct the sterilization in two
30-minute stages rather than a single continuous 60-minute run, although the
reason for this recommendation is not explicitly stated. The number of bags
that can be sterilized simultaneously depends on the capacity of the pressure
cooker; the source describes processing two bags at a time.

Pressure cooking at 15 psi achieves a temperature of approximately 121 degrees
C, which is sufficient to kill virtually all microbial life, including resilient
bacterial endospores. This level of sterilization is essential when working
with supplemented substrates, as the added nutrients create an environment
highly susceptible to rapid contamination.

## Filter Can Gas Exchange

After sterilization and during the subsequent colonization phase, the filter
can allows gas exchange between the colonizing mycelium and the external
environment. Mycelium requires oxygen for growth and produces carbon dioxide as
a metabolic byproduct. The filter can, constructed from a punctured food can
filled with vermiculite or rock wool, permits the passage of gases while
excluding airborne spores and bacteria. This filtration is critical because
even though the substrate has been sterilized, contamination can enter through
the filter if it does not function properly.

## Equipment Sterilization

Beyond the substrate itself, equipment used in [[mushroom-spore-syringe-preparation]] must also be sterilized. Syringes, needles, and water for spore
suspension are wrapped in tin foil and pressure cooked at 15 psi for 20
minutes. Glass jars used for collecting spore prints are sterilized in an oven
at half maximum heat for 30 minutes. These parallel sterilization processes
ensure that contamination is not introduced during inoculation.

## Common Issues

Inadequate sterilization is one of the most common causes of failure in home
mushroom cultivation. Signs of insufficient sterilization include unexpected
colours (green, blue, black, or pink) appearing in the substrate, foul odours,
and stalled mycelial growth. Ensuring proper pressure levels, maintaining
adequate sterilization duration, and allowing equipment to cool before handling
are all important for reliable results. Overly wet substrate, even if properly
sterilized, can also lead to bacterial problems during colonization.
