## DIY Organic Fertilizer Recipes and Application Methods

## Overview

Related topics: [[diy-fertilizer-recipes-and-application-methods]] [[organic-fertilizer-recipes]] [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-and-application]]
Making your [[query-how-to-make-your-own-organic-fertilizer-at-home]] is a logical extension of understanding how plants eat. With only fourteen essential mineral elements needed, gardeners can create effective, customized fertilizer mixes from readily available organic ingredients. This guide covers all-purpose vegetable and annual recipes, starter solutions, lawn fertilizers, and the key methods for applying them effectively.

## Principles of DIY Fertilizer Mixing

All recipe ingredients are combined by **volume, not weight**. A single scoop or cup is used for all measurements — the actual size of the scoop doesn't matter, as long as consistency is maintained within each batch. A bucket for mixing and a large scoop for measuring are the only tools required.

Gardeners can develop different strategies:
- **Universal mixes** for most plants
- **Plant-specific mixes** for lawns, vegetables, annual flowers, trees, and shrubs
- **Seasonal mixes** for different times of year
- **Fast-acting starter formulas** for transplanting and new gardens, followed by slow-release mixes later in the season

All recipes should be adjusted based on soil test results. If the soil doesn't need more of a particular nutrient, that ingredient can be reduced or omitted.

## All-Purpose Vegetable and Annual Fertilizers

### Grandpa Al's Can't Fail Recipe

| Ingredient | Parts |
|---|---|
| Fish meal (or soy/cottonseed meal) | 4 |
| Kelp meal | 1 |
| Rock phosphate (or ¾ part bone meal) | 1 |
| [[dolomitic-limestone]] (or [[calcitic-limestone]]) | 1 |

**Application**: 1 to 2 gallons per 100 sq ft (3.8–7.6 liters per 9.3 m²) at the start of the season, banded into the root zone. Side-dress under mulch every 4 weeks.

**Nutrient roles**: Nitrogen from the meals, phosphorus from rock phosphate or bone meal, potassium from sulfate of potash and kelp (which also supplies micronutrients and cofactor minerals for enzymatic reactions). The limestone here is **not** to change soil pH — it counteracts the acidity created by the other ingredients. In very alkaline soils, reduce dolomitic limestone since excess magnesium ties up other nutrients at high pH.

Adding 0.5 inch (1.3 cm) of compost, or 6 inches (15 cm) of grass clippings when compost is unavailable, as a cover over these mixes increases microbial activity and overall efficiency.

### Steve Solomon's Recipe

| Ingredient | Parts |
|---|---|
| Cottonseed meal | 3 |
| Blood meal | 1 |
| Dolomitic or calcitic limestone | 1 |
| Bone meal | ½ |
| Kelp meal | ½ |

**Application**: 6 quarts per 100 sq ft (5.7 liters per 9.3 m²). Band into the root zone for quicker results (only if urgently needed — established natural systems won't require mixing in). Side-dress after removing mulch once every 3 to 4 weeks; reapply mulch after application.

**Nutrient roles**: Cottonseed meal provides steady nitrogen, blood meal supplies a more immediate nitrogen pool. Phosphorus comes from bone meal, potassium from kelp meal. As with Grandpa Al's recipe, limestone counters the acidity of other ingredients rather than adjusting soil pH.

## Quick Starter Solutions for Transplanting

A major criticism of natural fertilizers is their slow startup time. Liquid starter solutions bypass this limitation by providing immediately water-soluble nutrients.

### Quick Starter for Planting

| Ingredient | Parts |
|---|---|
| Fish emulsion or fish powder | 1½ (or ½ part bat guano) |
| Liquid kelp | 1 |

**Dilution and application**:
- 3 tablespoons bat guano per gallon (3.8 L) of water
- 1 teaspoon fish powder per gallon of water
- 2 teaspoons fish emulsion per gallon of water
- One gallon services approximately 4 to 5 sq ft (0.4–0.5 m²) of garden

Transplants can be soaked in a diluted solution of 1 part starter to 4 parts water before planting.

**Critical warnings**:
1. **Do not use in clay soils** — high soluble content will deflocculate (break apart) clay structure
2. **Precise dilution is essential** — excess soluble nitrogen can damage or kill plants
3. **Always test on sample seedlings first** before full application

## Lawn Fertilizers

Lawns are classic fertilizer sinks because conventional gardeners violate the Law of Return by removing grass clippings and raking leaves instead of mulching them in. Once clippings and leaves are returned, lawns need very little additional fertilizer — especially if clover establishes, as it hosts Rhizobia bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen.

### Wayne Lewis's Graceland Lawn Food

| Ingredient | Parts |
|---|---|
| Soybean meal (or chicken litter meal) | 1 |
| Granulated molasses | 1 |

**Application**: A 50-pound (23 kg) bag of each, mixed, fertilizes approximately 2,000 sq ft (186 m²) of lawn. Apply when weekly watering alone no longer keeps the lawn green. Unlike synthetic lawn fertilizers, over-application is virtually impossible and burning will not occur. Apply once in spring if starting out or uncertain. For heavier nitrogen needs (confirmed by soil test), add 1 part nitrate of soda or bone meal for quicker green-up — though this will increase mowing frequency.

**How it works**: Soybean meal provides nitrogen and fungal substrates; granulated molasses serves as both a microbial stimulant and fungal food. Together they support a robust soil food web that cycles nutrients efficiently. Leaving grass clippings amplifies the effect through the Law of Return.

## Fertilizer Application Methods

### Broadcasting

The most common technique — spreading granular or powdered fertilizer over the surface by hand or with a drop/broadcast spreader. Suitable for lawns and large gardens, but inefficient in many situations:
- Areas between rows and plants receive fertilizer (wasted, encourages weeds)
- Phosphorus placed on the surface remains largely unavailable to plants
- Not the most resource-efficient method for most garden situations

### Banding

Banding places fertilizer in a concentrated band within the root zone before planting, giving plants a quick start:
- Position the band approximately **2 inches (5 cm) away** from where plants or seeds will be placed
- Place the band approximately **2 inches (5 cm) deeper** than seed planting depth
- For transplants, place the band **3 to 4 inches (7.5–10 cm) below** where transplants will go

Banding is especially valuable for immobile nutrients like phosphorus and potassium. Studies demonstrate a **50% increase** in phosphorus and potassium uptake when fertilizer is banded versus broadcast.

**Spot banding** places a small amount of fertilizer in an individual hole before planting — excellent for autumn application of slow-release phosphates and potassium, and for starting individual plants.

### Side Dressing

Mid-season application alongside rows or individual plants:
- Place fertilizer under mulch or bury 1 to 2 inches (2.5–5 cm) into the soil
- Make a slit in mulch and soil with a knife or trowel along the row side
- Pour fertilizer into the slit
- Avoid frequent dilute liquid applications, which cause runoff and create unstable soil conditions

### Foliar Feeding

Spraying nutrients onto leaves has significant limitations:
- Effective only as a temporary fix for deficiencies of mobile nutrients like iron and zinc
- Macronutrients can enter leaves but cannot be supplied in sufficient quantities through foliar application alone
- **Should not be relied upon** for anything but the most mobile micronutrients

## Applying Fertilizer to Perennials, Trees, and Shrubs

Established perennials, trees, and shrubs need minimal fertilizer in a natural system where the Law of Return operates. Exceptions include:
- **Planting time**: Ensure adequate nutrients while permanent root systems establish
- **Heavy pruning or harvesting**: When significant biomass is removed, nutrients are exported and may need replacement
- **Fruit production**: When fruit is consumed rather than left to decompose and return nutrients

Application should be on or near the soil surface, as feeder roots for most trees are concentrated in the top few inches of soil.

## Inoculants: Mycorrhizal Fungi and Rhizobia

### Purchased Inoculants

Rhizobia inoculants for legumes are widely available, but plant-microbe specificity must be verified before purchase. Mycorrhizal fungi mixes are also commercially available. Fungal spores and propagules only germinate upon receiving root exudates, so direct root contact is important.

[[mycorrhizal-inoculation]] is most needed in:
- New gardens and disturbed systems
- Annuals and vegetables started indoors (away from natural soil biology)
- Plants grown in compost, which contains few mycorrhizal spores
