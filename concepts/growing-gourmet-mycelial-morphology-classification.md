# Mycelial Morphology Classification

Each mushroom species produces a recognizable type of mycelium whose variations fall within a characteristic range of expressions. Within a species, multitudes of strains differ dramatically in appearance. Observing mycelial morphology on nutrified agar media reveals a wealth of information about the strain's health, vigor, and position in the mushroom life cycle. These macroscopic features visible on the two-dimensional plane of a petri dish define each strain's "personality."

## The Seven Classic Mycelial Forms

### 1. Linear Mycelium

Linear mycelium is arranged as diverging, longitudinal strands. The mycelium typically emanates from the center of the petri dish as a homogeneously forming mat. Shiitake (Lentinula edodes) and initially Oyster (Pleurotus ostreatus) mycelia fall into this category. Morel mycelium produces a rapidly growing, finely linear form so delicate that during the first few days it is nearly invisible, detectable only by tilting the dish to catch the reflective sheen of the agar surface.

### 2. Rhizomorphic Mycelium

Often called "ropey," rhizomorphic mycelium is composed of braided, twisted strands of varying diameters with a reflective, silky quality. This is the most desirable growth form for many species because rhizomorphic mycelium directly supports primordia formation. Its presence is encouraged by selectively transferring these zones for further subculture. The disappearance of rhizomorphs is a clear indication of loss of vigor.

Species producing classically rhizomorphic mycelia include Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus), King Stropharia (Stropharia rugoso-annulata), Button Mushrooms (Agaricus brunnescens, A. bitorquis), Psilocybe cubensis and P. cyanescens, and the Clustered Woodlovers (Hypholoma capnoides, H. sublateritium).

### 3. Cottony (Tomentose) Mycelium

Looking like tufts of cotton, this mycelium is nearly aerial in its growth. It is common with Oyster Mushrooms (Pleurotus species), Shaggy Manes (Coprinus comatus), and Hen-of-the-Woods (Grifola frondosa). When a rhizomorphic mycelium degenerates with age, tomentose formations typically take over — a warning sign of declining vigor. Cottony mycelium is the most easily recognized form by novice cultivators but is generally considered less desirable than rhizomorphic growth for fruiting potential.

### 4. Zonate Mycelium

Cottony mycelium often shows concentric circles of dense and light growth called zones. Zonations reflect natural changes in mycelial age, with the newest peripheral growth being lighter and the older central growth becoming strongly pigmented. Zonations can also follow circadian cycles, even under constant laboratory temperatures, with growth occurring in spurts that create visible rings. This feature is commonly seen in Ganoderma, Hypholoma, and Hypsizygus species.

### 5. Matted (Appressed) Mycelium

Dense, compressed mycelium typical of Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum) after two weeks on 2% malt extract agar. So dense that a factory-sharpened surgical blade cannot cut through it — the mycelium tears off in ragged sheaths. Many wood-decomposing species develop matted mycelia over time. Cultures that mysteriously die often show matted mycelium whose surface has become flat and highly reflective, indicating inviability.

### 6. Powdered Mycelium

Best exemplified by Chicken of the Woods (Laetiporus sulphureus). The mycelium breaks apart with minimal disturbance. In front of a laminar flow hood, the sterile wind can cause chains of hyphae to become airborne, creating serious cross-contamination risks within the laboratory.

### 7. Unique Formations

Several distinctive structures arise on the mycelial mat that can be distinguished from the background mycelium:

- **Hyphal aggregates:** Ball-like or shelf-like structures viewed as favorable when selecting rapidly fruiting strains of Shiitake. These often evolve into primordia, the youngest visible stages of mushroom formation.
- **Sclerotia:** Dense, spherical formations produced by Morel mycelium (Morchella angusticeps). Can be brightly colored and abundant, or dull and sparse (M. esculenta, M. crassipes).
- **Coremia:** Asexual, broom-like bundles of spores produced by some Oyster species (Pleurotus cystidiosus, P. abalonus, P. smithii) that resemble black mold contaminants. Several mycologists have mistakenly discarded valuable cultures thinking them contaminated.
- **Plate-like zones:** Uniquely flattened zones of dense and light growth seen in King Stropharia, upon which hyphal aggregates often form.

## Mycelial Color Progression

Most mushroom species produce mycelia that undergo mesmerizing transformations in pigmentation as they age. Learning the natural progression of colorations for each species is critical, since certain colors forebode contamination.

### White

The most common color, shared by Oyster, Shiitake, Hen-of-the-Woods, King Stropharia, and most Psilocybe species. Some imperfect fungi like Monilia also produce whitish mycelium, requiring careful microscopic examination.

### Yellow, Orange, and Pink

Nameko (Pholiota nameko) mycelium yellows over time. Oyster mushrooms exude yellowish to orangish metabolites. Pink Oyster (Pleurotus djamor) and Lion's Mane develop strong pinkish tones with age. Chicken-of-the-Woods has an overall orangish mycelium. Kuritake (Hypholoma sublateritium) transitions from white to dingy yellow-brown.

### Brown

Shiitake mycelium predictably browns with age — it would be abnormal for it not to. Agrocybe aegerita and Morel mycelium also brown after approximately one week of growth.

### Blue

Lepista nuda (Wood Blewit) produces a blue, cottony mycelium. Most psilocybian mushrooms bruise bluish when damaged. Blue tones are otherwise highly unusual and warrant microscopic examination to rule out Penicillium molds. One Oyster strain, P. ostreatus var. columbinus, produces whitish mycelium streaked with blue.

### Black

Few mushrooms produce black mycelium. Some Morel strains cause the malt extract medium to blacken. The parasitic Honey Mushroom (Armillaria mellea) forms uniquely black rhizomorphs. P. cystidiosus and relatives develop white mycelia that become speckled with black droplets (coremia).

### Multicolored

Zonate mycelia with multicolored concentric circles typically reflect diurnal growth patterns. The extraordinary Psilocybe mexicana produces mycelium that transitions through white, yellow, golden, brown, and sometimes bluish tones.

## Strain Degeneration Pattern

Healthy strains characteristically begin with rhizomorphic mycelium. Over months of repeated transfers, the culture sectors, forming diverging "fans" of linear, cottony, and appressed mycelium. The central mycelium, being genetically unstable, sends forth mutation-ridden chains of cells. Volunteer primordia formation on agar declines. Growth rate decelerates. The strain becomes prone to parasitic bacteria and eventually dies.

## Practical Significance

The extraordinary Psilocybe mexicana produces mycelium that transitions through white, yellow, golden, brown, and sometimes bluish tones — among the most beautiful mycelial displays known.

## Diagnostic Value of Morphology

Understanding mycelial morphology serves several critical functions for cultivators:

1. **Health assessment:** Daily observation of cultures reveals whether a strain is thriving or declining. Changes in growth form, rate, or coloration serve as early warning indicators.
2. **Strain selection:** When isolating strains from spore germinations or tissue clones, cultivators select for the most vigorous, typically rhizomorphic, sectors. Hyphal aggregates and the ability to form volunteer primordia on agar are positive indicators of fruiting potential.
3. **Contamination detection:** Recognizing the natural color progression of each species helps distinguish normal pigmentation from the green, blue-green, black, or pink colorations that signal mold contamination.
4. **Degeneration monitoring:** The progression from rhizomorphic to cottony to sectoring growth is a well-documented pathway of genetic decline. Cultivators who recognize these early signs can return to stock culture libraries before the working culture becomes useless.
5. **Species verification:** Each species has a characteristic mycelial "signature" — a combination of form, color progression, growth rate, and unique structures. Experienced cultivators can often identify species on agar before any fruiting occurs.

## The Stamets P-Value and Morphology

The Stamets P-Value system tracks generational expansion of mycelium through successive petri dish transfers. As the P-value increases (P1, P2, P3...), morphological changes typically accompany genetic drift. Strains maintained within P1-P5 show the most characteristic species-specific morphology. Beyond P10, morphology often becomes unreliable as a species identifier, and the strain may show clear signs of degeneration. This is one reason why maintaining a robust stock culture library with low P-value backups is essential for any serious cultivation operation.

## End-Substrate Influence on Morphology

The agar formula used can significantly influence mycelial appearance. Strains grown on one medium adapt to it and may lose their innate ability to digest more complex substrates. The addition of extracted end-substrates (sawdust, straw, or other fruiting substrate) to agar media at 1-3 g per liter helps maintain the full enzymatic repertoire of the strain. Mycelia grown with end-substrate supplementation typically show more robust, species-typical morphology and are better prepared for the transition to bulk substrate.
