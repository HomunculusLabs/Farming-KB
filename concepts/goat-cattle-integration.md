# Permaculture Goat and Cattle Integration

Permaculture goat and cattle integration is the design of grazing animal systems within permaculture landscapes to provide milk, meat, fibre, and manure while performing land management functions including weed control, pasture renovation, nutrient cycling, and fire prevention. Bill Mollison addresses goat and cattle management in various sections of Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, including the animal integration chapter, the humid tropics chapter, and the cool climates chapter. The integration of large herbivores into permaculture systems requires careful management to prevent overgrazing while maximising their beneficial functions.

## Functions of Grazing Animals

Grazing animals perform multiple functions in permaculture systems beyond meat and milk production. They convert pasture and browse that humans cannot digest into high-quality protein. Their manure distributes fertility across the landscape. Their grazing stimulates root growth in grasses and promotes pasture diversity. Their trampling incorporates litter into the soil surface. Selective grazing can control specific weed species.

Goats are particularly effective browsers, consuming woody plants, shrubs, and weeds that cattle avoid. This makes goats valuable for reclaiming overgrown or neglected land. Cattle are efficient grazers of grasses and legumes, converting pasture biomass into meat and milk. Both species can be integrated with cropping, forestry, and water management systems.

## Goat Systems in Permaculture

Goats are among the most versatile domestic animals for permaculture systems. They are browsers by preference, consuming leaves, twigs, and woody vegetation that cattle and sheep will not eat. This browsing behaviour makes goats effective for weed control, especially of woody weeds such as blackberry, gorse, Lantana, and thorny scrub.

In the tropics, goats browse on a wide range of tree legumes including Leucaena, Gliricidia, Sesbania, and various Acacia species. These high-protein browse species provide excellent nutrition for milk and meat production. Goats can be integrated with tree crop systems, browsing on the lower branches of fodder trees while providing manure for the trees.

Mollison notes that milk goats and chickens can be let out onto paths to eat greens when pens are bare, integrating animal foraging with garden and orchard systems. Goat manure is a valuable fertiliser for gardens and tree crops, though it should be composted before application to avoid burning plants with its high nitrogen content.

## Cattle Systems in Permaculture

Cattle are the primary large herbivore in many permaculture systems, particularly on larger properties and in extensive pastoral landscapes. When managed correctly, cattle can improve pasture quality, cycle nutrients, and generate income from meat, milk, and dairy products.

The large-scale pig tractor system described by Mollison demonstrates the integration of pigs and cattle. Pigs are used to pioneer high-quality milk-cow pasture of chicory, dandelion, comfrey, dock, grasses, and clover. Cows follow along 2-3 months behind the pig tractor. Excess milk product such as whey and skim milk is fed to the pigs as accessory food, creating a synergistic cycle.

Piglets ranging over such pasture rarely show iron anaemia deficiency. Parasite cycles are broken by the rotation, and the soil constantly improves in humus. Such large animals as pigs and cows need fenced tree strips, tree guards, and border hedgerows to supply tree forage crop.

## Rotational Grazing

Rotational grazing is the foundation of sustainable cattle and goat management in permaculture. Animals are moved through a series of paddocks on a regular schedule, allowing each paddock adequate rest and recovery between grazing events. The rotation period depends on the season, pasture growth rate, and stocking density.

Key principles of rotational grazing include grazing each paddock only once per rotation cycle, allowing adequate rest for pasture recovery (typically 30-90 days depending on season), adjusting stocking density to achieve uniform grazing without overgrazing, providing access to water and shade in every paddock, and monitoring pasture condition and adjusting management accordingly.

High-density, short-duration grazing maximises the beneficial effects of grazing while minimising damage. When animals are concentrated in a paddock for a short period, they graze more uniformly, trample more litter into the soil, and deposit manure more evenly than when left in a paddock for extended periods.

## Fodder Systems

Integrated fodder systems combine tree fodder, pasture, and crop residues to provide year-round nutrition for goats and cattle. Tree fodder is particularly important in tropical and subtropical systems where dry seasons reduce pasture availability. Fodder trees including Leucaena, Gliricidia, Calliandra, Sesbania, and various Acacia species provide protein-rich browse throughout the year.

Fodder trees can be integrated as hedgerows along fence lines, as alley cropping within pastures, or as dedicated woodlots. Coppicing management produces regular harvests of fresh fodder from the same trees. In cool climates, willow, poplar, and tagasaste provide fodder during periods when pasture growth is limited.

Crop residues including straw, corn stover, and legume hay provide dry-season feed. These can be supplemented with fodder trees, conserved hay, and silage. Mollison emphasises the importance of tree forage and tall grass hand-fed to domestic stock as a strategy for sustainable animal production in the tropics.

## Manure Management

Animal manure is a valuable resource in permaculture systems, providing nutrients for crops and organic matter for soil building. The challenge is to capture and distribute manure effectively across the landscape rather than allowing it to concentrate in holding areas where it becomes a pollution source.

In rotational grazing systems, manure is distributed naturally across paddocks as animals move. In confined systems, manure must be collected, composted, and applied to fields or gardens. Composting manure before application kills pathogens, reduces odour, and stabilises nutrients.

Manure can be integrated with biogas systems, where animal manure is digested to produce methane gas for energy. The digester effluent is a more readily available fertiliser than raw manure. This integration captures the energy value of manure while retaining its fertiliser value.

## Fencing and Infrastructure

Effective fencing is essential for managed grazing. Electric fencing is the most cost-effective option for rotational grazing, allowing flexible paddock layout and easy movement of fences. Permanent fencing of durable materials defines property boundaries and excludes livestock from sensitive areas such as waterways and forest reserves.

Water supply in each paddock is necessary for rotational grazing to function. Gravity-fed water systems using tanks and piping from elevated sources are the most energy-efficient. Solar-powered pumps can lift water from dams or bores. Nose pumps, operated by the animals themselves, provide water without external energy.

Shade and shelter should be available in every paddock. Trees provide natural shade in warm weather and windbreak in cold weather. Purpose-built shelters protect animals from extreme weather events. In permaculture design, shade trees serve multiple functions including fodder production, timber, and wildlife habitat.

## Integration with Other Systems

Goats and cattle integrate with many other permaculture systems. In agroforestry systems, animals graze between tree rows, controlling weeds and fertilising trees. In orchard systems, poultry and small ruminants clean up fallen fruit and control pests. In rice paddies, ducks and water buffalo control weeds and pests while fertilising the crop.

In cool climate systems, Mollison describes the integration of livestock with hay storage, field shelter, and root crop production for winter fodder. Barns and outbuildings must be solid, well-designed, and carefully assessed for thermal efficiency. Pastures and remnant prairie and meadow are uniquely suited to these regions.
