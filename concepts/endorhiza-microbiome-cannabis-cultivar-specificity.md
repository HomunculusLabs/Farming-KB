# Endorhiza Microbiome and Cannabis Cultivar Specificity

## Overview

The **endorhiza** — the community of microorganisms inhabiting the interior of plant roots — represents the most intimate plant-microbe interface in the soil ecosystem. Unlike rhizosphere communities (which occupy the soil surrounding roots) or bulk soil communities (which exist independently of plant influence), endorhiza communities live within root tissues themselves, in direct contact with plant cells and the compounds they produce. The 2014 study by Winston et al. provided the first comprehensive characterization of the [[cannabis-endorhiza-microbiome]] across five cultivars, revealing that this compartment shows the strongest host-specific differentiation of any root-associated microbial habitat.

## Defining the Endorhiza

The endorhiza encompasses all microorganisms found within root tissue, including those in the apoplast (cell wall spaces), intercellular spaces, and occasionally within cells themselves. These organisms are properly called **endophytes** when they colonize plant tissues without causing disease. Endorhiza bacteria enter roots through natural openings (root tip cracks, lateral root emergence sites), wounds, or via active penetration mechanisms, then establish populations in the root cortex and stele.

The term "endorhiza" was coined to distinguish internal root colonizers from rhizosphere inhabitants (bacteria in the soil immediately surrounding the root) and rhizoplane inhabitants (bacteria attached to the root surface). While these three compartments form a continuum, they differ markedly in their community composition, diversity, and functional characteristics. The endorhiza typically has lower alpha-diversity than the rhizosphere — fewer species, but often higher abundances of specific taxa — reflecting the stronger selective pressures of the root interior environment.

Endophytic bacteria support plant health through multiple mechanisms: production of phytohormones (auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins) that stimulate root growth; secretion of siderophores that chelate iron and make it bioavailable; enzymatic activity that solubilizes phosphate and other minerals; synthesis of antimicrobial compounds that suppress pathogens; and induction of [[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]] responses that prime the plant's immune system for broader defense.

The distinction between the endorhiza and the rhizosphere is not merely spatial but functional. Rhizosphere bacteria are influenced by root exudates but remain outside the plant, while endorhiza inhabitants experience the full biochemical environment of the root interior — including plant immune responses, intracellular signaling molecules, and metabolic intermediates. This closer association means endorhiza bacteria have more direct access to plant resources but also face more stringent selection pressures from the plant's innate immune system, which must distinguish beneficial colonizers from potential invaders.

## Cannabis Cultivars Studied

The research examined five cannabis cultivars across two experiments:

**Experiment 1** (Vista, California, November 2011):
- **Sour Diesel** — Cannabis sativa, high THC:CBD ratio
- **Bookoo Kush** — sativa-dominant hybrid, moderately high THC:CBD ratio
- **Burmese** — balanced sativa/indica hybrid, moderate THC:CBD ratio

**Experiment 2** (Vista and Orange County, California, August 2012):
- **White Widow** — balanced sativa/indica hybrid, moderate THC:CBD ratio
- **Maui Wowie** — Cannabis sativa, high THC:CBD ratio

This selection provided diversity across the sativa-indica spectrum and different chemotype profiles, enabling assessment of whether cannabinoid chemistry correlates with endorhiza community structure.

## Key Finding: Cultivar Effects Only in the Endorhiza

The most striking result was that cultivar-specific differences in [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]] were **exclusively detected in the endorhiza**. When samples were divided by strain, no significant clustering was observed for bulk soil (ADONIS R² = 0.11, p = 0.25) or rhizosphere samples. However, endorhiza communities showed highly significant cultivar differentiation (weighted UniFrac: R² = 0.59, p = 0.004; [[weighted-vs-unweighted-unifrac-cannabis-strain-microbiome]]: R² = 0.39, p = 0.003).

This finding is consistent with the two-tier selection model: soil properties determine which bacteria are available in the source pool (Tier 1), while the plant's internal biochemistry determines which subset successfully colonizes root tissues (Tier 2). The endorhiza is the compartment where Tier 2 selection is most strongly expressed because it is the most intimate with the host plant.

The R² values are particularly noteworthy: cultivar explained 59% of the variation in weighted endorhiza community structure. This means that knowing which cultivar a root sample came from allows you to predict the majority of its microbial community composition — a remarkably strong effect in microbial ecology, where most factors explain far less variance. For context, soil pH — often cited as the strongest predictor of soil microbial community structure — typically explains 10-20% of variation in field studies.

## The Methylophilus Anomaly

The most dramatic cultivar-specific signature was the enrichment of **Methylophilus** in the Bookoo Kush endorhiza. This methylotrophic bacterium constituted 13% of the Bookoo Kush endorhiza community but only 0.13% in Burmese and was entirely absent from Sour Diesel — a 100-fold difference between cultivars.

Methylophilus species are specialized C1 compound utilizers, capable of growing on methanol, methylamine, and formaldehyde as sole carbon sources. Their dramatic enrichment suggests that Bookoo Kush roots may produce elevated levels of methanol — a byproduct of pectin demethylation during cell wall remodeling — or other C1 compounds that selectively feed this population. Pectin methylesterase activity, which releases methanol, varies between plant species and genotypes, providing a plausible mechanism for cultivar-specific Methylophilus enrichment.

## Shared Core Community

Despite cultivar-specific differences, all five cannabis cultivars shared a conserved core endorhiza community dominated by well-known endophytic taxa within the Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria classes:

- **Pseudomonas** — among the most studied plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), producing siderophores, antibiotics, and phytohormones
- **Oxalobacteraceae** — including the genus Herbaspirillum, known nitrogen-fixing endophytes
- **Xanthomonadaceae** — a taxonomically diverse family with both pathogenic and beneficial members
- **Actinomycetales** — filamentous bacteria that produce the majority of naturally-derived antibiotics
- **Sphingobacteriales** — consistent root associates whose functional role remains under-characterized
- **Cellvibrio** — an aerobic cellulolytic bacterium, the only non-endophytic member of the core community

The consistent presence of this core across all cultivars suggests these taxa perform [[plant-micronutrients-and-their-essential-functions]] for root colonization and persistence. Their predominance within Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria aligns with observations from many other plant systems, suggesting certain bacterial lineages have evolved generalized root endophytism strategies that transcend individual host species and even plant families.

## Edaphic Factors Across Experiments

The two soil types used in Experiment 2 showed substantial physicochemical differences. The Mo-Bio soil (Vista) had moderate organic carbon (3.02%), total nitrogen (0.26%), pH 6.63, and low salinity (5.12 dS/m). The Orange County soil had dramatically higher organic carbon (20.0%), higher nitrogen (0.53%), similar pH (6.77), but much lower salinity (1.73 dS/m). Water content also differed substantially (0.113 vs 0.371).

Despite these large edaphic differences, the cultivar effect on endorhiza communities remained significant, confirming that host genotype can override soil chemistry in determining the root interior microbiome. This is a crucial finding for cannabis cultivation: it suggests that even in suboptimal soils, the plant's genetic capacity to select beneficial endophytes may partially compensate for edaphic limitations.

## Sample Processing and DNA Extraction

A critical methodological detail was the modification of the standard PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit protocol: samples were heated at 65°C for 10 minutes prior to the initial vortex step. This modification likely improved lysis of tough-walled bacterial cells and fungal spores, potentially capturing a more complete picture of the endorhiza community than standard protocols.

All root samples were surface-sterilized with alcohol and sterile water before DNA extraction, ensuring that sequences recovered represented true endophytes rather than rhizoplane contaminants adhering to root surfaces. This distinction is crucial because rhizoplane communities may differ substantially from true endorhiza communities, and failure to adequately surface-sterilize can conflate the two compartments.

The study used both closed and open reference OTU-picking methods for the two experiments, with sequences classified against the Greengenes database pre-clustered at 97% identity. Phylogenetic trees were built using FastTree, and taxonomy assigned with the RDP classifier. The comprehensive bioinformatic pipeline, combined with rarefaction to even sequencing depth, ensured that observed community differences reflected biological reality rather than technical artifacts.

## Implications for Cannabis Cultivation

The cultivar-specificity of the cannabis endorhiza has several practical implications for cultivation:

1. **Strain-specific probiotics**: Microbial inoculant products may need to be tailored to specific cultivars rather than marketed as generic cannabis biostimulants. A Pseudomonas strain that effectively colonizes Sour Diesel may fail to establish in White Widow.

2. **Consistent sourcing**: Growers who observe differences in plant vigor between runs may be partially observing microbiome effects. If mother plants harbor distinct endorhiza communities, clones taken from different mothers will carry different microbial legacies even when grown in identical media.

3. **Living soil approaches**: The finding that endorhiza communities are soil-derived (supporting the two-tier model) validates the principle that healthy, biodiverse soils provide the raw material from which plants select beneficial endophytes. Building soil biology through composting, mulching, and minimal disturbance should support more robust endorhiza communities.

4. **Chemotype-microbiome interactions**: The question of whether endorhiza communities influence cannabinoid and terpene production remains open. If specific endophytes modulate plant secondary metabolism, then microbiome management could become a tool for optimizing chemical profiles.

5. **Clone consistency**: Since cannabis is predominantly propagated clonally, the endorhiza community is effectively transmitted from mother to clone. This vertical transmission means that once a desirable microbiome is established, it can be maintained across generations through careful clone management. Conversely, a suboptimal microbiome established in a stressed mother plant may be perpetuated through subsequent cloning cycles, potentially contributing to the gradual decline in vigor that some growers observe over many generations of cloning from the same genetic line.

## Cannabinoid Data Considerations

Cannabinoid profiles were measured for buds from a subset of plants (three White Widow, one Maui Wowie) by Delta-9 Technologies. While the sample size was too small for robust correlation analysis between cannabinoid content and endorhiza community structure, this preliminary data opens an important avenue for future research. The interaction between endophytes and plant secondary metabolism is well-documented in other systems — endophytes of medicinal plants can modulate alkaloid production, and root-associated bacteria have been shown to influence terpene synthesis in aromatic herbs.

A particularly interesting hypothesis is that the endorhiza community might influence the relative proportions of THC, CBD, and their precursor cannabinoids. The biosynthetic pathway for cannabinoids involves olivetolic acid and geranyl pyrophosphate as precursors, and the availability of these compounds could theoretically be modulated by microbial activity. Furthermore, some soil bacteria produce enzymes that can degrade or transform plant secondary metabolites, potentially altering the final cannabinoid profile of harvested material.

## Comparison with Other Plant Systems

The degree of cultivar-specificity observed in cannabis is consistent with findings from other crop species. In barley, different cultivars support distinct root endophyte communities when grown in the same soil, and similar patterns have been reported in maize, wheat, and tomato. However, cannabis presents a unique case because of its extraordinarily diverse secondary metabolite profile — the plant produces over 500 known compounds including more than 100 cannabinoids and approximately 200 terpenes.

This chemical richness may drive particularly strong Tier 2 selection by creating a highly specialized root interior environment. Just as the human gut microbiome is shaped by our diet, the cannabis endorhiza may be shaped by the plant's own chemical output, with different cultivars — producing different ratios and profiles of secondary metabolites — selecting for different microbial assemblages. This creates a positive feedback loop: the plant's genetics determine its chemical profile, which shapes its microbiome, which in turn may influence the plant's growth, health, and chemical production. Breaking this feedback loop — for example, by growing a cultivar in sterile media — could reveal the relative contributions of host genetics versus microbial community to plant phenotype, though such experiments are technically challenging and may not reflect real-world growing conditions.

## Sampling Timing Effects

The two experiments sampled at different developmental stages relative to harvest. Experiment 1 collected samples eight weeks after bud harvest, while Experiment 2 sampled two weeks before harvest. Despite this difference, both experiments detected significant cultivar effects in the endorhiza, suggesting that cultivar-specificity is a robust feature that persists across growth stages. However, the absolute composition of endorhiza communities likely shifts with phenology, as root exudate profiles change during vegetative growth, flowering, and senescence.

Future studies employing longitudinal sampling across the full cannabis life cycle — from seedling through vegetative growth, flowering, and harvest — would provide valuable insight into how endorhiza communities develop and whether critical windows exist for microbial establishment that could be targeted by inoculation strategies. The practical benefit of such knowledge would be clear: if specific developmental windows exist when the endorhiza is most receptive to colonization, growers could time their probiotic applications to maximize establishment of beneficial organisms, much like pediatricians recommend specific windows for administering vaccines to maximize immune response.
