# Design by Analysis

Design by analysis is the method of listing the characteristics of components
to determine their placement and connections within a total design. The
components of a total design may range from simple technological elements to
more complex economic and legal systems.

## The Purpose of Functional Design

The purpose of a functional and self-regulating design is to place elements
or components in such a way that each serves the needs, and accepts the
products, of other elements. This is the core strategy of relative placement:
putting components where they can serve each other.

## Analysing Component Characteristics

For any component (e.g., a chicken), we list three categories:

1. **Products:** Materials, behaviours, and derived products the component
   yields.

2. **Needs:** What the component requires to lead a full life (inputs that
   must be supplied).

3. **Intrinsic Characteristics:** Breed or species characteristics including
   colour, form, weight, climate tolerances, and how these affect behaviour,
   space needed, and metabolism.

## Outputs, Inputs, and Their Consequences

A broader classification has only two categories: "outputs" and "inputs."

**OUTPUTS, YIELDS, or PRODUCTS** are RESOURCES if they are used
productively, or can become POLLUTANTS if not used in a constructive way by
some other part of the system.

**INPUTS, NEEDS, or DEMANDS** have to be supplied, and if not supplied by
other parts of the system, then EXTERNAL ENERGY or EXTRA WORK must be found
to satisfy these demands.

## Key Definitions

- **A POLLUTANT** is an output of any system component that is not being used
  productively by any other component of the system.

- **EXTRA WORK** is the result of an input not automatically provided by
  another component of the system.

Both pollution and extra work are unnecessary results of an incompletely
designed or unnatural system.

## The Chicken Example

Primary needs of a chicken: food, warmth, shelter, water, grit, calcium,
dust baths, and other chickens. Secondary needs: a tolerable social and
physical environment giving a healthy life of moderate stress.

Primary products: eggs, feathers, feather dust, manure, various exhaled or
excreted gases, sound, and heat. Derived products include foods from eggs,
dusters and insulation from feathers, compost heat from manure, methane from
anaerobic composting, and so on. The list is limited only by lack of
information and local needs.

## Making Connections Between Components

To enable a design component to function, we must put it in the right place
and arrange connections. The essentials are:

- That the inputs needed by the component are supplied by other components
  in the system
- That the outputs of the component are used by other components

For living components, right placement may be enough (ducks in a swamp may
take care of themselves). For non-living components, we must also arrange
connections (a solar collector linked by pipes to hot water storage).

## Placement and Linking Strategies

Having listed all information on a component, we proceed to placement and
linking strategies posed as questions:

- Of what use are the products of this component to the needs of other
  components?
- What needs of this component are supplied by other components?
- Where is this component incompatible with other components?
- Where does this component benefit other parts of the system?
- What connections need to be made between this and other components?
- Where is the best placement for this component?

## Self-Regulation as the Goal

Once components are placed and connected, we can relax and let the system
self-regulate. Regulation may involve confining or insulating the component,
or guiding it by fencing, hedging, or one-way valves. The goal is to
minimise ongoing work while maximising system function.

## Elements of a Total Design

A total design includes:

- **Social components:** Legal aids, people, culture, trade and finance
- **Energy components:** Technologies, structures, sources, connections
- **Landscape components:** Soil, water, vegetation, animals, earthworks

All must be analysed for inputs, outputs, and intrinsics, then placed in
relation to each other for mutual benefit.

## The Ground Strategy of Relative Placement

The overall strategy is one of relative placement: positioning every
component where it best serves and is best served by other components. This
eliminates pollution (unused outputs) and extra work (unsupplied inputs) from
the system, creating a self-regulating whole.
