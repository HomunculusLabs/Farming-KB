# Extending and Storing Yields in Permaculture

## Overview

The concentration of yields into one short period is a fiscal strategy, not an environmental or subsistence one. It produces a "feast and famine" regime in both markets and fields, with consequent high storage costs, price volatility, and food insecurity. [[permaculture-design]] aims to disperse food yield over time so that many products are available in every season, and to employ storage strategies that preserve abundance for lean periods without excessive energy input.

## Strategies for Temporal Yield Extension

### Variety Selection for Extended Harvest

- **Early, mid, and late season varieties:** Staggering maturity dates spreads production across months rather than concentrating it in a single harvest window. This approach requires planning at planting time but dramatically reduces the feast-or-famine pattern that characterizes monoculture harvests.
- **Microclimate manipulation:** Planting the same variety in early-ripening (warm, sheltered, north-facing) and late-ripening (cool, exposed, south-facing) situations extends the harvest window without requiring different genetics. Thermal mass walls, frost pockets, and elevation differences within a single property all create exploitable microclimate variation.
- **Long-season varieties:** Selecting cultivars that yield over extended periods rather than providing a single concentrated harvest. Cherry tomatoes, indeterminate climbing beans, and perennial greens are examples of plants that produce continuously rather than all at once.

### Diversity as a Temporal Strategy

Increasing overall system diversity ensures continuous production because different plant parts and species become available at different times throughout the year:

- **Leaf, fruit, seed, and root** are all product yields, each maturing on its own schedule. A diverse system might provide salad greens in spring, berries in summer, fruits in autumn, and root crops in winter — all from the same designed landscape.
- **Self-storing species** such as tubers, hard seeds, fuelwood, and rhizomes can be cropped on demand, serving as living storage systems that require no processing infrastructure and don't degrade while in the ground. Jerusalem artichokes, yacon, and groundnuts can be harvested whenever needed throughout winter.
- **Perennial systems** inherently produce across seasons, unlike annual crops that must be replanted each year. [[perennial-vegetables]], tree fruits, nut trees, and berry bushes provide reliable annual harvests without the seasonal gap that annual cultivation creates.

### Preservation Techniques

Traditional and modern methods extend the availability of seasonal abundance:

- **Drying:** Dehydrating fruits, herbs, and meats for shelf-stable storage with minimal energy input. Solar dehydrators use passive energy; traditional air drying exploits warm, dry conditions. Dried foods retain nutritional value for months or years.
- **Pitting:** Traditional underground storage using earth as insulation and temperature moderator — a low-energy method used worldwide for root vegetables and grains. Earth temperatures remain stable year-round, providing natural refrigeration without technology.
- **Cool storage:** Root cellars and cool rooms extending freshness without refrigeration energy. The passive cooling of underground or earth-sheltered structures preserves fruits and vegetables for weeks or months beyond their natural shelf life.
- **Preserving:** Fermentation, pickling, and other biological preservation methods that extend usability while often enhancing nutritional value. Fermented foods also provide probiotic benefits and represent some of the oldest and most reliable storage methods.

### Geographic Diversification

- **Regional trade** between communities at different latitudes or altitudes enables access to products from different growing seasons without long-distance transport from other continents
- **Altitude-based systems:** Utilizing land at different elevations within a region to access different temperature regimes and growing seasons. A single community with access to valley floor, hillside, and mountain properties can extend fresh production considerably.

## Yield Storage Principles

How yields endure is as important as how they are produced. Different resource types require different storage strategies, and some are inherently more storable than others.

### Food Storage

Food can be stored for days, weeks, or years depending on the method and the food type. The strategies of food storage are critical to food security — very little famine would occur if grains, fish, and fruit available in good times could be stored for lean times. Mollison suggests that people should "mulch their recipe books" — replacing recipes specifying out-of-season or imported foods with guides to low-energy food preservation and seasonal eating from the garden. This cultural shift is as important as the technical storage methods themselves.

### Water Storage

Water not open to evaporation and pollution, or containing natural cleansing organisms, will keep indefinitely. Properly designed storage systems — sealed tanks, covered reservoirs, protected aquifers, and living systems that filter and purify — preserve water quality without degradation over time. Water is among the most storable resources when properly protected from contamination and evaporation.

### Shelter and Materials

Shelters may outlast the forests that build them. Living materials (ivy walls, thatch from managed wetlands), durable materials (concrete, stone), or combinations of both create structures that store the energy invested in their construction for decades or centuries. The key is designing for durability from the start rather than accepting planned obsolescence.

### Energy Storage

Energy is the most difficult resource to store effectively. Batteries leak or decay over time, heat escapes through insulation, and all physical storage systems degrade. Only living things, like forests, increase their energy store over time rather than depleting it. This fundamental difference is why biological systems are preferred over technological systems for long-term energy capture and storage.

## Seasonal and Diurnal Cycles

Because of seasonal and daily cycles, close attention to storage strategies is essential for any sustainable system. The disconnect between abundance and scarcity is not fundamentally a production problem but a storage and distribution problem. Designing for storage capacity is as important as designing for production capacity — perhaps more so, since stored surplus provides resilience against drought, pest outbreaks, and other disruptions.

## Cultural Impediments to Yield

Mollison identifies cultural attitudes as the primary barrier to realizing potential yields, arguing that people themselves limit what they can produce through prejudice and convention.

### Zoophobia in Affluent Societies

Mollison describes an "almost total zoophobia" among affluent suburbanites — a fear of anything that moves. This cultural condition, most developed in affluent North American society, manifests in preferences for blue toilet dyes, air fresheners, excessive paper tissues, and hyper-sanitary practices that distance people from productive biological systems. The result is a population unable to benefit from the productive potential of integrated animal systems.

### Urban Animal Prohibitions

In the United States, it is nearly taboo to use rabbits, quail, pigs, poultry, or cows in city farms or urban gardens. Yet these are commonplace city animals in England, ordinary village animals in Asia, and the edible guinea-pig lives comfortably in South American homes. Australians feel no repulsion toward productive urban animals. Removing useful animals from American cities leaves ecological niches open for less desirable species: pigeons foraging streets, thousands of gulls defecating in New York City reservoirs (fresh from garbage piles), garbage-raiding dogs in Los Angeles, and rats tunneling under bus stops in Washington D.C. by the millions — not far from the White House.

### The Yield Limitation Effect

Just as yield can theoretically be increased without limit through creative design, it can be decreased by cultural restriction. When people refuse certain products as acceptable yields — snails but not lettuce, blackbirds but not strawberries, only certain approved types of toilets or plants in gardens — they become the main impediment to their own productive potential. Political, commercial, and religious restrictions on what counts as acceptable food or resource all narrow the productive base unnecessarily.

## Implications for Design

The permaculture designer must work on two fronts simultaneously:

1. **Technical design:** Creating systems that extend yields temporally and store them effectively through variety selection, preservation, diversity, and storage infrastructure
2. **Cultural design:** Removing barriers to resource use, expanding choices, making unusual resources acceptable, and fostering attitudes that recognize the value inherent in diverse biological systems

Cultural and administrative strategies for overcoming yield impediments include: removing socio-legal impediments to resource use, creating effective structures for resource management, costing all energy inputs and outputs honestly, cooperative endeavors and resource pooling, financial recycling within communities, and making harmonious connections between system components.
