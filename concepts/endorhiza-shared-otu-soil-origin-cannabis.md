# Endorhiza Shared OTUs and Soil Origin in Cannabis Microbiome Assembly

A critical test of the two-step selection model for root microbiome assembly
was performed by Winston et al. (2014) using a reciprocal soil experiment
with the [[cannabis-cultivar-effects-soil-microbiome]] White Widow. The experiment demonstrated that
endophytic microbes within the [[actinobacteria-enrichment-cannabis-endorhiza]] are primarily inherited
from the surrounding soil, confirming that the first step of root colonization
is fundamentally soil-driven rather than genotype-driven.

## Experimental Design: Reciprocal Soil Transplant

To rigorously test whether [[cannabis-rhizosphere-endorhiza-communities]] derive their membership from
the local soil environment, Winston et al. (2014) grew the White Widow
cultivar in two distinct soil types. By comparing the OTU composition of the
endorhiza communities with the OTU pools available in each soil, the
researchers could quantify the degree to which endophytes were recruited from
their native soil versus the alternative soil they did not experience.

This reciprocal design is a powerful approach because it controls for the
confounding influence of plant genotype — since White Widow was the cultivar
used in both soils, any differences in endorhiza composition can be attributed
to soil type rather than plant genetics. The experiment thus cleanly isolates
the first step of the two-step model (soil-driven recruitment) from the second
step (genotype-driven abundance tuning).

## Results: Shared OTU Analysis

The results were unambiguous. Endorhiza communities from White Widow plants
grown in soil A shared significantly more OTUs with soil A (mean = 2,934
shared OTUs) than with soil B (mean = 2,162 shared OTUs). Conversely,
endorhiza communities from plants grown in soil B shared more OTUs with soil B
than with soil A. The statistical significance of this difference was
extremely high (p = 1.2 x 10^-15), leaving no doubt that endorhiza
communities are preferentially composed of organisms originating from their
local soil environment.

The magnitude of the difference is noteworthy. Plants shared approximately
772 more OTUs on average with their own soil than with the foreign soil,
representing a substantial fraction of the total [[proteobacteria-dominance-cannabis-endorhiza]]. This
indicates that the soil acts as the primary reservoir from which the
endophytic community is drawn, with the majority of detected endorhiza OTUs
having detectable counterparts in the surrounding soil.

## Implications for the Two-Step Selection Model

The two-step selection model, as articulated by Winston et al. (2014),
proposes that root microbiome assembly proceeds through two sequential
filters. The first filter is imposed by the soil environment, which determines
which microbial taxa are available for colonization. The second filter is
imposed by the plant genotype, which modulates the relative abundance of the
available taxa through mechanisms such as exudate chemistry, root
architecture, and immune responses.

The shared OTU experiment provides strong support for the first step of this
model. The fact that endorhiza communities share far more OTUs with their
own soil than with a foreign soil confirms that soil is the primary source of
endophytic organisms. The plant does not recruit a unique set of endophytes
from outside the soil community; rather, it draws selectively from the pool
that the soil provides.

The second step of the model — genotype-driven abundance tuning — is not
directly tested by this particular experiment but is supported by the
complementary finding that [[cannabis-microbiome-otu-abundance-vs-presence-cannabis-strains]] show 71 significant weighted but
zero unweighted OTU differences. Together, these two results provide a
coherent picture: soil determines who is present, and the plant determines
who dominates.

## Soil as the Primary Reservoir of Endophytic Diversity

The shared OTU finding reinforces a principle that has emerged consistently
from plant [[cannabis-microbiome-research]] across diverse species: the soil is the
primary reservoir of root-associated microbial diversity. This has been
demonstrated in Arabidopsis, maize, rice, barley, and now Cannabis. In each
case, the composition of the root microbiome reflects the composition of the
surrounding soil, filtered through plant-specific selection pressures.

For Cannabis specifically, this finding has practical implications. It means
that efforts to manipulate the endorhiza community must begin with the soil.
Inoculating plants directly with desired endophytes may have limited success
if those organisms cannot establish populations in the soil first. Soil
amendment, composting, and pre-planting inoculation strategies are therefore
likely to be more effective than direct root or foliar application of
microbial products.

The finding also suggests that Cannabis cultivars grown in the same soil
will tend to share a broadly similar endorhiza composition, differing mainly
in the relative abundance of shared taxa rather than in the identity of those
taxa. This is consistent with the zero unweighted OTU differences observed
between cultivars in the broader study.

## Limitations and Considerations

While the shared OTU analysis provides compelling evidence for soil-driven
recruitment, several limitations should be noted. The study used DNA-based
sequencing, which detects both live and dead organisms. It is therefore
possible that some of the shared OTUs represent relic DNA from soil organisms
that entered root tissues but did not establish viable populations. RNA-based
or viability-based approaches would be needed to confirm that the shared
organisms are metabolically active endophytes.

Additionally, the study examined bulk soil rather than rhizosphere soil as
the reference community. The rhizosphere — the narrow zone of soil directly
influenced by root exudates — is the immediate source of endophytes and may
have a composition that differs from bulk soil due to root-driven selection.
Despite this, the strong signal observed even with bulk soil comparison
underscores the robustness of the soil-origin effect.

The use of only a single cultivar (White Widow) in the reciprocal soil
experiment is another limitation. While this design effectively isolates the
soil effect, it does not reveal whether different cultivars vary in their
propensity to recruit from soil versus from other sources such as seed
microbiomes or atmospheric deposition. These questions remain open for
future investigation.

## Broader Context in Cannabis Microbiome Research

The shared OTU experiment represents one of the most methodologically
rigorous components of the Winston et al. (2014) study. By using a single
cultivar across two soils, the researchers cleanly separated the soil effect
from the genotype effect, something that is difficult to achieve in
observational studies where soil and cultivar are often confounded in
agricultural settings. The resulting p-value of 1.2 x 10^-15 reflects the
very large effect size of soil on endorhiza community membership.

This finding also helps contextualize the observed differences between the
two experiments in the study. The first experiment (post-harvest samples)
and the second experiment (actively growing plants) used different growing
conditions and potentially different soil sources, which would contribute to
differences in the available OTU pool and thus in endorhiza composition.
