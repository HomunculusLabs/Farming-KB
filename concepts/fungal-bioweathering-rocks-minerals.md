## Fungal Bioweathering of Rocks and Minerals

## Overview

**Bioweathering** is the erosion, decay, and decomposition of rocks and minerals mediated by living organisms. Fungi are among the most important biological weathering agents, capable of dissolving minerals and mobilizing metals at higher pH values and over a wider redox range than bacteria. The study of fungal roles in geological processes is called **geomycology** (Gadd, 2006). Fungi have been components of terrestrial microbial communities since the Ordovician period (480–460 MYBP), and their filamentous growth habit, high surface-area-to-mass ratio, and ability to translocate nutrients make them integral to biogeochemical change.

## Why Fungi Are Effective Weathering Agents

Fungi possess several attributes that make them exceptionally well suited as geomicrobial agents:

- **Environmental resilience**: Highly resistant to metal toxicity, UV radiation, and desiccation.
- **Metabolic versatility**: Capable of adopting diverse growth strategies and exuding protons, organic acids, and metal-complexing metabolites.
- **Symbiotic capacity**: Form mutualistic associations with plants (mycorrhizas), algae, and cyanobacteria (lichens).
- **Filamentous growth**: Can increase or decrease surface area, adopt exploration or exploitation strategies, and form linear organs (cords, rhizomorphs) for protected translocation.
- **Nutrient translocation**: Internal redistribution of resources enables exploitation of [[fungal-foraging-strategies-heterogeneous-environments]].
- **Polymorphism**: Some fungi occur as both filamentous mycelium and unicellular yeasts, adapting to different conditions.

## Rock-Dwelling Fungal Communities

### Classification by Habitat

Rock- and stone-dwelling microorganisms are classified by their relationship to the mineral substrate:

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Epilithic** | On the surface of rocks and building stone |
| **Hypolithic** | Associated with the underside of pebbles |
| **Endolithic** | Within the rock sub-surface, forming distinct masses or coloured layers |
| **Chasmolithic** | Growing in pre-existing cracks and fissures, often visible from surface |
| **Cryptoendolithic** | Inside cavities and among crystal grains, not visible from surface |
| **Euendolithic** | Specialized cryptoendoliths capable of actively boring into rock |

### Microcolonial Fungi

Epiliths are often represented by **microcolonial black-coloured fungi** (MCF):

- Occur on rock surfaces as spherical clusters of tightly packed cells with thick pigmented walls.
- Alternatively appear as moniliform thick-walled hyphae.
- Common inhabitants of limestone, sandstone, marble, and granite in hot and cold deserts and semi-arid regions.
- Associated with pitting and etching of rock surfaces.

### Rock Types Colonized

Fungi have been found in virtually all rock types: limestone, soapstone, marble, granite, sandstone, andesite, basalt, gneiss, dolerite, amphibolite, and quartz. They are believed to be **ubiquitous** components of the microflora of all rocks and building stone across all geographical [[humid-tropics-introduction-and-climatic-zones]].

### Survival Strategies

Rock-inhabiting fungi must cope with extreme microclimatic variability (light, salinity, pH, water potential):

- **Melanin pigments** and **mycosporines** in cell walls provide UV protection.
- **Mucilaginous polysaccharide slime** embeds colonies and may entrap clay particles for extra protection.
- **Lichen symbiosis** with algae/cyanobacteria provides carbon and surface protection from light — one of the most successful means of surviving extreme sub-aerial environments.

## Nutrient Acquisition on Rock Surfaces

Many oligotrophic fungi can scavenge nutrients from air and rainwater, enabling growth on seemingly barren rock surfaces. Additional nutrient sources include:

- Organic and inorganic residues on mineral surfaces
- Material within cracks and fissures
- Waste products of other microorganisms
- Decaying plants and insects
- Dust particles, aerosols, and animal faeces

## The Geomycological Perspective

Fungal bioweathering operates alongside physical (mechanical) and chemical weathering in a synergistic manner. Near-surface weathering often involves interaction between all three types. The primary fungal impact on biogeochemical cycling results from:

1. **Acidolysis**: Excretion of protons and organic acids that dissolve minerals.
2. **Complexolysis**: Production of metal-chelating metabolites (carboxylic acids, amino acids, siderophores, phenolics) that mobilize metals.

These processes mobilize both essential nutrients (P, K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn) and potentially toxic metals (Cd, Pb, Hg, Al) from rock substrates into bioavailable forms.

## Significance

The ability of fungi to weather rocks has profound implications for:

- **Soil formation**: Initiation of pedogenesis from bare rock.
- **Nutrient cycling**: Release of elements locked in mineral matrices.
- **Carbon cycling**: Microbial [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-and-carbonate-precipitation]] coupled with silicate weathering as a CO₂ sink.
- **Bioremediation**: Mobilization of metals from [[mycoremediation-mushroom-cultivation-contaminated-substrates]].
- **Biodeterioration**: Degradation of building materials, concrete, and cultural heritage stone.
