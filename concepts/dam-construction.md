# Dam Construction in Permaculture

Dam construction in permaculture is the design and building of water storage structures for irrigation, stock water, domestic supply, aquaculture, fire protection, and landscape rehydration. Dams (also called ponds or impoundments) are central elements of permaculture water strategy, especially in arid and seasonally dry climates. Bill Mollison covers [[mollison-farm-dam-design-modifications]] extensively in his water chapter and dryland strategies chapter, emphasising that water storage is the foundation of productive and resilient landscapes.

## Purpose and Functions

Dams serve multiple functions in a permaculture system. They store water for irrigation during dry periods, provide drinking water for livestock, create habitat for aquaculture and wildlife, moderate microclimates by providing thermal mass and humidity, serve as fire-fighting water reserves, enable gravity-fed water distribution to lower parts of the property, create recreational and aesthetic landscape features, and recharge groundwater through controlled infiltration.

In arid landscapes, Mollison states that water is the dominant theme for designers, and the quantity of fresh water is the final arbiter of successful settlement and sustainable agriculture. Dams are the primary means of ensuring reliable water supply across seasons and years of variable rainfall.

## Site Selection

[[mollison-designers-small-dam-site-selection-design]] selection is the most critical step in dam construction. The ideal site has a naturally confined valley or depression with a narrow outlet that can be closed with a wall. The catchment area above the dam should be large enough to fill the dam from reliable rainfall, but not so large that flood flows exceed the spillway capacity.

Key site [[cannabis-breeding-traits-selection-criteria-clarke]] include a suitable foundation of impervious clay or rock for the dam wall, adequate catchment area to fill the dam, sufficient depth without excessive excavation, no toxic geological materials (acid sulfate soils, saline strata) in the catchment or dam bed, proximity to the point of use to minimise pumping or piping costs, and suitable topography that allows the dam wall to be built economically.

The relationship between catchment area, rainfall, evaporation, and dam volume must be calculated. A rule of thumb is that the catchment area should be at least 10-20 times the surface area of the dam for reliable filling in most climates. In very dry areas, larger catchment-to-dam ratios are needed.

## Dam Types

Earth dams are the most common type in permaculture, consisting of a compacted earth wall built across a valley or depression. The wall is constructed from excavated material or from borrow pits nearby. A clay core within the wall provides the waterproof barrier, while the outer shells of sandy or rocky material provide structural stability.

Rock-fill dams use a mass of rock with a clay or concrete facing for waterproofing. These are appropriate where rock is abundant and clay is scarce. Rock-fill dams can be built on foundations that would not support the weight of an earth dam.

Concrete dams are rarely used in permaculture due to their high cost and embodied energy. Small concrete structures may be used for spillways, outlet pipes, or as facing for earth dams in critical locations. Gabion dams, made from wire cages filled with rock, are useful for small check dams in watercourses.

## Dam Wall Design

The dam wall must be designed to withstand the hydraulic pressure of the stored water, seepage through and under the wall, flood flows over the spillway, earthquake loads, and the long-term effects of saturation and weathering. Key design elements include the crest width, the upstream and downstream slopes, the clay core or waterproof membrane, the cutoff trench, and the spillway.

Crest width depends on dam height and intended use. For small permaculture dams, a crest width of 2-3 metres is adequate for maintenance access. Larger dams may require wider crests for vehicle access. The upstream slope is typically 1:2.5 to 1:3 (horizontal to vertical), and the downstream slope is typically 1:2 to 1:2.5.

## Clay Core and Sealing

The clay core is the critical waterproof element of an earth dam. It should extend from the foundation cutoff trench up through the wall to above the full supply level. The core material must be well-compacted clay with low permeability. The cutoff trench extends into the foundation to prevent seepage under the wall.

Where natural clay is unavailable, alternative sealing methods include bentonite clay blankets applied to the dam bed and upstream face, synthetic geomembrane liners, compacted soil-cement mixtures, and gleying, a technique of sealing ponds with organic material that creates an anaerobic, impermeable layer.

Gleying involves clearing the dam bed, applying a thick layer of organic material such as manure, grass, or leaves, covering with soil or plastic sheet, and flooding. The anaerobic decomposition of the organic material creates a black, gelatinous layer that is effectively impermeable. This traditional technique has been used for centuries in Europe and Asia.

## Spillway Design

The spillway is the most critical safety feature of any dam. It must be designed to pass the maximum probable flood flow without overtopping the dam wall. Overtopping is the primary cause of dam failure and can result in catastrophic downstream flooding.

Spillways are typically constructed as a level channel cut into solid ground on one side of the dam wall. The spillway level determines the maximum water level in the dam. The spillway width must be sufficient to pass the design flood flow at a depth that does not exceed the freeboard (the height from spillway level to the top of the wall).

Drop structures or energy dissipaters at the spillway outlet prevent erosion of the downstream channel. These may include concrete aprons, rock-lined plunge pools, or cascading step structures. The spillway must be protected from erosion by the high-velocity water flow during flood events.

## Construction Process

Dam construction begins with site survey and design, followed by clearing the dam bed and foundation area. The cutoff trench is excavated along the centreline of the wall, extending into impervious material below the foundation. The foundation is prepared by removing topsoil, organic material, and any permeable or unstable material.

The clay core is built up in layers, with each layer compacted at the optimal moisture content. Compaction can be achieved by sheepsfoot rollers, vibrating plate compactors, or in small dams, by hand tamping or livestock trampling. The sandy or rocky outer shells are built up simultaneously with the core.

The spillway is excavated to the design level and lined with grass, rock, or concrete as required. The outlet pipe, if fitted, is installed during wall construction with an anti-seep collar to prevent water travelling along the pipe outside the wall. The dam is fenced to exclude livestock from the dam wall and spillway.

## Sizing and Volume

Dam sizing depends on the intended use, catchment area, and climate. For domestic water supply, a minimum of 20,000-50,000 litres per household is recommended, depending on rainfall reliability. For irrigation, the dam volume should provide sufficient water for the longest anticipated dry period. For aquaculture, ponds of 100 square metres to 5 hectares are appropriate depending on the scale of operation.

The ratio of dam depth to surface area affects evaporation losses. Deeper dams with smaller surface areas lose less water to evaporation than shallow dams of the same volume. In hot, dry climates, maximising depth and minimising surface area is important for water conservation.

## Aquaculture Integration

Dams designed for aquaculture require additional features. Variable depth zones provide different habitats for different species. Shallow edges support marginal plants and juvenile fish. Medium depth zones support water plants and forage species. Deep zones provide refuge during hot weather and overwintering habitat.

Pond walls for aquaculture need an economical clay seal, with a tamped clay cutoff core where clay is in short supply. A build-up of organic mulch and algae in ponds assists sealing, so that even ponds in sandy loams may gradually evolve to reduce water losses. ## Safety and Legal Considerations

Dam construction may be subject to local regulations and permitting requirements. Many jurisdictions require engineering design and supervision for dams above certain sizes or heights. Dam failure can cause loss of life and property downstream, so the consequences of failure must be considered in design.

Regular inspection and maintenance are essential for dam safety. Key maintenance tasks include keeping the spillway clear of debris and vegetation, monitoring for seepage or piping through or under the wall, repairing erosion on the downstream face, maintaining fencing around the dam, and checking outlet structures for function.
