# Morels (Morchella) Species and Cultivation Attempts

## Overview

The morels (genus *Morchella*), commonly known as sponge mushrooms in parts of the United States, are perhaps the most highly prized of all native wild mushroom species, and in Europe they rank next to the truffles in culinary esteem. Despite their high quality, wide distribution, and more than a century of cultivation attempts, morels have never been successfully grown commercially — remaining one of the most elusive targets in mycological cultivation.

## Description and Identification

Morels are best described as **stalked sponges**. The structure consists of:

- A **stem** usually one to three inches high, generally pale to buff in color
- An **ovoidal or conical cap** somewhat greater in diameter than the stalk
- A cap presenting an **irregular honeycombed structure** — the characteristic "sponge" appearance
- Cap color sometimes buff but more frequently darker: brownish, olivaceous, or smoky

The honeycombed cap is the most distinctive feature — even should related genera (Gyromitra, Helvella) be mistaken for morels, no harm would be done since all are edible when cooked.

## Distribution and Occurrence

Morels are widely distributed throughout temperate climates, at least in the Northern Hemisphere. They are found more frequently in:

- **Open woods** — the most productive habitat
- **Meadows and orchards**
- Dense woods and pastures — less commonly

They appear in the **early spring**, especially after warm rains. With favorable conditions the whole period of their occurrence in any locality is often limited to an interval of just **two weeks**. If the season is dry until summer weather prevails, they occur very sparingly and are of poor quality.

## Species

No careful study of United States species had been made at the time of Duggar's writing, but Boudier's *Icones Mycologicae* figured nineteen species and three varieties for France, of which at least half are large enough and sufficiently common to be of economic interest. Common larger species (or forms resembling them) include:

- **Morchella vulgaris** — More common in the eastern United States
- **M. crassipes** — Very abundant in the Central West
- **M. rotunda**
- **M. conica**
- Several smaller forms

## Cultivation Attempts: A Persistent Failure

### Pure Culture Success

Despite the failure to produce fruiting bodies, pure cultures of morel [[agaricus-campestris]]*
- **Spores germinate readily** in almost any nutrient medium — unlike truffle spores
- Vigorous mycelium grows on sterilized plant products of various kinds, especially **bean stems and pods** or pieces of **apple**
- Mycelium has been grown in large quantity on **sterile loam**
- The spawn is brownish in appearance and in loam cultures soon yields **sclerotial-like masses** thought by some to be an important preliminary to fruit production

### Fruiting Experiments — All Unsuccessful

Experiments with various substrates and conditions have uniformly failed to produce sporophores:

- **Compost of various kinds** — no success
- **Leaf mold** — no success
- **Products variously treated with fertilizers** — no success
- **Modified acidity and alkalinity** — no success
- In France, a few small specimens have been reported on fermented refuse apple pulp from cider mills

### The Parasitism Hypothesis

At one time the view prevailed that the morel mycelium is parasitic, or at least closely associated with the roots of higher plants (such as oak and sunflower), limiting distribution by the availability of host plants. This view has not been justified for any species. When morels are found in the spring, the mycelium can be observed extending through decaying leaves and into the soil, like that of most mushrooms of the woods.

### Fruiting Biology

The fruit bodies undergo much of their development **hidden beneath the leaves and other forest cover**, rapidly expanding into full size under favorable conditions. This hidden development phase may be key to understanding the conditions needed for sporophore formation — conditions that have not yet been replicated in cultivation.

## Related Genera

### Gyromitra

Generally larger than the morels and much less common. The gyromitras share the general habitat and spring fruiting season. Like morels, all species are edible when properly cooked.

### Helvella

Smaller than morels and not usually found in such great quantity in small areas. The larger species share the same woodland habitat. All are edible when cooked.

## Culinary Precautions

Morels should **not be used raw or partly cooked** — the same caution applies to all morel allies. Proper cooking is essential. Any surplus collected may be preserved by drying, although some of the characteristic aroma is lost.

## Market Significance

Next to wild *Agaricus campestris*, the morel is more often sold on American markets than any other wild species. The high quality and limited seasonal availability make them particularly valued. It is to be hoped that the conditions for sporophore formation will eventually be determined so that one or more of the larger species may be grown commercially, though this would require considerable equipment and is not a matter for amateur experimentation.
