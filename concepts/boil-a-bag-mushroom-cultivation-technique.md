# Boil-a-Bag Mushroom Cultivation Technique

The boil-a-bag technique is a method of mushroom-cultivation developed by
D.H. Jarrold for growing mushrooms indoors using heat-resistant plastic bags as
the primary growing container. The approach is designed to be accessible to
home cultivators, requiring relatively inexpensive equipment and readily
available materials. The technique combines elements of [[mushroom-spore-syringe-preparation]], and
controlled fruiting in a humidity chamber.

## Overview

At its core, the boil-a-bag method involves preparing a substrate of
chopped straw supplemented with brown rice flour, packing it into
pressure-resistant plastic bags, sterilizing the bags in a pressure cooker,
inoculating with a spore-syringe, and allowing mycelium to colonize the
substrate before inducing fruiting in a humidity-controlled environment. The
entire cycle from inoculation to harvest can be completed in a relatively short
timeframe, making it a popular choice for small-scale indoor cultivation.

The technique is named for the specialized "boil-a-bags" used throughout the
process. These are plastic bags manufactured to withstand the temperatures
inside a pressure cooker or microwave without melting. Bags produced by
companies such as Lakeland Plastics (sized approximately 20cm by 30cm) are
commonly used, typically sold in packs of 50 at low cost.

## Equipment Required

The boil-a-bag method requires a specific set of tools and materials:

- **Boil-a-bags**: Heat-resistant plastic bags that can withstand pressure
  cooking temperatures (approximately 15 psi / 121 degrees C).
- **Pressure cooker**: Used for sterilizing both substrate bags and spore
  syringe equipment at 15 psi for 60 minutes (or two 30-minute stages).
- **Filter cans**: Homemade filtration devices constructed from empty food cans
  (3 to 4 inches in diameter) fitted with rock wool and vermiculite to
  allow gas exchange while preventing contamination.
- **Straw**: Chopped to lengths of 3 to 10 cm, serving as the primary
  substrate base. Pre-cut straw is available from many pet stores.
- **Brown rice flour**: Sprinkled over the straw as an additional nutritional
  supplement (approximately 3 to 4 handfuls for every 6 bags).
- **Wide masking tape**: Used to secure the filter cans to the bags and to
  cover inoculation points.
- **Support structures**: Cardboard boxes or containers to hold bags upright
  during colonization and fruiting.

Additional tools include scissors, tweezers, a flat-edged metal scraper, cotton
wool buds, propyl alcohol for surface sterilization, and a spirit burner for
flame sterilization of instruments.

## Preparing the Straw Substrate

Straw preparation is a critical early step in the boil-a-bag process. The straw
is chopped to lengths of 3 to 10 cm and placed in a watertight container. A
nutritional supplement is prepared by dissolving approximately one heaped
tablespoon of Marmite, Vegemite, or yeast extract in boiling water, which is
then diluted with an additional 6 litres of boiling water. This enriched water
is poured over the straw and the mixture is left to soak for approximately one
hour, with the straw weighed down to ensure full submersion. After soaking, the
straw is spread out and allowed to drain thoroughly for another hour. Excess
moisture is squeezed out by hand if necessary, and brown rice flour is sprinkled
evenly over the straw and mixed in thoroughly.

## Filter Can Construction

Each bag requires a filter can to permit gas exchange during colonization while
excluding airborne contaminants. Filter cans are made from cleaned 3 to 4 inch
round food cans. The bottom is punctured with numerous holes using a hammer and
nail to create breath holes. The inside bottom is lined with approximately 1 cm
of rock wool or fiberglass loft insulation. The can is then filled with
vermiculite to within 1 cm of the top, or alternatively packed with small
pieces of rock wool. A final layer of rock wool is placed on top to hold
everything in place. The can's original lid is left partially attached (by
about an inch) during opening, allowing it to serve as a closeable cover.

## Filling and Sealing the Bags

The prepared straw mixture is firmly packed into the boil-a-bags until nearly
full, leaving enough space at the top to accommodate the filter can. The filter
can is then inserted into the neck of the bag and secured by wrapping wide
masking tape around the neck three times, pressing it firmly onto both the can
and the bag to create an airtight seal around the filter assembly.

## Sterilization

Filled bags are sterilized in a pressure cooker at 15 psi for 60 minutes. Jarrold
notes that it may be preferable to conduct the sterilization in two 30-minute
stages rather than a single continuous run. The number of bags that can be
sterilized simultaneously depends on the size of the pressure cooker. After
sterilization, bags must be allowed to cool completely before inoculation.

## Colonization and Fruiting

After inoculation, bags are placed in a cardboard box in a warm location for
spore germination and mycelium growth. Within a few days, visible mycelium
colonization should appear. Once the mycelium has fully colonized the substrate,
the filter cans are removed and the bags are transferred to a humidity chamber
for fruiting. The humidity chamber described in the source is a 4-foot by 2-foot
fish tank with a layer of soaked hydro pellets on the bottom, covered with
plastic sheeting to retain humidity and exclude insects. Fresh air is fanned
into the chamber twice daily. When mushroom pins begin to form, the bags are cut
open to expose the substrate. Mushrooms are harvested when mature.

## Advantages and Limitations

The boil-a-bag technique offers several advantages for home cultivators: low
cost of materials, relatively simple equipment requirements, and the ability to
process multiple bags simultaneously. The use of filter cans provides effective
contamination protection during colonization. However, the technique requires
careful attention to sterile procedure, particularly during inoculation and
spore handling. The method is best suited to species that colonize straw-based
substrates readily.
