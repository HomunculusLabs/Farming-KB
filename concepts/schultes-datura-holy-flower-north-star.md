## Datura Holy Flower of the North Star

The genus *Datura* encompasses some of the most powerful and dangerous
hallucinogenic plants known to humanity. Members of the Nightshade family
(Solanaceae), Datura species contain tropane alkaloids, primarily
scopolamine, hyosyamine, and atropine, which produce intoxication often
so violent that physical restraint is necessary before the onset of a deep
stupor during which visions are experienced. Used ceremonially in both the
Old and New Worlds, Datura occupies a paradoxical position among plants of
the gods: revered for its visionary power yet feared for its malevolence.

## Datura innoxia: Toloache of Mexico

The most extensive ceremonial use of Datura centers in Mexico and the
American Southwest, where the most important psychoactive species is
*[[datura-metel]]*. Native probably to
the mountainous regions of Pakistan or Afghanistan westward, *D. metel*
is a spreading herb, sometimes becoming shrubby, three to six feet tall.
The triangular-ovate, sinuate, and deeply toothed leaves measure five and
three-quarters to eight and three-quarters inches long. The solitary
flowers, which may be purple, yellowish, or white, are tubular, funnel-
or trumpet-shaped, almost circular when expanded, and may attain a length
of six and three-quarters inches. The flowers are primarily violet and
grow at an angle or upright toward the sky.

## Chemistry: Tropane Alkaloids

All types of Datura contain the hallucinogenic tropane alkaloids
scopolamine, hyosyamine, and some atropine. These compounds act as
anticholinergic agents, blocking the neurotransmitter acetylcholine at
muscarinic receptors in the brain and peripheral nervous system. The
resulting intoxication is characterized by dry mouth, dilated pupils,
tachycardia, amnesia, and profoundly realistic visual hallucinations.
The active dose is dangerously close to the toxic dose, making Datura
among the most hazardous of all hallucinogenic plants.
