# Mycorrhizal Mushrooms

Mycorrhizal mushrooms form mutually dependent, beneficial relationships with
the roots of host plants, ranging from trees to grasses. "Myco" means
mushroom while "rhizal" means roots. These mushrooms include some of the
most prized and expensive culinary fungi in the world — Matsutake, Chanterelles,
Boletes, and Truffles — yet they remain among the most difficult to cultivate
artificially. Their dependence on living host plants and complex soil
communities has frustrated [[lactarius-cultivation-attempts]] for centuries.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Associations

The mycelia of mycorrhizal mushrooms interact with plant roots in two
principal ways:

- **Ectomycorrhizal**: The fungal mycelium forms an exterior sheath
  covering the roots of host plants, with hyphae penetrating between
  root cells (but not into them) to form a Hartig net for nutrient
  exchange. Most gourmet mycorrhizal mushrooms are ectomycorrhizal,
  associating with forest trees.
- **Endomycorrhizal (arbuscular)**: The fungal hyphae actually invade
  the interior root cells of host plants, forming highly branched
  arbuscules within the cells. See [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]].

In both cases, both organisms benefit from the association. The plant
receives enhanced nutrient absorption — particularly phosphorus, nitrogen,
and essential trace elements (copper, zinc) — while the fungus receives
photosynthetically derived carbon compounds from the host.

## Prized Mycorrhizal Species

### Matsutake (*Tricholoma magnivelare*)

The Matsutake is one of the most expensive mushrooms in the world, with
premium Japanese specimens fetching hundreds of dollars per piece. It forms
[[ectomycorrhizal-associations]] with various coniferous trees, particularly
pine and fir. Despite its enormous commercial value, Matsutake remains
virtually impossible to cultivate artificially. On sterile media, its
mycelium grows extremely slowly — it may take weeks to fully colonize a
single petri dish. Its long-evolved dependence on specific root by-products
and complex soil chemistry makes laboratory cultivation inherently difficult.

### Chanterelles (*Cantharellus cibarius* and allies)

Chanterelles are among the most popularly collected wild mushrooms globally.
In the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] of North America, Chanterelle harvesting has become
a controversial, multi-million-dollar business. Chanterelles form
ectomycorrhizal associations with trees in both oak forests of the Midwest
and Douglas fir forests of the West.

Chanterelle cultivation is complicated by their unique interdependence on
soil yeasts. At least three organisms must be cultured simultaneously for
success: the host tree, the mushroom fungus, and specific soil yeasts. A
red soil yeast, *Rhodotorula glutinis*, is crucial in stimulating spore
germination. The timing of introduction of each organism appears critical,
and no one has yet grown Chanterelles to the fruitbody stage under
laboratory conditions.

### Truffles (*Tuber* spp.)

Truffle orchards are well established in France, Spain, and Italy, with the
renowned Périgord black truffle (*Tuber melanosporum*) fetching up to $500
per pound. Only in the past 30 years has tissue culture of Truffle mycelium
become widely practiced, allowing the development of planted truffle
orchards.

However, the success rate of truffle cultivation has been discouraging.
Several American companies have marketed truffle-inoculated trees for
commercial use, and tens of thousands of dollars have been exhausted in
the endeavor. Ten years after planting, Stamets noted knowing of only one
or two successes. Suffice it to say that the only ones to have made money
in the truffle tree industry are those who resold "inoculated" seedlings
to other would-be trufflateurs.

### Boletes

Boletes form mycorrhizal associations with both coniferous and deciduous
trees. The King Bolete (*Boletus edulis*, also known as Porcini) is one of
the most commercially important wild-harvested mushrooms worldwide. Like
other mycorrhizal species, it has defied reliable cultivation despite its
enormous market value.

## The Challenge of Time

Perhaps the greatest obstacle to [[mycorrhizal-mushroom-cultivation-challenges]] is the
time scale involved. Experiments attempting to mimic how Chanterelles or
Matsutake grow in nature may take 20-40 years for each iteration — the age
trees must reach to support healthy, fruiting colonies of these fungi. This
fundamental time constraint confounds the quick feedback loop that all
cultivators need to refine their techniques.

Senescence compounds the problem. Often the first sign of senescence is
not the inability of mycelium to grow vegetatively, but the loss of the
ability to form the sexually reproducing organ — the mushroom itself. A
strain may grow vigorously on artificial media for years but never produce
fruitbodies.

## Ecological Threats to Mycorrhizal Mushrooms

Mycorrhizal mushrooms in Europe have suffered a radical decline in recent
decades, while [[growing-gourmet-saprophytic-mushrooms-decomposers]] have increased in numbers. The
combined effects of acid rain, industrial pollutants, and environmental
disasters like Chernobyl have been suggested to explain the sudden decline
of both the quantity and diversity of wild mycorrhizal mushrooms.

In the past decade, the diversity of mycorrhizal mushrooms in Europe has
fallen by more than 50%. Some species, such as the Chanterelle, have all
but disappeared from regions in the Netherlands where they were abundant
only 20 years ago. Many biologists view these mushrooms as indicator
species — the first domino to fall in a series leading to the failure of
the forest's life-support systems.

The sudden availability of dead wood (from pollution-stressed and dying
trees) is thought to be responsible for the comparative increase in
saprophytic mushroom numbers. This trade-off — fewer mycorrhizal species
but more decomposers — may signal a fundamental shift in forest ecosystem
health.

## Low-Tech Approaches to Mycorrhizal Cultivation

Given the enormous hurdle of time, Stamets favors "low-tech" approaches
to working with mycorrhizal mushrooms:

1. **Tree transplantation**: Planting seedlings near the root zones of
   proven mushroom-producing trees, allowing natural mycorrhizal
   infection to occur over several years
2. **Spore mass broadcasting**: Casting spore mass of target species into
   forests with habitat similar to where they naturally proliferate
3. **Forest preservation**: Rather than attempting to cultivate these
   species, preserving the forest ecosystems that support them
