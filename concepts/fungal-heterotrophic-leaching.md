# Fungal Heterotrophic Leaching

## Overview

Heterotrophic leaching uses fungi and other heterotrophic microorganisms to solubilize metals from solid materials through organic acid production. Unlike autotrophic bioleaching (by Thiobacillus spp. of sulfide minerals), heterotrophic leaching is particularly effective for non-sulfide minerals — silicates, carbonates, oxides, and phosphates — which cannot be attacked by sulfur-oxidizing bacteria. See [[fungal-metal-redox-transformations-detailed]] for the underlying solubilization chemistry.

## Historical Background

Heterotrophic leaching may have ancient origins. Pliny the Elder (23–79 AD) described copper extraction from hydrated copper silicate (chrysocolla), a silicate mineral that suggests non-thiobacilli involvement. Slater (1856) documented oxalic acid decomposition of phosphates, arsenates, and chromates. Modern research dates from the mid-20th century, with systematic studies of fungal mineral weathering accelerating from the 1960s onward.

## Mechanisms of Metal Solubilization

### Organic Acid-Mediated Leaching
Three primary mechanisms operate:

1. **Acidolysis**: Proton attack on mineral surfaces displaces metal cations
2. **Complexolysis**: Organic acid anions form soluble metal-ligand complexes (e.g., Al-citrate, Fe-citrate)
3. **Redoxolysis**: Redox reactions change metal oxidation states (minor role for fungi)

A kinetic model (Furrer & Stumm, 1986) describes dissolution by both protonation of the mineral surface and surface concentration of complex-forming ligands (oxalate, malonate, citrate, succinate).

### Key Organic Acids

| Acid | Production Source | Leaching Role |
|---|---|---|
| Citric acid | TCA cycle (A. niger, P. simplicissimum) | Strong chelator; solubilizes Al, Fe, Ni |
| Oxalic acid | Metabolic byproduct (most fungi) | Forms soluble complexes; also insoluble oxalates |
| Gluconic acid | Glucose oxidase (A. niger) | Primary leaching agent from fly ash |
| Glutarate | Metabolic intermediate | Solubilizes K, Ca from silicates |
| Tartrate | TCA-related metabolite | Solubilizes K, Ca from silicates |

### Fly Ash Leaching
In the presence of fly ash, A. niger shifts from citric to mainly gluconic acid production. Low pH favors citric acid formation; higher pH (from alkaline fly ash) stimulates oxalic and gluconic acid formation. A. niger mobilizes nearly 100% of lead from fly ash suspensions at higher concentrations.

## Fungal Species for Heterotrophic Leaching

### Aspergillus niger
The most commonly used species for biohydrometallurgical applications:

- Excellent organic acid producer (citric, gluconic, oxalic)
- Mobilizes Al, Ca, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni, Zn from diverse substrates
- Effective on fly ash, red mud, lateritic nickel ores, bauxite, soils
- Nearly 100% lead mobilization from fly ash
- Operates at 25–35°C in batch and two-step processes

### Penicillium simplicissimum
Second most commonly used species:

- Produces citrate, oxalate, and gluconate
- Mobilizes Al, Ni, V, Zn, Co from filter dust, electrofilter dust, lateritic ores
- Can grow on electronic scrap at 10–100 g/L concentrations
- After 21 days: 5 mmol/L oxalate + 20 mmol/L citrate
- Generally mobilizes more metals than A. niger under the same conditions

### Other Notable Species
- **Aspergillus foetidus**: Cu from ferromanganese nodules; Zn, Pb, Cu from soil
- **Penicillium notatum**: Al from red mud
- **Cladosporium cladosporioides**: Multiple elements (Al, Ca, Fe, K, Mg, Mn, Na, Zn) from diverse rock types
- **Trichoderma viride**: Al from kaolinite/illite; Cu from ferromanganese nodules
- **Rhizopus arrhizus**: Pb from pyromorphite
- **Saccharomyces cerevisiae**: Cu, Pb, Sn from electronic waste; Al, Ca, Fe, K, Si, Mg from silicates
- **Yarrowia lipolytica**: Cu, Pb, Sn from electronic waste and catalysts

## Process Configurations

### Plate Technique
Fungi grow on solidified media containing powdered minerals. Clear zones indicate metal leaching. Useful for screening but not for industrial application.

### Batch (One-Step) Process
Organisms grown directly with solid substrates for days to months. Limitations: toxic elements can inhibit growth; biomass/mineral separation is difficult for downstream processing; solubilized metals may re-adsorb to biomass.

### Two-Step Process
Fungi grown in absence of metal-containing solids first. Cell-free supernatant (containing organic acids) is then used for leaching. Advantages:

- Higher organic acid concentrations achievable
- Biomass recycled; waste not contaminated by microbial material
- Higher temperatures possible (90°C), reducing treatment duration
- Higher waste concentrations applicable
- Better selectivity control

No industrial-scale two-step processes exist yet.

## Applications

### Fly Ash Treatment
Municipal solid waste incineration fly ash contains Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb, Zn as oxides. A. niger achieves good mobilization of Cd, Pb, and Zn. Some elements (Al, Zn) present in concentrations allowing economical recovery; others (Ag, Ni, Zr) are at low-grade ore levels where microbial leaching is the only viable technique.

### Electronic Waste
Electronic scrap contains ~237 g/kg Al, 80 g/kg Cu, 15 g/kg Ni, 20 g/kg Pb, 23 g/kg Sn, 26 g/kg Zn, plus Au and Ag. A. niger and P. simplicissimum mobilize these metals from dust-like residues. A. niger preferentially mobilizes Cu (65% removal); combined with 95% removal of Al, Ni, Pb, Zn.

### Soil Bioremediation
Fungal leaching for soil metal removal is largely unexplored despite broad fungal biodiversity. A. niger has been used as a bioassay organism for soil element bioavailability since the 1930s. Soil leaching occurs through acidolysis, complexolysis, and redoxolysis simultaneously.

### Ore Processing
Lateritic nickel ores (Ni, Fe, Co, Ca, Mg), bauxite (Al, Fe, Si), ferromanganese nodules (Cu), silicate ores (Al, Ni, Cr, Fe, Mg), and scheelite (W, Si, Fe, Al, Ca, Mg) have all been treated by fungal leaching.

## Limitations and Future Directions

### Current Limitations
- **Sterility requirements**: Broad pH range of fungal leaching allows contamination; sterility adds cost
- **Carbon source cost**: Organic carbon for fungal growth represents a major cost factor; inexpensive sources (molasses, food industry byproducts) are essential
- **No industrial applications**: Despite promising laboratory results, no commercial-scale heterotrophic leaching processes exist

### Future Potential
- **Thermophilic fungi**: Simple nutritional requirements, high growth rates, contamination resistance at high cultivation temperatures — not yet evaluated for metal mobilization
- **Combined fungal-bacterial processes**: Stepwise combination exploiting complementary leaching patterns (e.g., fungi for Pb/Sn, thiobacilli for sulfides)
- **Adaptation experiments**: Selection of strains tolerating elevated waste concentrations with selective leaching
- **Secondary resource recovery**: Treatment of industrial waste materials as metal sources reduces demand for primary ores
