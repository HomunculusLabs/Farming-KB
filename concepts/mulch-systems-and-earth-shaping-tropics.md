# Mulch Systems and Earth-Shaping in the Tropics

> Chapter 10 — *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, Bill Mollison

Mulch is the single most important tool for establishing productive systems in the tropics. Thick organic mulch suppresses weeds, conserves moisture, moderates soil temperature, prevents erosion, and feeds soil biology. Combined with earth-shaping techniques adapted to slope and drainage conditions, mulch-based strategies form the foundation of tropical permaculture design.

## Mulch Depth and Application

When establishing home gardens in the tropics, mulch should be applied thickly at **20–25 cm (8–10 inches)** deep. This depth is sufficient to smother competitive grasses (including persistent species like kikuyu grass, which is almost totally eradicated under thick mulch), retain soil moisture, and create conditions favorable for soil organism activity.

All crops thrive in thick mulch. The key principle is to never leave soil bare in tropical systems.

## Organic vs. Plastic Mulch

### Organic Mulches

Organic mulches are strongly preferred because they decompose into humus, build soil structure, and support diverse soil ecology. All organic mulch types tested increase crop yields compared to bare ground. Every form of plant waste—straw, leaves, sawdust, husks, stems—has value as mulch material.

### Plastic Sheet Mulch

Plastic sheet mulch prevents rain splash and nutrient leaching, and condenses groundwater at night beneath the sheet. It produces good crop yields. However, it **does not add humus** to the soil and therefore does not build long-term soil health. Mollison considers it a useful short-term technique but an inferior long-term strategy compared to organic mulching.

## Crop Yields Under Mulch (Okigbo and Lal, 1978)

Research data from Okigbo and Lal (1978) demonstrates the yield advantages of various mulch materials across staple tropical crops. Yields are given in tonnes per hectare.

| Crop | Best Mulch Type | Yield (t/ha) | Bare Ground (t/ha) |
|---|----------------|--------------|---------------------|
| Maize | [[legume-nitrogen-fixation-mechanism|Legume]] wastes | 4.3 | 3.0 |
| Cowpea | Millet straw | 2.4 | 0.6 |
| Soybean | Sawdust | 1.9 | 0.6 |
| [[permaculture-plants-comfrey-chop-and-drop|Cassava]] | Sawdust | 20.5 | 16.4 |

Key observations from the data:

- **Cowpea and soybean** show the most dramatic improvements (4× increase), suggesting these legumes are especially responsive to the microclimate and weed suppression that [[mulching-guide|mulch]] provides.
- **Cassava** yields are highest overall under sawdust mulch, though the percentage improvement over bare ground is more modest than for other crops.
- **Maize** benefits from legume wastes, likely receiving both the physical mulch effect and slow nitrogen release from decomposing legume residue.
- No organic mulch type reduced yields compared to bare ground.

## Special-Purpose Mulches

Different plants and [[soil-ph|soil]] conditions call for specific mulch materials:

- **Legumes, comfrey, and Pultanea** — [[plant-micronutrient-functions|nutrient]]-dense mulches supplying balanced NPK as they break down.
- **Pine needles and legume mulch** — ideal for bromeliads and other acid-loving plants, maintaining soil pH in the 4–5 range.
- **Buckwheat straw and nut husks** — used to raise soil pH where acidity is a problem.
- **Bark mulches** — slow-decomposing, useful for long-term weed suppression and moisture retention.

## Integrating Animals with Mulch Systems

Plant wastes can be productively routed through animal systems before becoming mulch. This strategy serves multiple functions simultaneously:

- **Pigs** are especially effective at eliminating problem plants including nut-grasses, rhizomes, bulbs, and sedges. Pigs root through mulch and soil, destroying persistent underground plant structures that would otherwise compete with crops.
- Leaf mulch and plant residues can be routed through **animal bedding**, adding manure value and reducing fire hazard.
- The animal-manured material then serves as enriched mulch for garden systems.

## Coconut Husks

Coconut husks are a versatile tropical mulch material, particularly valued for vanilla cultivation. However, they have a significant drawback: [[water-wise-vegetable-gardening|water]] retained in husks becomes a **mosquito breeding site**. Alternative uses for coconut husks include:

- Shredding for general garden mulch
- Burning to produce activated charcoal
- Processing as solid fuel

## Pioneer Crops for Continuous Mulch Supply

Establishing a self-sustaining mulch supply is critical for long-term system viability. Pioneer crops that produce continuous mulch include:

- **Quick-growing Acacias** — fast nitrogen-fixing trees that produce leaf litter
- **Bananas** — heavy biomass producers with large leaf and pseudostem material
- **Lablab (hyacinth bean)** — vigorous climbing legume producing abundant green matter
- **Comfrey** — deep-rooted nutrient accumulator with rapid regrowth after cutting
- **Bamboo** — produces both leaf litter and culm material
- **Palms** — provide frond material and trunk fiber when thinned

These species should be established early in system development to create closed-loop mulch production.

## Grassland Management

Converting grassland to productive garden or [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs|forest]] requires a phased approach:

1. **Strip-mow** existing grass rather than clearing it entirely
2. Apply the cut grass as **mulch** around newly planted trees and crops
3. Allow trees to **re-establish** under the protective mulch layer
4. Use **seed-free "second-cut grass"** in garden areas — the first cut removes seed heads, and the second regrowth is safe to use as mulch without introducing weed seeds

## Fire Control and Waste Philosophy

**There is no excuse for burning organic waste.** Even large logs rot quickly in tropical conditions through the action of fungi, termites, and beetle larvae. Fire destroys nutrients, kills soil biology, and releases carbon that could be building soil.

Fire prevention strategies include:

- **Raking under bamboo and clump canes** to remove dry material that could carry fire
- **Routing leaf mulch through animal bedding** rather than piling it as a fire hazard
- Maintaining continuous green mulch cover to eliminate dry fuel accumulation

## Earth-Shaping Techniques

Earth-shaping adapts the land surface to manage water, prevent [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder|erosion]], and create favorable growing conditions. The appropriate technique depends on slope and drainage.

### Gentle Slopes (2–8°)

On gentle slopes, **[[permaculture-terrace-design-slopes|contour]] banks or swales** are the primary earth-shaping tool. These shallow channels dug on contour:

- Capture and infiltrate runoff water
- Prevent soil erosion by slowing water flow
- Recharge groundwater
- Should be coupled with **[[terrace-design-and-construction|terrace]] development** and **mulch hedgerows** planted along the contour for stabilization and continuous organic input

### Very Flat Sites (Less Than 4°)

Flat land presents drainage challenges rather than erosion problems. Two complementary strategies apply:

- **Raised mounds and ridges** — lift root zones above the water table for improved drainage and aeration
- **Pits for water impoundment** — in areas where water retention is desired, shallow pits capture and hold water, creating localized moisture reserves

### Mounding for Specific Crops

Mounding is particularly important for crops susceptible to waterlogging:

- **Cassava** — tuber development suffers in poorly drained soils
- **Yam** — requires well-drained conditions for tuber enlargement
- **Cucurbits** (pumpkins, squash, melons) — benefit from raised planting to prevent root rot

### Pitting for Dry Areas

In dry regions or during savannah dry seasons, **pitting** is the preferred technique:

- Pits capture and concentrate rainfall and mulch material
- Mulch stays in place within pits rather than being blown or washed away
- Moisture is retained at depth where plant roots can access it
- Organic matter accumulates and decomposes in the pit, building soil over time

## Poor Soil Rehabilitation

Rehabilitating degraded or poor soils requires a strategic, patient approach:

1. **Start small** — establish a dense, intensely managed nucleus area
2. **Dense planting** of the nucleus with pioneer species plus heavy mulch application
3. **Expand at the periphery** — as the nucleus improves soil conditions, extend outward gradually
4. Use the pioneer mulch crops listed above to generate organic matter on-site
5. Integrate animal systems to accelerate nutrient cycling and problem plant control

The principle is to create a healthy, productive center that progressively improves surrounding land through edge effects and managed expansion.

## Design Principles Summary

- Never leave soil bare in the tropics
- Apply mulch 20–25 cm deep for establishment
- Route plant wastes through animals before mulching where possible
- Choose mulch materials based on [[cover-crops|crop]] and soil pH needs
- Match earth-shaping technique to slope and drainage conditions
- Use contour swales on slopes, mounds on wet flats, pits in dry areas
- Establish pioneer mulch-producing species early in system development
- Burn nothing — let decomposers recycle all organic matter
- Rehabilitate poor soils from a small, dense nucleus outward
