## Mulch Systems and Earth-Shaping in the Tropics

> Chapter 10 — *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, [[bill-mollison]]

Mulch is the single most important tool for establishing productive systems in the tropics. Thick organic mulch suppresses weeds, conserves moisture, moderates soil temperature, prevents erosion, and feeds soil biology. Combined with earth-shaping techniques adapted to slope and drainage conditions, mulch-based strategies form the foundation of [[comparison-arid-vs-tropical-permaculture]] design.

## Mulch Depth and Application

When establishing home gardens in the tropics, mulch should be applied thickly at **20–25 cm (8–10 inches)** deep. This depth is sufficient to smother competitive grasses (including persistent species like kikuyu grass, which is almost totally eradicated under thick mulch), retain soil moisture, and create conditions favorable for soil organism activity.

All crops thrive in thick mulch. The key principle is to never leave soil bare in tropical systems.

## Organic vs. Plastic Mulch

### Organic Mulches

Organic mulches are strongly preferred because they decompose into humus, build soil structure, and support diverse soil ecology. All organic mulch types tested increase crop yields compared to bare ground. Every form of plant waste—straw, leaves, sawdust, husks, stems—has value as mulch material.

### Plastic Sheet Mulch

Plastic sheet mulch prevents rain splash and [[green-cannabis-soil-flushing-nutrient-leaching]], and condenses groundwater at night beneath the sheet. It produces good crop yields. However, it **does not add humus** to the soil and therefore does not build long-term soil health. Mollison considers it a useful short-term technique but an inferior long-term strategy compared to [[organic-mulching-techniques]].

## Pioneer Crops for Continuous Mulch Supply

Establishing a self-sustaining mulch supply is critical for long-term system viability. Pioneer crops that produce continuous mulch include:

- **Quick-growing Acacias** — fast nitrogen-fixing trees that produce leaf litter
- **Bananas** — heavy biomass producers with large leaf and pseudostem material
- **Lablab (hyacinth bean)** — vigorous climbing legume producing abundant green matter
- **Comfrey** — deep-rooted nutrient accumulator with rapid regrowth after cutting
- **Bamboo** — produces both leaf litter and culm material
- **Palms** — provide frond material and trunk fiber when thinned

These species should be established early in system development to create closed-loop mulch production.

## Grassland Management

Converting grassland to productive garden or [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder|erosion]], and create favorable growing conditions. The appropriate technique depends on slope and drainage.

### Gentle Slopes (2–8°)

On gentle slopes, **[[terrace-design-and-construction|terrace]] development** and **mulch hedgerows** planted along the contour for stabilization and continuous organic input

### Very Flat Sites (Less Than 4°)

Flat land presents drainage challenges rather than erosion problems. Two complementary strategies apply:

- **Raised mounds and ridges** — lift root zones above the water table for improved drainage and aeration
- **Pits for water impoundment** — in areas where water retention is desired, shallow pits capture and hold water, creating localized moisture reserves

### Mounding for Specific Crops

Mounding is particularly important for crops susceptible to waterlogging:

- **Cassava** — tuber development suffers in poorly drained soils
- **Yam** — requires well-drained conditions for tuber enlargement
- **Cucurbits** (pumpkins, squash, melons) — benefit from raised planting to prevent root rot

### Pitting for Dry Areas

In dry regions or during savannah dry seasons, **pitting** is the preferred technique:

- Pits capture and concentrate rainfall and mulch material
- Mulch stays in place within pits rather than being blown or washed away
- Moisture is retained at depth where plant roots can access it
- Organic matter accumulates and decomposes in the pit, building soil over time

## Poor Soil Rehabilitation

Rehabilitating degraded or poor soils requires a strategic, patient approach:

1. **Start small** — establish a dense, intensely managed nucleus area
2. **Dense planting** of the nucleus with pioneer species plus heavy [[earth-shaping-and-mulch-application-tropics]]
3. **Expand at the periphery** — as the nucleus improves soil conditions, extend outward gradually
4. Use the pioneer mulch crops listed above to generate organic matter on-site
5. Integrate animal systems to accelerate [[soil-protozoa-nutrient-cycling-and-mineralization]] problem plant control
