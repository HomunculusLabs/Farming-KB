# Drip Irrigation Basics

Drip irrigation (also called trickle irrigation) delivers water directly to the root zone of plants through a network of tubes, pipes, and emitters. It is the most water-efficient active irrigation method, reducing water use by 30-50% compared to sprinkler systems while improving plant health and reducing disease.

## Core Components

### Water Source and Connection

The system begins at a water source — spigot, rain barrel, pump, or gravity-fed tank from [[rainwater-catchment-systems]]. A hose-thread-to-pipe-thread adapter connects the household supply to the irrigation system.

### Backflow Preventer

Required when connecting to a municipal water supply to prevent irrigation water (and potential contaminants) from flowing back into the potable water system. A simple brass or brass-and-plastic backflow preventer installs at the hose bib.

### Filter

Drip emitters have very small openings (0.5-2 mm) that clog easily. A filter is non-negotiable.

-  **Screen filter** (150-200 mesh / 75-100 micron) — Standard for clean water sources. Clean or replace the screen monthly.
-  **Disk filter** — Better for water with fine sediment. More expensive but requires less frequent cleaning.
-  **Sand media filter** — For very dirty water (pond water, well water with sediment). Overkill for most homestead applications.

### Pressure Regulator

Drip systems operate at low pressure (10-30 PSI / 0.7-2.0 bar). Standard household water pressure (40-80 PSI) will blow fittings apart and damage emitters without a regulator.

-  **20 PSI (1.4 bar) regulator** — Most common choice, works with most drip emitters and micro-sprinklers
-  **25 PSI (1.7 bar) regulator** — For longer runs or systems with significant elevation change
- Install the regulator after the filter, before the main distribution line

### Main Line (Header)

The main line distributes water from the pressure regulator to the garden. Use 1/2 inch (16 mm) poly tubing for most homestead systems. Lay the main line along the edge of the garden bed or along a path for easy access.

### Drip Line and Emitters

### Drip Line Types

-  **Drip tape (T-tape)** — Flat, thin-walled tubing with pre-installed emitters spaced 15, 20, or 30 cm apart. Very inexpensive ($30-50 per 1000 ft). Ideal for row crops in vegetable gardens. Lifespan: 1-5 seasons depending on wall thickness.
-  **Drip line (dripline)** — Round, thicker-walled tubing with inline emitters. More durable than drip tape. Emitter spacing: 15, 30, or 45 cm. Lifespan: 5-10+ seasons. Cost: $80-150 per 1000 ft.
-  **Individual emitters** — Button or barb-style emitters punched into 1/2 inch poly tubing at desired locations. Most flexible for irregular plant spacing. Available in flow rates from 0.5 to 4 GPH (2-15 L/hr).

### Emitter Flow Rates

-  **0.5 GPH (2 L/hr)** — Sandy soils, frequent watering intervals
-  **1.0 GPH (4 L/hr)** — Standard for most garden soils. Most versatile.
-  **2.0 GPH (8 L/hr)** — Heavy clay soils, larger plants, less frequent watering

### Pressure-Compensating (PC) Emitters

PC emitters deliver the same flow rate regardless of pressure variations along the line or changes in elevation. Essential for uneven terrain and long runs. Worth the small additional cost ($0.15-0.30 more per emitter) in most situations.

### End Cap and Flush Valve

Every drip line needs a closed end. Simple end caps work, but flush valves allow periodic flushing of sediment from the line. Flush lines by opening the end cap and running water for 2-3 minutes at the start of each season and monthly during use.

## Timer (Controller)

Automating drip irrigation saves time and ensures consistent watering. Options:

-  **Battery-powered hose timer** — Simplest option. Screws onto the hose bib, runs on 2 AA batteries, programs watering duration and frequency. Cost: $20-60. Good for a single zone.
-  **Multi-zone controller** — Programs multiple zones with different schedules. Needed if different garden areas have different watering requirements. Cost: $40-150.
-  **Smart/Wi-Fi controller** — Connects to weather data and adjusts watering based on rainfall, temperature, and soil moisture. Cost: $80-200. Reduces water waste significantly.
-  **Mechanical timer** — No batteries or power required. Twist to set duration. Simple but limited to single-zone, fixed schedules.

## Layout Design

### Step 1: Map the Garden

Draw a scaled plan of the garden showing plant types, bed dimensions, and water source location.

### Step 2: Determine Zones

Group plants with similar water needs into zones:

- Zone 1: Leafy greens and herbs (frequent, shallow watering)
- Zone 2: Fruiting vegetables (moderate, deeper watering)
- Zone 3: Perennials and fruit trees (less frequent, deep watering)

### Step 3: Size the System

- Determine total emitter output per zone
- Ensure water source can deliver the required flow rate
- A typical hose bib delivers 4-8 GPM (15-30 L/min)
- A zone with 100 emitters at 1 GPH requires 100 GPH (1.67 GPM) — well within hose bib capacity

### Step 4: Lay Out the Lines

- Run main line along the garden perimeter or down the center path
- Branch drip lines off the main line using tee fittings
- For row crops: run drip tape parallel to rows, one line per row or one line between two rows
- For raised beds: run drip line in a loop or zigzag pattern
- For individual plants: run 1/2 inch poly tubing and insert emitters at each plant location

## Maintenance

- **Flush lines** at the start of each season and monthly during use
- **Clean filters** monthly — more often if water source has sediment
- **Check emitters** for clogging — mineral buildup can be cleaned with vinegar soak; organic clogs require compressed air or replacement
- **Inspect for leaks** — rodent damage, UV degradation, and fitting failure are common causes
- **Winterize** in freezing climates: drain all lines, disconnect timers, store above-ground components indoors
- **System pressure test** — run the system before planting season to verify all components are functioning

## Common Problems

-  **Uneven watering** — Usually caused by pressure variation. Use PC emitters and keep runs under 60 m on 1/2 inch tubing.
-  **Clogged emitters** — Prevent with adequate filtration. Clean with vinegar or replace.
-  **Rodent damage** — Chipmunks and squirrels chew poly tubing. Bury lines 2-5 cm deep or use thicker-walled tubing.
-  **Overwatering** — Easy to overwater with drip because the soil surface looks dry while the root zone is saturated. Use a soil moisture probe or check soil moisture by hand at 10-15 cm depth.
-  **Underwatering** — Check that emitter output matches plant demand. Young plants may need additional hand-watering until roots reach the emitter wetting zone.

## Water Efficiency Tips

- Use mulch (5-10 cm of straw, wood chips, or leaves) over drip lines to reduce evaporation by 25-50%
- Water early morning (4-7 AM) to minimize evaporation
- Adjust timer seasonally — plants need less water in spring and fall than midsummer
- Add a soil moisture sensor to automate watering based on actual soil conditions rather than a fixed schedule

## Integration with Homestead Systems

Drip irrigation works alongside [[passive-irrigation-systems]] for comprehensive water management. Use passive systems (ollas, wicking beds) for日常 watering and drip for supplemental irrigation during peak demand or for crops not covered by passive systems. Connect to [[rainwater-catchment-systems]] with a pump or gravity feed for off-grid operation. In [[aquaponics-plant-selection]] systems, drip can distribute nutrient-rich water to adjacent garden beds.
