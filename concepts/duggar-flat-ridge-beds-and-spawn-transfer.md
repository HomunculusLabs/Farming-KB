# Flat Beds, Ridge Beds, and Spawn Transfer Methods
## Duggar (1915) — Bed Systems and Spawn Propagation Techniques

B.M. Duggar's 1915 manual details two fundamental bed systems [[grass-seed-substrate-for-mushroom-cultivation]] — the
flat bed (English type) and the ridge bed (French type) — along with critical guidance on
spawn transfer and the dangers of strain deterioration through improper propagation. These
practical considerations determined the productivity and sustainability of every mushroom
enterprise.

## The Flat Bed System (English Type)

The flat bed is more generally employed in the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] and requires less labor in
construction. It can be used in ordinary cellars or mushroom houses where beds are made on
the floor only, or in houses with deep cellars where shelves are arranged in tiers.

### Shelf-Bed Tier System

The shelf-bed system permits multiplication of available [[cotter-growing-space-design-fruiting-rooms]] four to five times
compared to single-tier arrangements. This multiplication comes at some sacrifice of
convenience, but is frequently the only practicable plan for operating on a paying basis,
since a single tier would require perhaps twice the investment in plant construction for
the same bed space.

### Bed Dimensions

- Wall beds should seldom be more than 3 feet across
- Beds approachable from either side may be 6 feet across
- Aisles should be at least 3 feet wide, with wider aisles in very large houses
- Flat beds should be 8-10 inches deep after suitable compression
- The bed should be firm enough to support a person without sinking more than 1-2 inches

### Sided vs. Open Beds

Duggar expressed a clear preference for sided flat beds, stating that this type encourages
the formation of heavier mushrooms when other conditions are equal. The boards retain
moisture and provide a more stable [[cannabis-growing-environment-setup]]. The same principle of gentle slope
applies to unsided beds, though sided beds eliminate the risk of clusters pulling away from
their attachment to the spawn below.

## The Ridge Bed System (French Type)

In the relatively low-roofed caves of Paris, the ridge bed system is exclusively practiced,
and it has distinct advantages in that environment.

### Bed Configuration

- Arranged in groups of two
- 10-12 inches of floor space left between each group (reducing to about 9 inches after
  casing)
- The two beds in a group may come in contact throughout their length
- Each ridge: 20-24 inches wide at the base, tapering to a top no more than 6 inches
  across
- Height: 12-14 inches
- Gentle slope from crest to floor is essential for heavy cluster production

### Advantages and Disadvantages

The ridge system requires more labor in both preparation and subsequent care. It permits
more effective sanitation and slightly increases floor space, and seems to increase yield
possibilities. However, Duggar found that the first and last parts of the crop from ridge
beds tended to run lighter in weight compared to the continuous flat bed.

### The Slope Factor

If the ridge slope is too steep, mushrooms develop smaller because they dry out more
rapidly and clusters pull away from their spawn attachment. The gentle slope allows heavy
clusters to form and mature to full size.

## Installation and Compression

Whatever the bed type, compost is pitched into the designated area and immediately arranged
and compressed with shovel blows or a compressing board. Compression prevents the bed from
becoming loose and dry. For ridge beds, compression should create a gentle slope, not a
steep one.

## Spawning the Beds

### Timing

After bed installation, temperature must be monitored daily. For a few days, temperature
may rise before declining. Spawning should not proceed until temperature falls to 70-75°F,
and spawning at 75°F is permissible only when temperature is unquestionably declining.
Duggar's later tests indicated 65-70°F is generally preferable when maintaining a growing
temperature of 55°F. Spawning at 50-65°F does no harm but growth is very slow below 50°F.
