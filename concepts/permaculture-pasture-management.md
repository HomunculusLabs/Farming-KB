## Perimeter Design

Even on large grazing areas, the fenced perimeter provides protection and forage. Build a second fence inside the first, with the space between planted with trees, hedges, and spiny shrubs. This provides protection, windbreak, fruit, nuts, wood, and forage for sheep, bees, and birds.

Key soil health indicators in pasture include:

- Earthworm populations: More than 10 worms per cubic foot indicates active biological soil

- Surface cover: At least 70% ground cover prevents erosion and maintains soil moisture

- Root depth: Deep-rooted forages like chicory and plantain break compaction layers

- Infiltration rate: Healthy pasture soil absorbs rainfall within seconds rather than pooling

Grazing animals themselves contribute to soil health through manure deposition, trampling of residual biomass into the soil surface, and stimulating root exudation through defoliation. The key is managing grazing intensity so plants have adequate recovery time to rebuild root reserves and photosynthesize. Overgrazing depletes root carbohydrate stores, weakens plants, and opens bare ground to weed invasion.

Rest periods between grazing events should be long enough for plants to fully recover -- typically 30-60 days depending on species and season. In fast growth periods (spring), shorter rest works; in slow growth periods (summer heat or winter dormancy), much longer rests are needed.
