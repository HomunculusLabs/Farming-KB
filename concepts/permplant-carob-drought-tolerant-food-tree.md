# Carob as Drought-Tolerant Food Tree in Permaculture

Carob (Ceratonia siliqua) is the largest of Europe's leguminous trees,
reaching up to 15 metres in height. A native to the Mediterranean region
belonging to the family Caesalpiniaceae, carob is an exceptionally
valuable [[permaculture-designers-manual-food-preserving]] renowned for its
drought tolerance, longevity, and versatile pod production. It is
particularly suited to [[arid-landscape-permaculture]] and [[dryland-permaculture-strategies]].

## Growth Requirements

Carob grows in minimum rainfall areas of 250 mm but does better with
higher rainfalls. It is sensitive to cold below -4 degrees C when young,
but mature trees suffer little damage at -8 degrees C. These evergreen
trees can spread their crowns out to 10 metres and prefer slightly
alkaline, sandy soils with good drainage. However, they will grow in
virtually any soil including rocky ground, with the exception of
compacted clay. Their life span exceeds 500 years, making them a
multigenerational planting investment.

## Root System

Tap roots of mature trees have been traced down to 20 metres. A carob
seedling develops a long tap root at a very early age: about 250 mm in
the first two weeks from germination. Trees that remain in shallow pots
do not develop ideal root systems and lose the advantage of drought
tolerance. This deep rooting habit makes carob an excellent |mineral accumulator, accessing nutrients far below the surface.

## Nutritional Composition of Pods

The pod is a fleshy bean up to 300 mm in length and is both flavoursome
and nutritious. Reported nutritional ranges include:

- Crude protein: 2.25-21.0%
- Crude fat: 0.5-1.5%
- Crude fibre: 4.65-13.3%
- Nitrogen-free extract: 74.1-75.97%
- Total sugars: 37.1-56.6%
- Ash: 1.52-2.8%
- Calcium: 0.13-0.28%

The pods are excellent stock feed, commercially sold as a chocolate
substitute for human food, and the seed contains gums with industrial
uses. The bean also has potential for alcohol fuel production.

## Yield and Economics

Yields of 1 tonne of pods from a single tree have been reported in good
seasons, but about 10% of that (100 kg per tree) is more typical, still
yielding approximately 8 tonnes per hectare. Economic projections
(1985 prices) showed year-10 returns of $6,765/ha with full irrigation,
$3,025 with minimum irrigation, and $1,100 with no irrigation at a
planting density of 90-100 trees/ha.

## Propagation

Carob seeds have very hard protective cases and need to be scarified.
The easiest method is to pour boiling water over the seeds and allow
them to soak overnight. The procedure is repeated on any seeds that have
not swollen. Seed can then be sown into pots.

Direct seeding is possible using protective markers such as old tyres.
However, during germination, moisture must be maintained, so starting
seedlings in deep pots (such as one-litre milk cartons with drainage
holes) and transplanting after a couple of weeks is often preferred.
Carobs can be sown at any time of the year, although young seedlings
are frost susceptible.

## Planting and Establishment

When transplanting, the seedling must be carefully removed from its pot
to avoid disturbing the tap root. The root ball should sit on firmed-down
soil at ground level. Closer planting of seedlings allows for natural
deaths and final selection of better-fruiting females and well-placed
males. Selection can be made after the first couple of seasons of
fruiting (7-8 years), or known cultivars can be grafted onto rootstock
for earlier production.

## Dioecious Nature

Carobs are generally dioecious, requiring male and female trees to set
pods, but are occasionally hermaphrodite. A ratio of approximately 1
male to 8-10 females is recommended for commercial production.

## Companion Plants

Carobs have been grown in Algeria as companions to grapes with good
results. They grow well with [[tagasaste]],
Albizia lophantha, and [[acacia]] decurrens as nurse plants.

## Environmental Resilience

Some degree of salt tolerance has been observed in carobs growing along
waterways and in low-lying areas in both Australia and Israel. The carob
tree is reported to not burn easily, and when scorched by fire, it
generally recovers, making it useful in [[fire-resistant-plants]]. Stock will also trim the foliage to within their reach.

## Medicinal and Industrial Uses

Carob pod powder (locust bean gum) is used as a thickening agent in
food processing. The seeds yield a gum used in cosmetics, pharmaceuticals,
and various industrial applications. Demand for carob powder has been
increasing at approximately 20% per year.

## Role in [[permaculture-design]]

In [[mollison-designers-olive-fig-and-mediterranean-tree-crops]], carob serves as a canopy-level element providing
shade, nitrogen fixation, and high-value pod production. Its deep tap
root accesses water and minerals unavailable to shallow-rooted
understorey crops. The long lifespan and low maintenance requirements
make it ideal for [[comparison-food-forest-design-vs-backyard-orchard-design]] systems in dry climates.
