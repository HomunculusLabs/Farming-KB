# Phytochrome and Plant Light Perception

Phytochrome is the molecular light switch at the heart of how plants
measure day length and control flowering. Discovered in the mid-twentieth
century, it exists in two interconvertible forms: one activated by red light
and the other by far-red light. This elegant binary system allows plants to
track the duration of continuous darkness with remarkable precision, forming
the biochemical basis of photoperiodism.

## The Red/Far-Red Switch

The story of phytochrome begins with a series of experiments around the
time of World War II. Scientists discovered they could manipulate flowering
by briefly interrupting the dark period with light. A short-day plant like
soybean, which normally flowers only when nights are long, would remain
vegetative if lights were turned on for just a few minutes in the middle of
the night. Conversely, a long-day plant like iris could be induced to
flower in midwinter (when days are naturally short) by a brief flash of
light during the night. These experiments proved that plants measure the
length of the continuous dark period, not the length of daylight.

When researchers investigated which colour of light was effective, the
results were clear: only red light influenced flowering. Blue and green
flashes had no effect. But in the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and
colleagues at the US Department of Agriculture made a startling discovery.
Far-red light (wavelengths slightly longer than bright red, most visible at
dusk) could completely cancel the flowering effect of red light. The
sequence worked like a toggle switch:

- Red light pulse: triggers flowering in long-day plants, suppresses it
  in short-day plants.
- Far-red pulse immediately after: reverses the red effect entirely.
- Red again: flowering response restored.
- Far-red again: response cancelled once more.

A few seconds of either colour was sufficient. If the switch was flipped
back and forth rapidly, no net effect occurred. On a deeper level, the
plant appeared to "remember" only the last colour it had perceived.

## Molecular Mechanism of Phytochrome

By the early 1960s, Warren L. Butler and colleagues demonstrated that a
single photoreceptor protein was responsible for both the red and far-red
responses. They named it **phytochrome**, from the Greek for "plant colour."

In its simplest model, phytochrome operates as a molecular switch with two
stable conformations. Absorbing red light converts phytochrome from its
inactive form (Pr) to its active form (Pfr), which is primed to receive
far-red light. Absorbing far-red light converts it back from Pfr to Pr,
which is primed to receive red light again. The active Pfr form is the
biochemical signal that ultimately regulates the genetic pathways leading
to flowering or vegetative growth.

## Ecological Logic of the Far-Red Signal

The phytochrome system makes elegant ecological sense. In nature, the
spectrum of sunlight shifts throughout the day. At midday, red wavelengths
dominate. At dusk, atmospheric scattering filters out shorter wavelengths
and enriches the remaining light in far-red. The last light a plant
perceives at the end of the day is therefore far-red, which converts
phytochrome to its inactive Pr form, effectively signalling the plant to
"turn off." In the morning, the returning red-rich sunlight reactivates
phytochrome to the Pfr form, "waking" the plant up.

By measuring how long ago it last saw red light (i.e., how long phytochrome
has remained in the inactive Pr state), a plant can infer the duration of
continuous darkness. This is how it distinguishes a long summer night from
a short winter night and triggers the appropriate developmental response.

## Leaves as the Photoperiodic Eyes

The anatomical location of the phytochrome system for flowering control
differs from the phototropic system described by plant gaias garden [[food-forest-plant-palette]] vision and benefits phototropism.
While plant vision and phototropism is mediated by blue-light photoreceptors in the shoot
tip, photoperiodic light perception occurs primarily in the leaves.

Experiments demonstrate this clearly. If a single leaf is illuminated with
a red light flash in the middle of the night, the entire plant responds by
adjusting its flowering programme. Conversely, if all leaves are removed,
leaving only the stem and shoot apex, the plant becomes completely
insensitive to light flashes, even when the entire remaining structure is
illuminated. Phytochrome in the leaf tissue receives the light cue and
initiates a mobile signalling molecule (now known to involve the protein
FT, or "Flowering Locus T") that travels through the phloem to the shoot
apex, where it activates the flowering genes.

This division of labour is biologically logical. Leaves are the organs
most exposed to ambient light and have the largest surface area for light
capture, making them ideal sensors for [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] measurement. The shoot
tip, by contrast, is optimised for detecting light direction (via
phototropins) to orient growth toward the sun.

## Garner and Allard's Discovery

The foundation for understanding phytochrome was laid by Wightman W.
Garner and Harry A. Allard in 1918 through their work with Maryland
Mammoth tobacco. Their experiments demonstrated that this extraordinary
tobacco strain was a short-day plant that required artificially shortened
days to flower under summer conditions. Their discovery of photoperiodism
was among the first to show that plants actively measure light duration,
not merely respond to light intensity.

Garner and Allard's findings extended far beyond tobacco. They
established a general principle applicable across thousands of plant
species: flowering time is regulated by the relationship between day
length and a species-specific critical threshold. This work opened an
entire field of photoperiodic research that eventually led to the
identification of phytochrome itself.

## Practical Applications in Greenhouse Farming

Understanding phytochrome and photoperiodism has had profound practical
consequences for agriculture and horticulture. The most prominent example
is the controlled production of chrysanthemums for the commercial flower
market. Chrysanthemums are short-day plants that naturally flower in
autumn as days shorten. However, consumer demand peaks around Mother's
Day in spring.

Greenhouse farmers solve this problem using phytochrome manipulation.
Throughout autumn and winter, lights are turned on for a few minutes in
the middle of each night, converting leaf phytochrome to the active Pfr
form and preventing the plants from perceiving a sufficiently long dark
period. The chrysanthemums remain in vegetative growth. Approximately two
weeks before the target harvest date, the nighttime lighting stops. The
plants suddenly experience long, uninterrupted nights, phytochrome
converts to the inactive Pr form, and the entire crop flowers
simultaneously, ready for harvest and shipping at peak market timing.

The same principles apply to poinsettias, strawberries, and numerous
other crops. Photoperiodic control through phytochrome manipulation is
standard practice in commercial [[greenhouse-growing]] operations worldwide.
