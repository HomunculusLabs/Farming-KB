## The Spice Trade and Medieval Drug Scarcity

McKenna opens his analysis of the Age of Exploration with a provocative reframing: the spice trade was not merely about commerce or culinary preference but about a civilization starved for psychoactive variety and sensory stimulation.

The breakup of medieval Europe occurred not from political or religious forces alone but from an epidemic obsession with the new, the exotic, and the delightful, in short with consciousness-expanding substances.

## Broadening Taste as Acquired Process

Broadening of tastes or acquiring a taste is a learned process with both a psychological and biochemical component. On one hand, it entails overcoming the inertia of established habits that exclude the potential new food item as exotic, unfamiliar, or poisonous.

On the other hand, it involves adaptation to a chemically exotic substance. This process brings into action involuntary systems such as the immune system and [[mckenna-dominator-culture-and-suppression-of-ecstasy]] has never been more powerfully entrenched than in Christian Europe after the eclipse of the Roman Empire. Scarcely ever have human populations existed in such a prolonged situation of drug scarcity and lack of chemical stimulation.

Medieval Europe was one of the most constipated, neurotic, and woman-hating societies ever to exist. It was a society dying to escape from itself, obsessed with moral rectitude and sexual repression.

It was a society chained to the land, ruled by gouty, beef-eating men wearing dresses but suppressing women. Is it any wonder that dyes and spices became an absolute mania for medieval Europe?

Such was the strength of this mania that the arts of shipbuilding, navigation, banking, and trading all turned to serve the near-addiction that most Europeans felt for these things. Spices gave food a variety never known before. Dyes and exotic fabrics revolutionized fashion.

The desire for novelty was not merely aesthetic but existential. It spoke to a deep human need for sensory engagement that the medieval order had systematically suppressed in the name of spiritual purity and hierarchical control.

After weeks in the jungle, one's mind fills with plans for the restaurants to visit, the music to hear, the movies seen. The bewildering multiplicity of civilized life can be forgotten and then hungered for almost like withdrawal from a powerful drug.

Our own culture has seen a similar though more superficial trend in the rise of the yuppie craze for novelty and new exotic restaurants from ethnic to nouvelle. The pattern repeats itself across centuries.

As schoolchildren we are taught that the spice trade ended the Middle Ages and created the basis of modern trade and commerce. What we are not made aware of is that this breakup occurred as a result of an epidemic obsession with consciousness-expanding substances.

Drugs such as coffee, wormwood, and opium, dyes, silks, rare woods, gems, and even human beings were brought back to Europe and displayed almost like the plunder of a looted extraterrestrial civilization.

## Oriental Splendor and Social Transformation

The notion of oriental splendor, with its luxury, its sensuality, and its unexpectedly outre design motifs, acted to transform not only aesthetic conventions but canons of social behavior and individual self-image.

The names of cities of the silk road, such as Samarkand and Ecbatana, became mantras, bespeaking worlds of refinement and luxury previously associated only with paradise. These names carried the weight of an entire imaginative universe that medieval Europeans had been denied.

Social boundaries were dissolved. Old problems were seen in a new light. New secular classes emerged to challenge the power monopoly of popes and kings. The spice trade was, in effect, a cognitive revolution delivered through the medium of botanical commodities.

The Turkish capture of Constantinople in 1453 cut off traditional trade routes through the eastern Mediterranean. This crisis placed enormous pressure on the sciences of navigation and shipbuilding to perfect the ocean route to the East via the Cape of Africa, a route discovered by Vasco da Gama in 1498.

When Dutch and Portuguese navigators eventually reached the Moluccas, the Spice Islands, spices became much cheaper in Europe and the struggle among all parties to create monopolies was joined. The British East India Company, destined to become the most important, was founded in 1600.

## The Unconscious Drive to Symbiosis

There was a sudden acceleration of novelty and appearance of new social forms, the telltale tracks of a quantum leap forward in the power of the European imagination. Once again, pursuit of plants and the mental stimulation they induce was propelling experimentation with new social forms, new technologies, and a sudden expansion of language and imagination.
