## Solomon's Sprinkler Irrigation System Design

[[steve-solomon]] dedicates an entire chapter of "[[gardening-when-it-counts]]" to
sprinkler irrigation, providing practical guidance for designing effective
watering systems for home vegetable gardens. His approach prioritizes
simplicity, reliability, and water efficiency over complex automated systems.

## Why Sprinkler Irrigation?

While [[drip-irrigation]] has become fashionable, Solomon argues that properly
designed sprinkler systems offer several advantages [[soil-ph-management-for-vegetable-gardens]]:

- **Complete coverage**: Sprinklers water the entire soil surface, promoting
  even moisture distribution and supporting capillary rise throughout the bed
- **Lower maintenance**: Simple sprinkler systems have fewer components that
  can clog or fail compared to drip emitters
- **Frost protection**: Overhead sprinklers can protect crops from light
  frosts by continuously applying water during freezing temperatures
- **Foliar feeding compatibility**: Sprinklers can double as a delivery
  system for [[solomon-liquid-fertilizers-and-foliar-feeding]] sprays
- **Lower initial cost**: Basic sprinkler systems cost significantly less
  than drip irrigation setups

## System Components

### Water Source and Supply

The starting point is your water source. Solomon recommends calculating your
available flow rate (gallons per minute) before designing the system. Most
home garden taps deliver 3-8 GPM, which constrains how many sprinklers can
operate simultaneously.

Use the bucket test: time how long it takes to fill a 5-gallon bucket, then
divide 5 by the time in minutes to get your GPM.

### Main Line and Laterals

The main line carries water from the source to the garden area. For most home
gardens, 3/4-inch polyethylene pipe is sufficient. Laterals branch off to
individual sprinkler heads. Use quality fittings and avoid cheap connectors
that leak under pressure.

### Sprinkler Head Selection

Solomon categorizes garden sprinklers by their coverage pattern:

1. **Impact sprinklers**: Classic rotating heads covering large circular areas
   (15-40 foot radius). Best for large gardens with few obstacles.
2. **Rotary nozzles**: Multi-stream rotating heads providing uniform coverage.
   Lower flow rate per head allows more heads per zone.
3. **Fixed spray heads**: Stationary pattern heads for small areas or narrow
   beds. Useful for irregularly shaped garden sections.
4. **Oscillating sprinklers**: Fan-shaped coverage that sweeps back and forth.
   Simple and inexpensive but less uniform than rotary heads.

### Hose Bibs and Quick Connectors

Solomon recommends installing multiple hose bibs around the garden perimeter
to avoid dragging long hoses across planted areas. Use quick-connect fittings
to make moving sprinklers between positions fast and easy.

## Design Principles

### Head-to-Head Coverage

The cardinal rule: each sprinkler"s spray should reach the adjacent sprinkler
head. This ensures uniform overlap and prevents dry spots. Underlap creates
dry zones; too much overlap wastes water.

### Zoning by Flow Rate

Calculate how many sprinklers your supply supports simultaneously. If your
flow is 5 GPM and each head uses 1.5 GPM, run 3 heads per zone. Design zones
accordingly and operate them sequentially.

### Avoiding Wind Effects

Wind displaces water distribution, causing overwatering downwind and
underwatering upwind. Solomon recommends:

- Water early morning when wind is typically calmest
- Use lower trajectory heads in windy locations
- Consider windbreaks (hedges, fences) around the garden

### Soil Infiltration Rate

Apply water no faster than your soil can absorb it. Clay absorbs slowly
(0.1-0.3 in/hr); sand absorbs rapidly (1-2 in/hr). Exceeding infiltration
causes runoff and erosion. Use the "can test" — place straight-sided cans
around the zone and measure accumulation after 30 minutes.

## Watering Schedule

Solomon advocates deep, infrequent watering:

1. **Frequency**: Every 3-7 days depending on weather and soil type. Let
   the top few inches dry between waterings.
2. **Depth**: Apply enough to wet the root zone (12-18 inches for most
   vegetables). Typically 1-1.5 inches per application.
3. **Timing**: Early morning (4-7 AM). Minimizes evaporation, lets foliage
   dry before nightfall, and coincides with calm wind.
4. **Adjust by season**: Reduce frequency in cool weather, increase during
   hot spells. Skip irrigation after significant rainfall.

## Night Watering Myths

Solomon debunks the myth that night watering saves water. While less water
evaporates at night, the trade-offs are severe: wet foliage promotes fungal
diseases, slower drying encourages slug activity, and plants cannot use the
water immediately for growth during dark hours. The small savings are far
outweighed by increased disease risk.

## Maintenance

- **Winterize**: Drain all lines before first freeze to prevent cracking
- **Check heads**: Inspect patterns monthly; adjust or replace clogged heads
- **Monitor pressure**: Ensure system operates within design parameters
  (typically 30-50 PSI for residential systems)
- **Clean filters**: Install and regularly clean inline filters
