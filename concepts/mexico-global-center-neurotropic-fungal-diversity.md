## Historical Documentation

The earliest European records of neurotropic fungi come from Mexico. The pre-Columbian codices provide iconographic evidence of mushroom worship, most famously the "mushroom stone" of the classic Maya period (ca. 300-900 AD) and various Mixtec and Aztec depictions. Fray Bernardino de Sahagún's "Historia General de las Cosas de la Nueva España" (1560) described several kinds of inebriating mushrooms used by the Aztecs, who called them "teonanacatl" (flesh of the gods). The Spanish Inquisition eventually suppressed these practices, driving them underground.

The modern scientific study began with [[psilocybe-mexicana]] specimens collected near Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca. Wasson's famous Life magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" (1957) and subsequent book "Mushrooms, Russia and History" (1957) with his wife Valentina Pavlovna Wasson brought Mexican mushroom ceremonies to worldwide attention.

## Conservation Concerns

Several Mexican neurotropic fungi are threatened by habitat destruction, particularly the loss of mesophytic cloud forests and high-altitude pine forests. Species restricted to small geographical areas or specialized habitats ([[psilocybe-ovoidocystidiata]] in specific oak-pine associations) are especially vulnerable. The continuing deforestation of southern Mexico, particularly in Oaxaca, Chiapas, and Veracruz, threatens the world's richest concentration of neurotropic fungal diversity.

## Comparison with Other Biodiversity Hotspots

Mexico's preeminence in neurotropic fungal diversity can be contextualized by comparison with other important regions:

**South America**: While South America as a whole harbors more Psilocybe species than any other continent, no single country approaches Mexico's species count. Colombia ranks second globally with approximately 20 species, followed by Brazil with approximately 15 species. The Amazon basin, despite being the most biodiverse terrestrial ecosystem on Earth, has a surprisingly low documented diversity of neurotropic Psilocybe — most South American species occur in Andean and sub-Andean forests rather than lowland tropical rainforest.

**Southeast Asia**: Despite extensive tropical forests, Southeast Asia has far fewer documented neurotropic Psilocybe species than Mexico. Thailand, the best-studied country in the region, has approximately 5 confirmed species. The disparity likely reflects both genuine ecological differences and the far greater mycological exploration effort in Mexico.

**USA and Canada**: The Pacific Northwest (Oregon, Washington, British Columbia) is the richest area in temperate North America, with approximately 10 species including the potent [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]. Florida supports several subtropical species including [[psilocybe-muliercula]] — restricted to Oaxaca and Chiapas, used in Trique Indian ceremonies, belonging to Section Zapotecorum
- Psilocybe chiapanensis — endemic to Chiapas state, described from montane forests
- Psilocybe laurae — a rare species known from cloud forest habitats, extremely vulnerable to deforestation
- [[psilocybe-collybioides]] — named for its Collybia-like appearance, known only from Mexico
- Psilocybe herrerae — another Mexican endemic described by Guzmán
- Psilocybe mammillata — characterized by mammillate pileus surface features
- [[psilocybe-naematoliformis]] — originally described from Mexico, now also known from New Caledonia
- Psilocybe uxpanapensis — named for the Uxpanapa region of Veracruz
- Psilocybe weldenii — a tropical species found in Mexican lowland forests
- [[psilocybe-yungensis]] — while also found in South America, the Mexican populations are distinctive

These endemic species represent an irreplaceable component of global fungal biodiversity. Many are known from only one or a few collections, and their conservation status has never been formally assessed. The ongoing deforestation of southern Mexico's cloud forests and pine-oak woodlands threatens to drive some of these species to extinction before their ecology, chemistry, and distribution can be fully studied.

## Indigenous Ceremonial Traditions

Mexico is unique among countries with neurotropic fungal diversity in maintaining living indigenous ceremonial traditions involving these fungi. While ceremonial mushroom use has been documented among several Mexican indigenous groups, the Mazatec, Zapotec, Mixtec, and Chinantec peoples of Oaxaca are the best documented:

**Mazatec ceremonies**: The Mazatec people of the Sierra Mazateca in northern Oaxaca are the most widely known practitioners of mushroom ceremonies, largely due to the work of Wasson and Hofmann. Ceremonies typically take place at night in darkness, guided by a curandero or curandera (healer). The mushrooms are consumed in pairs, with specific numbers of pairs being ritually significant. The ceremony includes chanting, incense burning (using copal resin), and often the consultation of a specific mushroom designated as the "spirit" or "director" of the session.

**Zapotec and Mixtec traditions**: Less thoroughly documented than Mazatec practices, but known to involve nocturnal ceremonies in which mushrooms are consumed for divination, healing, and communal bonding. The Mixtec also incorporate gasteromycetes (puffballs) into their ceremonies alongside genuine neurotropic species.

**Trique ceremonies**: The Trique Indians of Alta Mixteca use [[dictyophora-indusiata]] for divination ceremonies. This stinkhorn fungus (three recognized forms) represents the most unusual sacred mushroom tradition in Mexico, as it belongs to Group 4 (sacred fungi without confirmed neurotropic chemistry).

These living traditions provide invaluable ethnobotanical context for understanding neurotropic fungi. Indigenous knowledge of species identification, seasonal timing, preparation methods, and ritual use complements scientific investigation and in some cases has led to the discovery of species previously unknown to science.
