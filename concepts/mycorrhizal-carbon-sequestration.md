# Mycorrhizal Carbon Sequestration

The mycorrhizal symbiosis involves reciprocal exchange of photosynthetically-fixed plant carbon for nitrogen or phosphorus, but the importance of mycorrhizal fungi as a significant component of the terrestrial carbon cycle has historically been overlooked. Over 80% of plant species invest substantial below-ground carbon flow into these fungal symbionts. The annual C flux through soil respiration is ten times greater than fossil fuel combustion, recycling ~10% of atmospheric CO₂. Roots and their associated mycorrhizas constitute the single most important component of this flux, and mycorrhizal contributions are likely among the most sensitive components to anthropogenic disturbance of both C and N cycles.

## Three Major Mycorrhizal Types and Their Biomes

The global distribution of soil carbon stocks is closely correlated with the dominant mycorrhizal types across biomes:

| Mycorrhiza Type | Key Biomes | C Sequestration Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Ericoid (ERM)** | Tundra, heathlands, boreal understory | Highest — peat and raw humus accumulation |
| **Ectomycorrhizal (EM)** | Boreal forest, temperate coniferous & deciduous forest | High — boreal forest = world's largest vegetation system |
| **Arbuscular (AM)** | Temperate grasslands, tropical savannas and rainforests | Lower — faster decomposition, less soil C accumulation |

Heathland and boreal forest regions together cover ~70% of the vegetated land mass of the Northern Hemisphere and contain the majority of the world's soil carbon stocks, mainly in peat and raw humus.

## Direct Carbon Inputs to Soil

### Ectomycorrhizal Carbon Fluxes

EM fungi receive 10–30% of net photosynthate in many forest ecosystems. Key findings:

- EM entirely sheath root tips (>90% symbiotic at any time), so virtually all labile C from living roots in EM-dominated forests passes through fungi
- EM mycelium accounts for **~32% of soil microbial biomass** in boreal forest (based on girdling experiments; Högberg & Högberg, 2002)
- At least **50% of soil respiration** and half of dissolved organic C in soil solution is attributable to EM mycelium plus roots
- External EM mycelium production: **420–590 kg ha⁻¹ year⁻¹** (Wallander et al., 2004)
- EM mycelium constitutes **>70% of total soil microbial biomass** in some Swedish forests
- Approximately **80% of EM biomass is extra-radical mycelium** (not the sheath on roots)

### Ericoid Mycorrhizal Carbon Fluxes

- ERM fungal coils typically occupy 10–80% of hair root volume
- ¹⁴C pulse-labelling of subarctic tundra shows marked seasonality, with mycorrhizal hair roots as the dominant C sink during autumn
- Approximately **3–10% of total ¹⁴C assimilated** was found in hair roots; ~50% of C reaching hair roots passes to the fungus
- At seasonal peak, mycorrhizal allocation accounts for up to **4.8% of net fixation** (likely an underestimate)
- Ergosterol (fungal sterol) concentrations in hair roots correlate strongly with ecosystem net C fixation rates one week later, indicating fungal C demand is large enough to be limited when photosynthesis is suboptimal

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Carbon Fluxes

Early studies dramatically underestimated AM C fluxes. Current understanding:

- Up to **16% of AM mycelial C content is replaced within 24 hours** by new assimilate from a 5-hour labelling (Staddon et al., 2003)
- AM mycelia make up **~50% of fungal length** in soil under herbaceous plants (Rillig et al., 2002)
- AM constitutes **20–30% of total soil microbial biomass** in temperate grassland
- External AM mycelium receives **>9% of net C fixation** in permanent grassland in summer
- Most C passes through AM hyphae within the first 3 days after photosynthesis
- Including C allocated to internal (root) mycelium adds another **4–10% of net fixation**
- Respiratory release from AM hyphae peaks at **12–16 hours** after plant pulse-labelling

## Carbon Quality and Persistence

### EM Carbon Sequestration Mechanisms

The quality and persistence of mycorrhizal C inputs determines the extent of sequestration:

- EM mycelium has unusually **high C:N ratios (18.5–21.9)** — similar to or greater than Norway spruce forest organic matter — limiting decomposition rates
- Many EM fungi produce **robust hydrophobic multicellular hyphal cords** persistent for months
- Heavily **melanized hyphae and sclerotia** (e.g., *Cenococcum geophilum*) decay very slowly and are directly implicated in soil C sequestration
- EM mycelia are better **defended against fungivory** than saprotrophs, through melanin, polyphenolic compounds, and specific toxins (some EM fungi kill fungivorous microarthropods)
- Mycorrhizal colonization resulted in **30% lower root decomposition rates** compared with non-mycorrhizal roots (Langley & Hungate, 2003)
- EM fungi secrete nutrient-mobilizing organic acids (oxalic, citric acid); calcium oxalate forms extensive crystalline deposits coating hyphae and rhizomorphs

### AM Carbon Sequestration: Glomalin

AM fungi contribute uniquely to C sequestration through **glomalin**, a hydrophobic protein secreted into soil:

- Glomalin plays a major role in **soil macroaggregate formation** (>2 mm diameter)
- Residence time of **6–42 years** in soil
- Contributes **up to 15% of total stable soil organic carbon** in grasslands (Miller & Kling, 2000)
- Contributes **4–5%** of total stable SOC in tropical rainforest soils (Rillig et al., 2001)
- Organic matter entrapped inside macroaggregates is more biologically and chemically stabilized than bulk soil organic matter
- AM hyphae have highly thickened cell walls in main arterial hyphae, making them less palatable to collembolans

## Indirect Effects on Carbon Cycling

### ERM and EM: Suppressing Decomposition

Both ERM and EM create positive feedback loops that promote C sequestration:

- **Nutrient competition**: ERM and EM monopolize labile organic nutrients, restricting supplies to saprotrophic decomposer communities — the very nutrients that limit decomposer activity
- **Selective nutrient removal**: Mycorrhizas remove N and P from organic matter without fully decomposing lignocellulose, increasing C:N and C:P ratios of residues and enhancing their recalcitrance
- **Antagonism with saprotrophs**: EM mycelia can territorially exclude wood-decay saprotrophs — the organisms best adapted for decomposing lignocellulose. Antagonistic interactions between mycorrhizal and saprotrophic mycelia can further slow organic matter decomposition
- **Soil drying**: EM water extraction from soil reduces litter decomposition rates
- **Organic N uptake**: By using amino acids and proteins directly, EM short-circuits ammonification and nitrification pathways, resulting in very low inorganic N release
- **Plant litter quality**: ERM host plants (Ericaceae) produce extremely recalcitrant litter — highly enriched in polyphenolics, low-molecular-mass phenolics, and aliphatic acids that are fungistatic and fungitoxic, with extremely high C:N ratios

### AM: Soil Aggregation Effects

AM fungi promote C sequestration differently — primarily through soil stabilization rather than decomposition suppression:

- AM plants produce less recalcitrant litter with higher N content that decomposes relatively quickly
- Less C accumulates in soils under AM plants compared with ERM/EM-dominated biomes
- AM effects operate through **macroaggregate stabilization** via hyphal networks and glomalin secretions
- No evidence of strong antagonisms between AM fungi and saprotrophs

## Mycorrhizal Networks and Plant Communities

### Mycoheterotrophy

The magnitude of C flux through mycorrhizal networks has driven the evolution of plants that exploit fungi as their sole carbon source:

- Over **400 achlorophyllous plant species** (mycoheterotrophs) depend exclusively on fungal C across ~90 genera
- ~10% of all plant species depend on fungal C exploitation during at least part of their life cycle
- Orchidaceae (c. 17,500 species) — nearly half of all mycoheterotrophs — require fungal C for germination and establishment
- Mycoheterotrophs exploit both EM and AM fungi that co-infect roots of adjacent green plants
- Isotopic signatures (¹⁵N and ¹³C enrichment) have revealed previously unrecognized partial mycoheterotrophy in green orchids

### Network Effects on Plant Competition

- Mycorrhizal networks interconnect roots of different species, creating potential for C and nutrient transfer
- Grasses with extensive fibrous root systems may disproportionately support network C costs while forbs benefit most
- The almost complete monopoly of labile organic nutrients by ERM and EM in heathland/boreal ecosystems suppresses growth of AM-associated species, contributing to global biome distribution patterns
- C costs and benefits of mycorrhizal networks are fungus-specific and not shared equally among plants

## Implications for Carbon Cycle Models

Current ecosystem C flux models typically omit or significantly underestimate mycorrhizal contributions:

- Standard substrate-induced respiration techniques systematically discriminate against mycorrhizal biomass (mycelium fragments lose plant C supply, reducing respiration by 60–95%)
- Grassland C flux models assume only root exudation, sloughed cells, and dead roots contribute plant-fixed C to soil
- Farmland food-web diagrams often assume only saprotrophs are significant fungal components
- The mycorrhizal contribution is both **chemically distinct** (chitin vs. cellulose cell walls, melanin vs. lignin) and **quantitatively important** — it can no longer be ignored

The distinct δ¹³C signatures of mycorrhizal vs. saprotrophic fungi (mycorrhizal symbionts are much closer to host plant values) confirm that these represent functionally different C pathways. Mycorrhizal fungi add new C to soil rather than merely redistributing existing detrital stocks.
