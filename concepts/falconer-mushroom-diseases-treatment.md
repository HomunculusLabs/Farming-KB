# [[falconer-mushroom-cultivation-history]] Diseases and Their Treatment

Chapter XIX of Falconer's treatise, titled "Insect and Other Enemies,"
includes both insect pests and diseases. The diseases he described --
fogging off, black spot, and flock -- were the principal afflictions of
cultivated mushrooms in the 1890s.

## Fogging Off

The commonest ailment peculiar to cultivated mushrooms. Consisted in the
softening, shriveling, and perishing of part of the young mushrooms,
which assumed a brownish color. Affected mushrooms did not occur singly
but in patches; generally all or nearly all very small mushrooms in a
clump would turn brown and soft with no hope of recovery.

### Causes

Falconer was convinced that fogging off was due to destruction of the
mycelium threads supporting the mushrooms -- "a disease of the root."
Evidence: healthy contiguous clumps might be perfectly fine while a
neighboring clump was entirely withered. Contributing causes included:

- Keeping the bed continuously wet or dry
- Drip from above
- Abruptly pulling up large mushrooms, destroying [[mycelium]] threads
  supporting nearby small mushrooms
- Displacement of earth by large mushrooms pushing through, severing
  [[mycelium]]
- Cutting off mushrooms and leaving stumps in the ground -- the stumps
  developed a white fluffy substance that poisoned every thread of
  [[mycelium]] leading to it, affecting all attached mushrooms present and
  to come
- Any impure matter in the bed contacting the [[mycelium]]

Lachaume identified two beetle larvae (Aphodius fimetarius and
Dermestes tessellatus) that "cause great damage by eating the spawn,
thereby breaking up the reproductive filaments," causing fogging off.

### Prevention

- Always pull mushrooms by the root rather than cutting, to avoid leaving
  stumps
- If stumps were left, scoop them out before decay sets in and fill holes
  with fresh loam
- Avoid disturbing the bed surface unnecessarily
- Keep beds at proper moisture -- neither continuously wet nor dry

## Black Spot

All mushroom growers were familiar with this disease, appearing as dark
brown spots, streaks, or freckles on top of mushroom caps, increasing
with age.

### Cause

Caused by eel worms (Anguillula), confirmed by Dr. W.G. Farlow,
Professor of Cryptogamic Botany at Harvard, and Dr. Thomas Taylor,
microscopist to the U.S. Department of Agriculture. The minute creatures
entered mushrooms in their tiniest pin form, before emerging from the
ground. If a button arose clean it remained clean; if diseased, it
continued diseased. If one mushroom in a clump had black spot, every
mushroom in that clump usually had it -- but mushrooms from the same
spawn piece an inch or two away might be perfectly clean.

### Seasonal Pattern

- Very little between October and March (perhaps 1% affected)
- Increased as spring opened
- Up to 20% affected by May
- Never occurred in new beds; seldom in vigorously bearing beds
- Generally appeared in beds bearing for some weeks or declining

### Control Measures

1. **Rigid cleanliness**: Keep everything in and about the mushroom houses
   clean.
2. **Remove spent beds promptly**: As soon as a bed ceased to bear a crop
   worth picking, clear it out, lime-wash the place it occupied, and make
   a new bed.
3. **No accumulation**: Observe that no old loam or manure accumulates;
   no green scum on boards, paths, or walls.
4. **Boiling water with alum**: Poured over boards, walls, and scum-covered
   surfaces to kill eel worms. Not allowed to touch bearing beds.
5. **During gathering**: Remove every vestige of old stump and fogged-off
   mushrooms; keep holes filled with fresh loam.
6. **Salt solution**: After a fortnight of bearing, sprinkle with salt
   solution; next day, topdress with half-inch of finely sifted fresh loam,
   firmed with the back of the hand.
7. **Scalding water**: Dr. Taylor recommended sprinkling scalding water
   over the entire bed surface (especially near the boxing) before buttons
   appear, penetrating no more than 1/8 inch below surface.

### Wholesomeness

Falconer did not consider black spot unwholesome -- he had never known
ill effects from eating it. The spotted parts were merely flavorless and
tasteless, but the disease was disgusting and no one would knowingly eat
eel worms with their mushrooms.

## Flock

The worst of all mushroom diseases, and common wherever mushrooms were
grown artificially. Falconer had known it for twenty-five years.

### Nature

Caused by other fungi infesting the gills and frills, rendering them a
hard, flocky mass. Sometimes affected mushrooms preserved their white
skin, color, and normal form; at other times the cap became distorted.
Caused by one or more saprophytic and parasitic fungi of lowly origin --
thread-fungi whose individual threads were so minute as to be scarcely
visible to the naked eye. Generally two or more species were present
simultaneously, their multiplied crossing and interweaving producing the
whitish felted mass.

### Spread Pattern

Notably, flock did NOT spread contagiously through the bed. If one
mushroom from a piece of spawn was affected, every mushroom from that
piece was affected, but not one mushroom from adjacent pieces of spawn --
even if their [[mycelium]] formed an interlacing web. Other pieces from the
same brick were not more liable than pieces from other bricks.

### Frequency

In a bed 3 x 30 feet over two months' bearing: as few as five or as
many as fifty flocky mushrooms. Sometimes appearing among the earliest
mushrooms, but generally not until the bed had borne a week or two. The
disease was not caused by debility -- it attacked healthy, robust
mushrooms and debilitated ones indiscriminately.
