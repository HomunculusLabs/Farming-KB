## Passive Solar Design

Passive solar design uses building orientation, window placement, [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]], and overhangs to heat and cool a building without mechanical systems. By working with the sun's seasonal path, a well-designed passive solar building can reduce heating costs by 50-80% and cooling costs by 30-50%. This approach is foundational for any off-grid or energy-efficient homestead.

## Solar Principles

The sun's position changes seasonally:
- Winter: Sun rises south-southeast, arcs low across the southern sky, sets south-southwest
- Summer: Sun rises north-northeast, arcs high overhead, sets north-northwest
- Equinox: Sun rises due east, passes directly overhead at noon (at the equator), sets due west

In the Northern Hemisphere, south-facing walls receive the most winter sun and can be shaded from summer sun with properly designed overhangs.

## The Five Elements of Passive Solar Design

### 1. Orientation

The long axis of the building should face within 15 degrees of true south (not magnetic south — adjust for declination in your area). This maximizes winter solar gain on the south facade.

- South-facing: Maximum glazing for winter heating
- North-facing: Minimal glazing (just for daylight), well-insulated wall
- East-facing: Moderate glazing for morning light and warmth
- West-facing: Minimal glazing (afternoon sun causes overheating in summer)

### 2. Glazing (Windows)

**South-facing windows:**
- Collect solar energy during winter
- Typical area: 40-60% of south wall as glass
- Double or triple-glazed windows (R-2 to R-5)
- Low-emissivity (low-E) coatings: Low-E with high solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) on south windows

**Window specifications by climate:**
- [[avalanche-phenomenon]] (Zone 5-7): Triple-glazed, argon-filled, SHGC 0.50+
- Moderate climates (Zone 3-5): Double-glazed, argon-filled, SHGC 0.45+
- Warm climates (Zone 1-3): Double-glazed, low SHGC 0.30 or less

**Thermal performance:**
- Single-pane window: R-1
- Double-pane: R-2
- Double-pane with low-E: R-3
- Triple-pane with low-E and argon: R-5

### 3. Thermal Mass

Thermal mass stores heat from the sun during the day and releases it slowly at night. It moderates temperature swings and prevents overheating.

**Materials with high thermal mass:**
- Concrete slab floors: R-value per inch is low, but mass is excellent
- Stone: Granite, limestone, sandstone
- Adobe and compressed earth block
- [[cob-building-basics-detailed|Cob]] walls: Excellent thermal mass in a [[natural-building]]
- Water: The highest volumetric heat capacity of any common material
- [[comparison-lime-plaster-vs-earthen-plaster]] over straw bale: Moderate mass

**Placement:**
- Directly behind south-facing windows (sun must hit the mass)
- Floor slabs: Most common — sun heats the floor during the day
- Interior walls: Trombe walls (masonry wall behind glass)
- Direct gain: Sun enters windows and hits mass directly
- Indirect gain: Sun heats an attached sunspace that transfers heat to the main building

**Sizing thermal mass:**
- Rule of thumb: 4-6 square feet of 4-inch-thick masonry per square foot of south-facing glazing
- Too little mass: Overheating during the day, cold at night
- Too much mass: Takes too long to heat, never reaches comfortable temperature

### 4. Overhangs and Shading

Fixed overhangs are calculated to block summer sun while admitting winter sun.

**Overhang calculation:**
- Measure the angle of the sun at summer solstice noon (highest point) and winter solstice noon (lowest point)
- Design the overhang to shade the window completely at summer solstice noon
- Design the overhang to allow full sun at winter solstice noon

**Approximate overhang depths:**
- Latitude 35 degrees: 1.5-2 foot overhang for standard 8-foot windows
- Latitude 40 degrees: 2-2.5 foot overhang
- Latitude 45 degrees: 2.5-3 foot overhang
- Latitude 50 degrees: 3-3.5 foot overhang

**Adjustable shading options:**
- Exterior awnings: Retractable for seasonal adjustment
- Deciduous trees: Leaf out in summer for shade, drop leaves in winter for sun
- Vine-covered pergolas on south side
- Interior blinds: Less effective (heat already entered the building)

### 5. Insulation and Air Sealing

Even the best passive solar design fails without proper insulation and air sealing. Heat collected by passive solar must be retained.

**Insulation recommendations by climate:**
- Walls: R-20 to R-40 (higher [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]])
- Ceiling/Roof: R-40 to R-60 (heat rises — roof insulation is critical)
- Floor: R-20 to R-30 (especially over unheated crawlspaces)
- Foundation: R-10 to R-20 (frost-protected shallow foundations)

**Air sealing:**
- Blower door test: Target 3 ACH50 or less (air changes per hour at 50 Pascals)
- Seal all penetrations: electrical, plumbing, vents
- Caulk window and door frames
- Use house wrap (Tyvek) as air barrier
- Gaskets under sill plates

## Passive Cooling Strategies

### Natural Ventilation

- Operable windows on opposite sides create cross-ventilation
- Stack effect: Low inlet windows on the cool side, high outlet windows on the warm side
- Thermal chimneys: Enclosed shafts that heat up and draw air upward
- Clerestory windows: High windows that release hot air

### Thermal Mass Cooling

- Open windows at night to cool the thermal mass
- Close windows during the day — the cool mass absorbs heat from the interior
- This "night flushing" strategy works well in climates with cool nights

### Shading

- Prevent solar gain with properly sized overhangs
- Deciduous trees shade east and west walls
- Exterior shade screens block sun while allowing airflow
- Light-colored roof reflects [[albedo-and-solar-radiation]]

## Design for Specific Building Types

### [[tiny-house-on-wheels|Tiny Houses]]

- Maximize south-facing windows relative to wall area
- Heavy floor or a water-filled thermal mass container
- Minimal north-facing glazing
- Overhangs sized for your latitude
- Insulation priority: Tiny houses have high surface-to-volume ratio

### [[earthbag-building|Earthbag Structures]]

- Thick earth walls provide excellent thermal mass
- Small, well-placed windows on south side
