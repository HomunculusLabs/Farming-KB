# Garden Irrigation Systems

Garden irrigation in [[water-conservation]]** as the highest priority. Unguarded or unsheltered plots can waste up to 10% more water through edge evaporation alone. The most effective dryland garden watering methods are **trickle, drip, or seepage irrigation**, applied at whatever level of technology is appropriate for the context.

## Drip Irrigation Systems

[[humus]] pit waters a circle of vegetables through slow seepage.

### Home Slot-Pipe System

The top of the pipe (6-5 cm diameter) has slots cut at one level, with nylon mesh or old stockings bound around them. Wastewater is led in from handbasins or sinks past a grease trap or crude sieve. These are excellent for small-fruit beds, with slots at correct spacing for plants. Pipes should be laid to one side of plants for periodic checking, as they eventually block up.

## Nutrient Film and Injection Techniques

Several techniques prevent water loss by soil absorption; both are essentially hydroponic-intensive:

- **Nutrient drip** (Israel): Direct infusion of water and nutrients into tree or vine stems.
- **Plastic film technique** (India): Used to grow Napier grass (*Pennisetum*) for forage, with part of the root mass in earth and part in a nutrient solution in plastic gullies.

**Wick irrigation** uses slotted pipes with hessian or nylon wicks extending to nearby plant roots, minimizing water loss to soil while allowing roots to explore both soil and nutrient solution.

## Automatic Irrigation

In areas where electronic sensors are impractical or expensive, mechanical apparatus can permit automatic watering and self-regulation during rain. Rain automatically switches off the tap after approximately 3 cm of downpour, ensuring irrigation is sufficient without waste.

## Sea or Brackish Water Irrigation

Extremely salty seawater can be evaporated under plastic to provide root-zone water to plants via a sand and peat layer. This approach is warranted only in extreme conditions in arid soils where no fresh water can be spared.
