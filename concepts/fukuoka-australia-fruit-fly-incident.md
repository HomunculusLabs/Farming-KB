# Australia Fruit Fly Incident

updated: 2026-04-18

In April 1984, [[masanobu-fukuoka]] found himself at the center of a
national agricultural emergency when Queensland fruit fly maggots were
discovered in fruit given to him as a gift by an Australian visitor at
his farm in Iyo, Ehime Prefecture, Shikoku.

## The Discovery

On April 17, 1984, an Australian acquaintance visited Fukuoka,
traveled to his hilltop orchard, and stayed overnight. The next day
he gave Fukuoka three guavas as a present. When Fukuoka later cut
open the fruit, he found twenty to thirty small white worms about one
to two millimeters long in each fruit.

Having worked years earlier as a plant inspector at Yokohama Customs,
Fukuoka immediately recognized these as fruit fly maggots. He sent the
fruit to the plant inspection divisions at customs in Kobe and
Yokohama. A few hours later, he received a call confirming that these
were indeed fruit flies -- specifically the Queensland fruit fly
(Bactrocera tryoni).

## The National Emergency

When the news that a fruit fly had entered Japan was broadcast over
the national networks the next day, April 21, it created an uproar.
Tense directives went out from the Agriculture Ministry's Plant
Protection Division to all parts of the country. Two head inspectors
from Kobe Customs arrived immediately at Fukuoka's farm with a top
local official. Over the next ten days, they worked night and day
trying to track down the Australian visitor and combing Fukuoka's
orchard for signs of the fruit fly.

About fifty monitor traps were set up in [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]] and huts near
where the Australian had spent the night. The danger was that, given
another ten days, the maggots would emerge as adults. Once they had
taken wing, it would be too late to do anything.

## The Multiplication Threat

Fukuoka pointed out the staggering multiplication potential: in the
space of a single month, a single fruit fly can become a thousand
flies. In two months, these can multiply to ten million, and in three
months, to an essentially infinite number.

The Queensland fruit fly feeds on most other fruits and causes up to
one hundred percent damage. Japan bans the import of all fruits from
Australia except pineapples and unripened bananas, which the fruit fly
does not attack.

## Resolution

On the tenth day, just about the time the maggots would emerge, the
Australian visitor presented himself at the customs station in
Shimonoseki. At last Fukuoka and the authorities were able to get
some details and things settled down. Because the maggots had not yet
emerged and temperatures remained low, there seemed little danger of
the fruit fly spreading.

## Japan: A Country Without Fruit Flies

The incident highlighted a remarkable fact that surprised foreigners.
People in Europe, Africa, Australia, and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]] found it almost
a miracle that there is a country on earth without fruit flies. They
said that it is normal to find worms in fruit, and some just cut out
the affected part and throw it away while others eat the fruit, worm
and all, figuring it is nutrition.

An Australian visitor told Fukuoka that in Australia, fruit growers are
legally required to spray pesticides, creating a dependence on
pesticides that is the start of the destruction of nature. He asked:
"Why is it that in a country without fruit flies, the farmers spray
pesticides and destroy nature?"
