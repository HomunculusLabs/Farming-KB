## Magic Mushrooms in Ancient Mesoamerican Civilization

updated: 2026-04-24

Magic mushrooms played a significant role in the religious, social, and
political life of the Aztec civilisation and other Mesoamerican cultures
long before the arrival of Spanish conquistadors. The Spanish chroniclers
of the sixteenth century provide the most detailed accounts, though these
are inevitably filtered through the religious and imperialist ideology
of their authors.

## Teonanacatl: God's Flesh

The mushrooms were known in the local Nahuatl language as teonanacatl,
meaning "God's flesh." This terminology, with its resonances of Christian
communion, caused particular offence to Spanish friars, who saw the
practice as diabolical. Bernardino de Sahagun, the Franciscan friar who
spent some sixty years among the indigenous populations, compiled the
most comprehensive account in his Florentine Codex. He recorded that the
mushrooms grew in grassland, fields, moors and waste ground, had a round
cap and thin stem, and were so bitter as to hurt the tongue. Only two or
three were eaten at one sitting.

According to Sahagun, the mushrooms caused palpitations of the heart,
excited lust, and induced laughter and tears in equal measure. At "the
hour of blowing conches and flutes," tequila would be drunk and mushrooms
eaten, with honey taken to sweeten the bitterness. After dancing and
weeping, bemushroomed participants would have visions in which they
might see their destiny, or even the manner of their death, before
falling into a stupor. Later, they would discuss the meaning of what
they had seen -- a scene familiar to [[amanita-muscaria-feathered-serpent-phoenix]] god Quetzalcoatl clutching mushrooms.
The Lienzo de Zacatepec shows a man with mushrooms in his hair, while
another codex depicts a man eating mushrooms with a supernatural figure
behind him, possibly Mictlantecuhtli, the Aztec Lord of the Underworld.

Throughout [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], approximately three hundred stone and
pottery "mushroom" effigies have been uncovered, originating mainly in
the highlands of Guatemala. These sculptures, about a foot high and
free-standing, are mushroom-shaped with a stem and domed cap, often
depicting a human, animal or natural figure squatting at the base. The
earliest figures date from the pre-classic period, the first millennium
BCE; the latest from the late classic period. Given the historical
evidence and the discovery of other figurines depicting ecstatic-looking
humans with mushrooms, it is extremely likely that these figures were
connected with mushroom consumption, suggesting the practice extended
back not just hundreds but thousands of years.
