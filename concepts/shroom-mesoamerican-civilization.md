# Magic Mushrooms in Ancient Mesoamerican Civilization

updated: 2026-04-24

Magic mushrooms played a significant role in the religious, social, and
political life of the Aztec civilisation and other Mesoamerican cultures
long before the arrival of Spanish conquistadors. The Spanish chroniclers
of the sixteenth century provide the most detailed accounts, though these
are inevitably filtered through the religious and imperialist ideology
of their authors.

## Teonanacatl: God's Flesh

The mushrooms were known in the local Nahuatl language as teonanacatl,
meaning "God's flesh." This terminology, with its resonances of Christian
communion, caused particular offence to Spanish friars, who saw the
practice as diabolical. Bernardino de Sahagun, the Franciscan friar who
spent some sixty years among the indigenous populations, compiled the
most comprehensive account in his Florentine Codex. He recorded that the
mushrooms grew in grassland, fields, moors and waste ground, had a round
cap and thin stem, and were so bitter as to hurt the tongue. Only two or
three were eaten at one sitting.

According to Sahagun, the mushrooms caused palpitations of the heart,
excited lust, and induced laughter and tears in equal measure. At "the
hour of blowing conches and flutes," tequila would be drunk and mushrooms
eaten, with honey taken to sweeten the bitterness. After dancing and
weeping, bemushroomed participants would have visions in which they
might see their destiny, or even the manner of their death, before
falling into a stupor. Later, they would discuss the meaning of what
they had seen -- a scene familiar to modern mushroom enthusiasts.

## Feasts, Revelations, and Diplomacy

Several sixteenth-century accounts describe mushroom use at the highest
social levels. The Dominican friar Diego Duran (1537-1588) wrote that at
the coronation of the Aztec sovereign Tizoc in 1481, inebriating
mushrooms were provided for the guests. Aztec royalty rarely consumed
alcohol but preferred mushrooms which they ate raw, "with which they
would grow merry and become somewhat tipsy." Moctezuma II, the last
Aztec ruler conquered by Cortes, employed priests who consumed mushrooms
for divinatory purposes, especially to predict the outcome of battles.

Moctezuma also appeased his traditional enemies with a mushroom feast,
the "Feast of Revelations," which may have originated at his coronation
in 1502. Spies from a rival tribe were captured, pardoned, and plied
with mushrooms. According to one account, "they all lost their senses
and ended up in a state worse than if they had drunk much wine; so drunk
and senseless were they that many of them took their own lives."

## Codices and Mushroom Stones

Mesoamerican codices, the indigenous texts written in pictographic
language, portray mushrooms. The Codex Vindobonensis, a Mixtec work
dealing with the mythological origins of the world, shows several gods
including the feathered serpent god Quetzalcoatl clutching mushrooms.
The Lienzo de Zacatepec shows a man with mushrooms in his hair, while
another codex depicts a man eating mushrooms with a supernatural figure
behind him, possibly Mictlantecuhtli, the Aztec Lord of the Underworld.

Throughout Central America, approximately three hundred stone and
pottery "mushroom" effigies have been uncovered, originating mainly in
the highlands of Guatemala. These sculptures, about a foot high and
free-standing, are mushroom-shaped with a stem and domed cap, often
depicting a human, animal or natural figure squatting at the base. The
earliest figures date from the pre-classic period, the first millennium
BCE; the latest from the late classic period. Given the historical
evidence and the discovery of other figurines depicting ecstatic-looking
humans with mushrooms, it is extremely likely that these figures were
connected with mushroom consumption, suggesting the practice extended
back not just hundreds but thousands of years.

## The Colonial Eradication

The Spanish invaders demonised indigenous psychoactive plant use,
attributing curative and divinatory powers to the work of the Devil.
They began imposing Christian, "civilising" values upon the pagans. The
Franciscan friar Toribio de Benavente described the Aztecs eating
bitter-tasting mushrooms sweetened with honey, claiming the mushrooms
merely sharpened the Aztecs' "already savage cruelty." The cleric
Jacinto de la Serna, in the seventeenth century, described a mushroom
ceremony performed by an Indian named Juan Chichiten ("little dog"), who
celebrated a saint's day with a solemn mushroom vigil "after the manner
of Communion," so that all present "went out of their heads."

After the colonial period, knowledge of the mushrooms was to remain
unrecorded for another four hundred years, until the early twentieth
century discovery that indigenous Mexicans were still using mushrooms in
ways reminiscent of Chichiten's all-night syncretic religious
observances.
