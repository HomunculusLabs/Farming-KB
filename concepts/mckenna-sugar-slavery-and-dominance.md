## Sugar, Slavery, and the Rise of Dominator Culture

## The Sugar Revolution

In Food of the Gods, McKenna identified sugar as the drug that powered the rise of Western dominator culture. Sugar is, he argued, the perfect drug for a society built on hierarchy, exploitation, and endless growth: it provides a cheap, reliable energy rush that fuels labor without expanding consciousness. Unlike psychedelic mushrooms, which dissolve boundaries and promote egalitarianism, sugar reinforces the status quo by keeping workers energized but compliant.

The sugar trade, beginning in the 15th century with Portuguese plantations on Madeira and expanding to Brazil and the Caribbean, was the engine of the Atlantic slave trade. McKenna traced the connections between sugar, slavery, and the consolidation of European imperial power with grim precision. The wealth extracted from sugar plantations — built on the forced labor of millions of enslaved Africans — funded the Industrial Revolution and the global expansion of European capitalism.

The scale of this economic transformation is difficult to overstate. By the 18th century, sugar was the single most valuable commodity in European trade, surpassing even precious metals. Caribbean sugar colonies such as Saint-Domingue (now Haiti) and Jamaica were among the most profitable territories on Earth. The capital accumulated from sugar — alongside cotton and tobacco — financed the banks, insurance companies, and infrastructure projects that launched the Industrial Revolution. McKenna argued that this historical chain of causation means that modern Western prosperity is, at its foundation, built on the exploitation of both enslaved labor and the addictive properties of a psychoactive substance.

## Sugar as Drug

McKenna"s classification of sugar as a "drug" is central to his argument. From a pharmacological perspective, sugar triggers dopamine release in the brain's reward circuits, producing a brief euphoria followed by a crash that demands more. This cycle of craving and consumption is, McKenna argued, functionally identical to the cycles seen with addictive drugs like [[eleusinian-mysteries-history-and-structure]], Western civilization would have developed very differently — and probably much worse.

This asymmetry has been confirmed by modern research. A large-scale epidemiological study published in the Journal of Psychopharmacology (2015) found no significant association between lifetime psychedelic use and violent behavior, while alcohol consumption is consistently linked to aggression, domestic violence, and homicide worldwide. McKenna used this contrast to argue that the legal distinction between alcohol and psychedelics reflects not evidence-based public health policy but the dominator culture's systematic preference for consciousness-contracting over consciousness-expanding substances.

## Sugar and the commodification of pleasure

McKenna extended his analysis beyond economics into the realm of human consciousness itself. He argued that sugar fundamentally altered the relationship between humans and pleasure by commodifying it. In pre-agricultural societies, sweet foods — honey, ripe fruit — were rare, seasonal rewards that reinforced foraging behavior and maintained a direct connection to the natural world. Sugar plantations transformed sweetness from a rare gift of nature into a cheap, abundant commodity, severing the link between pleasure and ecological awareness.

This commodification of pleasure, McKenna suggested, set a template that would later be applied to every other form of human experience: entertainment, sexuality, spirituality, even community itself. The dominator culture's genius was not merely exploitation of labor but the systematic extraction and packaging of experiences that had once been embedded in living systems. Sugar was the prototype for this process.

## The sugar-alcohol connection

The relationship between sugar and alcohol is central to McKenna's broader argument about dominator drugs. Distilled spirits are produced from sugar-rich feedstocks — sugarcane rum, grain whiskey, grape brandy — and the alcohol industry has historically been intertwined with the sugar economy. The Caribbean rum trade was a direct extension of the sugar plantation system, with molasses, the waste product of sugar refining, distilled into rum for both domestic consumption and the slave trade itself.

McKenna noted that alcohol and sugar produce overlapping effects on the brain's reward system, both driving dopamine release in ways that promote compulsive consumption. Together, they form a synergistic pair: sugar provides the quick energy that fuels labor, while alcohol provides the escape that makes oppressive conditions tolerable. This combination, he argued, has been the emotional backbone of every exploitative economic system from the plantation era to the present.
