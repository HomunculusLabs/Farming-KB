# Sugar, Slavery, and the Rise of Dominator Culture

## The Sugar Revolution

In Food of the Gods, McKenna identified sugar as the drug that powered the rise of Western dominator culture. Sugar is, he argued, the perfect drug for a society built on hierarchy, exploitation, and endless growth: it provides a cheap, reliable energy rush that fuels labor without expanding consciousness. Unlike psychedelic mushrooms, which dissolve boundaries and promote egalitarianism, sugar reinforces the status quo by keeping workers energized but compliant.

The sugar trade, beginning in the 15th century with Portuguese plantations on Madeira and expanding to Brazil and the Caribbean, was the engine of the Atlantic slave trade. McKenna traced the connections between sugar, slavery, and the consolidation of European imperial power with grim precision. The wealth extracted from sugar plantations — built on the forced labor of millions of enslaved Africans — funded the Industrial Revolution and the global expansion of European capitalism.

The scale of this economic transformation is difficult to overstate. By the 18th century, sugar was the single most valuable commodity in European trade, surpassing even precious metals. Caribbean sugar colonies such as Saint-Domingue (now Haiti) and Jamaica were among the most profitable territories on Earth. The capital accumulated from sugar — alongside cotton and tobacco — financed the banks, insurance companies, and infrastructure projects that launched the Industrial Revolution. McKenna argued that this historical chain of causation means that modern Western prosperity is, at its foundation, built on the exploitation of both enslaved labor and the addictive properties of a psychoactive substance.

## Sugar as Drug

McKenna's classification of sugar as a "drug" is central to his argument. From a pharmacological perspective, sugar triggers dopamine release in the brain's reward circuits, producing a brief euphoria followed by a crash that demands more. This cycle of craving and consumption is, McKenna argued, functionally identical to the cycles seen with addictive drugs like [[mckenna-opium-tobacco-paradoxical-attitudes]] and alcohol.

The difference is that sugar addiction is socially sanctioned, indeed socially encouraged, while addiction to psychoactive plants is criminalized. McKenna saw this double standard as revealing: a dominator culture will always promote substances that reinforce social control while suppressing substances that threaten it.

Sugar's unique pharmacological profile makes it ideal for this purpose. Unlike alcohol, which impairs motor function and cognitive performance, sugar enhances short-term energy and focus, making workers more productive rather than less. Unlike caffeine, which can cause anxiety and insomnia at high doses, sugar produces a reliably pleasant sensation across a wide dose range. This combination of positive reinforcement and functional enhancement made sugar the ideal tool for extractive economies that required sustained physical labor from a subjugated workforce.

## The Triangular Trade

The sugar economy depended on the triangular trade between Europe, Africa, and the Americas. European manufactured goods were traded for enslaved Africans, who were transported to the New World and forced to work on sugar plantations, whose product was shipped back to Europe. This cycle enriched European nations while devastating Africa and decimating indigenous populations in the Americas.

McKenna connected this economic system to the broader pattern of dominator culture: the extraction of resources from peripheral regions to fuel the growth and power of the center. The same pattern, he argued, is visible in the modern drug trade, in which psychoactive plants (coca, opium poppy, cannabis) grown in the Global South are processed and consumed in the Global North, with the profits flowing to distributors and the violence concentrated at the source.

The demographic impact of the sugar economy was staggering. Between the 16th and 19th centuries, an estimated 12.5 million Africans were forcibly transported to the Americas, with the vast majority destined for sugar plantations in Brazil and the Caribbean. The mortality rate during the Middle Passage averaged 15-20%, and life expectancy for enslaved laborers on sugar plantations was typically only 7-10 years after arrival, requiring constant replenishment through new imports. This system of planned obsolescence of human life was, for McKenna, the clearest expression of dominator culture's fundamental logic.

## Cannabis and Hemp Suppression

McKenna drew a direct connection between the rise of sugar culture and the suppression of [[mckenna-cannabis-culture-history]]. Hemp — the industrial fiber of the cannabis plant — was a direct competitor to sugar-dependent industries. Hemp fiber could be used for rope, canvas, paper, and clothing, and hemp seed oil was a valuable food and fuel source. The suppression of hemp through [[mckenna-drug-war-and-human-freedom]] served the interests of the sugar, cotton, timber, and petrochemical industries that stood to benefit from hemp's elimination.

McKenna noted the historical irony: cannabis, one of humanity's oldest and most versatile plant partners, was criminalized at precisely the moment when its industrial applications posed the greatest threat to established economic interests. The campaign against marijuana, led by Harry Anslinger in the 1930s, was not driven by public health concerns but by economic and racial anxieties. The Marihuana Tax Act of 1937 effectively destroyed the domestic hemp industry, eliminating a renewable, locally producible material at the dawn of the petrochemical age.

The consequences of this suppression extended far beyond the loss of a single crop. Hemp had been a strategic material for centuries — used for sails, rope, and paper — and its replacement by petroleum-based synthetics (nylon, polyester, plastics) deepened Western dependence on fossil fuels. McKenna saw this substitution as characteristic of dominator culture's pattern: replacing living, renewable systems with dead, extractive ones, and then building elaborate justifications for the substitution.

## The Physiology of Dominance

McKenna argued that the dominator culture's preference for certain drugs — sugar, alcohol, caffeine, tobacco — reflects a deeper physiological pattern. These drugs stimulate the sympathetic nervous system, increasing alertness, aggression, and the capacity for sustained, repetitive labor. They are "dominator drugs," well-suited to a culture built on conquest, exploitation, and hierarchy.

By contrast, the drugs suppressed by dominator culture — psychedelic-integration|psychedelics, cannabis, entheogenic plants — tend to stimulate the parasympathetic nervous system, promoting relaxation, introspection, and a sense of connectedness with others and with nature. They are "partnership drugs," well-suited to egalitarian, sustainable societies. McKenna saw the legal status of different drugs as a reliable indicator of a culture's fundamental values.

This classification has implications for understanding cultural health. Societies that rely heavily on dominator drugs tend toward chronic stress, social fragmentation, and environmental destruction, because the physiological states these drugs produce — alertness without awareness, energy without empathy — mirror the culture's values. Partnership drugs, by contrast, tend to produce states of consciousness that reinforce ecological awareness, social cohesion, and the recognition of limits.

## Alcohol vs. Psychedelics

The contrast between alcohol and psychedelics is particularly telling for McKenna. Alcohol is a depressant that at low doses reduces social inhibitions but at higher doses promotes aggression, violence, and loss of control. Alcohol-related violence, both domestic and public, has been a constant feature of dominator cultures for thousands of years.

Psychedelics, by contrast, rarely produce violence. The psychedelic experience typically increases empathy, reduces ego boundaries, and promotes a sense of shared identity with others and with the natural world. McKenna argued that if the ancient Greeks had been an alcohol culture rather than a [[eleusinian-mysteries-history-and-structure]], Western civilization would have developed very differently — and probably much worse.

This asymmetry has been confirmed by modern research. A large-scale epidemiological study published in the Journal of Psychopharmacology (2015) found no significant association between lifetime psychedelic use and violent behavior, while alcohol consumption is consistently linked to aggression, domestic violence, and homicide worldwide. McKenna used this contrast to argue that the legal distinction between alcohol and psychedelics reflects not evidence-based public health policy but the dominator culture's systematic preference for consciousness-contracting over consciousness-expanding substances.

## Sugar and the commodification of pleasure

McKenna extended his analysis beyond economics into the realm of human consciousness itself. He argued that sugar fundamentally altered the relationship between humans and pleasure by commodifying it. In pre-agricultural societies, sweet foods — honey, ripe fruit — were rare, seasonal rewards that reinforced foraging behavior and maintained a direct connection to the natural world. Sugar plantations transformed sweetness from a rare gift of nature into a cheap, abundant commodity, severing the link between pleasure and ecological awareness.

This commodification of pleasure, McKenna suggested, set a template that would later be applied to every other form of human experience: entertainment, sexuality, spirituality, even community itself. The dominator culture's genius was not merely exploitation of labor but the systematic extraction and packaging of experiences that had once been embedded in living systems. Sugar was the prototype for this process.

## The sugar-alcohol connection

The relationship between sugar and alcohol is central to McKenna's broader argument about dominator drugs. Distilled spirits are produced from sugar-rich feedstocks — sugarcane rum, grain whiskey, grape brandy — and the alcohol industry has historically been intertwined with the sugar economy. The Caribbean rum trade was a direct extension of the sugar plantation system, with molasses, the waste product of sugar refining, distilled into rum for both domestic consumption and the slave trade itself.

McKenna noted that alcohol and sugar produce overlapping effects on the brain's reward system, both driving dopamine release in ways that promote compulsive consumption. Together, they form a synergistic pair: sugar provides the quick energy that fuels labor, while alcohol provides the escape that makes oppressive conditions tolerable. This combination, he argued, has been the emotional backbone of every exploitative economic system from the plantation era to the present.

## Legacy and Modern Parallels

McKenna's analysis of sugar, slavery, and dominance remains relevant in the 21st century. The global sugar industry continues to generate enormous profits while contributing to epidemics of obesity, diabetes, and metabolic disease, particularly in developing nations. The [[mckenna-drug-war-and-human-freedom]] continues to channel resources and violence toward the Global South while psychoactive substances are consumed primarily in wealthy nations.

The rise of artificial sweeteners, high-fructose corn syrup, and processed food represents, in McKenna's framework, the latest evolution of dominator drug culture: ever more refined ways to deliver a dopamine rush without nutritional value, consciousness expansion, or connection to the natural world.

The global health consequences of sugar consumption lend empirical weight to McKenna's framing. The World Health Organization has linked excess sugar intake to cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and dental caries, with the burden falling disproportionately on low-income populations who can least afford medical care. Meanwhile, research into psychedelics — the substances dominator culture suppresses — has shown remarkable therapeutic potential for treating depression, addiction, and end-of-life anxiety, conditions that sugar-based diets may actually exacerbate.

## Key Points

- McKenna identified sugar as the foundational drug of dominator culture, arguing that it fuels labor without expanding consciousness
- The Atlantic sugar economy was the primary engine of the transatlantic slave trade and European imperial expansion
- Sugar triggers dopamine-driven reward cycles functionally identical to those of addictive drugs, but its use is socially sanctioned
- The suppression of cannabis and hemp served the economic interests of sugar-dependent industries
- Dominator drugs (sugar, alcohol, caffeine, tobacco) stimulate the sympathetic nervous system, while partnership drugs (psychedelics, cannabis) stimulate the parasympathetic system
- The [[psychedelic-mushroom-history]] provides a broad context for understanding McKenna's specific claims
- The sugar-alcohol nexus has historically provided both the energy and the emotional escape needed to sustain exploitative labor systems

## Significance in Psychedelic Thought

McKenna's work represents one of the most ambitious attempts to construct a comprehensive theory of the role of psychoactive plants in human history and culture. While academic anthropologists have been skeptical of many specific claims, his ideas have been enormously influential in psychedelic culture and continue to inspire research into the relationship between psychoactive substances and human consciousness. The concept of an Archaic Revival — the recovery of ancient plant-based wisdom — has resonated with the growing interest in ethnobotany, ecological consciousness, and the therapeutic potential of psychedelic substances documented in [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]].

The sugar-dominator analysis specifically has influenced scholars working at the intersection of political ecology and drug policy. By framing the legal status of psychoactive substances as a function of their compatibility with capitalist economic structures rather than their pharmacological danger, McKenna anticipated arguments that would later be developed by critics of the war on drugs and by researchers investigating the commercial determinants of health. His insistence that sugar is a drug has moved from the fringes toward mainstream acceptance as evidence of sugar's addictive properties has accumulated.

## Related McKenna Topics

McKenna's analysis fits within his broader framework of the [[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-thesis]], which traces the relationship between humans and psychoactive plants from prehistory to the present. The themes explored here connect directly to his discussions of [[mckenna-dissolution-of-the-dominator-culture]] and the [[mckenna-archaic-revival]] as counter-movements to the suppression of plant-based ecstatic religion.

The sugar-dominator analysis also anticipates contemporary critiques of the global food system. Scholars like Sidney Mintz, in Sweetness and Power, independently traced the same connections between sugar, slavery, and the formation of industrial capitalism, lending historical weight to McKenna's more speculative claims. The convergence of McKenna's ethnobotanical perspective with mainstream economic history strengthens the argument that sugar was not merely a commodity but a transformative force in human social organization.

McKenna's framing of sugar as a drug has gained additional support from modern neuroscience. Research on the neurobiology of addiction has demonstrated that sugar consumption activates the same mesolimbic dopamine pathways as drugs of abuse, and that sugar can produce behavioral signs of dependence, withdrawal, and craving in animal models. These findings, published in journals such as Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews, suggest that McKenna's classification of sugar as a drug was pharmacologically prescient rather than merely rhetorical.
