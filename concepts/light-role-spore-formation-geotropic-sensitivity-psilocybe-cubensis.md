# Light Role in Spore Formation and Geotropic Sensitivity in Psilocybe cubensis

## Overview

Badham's (1982) wind tunnel experiments on [[psilocybe-cubensis-tropisms]] revealed that light plays at least three
distinct roles in the developmental biology of this mushroom: initiation of
basidiocarps, spore formation, and geotropic sensitivity. These three roles
are partially independent but interact in ways that complicate the
interpretation of light's effects on [[evaporative-demand-hypothesis-mushroom-growth]] and orientation. The
experiments provided evidence that light is necessary but not sufficient for
the negative geotropic response, and that this requirement may be indirect
— mediated through light's effect on [[spore-production-geotropic-sensitivity-coupling-basidiomycete-badham-1982]].

## Three Roles of Light

### 1. Basidiocarp Initiation

Badham's earlier study (1980) established that light is necessary for the
initiation of basidiocarps in P. cubensis. Cultures kept in complete
darkness fail to produce [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]], while those exposed to light
(regardles of direction) form primordia. The light requirement for
initiation is relatively non-specific — both overhead and underlight
(illumination from below) support [[light-initiation-basidiocarp-formation]], suggesting that
the light sensor for this response is not highly directional.

The initiation response was studied under "cool white" fluorescent light at
2685 lux, providing a quantitative reference point for the light intensity
required. However, the threshold for initiation was not precisely determined
in either study.

### 2. Spore Formation

Light is required for spore formation in P. cubensis. In Badham's
experiments, mushrooms grown in the absence of light produced few or no
spores. The 12-hour photoperiod used in the wind tunnel experiments
supported normal sporulation, while darkness resulted in sterile
basidiocarps. This light requirement for sporulation appears to be more
stringent than the requirement for basidiocarp initiation.

The light intensity used in the experiments was approximately one-tenth of
what might be expected in natural field conditions (Badham estimates the
experimental flux density at roughly 1/10 of natural levels), suggesting
that even relatively low light levels are sufficient to support spore
production. This is consistent with the natural ecology of P. cubensis,
which often fruits in grassland environments where the mushroom may be
partially shaded by surrounding vegetation.

### 3. Geotropic Sensitivity

The most complex light relationship observed was between light and the
negative geotropic response. Mushrooms displayed [[coprophilous-habitat-negative-geotropism-spore-dispersal-adaptation]] (upward
pileus curvature) under 12-hour photoperiod conditions, and this response
was present whether light came from above or below. However, the negative
geotropic response was absent or greatly reduced in darkness — and
crucially, it was also absent in sporeless mutants grown under normal
light conditions.

This convergence of observations led Badham to suggest that "some light is
necessary for this [[mushroom-negative-geotropism]] but possibly only because it is
necessary for spore production." In this interpretation, light does not
directly regulate the geotropic response; instead, light enables spore
formation, and the substance(s) produced during spore formation (the
proposed gill hormone) are what actually trigger the geotropic sensitivity
switch.

## The Gill Hormone Hypothesis

The connection between spore production and geotropic sensitivity supports
the gill hormone hypothesis proposed by Gruen (1963) and Hagimoto (1963).
In this model, the lamellae (gills) produce a growth-regulating substance
that influences tropic responses of the stipe. Evidence supporting this
includes:

**Decapitation experiments.** Removal of the pileus (and therefore the
gills) eliminates the geotropic response, which can be partially restored
by applying agar diffusates from gill tissue.

**Sporeless mutants.** Sporeless mutants lack both spore production and
geotropic sensitivity, suggesting that the hormone is either a product of
sporulation or is co-expressed with sporulation genes.
