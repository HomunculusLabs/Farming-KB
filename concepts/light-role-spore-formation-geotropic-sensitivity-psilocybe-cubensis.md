# Light Role in Spore Formation and Geotropic Sensitivity in Psilocybe cubensis

## Overview

Badham's (1982) wind tunnel experiments on [[evaporative-demand-hypothesis-mushroom-growth]] and orientation. The
experiments provided evidence that light is necessary but not sufficient for
the negative geotropic response, and that this requirement may be indirect
— mediated through light's effect on [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]], while those exposed to light
(regardles of direction) form primordia. The light requirement for
initiation is relatively non-specific — both overhead and underlight
(illumination from below) support [[coprophilous-habitat-negative-geotropism-spore-dispersal-adaptation]] (upward
pileus curvature) under 12-hour photoperiod conditions, and this response
was present whether light came from above or below. However, the negative
geotropic response was absent or greatly reduced in darkness — and
crucially, it was also absent in sporeless mutants grown under normal
light conditions.

This convergence of observations led Badham to suggest that "some light is
necessary for this [[mushroom-negative-geotropism]] but possibly only because it is
necessary for spore production." In this interpretation, light does not
directly regulate the geotropic response; instead, light enables spore
formation, and the substance(s) produced during spore formation (the
proposed gill hormone) are what actually trigger the geotropic sensitivity
switch.

## The Gill Hormone Hypothesis

The connection between spore production and geotropic sensitivity supports
the gill hormone hypothesis proposed by Gruen (1963) and Hagimoto (1963).
In this model, the lamellae (gills) produce a growth-regulating substance
that influences tropic responses of the stipe. Evidence supporting this
includes:

**Decapitation experiments.** Removal of the pileus (and therefore the
gills) eliminates the geotropic response, which can be partially restored
by applying agar diffusates from gill tissue.

**Sporeless mutants.** Sporeless mutants lack both spore production and
geotropic sensitivity, suggesting that the hormone is either a product of
sporulation or is co-expressed with sporulation genes.
