## Overview

The heat transfer coefficient is the proportionality constant that relates heat flux at an interface to a temperature difference.
It is usually written as h and has SI units of watts per square meter per kelvin.
In the simplest engineering form, q = h(T_surface - T_fluid), where q is heat flux through the surface.
For a finite surface area, the rate form is Qdot = h A delta T.
The coefficient is not a pure material property in the way thermal conductivity is.
It depends on geometry, flow speed, fluid properties, surface condition, phase change, and the temperature field used to define delta T.
Engineers use it because the detailed boundary-layer temperature gradient is often unavailable in system calculations.
A single h value compresses conduction through the stagnant near-wall layer and mixing by the moving fluid into one practical parameter.
This makes it central in heat exchangers, electronics cooling, boilers, condensers, building envelopes, and thermal safety margins.
The concept sits between thermodynamics, fluid dynamics, and applied design practice.

## Physical Meaning

At a solid-fluid boundary, molecular conduction carries heat directly through the fluid adjacent to the wall.
Farther away, bulk motion transports warm or cool fluid parcels by advection.
The wall heat flux equals the fluid thermal conductivity times the temperature gradient at the wall.
Because that gradient is hard to know without solving the flow, h represents the effective slope between the wall and a reference fluid temperature.
A high h means a small temperature difference can move a large heat flux.
A low h means the surface must become much hotter or colder to move the same heat flux.
The reference temperature is commonly the bulk mean temperature in internal flow and the free-stream temperature in external flow.
In natural convection, it may be an [[jadam-ambient-temperature-principle]] measured far enough from the thermal plume.
Changing this reference point changes the numerical h, so reported coefficients must always state the convention.
For radiation or boiling, an apparent h can be defined, but it hides nonlinear physics and should be interpreted carefully.

## Relation to Dimensionless Numbers

Most practical correlations compute h from the Nusselt number, Nu = h L / k.
Here L is a characteristic length and k is the thermal conductivity of the fluid.
The Nusselt number compares actual convective heat transfer with pure conduction across the same length scale.
A value near one indicates conduction-dominated transport.
A much larger value indicates that motion, turbulence, or phase change is strongly thinning the thermal resistance.
Correlations usually express Nu as a function of Reynolds, Prandtl, Grashof, or Rayleigh numbers.
The Reynolds number captures the ratio of inertial to viscous forces in forced flow.
The Prandtl number compares momentum diffusivity with thermal diffusivity.
The Grashof and Rayleigh numbers describe buoyancy-driven flow in natural convection.
These dimensionless groups connect h to reynolds number and flow regimes and boundary layer theory fluid dynamics.

## Natural and Forced Convection

In forced convection, pumps, fans, compressors, wind, or moving machinery impose the fluid motion.
Increasing velocity usually increases h by thinning the hydrodynamic and thermal boundary layers.
Turbulent forced convection often gives much higher h than [[comparison-laminar-flow-hood-vs-fruiting-chamber]] because eddies exchange hot and cold fluid rapidly.
In natural convection, the motion is generated by density differences caused by heating or cooling.
A warm vertical plate in still air, a radiator, and a heated electronics enclosure are common examples.
Natural-convection coefficients are often modest because buoyancy velocities are small.
Orientation matters because it determines whether a stable or unstable density stratification forms near the surface.
Horizontal hot surfaces facing upward usually transfer heat differently than the same surfaces facing downward.
Mixed convection occurs when imposed motion and buoyancy both matter.
Designers must check whether a forced-flow assumption remains valid during fan failure, low pump speed, or transient startup.

## Overall Heat Transfer Coefficient

Real devices often contain several thermal resistances in series.
A heat exchanger tube, for example, may include internal convection, metal-wall conduction, external convection, and fouling layers.
The overall heat transfer coefficient U combines those resistances into Qdot = U A delta T.
Using the wrong area basis is a frequent source of error because inner and outer tube areas differ.
Thermal resistance notation makes the bookkeeping clearer: each layer contributes a resistance, and U A is the reciprocal of the total resistance.
Thin, conductive metal walls may contribute little compared with gas-side convection.
A fouling deposit can dominate the resistance even when the original clean heat exchanger performed well.
Radiation can sometimes be added as a parallel or linearized coefficient, especially at high temperature.
For multi-pass heat exchangers, the driving temperature difference is usually a log-mean temperature difference rather than a simple arithmetic difference.
The coefficient therefore belongs to a full thermal model, not to a component label alone.

## Typical Ranges and Interpretation

Still air around small objects may have h values of only a few watts per square meter per kelvin.
Forced air cooling often ranges from tens to low hundreds, depending on velocity and geometry.
Liquid water convection can reach hundreds to thousands because water has higher thermal conductivity and heat capacity than air.
Boiling and condensation can produce extremely high apparent coefficients when phase change occurs at the surface.
Molten metals and liquid-metal coolants can also transfer heat rapidly, although their safety and materials issues are severe.
The large spread in values explains why changing the fluid or flow regime may be more powerful than increasing surface area alone.
However, published ranges are only screening numbers.
They cannot replace a correlation or experiment matched to the actual geometry, temperature, roughness, and flow regime.
Uncertainty in h is often the largest uncertainty in a thermal design calculation.
Good design practice treats h as a variable to be bounded, measured, or validated.

## Measurement and Estimation

Experimental h values are commonly inferred from measured heat input, surface area, and temperature difference.
This sounds simple but is difficult when heat losses, contact resistance, radiation, or nonuniform wall temperature are present.
Guard heaters, calorimeters, heat-flux sensors, and inverse conduction methods are used to improve estimates.
Thermocouple placement can bias results because the wall, coating, and fluid may not share a single temperature.
In electronics cooling, junction-to-case and case-to-ambient thermal resistances may be more useful than a local h.
