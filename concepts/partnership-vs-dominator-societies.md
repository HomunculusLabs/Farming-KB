# Partnership vs Dominator Societies

The partnership-dominator polarity is a cultural theory framework that Terence McKenna adopted from Riane Eisler's 1987 book "The Chalice and the Blade" and the archaeological work of Marija Gimbutas. McKenna used this framework as the central organizing principle of "Food of the Gods," arguing that human history can be understood as a struggle between partnership-oriented cultures (associated with goddess worship, plant hallucinogens, and egalitarian social organization) and dominator-oriented cultures (associated with male sky-god worship, alcohol, hierarchy, and warfare).

## The Framework

### Partnership Model
Partnership societies are characterized by:
- Egalitarian social structures without rigid hierarchies
- Matrifocal or matrilinear organization
- Emphasis on relationship, nurturing, and connection
- Reverence for the natural world and the Great Mother Goddess
- Association with psychoactive plants that dissolve ego boundaries
- Agricultural and sedentary lifestyles
- Relative peacefulness and absence of fortified cities
- Art characterized by naturalism and sensual celebration
- Gender equality and complementary gender roles

### Dominator Model
Dominator societies are characterized by:
- Hierarchical social structures with rigid ranking
- Patriarchal organization and male dominance
- Emphasis on control, conquest, and domination
- Reverence for male sky-gods and storm deities
- Association with alcohol and addictive narcotics
- Pastoral and mobile lifestyles organized around warfare
- Fortified cities, standing armies, and chariot warfare
- Art characterized by militarism and stylized abstraction
- Gender stratification and suppression of the feminine

## Prehistoric Partnership: The Tassili-n-Ajjer

McKenna traced the origins of partnership culture to the Tassili-n-Ajjer plateau in the central Sahara. During the wetter climatic periods of the African Holocene (roughly 10,000 to 5,000 B.C.), this region supported a rich ecosystem of grasslands and cattle. The Round Head rock paintings of the Tassili, created during this period, depict a culture of remarkable sophistication:

- Dancing figures, including the celebrated "Horned Goddess" of Inaouanrhat
- Elegant naturalistic renderings of cattle and other animals
- A range of pigments unmatched in the contemporary Near East
- Placement of art in inaccessible locations suggesting initiation ceremonies
- Themes of death, rebirth, and ecstatic celebration

McKenna proposed that this was the original "Eden" — a partnership culture catalyzed and maintained by the use of psilocybin mushrooms growing in cattle dung. The Tassili culture represented the pinnacle of what he called "the Paleolithic cult of the Great Horned Goddess." John Pfeiffer's analysis of Upper Paleolithic cave art supported this interpretation, noting that "twilight-state thinking" — characterized by loss of objectivity, temporal distortion, and mild hallucinations — was "a precondition to having great culturally sanctioned truths revealed" and that "there must have been an enormous selective premium on the twilight state during prehistoric times."

## Qatal Huyuk: The Neolithic Culmination

If the Tassili-n-Ajjer was the original Eden, then Qatal Huyuk in central Anatolia was its Neolithic and eastern culmination. Excavated by James Mellaart beginning in the 1950s, Qatal Huyuk was a huge settlement spreading over thirty-two acres and accommodating over seven thousand people at its peak around 6500 B.C.

The site displayed remarkable characteristics that puzzled archaeologists:

- Amazing shrines with cattle bas-reliefs and heads of extinct aurochs (Bos primigenius)
- Complex paintings of a sophisticated civilization
- Art traditions that were "three or four thousand years ahead of its time"
- Elaborate wall-painting, clay reliefs, and cut-plaster silhouettes
- Naturalistic representations of animals, human figures, and deities
- Developed geometric ornament including spirals and meanders
- Red-ochre burials and archaic amulets in the form of a bird-like steatopygous goddess
- A cult of the Mother Goddess

Mellaart himself was puzzled that the plant husbandry of Qatal Huyuk "must have a long prehistory somewhere else," and noted that the site "yielded a wealth of religious art and symbolism" that presupposed "an Upper Paleolithic ancestor of whom we have no trace." McKenna identified this missing ancestor as the Tassili culture, with the Natufian culture of Palestine serving as a transitional link. The Natufian sites contain dark, burnished monochrome pottery known as Sahara-Sudanese ware, found near the Egyptian-Sudanese border and in the Tassili-n-Ajjer itself.

The most common motifs at Qatal Huyuk are cattle, bulls, vultures, and leopards — all animals of the African grasslands. The elegant naturalism of the art is "an echo of the beautiful and sensitive renderings of cattle that typify the Tassili art finds." This naturalistic spirit was lost when Qatal Huyuk fell: "While the absence of the inspiring mushroom and the visual acuity it imparted cannot be the sole cause, its loss may well have sapped the vitality of the archaic vision."

## The Indo-European Invasions

The end of partnership culture came with the Indo-European invasions. Marija Gimbutas documented three waves of infiltration from the Russian steppe between 4500 and 2500 B.C.:

> "The term Old Europe is applied to a pre-Indo-European culture of Europe, a culture matrifocal and probably matrilinear, agricultural and sedentary, egalitarian and peaceful. It contrasted sharply with the ensuing proto-Indo-European culture which was patriarchal, stratified, pastoral, mobile, and war-oriented, superimposed on all Europe, except the southern and western fringes, in the course of three waves of infiltration from the Russian steppe, between 4500 and 2500 B.C."

During and after this period, "the female deities, or more accurately the Goddess Creatrix in her many aspects, were largely replaced by the predominantly male divinities of the Indo-Europeans."

McKenna argued that the domestication of the horse, rather than cattle, was the key technology of the dominator revolution. While cattle domestication in Africa had set the stage for mushroom-using, goddess-worshiping partnership societies, the horse "reinforced mobility, male dominance, and a social economy based on rape and plunder." Wheeled vehicles, first invented where woodland and steppe meet, soon spread among Indo-European tribes, enabling them to overwhelm the settled agricultural populations.

## The Fall of Qatal Huyuk and Rise of Minoan Crete

A disastrous series of fires swept through Qatal Huyuk around 6500 B.C., and the city was abandoned. McKenna interpreted this as marking "the end of the age of undefended cities, the age of partnership." Refugees scattered, some fleeing to the island of Crete, where Minoan civilization preserved the partnership model for another four thousand years.

On Crete, the Goddess remained supreme, there were no signs of war, the economy prospered, and the arts flourished. The snake-handling Minoan nature Goddess, with her full bare breasts and golden serpent, represents the values of this partnership culture. Even when the island came under Achaean dominion in the fifteenth century B.C., "the Goddess and the way of thinking and living she symbolized still appear to have held fast."

The mysterious Minoan civilization thus became "the inheritor of the style and gnosis of forgotten and far-off times. It was a living monument to the partnership ideal, enduring for three millennia after the triumph of the dominator style was everywhere else complete."

## Drug Preferences as Cultural Markers

McKenna drew a direct connection between drug preferences and the partnership-dominator polarity:

### Partnership Drugs (Boundary-Dissolving)
- **Psilocybin mushrooms**: Dissolve ego boundaries, promote community and ecstatic union
- **Cannabis**: Promotes social values of partnership, deconditions users from dominator values, encourages less competitive and less goal-oriented behavior
- **Ayahuasca**: Generates group-mind experiences, promotes healing, integration, and telepathy
- **Iboga**: Among the Fang, promotes group cohesion and pair bonding

### Dominator Drugs (Ego-Reinforcing)
- **Alcohol**: Originally fermented honey (mead), replaced the mushroom cult; reinforces hierarchy, aggression, and male dominance
- **Sugar**: Associated with slavery, exploitation, and the dominator economic style
- **Coffee**: Reinforces the values of industrial culture, workplace productivity, and competitive behavior
- **Tobacco**: Initially a shamanic plant, became a tool of colonial exploitation and addiction
- **Television**: Described by McKenna as "the dominator drug par excellence" — an addictive, content-controllable technology that induces trance states and flattens consciousness

McKenna argued that "no greater shift of social values is possible to imagine than that which would accompany the gradual changeover of a psilocybin cult to an alcohol cult."

## Cannabis and the Partnership Revival

McKenna devoted particular attention to cannabis as a force for partnership values within dominator culture. He noted that cannabis occurs in male and female forms, and it is the female plant that produces the resin with narcotic power. The care and propagation of the female plant "conspires to accentuate values that are oriented toward honoring and preserving the feminine."

He observed that the longer hair lengths adopted by men during the rise of marijuana use in the 1960s were "a textbook case of an influx of apparently feminine values accompanying the use of a boundary-dissolving plant." The "hysterical reaction" to this adjustment in folkways "revealed the insecurity and sense of danger felt by the male ego in the presence of any factor that might tend to restore the importance of partnership in human affairs."

Cannabis, McKenna argued, is "anathema to the dominator culture because it deconditions or decouples users from accepted values." Its "subliminally psychedelic effect" places users "in intuitive contact with less goal-oriented and less competitive behavior patterns." This is why "marijuana is unwelcome in the modern office environment, while a drug such as coffee, which reinforces the values of industrial culture, is both welcomed and encouraged."

## Modern Implications

McKenna argued that the suppression of psychedelics in the modern era is fundamentally a defense of dominator values against the threat of partnership revival:

> "The psychedelic issue is a civil rights and civil liberties issue. It is an issue concerned with the most basic of human freedoms: religious practice and the privacy of the individual mind."

He connected the War on Drugs to the broader dominator project of maintaining control over consciousness, arguing that "the unmanaged shift of consciousness that legal and available drugs, including plant psychedelics, would bring is extremely threatening to a dominator, ego-oriented culture."
