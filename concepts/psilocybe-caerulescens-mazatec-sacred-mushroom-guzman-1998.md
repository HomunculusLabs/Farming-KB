## Psilocybe caerulescens

## Overview

Psilocybe caerulescens is a neurotropic mushroom species in the family Strophariaceae, notable for its deep [[mexican-sacred-mushrooms-indigenous-use-and-ethnobotany]] in the 1950s.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

The species has a complex taxonomic history with several recognized synonyms and varieties:

- **Psilocybe caerulescens** Murrill var. **caerulescens** — the nominate variety
- P. caerulescens var. **albida** R. Heim
- P. caerulescens var. **mazatecorum** R. Heim (later synonymized)
- P. caerulescens var. **nigripes** R. Heim
- P. caerulescens var. **ombrophila** (R. Heim) Guzmán
- **Psilocybe mazatecorum** R. Heim (synonym)
- **Psilocybe mixaeensis** R. Heim (synonym)

Roger Heim described multiple varieties based on collections from different Mazatec communities, reflecting morphological variation within what Guzmán (1998) treats as a single polymorphic species. The epithet "caerulescens" refers to the bluing reaction that occurs when the flesh is bruised or damaged, a characteristic shared with many psilocybin-containing Psilocybe species.

## Mazatec Ethnobotanical Significance

Among the Mazatec people of Oaxaca, P. caerulescens is one of the most important sacred mushrooms, known locally as **"derrumbes"** (meaning "landslides"). This common name refers to the species" preferred habitat — it fruits prolifically on disturbed ground, particularly along landslide scars, road cuts, and earthen embankments in the mountainous terrain of the Sierra Mazateca.

The species occupies a central role alongside [[entheogenic-folk-religious-ceremonies]]. Wasson and Wasson (1957) documented its use during their famous 1955 velada with curandera María Sabina in Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca, though their initial investigations focused primarily on P. mexicana.

Guzmán (1997) catalogued over 200 common names for sacred mushrooms in Mexico, many in indigenous languages. The Mazatec name "derrumbes" is one of the most widely recognized among the traditional mushroom names and has entered the broader mycological and ethnobotanical literature as a semi-formal common name for the species.

## Morphology and Identification

Psilocybe caerulescens is a medium to large bluing Psilocybe with the following general characteristics:

- **Cap (pileus):** Convex to plane in maturity, typically brown to chestnut-colored, hygrophanous, becoming lighter when dry. Surface often smooth but may show remnants of a partial veil in young specimens.
- **Bluing reaction:** The flesh and mycelium turn distinctly blue or blue-green when bruised, cut, or handled, indicating the presence of psilocybin and related indole alkaloids.
- **Stipe (stem):** Cylindrical, equal, often slightly thickened at the base. Pallid to brownish, bruising blue.
- **Spore print:** Dark purple-brown to nearly black.
- **Habitat:** Gregarious to scattered on disturbed clay or loam soils, particularly landslide areas, road cuts, and recently disturbed ground in subtropical humid forests. Elevation typically 800–1,800 meters.

## Geographic Distribution

P. caerulescens is predominantly a Mexican and Central American species. According to Guzmán, Allen & Gartz (1998), its distribution includes:

- **Mexico:** Widespread in the states of Oaxaca (Sierra Mazateca, Sierra Juárez), Puebla, Veracruz, and Chiapas. The species is most commonly associated with the Mazatec, Chinantec, and Zapotec cultural regions.
- **Central America:** Reported from Guatemala and possibly Honduras in similar subtropical forest habitats.
- **South America:** Rare records from Colombia and Venezuela in comparable disturbed-soil habitats.

The species is strongly associated with subtropical humid forests and cloud forest margins, typically fruiting during the rainy season (June–September) when soil moisture and warm temperatures promote fruiting on disturbed ground.

## Ecological Role

P. caerulescens is a saprotrophic species that colonizes disturbed soils rich in organic matter. Its preference for landslide scars and road cuts suggests it plays an early successional role in soil colonization following disturbance. The species appears to benefit from soil disruption that breaks up compacted earth and exposes buried organic matter, creating favorable conditions for [[psilocybe-cubensis-anemotropism]]:** Larger, typically fruits on bovine dung rather than disturbed soil.
- **Psilocybe mexicana:** Smaller, typically found in grassy areas and pastures rather than landslide zones.
- **Psilocybe weilii:** Southeastern US species with similar habitat preferences for disturbed soils.
- **[[psilocybe-hoogshagenii]]:** Related species with a persistent annulus; found in similar Mexican habitats.

The greatest danger of misidentification lies with toxic species of Galerina or Conocybe that may share similar brownish coloration and habitat. However, the prominent bluing reaction of P. caerulescens provides a key diagnostic feature absent in most toxic lookalikes.

## Conservation Status

P. caerulescens is not formally evaluated for conservation status, but habitat loss through deforestation and agricultural expansion in the Sierra Mazateca and adjacent regions threatens wild populations. The species' dependence on specific microhabitats (disturbed clay soils in humid forests) makes it vulnerable to changes in land use patterns.
