# Jajarkot Advanced Polyculture

## Overview

The Jajarkot Permaculture Program (JPP), a village self-development organization in Nepal, created an expanded polyculture system that builds upon Ianto Evans's foundational seven-variety temperate polyculture. Modified by Toby Hemenway for broader North American application, the Jajarkot polyculture incorporates a larger number of species and a more complex successional pattern. With modifications, it can be planted in spring in most North American gardens and yields food for six to eight months of the year. In northern climates, spun row cover or [[coleman-wiggle-wire-channel-greenhouse-plastic-attachment-system]] can extend productivity well into fall and spring.

## Background and Origin

The Jajarkot Permaculture Program worked in the Jajarkot district of Nepal, one of the poorest and most remote regions in the country. The program adapted [[permaculture-principles]] to local subsistence farming conditions, where food security and soil fertility are constant challenges. Their polyculture designs drew on traditional Nepali interplanting knowledge combined with modern ecological understanding. The system was brought to wider attention through Toby Hemenway's documentation in Gaia's Garden, where he adapted it for temperate North American conditions.

## Species Composition

The Jajarkot polyculture uses a multi-layered approach with species selected for different phenological stages and [[fungal-melanin-biosynthesis-ecological-functions]]:

### Ground Cover Layer (Edible Greens)
- **Mustard greens**: Osaka purple mustard, tat tsoi, mizuna, garden cress
- **Cool-season greens**: Arugula, garden purslane, shiso
- **Buckwheat**: In warm spring regions (May temperatures reaching 80°F)
- **Salad crops**: Radishes, chard, lettuces, carrots

### Herb Layer
- **Umbellifers**: Fennel, dill, coriander (somewhat more densely sown than salad crops)
- **Summer herbs**: Basil (planted later when soil warms)

### Legume Layer
- **Fava beans**: Pushed into soil about 1 foot apart
- **Bush beans or bush peas**: Planted when soil reaches 60°F

### Allium Layer
- **Onions, garlic, garlic chives, or leeks**: Planted 6 to 12 inches apart

### Brassica Layer
- **Cabbage, cauliflower, or broccoli**: Seedlings planted about 18 inches apart in gaps
- Selected for a blend of varieties ripening over a long season

## Planting Schedule

### One Month Before Last Frost
Start a few seedlings each of cabbage, cauliflower, or broccoli indoors. Select a blend of varieties that will ripen over a long season to extend the harvest window.

### Week One (Last Frost Date)
Create a dense edible ground cover by sowing a mix of mustard greens and other cool-season greens. Lightly sow salad crop seeds (radishes, chard, lettuces, carrots) among the previously sown greens. Add herb seeds (fennel, dill, coriander) somewhat more densely than the salad crops. Push fava bean seeds into the soil about 1 foot apart. Add allium starts or seeds about 6 to 12 inches apart.

### Weeks Two to Four
Begin harvesting the edible ground cover. Pull whole plants to create openings rather than just trimming leaves. Take care not to disturb young beans or alliums. Pull a few young herbs to thin them. In the resulting gaps, plant cabbage, cauliflower, or broccoli seedlings about 18 inches apart.

### Late Spring/Early Summer
When soil temperatures reach 60°F, plant basil and bush beans in remaining openings. As spring warms, many greens will bolt. Accelerate harvesting to eliminate them before they set seed, or let a few go to seed and lay the whole plant on the soil to compost and reseed naturally.

### Summer Through Fall
Continue harvesting all plants as they mature or crowd. The overall yield is greater than if plants were grown in monocultural blocks covering the same area.

## Ecological Interactions

The Jajarkot polyculture benefits from the same mechanisms as Ianto Evans's simpler system, amplified by greater diversity:

- **Multiple umbelliferous herbs** (dill, fennel, coriander) attract a wider range of [[hamilton-beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]] provide more sustained nectar sources.
- **Several legume species** (fava beans, bush beans, bush peas) fix nitrogen through the season and attract parasitic wasps.
- **Dense greenery** shades and protects soil, excludes weeds, and maintains moisture.
- **Alliums** provide pest-repellent properties through their sulfur compounds.
- **Extended harvest** from a single major sowing and a few minutes of later care reduces labor inputs compared to successive monoculture plantings.

## Yield Comparison

In Hemenway's experience, the overall yield of the Jajarkot polyculture is greater than the combined yield of the same species grown in monocultural blocks covering equivalent area. This yield advantage comes from:
- More complete use of vertical space and root zones
- Reduced competition through niche partitioning
- Lower pest losses due to diversity
- Extended harvest period reducing waste and gluts
- Self-mulching effect reducing water stress
