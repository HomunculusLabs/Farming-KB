# Top Bar Hive Beekeeping

Top bar hives are a [[comparison-natural-beekeeping-vs-standard-beekeeping]] system that allows bees to build comb freely from suspended wooden bars rather than pre-formed frames. Popularized in the 1960s by the [[food-forest-design]] movement and proponents of low-intervention apiculture, top bar hives represent a departure from conventional Langstroth beekeeping.

## Design and Dimensions

A standard top bar hive is a single horizontal chamber, typically 40-48 inches long, with sloped sides angled at roughly 30 degrees. The angle prevents bees from attaching comb to the sidewalls. Each hive holds 20-30 top bars, each about 1.25-1.5 inches wide. Bars can be plain wood or fitted with a wax-coated starter strip to guide comb building.

### Key Dimensions

- Length: 40-48 inches
- Width at top: 15-18 inches
- Depth: 10-12 inches
- Bar width: 1.25-1.5 inches (follower board excluded)
- Side angle: approximately 30 degrees from vertical

The follower board acts as a movable wall, allowing the beekeeper to adjust the hive volume to match colony size -- smaller in spring, expanded as the colony grows.

## Natural Comb Advantages

Bees in a top bar hive build comb to their own specifications, adjusting cell size based on their needs. This natural approach yields several benefits:

- No plastic foundation or wax-coated wires
- Drone comb is proportionally larger, supporting mite population monitoring
- Comb is harvested as cut comb or crushed for [[candle-making]]
- Bees allocate resources according to colony dynamics rather than frame geometry
- Old comb is culled naturally during swarm management

## Management Practices

Top bar [[bee-forage-hive-management-mollison]] is inherently more hands-off than frame-based systems. Inspections are less frequent, and the beekeeper works from above rather than lifting heavy boxes.

### Seasonal Management

In spring, the beekeeper checks for queenright status and colony strength, expanding the follower board as the colony builds out. During the honey flow, empty bars are added ahead of the brood nest to encourage honey storage. Harvest is done by cutting comb from the outermost bars, leaving enough stores for the colony to overwinter.

### Swarm Management

Because top bar hives do not use queen excluders, swarm prevention relies on providing adequate space and managing brood congestion. Beekeepers may split colonies by moving the queen and several bars of brood to a new hive body.

### Overwintering

Top bar hives require careful winter preparation. The follower board is moved in to reduce empty space. Entrances are reduced, and [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]] the hive may be wrapped or sheltered. Some beekeepers combine weaker colonies or use quilt boxes for [[mushroom-casing-moisture-management]].

## Comparison with Langstroth Hives

| Feature | Top Bar Hive | Langstroth Hive |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| Comb type | Natural, free-built | Foundation-guided |
| Equipment cost | Low | Moderate to high |
| Honey yield | Lower (15-30 lbs) | Higher (40-60 lbs) |
| Hive weight per box | N/A (horizontal) | 50-80 lbs when full |
| Inspection style | One bar at a time | Full frame removal |
| Queen excluder | Not used | Optional |
| Comb harvest | Cut comb or crush | Extractor-compatible |
| Wax production | Lower per harvest | Higher, reusable frames |
| Learning curve | Moderate | Steeper (more equipment) |

## Getting Started

A top bar hive can be built from untreated lumber in a weekend. Cypress, cedar, and pine are common choices. The bars should be cut precisely to the proper width -- too wide and bees build double comb between bars, too narrow and they fill gaps with burr comb.

Place the hive on a stand 18-24 inches off the ground, facing southeast for early morning sun. Provide a water source within 50 feet. Catch or purchase a package of bees or a nucleus colony and install them onto the bars behind the follower board.

## Equipment Checklist

- Top bar hive body with 24-30 bars
- Follower board
- Bee brush
- Smoker
- Veil and gloves (minimal protective gear often sufficient)
- Hive tool (optional -- butter knife works)
- Bar storage box for harvested comb
