# Wildfire Spread Factors and Firestorm Conditions

## Overview

[[wildfire]] is a recurrent and powerful force in many landscapes, particularly in arid-border regions, dry sclerophyll forests, savannahs, and grasslands. Understanding the factors that drive wildfire spread and the conditions that create firestorms is essential for any land designer, particularly in [[permaculture]] contexts where settlement and food production systems must coexist with fire-prone environments.

## The Critical Factors for Firestorm

Four primary factors combine to create [[firestorm]] conditions:

### 1. Fuel Supply
Fuel supply encompasses the dryness, distribution, and quantity of available combustible material. Loose fuels greater than 6 cm in diameter are particularly significant. The moisture content of fuels is critical — grasses and sticks smaller than 6 cm diameter with less than 20% water content are especially dangerous. Pine woods, due to their high resin and oil content, can erupt at less than 30% humidity. A practical test involves weighing a chunk of 75 × 50 mm unpainted pine — when saturated it represents 100% moisture content.

### 2. Oxygen Supply
Winds that fan the flames, particularly hot winds, provide the oxygen supply needed to drive fire intensity. Wind speed has a dramatic effect on fire spread — the spread rate increases as the square of the velocity. At 20 km/h, if spread is 2 km² per hour, at 30 km/h the rate becomes 4 km² per hour. At speeds above 80 km/h, ground fires may actually self-extinguish.

### 3. Preheating
Preheating occurs through upslope effects, radiant heat in front of the flames, or advected desert winds in unprotected forests. For every 10° increase in the angle of upslope, fire speed and intensity doubles. At 0° slope, fire might travel at 16 km/h; at 10° it reaches 32 km/h; at 20° it reaches 64 km/h; and at 30° it reaches 128 km/h. This exponential relationship means slope effect alone can wipe out hill ridge settlements.

### 4. Unstable Air Masses
Unstable air is indicated by wind shear, ground whirlwinds (dust devils), scattered cumulus clouds, and shifting winds. In unstable air, smoke ascends to great heights rather than leveling out at low altitude, and the air is otherwise clear (no fog or smog before the fire). In some forests, volatile oils, terpenes, or resins can be smelled, and a light-blue haze develops — all presaging extreme fire danger when dry fuels reach less than 35% moisture.

## Fire Timing

Grass fires typically spread after 10–11 a.m., once dew has dried off. Forest fires are most dangerous from midday to 3 p.m. After an initial flareup, approximately one hour suffices to develop firestorm conditions.

## Fire Causes

Only a small proportion of fires start from lightning strikes, even in remote forests. Lightning and accidents account for perhaps only 4% of total fires. The vast majority are deliberately lit — by mischievous, psychopathic, or even well-meaning people. Some fires arise from previous "controlled" burns left smouldering (often lit to reduce fire risk). Freak fires can start from electrical shorts, vehicle backfire flashes, heat focused by bottles or curved glass, and welding, campfire, or cigarette accidents. Notably, some pyromaniacs light many fires and even enlist in volunteer firefighting organizations.

## Fire Frequency by Vegetation Type

Wildfire occurs on a more or less regular schedule depending on vegetation:
- **Wet sclerophyll forest**: approximately every 30 years
- **Dry savannah**: every 8–10 years
- **Unbrowsed grassland**: annually

This creates a specific problem for designers progressing from grassland to forest operations — the 3–5 year period until forest establishment carries high fire risk, requiring programmed planting mosaics to reduce district-level risk.

## Fire Tornadoes and Extreme Phenomena

In firestorm conditions, fire tornadoes can develop on the lee ridge side of hills, with ascent velocities of up to 250 km/h. These tornadoes travel downhill, lifting large logs, branches, and pieces of houses aloft, and creating massive aerial gaseous explosions. Ground winds near the base of these columns (some tens of metres across) can reach 100 km/h — enough to roll people over and over. The noise is described as deafening.

In these tornadoes, or in more minor whirlwinds, incandescent material is carried aloft and dropped 1–30 km downwind, starting fresh fires and fresh firestorm sequences.

## Design Implications

Wildfire will always occur on arid borders. Designers must first be able to live with fire, and secondarily design to exclude fire from settled areas through a combination of:
- Altering vegetation to create more fire-immune systems
- Designing dams to flood-flow over hillsides subject to fire
- Mechanical or grazing removal of fuels before fire-danger periods (dead brush, long dry grasses, dead lower branches of trees)

## Risk Assessment

Understanding wildfire spread factors and firestorm conditions helps communities prepare
for and mitigate natural disaster impacts. Early warning
systems and community preparedness plans are essential
components of effective disaster management.

## Ecological Context

Within broader ecological systems, wildfire spread factors and firestorm conditions
interacts with multiple environmental factors. Understanding
these relationships is key to effective implementation
and long-term sustainability of management practices.

## Management Strategies

Several approaches can be employed to address challenges
related to wildfire spread factors and firestorm conditions. These include preventive measures,
active management techniques, and adaptive strategies
that respond to changing conditions over time.
