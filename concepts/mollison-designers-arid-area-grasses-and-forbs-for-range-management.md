# Arid-Area Grasses and Forbs for Range Management

A very careful selection of perennial and annual grasses can greatly
increase the number and condition of animals on range in arid
environments. Mollison identifies good grasses for every situation from
sand seas to gilgais (natural swales developed from pockets of
expanding clays). This topic extends the [[mollison-grasslands-and-range-management-strategies]] and [[mollison-native-seed-forage-systems-and-arid-species-lists]] covered elsewhere.

## Multiple Functions of Arid Grasses

Arid-area grasses and forbs cover a range of uses beyond simple
forage. They serve as human food, poultry and waterfowl forage, thatch
material, green forage or hay, and soil stabilisers. Mollison recommends
a carefully selected sowing of some of the best grasses in plots for
quite specific purposes:

- Reclaim claypans and saltpans
- Filter out silt and nitrates in diversion drains
- Provide green hay for domestic stock
- Give durable thatch and cane fences, mats, screens, or baskets
- Provide bundles of seed-head for poultry and to feed wild birds
- Create a grassed waterway for downhill sheet flow
- Provide a seed resource for broadscale work

## Managing Grass Quality

Coarse tussock grasses become unpalatable if left unbrowsed, when dry
material accumulates, or at flowering and after, when food value is low.
Thus, mown or managed swale fields are the most productive. Like all
green crop, leaf material may contain dangerous levels of nitrates
(dangerous to people and domestic animals) if over-manured or grown in
heavily manure-polluted waters.

This factor does not so much concern permaculturists if the hay is used
as pit mulch for trees, but tests of garden greens should be made
periodically for nitrate levels.

## Wind-Dispersed Species: Wraiths and Golems

Whole plants, seed-heads, leaves, dried manurial pellets, seed pods,
and dust blow with the wind across desert plains, and are trapped in
depressions, pits, swales, and against fences and tree-lines. A set of
plants depend on the wind for dispersal, seen as substantial rolling
balls (golems) or dry, light, airy panicles (wraiths).

Black roly-poly (Bassia quinquecuspidata) is a short-lived perennial
shrub which breaks off and rolls, distributing spiny seeds. It occurs
thickly only where overgrazing occurs, and protects a wide range of
soils as a defending pioneer, under which more palatable or useful
species can grow. While considered "noxious" by conventional
agriculture, Mollison notes that what is really noxious is the state of
mind which ignores its useful function.

Tumbleweed (Amaranthus albus) is a lighter invader preferring towns and
settled areas. A mustard (Sisymbrium officinale), of some forage and
medicinal value, spreads its dry seed stems as a wraith in dust storms.

## Range Management Principles

On the broadscale, there is no substitute for small, intensively
managed perennial grass plots, fenced or protected to avoid
overgrazing, or managed and encouraged to provide seasonal fodders.
Range management is a real skill, and relies on good plant
identification and management, a keen eye for animal behaviour, and a
modest but sustainable stocking rate.

This connects to [[mollison-animal-range-management]] and [[mollison-rangelands-and-natural-productivity]].

## Runnel Traps and Water Spreading

Vetiver grass walls across runnels in sands spread water and hold silt
for hardy shrubs and trees. Logs, straw bales, wire netting, and
stones in sawdust or hessian all act as silt traps, building up
mini-deltas of silt and leaves. Beside these can be planted willow,
Acacia, or any other fibrous-rooted and hardy species.

The "boomerang" system uses small pans on awkward, steeper, or
restricted sites to catch and hold silt and water for spaced-out tree
sites. Runnels are thus spread across a site. These techniques
integrate with [[mollison-permaculture-two-arid-water-harvesting-and-erosion-control]].

## Stocking Rate Recommendations

If we consider stocking on deserts, Mollison recommends having at
least one-fourth (some say one-seventh) of the area free of stock at
any one time, and letting the area regenerate if rains occur. This may
be assisted with spring fire, pelleted seed, or broadscale water
harvesting.

It is also possible to set up core areas of more carefully tended trees
that can expand at their perimeter if seasons permit, which supply seed
and where reforestation trials and fire trials can be controlled. Some
successful Australian sheep graziers allow 7 to 9 years rest per run
and never suffer the animal stress brought on by drought.

It is infinitely preferable to run small, high-value herds on copious
range than to risk the inevitable collapse of range and flocks by
stressing the vegetation.

## Desert Aquatic and Swamp Species

Several aquatic and swamp plants occur in deserts due in part to exotic
rivers that flow into deserts, and in part to run-off from ranges.
There are even more species adapted to the claypans and swales
(gilgais) or run-off hollows, where they grow and tolerate very hot
and somewhat brackish or salty water.

There are thousands of sites in deserts where ponds and dams discharge
into interior flats or flood-outs, some of which may be below sea level.
The desert is, in a sense, like a series of small aquatic islands, with
no danger of species escaping to infest permanent streams. These are
therefore ideal sites for small aquacultural assemblies and experiments.

All growing plants use up salts in growth, and some dense algae will
desalinate large volumes of water, while many rushes and weeds remove
dissolved salts or unwanted pollutants. It is quite feasible and
sensible to set up part-enclosed and part-open natural water filtration
systems to remove faecal, nitrogenous, and metallic pollutants from
town water supplies.

## Forage Selection for Specific Sites

Mollison emphasises that a carefully selected sowing of some of the
best grasses in plots can serve quite specific purposes. They can be
pot-grown in the first place and divided, then planted for reclaiming
claypans and saltpans, filtering out silt and nitrates in diversion
drains, providing green hay for domestic stock, or creating grassed
waterways for downhill sheet flow. On the broadscale, small,
intensively managed perennial grass plots, fenced or protected to
avoid overgrazing, are the most productive approach to [[mollison-rangelands-and-natural-productivity]].
