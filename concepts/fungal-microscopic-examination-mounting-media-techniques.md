# Fungal Microscopic Examination and Mounting Media

Microscopic examination is fundamental to [[pcr-methods-fungal-identification-monitoring]]. While macroscopic features of fruiting bodies can narrow identification to genus or species group in many cases, definitive identification almost always requires examination of spores, hyphal anatomy, cystidia, asci, basidia, and other microscopic structures. The quality of the mount — the choice of mounting medium, the preparation technique, and the skill of the observer — directly determines the quality of the identification.

## Water Mounts

The simplest and most universally applicable mounting technique is the temporary water mount. A small piece of the fungal tissue (a section of gill or pore surface, a fragment of the cap cuticle, or a spore deposit) is placed in a drop of distilled water on a clean glass slide and covered with a cover slip. No special reagents are required, making water mounts accessible even in field conditions.

Water mounts are ideal for observing spore shape, size, and arrangement in their natural, unaltered state. Spore dimensions measured from water mounts are the most reliable because water does not cause swelling or shrinkage. Water mounts are also the preferred medium for observing living structures such as actively growing hyphal tips, basidial sterigmata, and [[growing-gourmet-spore-germination-strain-isolation]].

The primary limitation of water mounts is their impermanence. Water evaporates within minutes to hours, and the mount cannot be sealed or preserved. Water mounts are also unsuitable for observing certain features: water does not clear (make transparent) thick-walled tissues, and it does not provide sufficient contrast for observing hyaline (colorless) structures against a bright background.

## Lactophenol and Lactophenol Cotton Blue

Lactophenol is the workhorse mounting medium of mycology. It consists of lactic acid, phenol, glycerol, and water in roughly equal proportions. Each component serves a specific function: lactic acid is a clearing agent that makes thick tissues transparent, phenol kills and preserves the specimen, glycerol prevents the mount from drying out, and water provides the necessary fluidity.

The clearing action of lactophenol is its most valuable property. When fungal tissue is placed in lactophenol, the lactic acid gradually penetrates and clears the cell walls, making internal structures visible that would be opaque in water. This is particularly important for observing the [[fungal-woronin-bodies-and-septal-pore-organization]] apparatus of hyphae, the internal structure of asci, and the arrangement of tissues in the trama of mushroom gills.

Lactophenol cotton blue (LCB) is lactophenol supplemented with the dye aniline blue (often called cotton blue in the mycological tradition). The dye stains fungal cell walls blue-violet, providing contrast that makes hyaline structures visible against the background. LCB is particularly useful for observing delicate hyphae, thin-walled cystidia, and the paraphyses that surround asci in ascomycete fruiting bodies.

However, cotton blue has limitations. It stains all structures indiscriminately, which can obscure important details in dark-pigmented tissues. Cotton blue should not be used with dark-spored fungi because the stain deepens the natural coloration and makes spore ornamentation difficult to resolve. For dark-spored species, plain lactophenol (without dye) is preferred.

Lactophenol also dissolves calcareous structures — calcium carbonate deposits found in the peridia, capillitia, and stalks of certain myxomycetes and some gasteroid fungi. This can be either a problem (if those structures are important for identification) or a diagnostic tool (the release of carbon dioxide bubbles when lactophenol contacts calcium carbonate confirms the presence of calcareous deposits).

## Potassium Hydroxide (KOH)

Potassium hydroxide, typically used as a 2–5% aqueous solution, is the most commonly used chemical reagent in [[psilocybin-mushroom-identification-guide]]. A small piece of tissue is placed in a drop of KOH on a slide, and the reaction is observed. KOH causes spores and hyphal tissues to swell slightly, returning dehydrated specimens to approximately their natural dimensions. This is critical for accurate spore measurements from dried herbarium material.

KOH also produces color reactions with certain [[query-how-to-extract-and-use-fungal-pigments-for-natural-dyeing]]. Many boletes and russulas show distinctive color changes in KOH — turning red, yellow, green, or black — that are diagnostic at the species level. These KOH reactions are recorded in most field guides and monographs.

A 5% solution of KOH in glycerol can be used as a semipermanent mounting medium. The KOH swells and clears the tissue, while the glycerin prevents drying. Slides prepared this way can be sealed and stored for weeks to months, unlike plain water mounts.

## Melzer's Reagent

Melzer's reagent is a specialized mounting medium composed of iodine, potassium iodide, and chloral hydrate dissolved in water. Its primary function is to detect amyloid reactions — a distinctive blue-black coloration produced when certain fungal cell walls react with the iodine in the reagent.

Amyloidy is a critical taxonomic character in many groups of fungi. In agarics (gilled mushrooms), the amyloid or inamyloid reaction of the spore wall, the spore ornamentation, the ascus tip (in operculate and inoperculate discomycetes), and the tissues of the hymenophoral trama are used to discriminate between closely related species and genera.

Melzer's reagent is applied by placing a drop on the tissue and observing the color change under the microscope. True amyloidy produces a deep blue-black color, while dextrinoidy produces a reddish-brown color. Non-reactive tissues remain unchanged (negative or inamyloid). The distinction between amyloid, dextrinoid, and inamyloid reactions is a standard part of the species description for many fungi.

Chloral hydrate, the solvent in Melzer's reagent, is a controlled substance in many jurisdictions due to its sedative properties. Some laboratories have replaced it with alternative formulations, but the traditional Melzer's reagent remains the standard for amyloid testing.

## Glycerol Mounts

Aqueous glycerol (typically 5–15%) serves as a simple semipermanent mounting medium. Glycerol is hygroscopic and prevents the mount from drying, while water provides the fluidity needed for arranging the specimen. Glycerol mounts can be prepared by placing the specimen in a drop of aqueous glycerol and sealing the cover slip with nail polish or a commercial ringing compound.

As the water in the mount gradually evaporates, the glycerol becomes more concentrated, eventually reaching a state where the mount is effectively permanent. The cover slip should be resealed periodically as the glycerol concentrates. Glycerol is particularly useful for preserving spore preparations and delicate hyphal mounts.

The main drawback of glycerol is that it does not clear tissues or provide contrast. It is best used for specimens that are already thin and transparent, such as spore prints, single-spore isolates, or thin sections of fungal tissue.

## Specialized Mounting Media

Several other mounting media serve specific purposes in fungal microscopy:

**Polyvinyl lactophenol (PVL)** is a viscous, slowly polymerizing variant of lactophenol. It embeds delicate fruiting bodies in a clear matrix that hardens over several days, making it ideal for permanent mounts of small or fragile structures. PVL is particularly useful for preparing permanent mounts of myxomycete sporangia after the spores have been removed, revealing the capillitial architecture.

**Phloxine B** is a water-soluble red dye used as a counterstain. A 0.01% solution in water or KOH stains cystidia, hyphal strands, and other sterile elements pink or red, providing contrast against colorless spores and hyphae. It is often used in combination with Melzer's reagent for observing both amyloid reactions and tissue anatomy in the same mount.

**Sulphovanillin** is a solution of vanillin in sulphuric acid that detects the presence of certain phenolic compounds (grevillins) in the tissues of some agarics. A positive reaction produces a distinctive red color. While less commonly used than KOH or Melzer's, sulphovanillin can be valuable for identifying species in difficult genera such as Cortinarius and Inocybe.

**India ink** is used to visualize the gelatinous sheaths that surround hyphae in certain fungi, particularly in the Russulales and Boletales. A drop of India ink is mixed with a drop of the fungal tissue in water; the ink particles are excluded from the gelatinous zones, creating a dark halo around the hyaline hyphae.

## Sectioning Techniques

The quality of a microscopic mount depends as much on the quality of the section as on the choice of mounting medium. Freehand sections are cut with a sharp razor blade or scalpel, ideally under a dissecting microscope for precise control. The razor should be held at a low angle and drawn through the tissue in a single smooth stroke. Sections should be as thin as possible — ideally a single cell layer thick — to allow light to pass through.

For very hard or very soft tissues, a support medium can help. Soft tissues can be supported by placing them on a small piece of carrot or potato before sectioning. Hard tissues (such as the pileipellis of some polypores) may benefit from being softened in KOH before sectioning.

A freezing microtome provides more consistent section thickness but requires specialized equipment. Sliding microtomes and vibratomes offer additional options for laboratories that process large numbers of samples.

## Brightfield, Phase Contrast, and Interference Microscopy

Most fungal microscopy is performed using brightfield illumination, where light passes directly through the specimen. This works well for pigmented structures (dark spores, colored tissues) but provides poor contrast for hyaline (colorless) structures such as thin-walled hyphae, paraphyses, and cystidia of pale-spored species.

Phase contrast microscopy converts differences in the refractive index of cellular structures into visible differences in brightness. Hyaline hyphae and spores that are nearly invisible in brightfield become clearly visible in phase contrast, making it possible to observe septal pores, wall ornamentation, and organelle-like inclusions without staining. A 40× phase contrast objective is one of the most useful accessories for a fungal identification laboratory.

Differential interference contrast (DIC, also called Nomarski contrast) provides a pseudo-three-dimensional image that reveals surface topography. DIC is particularly effective for observing spore ornamentation — the ridges, warts, and reticulations on spore surfaces appear as shadow-cast relief. DIC requires specialized optics (Wollaston prisms) that are more expensive than phase contrast equipment but produce dramatically better images of surface features.

## Digital Photomicrography

Digital cameras mounted on compound microscopes have largely replaced film photography for fungal documentation. Modern digital microscope cameras produce images of sufficient resolution for publication and provide immediate feedback on image quality. Software packages allow measurement of spore dimensions, annotation of diagnostic features, and stacking of multiple focal planes to produce images with extended depth of field.

Extended depth of field (focus stacking) is particularly valuable for fungal microscopy because many structures of interest (spores, asci, basidia, cystidia) have significant three-dimensional depth that exceeds the shallow depth of field of high-magnification objectives. By capturing a series of images at different focal planes and combining the in-focus regions computationally, a single sharp image of the entire structure can be produced.

For documentation purposes, each image should include a scale bar calibrated to the specific objective and camera combination used. Measuring spores from calibrated images is more reliable than measuring from ocular micrometers because it allows repeated measurements and reduces observer error.

## Slide Curation and Long-Term Preservation

Semipermanent and permanent slide collections are valuable reference resources for herbaria and teaching laboratories. Slides should be labeled with the species name (or provisional identification), collection number, date, locality, substrate, collector, and the mounting medium used. A second label on the reverse side of the slide can record additional data such as spore dimensions, color reactions, and notes on microscopic features.

Permanent mounts should be sealed with a ringing compound to prevent the entry of air and the growth of contaminants. Clear nail polish is the most accessible ringing compound but degrades over several years. Commercial ringing compounds (such as Zut or Glyceel) provide more durable seals and are preferred for slides intended for long-term curation.

Slides should be stored flat in slide boxes or cabinets, protected from dust and direct sunlight. Glycerol mounts should be checked periodically and additional glycerol added if the mount begins to dry. Lactophenol mounts are generally stable but may develop crystalline deposits over time, particularly if the phenol component was not fully dissolved during preparation.

## Oil Immersion and High-Magnification Techniques

For observing the finest details of spore ornamentation, septal pore structures, and ascus tip anatomy, oil immersion objectives (100×) are essential. A drop of immersion oil is placed between the cover slip and the objective lens; the oil matches the refractive index of the glass, eliminating the air-glass interface that would otherwise cause light scattering and loss of resolution.

Oil immersion requires clean slides and cover slips. Dust, mounting medium residue, or finger oil on the cover slip degrades image quality at 1000× magnification. The oil must be cleaned from the objective immediately after use with lens tissue; dried oil can damage the lens coating and is difficult to remove.

Nuclear staining is occasionally useful for fungal identification, particularly in groups where nuclear number and arrangement are taxonomically informative. Aceto-carmine stains nuclei red and can reveal whether a species is monokaryotic or dikaryotic, or whether asci contain the expected number of ascospores. DAPI (4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) is a fluorescent DNA stain used with epifluorescence microscopy for precise nuclear counting.

## Common Errors and Best Practices

The most frequent error in fungal microscopy is using too much mounting medium. A large drop spreads the specimen over too wide an area and makes it difficult to find structures under the microscope. A small, concentrated drop in the center of the slide is far more effective.

Air bubbles trapped under the cover slip are another common problem. They can be minimized by lowering the cover slip at an angle, allowing air to escape from one side before the cover slip is fully lowered. Small bubbles that cannot be avoided can sometimes be useful: they create a lens effect that slightly magnifies structures in their vicinity.

Cover slip thickness matters. Standard cover slips (number 1, approximately 0.17 mm thick) are optimal for most compound microscopes with high-dry and oil-immersion objectives. Thicker cover slips introduce spherical aberration that degrades image quality at high magnification. For routine examination at 400×, cover slip thickness is less critical.

Finally, patience and practice are essential. The ability to cut thin sections, prepare clean mounts, and navigate the complex three-dimensional anatomy of fungal tissues under the microscope improves with experience. Developing these skills is fundamental to becoming a competent mycologist, and no amount of molecular data can substitute for the detailed morphological knowledge that comes from hours at the microscope.

## Choosing the Right Medium

Selecting the appropriate mounting medium is often the first decision a mycologist makes when examining an unknown specimen. The following decision framework can guide the choice: use water mounts for fresh material where natural dimensions and colors must be preserved; use lactophenol for clearing thick tissues and observing internal anatomy; use lactophenol cotton blue for staining hyaline structures; use KOH for measuring spores from dried material and testing color reactions; and use Melzer's reagent for amyloid testing. In practice, multiple mounts of the same specimen using different media are often prepared to capture the full range of diagnostic characters.
