# [[cover-crops]] for Soil Building

[[toby-hemenway]]'s comprehensive guide to choosing and using cover crops for [[building-soil]] fertility, structure, and biodiversity. From Gaia's Garden, Chapter 4.

## Why Cover Crops

Cover crops are plants grown primarily to benefit the soil rather than for harvest. Their roles include:

- Building [[gaias-garden-soil-food-web-and-humus]] deep in the soil through root shedding
- Fixing atmospheric nitrogen (legumes)
- Adding carbon to soil (grasses)
- Breaking up compacted or heavy soils
- Mining minerals from deep subsoil
- Attracting beneficial insects
- Suppressing weeds
- Preventing erosion

## The Root Shedding Cycle

Plants shed huge masses of roots constantly — not just in autumn when plants die. During wet/dry soil cycles:

1. Heavy rain or irrigation saturates soil; root hairs die from lack of oxygen
2. As soil dries, air flows into empty pores
3. Fueled by fresh oxygen and moisture, root hairs surge into growth
4. Soil becomes too dry for tender root hairs; they wither and die
5. Whole root sections die en masse, sloughing off and decomposing
6. This decaying organic matter builds humus deep in the soil

Many cover crops send roots 10-15 feet deep. No plow will ever incorporate organic matter to a fraction of that depth.

## Choosing Cover Crops: Key Decisions

### Annual vs. Perennial

- **Annuals:** For temporarily dormant garden beds (e.g., over winter)
- **Perennials:** For preparing a future orchard's soil, or areas not ready for immediate use
- Perennial clovers can cover garden paths; white Dutch clover as permanent living mulch

### Nitrogen-Fixing vs. Non-Fixing

Legumes host symbiotic microorganisms in root nodules that fix atmospheric nitrogen. Evidence that they feed companion plants while alive:

- Corn planted with beans is decidedly larger than corn alone
- Fruit trees planted with autumn olive or Siberian pea shrub grow faster
- Research shows 80% of nitrogen in interplanted ryegrass came from companion clover
- During root dieback cycles, nitrogen-fixing nodules slough off and feed surrounding plants

### Balancing Nitrogen and Carbon

Soil organisms consume 10-50x more carbon than nitrogen. A cover crop rich in nitrogen can deplete more organic matter than it adds. Always blend grasses or other non-legumes (10-40% of mix) to provide carbon balance.

## Cover Crop Functions

### Carbon Builders (Grasses)

- Add bulk organic matter
- Build soil structure
- Examples: annual ryegrass, oats, Sudan grass, perennial ryegrass, orchard grass, fescue, timothy

### Nitrogen Fixers (Legumes)

- Fix atmospheric nitrogen via root nodules
- Most abundant in early successional ecosystems
- Examples: clovers (crimson, white Dutch, white Ladino, New Zealand, strawberry), vetch, fava beans, cowpeas, field peas, soybeans, lablab, sesbania

### Soil Openers

Break up compacted or heavy soils with extensive root systems:

- **Rapeseed and mustard:** Extensive roots punch through hard subsoil
- **Alfalfa:** Deep roots aerate earth and add humus (requires fertile soil)
- **Daikon radish:** Grow in heavy soil, snap off at ground level; each radish breaks up clay and rots leaving a forearm-sized load of organic matter

### Mineral Miners

Ferry minerals from deep subsoil to the surface through their leaves:

- **Chicory:** Lengthy taproot seeks potassium, sulfur, calcium, magnesium
- **Buckwheat:** Converts insoluble phosphorus to plant-available form

### Insect Attractors

Blossoms attract beneficial insects:

- Buckwheat, phacelia, fava beans, clovers, bell beans, mustard, vetch

## The Multi-Function Cover Crop Mix

A blend of 5-10 varieties can simultaneously: build humus, add nitrogen, mine minerals, bust up heavy soil, and attract beneficial insects. Diversity in cover crops means diversity in [[soil-life]], which subdues disease and boosts plant growth.
