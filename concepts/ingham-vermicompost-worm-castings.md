# Vermicompost and Worm Castings in Compost Tea

Vermicompost (worm compost) and worm castings are among the most
valuable starting materials for [[compost-tea-aact]]. According to
Elaine Ingham's field guide, all good worm composts contain great
numbers of protozoa that are easily extracted into tea. Worm compost
is particularly noted for its high diversity of beneficial organisms,
making it an excellent inoculum source for restoring soil biology.

## Worm Castings vs. Worm Compost

Ingham draws an important technical distinction:
- **Worm castings** are technically the fecal pellets from worms
  only. If you have anything besides the little fecal pellets, it
  is not worm castings.
- **Worm compost** includes castings mixed with other material that
  has passed through the worm bin but may not be pure fecal pellets.
- Whether worm compost is better than pure worm castings remains an
  open question.

## Thermal vs. Worm Compost

A common question is whether thermal (heat-produced) compost is
better than worm compost. Ingham's answer is clear: equally good
compost can be made from either process. Neither is inherently
better:
- Either can be fungal-dominated or bacterial-dominated
- Either can become anaerobic if not managed properly
- Both can contain great sets of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and
  nematodes
- The more important consideration is finding a quality source
  close to you

The finished thermal compost mixed with worm compost works well at
about a 50-50 ratio, or 25-75 thermal-to-worm composts.

## Why Worm Compost Excels for Tea

Worm compost offers several advantages for compost tea production:

1. **High protozoa numbers**: All good worm composts contain great
   numbers of protozoa, easily extracted into AACT. Protozoa are
   critical for nutrient cycling -- they consume bacteria and
   release plant-available nitrogen.

2. **Beneficial nematodes**: Worm compost provides habitat
   conditions that support beneficial nematodes including
   bacterial-feeders, fungal-feeders, and predators. The
   maintenance of adequate moisture and high bacterial/fungal
   populations creates ideal conditions.

3. **Pathogen elimination**: Pathogens are killed by passage
   through the worm gut and contact with worm surfaces, not by
   heat. At least 75-80% of material must actually pass through
   the worm digestive system.

4. **Microarthropods**: Worm compost often contains microarthropods
   that are absent from thermal compost, adding another trophic
   level to the soil foodweb.

## Organism Requirements for Quality Vermicompost

Per Ingham's standards, quality vermicompost should contain
(measured per gram dry weight):
- 15-30+ micrograms active bacteria
- 150+ micrograms total bacteria
- 2-10+ micrograms active fungi
- 150+ micrograms total fungal biomass (fungal compost)
- 50,000+ protozoa per gram (25,000+ flagellates, 25,000+
  amoebae, 50-100 ciliates)
- 20-100 beneficial nematodes per gram
- No root-feeding nematodes

## Habitat Requirements

For vermicompost production:
- Room temperature conditions
- Adequate earthworm population to process material correctly
- At least 75-80% of material must pass through worm digestive
  system
- No weed seeds added, or materials must be pre-composted
- Pleasant smell (no anaerobic conditions)
- Moisture maintained at 45-75%

Testing requirements include documenting worm numbers per unit
volume, temperature, and oxygen data.

## Paper and Cardboard as Worm Feed

Paper is cellulose and provides food for fungi. Cardboard, being
thicker and longer-fiber, is even better for fungal growth. Key
considerations:
- Most modern inks are soy-based and safe
- Avoid paper with pre-1980s heavy-metal-based inks
- Question the glues, tackifiers, dyes in glossy or finished
  papers
- Cardboard layers help block sunlight from weed seeds
- Shredded paper and cardboard decompose faster
- Addition of paper and cardboard selects for fungi, including
  beneficial species
- Worm compost made with cardboard and paper tends toward fungal
  dominance

## Using Vermicompost in Tea

Ingham recommends a mix of good aerobic thermal compost with
aerobic worm castings in about a 50-50 or 25-75 ratio for AACT
production. The worm compost provides the protozoa and nematodes,
while thermal compost ensures pathogen-free starting material and
can provide higher fungal biomass if properly made.

## Compost Leachate vs. Compost Tea

Important distinction from the field guide:
- **Compost tea** is a water extract of compost that is BREWED,
  with nutrients added to enhance beneficial microbial growth
- **Compost leachate** is a passive process containing only
  soluble nutrients
- **Compost extracts** have no brewing period; organisms have not
  woken up or been encouraged to grow
- Leachates lack the complete organism diversity of properly
  brewed tea, though they contain enzymes, hormones, and humics
