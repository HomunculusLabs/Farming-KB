# Circadian Rhythm and the Internal Clock in Plants

## Overview

Plants possess an internal biological clock that allows them to track time
with remarkable precision, maintaining approximately 24-hour rhythms in
physiology and behavior even without external time cues. In "What a Plant
Knows," [[daniel-chamovitz]] explores how this circadian clock operates as a
form of [[biological-memory-non-neural]] — a molecular mechanism allowing plants to
anticipate regular environmental changes rather than merely reacting to
them. The plant circadian clock is a distributed network of interacting
genes and proteins that generates self-sustaining oscillations in gene
expression, hormone levels, enzyme activity, and metabolism throughout the
plant.

## The Nature of the Plant Circadian Clock

A circadian rhythm is defined by three characteristics: it persists under
constant conditions with a period of approximately 24 hours, it can be
reset (entrained) by external cues such as light and temperature, and its
period remains relatively constant across temperatures — a property known
as temperature compensation. These features distinguish true circadian
rhythms from simple environmental responses.

Plants were among the first organisms in which [[plant-circadian-rhythms]] were
documented. The daily movements of plant leaves, described by Androsthenes
in the fourth century BCE when he observed tamarind leaves moving up and
down each day, represent one of the oldest known examples of circadian
behavior. Linnaeus later catalogued flowers opening and closing at specific
times, creating floral clocks that could predict the time of day.

## The Molecular Architecture: CCA1, LHY, and TOC1

The molecular basis of the plant circadian clock centers on a feedback
loop involving several key genes. The two prominent morning-expressed
genes are CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1) and LATE ELONGATED
HYPOCOTYL (LHY). These encode related transcription factors whose
expression peaks at dawn. CCA1 and LHY proteins function together to
repress the expression of evening genes, most notably TIMING OF CAB
EXPRESSION 1 (TOC1).

TOC1 is a member of the PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATOR (PRR) family and its
expression peaks in the evening. As TOC1 protein accumulates, it promotes
the expression of CCA1 and LHY, completing a negative feedback loop. The
cycle takes approximately 24 hours: CCA1 and LHY rise at dawn, suppress
TOC1, then decline through the day; as they fall, TOC1 rises in the
evening and triggers the next cycle of CCA1 and LHY expression.

This core oscillator is reinforced by additional interlocking loops
involving other PRR family members (PRR7, PRR9) and ELF3, creating a
robust timing mechanism resistant to disruption. The entire system
operates in virtually every plant cell, meaning the clock is distributed
throughout the organism rather than centralized.

## Morning Versus Evening Gene Expression

The circadian clock regulates thousands of plant genes, organizing them
into morning and evening expression phases. Morning-phased genes include
CCA1 and LHY along with genes involved in photosynthesis, light
harvesting, and chlorophyll biosynthesis. These are activated in
anticipation of dawn, ensuring the photosynthetic machinery is fully
operational when sunlight arrives. This anticipatory regulation gives
plants a significant competitive advantage.

Evening-phased genes include TOC1 as well as genes involved in starch
metabolism, nitrogen assimilation, and defense responses. The clock
ensures that starch accumulated during the day is metabolized at the
appropriate rate through the night, preventing energy depletion before
dawn. Plants with disrupted circadian clocks grow significantly slower
than those with properly functioning clocks.
