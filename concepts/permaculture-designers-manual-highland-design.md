# Highland and Mountain Climate Design

> Source: [[bill-mollison]], *[[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual* (1988), Chapters 5, 10, and 12

## Overview

Highland and mountain environments present unique design challenges: steep slopes, altitude-related temperature drops, frost, snow, avalanche risk, permafrost, and rapid weather changes. The integrated land management approach is essential, working from protected hilltops to productive lowlands.

## Integrated Hill-to-Shore Profile

For a stable tropical land system on volcanic or hill profiles, the following features are needed:

1. **Protected skyline and hill forests**: Mine and release plant nutrients from upper slopes; used for limited forage and mulch. Their clearing brings compound catastrophes -- landslide, loss of nutrient, [[desertification]]. At the base of these forests, as slope eases to 15 degrees or less, water can be diverted or harvested.

2. **Midslope or keypoint**: Diversion of stream water here leads water out to ridges for terrace crop and village use. Complex cultivated forests and gardens established downslope. The stable plateau, the hill rising above the valley, and bench sites above flood reach and sheltered from hurricane are prime cluster settlement sites.

3. **Low slopes (2-15 degrees)**: Well suited to earth-shaping as terrace and padi, with limited grazing and innovative forestry. Sustainable agricultural areas with attention to sub-contoured agriculture, windbreak, and access.

4. **Coastal flats and valley floors**: Rich deep humus soils; extensive aquacultures or rich forest/orchard crop.

5. **Shorelines**: Preserved for 100 m or so inland for essential windbreak, shore stability, and cloud generation.

## Snow and Avalanche Management

### Snow Properties
- Snow is a near "black body" radiator (0.962-0.986 emissivity)
- Thin snow chills soil to 35 degrees C below air temperature
- Snow deeper than 15 cm acts as insulator, preventing deeper frost penetration
- Albedo ranges from 20% (muddy slush) to nearly 100% (fresh snow)
- Snow reflection at low sun angles adds 60-70% heat to walls

### Avalanche Risk
- Risk on slopes greater than 6 degrees with more than 15 cm of snow
- Avalanche chutes marked by aspen (downslope lean, lack of lower limbs, bare "trains" of different age classes)
- Blown-down forests radiating from avalanche snouts reveal past instability
- Rock avalanches produce similar effects, persisting 20-30 years in coniferous forests

### Snow Movement Modes
- **Microcreep**: advances downslope a few cm/day under freeze/thaw
- **Slip**: thick snow masses cover metres per day
- **Snow mound**: streamflow effect from rocky uplands
- **Snow slides**: velocities of 4-5 m/sec, can be arrested by terraces
- **Avalanches**: speeds up to 6.5 m/sec; impact 4-54 tonnes/sq m; compressed air blast travels at 330 m/sec

Eighty percent of avalanches occur where old, compact, or dry snows are dislodged by condensation moisture from air of more than 70% humidity.

### Avalanche Prevention
- Terraces can arrest snow slides
- Trees help hold snow above slopes, lessening load
- Solar radiation crust preserves slope stability (seasonal protection only)
- Avoid building in identified avalanche paths

## Permafrost

Permafrost occupies 47-50% of such areas as Canada, Russia, and Greenland:
- Peaty surfaces both prevent melting and rapidly admit cold
- Clearing, fire, or overgrazing plus ice particle scour can erode peaty layers
- Thaw creates bare sunken areas of clayey soils (mollisols) 2-45 m deep
- Erosion effects plus 9% volume loss of ice can be severe
- Fossil permafrost to 400 m thick; normal melting under peat is slow (~1 cm/year)
- Solar thaw about 5,000 times more effective than earth heat if peat is removed

### Loess Soils
Loess deposits (50-80% silica, 20-25% felspar) occur throughout Europe, Russia, China. They support excellent forests but dry out quickly. Loess is stable as steep walls to 300 m high, much used for underground housing in China.

## Cold Highland Grazing

Where frost penetration to 1.2 m in pastures is common (e.g. Iceland):
- Open ditches at 50 m spacing take spring thaw runoff from bogs
- Sheep, cattle, and horses shedded and hay-fed for 6 months/year
- Grazed in uplands for 3+ months, on lowland pastures for 3 months
- Frost heave needs levelling, re-sodding with peats, or rest from grazing
- Mixed-livestock economy and long-term [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]] help control Helminth worm parasites
- Kale, rape, ryegrass, barley, rye used as fodder, silage, or winter hay

### Deficiency Monitoring
- Cobalt and selenium deficiencies in sheep
- Calcium, copper, and phosphorus in pasture
- Lowland sheep have about ten times the nematode worm population of highland animals
