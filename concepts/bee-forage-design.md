# Bee Forage Design

**Bee forage design** is the practice of planning and planting landscapes to provide continuous nectar, pollen, and propolis resources for honeybee colonies throughout the active season. Within [[bill-mollison]] devotes considerable attention to this topic, framing the bee range as a productive landscape system that mirrors the principles of good farm design.

## Apiary Site Selection

Hive placement follows specific biological constraints. Commercial registered hive sites are sited approximately **5 kilometres apart** to restrict disease transmission between apiaries. Each site typically supports **100–150 hives**, scaled to the richness of the local forage resource.

The site itself must balance two competing demands:

- **Shelter** from extremes of cold, wet, wind, and direct sun
- **Representativeness** — the site should not be so sheltered that it creates a microclimate atypical of the surrounding range, as bees venturing out into genuinely adverse weather may perish

Bees prefer to fly at least **100 metres** from the hive before settling to forage. This flight distance assists in the evaporation of nectar into honey, so dense forage plantings should be established at a moderate distance rather than immediately adjacent to hives. Bees also harvest more efficiently from **clumped** nectar sources — hedgerows, fields, or defined blocks — than from the same number of plants scattered individually across a mosaic.

## Shelter and Hedgerow Systems

Cold winds are the single greatest restriction on bee foraging. Mollison recommends establishing **low hedgerows of 0.5–1.0 metres in height** leading from the hive site toward forage areas. Suitable hedgerow species include rosemary (*Salvia rosmarinus*), acacias (*[[acacia]]* spp.), and ground-level plantings of catmint (*Nepeta* spp.), capeweed, thyme (*Thymus* spp.), and field daisies. These hedgerows serve a dual purpose: they reduce wind stress on foraging bees while also contributing to the forage resource itself.

## Water Access

Clustered hives consume large quantities of water, primarily for hive cooling. The colony ventilates as a **superorganism**, inhaling and exhaling in an approximately **1.2-minute rhythm** to regulate internal temperature. Hive temperatures are normally maintained around **21°C**. In cold climates, hive insulation is critical — Danish beekeepers historically over-wintered colonies in thatched attic spaces, while elsewhere in Europe insulated hive houses are common. Conversely, in hot climates, hives require shade and ventilation to prevent honey loss through the energy expenditure of fanning.

Water access should be provided via **soaked mats or hessian** placed at pond edges so that bees cannot drown while drinking. Small ponds or troughs free of dragonflies are preferred over large ponds, where dragonflies are efficient bee predators.

## Pollen and Propolis Resources

While nectar is the primary energy source, **pollen** is essential for rearing replacement workers, particularly early in the season. A healthy colony requires reliable pollen access from the first warm days of spring through late autumn. Pollen diversity is as important as quantity — different pollen sources provide different amino acid profiles, and colonies with access to a wider range of pollen sources demonstrate better brood development and disease resistance.

Early-season pollen sources are critical for colony build-up after winter and include:

- **Willows** (*Salix* spp.), especially pussywillow
- **Acacias** (*Acacia* spp.)
- **Pines** (*Pinus* spp.)
- Vine crops that yield early pollen

**Propolis** — the hard, waxy, antimicrobial resin bees use to seal wind gaps, reinforce comb structure, and coat hive interiors — is gathered from relatively few botanical sources. Within the hive, propolis serves as a natural antimicrobial agent, reducing bacterial and fungal loads. Mollison identifies poplars, pines, and *Xanthorrhoea* spp. as key propolis providers. Only a small number of such sources are needed across the entire forage range, making their inclusion in planting plans straightforward.

## Forage Species Selection

Mollison recommends a minimum of **35 main forage species** distributed across the season to ensure reliability, as species such as clover and eucalypts may vary significantly in yield from year to year. Forage is classified along two axes:

### Seasonal Classification

| Period | Function |
|--------|----------|
| **Early season** | Colony build-up after winter; pollen-critical |
| **Mid season** | Peak honey production |
| **Late season** | Winter stores and late pollen |

### Product Classification

- **Classical (premium) honeys** — highly preferred by consumers and beekeepers. Examples include heather honeys (sage, thyme, catmint, rosemary), pine or basswood (*Tilia* spp.) honeys, marsh tupelo honeys, leatherwood (*Eucryphia*) honeys, and citrus honeys
- **Bulk honeys** — high-volume producers suitable for blending. Sources include clovers, eucalypts, acacias, field crops (mustard, buckwheat), and Rosaceae family fruits (blackberry, raspberry, apple)
- **Special honeys and pollens** — distinctive products from species such as some acacias and goldenrod. Notably, certain pollens that may cause asthma if inhaled by susceptible individuals (e.g., goldenrod) can, when consumed in honey form, act to **desensitise allergic reactions** — a form of natural immunotherapy. This property has been traditionally recognised and is supported by modern allergological understanding of oral tolerance mechanisms.

## The Pollination Dividend

Crops dependent on bee pollination — including kiwifruit, bramble berries, small fruits, mustards and brassicas, clovers, pome fruits, buckwheat, acacias, and stone pine — can outyield equivalent crops in bee-deficient areas by a factor of **3–10×** when grown within one mile of hives. This pollination dividend makes apiary placement a form of **shared agricultural infrastructure** that benefits not only the beekeeper but every neighbouring farm and garden within the forage radius.

## Hive Insulation and Climate Adaptation

Hive temperature management is a critical design consideration that varies with climate:

- **Cold climates** — insulation is essential. Danish beekeepers historically over-wintered colonies in thatched attic spaces. Elsewhere in Europe, purpose-built insulated hive houses protect colonies from freezing temperatures. Insulation reduces the energy bees must expend on heating, preserving honey stores for winter survival rather than thermoregulation.
- **Hot climates** — hives require shade and good ventilation. Without adequate cooling, colonies lose honey energy through the metabolic cost of fanning. Reflective hive covers, shade trees, and open-sided hive houses all contribute to heat management.
- **Temperate climates** — a balance of morning sun (to warm colonies for early foraging) and afternoon shade (to prevent overheating) is ideal.

The bee colony's internal temperature of approximately **21°C** is maintained through collective fanning behaviour. When ambient temperatures rise significantly above this threshold, the colony's water consumption increases dramatically, making reliable water access even more critical in hot weather.

## Hive Management Considerations

Several practical management factors complement the landscape design of a bee forage system:

- **Pollen traps** should be fitted to hives during periods of high pollen yield. Mollison notes that well-managed hives should yield approximately the **same weight of pollen as honey**. Pollen provides a high-protein additive suitable for supplementing flours and starchy foods.
- **Hive density** at each site (100–150 hives) must be matched to the carrying capacity of the surrounding forage. Overstocking leads to competition between colonies, reduced honey yields per hive, and increased susceptibility to disease.
- **Disease management** through site spacing (5 km between apiaries) is a landscape-level intervention that reduces pathogen transmission without requiring chemical treatments.
- **Honey house design** — a well-designed processing facility should be positioned centrally within the bee farm, with adequate workspace, storage, and equipment for extraction, settling, and bottling.

## Co-Benefits of Bee Forage Plantings

The plant species selected for bee forage serve multiple functions beyond nectar and pollen production:

- **Windbreaks** — hedgerow forage species simultaneously shelter livestock, crops, and buildings
- **Soil stabilisation** — deep-rooted species such as clovers and [[comfrey]] prevent erosion on slopes and watercourses
- **Wildlife habitat** — flowering plants attract predatory insects, insectivorous birds, and butterflies
- **Nitrogen fixation** — leguminous forage species (clovers, acacias) improve soil fertility for neighbouring crops
- **Aesthetic value** — successive bloom across the season provides continuous colour and fragrance in the landscape. This has practical as well as ornamental value: landscapes that look and smell productive tend to receive better stewardship from landholders and the broader community.

## Regional Adaptation

Bee forage design is inherently region-specific. The species listed in Mollison's cool temperate table are illustrative rather than prescriptive. Designers in Mediterranean, subtropical, tropical, or arid regions must develop their own locally adapted forage lists by consulting:

- Local beekeeping associations and experienced beekeepers
- Regional herbaria and botanical records
- Observation of existing plant communities and their attractiveness to pollinators
- Agricultural extension services and forestry departments

The underlying design principles — temporal spread across the season, clumped plantings, hedgerow shelter, reliable water, and minimum species diversity of 35 — remain constant regardless of climate zone.

## Designing the Bee Farm

The ideal bee farm integrates apiary placement with crop production. Mollison sketches a layout in which cross-wind forage hedgerows shelter bee-dependent seed crops, herbs, and vines, while adequate water, pollen sources, shelter, and a well-designed honey house complete the system. The bee range and the production farm are, in this model, a single designed landscape.

The bee farm concept represents a synthesis of all the elements discussed: thoughtful apiary placement, diverse forage plantings across the season, water provision, and integration with pollination-dependent crops. When these elements are combined, the result is a productive system that yields honey and pollen for the beekeeper while dramatically increasing the yields of every neighbouring crop within pollination range.
