# Purdue Garden Planning and Layout

A guide to planning and laying out a home [[fukuoka-dutch-priest-vegetable-garden-spiritual-renewal]], covering site selection, soil preparation, crop arrangement, [[query-what-is-succession-planting-and-how-do-i-plan-a-succession-planting-schedule]], and environmental hazards such as juglone toxicity from walnut trees. Based on Purdue University Cooperative Extension Service guidelines (HO-32-W).

## Why Planning Matters

Planning is the first and most basic step in home [[home-vegetable-garden-planning]]. Proper planning saves time at planting, provides accurate [[vegetable-seed-and-transplant-guide]] quantities, and ensures the garden satisfies household needs throughout the growing season. A well-planned garden prevents overcrowding, poor sun exposure, and inefficient use of space. Planning also lets you calculate how much seed, how many transplants, and what supplies you will need before the season begins.

## Garden Location

### Ideal Conditions

Vegetables grow best in an open, level area with loose, rich, well-drained soil. Most vegetables require a minimum of 6 hours of direct sunlight daily. Avoid heavy clays, excessively sandy soils, and shady spots. The garden should be accessible to a water source and close enough to the house for convenient daily attention during the growing season.

### Soil Improvement for Poor Sites

If soil quality is poor, incorporate 2 to 3 inches of topsoil, peat moss, strawy manure, compost, or decomposed leaves plus balanced fertilizer. The more organic matter added, the more the soil improves. Sandy soils benefit particularly from organic additions for moisture retention, while clay soils need organic matter to improve drainage and structure.

### Avoiding Tree and Shrub Competition

Do not plant vegetables near trees and shrubs. In these locations, vegetables must compete with established plants for light, nutrients, and water. Tree roots extend well beyond the drip line and aggressively draw moisture and nutrients from garden soil, leaving vegetables stressed and underperforming. Even trees that appear a reasonable distance away can have extensive root systems reaching into garden beds.

## Juglone Toxicity from Walnut Trees

### The Problem

Walnut trees (particularly black walnut, Juglans nigra) produce a chemical compound called juglone (5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone) that is toxic to many plants, especially tomatoes. Juglone is present in all parts of the walnut tree including roots, leaves, bark, and nut hulls, and is released into surrounding soil through root exudation, leaf [[dighton-pine-needle-litter-decomposition-fungal-succession]], and rain washing from canopy surfaces.

### Sensitive Plants

The most notoriously sensitive vegetable is the tomato. Other susceptible plants include potatoes, eggplants, peppers, and some cole crops. Sensitive plants may eventually wilt and die when grown within the root zone of a walnut tree, even when all other growing conditions appear ideal.

### Safe Distance

Because walnut tree roots remain active and continue releasing juglone for several years even after the tree is cut down, a **minimum distance of 50 feet** is recommended between walnut trees and juglone-sensitive plants. This is one of the most critical site-selection considerations for gardens located near walnut trees. See [[purdue-vegetable-planting-reference-table]] for specific spacing and timing data for each crop.

### Recommended Layout: 20 by 50 Foot Garden

The Purdue guide provides a sample 20 by 50 foot garden plan illustrating these key layout principles:

- **Early cool-season crops** (spinach, lettuce, radishes, beets, onions, carrots) placed together in the southern portion for quick access and early harvest
- **Warm-season crops** (snap beans, tomatoes, corn) in the center and northern portion
- **Tall-growing crops** (sweet corn, tomatoes, pole beans) grouped at the north end to avoid shading
- **Succession plantings** of the same crop at staggered dates for extended harvest

## Layout Principles

### Tall Crops at the North End

Place tall-growing crops at the north end of the garden to prevent them from casting shade on shorter vegetables to the south. Crops that should go at the north end include sweet corn, tomatoes (especially indeterminate varieties), and pole beans on trellises. This simple principle alone can significantly improve overall garden productivity by ensuring all plants receive adequate sunlight.

### Grouping Early and Late Crops

Keep early, cool-season vegetables together so the space can be reused after harvest. Good early crops include lettuce, radishes, green onions, spinach, and beets. After these early crops are harvested, replant the rows with late-season vegetables such as snap beans, carrots, broccoli, cabbage, or fall greens for a second harvest from the same space.

### Block Planting for Corn

Plant sweet corn in blocks of several short rows rather than a single long row. Corn is wind-pollinated, and block planting ensures adequate pollen transfer between plants for full ear development. A minimum of three to four short rows is recommended for reliable pollination and well-filled ears.

## Succession Planting

Succession planting stretches the harvest season and maximizes garden productivity. Instead of one large planting, make several smaller weekly or biweekly plantings of the same crop.

### Crops for Succession Planting

- **Snap beans:** Every 2-3 weeks from May 10 through July 20
- **Sweet corn:** 2-week intervals from May 10 through July 10
- **Cabbage:** Multiple plantings from April 5 through June 5
- **Lettuce:** Mar 20 to Jun 15, then again Aug 15 to Sep 1
- **Radishes:** Mar 20 to Jun 15, then Aug 15 to Sep 15
- **Spinach:** Mar 20 to Jun 15, then Aug 15 to Sep 1

### Replanting Strategies

Rows of early-harvested crops can be replanted immediately with a different crop. Radish rows (25-30 days) followed by snap beans or carrots. Spinach rows (40-45 days) followed by broccoli or cabbage. Lettuce rows (40-50 days) followed by a second lettuce planting or fall greens. This relay approach doubles the productivity of limited garden space.

## Soil pH Considerations

Most vegetables perform best in slightly acidic soil with a pH of 6.0 to 6.8. Before planting, test your soil pH through your local county Extension office. Agricultural sulfur can be used to lower pH for crops like potatoes that prefer more acidic conditions. Lime is rarely needed in many midwestern soils and may actually adversely affect plant nutrition if applied unnecessarily. See [[purdue-vegetable-planting-reference-table]]
