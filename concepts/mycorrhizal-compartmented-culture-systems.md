# Mycorrhizal Compartmented Culture Systems

Mycorrhizal compartmented culture systems are experimental designs that physically separate the root zone from the hyphal zone, enabling researchers to study nutrient and carbon transport by mycorrhizal fungi without interference from direct root uptake. These systems range from simple pot-based designs to sophisticated in vitro monoxenic cultures, and have been instrumental in demonstrating the role of mycorrhizal fungi in phosphorus uptake and translocation.

## Historical Development

The use of compartmented systems began in the late 1960s and early 1970s, when studies by Hattingh et al. (1973), Pearson and Tinker (1975), Rhodes and Gerdemann (1975), and Cooper and Tinker (1978, 1981) first convincingly demonstrated that AM fungi transport phosphorus from soil to host plants. These pioneering studies used bi-compartmented pot culture systems with isotopic tracers, where roots and fungi developed in one compartment (root compartment, RC) while the other compartment (hyphal compartment, HC) was restricted to the exclusive development of the extraradical mycelium (ERM).

## Principles of Compartmented Systems

The fundamental principle is the physical separation of root and hyphal growth zones using barriers that allow fungal hyphae but not roots to pass. Typically, this is achieved using nylon or mesh screens (25-37 micrometer pore size) between compartments. The root compartment contains the plant and mycorrhizal inoculum, while the hyphal compartment contains only the extraradical mycelium growing through the barrier. Isotopic tracers or test substances can then be applied exclusively to the hyphal compartment to study transport.

## In Vitro Monoxenic Systems

Compartmented in vitro (monoxenic) culture systems were developed to overcome limitations of soil-based systems. These systems use root-organ cultures (typically Ri T-DNA transformed carrot roots) as hosts, with the two compartments contained within bi-compartmented Petri plates. The advantages include axenic conditions (no contaminating microorganisms), direct visualization of both partners, easy recovery of roots and fungal mycelium, and the ability to perform physiological and molecular studies on extraradical fungal mycelium.

## Root-Organ Culture Systems

Most monoxenic cultures of AM fungi are maintained on Ri T-DNA transformed root-organs, particularly Ri T-DNA transformed carrot (Daucus carota L.) roots. These transformed roots offer greater growth potential than non-transformed roots in nutrient-limited culture media. AM fungi in association with these transformed roots show greater intraradical colonization and sustain higher extraradical mycelium development compared to non-transformed systems.

## Culture Media

The most widely used media for in vitro culture of AM fungi are the modified Strullu-Romand (MSR) medium and the minimal (M) medium. These media contain specific mineral and vitamin compositions optimized for fungal growth. A key consideration is that gelling agents such as Gel-gro and Phytagel may contain significant quantities of potassium and phosphorus that should be accounted for in experimental designs. Media pH is adjusted to 5.5-5.7, and sucrose (10 g/L) is added as a carbon source.

## Advantages Over Soil-Based Systems

Compartmented in vitro systems overcome several limitations of soil-based compartmented pot systems. These include: (1) elimination of undesirable microorganisms that could influence element bioavailability, (2) direct visualization of both partners' development, (3) simplified recovery of roots and extraradical mycelium, (4) elimination of element interaction with the soil matrix, (5) prevention of tracer leakage from the hyphal to root compartment, and (6) easier performance of physiological and molecular studies.

## Applications in Transport Studies

These systems have been widely used to study phosphorus, nitrogen, and micronutrient transport by AM fungi. By applying isotopically labelled elements exclusively to the hyphal compartment, researchers can quantify the rate and extent of fungal-mediated nutrient uptake and translocation to the host plant. The systems have also been used to study carbon allocation from host to fungus, heavy metal transport, and the effects of environmental factors on mycorrhizal function.

## Heavy Metal and Contaminant Studies

Compartmented systems have proven especially valuable for studying heavy metal uptake and translocation by mycorrhizal fungi, including elements such as radiocaesium, cadmium, and zinc. By restricting labelled contaminants to the hyphal compartment, researchers can definitively demonstrate fungal-mediated metal transport without confounding root uptake. This application is particularly relevant for understanding [[mycorrhizal-mineral-weathering-nutrient-acquisition]] and the role of [[siderophores-mycorrhizal-fungi]] in metal chelation and transport.

## Applications in Inoculum Production

Beyond research, compartmented culture systems have practical applications in [[mycorrhizal-inoculum-production]]. The ability to grow AM fungi axenically on transformed root organs allows for the production of clean, contaminant-free inoculum. Bi-compartmented designs facilitate the harvesting of extraradical mycelium and spores from the hyphal compartment without root contamination, providing high-quality propagules for [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-techniques]] in agriculture and restoration.

## Multi-Compartment and Advanced Designs

More recent innovations include three-compartment systems that separate the root zone, a proximal hyphal zone, and a distal hyphal zone, enabling the study of translocation processes within the fungal mycelium itself. These advanced designs allow researchers to measure nutrient uptake at hyphal tips and subsequent transport back to the root, providing insights into the spatial dynamics of [[mycorrhizal-nutrient-transport]] that simpler two-compartment systems cannot resolve.

## Standardization and Reproducibility

A key challenge in mycorrhizal research using compartmented systems is the lack of standardized protocols across laboratories. Variations in mesh pore size, compartment dimensions, growth media composition, plant species, and fungal isolates can all influence experimental outcomes and make comparisons between studies difficult. Efforts to develop standard operating procedures for [[in-vitro-mycorrhizal-systems]] are ongoing, with the goal of improving reproducibility and enabling meta-analyses across different research groups studying [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]].

## Nitrogen Transport Studies

While phosphorus transport has been the primary focus of compartmented system research, these systems are equally valuable for studying nitrogen uptake and translocation by mycorrhizal fungi. Using 15N-labelled compounds in the hyphal compartment, researchers have demonstrated that AM fungi can take up both inorganic (ammonium, nitrate) and organic (amino acids) nitrogen forms and translocate them to host plants. This has important implications for understanding [[mycorrhizal-nutrient-uptake]] and the role of mycorrhizal fungi in the [[composting-and-soil-biology]].

## Molecular and Genomic Applications

Compartmented culture systems provide unique opportunities for molecular and genomic studies of mycorrhizal function. The axenic conditions of in vitro systems allow for RNA extraction from pure fungal mycelium without root or soil contamination, enabling transcriptomic analyses of fungal gene expression during nutrient transport. Proteomic studies using [[mycorrhizal-diversity-assessment]] tools have identified key transporter proteins involved in phosphorus and metal uptake by the extraradical mycelium. These molecular insights complement physiological measurements obtained from compartmented systems.

## Combining Compartmented Systems with Microscopy

Compartmented culture systems are increasingly combined with advanced microscopy techniques to visualize nutrient transport in real time. Confocal laser scanning microscopy and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) allow researchers to observe hyphal architecture and physiological activity within the hyphal compartment. When coupled with isotopic tracers used in [[isotopic-tracers-mycorrhizal-research]], these imaging approaches provide spatial resolution of transport processes that bulk measurements alone cannot achieve. The integration of structural and functional data from compartmented systems continues to advance our understanding of how [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] interface with soil environments.

## Limitations

Despite their advantages, compartmented systems have limitations. The artificial conditions of in vitro systems may not fully represent field conditions. The use of transformed roots may introduce artifacts. The scale of in vitro systems is small, limiting the amount of material available for biochemical analyses. Air gaps between compartments may not completely eliminate solute movement between compartments. Additionally, the growth rates and morphology of fungal structures may differ from those in soil.
