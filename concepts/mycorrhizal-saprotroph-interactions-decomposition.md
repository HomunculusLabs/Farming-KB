# Mycorrhizal-Saprotroph Interactions in Decomposition

## Overview

The interaction between [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and [[saprotrophic-fungi]] in soil represents one of the most important yet poorly understood relationships in ecosystem ecology. These two functional groups compete for and share organic resources, with consequences for decomposition rates, [[nutrient-mineralization]], and the ultimate fate of nutrients released during litter breakdown. The balance between them significantly shapes forest nutrient cycling.

## The Gadgil Effect

Gadgil and Gadgil (1971, 1975) first proposed that mycorrhizal tree roots suppress leaf litter decomposition by competing with saprotrophic fungi for resources. Their experiments showed that leaf litter decomposed more slowly in the presence of mycorrhizal plants than in their absence (Table 1). This phenomenon, known as the "Gadgil effect," has been both supported and contradicted by subsequent studies:

- **Support**: Dighton et al. (1987) found that the saprotroph *[[mycena-galopus]]* reduced the decomposition potential of the EM fungi *[[suillus-luteus]]* and *Hebeloma crustuliniforme* on pine seedlings.
- **Contradiction**: Berg and Lindberg (1980) found no effect of tree roots on litter decomposition in a northern coniferous forest.
- **Reversal**: Zhu and Ehrenfeld (1996) showed that roots actually increased saprotrophic activity and decomposition rates in an oligotrophic forest, with enhanced nematode populations in rooted chambers.

The inconsistency among studies likely reflects differences in overall soil fertility and whether the system depends on readily available or unavailable nutrient sources.

## Species-Specific Decomposition Capacity

Colpaert and van Tichelen (1996) demonstrated that the saprotroph *Lepista nuda* decomposed beech leaf litter far more effectively than the EM species *Thelephora terrestris*, *Suillus bovinus*, or *Paxillus involutus*. Nitrogen mineralization occurred only in the presence of *Lepista*. They concluded that while EM fungi can decompose litter in the absence of competing saprotrophs, saprotrophs are superior competitors for organic resources and suppress EM decomposing abilities.

## Phosphorus Transfer from Saprotrophs to Mycorrhizae

Lindahl et al. (1999) revealed a synergistic interaction: EM fungi (*Paxillus involutus* and *Suillus variegatus*) captured up to 25% of the phosphorus present in the mycelium of the wood-decomposing saprotroph *Hypholoma fasciculare* and translocated it to host pine seedlings within 30 days. The reciprocal transfer was three orders of magnitude lower. This demonstrates that EM fungi can act as "nutrient pirates," siphoning mineral nutrients derived from saprotrophic activity more efficiently than the saprotroph itself can retain them.

## Defoliation and Photosynthate Effects

Changes in photosynthate availability alter mycorrhizal community composition. Cullings et al. (2001) showed that 50% defoliation of lodgepole pine in a mixed pine-spruce forest in Yellowstone altered EM species composition without significantly changing total colonization or species richness. The dominant *Inocybe* species became rare in defoliated plots, while Agaricoid and Suilloid species dominated both defoliated and control plots.

### Herbivory and Belowground Allocation

Winter browsing of willow by elk induces higher shoot biomass production, fewer but larger shoots and leaves, and flower inhibition (Peinetti et al., 2001). These changes in aboveground architecture shift resource allocation patterns, placing different demands on mycorrhizal communities. Reduced belowground carbohydrate allocation would impair mycorrhizal support, but increased nitrogen demand would require active mycorrhizal function—the net effect remains largely unknown.

## Ecosystem Context and Tropical Forests

The dependence of tropical trees on EM versus AM associations may be linked to the ability of EM associates to decompose leaf litter. Singer and da Silva Araujo (1979) showed that in white podsol campinarana soils with large humus accumulations and slow decomposition, trees were obligately ectomycorrhizal. In latisol-terra-firma soils with faster breakdown and higher nutrient availability, saprotrophs dominated and trees primarily associated with AM fungi.

## Stable Isotope Evidence

Hobbie et al. (1999) used natural abundance δ¹⁵N and δ¹³C signatures to show that mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi occupy distinct ecological niches. EM fruit bodies had consistently higher δ¹⁵N values and lower δ¹³C values than saprotrophs, suggesting minimal direct competition for nitrogen or carbohydrates. The enriched ¹⁵N in mycorrhizal fungi may result from isotopic fractionation during enzymatic reactions producing depleted amino acids passed to host plants. Enriched carbon signatures of EM fungi likely reflect their use of isotopically enriched simple photosynthates compared to the complex compound mixtures in decomposing leaves.

## Ecological Significance

The interaction between mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi determines the balance between:

1. **Nutrient retention** in the plant community vs. release to soil pools
2. **Decomposition rate** of organic matter and associated carbon fluxes
3. **Nutrient acquisition efficiency** of the plant community
4. **Carbon allocation patterns** between aboveground and belowground compartments

In temperate forest ecosystems dominated by EM fungi, the interconnectedness of plants through mycorrhizal bridges optimizes nutrient retention and maximizes capture of nutrients mineralized from organic residues, either directly by EM fungi or through their interaction with saprotrophs.
