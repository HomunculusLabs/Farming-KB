## Psilocybe coprinifacies Taxonomic History

The taxonomic story of Psilocybe coprinifacies illustrates the complexity of neurotropic fungal classification. Guzmán (1983) initially considered it a doubtful species due to insufficient understanding and misidentifications. European authors (Herink 1950, Pouzar 1953, Semerdzieva and Nerud 1973, Auer et al. 1980, Wurst et al. 1984, Semerdzieva et al. 1986) recognized it in Czechoslovakia. Sebek (1983) then described [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] emend. Krieglsteiner, and further placed *P. bohemica*, *P. mairei*, and *P. serbica* as synonyms of *P. cyanescens*.

## Implications for Field Research

These patterns of confusion have several important implications:

- **Ethnographic records must be verified chemically**: Indigenous names and traditional uses cannot be assumed to correlate with neurotropic properties
- **Market specimens may be unreliable**: Specimens collected from markets rather than directly from the field may have degraded active compounds or be entirely different species
- **Recreational identification is particularly error-prone**: Users who identify fungi based on visual similarity alone are at high risk of consuming the wrong species
- **Literature errors compound over time**: A single misidentification in a seminal paper can propagate through decades of subsequent research and popular writing
- **Bluing reaction is a useful but imperfect indicator**: While bluing is generally associated with psilocybin content, not all bluing species have been chemically confirmed, and some non-bluing species may contain active compounds

The authors of the 1998 worldwide survey concluded that whatever region mycologists study materials from, they find new species, suggesting that the true diversity of neurotropic fungi remains significantly underdocumented and that careful taxonomic work remains essential.

## Five Historical Periods of Neurotropic Fungi Knowledge

Guzmán, following Stamets (1978, 1996), identified five historical periods in the study of neurotropic fungi:

1. **Ancient use**: Indigenous peoples across North America, Mesoamerica, Siberia, and New Guinea used neurotropic fungi in their traditions, long before any scientific documentation.

2. **Uncertain early studies**: The beginning of the 20th century saw erroneous or uncertain identifications, most notably Schultes's (1939) assignment of all Mexican hallucinogenic fungi to Panaeolus.

3. **Scientific investigation**: Beginning with Wasson's studies in the 1950s, which followed Schultes's earlier work, rigorous mycological and chemical investigation began. This period saw the involvement of Heim, Singer, Hofmann, and others who established the taxonomic framework still largely in use.

4. **Recreational use and cultural degradation**: The 1960s brought the adoption of neurotropic fungi as recreational drugs, leading to a decline in traditional indigenous use, particularly in Mexico. This period forced governments worldwide to prohibit the use and commerce of these fungi.

5. **Recapitulation**: The current period involves a return to careful scientific study, with new species descriptions and more sophisticated chemical analyses building on the foundations laid in earlier decades.

## The Bluing Reaction as Taxonomic Indicator

The authors of the 1998 survey used the bluing reaction as a key characteristic for confirming neurotropic status in Psilocybe. Species belonging to sections Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae, Cubensies, Mexicanae, Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae, and Zapotecorum typically exhibit bluing. However, the bluing reaction is not universal among neurotropic species — some confirmed psilocybin-containing species do not blue conspicuously, and some non-neurotropic species may show discoloration that could be confused with bluing.

Species such as [[psilocybe-coprophila]], and Psilocybe pseudobullacea were specifically excluded as neurotropic fungi despite being confusedly reported as hallucinogenic in some literature.
