# Neurotropic Fungi Taxonomic Confusion and Misidentification

The taxonomy of neurotropic (hallucinogenic) fungi has been plagued by confusion since the earliest scientific studies. Misidentifications between edible, poisonous, and psychoactive species have led to erroneous reports, dangerous exposures, and distorted ethnographic records. The Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998) worldwide survey identified numerous instances where taxonomic errors propagated through the literature for decades.

## Historical Sources of Confusion

### Early Misidentification of Mexican Species

When Richard Evans Schultes first studied neurotropic fungi in Mexico in 1939, he concluded that all hallucinogenic Mexican mushrooms belonged to the genus Panaeolus, specifically identifying them as *P. campanulatus* var. *sphinctrinus*. Rolf Singer followed this classification in 1949 and also considered [[psilocybe-cubensis]] as a neurotropic species based on Schultes's unidentified collections. These early errors established a foundation of taxonomic confusion that took years to correct.

It was not until Roger Heim's thorough studies in the 1950s, combined with the Wassons' ethnographic work, that the genus Psilocybe was recognized as the primary genus containing psilocybin in Mexico. By 1958, Singer and Smith had begun to clarify the taxonomy, but many of their contemporaries continued to perpetuate earlier errors. The early confusion was compounded by the fact that several different indigenous groups in Mexico used their own names for sacred fungi, making cross-referencing between ethnographic and taxonomic records extremely difficult.

### The Stropharia-Psilocybe Reclassification

The species now known as [[psilocybe-cubensis]] was originally described as *Stropharia cubensis* by Earle in 1906 from a specimen collected in Cuba. It was later transferred to the genus Psilocybe, a move that itself has been debated. Some mycologists have argued that certain species placed in Psilocybe may belong in other genera. Kühner and Romagnesi (1953) and Kühner (1980) considered Psilocybe, Hypholoma, and Stropharia as belonging to the same genus (Psilocybe sensu Kühner). Guzmán (1983, 1995) followed a modification of Singer (1986) excluding Section Chrysocystidiatae, which is the classification system used in the 1998 worldwide survey. Noordeloos (1995) proposed yet another arrangement that included species such as *P. aeruginosa*, *P. albonitens*, *P. aurantiaca*, and *P. capnoides* within Psilocybe, which the survey authors did not accept.

### Panaeolus antillarum Confusion

One of the most persistent and dangerous confusions involves Panaeolus antillarum, a species often erroneously identified as Copelandia spp. by recreational users. Both fungi present white fruit bodies and grow together in the same cow dung substrates. However, *Panaeolus antillarum* does not turn blue when bruised and is widely considered an edible species distributed throughout the tropics, with occasional appearances in Europe (where it has been reported as *P. phalenarum* by Palacios and Laskibar, 1995).

The danger of this confusion is significant: recreational users seeking the potent psilocybin-containing [[copelandia-cyanescens]] may inadvertently collect and consume large quantities of the inactive *P. antillarum*, potentially exposing themselves to other truly toxic species found in the same habitats. The problem is compounded by the fact that both genera belong to the Panaeoloideae and share similar morphological features to the untrained eye.

### Schizophyllum commune and Psilocybe mexicana Naming Collision

The Mazatec Indians of Oaxaca, Mexico, refer to certain mushrooms by the name "nise" (meaning "little bird"). This name is used for both [[psilocybe-mexicana]], a confirmed psilocybin-containing species, and [[schizophyllum-commune]], a common edible fungus sold in popular markets throughout Guatemala and southeastern Mexico. The naming collision led Southcott (1974) to report *S. commune* as a hallucinogenic fungus in Australia, a claim that has no basis in chemistry. *S. commune* is a globally distributed wood-decay fungus with no neurotropic properties whatsoever. The Mazatec use of the same name for chemically unrelated species illustrates the fundamental limitation of relying on indigenous nomenclature for chemical identification.

### Chlorophyllum molybdites Toxicity Debate

[[chlorophyllum-molybdites]] illustrates how ethnobotanical reports can create contradictory assessments. The Yoruba tribe of Africa calls it "a jegba ariwo-orun" (meaning "eat and hear voices from heaven"), suggesting neurotropic use. Adewusi et al. (1993) reported neurotropic properties based on experiments with weanling rats. Yet Lincoff (1981), Guzmán (1977), Portugal et al. (1992), and Duffy and Vergeer (1977) all classify it as poisonous. Singer (1969) noted that "apparently not all forms or races are poisonous" and reported poisonings in the USA, Argentina, Philippines, and East Africa, while Heim (1978) considered it edible in Africa. Pegler (1977, 1983) commented that there is a considerable amount of confusion about whether this species is toxic or edible.

## Cordyceps and Elaphomyces in Mexican Ceremonies

The Trique Indians of Alta Mixteca, Oaxaca, use Cordyceps capitata and Cordyceps ophioglossoides in special nocturnal ceremonies. These species are taxonomically related to [[claviceps-purpurea]], the ergot fungus known for producing hallucinogenic alkaloids. In ceremony, they are eaten either with [[psilocybe-muliercula]] or alone. A specimen of Elaphomyces (truffle-like fungi including *E. granulatus*, *E. muricatus*, and *E. reticulatus*), which serve as hosts for the Cordyceps, is placed at the center of the ceremony room as a "director" of the ritual.

Hofmann's failure to detect indole compounds in specimens of *Cordyceps capitata* and *C. ophioglossoides* may be explained by the age of the specimens. Wasson had collected them from a popular market in Mexico, where they had been preserved for a long time. In one documented case, a participant experienced vivid hallucinations while in a closed room with the fungi, and the hallucinations stopped upon leaving the room to breathe fresh air — suggesting the active compounds may be volatile. The Trique also use Elaphomyces species for wound healing and what they describe as "rejuvenation of the organism" (Trappe et al. 1979).

## Puffballs and Gasteromycetes as Sacred Fungi

Several gasteromycetes (puffballs and stinkhorns) have been used in sacred contexts despite lacking neurotropic properties. Burk (1983) described magic and religious uses of puffballs among North American Indian peoples, where fungi growing in fairy rings on prairies were called "fallen stars." Guzmán (1994) documented puffballs used by Mexican Indians in traditional medicine, including [[lycoperdon-perlatum]] which forms fairy rings in grasslands.

The most notable cases are [[lycoperdon-mixtecorum]] and [[lycoperdon-marginatum]] (synonyms Vascellum qudenii and Lycoperdon candidum), reported by Heim and Wasson (1962) as narcotic fungi among the Mixtec Indians. However, Ott et al. (1975) later determined these were the same common edible puffballs found throughout Mexico. The Mixtec use appeared to stem from confusion with traditional neurotropic fungi rather than genuine psychoactivity. Guzmán (1977, 1997) confirmed these species are edible and common in Mexico.

## Ganoderma lobatum: The Fungus as Saint

In Chignahuapan, Puebla, Mexico, a church was built in honor of Ganoderma lobatum. The Indians of the region regard the fungus itself as a saint. The inner surface of a specimen found in the last century bore an arresting sketch of Christ with a sun and moon on each side. While there is no evidence of neurotropic properties, the cult likely relates to the use of active Psilocybe species common in the region. The [[dictyophora-indusiata]] (with three varieties) from the Phallales order was also listed among sacred fungi despite uncertain chemical status.

## Polypores as Alleged Psychotropics

Oldridge et al. (1989) classified certain polypores containing hordenine, N-methyltyramine, and tyramine as psychotropic, including [[laetiporus-sulphureus]] and [[meripilus-giganteus]]. These compounds produce dizziness and disorientation but are not true hallucinogens. *L. sulphureus* is one of the most important edible fungi in Mexico and worldwide. Thoen (1982) documented the use of polypores in religious ceremonies for magic, including Polyporus tuberaster, [[poria-cocos]], [[ganoderma-lucidum]], and [[fomes-fomentarius]]. In Alaska, Phellinus igniarus and *F. fomentarius* were observed as narcotic fungi, though their psychoactivity remains unverified.

## Psilocybe coprinifacies Taxonomic History

The taxonomic story of Psilocybe coprinifacies illustrates the complexity of neurotropic fungal classification. Guzmán (1983) initially considered it a doubtful species due to insufficient understanding and misidentifications. European authors (Herink 1950, Pouzar 1953, Semerdzieva and Nerud 1973, Auer et al. 1980, Wurst et al. 1984, Semerdzieva et al. 1986) recognized it in Czechoslovakia. Sebek (1983) then described [[psilocybe-bohemica]] based on Czech specimens identified as *P. coprinifacies*. Krieglsteiner (1984, 1986) considered *P. coprinifacies* a synonym of [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] emend. Krieglsteiner, and further placed *P. bohemica*, *P. mairei*, and *P. serbica* as synonyms of *P. cyanescens*.

## Implications for Field Research

These patterns of confusion have several important implications:

- **Ethnographic records must be verified chemically**: Indigenous names and traditional uses cannot be assumed to correlate with neurotropic properties
- **Market specimens may be unreliable**: Specimens collected from markets rather than directly from the field may have degraded active compounds or be entirely different species
- **Recreational identification is particularly error-prone**: Users who identify fungi based on visual similarity alone are at high risk of consuming the wrong species
- **Literature errors compound over time**: A single misidentification in a seminal paper can propagate through decades of subsequent research and popular writing
- **Bluing reaction is a useful but imperfect indicator**: While bluing is generally associated with psilocybin content, not all bluing species have been chemically confirmed, and some non-bluing species may contain active compounds

The authors of the 1998 worldwide survey concluded that whatever region mycologists study materials from, they find new species, suggesting that the true diversity of neurotropic fungi remains significantly underdocumented and that careful taxonomic work remains essential.

## Five Historical Periods of Neurotropic Fungi Knowledge

Guzmán, following Stamets (1978, 1996), identified five historical periods in the study of neurotropic fungi:

1. **Ancient use**: Indigenous peoples across North America, Mesoamerica, Siberia, and New Guinea used neurotropic fungi in their traditions, long before any scientific documentation.

2. **Uncertain early studies**: The beginning of the 20th century saw erroneous or uncertain identifications, most notably Schultes's (1939) assignment of all Mexican hallucinogenic fungi to Panaeolus.

3. **Scientific investigation**: Beginning with Wasson's studies in the 1950s, which followed Schultes's earlier work, rigorous mycological and chemical investigation began. This period saw the involvement of Heim, Singer, Hofmann, and others who established the taxonomic framework still largely in use.

4. **Recreational use and cultural degradation**: The 1960s brought the adoption of neurotropic fungi as recreational drugs, leading to a decline in traditional indigenous use, particularly in Mexico. This period forced governments worldwide to prohibit the use and commerce of these fungi.

5. **Recapitulation**: The current period involves a return to careful scientific study, with new species descriptions and more sophisticated chemical analyses building on the foundations laid in earlier decades.

## The Bluing Reaction as Taxonomic Indicator

The authors of the 1998 survey used the bluing reaction as a key characteristic for confirming neurotropic status in Psilocybe. Species belonging to sections Aztecorum, Brunneocystidiatae, Cordisporae, Cubensies, Mexicanae, Semilanceatae, Stuntzae, Subaeruginosae, and Zapotecorum typically exhibit bluing. However, the bluing reaction is not universal among neurotropic species — some confirmed psilocybin-containing species do not blue conspicuously, and some non-neurotropic species may show discoloration that could be confused with bluing.

Species such as [[psilocybe-atrobrunnea]], [[psilocybe-coprophila]], and Psilocybe pseudobullacea were specifically excluded as neurotropic fungi despite being confusedly reported as hallucinogenic in some literature. Similarly, Psathyrella sepulchralis was excluded because it was wrongly confused with [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]] and does not contain psilocybin (Ott and Guzmán, 1976).

## Criteria for Neurotropic Classification

The 1998 survey divided neurotropic fungi into four groups based on chemical and cultural criteria:

1. **Indole-containing fungi**: Those with psilocybin, psilocin, baeocystin, and norbaeocystin, mainly the bluing species of Psilocybe, Conocybe, and Copelandia, but also found or probably found in some non-bluing species of Agrocybe, Galerina, Gerronema, Gymnopilus, Inocybe, Mycena, Panaeolina, Panaeolus, and Pluteus.

2. **Ibotenic acid fungi**: [[amanita-muscaria]], [[amanita-pantherina]], and [[amanita-regalis]].

3. **Ergot fungi**: [[claviceps-purpurea]] and allies, including C. nigricans, C. paspali, C. rolfesii, and C. tripsicii, plus probably two species of Cordyceps.

4. **Sacred fungi without chemical confirmation**: Bolete genera Boletus and Heimiella (8 species), Russula (6 species), and gasteromycetes (6 species in Lycoperdales and Phallales) used as sacred fungi by various tribes but without reliable chemical studies confirming neurotropic properties.

This four-group system provides a practical framework for organizing the diverse and sometimes contradictory literature on psychoactive fungi, while acknowledging the gaps in chemical knowledge that persist for many traditionally used species.

## [[dictyophora-indusiata]]: The Veiled Stinkhorn

Among the more unusual entries in the sacred fungi category is [[dictyophora-indusiata]], a striking stinkhorn fungus belonging to the Phallales order. Recorded with three varieties, this species has been used in ceremonial contexts despite uncertain chemical status. The fungus is notable for its elaborate net-like indusium (skirt) that hangs from the cap, giving it a dramatic appearance that would lend itself naturally to ritual use. Like many gasteromycetes and Phallales used as sacred fungi, it may function more as a ceremonial object than as a genuine psychoactive substance.

## Confusion Between [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]] and Related Species

The taxonomic history of [[psilocybe-zapotecorum]] and its relatives illustrates the complexity of Section Zapotecorum. Several species described independently were later found to be synonyms or close relatives: Psilocybe subzapotecorum was described as a distinct species, as was Psilocybe ramulosa (originally *P. zapotecorum* var. *ramulosum*). [[psilocybe-aucklandii]] from New Zealand was found to be closely related to *P. zapotecorum* from Mexico and South America, despite the vast geographic separation.

[[psilocybe-liniformans]] presents a similar story, with two varieties recognized: var. *liniformans* and var. *americana* (described by Guzmán and Stamets). The Section Zapotecorum as a whole shows a pattern of wide geographic distribution with local variants, suggesting either ancient dispersal or ongoing gene flow between widely separated populations.

## The Volatility Problem in Chemical Analysis

The experience with Cordyceps species used in Mexican ceremonies highlights a general problem in the chemical analysis of neurotropic fungi. Active compounds may be volatile, degrading during storage or transport. Hofmann's inability to detect indoles in market-purchased Cordyceps specimens may reflect genuine absence of active compounds, or it may reflect degradation during preservation. The participant who experienced hallucinations in a closed room with the fungi, with hallucinations ceasing upon leaving the room, suggests that at least some active component was present and volatile.

This volatility problem means that negative chemical results cannot be taken as definitive proof that a traditionally used species lacks neurotropic properties. The conditions of collection, preservation, transport, and analysis all affect whether active compounds will be detected. This is particularly relevant for species used in traditional contexts far from modern laboratory facilities.
