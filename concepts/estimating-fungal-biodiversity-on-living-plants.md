## Estimating Fungal Biodiversity on Living Plants

## Overview

Estimating the number of fungi on living plants in a specified area requires two types of information: (1) the numbers and types of plant hosts present in the area and (2) estimates of the number of fungal species associated with those plants. This page covers the methodologies, challenges, and key findings related to estimating fungal species richness and abundance on living plant substrata.

## The Role of Host Plant Diversity

Plant-species diversity is the primary determinant of fungal species richness on living plants, due to the preponderance of host-specific taxa. Good estimates of plant species numbers in tropical and [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] are available, as are estimates of the proportions of trees, shrubs, and herbs. However, study sites generally cannot be compared directly because of differences in plot size and sampling techniques.

### Growth Form and Parasite Load

Strong and Levin (1979) determined that species richness of plant-parasitic fungi in temperate zones was highly correlated with the growth form of hosts — the more complex the growth form, the greater the number of associated [[fungal-parasites]]:

- **Herbs:** mean of 5.4 fungal parasite species per host species
- **Shrubs:** mean of 7.1 fungal parasite species per host species
- **Trees:** mean of 15.4 fungal parasite species per host species

The same study reported that host size appeared to be the major determinant of species richness among grasses, with ratios of 35:1 for *Zea*, 21.2:1 for *Phragmites*, and 8.1:1 for *Triticum*.

## Tropical vs. Temperate Diversity

### Neotropical Estimates

The highest known alpha diversity of trees occurs in the Neotropics (Gentry 1992). Epiphytes account for 12–16% of species at moist forest sites and about 25% in wet forests. Up to 50% of plant species at individual sites are herbs and shrubs. Gentry estimated that less than 25% of species at most neotropical sites are trees.

Hubbell (1979) recorded 135 species of woody plants (>2 cm dbh) in a 13.4-ha tract in Costa Rica: 87 trees, 38 shrubs, and 10 vines. Using Gentry"s estimates of 25% tree species at neotropical sites, an estimated total of 348 plant species should occur at this site. Applying Strong and Levin's ratios yields an estimated **2,833 species of plant-parasitic fungi** for this small forest tract.

At a larger scale, approximately 10,000 plant species have been recorded in a 110,000-ha plot in Costa Rica. Using proportional estimates (25% trees, 16% epiphytes, 14% shrubs/vines, 45% herbs), the estimated total number of plant-parasitic fungi is **81,457** — more than the number of all currently described fungal species.

In paleotropical forests, plants other than trees predominate. At a forest site in Gabon, 34% of species were trees, 25% climbers, and 37% herbs or shrubs (Gentry 1992). Two 1-ha sample sites from lowland forests near Iquitos, Peru, each had nearly 300 species of woody plants greater than 10 cm dbh.

### Temperate Estimates

Plant-species diversity in temperate forests is generally measured as species richness (number of species per 0.1 ha). Whittaker (1965) used this metric to compare diversity among temperate communities. He noted that low tree-species diversity can be associated with highly productive climax forest, such as the [[california-redwood-forests-mollison]] of coastal California and Oregon.

In the Great Smoky Mountains (eastern US), approximately 1,300 species of flowering plants including 130 native trees have been found. Assuming 10% trees, 25% shrubs, and 65% herbs, the estimated number of fungal species is **8,873** — slightly more than one-tenth of the Costa Rican prediction.

The hardwood stand composition of this region has been dramatically altered by the introduction of chestnut blight (*[[cryphonectria-parasitica]]*). Prior to introduction, American chestnut (*[[castanea-dentata]]*) formed 30–60% of canopy stems; now 70% of tree stems are composed of other, smaller species.

## Pathogen-Specific Host Ratios

Hirata (1986) estimated that **4.5% of vascular plant species** host [[biodiversity-powdery-mildews]]. This is in line with the percentage of plant species hosting smut fungi in the British Isles (4.8%) and the United States (3.2%). In Latin America, however, only **0.3%** of the estimated 88,000+ vascular plant species are known hosts for smut fungi — likely reflecting severe under-sampling rather than true low incidence.

Of collections made by Durán (1987) in Mexico over 22 years, 17% were species new to science. Vánky estimated that only half of extant smut species have been described.

Powdery mildews and smuts may not be representative of plant-parasitic fungal diversity [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]], given that neither group is abundant on trees and shrubs. Rusts may be more representative — Nishida (1989) noted that one in three plant species in a neotropical study area was parasitized by a unique rust species.

## Sampling Considerations

### Non-Uniform Distribution

Diversity of plant-parasitic fungi depends not only on host-species composition but also on the **density and distribution** of host plants. Studies show increasing diversity with increasing moisture and decreasing diversity with increasing elevation. Phytoparasitic fungi are often especially diverse in border-plant communities. Sampling strategies must account for this uneven distribution.

### Disease Incidence

Kranz (1990) noted that when a plant ecosystem is explored, the majority of plant species encountered are likely to be healthy. In a 3-year study of six distinct plant communities at three German sites, only 28–52% of plants were hosts for plant pathogens. Conversely, the leaf-surface mycota is usually richly developed, often consisting of 12 or more species of mostly saprobic sooty molds.

## Species Abundance Patterns

In most ecosystems, species abundances approximate a log normal distribution. Among fungi associated with plants, a few species are dominant but most species are rare (Dix 1964; Swift 1976; Bills and Polishook 1991, 1994). The frequency of a specific parasite depends on the sampling scheme, vegetation type, type of fungus, and its interaction with the host.

### Incidence vs. Severity

Systemic infections (e.g., many *Tilletia* species) can be detected from sori in reproductive organs, with several sori on one plant counted as a single infection. Localized infections (e.g., *Colletotrichum* anthracnose) each represent a unique infection event. Surveyors must decide whether to assess incidence (percent of diseased plants or plant parts) or severity (amount of tissue affected).

## The Role of Plant Host Size

Cannon and Hawksworth (1995) noted that host size appeared to be a major determinant of fungal species richness among grasses. The ratio of fungal species to host species was 35:1 for *Zea* (maize) and 21.2:1 for *Phragmites* (common reed), compared to only 8.1:1 for *Triticum* (wheat). The same study reported relatively low ratios for *Saccharum* (sugarcane) at 4.3:1, though the authors suggested that host associations in this genus were probably under-recorded.

Parasitic fungi associated with epiphytic plants are poorly known and may represent another potentially diverse group in the tropics based on the species richness of the potential hosts. Sillett and colleagues (1995) recorded 127 bryophyte species in the inner crowns of just six *Ficus* trees in Costa Rica, illustrating the extraordinary microhabitat diversity that epiphytes can support.

## Endemism

The highest levels of endemism among plant-parasitic fungi should occur in areas with the highest levels of host-species endemism. Gentry (1992) identified four such areas in the Neotropics representing biomes isolated as islands, intermountain regions, mountain ridges, and other unusual habitats. Determining endemism requires much more complete distributional data than currently exists. Many apparently endemic species may prove to have wider distributions with further study.

## Recommendations

Standard techniques designed for monoculture crop fields are not appropriate for measuring parasite populations in natural ecosystems. Wild host populations have higher genetic variability, mixed ages, and interactions with non-host species. Mycologists should work closely with botanists and plant ecologists to determine plant identity and heterogeneity, which is essential for estimating fungal diversity. Species accumulation curves for plant-parasitic fungi should be related to, but not necessarily parallel, those of their host plants.

According to Williams and Humphries (1994), unweighted species richness provides a good first approximation to more demanding taxonomic diversity measures in the absence of all other information. Endemism, however, is not a measure of biodiversity but of range size, and its exclusive use can reduce the effectiveness of procedures for conservation of biodiversity.

It would seem logical, considering that the fungi in this functional group are fruiting on living plants, to survey for parasitic fungi in conjunction with botanical surveys. This approach ensures that host identification is accurate and complete, which is critical given the prevalence of host-specific taxa. The surveyor must also decide whether to assess incidence (percent of diseased plants or plant parts per sample) or severity (amount of tissue affected), depending on the objectives and the type of pathogen involved.

## Fungal Diversity on Fruits, Nuts, and Flowers

Many of the fungi that infect leaves also infect flowers and fruits on the same host. Other fungal parasites are encountered more frequently on flowers and fruits. Symptoms include discoloration, shriveling, wilting, and premature drop of flowers — collectively described as blossom blight. Predominant fungi associated with blossom blights include species of Sclerotiniaceae, such as those in the anamorphic genus *Botrytis* and in *Monilinia*. These fungi are also associated with fruit rots. Fruits infected by *Monilinia* species frequently become "mummies" — dried shriveled fruits that may remain attached to the plant. *Monilinia* species are also associated with brown rots of fruits as well as twig cankers and gummosis.

Other symptoms of infected fruits and nuts include discolored lesions or streaks, shriveling, wrinkling, hardening, and cracking. Scabs — roughened, crustlike diseased areas — are caused predominantly by species of *Elsinoë* and their associated *Sphaceloma* anamorphs in the tropics, and by *Venturia* species in temperate regions. Scabs occur on avocado, cashew, castor, groundnut, papaw, poinsettia, and soybean in the tropics, and on Rosaceae in temperate regions. Additional fungi associated with fruit rots include *Aspergillus*, *Colletotrichum*, *Dothiorella*, *Guignardia*, *Phytophthora*, and *Rhizopus* species.

## The Clavicipitaceae on Grasses

The Clavicipitaceae (Hypocreales, Ascomycota) contains 27 genera, most of which are systemic endophytes or insect parasites. A notable exception is *Claviceps*, with approximately 35 species worldwide. In grasses, *Claviceps* causes local ovarian infection known as ergot, in which the seed is replaced by a black sclerotium. The notorious *C. purpurea* causes ergot of rye and other grasses. Ergot is known for its toxic effect (ergotism, caused by fungus alkaloids) on humans and other mammals who ingest sclerotia mixed with grain.

Field collection and identification of *Claviceps* can be difficult. In warm, humid climates the hyperparasitic fungi *Cerebella* and *Fusarium* infect the honeydew stage of *Claviceps* and inhibit sclerotium development. However, the prominent black *Cerebella* fruiting bodies can serve as indicators of cryptic early *Claviceps* infections. Germination of sclerotia can be stimulated by placing them on moist filter paper, though cold pretreatment may be required and ripening periods of up to 9 months may be necessary for some species. Several small Clavicipitaceous genera are associated with bamboo and warrant further study.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

## Research Directions
Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.
Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.
