# White Clover Living Mulch

White clover (*Trifolium repens*) is the foundational [[comparison-crop-rotation-vs-cover-cropping]] in Masanobu Fukuoka's [[natural-farming-philosophy]] system, serving simultaneously as nitrogen fixer, weed suppressor, soil builder, and erosion control. In his fields, clover grows year-round beneath both rice and winter grain, forming a permanent living mulch that replaces chemical fertilizer, herbicides, and much of the labor of conventional farming.

## Role in the Rice/Grain Rotation

The clover cycle is integral to the [[fukuoka-rice-and-barley-natural-farming]]:

1. **Early October**: White clover seed (about 1 pound per quarter acre) and winter grain seed (6.5 to 13 pounds per quarter acre) are broadcast among the standing, ripening rice
2. **November**: Rice is harvested; the straw is spread as mulch. Clover and grain sprouts are trampled during harvest but recover quickly
3. **Winter**: Clover and winter grain grow together, the grain providing a canopy
4. **Late May**: After winter grain harvest, the field is flooded for 7-10 days. The clover weakens and turns yellow but is not killed
5. **June**: As soon as water is drained, "the clover recovers and spreads to cover the field's surface" beneath the emerging rice
6. **Summer**: Clover grows beneath rice, providing nitrogen and moisture retention

The key insight: "The idea is not to kill the clover, but only to weaken it enough to allow rice seedlings to become established." This temporary setback is enough; the clover's deep root system and energy reserves allow rapid recovery.

## Functions

### Nitrogen Fixation
As a legume, white clover hosts Rhizobium bacteria in its root nodules that fix atmospheric nitrogen, converting it into plant-available form. This is Fukuoka's primary fertility source, eliminating the need for chemical or prepared fertilizers. Combined with the return of all straw to the field, clover maintains soil fertility indefinitely.

### Weed Suppression
"I have found white clover useful in holding back weeds. It grows thickly and can smother out even strong weeds such as mugwort and crabgrass." Combined with [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-philosophy]], clover provides effective weed control without tillage or herbicides. Where the clover and weeds are not so thick, seeds can simply be tossed out directly.

### Soil Protection and Building
The clover mat protects the soil surface from erosion by rain and wind. Its roots improve soil structure, and when portions die back, they add organic matter. Over twenty-five years, Fukuoka's orchard soil — originally hard red clay "so hard you could not stick a shovel into it" — became "loose, dark coloured, and rich with earthworms and organic matter" largely due to clover and straw.

### Moisture Retention
Clover keeps the ground moist and well-aerated. During hot months, the clover layer reduces evaporation, and even in years with very little rain, the soil stayed moist below the layer of straw and green manure.

### Erosion Control on Slopes
On the hillside orchards, clover was essential for preventing erosion on what had been bare red clay. Sown mixed with alfalfa, it took several years to establish but eventually covered the hillsides, initiating a cycle of soil regeneration. "It was several years before they could take hold, but finally they came up and covered the orchard hillsides."

## Sowing and Management

### Timing
Late summer or fall sowing is best. "It is important to choose the right time to sow the clover." The roots develop during the cold months, giving clover a jump on the annual spring grasses. It can also be sown in early spring.

### Methods
Either broadcasting or planting in rows about twelve inches apart works. "Once the clover takes hold, you do not need to sow it again for five or six years."

### Re-establishment
In the citrus orchards, after seven or eight years, weeds began to outcompete the clover, so Fukuoka tossed out additional clover seed in late summer after cutting back weeds with a scythe. This periodic reseeding maintains the balance.

## Interplanting with Vegetables

In semi-wild vegetable growing, clover serves as a living mulch between crop rows. "White clover sowed with the grain as a ground cover, and temporary flooding of the field" replace man-made chemicals. Sown mixed with vegetable seeds, clover enriches the soil, keeps the ground moist and aerated, and holds back weeds. It is particularly useful with root crops and leafy greens. "Where the weeds and clover are not so thick, you can simply toss out the seeds."

## The Clover-Weed Balance Over Time

Fukuoka observed a natural cycle in the orchard:

1. **Years 1-3**: Clover and alfalfa struggling to establish on bare red clay
2. **Years 4-7**: Clover covering well; weeds beginning to appear (a positive sign of soil recovery)
3. **Years 7-8**: Weeds outcompeting clover; manual reseeding needed
4. **Years 8-25**: Dynamic balance between clover, weeds, and crop plants; periodic management only

This is not a problem to be solved but a natural cycle to be managed minimally. The weeds that appear are themselves soil builders and part of the ecosystem.

## Integration with Acacia Windbreaks

In the orchard system, clover works alongside Morishima acacia trees planted as windbreaks. The acacia's deep roots improve subsoil while clover builds topsoil. The acacia's flowers attract aphids, which attract ladybirds, which then move to citrus trees to feed on pest insects — an elegant biological chain that depends on the whole ecosystem, including the clover layer, functioning together.

## Comparison with Other Cover Crops

Fukuoka noted that [[natural-farming-philosophy]] takes a distinctive form according to local conditions. In areas where white clover is not suitable, other legumes can fill the same role: other clover varieties, alfalfa, vetch, or lupine. The principle remains the same — a permanent leguminous ground cover that fixes nitrogen and suppresses weeds.
