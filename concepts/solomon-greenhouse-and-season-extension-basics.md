# Greenhouse and Season Extension Basics

Steve Solomon takes a deliberately minimalist approach to season
extension. He advises that for most food gardeners, greenhouses, hot
frames, heat cables, and elaborate structures are rarely necessary.
His philosophy is to grow a lot of good food in every month it is
possible to get it, but without incurring unnecessary expenses and
stresses. However, he does discuss several simple season extension
tools that are practical and effective.

## Solomon's Minimalist Philosophy

Solomon states plainly that you do not need greenhouses, hot frames,
heat cables, or [[hamilton-cold-frames-and-hardening-off]] for most [[dry-gardening-unirrigated-vegetable-production-solomon]]. If you want
to push the limits, there is no shortage of books on greenhouses and
hot and cold frames, and plenty of gear available to equip you with the
neatest of seedling-raising setup. But his advice is to grow seedlings
only for those species that genuinely benefit from every possible
frost-free day: tomatoes, peppers, and eggplants.

In warm climates with more than 150 frost-free days, even these crops
can often be direct-seeded, making transplant production unnecessary.
The emphasis should be on choosing the right varieties for your climate
rather than building expensive structures to grow the wrong varieties.

## Spun-Fabric Row Covers

The one season extension tool Solomon enthusiastically endorses is
spun-fabric row covers (such as Reemay). These provide most of the
benefits of a cloche or mini-greenhouse without needing to erect any
structure. They also provide protection against flying insect pests
without needing to spray.

These fabrics are about five to six feet wide (150 to 180 centimeters)
and come in lengths from 20 feet to thousands of feet. They are not
completely transparent; the amount of light lost varies by brand. The
fabric is spread over a growing row or bed and loosely anchored with
soil. As plants grow, they lift the almost weightless fabric, so no
supporting structure is needed.

Reemay provides a few degrees of [[holzer-frost-protection-techniques]] and raises soil and
air temperatures, enhancing springtime growth. However, Reemay reduces
light levels by about 25 percent, which is not desirable for all crops.
Other brands that are more porous and allow more airflow may not reduce
light as much but may not last as long.

## Cloches and Tunnels

Solomon mentions cloches and tunnel structures as useful for providing
a few degrees of frost protection in spring and autumn. These can be as
simple as clear plastic bags placed over individual seedlings, or more
elaborate wire-supported tunnels covered with plastic or spun fabric.

Microirrigation systems are particularly useful inside tunnels and
cloches to keep plants watered for a few weeks until the cover is
removed. The small-scale, low-volume watering capability of these
systems makes them ideal for enclosed growing spaces.

## Cold Frames for Seedling Production

Where seedlings must be grown for transplanting (in short-season
climates), a simple cold frame can provide protection without the
expense of a greenhouse. Solomon describes using clear plastic bags
slipped over individual seedling pots to create miniature greenhouses.
This low-tech approach costs almost nothing and provides adequate frost
protection for hardening off seedlings before transplanting.

## Winter Gardening Without Structures

In mild winter climates, Solomon argues that elaborate structures are
unnecessary. In his Tasmanian garden, he grows vegetables 12 months a
year with minimal protection. An unheated outbuilding was plenty good
enough for storing spuds and apples through the winter. Greens
remained in their growing beds with only a bit of soil mounded over
the roots for protection against shallow freezes.

In regions where the soil rarely freezes solid and winters are mild,
the main requirement for winter gardening is choosing the right
varieties: kale, Brussels sprouts, leeks, parsnips, and certain
hardy greens will survive with little or no protection.

## When Structures Make Sense

Solomon acknowledges that season extension structures do have their
place in certain situations:

-  **Short-season climates:** Where frost-free days are fewer than
    120, some form of protection is needed to grow heat-loving crops.
-  **Early harvest:** Gardeners willing to invest in simple structures
    can gain two to four weeks at the start and end of the season.
-  **Seedling production:** Even Solomon's minimalist approach requires
    some protection for tomato, pepper, and eggplant seedlings in
    cooler climates.
-  **Frost protection:** A few degrees of protection can save crops
    from unexpected early or late frosts.

## The Economics of Season Extension

Solomon consistently asks whether the investment in structures and
equipment is justified by the additional food produced. For a gardener
focused on self-sufficiency and growing a significant portion of their
family's food, the calculation must include not just the cost of
materials but the ongoing maintenance and the learning curve required
to use these tools effectively.

His recommendation is to start simple: use row covers for frost
protection and insect exclusion, direct-seed whenever possible, and
only invest in more elaborate structures if your climate genuinely
demands it and you have mastered the basics of [[fukuoka-semi-wild-vegetable-growing-natural-method]] first.
