## Mahayana, Hinayana, and Scientific Farming: Three Ways of Farming

## Overview

Fukuoka classifies all approaches to agriculture into three fundamental types, borrowing terminology from Buddhist philosophy. This framework provides the [[comparison-natural-farming-vs-permaculture]] is inherently superior to [[fukuoka-natural-farming-transcends-time-space]] and space and reaches the zenith of understanding and enlightenment. It cannot be directly compared with scientific farming because they exist on entirely different planes.

### Hinayana Natural Farming (Idealistic Farming)

Hinayana natural farming belongs to the relative world, alongside scientific farming. It arises when man earnestly seeks entry to the realm of Mahayana farming — desirous of the true blessings and bounty of nature, he prepares himself to receive it. This is the road leading directly to complete enlightenment, but falls short of that perfect state.

The relationship here is like a lover who yearns after his loved one and asks for her hand, but has not realized full union. Hinayana farming includes [[non-discriminating-knowledge-and-critique-of-scientific-reductionism]].

### Scientific Farming (Dialectical Materialism)

Scientific farming exists in a state of contradiction: man is basically estranged from nature, living in a totally artificial world, yet longs for a return to nature. This approach wanders blindly back and forth, now calling upon the blessings of nature, now rejecting it in favor of human knowledge and action. The lover here is unable to decide whose hand to ask in marriage and, while agonizing over indecision, imprudently courts the ladies heedless of social proprieties.

## The Sword Fighting Analogy

Fukuoka borrows an analogy from Japanese sword fighting to clarify the relationships:

- **Mahayana natural farming** is the unmoving "no-sword" school. Comparison with it is impossible; it transcends the realm of contest entirely.
- **Hinayana natural farming** is the "one-sword" school directed toward the center — reducing means toward a single point of unity with nature.
- **Scientific farming** is the "two-sword" school directed outward — using all possible means, increasing the number of swords.

Scientific farming tries to obtain results by accumulating methods and tools. Hinayana natural farming tries to obtain the best possible results while rendering all means useless (reducing the number of swords). Mahayana natural farming does entirely without swords.

## Relative Merits Comparison

### Mahayana vs Scientific Farming

These exist on entirely different planes. Scientific farming is the sum of knowledge and forces extracted from nature by the human intellect — finite human knowledge. No matter how totaled, this amounts to a tiny, closely circumscribed fraction of the infinitude of the natural world. As imperfection can never equal perfection, scientific farming must always yield to Mahayana natural farming.

### Hinayana vs Scientific Farming

These belong to the same world of relativity and can be directly compared. Both are derived from nature as verified with [[critique-of-scientific-agriculture]] because it takes full advantage of nature's forces. Man is no more than a student of nature; were nature the teacher to use its full powers, man the student would lose out in any confrontation.

## The Diagram of Relative Merits

Fukuoka represents the three ways geometrically:

- **Mahayana** is the largest circle, representing the vast, boundless, perfect knowledge and power of nature
- **Hinayana** is a smaller circle within the relative world, representing an imperfect but well-intentioned effort to align with nature — its contour is nearly perfect
- **Scientific farming** is a circle of similar size to Hinayana but with a highly irregular contour, representing the distortions and imperfections arising from the collection of narrow research findings of which it is made
