## New World Hallucinogen Clustering

One of the most striking patterns in the global distribution of psychoactive
plants is the extraordinary concentration of potent hallucinogens in the
Americas. Terence McKenna argued that this geographical clustering was not
random but reflected a deep co-evolutionary relationship between psychoactive
plants and the human cultures that used them — a relationship that shaped
the trajectory of New World civilizations in fundamental ways.

## The Old World-New World Divide

The major psychoactive plants of the Old World and the New World belong to
entirely different chemical and botanical families, and produce characteristically
different experiential effects:

**Old World hallucinogens** — the solanaceous plants (nightshade, mandrake,
henbane, thornapple) — produce [[sacred-mexican-morning-glories-ololiuqui-and-tlitliltzin]],
ayahuasca, Virola snuff, peyote, and DMT-containing snuffs — produce
tryptamine and isoquinoline alkaloids that cause visionary experiences:
enhanced pattern recognition, feelings of unity and interconnectedness,
encounters with benevolent beings, and profound insights that users
consistently describe as meaningful and transformative.

McKenna considered this difference crucial and potentially civilization-
shaping. The Old World hallucinogens tended to produce experiences that
reinforced fear of the supernatural and the authority of those who claimed
to control or interpret it — priests, inquisitors, and rulers. The New
World hallucinogens tended to produce experiences that reinforced a sense
of personal connection to the divine and the natural world, empowering
individuals rather than subordinating them to institutional authority.
This difference, McKenna suggested, may help explain why the relationship
between psychoactive plants and religious authority developed so
differently in the two hemispheres.
