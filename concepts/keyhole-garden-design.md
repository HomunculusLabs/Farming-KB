# Keyhole Garden Design

A keyhole garden is a raised, circular garden bed with a central compost basket and a wedge-shaped notch (the "keyhole") that provides access to the center. Originally developed in Africa for drought-prone regions, keyhole gardens combine composting, water conservation, and intensive [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]] in a compact, accessible design.

## Origin and Purpose

Keyhole gardens were developed by humanitarian organizations (notably Send a Cow, now Send a Cow Rwanda) working in Lesotho, southern Africa, where drought, poor soils, and physical limitations of elderly or disabled gardeners made traditional farming difficult. The design has since spread worldwide as an efficient, productive garden system.

Key advantages of the keyhole design:

- **Integrated composting:** A central basket continuously feeds the garden with nutrients from kitchen scraps and yard waste
- **Water efficiency:** The compost basket distributes moisture throughout the bed via wicking action
- **Accessibility:** The raised bed design reduces bending; the keyhole path provides easy access to the entire bed from one spot
- **[[mollison-designers-market-garden-intensive-production-design]]:** The circular design maximizes growing area per square foot
- **Soil building:** Continuous composting builds rich, living soil over time

## Design and Dimensions

### Standard Dimensions

- **Overall diameter:** 6 feet (1.8 meters) — fits in most yards and is accessible from the keyhole
- **Bed height:** 24-36 inches — tall enough to avoid bending, short enough to reach across
- **Central basket diameter:** 12-18 inches — wide enough to hold adequate compost, narrow enough to leave ample growing space
- **Keyhole notch width:** 18-24 inches at the widest point — comfortable walking path
- **Keyhole depth:** Extends to the center basket

### Scaling

- **Small keyhole:** 3-4 foot diameter for patios or small spaces
- **Large keyhole:** 8-10 foot diameter for [[gaias-garden-creating-community-gardens]] (the keyhole path must still reach the center)
- **Multiple beds:** Arrange several keyhole gardens in a cluster with shared pathways

## Construction

### Materials

- **Wall material:** Stone, concrete blocks, bricks, timber, corrugated metal, or earth bags. Stone is traditional and provides thermal mass.
- **Central basket:** Wire mesh cylinder (hardware cloth or chicken wire), a section of perforated PVC pipe, or a woven basket. Must allow water and nutrients to seep out into the surrounding soil.
- **Fill materials:** Cardboard (bottom weed barrier), organic matter, topsoil, compost
- **Path surface:** Gravel, wood chips, or stepping stones

### Step-by-Step Construction

1. **Mark the circle:** Use a string and stake to mark a 6-foot diameter circle on the ground. Mark the keyhole notch — a wedge cut from one side extending to the center.

2. **Prepare the ground:** Remove grass or weeds from the circle. Loosen the soil surface with a fork. Optionally, dig a shallow depression (6-12 inches) to reduce the amount of fill needed.

3. **Build the outer wall:** Lay the first course of stones, blocks, or bricks around the circle, leaving the keyhole notch open. Stack courses to the desired height (24-36 inches). The wall should lean slightly inward (batter) for stability.

4. **Install the central basket:** Place the wire mesh cylinder or basket in the center of the circle. It should extend from the ground to 6-12 inches above the top of the wall. Secure it in place.

5. **Line the bottom:** Lay down a thick layer of cardboard or newspaper inside the circle to suppress weeds. This layer will decompose over time.

6. **Add fill layers:** Build up the bed in layers from the outside inward:
   - Bottom layer (6-12 inches): Woody branches, twigs, and rough organic matter (hugelkultur-style base for drainage and long-term decomposition)
   - Middle layer (6-12 inches): Leaves, grass clippings, straw, and partially decomposed compost
   - Top layer (6-8 inches): A mix of topsoil and finished compost — this is the growing layer
   - Slope the soil slightly from the outer wall toward the central basket so water flows inward

7. **Fill the central basket:** Start filling the basket with kitchen scraps, yard waste, green manure, and other compostable materials. As it decomposes, nutrients and moisture wick outward into the surrounding soil.

8. **Create the keyhole path:** Lay down gravel, wood chips, or stepping stones in the wedge-shaped notch for a comfortable, non-slip walking surface.

## Soil Layers Explained

The layered fill approach is essential to keyhole garden success:

- **Wood layer (bottom):** Provides drainage, creates air spaces, slowly breaks down to feed soil biology
- **Organic matter layer (middle):** Spongy material that retains moisture, feeds worms and microbes, continues to break down
- **Soil-compost layer (top):** The active growing medium. Rich, loose, and biologically active. This is where plant roots establish.

As the lower layers decompose over months and years, the bed will slowly settle. Add more [[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] compost to maintain the soil level.

## Planting

### Planting Zones

The circular shape creates microclimates:

- **Inner ring (near basket):** Most fertile and moist zone. Plant heavy feeders: tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, squash, brassicas
- **Middle ring:** Balanced fertility and moisture. Plant: beans, peas, lettuce, chard, spinach, onions, carrots, beets
- **Outer ring (near wall):** Drier zone (especially stone walls that absorb heat). Plant: herbs, radishes, small-rooted vegetables, flowers for pollinators

### Companion Planting

The keyhole garden's compact space benefits from companion planting:

- **Three sisters:** Corn (center support), beans (nitrogen fixation), squash (ground cover) in adjacent sections
- **Tomato + basil + marigold:** Classic combination for pest deterrence
- **Carrots + onions:** Companion planting that deters each other's pests

### Succession Planting

Because the central compost basket continuously feeds the soil, you can replant sections as crops are harvested:

- Plant quick crops (radishes, lettuce, arugula) in gaps between slower crops
- Replace harvested plants immediately with new seedlings
- The bed can produce food year-round in mild climates

## Watering

### Watering Through the Basket

- Pour water directly into the central compost basket
- The water percolates through the compost and wicks outward into the surrounding soil
- This method is far more efficient than surface watering — water goes directly to the root zone

### Watering Frequency

- **Newly built bed:** Water daily for the first 2 weeks as plants establish
- **Established bed:** 1-2 times per week in warm weather, less in cool weather
- **After heavy rain:** Check that water is not pooling — the bed should drain freely
- **Drought conditions:** Water only through the basket; mulch the surface heavily

### Rainwater Harvesting

- Position the keyhole garden near a downspout or under a roof edge to capture rainwater naturally
- Add a rain barrel with a hose directed to the compost basket

## Composting in the Central Basket

The central basket is the engine of the keyhole garden:

- **What to add:** Kitchen scraps (vegetable peels, fruit waste, coffee grounds, eggshells), yard waste (grass clippings, leaves, small prunings), green manure, aged manure
- **What to avoid:** Meat, dairy, oils, diseased plant material, pet waste, weed seeds
- **Layer green and brown:** Alternate nitrogen-rich (green) and carbon-rich (brown) materials for balanced decomposition
- **Keep it moist:** The basket should be damp like a wrung-out sponge
- **Add continuously:** Feed the basket weekly for steady nutrient supply

As the compost breaks down, it shrinks. Keep topping it up with fresh materials.

## Maintenance

- **Mulch:** Apply 2-3 inches of organic mulch to the soil surface to retain moisture and suppress weeds
- **Replenish soil:** Add compost to the top layer annually
- **Rebuild the wall:** Check stone or block walls annually for settling or displacement
- **Basket maintenance:** If the wire basket degrades, replace it (every 2-4 years depending on material)
