# Fukuoka Duck Rice Fish Polyculture

Updated: 2026-04-19

Masanobu Fukuoka advocates integrating ducks and fish into rice paddies as part of his natural farming system in *The Natural Way of Farming*, presenting it as an alternative to chemical fertilizers and pesticides through three-dimensional use of the field.

This polyculture approach embodies Fukuoka's principle that [[fukuoka-natural-farming]] works as an integrated whole where each element supports the others. It connects to [[duck-rice-paddy]] traditions and the [[fukuoka-five-principles-natural-farming]].

## The Duck Method

From the standpoint of natural farming, it would be preferable and much easier to release ten ducklings per quarter-acre onto the field when rice seedlings have become established. The ducks provide multiple functions:

- **Weeding**: Ducks eat weeds and weed seeds that grow among the rice

- **Pest control**: Ducks pick off insects harmful to rice

- **Soil aeration**: Ducks turn the soil as they forage, improving soil structure

- **Fertilization**: Duck droppings add nitrogen and nutrients to the field

Fukuoka states: "Not only do the ducks weed and pick off insects, they turn the soil." This single integration replaces three categories of agricultural chemicals simultaneously, making it perhaps the most elegant element of his farming system.

## Protective Measures

The ducks do have to be protected from stray dogs and hawks. Simple fencing and netting arrangements are sufficient for this purpose. This minimal infrastructure requirement means the duck-rice system adds little complexity to the overall method.

## Fish Integration

Fukuoka also recommends releasing young carp into the flooded rice field. The fish provide additional protein food production while contributing to the field ecology through their feeding and waste cycling.

"Another good idea might be to release young carp." The fish swim in the shallow water between rice plants, feeding on plankton and small organisms, while their waste fertilizes the rice. This creates a truly three-dimensional production system with plants, birds, and fish all contributing to each other's success.

## Three-Dimensional Field Use

By making "full, three-dimensional use of the field," the farmer can simultaneously produce grain protein (rice and barley) and animal protein (duck eggs and meat, fish). This diversification creates a more resilient and productive farming system without external inputs.

The concept of three-dimensional use is significant because conventional rice monoculture exploits only the surface layer of the field. By adding ducks on the surface and fish in the water, every layer of the paddy ecosystem becomes productive. See [[fukuoka-rice-barley-dual-culture]].

## Alternative to Chemical Inputs

The duck-fish-rice system replaces three major categories of agricultural chemicals:

1. **Herbicides** — ducks handle weed control

2. **Insecticides** — ducks consume pest insects

3. **Fertilizers** — duck and fish manure, plus the soil-turning action that improves nutrient availability

This connects to [[fukuoka-natural-pest-balance]] and [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] approaches. From the standpoint of pure natural farming, the duck-rice-fish polyculture is the ideal — more natural than even the chicken manure applications described in the [[fukuoka-rice-barley-step-by-step-method]].

## Connection to Asian Tradition

Fukuoka's advocacy of duck-rice integration draws on centuries-old Asian rice paddy practices. The technique has been widely adopted and adapted in Southeast Asian countries and is promoted by organizations such as the Duck-Rice Network in Japan.

## Fukuoka's Vision of Integrated Livestock

Fukuoka describes his idealized vision of livestock farming in vivid terms: "I see bees busily making the rounds of clover and vegetable blossoms thickly flowering beneath trees laden heavy with fruit; I see semi-wild chickens and rabbits frolicking with dogs in fields of growing wheat, and great numbers of ducks and mallards playing in the rice paddy; at the foot of the hills and in the valleys, black pigs and boars grow fat on worms and crayfish."

This vision represents the fullest expression of [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy]] — a farm that functions as a complete ecosystem where every element supports every other element without human management.

## Relevance to Modern Natural Farming

The duck-rice-fish polyculture exemplifies Fukuoka's principle that natural farming systems work as integrated wholes where each element supports the others. It contrasts with conventional monoculture rice production that requires separate applications of fertilizer, herbicide, and pesticide.

Modern adaptations of this method have been adopted throughout Asia and are being explored in other rice-growing regions worldwide. The system demonstrates that high productivity and ecological sustainability are not mutually exclusive. See [[fukuoka-high-yield-rice-barley]].

## Critique of Modern Livestock

Fukuoka contrasts this integrated approach with modern livestock farming: "Raising one yellowtail to a marketable size requires ten times its weight in sardines. Similarly, a silver fox consumes ten times its weight in rabbit meat, and a rabbit ten times its weight in grass."

The caloric inefficiency of concentrated animal feeding operations is, in his view, one of the fundamental flaws of modern agriculture. In terms of caloric production and consumption, someone will have to work at least twice as hard if they want to eat eggs and milk rather than grains and vegetables. If they like meat, they will have to put out seven times the effort.

This connects directly to [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] and [[fukuoka-agriculture-run-amok]].

## The Ecological Principle

The duck-rice-fish system illustrates a core principle of Fukuoka's thought: the earth is an organically interwoven community of plants, animals, and microorganisms. When each element is allowed to fulfill its natural role, the system as a whole becomes more productive and more stable than any monoculture. See [[fukuoka-nature-transcends-human-knowledge]].
