## Desert Food Systems and Indigenous Knowledge

The question of food security in deserts is not merely one of gardening or farming, but of understanding and restoring a relationship between people and arid landscapes that was developed over tens of thousands of years by indigenous peoples.

## Defining Staple Foods

A staple food is defined as one supplying 50% or more of diet when in season. Today, some 70% of the food in Western societies is supplied by just 8 staple crops. No European family has 20–30 basic staple foods; they typically have a choice of 62–100 foods in all. A very good home garden and livestock situation can produce about 20 vegetables, 6–9 fruits, and 3–6 staples — well beyond the choice range of an average family. Of these, 2–4 would be considered staples depending on the culture.

## The Destruction of Desert Food Systems

The introduction of cattle and sheep, and the release of rabbits, destroyed the bulk of useful vegetation in Australian deserts and almost every common marsupial or adapted mammal of medium size (although small marsupials and mice survive). This destruction extended to indigenous ways of living and the profound knowledge of lands held by integrated tribal people for tens of thousands of years.

This replacement makes no ecological, economic, genetic, informational, or social sense. It is a denial of human rights. Commonsense was replaced by a situation of downright exploitation and rapid loss. Broadscale commercial herding is to the desert what broadscale sugar cane cropping is to the tropical rainforest, and broadscale soybeans to floodplain forests — a disaster in every way.

### Loss of Genetic Resources and Knowledge

People who managed deserts well for 10,000–40,000 years possessed deep knowledge of these landscapes. This knowledge and its right to continue to exist have been suppressed. Those who were teachers have become fringe dwellers. Both genetic resources and information on management have been destroyed by the "right-to-ruin" mentality claimed by private owners and governments.

Even the plants developed by the Papago and Hopi Indians are being lost as store foods replace desert foods. Yet desert plant lists show a present potential for several staple foods, and an untapped potential for developing better cultivars of many desert plants.

### Building Humus and Mulch Production

It is always essential to build [[humus]] and mulch production into crop systems. Species used for this purpose include:

**Edible mulch producers:**
- [[dolichos]], edible lupin, Leucaena, edible-seeded Acacias

**Shelter, edge, weed barrier, and hedgerow species:**
- [[vetiver-grass]]

**Trees for shelter and leaf mulch:**
- Casuarina, some figs, Acacia victoriae, Pongamia
- Clump species such as bamboo

**Ground covers** (good "soft" ground covers outside Zone 1):
- Vetches, nasturtium, runner legumes, comfrey, annual lupins, [[daikon-radish]]

### Tree Spacing and Interplanting

The spacing of fruit trees in Zones 1 and 2 can be as usual, but it is essential to provide:

- **High shade** from interplants of tall thin-foliage legumes or palms
- **Fast-growing small and large woody legumes** that either die out (pigeon pea) or can be coppiced (tagasaste) or felled or ring-barked to rot to humus (many Acacia species)

### Results of Well-Planned Systems

Such well-planned systems are:
- Very productive
- Drought resistant
- Cool (through shade and mulch)
- Eventually humus-rich

They demonstrate how quickly a barren or hard soil area can be brought into production through intensive biomass production and wastewater use.

## The Role of Teachers and Designers

The role of teachers and designers in desert food systems is to:

1. Help plan and place trial systems in villages
2. Locate and provide seeds and propagules of new species
3. Teach the benefits of soil humus and rainwater harvest
4. Stress food preparation and good nutrition

## Restoring the Knowledge Base

The fundamental insight from studying indigenous desert food systems is that deserts are not inherently food-poor. They contain a diversity of edible plants, and indigenous peoples developed sophisticated strategies for harvesting, storing, and managing these resources. The path forward involves not just introducing new techniques, but recovering and respecting the knowledge systems that enabled human survival in arid landscapes for millennia.
