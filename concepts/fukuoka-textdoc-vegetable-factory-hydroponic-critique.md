# Fukuoka's Critique of Artificial Cultivation and Hydroponics

Masanobu Fukuoka traced the degradation of agricultural food quality through a progression of increasingly artificial [[fukuoka-textdoc-semi-wild-vegetable-cultivation]] to hothouse horticulture as the first step in agriculture's divorce from nature.

It began innocently enough with melons and tomatoes grown in soil within hot beds or vinyl houses.

The next stage replaced soil entirely with sand or gravel, chosen because these materials harbor fewer bacteria and are therefore "cleaner."

This substitution was accompanied by a conceptual shift: instead of forming rich soil, the grower now administers nutrients directly.

The growing medium became nothing more than a physical support structure for the plant.

Sand and gravel were eventually replaced by plastic netting and polymer containers.

The growing chamber was made completely sterile, eliminating insect damage and disease — at least initially.

## The Spraying Chamber

In the fully developed artificial cultivation system that Fukuoka described, plants are grown in sealed, sterile chambers.

Nutrient solutions are sprayed over the entire plant at regular intervals because root absorption from water is inefficient.

Nutrients are absorbed not only through roots but through leaf surfaces, making them more immediately available and accelerating growth rates.

Temperature is controlled, artificial lighting increases light exposure, and carbon dioxide and oxygen are actively managed.

Under these conditions, plant growth becomes several times faster than in [[fukuoka-textdoc-fallacy-infinite-nutritional-analysis]].

He believed that food quality encompasses dimensions beyond measurable vitamins, minerals, and calories.

Soil-grown food carries qualities derived from the complex biological community of the living earth — qualities that sterile hydroponic systems cannot replicate.

Nature struck back against artificial cultivation, in Fukuoka's observation, through increased insect damage and disease pressure.

The sealed sterile chamber is not truly sterile; organisms adapt and infiltrate, often with more virulence than in balanced natural systems.

The predictable response — more pesticides, more chemical controls — only deepens the cycle of intervention and degradation.

## The Vegetable Factory Vision

Fukuoka extrapolated current trends to their logical conclusion: the creation of "vegetable factories" that would render farms and gardens entirely unnecessary.

These facilities would employ fully automated production under the supervision of a single technician.

Central heating, air conditioning, artificial lighting, and automatic spraying devices would manage every environmental variable.

Such a factory cannot be built and operated without enormous capital investment in materials and equipment.

The vegetables produced would necessarily be expensive, no matter how efficient the technology.

Fukuoka argued that no artificial system can produce food more cheaply than crops grown naturally with sunlight and soil.

Nature produces without demanding supplies or remuneration, but human effort always requires payment.
