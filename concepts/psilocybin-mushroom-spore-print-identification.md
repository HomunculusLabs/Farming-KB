# Spore Print Identification for Psilocybin Mushrooms

Spore printing is the single most important identification technique for safely distinguishing psilocybin-containing mushrooms from deadly look-alikes. The color of the spore deposit in mass is a fairly constant and reliable feature within each genus. Stamets' generic identification key is organized primarily around spore color.

## Why Spore Color Is Critical

The majority of distinctions between gilled mushroom genera are at least partially based on spore color. The deadly genera Galerina and Pholiotina produce rusty brown spores, while the principal psilocybin genera Psilocybe and Panaeolus produce purplish brown to black spores. Distinguishing between a purple-brown spore print and a rusty-brown spore print can be a distinction with deadly potential.

Perceiving spore color is a subjective experience with margin for variation and interpretation. People who are color impaired should not make critical decisions about spore color.

## Spore Colors by Genus

### Psilocybin-Containing Genera

| Genus | Spore Print Color | Key Features |
|-------|------------------|-------------|
| Psilocybe | Dark purplish brown to purplish black | Viscid caps when moist, bluing reaction, safest genus |
| Panaeolus | Black to blackish brown | Mottled gills, dung/grassland habitat, no poisonous species |
| Pluteus | Pinkish to salmon | Free gills, wood-inhabiting, P. salicinus is primary active species |
| Gymnopilus | Rusty orange to yellow-orange | Dry scaly caps, large mushrooms, variable potency |
| Conocybe | Rusty brown | Small delicate cones, dangerous genus due to Pholiotina filaris |
| Inocybe | Clay brown to dull brown | Difficult identification, many toxic species, avoid entirely |

### Dangerous Look-alike Genera

| Genus | Spore Print Color | Toxin Type |
|-------|------------------|------------|
| Galerina | Rusty brown | Amatoxins (lethal -- same as Amanita phalloides) |
| Pholiotina | Rusty brown | Amatoxins (lethal) |
| Cortinarius | Rusty brown to ochre | Various (some lethal, including orellanine) |
| Hebeloma | Dull brown | Various toxic (gastrointestinal) |
| Entoloma | Pinkish brown | Various toxic |
| Amanita | White (mostly) | Amatoxins (lethal) |

The critical safety distinction: if your mushroom has purplish brown to black spores, it is extremely unlikely to be a deadly Galerina or Pholiotina. If it has rusty brown spores, exercise extreme caution -- it could be a Gymnopilus (possibly active) or a Galerina (possibly deadly). The rusty brown spore zone is the most dangerous for collectors because both active and deadly genera share this color.

## How to Take a Spore Print

1. Collect fresh, fairly mature specimens. Specimens with convex caps are better than those that have fully flattened.
2. Isolate candidates in wax paper bags (not plastic -- mushrooms must breathe to prevent decomposition).
3. When home, separate the cap from the stem using a knife or scissors.
4. Place the cap gills-down on a piece of white paper or a microscope slide.
5. Cover with a glass or cup to reduce dehydration and air current disturbance.
6. Moistening the inside of the glass also helps spore-printing ability of partially dehydrated mushrooms.
7. Wait a few hours. Spores will deposit according to the radiating symmetry of the gills.
8. If you suspect white spores, use dark paper instead. For uncertain spore colors, use half white and half dark paper.
9. Label the print with date, location, and habitat. Seal in a separate plastic bag and save with the collection.

Best done within the first few hours from picking, as spore production declines as the mushroom dries. A clean spore print from a reliably identified specimen can also be used to start a culture, particularly relevant for P. cubensis.

## Spore Print Colors and the Generic Key

The generic identification key in Stamets' guide is organized primarily around spore color:

- Black spore deposit: leads to Psathyrella, Panaeolus, or Coprinus
- Purplish brown to dark purplish brown: leads to Psilocybe or Stropharia
- Earthy brown (cellular cap cuticle): Agrocybe
- Yellowish brown (filamentous cap cuticle): Pholiota
- Chocolate brown, free gills: Agaricus
- Rusty brown: Galerina, Gymnopilus, Conocybe, Inocybe (danger zone)
- Pinkish salmon, free gills: Pluteus
- White: various genera including Amanita (deadly species present)

## Microscopic Spore Features

Beyond color, spore morphology under a microscope provides definitive identification:

### Psilocybe Spores
Dark purplish brown in deposit. Typically subellipsoid to ellipsoid, 8-15 by 5-8 micrometers depending on species. Basidia typically 4-spored, occasionally 2-spored (2-spored basidia tend to produce larger spores). Cheilocystidia are usually present and take various forms: ventricose, lageniform, or fusoid. Pleurocystidia may be present or absent depending on species.

### Panaeolus Spores
Black in deposit. Typically lemon-shaped to ellipsoid, 10-17 by 7-10 micrometers. A distinguishing feature: Panaeolus spores are often roughened or wrinkled in surface appearance, while similar-looking Psathyrella spores are smooth. In concentrated sulfuric acid, Panaeolus spores do not fade or discolor while Psathyrella spores do -- a useful chemical test.

### Gymnopilus Spores
Rusty orange in deposit. Ovoid to ellipsoid, roughened (warted), 7-10.5 by 4.5-6 micrometers. The roughened surface is a key microscopic feature. Basidia are 2- and 4-spored.

## Important Exceptions and Cautions

- Spore color sometimes does not reflect true spore color due to density of spore-producing cells and color of underlying flesh.
- Spores also collect on the stem, veil fragments (annulus), and adjoining shorter mushrooms -- a quick visual check for experienced observers.
- Within the minor psilocybin genera (Gymnopilus, Conocybe, Inocybe), spore color overlaps with deadly genera. Only skilled mycologists should attempt identification in these groups.
- Some species can be variable: G. spectabilis is active in some regions and inactive in others, making spore-print identification insufficient to confirm activity.
- Freshness matters: old or dehydrated specimens may produce faint or incomplete spore prints.

## The Bluing Reaction as Supplementary Evidence

While spore print color is the primary identification tool, the bluing reaction provides supplementary evidence. Active Psilocybe and Panaeolus species typically bruise bluish where injured due to the oxidation of psilocin. However, not all active species bruise visibly, and some inactive species can have bluish tones for other reasons. The bluing reaction should never be used as the sole identification criterion -- always make a spore print.
