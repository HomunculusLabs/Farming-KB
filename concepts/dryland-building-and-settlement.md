# Dryland Building and Settlement

Dryland environments present unique challenges for human habitation and agriculture. Permaculture approaches to building and settlement in arid and semi-arid regions emphasize earth-sheltered construction, passive cooling, water harvesting, and the strategic use of desert-adapted plant species.

## Underground and Earth-Sheltered Structures

Underground and earth-sheltered buildings are among the most effective strategies for dryland habitation. These structures leverage the thermal mass of earth to maintain stable indoor temperatures—cool in summer and warm in winter—dramatically reducing energy needs for climate control.

**Underground Orchards (Fresno, California):** Hand-dug cave systems open into shaded pits containing fig, grape, citrus, and pomegranate plantings. Looking up from underground, the sky is framed by fruit-laden branches. Roots in the pits remain cool and easily mulched. The ground surface can be shaped into a funnel to catch and direct water to plant containers, creating a self-irrigating system.

**Traditional Earthed-Up Barns (Aspen, Colorado):** In high cold desert environments, traditional earth-sheltered barns provide winter hay storage and livestock shelter. These structures are naturally insulated by snow accumulation on their earth-covered roofs.

**Buried Dairy Sheds (Greenock, South Australia):** Traditional buried structures maintain cool temperatures for milk and food storage. Supplementary cooling is achieved through vine, earth, or tree shade.

**Biodome Greenhouses (Old Snowmass, Colorado):** At 7,500 feet elevation in a cold, dryland climate with an average 60-day growing season, domed greenhouse structures enable year-round cultivation. These systems integrate solar electricity, heating ponds, and biological pest control—frogs and finches manage insect populations within the greenhouse. Tilapia fish and hydroponic vegetables are grown in integrated pond systems.

## Passive Cooling Techniques

In drylands, passive cooling is essential for both human comfort and agricultural productivity. Several proven techniques include:

- **Living Shade Screens:** At St. Theresa Mission in Central Australia, house verandas are screened with climbing plants such as Bryona (a cucurbit), lab-lab bean, and tomato vines. These living screens cool west-facing and sunward walls and are sustained by waste wash-water and grey water.
- **Turf Roofs:** Earth-sheltered houses with turf roofs and slab floors provide excellent comfort control. The combination of thermal mass and living vegetation creates natural insulation.
- **Transpiration Cooling:** Roofs planted with ice plants and herbs provide active cooling through transpiration and shade, significantly reducing interior temperatures.

## Desert Plant Species for Settlement

Several desert-adapted plant species serve multiple functions around dryland settlements:

### Opuntia (Prickly Pear)
Opuntia provides fruit (tuna), vegetable food, cattle forage, and living hedge barriers. In the Canary Islands, it also hosts the cochineal scale insect, which has been used locally as a food colorant for centuries.

### Desert Acacias
Many desert Acacia species provide abundant poultry seed and forage. Acacia cambagei (gidgee) creates desert mounds or nebkas approximately 1.5 meters high and 12 meters across through dust accretion. Many sessile or weeping trees in deserts cause dust to accumulate in this way. Acacia species in the Kalahari perform the same function, providing deep absorbent soils and mulch for vines, other trees, and burrowing animals.

### Lycium and Solanum Species
Lycium species and Solanum species provide abundant fruit and seed suitable for chicken forage. Many woolly Solanum species serve as dryland spinach or pot herbs, providing nutritious greens in arid environments.

### Ocotillo (Fouquieria splendens)
In the Sonoran Desert around Tucson, Arizona, spiny Ocotillo hedges guard traditional O'odham gardens. This species is cultivated for fencing, can be live-set to grow gated, and is widely used as reinforcement or base for mud wall construction. It serves as a useful construction material for desert structures.

## Cinder Mulch Technique

In the Canary Islands, a deep mulch technique using volcanic cinder (approximately 0.3 meters deep) enables apple and pear cultivation in areas receiving mainly night fog and condensation. This technique also produces potato and fig crops in regions with virtually no conventional rainfall, relying entirely on fog harvesting and condensation capture through the cinder medium.

## Extensive Discing for Dust Storm Control

At Alice Springs in Central Australia, extensive discing over 600 square kilometers has significantly reduced dust storms around the town. This large-scale earthworking technique demonstrates how broad landscape management can improve conditions for human settlement in arid regions.

## Key Design Principles

Successful dryland settlement follows several permaculture principles:

1. **Minimize exposed surface area:** Earth-sheltered and underground structures reduce heat gain and water loss.
2. **Harvest every drop of water:** Funnel-shaped surfaces, fog harvesting through cinder mulch, and grey water recycling maximize water availability.
3. **Use living systems for climate control:** Plant screens, turf roofs, and transpiration cooling replace mechanical systems.
4. **Select multi-purpose species:** Every plant around a dryland settlement should serve multiple functions—food, forage, shelter, and construction material.
5. **Work with natural processes:** Dust accretion by desert trees creates fertile microsites; fog and condensation can substitute for rainfall when properly harvested.
