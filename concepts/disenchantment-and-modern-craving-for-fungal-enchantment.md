# Disenchantment and the Modern Craving for Fungal Enchantment

Why has the modern West embraced the [[australian-magic-mushroom-common-names]] with such
enthusiasm? The answer lies not in pharmacology but in culture. The
sociologist Max Weber's concept of the "disenchantment of the world"
provides a framework for understanding why a significant residuum of
modern people, caught between scientific rationalism and traditional
religion, have turned to mushrooms and other psychedelics in search of
re-enchantment.

## Weber's Disenchantment Thesis

The German sociologist Max Weber (1864-1920) argued that the
character of modern times is defined by rationalisation,
bureaucratisation, and above all by the "disenchantment of the world."
He meant that there was no longer any need for mysterious forces to
explain the phenomena of the world; one could, in principle, master
all things by calculation. The scientific tide had steadily scoured
away belief in fairies, djinns, God even, and with it any notion that
our existence was ordained or had meaning.

Though Weber died decades before its advent, the nuclear bomb threw
this disenchantment into dramatic relief, revealing in terrifying form
the godlike power of science and, by contrast, the impotence and
obsolescence of other explanatory works. The oft-recycled image of the
mushroom cloud was a constant reminder throughout the Cold War.

## The Unbridgeable Tension

Weber thought the "tension between the value-spheres of science and
the sphere of the holy" was unbridgeable, but modern people have
managed to adopt an array of "softer" post-rational positions between
the extreme poles of religious and scientific materialism. There
remains a significant residuum for whom progress has left an
unaccountable feeling of loss. Possessed of romantic longings, and an
appetite for mystery that science can never satisfy (its raison d'etre
is to banish mystery, after all), they are nevertheless unable to
return to traditional religion.

Caught between science on the one hand and religion on the other,
they are continually driven to find re-enchantment elsewhere.

## The Limits of Cultural Tolerance

The prevailing cultural discourses do not make this easy. Anyone who
stands up and proclaims the existence of hives of [[dmt-self-transforming-machine-elves-mckenna]]
elves in a parallel dimension is likely to meet with a cynical and
derisive response. Culture can only tolerate elves and faerie denizens
if they are bound firmly into film, the realm of fiction, or the
symbolic language of the unconscious, where the question of their
actual physical existence need never be settled. As [[robert-graves-british-mushroom-connection]]
noted, "the dirty synthetic world has asserted itself as the sole
factual truth."

## Oases of Enchantment

Yet there is a palpable thirst for these oases of enchantment.
Children's literature and fantasy fiction have never been more
enthusiastically consumed by adults. The huge popularity of Philip
Pullman's His Dark Materials trilogy, J.K. Rowling's Harry Potter
books, and Peter Jackson's epic screen version of Tolkien's Lord of
the Rings testifies to this. According to some critics, we are all
"kidults" now, stuck in a perpetual "middle youth."

The burgeoning smorgasbord of alternative spiritualities, New Age,
Pagan and neo-Shamanic, eclectic assemblages that sit somewhere
outside science and traditional religion yet proclaim a special place
for humanity in the greater scheme of things, represents genuine
attempts to answer the spiritual anxieties of our times. Weber himself
took a sideswipe at those who "play at decorating a sort of domestic
chapel with small idols from all over the world," but still
lambasted by the mainstream, these alternatives persist.
