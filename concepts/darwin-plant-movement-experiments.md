# Darwin's Plant Movement Experiments

For the twenty years following the 1859 publication of *On the Origin of Species*,
Charles Darwin conducted an intensive series of experiments on plant movement that
fundamentally shaped plant biology. His 1880 book *The Power of Movement in Plants*,
co-authored with his son Francis, introduced concepts and experimental approaches
that remain central to modern plant science. Darwin's work demonstrated that plants
possess sensory capacities — the ability to perceive light, gravity, touch, and
other stimuli — and to translate these perceptions into [[two-phase-directed-growth-psilocybe-cubensis-basidiocarp-development]] responses.

## Context: Darwin's Botanical Career

Charles Darwin's botanical work is less well-known than his [[tompkins-goethe-plant-metamorphosis-evolutionary-theory]] but
was equally rigorous and groundbreaking. His plant research addressed fundamental
questions about how organisms interact with their environment, providing crucial
evidence for the universality of sensory responsiveness across life forms.

Darwin's botanical investigations spanned multiple phenomena:

- **Phototropism:** Plant bending toward light
- **Gravitropism:** Root and shoot orientation relative to gravity
- **Thigmotropism:** Plant responses to touch and mechanical stimulation
- **Circumnutation:** The spontaneous circular movements of growing plant tips
- **Carnivorous plant responses:** The rapid movements of Venus flytraps and
  sundews in response to prey

## The Phototropism Experiments

Darwin's phototropism work is his most celebrated botanical contribution.

### The Canary Grass Setup

Darwin and Francis grew canary grass (*Phalaris canariensis*) seedlings in complete
darkness for several days to eliminate any prior light exposure effects. They then
introduced a single gas lamp at a distance of twelve feet (3.5 meters) — so dim
that they "could not see the seedlings themselves, nor see a pencil line on paper."

Despite the minimal light intensity, the seedlings bent toward the source within
three hours. This proved that phototropism operates at extremely [[darkness-low-light-facilitation-entheogenic-ceremonies]] levels,
far below the threshold for photosynthesis — establishing that it is a dedicated
sensory response, not a photosynthetic byproduct.

### The Five Treatment Experiment

The key experiment tested five conditions:

| Treatment | Description | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A | Untreated control | Bent toward light |
| B | Tip pruned off | No bending |
| C | Tip covered with opaque cap | No bending |
| D | Tip covered with clear glass cap | Bent toward light |
| E | Middle covered with opaque tube | Bent toward light |

This elegantly demonstrated that:
- The shoot tip is necessary for phototropism (B)
- The tip needs light access, not just physical presence (C vs D)
- The bending region does not need to directly perceive light (E)
- A signal must travel from the light-sensing tip to the bending region

### The Transmissible Signal

Darwin wrote that when the tip is illuminated, "it transmits an influence to the
lower part, causing it to bend." He proposed that this influence moves down the
stem at a measurable speed. This was confirmed by his experiments showing that
if a cut is made between the tip and the bending region, the bending response
is blocked — proving the signal must physically traverse the intervening tissue.

The signal was later identified as the plant hormone **auxin** (indole-3-acetic
acid), which redistributes asymmetrically in response to directional light. Auxin
accumulates on the shaded side of the stem, promoting [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]] there,
causing the plant to bend toward the light.

## Circumnutation: The Spontaneous Dance

One of Darwin's most prescient observations was the phenomenon of circumnutation —
the continuous circular movements made by growing plant tips, visible in time-lapse
photography. Darwin documented circumnutation in a wide range of plant species:

- Growing shoots trace elliptical or circular paths as they extend
- Roots also circumnutate, though less visibly
- Tendrils show exaggerated circumnutation as they search for support structures
