## Maggots (Fly Larvae)

The most serious and intractable pest. Common name for the larvae of
fly species (Diptera) which from April through warm summer months
rendered mushroom-growing unprofitable.

### Description and Damage

- Larvae 3/16 to 4/16 inch long, white with black head.
- Appeared in all parts of the mushroom but mostly in the cap and at the
  base of the stem.
- Perforated hither and thither, leaving a disgusting network of burrows.
- Infested tiny buttons as soon as they appeared at the surface; this did
  not check their growth.
- Inexperienced eyes often missed infestation -- only occasional dark
  punctures or tracings on caps and stems.
- In large, full-grown, white-skinned varieties, presence was visible.

### Seasonal Pattern

Evil began in April, increased through summer, peaked around August, then
decreased, stopping completely by October. It was unavoidable and so far
had proved invincible.

### Only Remedy

"We simply stop growing mushrooms in summer." Mr. J.F. Barter of London
confirmed: "I know of no sure cure for them." Although not poisonous,
maggoty mushrooms were "very repugnant and utterly unfit for food."

## Manure Flies (Sciara)

Small flies appearing in large numbers in spring and summer in mushroom
houses, hotbeds, or any structure where manure was used.

### Damage

Hopped about the house continually, running over mushrooms, beds, and
walls "in the most suspicious manner." However, Falconer regarded them
as perfectly harmless except for soiling mushrooms with their muddy feet.
They were often mistakenly destroyed in attempts to control the maggot
fly.

### Control

- **Turpentine water traps**: Pans of water with a few drops of oil of
  turpentine; flies attracted by the odor drowned themselves.
- **Floating light traps**: Flies burned their wings and fell into water.
- **Buhach powder**: Dusted into the air or burned on a hot shovel --
  Falconer found this more effective than lamp or drowning methods.

## Slugs

Serious pests, especially in above-ground structures, also occurring in
annoying numbers in cellars. Most numerous where hay or straw covered
beds or much woodwork was present.

### Damage

- Attacked mushrooms in all stages from tiny buttons to fully developed
  plants.
- Buttons/small mushrooms: ate out pieces on top or side of cap; wounds
  spread open as mushroom grew, displaying ugly scars.
- Full-grown mushrooms: particular liking for the gills, eating patches
  out of them.

### "Bullet" or "Shot" Holes

Little holes cut clear through mushroom caps, as if perforated by
buckshot. Falconer initially suspected myriapods (Julus spp.) based on
Prof. C.V. Riley's opinion. However, by leaving part of a bed uncared
for and observing at night with a lantern, he discovered the holes were
made by [[falconer-mushroom-bed-construction]].
- **Rigid cleanliness** and an ever-alert eye for slugs.

## Wood Lice

Sure to be more or less abundant in every [[falconer-mushroom-growing-house-design]], even cellars.
Crawled in through doors, ventilators, or other interstices, or brought
in with manure. Found shelter about woodwork, manure, or dry litter.

### Damage

Bit out little patches in tops and sides of pinhead and small button
mushrooms. As mushrooms grew, the blemish spread into an objectionable
feature.

### Control

- **Potato traps**: Half-boiled potato (no salt) in a small pasteboard
  box covered with dry swamp moss. Wood lice gathered to eat potato and
  remained in the cozy hiding place. Examine in the morning, shake into a
  pail of water and kerosene. Replace potato periodically.
- **Board traps**: 6-8 inch square pieces of half-rotten very dry boards
  laid in pairs one above another; wood lice gathered to hide. Examine
  frequently.
- **Hot water or kerosene emulsion**: Poured about woodwork, walls, and
  pathways -- not allowed to touch beds.
- **Poisoned baits** (sweet apples, potatoes, parsnips): recommended by
  some but Falconer discouraged any poisons in the mushroom house.
