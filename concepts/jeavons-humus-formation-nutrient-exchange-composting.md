## Humus Formation and Nutrient Exchange

## Overview

[[humus]] is the end product of compost decomposition and the most reliable plant
food available in a biointensive system. Understanding how humus forms, stores
nutrients, and exchanges them with plant roots is essential to sustainable soil
management.

## The Composting Process

Compost is created from the decomposition and recombination of various forms
of plant and animal life — leaves, grass, wood, garbage, natural-fiber clothes,
hair, and bones. These materials are organic matter, which makes up only a
small fraction of total soil volume (generally 1% to 8%), yet is absolutely
essential to [[soil-life]] and fertility.

Organic matter includes dead plant and animal residues in all stages of
breakdown or decay, inseparable from the living microorganisms that decompose
them.

### Microbial Decomposition

Microscopic life-forms (bacteria and fungi) produce the recombining process
that creates warmth in the compost pile. Most decomposition involves the
formation of carbon dioxide and water as organic material is broken down.

**Monitoring methods:**
- Use a compost thermometer to track temperature
- Insert a 1-inch by 1-inch piece of wood into the pile, remove periodically,
  and feel the warmth with your hand
- Compare successive measurements to determine whether the pile is heating or
  cooling

### The Cooling Phase and Humus Formation

As available energy is consumed, microbial activity slows, their numbers
diminish, and the pile cools. Most remaining organic matter converts to humus
compounds — the living and dead bodies of microbial life.

As humus forms, nitrogen becomes part of its structure. This stabilizes
nitrogen in the soil because humus compounds are resistant to decomposition.
They are worked on slowly by soil organisms, but nitrogen and other essential
nutrients are protected from too rapid solubility and dissipation.

## Cation Exchange — How Plants Feed from Humus

### The Electrical Basis of Nutrient Exchange

Humus acts as a site of nutrient adsorption and exchange for plants in the
soil. The surfaces of humus particles carry a **negative electric charge**.
Many plant nutrients carry a **positive electrical charge** in soil solution:

**Positively charged (cations):**
- Calcium (Ca²⁺)
- Sodium (Na⁺)
- Magnesium (Mg²⁺)
- Potassium (K⁺)
- Most trace minerals

These positively charged nutrients are attracted to and adhere to the
negatively charged surface of humus particles.

**Negatively charged nutrients:**
- Phosphorus
- Sulfur
- Available nitrogen (NO₃⁻ form)

These nutrients become available to plants through biological transformation
in the compost pile and soil, rather than through direct electrical attraction.

### The Root-Humus Bargain

As plant roots grow through the soil in search of nutrients, they feed on
humus. Each root is surrounded by a halo of hydrogen ions (H⁺) produced by
root respiration. These hydrogen ions carry a positive electric charge.

The root "bargains" with humus, exchanging some of its positively charged
hydrogen ions for positively charged nutrient ions stuck onto the humus
surface. An active exchange is set up between humus and roots, with plants
"choosing" which nutrients they need to balance their own inner chemistry.

## Why Humus Is Superior to Chemical Fertilizers

### Fertilization vs. Fertility

There is an important difference between fertilization and fertility. There
can be plenty of fertilizer in the soil, and plants still may not grow well.
Compost's organic acids release hidden nutrients in forms available to plants.

GROW BIOINTENSIVE practices rely on this natural, continual, slow-releasing
biological process for nutrient release, rather than making available all the
season's nutrients chemically at one time.

### Dual Benefits of Humus

1. **Feeds plants** — Plants pull off combinations of nutrients they choose
   from humus surfaces
2. **Stores nutrients safely** — Nutrients are held in forms that are not
   readily leached by rainfall or irrigation

The humus contains much of the original nitrogen from compost inputs (grass,
kitchen wastes, etc.) that was resynthesized by numerous species of
microorganisms feeding on that material.

## Fertilization vs. Fertility

A healthy soil produces healthy plants better able to resist insect and
disease attacks. Most insects look for sick plants to eat. The best way to
control insects and diseases in plants is with a living, healthy soil rather
than with poisons that kill beneficial soil life.

Compost keeps soil at maximum health with minimum expense. Initially,
organic fertilizers may be needed to bring soil to satisfactory fertility.
Once established, soil health can be maintained with compost, good crop
rotation, and recycling of plant residues.

## Maximizing Compost Quality

**Goal:** Maximize quality and quantity of cured compost produced per unit
of compost built, and maximize microbiodiversity.

Composting is an important way to recycle elements essential to biological
life cycles: carbon, nitrogen, magnesium, sulfur, calcium, phosphorus,
potash, and trace minerals. All too often, conventional agriculture
participates in strip-mining these elements from the soil instead.
