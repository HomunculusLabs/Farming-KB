# Psilocybe Genus Taxonomy and Diversity

## Overview

The genus *Psilocybe* is the most diverse and widely distributed group of
neurotropic (hallucinogenic) fungi known to science. As documented by
Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (2000), *Psilocybe* contains 116 recognized species
with psilocybin and related indole compounds, representing the single largest
contribution to the 216 total neurotropic fungal species identified worldwide.
The genus belongs to the family Strophariaceae and is characterized by
mushrooms that typically bruise blue when handled due to the oxidation of
[[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]].

## Taxonomic History

The modern taxonomy of *Psilocybe* was established through the work of several
key mycologists. Schultes (1939) initially believed neurotropic fungi in Mexico
belonged only to *[[panaeolus-campanulatus]]* var. *sphinctrinus*. Singer (1949)
expanded this to include *Psilocybe cubensis*, though based on unidentified
materials. The definitive monograph by Guzmán (1983) established the current
framework, treating *Psilocybe* as distinct from *Hypholoma* and *Stropharia*,
following a modification of Singer's (1986) classification that excluded
Section Chrysocystidiatae.

The classification follows Guzmán (1983, 1995), which does not accept the
broader concept of Kühner and Romagnesi (1953) and Kühner (1980), who
considered *Psilocybe*, *Hypholoma*, and *Stropharia* as a single genus.
Similarly, species of *Psilocybe* sensu Noordeloos (1995) such as
*P. aeruginosa*, *P. albonitens*, *P. aurantiaca*, and *P. capnoides* are
not accepted within the neurotropic *Psilocybe* concept used here.

## Species Diversity by Genus

The neurotropic fungi are distributed across multiple genera, with the
following species counts among those containing psilocybin and related
indoles:

- **Psilocybe**: 116 species (the dominant genus)
- **Gymnopilus**: 14 species
- **Panaeolus**: 13 species
- **Copelandia**: 12 species
- **Hypholoma**: 6 species
- **Pluteus**: 6 species
- **Inocybe**: 6 species
- **Conocybe**: 4 species
- **Panaeolina**: 4 species
- **Gerronema**: 2 species
- **Agrocybe**: 1 species
- **Galerina**: 1 species
- **Mycena**: 1 species

Beyond the psilocybin-containing group, neurotropic fungi include *Amanita
muscaria*, *A. pantherina*, and *A. regalis* (ibotenic acid group), 5 species
of *Claviceps* and 2 of *Cordyceps* (ergot fungi), and various boletes,
Russula species, and gasteromycetes used as sacred fungi without confirmed
chemical analysis.

## Geographic Distribution Patterns

The majority of *Psilocybe* species are concentrated in or near the Austral
hemisphere, primarily in subtropical humid forests. Mexico has the highest
diversity of neurotropic fungi globally, with 76 species total, of which 44
belong to *Psilocybe* alone, representing 39% of all known *Psilocybe*
species worldwide.

Key distribution patterns include:

- **Mexico**: 76 neurotropic species (44 *Psilocybe*), the global center of
  diversity and the most well-documented region for indigenous use of
  sacred mushrooms.
- **Southeast Asia and Oceania**: Significant diversity, particularly in
  subtropical and tropical forest habitats. Thailand, New Zealand, and
  Papua New Guinea are notable hotspots.
- **South America**: Colombia, Brazil, and other tropical nations harbor
  numerous species, especially in humid lowland and montane forests.
- **Europe**: Fewer native species, with *P. semilanceata* (liberty caps)
  being the most common and widely distributed wild species across
  central and northern Europe.
- **North America (USA)**: Several species documented, particularly in
  the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] and Gulf Coast regions.

## Ecological Niches

*Psilocybe* species occupy diverse ecological niches:

- **Dung-inhabiting species**: Many tropical *Psilocybe* species, including
  the widely cultivated *P. cubensis*, grow on herbivore dung in pastures.
  These include species from sections *Coprophilae* and *Stuntzii*.
- **Lignicolous species**: Temperate species often grow on decaying wood,
  wood chips, mulch, and forest debris. *P. cyanescens* and *P. azurescens*
  are classic examples found on woody substrates.
- **Terricolous species**: Grassland species such as *P. semilanceata*
  fruit directly from soil in pastures, meadows, and fields, often in
  association with grasses.

## Identification Challenges

Misidentification is a persistent problem in neurotropic mycology. Chemical
studies sometimes report psilocybin in species later shown not to contain it,
often due to [[growing-gourmet-pleurotus-sajor-caju-taxonomic-confusion]]. For example, *[[psilocybe-atrobrunnea]]* was
reported to contain psilocybin by Høiland (1978), but the material likely
belonged to a different species such as *P. coprinifacies* or *P. maire*,
since *P. atrobrunnea* is not neurotropic. Similarly, *Psilocybe
pseudobullacea* (a non-bluing, non-neurotropic species) was reported to
contain psilocybin from Venezuelan specimens that probably represented an
undescribed neurotropic species.

The age of studied specimens is another critical variable. Repke et al.
(1977) showed that baeocystin content varies with specimen age, and
specimens analyzed 20-60 days after collection may show no trace of indoles.
Ohenoja et al. (1987) documented decreasing psilocybin in herbarium
specimens of *P. semilanceata*: 0.014% dry weight (1869 specimen), 0.67%
(1954), and 0.84% (1976).

## Historical Periods of Study

The study of neurotropic fungi has progressed through five periods:

1. **Ancient use**: Indigenous peoples in Mesoamerica, Siberia, and New
   Guinea used these fungi for religious and healing purposes over
   millennia.
2. **Early uncertain studies** (early 20th century): Initial but often
   erroneous identifications by Schultes and Singer.
3. **Scientific investigation** (1950s onward): Wasson, Heim, Singer, and
   Guzmán conducted rigorous field and laboratory studies.
4. **Recreational use era** (1960s): Widespread recreational use developed,
   particularly in the United States and Europe.
5. **Recapitulation** (present): New species descriptions, refined chemical
   analysis, and taxonomic revisions continue.
