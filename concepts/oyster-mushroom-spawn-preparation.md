# Oyster Mushroom Spawn Preparation

Spawn is the living mycelial inoculum used to seed bulk substrates for mushroom
cultivation. High-quality spawn is the single most important input determining
cultivation success. Poor spawn leads to slow colonization, low yields, and
frequent contamination failures. [[malt-extract-agar]] or potato dextrose agar. Cultures are stored at 2 to 4
degrees Celsius, where growth slows dramatically and the culture can remain
viable for 6 to 12 months. Periodic transfer to fresh media is necessary to
maintain vigor. Strain selection is critical: commercial strains are bred for
yield, disease resistance, temperature tolerance, and fruiting characteristics.
Wild isolates require extensive testing before use in production.

## Grain Spawn Production

Grain is the preferred spawn medium for oyster mushrooms. Rye grain, millet,
sorghum, and wheat are commonly used, each offering different advantages. Rye
grain is the traditional choice due to its ideal size, nutrient content, and
moisture retention. Millet produces more inoculation points per volume due to
its small size, enabling faster substrate colonization. The grain is hydrated to
approximately 50 percent moisture content, then sterilized in a pressure cooker
or autoclave at 121 degrees Celsius for 60 to 90 minutes at 15 psi. Complete
sterilization is non-negotiable; even a few surviving bacterial endospores can
ruin an entire batch.

## Inoculation Under Sterile Conditions

Grain jars or bags are inoculated in a clean environment to prevent
contamination. Professional operations use laminar flow hoods providing HEPA-
filtered air. Hobby growers often use still air boxes (simple containers with
arm holes) or work in a small, still room treated with disinfectant. Agar wedges
or liquid culture are transferred to the grain under sterile conditions. The
inoculation tool (scalpel, syringe, or loop) is flame-sterilized between each
transfer. Speed and confidence are important; the longer the container remains
open, the greater the contamination risk.

## Incubation and Colonization

After inoculation, grain spawn containers are shaken to distribute the mycelium
evenly, then incubated at 22 to 26 degrees Celsius in darkness. *Pleurotus*
mycelium typically colonizes grain in 10 to 21 days depending on species,
grain type, and inoculation rate. Containers are shaken once or twice during
colonization to redistribute the mycelium and accelerate the process. Full
colonization is indicated by a solid white mycelial mat throughout the grain
with no uncolonized kernels visible. A slight grainy or fluffy appearance at
the surface is normal and desirable.

## Liquid Culture Spawn

Liquid culture (LC) is an alternative spawn format where mycelium is grown in a
sterilized nutrient broth, typically based on malt extract, honey, or light
malt syrup. LC offers several advantages over grain spawn: it can be expanded
more rapidly, provides more even distribution in substrate, and is easier to
store and ship. LC is produced in Erlenmeyer flasks or specialized jars fitted
with self-healing injection ports and breathable filters. The culture is
agitated regularly (by manual swirling or magnetic stirrers) to promote
fragmentation into smaller mycelial pieces that serve as inoculation points.

## Spawn Quality Indicators

Healthy spawn exhibits vigorous, white, cottony mycelium with a clean mushroom
odor. Contaminated spawn shows discolored patches (green for mold, pink or
yellow for bacteria), slimy textures, or off-putting sour or rotten smells.
Grain should remain individual and separate, not clumped together (clumping
indicates bacterial contamination). Spawn should be used within 2 to 4 weeks of
full colonization; aged spawn loses vigor and becomes increasingly susceptible
to contamination during substrate inoculation.
