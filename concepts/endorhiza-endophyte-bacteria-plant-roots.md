# Endorhiza — Endophytic Bacteria in Plant Roots

The endorhiza (also called the root endosphere) is the community of microorganisms that colonize the interior of plant root tissues. Unlike rhizosphere bacteria, which inhabit the soil immediately surrounding roots, endorhiza bacteria have crossed the root epidermis and established themselves within the root cortex, vascular tissue, or intercellular spaces. These endophytic communities are critical to plant health, providing growth promotion, disease suppression, stress tolerance, and influencing [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]].

## Defining the Endorhiza

The endorhiza occupies a distinct ecological niche from the rhizosphere and bulk soil. Three root-associated compartments are typically recognized:

1. **Bulk soil** — soil not directly influenced by roots, containing the most diverse microbial community
2. **Rhizosphere** — soil adhering to root surfaces after gentle shaking, enriched by root exudates
3. **Endorhiza (endosphere)** — the interior of the root, colonized by endophytic microorganisms

The boundary between rhizosphere and endorhiza is the root epidermis. Endophytes must penetrate this barrier through natural openings (root cracks, lateral root emergence points) or through active mechanisms involving cell wall-degrading enzymes and motility structures.

## Functions of Endorhiza Bacteria

Endorhiza bacteria provide multiple benefits to their host plants:

- **Phytohormone production** — endophytes synthesize auxins (IAA), cytokinins, and gibberellins that directly stimulate root growth and branching, increasing nutrient and water uptake capacity
- **Nitrogen fixation** — some endorhiza bacteria possess nitrogenase enzymes and can convert atmospheric N2 into ammonia, providing bioavailable nitrogen to the plant
- **Phosphate solubilization** — endophytes produce organic acids that solubilize mineral phosphate, making this essential nutrient available for plant uptake
- **Disease suppression** — endorhiza bacteria compete with pathogens for niche space and nutrients, produce antimicrobial compounds, and can prime the plant's systemic immune responses ([[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]])
- **Stress tolerance** — endophytes help plants tolerate environmental stresses including drought, salinity, heavy metals, and phytotoxic contaminants through ACC deaminase activity (reducing ethylene stress signaling) and other mechanisms
- **Secondary metabolite modulation** — emerging evidence suggests endorhiza communities may influence the production of plant [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]], including cannabinoids in Cannabis

## The Core Cannabis Endorhiza Community

The first comprehensive characterization of the Cannabis endorhiza (Winston et al., 2014) identified a core community present across all cultivars studied. This core consisted of well-known endophytic taxa:

- **Pseudomonas** — one of the most ubiquitous endophyte genera, known for plant growth promotion, biocontrol activity, and production of siderophores and antibiotics
- **Cellvibrio** — an aerobic cellulolytic bacterium; its high abundance (16.9%) in post-harvest samples likely reflected root decay rather than true endophytic colonization
- **Oxalobacteraceae** — a family within Betaproteobacteria commonly found in soil and plant-associated environments
- **Xanthomonadaceae** — a family of Gammaproteobacteria including both plant-pathogenic and plant-beneficial species
- **Actinomycetales** — an order of Actinobacteria known for producing antibiotics and antifungal compounds that may protect the host plant
- **Sphingobacteriales** — an order of Bacteroidetes commonly isolated from soil and rhizosphere environments

All prevalent core members, with the notable exception of Cellvibrio, are well-known endophytic bacteria primarily within the orders Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria. This is consistent with observations from other plant systems, including [[arabidopsis-thaliana]] and [[populus-deltoides]].

## Endorhiza vs Rhizosphere: Key Differences

### Diversity

Endorhiza communities have significantly lower alpha diversity than either rhizosphere or bulk soil communities. In the Cannabis study, Chao1 diversity declined from 4947 (bulk soil) to 4525 (rhizosphere) to 3321 (endorhiza), reflecting the stronger selective pressures within root tissues.

### Community Structure

Endorhiza communities are phylogenetically distinct from rhizosphere and bulk soil. PCoA analysis showed clear separation of endorhiza samples from other compartments. The endorhiza is enriched in Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria while being depleted in Acidobacteria compared to bulk soil.

### Cultivar Specificity

A defining feature of the endorhiza is its strong cultivar-specificity. While rhizosphere communities are primarily shaped by soil type, endorhiza communities show significant differences between plant cultivars even when grown in the same soil. In the Cannabis study, strain was the dominant factor in weighted analysis of endorhiza communities (ADONIS: R2 = 0.59, p = 0.004).

### OTU Overlap with Soil

Endorhiza communities share more OTUs with their own soil than with foreign soils, confirming they are ultimately soil-derived. However, the correlation of OTU abundances between bulk soil and endorhiza (Pearson's rho = 0.42) is much lower than between bulk soil and rhizosphere (rho = 0.92), indicating strong host-driven selection.

## Sampling Considerations

The timing of root sampling critically affects endorhiza community characterization:

- **Pre-harvest sampling** captures the active, plant-selected endorhiza community shaped by living root metabolism
- **Post-harvest sampling** introduces decomposer organisms that colonize senescing root tissue, potentially confounding results

In the Cannabis microbiome study, the first experiment sampled roots 8 weeks post-harvest, which likely explained the anomalously high abundance of Cellvibrio (16.9% vs 0.095% in the pre-harvest second experiment). This highlights the importance of sampling timing for accurate endorhiza characterization.

## Endorhiza and Terroir

Endorhiza bacteria, along with epiphytic microorganisms, may contribute to localized crop characteristics — a phenomenon analogous to terroir in wine production. Just as Gilbert et al. (2014) demonstrated that microbial communities influence the flavor profile of wine grapes, the endorhiza community of Cannabis may contribute to the distinctive chemical profiles (terpene and cannabinoid compositions) associated with specific growing regions and cultivars.

## Mechanisms of Root Colonization

Endophytic bacteria must overcome several barriers to colonize the root interior. The process typically involves the following steps:

1. **Root attraction** — bacteria are initially attracted to root surfaces by chemical signals including root exudates, mucilage, and specific amino acids
2. **Attachment** — bacteria adhere to the root epidermis using surface structures such as pili, flagella, and exopolysaccharides
3. **Entry** — bacteria enter root tissues through natural openings including sites of lateral root emergence, root cracks caused by soil pressure or growth, and wounds. Some endophytes actively produce cellulases and pectinases to facilitate entry
4. **Internal colonization** — once inside, bacteria spread through the apoplast (intercellular spaces), the xylem vessels, or within actual plant cells
5. **Establishment** — successful endophytes must evade or suppress the plant's immune response while competing with other microorganisms for resources

The plant's innate immune system poses a significant barrier. Pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) detects conserved microbial molecules (MAMPs/PAMPs), while effector-triggered immunity (ETI) recognizes specific microbial effector proteins. Compatible endophytes have evolved mechanisms to avoid triggering these defenses, including modifying their surface molecules, producing immunosuppressive compounds, or establishing symbiotic signaling pathways that actively suppress defense responses.

## Methods for Studying the Endorhiza

Studying endorhiza communities requires careful surface sterilization of root samples to eliminate rhizosphere contamination:

1. Roots are excised and shaken to remove loosely adhering soil
2. Surface sterilization typically involves sequential rinsing with ethanol and sterile water
3. DNA is extracted from root tissue using soil-optimized extraction kits (e.g., PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit)
4. 16S rRNA gene sequencing (typically the V4 region on Illumina platforms) provides taxonomic identification
5. Bioinformatic processing through pipelines like QIIME assigns taxonomy and calculates diversity metrics

## Future Directions

Key unanswered questions about the endorhiza include:

- Which specific plant genes control endophyte selection, and how do they vary between cultivars? Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) linking plant genotype to microbiome composition are beginning to address this question.
- How do endorhiza communities change across the plant life cycle, from seedling through vegetative growth to flowering? Temporal studies tracking the same plants over time are needed.
- Can targeted manipulation of the endorhiza enhance cannabinoid or terpene [[query-how-do-i-maximize-terpene-and-resin-production-in-cannabis]]? If endorhiza communities influence secondary metabolism, microbiome engineering could become a cultivation tool.
- What role do fungal endophytes (including mycorrhizae) play alongside bacterial communities? Most studies focus on bacteria, but fungal symbionts may be equally important.
- How does the endorhiza respond to environmental perturbations such as drought, nutrient stress, or pathogen attack? Understanding resilience will be key for agricultural applications.

## Key Quantitative Findings

Several quantitative results from the Cannabis endorhiza study are worth highlighting:

- Mean alpha diversity (Chao1) in the endorhiza: 3321 (SD = 420), compared to 4525 in the rhizosphere
- OTU abundance correlation between bulk soil and endorhiza: Pearson's rho = 0.42
- OTU abundance correlation between rhizosphere and endorhiza: Pearson's rho = 0.63
- 51 OTUs significantly differentiated between sample types (endorhiza, rhizosphere, bulk soil)
- 71 OTUs showed significant abundance differences between Cannabis strains in weighted analysis
- Zero OTUs showed significant presence/absence differences between strains in unweighted analysis

These numbers underscore that cultivar-specificity acts primarily through abundance modulation rather than through determining which taxa can colonize the root.

## Agricultural Applications

The ability to manipulate endorhiza communities has significant potential for sustainable agriculture. Rather than applying chemical fertilizers and pesticides, farmers could potentially manage their crops' endophytic communities to achieve similar results through biological means. Approaches under investigation include soil amendments that promote beneficial endophytes, seed coatings with selected bacterial strains, and breeding programs that select for cultivars with naturally beneficial microbiome associations. In Cannabis cultivation specifically, understanding the endorhiza may lead to cultivation practices that optimize both yield and cannabinoid profiles while reducing chemical inputs.
