# Earthworks Introduction and Ethics

## Overview

Earthworks — the deliberate moving, shaping, and restructuring of soil and rock — represent one of the oldest and most fundamental human activities. In permaculture design, earthworks are approached as both a practical tool and an ethical undertaking. The power to reshape land carries with it the responsibility to ensure that alterations serve long-term ecological stability, productivity, and rehabilitation rather than degradation.

## The Human Relationship with Earth

Throughout history, humans and animals have mined soils and earth deposits for specific resources. Animal migrations to salt licks, birds and mammals rolling in dusting sites to rid themselves of parasites, and the use of silica minerals (obsidian, chert, chalcedony) as tools all predate agriculture. Soils rich in iron oxides have been mixed with acorn meals to fix bitter tannins as insoluble ferric tannates, and various clays (illite, smectite, kaolin, ferrous oxide "red" clays) have been traditionally used to absorb poisons from foods like Solanum species and yams, to reduce diarrhoea, and to relieve nausea.

Earth-eating (geophagy) is a widespread and natural habit among children, tribal peoples, and animals such as deer, kangaroo, cattle, and antelope, who commonly visit clay and clay-salt licks. The mineral-rich white ash of trees is also widely used as a food dip. Cooking food in clay vessels is prevalent throughout the world, reflecting the deep integration of earth materials into human culture.

## Historical Development of Earthworking

Earthworking for agriculture and monuments has existed for at least 17,000 years. The development of mineral smelting, pottery, and peat or coal mining expanded the scope of earthworks. The modern era of earthworking began around 1800 with the development of large machinery for removing ores and fuels, and the post-World War II years (1947 onward) saw the widespread adoption of self-transporting machines following wartime innovations in tracked tanks, earth scoops for airfield construction, and large pneumatic tyres.

Today, the power to move earth with modern machines is virtually unlimited — new hills can be raised, existing ones planed off, and minor landscape features created or obliterated. Despite this capability, small earthworks remain immediately effective, cheap, and permanent solutions to problems like drought, seawinds, noise, erosion, and flooding.

## Ethical Criteria for Earthworks

Earthworks are considered ethical and necessary when they:

- **Reduce energy needs** — such as underground housing in deserts that dramatically lowers cooling and heating requirements
- **Diversify the landscape for food production** — including fish culture ponds and water-harvesting earthworks
- **Permanently rehabilitate damage** — through contour banks, interceptor banks, and erosion control structures
- **Save materials** — through intelligent house site design that minimizes imported construction materials
- **Enable better land use** — or help revegetate degraded earth

## Productive Reasons for Moving Earth

Earth can be moved for many productive purposes classified as landscape restitution:

1. **Creating shelter** — assisting with foundations and making areas level for floors
2. **Terracing hill slopes** — for stable paddy crops, wet terraces, or gardens
3. **Raising banks or digging ditches** — as defences against flood, fire, attack, or wandering herbivores
4. **Draining or filling areas** — to direct water flow or run-off
5. **Creating access roads** — to commonly visited places
6. **Extracting earth materials** — ochres, clays, minerals, fuels
7. **Making holes** — from fence-post holes to dams, wells, and deeply drilled bores
8. **Creating special storages** — cellars and caves that enlarge living space
9. **Stopping erosive forces** — soil conditioning and erosion control
10. **Preventing noise pollution** — through embankments
11. **Permitting groundwater recharge** — through swales and ripping

## Tools and Techniques

The full range of earth-moving implements includes hand-held and mechanical diggers, ditchers, augers, drills, blades, buckets, rakes, ploughs, rippers, delvers, scoops, earthplanes, loaders, rock-cutters, draglines, excavators, and dredgers. Earth is also moved with explosives, hydraulic jets, and as an unintentional result of erosive processes.

Erosion itself has been used to build soil terraces on lower slopes in more than one culture, though it is debatable whether the terrace concept originated from attempts to stop erosion or from the deliberate use of erosion to create terraces.

## The Principle of Multiple Use

A core permaculture principle applied to earthworks is that of multiple function. It is considered wasteful to see quarries, mines, and roads serving a single purpose and typically left as sterile systems, when they could be shaped or planted to assist landscape diversity. Every earthwork should be designed to serve as many functions as possible — a dam that also provides irrigation, fire protection, habitat, and recreation; a road that also functions as a firebreak and water-harvesting surface; a house pad that also provides thermal mass and wind protection.
