# Client Interview Process in [[permaculture]] design will always be done for a client. Even if you are
doing a design for yourself, think of yourself as your own client and
treat yourself accordingly. The client interview is one of the most
critical steps in the design process, establishing the foundation upon
which all subsequent design decisions are built.
## Purpose of the Client Interview
The goal of the interview is for the designer to get into the client's
head and learn to think like them when sitting down to design. Remember,
design is the manifestation of philosophy. When you sit down to design,
you are attempting to manifest your client's philosophy, not yours. If
you make a design that manifests your philosophy, it will not fit your
client's needs. In permaculture design is a service for others, not an
indulgence for oneself.
## Co-Creation Over Designing For
One of the main goals of being a permaculture designer is not to
design FOR a client, but for you and the client to co-create the design.
To enable this co-creative process, you need to establish a rapport
with your client, enabling you to get into each other's head and
communicate synergistically. This is what the client interview enables.
## Interview Format
The client interview is typically conducted as a questionnaire or
question-and-answer period. Conducting a client interview is more of an
art than a science. The designer crafts a set of guiding questions to
elicit the type of perspectives and information needed from the client
to enable manifesting their philosophy.
## Essential Questions to Ask
While exact questions can vary, the following information should be
elicited from every client:
### Permaculture Knowledge
How well does your client understand permaculture? The goal is to
ensure you speak at a level they will understand and that you can fill
in knowledge gaps for the client.
### Succession Preferences
Are they willing to have a design that naturally progresses through
the stages of and ecological succession, or do they want the design to
jump to the end state?
### Health Considerations
Aside from general health, does the client have any particular health
needs? Are there any health practices they wish to uphold? This affects
plant selection, layout for physical accessibility, and overall design
decisions.
### Dietary Needs and Preferences
Aside from general nutrition requirements, does the client have any
particular dietary needs? What types of foods does the client like or
want? This directly informs the food systems design.
### Physical or Mental Limitations
Does the client possess any physical or mental limitations or issues?
This affects everything from garden bed heights to pathway widths to
the complexity of systems that can be maintained.
### Budget and Time Frame
Get the client's budget and time frame. This constrains what is
possible and helps set realistic expectations for phased
implementation.
### Vision
Get the client's vision. Ask them to describe what they see life in
their design being like. This reveals aspirations and values that may
not emerge from direct questions.
### Mission Statement
Have your client make a mission statement for this design. What are
the client's goals in creating this permaculture design? This becomes
the guiding reference for all design decisions.
### Hands-On Involvement
How hands-on does the client want to be in the design once it is
installed and running? This affects the complexity and maintenance
requirements of designed systems.
### Scale of Use
How many people will the design serve? Do they have or want pets?
This determines the scale of food, water, and shelter systems.
### Social Habits
What are the client's social habits? Will they have social events that
cause intermittent or regularly occurring spikes in impact and
resource consumption? This affects the design of community spaces,
parking, and water systems, and food production capacity.
### Additional Considerations
Are there any other considerations the client has that you did not
think to ask? Always leave space for the unexpected.
## Finding Clients
The best way to look for permaculture clients is to be part of a
community. Join your local permaculture guild and build connections.
Word of mouth and community involvement are the primary ways design
opportunities arise.
## Assembling a Design Team
Teams should be composed of people with expertise in various areas of
permaculture elements: gardeners, builders, landscapers, illustrators,
and community organizers. If they do not have specific expertise, they
should at least have the interest and passion for necessary research.
It is not necessary that everyone on the team have a PDC.
## Organizing the Design Team
The team should follow an organizational mandala structure where each
member is a lobe representing a Design Steward. Sub-circles within
each lobe represent steps of the design process. Every task must be
assigned to someone. A central coordinator can track progress, or the
team can use the organizational structure and trust.
