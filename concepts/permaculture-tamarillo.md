# Tamarillo (Cyphomandra betacea)

Source: Permaculture Plants: A Selection (Nugent & Boniface, 2nd ed 2004)

## Description

- Family: Solanaceae

- Also called: tree tomato

- Native: temperate Andes of South America; only known as domestic plant

- Erect, shrubby, fast growing evergreen to 4m

- Easy to grow but fairly short lived

- Slightly frost tolerant (to -2°C)

- Ideally suited to small gardens in tropical and subtropical climates; grows in cooler areas if sunny and sheltered

The tamarillo is a versatile addition to [[mollison-tree-crop-systems-permaculture]], valued for its rapid growth and early bearing. Within a [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]], it occupies the shrub layer and can provide useful shade for understory plants while producing a reliable crop.

## Growing Conditions

- Rich, moist, well-drained soil

- Will NOT tolerate waterlogging or drought

- Very shallow rooted — heavy mulching recommended

- If roots become too dry or damaged, fruits will not mature and will fall off

Shallow rooting means tamarillos compete poorly with aggressive ground covers. Site them in [[mollison-permaculture-two-zone-planning-and-site-layout]] or Zone 2 where they receive regular attention. See [[permaculture-soil-strategies]] and mulching-guide|mulching for maintaining the moisture and organic matter these trees need.

In cooler climates, tamarillos benefit from [[permaculture-designers-manual-frost-protection]] during their first two years. Planting against a north-facing wall or under the canopy of a deciduous nurse tree can provide enough shelter.

## Fruit

- Egg-shaped, ~5cm long; yellow, red, or dark red depending on variety

- Yellow fruits sweetest; red have harsher, more acid flavour

- Best eaten fresh; also jams, jellies, preserves

- Skin easily removed by dipping in hot water

Tamarillos pair well with permaculture-fig|figs and persimmon|persimmons in a diverse fruiting hedge. Their main harvest period fills a useful gap between summer berries and winter citrus in subtropical gardens.

## Nutrition (per 100g)

Water 82.7-87.8%, Fat 0.0-1.28g, Carbohydrates 10.3g, Protein 1.5g, Calcium 3.9-11.3mg, Phosphorous 52.5-65.5mg (mostly in seeds), Vitamin A 540 IU, Ascorbic acid 23.3-33.9mg

High vitamin C content makes tamarillo a valuable nutritional complement in [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]] designs, where diversity of micronutrients is a key design goal.

## Other Uses

- Some value as bee forage

- Propagation: seed or cuttings (cutting-grown plants tend to be lower and bushier)

As bee forage, tamarillo contributes to [[permaculture-bee-forage-system]] during flowering periods. Cutting-grown plants fruit earlier than seedlings and produce a more manageable canopy for small gardens.

Seed-grown trees show more genetic variation, which can be advantageous for selecting locally adapted [[permaculture-plant-propagation-guide]] material.

## Companions and Guild Considerations

- Tamarillo's shallow roots pair well with deep-rooted companions such as permaculture-macadamia|macadamia or permaculture-chestnut|chestnut that access different soil horizons

- Leguminous underplantings can supply nitrogen without competing at the surface root zone

- In [[permaculture-guild-design-method]], tamarillo serves as a productive canopy element where taller trees are absent

See [[permaculture-companion-planting]] for more on integrating tamarillos into mixed plantings.

## Limitations

- Short lifespan (typically 5-7 years of productive life)

- Vulnerable to wind due to shallow root system and brittle wood

- Susceptible to several Solanaceae pests and diseases

Planning for succession with replacement seedlings ensures continuous production. See [[mollison-permaculture-two-zone-planning-and-site-layout]] for strategies.
