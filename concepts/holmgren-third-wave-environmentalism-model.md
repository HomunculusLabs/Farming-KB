## The Second Wave: Late 1980s to Early 1990s
After the economic growth and conservative politics of the Reagan-Thatcher era
in affluent nations during the 1980s, public awareness of environmental issues
resurged. The second wave was catalysed by growing scientific consensus on
climate change, the ozone hole crisis, and rainforest destruction. This period
saw the institutionalisation of environmentalism through international summits,
government agencies, and the rise of large environmental NGOs. Permaculture
spread globally through the Permaculture Design Certificate (PDC) system and
Mollison's extensive teaching travels. However, Holmgren notes that second-wave
environmentalism was increasingly captured by reformist approaches that sought
to green industrial civilisation rather than fundamentally redesign it. The
focus shifted toward technological fixes (renewable energy, carbon capture) and
policy mechanisms (emissions trading, environmental regulation) rather than
the systemic redesign of settlements, economies, and livelihoods that
permaculture advocates.
## The Third Wave: Post-2008 Crisis
Holmgren identifies the global financial crisis of 2008 and its aftermath as
the trigger for a third wave of environmental awareness. This wave is
characterised by a convergence of crises -- financial instability, climate
disruption, energy constraints, and growing inequality -- that make the
limitations of reformist environmentalism increasingly apparent. The third wave
differs from its predecessors in several important ways: it is more
decentralised and grassroots-driven, less reliant on institutional authority,
and more willing to embrace radical solutions. The rise of the transition
towns movement, community energy projects, local food systems, and alternative
economics all reflect third-wave dynamics.
