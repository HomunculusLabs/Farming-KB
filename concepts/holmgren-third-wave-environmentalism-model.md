# Holmgren's Third Wave Environmentalism Model

David Holmgren proposes a wave model of modern environmental awareness in
which intense periods of innovation and public engagement are followed by
longer phases of consolidation. These waves of environmentalism, as he
describes them, tend to coincide with economic recession in the mainstream
economy, when the limitations of growth-based industrial civilization
become most visible and the appeal of alternatives is strongest. Permaculture
emerged from the first wave and has been carried forward by subsequent waves.
Understanding this cyclical pattern helps contextualize permaculture's
evolution and anticipate its role in future phases of environmental and
social transformation.

## The First Wave: 1970s Origins

The first great wave of modern environmental awareness followed the
publication of the Club of Rome report *The Limits to Growth* in 1972
and the oil shocks of 1973 and 1975. These events shattered the
post-war assumption of endless economic growth and abundant cheap energy,
creating fertile conditions for alternative thinking about human settlement,
agriculture, and resource use. Permaculture was one of several environmental
alternatives that emerged from this first wave, alongside appropriate
technology, biodynamic agriculture, deep ecology, and the early organic
farming movement. The founding text *Permaculture One* was published in
1978, at the peak of first-wave environmental consciousness. Bill
Mollison and David Holmgren's concept offered a coherent design framework
that distinguished it from the more diffuse environmental activism of the
period. First-wave permaculture focused primarily on land and nature
stewardship, developing the core design principles, zone and sector
analysis, and polyculture systems that remain foundational today.

## The Second Wave: Late 1980s to Early 1990s

After the economic growth and conservative politics of the Reagan-Thatcher
era in affluent nations during the 1980s, public awareness of the
greenhouse effect in the late 1980s triggered a second wave of
environmentalism. This wave accelerated interest in permaculture as
climate change entered mainstream public discourse and the limitations
of technological fixes became more apparent. The second wave broadened
permaculture's scope beyond its original Australian temperate-zone
agricultural focus to encompass tropical and urban applications. The
global spread of permaculture design courses during this period created
an international network of practitioners and teachers. However, the
1990s brought a third phase of consolidation as new technology and
global economic expansion diverted public attention from environmental
concerns. Permaculture continued to grow during this period but at a
slower pace, with energy directed toward institutional development
rather than public outreach.

## The Third Wave: 1999 Onward

By 1999, Holmgren identified the signs of a third wave of
environmentalism taking shape. This wave was characterized by the
mainstreaming of many second-wave innovations: organic food moved from
niche to supermarket, renewable energy from demonstration to commercial
deployment, and green building from eccentricity to building code. The
third wave differed from its predecessors in scale and urgency, driven
by mounting evidence of climate change acceleration, peak oil, biodiversity
loss, and the growing recognition that environmental crisis and social
justice are inseparable. Holmgren's *Permaculture: Principles & Pathways
Beyond Sustainability*, published in 2002, was explicitly conceived as
his contribution to this third wave, offering a more evolved and
intellectually rigorous presentation of permaculture principles to meet
the demands of a mainstream audience grappling with sustainability.

## The Recession-Environmentalism Connection

A key insight of Holmgren's wave model is the correlation between
economic recession and environmental innovation. During economic booms,
cheap energy and abundant resources reduce the perceived urgency of
alternatives to the growth-based industrial model. During recessions,
the fragility of dependent consumerism becomes visible, resources become
constrained, and the appeal of self-reliance, local production, and
low-impact living increases dramatically. Holmgren notes that sustainable
agricultural innovation in Australia has historically been clustered in
periods of economic recession: the 1880s-1890s, the 1930s and 1940s,
and continuously since the 1970s. This pattern suggests that
environmental innovation is not a luxury of prosperity but a response to
its absence, driven by material necessity rather than ideological
preference.

## Each Wave Challenges the Previous

Holmgren observes that each new wave of environmentalism throws up
insights and innovations that challenge the assumptions of the previous
wave. The third wave, in particular, questioned some of the techno-
optimism that had crept into second-wave environmentalism through the
natural capitalism and green technology movements. While these approaches
valuablely demonstrated that resource efficiency could be economically
advantageous, they largely assumed the continuation of industrial growth
and technological progress. The third wave, influenced by peak oil
awareness and climate science, reintroduced the possibility that
fundamental contraction rather than green growth might be the more
realistic trajectory. Permaculture, with its design emphasis on low
energy systems and local self-reliance, was better positioned for this
contractionary scenario than approaches dependent on continued economic
expansion.

## The Wave Model as Strategic Framework

For permaculture practitioners and activists, the wave model provides
a strategic framework for timing interventions and allocating resources.
During consolidation phases, energy is best directed toward
institutional strengthening, education, demonstration site development,
and internal community building. During wave peaks, when public
attention and resources flow toward environmental solutions, permaculture
must be ready with mature, tested designs, trained teachers, and
compelling narratives. The wave model also cautions against despair
during consolidation phases, reminding practitioners that the cyclical
nature of environmental awareness means that another wave of opportunity
will come, and that the work done during quiet periods determines how
much influence permaculture can exert when the next wave arrives.
