# Nutritional Composition of Fungi as Animal Food Resources

The [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] of fungi as food for animals is a critical determinant
of fungal roles in supporting secondary production within ecosystems. In
, understanding the chemical
composition of fungal tissue explains why fungi are preferred food items for
many vertebrate and invertebrate consumers and how fungi sustain fungal-soil-food-web dynamics.

## Macronutrient Composition

Fungi vary considerably in their macronutrient composition, but generally
offer a favorable balance of protein, carbohydrate, and minerals compared to
many plant food sources. Representative values from Fogel and Trappe (1978)
include:

| Boletus edulis         | 33          | 5       | 58                | 7       |
| [[lactarius-deliciosus]]   | 19          | 7       | 28                | 6       |
| [[lentinus-edodes-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]]        | 40          | 5       | 54                | 3       |

| [[tuber-melanosporum]]     | 11          | 2       | 42                | 8       |

For comparison, chipped beef provides approximately 30% protein and 6% fat,
while nuts range from 11 to 30% protein with 7 to 72% fat. Fungi thus occupy
a unique nutritional niche: high protein relative to most plant foods, but
low fat relative to seeds and nuts.

## Micronutrient and Mineral Content

Clinton et al. (1999) measured [[solomon-manure-quality-variable-nutrient-content-horse-steer-chicken]] of fungal fruit bodies from
both mycorrhizal and saprotrophic basidiomycetes in a Nothofagus forest.
They found that all elements except calcium were more concentrated in fungal
tissue than in surrounding forest floor material. This nutrient density
explains why fungi are preferred food resources for many animals.

[[dighton-hypogeous-fungi-mammal-spore-dispersal]] contain higher concentrations of nitrogen, phosphorus,
potassium, and micronutrients than epigeous fungi, making them particularly
valuable food items for mammals that can locate them.

## Digestibility Constraints

Despite favorable nutrient profiles, much of the nitrogen in fungi occurs in
complex forms within indigestible cell walls (Cork and Kenagy, 1989a).
Animals require complex communities of gut symbionts to effectively break
down and access fungal nutrients. Experiments with ground squirrels showed
weight decline when fed entirely on fungal fruit bodies, likely due to these
digestibility limitations.

Lichen hemicellulose contains xylan and lichen starch in beta-1-4 and
beta-1-3 glucoside linkages. Mathiesen et al. (2000) suggest this composition
induces increased bacterial fermentation in the reindeer gut, resulting in
development of food-absorptive papillae on the gut wall. This morphological
adaptation enables reindeer to subsist on an otherwise difficult-to-digest
lichen diet during winter months.

## Comparison with Plant Food Sources

Compared to available plant parts, many fungi have similar food value but
less fat content for herbivorous small mammals. This nutritional profile
positions fungi as complementary food sources that provide protein and
minerals that may be limiting in herbivorous diets, particularly during
seasons when fresh plant material is unavailable.

The lower fat content of fungi compared to seeds and nuts means that
mycophagous animals may need to supplement fungal diets with higher-energy
food sources during periods of high metabolic demand. This may explain why
many animals consume fungi as part of a mixed diet rather than exclusively.
