## Mushroom Poisoning and Remedies

Julius A. Palmer's personal experiences with mushroom poisoning, combined with
his systematic study of fungal toxicology, led him to several important
conclusions about the nature of [[mushroom-toxins-and-poisoning]], the varying symptoms they
produce, and the appropriate remedies for each type. His insights were genuinely
ahead of their time, particularly his recognition that different types of
mushroom poisoning required fundamentally different treatments — a distinction
that the medical profession of his era entirely failed to make.

## Types of Mushroom Poisoning

### Amanitine Poisoning

The most dangerous form of mushroom poisoning is caused by species of *Amanita*,
particularly *A. phalloides* ([[query-how-to-identify-and-avoid-the-death-cap-amanita-phalloides]]) and *A. virosa* (the destroying
angel). These contain amanitin, a cyclic peptide toxin that attacks the liver
and kidneys. Palmer recognized that this type of poisoning was fundamentally
different from all others. While standard medical practice called for emetics or
purgatives in every case of suspected mushroom poisoning, Palmer found that such
treatment was "an aggravation" in cases of [[amanitine]] poisoning. The toxin is
rapidly absorbed, and inducing vomiting after symptoms appear may actually
worsen the patient's condition rather than helping.

Modern toxicology confirms Palmer's insight: amanitin poisoning has a
characteristic biphasic course, with an initial asymptomatic period of 6 to 12
hours followed by severe gastrointestinal symptoms and, in fatal cases, hepatic
failure. The delay between consumption and symptoms means that by the time
vomiting occurs, the toxin has already been fully absorbed into the bloodstream.

### Acridity Poisoning

Species of *Russula* and *Lactarius* with hot, peppery, or acrid flesh cause a
distinct type of digestive disturbance. Unlike amanitine poisoning, this form
responded to purgative treatment. Palmer noted that acridity was nature's
warning signal — the sharp, biting taste affecting the tongue, throat, and
tonsils. However, he cautioned that this guide was reliable only for certain
families (Russule, Boleti, Clavarie) and "utterly worthless, even illusive" when
applied to the deadly Amanite, which could be mild or even pleasant-tasting.

### Decomposition Poisoning

Palmer documented his own poisoning from eating common mushrooms that had been
contaminated by decomposing *[[coprinus-deliquescens]]* packed in the same shipping
container. The symptoms — dizziness, nausea, purging, perspiration, and
alternating cold spells — passed within an hour, demonstrating the rapid effect
of what Palmer called "mushroom virus." This type of poisoning responded to both
emetics and purgatives, and was fundamentally a form of food poisoning from
bacterial action rather than from specific fungal toxins.

## Palmer's Recommended Remedy: Sweet Oil

Palmer's most specific remedy recommendation was the liberal use of sweet oil
(essentially olive oil), optionally combined with whiskey or vinegar. He
reported that "two wineglasses of whiskey and sweet-oil (equal parts)
neutralized the poison" from his own decomposition poisoning incident, and that
"in a few hours I was no worse for the experience."

His reasoning was rooted in chemistry: he believed that the noxious qualities of
many mushrooms were due to "a superfluity" of alkali, and that the oil would
neutralize this alkaline principle. He pointed to Italian practice as evidence,
noting that Italians "eat many dangerous fungi with impunity" because fresh
specimens' properties were changed by sweet oil, and preserved specimens were
neutralized by pickle. Either element "renders harmless the peculiar alkali"
responsible for toxicity.

Palmer prescribed this remedy in all cases rather than the use of emetics: "Omit
the whiskey, if you please, or substitute vinegar for spirits, but take sweet-
oil liberally in case of mistake." Modern understanding does not fully support
the alkali theory, but the practice of consuming mushrooms with oil has
practical merit: fats can slow gastric absorption and may help dilute toxins
before they enter the bloodstream.

## Environmental Influence on Toxicity

Palmer made several important observations about how growing conditions affect
mushroom quality and safety, demonstrating the extremely susceptible character
of fungi:

- **Acidulous fermentation**: The *[[boletus-subtomentosus]]* (olive-red boletus),
  normally edible, became poisonous when heavily charged with water from dew or
  rain. Its spongy substance entered a state of acidulous fermentation, exhibiting
  brilliant blue when pressed, cut, or broken. Three cases of illness from this
  cause were brought to Palmer's notice almost simultaneously, though none was
  more serious than excessive purging and vomiting. The lesson: only young
  specimens with substance too solid to admit fermentation should ever be eaten.

- **Chemical contamination**: A gardener sent Palmer horse-mushrooms that had been
  forced with potash — they cooked "like pieces of soap" and the liquor resembled
  strong lye. Specimens of *Boletus astivalis* were found impregnated with the
  odor of decayed fish and were unfit for food. The maned mushroom (*Coprinus
  comatus*) from lawns was delicious, but specimens from dust-heaps, brick-yards,
  or city offal had no flavor and would doubtless prove deleterious in large
  quantities.

- **Flavor transfer**: *[[boletus-pachypus]]* communicated its strong nutty flavor,
  without contact, to potatoes placed beside it in Palmer's refrigerator —
  demonstrating the extremely volatile and permeable character of fungal
  chemistry.

## The Cooking Theory of Safety

Palmer advanced a provocative theory: "no fungus which, unspiced, being plainly
cooked with dry heat, commends itself to the taste, can be, in the least,
dangerous to human life." He argued that mushrooms should never be disguised by
mixing with gravies, frying in batter, or seasoning heavily, because such
preparation "destroys the delicate flavor" and "disguises the very alarm which
Nature has placed at the gateway." One of the most common signs of hurtful
fungus is a stinging sensation affecting the tongue, throat, and tonsils — a
warning that heavy seasoning or fatty cooking could mask.

He prescribed exactly two proper [[comparison-biogas-digester-basics-vs-solar-cooking-methods]]: broiling on a fine-wire
gridiron with salt and butter, or stewing in milk exactly as one would make an
oyster stew. Species tasting raw of grass or trees were best broiled, while the
meadow mushroom, horse-mushroom, and coprinus were excellent cooked either way.
He noted that during mushroom season he ate them three times a day, sometimes of
a half dozen kinds at a meal, and enjoyed his best digestion during "toadstool
time."

## Drying and Preservation

Palmer dried mushrooms for winter use, noting that the Chinese made dried fungus
"a very general article of diet." Based on personal experience in [[san-francisco-shipibo-ayahuasca-ceremonial-center]],
Honolulu, and Hong Kong, he declared that "no better substitute for meat than
fungus can be found." Neither its cooking odor nor its gravy resembled any form
of vegetable food — a natural consequence, as he explained, of the fungus
absorbing oxygen from the air "after the manner of animals," placing it closer
to animal protein in its nutritional character than to plant matter.

## Lessons for Modern Foragers

Palmer's experiences underscore principles that remain valid for [[modern-mushroom-festival-culture]]
foragers: freshness is paramount, growing conditions affect edibility, and the
same species can be wholesome in one context and harmful in another. His
insistence on simple cooking methods that preserve the natural taste of the
mushroom — allowing any off-flavors to serve as warning signals — is advice that
contemporary foraging guides continue to recommend.
