# Recovery and Grazing Periods

periods are inextricably linked: any change to one inevitably affects the
other, and failing to understand this linkage is a primary cause of both
[[savory-land-planning-and-grazing-cell-design]]. The timing of
herd moves within the cell depends on the number of subdivisions (paddocks)
per herd. Given a selected recovery period, the grazing period is computed
using a formula originally credited to Andre Voisin:

    Grazing Period = Recovery Period / (Number of Paddocks - 1)

For example, if nine equal paddocks exist and a sixty-day recovery period
is needed, the grazing period in each paddock is 7.5 days. After leaving
any paddock, the animals pass through eight others before returning.

## More Paddocks, Shorter Grazing

As the number of paddocks increases, the grazing period decreases because
the same recovery period is divided by a larger number. A severely grazed
plant takes a certain time to recover regardless of how many paddocks exist.

Savory notes that the contention that a grazing cell should not contain
more than eight paddocks is bizarre. Both scientific theory and practical
evidence show tremendous advantages from having many paddocks in a cell.
More paddocks in no way frustrate the freedom to plan or provide a given
recovery period for the grass plant.

## Varying Recovery Periods

In reality the situation is more complex than the simple case, because
growth rates vary throughout the season and various recovery periods will
be necessary. Unless you have a great many paddocks, no single recovery
period will suit all conditions because the rate of growth varies greatly.

The fundamental challenge is that domestic stock, being severe grazers,
will predictably defoliate some plants severely soon after entering a
paddock, regardless of how few animals there are. This means the first
severely grazed plant needs its full recovery period before the herd
returns.

## Seasonal Adjustments

Recovery periods must vary with the seasons. During rapid spring growth,
plants may recover in as few as twenty to thirty days. During slow summer
growth or drought, recovery may require ninety days or more. During
dormancy, plants that have been bitten are unlikely to be overgrazed since
they have translocated energy to stem bases and roots.

The planning process must account for these variations by adjusting either
the number of paddocks in use or the speed of moves through them. This is
where the [[savory-ecosystem-foundation-blocks]] blocks. Adequate recovery allows plants to rebuild
root systems, which improves the [[savory-plant-succession-and-community-dynamics]] toward more complex,
stable communities.
