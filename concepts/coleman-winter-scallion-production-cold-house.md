Scallions (green onions) are extr [[coleman-cold-hardy-varieties]] hardy and represent
one of the most resilient crops in the winter harvest system.

Coleman notes that they can even be harvested while completely
frozen and will recover nicely once they thaw. This ability to
withstand and recover from freezing makes them an ideal candidate
for unheated cold-house production where temperatures regularly
dip well below freezing at night throughout the winter months.

The cold hardiness of scallions means they can continue growing
slowly through the coldest months whenever daytime temperatures
under the inner cover rise above freezing. While growth is
minimal during the deepest cold of December and January, the
plants remain viable and resume active growth as soon as
conditions improve in February. This provides a reliable harvest
window from late winter through early spring, bridging the gap
between the final fall harvests and the first spring crops.

In the harvest season charts published in the Winter Harvest
Handbook, green onions (scallions) are listed as harvestable
from October through May in the cool house, and from November
through April in the unheated cold house. The cool house, with
its minimal supplementary heat set just above 32°F (0°C), extends
the harvest window on both ends compared to the unheated houses.

## Purchased Short-Day Onion Plants

Coleman purchases onion plants from Texas growers and sets them
out in the cool house during the first week of January. These are
the same short-day varieties traditionally grown in Vidalia,
Georgia, which are prized for producing very large, sweet, round
bulbs. The harvest in early May arrives at approximately the same
time as the Vidalia crop in Georgia, making these a great hit
with local restaurants looking for an early-season specialty crop
to feature on their menus.

The use of short-day varieties in a northern climate (Zone 5
Maine) is made possible by the greenhouse environment. In the
open field at this latitude, short-day onion varieties would not
perform well because they initiate bulbing based on day length
too early in the season. However, the extended growing season and
moderated temperatures provided by the cool house compensate for
this day-length incompatibility.

The January planting in the cool house gives the plants a full
four months of growth before the May harvest. This effectively
imports the Vidalia onion concept to Maine, allowing northern
growers to compete with the famous Georgia sweet onion by using
greenhouse season extension. The quality of the bulbs — large,
sweet, and round — matches or exceeds what is possible outdoors
in the South.

## Overwintered Onion Seed System

Coleman's second scallion strategy involves a more self-sufficient
approach using overwintered onion seed. Onion seed is sown
outdoors in late August, well before the first hard frost. By late
November, the seedlings are established enough to have developed
several true leaves and a small root system, making them ready to
be covered with a mobile greenhouse when it moves into position.

The sowing density is five rows per bed. This high density is
intentional, as Coleman's system uses the intermediate rows for
scallion harvest while the outer rows are reserved for bulb
production. In spring, once the plants are large enough to have
usable green tops, Coleman harvests the scallions from the two
intermediate rows, selling them as green onions at market.
