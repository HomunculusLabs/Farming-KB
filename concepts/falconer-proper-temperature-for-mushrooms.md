## Effects of High Temperature

A high temperature in a close house or cellar was injurious in several
ways. It hurried in the crop and forced up mushrooms that were weak and
thin-fleshed with ungainly, long stems. It exhausted the bed quickly. The
evil effects were least visible in early fall and late spring when outside
temperature was high and beds were in airy rather than close quarters.

## Evidence from the Dosoris Cellars

In the Dosoris cellars there was a steady difference of about five degrees
between the end near the boiler (kept at precisely 60 degrees) and the
farther end (55 degrees steady). There was very little difference in crop
weight between the ends, but what small advantage existed favored the
cooler end.

At 60 degrees, the crop began appearing six to seven weeks after spawning,
lasted three to four weeks in heavy bearing plus a week or more in light
bearing, then gradually dwindled.

## Cool Temperature Results

At 55 degrees, mushrooms might take seven weeks to appear. At 50 degrees,
a few days longer, but the mushrooms were firm, heavy, short-stemmed,
perhaps a little furry on top and clammy to the touch, and the beds lasted
in good bearing for two months -- "indeed, often a whole winter long."
However, the total crop from a bed at 45 to 50 degrees was no greater than
from a like bed at 55 to 57 degrees. It was merely a case of getting the
same yield in six weeks from the warmer house versus ten weeks from the
cooler one.

## Outdoor Temperature Protection

For outdoor beds, London market gardeners used heavy covering with hay or
other material to maintain warmth in sharp frosty weather. This practice
allowed ridge beds to continue bearing through winter months, though the
protective work became more trouble than the crop was worth in the most
severe weather.
