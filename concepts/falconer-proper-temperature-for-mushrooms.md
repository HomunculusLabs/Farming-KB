# Falconer Proper Temperature for Mushrooms

Chapter XV of William Falconer's *Mushrooms: How to Grow Them* (1891)
provides specific guidance on atmospheric temperature management for
mushroom cultivation, a subject Falconer considered vital to success.
Temperature directly affected crop speed, mushroom quality, stem length,
flesh density, and bed longevity.

## The Ideal Range

The best temperature for the mushroom house or
[[falconer-mushroom-cellar-management]] was 55 to 57 degrees
Fahrenheit. However, much depended upon the method of growing, the
construction of the building, and other circumstances. Mushrooms could be
grown successfully in buildings ranging from 20 degrees to 65 degrees, but
when the average reached 70 degrees success could not be hoped for.

## Effects of High Temperature

A high temperature in a close house or cellar was injurious in several
ways. It hurried in the crop and forced up mushrooms that were weak and
thin-fleshed with ungainly, long stems. It exhausted the bed quickly. The
evil effects were least visible in early fall and late spring when outside
temperature was high and beds were in airy rather than close quarters.

## Evidence from the Dosoris Cellars

In the Dosoris cellars there was a steady difference of about five degrees
between the end near the boiler (kept at precisely 60 degrees) and the
farther end (55 degrees steady). There was very little difference in crop
weight between the ends, but what small advantage existed favored the
cooler end.

At 60 degrees, the crop began appearing six to seven weeks after spawning,
lasted three to four weeks in heavy bearing plus a week or more in light
bearing, then gradually dwindled.

## Cool Temperature Results

At 55 degrees, mushrooms might take seven weeks to appear. At 50 degrees,
a few days longer, but the mushrooms were firm, heavy, short-stemmed,
perhaps a little furry on top and clammy to the touch, and the beds lasted
in good bearing for two months -- "indeed, often a whole winter long."
However, the total crop from a bed at 45 to 50 degrees was no greater than
from a like bed at 55 to 57 degrees. It was merely a case of getting the
same yield in six weeks from the warmer house versus ten weeks from the
cooler one.

## Bed Temperature vs. Atmospheric Temperature

Falconer distinguished between bed temperature and atmospheric
temperature. The warmth generated by the fermenting manure within the bed
was the primary heat source for the [[falconer-mushroom-spawn-preparation]] and developing mushrooms. Atmospheric temperature
supplemented this but also moderated it, preventing overheating of the bed
surface while maintaining conditions favorable for mushroom development.

## Covering for Warmth

At 50 degrees it was not necessary to cover beds to increase warmth, nor
even at 45 degrees if there was fair warmth in the body of the bed to
keep the spawn working. But if bed interior warmth fell under 57 degrees
and atmospheric temperature under 45 degrees, the bed should be kept warm
by covering with hay, straw, matting, or other material, or better still
by boxing it over and laying covering on the outside of the boxing. "When
cold thicken the covering, when warm lessen it."

## Seasonal Considerations

Temperature management varied by season. In summer, the challenge was
keeping structures cool enough, and many growers simply stopped production
during the warmest months. In winter, the challenge was maintaining
adequate warmth without overheating. [[query-how-to-manage-pests-and-diseases-in-mushroom-cultivation]] appeared in April regardless of cellar temperature, making summer
cultivation impractical.

## Outdoor Temperature Protection

For outdoor beds, London market gardeners used heavy covering with hay or
other material to maintain warmth in sharp frosty weather. This practice
allowed ridge beds to continue bearing through winter months, though the
protective work became more trouble than the crop was worth in the most
severe weather.
