# Mollison: Tropical Soils and Deep Granitic Sands

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 10, Section 10.3)

## Special Problems of Tropical Soils

Except in areas of recent vulcanism such as Indonesia, tropical soils are old (not renewed by glaciation) and deeply leached. Most silica and calcium is in low supply. In clays, aluminium ions substitute for some silica ions, giving soil particles a net negative charge. Especially in oxidic kaolinitic soils (common in weathered volcanics), only kaolin clays and oxides of iron-aluminium remain.

In these soils, the charge or cation exchange capacity (CEC) of the soils is affected by pH. Once cleared, the humic particles leach out to about 30 percent of prior levels, and infertility appears in crops such as banana and sugar cane.

## Strategies for Soil Restoration

Mollison cites Wayne Ralph ("Managing Some Tropical Soils" in Rural Research No. 117) for several approaches:

1. Restore humus with green crop, and especially perennials such as Leucaena and tree legumes generally. Any cultivation loses humus as carbon dioxide, so try to grow plants with intercrop.

2. Add small quantities of superphosphate at frequent intervals so that plants can take it up before leaching. If possible, add fine crushed basalt, a scatter of cement powder, and use shredded bamboo or cane mulches for silica and calcium. Increase pH with lime after trees and green crop are growing well.

3. Whatever is added or available as fertiliser, give as a light spread all year at 6-week intervals until plants are well grown. If at all possible, substitute perennial for annual crop, and never practice frequent cultivation.

Basalt, cement powder, coral, and bamboo mulch supply essential nutrients and increase soil pH, hence increase the negative charge on soil particles and their ability to hold calcium, sodium, and phosphates against leaching.

## Coral Cay Soils

On coral cays, calcium-rich sands bind to phosphate to form insoluble calcium triphosphate, so that a sort of cement (platin or calcrete) forms. This may be naturally evolved from guano of seabirds, but superphosphate rapidly forms the platin by its greater solubility. In calcium-rich tropical soils, fine rock phosphate yields more slowly and is therefore more likely to provide long-term benefits.

A return of crop wastes as mulch is also essential, which can reduce pH in coral sands (pH 8 to 9) to a level nearer pH 6.5 or 7, suitable for gardens.

## Deep Granitic Sands

In soils over rotted granites (Indian Ocean high islands, the Deccan in India, inselbergs), a peculiar problem arises: open, coarse, granitic sands, often very deep, will not retain mulch beyond one growing season.

### Broadscale Strategy

Palms, Albizzia spp., Inga spp., Acacia spp., and a general planting of adapted leguminous trees establish a light canopy of leaves if small amounts of nutrient are added at regular intervals. Palms planted in mulch-filled pits establish high crown cover, and detritus from legumes can establish more valuable fruit trees (mango, cashew, pomegranate, persimmon, citrus, tamarind, lychee, custard apple, avocado). Niches and clefts in the granite mass hold pockets of soil and mulch for valuable trees, and vines establish to cover granite slabs, which maintain heat and ripen crop effectively.

It is the mycelial web of pioneer legume roots which enables maintenance of the benefit of applied nutrients, reduction of water use, and establishment of fruiting trees in sparse and drought-prone soils. Drainage is excellent, and deep-rooting trees thrive. Palms suited to these sites: date, coconut, doum, and Borassus palms.

### Garden Strategy

Excavate long trenches in loose sand (1 to 2 m wide, 1 to 1.5 m deep), lay in a sheet-plastic base (upturned at one end only) or line the trench thickly with cardboard, paper, carpet, and leaf, then backfill with sandy loam. The deep sealed layer holds water and leached mulch, and household waste water can be led into these trenches.

Alternatively, build log-boxes above ground level, carpet the base with plastic or thick paper, and fill with humus-sand mixes for green crop and vegetables, top-mulching as needed. Large domestic water tanks, gleyed ponds, solid granite dams, and underground plastic-lined cisterns back-filled with sand will hold water.

## Soil Life in the Tropics

Termites, ants, and some worms are the obvious soil mesofauna. Both ants and termites are very active in transport of rotted rock and subsoil to the surface, in opening galleries for water infiltration, and in breakdown of woody and leafy plant material.

Harris (1971) records that both leaf-cutter ants in South America and termite mounds in Uganda assist forest spread or establish islands of taller vegetation in grasslands on their mounds or colonies. Termite and ant mounds present rich deposits of calcium and potash, better aeration of soil, and faster infiltration of water to release minerals from rocks such as granites.

Planting in ant or termite mounds is a particular example of niche gardening widely applicable to the tropics. Mollison has successfully germinated daikon radish in ant heaps in grasslands as part of a changeover to crop production. Useful crops deliberately cultivated on large mounds include sisal, cotton, and tobacco. Palms and coffee can have much of their outer bark removed by termites without suffering loss of production.

## Sources of Humus for Tropical Soils

- Logs and branches of trees, sometimes rotted in wet terraces or piled as rough mulch around new tree plantings. Palm wastes are often plentiful.
- Detritus from stands of bamboo, pines, Casuarina.
- Aquatic weed mats and emergent water weeds.
- Crop wastes and manures, household wastes.
- Hedgerow, forage, and specially-grown mulch plants.
- Green mulch and ground cover.

The rapid breakdown of wood under rain, heat, termites, and fungi means whole logs can be levered together or inlined cross-slope to act as planting sites. This technique is most useful on bare clay soils, eroded areas, and isolated atolls.

In Hawaii, a traditional strategy is to rot kukui tree (Aleurites moluccensis) logs in shallow water of taro terraces. As logs rot, an edible fungi appears, taken off as crop. The remaining log is then crushed and spread in the taro terrace.
