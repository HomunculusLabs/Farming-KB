# Growing and Season Extension
Successful gardening requires knowing not just what to plant, but when and how to
care for plants through the season. Permaculture gardens are intensively managed
with constant succession planting.
## Seasonal Planting Schedule
**Before first frost (2 weeks):** Start sprouting cold-hardy brassicas indoors
(cabbage, broccoli)
**Last frost date:** Plant cold-hardy plants outside — lettuce, kale, dill, radish,
parsnip, mustard, arugula, carrots. Plant close together, especially lettuce.
**1 month after last frost:** Radishes ready to eat. Transplant brassica seedlings
into radish space. Herbs and leaf vegetables begin ready.
**Early summer:** Remove some whole lettuce heads, plant bush beans in their
spaces. Cabbages begin ripening, remaining greens try to bolt — harvest leaves
quickly.
**Fall:** Harvest parsnips. As plants deplete, put in fava beans or push garlic
cloves into the ground.
## Making Annuals Perennial
Several techniques can reduce replanting work:
- **Leek:** Cut at ground instead of pulling — grows a second time. Or let go to
  seed, dig up secondary bulbs at stem base
- **Garlic:** Leave in ground 2+ years for everlasting crop
- **Broad bean:** Large pods near base fall and dry; mulch with straw in late
  summer, they sprout in fall
- **Potato:** Leave seed potatoes in ground, mulch well in fall, they sprout in
  spring
- **Lettuce:** Let go to seed — it self-scatters and replants
- **Tomatoes/pumpkins/melons:** Leave in garden covered with mulch, they rot and
  spill seeds
- **Carrot:** Keep tops in dark cool place until they sprout, set out to grow
- **Cabbage:** Cut stalk high leaving a few leaves — small heads spring up from
  the stalk
## Frost Protection
**Cloche:** Miniature greenhouse — hoop house over raised bed with plastic, or
tiny glass box over a bed. Plastic milk jugs with bottom cut off work too. Pop
over plants at night, lift off in morning.
**Cold frame:** Wooden box (raised bed) with glass lid on hinges. Old house
windows work well. Open during day, shut at night.
## Cold-Hardy Quick-Growing Plants
Anise, basil, broccoli, caraway, celery, chamomile, cherry tomato, chervil,
chive, cilantro, cucumber, endive, Japanese greens, lettuce, mint, nasturtium,
okra, oriental cabbage, parsley, rosemary, sage, Swiss chard, tarragon, thyme,
turnip tops.
## Cold Climate Strategy
In colder climates, supplement greenhouse production with:
1. **Extending the growing season** via rotating planting schedule
2. **Preserving food** (drying, freezing, fermenting, salting)
3. Plant cold hardy salad vegetables in early spring, summer vegetables, then
   more cold-hardy plants, then root crops that stay in ground through winter
4. In some places, grow green manure before spring
Root crops, leeks, and greens like kale are frost-hardy — leave in ground, cover
soil with thick hay to prevent freezing. Unripe green tomatoes? Pull the whole
plant and hang upside down in basement/cellar to ripen.
## Container Gardening (City)
Even with just a patio or windowsill, you can grow surprising amounts:
- Root crops: deep metal drum, wood box, or stacked tires filled with mulch
- Best container varieties: pepper, parsley, tomato, chive, Swiss chard, lettuce
- Maximize space: hang baskets, window greenhouse, wall shelving, sprouts in
  kitchen, mushrooms in dark place
- Fruit trees: dwarf varieties or espalier against a wall/fence
## Trellising
Any framework supporting plants — house, fence, outbuildings, shaderoom, patio.
Forms: tripods, circular baskets, wire frames, archways, over streams. Can create
hedges with perennials (hops), cool areas, and living spaces. Needs trellising:
ivy, grape, wisteria, roses, beans, cucumbers, melons, squash, peas, tomatoes.
## Succession Planting in Detail
Succession planting is the backbone of continuous production in a permaculture
garden. Rather than planting a single crop and waiting for harvest, overlapping
plantings ensure that as one crop finishes, another is already growing. A
practical approach involves dividing beds into sections and planting each section
one to two weeks apart. Quick-maturing crops like radishes (25-30 days), lettuce
(40-50 days), and bush beans (50-60 days) are ideal for succession planting. When
an early crop is harvested, its space is immediately replanted with a follow-up
crop. For example, peas finishing in early summer give way to bush beans, which
are followed by fall brassicas. Keeping a garden journal with planting and
harvest dates allows refinement of the succession schedule each year for the
specific local climate.
## Winter Gardening Strategies
Winter gardening extends the productive season well beyond the first frost. In
addition to cloches and cold frames, a walipini (sunken greenhouse) or low
tunnel covered with heavy row cover fabric can protect hardy crops through
temperatures as low as -10°C. Winter crops include kale, spinach, mache (corn
salad), claytonia, miner's lettuce, parsley, and Brussels sprouts, all of which
improve in flavor after frost as the plants convert starches to sugars. Mulching
root crops like parsnips, carrots, and leeks with 30 cm or more of straw allows
in-ground storage and harvest throughout winter in many climates. Garlic and
shallots planted in autumn establish roots before winter dormancy and emerge early
in spring for one of the first fresh harvests.
## Seed Saving for Self-Reliance
Saving seed from the garden closes the loop and reduces dependence on purchased
inputs. Open-pollinated (not hybrid) varieties produce seed that grows true to
type in subsequent generations. Biennial crops like carrots, beets, cabbage, and
kale must overwinter before flowering and setting seed in their second year.
Annual crops like tomatoes, peppers, lettuce, beans, and peas produce seed in a
single season. Fermenting tomato seeds in their own juice for three days before
drying removes the gelatinous coating and reduces seed-borne diseases. Storing
dried seeds in cool, dark, dry conditions (ideally in sealed glass jars in the
refrigerator) maintains viability for three to ten years depending on the species.
Selecting seed from the healthiest, most productive plants each season gradually
adapts varieties to local conditions — a process known as
[[permaculture-seed-saving-breeding]].
## Soil Building Through the Season
Soil health must be maintained and improved continuously throughout the growing
season. Green manure crops like buckwheat (summer) and crimson clover (autumn)
add organic matter and fix nitrogen between main crops. Buckwheat grows quickly
in warm weather, suppresses weeds, and can be turned under at flowering (about 40
days) to add phosphorus-rich biomass. Crimson clover sown in late summer provides
nitrogen and winter ground cover before being turned under in spring. Compost
applications of 2-5 cm spread across beds before each major planting replenish
nutrients. Avoid walking on planted beds to prevent soil compaction — use
designated paths and reaching boards. Worm composting (vermicomposting) in a bin
near the kitchen provides a steady supply of nutrient-dense castings for
top-dressing heavy feeders.
## Microclimate Creation
Creating favorable microclimates allows gardeners to push the boundaries of their
growing zone. Stone walls absorb heat during the day and release it at night,
raising nearby air temperatures by several degrees and allowing tender plants to
survive further into autumn. South-facing walls painted white reflect light onto
nearby beds, increasing photosynthesis. Windbreaks of dense evergreen hedges on
the north and west sides of the garden reduce wind chill and desiccation. Water
bodies (ponds, barrels) moderate temperature extremes through thermal mass — they
warm slowly in spring and cool slowly in autumn, buffering nearby plants. Dense
plantings create a humid microclimate at soil level that benefits seedlings and
reduces water loss. These [[holzer-raised-bed-techniques-hugelkultur]] are especially valuable in
