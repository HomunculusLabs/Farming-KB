# Permaculture Zone Planning Detailed

updated: 2026-18-04

Zone planning is the spatial organisation of a permaculture site based on frequency of human visit and management intensity. It is one of the core analytical tools in permaculture design, described in detail in Chapter 3 of Mollison's Designers' Manual.

## The Zonation Concept

In zonation, the village or dwelling itself is Zone 0, the origin from which we work. The available energy in Zone 0 is human, animal, piped-in, or created on site. Whatever the sources, these energies can be thought of as available or on-site energies. To conserve work and time, components are placed according to visit frequency.

Zoning is decided on two factors:
1. The number of times you need to visit the plant, animal, or structure
2. The number of times the plant, animal, or structure needs you to visit it

The golden rule is to develop the nearest area first, get it under control, and then expand the perimeter.

## Zone 0: The House or Village

This zone encompasses good house design, attached glasshouse or shadehouse, and the integration of living components: sod roof, vines, trellis, pot plants, roof gardens, and companion animals. In some climates, many of these structures are formed of the natural environment and will in time return to it (bamboo, rattan, wattle and daub, thatch, earth-covered structures).

## Zone 1: The Home Garden

Components needing continual observation, frequent visits, and work input go here: fully-mulched and pruned gardens, chicken laying boxes, parsley and culinary herbs. Within 6 m of a home, householders can produce most food necessary to existence.

Contents: seedlings, young trees for outer zone placement, mother plants for cuttings, rare and delicate species, small domestic and quiet animals (fish, rabbits, pigeons, guinea pigs), culinary herbs, rainwater catchment tanks. Techniques include complete mulching, intensive pruning of trees, annuals with fast replacement, full land use, and nutrient recycling of household wastes. In this zone, we arrange nature to serve our needs.

## Zone 2: The Orchard and Poultry

Less intensively managed: spot-mulched orchards, main-crop beds, ranging domestic animals. Structures such as terraces, small ponds, hedges, and trellis. Where winter forces all people and animals indoors, joint accommodation units are normal. In milder climates, forage ranges for milk cows, goats, or poultry can be placed in Zone 2. Home orchards established here with less intensive pruning. Water may be piped from Zone 3, or conserved by species selection.

## Zone 3: The Farm Zone

Commercial crop and animals for sale or barter. Managed by green manuring, spreading manure from Zone 2, and soil conditioning. Contains natural or little-pruned trees, broadscale farming systems, large water storages, soil absorption of water, feed-store or barns, and field shelters as hedgerow or windbreak.

## Zone 4: Gathering and Forestry

Borders on forest or wilderness, still managed for wild gathering, forest and fuel needs, pasture or range. Planted to hardy, unpruned, or volunteer trees. Water stored as dams only, with piped input to other zones. Wind energy may be used to lift water to other areas.

## Zone 5: The Natural Environment

Characterised as natural, unmanaged environment used for occasional foraging, recreation, or just let be. This is where we learn the rules that we try to apply elsewhere.

## Deforming Zones to Fit Landscape

As the perfect target model does not fit on real sites, we deform it to fit the landscape. Wedges of a wilderness zone can extend right to the front door (a corridor for wildlife, birds, and nature). A regularly used zone can extend along a frequently used path.

## Zonal Changes with Distance

As distance increases from Zone 0:
- Establishment changes from complete sheet mulch to soil conditioning only
- Pruning changes from intensive cup-shaped trellis to unpruned and thinned
- Tree selection changes from selected dwarf and multi-graft to thinned seedlings
- Water provision changes from rainwater tanks and bores to dams and rivers
- Information changes from stored/people-generated to arising from natural processes

## Integration with Sector Analysis

Every component is placed according to both zone and sector criteria. The two diagrams combine to make a spiderweb of placements, putting every main system in its right place. There should be no tree, plant, structure, or activity not placed according to these criteria.

## Sector-Zone Interaction in Practice
The integration of sector analysis with zone planning produces the most
sophisticated permaculture layouts. For example, a windbreak forest placed
on the cold wind sector serves both sector (wind deflection) and zone
(Zone 4 forestry production) functions simultaneously. A pond placed to
intercept runoff from a slope serves water harvesting (sector) while
providing irrigation for Zone 1-2 gardens. Mollison emphasises that every
element should serve multiple functions, and the zone-sector overlay
ensures no element is placed without considering both its frequency-of-use
needs and its relationship to external energies. This dual analysis is
the core of [[permaculture-design-course-methodology]].

## Microclimate Creation Through Zoning
Each zone naturally creates microclimates that influence adjacent zones.
The intensive planting of Zone 1 generates humidity and shade that
moderates conditions for nearby Zone 2 plantings. Zone 2 orchards cast
afternoon shade that can protect Zone 1 gardens from excessive heat.
Zone 3 field shelterbelts reduce wind speed across all inner zones.
Zone 4 forests generate leaf litter that accumulates on their Zone 3
borders, building soil for cropland. Understanding these inter-zone
microclimate effects allows the designer to position elements so that
each zone benefits from its neighbours.

## Zone Planning for Non-Rural Sites
Zone planning is not limited to rural properties. Mollison applies the
same principles to urban and suburban contexts. In an apartment, Zone 0
is the living space, Zone 1 is the balcony or windowsill with herbs and
sprouts, Zone 2 is a community garden plot within walking distance, Zone
3 is a nearby farm or CSA, Zone 4 is regional parkland for foraging and
recreation, and Zone 5 is the nearest wilderness area. For suburban
properties, the front yard may function as Zone 3 (low-maintenance,
productive trees, native plantings) while the backyard functions as
Zones 0-2. The key insight is that zoning is about energy and attention,
not absolute distance -- a frequently visited community garden may be
"closer" in zone terms than a neglected corner of one's own large
property.

## Time-Based Zoning and Succession
Zones are not static. Over time, as systems mature and the designer's
skill increases, zones effectively expand outward. A well-established
Zone 1 requires less frequent attention, freeing energy to develop Zone
2. This temporal dimension of zoning parallels [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]] in natural systems. Mollison advises designers
to plan for this evolution from the outset, placing pioneer species and
quick-yielding crops in outer zones that will later be replaced or
supplemented by longer-lived perennial systems.

## Energy Accounting Across Zones
Each zone has a characteristic energy budget. Zone 0 consumes household
energy (cooking fuel, electricity, heating/cooling) and generates human
labour energy. Zone 1 is energy-intensive in human labour but produces
high yields per unit area. Zone 2 is moderately labour-intensive with
significant animal energy inputs. Zone 3 relies more on fossil fuel or
animal draft power for broadscale operations. Zone 4 and 5 are largely
self-maintaining, requiring minimal energy input. The overall design
should balance energy flows so that surpluses in one zone (e.g., manure
from Zone 2 animals, wood from Zone 4) flow to zones that need them
(e.g., compost for Zone 1 gardens, fuel for Zone 0 heating). This
energy accounting is a key aspect of [[permaculture-pattern-understanding]] in design.

## Access Design and Zone Connections
The pathways connecting zones are themselves critical design elements.
Mollison recommends that main access routes follow contour lines where
possible, to minimise erosion and make travel between zones easier.
[[permaculture-access-design]] should provide direct
routes for frequent movements (Zone 0 to Zone 1) while allowing
indirect, meandering paths to less-visited zones. Gateway points
between zones serve as functional nodes where resources are transferred
-- for example, the gate between Zone 1 and Zone 2 is where compost
is moved and harvested food passes through.
