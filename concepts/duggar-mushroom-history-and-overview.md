# Mushroom Cultivation History and Overview

The word "mushroom" is diversely employed. Our best usage sanctions it as a comprehensive term applicable to most if not all of the fleshy fungi, whether good, bad, or indifferent with respect to edible qualities. In a commercial sense, the cultivated mushroom, Agaricus campestris, and the allies of this form are everywhere in America the dominant species of interest.

## Origins of Cultivation

Mushroom culture had its origin in France, so from the beginning France has been, and still remains, the chief home of the industry. Mushrooms have long been extensively grown in England, and to a much more limited extent in Belgium, Germany, the United States and other countries. On the Continent of Europe, Italy has paid least attention to the cultivation of Agaricus, proportionally.

## Development in the United States

Fifteen years ago, one could almost count upon one's fingers the important and successful growers of the country, and any one planting 5,000 square feet of beds was considered an extensive grower. Today the largest mushroom planters report areas of 100,000 square feet or more.

Formerly, those who attempted mushroom growing in the United States were, for the most part, English or French gardeners, or persons who had come in contact with such work in Europe. The production for home consumption was very small, and altogether there was nothing to be spoken of as a mushroom industry. There was a tendency to look upon mushroom growing as a great secret, a mystery which few could fathom.

Unusual interest has become evident during recent years, and the work has been undergoing a wholesome development. The number of inquiries received by various educational institutions show that this interest is common throughout the country and that it comes from practically all classes. The production of satisfactory grades of American spawn under the stimulus of the pure culture method is directly responsible for this development and increased attention.

## Wherever and Whenever Success Has Been Attained

Wherever and whenever a measure of success has been attained mushroom growing has fallen heir to the subterranean quarries which now constitute a very extensive array of artificial caves, especially under Paris or in its environs. The industry developed in France primarily because of the enormous cave systems created by limestone mining operations under and around Paris.

## The Mushroom Life Cycle

Mushrooms produce spores, which are their propagative structures. The spores cannot, however, be used directly in propagation. Under natural conditions the spores germinate to produce a mycelium (the vegetative, thread-like body), which penetrates the substratum in every direction and absorbs the available food. Mycelium growing in any suitable substratum yields a characteristic "spawn," and "spawn" refers merely to the vegetative stage of the fungus. Spawn may also be produced from cultures of the mycelium.

When the spawn has developed sufficiently and the environmental conditions are favorable, the mycelium enters the reproductive phase and produces the fruiting bodies -- the mushrooms that are harvested for food.

## Early Historical Accounts

One of the earliest detailed accounts of [[mushroom-cultivation]] comes from the French botanist Tournefort, who in 1707 published a description of the methods then practiced in France. He noted that mushrooms were grown in the subterranean quarries around Paris, where the constant temperature and humidity provided ideal conditions. The cultivators of that era understood little of the biology involved, relying entirely on empirical methods passed down through generations of underground growers.

The Parisian cave culture system was remarkably productive. The abandoned limestone quarries extended for miles beneath the city and its surroundings, creating vast underground galleries. These spaces maintained a nearly constant temperature year-round, free from frost and excessive heat -- conditions perfectly suited to mushroom cultivation. The beds were prepared directly on the quarry floors, and the mushrooms were harvested by candlelight or lamplight.

## Spawn Development History

For many decades, mushroom spawn was produced entirely by traditional methods, essentially propagating the mycelium from one batch of compost to the next. The quality of spawn was highly variable, and failures were common. The introduction of pure culture methods -- growing mycelium from isolated spores on sterile media -- revolutionized the industry by making it possible to produce spawn of consistent quality and known vitality.

The development of American spawn was a significant milestone. Previously, the United States had depended largely on imported spawn from England, which often arrived in poor condition after long ocean transit. American growers who attempted to produce their own spawn using traditional methods frequently met with disappointment. The pure culture method enabled the production of reliable American spawn, which in turn stimulated the rapid expansion of the industry in the United States.

Brick spawn, so called because the mycelium was grown into blocks of compressed compost, became the standard commercial form. Flake spawn, consisting of loose pieces of inoculated compost, was also used but was generally considered inferior in vitality and keeping quality.

## The Commercial Market

In Duggar's time, the United States was still a significant importer of canned mushrooms from France, despite the growing domestic industry. The domestic product was considered superior in quality by many, but French canned mushrooms continued to find a market due to established trade channels and competitive pricing.

A practical consideration noted by Duggar: the minimum area recommended for a commercial mushroom enterprise was 7,500 square feet of bed space. Anything smaller made it difficult to achieve profitable returns, and growers were advised not to count on more than average returns in their early years. The business required both horticultural skill and commercial acumen.

## The Mushroom in Popular Understanding

Among the public there was long a tendency to look upon mushroom growing as a great mystery. The actual processes involved were poorly understood, and misinformation was widespread. Many people believed mushrooms grew spontaneously from decaying matter or appeared by magic after thunderstorms. This aura of mystery both hindered and helped the industry -- it discouraged many potential growers but also created a mystique that supported premium pricing.

## The Role of Temperature

Temperature is one of the most critical factors in mushroom cultivation. The cultivated mushroom thrives in a relatively narrow range, with optimal growing temperatures between 54 and 60 degrees Fahrenheit. At higher temperatures, bearing comes earlier but the production period is shorter and the mushrooms tend to be smaller. At lower temperatures, development is slower but the crop may be more sustained. This sensitivity to temperature is one reason why the underground cave system of Paris proved so successful -- the constant, cool temperatures were ideal.

## Seasonal Patterns

In open-air culture, mushrooms follow seasonal patterns. Mid-winter bearing beds typically cease production by May or June as temperatures rise. The production period from a single planting may range from a few weeks to several months depending on conditions, variety, and management practices. After apparent cessation of bearing, heavier watering may sometimes stimulate an additional flush before the bed is finally spent.

## Spores and Propagation

The spores of mushrooms are produced on the gills in enormous numbers -- a single large mushroom may produce millions. Under favorable conditions, spores germinate to produce thread-like filaments (hyphae) that branch and interweave to form the mycelium. However, spores cannot be used directly for commercial propagation because they are too small to handle practically and because each spore is haploid, requiring mating with another compatible spore to produce a fertile mycelium. This is why spawn -- pre-grown mycelium in a carrier medium -- is the standard planting material.

## Beds and Bearing Surfaces

The surface on which mushrooms are grown is called the "bed" or "bearing surface." Beds may be located in caves, cellars, specially constructed mushroom houses, or in the open air. The choice of location depends on climate, available infrastructure, and the scale of the operation. Each environment presents its own advantages and challenges with respect to temperature control, ventilation, moisture management, and pest protection.

## Compost and Nutrition

Mushrooms are not green plants and do not perform photosynthesis. They obtain all their nutrition from the organic material on which they grow. The traditional substrate for cultivated mushrooms is a compost prepared from horse manure mixed with straw, though many other recipes exist. The composting process itself is critical -- it must be carefully managed to produce a substrate that is rich in available nutrients but free from competing organisms and harmful substances. The quality of the compost directly affects the yield and quality of the mushroom crop.

## Global Distribution of the Industry

By the early 20th century, mushroom cultivation had spread from its French origins to England, Belgium, Germany, the United States, and other countries. France remained the dominant center of production, with the extensive Parisian cave system continuing to be the heart of the industry. England had developed a substantial industry of its own, particularly in the areas around London. In the United States, the industry was concentrated in the northeastern states where climate conditions were most favorable. Italy, despite its rich fungal flora, had devoted the least attention to cultivated mushroom production proportionally among European nations.

The growth of the American industry was remarkable. Where once a grower with 5,000 square feet of beds was considered extensive, the largest operations now reported areas of 100,000 square feet or more. This expansion was driven by increasing consumer demand, improved spawn quality through pure culture methods, and the accumulation of practical knowledge shared through agricultural publications and university extension services.
