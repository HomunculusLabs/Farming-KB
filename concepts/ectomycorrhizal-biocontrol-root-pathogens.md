# Ectomycorrhizal Biocontrol of Root Pathogens

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi have demonstrated significant potential as biocontrol agents against root pathogenic fungi in tree seedlings. This protective function operates through both physical and chemical mechanisms, and has important implications for forest [[nursery-management]], reforestation, and our understanding of plant community dynamics in natural ecosystems.

## Biocontrol Mechanisms

### Physical Protection

ECM fungi form a dense fungal sheath (mantle) that completely envelops the short roots of colonized plants. This physical barrier:

- Prevents direct contact between pathogenic fungi and the root surface
- Blocks entry points that pathogens might use to penetrate root tissue
- Creates a zone of exclusion around the root that is difficult for pathogens to traverse

This mechanism was first described by Marx (1973), who noted that the fungal sheath provides a structural defense that is absent in arbuscular mycorrhizal systems, where considerably less mycorrhizal fungal tissue exists at the root surface.

### Chemical (Antibiotic) Protection

ECM fungi produce antimicrobial compounds that directly inhibit the growth of pathogenic fungi. Research has documented:

- Antibiotic production by ECM fungi that suppresses pathogen development (Fravel, 1988)
- Chemical interference with pathogen growth through metabolite secretion (Chakravarty and Hwang, 1991)
- The role of these compounds in creating an unfavorable environment for pathogen establishment (Duchesne, 1994)

The combination of physical and chemical defenses makes ECM associations particularly effective against soil-borne root pathogens.

## Documented Biocontrol Effects

### Pine Seedling Protection

The earliest and most extensive evidence for ECM biocontrol comes from pine seedling studies in nursery conditions:

- **Marx (1969, 1980)**: Demonstrated that ECM fungi influence the resistance of pine roots to pathogenic infections, showing antagonism of [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] to both pathogenic fungi and soil bacteria
- **Marx and Davey (1969a, b)**: Showed resistance of both aseptically formed and naturally occurring mycorrhizae to infections by *Phytophthora cinnamomi*
- **Chakravarty and Hwang (1991)**: Demonstrated that *[[laccaria-laccata]]* reduced Fusarium damping-off in *[[pinus-banksiana]]* seedlings
- **Sylvia and Sinclair (1983)**: Showed that *L. laccata* suppressed *[[fusarium-oxysporum]]* on Douglas fir seedlings

### Chestnut Seedling Protection

Branzanti et al. (1999) provided particularly compelling evidence by testing four ECM fungi (*Laccaria laccata*, *Hebeloma crustuliniforme*, *H. sinapizans*, and *Paxillus involutus*) against chestnut seedling pathogens:

1. ECM seedlings were grown for one full growing season
2. Half of the mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal seedlings were then challenged with *Phytophthora cambivora* or *P. cinnamomi* spores
3. Five months after pathogen challenge, mycorrhizal plants showed **no sign of infection**
4. ECM fungi increased seedling growth and biomass even in the presence of the pathogen

### Additional Evidence

Chakravarty et al. (1991) studied interactions between the ECM fungus *Paxillus involutus*, damping-off fungi, and *Pinus resinosa* seedlings, further confirming the protective role of ECM associations.

## Implications for Forest Nursery Management

The biocontrol properties of ECM fungi have led to their investigation and use in practical forestry applications (Quarles, 1999):

- **Nursery inoculation**: Deliberate inoculation of seedlings with protective ECM fungi before outplanting
- **Reforestation on disturbed sites**: ECM fungi help protect seedlings planted on surface mines and other disturbed sites (Marx, 1980)
- **Reduced fungicide dependence**: ECM biocontrol offers an alternative to chemical disease control in nursery operations

## Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

### Confinement to Artificial Conditions

The biocontrol function of ECM fungi has primarily been observed on seedling trees in the artificial confines of nurseries. Critical limitations in our understanding include:

- The potential role of ECM-pathogen interactions in maintaining plant communities in natural ecosystems has only been speculated upon (Rayner, 1993)
- Most studies involve monospecific crops, unlike the diverse plant communities found in natural ecosystems
- Long-term effects of ECM-mediated protection in established forest stands are poorly understood

### Complexity of Tripartite Interactions

The interaction between ECM fungi, pathogenic fungi, and host plants involves multiple simultaneous processes:

- Pathogenic fungi can reduce ECM fungal growth and colonization
- ECM fungi can directly inhibit pathogen growth through antibiotics
- Both fungal groups compete for root exudates and rhizosphere resources
- The outcome depends on environmental conditions, plant phenology, and fungal species identity

## Ecological Significance in Natural Ecosystems

Several fundamental questions about ECM biocontrol in natural settings remain unanswered:

- How important are fungal pathogens in structuring natural plant communities?
- Have current ecosystems evolved a balance between plants and fungi that includes natural resistance, where only damaged or weak individuals are significantly impacted by pathogens?
- How does the balance between mycorrhizal and pathogenic fungi shift with environmental change?
- Do these interactions play a significant role in determining plant fitness and community composition?

## The Race Against Emerging Pathogens

Climate change is predicted to increase the risk and severity of fungal pathogen outbreaks. Simulation models predict enhanced risks with increased temperature and reduced rainfall (Jahn et al., 1996; Luo et al., 1995), and Phytophthora-induced oak decline in Europe is expected to worsen (Brasier, 1996). This has intensified the race to:

- Promote natural disease resistance in crop plants through selection (Hines and Marx, 2001)
- Develop genetically engineered disease resistance (Moffat, 2001)
- Harness the natural biocontrol properties of ECM fungi

Emerging plant pathogens of global concern include late blight of potato and tomato, downy mildew of corn, soybean rust, Karnal bunt of wheat, and various viral and bacterial diseases affecting staple crops worldwide.

## Fungi in the Broader Context of Primary Production

ECM biocontrol is one facet of the broader role fungi play in moderating primary production in ecosystems. As partners in mycorrhizal symbiosis, fungi regulate nutrient supply for plant growth. The existence of linkages among plants by mycorrhizal hyphae, and the sharing of nutrients among plants of different species, has begun to alter our understanding of plant community interactions (Simard et al., 1997a, b). How these interplant connections interact with pathogen defense mechanisms remains an exciting frontier in fungal ecology.
