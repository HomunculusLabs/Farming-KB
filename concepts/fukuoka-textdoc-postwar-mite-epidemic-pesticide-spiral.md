# Postwar Mite Epidemic and the Pesticide Spiral

## The Pre-Pesticide Era of Mite Control

Before World War II, mites were never a significant problem for Japanese fruit growers. A simple mixture of lime and sulfur, applied twice each
summer, was regarded as effective against fruit mites. With this modest treatment, mites never rose to the level of a serious pest. The prewar
approach was minimal, inexpensive, and successful — a baseline that would later be destroyed by the introduction of modern chemical
pesticides.

This historical baseline establishes that the mite problem was not inherent to Japanese fruit cultivation. Mites existed in orchards
before the war, but they were kept at manageable levels by a combination of the mild lime-sulfur treatment and the [[solomon-root-exudates-and-allelopathy]]. The more they sprayed, the worse the mite problem became. The irony was
bitter: the most powerful pesticides ever devised had made the pest situation worse, not better.

## Competing Scientific Explanations

Researchers offered several explanations for recurring mite outbreaks, each leading to a different strategy:

- **Resistance theory**: Mites had developed resistance to pesticides,
  requiring ever-stronger formulations.
- **Species shift**: A different, more resistant mite species had
  emerged to fill the vacated ecological niche.
- **Natural enemy disappearance**: Powerful pesticides were killing
  natural predators — [[fukuoka-ecological-balance-principles]] that had naturally kept mite populations in check. The prewar stability was not an accident but
the product of an intact ecosystem functioning as designed.

## Mite Sensitivity to Microclimate

Mites are even more sensitive to microclimatic changes in the tree than are scales. This sensitivity provides a natural lever for managing
mite populations without chemicals:

- When black wattle is used as a windbreak or shade tree, depending on
  sunlight and breeze, the number of mites and scales may drop   dramatically or almost entirely vanish.
- Part of the reason is that black wattle, which produces tannin,
  excretes a substance that repels insects.
- The most direct cause of rapid population changes are changes in
  microclimate — light, ventilation, temperature, and humidity.
- Interplanting evergreen trees with deciduous trees creates a more
  stable, diverse microenvironment that does not favor mite population   explosions.

Environmental management — through tree selection, spacing, and canopy management — can achieve what chemical sprays cannot: long-term
suppression of pest populations without collateral damage to the ecosystem.
