Poor seeds represent a particularly insidious failure for food gardeners. Unlike ornamental gardening where a disappointing plant is merely aesthetic, a failed vegetable [[vegetable-seed-saving-and-germination]] crop means lost food production, wasted growing season, and potentially inadequate nutrition. The cost of seed is trivial compared to the cost of the land, labor, water, and time invested in growing a crop — making seed quality the single most impactful variable in garden productivity.

## Defining Ethics in the Seed Business

Solomon defines an ethical seed business as one that exists primarily to help independent, self-sufficient people grow more of their own food more economically. The word "ethics" means doing what would be good for the greatest number of people. The opposite is criminality — operating in business primarily for profit and self, with little concern for others.

An ethical vegetable-garden seed business is one that:

1. **Sells seeds that actually perform as described** — uniform, productive, true-to-type
2. **Tests germination** of seeds before sale, with standards at least matching commercial agriculture
3. **Conducts variety trials** to evaluate performance before adding varieties to the catalog
4. **Sources seeds adapted to the climatic zone** being served by the catalog

These four criteria form the foundation for evaluating any seed company's integrity.

## The Germination Standard

Johnny's Selected Seeds, a well-regarded mail-order company, includes minimum germination percentages on their seed packets. Other ethical mail-order companies maintain germination standards significantly higher than commercial suppliers. Commercial seed companies typically test germination twice a year on seed in their warehouses.

An ethical company will either:

- Sell the same high-quality seedlots to home gardeners and commercial farmers, OR
- Maintain separate inventory for the home-garden trade with germination standards that meet or exceed commercial requirements

Germination percentage is easily and inexpensively verified by the buyer: simply count out 100 seeds, sprout them, and count the result within one week of receiving the seed. This transparency is the most basic quality metric any seed company should be able to meet.

## The Critical Importance of Variety Trials

Reading catalog descriptions, accepting supplier claims, or relying on government variety trials conducted by non-serious vegetable growers is insufficient. For real quality assurance, a seed company must conduct its own variety trials.

### What Trials Reveal

A well-conducted trial evaluates varieties on criteria that matter to home food gardeners — criteria that commercial variety trials often ignore:

- **Flavor and culinary quality**: The most important factor for home gardeners, yet often absent from commercial trials that rank varieties by appearance, storage potential, and ease of mechanical harvesting
- **Uniformity**: Do all plants produce equally, or is the harvest spread unevenly?
- **Maturity timing**: Do plants all mature at once (preferred by commercial growers for single-harvest efficiency) or over a spread period (preferred by kitchen gardeners for continuous harvest)?
- **Storage potential in root cellars**: Relevant for winter food self-sufficiency
- **Frost tolerance**: Critical for extending the growing season
- **Disease and pest resistance**: Particularly important for organic growers who avoid synthetic pesticides

### Trial Scale

Solomon estimates the minimum meaningful trials ground for a small homestead seed business at about half an acre (2,000 m²). A medium-sized mail-order company might use a few acres. He knows of three large mail-order businesses using ten acres (four hectares) each.

A worthwhile cabbage trial, for example, might grow five plants each of 20 varieties being evaluated — 100 plants total. This sample size is sufficient to determine head size, maturity timing, holding ability, frost tolerance, and eating quality.

## The Commodity Most Subject to Misrepresentation

There is no commodity more subject to misrepresentation than vegetable seeds. A bag of cabbage seed might cost $10 per pound wholesale or $250 per pound wholesale — the contents of both bags look identical (small round black seeds). The bag is labeled with a germination percentage, which is easily verified. But what the seeds will actually produce is another matter entirely.

Cheap cabbage seed may produce rows where many plants never head up at all, or heads that are irregular and non-uniform. Expensive seed from a reputable breeder should produce uniform, reliable results.

Solomon reports that primary seed growers have occasionally "accidentally" mislabeled seed — substituting a discontinued variety worth next to nothing in place of their finest product. This never seems to happen to commercial customers — only to home gardeners. The only defense is for seed companies to grow small sample inventories of everything they purchase for resale, keeping their suppliers honest through the possibility of being caught.

## Adapted to the Region

It would be profitable to sell fine vegetable varieties that grow anywhere, but that is not possible. To ethically sell seeds across the English-speaking world, a company would need trial grounds in each broad climatic zone being served. Instead, seed companies tend to be regional — or they don't bother growing trials at all.

Solomon provides a broad climate classification:

- **Short-season climates**: Northern tier of US states, southern Canada within a few hundred miles of the US border
- **Moderate climates**: Middle American states, east coast of Australia south of Sydney, North Island of New Zealand — hot summers, freezing winters
- **Warm climates**: Southern American states, Australia from Sydney north — long hot summers, soil rarely freezes solid
- **Mild climates**: Northern California, Pacific Northwest coast, southern coastal Victoria, Tasmania, South Island of New Zealand — rarely freezes, mild winters amenable to winter gardening

Purchasers have a far higher likelihood of success when their seed supplier's trial grounds are located in roughly the same climatic zone as their garden.

## Solomon's Evaluation Methodology

In 1989, Solomon conducted a comprehensive evaluation of mail-order seed companies for *Harrowsmith* magazine. As the ex-owner of [[territorial-seed-company]], he brought insider knowledge to the evaluation:

1. Sent 69 questionnaires asking about trials grounds, germination testing, and self-produced varieties
2. About half declined to respond (which Solomon found unsurprising)
3. Eliminated companies without trial grounds — leaving only 20 of 69
