# Garden Size and Food Self-Sufficiency

## Overview

One of the most practical questions facing any serious food gardener is: how much land do I actually need? Steve Solomon draws on historical examples, [[palmer-mushroom-cooking-and-personal-experience]], and [[fukuoka-inductive-deductive-intuitive-reasoning-agricultural-research]] to provide concrete guidance on garden sizing for meaningful food production.

## Historical Benchmarks

### The British Wartime Allotment

The most instructive historical model is the British allotment system during and after World War II (approximately 1930–1950). With national survival at stake, the UK government mandated that every local council provide allotment plots to any resident who requested one. By law, each plot had to be at least **300 square yards (2,700 square feet or about 250 square meters)**.

During the war, Britain's farmland was devoted to essential cereals and potatoes, making most vegetables scarce. The allotment gardens filled this gap. Vegetables including potatoes made up perhaps half of a family's total caloric intake during those years — supplemented by bread, whatever meat and cheese could be obtained, and milk for children.

Key observations from the British experience:

- 2,700 square feet of garden made the difference between health and sickness
- Britain's cool, frequently cloudy summers meant vegetables grew more slowly than in most [[huckleberries-and-wild-berries-of-north-america]]
- Mild English winters allowed year-round growing of frost-hardy crops, partially compensating for the slower summer growth
- After the economy recovered in the early 1980s, most allotments disappeared — a pattern that may reverse in future hard times

### The Cuban Urban Garden Revolution

After the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, Cuba lost its primary trading partner for sugar exports and food imports. The government responded by:

- Breaking up large cooperative sugar farms into individual holdings
- Freely granting garden blocks of **one-third of an acre** at the edges of towns and cities
- Encouraging community horticultural groups

Results by the year 2000:

- Half of all produce consumed in Havana was grown in urban gardens
- Urban gardens produced 60% of vegetables consumed in all of Cuba
- Over 500 community vegetable stands operated at 30–50% below farmers' market prices
- All produce was organically grown
- No one went hungry despite ongoing US economic sanctions
- Most areas produced two crops per year
- [[gaias-garden-creating-community-gardens]] donated surplus to schools, clinics, and senior centers

Cuba became a world leader in holistic, non-petroleum-based horticulture — a practical demonstration that large-scale organic food production is achievable.

## Solomon's Personal Garden

Solomon provides a transparent accounting of his own garden in Tasmania:

- **2,000 square feet** (about 185 square meters) of active growing beds
- This produces roughly **half the daily calories** for a family of two
- Potatoes make up over half the vegetable caloric contribution
- Paths and surrounds approximately double the footprint to about **4,000 square feet** of total managed area

### The Ley Rotation System

Because Tasmania has mild winters without deep soil freezing, Solomon cannot grow vegetables on the same land indefinitely. Soil-borne diseases and pests build up without a killing freeze. His solution is the **ley system**:

- Every 4–5 years, vegetable beds are put to rest in grass and clover
- The grass is mowed several times each summer, with clippings left to decompose
- After the rest period, the sod is turned over for vegetables
- The first two years after breaking sod, vegetables grow noticeably better
- By the fourth year, disease and slower growth signal it's time to rotate again

This means his actual land requirement doubles from 2,000 to over 4,000 square feet of growing beds. Including paths, fruit trees, currant bushes, asparagus, perennial herbs, and wildlife-proof fencing, the total enclosed area is **about 6,500 square feet (600 square meters)**.

The fencing dimension is practical: standard fencing comes in 100-meter rolls, and wrapping that around a square encloses approximately this area.

## Sizing Guidelines by Goal

### Partial Supplementation (Half of Calories)

- **2,000–2,700 square feet** of growing beds
- Plus an equal area for paths, rotations, and perennials
- Relies on purchased staples (grains, oils, protein) for the other half

### Near-Complete Self-Sufficiency

- **3,000–4,500 square feet** (280–420 square meters) of growing beds per adult
- Plus paths, rotation areas, and perennials
- Depends heavily on potatoes or sweet potatoes as the caloric staple
- Requires root cellar or other preservation for winter in cold climates
- Each additional adult in the family adds approximately **450 square feet** (45 square meters)

### Without Irrigation (Dry Gardening)

- East of the 98th meridian in North America (the roughly north-south line through the center of the continent), approximately **700 square feet** (65 square meters) per person is needed if relying on rainfall alone
- Growing cereal grains for calories would require 5–10 times as much land as potatoes

### Climate Adjustments

- **Cooler, cloudier climates** (like the UK): Vegetables grow more slowly but mild winters extend the season
- **Warmer, sunnier climates**: Faster growth but may need more water
- **Frost-free days and latitude** significantly affect what can be grown and for how long
- **Soil quality, gardener skill, water availability, [[seed-saving-and-variety-selection]]** all influence actual production

## Why Postage-Stamp Gardens Are Insufficient

The popular intensive-gardening approach of several small raised beds in a tiny backyard cannot produce meaningful quantities of food. Solomon is blunt: you need a fair bit of land — not acres, but significantly more than a few small beds. The intensive method's promise of "lots of food from a small space" is misleading when measured against actual caloric output.

## Key Takeaways

1. The British wartime allotment of 2,700 square feet is a proven minimum for meaningful food production
2. Cuba's post-Soviet urban gardens demonstrate that community-scale organic food production can feed a population
3. Plan for double the growing area to account for ley rotations, paths, and perennials
4. Potatoes are the most land-efficient calorie crop for [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]]
5. Near-complete caloric self-sufficiency requires approximately 3,000–4,500 square feet of growing beds per adult
6. Climate, soil quality, water availability, and gardener skill all significantly affect the actual area needed
