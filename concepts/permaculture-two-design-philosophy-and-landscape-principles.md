# Permaculture Two Design Philosophy and Landscape Principles

*Permaculture Two: Practical Design and Further Theory in Permanent Agriculture* (1979) is [[bill-mollison]]'s second book, written with greater leisure than the hurried *Permaculture One*. It shifts emphasis from the theoretical foundations of permanent agriculture toward practical design methodology—"how to design, rather than designing your site."

## Core Definition

Mollison's distinguishing claim for permaculture is that it is "primarily a *consciously designed* agricultural system—a system that combines landscape design with perennial plants and animals to make a safe and sustainable resource for town and country." It is "truly [[appropriate-technology]] giving high yields for low energy inputs, and using only human skill and intellect to achieve a stable resource of great complexity and stability."

The book is explicitly about design, "not gardening or livestock per se but as elements in a system intended to serve man, and the ends of good ecology."

## The Underlying Philosophy

Mollison credits [[masanobu-fukuoka]]'s *The One Straw Revolution* as the best statement of permaculture's basic philosophy. In summary:

1. **Work with nature, not against it**: The contrast is drawn between the Aboriginal, who "takes only what is given freely, and takes it with due reverence," and the ploughman, who "would cut open his mother's breast to obtain milk."
2. **Protracted and thoughtful observation rather than protracted and thoughtless labor**: Design replaces drudgery.
3. **Look at plants and animals in all their functions**: No area is treated as a single-product system.

### The Philosophical Question

Mollison frames the fundamental choice between two questions:
- "What *can* I demand this land to do?" → leads to forcible rape of land by machinery
- "What *does* this land have to give me?" → leads to sustained ecology supported by intelligent human control

He concludes: "It is war or peace, and the latter takes more thought than the former."

## Evidence from Natural Systems

Mollison presents compelling evidence that natural systems outperform agricultural ones:

- **African meat production**: When land was cleared, fenced, ploughed, sown with pasture, and stocked with exotic cattle, meat protein production fell to **1/160th** of its natural level
- **Australian carrying capacity**: A single grazier on land that once supported 200 to 300 Aboriginal people represents a catastrophic decline in productivity
- **Central American dooryard gardens**: Andersen's description of Honduran gardens reveals intensely productive polycultures where "it is no longer clear where orchard, field, house and garden have their boundaries, where annuals and perennials belong, or indeed where cultivation gives way to naturally-evolved systems"

## The Dooryard Garden Model

Andersen's description of Central American gardens serves as a practical model:

### Structure
- Compact garden-orchards of several hundred square feet surrounding houses
- No two are exactly alike
- Framework of fruit trees (nance, citrus, melias, mango) with coffee bushes in shade
- Tapioca plants in rows at the edge of trees
- Patches of taro throughout
- Corn and beans in rows or patches
- Climbing vines (squashes, chayote, luffa gourd) over everything—eaves, ridgepoles, fences, trees
- Flowers and useful weeds (dahlias, gladioli, climbing roses, asparagus fern, cannas) as setting
- Grain amaranth as a "sort of encouraged weed that sows itself"

### Key Insight
The house and fence form essential trellis for the garden. Boundaries between orchard, field, house, and garden dissolve. The system represents a "semi-natural order of plants, in their right relationship to each other, but not separated into various artificial groupings."

## The Problem of Monoculture

Mollison personifies "Monoculture Man" as "a pompous figure I often imagine to exist, sometimes fat and white like a consumer, sometimes stern and straight like a row-crop farmer" who "cannot abide this complexity in his garden or his life." The monoculture mindset demands order and simplicity where nature provides productive complexity.

## Design in Landscape

The book's design methodology covers several critical areas:

### Zone and Sector Ground Planning
- **Zones**: Elements placed according to frequency of human visitation (Zone 0 = house, Zone 1 = intensive garden, expanding outward to Zone 5 = wilderness)
- **Sectors**: Elements placed according to external energies (sun, wind, fire, water, views)
- The interaction of zones and sectors creates the basic layout of any permaculture site

### Broadscale Landscape Analysis
- Taking advantage of slope for [[water-management]] and frost drainage
- Analysis of existing landforms, water flows, and microclimates
- How much land is actually needed for self-sufficiency

### The Stacking of Plants
Vertical stacking (ground covers, herbs, shrubs, canopy trees, vines) multiplies yields per unit area. System establishment sequences are critical—the order in which species are introduced determines long-term success.

## Soil Improvement

### Broadscale Soil Improvement
Techniques for building soil across large areas, including the use of [[green-manure-crops]], pulses, legumes, and hedgerow systems.

### No-Tillage Cropping
- **Green crops**: Living mulches that build soil while suppressing weeds
- **Pulses and legumes**: Nitrogen fixation and biomass production
- **Distribution of yield**: Ensuring that soil-building phases don't compromise food production

### Sheet Mulch for Home Gardens
- **Living mulch**: Plants that serve as ground cover while building soil
- **Stone mulch**: Thermal mass and moisture retention
- **Keeping annuals perennial**: Techniques for extending the productive life of annual plants

## Broadscale Techniques

### Planning Even Fodder Distribution
Designing grazing systems that distribute animal impact evenly across the landscape rather than concentrating it in overgrazed areas near water points.

### Rolling Permaculture
A system of linked sun-trap/windbreak plantings, each containing an element that takes advantage of extra heat and light. Elements include:
- A dwelling (glasshouse-fronted with reflection pond)
- An annual garden
- A milking shed
- A polycultural pond
- A simultaneous rotation Fukuoka grain/legume/pulse plot

This "rolling" design moves across the landscape, progressively transforming larger properties.

### On-Farm and Urban Fuel Production
Systems for producing fuels from plants, reducing dependence on external energy inputs.

### Orchards and Woodlots
- **Pruning**: Mollison questions whether pruning is necessity or habit
- **Woodlots and hedgerows**: Integrated systems providing timber, fuel, fodder, wind protection, and wildlife habitat

## Design for Difficult Climates

### Arid Lands
- Local strategies for water conservation and heat management
- Livestock management in dry conditions
- Aboriginal skills for desert survival and land management
- Permanent grain plots that produce without irrigation

### Tropics
- Humid tropical design
- Sea coast systems
- Creation of small microclimates within larger tropical landscapes

## Structures

### The Reactive House
Houses designed to respond to their environment rather than resist it:
- Basic sun-wind defenses or alliances
- Earth houses and plant houses
- Sound walls, sod roofs, fire mandalas
- Windows designed for light, heat, and ventilation
- Sewage and waste treatment systems integrated with the house

## Waterworks

### Aquatic Polyculture
Pond and lake systems designed for multiple species and functions:
- Construction techniques and nomenclature
- Pond culture for fish and plants
- Public policy and the sport fishery
- Salicornia flats for salt-tolerant production
- Mariculture including tidal stone traps and seagrass beds
