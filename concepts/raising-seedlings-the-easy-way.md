## Raising Seedlings the Easy Way

Growing your own seedlings is not complicated, does not require greenhouses or hot frames, and produces far better results than buying from garden centers. The approach described here is deliberately simple — it uses ordinary garden soil, basic containers, and minimal equipment to produce tough, healthy transplants for the crops that genuinely benefit from being started indoors.

## Which Crops Need Transplanting

Most vegetables should be direct-seeded. Only a few species genuinely benefit from being given every possible frost-free day by [[comparison-cannabis-cloning-vs-seed-starting-indoors]]:

- **Tomatoes, peppers, and eggplants** (the solanum family): These benefit enormously from a head start in all but the longest-season climates
- **Melons and winter squash** (in short-season areas only): A couple of transplants can extend the harvest window
- **Some cool-season crops**: If you want to harvest chill-hardy crops a few weeks earlier, transplants can help — but this takes a lot of work and may seem unnecessary

**What you do NOT need**: Greenhouses, hot frames, heat cables, grow lights, or expensive seedling-raising gear. With strong seeds and good soil preparation, most vegetables will come up handsomely from direct seeding.

## Seedling Soil Mix

### The Problem with Commercial Potting Mix

Commercial potting mixes are designed for greenhouse bedding plants adapted to growing in sterile medium — not for vigorous vegetable seedlings. They are often not soil at all, but a mix of peat moss, perlite, and [[solomon-chemical-fertilizer-incomplete-potassium-yield-tradeoff]].

Vegetable seedlings should start out in soil fertile with rich compost, which provides the biological foundation for vigorous growth. Starting in sterile medium creates plants dependent on constant liquid feeding.

### Making Your Own Seedling Soil

Use a 5-gallon (20-liter) plastic bucket and follow these proportions:

**For loamy or sandy garden soil:**
1. Half-fill the bucket with ordinary garden soil
2. Blend in about 1.25 gallons (5 liters) of well-rotted manure or well-ripened compost
3. Add **one cup (250 ml) of [[complete-organic-fertilizer]] (COF)** per 3-4 gallons (12-15 liters) of mix
4. If no COF is available, add **one-quarter cup (60 ml) of ordinary all-purpose organic fertilizer** per gallon

**For clayey garden soil:**
1. Half-fill with garden soil
2. Thoroughly mix in an equal volume of sphagnum moss (well-crumbled)
3. Do NOT try to lighten clay with manure or compost — this makes the mix too rich
4. Add COF or fertilizer as above

### Why Garden Soil Works

- Seedlings that thrive in your own garden soil from the beginning will likely continue to thrive to the end — they are already adapted to your conditions
- You do not need a sterile environment; most seedlings from strong seeds will thrive
- A few losses due to soil diseases actually help — the ones that survive are naturally the most vigorous for your conditions
- The seedling pathogens help cull the weaker individuals, performing natural selection

### Sphagnum Moss Notes

- Sphagnum moss contains almost no plant nutrients and does not decompose rapidly
- It creates an airy mix that stays loose for several months
- One small bale should be enough for many seasons of [[jeavons-flat-preparation-seedling-raising]]
- Beware of using bagged "steer manure" sold in garden centers — its quality varies enormously

## Containers for Seedlings

### Pot Size

The seedling pot should hold a bit more than a half pint (250 ml) of soil. This provides enough root room without being wasteful of space or soil mix.

### Pot Options

- **Plastic pots**: Leftover pots from previous years work fine
- **Newspaper pots**: Especially useful for cucurbits (squash, melons, cucumbers) which have delicate roots easily damaged by transplanting. Make a 3-inch-wide (8 cm) strip of newspaper rolled into a squat cylinder, filled with mix, held together with a rubber band and/or string. The whole pot can be planted without disturbing the roots
- **Seedling flats**: Good for starting large numbers of seeds, with subsequent transplanting to individual pots

## The Germination Cabinet (Germ Box)

### Why You Need One

Unless you have a place in your house where the temperature stays over 75°F (24°C) all day and night, a germination cabinet is the easiest solution for reliable seed sprouting. It is essentially a small, warm, enclosed space — the size of a cardboard box — where temperature can be maintained at optimal germination levels.

### Construction

A simple germ box can be made from:

- A cardboard box (an apple case works well)
- A light bulb on the bottom (25-40 watts)
- A sliding glass top (cut from scrap window glass) sitting in grooves or tracks

By changing the lightbulb from 25 to 40 watts and/or adjusting the top opening, you can control temperature to suit. **Beware**: temperatures exceeding 80°F (27°C) may lessen germination, and consider the fire risk in a cardboard box when using bulbs larger than 25 watts.

### Optimal Temperature

Aim for approximately 75-80°F (24-27°C). This is warm enough for rapid germination of most vegetable seeds without being so hot as to reduce germination rates.

### Moisture Control

**The plastic bag technique**: When sowing seeds, cover each pot with a small, lightweight, thin, clear plastic bag. Twist it closed (put extra plastic under the pot and hold shut with the pot's weight). This creates a nearly airtight environment where almost no moisture is lost. The soil starts at perfect moisture content and stays that way until seedlings emerge.

**Why this matters**: Each time you water a sprouting seed, the medium gets too wet and the moisture makes the temperature drop. Neither is helpful. The bag eliminates both problems.

**When to remove the bag**: As soon as seedlings have emerged, remove the bag and begin watering as needed.

## Sowing Schedule

Start solanum seeds (tomato, pepper, eggplant) about **six weeks** before you want to transplant:

1. Sow tomatoes first
2. When tomatoes are up and growing, start peppers and eggplants
3. When those are up and growing, consider starting the hardiest cucurbits — first zucchini
4. About two weeks later, when the soil has become warm enough to put out the tomatoes, think about starting melon and winter squash seeds

This schedule works nicely with one small germination cabinet.

## Seedling Care After Emergence

### Light

- Place seedlings in the brightest, most sunlit area available
- If light is insufficient (seedlings lean toward light or grow spindly), make a reflector from cardboard covered with aluminum foil or painted white
- Prop the reflector immediately behind the seedlings to significantly increase light levels

### Thinning

Use small scissors or fingernails to thin seedlings:

1. When seedlings have fully opened their seed leaves (cotyledons): thin to all but the two strongest per pot
2. When seedlings have developed three true leaves: thin to one seedling per pot
3. When only one seedling remains and the weather is pleasant, keep it outside during the day to begin hardening off

### Fertilizing

**Initial phase (sowing to first true leaf)**: The seedling mix should provide only minimal NPK but plenty of calcium. Calcium cannot be conveniently added through liquid fertilizer later, so it must be in the soil mix from the start.

**Growth phase (after first true leaf)**: When seedlings have developed enough strength to resist fungal diseases, start pushing them with liquid fertilizer.

**Important notes on fertilizers**:
- The most effective liquid fertilizers for seedlings are often not strictly organic — nearly impossible to get organic phosphorus into solution
- [[solomon-foliar-feeding-fish-emulsion-and-kelp]] liquid seaweed are deficient in phosphorus, which seedlings need to grow wide, strong, and fast
- Any organic liquid fertilizer claiming high phosphorus levels likely contains phosphoric acid, which does not meet [[fukuoka-critique-organic-certification]] standards
- Dilute any liquid fertilizer to **one-third strength** and use it about every other watering
- Seedlings mainly need fertilizer when the sun is shining — during cloudy spells, water without fertilizer
- **Overfertilizing is dangerous**: Even slight toxicity prevents seedlings from growing normally. The typical response is to add more fertilizer, which makes things worse. Vigilance is essential.
