The "taxonomic impediment" — the shortage of trained taxonomists, inadequate infrastructure, and methodological limitations — severely constrains our ability to inventory, describe, and understand fungal diversity.

## Estimated Global Diversity

Conservative estimates suggest at least 1.5 million fungal species exist, though evidence accumulated since the 1990s indicates this figure may be too low. At most, only about 5% of fungal species have been discovered and named. The ratio of fungi to plants in a well-surveyed temperate site is approximately 6:1, suggesting that a comprehensive survey of 200 hectares by multiple specialists over more than 25 years could reveal 2,500 to 3,000 species. No site on Earth has been comprehensively surveyed for fungi.

## Challenges in Fungal Species Delimitation

### Morphological Limitations

Most species of microfungi associated with dead plant tissue are identified based on morphological characters observed from collections rather than from cultures or molecular data. This approach has inherent problems:

- **Environmental modification**: Key morphological features are often subject to environmental modification over time, to frequently unquantifiable degrees.
- **Culture artifacts**: Fruiting bodies and conidiophores used for identification may be absent or poorly developed in culture.
- **Non-sporulating cultures**: Many isolates fail to produce spores or identifiable structures, making identification impossible by traditional means.
- **Quantitative vs. qualitative characters**: Species boundaries often rely on continuous morphological variation rather than discrete characters.

### Host-Based Species Concepts

Many necrotrophic fungal genera have species defined primarily by host identity rather than by features of the fungi themselves. This practice has created a plethora of names that do not reflect evolutionary relationships:

- **Colletotrichum**: Species traditionally defined by host plant are being revised using molecular data.
- **Phomopsis**: Host-based classification poorly reflects phylogenetic relationships.
- **Didymosphaeria**: Distribution of common species on decaying dicotyledonous plants does not correlate with host-based taxonomy.

DNA analysis, particularly ribosomal DNA sequencing, is revealing that host-based genera are systematically diverse, and molecularly recognized species do not always correlate with traditional classifications.

### Anamorph-Teleomorph Complexity

The dual (or multiple) naming system for fungi, where different morphological stages receive separate names, creates enormous confusion:

- A single biological species may have a teleomorph (sexual stage), one or more anamorphs (asexual stages), and even synanamorphs (multiple asexual stages).
- Only one morph may be known for any given collection, preventing reliable species identification.
- The timing of morph production is often unknown — the anamorph may precede, follow, or occur simultaneously with the teleomorph.
- Connections between morphs remain unknown for the majority of described species.
- Some species have been described multiple times under different names for different morphs.
