# Avocado, Mango, and Subtropical Tree Crops

Subtropical tree crops occupy the climatic niche between temperate deciduous
fruits and fully tropical species, thriving in frost-free or near-frost-free
environments with warm summers and mild winters. Mollison treats these species
as primary canopy elements in subtropical food forests and home garden systems,
valued for their high nutritional value, long productive lives, and integration
potential with diverse understory systems. These crops align with
[[mollison-climate-zones-permaculture-strategies]] and
the [[mollison-tropical-and-coastal-permaculture-design]] framework.

## The Avocado (Persea americana)

The avocado is one of the most nutritionally dense fruits available, providing
high-quality fats, protein, fibre, and a wide range of vitamins and minerals.
Mollison considers it a keystone species for subtropical food forest design.

### Environmental Requirements
- **Temperature**: Optimal growth at 20-25 degrees Celsius. Leaves damaged
  below -1 to -3 degrees depending on variety. Mexican race most cold-hardy
  (to -6 degrees briefly); Guatemalan race intermediate; West Indian race
  least cold-tolerant. See
  [[mollison-frost-protection-and-thermal-belt-design]].
- **Rainfall**: Requires 750-1500 mm annually, distributed throughout the
  year or supplemented by irrigation. Drought stress during flowering and
  fruit set causes significant yield loss.
- **Soil**: Deep, well-drained soils (pH 5.5-7.0) are essential. Avocados
  are extremely sensitive to waterlogging; root rot (Phytophthora cinnamomi)
  is the primary disease constraint. Raised beds, mound  planting, and [[mollison-pdc-swale-design-water-harvesting]] integration help
  manage drainage in heavy soils.
- **Wind**: Avocado branches are brittle; sheltered sites or windbreaks are
  essential. See [[mollison-windbreak-design]].

### Avocado Guild Design
The avocado guild follows the standard [[mollison-guild-design]]
framework with species selected for the subtropical environment:

- **Nitrogen fixers**: Pigeon pea (Cajanus cajan) as a short-lived shrub;
  ice-cream bean (Inga edulis) as a canopy nitrogen fixer; sesbania as a
  fast-establishing pioneer. See
  [[mollison-designers-legume-tree-inoculation-and-nitrogen-distribution]].
- **Accumulators**: Comfrey (Symphytum officinale), chicory, and dandelion
  for potassium and mineral cycling. Taro (Colocasia esculenta) as a
  moisture-loving accumulator in the understory.
- **Insectary plants**: Clover, alyssum, and buckwheat attract pollinators
  essential for avocado fruit set. Avocado flowers have a unique
  synchronous dichogamy (type A and type B flowering patterns) that requires
  cross-pollination between compatible varieties for optimal set.
- **Ground covers**: Sweet potato, nasturtium, and peanut as living mulches
  that suppress weeds, cycle nutrients, and provide additional food yield.
- **Mulch producers**: Pigeon pea prunings, bamboo leaf fall, and
  [[mollison-mulch-materials-sources-and-tropical-application]] maintain the thick mulch layer that avocado surface roots
  require.

### Root Rot Management
Phytophthora root rot is the most serious disease of avocado worldwide.
Mollison recommends integrated management strategies:
- Maintain thick organic mulch (15-20 cm) to suppress soil splash and
  support beneficial microorganisms that compete with Phytophthora.
- Ensure excellent drainage; never allow water to pool around root zones.
- Apply [[mollison-designers-mycorrhizal-fungi-and-soil-microbiology]]
  inoculants at planting to build root disease resistance.
- Avoid excessive nitrogen application that promotes susceptible soft growth.
- Use resistant rootstocks where available (Mexican race rootstocks show
  greatest resistance).

## The Mango (Mangifera indica)

The mango is the most widely cultivated tropical fruit globally, valued for
its exceptional flavour, high vitamin A content, and productive reliability
in warm climates. Mollison positions mango as a dominant canopy species in
tropical and subtropical food forests.

### Environmental Requirements
- **Temperature**: Optimal growth at 25-35 degrees Celsius. Young trees are
  killed by frost at -1 degree; mature trees tolerate brief exposure to -2
  degrees with leaf drop but recover. Best in truly frost-free climates.
- **Rainfall**: 750-2500 mm annually. A distinct dry season (2-4 months)
  actually benefits mango by inducing uniform flowering. Excessive rain
  during flowering causes flower drop and fungal disease.
- **Soil**: Remarkably adaptable to soil types, from sandy loams to heavy
  clays, provided drainage is adequate. Tolerates pH 5.5-7.5.
- **Establishment**: Mango seedlings are sensitive to transplanting. Plant
  young trees (1-2 years old) in prepared holes with good drainage. See
  [[mollison-designers-tree-planting-techniques-staking-guys]].

### Mango in Polyculture Systems
Mango integrates into tropical food forests as a large canopy species:
- **Canopy role**: Mature mango trees reach 15-30 metres, providing shade
  for an understory of [[mollison-designers-shade-grown-coffee-and-cacao-systems]], citrus, and tropical vegetables.
- **Companion trees**: Coconut, jackfruit, breadfruit, and lychee form
  complementary canopy layers. The
  [[mollison-designers-coconut-palm-dominant-polyculture]] model
  applies.
- **Understory crops**: Bananas, papayas, taro, sweet potato, and
  [[mollison-wild-rice-and-taro-polycultures]] thrive
  beneath partial mango canopy.
- **Nitrogen fixers**: Gliricidia, Leucaena, and Calliandra as interplanted
  nurse trees and mulch producers. See
  [[mollison-pioneer-species-and-accelerated-succession]].
- **Livestock integration**: Fallen mango fruit is excellent poultry and
  pig forage. Goats will browse mango foliage when other forage is scarce.
  See [[mollison-tropical-animal-integration-pest-control-and-recycling]].

### Mango Flowering and Fruiting
Mollison notes that mango flowering is triggered by dry, cool conditions:
- A dry period of 2-4 months during cool season induces prolific, uniform
  flowering.
- Irrigation during the dry season suppresses flowering and favours
  vegetative growth; strategic water management is essential.
- Multiple varieties extend the harvest season from early to late summer.
- Cross-pollination improves fruit set; planting multiple varieties is
  recommended.

## Macadamia (Macadamia integrifolia and M. tetraphylla)

The macadamia nut is the highest-value subtropical tree nut crop, native to
Australian rainforests and now cultivated worldwide in suitable climates.
See [[mollison-designers-macadamia-nut-in-tropical-polyculture]].

### Design Considerations
- **Climate**: Optimal at 15-25 degrees Celsius with 1000-2000 mm rainfall.
  Tolerates light frost when mature. Requires protection from strong winds,
  especially during flowering and nut set.
- **Soil**: Deep, well-drained, slightly acidic soils (pH 5.5-6.5).
  Shallow-rooted compared to other tree crops; sensitive to drought and
  waterlogging.
- **Harvest**: Nuts fall when ripe; ground collection or catching nets.
  Dehusking within 24 hours prevents mould.
- **Guild integration**: Macadamias combine well with coffee, citrus, and
  subtropical berries as an understory. Nitrogen-fixing companions include
  silky oak (Grevillea robusta) as a tall nurse tree.
