---# Plant Succession and Community Dynamics

Succession is one of the four [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]]. It describes the continuous, orderly process by
which living communities advance from simplicity to complexity. All
management of land by definition affects succession, yet its full
ramifications in any given case remain so complex as to lie beyond the
power of any single science. Understanding its basic principles is
essential for effective land management.

## The Nature of Succession

All living communities, together with their microenvironment, undergo
continuous change as they advance from simplicity to complexity. The
concept entered science through plant ecologists who observed that

other organisms invade and simple soil creation begins.

As the community develops, succession accelerates. Moisture is retained
longer, parent rock breaks down faster, and more soil forms. Complexity,
productivity, and stability increase until climate or some obstruction
to soil formation limits further advance. The process never stops
entirely; even in mature communities, constant death, decay, rebirth,
and change continue.

## Succession and Population Dynamics

Understanding population dynamics at various successional levels
determines when certain species thrive, in what numbers, and why. A
particular population begins to appear as its requirements for
establishment are met. It increases as conditions improve through the
growth of the [[ingham-single-species-vs-whole-community]]. Each population has specific requirements
for survival and specific contributions to the community.

### Complexity and Stability

As succession advances, the community becomes more complex and
populations become more stable. A simple community at a low successional
level is inherently unstable. A grasshopper population facing two
hundred possible predators and parasites will less likely become a plague
than the same creature facing only half that opposition.

Chemical spraying, which simplifies a community, actually increases
the possibility of pest outbreaks. When a few fast-breeding organisms
develop pesticide immunity, the problem compounds further when damaged
water cycles increase breeding success for species adapted to bare,
warm soil.
