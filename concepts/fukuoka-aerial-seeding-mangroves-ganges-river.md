# Aerial Seeding of Mangroves at the Ganges River Mouth

## Overview

During his travels to India, Masanobu Fukuoka oversaw an aerial seeding project at the mouth of the Ganges River in West Bengal that resulted in the successful establishment of twenty to thirty varieties of mangrove seedlings growing "as far as the eye could see." This project stands as one of the most concrete demonstrations of his [[fukuoka-clay-seed-pellets]] technique applied at scale, and represents a rare, verified success story in large-scale desert and wasteland revegetation using natural farming principles.

## Background and Context

Fukuoka's involvement with India began through his friendship with the Tagore Society, a worldwide network dedicated to carrying out the philosophy of Rabindranath Tagore. His dream of regreening the deserts of India received a critical boost when Kimiko Kubo of Chiba Prefecture visited his hillside hut on Christmas night and placed a large sum of money in his rice bin, explaining she wanted to give a gift to the children of India as thanks for the elephant Hanako that Prime Minister Nehru had sent to Japan after the war.

Fukuoka used these funds to travel to India and work with government officials on aerial seeding projects. The project at the Ganges River mouth had been initiated during an earlier visit and was now ready for assessment.

## The Site Visit

Accompanied by his guide Mr. Dasgupta, Fukuoka traveled to the mouth of the Ganges:

- They sailed up the vast Ganges for about one hour in a sailboat from the Ministry of Forests and Environment
- They transferred to a motorboat and plied the shallow waters to their destination
- The seedlings of twenty to thirty varieties of mangroves had sprouted on the sandbar and grew as far as the eye could see
- Fukuoka shared his amazement and delight with Mr. Dasgupta

## Ecological Discovery

When Fukuoka pulled one of the mangrove plants from the sand, he made an important ecological discovery:

- A number of small shellfish and hermit crabs crawled out from the roots
- These tiny creatures had made their home in this great river system
- The mangrove roots were already creating habitat for marine organisms
- The revegetation was not just establishing plants but rebuilding an entire ecosystem

## Wild Rice Discovery

Near the mangrove site, Fukuoka found another significant discovery:

- **Wild rice** was growing in a marshy belt along the riverbank
- It had grown on its own without being planted
- The soil was fertile, with a rare alga producing nitrogen and potassium growing over the entire surface
- These were considered wildlands and were not being used for agriculture
- The rice appeared to be a primitive form, with forty to fifty grains per head

Fukuoka concluded that sowing pellets of unhulled rice directly from airplanes in areas like this would almost guarantee success. He felt there were many productive things that could be done immediately in Bengal alone.

## The On-Site Operations

Fukuoka observed the working conditions of the forestry officials at the site:

- Their office was on the riverbank in a simple building
- Workers lived in houses with about 150 square feet of floor space
- A beautiful multicolored image of a Hindu goddess in the garden served as the workers' guardian deity of the forest
- The laborers took pride in their role as forest guardians

## The Broader Indian Revegetation Effort

The Ganges mangrove project was part of a larger engagement with the Indian government:

- The Office of Wasteland Development and Forest Division within the Ministry of the Environment were involved
- Aerial seeding had also been carried out at Gwalior in Madhya Pradesh
- The Chambal Gorge site showed more challenging results — seedlings had been eaten by goats, and deeply tilled trenches and swales showed few plants growing
- In the Madras area, clay pellets had been sown successfully
- The prime minister, Rao, who had studied agronomy, expressed support for natural farming

Moved by the experience at the Ganges, Fukuoka wrote an impromptu poem with a brush on a large sheet of paper to hang in the cabin of the boat. He noted with satisfaction that well-formed characters emerged smoothly from the tip of the brush.

## Significance for Global Revegetation

Fukuoka was frustrated that this example of success had not spread to other countries concerned about disappearing tropical rain forests. He did not know whether it was because India was an isolated country or because academics had not yet heard about it. Regardless, he considered it definitive proof of the usefulness of aerial seeding for revegetating large areas in a short period of time.

## Lessons and Principles

The Ganges mangrove project demonstrated several key principles of Fukuoka's approach:

1. **Clay pellets protect seeds** from being washed away by river water or eaten by animals
2. **Multiple species sown together** creates more resilient and diverse ecosystems
3. **Nature does most of the work** — the seeds, soil conditions, and river environment did the actual growing
4. **Rapid visible results** are possible when conditions are right
5. **Ecological cascading effects** occur — plants create habitat for animals, which further enrich the ecosystem
6. **Minimal human intervention** is needed after sowing

## Contrast with Failed Conventional Approaches

The success at the Ganges stood in stark contrast to the conventional desert farming Fukuoka witnessed elsewhere in Africa and India, where:

- Modern farms pumped river water through concrete waterways
- Fields were watered in succession with precious water
- Water quickly evaporated, leaving salt buildup
- Farms were abandoned after just a few years
- Not a single tree or blade of grass remained

The mangrove project succeeded precisely because it worked with natural systems rather than against them.

## Meeting with Indian Prime Minister Rao

During this India trip, Fukuoka was unexpectedly granted a meeting with Prime Minister Rao, originally scheduled for fifteen minutes but lasting nearly an hour. He brought a small container of rice from his natural farm and seeds of Egyptian clover suited to the tropics. He asked the prime minister to promote an agricultural revolution and encourage desert revegetation through the spread of clover. The prime minister, who had studied agronomy, was sincerely interested and subsequently asked that Fukuoka be introduced to the head of the Ministry of Agriculture.

Fukuoka's discussions with Indian government officials, interpreted by Sister Nagashima, were carried throughout the country on national television and newspapers. The prime minister's support of natural farming was widely reported in the media, and Fukuoka felt that great interest in the revegetation of India had suddenly arisen.

## The Wasteland Development Office

Within India's Ministry of the Environment, Fukuoka worked with the Office of Wasteland Development and its Forest Division. He advocated for simpler application procedures: in Japan, farmers simply indicate on a single sheet of paper the date, place, and area where they want to plant trees. In India, farmers had to submit a detailed plan twenty-five pages long, recording in minute detail who heads the business, their class, who receives profits, responsibility in case of failure, and more. Fukuoka asked that more trust be given to farmers and procedures simplified.
