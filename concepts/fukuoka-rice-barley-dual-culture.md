## Nitrogen Economics of the System

Fukuoka calculated that approximately seventy percent of the nitrogenous components absorbed by rice and barley are supplied directly by the soil, while about thirty percent are furnished artificially by fertilization. If all the straw and chaff from the threshed grain were returned to the fields, farmers would only have to apply at most fifteen percent of the nitrogenous components required by the plants. The clover cover and returning all organic matter to the field closes most of the nitrogen gap, making the system nearly self-sufficient in fertility.

## The Rice/Barley Cropping Tradition

Japanese farmers have long practiced the continuous rotation of rice with barley. Fukuoka noted this "has enabled them to reap the same harvest year after year indefinitely, something which they have always regarded as perfectly natural." He considered this "an extraordinary method of farming that has taken hold nowhere else in the world." The reason it works is that "the rice is grown in paddy fields, the soil fertility of which has been built up by a superior method of irrigation."

## Straw as the Foundation

The entire system rests on returning all straw and chaff to the field. Fukuoka called spreading fresh straw on a rice field "really quite a bold and revolutionary step in rice farming." Agricultural technicians had long regarded rice straw as a source of disease and advocated burning it. Fukuoka deliberately proposed that all fresh rice straw be scattered over the field during barley cultivation and all barley straw be spread over the field during rice cultivation. "It is from just such a small matter that shall emerge the future of Japanese agriculture."

## Water Management

Water management is the most critical part of crop management in rice cultivation. Fukuoka's intermittent irrigation approach -- passing water through the field once every 7-10 days rather than maintaining standing water -- is essential to the system. Prolonged flooding rots roots and creates conditions favoring disease. The brief flooding in June to suppress clover is the only time standing water is used.
