# Urine and Human Waste Separation Systems

## Overview

Mollison advocates for the separation of sewage into solids and liquids
at the domestic level, arguing that this has significant productive
advantages. The separation of urine from faeces, and of both from
greywater, allows each waste stream to be treated by the most
appropriate biological system and each to yield useful products.

## The Nutrient Value of Urine

Urine is a concentrated source of plant nutrients, particularly
nitrogen and phosphorus. Mollison cites research showing that 2% urea
sprayed on the foliage of rice plants in padi has increased grain
protein yields to 40% (11% protein by weight). This demonstrates that
urine, properly diluted and applied, can significantly enhance crop
nutrition without the losses and pollution risks associated with
discharging it into sewage systems.

### Garden Applications

Urine diluted with water to a 5% solution controls moulds on cucurbits
and aids garden growth or compost activity generally. As a nitrogen-
rich liquid, it serves as an activator for [[mollison-dry-toilet-composting-systems]] and as a direct foliar feed for
heavy-feeding crops.

### Chemical Recovery

Separation of urine from faecal waste enables recovery of alcohol and
chemicals from urine wastes. This represents a largely untapped
resource stream that could contribute to household or community
self-reliance in fuels and industrial chemicals.

## Dry Toilets and Their Limitations

Mollison recommends dry toilets in remote, unsewered, or unsuitable
areas. However, he notes an important limitation: dry toilets reduce
the potential uses of sewage. When faeces are separated from the liquid
stream, they cannot be used to produce methane by [[mollison-biogas-and-methane-production-from-sewage]] in
septic tanks. This trade-off must be weighed against the benefits of
water conservation and simplicity.

In the [[mollison-dry-toilet-composting-systems]], if no septic tank is present, a
dry toilet will suffice, and the manure can be put under trees in pits.
Even "toilet paper" can be built into the hedges (Nicotiana is great,
as is Leucaena).

## The Full Treatment Chain

Mollison outlines the ideal sewage treatment chain that maximises
resource recovery:

1. Separation at source into solids, urine, and greywater
2. Faecal matter to biogas digester for methane production
3. Digester effluent to aquatic plant systems for nutrient recovery
4. Urine applied directly to crops as liquid fertiliser
5. Greywater routed to gardens via swales or irrigation
6. Each step produces useful yields: energy, water, nutrients, habitat

## Comfrey as a Biofilter for Animal Waste

The principle of waste absorption extends to animal systems. Comfrey
can stand heavy inputs of raw faeces in solution, and the crop may then
be used for fodder or trenched for "instant compost" under other
species such as potatoes. Flow-through systems for methane production
take little plant nutrient from faecal matter, and comfrey or algae
ponds deal with the residues while producing useful by-products for
compost and stock feed.

## Safety Considerations

### Pathogen Destruction
- In anaerobic digesters, 90-94% of worm eggs are destroyed
- Reed bed treatment eliminates viruses and worm eggs
- Proper composting at 55-60 degrees C destroys pathogens
- The 10-12 day holding time in biological treatment systems ensures
  safety

### Heavy Metal Concerns
- Manures from pigs, poultry, cattle, or sewage sludges can add
  excessive copper, zinc, nickel, boron, lead, or cadmium to soils
- These contaminants are especially problematic in acid sandy soils
- Excess zinc can build up from earthworm casts in contaminated
  systems
- Handreck recommends a limit of 10% worm casts in potting mix

### Nitrate Accumulation
- Too much animal manure releases surplus nitrogen
- Reduce cattle and run them on pastures, not on feedlots
- Dispose of sewage in forests to aid tree growth
- Constantly measure nitrate levels in leaves, water, and soil
- Accepted European standards: 50 mg/l in water (60-80 ppm)

## Integration with Food Production

The key principle is that separated waste streams become productive
inputs. Urine becomes fertiliser, faeces become energy and compost,
and greywater becomes irrigation. Each waste product is matched with
the biological system that can most efficiently process it:

- Urine to crops (direct nutrient source)
- Faeces to biogas digesters (energy production)
- Biogas sludge to algae ponds (protein production)
- Algae to poultry feed (food production)
- Effluent to reed beds (water purification)
- Reed bed plants to mulch (soil building)

This cascade ensures no nutrient is wasted and every element serves
multiple functions.
