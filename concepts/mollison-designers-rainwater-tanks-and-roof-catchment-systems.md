# Rainwater Tanks and Roof Catchment Systems

Rainwater tanks and roof catchment systems are fundamental to
permaculture water conservation. Mollison argues passionately that
every roof, whether domestic or industrial, should feed into tank
storage rather than relying on centralised piped water supplies. This
connects to the broader [[mollison-water-harvesting-management-permaculture]] and [[mollison-designers-check-dam-types-and-landscape-placement]] strategies in permaculture design.

## The Case for Domestic Water Tanks

Cultural and historical precedent may determine how earth is moved and
used, or even if it can be moved at all. Mollison observes that an
Australian, accustomed to a great variety of surface storage, is
astounded that there are no significant domestic rainwater tanks in
Europe, the USA, or India (where clean drinking water is rare). British,
American, and Brazilian farmers rarely use multiple earth storages of
water, and expensive pipelines and bores are the preferred alternative,
even where local rainfall often exceeds local needs.

The simple forms for making concrete tanks cost a few hundred dollars
and may be used hundreds of times. About 22,500 litres provides a
family with all needed water (drinking, showers, cooking, modest garden
water on trickle) for a year. Tank water is renewed by rain at any
time of year.

## Cost Comparison: Tanks Versus Dams

If a 22,500 litre tank costs 20 units of money, the same units in a
sensible earth storage pays for 2,500,000 litres, or about 100 times
as much water. Up to 135,000 to 2,500,000 litre tanks get cheaper as
less concrete is used for more water. A large tank is relatively
cheaper than a small tank. Above 22,500 litres, such tanks are usually
poured on site; below this, they are carted from a central
manufacturing site.

Dams, in contrast, begin to cost more as the height of the wall rises.
About 3 metres of retaining wall is the limit of cheap dams. Above
this, costs rise rapidly as greater skills, more expensive and massive
materials, more complex controls, and much greater environmental risks
take their toll.

## Roof Water Quality and First Flush

Granted that roof areas themselves can be contaminated by birds, dust,
or industry, the first precaution is to reject the first flow-off of
water and use it on gardens or in swales. Two methods are described for
this automatic rejection system.

As for the entry of insects, birds, or rodents to tanks (including
mosquitoes), a "U" pipe entry and exit, a sealed tank roof, and an
overflow pipe emptying to a gravel-filled swale all effectively exclude
these potential nuisances. If birds persistently perch on roof ridges,
a few very fine wires or thread stretched along the ridge as a 10 cm
high "fence" will discourage them.

## Gutters and Maintenance

Gutters on roofing can be cleaned out regularly, or leaf-free gutters
and downpipes fitted. Several types are commercially manufactured.
Given that most dust and leaves are removed, residual organics are
usually harmless. These fix as an active biological velvety film on
tank walls and bases.

Taps or outlet pipes are normally fitted 15 to 20 cm above any tank
floor to allow such a film to remain. Self-cleaning and leaf-free
gutters or downpipes are useful where trees overhang a house. Cone to
pipe designs are also useful to collect water from rock domes.

## Water Alkalinity and Health

A net or bag of limestone, shell, or marble chips is suspended in the
tank. This creates hard (alkaline) water, preventing heavy metal uptake
from the water and decreasing the incidence of heart attacks in those
using the tank. Washing and shower water can be soft (acid) but the
water we drink is best made alkaline for the sake of health.

## Placement and Design

Wherever possible, spouting from all roof areas should be run to
covered concrete tanks. Located on the shade side of the house, under
trellis, such tanks provide cool drinking and cooking water. Every
roof would fill many such tanks, and simple calculations (roof area
times average rainfall in millimetres or inches) and conversion to
litres or gallons gives the expected yield.

## Policy Implications

Mollison argues that it makes far more sense to legislate for tanks on
every roof than to bring exotic water for miles to towns. This would
ensure that clean air regulations are better observed locally, that
every house has a strategic water reserve, and that householders are
conservative in their water use.

Water stored in soil and humus is the cheapest and of greatest volume,
surface dams next cheapest, and tanks dear but still much less expensive
than piped water from mains supply. The chlorinated, metallic,
asbestos-fibred, poisonous water of modern centralised systems is
producing epidemic disease and illness. This connects to
[[mollison-sewage-reduction-and-water-conservation]]
and [[mollison-greywater-domestic-water-recycling]]
strategies.

## Where Tanks Are Appropriate

Tanks are appropriate on isolated dwellings, in flatlands, and
everywhere in cities and urbanised areas. Dams of from 22,500 to
4.5 million litres are best built on any good site in country and
parkland areas. Massive dams are appropriate hardly anywhere but the
rock-bermed or glaciated uplands of solid and forested hills, subject
to low earthquake risk, and then only for modest domestic (not dirty
industrial) power generation.

## Desert House Water Conservation

In desert settlements, modest water use is easily achievable if
efficient shower heads are used for washing, and both shower and
handbasin or laundry water is first diverted to the flush tank of
toilets or to the garden where dry pit toilets are used. To get shower
water to a flush toilet cistern, the shower and handbasin can be
raised a few steps above floor level, and a low-level cistern used.

Wherever possible, spouting from all roof areas should be run to
covered concrete tanks. Located on the shade side of the house, under
trellis, such tanks provide cool drinking and cooking water. See
[[mollison-desert-house-cooling-heating]] for
integrated water systems in dry climates.

## Solar Hot Water from Tank Systems

Heat can be collected from several sources: a solar attic with a water
tank, a glass cover, and aluminium foil over thick insulation; a grid
of plastic pipe in soot-covered sand under glass; or a simple coil of
pipe on a metal roof so that enough warm to hot water is available.

For groups of 5 to 10 houses, a solar pond of 4 to 5 m sides and 1 to
2 m deep will supply not only hot water but house heating. Space heat
and hot water together are about 80% of the energy needs of modern
housing. This connects to [[mollison-greenhouse-design-solar-inventions]] and [[mollison-thermal-mass-passive-building]].
