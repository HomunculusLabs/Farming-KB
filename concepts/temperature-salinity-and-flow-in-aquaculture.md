# Temperature, Salinity, and Flow in Aquaculture

## Overview

Temperature, salinity, and water flow are the three primary physical factors governing species selection, growth rates, and survival in aquaculture systems. Understanding how these factors interact—and how to manipulate them through design—enables the creation of productive pond systems across a wide range of climates and geographic settings.

## Temperature and Thermal Stratification

### The 21°C Divide

At approximately **21°C (70°F)**, a categorical difference exists between two groups of fish:

- **Coldwater fish**: Temperatures at or above 21°C are lethal or debilitating (optimum feeding and spawning: 15–18°C). Rainbow trout will die if kept for long periods above 14°C.
- **Tropical fish**: Suffer cold stress or will not breed below 21°C (optimum: 20–25°C). [[tilapia]] die below 12°C.

A few common species are temperate-tolerant or eurythermal (1–36°C), notably *[[gambusia]]*, the mosquito-eating fish. However, most cultured species have narrow thermal preferences that must be respected in system design.

### Thermoclines and Gradients

Within water, surfaces and gradients develop from the effects of heat, solutes, particles, or stream flow. The interface between cold and hot water is termed a **[[thermocline]]**. Such boundaries can be abrupt transitions if no turbulent flow exists. Where no pronounced surface separation occurs, gradients still develop, especially where wind overturn or current flow is a factor.

Fish, molluscs, and plants occupy specific niches in thermal gradients, and may move with tide or current to adjust to their needs. A river or lagoon opening by a shallow bar to the sea may have coldwater species at depth and warm-water species on the upper section—or, in icy winters, warm refuges at depth where fish kettle (crowd together) until warmer conditions develop.

### Heating Strategies

Several methods can maintain winter temperatures at optimum levels for selected species mixes:

1. **Solar ponds**: A heater pond included within or adjacent to the main pond
2. **Waste heat**: Using heated water from power stations, salt works, or food processing plants
3. **Glazed structures**: Glazing over canals, refuges, or building raft columns insulated from earth and colder waters
4. **Deep refuges**: Earth-covered pipes, shaded canals, or kettles 5 m or so deep in shallow ponds
5. **Solar collectors**: Pond water pumped over dark roofs, rocks, or cement areas extracts solar heat at close to 100% efficiency
6. **[[thermosiphon]] systems**: Pipes buried 1–5 cm under bitumen road surfaces or dark collectors, with non-return valves for passive heat transfer
7. **Glasshouses**: Can raise water temperatures 3°C or more, extending growing periods by 30–40%
8. **[[compost]] heat**: Utilising decomposition heat from compost piles adjacent to or beneath ponds
9. **Heat pumps**: From canals, delivering heat to ponds and shortening maturation times to about one-third

Passive collector systems are ideal and cheap to run, using thermosiphon and non-return valves when heat sources are placed below the pond to be heated. Gains in yields of **2 to 5 times** are common where ponds are regulated to optimise feeding activity and food growth.

## Salinity and Haloclines

### Salinity Classification

| Classification | Salinity (ppt) | Characteristics |
|----------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Fresh water | <1 | Very little salt; most freshwater species |
| Safe for consumption | ≤7 | Upper limit for human drinking water; few frogs breed above this |
| Brackish | 11–12 | Detectably salty; oysters may be found from this level |
| Estuarine | 7–27 | Transitional zone; high species diversity |
| Marine | 33–35 | Full seawater; most marine organisms |
| Hypersaline | 40–60 | Desert organisms and some desert fish survive to 60 ppt |

### Species-Specific Tolerance

Fish which live all their lives in freshwater rarely do well above **8 ppt**. A great many fish and shrimp, however, migrate fresh to salt and vice versa—some spawning in rivers (trout), some in the sea (eels). Because of their wide tolerances, these groups are called **euryhaline**. Many mullet, salmonids, and some oysters and shrimp can be kept across a range from fresh to saline waters. Saltwater fish rarely tolerate salinity below 28 ppt.

### Haloclines

In estuaries and lagoons, a halocline (salt-fresh interface) can develop, with denser salt water at depth or pushing up under the surface as a tidal wedge below fresh water. Haloclines are common in deep lagoons or rivers with barways. Marine forms live at depth, and freshwater fish at the surface, sometimes for many miles inland.

As tides push into and recede from large river estuaries, extreme changes in temperature (especially in winter) and salinity can occur. A mud-living shellfish might be in fresh water at 4°C at low tide, and in seawater at 20°C at high tide. Many species in this zone burrow, migrate with the tide, or return to the river at high tide.

### Salinity Management

Changes in salinity may kill pond fish not adapted to estuaries. Brief immersion in salt water is an old remedy for reducing fungal disease in freshwater fish. Hazards of marine pond culture in the humid tropics include torrential rain that can dilute seawater sufficiently to kill lobsters, prawns, or milkfish in marine impoundments—even with flood diversion drains.

Very rich brackish-water polycultures of algae, crabs, mullet, sea bass, milkfish, eels, and trout are possible in estuarine and desert or atoll impoundments. Salinity is controlled by flood by-pass canals and sea weirs or locks. A **salinity mosaic** can be established to mix species as diverse as carp and mullet, or freshwater and marine species can be cultivated in one pond series where tide and streams interact, as in some Hawaiian fish ponds.

## Water Flow

### Benefits of Flow

Even very modest flow stimulates gaseous exchange, photosynthesis, and therefore growth in plants, up to a level where mechanical damage by turbulence can occur. Flow in food-rich waters increases yields far beyond what is possible in still ponds. However, most streams do not have heavy natural food yields unless they are fertilised or manured.

### Flow and Aeration

The great advantage of flow is in the oxygenation of water, either via Flowforms, aerators, or a series of rippled falls or weirs in the stream bed. Even gentle current maintains dissolved oxygen at levels that support dense fish populations.

### Weed Management in Flowing Systems

When streams are fertilised, a problem of clogging with aquatic weeds can occur, necessitating control by nutria, grass carp (White Amur carp), or another efficient herbivore. This creates an opportunity to integrate yet another productive species into the system.

### The Flow Balance

The balance to achieve in flow is:

- **Sufficient flow** for aeration and vigorous plant growth
- **Restricted flow** to prevent rapid leaching of soluble nutrients and calcium

For this reason, flow is often piped or channelled to fish and terrace areas rather than working in the stream itself, where floods can cause fish losses, physical damage to structures, and a wipe-out of balanced fertiliser levels.

### Flow Management in Ponds

In ponds, flow may be held back for a few days to allow fertiliser uptake, then resumed for aeration. This pulse-flow approach maximises both nutrient retention and oxygenation, and represents a sophisticated yet practical management technique available to any pond designer.
