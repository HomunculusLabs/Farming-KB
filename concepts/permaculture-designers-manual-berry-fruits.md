# Berry Fruits

Source: Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Bill Mollison, 1988), Section 12.7

Updated: 2026-04-18

No region so suits berry production as the cool humid climates. Berries occupy niches from high montane to seashore sites, and natural stands of berries fill forest clearings, edge roadways, and paths. They are food for a variety of birds, foxes, rodents, and people.

## Natural Habitat and Ecology

In coastal or upland bogs, Vaccinium spp. (blueberries, cranberries) thrive in oxygen-poor soil. Each has a specific heath-family root associate to provide nitrogen. In the clearings of forests, bird-carried berry seed germinates on fallen logs and tree stumps, where 46 species may compete for space.

So favourable are these stump sites that slabs of fallen trees will proliferate berry mounds on lawns or in gardens, and peat-like bricks of groundcover berries such as salal will convert lawn edges to berry groves.

## Growing Conditions

Berries demand little but:

- Humus-rich and somewhat acid soil
- High ammonia nitrogen (provided by birds and rodents in the wild as urea)
- A thick [[permaculture-designers-manual-mulch-systems]] to discourage grasses

Berries pioneer for, and protect, seedling trees, so that advancing forest edges often develop bramble and cane thickets. In these thickets oak, chestnut, plum, apple, and birch thrive. Brambles, in particular, protect and nourish young fruit trees. On farms, bramble clumps (blackberry or related cultivars) can be used to exclude deer and cattle from newly set [[permaculture-designers-manual-cover-crops-detailed]].

## Successional Orchard Evolution

As trees (apple, quince, plum, citrus, fig) age and brambles are shaded out, hoofed animals come to eat fallen fruit, and the mature trees (7+ years old) are sufficiently hardy to withstand browsing. Our forest ancestors may well have followed some such sequences for [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]] evolution, assisted by indigenous birds and mammals.

## Cage Culture for Intensive Production

Growers of sweet cherries, blueberry, and table-quality raspberry report economic advantage in completely caging over the area of crop. Such an advantage gives yields of up to 30 times that of open field conditions.

In these cages (about 20 m by 10 m by 2.4 m high, using 10 mm mesh), a polyculture of small fruit plus espalier fruits (nectarine, peach) is safe from birds and even large moth species. One or two such cages of piping plus mesh will support a grower aiming for high quality markets.

Ground preparation for cage culture:
- Ridging for species such as blueberry (ridges of 1.5 m wide, 0.6 m high, with a drainpipe at base is ideal)
- Fitting of drip [[permaculture-designers-manual-irrigation-methods]]
- Deep mulch for pH adjustment and soil moisture conservation
- Careful schedule of liquid manures selected for crop

It is beneficial to use species such as tree frogs, small lizards, and insectivorous birds (such as quail) in the cages for [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]], and to select plants of high fruit yield. Cage walls may support trellis of bramble fruits (youngberry, boysenberry).

## Field Culture and Self-Pick Sales

In field conditions, losses to birds are unavoidable but may be minimised by using tethered predator hawk kites above the crop at critical ripening periods. Such kites imitate local hawks or eagles and are very effective bird repellents. They should be removed after the crop is picked so birds do not get accustomed to them.

As much of the berry crop cost is in picking, it is preferable to plant for self-pick sales where farms are within 15-20 km of town markets. The berries (currant, gooseberry, raspberry, strawberry) are best planted double row on contour mounds 1-1.5 m high, with grassed paths and wider spacing of mounds or ridges than in cage culture, so pickers have easy access.

Self-pick farms need parking and weigh-out centres, buckets for pickers, and produce tubs for crop.

## Small Fruit in Cool Areas

Small fruit -- currants, gooseberry, raspberry, trellis blackberry -- is a feature of cool-area gardens. Six to fifteen plants of 78 species yields a great deal of fresh or frozen food of high vitamin C value. Many such rows can act as [[permaculture-designers-manual-windbreak-species]] in the garden.

## Rampant Blackberry Control

1. Individual Plants (up to 0.1-0.25 ha): Cover clumps with strong rubber-backed carpet, tearproof fibre, or tough plastic, weighted for 7-10 days. When leaves have rotted and branches blanched, uncover and dig out roots.

2. Areas of 0.1-2.0 ha: Erect permanent electric fencing to reduce to 0.4 ha lots, release 20 pigs per lot followed by 12 goats (on rotation). In 2-4 years, pigs have eaten the roots and goats any regrowth. This is permanent if the areas are later grazed by sheep or goats, planted to thickish forest, or used for hay and regularly cut.

3. Large Areas in Steep Gullies:
   - Feed cattle in winter with untied bales of fodder thrown into blackberry gullies. They trample and destroy vines and "sow" grass. Such gullies need reafforestation later.
   - Plant ungrafted fruit tree whips (apple, fig, quince, pear, plum) inside the edge of blackberry clumps, 12 metres in from the edge. Trees grow and fruit in 4-6 years. Cattle come in to eat fallen fruit and trample out the blackberries.

## Specific Berry Varieties and Their Requirements

### Blueberry (Vaccinium spp.)
Blueberries require strongly acidic soil (pH 4.0-5.5) and are among the most demanding berries for soil conditions. They have shallow, fibrous root systems that depend on mycorrhizal associations with ericoid fungi for nutrient uptake. Highbush blueberries (V. corymbosum) prefer well-drained loam, while lowbush (V. angustifolium) tolerate poorer sandy soils. Plant in full sun with a minimum of 6 hours direct light. Mulch heavily with pine needles or acidic leaf mold to maintain low pH. Prune highbush varieties in late winter by removing the oldest canes (6+ years) at ground level, keeping 6-8 vigorous canes per plant. Birds are the primary pest; use netting or the cage culture method described above.

### Raspberry (Rubus idaeus)
Raspberries prefer slightly acidic soil (pH 5.5-6.5) with good organic matter content. Summer-bearing (floricane) varieties fruit on second-year canes; everbearing (primocane) varieties like 'Heritage' fruit on first-year canes in autumn. Trellising is essential for most varieties -- a simple two-wire T-bar trellis keeps canes upright and improves air circulation. Prune summer-bearing types after harvest by cutting fruited canes to the ground and thinning new canes to 6-8 per metre. The main insect pests are raspberry beetle and cane midge; [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]] with garlic, alliums, or tansy beneath rows helps deter them.

### Blackberry (Rubus fruticosus agg.)
Blackberries are among the most vigorous and productive berries, tolerating a wide range of soils (pH 5.5-7.0) but preferring deep, well-drained loam. Thornless cultivars like 'Navaho' and 'Triple Crown' are preferred for pick-your-own operations. They fruit on floricanes; prune after harvest by removing all canes that bore fruit. Blackberries can become invasive -- see the control section above. Train on a wire fence or trellis spaced 3m apart. Excellent companions include comfrey (dynamic accumulator), yarrow (attracts beneficial insects), and clover (nitrogen fixation). They are tolerant of partial shade, making them useful for forest garden edges.

### Strawberry (Fragaria x ananassa)
Strawberries require well-drained soil (pH 5.5-6.8) rich in organic matter. They are shallow-rooted and sensitive to waterlogging. In permaculture systems, strawberries function as excellent ground covers beneath fruit trees. The perennial matted-row system allows runners to fill in naturally, while the annual hill system removes runners for maximum fruit production. Replace plants every 3-4 years as virus load accumulates. Mulch with straw or pine needles to keep fruit clean and suppress weeds. The primary pests are slugs, tarnished plant bugs, and spider mites. Companion with borage (attracts pollinators), thyme (deters worms), and lettuce (living mulch).

### Currant and Gooseberry (Ribes spp.)
Currants and gooseberries thrive in cool climates with partial shade and slightly acidic to neutral soil (pH 5.5-7.0). Red currants (R. rubrum) produce tart berries excellent for preserves and jelly. Black currants (R. nigrum) are rich in vitamin C and grow best in deep, fertile soil. Gooseberries (R. uva-crispa) tolerate heavier soils and some drought. Prune in late winter by removing old wood and maintaining an open goblet shape. White pine blister rust can be a problem in areas near five-needle pines; check local regulations before planting. Companion plants include chives (deter aphids), nasturtiums (trap crop for aphids), and comfrey.

### Elderberry (Sambucus nigra)
Elderberry is a hardy, adaptable shrub growing in almost any soil (pH 5.5-6.5) including wet sites. It is among the most productive berries per unit area, yielding 10-15 kg per mature bush. Elderflowers are also harvested for cordials and fritters. Plant at least two varieties for cross-pollination (e.g., 'Adams' and 'York'). Prune in early spring by removing the oldest canes (3+ years). Elderberry is relatively pest-free but can suffer from elder shoot borer. Excellent in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]] designs as a nitrogen-fertilised mid-layer shrub.

## Companion Planting Guide for Berries

A well-designed berry polyculture uses companion plants to reduce pest pressure, attract pollinators, and build soil. Allium family members (garlic, chives, onions) planted between berry rows deter aphids and spider mites. Comfrey grown adjacent to berry rows mines deep minerals and provides nutrient-rich mulch when cut. Borage, yarrow, and dill attract predatory insects including hoverflies and parasitic wasps. Lupins and clover fix nitrogen between rows. Tansy repels cucumber beetles and cutworms. Marigolds (Tagetes spp.) deter root-knot nematodes when interplanted as a living mulch.

## Integrated Pest Management for Berries

- **Netting**: Use 10mm mesh bird netting over cages or individual rows during ripening
- **Hawk kites**: Deploy tethered predator kites in open field conditions during critical ripening periods
- **Biological controls**: Encourage ladybirds (aphid control), ground beetles (slug control), and lacewings (mite control)
- **Hygiene**: Remove mummified fruit and fallen berries to break pest and disease cycles
- **Beer traps**: Sink shallow dishes of beer at soil level to trap slugs (especially for strawberries)
- **Sulfur dust**: Apply to gooseberries and currants for powdery mildew control (acceptable in organic systems)
