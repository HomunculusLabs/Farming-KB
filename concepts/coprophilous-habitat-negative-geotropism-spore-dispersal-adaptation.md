# Coprophilous Habitat and Negative Geotropism as Spore Dispersal Adaptation

## Overview

Plunkett (1961), cited by Badham (1982) in his study of [[mushroom-tropisms-phototropism-geotropism|tropisms in Psilocybe cubensis]], proposed that the negative geotropic response
(upward curvature of the stipe) is particularly important for [[coprophilous-fungi|coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungi]]. The argument is that the
dung substrate is inherently unstable — it dries, crumbles, is consumed by
invertebrates, and may be disturbed by the animals that deposited it. In this
unstable environment, a horizontal pileus orientation (achieved through
negative geotropism) maximizes the probability of effective spore dispersal
before the substrate degrades beyond viability.

## The Instability of Dung Substrates

Dung as a fungal substrate has several properties that make it a challenging
and ephemeral environment for fruiting:

**Rapid desiccation.** Exposed dung pats lose moisture quickly through
evaporation. In tropical and subtropical environments where P. cubensis is
found, high temperatures accelerate this drying. A mushroom growing on a
rapidly drying substrate has a limited window for spore production and
release.

**Physical disturbance.** Dung deposits are subject to trampling by grazing
animals, consumption by dung beetles and other invertebrates, and
redistribution by rain. Any of these events can damage or destroy developing
fruiting bodies.

**Nutrient competition.** Dung is a rich substrate that supports intense
microbial competition. Bacteria, other fungi, and invertebrates all compete
for the available nutrients, potentially outcompeting the mushroom mycelium
over time.

**Vertical compression.** As dung dries and weathers, it compresses and
flattens. A mushroom that grew straight up from the substrate surface may
find itself progressively closer to the ground as the substrate beneath it
compresses, potentially bringing the pileus into contact with the ground
surface where spores cannot disperse effectively.

## Why Horizontal Pileus Orientation Matters

For a coprophilous fungus whose primary [[stinkhorn-fungi-phallales-spore-dispersal-strategy]] is to get spores
onto fresh vegetation that will be consumed by herbivores (completing the
life cycle through gut passage), the orientation of the pileus relative to
the wind is critical. A horizontal pileus with gills facing downward offers
several advantages:

**Maximum exposure to horizontal wind currents.** Wind at ground level flows
predominantly horizontally. A horizontal pileus presents the maximum gill
surface area perpendicular to these air currents, optimizing spore release
into the wind stream.

**Gravity-assisted spore release.** Basidiomycete spores are actively
discharged from gills through a [[bloomfield-buller-drop-surface-tension-spore-catapult-basidiospore-discharge]] [[bloomfield-buller-drops-and-surface-tension-spore-catapult-mechanism]]
(Buller's drop), which launches spores horizontally from the gill surface.
A horizontal pileus ensures that this horizontal discharge is directed into
the air space between the gills and the ground, rather than being directed
downward into the substrate surface or sideways into the stipe.

**Even spore distribution.** A horizontal pileus distributes spores in a
roughly circular pattern around the mushroom, increasing the probability
that some spores will land on nearby vegetation at various heights and
positions. A tilted or vertical pileus would create an asymmetric dispersal
pattern with some directions receiving many spores and others receiving few.

**Clearance from substrate.** The negative geotropic response elevates the
pileus above the substrate surface. As the dung compresses, the stipe
continues to grow upward (negative geotropism), maintaining the pileus at a
height where spores can be caught by air currents rather than settling back
onto the substrate.

## Plunkett's Original Proposal

Plunkett (1961) studied tropisms in Polyporus brumalis and observed that the
change in tropic responses during stipe development — from initial
orthotropism (upward growth) to later diagravitropism (horizontal
orientation) — correlated with the onset of spore production. He proposed
that this developmental shift in tropism was an adaptation for spore
dispersal, particularly important for species growing on unstable substrates
like dung or decaying wood.

Badham adopted this interpretation for P. cubensis, noting that the
[[mushroom-geotropism|negative geotropic response]] in his wind tunnel
experiments occurred at Stage 2 (pileus nearly spherical), which
corresponded to the onset of spore production. The coordination of geotropic
sensitivity with sporulation supports Plunkett's hypothesis that the two
processes are functionally linked through an adaptation for spore dispersal.

## The Gill Hormone Connection

The link between spore production and [[light-role-spore-formation-geotropic-sensitivity-psilocybe-cubensis]] is reinforced by
the observation that sporeless mutants of P. cubensis did not exhibit
negative geotropism. This strongly suggests that a substance produced in or
by the developing gills — the "mushroom hormone" proposed by Gruen (1963)
and Hagimoto (1963) — mediates the geotropic response. In this model, the
hormone is produced only when the gills are actively forming spores, and its
presence switches the stipe's tropic response from anemotropic (wind-seeking)
to negatively geotropic (upward-curving).

For coprophilous species, this hormonal mechanism provides an elegant
solution to the timing problem: the mushroom grows toward the wind during
early development (positioning itself optimally) and then switches to upward
growth precisely when spore production begins (orienting the cap for
horizontal dispersal). The switch is triggered automatically by the onset of
sporulation, ensuring that the timing is always correct regardless of
environmental conditions.

## Broader Applicability

While the coprophilous adaptation hypothesis is most directly applicable to
dung-inhabiting species, similar arguments apply to other mushrooms growing
on unstable or ephemeral substrates:

**Wood-inhabiting species.** Fungi fruiting on fallen logs or branches face
similar constraints — the substrate may be consumed by other organisms,
desiccate, or physically break apart. Negative geotropism ensures that the
pileus achieves horizontal orientation before the substrate degrades.

**Leaf litter species.** Mushrooms growing in leaf litter are subject to
compression as the litter layer compacts. Upward growth maintains pileus
clearance above the increasingly compact substrate.

**Grassland species.** P. cubensis and similar grassland species grow in
environments with strong horizontal wind currents. The combination of
anemotropism (to reach the wind) and negative geotropism (to orient the cap
horizontally) is particularly effective in these open habitats.

The universality of [[negative-geotropism-in-mushroom-fruiting]] mushroom fruiting across diverse
ecological niches suggests that the coprophilous adaptation described by
Plunkett represents a special case of a more general principle: the
developmental timing of the geotropic switch ensures that the pileus reaches
its final, spore-release-optimized orientation at the precise moment when
spore production begins.
