## Overview

Fungi play critical roles in nitrogen (N) cycling in terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems. They mediate nitrogen transformations including mineralization, immobilization, nitrification-linked processes, and nitrogen transport through mycorrhizal networks. In forest ecosystems, nitrogen cycling by saprotrophic fungi is a major pathway for making N available to plants (Dighton and Boddy, 1989).

## Nitrogen Mineralization

Saprotrophic fungi decompose organic matter containing nitrogen (proteins, chitin, nucleic acids) and release ammonium (NH4+) through the action of extracellular proteases, chitinases, and nucleases. This mineralization converts organic N into plant-available inorganic forms. The balance between mineralization (N release) and immobilization (N incorporation into fungal biomass) depends on the C:N ratio of the substrate — high C:N substrates (e.g., wood) favor immobilization, while low C:N substrates (e.g., leaf litter with C:N < 20) favor net mineralization.

## Nitrogen Immobilization

Fungi efficiently immobilize nitrogen into their biomass when decomposing nitrogen-poor substrates. Fungal biomass has a relatively low C:N ratio (approximately 10-15:1), meaning fungi require nitrogen even when decomposing carbon-rich materials like wood. This immobilization can temporarily reduce plant-available N in soil.

## Ectomycorrhizal Nitrogen Dynamics

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi are particularly important in nitrogen-limited boreal and temperate forests:
- ECM fungi access organic nitrogen sources unavailable to plant roots alone
- Some ECM species produce proteases and peptidases to decompose soil organic matter proteins
- Nitrogen is transported from soil to host plant through the mycorrhizal mycelium
- ECM fungi can suppress nitrification, retaining N in the ammonium form preferred by many forest trees

## Nitrogen and Ligninolytic Enzymes

Nitrogen limitation is a key trigger for ligninolytic enzyme production in white-rot fungi. Under nitrogen-limited conditions, Phanerochaete chrysosporium and related species produce lignin peroxidase (LiP) and manganese peroxidase (MnP), switching from primary to secondary metabolism. This has implications for [[mycoremediation-enzymology]] and pollutant degradation efficiency.

## Denitrification and Nitrous Oxide

Certain fungi can denitrify, reducing nitrate to nitrous oxide (N2O) and sometimes dinitrogen (N2). Fungal denitrification is an emerging area of research with implications for greenhouse gas emissions and nitrogen loss from ecosystems.
