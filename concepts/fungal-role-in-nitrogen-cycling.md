# Fungal Role in Nitrogen Cycling

## Overview

Fungi play critical roles in nitrogen (N) cycling in terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems.

They mediate nitrogen transformations including mineralization, immobilization, nitrification-linked processes, and nitrogen transport through mycorrhizal networks.

In forest ecosystems, nitrogen cycling by saprotrophic fungi is a major pathway for making N available to plants (Dighton and Boddy, 1989).

## Nitrogen Mineralization

Saprotrophic fungi decompose organic matter containing nitrogen (proteins, chitin, nucleic acids) and release ammonium (NH4+) through the action of extracellular proteases, chitinases, and nucleases.

This mineralization converts organic N into plant-available inorganic forms.

The balance between mineralization (N release) and immobilization (N incorporation into fungal biomass) depends on the C:N ratio of the substrate — high C:N substrates (e.g., wood) favor immobilization, while low C:N substrates (e.g., leaf litter with C:N < 20) favor net mineralization.

### The C:N Ratio and Nitrogen Dynamics

The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of organic matter is the primary determinant of whether decomposition results in net nitrogen mineralization or net immobilization.

Fungal biomass has a relatively consistent C:N ratio of approximately 10-15:1.

When fungi decompose substrate with a C:N ratio below this threshold (such as legume residues with C:N of 15-25:1), they release excess nitrogen as ammonium — this is net mineralization.

When decomposing substrate with a higher C:N ratio (such as wood chips with C:N of 400-1000:1), fungi must scavenge nitrogen from the surrounding soil to meet their metabolic needs — this is net immobilization, which can temporarily reduce plant-available N.

This dynamic has direct implications for [[chop-and-drop-mulching]] practices: mulching with high-C:N materials like wood chips can create temporary nitrogen deficiency for adjacent plants unless compensated by nitrogen-fixing species.

### Enzymatic Mineralization

Fungi produce a suite of extracellular enzymes for nitrogen mineralization.

Proteases (including serine proteases, aspartic proteases, and metalloproteases) hydrolyze proteins into amino acids and peptides.

Aminopeptidases further break peptides into individual amino acids. Deaminases remove amino groups from amino acids, releasing ammonium.

Chitinases, produced by many soil fungi, decompose chitin (a polymer of N-acetylglucosamine found in fungal cell walls and insect exoskeletons), releasing both carbon and nitrogen.

Nucleases decompose nucleic acids into nucleotides and eventually ammonium. The efficiency of this enzymatic machinery gives fungi a competitive advantage over bacteria in decomposing complex organic nitrogen compounds.

## Nitrogen Immobilization

Fungi efficiently immobilize nitrogen into their biomass when decomposing nitrogen-poor substrates.

Fungal biomass has a relatively low C:N ratio (approximately 10-15:1), meaning fungi require nitrogen even when decomposing carbon-rich materials like wood.

This immobilization can temporarily reduce plant-available N in soil.

### Translocation and Recycling

Fungi have a unique ability to translocate nitrogen through their mycelial networks.

When a fungus colonizes a nitrogen-rich patch (such as a dead insect or an area of fresh leaf litter), it can absorb nitrogen and transport it through hyphal cytoplasmic streaming to nitrogen-poor areas of the mycelium.

This internal recycling means that fungi can maintain growth even in nitrogen-limited environments by efficiently redistributing nitrogen within their network.

Saprotrophic cord-forming fungi (such as Marasmius androsaceus in forest litter) create persistent mycelial cords that function as nitrogen transport highways, connecting spatially separated resource patches across the forest floor.

## Ectomycorrhizal Nitrogen Dynamics

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi are particularly important in nitrogen-limited boreal and temperate forests:

- ECM fungi access organic nitrogen sources unavailable to plant roots alone
- Some ECM species produce proteases and peptidases to decompose soil organic matter proteins
- Nitrogen is transported from soil to host plant through the mycorrhizal mycelium
- ECM fungi can suppress nitrification, retaining N in the ammonium form preferred by many forest trees

### Access to Organic Nitrogen

One of the most significant contributions of ECM fungi to nitrogen cycling is their ability to access organic nitrogen forms that are unavailable to unaided plant roots.

Many forest soils contain large pools of nitrogen locked in protein-tannin complexes and other recalcitrant organic forms. ECM fungi produce extracellular proteases and peptidases that depolymerize these molecules, releasing amino acids the fungus absorbs and transports to the host plant.

This capability is particularly important in boreal forests, where cold temperatures slow microbial decomposition and organic nitrogen accumulation in soil is greatest.

### The Nitrogen Mining Hypothesis

ECM fungi have been described as "nitrogen miners" — organisms that actively seek out and decompose organic matter to extract nitrogen, much as a miner extracts ore from rock.

In exchange, the host plant supplies the fungus with carbohydrates from photosynthesis.

This mutualistic exchange creates a powerful positive feedback: the plant invests carbon in fungal growth, the fungus expands its network and mines more nitrogen, the additional nitrogen supports more photosynthesis, and the increased carbon supply supports further fungal growth.

This feedback loop is a key driver of carbon and nitrogen cycling in forest ecosystems.

## Nitrogen and Ligninolytic Enzymes

Nitrogen limitation is a key trigger for ligninolytic enzyme production in white-rot fungi.

Under nitrogen-limited conditions, Phanerochaete chrysosporium and related species produce lignin peroxidase (LiP) and manganese peroxidase (MnP), switching from primary to secondary metabolism.

This has implications for [[mycoremediation-enzymology]] and pollutant degradation efficiency.

### The Secondary Metabolism Switch

The relationship between nitrogen availability and lignin degradation is a critical regulatory mechanism. When nitrogen is abundant, white-rot fungi prioritize growth. Under nitrogen limitation, growth slows and the fungus redirects resources toward secondary metabolism, including lignin-degrading peroxidases.

## Denitrification and Nitrous Oxide

Certain fungi can denitrify, reducing nitrate to nitrous oxide (N2O) and sometimes dinitrogen (N2). Fungal denitrification is an emerging area of research with implications for greenhouse gas emissions and nitrogen loss from ecosystems.

### Fungal vs. Bacterial Denitrification

Fungal denitrification differs from bacterial denitrification in several important ways.

Fungi typically lack the complete denitrification pathway — they reduce nitrate to nitrite and then to nitrous oxide, but generally cannot complete the reduction to dinitrogen (N2).

This means that fungal denitrification is a net source of N2O (a potent greenhouse gas with 298 times the warming potential of CO2 over 100 years), whereas complete bacterial denitrification converts nitrate to harmless N2 gas.

Fungal denitrification also occurs under aerobic conditions (unlike bacterial denitrification, which requires anaerobic conditions), which means it can proceed in well-aerated soils where bacterial denitrification is suppressed.

Fungi such as Fusarium oxysporum and various Ascomycota and Basidiomycota species have confirmed denitrifying capacity, contributing an estimated 10-30% of total soil N2O emissions in some agricultural soils.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi and Nitrogen

While ECM fungi dominate nitrogen cycling in boreal and temperate forests, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) play a comparable role in grasslands, tropical forests, and agricultural systems.

AMF enhance plant nitrogen uptake primarily by increasing the effective soil volume explored — the hyphal network extends far beyond the root depletion zone, accessing nitrate and ammonium that would otherwise be unavailable.

AMF also influence nitrogen cycling through their interactions with the soil microbial community, stimulating nitrogen-fixing bacteria and influencing nitrogen mineralization rates.

See [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for details on both AMF and ECM nitrogen relationships.

## Nitrogen in the Fungal Food Web

Fungi occupy a central position in the soil nitrogen food web. Bacterial-feeding nematodes and microarthropods consume fungi, releasing nitrogen immobilized in fungal biomass back into the soil as ammonium — a process known as the microbial loop.

The fungal-based food web channel is generally considered more efficient at conserving nitrogen than the bacterial channel, because fungal biomass turns over more slowly and supports higher trophic levels before nitrogen is mineralized.
