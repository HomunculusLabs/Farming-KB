# Seed Saving

Seed saving is the practice of harvesting, drying, and storing seeds from open-pollinated varieties for replanting. It preserves genetic diversity, adapts crops to local conditions over successive seasons, reduces input costs, and builds [[regenerative-agriculture]] resilience by reducing dependence on commercial seed suppliers.

## Open-Pollinated vs Hybrid

- **Open-pollinated (OP):** Stable genetics bred true-to-type from seed. Required for seed saving. Includes heirlooms and landraces.
- **Hybrid (F1):** Cross of two inbred lines. First generation performs well but subsequent generations segregate unpredictably. Do not save seeds from hybrids unless breeding intentionally.
- **Check seed packets or catalogs** for "OP," "heirloom," or "open-pollinated" designation.

## Isolation Distances by Crop Family

Prevent cross-pollination by maintaining minimum isolation distances between varieties of the same species:

| Crop Family | Examples | Isolation Distance | Pollination |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brassicaceae | Broccoli, kale, cabbage, radish, turnip | 800 ft - 1/4 mile | Insect |
| Cucurbitaceae | Squash, cucumber, melon | 1/2 mile (same species) | Insect |
| Solanaceae | Tomato, pepper, eggplant | 10-50 ft (peppers), 25-100 ft (tomato) | Self/insect |
| Alliaceae | Onions, leeks, garlic | 100 ft - 1/4 mile | Insect |
| Fabaceae (Legumes) | Beans, peas | 10-50 ft | Self |
| Asteraceae | Lettuce | 20-50 ft | Self/insect |

Solanaceae and legumes are largely self-pollinating and need minimal isolation. Cucurbits and brassicas are outcrossing and require the most separation — use caging with introduced pollinators or staggered flowering if space is limited.

## Seed Processing

### Dry Seed Processing
For plants with seeds in dry pods or husks (beans, peas, lettuce, brassicas, grains, onions):
1. Allow seed heads/pods to dry fully on the plant or harvest and dry undercover.
2. Thresh (crush pods, flail, or walk on seed heads) to release seeds.
3. Winnow — pour seed between containers in front of a fan to separate chaff.
4. Screen through mesh to remove debris.
5. Dry further to <10% moisture before storage.

### Wet Seed Processing
For plants with seeds embedded in fleshy fruit (tomatoes, cucumbers, squash, peppers, melons):
1. Cut fruit open and scoop seeds into a container.
2. Add water and let ferment (tomatoes: 2-4 days; others: 1-2 days). Fermentation removes the gel coating that inhibits germination and reduces seed-borne diseases.
3. Viable seeds sink; pour off floating debris and mold.
4. Rinse seeds thoroughly in a fine-mesh strainer.
5. Spread on a non-stick surface (glass, ceramic, screen) and dry 5-14 days at room temperature out of direct sun.

## Seed Viability Testing

Perform a simple germination test before planting saved seed:
1. Moisten a paper towel (not dripping wet).
2. Place 10 seeds evenly spaced, fold towel, place in a labeled plastic bag.
3. Keep at 70-75°F; check daily for 7-14 days.
4. Count sprouted seeds. 7/10 = 70% germination — adequate for most crops.
5. If germination is low, sow more thickly or discard and use fresher seed.

## Optimal Storage Conditions

Seeds store longest when cool, dry, and dark:
- **Temperature:** Below 50°F; refrigerator (35-40°F) or freezer (0°F) for long-term.
- **Moisture:** Below 8-10% relative humidity. Use silica gel desiccant packets in sealed containers.
- **Light:** Store in opaque containers — glass jars with tight lids, mylar bags, or paper envelopes inside a sealed container.
- **Container options:** Mason jars with silica gel, vacuum-sealed mylar bags, coin envelopes in airtight tins.
- **Freezer storage:** Seeds must be thoroughly dry first. Some crops (lettuce, some peppers) are sensitive to freezing — test a small batch.
- **Viability by crop:** Onion (1-2 yr), pepper (2-3 yr), tomato (4-6 yr), bean (3-5 yr), squash (4-6 yr).

## Seed Saving by Crop Difficulty

### Easy (self-pollinating, minimal isolation)
- **Beans and peas:** Let pods dry on plant. Shell and store.
- **Lettuce:** Allow to bolt, harvest seed heads, dry and thresh.
- **Tomato:** Wet process with fermentation. Easy and reliable.
- **Peppers:** Cut open ripe fruit, scrape seeds, dry on paper. Isolate sweet from hot to avoid cross-pollination (capsaicin transfer).

### Moderate (require some isolation or hand-pollination)
- **Squash and cucumber:** Wet process. Isolate same-species varieties by 1/2 mile, or hand-pollinate and tape flowers shut. Squash crosses within species (C. pepo with pepo, C. maxima with maxima) but not between species.
- **Melon:** Similar to cucumber; wet process.

### Hard (biennial, outcrossing, require large populations)
- **Brassicas:** Biennial for most (cabbage, kale, broccoli) — must overwinter. Heavy outcrossers needing 1/4 mile isolation. Keep 20-80 plants minimum for genetic diversity.
- **Carrots and beets:** Biennials. Overwinter in ground or store roots and replant. Carrots cross with Queen Anne's Lace — serious isolation needed.
- **Corn:** Wind-pollinated, heavy outcrosser. Isolate 1/4+ mile or use staggered planting (10-14 day intervals). Minimum 200 plants for genetic health.

## Population Size for Genetic Diversity

Saving from too few plants narrows the gene pool and leads to inbreeding depression over generations:
- **Self-pollinators:** Minimum 10-20 plants for home scale.
- **Outcrossers (insect-pollinated):** 20-80 plants depending on crop.
- **Corn:** 200+ plants minimum.
- **Biennials:** At least 20-40 roots for replanting.

## Record Keeping

Track essential information each season:
- Variety name, source, year saved
- Number of plants harvested from (population size)
- Isolation method used (distance, caging, bagging)
- Germination test results
- Any observed off-types or crosses
- Storage conditions and container type

Simple spreadsheet or index card system. Seed Savers Exchange provides free record-keeping templates.
