## The Wild Fungi Problem

A fundamental legal challenge has been how to control mushrooms that
contain internationally scheduled substances yet grow wild in nature.
Countries have struggled with the question of whether to criminalise
people who may own land where mushrooms grow naturally. Several creative
legal solutions have been attempted:

- **"Treated or prepared" standard**: Ireland and the UK initially
  controlled mushrooms only if they had been "treated or prepared,"
  indicating intent of use. The UK later argued that even packaging
  constituted preparation, but ultimately changed the law to eliminate
  this distinction entirely.
- **"Dried or processed" standard**: The Dutch Supreme Court ruled that
  the Opium Act applied to mushrooms that had been dried, mashed, or
  processed into food, but not to fresh mushrooms. This created a
  loophole exploited by smartshops selling fresh product.
- **"Organism" redefinition**: Germany changed its legal definition from
  "plant" to "organism" in March 2005 to clarify that mushrooms, as
  fungi rather than plants, were included under controlled substance
  legislation.
- **Catch-all plant prohibition**: Italy, Greece, Cyprus, and Lithuania
  used laws prohibiting cultivation of plants from which narcotic
  substances can be extracted, though the classification of mushrooms as
  non-plants created potential loopholes.
