# Hallucinogenic Mushroom Legal Status in Europe

The legal status of hallucinogenic mushrooms in the European Union has
been characterised by complexity, inconsistency, and rapid change. While
the active compounds psilocybin and psilocin have been internationally
controlled since 1971, the mushrooms that contain them have occupied a
legal grey area in many countries, leading to a patchwork of national
approaches that evolved significantly between 2001 and 2006.

## International Control

Psilocin and psilocybin are controlled under the 1971 UN Convention on
Psychotropic Substances, listed in Schedule I under the names "psilocybine"
and "psilocine" (or "psilotsin") respectively. All EU Member States
control these substances accordingly. However, international control of
the chemical compounds did not automatically translate into control of
the biological organisms that produce them, creating a persistent legal
ambiguity.

## The Wild Fungi Problem

A fundamental legal challenge has been how to control mushrooms that
contain internationally scheduled substances yet grow wild in nature.
Countries have struggled with the question of whether to criminalise
people who may own land where mushrooms grow naturally. Several creative
legal solutions have been attempted:

- **"Treated or prepared" standard**: Ireland and the UK initially
  controlled mushrooms only if they had been "treated or prepared,"
  indicating intent of use. The UK later argued that even packaging
  constituted preparation, but ultimately changed the law to eliminate
  this distinction entirely.
- **"Dried or processed" standard**: The Dutch Supreme Court ruled that
  the Opium Act applied to mushrooms that had been dried, mashed, or
  processed into food, but not to fresh mushrooms. This created a
  loophole exploited by smartshops selling fresh product.
- **"Organism" redefinition**: Germany changed its legal definition from
  "plant" to "organism" in March 2005 to clarify that mushrooms, as
  fungi rather than plants, were included under controlled substance
  legislation.
- **Catch-all plant prohibition**: Italy, Greece, Cyprus, and Lithuania
  used laws prohibiting cultivation of plants from which narcotic
  substances can be extracted, though the classification of mushrooms as
  non-plants created potential loopholes.

## Timeline of Legislative Changes (2001 to 2006)

Six EU Member States tightened their legislation on hallucinogenic
mushrooms between 2001 and 2006:

### Denmark (July 2001)
Added mushrooms or spores of Psilocybe semilanceata, Psilocybe cubensis,
or other fungi containing psilocin or psilocybin (including grown, dried,
or processed forms) as a controlled substance.

### Netherlands (November 2002)
The Dutch Supreme Court clarified that the Opium Act applies to
hallucinogenic mushrooms containing prohibited substances that have been
dried, mashed, or processed into food, but not to fresh mushrooms. This
allowed the continued sale of fresh mushrooms through smartshops for
several more years.

### Germany (March 2005)
Changed the definition of controlled substance from "plant" to
"organism" to clarify that mushrooms are included under drug control
legislation, closing a loophole created by the botanical classification
of fungi.

### Estonia (May 2005)
Clarified that cultivation of hallucinogenic mushrooms is a criminal
offence.

### United Kingdom (July 2005)
Added "any fungus of any kind which contains psilocin or an ester of
psilocin" as a Class A controlled drug, removing the previous limitation
that applied only to mushrooms "if treated or prepared." The change
immediately affected availability; the Mixmag 2005 survey found 67% of
respondents reporting reduced access.

### Ireland (February 2006)
Criminalised possession or sale of "fungus of any kind or description
which contains psilocin or an ester of psilocin," with an exemption for
mushrooms growing uncultivated. The change was initiated following the
increasingly common sale of magic mushrooms and a death linked to their
consumption.

## Variation in Legal Definitions

Legal controls do not apply uniformly to the same mushrooms across
countries. Denmark controls named species (Psilocybe semilanceata,
Psilocybe cubensis, and other fungi containing psilocin or psilocybin),
while Italy lists entire genera (Stropharia, Conocybe, and Psilocybe).
The UK adopted a chemical definition (any fungus containing psilocin or
an ester of psilocin). This inconsistency creates challenges for
cross-border enforcement and legal clarity.

## Enforcement and Seizures

Police or customs seizures of hallucinogenic mushrooms were reported in
at least 12 European countries in 2004, including Czech Republic, Estonia,
Germany, Greece, Hungary, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland,
Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia, and Sweden. The largest single seizure
was 11.5 kg reported in Poland. Estonia confiscated 14 mail deliveries
of mushrooms, spores, or mycelium.

Drug law offence data remain sparse. The Czech Republic reported 9
offences related to hallucinogenic mushrooms in 2003 and 2004. Greece
reported 37 and 20 offences in 2003 and 2004 respectively. Following the
UK reclassification, seizures by law enforcement increased, including a
£6,000 seizure in Glasgow in 2005.

## The Legal Alternative Market

Prohibitions on psilocybin-containing mushrooms have prompted retailers
to seek legal alternatives. Following UK restrictions, online headshops
began marketing [[amanita-pantherina|Amanita muscaria]]
(panther cap) as substitutes. Purple Ohms capsules containing Hawaiian
Baby Woodrose with LSA have also been promoted as legal alternatives.
This substitution effect raises new concerns, as the active compounds in
Amanita species (muscimol, ibotenic acid) carry substantial toxicity
risks, and some closely related Amanita species are lethal.
