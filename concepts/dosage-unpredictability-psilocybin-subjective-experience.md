## Dosage Unpredictability and Subjective Experience Variation

## Overview

Bigwood and Beug (1982) concluded their study with a significant observation about the practical
implications of alkaloid variability for [[psilocin]]
levels means that users have no reliable way to predict the dose they are ingesting based on dry
weight alone. This dosage unpredictability contributes to subjective experience variation beyond
what can be attributed to [[lsd-set-and-setting-framework]] but will also stem in very real measure from large
dosage differences."

This conclusion is grounded in hard analytical data rather than anecdotal observation. The
implication is that two individuals taking the same nominal weight of dried mushrooms in similar
settings with similar mindsets could have fundamentally different experiences simply because their
mushrooms came from different sources or flushes. The psychedelic literature has historically
attributed such differences primarily to psychological and contextual factors, but Bigwood and Beug
demonstrated that pharmacological dose variation is an equally important and often underappreciated
contributor.

## Street Sample Variability in Detail

The five street samples analyzed by Bigwood and Beug showed particularly dramatic inconsistency:

| Sample | Psilocybin (mg/g) | Psilocin (mg/g) | Relative Potency |
|--------|-------------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 1      | 5.6               | 0               | High            |
| 2      | 6.2               | 0               | Highest         |
| 3      | 0.7               | 0.3             | Very low        |
| 4      | 0.7               | 0.3             | Very low        |
| 5      | 1.3               | 0.3             | Low             |

Samples 3 and 4 contained less than one-eighth the psilocybin of samples 1 and 2. All samples were
reportedly cultivated on rye-grain substrate, so the variability was not attributable to different
growing media but likely reflected differences in strain genetics, flush number, drying methodology,
storage duration, and possible misidentification.

Notably, the street samples showed consistently low psilocin levels (0 to 0.3 mg/g), suggesting they
were primarily from early flushes where psilocin has not yet accumulated. This is consistent with
the controlled study finding that psilocin is typically absent or minimal in the first flush. The
consistently low psilocin across all five street samples may indicate that commercial cultivators
typically harvest from early flushes when yields are highest, before psilocin has had time to
accumulate.
