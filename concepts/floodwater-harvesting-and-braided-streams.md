## Floodwater Harvesting and Braided Streams

## Overview

Floodwater harvesting is the deliberate capture, diversion, and infiltration of flood flows for agricultural and ecological benefit. In arid and semi-arid regions, flood events — though infrequent — deliver enormous volumes of water that can be managed to support crops, trees, and permanent water systems. Understanding the dynamics of floodwater flow, particularly in [[wadi]] dams** — effective for local food forests where basic machinery is available.
- **Levelled gardens** — flat areas designed to hold and infiltrate water.
- **Soakage pits** — excavated pits filled with water to promote deep infiltration.
- **[[vetiver-grass]] lines** on contour — greatly assist water infiltration and slope stability in tropical and subtropical areas.
- **Tussock grasses and mosses** — perform the same stabilisation function in cold deserts for unstable rock slopes and soil flows.

## Braided Stream Systems

### Understanding Braiding

A close study of braiding patterns in streams reveals a critical insight: there is an **inverse relationship between the land areas wetted or saturated and the channel width**. Small multiple streams produce much larger wetted areas than simple main channels. This observation has profound implications for water harvesting design.

### The Diamond Braid Pattern

The fundamental unit of braiding is initially a diamond (rough diamond shape) with plunge pools at each corner, caused by the confluence of side streams. This pattern occurs in shallow water flow over extensive deep sand sheets — braiding does not occur underwater.

In low flow conditions, only the channel flow persists and sheet flow across the interfluve is absent. The exact mechanism that initiates braiding is not fully understood, although similar flow patterns arise from scattered pebbles or shells in sand.

### Designing for Enhanced Braiding

An untried but promising strategy involves building concreted, deeply bedded deflections in main channels during dry periods, set out to split flood flow as often as there is space to create small channels. On most arid flatland flood plains, there is ample space for this approach.

The aim is to **widen and slow the floodwaters**, wetting as much alluvium as possible. Since floods more frequently arise from headwater runoff rather than local rain, spreading and harvesting such water before it is lost to saltpans in the desert is highly beneficial.

### Notched Weirs for Braiding Initiation

A level, notched wall erected across the braided area can spread floodwaters more fully across plains and initiate a finer mesh of the braiding pattern. The notches function as imitations of natural plunge pools, creating the hydraulic conditions that lead to braiding. This technique could be modelled in experimental sand channel flows before full-scale implementation.

## Gridiron Bank Systems

Where flooded land is relatively flat, a **"gridiron" of low banks** keeps floodwater and silt settled and absorbed:

- **Bank height**: only 0.5-1 m.
- **Bank stabilisation**: best achieved by stone on the upstream side, tussock grasses, tough low shrubs, live-set sticks and brush, or heavy rock walls from the main river bed.
- **Nutrient cycling**: such systems maintain high levels of major plant nutrients and were the basis of rich Nilotic agriculture.

## Scour Hole Lagoons

Within 10-50 km of ranges, permanent desert lagoons may occur. These form through a natural process:

1. A strong natural ridge (often unnoticed) approaches the river from one or both banks.
2. This ridge constricts flood flow, forcing increased velocity and powerful hydraulic digging action.
3. A deep scour hole is excavated, forming a permanent lagoon.

### Constructing Scour Holes

It is simple to replace or construct such ridges using a combination of rock-cement walls and a bulldozed bank, stronger and deeper near the river bed itself. The ponds remain full in drought because deep sands of the river contain millions of litres of slow seepage.

### Important Design Consideration

Open water systems should not be created too frequently unless **silt dams of up to 9-10 times the capacity** of each pool have been constructed, as open water will evaporate over time. The silt dam serves as a recharge reservoir that keeps the pool filled through subsurface flow.

## Scoop Fences for Mulch Capture

Along broad braided rivers, high-water marks leave lines of organic mulch — a mixture of seed, manure, twigs, molluscs, and debris that is excellent garden material. This material can be trapped using a sequence of **stout scoop fences** on the flood plain for use in gardens and tree planting.

## Crop Establishment in Flood Systems

When rivers flood, fields of millet, sorghum, and legumes can be broadcast sown into the wet silt and later harvested as stored crop. Further from the main stream, broader deep dykes hold waters in narrower and deeper channels for tree crops, and the trees should overshade the deep absorption swales so developed.

## Key Design Principles

1. **Spread, don't concentrate** — the goal is to widen and slow floodwaters to maximise wetted area.
2. **Use natural patterns** — braiding, scour holes, and flood-deposited mulch are natural processes to emulate and enhance.
3. **Protect from burning** — fire destroys the natural infiltration structures created by tussock grasses.
4. **Sequence water flow** — use fences, banks, and weirs to sort and direct water and its sediment load to where it is most useful.
5. **Plan for silt** — silt deposition is both inevitable and beneficial when properly managed as a soil-building resource.
