# [[fungal-heavy-metal-anion-biosorption|Fungal]] Mercury Transformation and Heavy Metal Accumulation

Fungi play a central role in the transformation, accumulation, and environmental
cycling of heavy metals. Their ability to both concentrate and chemically modify
pollutant metals makes them critical mediators of metal toxicity in terrestrial
ecosystems, with significant implications for food chain transfer and
bioremediation.

The dual capacity of fungi to accumulate metals into biomass while simultaneously
altering their chemical speciation positions them at the intersection of metal
immobilization and mobilization pathways in soil. This duality has important
consequences for ecosystem health and contaminant dynamics.

## Mercury Methylation in Fungi

The methylation of mercury by fungi represents one of the most ecologically
significant metal transformations in fungal physiology. Methyl mercury (MeHg)
bioaccumulation factors in fungal fruit bodies range from 3 to 199 relative to
soil humus concentrations, whereas total mercury accumulation factors typically
remain below 1, demonstrating that methylation substantially increases the
concentration factor within fungal tissue.

Species-level data from Fischer et al. (1995) illustrate this pattern: *Xercomus
badius* shows MeHg bioaccumulation factors of 9.6 to 12.3, *[[leccinum-scabrum]]*
10.0, and *Amanita muscaria* 3.0 to 10.4. The most extreme case is *Coprinus
comatus*, with a factor of 198.5 — methyl mercury concentrated nearly 200-fold
from soil into the fruit body. By contrast, total mercury concentration factors
rarely exceed 1.7.

Before the work of Byrne et al. (1997), mercury methylation was commonly
regarded as an exclusively anaerobic, bacterially controlled process. The
demonstration that fungi also perform this transformation revealed an additional
pathway for mercury movement and toxicity in the environment. Changes in the
chemical state of heavy metals can significantly alter their toxicity to other
organisms, and fungal methylation could have important implications for the
movement of arsenic and mercury in the environment and their transfer within food
chains.

## Biosorption by Dead Fungal Biomass

Dead fungal mycelium exhibits remarkable metal-binding capacity through ion
exchange between metal ions and reactive groups on the cell wall. Tobin et al.
(1984) demonstrated that dried mycelium of *[[rhizopus-arrhizus]]* efficiently
adsorbs a wide range of metal ions, with the notable exception of alkali metals,
making it potentially useful for industrial metal retrieval from effluent
solutions.

Metal uptake capacities (mM per gram dry weight) for *R. arrhizus*:

| Metal Ion | Uptake (mM g⁻¹) |
|-----------|-----------------|
| UO₂²⁺    | 820             |
| Pb²⁺     | 500             |
| Ag⁺      | 500             |
| Ba²⁺     | 410             |
| Cr³⁺     | 590             |
| La³⁺     | 350             |
| Zn²⁺     | 300             |
| Hg²⁺     | 290             |
| Cd²⁺     | 270             |
| Cu²⁺     | 250             |
| Mn²⁺     | 220             |

Alkali metals (Na⁺, K⁺, Rb⁺, Cs⁺) show zero uptake, demonstrating that the
biosorption mechanism involves specific binding sites rather than passive
absorption.

The high affinity for uranium (UO₂²⁺ at 820 mM g⁻¹) is particularly notable
and suggests potential applications in nuclear waste management.

## Metal Uptake in Living Fungal Tissue

In living fungal tissue, metal ion accumulation results from both metabolic
(energy-dependent) uptake and passive cation exchange on cell wall binding sites.
These two mechanisms operate simultaneously but differ in their selectivity,
capacity, and reversibility.

McKnight et al. (1990) demonstrated that magnesium concentration was positively
related to cation-exchange capacity in the stipe tissue of 18 basidiomycete
species, linking structural properties of the fungal cell wall to metal
accumulation capacity.

Research on zinc uptake in *[[penicillium-notatum]]* by Starling and Ross (1991)
revealed complex metal interaction dynamics. Zinc is an essential element for
this fungus, but its uptake kinetics differ at low and high solution
concentrations, suggesting multiple transport systems operating at different
concentration ranges.

Importantly, zinc uptake is competitively inhibited by cadmium, indicating that
Zn and Cd share transport pathways across the plasma membrane. Copper acts as a
noncompetitive inhibitor of zinc uptake, suggesting interference at a different
physiological level — possibly through disruption of metabolic processes or
enzyme systems rather than direct competition for transporters.

These differential interaction modes highlight the complexity of multi-metal
environments. In natural soils where multiple heavy metals co-occur, the net
effect on fungal metal accumulation depends on the specific combination of metals
present, their relative concentrations, and the specific transport and
detoxification mechanisms of the fungal species involved.

## Fungal Bioremediation Potential

The white-rot fungus *[[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]]* has demonstrated considerable
bioremediation potential in field trials. This wood-decaying basidiomycete can
degrade toxic organic compounds by converting chlorine bound in organic form to
harmless inorganic forms and degrading [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]] completely to CO₂
and water (Coghlan, 1994). Its lignin-degrading enzyme system has broad substrate
specificity and can attack a wide range of recalcitrant organic pollutants.

Combined with the metal accumulation capabilities of fungi, these enzymatic
competencies suggest that fungi could be deployed to detoxify contaminated land
simultaneously from both organic and inorganic pollutants.

## Ectomycorrhizal Tolerance to Heavy Metals

Ectomycorrhizal fungal species demonstrate greater tolerance to heavy metal
pollution than saprotrophic fungi. In a pollution transect survey in Sweden by
Rühling and Söderström (1990), where dominant soil contaminants were As, Cu,
Cd, Pb, and Zn, both abundance and diversity of fungi decreased with increasing
pollutant loading, but ectomycorrhizal species proved significantly more tolerant. Rühling et al. (1984) identified
*[[laccaria-laccata]]* as the most heavy-metal-tolerant fungus among those surveyed.

While the total number of microfungi isolated from soil did not decrease with
increasing metal loading, common genera such as *Penicillium* and *Oidiodendron*
declined in frequency. Some species, including *Paecilomyces* and several sterile
mycelial forms, persisted even under high contamination.

## Environmental Influences on Metal Binding

The interaction between live fungal biomass and environmental carbon, nitrogen,
and phosphorus availability influences heavy metal solubility in soil
(Dixon-Hardy et al., 1998), meaning that the role of fungi in metal binding is
greatly influenced by ambient environmental conditions. Nutrient availability
can affect fungal growth rates, metabolic activity, and the production of
metal-chelating compounds such as organic acids and metallothioneins.
the rhizosphere.
