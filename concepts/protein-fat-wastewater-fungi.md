# Protein and Fat Wastewater Fungal Treatment ## Overview Large quantities of protein- and fat-containing wastewaters are released from slaughterhouses and tanneries, representing significant pollution challenges. Fungi can be effectively employed to treat these waste streams, simultaneously achieving waste reduction and producing valuable yeast biomass as an animal feed supplement. This application is part of the broader fungal wastewater treatment approach. ## Wastewater Composition Typical slaughterhouse and tannery wastewater contains approximately 0.05% protein and 0.03% fat. One Swedish facility processed approximately 3000 tons per month with 6-8 g/L fat and 10-13 g/L protein. The fat content includes: - 43% oleic acid - 23% palmitic acid - 14% stearic acid - 7% linoleic acid - 4% palmitoleic acid - 2% myristic acid - 2% linolenic acid The amino acid profile is similar to gelatin, and the fat contains approximately 90% free fatty acids with some iron and calcium in the ash. ## Fungal Treatment Process ### Biological Objectives Biodegradation of fat into yeast biomass achieves two beneficial objectives: 1. Removal of fat from wastewater, simplifying subsequent processing (protein extraction or methane generation) 2. Production of yeast biomass as a feed supplement ### Candida tropicalis Treatment Candida tropicalis S001 has been extensively studied for protein-fat wastewater treatment. The process involves: 1. **pH lowering** (heat treatment) 2. **Yeast culture** at pH 4.0 with 10% CO2 in gas phase 3. **Sedimentation** to separate biomass 4. **Output:** Soluble protein (for methane production) and yeast biomass (for feed) ### Growth Characteristics The maximum specific growth rate of C. tropicalis S001 increases in order: triolein < glycerol < wastewater < glucose. Biomass yields: - Glycerol: 0.97 g dry weight/g fat - Triolein: 0.68 g dry weight/g fat - Glucose: 0.57 g dry weight/g fat ### Reactor Performance A continuous culture reactor reduces 86% of COD at a feed rate of 40 kg COD/m3 per day, producing methane at 10.8 m3/m3 reactor per day (yield: 0.31 m3 methane/kg COD removed). ## Lipolytic Yeast Species Several yeasts are recognized for growth on lipids at pH 3.5-4.0: - **Candida lipolytica:** Grown on fish oil since 1974 - **Saccharomycopsis lipolytica:** Capable of growth on tallow or lard as sole carbon and nitrogen source - **Candida tropicalis:** Effective on protein-fat mixtures - **Geotrichum candidum:** Used with stickwater (fish meal and oil industry by-product) ## Competitive Advantages of Low pH Operation Growth of yeasts at low pH provides a sterilizing effect in continuous systems, as most bacteria cannot grow or compete at pH 3.5-4.0. This eliminates the need for sterile operating conditions and reduces contamination risk in continuous processes. This principle applies broadly across treatment.

# Fungal Treatment of Industrial Wastewaters

## Overview

Fungal treatment of industrial wastewaters represents a major application of mycoremediation, leveraging the metabolic capabilities of yeasts and filamentous fungi to reduce organic loads, degrade specific contaminants, and recover valuable byproducts from waste streams. The approach dates back to the 1960s and has been applied across diverse industrial sectors, including food processing, dairy, distillery, pulp and paper, and textile industries.

## Industrial Context

Growing global concern over environmental pollution from industrial wastewaters has driven increasingly stringent discharge regulations. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) developed industrial wastewater regulations decades before European Commission directives were established in 2000. Key drivers include water reuse economics, rising disposal costs, and the need for biosensor-based monitoring of treatment efficacy.

## Fungal Advantages in Wastewater Treatment

Fungi offer several distinctive advantages for wastewater treatment compared to bacterial systems:

- **Low pH tolerance:** Many yeasts grow optimally at pH 3.5-4.0, providing a sterilizing effect against bacterial competitors
- **Easy biomass recovery:** Filamentous fungi form pellets or flocs recoverable by simple filtration
- **Value-added products:** [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]

The most commonly used yeast is Candida utilis, valued for its ability to utilize a wide variety of carbon and nitrogen sources, rapid growth at high yields, and tolerance of low pH. Fungi Imperfecti convert biodegradable organic matter into mycelium with high protein content that forms recoverable flocs.

## Bioreactor Types for Fungal Wastewater Treatment

Multiple reactor configurations have been adapted for fungal wastewater treatment:

- **Stirred tank reactors:** Most common for submerged cultivation
- **Packed-bed reactors:** Prone to clogging from [[singh-fungal-pellet-formation-bioremediation]]. Above a critical pellet size, oxygen depletion leads to hollow pellet formation and cell death.

## Wastewater Types and Treatment Approaches

Fungal treatment has been applied to specific industrial wastewater categories including protein-fat wastewaters, [[staycare-fungal-treatment-of-olive-oil-mill-wastewater]]. Each waste stream presents unique characteristics requiring tailored fungal species selection and process optimization.

## Treatment Efficiency and Economic Considerations

Fungal wastewater treatment systems typically achieve COD removal efficiencies of 60-90% depending on waste stream composition, reactor configuration, and operating conditions. The economic viability depends on the value of recovered byproducts (single-cell protein, enzymes, biogas) offsetting treatment costs. Anaerobic digestion of fungal-treated effluent can recover additional energy as methane, improving the overall economic balance.

## Integration with Conventional Treatment

Fungal treatment is rarely used as a standalone process in full-scale applications. Instead, it is most effective when integrated with conventional wastewater treatment stages. A typical configuration uses fungal bioreactors as a pretreatment step to reduce organic loads and remove recalcitrant compounds before secondary biological treatment. This integration can reduce the hydraulic retention time and energy requirements of downstream aerobic treatment stages, lowering overall operating costs.

## Monitoring and Quality Assurance

Biological oxygen demand (BOD) biosensors using immobilized yeasts have been developed for monitoring effluents from wastewater treatment plants. Performance optimization through kinetics and modeling remains a key focus. Combined wastewater treatment strategies may be needed to achieve full remediation goals.

## Challenges and Limitations

Despite their advantages, fungal wastewater treatment systems face several practical challenges. Scale-up from laboratory to industrial scale is often complicated by maintaining optimal fungal growth conditions in large-volume reactors. Oxygen transfer limitations in high-density fungal cultures can reduce treatment efficiency. Process upsets from temperature fluctuations, pH drift, or toxic shock loads may require extended recovery periods. Additionally, the regulatory approval process for fungal-based treatment systems remains less developed than for conventional bacterial treatment technologies, creating barriers to commercial adoption.
