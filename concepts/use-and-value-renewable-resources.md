# Use and Value Renewable Resources and Services — Permaculture Principle 5

Use and Value Renewable Resources and Services is the fifth of [[david-holmgren]]'s
twelve permaculture-design-principles. It advocates for the appropriate use
of natural resources and services that can be renewed or that nature provides
freely, in contrast to non-renewable resources that are depleted by use. The
principle emphasises total process evaluation, considering the full life-cycle
costs and benefits of resource use.

## Renewable versus Non-Renewable Resources

A renewable resource is one that can be replenished within a human time frame
through natural processes. Trees, soil fertility, water, and solar energy are
renewable resources. Non-renewable resources such as fossil fuels, phosphate
rock, and many mineral deposits are finite and cannot be replaced once
consumed. The principle does not demand absolute avoidance of non-renewable
resources but calls for their strategic use and gradual replacement with
renewable alternatives.

The concept extends beyond physical resources to include environmental
services: the purification of air and water, pollination of crops, climate
regulation, and biological pest control that nature provides freely. These
services are often undervalued because they are not captured in economic
accounting systems.

## Total Process Evaluation

Evaluating the appropriateness of any resource use requires considering the
total process from extraction through use to disposal. This includes:
- The energy cost of obtaining and processing the resource
- The environmental impact of extraction and processing
- The longevity and efficiency of the resource in use
- The consequences of disposal or recycling

Simple metrics like EMERGY analysis, developed by Howard Odum, can help compare
the true energetic cost of different resource pathways. Without such total
process evaluation, apparently "green" choices may prove to have hidden costs
that outweigh their benefits.

## Investment of Non-Renewable Energy in Agriculture

A key application of this principle is evaluating the use of non-renewable
energy in agricultural systems. Industrial agriculture depends heavily on fossil
fuel inputs for fertiliser production, mechanisation, irrigation pumping, and
transport. When these inputs become scarce or expensive, agricultural systems
that have been designed around them will face serious challenges.

[[permaculture-design]] seeks to progressively replace non-renewable inputs
with renewable ones. This includes using biological nitrogen fixation instead
of synthetic fertilisers, animal labour or gravity-fed systems instead of
fossil-powered machinery, and on-site renewable energy instead of grid
electricity.

## Biological Examples

### Agroforestry in Kenya

The International Centre for Research in Agroforestry (ICRAF) in Kenya
demonstrated that incorporating leguminous shrubs into maize farming systems
increased corn yields up to tenfold compared to conventional methods. The
shrubs fixed nitrogen, improved soil structure, provided mulch, and created
microclimate benefits — all renewable services that replaced purchased
fertiliser inputs.

### Working Animals

Historically, working animals provided essential energy for agriculture and
transport. While largely replaced by fossil fuels in industrial societies,
animal power remains appropriate in many contexts and represents a renewable
energy source fed by biomass that can be sustainably produced.

### Stormwater Treatment

Comparison of stormwater treatment approaches illustrates the principle:
biological treatment systems using cumbungi reed ponds provide water
purification as a free environmental service, while concrete continuous
deflective separation (CDS) litter traps require energy-intensive manufacture,
installation, and maintenance. The biological approach values nature's services;
the engineering approach substitutes non-renewable resources for them.

## Services versus Products

The distinction between resources and services is important. A tree provides
both a product (timber) and services (shade, windbreak, habitat, water
management, carbon sequestration). The services often have greater total value
than the product. [[permaculture-design]] prioritises systems that maximise
the service component of resource use, as services are typically renewable and
self-maintaining while products require harvesting and processing.

## Capital Asset Size

The principle relates to the concept of capital asset size. Large capital
investments in non-renewable infrastructure create path dependencies that are
difficult to reverse. Smaller, more modular systems using renewable resources
are more adaptable to changing conditions. This connects to [[use-small-and-slow-solutions]] and the broader permaculture emphasis on appropriate scale.

## Strategic Use of Non-Renewables

Holmgren does not advocate absolute rejection of non-renewable resources. Fossil
fuels are described as very useful but massively overused, with most uses being
destructive or banal. The principle calls for strategic use: employing
non-renewable resources to establish renewable systems that will outlast the
availability of the non-renewable inputs. This might include using fossil
fuels to establish tree plantations, build water harvesting infrastructure, or
create soil fertility that will sustain future production.

At Melliodora, mains electricity is used but consumption is less than
one-fifth of typical households through passive solar and wood heating. The
household buys renewable energy tariffs to stimulate development of renewable
sources, but low consumption is considered more important than green purchasing.

## Gradual Transition

The transition from non-renewable to renewable resources should be gradual and
strategic rather than abrupt. Each use of a non-renewable resource should be
evaluated: Is this the best use of a finite resource? Could a renewable
alternative serve the same function? Can this non-renewable investment create
lasting renewable capacity?
