## Water Quality in Aquaculture Ponds

## Overview

All life processes and [[decomposition]] in aerated waters consume oxygen. Water quality—particularly dissolved oxygen levels, pH, and the balance of aerobic and anaerobic processes—is the single most critical factor determining the success or failure of aquaculture systems. Understanding water quality management is essential for anyone designing or managing productive pond systems.

## Dissolved Oxygen

### Critical Thresholds

A general dissolved oxygen level of **5 parts per million (ppm)** is very satisfactory in ponds. At **1 ppm**, many fish species will die, except for a select group of adapted air-breathers used in stagnant and weedy tropical still ponds.

### Air-Breathing Fish Species

Several fish species possess special adaptations enabling them to survive in oxygen-depleted waters:

- **Snakeheads** (*Ophiocephalus*): Possess accessory air-breathing organs
- **Singhi** (*Heteropneustes*): Air-breathing catfish
- **Walking catfish** (*[[clarias]]*): Labyrinth organ for atmospheric oxygen extraction
- **Mahi** (*Notopterus*): Adapted to low-oxygen tropical shallows
- **Climbing perch** (*Anabas*): Can breathe atmospheric air via labyrinth organs

These species have sacs, labyrinth organs, or special air chambers near the gills from which they can derive oxygen by breathing air. Even derelict swampy ponds can yield **2,400 kg of protein per hectare per year** if a polyculture of air-breathers is stocked, using three or more of these hardy species.

### The Nighttime Oxygen Crisis

Less oxygen can be dissolved in warm water than in cold water, and plants that produce oxygen by day consume it at night. Healthy, weed-filled shallow ponds may develop severe oxygen deficiency on warm summer nights. If valuable fish stocks are present, managers should:

- Reduce the level of wastes in the pond
- Arrange for automatic aeration at critically low dissolved oxygen levels

These dangers are reduced in clear, cold, open, or flowing pond systems. However, oxygen supply becomes a limiting factor in fish stock density at levels above approximately **5,000 kg/ha**, at which point oxygen must be supplied as air bubbles.

### Aeration Methods

Several methods are available for pond aeration:

- **Commercial aerators**: Many models available, some solar-powered
- **Gravity-fed systems**: Fountains, showers, and Flowforms where water can be led from a head of 2 metres or more
- **Partial aeration**: A considerable energy saving is achieved if only part of the pond is aerated at critical periods; fish will kettle (crowd) there in times of need
- **Cone aerators (trompes)**: Simple devices where air enters fine tubes, joins water flow, and oxygenated water is piped to refuges in ponds

Combining the feeding station with the oxygenated area is often ideal, as food wastes are also oxidised and waste control simplified.

## Classification of Water Bodies

Waters are generally classified into three categories:

### Aerobic

Oxygen is well-supplied by wind overturn, turbulent flow, aerators, or rapids, with a light load of decaying organic material. These are the most productive and stable aquaculture environments.

### Facultative

Found in many swamps and weed-clogged ponds, and secondary treatment sewage ponds. The surface may be aerated, but sediment or sludge collects in cold periods and the pond base becomes anaerobic. Sulphur-fixing bacteria decompose sediments under these conditions.

### Anaerobic

Primary sewage ponds and over-fertilised shallows where dissolved oxygen status is low (1 ppm or less). Only the most hardy adapted species survive in these conditions.

## Acidity and Alkalinity (pH)

### Problematic Source Areas

Ponds in areas of peats, mangroves, cordgrass flats, samphires, and with water derived from heaths and granites or siliceous soils can be very acid (pH 4.0 or less). The acids involved include humic acids, tannins, and minor organic acids. Hydrogen sulphide may also be released by ponded peats or swamps, creating sulphuric acid.

### Remediation Strategies

Several approaches can bring acidic ponds into production:

- **Peat stripping and mounding**: Removing acidic surface material
- **Freshwater or rain leaching**: Flushing acidic compounds
- **Liming**: Both burnt lime for initial treatment and unburnt lime for ongoing pH adjustment

Below peats, especially in basaltic soils, bluish clays produced under reducing conditions may occur. Fish will thrive in quite acid (natural) waters if calcium is available.

### Optimal pH Range

Pond pH is ideally **6.0–8.0**, extending to 8.5 for some species. Only a few fish grow well below these levels, notably salmonids—which are also one of the few satisfactory fish in monocultures. Most culture ponds are routinely limed when constructed and regularly checked, with pH adjusted using unburnt lime after initial burnt lime dressing.

**Lethal limits** for most fish: pH 3.7 (acid) and pH 10.5 (alkaline).

### Creating a pH Mosaic

In practice, a pH mosaic across ponds and pond series is desirable:

- **Soft (acid) water**: Preferred by many valuable food organisms and soft-water fish
- **Hard (alkaline) water**: Preferred by fish, molluscs, and freshwater lobsters and prawns

Slow-release calcium can be provided through crushed [[dolomite]], marble chips, hard limestone gravels, and oyster or mussel shells in tanks, small ponds, intake filter systems, and upper sections of canal systems.

### pH Interaction with Other Factors

There is an interaction between pH, respiration, and plant density. pH may rise at night or in clogged coastal waters with *Chara* and other algal forms, or fall as peats form and anaerobic conditions develop. For soft-water fish breeding, organic acids (hydrochloric, humic, or phosphoric) or peat filtration can be used to lower pH naturally.

## Integrated Rice Paddy Aquaculture

The modification of rice paddies to accept specialised species assemblies demonstrates effective water quality management in challenging systems:

- **Fish refuges**: Peripheral canals or sumps contain fish between flooding for crop
- **Temperatures**: May reach 34°C, with optimum fish growth at 22–28°C
- **Growth rates**: Pan-size fish in 10–12 weeks in Indonesia; 2–3 summer seasons in cooler Japan
- **Weed control**: Species such as *Trichogaster pectoralis*, *Clarias macrocephalus*, and *Anabas testudineus* are used for weed control
- **Yield**: Extra crop of fish in rice reaches 70–400 kg/ha/year

Effective polyculture mixtures for rice paddies include combinations of *Helostoma temmincki* (35%), *Osteochilus hasselti* (15%), *[[cyprinus-carpio]]* (15%), and *Puntius gonionotus* (35%) by fry or fingerling proportion. All such polycultures use weed-eating species and predators to control small fish.

## The Euryale Integration

Fish in tropical still ponds combine well with the crop plant *[[euryale-ferox]]*, a spiny floating plant of the water-lily family whose period of yield and harvest coincides with fish maturation. Ponds used are 1–1.5 m deep, and the fish help control malarial mosquitoes, providing multiple benefits. Seeds of *Euryale* are marketed as a "popcorn" in India.
