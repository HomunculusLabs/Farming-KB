# Greenhouse Heating Options for Winter Vegetable Production

## Overview

Eliot Coleman's Four Season Farm in Harborside, Maine, has pioneered techniques for year-round vegetable production in unheated and minimally heated greenhouses. While his core philosophy favors the simplicity of unheated "cold houses," his practical experience with adding minimal heat provides valuable insights for growers considering heating options. This concept covers the heating systems Coleman has tested, their economics, and his philosophical stance on energy use in winter growing.

## The Cold House Foundation

### The Unheated Greenhouse Concept

Coleman's winter harvest system is built on standard, plastic-covered, gothic-style greenhouses without supplementary heat. These "cold houses" rely on:
- The heat-storing capacity of the soil itself
- A second inner layer of lightweight row cover suspended on wire wickets
- Solar gain during the day
- The inherent cold-hardiness of selected crops

The soil acts as a thermal mass, absorbing heat during daylight hours and releasing it slowly at night. Wickets (wire supports) are spaced every 4 feet along the beds, holding the inner layer 12 inches above the crop canopy. This double-layer system can maintain temperatures 10–15°F warmer inside the inner cover compared to the outer greenhouse air on cold nights.

### Why Unheated Works

The cold house approach is refreshingly uncomplicated because it eliminates three major challenges of heated greenhouse production: fuel cost, equipment maintenance, and the environmental footprint of energy consumption. Crops planted in the cold house grow more slowly, but the extended growing season from August through April allows multiple successions that compensate for slower growth rates.

## The "Cool" Greenhouse — Minimal Heat

### Rationale for Adding Heat

Coleman added heat to 60% of his greenhouse space because demand for produce consistently exceeded supply from the unheated houses. The economic logic is compelling: adding a heater to an existing cold house effectively doubles the number of winter harvests, which is equivalent to building an entirely new greenhouse at a fraction of the cost. Additional benefits include:
- No new greenhouse structure to cover and maintain
- Better utilization of soil already built up through years of [[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]]
- Improved working conditions for the farmer in midwinter

### Temperature Target

The "cool" greenhouse is maintained at a minimum nighttime temperature just above 32°F (0°C). This modest target is sufficient to prevent freezing while using far less energy than maintaining tropical or subtropical temperatures. The cool house increases both the quantity and variety of crops available during the coldest months.

### Crop Expansion with Minimal Heat

Crops that struggle in unheated cold houses during December through February but perform well in the cool house include:
- Turnips
- Radishes
- 'Bianca Riccia' endive
- Arugula (standard varieties)
- Lettuces (most varieties except the hardiest oak-leaf types)

As cool-weather crops, their quality in the minimally heated house is outstanding — often better than equivalent greenhouse crops grown at warmer temperatures, which can become bitter or bolt prematurely.

## Heating System Options

### Propane-Fueled Greenhouse Heaters

The standard option Coleman used during early trials. Propane heaters sized for minimal-heat applications (maintaining just above freezing) are much smaller and cheaper than those required for maintaining 65°F (19°C) night temperatures for crops like tomatoes. Propane provides reliable, on-demand heat with straightforward [[hamilton-greenhouse-heating-and-temperature-control]].

### Wood Furnace Systems

Coleman recently installed a large wood furnace in his [[coleman-winter-harvest-washing-and-packing]] greenhouse to replace propane heating (except during exceptionally cold weather). This represents a move toward renewable energy, though it requires manual loading and monitoring. The wood furnace approach reduces fuel costs but increases labor requirements.

### Wood-Fired Hot Water Boiler Systems

Coleman investigated wood-fired hot water boiler systems that could heat all cool houses simultaneously. While attractive in principle, the initial cost was approximately ten times the investment already made in propane heaters. Additionally, unless a self-feeding wood-chip system is used, significant labor would be required for nighttime furnace loading. These systems are expensive but represent the most scalable renewable heating option.

### Used Cooking Oil

Friends of Coleman built an ingenious system that burns used cooking oil directly for greenhouse heating. Information on the design is available at laughingstockfarm.com. This creative approach repurposes waste oil as a fuel source, though it requires a consistent supply of used cooking oil and appropriate burner equipment.

### Earth Tube Concept

One minimal heating option Coleman seriously considered but hasn't implemented is the "earth tube" system:
- A smooth-walled, rigid plastic pipe (12-inch diameter) is buried approximately 6 feet deep
- The pipe runs about 100 feet across a field and into the greenhouse
- A fan draws outside air through the buried pipe
- At 6 feet depth, the earth temperature is approximately 45°F (7°C) year-round
- The warmed air is blown under the inner layer through perforated plastic tubes

Research suggests the energy used by the fans would be only about 15% of the energy required to create equivalent heat artificially. This system is particularly attractive for its extremely low energy consumption and minimal ongoing maintenance.

## Approaches Coleman Has Not Pursued

### Artificial Lighting

Coleman has not experimented with supplemental lighting because:
- It adds a major energy input to the production system
- No light bulb can truly duplicate the full spectrum of sunlight
- There is concern that incomplete, artificial light would reduce produce quality
- The philosophy prioritizes natural systems over technological intervention

### Soil Heating Pipes

Warm-water pipes buried in the soil to raise soil temperature have not been tested, partly because Coleman tries to keep production systems as natural as possible. In nature, agricultural soils are never warmer than the air above them in midwinter. However, Coleman acknowledges the scientific evidence:

- Lettuce growth slows in winter not because of shorter days (lettuce can only use 8 hours of light) but because of cooler soil temperatures
- At 4-inch depth, soil temperature in minimally heated houses drops to approximately 42°F (6°C) by midwinter
- Soil heating can supply about 20% of total heat needed by a [[coleman-aphid-management-winter-greenhouse-nitrogen-flushing]]
- A soil-heated greenhouse with wickets and inner layer might keep the crop zone above freezing without heating the air

## Coleman's Philosophical Position

### The Minimalist Ideal

Despite having tested multiple heating options, Coleman remains philosophically committed to the unheated approach: "The challenge of the simple, minimalist, unheated production is where our hearts lie and where we will concentrate our efforts." The farm now adds minimal heat to only one small growing area for continued trials, and may end even that.

### Renewable Energy Pursuit

The farm continues to seek renewable heating solutions. Recent developments in very hot-burning wood furnaces for heating water during the day (stored in large insulated tanks for nighttime use) are promising because they avoid the air pollution and creosote of damped-down wood fires and eliminate the need for 24-hour furnace stoking.

### Regional Considerations

Coleman acknowledges that heating concerns vary dramatically by region. Growers in more temperate parts of the country face minimal heating challenges, while those in northern climates must carefully weigh the cost-benefit of any heating investment against the reality of their local climate and market conditions.
