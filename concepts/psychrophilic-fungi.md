# Psychrophilic and Cold-Tolerant Fungi

Psychrophilic (cold-loving) and cold-tolerant fungi are adapted to grow at low temperatures, playing important ecological roles in polar, alpine, boreal, and winter-temperate environments. They contribute significantly to nutrient cycling and decomposition in cold ecosystems where biological activity might otherwise be severely limited.

## Temperature Classification

Fungi adapted to cold environments fall into several categories based on their temperature responses:

- **Psychrophiles**: obligate cold-adapted fungi with maximum growth temperatures below 20 degrees C and optima below 15 degrees C.

- **Psychrotolerants**: capable of growth at or near 0 degrees C but with optima above 20 degrees C; these are far more common than true psychrophiles.

- **Cold-tolerant mesophiles**: mesophilic fungi that can survive freezing temperatures and grow slowly at low temperatures.

True psychrophilic fungi are relatively rare; most fungi isolated from cold environments are psychrotolerant. The distinction is ecologically important because psychrotolerant species compete effectively at low temperatures but can also thrive when conditions warm, whereas psychrophiles are restricted to permanently cold habitats.

## Ecological Roles in Cold Ecosystems

### Snow Molds

Snow molds are low-temperature-tolerant soil-borne fungi that can damage and kill grasses, cereals, and other plants (Lebeau and Logsdon 1958; Nelson and Sturges 1982). They grow at the bases of snow-covered plants, and their extensive mycelial mats are found associated with damaged plants soon after snow melts. The most common species include:

- **Coprinus psychromorbidus** (often called low-temperature basidiomycete).

- **Microdochium nivale**.

- **Myriosclerotinia borealis**.

- **Typhula ishikariensis** and other Typhula species.

These fungi are significant pathogens in agricultural and natural grassland systems in northern latitudes, causing winter kill of forage grasses and cereal crops.

### Decomposition in Polar and Alpine Soils

Fungi are the primary decomposers in cold soils. In Antarctic, arctic, alpine, and boreal regions, characteristic soil fungi include Cylindrocarpon, Chrysosporium, Mortierella, Oidiodendron, certain Penicillium species, and sterile fungal strains (Dowding and Widden 1974; Bissett and Parkinson 1979a; Bailey and Wynn-Williams 1982; Pugh and Allsopp 1982). In cold bogs and coniferous swamps, yeasts, yeastlike forms, Tolypocladium, Trichoderma species, sterile dematiaceous forms, and certain penicillia and eurotialean fungi predominate.

Beiswenger and Christensen (1989) found soil fungi characteristic of present-day tundra communities at 1 m below native short-grass vegetation in an intermountain basin in Wyoming, interpreting them as relicts of the mycota that existed under periglacial conditions during the late Pleistocene.

## Cold Soils of the Arctic and Antarctic

Fungal communities in Arctic and Antarctic soils have been less studied than temperate counterparts but show distinct patterns. Soils of beech forests of North America and Europe show striking similarities in their principal saprobic fungal species (Christensen 1989), suggesting strong environmental regulation of community composition. Species diversity of [[fungal-biodiversity-alpine-arctic]] fungal communities provides insights into both adaptation mechanisms and the evolutionary history of these organisms.

## Climate Change Impacts

Warming temperatures in polar and alpine regions threaten cold-adapted fungal communities. As permafrost thaws and growing seasons lengthen, psychrophilic species may be outcompeted by psychrotolerant and mesophilic fungi migrating poleward. This shift in fungal community composition could have cascading effects on nutrient cycling, plant-fungal interactions, and ecosystem carbon storage in some of the world's most climate-sensitive regions.
