# Composting and Vermicomposting

Composting transforms organic waste into rich, fertile soil amendment. Food
scraps and kitchen waste that would otherwise fill about 30 percent of your
garbage can instead decompose and return to the soil from whence they came.
Composting is a cornerstone of [[natural-farm-soil-health-mulching]] and a
practical application of [[natural-farm-permaculture-ethics-principles]]
circular systems where every output becomes an input.

## Kitchen Composting Basics

Keep two buckets under the kitchen sink and add all food scraps except meat
and dairy. Orange peels, banana peels, and eggshells should be crushed and
cut up so they break down faster. Small paper scraps can also be added. Once
a bucket is full, dump it on the compost heap. Commercial black plastic bins
with lids work well in urban environments because they absorb heat, helping
compost break down faster. For a composting toilet setup, a much larger bin
is needed. Wooden bins outside should be at least 5 feet squared. Greenhouse
compost used as thermal mass should be at least 5 feet in diameter.

## What to Compost

Everything not being used as [[natural-farm-soil-health-mulching]] should go
into the pile: food scraps, composting toilet solids, leaves, garden waste,
and manure. It is also a good idea to collect material from elsewhere, such
as seaweed or organic material from commercial garden services, provided it
was not treated with chemicals. The pile must be sheltered from rain unless
you live in a very dry climate.

### Materials for Composting

- Banana peels, coffee grounds, corn cobs, dust, eggshells, hair, lint,
  manure, rhubarb stems, seafood shells, sod, soil, tea leaves, toadstools,
  and veggie scraps
- Avoid meat, dairy, and bones in standard compost piles

### Materials for Mulch (Keep Separate)

- Cardboard, pine needles, hay, straw, leaves, newspaper, sawdust
  (untreated only), weeds, wood chips, nutshells, and fabric

## The Composting Process

Once collected, materials will begin heating up, usually within 24 hours.
Organisms gather to feast on the materials, causing the temperature to rise.
During this time, leave the pile alone and keep adding to the top. The pile
should rise above 120 degrees Fahrenheit (49 degrees Celsius). When
microorganisms finish, the temperature drops and you can stir the pile to
introduce oxygen. Insects will move in, or you can begin raising worms in it.

## Troubleshooting Your Compost

Sometimes compost piles do not heat up, which is fine but means the process
takes longer. Common causes and solutions include:

- **Pile too small:** Needs to be at least 3 feet square to generate heat
- **Too wet:** Add more brown (carbon-rich) materials like paper, leaves,
  and dry grass
- **Too dry:** Add more green (nitrogen-rich) materials like manure and
  garden waste; aim for the moisture level of a damp sponge
- **Imbalanced materials:** Woody brown materials should make up about half
  the bulk. This is not exact; experience will guide you

Chopping up all materials before adding them helps them break down faster.
The ratio of carbon-rich (brown) to nitrogen-rich (green) materials is
critical. Brown materials include paper, leaves, dry grass, and straw. Green
materials include manure, kitchen scraps, and fresh garden waste.

## Worms and Vermicomposting

Raising worms, or vermicomposting, is no more difficult than composting. It
is especially valuable to urban dwellers and can be done in an apartment.
The worms need a covered bin with holes in the sides and bottom, lined with
mesh fabric to keep small ones from crawling out. Set the bin on a drainage
tray and water it like a houseplant.

### Setting Up a Worm Bin

1. Fill the bin with bedding material: black soil, shredded newspapers, or
   a mixture of both.

2. Obtain red wigglers (also called redworms or branding worms), sold for
   fishing. You need about 0.5 pound of worms, or 500 regular-sized worms
   per cubic foot.

3. The worms will eat their weight in kitchen scraps per day. Bury
   leftovers in the bedding after each meal. Follow the same rules as
   regular composting: no meat, dairy, garlic, or potato peelings. They need
   a steady diet of crushed eggshells sprinkled on top.

4. They do not tolerate cold temperatures. Keep them outside in summer but
   bring them inside when nights get chilly.

5. Every couple of months, open the bin in bright light. The worms will
   dive to the bottom because they hate light. After ten minutes, scrape
   off the top layer of valuable worm castings. When you see worms, wait
   another ten minutes and repeat. Continue until all castings are harvested,
   then refill the bin with fresh bedding.

Worm castings are among the finest soil amendments available. They are rich
in nutrients, improve soil structure, increase water retention, and introduce
beneficial microorganisms to the soil. Vermicomposting can be done year-round
indoors, providing a continuous supply of high-quality compost even in small
spaces.

## [[composting-toilets]]

A composting toilet separates human waste, allowing solids to decompose into
safe compost over time. In a well-managed system, the solids break down with
the help of carbon materials like sawdust or straw added after each use. The
resulting compost, after sufficient aging (typically one to two years), can
be safely used on ornamental plants and fruit trees. Liquid waste can be
diverted separately and used as a nitrogen source for [[natural-farm-soil-health-mulching]] systems like [[hugelkultur-beds]].

## Connecting Compost to the Larger System

Composting fits into the broader [[permaculture-design]] by closing the nutrient
loop. Kitchen scraps become compost, which feeds the garden, which produces
food, which generates more scraps. Animal manure from chickens, rabbits, or
goats on the property adds to the compost pile. The finished compost
improves soil structure, water retention, and nutrient availability in
garden beds, reducing or eliminating the need for external fertilizers. In
a well-designed system, compost also serves as thermal mass in greenhouses,
generating heat as it decomposes and helping to maintain growing temperatures
through cooler months.
