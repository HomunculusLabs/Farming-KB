# Legal Structures

**Source:** Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Bill Mollison (1988)
**Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Overview

Mollison devotes significant attention to legal and financial structures that support permaculture aims. Chapter 14 (Strategies for an Alternative Nation) covers trusts, cooperatives, credit unions, ethical investment, and community financial systems. The central message is that appropriate legal structures enable earth care and people care at scale.

## Trust Structures

### Trust A: The Land Trust (For the Public Good)
Trust A holds all real property which is not at risk -- land, buildings, copyrights, leases, and important equipment clear of debt. This trust:
- Takes no risks (does not trade)
- Holds assets for the public good
- Is immune from company tax
- Accepts volunteer or contract labour
- Can pay employees
- Can fund any other charity or trust
- Is set up by a foundation director (settlor) who should retire once the site has 10-12 residents

Trustees should be limited to 3-4 directors, as larger boards give endless trouble and necessitate agreement among many people, involving distasteful accommodations and compromises.

### Trust B: The Trading Trust
Trust B is a separate unit discretionary trust for trading operations. It:
- Accepts volunteer or contract labour and can pay employees
- Takes normal business risk
- Can rent or lease real property from Trust A
- Is currently immune from company tax
- Reduces family tax
- Enables a wide variety of enterprises under one organisation
- Can duplicate or triplicate itself for new enterprises
- Can insulate successful operations from risk
- Has Trust A as one of its beneficiaries
- Can handle financial systems such as leasing and lending units

## Developmental and Property Trusts (Section 14.8)

No investment in glamour stocks (coal, oil, uranium, properties, paper pulp, agrochemicals, mining) is likely to yield anything but more pollution and global collapse. Instead, Mollison advocates turning money resources to truly rehabilitative ends.

### Purpose
A property trust purchases real estate for improvement, lease, or rental on behalf of many small investors who cannot afford to individually own or develop such properties. By improving properties, their value increases, and (under present rulings) taxation is not incurred on that increase if the property is held for 10 or more years.

### Structure
- Low unit price ($100) enables even poorer people to invest
- A single unit can be held by a partnership, society, or corporate group
- No limit to the number of units held by any person
- Costs should not exceed 4-8% of funds for management
- Investors given opportunity for on-site involvement

### Ethical Trust Project Areas
- Purchase of threatened wildlife and forest habitat
- Purchase and rehabilitation of eroded, salted, deforested, or misused land
- Development of energy-efficient, sustainable village developments
- Purchase of selected properties for assisting developing countries
- Bioregional clean energy systems or clean transport methods

## Village Trust Structures (Section 14.9)

For village development, Mollison specifies a dual-trust structure:

**Development Stage (for 100 titles):**
- 60% sold by developers at best prices (30% covers all costs)
- 30% given to the village group for later use
- 10% allotted as labour equity to surveyors, earthmovers, landscapers

**Village Trust Stage (titles held by village group):**
- 20% sold to fund village projects (can be sold at 50% or less to low-income families)
- 10% reserved for crucial new recruits (medical, computer, energy people)

## Cooperatives

Other simple legal structures necessary to companies, cooperatives, credit unions, and public investment trusts are all well-outlined in company law, have excellent support services, and are routine arrangements.

The Mondragon model (Section 14.12) demonstrates:
- 96 worker-cooperatives, 17,000 worker-members in 30 years
- $5,000 investment per person (can be borrowed)
- 20% collective funds, 80% individual capital account
- Profit distribution: 10% community, 20% reserves, 70% to workers
- Pay ratio maximum 1:5
- One worker, one vote

## Legal Advice

A careful search for the right lawyer is essential -- corporate lawyers are often knowledgeable about trusts. A good accountant to lay out bookkeeping and give advice is necessary, as is an efficient office manager to communicate with the trust's target population.

## Key Principles

1. **Separate risk from assets:** Trust A holds assets safely; Trust B takes trading risks
2. **Small trustee groups:** Limit to 3-4 directors to avoid decision paralysis
3. **Ethical investment only:** No glamour stocks; invest only in rehabilitative enterprises
4. **Community involvement:** Give investors opportunity for hands-on participation
5. **Low entry cost:** Enable poor people to invest with small unit prices
6. **Tax efficiency:** Use trust structures legally to minimise tax and maximise reinvestment
7. **Plan for succession:** Foundation directors should retire as community matures
