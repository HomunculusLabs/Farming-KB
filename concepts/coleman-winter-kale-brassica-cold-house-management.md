## Coleman Winter Kale and Brassica Cold House Management

## Overview

Kale and other brassica greens represent some of the most cold-tolerant vegetables available for winter harvest systems. Eliot Coleman's Four Season Farm in coastal Maine (USDA Zone 5) has demonstrated that many brassica species can survive and even thrive in unheated cold houses [[coleman-cool-house-vs-cold-house-economics-winter-production]].
- **Collards** (B. oleracea var. viridis): Similar cold tolerance to kale with thicker leaves. (see [[coleman-winter-harvest-greenhouse-temperature-management]].
- **Tatsoi** (B. rapa var. narinosa): Rosette-forming; very cold-hardy [[lettuce-and-leafy-greens]]).
- **Mustard greens** (B. juncea): Spicy flavor intensifies with cold; moderate cold tolerance.
- **Pak choi** (B. rapa var. chinensis): Thick-stemmed; excellent for baby-leaf or full-head harvest.
- **Radish** ([[raphanus-sativus]]): Both root and leaf production; some varieties bred for winter.
- **Turnip** (B. rapa var. rapa): Roots and greens; Hakurei variety particularly suited to winter.
- **Kohlrabi** (B. oleracea var. gongylodes): Can be stored or harvested in situ in mild winters.

## Cold House vs. Cool House Suitability

Coleman distinguishes between two levels of winter protection:

**Cold houses** (unheated, single or double-layer plastic): Suitable for the hardiest brassicas—kale, collards, tatsoi, and mizuna. These crops can survive temperatures down to 10°F (-12°C) or lower under the inner row cover layer. The limiting factor is not killing temperature but growth rate: below approximately 35°F (2°C) daytime temperatures, brassica growth essentially stops.

**Cool houses** ([[coleman-cool-house-minimal-supplementary-heat]] maintaining temperatures just above freezing): Enable active growth of a wider range of brassicas through the winter months, including pak choi, mustard greens, and broccoli raab. The small amount of heat—just enough to keep temperatures above freezing—makes the difference between survival and active production.

## Sowing Schedule for Winter Brassicas

Coleman's winter-harvest schedule reverses the conventional spring-planting logic. For brassicas, the sowing window is critical because day length is contracting rather than expanding:

**For fall harvest (November–December):**
- **Broccoli raab**: Sow September 1–15 for harvest beginning late October.
- **Tatsoi**: Sow September 1–15 for harvest November through December.
- **Mizuna**: Sow September 1–15; multiple cuttings possible through December.
- **Mustard greens**: Sow September 1–15 for fall harvest.
- **Pak choi**: Sow August 15–September 1 for full-size heads; September 15–30 for baby leaf.
- **Radish**: Sow September 1–October 1 for winter harvest.
- **Turnip (Hakurei)**: Sow September 1–15 for harvest beginning November.

**For overwintering and spring harvest (January–April):**
- **Kale**: Sow mid-August for overwintered plants. Harvest from November through March as needed. New growth resumes in late February to early March.
- **Collards**: Similar timing to kale; slightly less cold-hardy in some varieties.
- **Tatsoi**: Sow late September to early October for early spring harvest.
- **Mizuna**: Sow late September for spring cutting.

## Growth Behavior in Cold Conditions

Coleman makes a crucial observation about winter brassica growth patterns: the relationship between temperature and growth rate is not linear but follows a threshold pattern. Above approximately 40°F (4°C) daytime temperatures, most brassicas grow at a useful rate. Between 35°F and 40°F, growth is very slow. Below 35°F, growth effectively ceases.

This has important management implications:
- **Plan for harvest, not growth, during deep winter** (December through mid-February in Maine). The crops present in the cold house during this period should be at harvestable size before the coldest weather arrives.
- **Size going into winter matters**: Plants that are too small in late November may not reach harvestable size before growth stops. Plants that are too large may bolt prematurely in spring.
- **Spring regrowth is reliable**: Once temperatures rise above the growth threshold in late February, overwintered brassicas (especially kale) resume vigorous growth and can provide multiple additional harvests.

## Cut-and-Come-Again vs. Once-and-Done

Coleman categorizes winter brassicas by their harvest style:

**Cut-and-come-again** (multiple harvests from the same plant):
- Mizuna: Regrows reliably after cutting at 2 inches above the crown, provided temperatures are above the growth threshold.
- Mustard greens: Similar regrowth capacity to mizuna.
- Tatsoi: Can be harvested outer leaves individually, though regrowth is slower in cold.
- Kale: Overwintered plants produce new leaves in spring but not during deep winter.

**Once-and-done** (single harvest, then replant):
- Pak choi (baby leaf or full head): Harvest the entire plant. In cold houses, pak choi is typically grown as a single-harvest crop with the space replanted to the next succession.
- Radish and turnip: Harvest the root (and greens if desired), then replant the space.
- Broccoli raab: Harvest the entire flowering stem; does not reliably regrow in cold conditions.

## Variety Selection

Coleman's variety recommendations for winter brassica production emphasize cold tolerance and flavor quality at low temperatures:

**Kale**: Winterbor, Red Russian, and Siberian types all perform well. Winterbor has superior cold tolerance with tightly curled leaves that shed snow and ice. Red Russian develops sweeter flavor with cold exposure but is slightly less cold-hardy.
