# Stony (Gibber) Desert Rehabilitation

## Overview

Vast areas of desert develop a stone layer (gibber pavement) over fairly well-structured soils. The stones were once part of a soil matrix but are now exposed after centuries of wind erosion. Old stony deserts display wind-faceted stones whose facets reveal the direction and intensity of local sandstorms or the saltation of sand grains in wind. Rehabilitation of gibber deserts focuses on leveraging the stone layer itself as a resource for water capture and tree establishment.

## The Nature of Gibber Deserts

REG (gibber desert) is stabilised solely by its layer of stones. Active dunes can also be stabilised by laying pebble beds over them. This stone layer is the key to both the desert's stability and its vulnerability:

- **Stability**: The stone armour prevents further wind erosion of the underlying soil
- **Vulnerability**: Any disturbance of the stone layer allows erosion to recommence

Windrowing stones for annual cropping is therefore certain to increase erosion. The challenge is to use the stone resource without destabilising the system.

## Stone Windrows as a Rehabilitation Tool

Wherever a road grader passes across gibber country, a windrow of stones results. Large sturdy side-rakes have been developed specifically for windrowing stones in fields or on gibber plains. These windrows present a significant opportunity for rehabilitation.

### How Windrows Function

Windrows create beneficial conditions through several mechanisms:

- **Water impoundment**: Water is impeded and largely absorbed below the stone piles
- **Dew formation**: Aerated heaps promote dew condensation
- **Organic accumulation**: Insects, reptiles, and birds leave manures and their remains in the windrows
- **Run-off concentration**: The bare area between windrows provides greater run-off, concentrating water toward the windrows
- **Seed bed creation**: The bare areas between windrows provide seed beds for smaller shrubs, legumes, and forbs

### Natural Windrow Analogues

Some natural stone windrows evolve below cliff shorelines on receding lakes. Below the stones, the soil is cool, moist, and well-structured due to insect occupancy. Many tree species thrive in these conditions, providing a model for constructed windrow systems.

### Implementation Strategy

**Spacing**: Windrows are placed across slope at 10–30 m intervals. The spacing should be staged—initial limited trials are needed before broadscale windrow systems are implemented, to assess whether trees can successfully establish.

**[[swale]] integration**: Swales can be made just upslope of windrows to maximise water capture and infiltration.

**Soil preparation**: If practical, ripping the pavement below or between windrows helps root penetration. A touch of fertiliser greatly aids initial growth.

**Species selection**: Trees are essential between windrows to prevent dust. The bare areas between windrows support smaller shrubs, legumes, and forbs.

### Historical Precedent

The Nabateans are thought to have made windrows on steep slopes to increase run-off and lead water by hill-base channels to fields. Good absorption occurs in windrows over clay or clay-loam soils on gentle slopes, suggesting the technique has broad applicability beyond desert environments.

## Design Considerations

### Trial Before Scale

Like many desert techniques, a limited trial should precede broadscale implementation. This is critical because:

- Not all gibber surfaces will support tree growth
- Windrowing that fails can accelerate rather than reduce erosion
- Local conditions (soil type, rainfall, wind patterns) determine feasibility

### Staged Implementation

Windrowing should proceed in stages:

1. **Initial trial**: Small area windrowed and planted with hardy tree species
2. **Assessment**: Evaluate survival rates, growth, and erosion effects over 1–3 seasons
3. **Expansion**: If successful, progressively windrow additional strips
4. **Inter-planting**: As tree canopy develops, introduce understory species between windrows

### Slope Considerations

- **Gentle slopes** over clay or clay-loam soils show the best absorption results
- **Steep slopes** may require additional water diversion structures
- Windrows should follow contour where possible to maximise water retention

## Complementary Techniques

Windrow rehabilitation can be combined with other dryland strategies:

- **Circle swales**: Ditch-and-bank patterns that trap seed, dust, and water
- **Contour banks**: Graded walls that back up and absorb water over long distances
- **Ripping**: Subsoil ripping between windrows to improve root penetration
- **Pelleted seed**: Broadcasting pelleted seed in windrow ditches to aid revegetation

## Ecological Benefits

When properly implemented, windrow systems on gibber desert produce a cascade of ecological benefits:

- Reduced wind erosion through tree shelterbelts
- Increased soil moisture through concentrated infiltration
- Enhanced soil biology through organic matter accumulation in windrows
- Expanded plant diversity through varied micro-habitats
- Dust and sand trapping from surrounding areas
- Progressive creation of more favourable microclimates
