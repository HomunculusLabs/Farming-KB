# JADAM Crop Residue as Fertilizer

## Overview

One of JADAM's most radical departures from conventional farming advice is its insistence that crop residues (branches, leaves, stems, and any remaining plant parts after harvest) must be returned to the soil rather than removed or destroyed. JADAM views crop residue as the perfect fertilizer -- with the ideal nutritional balance for the crop that produced it.

## The Conventional Error: Field Sanitation

The standard practice promoted by agricultural experts is "field sanitation" -- completely eliminating all crop residues to reduce disease and pest pressure the following season. Farmers who diligently remove every scrap expect reduced pesticide needs. But JADAM points out that pesticide use has only increased over the years, not decreased. The practice has failed.

The "field sanitation" approach is based on a flawed understanding of disease. Pathogens like canker and powdery mildew organisms are so common and prolific (one spore can propagate into one billion in 10 hours) that eliminating crop residues cannot possibly control them. They are everywhere in the soil.

## The Athlete's Foot Analogy

JADAM uses a powerful analogy: treat crop diseases the same way as athlete's foot fungus. People who walk barefoot never get athlete's foot because their feet host a diverse community of microorganisms. When athlete's foot fungi coexist with other microorganisms, they cannot monopolize the food source. Competition keeps them in check.

Apply the same principle to farming: increase the population and diversity of microorganisms in the soil, and disease-causing organisms will not gain dominance. Change from **disinfection to diversity**.

## The Nutritional Balance Argument

Crop residues contain the optimal nutritional balance for the crop that produced them. Consider what happens:
- Roots stretch into the soil and selectively absorb the specific nutrients the plant needs
- Stems, branches, and leaves are built from these selectively absorbed nutrients
- These plant parts therefore contain the exact nutritional profile the crop requires

JADAM's position is clear: "If you want your farming to be awesome, you must give awesome food for crops. Crop residue is the substance with the best nutritional balance for the crop."

When experts tell farmers to destroy crop residues and instead buy fertilizer to provide "optimal nutritional balance," JADAM asks: who benefits? The fertilizer companies.

## The Zero-Input Critique

Some advocates of zero-input agriculture argue that farming can succeed without adding anything. JADAM disagrees. In nature, all leaves and fruits fall to the ground and nutrients are recycled in a closed loop. But in farming, humans remove all the fruits -- sometimes harvesting more than once per year. The cycle of nutrients is broken.

What is taken out must be put back in. Crop residue is nature's way of returning nutrients to the soil. Removing it accelerates mineral depletion.

## Natural vs. Forced Leaching

JADAM distinguishes two forms of nutrient loss:
- **Natural leaching**: Minerals removed when fruits are harvested -- this is inevitable in farming
- **Forced leaching**: Nutrients removed when crop residues are destroyed -- this is entirely preventable

Both deplete soil minerals over time, but forced leaching represents a direct harm to agriculture. Farming that treats crop residue as waste is itself wasteful.

## Practical Implementation

The JADAM approach to crop residues:
- Never burn or remove crop residues
- Cut them and leave them on the soil surface
- Apply JMS to accelerate decomposition
- Allow microorganisms to break down residues and release nutrients back to soil
- For fruit trees, fallen leaves are caught by cover crop grass and decomposed within one year
- For field crops, incorporate residues with a tiller after harvest

## The Recycling Principle

When leaves fall from persimmon trees, the best nutrient for persimmons is its own leaves. If rye seeds are broadcast before harvesting, they sprout and catch the fallen leaves, preventing nutrient loss. No nutrient is lost; all is recycled back into the soil. This is the perfect fertilizer program.

# JADAM Mineral Leaching in Agriculture

## Overview

JADAM identifies mineral depletion as one of the most critical yet overlooked problems in modern agriculture. Unlike organic nutrients (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen) which are infinitely renewable through photosynthesis, inorganic minerals are finite resources that, once removed from the soil through harvest, are nearly impossible to replace.

## The Two Forms of Leaching

### Natural Leaching

Natural leaching is the inevitable loss of minerals when crops are harvested and removed from the field. The nutrients that built the fruits came from the soil; when the fruits are taken away, those minerals are gone. This process is intrinsic to all agriculture and cannot be entirely eliminated.

JADAM has coined the term "the natural leaching of minerals" to describe this inevitable nutrient drain. The "plow layer" (approximately 1 meter / 3.3 feet deep) is where most crop roots are located. As fruits are continuously harvested, the mineral content of this layer steadily declines.

### Forced Leaching

Forced leaching is the preventable loss of nutrients caused by removing crop residues from the field. When farmers remove stems, leaves, branches, and other plant parts instead of returning them to the soil, they are throwing away minerals that were selectively absorbed from the soil by the roots.

This is a human-caused problem with a simple solution: stop removing crop residues. See jadam crop residue fertilizer for the full argument.

## The Apple Example

In the 1940s, the saying in America was "an apple a day keeps the doctor away" because a single apple contained all the minerals a person needed in a day. Today, you would need 32 apples to get the same mineral content. Modern apples (and all fruits and vegetables) are severely deficient in minerals compared to their historical counterparts.

JADAM traces this decline directly to the continued process of mineral leaching -- both natural and forced -- compounded by the shift from self-sufficient farming to commercial agriculture.
