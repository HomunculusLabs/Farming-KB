### Nitrogen Acquisition

Nitrogen is typically limiting for fungal growth on woody substrates (C:N
ratios often exceed 500:1). Saprotrophs have evolved multiple strategies:

- **Protease secretion:** Extracellular proteases break down proteins,
  releasing amino acids and peptides
- **Peptide transport:** Direct absorption of oligopeptides through specific
  membrane transporters, more nitrogen-efficient than free amino acid uptake
- **Inorganic assimilation:** Most saprotrophs use nitrate and ammonium when
  available, but organic nitrogen dominates in woody substrates
- **Nitrogen scavenging:** Some wood-inhabiting fungi fix atmospheric nitrogen
  indirectly through associated bacteria
- **Nitrification:** Certain fungi oxidize ammonium to nitrite and nitrate

### Phosphorus Acquisition

Phosphorus is often locked in [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]] or mineral complexes:

- **Phosphatases:** Acid and alkaline phosphatases liberate orthophosphate
  from organic esters
- **Phytases:** Specialized phosphatases hydrolyzing phytate, the principal
  stored phosphorus form in plant tissues
- **Organic acid exudation:** Citric and oxalic acids solubilize mineral
  phosphates (calcium, iron/aluminum phosphate)
- **Phosphate transporters:** High-affinity transporters enable uptake at
  low soil solution concentrations

## Competitive Strategies

### Primary Resource Capture

Fungi compete intensely for access to freshly dead organic matter:

- **Rapid colonization:** Spores germinate quickly in response to nutrient
  cues; hyphal extension rates can exceed 1 mm/hour in favorable conditions
- **Spatial preemption:** First colonizers establish priority effects that
  suppress later arrivals through resource depletion and chemical inhibition
- **Dispersal trade-offs:** Species optimize for either rapid short-distance
  dispersal (dense spore drops) or long-distance dispersal (wind-carried
  spores), trading arrival speed for arrival frequency

### Combat and Defense

[[dighton-fungal-competition-resource-partitioning]] often involves direct antagonism:

- **Antibiotic production:** Many saprotrophs produce antimicrobial compounds
  that inhibit competitors (many medical antibiotics were discovered from
  saprotrophic fungi)
- **Mycofouling:** Some fungi grow over and smother competitors
- **Enzyme warfare:** Fungi secrete enzymes that degrade competitors' hyphal
  walls (chitinases, glucanases)
- **Barrage zones:** Pigmented, dense mycelial boundaries that limit resource
  exchange where competitors meet

### Stress Tolerance

Saprotrophs vary in environmental stress tolerance:

- **Desiccation tolerance:** Melanin production, compatible solute accumulation,
  and spore formation enable dry-period survival
- **Temperature extremes:** Thermophilic fungi decompose compost above 50
  degrees C; psychrotolerant species remain active near freezing
- **Heavy metal resistance:** Some saprotrophs sequester metals in vacuoles or
  precipitate them extracellularly, enabling growth on [[soil-food-web-structure]] describes how these interact with bacteria
  and microfauna.
- **Fungal saprotrophs:** Mycophagous fungi that decompose other fungi

## Implications for Ecosystem Models

Accurate representation of saprotrophic strategies is critical for predicting
ecosystem responses to environmental change:

- **Warming effects:** Temperature-driven decomposition increases may be
  limited by fungal-enzyme-activity-nutrient-availability if warming increases metabolic demand
  faster than nutrient supply
- **[[dighton-nitrogen-deposition-mycorrhizal-community-shifts]]:** Anthropogenic nitrogen may shift fungal communities
  from resource-efficient to resource-expensive strategies, reducing soil
  [[holmgren-soil-humus-carbon-storage-rebuilding]]
- **Carbon feedbacks:** The balance between fungal CUE and decomposition rate
  determines whether warming accelerates or decelerates soil carbon loss.
