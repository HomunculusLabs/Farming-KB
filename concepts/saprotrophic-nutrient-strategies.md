### Nitrogen Acquisition

Nitrogen is typically limiting for fungal growth on woody substrates (C:N
ratios often exceed 500:1). Saprotrophs have evolved multiple strategies:

- **Protease secretion:** Extracellular proteases break down proteins,
  releasing amino acids and peptides
- **Peptide transport:** Direct absorption of oligopeptides through specific
  membrane transporters, more nitrogen-efficient than free amino acid uptake
- **Inorganic assimilation:** Most saprotrophs use nitrate and ammonium when
  available, but organic nitrogen dominates in woody substrates
- **Nitrogen scavenging:** Some wood-inhabiting fungi fix atmospheric nitrogen
  indirectly through associated bacteria
- **Nitrification:** Certain fungi oxidize ammonium to nitrite and nitrate

### Phosphorus Acquisition

Phosphorus is often locked in [[dighton-fungal-competition-resource-partitioning]] often involves direct antagonism:

- **Antibiotic production:** Many saprotrophs produce antimicrobial compounds
  that inhibit competitors (many medical antibiotics were discovered from
  saprotrophic fungi)
- **Mycofouling:** Some fungi grow over and smother competitors
- **Enzyme warfare:** Fungi secrete enzymes that degrade competitors' hyphal
  walls (chitinases, glucanases)
- **Barrage zones:** Pigmented, dense mycelial boundaries that limit resource
  exchange where competitors meet

### Stress Tolerance

Saprotrophs vary in environmental stress tolerance:

- **Desiccation tolerance:** Melanin production, compatible solute accumulation,
  and spore formation enable dry-period survival
- **Temperature extremes:** Thermophilic fungi decompose compost above 50
  degrees C; psychrotolerant species remain active near freezing
- **Heavy metal resistance:** Some saprotrophs sequester metals in vacuoles or
  precipitate them extracellularly, enabling growth on [[soil-food-web-structure]] describes how these interact with bacteria
  and microfauna.
- **Fungal saprotrophs:** Mycophagous fungi that decompose other fungi
