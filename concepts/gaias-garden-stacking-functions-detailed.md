# Stacking Functions in Garden Design

gaias garden stacking functions detailed is one of the most important design principles in [[hemenway-ecological-garden-design]]. The rule states that every element of a design -- each plant, structure, or feature -- should perform more than one job. Nothing in nature has only one function; nature is supremely efficient, squeezing every erg of energy out of each investment in matter and growth. This principle, which [[polyculture-design-hemenway]] calls "the most important to follow," distinguishes ecological garden design from conventional landscaping, where elements typically serve single purposes.

## The Principle Explained

Nature stacks functions because every living thing represents a big investment in matter and energy, two things that nature husbands with immense stinginess. She is supreme at getting the most bang for her buck, squeezing every erg of energy out of that shrub, tying it into lots of other cycles to maximize the return.

A shrub does not just cast shade. It feeds winter-starved birds with its berries, offers shelter, [[faires-permaculture-soil-and-sheet-mulching]] with its leaves, provides browse for hungry deer and porcupines, blocks the wind, holds soil with its roots, collects and channels rainwater to its root zone, and on and on. The shrub's berries took energy to grow, so when a bird eats them, the plant trades its effort for seed dispersal, making hard seeds that will pass unharmed through the bird's gut to germinate on new ground. By making plants perform multiple functions, nature uses her energy investment very efficiently.

Most human designs, in contrast, are prodigiously wasteful. We seem to hurry our goods from source to landfill in an arrow-straight stream, while nature would bend and re-bend that stream into a zigzag course, extracting benefit at every turn and recycling what is left. By designing our gardens with the same principles in mind, they become far less wasteful and troublesome, far more productive and bountiful.

## The Two Halves of Stacking Functions

### Rule 1: Each Element Does Multiple Jobs

Every plant, structure, or feature in the garden should serve at least two or three functions. The grape arbor over the water tank illustrates this rule: the vine shades the deck while letting in light in winter, cools the house, provides food, mulching-techniques|mulch, and propagation stock, and helps beautify an otherwise ugly water tank. Nearly every element does several jobs.

A simple [[gaias-garden-compost-worm-methods]] is multifunctional: it disposes of waste, creates fertile humus, boosts [[hemenway-soil-food-web-garden]], and even offers the gardener a little exercise through turning and spreading. Even a single-species hedge of privet can serve as a windbreak, privacy screen, and [[gaias-garden-beneficial-birds-wildlife]].

When evaluating any plant for the garden, consider all the roles it might play. A [[gaias-garden-multipurpose-plants]] like comfrey produces mulch, attracts pollinators, breaks compacted soil, accumulates nutrients, provides medicine, and serves as a [[gaias-garden-living-plant-systems-insectary]] for young trees.

### Rule 2: Each Job Has Multiple Elements

The complement of the first rule: each function that needs to be performed should be supported by more than one element. In other words, always have backups in place.

Gardeners already follow this rule more or less unconsciously. We plant several varieties of vegetables in case one fails, or different fruits or flowers to yield over a long season. And every gardener has an array of sprinklers, drip irrigation gadgets, soaker hoses, special hose nozzles, and watering cans, all for the single purpose of delivering water to our plants. Multiple, layered systems such as these are more effective at doing the total job than any one device would be.

## Benefits of Redundancy

Multiple, layered systems are more effective than any single element. A quick glance at how nature does it shows some of the benefits:

### Disaster Protection
Most important functions in organisms and ecosystems have backups, often several layers deep. Look at our sense of balance. We use three independent methods to keep our equilibrium: our eyes tell us what position we are in; our ears contain fluid-filled chambers lined with sensitive hairs that signal orientation; and our muscles and tendons have receptors that telegraph data on limb movements. By devoting energy and organs to this "tell me three times" strategy, our bodies make a big investment in not falling over.

Any organism or system with backups survives longer. If the soil is well [[gaias-garden-sheet-mulching]], when the irrigation fails the mulch holds moisture. If pest insects attack one crop, diverse plantings ensure others survive. If one [[gaias-garden-fruit-tree-selection]] variety succumbs to disease, other varieties continue producing.

### Increased Yield
Multiple elements performing the same function often produce more total output than a single element would. Three types of nitrogen-fixing plants in a [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]] build soil fertility more reliably and completely than one species alone.

### Resilience
Systems with backups survive disturbance and recover faster. The [[gaias-garden-backyard-ecosystem]] with many connections between elements can absorb the loss of any single component without collapsing.

### Nutrient Cycling
When multiple species fill similar roles, nutrient flows are more continuous and efficient. Different plants access nutrients at different depths, in different chemical forms, and at different times of year.

## Examples of Stacking Functions

### Water Tank, Grapevine, and Deck
A 5,000-gallon rainwater tank with a projecting concrete lid was an eyesore next to the kitchen. To hide the concrete, a cedar deck was nailed over it, but in the blazing summer sun the deck was too hot to enjoy. An arbor was built over the deck, and two seedless grapes were trained on it. A trellis on the tank's side was entwined by jasmine. The result:

- A cool, shady lunch spot beneath fast-growing grapes
- Dessert of sweet grapes in late summer, simply reached overhead
- Kitchen cooling in summer from grape leaf shade
- Winter sun access after leaf fall
- Leaf mulch for garden beds or the [[gaias-garden-compost-worm-methods]]
- Propagation cuttings from winter grape pruning
- Overflow irrigation for nearby plantings from the cistern
- Fragrant jasmine perfume over the deck
- Houseplants spending summer vacation in one light-dappled corner

By combining the water tank, grapevine, and deck in the right arrangement, the usefulness of each was increased, and benefits appeared that none alone would provide. Nearly every element does several jobs.

## Applying Stacking Functions in Design

When evaluating any element for inclusion in the [[gaias-garden-backyard-wetland-design]], ask:

1. What functions does this element perform?
2. Can any of these functions be performed by elements already in the design?
3. What other functions could this element serve with minor modification or companion planting?
4. Are there backups for this element's most critical functions?
5. Does this element connect to and support other elements in beneficial ways?

The goal is to minimize the number of elements while maximizing the number of functions and connections between them. This creates the tightly interconnected, self-maintaining systems seen in natural ecosystems. The [[gaias-garden-many-gardens-garden-design-process]] should systematically evaluate each proposed element against these criteria.

## Stacking Functions Across Scales

The principle applies at every scale of garden design:

- **Individual plants**: [[gaias-garden-multipurpose-plants]] like comfrey, bamboo, sea buckthorn, or elderberry that each serve five or more functions
- **Plant communities**: [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-guild-design-in-permaculture]] and [[polyculture-design-hemenway]] where multiple species share space and create connections between their respective functions
- **Garden zones**: [[hemenway-zone-and-sector-planning]] where placement optimizes multiple interactions between elements based on frequency of use
- **Whole landscapes**: [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-forest-gardens-and-food-forests]] that stack ecological functions vertically through [[gaias-garden-food-forest-layers]] and horizontally through [[gaias-garden-super-guild-design]]
