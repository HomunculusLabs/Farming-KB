## Fungal Morphology

The fungal kingdom encompasses organisms with great structural variety. Most fungi are composed of vegetative structures called **mycelia** that grow from their tips as binary branching trees. Mycelia are unique threadlike, tubular structures forming extensive branched and anastomosing (interconnected) systems.

### Hyphal Structure
- Each mycelium is analogous to a forest, where individual "trees" are called **hyphae**
- Hyphae of most fungi are **septate** (divided by cross-walls), with pores allowing mass movement of cytoplasm, organelles, and nuclei
- Each hypha originates from a spore that produces germinal tubes upon germination
- Hyphae are bounded by a rigid heteropolymer wall of **chitin** (composed of glucosamine)
- Two types of cytoplasmic vesicles participate in apical growth:
  - **Macrovesicles**: Secrete polymers and enzymes for the amorphous wall phase
  - **Microvesicles**: Constitute the chitin skeleton of the wall

### Yeast Morphology
Yeasts are unicellular with a cell wall and single nucleus per cell. Two basic types exist: budding yeasts and fission yeasts.

## Morphology in Submerged Cultures

Filamentous fungi show several distinct morphologies in submerged fermentation:

| Form | Description | Rheological Impact |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| Dispersed mycelia | Freely dispersed hyphal filaments | Non-Newtonian, viscous |
| Clumps | Loose hyphal aggregates | Variable viscosity |
| Pellets | Dense, often spherical aggregates | Less viscous, near-Newtonian |

Fungal cell morphology is influenced by:
- Agitation intensity
- Culture CO2 concentration
- pH
- Specific growth rate
- Dissolved O2

## Pellet Formation and Structure

Pellet formation in filamentous fungi is not fully understood, but two mechanisms have been proposed:

### Coagulating Pelletization
A two-step process:
1. Aggregation of spores
2. Subsequent aggregation of germinating spore agglomerates

### Non-Coagulating Pelletization
Results from germination of a single spore, expanding outward.

[Phanerochaete chrysosporium appears to involve surface polysaccharide bridging during initial aggregation, though whether this applies to all fungi remains unclear.

### Factors Affecting Pellet Formation
- Inoculum concentration and preparation
- Agitation intensity
- Medium composition (pH, carbon level, phosphorus concentration, surfactants)
- Strain-specific characteristics

### Pellet Structure and Metabolism
Pellet structure ranges from loose, irregular aggregates to tight, compact spheres:
- Pelleted suspensions are less viscous than dispersed mycelia
- Tightly compact pellets develop nutrient transfer limitations
- Centers of large pellets become hollow due to autolysis
- In Penicillium chrysogenum, pellets under 400 micrometers contain all active cells
- Larger pellets exhibit four distinct zones: outer active layer, two less active layers, hollow center
- Only the outer 100-200 micrometers of [Phanerochaete chrysosporium pellets assimilate glucose

A possible relationship exists between fungal morphology and metabolite production, with implications for [enzyme production in bioremediation.

## Growth Measurement Methods

| Method | Application |
|--------|------------|
| Dry weight measurement | Standard for liquid culture (stationary or shake flasks) |
| Chitin measurement | Fungal biomass estimation in solid-state fermentation |
| Ergosterol quantification | Fungal biomass indicator (subject to photochemical degradation) |
| Colony-forming units (CFU) | Routine fungal quantification |
| Microtiter plate reader | Rapid, efficient 96-well plate growth measurement |
| Electrochemical/voltammetric methods | Growth curve matching via metabolite detection |
| Image analysis | Hyphal length, branching frequency, pellet size |

## Surface Tension and Aerial Growth

Filamentous fungi colonize moist substrates and can breach the water-air interface to grow into air. [Schizophyllum commune reduces surface tension by secreting large amounts of **hydrophobin**, forming stable amphipathic protein films at the water-air interface. This enables aerial hyphae and fruiting body formation. In continuous liquid flow, hyphae of mycelial fungi (especially Aspergillus niger) reorient to grow upstream.
