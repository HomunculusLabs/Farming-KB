# Solomon on Crop Rotation and Garden Planning

From [[solomon-gardening-when-it-counts-index]] (2005) by Steve Solomon.

## Why Rotation Matters

If the soil where you live does not freeze solid in winter to a depth of at least 18 inches for at least a few continuous months, it is probably not possible to grow a vegetable garden on the same land for more than three to five years before serious troubles arise with diseases and soil-dwelling insects.

Many people in mild climates have grown the family garden in the same place for more than a generation and think everything is fine. But they have forgotten -- or were never told -- that some vegetables that once were easy to produce on that plot now seem impossible to grow. They have also never known that the output is considerably lower than during the first few years.

## The Ley System: Solomon's Rotation Method

Solomon's solution is a ley system -- alternating between garden and pasture. Every four or five years, half the beds are put to rest in grass and clover, while the grass beds are turned over to grow vegetables.

### How It Works

1. Grow vegetables on a section of land for three to five years.
2. Let that section rest, sowing it to rough grass and clover like a pasture.
3. Roughly mow the resting section a few times each summer. Let all the clippings lie in place to decompose.
4. After three to five years of rest, a ley rebuilds the soil's content of organic matter and restores the biological process to a stable, healthy balance.
5. Turn the resting section over and begin growing vegetables again.

### Benefits

- Doubles the effective garden size (half growing, half resting)
- During the first two years after breaking the sod, vegetables on the new ground grow noticeably better than the ones on the old beds were doing
- Eliminates soil-borne disease and pest buildup without chemicals
- Requires no irrigation on the resting sections
- Adds zero-cost organic matter through grass/clover decomposition

## Garden Size Planning

Solomon's own garden uses about 2,000 square feet of actual growing beds (not counting paths). With the ley system, he has nearly doubled this to over 4,000 square feet. Add paths, a couple of currant bushes, four small fruit trees, some asparagus and perennial herbs around the fringes, and he uses about 6,500 square feet inside a wildlife-proof fence.

For food self-sufficiency:

- 2,700 square feet can supply about half the daily calories for two adults (mainly from potatoes or sweet potatoes)
- For nearly complete caloric self-sufficiency, about 4,500 square feet per adult is needed
- This is helped by root cellaring or other winter storage methods
- Growing cereal grains would require five to ten times as much land per person

## Crop Families and Rotation

Solomon implicitly follows family-based rotation through his crop chapters. The key principle: do not follow a crop with another member of the same family. The main families to track:

- **Solanaceae:** tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, eggplants
- **Brassicas:** cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts, kohlrabi, turnips
- **Cucurbits:** squash, cucumbers, melons, pumpkins
- **Legumes:** beans, peas
- **Alliums:** onions, garlic, leeks
- **Root crops:** carrots, beets, parsnips (beets and Swiss chard are the same species)

### Specific Rotation Schemes

Solomon does not prescribe rigid rotation formulas, but several practical schemes emerge from his principles:

- **Three-year rotation**: Solanaceae followed by brassicas followed by legumes, with root crops and alliums fitting wherever they do not follow their own family. This is the minimum rotation that meaningfully disrupts pest and disease cycles
- **Four-year rotation with ley**: Three years of vegetable production followed by one year of grass/clover rest. The first year after breaking sod is ideal for heavy-feeding crops like potatoes, corn, or brassicas, as the decomposing sod provides abundant nutrients
- **Root crop sequence**: Carrots, beets, and parsnips do best in soil that was heavily manured or composted the previous year for a different crop. Fresh manure applied directly to root crops causes forking and hairy roots
- **Legume placement**: Peas and beans should precede nitrogen-hungry crops like brassicas or corn. The nitrogen fixed by legume rhizobia becomes available to the following crop through soil food web mineralization

### Interplanting Examples

While Solomon generally advocates wider spacing rather than intensive interplanting, he does suggest certain compatible combinations:

- **Corn, beans, and squash** (the traditional Three Sisters): Corn provides support for climbing beans, beans fix nitrogen, and squash shades the soil. However, Solomon notes this combination requires adequate space and may not produce maximum yields of any single crop
- **Lettuce between slower crops**: Fast-maturing lettuce can be planted between brassicas or tomatoes, harvested before the larger crop needs the full space
- **Radishes with parsnips**: Radishes germinate quickly and mark the row; they are harvested in 3-4 weeks, well before parsnips need the space

### Seasonal Calendar

Solomon's approach follows the natural seasons rather than forcing production year-round:

- **Early spring** (as soon as soil can be worked): Peas, radishes, spinach, onion sets, potatoes. These crops tolerate cool soil and light frosts
- **Late spring**: Transplant tomatoes, peppers, eggplant. Direct sow beans, corn, squash, cucumbers after last frost. Soil should be at least 60 deg F for warm-season crops
- **Midsummer**: Succession plantings of beans, carrots, beets. Start fall brassica transplants in a shaded seedbed
- **Late summer/early fall**: Transplant fall brassicas, plant garlic for next year, sow cover crops on beds being put to rest
- **Autumn**: Harvest root crops for storage. Let remaining crop residue stand through winter to protect soil surface and provide habitat for beneficial insects

## Planning for Succession

Solomon advises against the intensive-gardening approach of constant succession planting. Instead, plan for each crop to have adequate space and a full season. Plant twice as much as you think you'll need -- some years will be difficult, and having a surplus buffer is wiser than planting exactly what you need and hoping everything goes perfectly.

## The British Allotment Model

Solomon cites the British wartime allotment system as a proven model. By law, each plot had to be at least 300 square yards (2,700 square feet). During hard times from 1930 to the early 1950s, this amount of land made all the difference between health and sickness, between having enough to eat and low-grade hunger.
