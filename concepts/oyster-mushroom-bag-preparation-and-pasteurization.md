# Oyster Mushroom Bag Preparation and Pasteurization

Bag cultivation is the most widely practiced method for oyster mushroom (Pleurotus spp.) production worldwide, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. The Mushroom Growers' Handbook 1: *Oyster Mushroom Cultivation* (MushWorld, 2004) provides detailed technical guidance on substrate bag preparation and pasteurization methods ranging from low-cost country techniques to commercial steam systems.

## Advantages of Bag Cultivation

Bag cultivation has become dominant for several reasons:

- **No composting required**: Oyster mushrooms are primary decomposers that grow on raw lignocellulosic materials without composting, unlike button or straw mushrooms
- **Low capital investment**: Requires minimal infrastructure compared to shelf or tray systems
- **Space efficiency**: Bags stack vertically, making efficient use of limited space
- **Contamination control**: Each bag is an isolated unit; contamination in one does not spread to others
- **Portability**: Bags can be moved between rooms for different growing phases
- **Scalability**: Production ranges from a few dozen bags for home use to thousands for commercial operations

## Substrate Selection

Oyster mushrooms grow on a wide range of agricultural waste materials. Common substrate materials include:

- **Cereal straw**: Wheat straw, rice straw (paddy straw), barley straw
- **Sawdust**: From rubber tree, coconut lumber, and other timber species
- **Sunflower seed hulls**: Available in regions with sunflower oil production
- **Grass**: Various grass species including elephant grass
- **Cottonseed hulls**: Byproduct of cotton processing
- **Sugarcane bagasse**: Fibrous residue from sugar cane crushing
- **Groundnut shells**: Peanut shells from oil production
- **Corncobs**: Abundant in maize-growing regions

### Nutritional Requirements

Mushroom mycelia secrete digestive enzymes into the substrate and absorb simpler molecules. Requirements include:

- **Carbon source**: Cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin from plant materials
- **Nitrogen source**: Usually supplemented through rice bran at approximately 20% of substrate volume
- **Minerals**: Added through gypsum, lime, calcium sulfate, or magnesium sulfate
- **Moisture**: Adjusted to 60-65% for optimal mycelial growth
- **pH**: Optimal range of 6 to 8, depending on species

### The Squeeze Test

A practical rule of thumb for assessing substrate moisture: squeeze a handful firmly in the palm. When one or two drops of water barely escape between the fingers, the mixture has proper water content. Too dry slows mycelial growth; too wet promotes bacterial contamination.

## Substrate Mixing and Bag Filling

### Standard Formula

The standard formula documented for Thai oyster mushroom cultivation is:

- Sawdust (rubber tree) as base material
- Rice bran at 20% of sawdust volume
- Additives: gypsum, lime, calcium sulfate, or magnesium sulfate as needed
- Water to achieve 60-65% moisture content

Large-scale operations use mechanical ribbon mixers; small-scale growers mix by hand on clean concrete floors or in large tubs.

### Bag Filling and Compacting

Substrate is loaded into polypropylene bags and compacted firmly but not excessively. Over-compaction restricts air exchange and slows colonization; under-compaction creates air pockets where contamination develops. Space is left at the top for the neck and plug.

### The Bottle Neck Method

A widely used technique for bag closure:

1. Place a plastic ring on the open end of the filled bag
2. Pull the bag end through the ring
3. Fold down the protruding part over the outside of the ring
4. Secure with a rubber band
5. Plug the neck with cotton, paper, or a cotton-topped plastic plug

This creates a narrow, easily sealable opening facilitating inoculation while maintaining a clean air filter.

## Pasteurization Methods

Pasteurization eliminates or suppresses competing microorganisms, creating favorable conditions for introduced mushroom mycelium. Three main approaches are documented:

### Country-Type Pasteurization (Oil Drum)

The most common low-cost setup uses a modified 200-liter oil drum:

- A metal grate or rack is positioned above the water line
- Substrate bags are placed on the grate, above boiling water
- The drum is covered and heated over a fire or gas burner
- Steam from boiling water pasteurizes the bags at 90-100 degrees Celsius for 3-4 hours
- The grate prevents bags from sitting in water, which would waterlog the substrate

This method is widely used in developing countries where autoclaves are unavailable. It is effective for oyster mushroom substrates, which do not require complete sterilization.

### Commercial Pasteurization

Commercial operations use purpose-built chambers with steam boilers:

- Continuous steam at controlled pressure
- Chambers holding large numbers of bags simultaneously
- Temperature precisely controlled, typically 100 degrees Celsius for 8-12 hours
- Forced ventilation ensures uniform heat distribution
- Automated monitoring and recording of temperature profiles

Commercial systems offer superior consistency and throughput but require significant capital investment.

### Solar Heating Pasteurization

Solar pasteurization uses passive solar energy:

- Substrate bags loaded inside a solar collector
- Transparent or insulated enclosure traps heat
- Temperatures can reach pasteurization levels in hot climates
- No fuel costs, but dependent on weather conditions
- Most suitable for tropical regions with consistent sunlight

Solar pasteurization is the lowest-cost approach but least controllable and reliable.

## Cooling and Inoculation

After pasteurization, bags must cool to approximately 25 degrees Celsius before inoculation. Cooling should occur in a clean, draft-free area. Bags should not be opened during cooling.

For inoculation, work in a clean environment, flame-sterilize all tools, open the bag neck briefly, introduce spawn (typically a handful of colonized grain per bag), and reseal immediately. Gently shake the bag to distribute spawn through the substrate.

## Species-Specific Considerations

Different Pleurotus species have varying substrate preferences and temperature ranges:

- **Pleurotus ostreatus**: Paddy straw, paddy husk, wheat bran; grows at 12-25 degrees Celsius
- **Pleurotus sajor-caju**: Paddy straw, bagasse, wheat bran; grows at 22-35 degrees Celsius
- **Pleurotus florida**: Wheat straw, paddy straw; grows at 20-32 degrees Celsius

## Spent Substrate Recycling

After harvest, spent substrate retains significant nutritional value. Studies show increased crude protein content compared with raw straw. It can be used as animal feed for poultry and ruminants, as compost for field application, or as substrate for growing other mushroom species. Converting agricultural waste into mushrooms, then into feed or soil amendment, represents a zero-waste approach that is a primary justification for promoting oyster mushroom cultivation in developing countries.
