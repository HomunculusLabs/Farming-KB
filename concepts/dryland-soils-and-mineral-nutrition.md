# Dryland Soils and Mineral Nutrition

Dryland soils present some of the most challenging conditions for plant growth and agricultural productivity. In arid and semi-arid regions, the interplay of low rainfall, high evaporation, and geological history produces soils that are dominantly alkaline, often saline, and frequently deficient in essential trace elements. Understanding these soil dynamics is critical for any permaculture design operating in dryland environments, as mineral nutrition underpins every living system on the site.

## Alkaline Desert Soils

Desert soils are dominantly alkaline, typically registering pH values between **8.5 and 9.0**. This elevated alkalinity is driven by the accumulation of calcium carbonate and other basic minerals that remain in the soil profile as water evaporates rather than leaching through. In extreme cases, such as drying waterholes and evaporating drainage lines, pH can climb to **10–11**, creating conditions that are effectively toxic to most plant life.

High pH has profound consequences for nutrient availability. While macronutrients like calcium and magnesium may be abundant, the solubility of many essential micronutrients drops dramatically as pH rises.

This creates a paradoxical situation where the soil contains adequate total mineral content but plants still exhibit deficiency symptoms because those minerals are chemically locked in unavailable forms. The challenge for the permaculture designer is not merely identifying what is missing but understanding what is present yet inaccessible. Soil tests may show sufficient iron or zinc levels, yet plants continue to display classic deficiency symptoms because the high pH prevents root uptake.

## Trace Element Deficiencies at High pH

The most commonly affected trace elements in alkaline dryland soils are **zinc, iron, copper, and manganese**. Each plays a critical role in plant metabolism, and deficiency produces characteristic symptoms diagnosable through careful observation.

### Iron and Zinc Deficiency

Iron deficiency manifests as a progressive loss of green colour between the leaf veins — a condition known as **interveinal chlorosis**. As the deficiency worsens, the entire leaf may turn pale yellow while the veins remain dark green. In citrus trees, this is one of the most commonly observed nutritional problems in alkaline soils. Zinc deficiency produces similar interveinal colour loss but is also associated with **leaf thinning** — the leaves become noticeably smaller, narrower, and more fragile than healthy specimens. Both deficiencies are often present simultaneously, compounding their visual effects and making diagnosis more difficult without soil or tissue testing.

### Copper Deficiency

Copper deficiency produces some of the most visually dramatic symptoms. Affected plants may develop **giant leaves** that are oversized yet structurally weak, and woody species like citrus can develop **gum pockets** — accumulations of resinous gum in the bark and cambium layer. These gum pockets indicate that the plant's metabolic processes have been severely disrupted, affecting lignin formation and structural integrity. Without adequate copper, cell walls fail to lignify properly, leaving the plant vulnerable to wind damage, fungal infection, and mechanical failure.

### Manganese Deficiency

Manganese deficiency also causes interveinal chlorosis but tends to produce more spotted or mottled patterns on the leaf surface rather than the uniform fading seen with iron deficiency. In severe cases, **leaf tips may curl or roll inward**, another symptom commonly observed in citrus growing on alkaline dryland soils. Manganese plays a central role in photosynthesis as part of the water-splitting complex, so deficiency directly reduces the plant's energy production capacity.

## Remedies for Trace Element Deficiencies

Two primary strategies exist for addressing trace element deficiencies in alkaline soils: **foliar sprays** and **soil amendment with elemental sulphur**.

### Foliar Sprays

Foliar application bypasses the soil chemistry problem entirely by delivering dissolved minerals directly to the leaf surface where they can be absorbed through the stomata and cuticle. This is often the fastest and most effective short-term remedy, particularly for acute deficiency symptoms in established trees. Chelated forms of iron, zinc, and manganese are preferred for foliar application because they remain soluble and available for absorption. Applications are best made in the early morning or late evening to maximise absorption and minimise leaf burn.

### Elemental Sulphur

Elemental sulphur applied to the soil acts as a long-term corrective measure. Soil bacteria oxidise elemental sulphur to sulphuric acid, which gradually lowers the soil pH and thereby increases the solubility of the locked trace elements. This is a slower process than foliar spraying but addresses the root cause of the deficiency. The rate of pH change depends on soil temperature, moisture, and microbial activity — all of which can be limiting factors in dryland environments, so sulphur application is most effective when combined with irrigation or during periods of reliable rainfall.

In practice, sulphur and foliar sprays work best in combination. Foliar sprays provide immediate relief while the sulphur takes effect over weeks to months. For established orchards and tree systems, this dual approach prevents yield loss in the current season while building toward a longer-term solution.

## Non-Wetting Sands

Many dryland regions feature sandy soils that exhibit a peculiar behaviour: they **repel water** rather than absorbing it. This non-wetting characteristic is caused by **fungal activity** — specifically, hydrophobic organic coatings deposited by soil fungi on individual sand grains. When water is applied to these soils, it beads on the surface and runs off into low points rather than infiltrating the root zone, rendering irrigation ineffective and deep-rooted plants entirely dependent on sporadic rainfall.

Several permaculture strategies address non-wetting sands:

- **Bentonite clay** — When incorporated into sandy soil at rates of 5–10 kg per square metre, bentonite dramatically improves water retention and eliminates the non-wetting behaviour by disrupting the hydrophobic coatings on sand grains.
- **Humus addition** — Adding compost, aged manure, or other organic matter introduces surfactants and improves soil structure, breaking up the water-repellent layer.
- **Swales** — On-contour earthworks capture runoff and hold water on the surface long enough for infiltration to occur, even in non-wetting soils.
- **Raised beds** — Concentrating organic matter and clay amendments in raised beds creates zones of improved soil structure within the broader sandy landscape, allowing intensive production even when the surrounding soil remains hydrophobic. Raised beds also warm faster in spring and allow more precise control of soil amendments.

## Salt Problems

Salinity is one of the most serious constraints on dryland agriculture. As water evaporates from the soil surface, dissolved salts are drawn upward and concentrated in the root zone. Over time, salt accumulation can reach levels that inhibit or prevent plant growth entirely, rendering fertile land barren and forcing abandonment of established agricultural areas. Salinisation is often accelerated by inappropriate irrigation practices — applying water that is itself slightly saline, or irrigating without provision for adequate drainage, causes salts to accumulate progressively with each watering cycle.

### Strategies for Managing Salinity

- **Flushing** — Applying large volumes of low-salt irrigation water to leach accumulated salts below the root zone. This requires adequate drainage to prevent waterlogging and is most effective on free-draining soils with sub-surface drainage infrastructure.
- **Humus** — Organic matter improves soil structure, enhances drainage, and provides a buffer against salt stress by improving the soil's cation exchange capacity and supporting microbial communities that help buffer root zones.
- **Algae ponds** — Constructed algae ponds can be used to biologically desalinate water. Algae absorb salts and can be harvested, producing both fresh water and a nutrient-rich biomass suitable for composting or animal feed.
- **Distillation** — Solar stills and other distillation systems can produce fresh water from saline sources for sensitive crops and household use, providing a reliable supply even when groundwater is too saline for direct irrigation.
- **Salt-tolerant crops** — Selecting species adapted to saline conditions is essential. Many halophytic plants are valuable for forage, fuel, or soil stabilisation, and can be integrated into broader farm systems. Species such as saltbush (Atriplex), date palms, and certain grasses can thrive where conventional crops fail.
- **Free-draining sands** — Sandy soils with good drainage can tolerate salinity levels up to approximately **1500 ppm** without significant yield reduction, making them preferable to heavy clay soils in saline environments where leaching is impractical.

## Phosphorus Availability

Phosphorus is notoriously unavailable in both acidic and calcareous (alkaline) soils. In acid soils, phosphorus reacts with iron and aluminium to form insoluble compounds. In calcareous soils, it precipitates as calcium phosphate. In both cases, the phosphorus may be present in the soil in large quantities but remains locked in forms that plants cannot access. This dual inactivation makes phosphorus one of the most commonly limiting nutrients in dryland systems worldwide. Phosphorus fertiliser applied to calcareous soils can be almost entirely wasted, with as little as 10–20% of applied phosphorus becoming available to crops in the first season.

### Biological Pathways to Phosphorus Availability

The most effective strategy for improving phosphorus availability is biological rather than chemical:

- **Humus-rich soils** — The organic acids produced during decomposition help solubilise bound phosphorus, making it available for plant uptake over extended periods.
- **Forest systems** — The continuous cycling of organic matter through leaf fall, root turnover, and fungal decomposition maintains a steady supply of available phosphorus, making tree-based systems inherently more efficient at phosphorus cycling than annual crop monocultures.
- **Pond and wetland systems** — Aquatic ecosystems are exceptionally efficient at cycling phosphorus, making ponds and wetlands valuable components of dryland permaculture designs that serve dual roles as water storage and nutrient production systems.
- **Mycorrhizal inoculation** — Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots and can access phosphorus from soil pools that are otherwise unavailable to plants. Their hyphal networks extend far beyond the root zone, effectively multiplying the root's absorptive surface area by orders of magnitude. This is particularly important for establishing trees in phosphorus-poor dryland soils. Species that benefit strongly from mycorrhizal inoculation include **acacias, eucalypts, legumes, pines, and casuarinas** — all of which are key genera in dryland reforestation and rehabilitation projects. Inoculation is most effective when applied at the time of planting, either as a root dip or incorporated into the planting hole.

## Mineral Toxicities at Extreme pH

At the extremes of the pH spectrum, mineral toxicity becomes as significant a problem as deficiency. While dryland soils tend toward alkalinity, some sandy soils derived from acidic parent rock can reach low pH values, and the deliberate addition of sulphur or acidic organic matter can push soils into toxicity ranges if not monitored. Designers working in drylands must be alert to toxicity symptoms as well as deficiency signs:

- **Aluminium toxicity** — In strongly acidic soils (pH below 5.0), aluminium becomes soluble and damages root systems, stunting growth and reducing nutrient uptake capacity. Affected roots appear thickened and shortened.
- **Boron toxicity** — Can occur in both very alkaline and very acidic soils, causing leaf tip necrosis and reduced fruit set, particularly in sensitive fruit tree species.
- **Sodium toxicity** — A direct consequence of salinity, sodium displaces other cations on the exchange complex and disrupts osmotic regulation, leading to wilting even when soil moisture is adequate.
- **Manganese toxicity** — In acidic soils, manganese becomes excessively available and can cause necrotic spots on leaves and interfere with iron metabolism, creating cascading nutrient imbalances.

Monitoring soil pH regularly and observing plant responses to amendments is essential to avoid pushing soils from the corrective range into the toxic range.

## The Central Role of Humus

Humus is the single most important factor in buffering mineral uptake across all soil types and pH conditions. By providing a high cation exchange capacity, humus acts as a reservoir for essential nutrients, holding them in plant-available forms and releasing them gradually in response to root demand.

Humus also chelates (binds) trace elements, preventing them from becoming locked in unavailable compounds or, conversely, from reaching toxic concentrations. This chelation effect is particularly important in dryland soils where pH extremes are common and mineral imbalances are the norm rather than the exception.

In dryland soils specifically, humus performs the additional function of improving water-holding capacity, supporting the microbial communities that drive nutrient cycling, and moderating soil temperature fluctuations. Every permaculture strategy for dryland soil management — from swales and composting to mycorrhizal inoculation and organic mulching — ultimately serves to build and maintain the humus layer that underpins the entire mineral nutrition cycle. Building humus in drylands is slower than in humid climates, but the returns are proportionally greater because every percentage point of organic matter improvement yields disproportionately large gains in water and nutrient retention.

## Design Implications

For the permaculture designer working in drylands, mineral nutrition management is not an afterthought but a foundational design consideration. Soil pH testing, observation of plant deficiency symptoms, and strategic amendment with sulphur, organic matter, and biological inoculants should be integrated into the earliest phases of site assessment. Understanding the specific pH, salinity, and mineral profile of a site allows the designer to select appropriate species and soil management strategies from the outset, avoiding costly corrective interventions later.

The designer must work with the inherent limitations of alkaline, saline, and sandy soils while leveraging biological systems — forests, ponds, mycorrhizal networks, and organic matter cycling — to create productive and resilient landscapes. In dryland contexts, the most successful designs are those that use succession and biological processes to gradually transform degraded soils into productive growing media, rather than attempting to force immediate production through chemical inputs.
