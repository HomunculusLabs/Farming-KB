## Overview

Common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs), sometimes called "wood wide webs," are underground fungal networks that connect individual plants and trees, enabling resource sharing, chemical signaling, and information transfer between forest organisms. Research has revealed that these networks facilitate cooperative behaviors between trees, including the support of seedlings by mature "mother trees."

## Types of Mycorrhizal Networks

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Networks
- Formed by Glomeromycota fungi
- Associate with most herbaceous plants and tropical trees
- Hyphal connections are relatively short-range (cm scale)
- Cannot directly link individual tree root systems in most cases
- More important in grasslands and agricultural systems

### Ectomycorrhizal Networks
- Formed by Basidiomycota and Ascomycota fungi
- Associate primarily with temperate and boreal forest trees (pine, oak, spruce, birch, Douglas-fir)
- Produce extensive, long-range hyphal networks (meters to tens of meters)
- Form direct hyphal links (hartig net) between tree root tips
- Most research on forest communication involves EM networks

## Resource Sharing Through Mycorrhizal Networks

### Carbon Transfer
- Trees transfer photosynthetic carbon (sugars) through mycorrhizal networks
- Dominant "mother trees" (large, well-lit individuals) supply carbon to shaded seedlings
- Carbon flows from canopy trees to understory seedlings, especially in low-light conditions
- Transfer rates: 5-15% of total carbon fixed by donor plants can move through CMNs
- Douglas-fir seedlings receiving carbon from mature trees show 2-3x growth increase

### Nutrient Transfer
- Nitrogen moves from nitrogen-fixing plants to neighboring trees through CMNs
- Phosphorus transferred from phosphorus-rich microsites to deficient plants
- Water movement through fungal hyphae supports drought-stressed neighbors
- Nutrient sharing is bidirectional; plants contribute based on supply and demand

### The "Mother Tree" Concept
Suzanne Simard's pioneering research at the University of British Columbia:
- Large, old trees ("hub" or "mother" trees) serve as central nodes in forest networks
- These trees have the most mycorrhizal connections and transfer the most resources
- Mother trees preferentially supply their own offspring (kin selection)
- Kin recognition: trees can distinguish their own seedlings from unrelated seedlings
- Dying mother trees dump carbon into the network, supporting their seedlings
- Hub trees increase connectivity across the forest, supporting overall ecosystem resilience

## Chemical Signaling

### Defense Signaling
- Plants under insect or pathogen attack send chemical signals through CMNs
- Neighboring plants receive these signals and activate defense responses
- Volatile organic compounds and systemic signals transmitted through fungal hyphae
- Documented in: tomato (defense against aphids), bean (defense against Botrytis), ponderosa pine (defense against bark beetles)
- Primer effect: pre-exposed plants respond faster and stronger to subsequent attacks

### Allelopathic Signaling
- Some plants use CMNs to send inhibitory signals to competing species
- Black walnut (Juglans nigra) may transmit juglone through fungal networks
- Invasive plants may exploit CMNs to suppress native species
- Garlic mustard (Alliaria petiolata) disrupts native mycorrhizal networks

### Drought and Stress Signals
- Drought-stressed plants communicate water stress through CMNs
- Neighboring plants upregulate drought-response genes before experiencing stress
- Salinity stress signals transmitted through common networks
- Enables coordinated community-level responses to environmental stress

## Network Architecture and Ecology

### Network Topology
- Forest mycorrhizal networks follow scale-free topology (like the internet)
- A few hub trees (mother trees) have many connections
- Most trees have relatively few connections
- This architecture provides robustness: removing random nodes does not disconnect the network
- But removing hub trees (through logging) can fragment the network

### Fungal Specificity
- Individual fungal species connect specific tree species
- Different fungal taxa create parallel networks at different soil depths
- "Generalist" fungi connect across species; "specialist" fungi connect within species
- Network redundancy: multiple fungal species often connect the same tree pair
- Loss of key fungal species can disconnect network segments

### Succession and Network Development
- Pioneer fungal species (Rhizopogon, Laccaria) establish in disturbed sites
- Late-successional species (Cortinarius, Russula) dominate mature forests
- Network complexity increases with forest age
- Old-growth forests have the most extensive and interconnected networks

## Threats to Mycorrhizal Networks

### Forest Management
- Clear-cutting removes hub trees, fragmenting networks
- Even selective logging can damage critical network connections
- Network recovery takes decades after major disturbance
- Seedling regeneration in logged areas benefits from network preservation

### Climate Change
- Warming may shift fungal community composition
- Drought reduces mycorrhizal connectivity
- CO2 enrichment effects on networks are complex and context-dependent
- See [[fungi-in-climate-change]] for detailed climate impacts

### Soil Disturbance
- Compaction from heavy equipment destroys hyphal networks
- Tillage in agroforestry systems severs connections
- Soil erosion removes the organic horizon where most networks occur
- Chemical contamination (herbicides, fungicides) can damage networks

### Invasive Species
- Invasive plants may not form mycorrhizal associations, disrupting network continuity
- Some invasive fungi can replace native network-forming species
- Garlic mustard produces anti-mycorrhizal compounds that suppress native networks

## Implications for Forest Management

### Retention Forestry
- Preserve mother trees and network hubs during harvesting
- Variable retention harvesting maintains network connectivity
- Leave tree patches and corridors to sustain network function
- Buffer zones around old-growth reserves protect network integrity

### Reforestation
- Inoculate seedlings with mycorrhizal fungi matching local network species
- Retain nurse logs and organic matter that support network development
- Consider network connectivity in species selection and planting layout
- Allow time for network establishment before additional disturbance

### Monitoring
- DNA metabarcoding to map fungal network composition
- Stable isotope tracing to quantify carbon and nutrient transfer
- Long-term monitoring of network responses to management practices
- Research connecting network health to forest productivity and resilience

## Controversies and Open Questions

- The extent and ecological significance of resource transfer through CMNs is debated
- Some researchers argue that resource movement is small relative to total plant budgets
- Kin selection through CMNs is supported by some studies but not universally accepted
- The signaling function of CMNs is well-documented but field significance is debated
- Popular accounts (including Simand's "Finding the Mother Tree") have been criticized for over-stating the cooperation narrative
