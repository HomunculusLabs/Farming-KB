# Cover Crops and Soil Health

While Lowenfels and Lewis do not devote a standalone chapter to cover crops, the principles throughout *Teaming with Microbes* directly support the practice. Cover crops align with [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]] science by maintaining living roots in soil, feeding the web during fallow periods, and supporting [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]] as needed for subsequent crops.

## The succession principle

The book's [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]] explains why cover crops work. Early succession communities are bacterially dominated — weeds and annual grasses colonize bare soil first. As organic litter accumulates, fungal populations increase and more permanent plant communities establish. Cover crops let the gardener manage where on this continuum the soil food web sits.

Bare soil left fallow loses its soil food web. Without living roots producing exudates, [[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria-the-smallest-workers]] and [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-the-underground-network]] starve. [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] that depend on them decline. The nutrient cycling system breaks down. Cover crops prevent this collapse by maintaining the root-exudate pipeline that feeds the entire web.

## Green mulches as living cover

The book's mulch principles (Rule #6) apply directly to cover crops. Fresh, green organic materials support bacteria. When a cover crop of grasses or legumes is incorporated into soil, it acts as a green [[teaming-with-microbes-mulch-and-soil-food-web]] — providing simple sugars that feed bacterial populations and support the bacterially dominated soils preferred by most vegetables and annuals (Rule #2).

In contrast, allowing a cover crop to die in place on the surface (no-till termination) functions as a brown mulch, supporting fungal dominance. The gardener can choose the termination method to shift the soil food web in the desired direction.

## Nitrogen fixation and legumes

Legumes form symbiotic relationships with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (rhizobia) in root nodules. These bacteria convert atmospheric N2 into forms plants can use. The book notes that nitrogen-fixing bacteria generally require a [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-ph-and-biology]] for good populations — a consideration when selecting legume cover crops for acidic soils.

When legume cover crops are terminated and decomposed, the nitrogen fixed in their tissues becomes available to subsequent crops through the soil food web's [[teaming-with-microbes-mineralization-and-immobilization]] cycle. Bacteria and fungi decompose the legume residue; protozoa and nematodes consume the microbes and release plant-available nitrogen as ammonium in their wastes, deposited right in the rhizosphere.

## Root exudates and mycorrhizae

Living roots of cover crops continuously produce exudates that feed soil biology. For perennial or overwintering cover crops, this means the [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-the-underground-network]] is maintained rather than broken. The longer a root is alive, the longer and more branched the associated mycorrhizal fungi become (Rule #19).

This is especially important for [[teaming-with-microbes-tilling-damage-to-fungal-networks]], where maintaining mycorrhizal networks between cash crops is a primary goal. Cover crops serve as living bridges that keep mycorrhizal connections alive during fallow periods. Without living roots, mycorrhizal fungi have no exudate source and their networks collapse.

## Feeding the soil food web in autumn

The book recommends adding organics to garden soils in autumn so they have time to break down before spring planting. Alfalfa meal, straw, or grass clippings — all good bacterial food — can be combined with a winter cover crop. The bacteria get started in autumn and combine nitrogen with available carbon without interfering with plant needs. Any nitrogen tie-up at the soil-mulch interface, if it occurs at all, will be over by spring.

Lawn clippings are a terrific green mulch around annual flowers and vegetables during the growing season. Even though they lose their color and turn "brown," they are still considered "green" mulch because the sugars they contained when cut remain even after chlorophyll has faded. Straw has the same property.

## Cover crops and the no-till approach

The book's strong recommendation against tilling (Rule #18) makes cover crops particularly valuable. Since the gardener should not rototill, cover crops provide the organic matter and biological stimulation that tilling traditionally delivered — but without the damage to fungal networks and soil structure.

A no-till approach to cover crops:
- Terminate by crimping or mowing, leaving residue on the surface as brown mulch
- This supports fungal dominance and maintains soil structure
- Apply compost before planting to inoculate biology
- Use [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-tea-brewing-guide]] to boost microbial diversity
- Plant through the residue using a trowel, dowel, or dibble to make discrete holes

## Protozoa and nutrient cycling

The book emphasizes that having bacteria and fungi without [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] to cycle their nutrients is ineffective. Cover crops support all levels of the food web: roots feed bacteria and fungi, which attract protozoa and nematodes, which mineralize nutrients for the next crop. The entire web must be present and functioning.

Apply protozoa soup (fresh grass clippings or alfalfa soaked in dechlorinated water for 3-4 days with aeration) as a soil drench to help ensure nutrient cycling organisms are present.

## Practical cover crop considerations

Based on the book's principles:
- Choose bacterial-supporting cover crops (grasses, legumes) before nitrogen-preferring vegetables and annuals
- Choose fungal-supporting cover crops (broad-leaved species, allowing surface residue) before trees, shrubs, and perennials
- Avoid tilling cover crops in -- this destroys fungal hyphae and disrupts soil structure
- Apply [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-tea-brewing-guide]] to support nutrient cycling organisms
- Keep all fertilizer NPK numbers below 10 to protect fragile soil food web organisms
- Time autumn applications so decomposition occurs before spring planting needs

## Specific Cover Crop Species and Their Effects

Different cover crop species support distinct soil food web communities. Selecting the right species depends on what crop follows and what soil conditions need addressing:

- **Cereal rye (Secale cereale)**: Perhaps the most widely used winter cover crop. Its extensive fibrous root system supports bacterial communities and produces significant biomass that becomes brown mulch when terminated. Rye roots release allelopathic compounds that suppress weed germination, providing a natural weed-management benefit. It is extremely cold-hardy and can be planted late into autumn
- **Crimson clover (Trifolium incarnatum)**: A winter annual legume that fixes 100-150 lbs of nitrogen per acre under good conditions. Its flowers attract beneficial insects before termination. Crimson clover supports both bacterial communities (through root exudates) and mycorrhizal networks
- **Hairy vetch (Vicia villosa)**: A vigorous climbing legume that produces substantial biomass and fixes large amounts of nitrogen (up to 200 lbs/acre). Its viney growth habit makes it effective at suppressing weeds. When terminated no-till, the dense mat of residue provides extended surface mulch
- **Buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum)**: A warm-season broadleaf that grows rapidly in poor soils. It mines phosphorus from soil minerals and makes it available to subsequent crops through its root exudates. Buckwheat flowers attract pollinators and beneficial insects. It supports more fungal-dominant soil biology than grass cover crops
- **Oats (Avena sativa)**: A quick-growing grass that produces significant root mass in a short season. Winter-killed oats leave a dead mulch on the surface through winter, providing erosion protection and early spring planting without termination. The killed root channels create preferential water infiltration paths
- **Field peas (Pisum sativum subsp. arvense)**: A cool-season legume that fixes nitrogen effectively and can be planted in early spring or late summer. Their moderate root system supports mycorrhizal connections and bacterial communities

## Termination Methods and Their Soil Food Web Effects

How a cover crop is terminated significantly affects the soil biology it leaves behind:

- **Crimping/rolling**: A mechanical roller-crimper folds the cover crop stems flat, breaking them without cutting. The cover crop dies in place, creating a thick thatch of surface residue that supports fungal dominance. This is the preferred no-till method for winter annuals like rye and vetch
- **Mowing high**: Cutting the cover crop high (6+ inches) leaves stubble and residue on the surface, supporting fungal communities. Lower mowing incorporates more material into the soil surface, favoring bacteria
- **Tillage**: Incorporating cover crops by rototilling or disking destroys fungal hyphae and disrupts soil aggregates. The soil food web shifts toward bacterial dominance. While this speeds decomposition and nutrient release, it sacrifices the long-term soil structure benefits that cover crops could provide
- **Herbicide termination**: Glyphosate kills the cover crop without soil disturbance, leaving roots intact. The root exudates stop but the mycorrhizal connections from the cover crop roots persist temporarily. While effective for no-till systems, this approach conflicts with organic gardening principles

## Cash Crop Interactions with Cover Crops

The soil food web effects of cover crops directly influence the performance of subsequent cash crops:

- **Vegetables and annuals**: Most vegetables prefer bacterially dominated soils. Following a cover crop of grasses or legumes terminated by light incorporation creates favorable conditions. A 2-3 week decomposition period after termination allows nitrogen mineralization before planting
- **Tomatoes and peppers**: These solanaceous crops benefit from moderate mycorrhizal colonization. A no-till cover crop termination that preserves fungal networks gives them a head start. Avoid cover crops in the Solanaceae family (such as potato) before these crops to prevent disease carryover
- **Brassicas**: Brassicas are non-mycorrhizal -- they do not form mycorrhizal associations and are not supported by fungal networks. Bacterially dominated soils from grass cover crops are ideal. Mustard cover crops release biofumigant compounds (glucosinolates) when incorporated, which can suppress soil-borne pathogens like Fusarium and Rhizoctonia
- **Perennials and fruit trees**: These crops benefit most from fungal dominance. Broadleaf cover crops terminated no-till support the transition to fungal soils. Long-lived perennials benefit from the extended mycorrhizal networks maintained by living mulch systems between rows
