## The Wasson Expedition of 1955

### The Discovery

On June 29-30, 1955, R. Gordon Wasson and his wife Valentina Pavlovna became the first known outsiders to participate in a Mazatec velada. The ceremony was conducted by Maria Sabina in her home in Huautla de Jimenez, a remote village in the Sierra Mazateca of Oaxaca state.

### The Mushrooms Used

Maria Sabina used species from the genus [[psilocybe-mexicana-cultivation]] in her ceremonies. The Mazatec call the mushrooms "nti xi tho" ("little things that spring forth") or, more commonly, they use the Nahuatl-derived term "di xito" which is equivalent to the Aztec "teonanacatl" -- "flesh of the gods."

### Wasson's Description

Wasson's account of the ceremony, published in "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" in Life Magazine (May 13, 1957), described Maria Sabina's chanting, the ritual consumption of mushrooms, and the visionary experiences that followed. The article brought the existence of Mesoamerican mushroom ceremonies to worldwide attention.

## The Velada Ceremony

### Structure

The Mazatec velada is an all-night ceremony with a specific structure:

1. **Preparation:** The participants gather in the curandera's home; candles are lit
2. **Invocation:** Maria Sabina begins chanting and praying, calling upon the spirits of the mushrooms
3. **Consumption:** Mushrooms are distributed to participants, typically in pairs
4. **The journey:** The effects begin 30-60 minutes after consumption; participants lie down on mats
5. **Chanting continues:** Maria Sabina chants throughout the night, guiding the experience
6. **Dawn:** The ceremony concludes as daylight returns; participants share their experiences

### Maria Sabina's Chanting

Maria Sabina's chants (called "alabanzas" -- praises) are a central element of the ceremony:

- Performed in the Mazatec language
- Addressed to the "saints" or "spirits" of the mushrooms
- Described as the mushrooms "speaking through" the curandera
- Musical, rhythmic, and hypnotic in quality
- Serve to structure and guide the participants' experiences

### The Role of the Curandera

The curandera serves multiple roles in the velada:

- **Intermediary:** Communicates between participants and mushroom spirits
- **Guide:** Structures and directs the experience through chanting
- **Healer:** Diagnoses and treats illnesses revealed during the ceremony
- **Protector:** Ensures physical and psychological safety of participants
- **Teacher:** Transmits cultural knowledge about the mushrooms and their proper use

## Maria Sabina's Background

### Training

Maria Sabina learned the use of psychoactive mushrooms from her family:

- Her uncle (a curandero) introduced her to the mushrooms as a child
- She received her calling to become a curandera through mushroom experiences
- She practiced for decades before Wasson's arrival
- She was considered one of the most skilled curanderas in the region

### Reputation

Within the Mazatec community, Maria Sabina was renowned for:

- The power and clarity of her visions
- The beauty and efficacy of her chanting
- Her ability to diagnose and heal illness
- Her deep knowledge of the mushroom spirits
- Her integrity and ethical conduct

## The Aftermath of Wasson's Visit

### International Attention

Wasson's Life Magazine article made Maria Sabina internationally famous:

- Her name and photograph were published worldwide
- Huautla de Jimenez became a destination for psychedelic tourists
- Scientists (including gordon-wasson) visited to study the mushrooms
- Counter-culture figures (including Bob Dylan, John Lennon) reportedly visited

### The Invasion

The influx of outsiders had devastating consequences:

- The mushroom ceremonies were disrupted by tourism
- Maria Sabina was overwhelmed by visitors demanding ceremonies
- The commercialization of the sacred tradition caused social problems
- The Mazatec community was divided over how to respond
- Maria Sabina was ostracized by some community members

### Maria Sabina's Regret

Maria Sabina reportedly expressed regret that she had allowed Wasson to participate:

"From the moment the foreigners arrived, the 'holy children' [the mushrooms] lost their purity. They lost their force; they ruined them. Henceforth, they will no longer work. There is no remedy for it."

This poignant statement reflects the destruction of a sacred tradition through its exposure to the outside world.

### Repression

The Mexican authorities eventually cracked down on mushroom use:

- Maria Sabina was briefly imprisoned
- Her house was burned by unknown arsonists
- She spent her final years in poverty and ostracism
- She died on November 22, 1985

## The Scientific Legacy

### Hofmann's Visit

Albert Hofmann visited Huautla de Jimenez in 1957 and was hosted by Maria Sabina. He collected specimens of the mushrooms used in the ceremonies, which led to the identification of the active compounds [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-psilocybin-and-psilocin]].

### The Naming

Hofmann named the new compounds after the Mazatec term for the mushrooms. "Psilocybin" derives from the Greek "psilos" (bare) and "kubē" (head), combined with "-in" -- a reference to the mushroom's bare head, though some sources connect the name to the Mazatec terminology.

### Specimen Types

The type specimens of Psilocybe mexicana and several other psychoactive Psilocybe species were collected during the Wasson expeditions to Huautla de Jimenez.

Maria Sabina's story raises profound ethical questions:

- What responsibilities do researchers have to their informants?
