# Keyline Water Harvesting and [[dam-construction]]

The humid landscape is water controlled, and unless it is an extremely
new landscape, volcanic or newly faulted, it has softly rounded outlines.
When you are walking up the valley, or walking on the ridge, observe that
there is a rounded S-shaped profile to the hills. Where the landscape
turns from convex to concave occurs a critical point that we call a
keypoint.

## Locating the Keypoint

The main valley is the main flow, with many little creeks entering. At
the valley head where these creeks start, we locate the major keypoint.
From there on, the keyline starts to fall from one in 1,000 to one in
2,000 below contour. The dams we make in the lower valleys will be
slightly lower at each point. They will not be at the keypoint.

Rain falling on the hilltop runs off. The paths described by single
raindrops, wherever they fall, are similar in that they cross contours at
right angles, because that is the shortest drop between two contours.
Water takes the shortest path across the landscape from where it falls to
where it hits the river line. It is along this path that raindrops are
doing their thing. As soon as they are in the river valley, they are off
to the sea.

## The Keyline System

Having found the keypoint, we can now treat the whole landscape as if it
were a roof and a tank. In a fairly descending line, falling gently away
from the horizontal, we put in a groove around the hill. This is the
highest point at which we can work with mechanical tools. Above that, it
is too steep. We make a little shelf around the hill leading to the
keypoint.

No matter where this water was going, we have now started to divert it,
bringing it right around the hill to the keypoint. In effect, we have put
a gutter around our roof, a very gently falling gutter. We started at the
keypoint and extended a line that we lifted one foot at every 2,000 feet.
We want to create a very, very gentle fall. Water just moves along it,
and that is all. We have directed the water to our keypoint.

## Keypoint Dams

At the keypoint, we put a little dam; for it is the highest point in the
profile of the valley that we can economically store water. It is a rather
deep little dam, and we need a fair amount of earth to build it. It is
not the most economical dam we will have, but it gathers all the water
from the top of the hill to that point.

We can make that keypoint dam as large as we can afford. It will enable
us at any time of the year to run water right around this contour and let
it fall on any area that we want. We lead the water out through the wall
of the dam, either by siphon or a lock-pipe, allowing it to enter a
contour drain. We control the flow in the drain by a sheet of canvas or
plastic, fastening it like a flag to a very light plastic pipe.

## Irrigation Without Pumps

One person can water hundreds of acres this way with no effort at all. It
is very light work. No pumps. For very large dams, holding five or six
million gallons, you merely put a sliding gate or lock-pipe in the dam
wall, generally about 18 inches square. This water will flow out about as
fast as you can walk, walking fairly slowly.

The most restful way to irrigate a large area in this way is to have two
people and two flags. We peg here, and our friend goes 100 feet ahead and
pegs. When we have soaked our part of the field, we just pull our flag,
and our water flows on to his flag. This flag system allows precise
control of water distribution across large areas.

## Saddle Dams and Ridge Storage

Making walls on either side of the saddle, we can get very large and very
high water storages. These are the highest water storages you can get on
any property. These are real power storages. Let us consider what these
storages would be useful for. They are marvelous places for your house
water supply. It might be possible to generate electricity with them.

When it rains heavily, these high storages fill first. So we have buffered
the erosion by taking the first shock of water. After that, these dams
continue to give to the water table as the water table dries out, so they
are moderating systems.

## Contour Dams

For contour dams, we choose the least sloping site. We build an earth
wall, and we run our diversion drains as usual. These contour dams can
perch on the knoll of a hill, where it dwindles out. The contour dam is a
shallow dam with a large surface area. It will be a very, very cheap dam.
For the amount of earth moved, we are going to get a lot more water.

## Integration with Other Systems

Once you set the water systems, you also have set a lot of other systems.
Wherever possible, your fencing and your access roads naturally follow
your water systems, and can be well integrated. Both assist the water
systems. Your forests follow. Your forests grow above those channels.
They are themselves very water-conserving and ensure steady water-flow
systems. Animals walking along fences keyed to contour become beneficial
to runoff rather than destructive.

## Climate Considerations

[[keyline-design]] works in both humid and dry landscapes but the emphasis
shifts. In humid landscapes the focus is on preventing waterlogging and
erosion by guiding excess water safely off the land while storing surplus
in high dams. In arid landscapes every drop is precious and the emphasis
is on maximum infiltration and storage, with careful attention to
evaporation losses from exposed water surfaces.
