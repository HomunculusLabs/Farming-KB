# Pioneer Species in Permaculture

> Source: Bill Mollison, *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (1988), Chapters 3 and 10

Updated: 2026-04-18

## Role in Succession

Nature shows us that a sequence of processes arise in the establishment of "new" systems on devastated landscapes such as basalt flows, ice-planed or flood-swept sites. The first living components are hardy pioneer species, which establish on these damaged or impoverished environments.

Thus we see "weeds" (thistles, Lantana) occupying overgrazed, eroded, or fired areas. These pioneer species assist the area by stabilising water flow in the landscape, and later they give shelter, provide mulch, or improve soil quality for their successors (the longer-term forest or tree crop species).

## Succession Planning in Design

To enable a cultivated system to evolve towards a long-term stable state, we can construct a system of mixed tree, shrub, and vegetable crop, utilising livestock to act as foragers, and carefully planning the succession of plants and animals so that we receive short-, medium-, and long-term benefits.

Unlike natural processes, we can place most of the elements of a succession in one planting, so that the pioneers, ground covers, understory species, tree legumes, herbage crop, mulch species, the long-term windbreak and the tree crop are all set out at once.

## Key Pioneer Species by Region

### Tropical and Subtropical
- **Acacia species** (A. mearnsii, A. albida): nitrogen-fixing, rapid growth, mulch production
- **Leucaena leucocephala**: fast-growing legume, forage and mulch
- **Inga edulis**: shade and mulch for understory
- **Casuarina equisetifolia**: coastal pioneer, windbreak, nitrogen fixer
- **Eucalyptus**: rapid canopy establishment
- **Scalesia pedunculata**: volcanic island pioneer
- **Prosopis pallida**: arid zone pioneer
- **Glyricidia**: nitrogen-fixing, green mulch
- **Tephrosia**: high leaf nitrogen yield (1,000-1,500 kg/ha/year)
- **Sea grape (Coccolobus unifera)**: coastal stabiliser
- **Coastal shrubs**: Scaevola, Tornefortia, Pemphis
- **Mangroves**: coastal edge species
- **Barringtonia**: atoll pioneer
- **Vines**: Chayote for rapid canopy coverage
- **Ground legumes**: Dolichos, Desmodium

### Cool Temperate
- **Gorse**: acts as "rough mulch" to protect fruit and forage trees; reduces weed species; tree crops shade out gorse in 2-3 years
- **Acacia**: used under rainforest establishment; after 5-6 years gorse has rotted and rainforest establishes under acacia
- **Tagasaste**: fast-growing forage and shelter in cool climates, yields up to 7m of mulch trimmings per annum
- **Pampas grass**: fast shelter for lambing ewes, preferred forage, propagated by divisions
- **Bamboo**: rapid establishment, soil stabilisation

### Arid and Semi-Arid
- **Pampas, bamboo, lemongrass**: root-mat pioneers on steep slopes
- **Chestnut, Acacia, carob, olive**: upslope pioneer plantings that cascade seed downslope
- **Fukuoka method**: Uses radish and Acacia as biological "chisel ploughs"
- **African method**: Acacia albida or Glyricidia
- **New Guinea method**: Casuarina
- **Mediterranean method**: Tamarix

## Biological "Chisel Ploughs"

Fukuoka uses radish and Acacia; Africans use Acacia albida or Glyricidia; New Guineans use Casuarina; and Mediterranean farmers use Tamarix for biological "chisel ploughs" where land is too steep and stony for implements.

## Pioneering on Difficult Terrain

On man-made and natural landslide or volcanic areas of the tropics:
1. Pocket the area with soil-mulch mixtures (nut husks from coconut and macadamia are excellent)
2. Plant species such as Inga edulis, Leucaena, various Acacias, Scalesia, Prosopis, and legumes (Dolichos, Desmodium)
3. These prepare the area for palms, figs, and useful fruit trees by providing shelter and mulch

It is better to plant small assemblies than to space out a lot of species on their own.

## Scattered Forest Nuclei

Forestry proceeds more surely as a scattered set of pioneer tree and herbaceous nuclei -- the steady establishment of CLUMPED pioneer trees in open grassland. This is a "natural" process which many native peoples replicate by evolving scattered forest nuclei based on a set of pioneer trees, termite mounds, compost heaps.

## Secondary Forest Growth (Lantana Control)

The essential process for reclaiming Lantana-dominated land:
1. Roll down, crush, or cut out contour strips
2. Plant advanced, vigorous mixes of Acacia, Eucalyptus, vines (chayote), ground legumes, and local pioneer species
3. Manure each plant and mark small plants
4. Slash every few weeks in wet season until trees are above the Lantana canopy
5. Free any new natural tree seedlings
6. Final slash at about 18 months
7. Cut or roll adjacent strips to extend the system
8. Use early pioneers (Acacia) as mulch for selected high-value species

Shading-out of Lantana takes 2-6 years, and only remnant weak shoots remain under productive forest.
