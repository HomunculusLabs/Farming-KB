# Winter Harvesting Logistics and Workflow

## The Harvest Schedule

[[eliot-coleman]]'s winter harvest operation runs on a strict twice-weekly schedule:
Mondays and Thursdays. This rhythm provides customers with fresh produce at
regular intervals, gives greenhouse crops adequate recovery time between
harvests, and structures the farm crew's work week around a predictable cadence.
Consistency is essential because winter customers build their menus and
purchasing patterns around reliable delivery. Coleman treats the schedule as
inviolable, adjusting other farm tasks around it rather than the reverse.

## Waiting for the Thaw and the Harvest Window

Winter harvesting cannot begin until the [[coleman-cool-greenhouse-vs-cold-greenhouse-temperature-comparison]] rises above
32°F. Harvesting frozen plants causes cellular damage as ice crystals rupture
cell walls, producing mushy unmarketable tissue upon thawing. Coleman waits
for the greenhouse to thaw naturally, meaning harvest start time shifts with
weather — 9AM on mild mornings, potentially noon or later after a hard freeze.

Even after thawing, the harvest window is constrained by the need to finish
before temperatures drop again in late afternoon. Coleman identifies 10AM to
3PM as the reliable working window. Within this five-hour period the crew must
cut all scheduled crops, transport them to the wash/pack area, and begin
cleaning. Every aspect of the harvest must be streamlined within this narrow
temporal window.

## The Cool Greenhouse Advantage

A counterintuitive benefit of winter harvesting from minimally heated
greenhouses is the quality advantage that cool temperatures confer. Vegetables
cut at 35-40°F are already at ideal storage temperature when they leave the
greenhouse, eliminating the need for expensive post-harvest cooling equipment.
Cool harvest temperatures also preserve texture and flavor: [[coleman-aphid-management-winter-greenhouse-nitrogen-flushing]]
greens cut at near-freezing temperatures are crisp, sweet, and exceptionally
long-lasting. Customers consistently report that winter-harvested greens taste
better and keep longer than warm-season counterparts.

## The Propane Salamander Heater

Coleman keeps a propane salamander heater — a forced-air construction-site
heater — for extreme cold events. He estimates using it approximately once per
winter, during the most severe cold snaps when greenhouse temperatures remain
below freezing even at midday. The heater is used strictly as an emergency thaw
tool, not to maintain growing temperatures. Firing it up brings the greenhouse
above 32°F so the scheduled harvest can proceed. Fuel cost is negligible given
infrequent use, and the heater pays for itself in preserved harvest days.

## Personal Gear and Row Cover Management

Coleman advocates dressing in layers: a vest rather than a parka, because
physical labor generates significant body heat. A heavy coat causes sweating
followed by chilling during less active periods. Key equipment includes
Victorinox 7-inch knives for sharpness, durability, and comfortable grip.
Five-gallon food-grade buckets serve as standard harvest containers. Neoprene
gloves provide insulation while maintaining dexterity.

Inner row covers — lightweight spun-bond fabric laid directly over crops — must
be removed for harvesting and replaced immediately. In subfreezing conditions
removed covers freeze to themselves within minutes, making them impossible to
unfold without damage. Coleman's protocol is sequential: remove a section, cut
the exposed bed, replace the cover before moving to the next section.

## Washing and Packing Area Protocol

The wash/pack area is the post-harvest nerve center. Workers wear hairnets at
all times. The farm's well water is tested regularly to ensure potable
standards. Hot water scrubs all food contact surfaces between harvest days.
Salad ingredients are washed separately rather than mixed before washing,
preventing cross-contamination of flavors and allowing each component to be
handled according to its specific needs.

## The "Pac Man" Salad Mixer

The centerpiece of Coleman's wash/pack operation is a custom-built salad mixer
known as the "Pac Man." This device consists of a barrel measuring 48 by 36
inches, mounted on a rotating frame. Greens are loaded along with a small
amount of water, and the barrel is rotated 10 revolutions in one direction,
then 10 revolutions in the opposite direction. This tumbling action gently
washes and dries greens without bruising. The Pac Man holds approximately 80
pounds of salad mix per batch, making it suitable for commercial-scale
production. Coleman considers it one of the most important labor-saving
devices on the farm.

## Sink Efficiency and Crew Coordination

Over years of operation Coleman has iterated on wash station design: larger
drain pipes prevent clogging, higher water pressure reduces scrubbing time,
and linear layout of sinks, drying racks, and packing stations eliminates
backtracking. These improvements compound — a wash station that handles greens
20% faster means the crew finishes earlier on cold days when comfort matters.

Coleman emphasizes minimizing hand travel, borrowing from industrial
ergonomics. Cutting tools, harvest containers, and packing supplies are
positioned within easy arm's reach. Experienced crews develop a choreographed
rhythm: one person cuts while another carries buckets, a third manages row
covers, and a fourth begins washing as soon as the first greens arrive.

## Mechanized Harvesting: The Quality vs. Speed Balance

Coleman has explored mechanized options including the Greens Harvester from
Johnny's [[johnnys-selected-seeds]], a hand-pushed device using a reciprocating knife to
cut salad greens at adjustable heights. He has also followed development of
battery-powered harvesters. However, machine-cut greens are not always as
clean or precise as hand-cut greens. For premium markets paying top dollar for
immaculate winter salad mix, hand cutting remains the gold standard. The goal
is not to harvest faster but to harvest smarter: eliminating wasted motion,
using the right tools, and structuring workflow so speed emerges from
organization rather than haste.
