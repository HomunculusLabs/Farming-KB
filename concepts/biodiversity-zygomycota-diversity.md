# Zygomycota Diversity and Phylogeny

The Zygomycota is a phylum of fungi comprising more than 1,000
described species, including many ecologically important
organisms. Zygomycetes range from common soil saprobes and
plant pathogens to obligate parasites of insects and other
animals. Despite their ubiquity and ecological significance,
the phylogeny and taxonomy of Zygomycota remain in flux, as
molecular evidence has revealed that the group, as
traditionally defined, is not monophyletic.

## Phylogenetic Relationships

Zygomycetes comprise a monophyletic group only if the
Glomales (now placed in Glomeromycota) and possibly
*Basidiobolus ranarum* (Entomophthorales) are excluded. The
placement of *Basidiobolus* has been particularly problematic:
while commonly found in terrestrial habitats with insects and
amphibians, it has been placed within the core chytrid group
in some rDNA-based analyses, yet falls within the zygomycetes
in trees based on alpha- and beta-tubulin genes. This
discrepancy may result from an accelerated rate of sequence
evolution in zygomycete SSU rDNA and resulting long-branch
attraction in phylogenetic analyses.

Several traditional zygomycete lineages are considered
monophyletic. These include Entomophthorales, Trichomycetes
(Harpellales, excluding Amoebidiales), Kickxellales (excluding
*Spiromyces*), Mucorales, Mortierellales, Dimargaritales, and
Zoopagales. However, the relationships among these monophyletic
lineages have not been well supported in most analyses.

## Major Orders and Their Ecology

The Mucorales is the largest and most familiar order of
Zygomycota, containing many common saprotrophic species such
as *Mucor*, *Rhizopus*, and *Rhizomucor*. Phylogenetic analyses
of 54 genera using SSU rDNA, LSU rDNA, and EF-1-alpha gene data
support a monophyletic grouping of Mucorales but found that
traditionally recognized families, including Mucoraceae,
Thamnidiaceae, and Pilobolaceae, are polyphyletic. Several
large genera, including *Mucor* and *Absidia*, are also
polyphyletic, requiring significant taxonomic revision.

Members of the Mortierellaceae represent a basal sister group
of the Mucorales. The Harpellales and Kickxellales are sister
taxa, sharing the ultrastructural feature of plugged, flared
septal pores. The enlarged Harpellales-Kickxellales clade also
contains Zoopagales, most species of which are parasites of
fungi or invertebrate animals.

The Entomophthorales include entomopathogenic fungi that are
important natural regulators of insect populations. The
Dimargaritales are obligate mycoparasites of other Mucorales,
while the Zoopagales prey on amoebae and other small soil
organisms.

## Trichomycetes: Gut Symbionts

The Trichomycetes, traditionally placed within Zygomycota,
are obligate symbionts living in the guts of arthropods.
Only the Harpellales and the Amoebidiales have been cultured.
The Amoebidiales has been determined to represent a protozoan
lineage and thus has been excluded from the Trichomycetes.
Harpellalean fungi attach to the hindgut cuticle of aquatic
insect larvae and may play roles in host nutrition. Their
phylogenetic placement and relationship to free-living
zygomycetes continues to be investigated.

## Reproduction and Zygospore Formation

Zygomycetes reproduce both asexually, through sporangiospores
and other spore types, and sexually, through the formation of
zygospores. Zygospores are thick-walled resting spores formed
by the fusion of specialized hyphae (gametangia) from
compatible mating types. Zygospore germination is rare in the
literature, making the study of sexual reproduction in this
group particularly challenging.

The diversity of asexual reproductive structures in Zygomycota
is remarkable, including sporangia, merosporangia, sporangiola,
and various types of conidia. These structures are important
for species identification but have proven to be unreliable
indicators of phylogenetic relationships.

## Ecological Importance

Zygomycetes are ecologically significant as decomposers,
pathogens, and symbionts. Many species are among the first
colonizers of fresh organic matter and are prominent in
coprophilous succession, appearing within 5 to 14 days on
dung substrates. *Rhizomucor pusillus* is a primary sugar
fungus on wheat-straw compost, while other species degrade
cellulose, keratin, and other complex polymers.

In soil ecosystems, Zygomycetes contribute to the early
stages of decomposition and nutrient cycling. Species of
*Mortierella* are particularly common in temperate and boreal
forest soils. Some zygomycetes are thermophilic, growing at
temperatures between 45 and 50 degrees Celsius, and play
important roles in the decomposition of heated organic matter
such as compost. These thermotolerant species can be isolated
from soil and plant materials through enrichment cultures
with selective carbon sources including cellulose, chitin,
keratin, and lignin.

Some Zygomycete species are important human pathogens,
particularly mucormycosis-causing agents in immunocompromised
patients. Zygomycetes also include pin molds, some of which
are common contaminants in laboratories and food storage
facilities, causing spoilage of fruits, vegetables, and
grain products. The economic impact of these fungi on
agriculture and food security underscores the importance
of understanding their biology and diversity.

## Relationship to Crown Fungi

The Glomales (arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, now placed in Glomeromycota) have been shown to be a sister clade to the Ascomycota/Basidiomycota lineage. Together, these three groups constitute the monophyletic "crown fungi." An estimated 80% of all plant species are associated with Glomales, underscoring the critical importance of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi for life on land. The timing of the crown-fungus clade's appearance probably coincided with the origin and diversification of land plants. See [[biodiversity-glomeromycota-diversity]] for details on this now-separate phylum.

## Taxonomic Challenges and Future Directions

The polyphyly of many traditional zygomycete taxa means that significant taxonomic revision is needed. The ancestor of the Glomales and other crown fungi among the more basal lineages of Zygomycota and Chytridiomycota has not yet been identified. The loss of the flagellated stage, possibly from a blastocladialean ancestor, unites the group ecologically but not phylogenetically. Molecular phylogenetic studies are rapidly reshaping our understanding of relationships, but comprehensive taxonomic revisions lag behind molecular discoveries. The integration of molecular data with morphological and ecological information will be essential for developing a stable classification system. As sampling of undersampled habitats and geographic regions continues, many new species and lineages of Zygomycota will undoubtedly be discovered.
