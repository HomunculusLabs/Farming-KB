# Zygomycota Diversity and Phylogeny

The Zygomycota is a phylum of fungi comprising more than 1,000
described species, including many ecologically important organisms.
Zygomycetes range from common soil saprobes and plant pathogens to
obligate [[lichen-biology-guide]], the phylogeny and taxonomy of
Zygomycota remain in flux, as molecular evidence has revealed that
the group, as traditionally defined, is not monophyletic.

## Phylogenetic Relationships

Zygomycetes comprise a monophyletic group only if the Glomales (now
placed in Glomeromycota) and possibly *Basidiobolus ranarum*
(Entomophthorales) are excluded. The placement of *Basidiobolus* has
been particularly problematic: while commonly found in terrestrial
habitats with insects and amphibians, it has been placed within the
core chytrid group in some rDNA-based analyses, yet falls within the
zygomycetes in trees based on alpha- and beta-tubulin genes.

This discrepancy may result from an accelerated rate of sequence
evolution in zygomycete SSU rDNA and resulting long-branch attraction
in phylogenetic analyses. Several traditional zygomycete lineages are
considered monophyletic, including Entomophthorales, Trichomycetes,
Kickxellales, Mucorales, Mortierellales, Dimargaritales, and
Zoopagales.

## Major Orders and Their Ecology

The Mucorales is the largest and most familiar order of Zygomycota,
containing many common saprotrophic species such as *Mucor*, *Rhizopus*,
and *Rhizomucor*. Phylogenetic analyses of 54 genera using SSU rDNA,
LSU rDNA, and EF-1-alpha gene data support a monophyletic grouping
of Mucorales but found that traditionally recognized families are
polyphyletic.

Several large genera, including *Mucor* and *Absidia*, are also
polyphyletic, requiring significant taxonomic revision. Members of
the Mortierellaceae represent a basal sister group of the Mucorales.
The Harpellales and Kickxellales are sister taxa, sharing the
ultrastructural feature of plugged, flared septal pores.

The enlarged Harpellales-Kickxellales clade also contains Zoopagales,
most species of which are parasites of fungi or invertebrate animals.
