## JADAM Green Manure Crops — Milk Vetch, Rye, and Cover Cropping

## Overview

JADAM places heavy emphasis on green manure crops—plants grown specifically to be incorporated into the soil for fertility building. This concept page examines JADAM's approach to two key green manure species: milk vetch (Chinese milk vetch / *Astragalus sinicus*) and rye, and the principles that guide cover crop selection.

## Milk Vetch

### Leguminous Nitrogen Fixation

"Milk vetch is a leguminous crop like the hairy vetch. It fixes nitrogen from the air and provides 15 kg (33 lb) of nitrogen per 0.1 ha (¼ ac)."

Milk vetch provides substantial nitrogen inputs through its symbiotic relationship with Rhizobia bacteria, reducing or eliminating the need for external nitrogen fertilization.

### Biomass Production

"Its total biomass is about 17 tons (37,500 lb)." The high biomass production means that milk vetch contributes not only nitrogen but also large quantities of organic matter, which improves soil structure, water retention, and biological activity.

### Historical Use

"In Asia it has been used as a green manure plant for thousands of years. It had been composted after having been mixed with soil." Milk vetch has a long history of use in East Asian agriculture, validating its effectiveness through centuries of practice.

### Planting Windows

Sowing of milk vetch is timed to the regional climate: before September 20-25 in the southern area and before mid-September in the central area of Korea. This timing ensures establishment before winter while allowing maximum spring growth before incorporation.

## Rye as Cover Crop

### Sandy Soil Adaptation

"They grow well in sandy soil or loam with good drainage and are weak to excessive moisture." Rye is particularly well-suited to well-drained soils where other cover crops may struggle.

### Low Carbon-Nitrogen Ratio

"With a low carbon-nitrogen (C/N) ratio of around 10, it breaks down fast." The low C/N ratio means that rye residues decompose quickly, releasing nutrients rapidly into the soil. This contrasts with high-C/N cover crops like cereal rye at maturity, which can temporarily tie up nitrogen during decomposition.

### Dismissing Expert Concerns

"Experts' say that because rye has low nitrogen content, when it decomposes it will cause nitrogen suck-in by microorganisms and thereby damage the growth of fruit trees. However, in the fields, such concern is dismissed."

JADAM consistently privileges field observation over theoretical concerns. The nitrogen immobilization predicted by experts does not occur in practice, either because the effect is smaller than predicted or because other factors compensate.

### Long-Term Tree-Rye Symbiosis

"The orchard produces more every year. The owner of this farm has done tree-rye symbiosis cultivation for over 20 years." This long-term case study demonstrates that rye cover cropping under fruit trees is not only harmless but beneficial, increasing production year over year.

## Principles of Cover Crop Selection

### Local Adaptation

JADAM emphasizes choosing cover crops that are adapted to local conditions—soil type, climate, and farming system. Both milk vetch and rye are proven performers in the Korean context.

### Multiple Functions

Green manure crops serve multiple functions simultaneously:
- **Nitrogen fixation** (legumes)
- **Biomass production** (organic matter addition)
- **Weed suppression** (competition for light and nutrients)
- **Soil protection** (erosion prevention)
- **Moisture conservation** (mulch effect after incorporation)
- **Biodiversity support** (habitat for beneficial organisms)

### Cost Elimination

By growing fertility on-farm through green manure crops, the farmer eliminates the cost of purchasing nitrogen fertilizer and organic matter amendments. The only costs are seed and the labor of planting and incorporation.

## The Philosophy of Observation

JADAM's approach to green manure management exemplifies its broader philosophy: trust field observation over expert opinion, prefer simple local solutions over complex imported ones, and measure success by long-term productivity rather than short-term nutrient analysis.
