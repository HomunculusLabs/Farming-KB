## What Are Mycelial Metabolites?

Mycelial metabolites are secondary compounds produced by [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] but has limited substrate resources remaining, leading to the accumulation of waste products as the mycelium begins to consume its own stored reserves.

### Bacterial Contamination

Low-level bacterial contamination that is not immediately obvious can stress the mycelium and trigger [[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]], some of which may appear as colored exudates on the grain surface.

### Over-Hydration

Excess water in the grain substrate can create conditions that stress the mycelium, particularly if the water creates anaerobic zones where normal aerobic metabolism is impaired.

### Species and Strain Factors

Some species and strains are more prone to metabolite excretion than others. This appears to be a genetic characteristic rather than purely an environmental response, though environmental conditions modulate the expression of the trait.

## What the Metabolites Represent

The yellowish metabolites excreted by stressed mycelium are likely a complex mixture of compounds including:

- **Oxidized [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]]** — compounds produced in response to perceived competition from bacteria or other microorganisms
- **Waste products of accelerated metabolism** — nitrogenous compounds and organic acids that accumulate when metabolic throughput exceeds processing capacity

The exact composition varies by species, strain, and stressor, but the visual appearance is remarkably consistent: yellowish, sometimes sticky or resinous droplets that appear on the grain surface and between kernels.

## The Guide"s Recommendation

The grass seed cultivation guide states that jars showing metabolite excretion "should be cased as soon as possible." This is the primary recommended response, and it reflects a practical understanding that the stressed mycelium needs to be moved to its next growth phase before conditions deteriorate further.

### Why Casing Helps

Moving stressed mycelium to a casing tray provides several benefits:

1. **Fresh substrate interface** — the casing layer gives the mycelium new material to interact with
2. **Reduced CO₂** — opening the jar and spreading the grain in a tray releases accumulated CO₂
3. **Initiation of fruiting** — the environmental change from jar to cased tray triggers the shift from vegetative growth to reproductive development, redirecting metabolic resources
4. **Improved gas exchange** — trays have far more surface area exposed to air than sealed jars

### When Not to Case

Jars with metabolite excretion should NOT be cased if:

- The metabolites are accompanied by obvious mold growth (green, black, or pink colonies)
- The grain has a foul or sour smell indicating bacterial contamination
- The mycelium has stalled completely and appears to be dying back
- Only a small portion of the jar is colonized (suggesting contamination rather than stress)

In these cases, the jar should be discarded rather than cased, as the contamination risk to the casing operation outweighs the value of the partially colonized grain.

## Prevention Strategies

### Temperature Control

The most effective prevention is maintaining proper incubation temperature. For most Psilocybe and Panaeolus species, 24–26°C (75–79°F) is ideal. Avoid placing jars near heat sources, on top of appliances, or in direct sunlight.

Use a thermometer to verify actual jar temperature rather than relying on ambient room temperature, as the metabolic activity of colonizing mycelium can generate several degrees of internal heat.

### Timely Processing

Check jars regularly and process them promptly once fully colonized. The guide's 5–20 day colonization window should be monitored actively, not passively.

### Proper Hydration

Follow the water ratio recommendations carefully and calibrate for each seed source. Refer to the water [[cultivator-sterile-technique-and-agar-culture]] during inoculation reduces the likelihood of low-level bacterial contamination that can stress the mycelium and trigger metabolite production. Flame needles between jars, work quickly, and maintain a clean workspace.
