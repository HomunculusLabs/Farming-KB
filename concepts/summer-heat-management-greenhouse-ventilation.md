# Summer Heat Management and Greenhouse Ventilation

## Overview

While winter harvest is the focus of [[eliot-coleman]]'s work, summer heat management is equally critical for year-round greenhouse productivity. Summer temperature [[maps]] of the United States reveal extreme variation in maximum temperatures across regions, and the hotter the summer, the better the ventilation system a greenhouse must have for summer crop production. This page covers temperature factors affecting winter salads, summer heat considerations, and strategies for managing greenhouse temperatures during hot months.

## Temperature Scale for Protected Cultivation

Coleman provides a practical temperature reference scale for [[greenhouse-growing]]:

| Temperature | Significance |
|-------------|-------------|
| **115°F** | Upper limit — plants die |
| **110°F** | Danger zone |
| **100°F** | Stress zone |
| **95°F** | Maximum photosynthesis; too hot if sustained |
| **90°F** | Warm zone |
| **80°F** | Comfortable growing range |
| **75°F** | Optimum photosynthesis and plant growth |
| **70°F** | Excellent growing conditions |
| **60°F** | Good growing conditions |
| **55°F** | Average daytime greenhouse temperature during coldest months |
| **50°F** | Cool growing conditions |
| **40°F** | Cold — slow growth |
| **35°F** | Ideal minimum greenhouse air temperature; no freezing of crops |
| **30°F** | Cold tolerance range |
| **20°F** | Hard freeze threshold for many crops |
| **15°F** | Lower limit for quality harvest of most winter salad crops |
| **10°F** | Extreme cold; crops survive only with protection |
| **0°F** | Severe cold; only hardy crops under heavy protection survive |

## Four Temperature Factors for Winter Salad Crops

Winter salad crops in protected cultivation are affected by four key temperature factors:

1. **Rate of temperature drop** — How quickly the temperature falls matters. Gradual cooling allows plants to acclimate; rapid freezes cause more cellular damage. Greenhouses with thermal mass (soil, water barrels, stone walls) moderate the rate of temperature change.

2. **Depth of temperature drop** — The absolute minimum temperature reached determines which crops survive. Most winter salad greens tolerate down to 15°F under double protection (greenhouse plus inner row cover). Some, like mâche and claytonia, can survive even lower.

3. **Duration of temperature drop** — A brief dip to 15°F is far less damaging than sustained temperatures at that level. Short cold snaps allow plants to recover; prolonged freezes deplete stored energy reserves and cause more tissue damage.

4. **Frequency of temperature drop** — Repeated freeze-thaw cycles are more stressful than a single deep freeze. Plants that experience frequent cold episodes expend more energy on cold-hardening responses, potentially reducing growth and yield.

## Summer Heat Considerations

### Regional Variation

The continental United States spans an enormous range of summer maximum temperatures. The southern plains and desert Southwest regularly exceed 100°F, while the Pacific Northwest and coastal New England rarely see temperatures above 90°F. This variation has direct implications for [[greenhouse-design]] and management.

### Ventilation Requirements

The hotter the summer in your region, the more critical effective ventilation becomes. Without adequate ventilation, greenhouse temperatures can quickly exceed the 95°F threshold where photosynthesis declines, and approach 115°F where plants die.

Key ventilation strategies include:

- **Roll-up or drop-down sides** that can be fully opened during hot weather
- **Roof vents** or ridge ventilation for hot air exhaust (hot air rises)
- **End-wall ventilation** with large doors or removable panels
- **Fan-and-pad evaporative cooling systems** for hot, dry climates
- **Shade cloth** to reduce solar heat gain during peak summer

### Soil Solarization

For regions with very hot summers, Coleman suggests an alternative approach: close the greenhouse during the hottest weeks and use the trapped heat to solarize the soil. Soil solarization kills soilborne pathogens, weed seeds, and certain nematodes by raising soil temperatures to lethal levels (typically above 120°F at 2-6 inches depth) for several weeks.

Solarization requirements:

- Soil must be moist before covering
- Clear plastic (not black) is used to trap solar radiation
- Coverage period: 4-8 weeks during the hottest part of summer
- Most effective when soil reaches 120-140°F at depth
- Benefits persist for multiple growing seasons

This approach turns a period when the greenhouse would otherwise be unproductive into a soil health treatment, making the summer heat an asset rather than a liability.

## Designing for Year-Round Use

The best greenhouse designs accommodate both winter cold protection and summer ventilation. Features that serve both purposes include:

- **Removable or operable end walls** for maximum airflow in summer and sealing in winter
- **Double-layer inflation systems** with the ability to run single-layer in summer for better ventilation
- **High ridge height** for greater air volume and natural convection
- **Orientation** perpendicular to prevailing summer winds for cross-ventilation
- **[[mobile-greenhouse]] designs** that can be moved to different positions seasonally

## Practical Takeaways

- Winter salad crops are remarkably cold-tolerant but respond to four distinct temperature factors (rate, depth, duration, frequency)
- 75°F is the optimum for plant growth; 95°F is the maximum for sustained photosynthesis
- Summer greenhouse management requires either excellent ventilation or strategic closure for soil solarization
- The same protective structures that make winter harvest possible must be designed to handle summer heat extremes
- Regional climate determines the level of ventilation investment needed; growers in hot climates should prioritize airflow capacity
