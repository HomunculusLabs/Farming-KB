# Organic Propagation Techniques (Hamilton Method)

Updated: 2026-04-20

For the organic gardener, raising plants from seed and cuttings is essential. Few commercial nurseries can guarantee their plants have been raised organically, and growing your own ensures everything has been cultivated without chemical sprays or fertilizers. [[comparison-cannabis-cloning-vs-seed-starting-indoors]] for detailed guidance.

**Containers**: Any shallow container with drainage holes works -- from commercial trays to yogurt cups, styrofoam coffee cups, or cut-off plastic bottles. Clean thoroughly before use.

**Compost**: Use moist peat-free seed compost. Firm lightly (peat-free requires very little firming). Level the top and firm with a firming board (for trays) or bottom of another pot.

**Moistening**: Always moisten compost thoroughly before sowing. Make a well in the compost, pour in water, work through by hand. Fill container, then water again since watering after sowing washes seeds into one spot.

**Covering**: Cover all except very small seeds with their own depth of vermiculite. Cover pot/tray with opaque plastic and place in a warm position. Check daily -- as soon as the first seedling pushes to the surface, remove to a light place. Do not put in direct sunlight.

**Windowsill growing**: Light comes from one direction only, making seedlings spindly. Make a "light box" from a cardboard box lined with kitchen foil to reflect light all around.

### Sowing Alpines and Trees
Many alpine and tree seeds require a cold period before germination. Sow in pots: use equal parts peat, soil, and coarse sand for alpines; peat and coarse sand for trees. Sow thinly on top, cover with coarse sand or grit. Water by standing pot in a bowl of water. Leave outside in a shaded spot, covered with wire netting for bird and mouse protection.

## Taking Cuttings

Cuttings are the most important vegetative propagation method for perennials, shrubs, and many fruit plants. For the organic gardener, the key is using clean tools and materials to prevent infection.

### Softwood Cuttings
Taken in late spring and early summer from new, flexible growth. Use shoots that snap when bent. Cut just below a leaf node, remove lower leaves, dip the cut end in water, then insert into a free-draining compost mix of equal parts perlite and peat-free compost. Cover with a plastic bag or propagator lid to maintain humidity. Roots usually form in 2-4 weeks.

### Semi-Hardwood Cuttings
Taken in late summer from partially ripened wood. Suitable for many shrubs including lavender, rosemary, and hydrangeas. Prepare as for softwood cuttings but use a slightly firmer compost mix. These take longer to root (4-8 weeks) but are more robust.

### Hardwood Cuttings
Taken in autumn and winter from fully ripened wood. These are the easiest cuttings for beginners. Cut 6-12in (15-30cm) lengths of stem, insert two-thirds into the ground or a pot of sandy compost, and leave in a sheltered position outdoors. Many will root by the following spring.

## Layering

Layering is the simplest and most reliable method of vegetative propagation for many shrubs and climbers. A low branch is bent down to soil level, a small section of bark is removed where it touches the ground, and the wounded section is buried, held in place with a peg. Roots form at the wounded point. Once well-rooted (usually the following year), the new plant is severed from the parent and transplanted.

This method is particularly effective for:

- Raspberries and blackberries
- Climbing roses and clematis
- Thyme, rosemary, and other spreading herbs

## Seed Saving

Saving seed from your own best plants is the ultimate form of self-sufficiency. Select open-pollinated (non-F1) varieties, allow a few plants to go to seed, harvest when fully dry, and store in cool, dry conditions. Label with variety name and harvest year. Some seeds remain viable for many years if stored properly; others (parsnip, onion) must be used within one season.
