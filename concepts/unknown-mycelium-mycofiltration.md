# Mycofiltration

Mycofiltration is the use of fungal mycelium as a membrane for filtering
microorganisms, pollutants, and silt from water. Developed and popularized by
Paul Stamets, the technique leverages the dense, threadlike networks of
mycelium — which can extend more than a mile of hyphae per cubic inch of soil —
to intercept and break down contaminants before they reach waterways.

## How Mycofiltration Works

Mycelium acts as a biological filter membrane when installed in the path of
water flow. As water percolates through the mycelial mat, several processes
occur simultaneously:

- **Mechanical filtration**: The dense hyphal network physically traps
  particulate matter, silt, and sediment.
- **Biological capture**: Bacteria, protozoa, and other microorganisms are
  ensnared by the mycelium. Many are subsequently consumed or outcompeted.
- **Chemical decomposition**: Extracellular enzymes secreted by the mycelium
  break down organic pollutants, including some agricultural chemicals and
  hydrocarbons.
- **Flow modulation**: Mycelial mats slow water movement through soil, reducing
  erosion and allowing more time for natural filtration processes.

## Applications

Mycofiltration membranes are particularly effective in agricultural and
residential settings where runoff carries biological and chemical contaminants:

- **Farms and feedlots**: Cattle and pig operations produce coliform bacteria,
  nitrates, and phosphorus that contaminate downstream watersheds, especially
  during heavy rain. Mycofiltration beds placed between feedlots and waterways
  significantly reduce this outflow.
- **Road construction**: Sediment-laden runoff from roads and construction
  sites is captured before reaching streams and rivers.
- **Stormwater management**: Mycelial filters installed in drainage pathways
  reduce the biological and particulate load entering municipal water systems.
- **Restoration of damaged habitats**: Stressed or malnourished ecosystems
  benefit from the dual action of water filtration and soil building that
  mycelial mats provide.

## Installation Methods

The core principle is to grow mats of mycelium matched to the volume of the
contamination source. Common approaches include:

- **Sheet mulch beds**: Shallow compost beds 6 inches to 2 feet thick,
  constructed from agricultural byproducts such as wheat straw, cornstalks,
  or wood chips. These are inoculated with target-specific mushroom species.
- **Bunker spawn**: Burlap bags filled with myceliated substrate, stacked like
  sandbags along contamination pathways. This method is portable and can be
  deployed rapidly.
- **Spore mass slurries**: Spore-laden water solutions applied to existing
  organic debris in the filtration zone, allowing natural colonization.

## Species Selection

Species selection depends on the target contaminant. A field survey of the
affected site often reveals mushrooms already tolerating local conditions.
These native species are naturally selected and generally outperform imported
strains. Reference charts matching mushroom species to specific pathogens and
toxins help guide selection. Key genera used in mycofiltration include
Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster mushroom) for bacterial filtration and Stropharia
rugosoannulata (wine-cap mushroom) for general runoff treatment.

## Maintenance

Mycological landscapes must be replenished with fresh woody debris and
sometimes recharged with spawn to maintain active saprophytic mushroom
communities. In woodlands, falling debris naturally feeds the fungal floor,
but in managed mycofiltration installations, periodic addition of substrate
is essential. Without renewal, beneficial species like Hypholoma and
Psilocybe become scarce as competing organisms dominate.

## Limitations

Mycofiltration is most effective as part of an integrated approach. It
complements rather than replaces conventional water treatment. Seasonal
variation affects performance — mycelial activity slows in cold or dry
conditions. The cubic capacity of the mycelial mat must be sized
appropriately for the expected flow volume, and toxin concentrations beyond
a species tolerance threshold will require alternative treatments.

## Cost Considerations

Mycofiltration installations are remarkably inexpensive compared to
conventional water treatment infrastructure. The primary costs are the
substrate materials (often available as agricultural byproducts at minimal
cost) and the spawn required for inoculation. Burlap bag installations
can be deployed with minimal equipment. The long-term maintenance costs
are similarly low, requiring only periodic substrate replenishment rather
than the ongoing chemical inputs and energy consumption of mechanical
filtration systems.

## Research Directions

The science of mycofiltration is still in its early stages. Key areas of
ongoing research include quantifying the filtration capacity of different
mycelial species, optimizing substrate compositions for specific
contaminant profiles, developing standardized installation protocols for
municipal applications, and studying the long-term ecological effects of
large-scale mycelial installations on soil microbiology and hydrology.
Field trials have demonstrated significant reductions in coliform bacteria,
but more controlled studies are needed to establish optimal design
parameters for different climatic conditions and contamination scenarios.

Mycofiltration is part of a broader toolkit of [[mycorestoration|mycorestoration]]
practices that position fungi as keystone organisms for environmental healing.
Combined with [[mycoremediation|mycoremediation]] for chemical
cleanup and mycoforestry for long-term forest management, mycofiltration
represents a paradigm shift in how we approach water quality — from
mechanical and chemical treatment to biological filtration that simultaneously
builds soil, supports biodiversity, and cleans water.

The continued development of mycofiltration depends on bridging the gap
between laboratory research and field implementation. As climate change
intensifies rainfall patterns and agricultural runoff, the demand for
low-cost, sustainable water treatment solutions will only grow. Fungi, with
their extraordinary capacity to process water and degrade contaminants,
are uniquely positioned to meet this challenge.
