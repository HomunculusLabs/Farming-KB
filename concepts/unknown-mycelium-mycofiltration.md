# Mycofiltration

Mycofiltration is the use of fungal mycelium as a membrane for filtering
microorganisms, pollutants, and silt from water. Developed and popularized by
[[paul-stamets]], the technique leverages the dense, threadlike networks of
mycelium — which can extend more than a mile of hyphae per cubic inch of soil
— to intercept and break down contaminants before they reach waterways.

## How Mycofiltration Works

Mycelium acts as a biological filter membrane when installed in the path of
water flow. As water percolates through the mycelial mat, several processes
occur simultaneously:

- **Mechanical filtration**: The dense hyphal network physically traps
  particulate matter, silt, and sediment.
- **Biological capture**: Bacteria, protozoa, and other microorganisms are
  ensnared by the mycelium. Many are subsequently consumed or outcompeted.
- **Chemical decomposition**: [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]] secreted by the mycelium
  break down organic pollutants, including some [[agricultural-chemicals-mycorrhizal-impact]] and
  hydrocarbons.
- **Flow modulation**: Mycelial mats slow water movement through soil,
  reducing erosion and allowing more time for natural filtration processes.

## Applications

Mycofiltration membranes are particularly effective in agricultural and
residential settings where runoff carries biological and chemical contaminants:

- **Farms and feedlots**: Cattle and pig operations produce coliform bacteria,
  nitrates, and phosphorus that contaminate downstream watersheds, especially
  during heavy rain. Mycofiltration beds placed between feedlots and waterways
  significantly reduce this outflow.
- **Road construction**: Sediment-laden runoff from roads and construction
  sites is captured before reaching streams and rivers.
- **[[mollison-designers-cloudburst-and-stormwater-management]]**: Mycelial filters installed in drainage pathways
  reduce the biological and particulate load entering municipal water systems.
- **Restoration of damaged habitats**: Stressed or malnourished ecosystems
  benefit from the dual action of water filtration and soil building that
  mycelial mats provide.

## Installation Methods

The core principle is to grow mats of mycelium matched to the volume of the
contamination source. Common approaches include:

- **Sheet mulch beds**: Shallow compost beds 6 inches to 2 feet thick,
  constructed from agricultural byproducts such as wheat straw, cornstalks, or
  wood chips. These are inoculated with target-specific mushroom species.
- **Bunker spawn**: Burlap bags filled with myceliated substrate, stacked
  like sandbags along contamination pathways. This method is portable and can
  be deployed rapidly.
- **Spore mass slurries**: Spore-laden water solutions applied to existing
  organic debris in the filtration zone, allowing natural colonization.

## Species Selection

Species selection depends on the target contaminant. A field survey of the
affected site often reveals mushrooms already tolerating local conditions.
These native species are naturally selected and generally outperform imported
strains. Reference charts matching mushroom species to specific pathogens and
toxins help guide selection.

Key genera used in mycofiltration include [[oyster-mushroom]]
([[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]) for bacterial filtration and [[wine-cap]]
([[stropharia-rugosoannulata]]) for general runoff treatment.

## Maintenance

Mycological landscapes must be replenished with fresh woody debris and
sometimes recharged with spawn to maintain active saprophytic mushroom
communities. In woodlands, falling debris naturally feeds the fungal floor,
but in managed mycofiltration installations, periodic addition of substrate
is essential. Without renewal, beneficial species become scarce as competing
organisms dominate.
