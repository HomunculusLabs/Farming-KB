# Evolving Polyculture Systems for Settlements

Mollison outlines a practical methodology for evolving perennial
polyculture systems on degraded or compacted lands. This process
begins with thorough site preparation and proceeds through a staged
sequence of planting, eventually producing a complex, self-maintaining
food forest. The approach is particularly relevant to humid tropical
and subtropical environments where rapid growth enables quick canopy
closure.

## Initial Site Preparation

If starting to evolve a permaculture on grasslands or compacted soils,
the very first step is to thoroughly plan the site and then rip, swale,
pit, or dam every area to be planted. This earthworking phase ensures
maximum wet-season soil water storage to carry over to dry periods.
The process should commence at the highest point of the property and
work downward around the house or village site. See
[[mollison-earthworks-planning-and-post-earthworks-planting]] for detailed methods.

## Initial Planting Strategy

On the loosened soils and in mulched swales, a diverse mix of species
is co-planted from the outset. The initial mix includes:

- Tree legumes for nitrogen fixation and mulch production
- Fruits including bananas and papayas
- Root crops such as arrowroot (Canna), cassava, and sweet potato
- Comfrey for dynamic mineral accumulation
- Acacia species at 3 x 3 metre spacing
- Banana at 2 x 2 metre spacing
- Fruits at 5 x 8 metre spacing
- Palms at 10 x 10 metre spacing
- Smaller species as gap fillers between larger plantings

The target density is approximately one plant every 1 to 1.5 metres
across the planted area.

## Companion Ground Cover

All larger planting holes should be seeded with a diverse mix of useful
non-grass species. Mollison recommends including nasturtium, Dolichos,
Haifa clover, broad bean (fava), buckwheat, Umbelliferae (dill,
fennel), lupin, vetch (hairy or woolly), dun peas, chilies, pigeon
pea, or any useful non-grass mix available and suitable to the local
climate and landscape. The end aim is to completely carpet and
overshade the ground within the first 18 to 20 months of growth.

## Mulching and Establishment

Ideally, dense plantings of this type should be grass or hay mulched
using monsoon grasses, swamp grasses, and later the tops of arrowroot,
comfrey, banana, Acacia, and green crop. As the system matures,
shade-loving species such as coffee and dry taro can be placed in any
open spots that remain. Paths for access, openings for annual crops,
bee plants on the edges, flowers, and fire-resistant ground covers
such as comfrey, Tradescantia, Impatiens, and succulents are
introduced progressively.

## Intensive vs. Extensive Approaches

Mollison emphasises a critical principle: it is far better to occupy
a quarter hectare thoroughly than to scatter trees over 2 hectares.
Production is higher and maintenance is less when areas are managed
intensively. Moisture is conserved, and frost can be excluded from
well-planted compact sites. This principle of intensive management at
small scale underlies much of permaculture design philosophy.

## Factors Determining Species Richness

The number of productive, managed, and effectively-harvested species
in a polyculture is decided by a complex of interrelated factors:

1. **Labour**: The number of people responsible for managing one or
   two crops or animal species. More hands enable greater complexity.

2. **Proximity to settlement**: Zonation determines how intensively
   any given area can be managed. See
   [[mollison-zone-planning-frequency-distance-and-placement]].

3. **Cost-benefit balance**: The relative economic return on
   increasing inputs to optimum levels.

4. **Plant guild harmony**: The need for effective plant guilds
   composed of mutually beneficial ecological assemblies. See
   [[mollison-guild-analysis-and-species-interaction]].

5. **Marketing and processing capacity**: Whether local systems can
   cope with complex product diversity.

6. **Total controlled area**: Larger areas demand increasing
   simplicity, at a cost to guild complexity.

7. **System maturity**: Older systems provide more niches and support
   greater diversity; younger systems produce more regular but simpler
   product yields.

## Optimal Species Counts

In practice, the gardens, walls, roof areas, trellis systems, and
compounds of villages are the most complex and rich areas. The
potential species breakdown for a complex village situation includes
155-280 cultivated species plus 40-80 wild-gathered species, totalling
195-360 utilised species. The main usage categories are:

- Basic food species: 70-90
- Fodder provision: 10-30
- Medicinals and biocides: 20-50
- Structural and craft materials: 5-20
- Culinary herbs: 20-30
- Beverages and export specialities: 10-15
- Fuelwood and coppice: 12-30
- Special and sacred uses: 8-15

## Economic Considerations

While complex polycultures of many hundreds of species benefit both
the naturalist and the householder, it becomes difficult to control an
extensive rich polyculture and collect its products at large scale.
Very complex polycultures work best at small scale with close
attention from people. Mollison warns that the depopulated, dehumanised
wastelands of modern agriculture are unable to cope with any but the
most basic and simple intercrop systems, thus sacrificing yield,
quality, stability, and ultimately people themselves.
