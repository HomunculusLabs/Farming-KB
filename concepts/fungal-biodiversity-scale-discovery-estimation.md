## Overview
Fungi constitute one of the most diverse and least-understood kingdoms of life
on Earth. Current estimates suggest that between 1.5 million and 5.1 million
fungal species exist, yet only approximately 74,000 to 120,000 have been
formally described and named. This means that at best, we have identified

Fourth, the taxonomic expertise required to identify fungi is scarce and
declining worldwide. Fungal systematics is a specialized field with relatively
few practitioners, and the identification of many groups requires expertise in
microscopic morphology, spore characteristics, cultural features, and
increasingly, molecular phylogenetics. The bottleneck of taxonomic expertise
fundamentally limits the rate at which new species can be formally described
and incorporated into biodiversity databases.

Fifth, many fungi are obligately associated with other organisms — as
endophytes within plant tissues, as parasites of insects or other animals, or
as partners in mycorrhizal symbioses — and can only be detected by examining
their hosts. A single plant species may harbor different fungal associates
during different stages of its life cycle, from seedling through maturity to
decomposition, and these associates may be present only transiently or at very
low abundance.
