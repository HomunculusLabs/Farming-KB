# Shamanic Journey Structure and Technique

## Overview

[[ralph-metzner]]'s analysis of [[hallucinogenic-drugs]] in [[metzner-hallucinogens-psychotherapy-and-shamanism]] includes a detailed examination of the structure and technique of the shamanic journey.

The shamanic journey is the core practice of shamanism worldwide: a deliberate, structured entry into non-ordinary states of consciousness for purposes of healing, divination, and spiritual exploration.

Metzner identifies consistent structural features of the shamanic journey across cultures, suggesting that it represents a universal human technology for accessing altered states.

The journey typically involves three phases: departure, encounter, and return, each of which has specific phenomenological characteristics and functional roles.

The shamanic use of hallucinogenic plants is understood not as recreational drug use but as a sacred technology—a means of accessing realms of experience that are essential to the well-being of the individual and the community.

Metzner's analysis bridges the gap between indigenous shamanic practice and modern psychedelic therapy, revealing deep structural parallels between the two traditions.

## The Three Worlds

Shamanic cosmologies around the world describe a universe organized into three primary realms: the lower world, the middle world, and the upper world.

The middle world is the ordinary world of everyday experience—the world of physical objects, other people, and mundane events.

The lower world is typically described as a realm of natural forces, animal spirits, and ancestral beings; it is accessed by traveling downward through an opening in the earth.

The upper world is typically described as a realm of celestial beings, divine teachers, and transcendent wisdom; it is accessed by traveling upward through the sky or through a cosmic axis.

Each world has its own inhabitants, its own physics, and its own kind of knowledge, and the shaman travels between them to gather information, retrieve lost souls, or negotiate with spirits.

The three-world model is remarkably consistent across cultures separated by vast distances and [[neurotropic-fungi-five-historical-periods]], from Siberia to the Amazon to Mesoamerica.

## The Departure Phase

The shamanic journey begins with a deliberate departure from ordinary consciousness, typically facilitated by drumming, chanting, dancing, or the ingestion of psychoactive plants.

The departure is not an accidental or passive process but a skilled, intentional transition managed by the shaman through years of training and practice.

In plant-based traditions, the departure is facilitated by the ingestion of specific hallucinogenic preparations such as ayahuasca, peyote, [[psilocybin-mushrooms]], or iboga.

The pharmacological effects of the plant are understood not as the cause of the journey but as a vehicle or bridge that carries the shaman's consciousness from the middle world into the other worlds.

The shaman typically enters the lower or upper world through a specific portal: a hole in the ground, a tree, a mountain, a river, or a celestial pathway.

The departure phase is characterized by a shift in sensory experience, a dissolution of ordinary self-awareness, and the onset of the phenomenological features of the non-ordinary state.

## The Encounter Phase

The central phase of the shamanic journey is the encounter with the beings, forces, or landscapes of the non-ordinary world.

In the lower world, the shaman typically encounters power animals, spirit guides, and the spirits of nature; these beings provide information, protection, and healing power.

In the upper world, the shaman typically encounters divine teachers, celestial beings, and ancestral wisdom; these beings provide guidance, prophecy, and spiritual instruction.

The encounter is not a passive vision but an active interaction: the shaman engages with the beings, asks questions, receives teachings, and often undergoes trials or challenges.

The information received during the encounter phase is the primary purpose of the journey: it is this information that the shaman brings back to the community for healing, divination, or guidance.

The phenomenology of the encounter phase closely parallels the entity encounters reported by modern psychedelic researchers, suggesting that the shamanic and psychedelic experiences may be accessing the same underlying reality.

## The Return Phase

The return phase involves the shaman's re-entry into ordinary consciousness and the middle world, bringing back the information or healing obtained during the encounter.

The return is a critical and sometimes dangerous phase: the shaman must navigate back through the non-ordinary world, reintegrate the fragmented aspects of consciousness, and re-establish contact with ordinary reality.

In many traditions, the return is facilitated by specific rituals: the cessation of drumming or chanting, the administration of purgative plants, or the performance of specific songs or prayers.

The integration of the experience into the framework of everyday life is an essential part of the shamanic process: the knowledge gained in the non-ordinary world must be translated into action in the ordinary world.

The return phase parallels the integration phase of modern psychedelic therapy, in which the patient works with a therapist to process and apply the insights gained during the psychedelic session.

Metzner notes that the neglect of the return phase in modern recreational psychedelic use is one of the primary sources of psychological difficulty, as the experiencer returns to ordinary consciousness without the structure and support needed for integration.

## The Role of the Shaman as Guide

The shaman functions as a guide for both the journeyer and the community, using specialized knowledge and skills to navigate the non-ordinary worlds safely and effectively.

The shaman's knowledge includes the pharmacology of psychoactive plants, the geography of the non-ordinary worlds, the languages of the spirits, and the techniques for diagnosis and healing.

In group ceremonies, the shaman manages the [[set-and-setting]] for all participants, using songs, chants, and rituals to create a safe container for the collective journey.

The shaman's songs—known as icaros in the Amazonian tradition—are believed to have power in themselves, structuring the experience and communicating with the spirits.

The role of the shaman as guide parallels the role of the therapist in modern psychedelic therapy, where the clinician provides preparation, support, and integration for the patient.

Metzner argues that the modern psychedelic therapist can learn from the shamanic model, particularly in the areas of ceremonial structure, the management of non-ordinary states, and the integration of the experience into everyday life.

## Parallels with Modern Psychotherapy

Metzner identifies deep structural parallels between the shamanic journey and modern psychotherapeutic approaches to altered states of consciousness.

Both the shamanic journey and psychedelic therapy involve a deliberate induction of non-ordinary consciousness, a structured encounter with the contents of the altered state, and a guided return to ordinary awareness.

Both traditions recognize the importance of preparation, the management of set and setting, and the integration of the experience into the framework of everyday life.

Both traditions view the altered state not as an end in itself but as a means of accessing information and producing transformation that benefits the individual and the community.

The primary difference is that the shamanic tradition operates within a cosmological framework that includes spirits, non-ordinary worlds, and divine beings, while modern psychotherapy typically operates within a psychological framework that attributes the experience to unconscious processes.

Metzner suggests that these frameworks are not mutually exclusive and that the most effective approach to the therapeutic use of altered states may incorporate elements of both.

## Cross-Cultural Consistency

One of the most striking features of the shamanic journey is its consistency across cultures that have had no contact with one another.

The three-world cosmology, the departure-encounter-return structure, the role of the guide, and the use of psychoactive plants are found in shamanic traditions from Siberia to the Amazon to Southern Africa.

This consistency is difficult to explain as a result of cultural diffusion, given the geographical isolation of many of these traditions.

Metzner suggests that the consistency reflects a universal feature of human consciousness: the capacity to access non-ordinary states of consciousness through specific techniques, and the tendency of those states to exhibit certain consistent phenomenological features.

The cross-cultural consistency of the shamanic journey is one of the strongest arguments for the reality of the non-ordinary worlds described by shamans, as it suggests that these worlds are not cultural inventions but genuine dimensions of experience accessible to all human beings.

The universality of the shamanic journey also supports the therapeutic potential of psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy, as it suggests that the states accessed by these substances are not pathological but are part of the natural range of human consciousness.
