Source: Paul Stamets, *The Mushroom Cultivator* (1983)

Compost preparation is the foundation of Agaricus cultivation. The process transforms raw organic materials into a selective medium favouring mushroom mycelium while eliminating competitors. Two main methods exist: Long Composting (outdoor, microbe-driven) and Short Composting (faster, chemical-process driven).

## Raw Materials

### Base Materials
- Horse manure (traditional base -- contains both nitrogen source and straw)
- Wheat straw (for synthetic composts)
- Various organic waste materials

### Supplements (Nitrogen Sources)
- Group 1: Chicken manure, blood meal (highly soluble)
- Group 4: Malt sprouts, brewers grain (moderately soluble)
- Group 5: Cottonseed meal, feather meal (slowly soluble)
- Gypsum (calcium sulfate): added to improve structure and buffer pH

## Phase I: Outdoor Composting

### Compost Pile Construction
- Pile dimensions: 6 feet wide by 4-5 feet high
- Vertical sides tightly compressed; middle of pile kept loose
- Use pile formers (constructed from 2x4s and plywood, 4-5 feet high by 8 feet long) to achieve vertical sides
- Stomp the sides from the top for adequate compression
- Thoroughly wet and mix all raw materials and supplements (except gypsum)
- A cement floor is highly desirable (prevents water migration and soil contamination)

### Temperature Zonation
Three distinct zones develop in the pile:
- Zone 1 (outer): cool and dry, aerobic zone
- Zone 2 (middle): 120-140F, actinomycete-rich zone -- produces highest yielding crops
- Zone 3 (inner core): 150-180F, thermophilic bacteria, chemical reactions

### Microbial Succession
- Mesophiles (active below 90F): initial decomposition of carbohydrates, release ammonia
- Thermophiles (active 90-160F): actinomycetes (visible as whitish flecks), fungi, bacteria dominate the interior
- Above 150F: microbial action slows, chemical processes begin
- 150-165F: microbial and chemical actions occur simultaneously
- 165-180F: mainly humification and caramellization (chemical reactions at high temp, high pH 8.5, with ammonia and oxygen)

### Turning Schedule
The pile is turned when the temperature begins to drop, indicating oxygen depletion and slowing microbial action. During turning:
- Move center anaerobic zone to the outside of the new pile
- Move outer cool zones to the center
- Water dry areas
- Maintain pile height and length; reduce width as decomposition proceeds
- Gypsum added at the second turn (adding earlier believed to depress ammonia production)

## Long Composting (Rasmussen Method)

Designed to carry out complete composting outdoors (excluding pasteurization). Characterised by:
- Avoidance of high-temperature chemical decomposition
- Reliance on purely microbial action
- Promotion of actinomycete growth
- Elimination of all ammonia by filling time

Timeline:
- Day -10: Wet straw, add supplements, windrow
- Day 0: Make up pile (6ft wide, 4ft high)
- Day 6: First turn
- Day 10-12: Second turn, add gypsum
- Day 13-15: Third turn, reduce pile height to 24 inches
- Day 15-17: Fourth turn -- compost should be dark brown, well flecked with actinomycetes, no ammonia smell, 67-70% moisture, pH 7.0-7.5

Finished compost is pasteurized at 135F for four hours.

## Short Composting (Sinden Method)

Centred on the fast-acting chemical reactions of zone 3. Advantages:
- Shorter preparation time
- Greater preservation of dry matter and nutrients
- Compost is biologically "active" with sufficient food reserves for Phase II

Timeline (horse manure):
- Day -1: Wet starting materials, windrow
- Day 0: Make up pile (6ft wide, 5-6ft high), add all supplements except gypsum
- Day 2-3: First turn, add gypsum
- Day 5: Second turn
- Day 7: Third turn -- compost ready to fill

Synthetic compost pre-composting begins 10 days before pile make-up.

## Supplementation at Filling

Key to successful Phase II is the heat-generating capability of the compost. Supplementation with soluble carbohydrates (molasses) or vegetable oils (cottonseed oil) at filling provides:
1. Accelerated thermogenesis -- nutrients act as "super-charger" for microbial populations
2. Better compost ventilation -- heightened thermogenesis requires lower room temperature, improving air movement
3. Rapid reduction of free ammonia -- Phase II period reduced by up to 3 days
4. Reduced spawn running period -- oil-supplemented composts show increased mycelial activity

Oil supplementation increases actinomycete and thermophilic fungi populations; carbohydrate supplementation enhances bacterial populations.

## Compost Characteristics at Filling
1. Uniformly deep brown
2. Straw still long and fibrous but shearable with some resistance
3. Liquid appears between fingers when firmly squeezed
4. Strong ammonia smell, pH 8.0-8.5
5. Lightly flecked with whitish actinomycete colonies
6. Kjeldahl nitrogen: 1.5% (horse manure), 1.7% (synthetic)

## Composting Tools
1. Cement floor (or heavy plastic sheet)
2. Bobcat or small tractor loader (for large scale)
3. Pile formers (2x4s and plywood)
4. Long-handled pitchfork (4-5 prongs)
5. Flat-bladed shovel for supplements
6. Hose with spray nozzle
7. Long-stemmed thermometer
