# Site Planning and Pest Management in the Tropics

## Overview

Permaculture pest management in tropical systems relies on integrated biological approaches rather than chemical interventions. Mollison catalogs a comprehensive toolkit of plant-derived biocides, animal pest controllers, and ecological design strategies that together create resilient production systems. Combined with systematic site planning — from market assessment through earthworks to continuous improvement — these approaches form the foundation for productive tropical polycultures.

## Natural Biocides and Plant-Derived Pest Control

### Insecticidal Plants

Several plant species provide powerful, biodegradable insecticides that are harmless or short-term in their environmental effects:

- **Pyrethrum** (*Chrysanthemum* spp.) — One of the most widely used botanical insecticides, effective against a broad spectrum of insects
- **Derris** (*Derris* spp., source of rotenone) — Potent insecticide, also controls aquatic organisms including nuisance fish and amphibians
- **Neem** (*Melia azadirachta* / *Azadirachta indica*) — Perhaps the most versatile botanical pesticide; controls insects, aquatic organisms, and pests in stored foods. Neem leaves and oil have been used for centuries in India to protect stored grain.

These plants should be integrated into home gardens and planted in small clumps throughout crop areas to provide a ready source of insect control material.

### Broad-Scale Mosquito Control

For larger-scale applications, mosquito control can combine multiple agents:
- **Fats or oils** (e.g., lecithin) as surfactants
- **Neem oil** as the poison
- **Bacillus thuringiensis** (*Bti*) as the biological infective agent

These are potentially enhanced by biological control agents in open water systems:
- **Small fish** that consume mosquito larvae
- **Notonectids** (backswimmers) — predatory aquatic insects that consume mosquito larvae

## Integrated Animal Pest Control

### Ground Foragers

Ground-dwelling domestic animals provide essential pest control services:

- **Chickens** — Eat fallen fruit and larval insect infestations; control rhinoceros beetle larvae in palm groves; especially valuable for weed control in pineapple, ginger, and taro
- **Pigs** — Ideal scavengers in tree crops below palms and fruit trees; consume fallen fruit and ground-level pests
- **Cattle** — Consume fallen fruit and larvae
- **Large tortoises** — Ground-level foraging on fallen fruit and larvae
- **Guinea pigs** — On range in small houses, they free trees from grass competition (especially kikuyu grass) and provide manures

### Canopy Foragers

- **Birds** — Attend to infestations in the canopy, consuming insects at all life stages
- **Frogs** — Important predators of canopy and ground-level insects
- **Skink lizards** — A variety of small skinks forage for insects in the canopy
- **Tiliqua lizards** — Forage specifically for snails and slugs at ground level
- **Ducks** — Consume snails, slugs, and aquatic insect larvae; especially valuable in paddy and pond systems

### Pasture and Soil Pest Controllers

- **A variety of birds and small mammalian/marsupial insectivores** seek out pasture grubs
- **Tropical land crabs** — Seek larval insects in mulch and provide useful food themselves
- **Geese** — Control pond-edge grasses and can be encouraged to clip specific areas by feeding-out bran or pollard
- **Wallaby or small grazers** — Keep forest clearings and paths open; also encouraged by strategic feeding

### Rodent Control

- **Pythons** — Effective rat and mouse control in tropical tree crops
- **Owls** — Identified as the best rodent predator; should be encouraged through habitat provision

## Managing Nematodes and Soil Fungi

Soil fungi and nematodes can become persistent pests in tropical tree crop monocultures. Two specific biological controls are highlighted:

### Marigolds (*Tagetes* spp.)
Marigolds often serve to reduce or eliminate nematode populations when interplanted with susceptible crops. Their root exudates are toxic to nematodes, making them a simple and effective companion plant.

### Crotolaria
Crotolaria serves as a leguminous green crop that traps nematodes in its root mycelia. As a nitrogen-fixing legume, it simultaneously improves soil fertility while reducing nematode pressure.

### Mulch and Green Manures
Mulch and green manures (soil humus) often buffer the effects of nematodes and fungal pests by hosting fungal predators. A healthy soil food web provides natural suppression of soil-borne pathogens.

## Animal Species in the Tropical System

### Primary Functions of Domestic Animals

The main function of animal species in the tropics is to "recycle" plant wastes and help control the rampant growth of ground cover. The usual domestic species, often penned, include:

- Pigs, chickens, geese, guinea pigs, rabbits, pigeons
- Milking goats, cattle, or buffalo
- Horses, bullocks, or oxen for draught functions

### Poultry Management Considerations

Where no foxes or pythons threaten poultry, flocks of guinea fowl, hens, ducks, and bantams perform invaluable pest-control and manurial/scavenging roles. Where predators are a problem:

- **Special housing** is required to protect flocks
- **Pigeons in safe elevated roosts** may be the only way to keep fowl in predator-prone areas

### Electric Fence Limitations in the Tropics

The effects of electric fences in tropical areas are often nullified by the rank growth of coarse grasses, rendering them inoperative against python, pig, bandicoot, and fox. For this reason:

- Fence lines must be planted with a dense perennial ground carpet of low herbaceous plants to exclude grasses
- Alternatively, fence lines should be overshaded by tree canopies that suppress grass growth beneath them

### Endemic Species for Broader Trials

Useful endemic animal species — especially island species of restricted natural range — need to be more widely examined for their particular uses and selected for trials outside their normal range. This represents a significant area for research and species development.

## Palm Grove Integration

Palm groves provide sheltered and shaded aspects for both intercrop and livestock, making them ideal nuclei for integrated polyculture systems. The recommended animal complement includes:

- **Chickens** — Control rhinoceros beetle larvae and other palm pests
- **Guinea pigs** — Reduce grass competition at the base of palms
- **Geese and land tortoises** — Control grass competition
- **Pythons** — Rat and mouse control
- **Owls** — The best rodent predator
- **Bees** — Pollination services
- **Pigs** — Ideal scavengers for fallen fruit and waste beneath palms

All species contribute manurial value and serve as beneficial additions to the palm/crop/interplant complex, generating complex yields as by-products of their pest-control functions.

## Coconut and Palm-Dominant Polyculture Design

Planning a coconut or palm-dominant polyculture involves six key themes:

### 1. Structure and Zoning
The spatial arrangement of palms, intercrops, livestock, and infrastructure follows permaculture zone principles, with the most intensive and diverse elements closest to settlement.

### 2. Species Selection
Choosing species based on multiple functions: food, forage, mulch, nitrogen fixation, pest control, timber, and market value. Every species should serve at least two functions.

### 3. Patterning
The arrangement of species in space and time — including strip cropping, alley cropping, and temporal succession patterns that maximize productivity and minimize competition.

### 4. Economics
Analysis of both fiscal returns and total nutritional yield. Cash crops are concentrated in accessible areas while diverse food production is maintained near the home.

### 5. Re-working Old Plantations
Strategies for converting monoculture palm plantations to diverse polycultures through understory planting, intercropping, and gradual introduction of animal systems.

### 6. Effects of Plantation Monoculture
Understanding the negative consequences of monoculture — soil degradation, pest vulnerability, and yield decline — provides the rationale for conversion to polyculture.

## Pest Management Philosophy

Mollison's fundamental conviction is that "there is no pest problem that will not yield to our applied commonsense and an integrated natural approach." This philosophy rests on several principles:

1. **Diversity creates stability** — Complex polycultures resist pest outbreaks naturally
2. **Biological controls are self-reinforcing** — Predators and parasites multiply in response to pest populations
3. **Plant-derived controls are biodegradable** — Unlike synthetic pesticides, botanical controls break down naturally
4. **Animals recycle waste** — Every domestic animal species converts plant waste into manure while controlling pests
5. **Integrated systems are resilient** — When one control method fails, others compensate
6. **Observation replaces intervention** — Understanding pest ecology allows preventive rather than reactive management

## Key Principles

1. **Biological before chemical** — Plant-derived biocides and animal predators should be the first line of defense
2. **Every animal serves multiple functions** — Pest control, manure, and food production are inseparable
3. **Soil health prevents soil pests** — Mulch, green manures, and biological diversity suppress nematodes and fungi
4. **Fence ecology matters** — Fence lines must be managed as ecosystems, not just barriers
5. **Endemic species are underutilized** — Local animals adapted to tropical conditions deserve wider trial
6. **Palm groves are polyculture nuclei** — The shade and shelter of palms create ideal conditions for integration
7. **No pest problem is intractable** — Applied commonsense and integrated natural approaches can address any pest issue
8. **Stored food protection** — Neem provides centuries-proven protection for stored grains and foods
