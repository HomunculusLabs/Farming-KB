# Sacred Mushroom Traditions in Mexico

Mexico possesses the richest and most complex tradition of sacred mushroom use
in the world. The indigenous peoples of central and southern Mexico have used
neurotropic fungi in religious, divinatory, and healing ceremonies for
centuries, possibly millennia. The rediscovery of these traditions by
Western science in the mid-20th century, catalyzed by the work of R. Gordon
Wasson, Roger Heim, and Rolf Singer, profoundly influenced both mycology and
the broader study of psychoactive substances. Understanding these traditions
requires appreciation of their linguistic diversity, ceremonial contexts, and
the complex interplay between indigenous knowledge and colonial cultural
transformation.

## Historical Overview

The use of sacred mushrooms in Mexico was first documented in the 16th century
by Fray Bernardino de Sahagun (1569-1582), who recorded the Nahuatl word
"teonanacatl" (divine mushroom). Richard Evans Schultes (1939) brought modern
scholarly attention to Sahagun's observations. The modern era began with
Wasson and Wasson (1957), whose participation in a Mazatec ceremony in
Huautla de Jimenez, Oaxaca, published in Life magazine, brought global
attention. Roger Heim (1956-1958) conducted taxonomic and cultivation studies,
while Rolf Singer provided systematic mycological analysis. Gaston Guzman
subsequently devoted his career to Psilocybe monography and documentation of
Mexican sacred mushroom traditions.

Guzman (1990a, 1990b) identified the studies of Wasson, Heim, and Singer
(1954-1958) as a watershed dividing the history of Mexican mycology into two
periods: before and after the investigation of sacred mushrooms. The
international attention generated by these studies stimulated interest in all
aspects of fungal biology in Mexico and led to the establishment of
mycological research institutions throughout the country.

Stamets (1978, 1996) proposed five historical periods: 1) ancient use by
indigenous peoples; 2) uncertain early identifications; 3) systematic
scientific investigation from the 1950s; 4) recreational use and
degeneration of traditional practices in the 1960s; and 5) a current period
of recapitulation with new species descriptions and improved chemical
analysis.

## Indigenous Names and Linguistic Diversity

Guzman (1997) documented more than 200 common names for sacred mushrooms in
Mexico, many in indigenous languages. This extraordinary linguistic diversity
reflects the importance of these fungi in traditional cultures and the
multiplicity of ethnic groups that use them. Some notable names and their
translations include:

- "Teonanacatl" (Nahuatl): divine mushroom, the most widely known term
- "Apipiltzin" (Nahuatl): kid or little boy, referring to the small stature
  of certain Psilocybe species
- "Atkad" (indigenous): mayor or leader, reflecting the authority conferred
  by the mushroom experience
- "Di nize taaya" (Mazatec): fungus of the genius, suggesting the
  supernatural wisdom believed to be imparted
- "Shi tho" (Mazatec): that eruptions thing, possibly referring to the
  sudden onset of visionary experiences
- "Teotlaquilnanacatl" (Nahuatl): divine fungus

The most commonly used Spanish-language names among indigenous communities
are "san isidros" (after Saint Isidore, patron saint of agriculture),
"pajaritos" (little birds), and "derrumbes" (landslides). These names refer
respectively to Psilocybe cubensis, P. mexicana, and P. zapotecorum.

## Major Ethnic Groups and Their Practices

### The Mazatec

The Mazatec people of the Sierra Mazateca in Oaxaca are the most widely
known practitioners of sacred mushroom ceremonies, largely due to the
publicity surrounding Maria Sabina, the curandera who hosted Wasson's famous
1955 velada. Mazatec ceremonies are conducted at night by a curandero or
curandera (healer) who ingests the mushrooms to diagnose illness, locate
lost objects, predict the future, or communicate with spirits. The mushrooms
are regarded as sentient beings that speak through the healer during the
ceremony.

### The Mixtec

The Mixtec people of Oaxaca have a distinctive tradition involving not only
psilocybin-containing gilled mushrooms but also gasteromycetes (puffballs).
Heim and Wasson (1962) first reported the use of Lycoperdon mixtecorum and L.
marginatum as "narcotic fungi" among the Mixtec. Ott et al. (1975) later
investigated these claims and found that the Mixtec narcotic puffballs were a
mixture of at least nine species of fungi, most without confirmed neurotropic
properties. The puffballs were used together with psilocybin-containing
species, and their inclusion in ceremonial practice likely reflects
traditional beliefs about the spiritual properties of the puffball form
rather than specific pharmacological effects.

### The Chinantec

The Chinantec people of Oaxaca use Dictyophora indusiata (the veiled lady
stinkhorn) in three varieties as a special narcotic fungus for divination
purposes. This unusual practice was documented by Heim and Wasson (1958) and
Wasson (1959). Dictyophora indusiata is a phalloid fungus with an elaborate
lacy veil (indusium), and its inclusion in ceremonial practice reflects the
Chinantec recognition of the fungus's distinctive appearance and possibly
its mild psychoactive or sensory-altering properties.

The Trique Indians of Alta Mixteca, Oaxaca, use Cordyceps capitata and C.
ophioglossoides in nocturnal ceremonies, eaten with Psilocybe muliercula or
alone. They place Elaphomyces specimens at the center of the ceremony room as
a "director" of the ritual, and also use Elaphomyces medicinally for wound
healing and rejuvenation.

## Ceremonial Context and Practices

Ceremonies typically take place at night in a dark room, conducted by a
curandero or curandera who ingests mushrooms to diagnose illness, locate lost
objects, or communicate with spirits. Practices may involve chanting, copal
incense, and sprinkling of liquor. Mushrooms are consumed in specific numbers
and the species used varies by ceremony purpose. Traditional knowledge
includes detailed species identification, dosing, and seasonal availability.

## Syncretism and Cultural Transformation

The encounter between indigenous mushroom traditions and Spanish Catholic
culture produced significant syncretism. Names like "san isidros" blend pre-
Columbian practices with Catholic saint veneration. A church in Chignahuapan,
Puebla, was built specifically dedicated to Ganoderma lobatum, decorated with
an image of Christ flanked by sun and moon, regarded by local indigenous
people as a saint.

The publication of Wasson's account in Life magazine in 1957 had devastating
consequences for traditional practices. The influx of counterculture
tourists seeking mushroom experiences disrupted indigenous communities and
commercialized what had been sacred traditions. The 1960s saw a degeneration
of traditional use as recreational drug culture appropriated sacred
practices, leading many countries to prohibit the use and commerce of
neurotropic fungi.

## Comparative Global Context

Mexico and New Guinea are the two major centers of documented traditional
neurotropic mushroom use. The Siberian tradition of Amanita muscaria use
among Chukchee and Koryak peoples represents a third center. Possible
traditional use has also been suggested for Africa and Colombia. However,
Mexico remains the preeminent center in terms of species diversity and
cultural complexity.
