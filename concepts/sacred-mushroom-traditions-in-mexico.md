# Sacred Mushroom Traditions in Mexico

Mexico possesses the richest and most complex tradition of sacred mushroom use
in the world. The indigenous peoples of central and southern Mexico have used
[[ethnobotanical-use-of-neurotropic-fungi-in-mexico]] religious, divinatory, and healing ceremonies for
centuries, possibly millennia. The rediscovery of these traditions by
Western science in the mid-20th century, catalyzed by the work of R. Gordon
Wasson, Roger Heim, and Rolf Singer, profoundly influenced both mycology and
the broader study of [[cultural-evolution-and-psychoactive-substances]]. Understanding these traditions
requires appreciation of their linguistic diversity, ceremonial contexts, and
the complex interplay between [[psilocybin-chemistry-stability-indigenous-knowledge]] and colonial cultural
transformation.

## Historical Overview

The use of sacred mushrooms in Mexico was first documented in the 16th century
by Fray Bernardino de Sahagun (1569-1582), who recorded the Nahuatl word
"teonanacatl" (divine mushroom). Richard [[richard-evans-schultes-updated]] (1939) brought modern
scholarly attention to Sahagun's observations. The modern era began with
Wasson and Wasson (1957), whose participation in a Mazatec ceremony in
Huautla de Jimenez, Oaxaca, published in Life magazine, brought global
attention. Roger Heim (1956-1958) conducted taxonomic and cultivation studies,
while Rolf Singer provided systematic mycological analysis. Gaston Guzman
subsequently devoted his career to Psilocybe monography and documentation of
Mexican sacred mushroom traditions.

Guzman (1990a, 1990b) identified the studies of Wasson, Heim, and Singer
(1954-1958) as a watershed dividing the history of Mexican mycology into two
periods: before and after the investigation of sacred mushrooms. The
international attention generated by these studies stimulated interest in all
aspects of fungal biology in Mexico and led to the establishment of
mycological research institutions throughout the country.

Stamets (1978, 1996) proposed [[five-historical-periods-neurotropic-fungi-discovery-guzman-1998]]: 1) ancient use by
indigenous peoples; 2) uncertain early identifications; 3) systematic
scientific investigation from the 1950s; 4) recreational use and
degeneration of traditional practices in the 1960s; and 5) a current period
of recapitulation with new species descriptions and improved chemical
analysis.

## Indigenous Names and Linguistic Diversity

Guzman (1997) documented more than 200 common names for sacred mushrooms in
Mexico, many in indigenous languages. This extraordinary linguistic diversity
reflects the importance of these fungi in traditional cultures and the
multiplicity of ethnic groups that use them. Some notable names and their
translations include:

- "Teonanacatl" (Nahuatl): divine mushroom, the most widely known term
- "Apipiltzin" (Nahuatl): kid or little boy, referring to the small stature
  of certain Psilocybe species
- "Atkad" (indigenous): mayor or leader, reflecting the authority conferred
  by the mushroom experience
- "Di nize taaya" (Mazatec): fungus of the genius, suggesting the
  supernatural wisdom believed to be imparted
- "Shi tho" (Mazatec): that eruptions thing, possibly referring to the
  sudden onset of visionary experiences
- "Teotlaquilnanacatl" (Nahuatl): divine fungus

The most commonly used Spanish-language names among indigenous communities
are "san isidros" (after Saint Isidore, patron saint of agriculture),
"pajaritos" (little birds), and "derrumbes" (landslides). These names refer
respectively to [[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]], P. mexicana, and P. zapotecorum.

## Major Ethnic Groups and Their Practices

### The Mazatec

The Mazatec people of the Sierra Mazateca in Oaxaca are the most widely
known practitioners of sacred [[stamets-psi-mazatec-mushroom-ceremonies-ethnobotany]], largely due to the
publicity surrounding Maria Sabina, the curandera who hosted Wasson's famous
1955 velada. Mazatec ceremonies are conducted at night by a curandero or
curandera (healer) who ingests the mushrooms to diagnose illness, locate
lost objects, predict the future, or communicate with spirits. The mushrooms
are regarded as sentient beings that speak through the healer during the
ceremony.
