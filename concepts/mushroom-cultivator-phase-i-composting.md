# Compost Phase I Preparation and Raw Materials

The purpose of composting is to prepare a nutritious medium that
promotes the growth of mushroom [[mycelium]] to the practical exclusion
of competitor organisms. Specifically, composting aims to: create a
physically and chemically homogeneous [[mushroom-cultivator-bulk-substrate]]; create a selective medium favoring mushrooms over
competitors; concentrate nutrients for mushroom use while exhausting
nutrients favored by competitors; and remove the heat-generating
capabilities of the substrate.

## Two Stages of Composting

The composting process is divided into two stages:

- **Phase I**: Outdoor composting involving mixing and primary
  [[decomposition]] of raw materials
- **Phase II**: Indoor pasteurization and conditioning within strict
  temperature zones in specially designed rooms

## Basic Raw Materials

### Cereal Straw

The basic raw material is cereal straw from wheat, rye, oat, barley,
and rye grass. Wheat straw is preferred due to its resilient nature,
which provides structure to the compost. Oat and barley straw tend
to flatten out and waterlog, leading to anaerobic conditions. Rye
grass straw is more resistant to decomposition, taking longer to
compost.

Wheat straw is approximately 36% cellulose, 25% pentosan, and 16%
lignin. Cellulose and pentosan are carbohydrates that yield simple
sugars upon breakdown. Lignin changes during composting to a
nitrogen-rich-lignin-humus-complex, a source of protein.

### [[falconer-horse-manure-varieties]]

When cereal straw is gathered from horse stables, it is called
"horse manure," though it is actually approximately 90% straw and
10% manure. The material includes droppings, urine, and straw
bedding. Horse manure is favored because 30-40% of the droppings
are living microorganisms that accelerate the composting process.

Commercial mushroom farms generally source horse manure from race
tracks where bedding is changed frequently, producing a lighter
material. Boarding stables generate heavier material with less
frequent bedding changes. If sawdust or shavings replace straw as
bedding, the material should be treated as a supplement, not a basic
ingredient.

### Synthetic Compost Base

Synthetic compost uses no horse manure. Straw, sometimes mixed with
hay, serves as the base ingredient. Because straw is low in potassium
and phosphorus, chicken manure is the standard additive for synthetic
composts. No composts are made exclusively of hay due to high cost
and small fiber content.

## Supplement Groups

Supplements are grouped by nitrogen content:

### Group I: High Nitrogen, No [[solomon-compost-organic-matter]]
- Ammonium sulfate (21% N)
- Ammonium nitrate (26% N)
- Urea (46% N)
- Maximum rate: 25 lbs per dry ton. Not recommended for horse
  manure composts. Used for initial straw softening in synthetics.

### Group II: 10-14% N
- Blood meal (13.5% N)
- Fish meal (10.5% N)
- Rarely used due to high cost.

### Group III: 3-7% N (Most Widely Used)
- Malt sprouts (4% N)
- Brewers' grains (3-5% N)
- Cottonseed meal (6.5% N)
- Peanut meal (6.5% N)
- Chicken manure (3-6% N)

### Group IV: Low Nitrogen, High Carbohydrate
- Grape pomace (1.5% N)
- Sugar beet pulp (1.5% N)
- Molasses (0.5% N)
- Cottonseed hulls (1% N)
- Excellent temperature boosters; add at 250 lbs per dry ton.

### Group V: [[animal-manure-guide]]s
- Cow manure (0.5% N)
- Pig manure (0.3-0.8% N)
- Rarely used except where horses and chickens are unavailable.

### Group VI: Hay
- Alfalfa (2.0-2.5% N) and clover (2% N), used at 20% of dry weight.

### Group VII: Minerals
- **Gypsum** (calcium sulfate): Essential for all composts at 50-100
  lbs per dry ton. Improves physical structure, increases water
  holding capacity, counteracts harmful concentrations of K, Mg, P,
  and Na, and supplies calcium for mushroom metabolism.
- **Limestone flour** (calcium carbonate): Used to buffer acidic
  supplements like grape pomace (pH 4).

## Carbon:Nitrogen Ratio

A well-balanced compost holds an optimum nutritional level. The C:N
ratio gradually decreases: approximately 30:1 at make-up, 20:1 at
filling, and 17:1 at spawning. Over-supplementation with nitrogen
causes prolonged ammonia release, while excess carbohydrates leave
residual carbon compounds that feed competitors.
