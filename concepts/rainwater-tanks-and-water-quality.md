# Rainwater Tanks and Water Quality

## Overview

Rainwater tanks are the most direct form of water harvesting — collecting precipitation from roofs and storing it for domestic and agricultural use. In [[water-management]].

## Cultural Context and Precedent

The use of domestic rainwater tanks varies dramatically across cultures, often more due to habit and regulation than to rainfall availability:

- **Australia** has a strong tradition of domestic rainwater tanks across a wide variety of surface storage types
- **Europe, the USA, and India** (where clean drinking water is rare) have surprisingly few significant domestic rainwater tanks
- **British, American, and Brazilian farmers** rarely use multiple earth storages of water, preferring expensive pipelines and bores even where local rainfall exceeds needs

This discrepancy illustrates how cultural inertia and institutional preferences can override logical, low-cost water solutions. Mollison advocates that legislation requiring tanks on every roof would produce multiple benefits: ensuring strategic water reserves, promoting clean air regulations, and encouraging conservative water use.

## Tank Sizing and Yield Calculation

### Sizing Guidelines

- Approximately **22,500 liters** (about 6,000 gallons) provides a family with all needed water for a year, including drinking, showers, cooking, and modest garden irrigation via trickle systems
- Tank water is renewed by rainfall at any time of year
- Every roof, whether domestic or industrial, would fill multiple tanks of this size

### Yield Calculation

The expected annual yield from a roof catchment is calculated simply:

```
Yield (liters) = Roof Area (m²) × Average Annual Rainfall (mm)
```

For imperial units:
```
Yield (gallons) = Roof Area (ft²) × Average Annual Rainfall (inches) × 0.623
```

## Water Quality and Contamination Prevention

### First Flush Diversion

The first flow-off of water from a roof carries the highest concentration of contaminants — accumulated dust, bird droppings, leaf litter, and atmospheric pollutants. This first flush must be rejected and diverted to gardens or swales rather than entering the tank.

Two principal methods for first flush rejection are described:

1. **Diverter pipe systems** — The initial flow fills a chamber or pipe section; once full, subsequent cleaner water flows to the tank
2. **Flip-top or float-valve systems** — Mechanical devices that automatically divert the first flow and then switch to tank filling

### Excluding Insects, Birds, and Rodents

Multiple design features work together to exclude pests from stored water:

- **"U" pipe entry and exit** — Water enters and exits through pipes configured with U-bends that create water seals preventing insect entry
- **Sealed tank roof** — Prevents direct access by birds, rodents, and insects
- **Overflow pipe** — Empties to a gravel-filled [[swale]], maintaining the water seal and preventing backflow of contaminants

For birds that persistently perch on roof ridges (a source of droppings), stretching fine wires or thread along the ridge as a 10 cm (4 inch) high "fence" effectively discourages them without harm.

### Gutter Systems

Gutters on roofing collect and direct rainwater to downpipes and tanks. Several approaches keep gutters clean and functional:

- **Regular cleaning** of standard gutters
- **Leaf-free gutter systems** — Several commercial designs exist (3–4 major types) that exclude leaves while allowing water flow
- **Cone-to-pipe systems** — Useful for collecting water from rock domes and irregular surfaces
- **Self-cleaning gutters** — Designed with features that prevent leaf accumulation

Contamination from dust and leaves that does reach the tank is generally harmless. Organic materials "fix" as an active biological velvety film on tank walls and bases — this is a natural and beneficial process.

### Outlet Placement

Taps or outlet pipes should be fitted **15–20 cm (6–8 inches) above the tank floor**. This allows the biological film and any settled sediment to remain undisturbed at the bottom, ensuring that drawn water is clean and clear.

### Water Hardness and Health

A net or bag of **limestone, shell, or marble chips** suspended in the tank creates hard (alkaline) water through slow dissolution of calcium carbonate. This has important health implications:

- Alkaline water **prevents heavy metal uptake** from tank walls and piping
- Epidemiological evidence links hard water to **decreased incidence of heart attacks** in communities using tank water
- While washing and shower water can be soft (acidic), drinking water is best made alkaline

## Concrete Tank Construction

### Forms and Economics

- Simple forms for making concrete tanks cost a few hundred dollars and can be reused hundreds of times
- Larger tanks are relatively cheaper than smaller ones (less concrete surface area per unit of volume)
- Above approximately 22,500 liters, tanks are usually **poured on site**
- Below this size, they are typically **manufactured centrally and transported**
- Earth tanks excavated below grade cost approximately **one-tenth** the price of concrete tanks above grade

## Integration with Site Water Systems

Rainwater tanks should not be considered in isolation but as part of an integrated site water management system:

### Tank Overflow

Every tank should have an overflow that leads to productive use:

- **Swales** — Tank overflow can feed swale systems for groundwater recharge and tree irrigation
- **Ponds and wetlands** — Excess water supports aquatic ecosystems
- **Greywater systems** — Combined with household greywater for additional irrigation capacity (provided boron-based detergents are not used)

### Sequential Use Principle

In a well-designed system, water flows through multiple uses before leaving the site:

1. **First use:** Domestic drinking, cooking, and washing from roof tank
2. **Second use:** Greywater from showers and sinks irrigates gardens
3. **Third use:** Tank overflow and stormwater feed swales and trees
4. **Fourth use:** Excess from swales recharges groundwater or feeds downstream storages

## Policy Implications

Mollison makes a strong case for policy reform around water storage:

- Legislation requiring rainwater tanks on every new building would ensure strategic water reserves at every household
- Local clean air regulations would be better observed when residents directly consume roof-collected water
- Householders would naturally become more conservative in water use when they manage their own supply
- Distributed tank systems reduce the enormous infrastructure costs and energy requirements of centralized water supply
- The chlorinated, metallic, and potentially asbestos-contaminated water of centralized systems is linked to serious health problems including cancer, bone marrow failure, and gastrointestinal disorders

## Practical Recommendations

1. Install the largest practical tank for the site — economies of scale favor larger tanks
2. Always include a first-flush diverter system
3. Ensure all entry and exit points are sealed against pest entry
4. Position outlets above the tank floor to allow sediment settlement
5. Suspend limestone or marble chips in the tank for alkaline water
6. Connect tank overflow to swales or other productive water use
7. Use self-cleaning gutters or maintain gutters regularly
8. In areas with bird problems, install wire deterrents on roof ridges
9. Consider below-grade (earth) tanks where possible for cost savings
10. Treat drinking water tanks with biological methods rather than chemical treatments
