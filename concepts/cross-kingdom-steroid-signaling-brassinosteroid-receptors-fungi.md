## Cross-Kingdom Steroid Signaling: Brassinosteroid Receptors in Fungi

## Overview

The observation that brassinosteroids — a class of steroidal plant growth regulators — produce dramatic growth-promoting effects in fungal cultures raises a fundamental question about cross-kingdom signaling mechanisms. Brassinosteroids were identified as a new group of phytohormones, with approximately 30 members detected across Angiospermae, Gymnospermae, and green algae. The 1990 Gartz study demonstrating that 22S,23S-homobrassinolide accelerates mycelial growth and fruiting in *Psilocybe cubensis* suggests that fungi possess molecular machinery capable of responding to plant-derived steroidal signals.

## The Cross-Kingdom Signaling Paradox

In plants, brassinosteroids act through well-characterized receptor pathways:

- **BRI1 (BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE 1)**: A leucine-rich repeat receptor-like kinase (LRR-RLK) that perceives brassinosteroids at the cell surface.
- **BAK1 (BRI1-ASSOCIATED KINASE 1)**: A co-receptor that forms a heterodimer with BRI1 upon brassinolide binding.
- **Downstream cascade**: Phosphorylation events activate BSK kinases, inhibit BIN2 (a GSK3-like kinase), and ultimately release transcription factors (BZR1/BES1) that regulate growth-related gene expression.

Fungi do not possess homologs of BRI1 or BAK1. The fungal cell signaling toolkit relies on different receptor classes: G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs), two-component histidine kinases, and receptor-type guanylyl cyclases. This raises the question: how do fungi detect and respond to brassinosteroids?

## Proposed Mechanisms of Fungal Brassinosteroid Perception

Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain fungal sensitivity to brassinosteroids:

### 1. Membrane Perturbation Hypothesis

Brassinosteroids are amphipathic steroidal molecules that may directly interact with fungal plasma membrane lipids. The steroid ring system could insert into the lipid bilayer, altering membrane fluidity and affecting the function of membrane-embedded receptors and transporters. This nonspecific mechanism would not require a dedicated brassinosteroid receptor:

- Steroid insertion changes membrane packing density, potentially activating mechanosensitive ion channels.
- Altered membrane properties could enhance nutrient uptake rates, explaining the observed growth acceleration.
- This mechanism is consistent with the dose-response pattern observed, where extremely low concentrations (10⁻² ppm) produce measurable effects.

### 2. Shared Steroid-Binding Domains

Fungi produce their own sterols (ergosterol, fungisterol) and possess enzymes that bind and modify steroidal compounds. It is plausible that existing fungal steroid-processing enzymes or binding proteins could interact with brassinosteroids:

- [[brassinosteroid-growth-promotion-psilocybe-cubensis]]
- [[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]]** responds to mammalian estrogen and progesterone, modulating hyphal growth and biofilm formation.
- **[[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] in several mushroom species.
- **Abscisic acid**: Certain fungal pathogens produce abscisic acid as a virulence factor, and some saprophytic fungi respond to exogenous ABA.

This broad sensitivity to plant hormones suggests that fungi possess a general capacity for cross-kingdom chemical communication, of which brassinosteroid responsiveness may be one component of a larger signaling repertoire.
