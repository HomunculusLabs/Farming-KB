# Tricholoma, Pleurotus, and White-Spored Edible Genera

## Overview

Among the white-spored agarics, several genera beyond Lepiota offer edible species of considerable interest to foragers, cultivators, and mycologists. The genera **Tricholoma** (blewitts and allies), **Pleurotus** ([[oyster-mushrooms]]), **Collybia**, **Lactarius**, **Russula**, and **Cantharellus** together represent a broad spectrum of edible fungi with diverse habitats, flavors, and cultivation potential. Their study illuminates both the possibilities and challenges of expanding [[mushroom-cultivation]] beyond the traditional *[[agaricus-campestris]]*.

## Tricholoma: The Blewitts

### Genus Characteristics

Tricholoma embraces a very large number of mushroom species, nearly all of considerable size. Caps seldom measure less than two inches in diameter and often reach five inches. Key identifying features include:

- A **central fleshy stem**, homogeneous with the cap
- **Nearly white gills**, though frequently somewhat dingy or slightly stained
- Spores always **white or grayish lilac** when caught in quantity
- Gills terminate with a **sinus or notch** before attaching to the stem
- **No ring** on the stem
- **No basal cup** (volva)

### Edibility and Safety

Remarkably, among the 200 or more species in the genus, only a **single species has been marked even suspicious**, and this inedible one is uncommon and could never be mistaken for any of the larger species. This makes Tricholoma one of the safest genera for experimentation by knowledgeable foragers — though collectors should first be sure they can distinguish Tricholoma from the milk-producing fungi (Lactarius) and the related genus Russula.

### Key Species

**Tricholoma personatum** — The Masked Tricholoma. Named for its slight variation in color. Generally nearly white, but both stem and cap are usually somewhat relieved by a light purplish or lilac tint, sometimes more nearly ashen. The flesh is thick, firm, and white; gills, while often violet-tinted when young, may become pallid or brown-stained. This species is found on the markets of Europe and Asia and is invariably considered edible where it occurs in sufficient quantity to be of economic importance.

**Tricholoma nudum** — The Naked Tricholoma. Named for the smooth surface and margin of the cap. Not always readily distinguished from T. personatum, but figured as somewhat smaller and becoming reddish-tinted with age. The flesh also partakes somewhat lightly of this color.

**Tricholoma gambosum** — St. George's Mushroom. Sold on the Munich market in 1901 to the extent of about 25,000 pounds. Cream white, often somewhat darker at the middle, may appear in definite "fairy rings" in pastures and meadows. Has thick flesh and a very strong odor — fresh mushrooms become objectionable in a closed room, but in cooking all objectionable characters are said to be lost. In England it is known as St. George's mushroom, appearing usually in the spring.

### Cultivation Experiments

Duggar grew mycelium of T. personatum in pure culture since 1903. It grows readily upon sterilized leaves or leaf-mould as well as upon stable manure. Key findings:

- A large pot of sterilized leaves was completely penetrated with mycelium in about six weeks
- Numerous small sporophores had begun to form before the experiment was accidentally lost
- Spawn was prepared and a bed made under ordinary conditions using more completely fermented stable manure than for Agaricus
- Despite variable temperature in the test room (which prevented Agaricus growth), Tricholoma began fruiting on one side of the bed after two months
- Fine heavy mushrooms were produced in the small fruiting area
- Later, Tricholoma was grown satisfactorily in a small bed of well-fermented leaves (maple, birch, and beech)
- Matruchot in Paris also announced successful culture of this species

### Cultivation Potential

Tricholoma offers several potential advantages over Agaricus:

- Can be grown on **leaves** — a substrate free from all objections to compost
- Capacity to fruit at a **wider range of temperatures**
- Some consider the quality superior to Agaricus campestris

However, limitations exist:

- No indication it will **excel Agaricus in yield**
- **Not so satisfactory for marketing and shipping**
- Requires more completely fermented materials

## Pleurotus: The Oyster Mushrooms

### Pleurotus ostreatus — The Oyster Mushroom

Widely distributed and often abundant throughout moist temperate regions. Key characteristics:

- Produced in **clusters or aggregates** forming an attractive bracket or "comb"
- Upper surface varies from white to grayish (gray when conditions are less favorable)
- **White gills** that are strongly **decurrent** (coursing down the stem)
- Each individual has pileus and fairly distinct stem, the latter **excentric** (clearly at one edge)
- Found from early spring until frost; early autumn is generally the season of heaviest fruitage
- Special habitat: decaying trunks and fallen logs of many deciduous trees

The name "Oyster Mushroom" may derive from the wavy outline of the pileus, the overall form looking down upon it, and the grayish tint sometimes observed. The mycelium grows vigorously in pure culture on a variety of substrata. While not regarded as one of the better-flavored species, it possesses obvious advantages in **frequency, size, and unmistakable form characters**. No species of Pleurotus is considered even suspicious — making it one of the safest genera for foragers.

### Related Species

- **P. ulmarius** — distinguished by a somewhat more definite stem
- **P. sapidus** — regarded by many as synonymous with P. ostreatus, though some differentiate it on account of a **lilac tint** exhibited by spores when caught fresh in quantity

## Collybia

Many edible species exist in this genus, but with one exception they do not occur in sufficient abundance for horticultural interest. Spores are white and the pileus may be white or variously colored.

**Collybia velutipes** — Occurs in clusters on stumps and roots in late fall or early winter. Pileus is reddish brown; plants are somewhat tough in texture but pronounced to be of **excellent flavor**.

## Lactarius: The Milk Mushrooms

Species yielding a milky juice when gills are cut or injured. Common edible species include:

- **Lactarius deliciosus** — Yellow buff or light orange mottled with darker spots or zones; the juice is **colored** (orange-red). Often about three inches high.
- **L. volemus** — Somewhat smaller, uniform brown-orange or tawny color with **white juice**
- The Peppery Lactarius — A large white species that is very acrid and should be avoided

## Russula

Closely related to Lactarius but **lacking milky juice**. Various species occur with pilei white, greenish, violaceous, or red. Found in woods in summer and early autumn. Some are edible and some are not — careful identification is essential.

## Cantharellus cibarius: The Chanterelle

Barely an agaric, as the gills are often almost **vein-like, rounded on the margin, and often reticulate**. Uniformly yellow to orange in color, small, and more or less unsymmetrical in form. An inhabitant of woods, much used for food in Europe. The Chanterelle is one of the most prized wild edible mushrooms, valued for its peppery, apricot-like flavor and firm texture.

## Polyporaceae: The Pore Fungi

The Polyporaceae have a fruiting layer consisting of the lining surface of minute tubes, with pore mouths appearing on the under surface of the pileus. While most are shelving, stalkless bracket fungi, the fleshy representatives include important edible genera:

- **Boletus** — The most important genus for edible species; characterized by a central stalk, thick fleshy cap with a separable pore layer, often 6+ inches in diameter. Flesh frequently changes color rapidly when cut or bruised
- **Fistulina** — Fleshy bracket-forming genera
- **Polyporus sulphureus** — Forms immense clusters of sulphur-yellow and orange bracket-like sporophores on trees and stumps ([[chicken-of-the-woods]])
