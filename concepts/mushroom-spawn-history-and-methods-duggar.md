## Mushroom Spawn History and Methods

Benjamin Minge Duggar's 1905 work *Mushroom Growing* provides one of the most thorough early American accounts of mushroom [[agar-culture-techniques-detailed]] that revolutionized the industry.

## Types of Commercial Spawn

Two principal types of mushroom spawn dominated the early commercial trade. **Brick spawn** was the standard in England and the United States — compact blocks of manure-based compost measuring approximately 5.5 by 8.5 by 1.5 inches, dried and inoculated with mycelium. Brick spawn exhibited superior keeping quality and ease of handling, making it the preferred form for American growers.

**Flake spawn** held a monopoly in the French market. French growers typically prepared their own supply using a "starter" of virgin spawn to inoculate compost beds, which were then broken apart into flakes for distribution. However, flake spawn rapidly lost vitality under the conditions of international importation, making it unreliable for American use. The paradox was noted that America imported canned mushrooms from France but sourced most of its spawn from England.

## Virgin Spawn and the Chance Method

From the earliest records of mushroom culture, growers depended upon the spontaneous or native occurrence of spawn in pastures, compost piles, or elsewhere. This material was known as **virgin spawn** and presumably arose from the accidental germination of spores. In England, "mill-track" virgin spawn — found in the well-trodden soil and manure of horse paths at mills — was especially sought, though it later became merely a trade name.

The process of working with virgin spawn involved finding natural mushroom occurrences in a favorable locality, removing the sod, preparing small trenches filled with stable manure, and returning the sod. Into this prepared ground the spawn would grow and spread over several weeks. This material could then be used directly to inoculate mushroom beds, or more commonly to spawn compost intended for producing larger quantities of crop spawn.

In the vicinity of Paris, some persons made a business of searching for virgin spawn, selling it to growers at premium prices. They became adept at detecting differences in the character of growth and quality of odors, enabling them to distinguish not only *[[gartz-gymnopilus-purpuratus-south-american-mushroom-in-europe]] growing. Without pure culture methods, growers were entirely dependent on chance discoveries of wild spawn, with no ability to select for desirable characteristics or maintain strain quality.

## The Tissue Culture Revolution

In 1902, while at the Bureau of Plant Industry, U.S. Department of Agriculture, Duggar originated a method of securing pure cultures of *Agaricus campestris* and other mushrooms through **tissue culture**. The principle was simple but revolutionary: a small piece of the inner tissue of a fresh mushroom, when placed on any suitable sterile nutrient medium, would promptly develop mycelium, yielding a pure culture of spawn.

The technique required careful aseptic procedure. The mushroom was first washed to remove dust, then broken open longitudinally. With a sterilized scalpel or forceps, small pieces of internal tissue were removed and transferred to sterile test tubes containing manure or half-decayed leaves as substrate. The tubes were plugged with cotton batting to prevent airborne contamination.

Selection could be practiced at the inoculation stage. One procured mushrooms from beds in full bearing that represented the most desirable qualities — productivity, flavor, color, and other characteristics were all considered. Plants as young as possible were preferred, with the veil still intact being especially desirable.

## Pure Culture Methods and Sterilization

Duggar's method, described in Bulletin 85 of the Bureau of Plant Industry, involved sterilizing culture tubes at approximately 150 degrees C in a dry oven for nearly an hour, or alternatively steaming at 15 pounds pressure in an autoclave for 15 to 30 minutes. When using an open water bath at 100 degrees C, boiling was required for at least an hour on each of two or three successive days to ensure complete sterilization.

The inoculated tubes were kept out of dust at approximately room temperature. Within several days slight growth from the tissue became evident, and in three weeks the mold-like development of mycelium should spread throughout the medium in the tube. The contents of one large tube could spawn several bricks when carefully employed.

Duggar later regarded it as wiser to use larger containers for spawn and to make no transfers whatever. When containers exceeded about one inch in diameter, steaming times needed to be increased. A mixture of leaf mould and well-fermented manure as substratum produced more rapid growth than manure alone.

## Impact on American Mushroom Growing

The experimental pure culture spawn made and distributed under the auspices of the Department of Agriculture was very successful. By the 1913-1914 season, 80 to 90 percent of the mushrooms grown in America were reported to be from American spawn of pure culture origin, and some American spawn was actually exported to Europe. Poor spawn had been largely driven from the markets, and the quality of imported English spawn had necessarily improved as a result of successful competition.

Duggar tested one strain by continuous propagation from season to season through tissue cultures for five years, and it consistently showed high yield and quality, suggesting that running-out was more likely due to improper care of pure cultures than to any inherent limitation of the tissue culture method itself.

## Spawn Vitality and Storage

Tests of spawn of various ages and [[oyster-mushrooms]], and certain other species, the spores of the common cultivated mushroom germinated only sporadically at best. Costantin and Matruchot reported producing mushroom spawn from spores, but their methods remained secret. Their process found practical application at a department of the Pasteur Institute and was later taken over by a Parisian corporation.

Dr. Ferguson's investigations on spore germination were reported in Bulletin 16 of the Bureau of Plant Industry. Despite the difficulty, Duggar did not abandon hope that a practical spore method might be developed, though he acknowledged that even if found, it might not prove more practicable than the tissue culture method that had already driven the rapid development of American mushroom growing.

Repin in Paris also referenced a secret method for effecting spore germination, which was practically applied in making "plate" spawn by one of the large seed firms of Paris, though this spawn never came into very general use.
