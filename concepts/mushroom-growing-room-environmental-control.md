    11|# Mushroom Growing Room Environmental Control System
    12|
    13|The mushroom growing room must maintain a selected temperature range at
    14|high relative humidities. This is accomplished through adequate
    15|insulation and an environmental control system providing heating,
    16|cooling, humidification, and air handling. [[paul-stamets]] details the
    17|design parameters in Chapter IV of The Mushroom Cultivator.
    18|
    19|## Fresh Air Supply and Ventilation
    20|
    21|Fresh air serves critical functions in [[mushroom-cultivator-fruiting-conditions]]:
    22|
    23|-   Supplies oxygen to growing mushrooms.
    24|-   Carries away CO2 produced by mycelium and fruitbodies.
    25|-   Facilitates moisture evaporation from the cropping surface.
    26|
    27|### Fan Sizing
    28|
    29|**Air changes per hour** determine fan requirements:
    30|
    31|-   *[[agaricus-brunnescens]]*: 4-6 changes per hour.
    32|-   *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]*: 2-3 changes per hour.
    33|
    34|**Formula for required CFM:**
    35|CFM = (air changes/hour x free air space in cu. ft.) / 60
    36|
    37|Example: For an 8 x 8 x 15 ft room (960 cu. ft.) with beds occupying
    38|160 cu. ft. (free air = 800 cu. ft.):
    39|4 x 800 / 60 = 53.3 CFM required
    40|
    41|### Air-to-Bed Ratio
    42|
    43|The cubic feet of free air space divided by square feet of cropping
    44|surface. Agaricus growers find 5:1 optimum. This ratio is critical
    45|because increased substrate generates heat and CO2 beyond handling
    46|capacity. A large free air space buffers these changes. Growing on thin
    47|grain layers (1-3 inches) produces less CO2 than 8 inches of compost,
    48|allowing lower ratios (4:1 or less).
    49|
    50|### Air Ducting
    51|
    52|Standard inflatable polyethylene tubing sized to the fan diameter:
    53|-   Ducts run at ceiling level along the room.
    54|-   Outlet holes are sized so total hole area equals the duct cross
    55|    section.
    56|-   Hole spacing equals 1/4 the distance from duct to wall or floor.
    57|-   **Entrainment** — air discharge at sufficient velocity draws in
    58|    surrounding room air, enhancing circulation and eliminating dead-air
    59|    pockets.
    60|
    61|### Air Recirculation
    62|
    63|A mixing box with adjustable damper proportions fresh and recirculated
    64|air. During spawn running, the damper seals the fresh air inlet for full
    65|recirculation. As fresh air is introduced, room air is displaced through
    66|exhaust vents or cracks around the door.
    67|
    68|## Air Filtration
    69|
    70|Filters screen atmospheric dust, spores, bacteria, and plant pollen:
    71|
    72|-   **High-efficiency extended surface filters** — 0.3 micron at 90-95%
    73|    efficiency; 5.0 micron at 99% efficiency.
    74|-   **HEPA filters** — 0.1-0.3 microns at 99.96-99.99% efficiency;
    75|    0.75-1.00 inches static pressure. Special waterproof filters operate
    76|    in 100% humidity.
    77|-   Protect HEPA filters with a 1-inch prefilter of open-celled foam or
    78|    fiberglass.
    79|
    80|## Heating Systems
    81|
    82|Two approaches:
    83|
    84|-   **Dry heat** — hot water or steam through closed pipe systems,
    85|    resistance coils, or baseboard heaters. Lowers air moisture content.
    86|-   **Live steam** — maintains temperature AND humidity simultaneously.
    87|    Control volumetrically via hand valve rather than on/off.
    88|
    89|The growing room should take full advantage of substrate heat generation
    90|by matching the air-to-bed ratio to substrate type.
    91|
    92|## Cooling Systems
    93|
    94|-   **Commercial**: cooling coils with cold water or glycol, central
    95|    chillers, or refrigeration units. These dehumidify by condensation.
    96|-   **Home cultivator**: fresh air cooling is most practical. Shut off or
    97|    reduce fresh air during hot days; fully open at night when
    98|    temperatures are lowest.
    99|
   100|## Humidification
   101|
   102|Three primary methods:
   103|
   104|1.  **Steam injection** — into the air duct downstream of the fan and
   105|    filter. Household vaporizers work for small rooms. Cold fresh air
   106|    has less moisture absorption capacity.
   107|2.  **Atomizing nozzles** — project a fine mist into the air stream.
   108|    Appropriate nozzles emit 0.5-1.0 gallons/hour at 20-30 psi.
   109|3.  **Evaporative pad systems** — air passes through water-saturated
   110|    absorbent material, similar to a "swamp cooler." Provides both
   111|    humidification and temperature regulation.
   112|
   113|## Thermostats and Humidistats
   114|
   115|Standard on/off instruments cause surges in heat and humidity. **Modulating
   116|thermostats** supply heat continuously in proportion to deviation from
   117|the desired temperature. Hand valves used alone or with on/off
   118|instruments provide similar positive control by keeping supply volume
   119|just below the cut-off point.
   120|
   121|## Lighting
   122|
   123|Photosensitive species require light for proper development:
   124|
   125|-   Fluorescent fixtures provide broadest coverage; mount on side walls
   126|    or ceiling above the center aisle.
   127|-   Remove ballasts outside the room to eliminate heat and drying.
   128|-   Best tubes: those closest to natural outdoor light with at least 140
   129|    microwatts/10nm/lumen of blue spectra (440-495 nm).
   130|-   "Daylite 65" (6500K) and "Vita-Lite" (5500K) fluorescent tubes are
   131|    recommended.
   132|
   133|## Monitoring Equipment
   134|
   135|Essential for the sensitive growing environment:
   136|
   137|-   Maximum-minimum thermometers for temperature fluctuations.
   138|-   Hygrometers or sling psychrometers for humidity (calibrate
   139|    periodically).
   140|-   Advanced equipment: CO2 detectors, moisture meters, anemometers,
   141|    and light measuring devices.
