# Ecological Succession in Design

[[permaculture]], succession is not merely observed — it is actively designed and accelerated, allowing designers to establish productive, self-maintaining systems in a fraction of the time natural succession would require.

## Natural Succession

Nature demonstrates a predictable sequence of establishment on devastated landscapes such as basalt flows, ice-planed sites, and flood-swept areas:

1. **Pioneer phase:** Hardy, fast-growing "weeds" (thistles, Lantana, bracken) colonize overgrazed, eroded, or burned areas. These species stabilize water flow, begin rebuilding soil, and create microclimates.
2. **Transitional phase:** Shrubs and short-lived trees establish, further improving soil quality and providing shelter for subsequent species.
3. **Climax phase:** Long-term forest or tree crop species take dominance, creating a stable, self-regulating ecosystem.

Each stage prepares conditions for the next. Pioneer species are not failures or mistakes — they are essential soil-builders, windbreaks, and nurse crops that enable their own successors to thrive.

## Designed Succession: Accelerating Evolution

Unlike natural succession, which unfolds over decades or centuries, **permaculture designers can place most succession elements in a single planting**. The pioneers, ground covers, understory species, tree legumes, herbaceous crops, mulch species, long-term windbreaks, and final tree crops are all established simultaneously.

### The Stacked Planting Approach

A hectare designed for succession typically requires 4,000–8,000 plants, demanding a preliminary nursery phase. The workflow proceeds as:

1. **Nursery phase:** Propagate all required species in pots while site preparation proceeds
2. **Site preparation:** Fence, shape soil (roads, dams, swales, terraces, paths), establish water supply
3. **Planting:** Install all species to a carefully-designed long-term plan
4. **Evolution:** Manage the system through its successional stages over 5–50 years

### Timeline of Yields

A well-designed forest succession delivers continuous yields:

- **Short-term (1–3 years):** Annual vegetables, herbs, coppice from fast-growing trees
- **Medium-term (3–15 years):** Pole timbers, fruit from establishing trees, honey, livestock forage
- **Long-term (15–50+ years):** Mature fruit, nuts, bark products, plank timber, stable ecosystem services

### Benefits of Immediate Multi-Species Planting

When the full successional array is planted at once, remarkable changes occur within two to three years:

- **On-site mulch production** eliminates the need for imported organic matter
- **Weed suppression** through strategic ground covers and shading
- **Wind moderation** from fast-growing pioneer and nurse trees
- **Frost protection** from established canopy layers
- **Continuous cropping** as annuals and herbaceous perennials control unwanted grasses

For example, radish or turnip planted with tree seedlings control grasses until the young tree provides its own grass suppression through shading. This relay approach ensures no niche is left unoccupied by undesired species.

## System Evolution Stages

Mollison identifies three broad phases in the evolution of a designed system:

**Stage A — Establishment:** The area is fenced and a complex of species is planted and protected from grazers. Ponds are established. Only small livestock (chickens) and some annual crops can be harvested. The focus is on soil building and shelter creation.

**Stage B — Semi-Hardy Phase:** Geese, fish, and shellfish are introduced. Crops expand to include aquatic plant species. The system begins generating its own fertility cycles. Diversity and biomass increase significantly.

**Stage C — Mature/Evolved Phase:** The system provides forage, firewood, aquatic products, and animal products. Larger foragers (sheep, pigs) can be grown seasonally. The system produces its own mulch and fertilizers. **Management replaces energy input** — the system requires tending rather than fueling. A variety of marketable yields becomes available, including information and educational value.

## The Nucleus Development Strategy

Perhaps the most important practical principle in system establishment is **starting small and expanding outward**:

1. **Design thoroughly on paper** before breaking ground
2. **Set priorities** based on economic reality and available resources
3. **Locate and trade for components** locally or cheaply
4. **Develop a nucleus completely** before expanding
5. **Expand using species proved suited** to the site

### Why Nucleus Development Works

- A well-managed 4m² garden can supply 40–60% of a household's food (as demonstrated in the Philippines)
- Ten critical trees (oils, citrus, nuts, storable fruit), well protected, manured, and watered, will outperform a hundred or a thousand poorly established trees where 60% may be lost
- Broadscale systems incur broadscale losses and inefficiencies
- Small successes build morale and competence

### The Progression

The recommended progression follows a clear path:
1. **Gain a foothold** — establish the first productive elements
2. **Stabilize a small area** — prove the system works locally
3. **Develop self-reliance** — reduce external inputs
4. **Look for exportable yields** — only after self-sufficiency is achieved

## Practical Pre-Conditions for Establishment

Before any planting begins, four essential precursors must be in place:

1. **Fencing or hedgerow** — protection from grazing animals
2. **Soil rehabilitation** — mulching, loosening compacted soil
3. **Erosion control** — swales, contour planting, ground cover
4. **Water supply** — reliable irrigation or water harvesting

Soil shaping for roads, dams, swales, terraces, and paths must be finalized before planting commences. Zone 1 and Zone 2 receive priority as they support the household and save the most expense.

## Managing the Transition

Succession design is not a rigid plan but a **process of constant transition** guided by observation and flexibility:

- Break the job into small, easily achieved stages
- Never draw up long task lists — just plan the next stage
- The overall design provides context for each smaller stage
- Adjust plans based on trial results and new information
- Remain open and non-discriminatory in techniques

The success of any design ultimately depends on how it is accepted and implemented by the people living within it. Grand centralized schemes more often result in ruins than in stable, well-maintained ecologies. Responsible design recommends to others the way you have found it possible to work or live in a similar situation.
