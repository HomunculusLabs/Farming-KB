# Farm Strategies for Yield Increase

[[permaculture]] applies its design principles to the practical scale of farms and agricultural holdings through a comprehensive set of site strategies. These strategies address water, land form, soil, forestry, shelter, markets, and social organization. When combined, their interactive effects raise yields far beyond what any single intervention can achieve — and they do so through permanent, durable modifications rather than ongoing expensive inputs.

## Water Storage (12-20% of Landscape)

Dedicating 12-20% of the landscape to water storage is one of the highest-return strategies available. The benefits cascade across multiple systems:

1. **Direct product increase:** Water is more productive per unit area than land. Fish are more efficient at food conversion than cattle, so aquaculture yields more animal protein per hectare than pasture.
2. **Increased land productivity:** Irrigation from stored water and nutrient-rich fish manure both boost crop yields on remaining land.
3. **Integration effects:** Ducks on water bodies provide pest and weed control plus manure, increasing yields in and around ponds.
4. **Microclimatic buffering:** Water bodies moderate temperature extremes, reducing frost damage and heat stress for adjacent crops and livestock.

## Land Forming

Reshaping land contours to manage water flow produces multiple permanent benefits:

1. **Even irrigation:** Elimination of dry patches and waterlogging through laser-levelled fields.
2. **Land stability:** Dramatic reduction of soil loss from water run-off and salting.
3. **Energy savings:** Gravity flow replaces pumped water where site topography permits.
4. **Water recycling:** Formed land enables capture and reuse of water that would otherwise be lost.

## Soil Development

Investment in soil pays compound returns over time:

1. **Deeper root penetration:** Well-structured soil allows crops to access water and nutrients from greater depth.
2. **Zero run-off:** Absorptive soils capture all rainfall, eliminating erosion and maximizing moisture availability.
3. **Microclimate buffering:** Soils rich in organic matter moderate temperature fluctuations around root zones.
4. **Nutrient supply:** Healthy soil biology continuously cycles and makes available essential plant nutrients.

## Forestry and Tree Systems (20-30% of Landscape)

Allocating 20-30% of farmland to integrated tree systems (not industrial forestry) generates an extraordinary range of benefits:

1. **Shelter effects:** Windbreaks increase plant yields, animal protein production, and microclimate buffering both above and below ground.
2. **Increased carrying capacity:** Shrubs and trees provide additional forage, extending the productive capacity of the land.
3. **Nutrient recycling:** Leguminous trees and deep-rooted species cycle nutrients from subsoil layers, reducing fertilizer requirements.
4. **Intrinsic forest products:** Nectar for honey, seeds, nuts, fruits, and firewood from fallen timber provide diversified income streams.
5. **Biological pest control:** Tree systems provide habitat for insect predators and birds that suppress pest populations in adjacent crops.
6. **Wildlife corridors:** Connected tree belts enable wildlife movement across the landscape, supporting biodiversity and ecological resilience.

## Shelterbelt and Tree Benefits

Beyond the forestry allocation, individual shelterbelt plantings provide specific measurable benefits:

1. **Precipitation increase:** Night condensation on tree canopies and improved water penetration add effective rainfall.
2. **Perennial advantage:** Perennials outperform annuals in bulk production, energy savings, and length of productive yield period.
3. **Cross-wind rainfall:** Trees planted across prevailing wind directions can increase local rainfall through orographic effects.
4. **Drought-proof stockfeed:** Selected self-foraging browse species provide feed during dry periods when pastures fail.
5. **On-farm timber:** Durable timber for fence posts and construction reduces purchased inputs.
6. **Reduced animal losses:** Sheltered livestock suffer less carcass loss from shivering, sweating, and exposure.
7. **Increased grazing:** On hot days, cattle graze all day in shaded pasture instead of sheltering from the sun, increasing food intake and carcass weight.
8. **Reduced evaporation:** Shelterbelts reduce wind speed over water surfaces, cutting evaporation from ponds and dams.

## Market and Process Strategies

Yield increases extend beyond biological production into how products are marketed and processed:

1. **Specialty crops:** Selecting crops for nearby specialty markets — fresh herbs near restaurants, unusual fruits for ethnic markets — commands premium prices per hectare.
2. **Direct marketing:** Self-pick operations, mail order, direct dispatch, and wayside sales eliminate middleman margins.
3. **Value-added processing:** Converting raw products to higher-order products (seed to oil, crude eucalyptus oil to refined fractions) multiplies value.
4. **On-farm energy:** Processing fuels on-farm saves purchase costs and enables sale of surplus.

## Social and Financial Strategies

The social dimension of farm strategy significantly affects economic sustainability:

1. **Farm-link contracts:** Urban groups contract to buy specific produce, providing market stability.
2. **Education income:** Field days and educational courses generate revenue while building community connections.
3. **Agri-tourism:** Guest houses and holiday farms create income from urban visitors.
4. **Direct investment:** City people investing in particular farms provides capital without bank dependency.
5. **Local credit unions:** Formation of district banking institutions recycles money locally rather than extracting it to distant financial centers.
6. **Equipment pooling:** Vehicle and implement sharing with neighbors, with coordinated sowing and reaping schedules, can save up to 90% of capital costs.
7. **Labor exchange:** Cooperative labor arrangements between neighbors reduce individual workload.
8. **Producer cooperatives:** Collective marketing and purchasing power improves terms for individual farmers.

## Real-World Evidence

The integrated application of these strategies produces dramatic results. Farmer Norm Sims reported: doubling production using half the irrigation water (four times benefit), reduced salinity, pasture production maintained through severe drought, irrigation time reduced from six days to two, and the ability to milk 185 cows on just 24 hectares through laser levelling, water reticulation, storage and recycling, 21-day grazing rotation, and central access roads. These permanent improvements benefit the farmer directly, which is precisely why they are not commercially promoted by input suppliers and middlemen whose role diminishes when farms become self-reliant.
