# Produce No Waste — Permaculture Principle 6

Produce No Waste is the sixth of [[david-holmgren]]'s twelve permaculture-design-principles. It recognises that in nature, the concept of waste does not
exist: the output of every process becomes the input for another. In contrast,
industrial culture creates enormous waste streams through linear material flows
that extract resources, manufacture products, use them, and discard them. The
principle calls for designing systems that close material loops and eliminate
the concept of waste entirely.

## Nature Has No Waste

In natural ecosystems, every organism's waste product becomes food or resource
for another organism. Fallen leaves feed soil organisms that build humus, which
nurtures the trees that produced the leaves. Animal manures fertilise the
plants that feed the animals. This circular flow of materials is fundamental to
the stability and productivity of natural systems. The concept of "waste" is
entirely a human cultural construction.

[[permaculture-design]] seeks to mimic these closed-loop material cycles. When
designers view "waste" as a resource in the wrong place, new opportunities
emerge for [[integrate-rather-than-segregate]] system elements that would
otherwise be separated.

## Industrial Versus Sustainable Material Flows

Holmgren contrasts industrial culture with sustainable culture across a
characteristic polarity: industrial culture has linear material flows, while
sustainable culture has cyclical material flows. This distinction is one of the
fundamental differences between the two cultural systems and connects directly
to [[permaculture-ethics-earth-care-people-care-fair-share]].

Linear flows — extract, manufacture, use, discard — depend on continuous
energy input and create accumulating pollution. Cyclical flows — where outputs
become inputs — are self-sustaining and can be maintained on renewable energy.
The transition from linear to cyclical material flows is essential for any
sustainable sustainable-culture.

## Waste as Misplaced Resource

The permaculture principle "the problem is the solution," described in
[[observe-and-interact-permaculture-principle]], applies directly to waste.
Many so-called waste products are valuable resources that cultural biases or
economic structures prevent us from using. Examples include:
- Kitchen scraps and garden waste as compost and soil-building material
- Greywater from households as irrigation water
- "Weed" species as nutrient accumulators, soil conditioners, or animal fodder
- Human excrement as a source of nutrients and organic matter when safely
  processed
- Building waste from demolition as construction material

## The Efficiency Trap

The conservation ethic focuses on maximising efficiency of resource use, which
is necessary to correct the dysfunction of consumption. However, the [[obtain-a-yield-permaculture-principle]] warns that at the extreme, efficiency focus
is analogous to an engine loaded almost to stalling — efficient but not
producing much useful work. Produce No Waste is not about obsessive
efficiency but about designing systems where waste simply does not arise
because outputs naturally connect to inputs.

## Practical Strategies

### Composting and Nutrient Cycling

Composting is one of the most direct applications of this principle. Organic
waste materials are transformed through biological processes into humus, which
builds soil fertility and supports plant growth. This closes the nutrient loop
between food production and consumption.

### Mulching

Mulching with organic materials serves multiple functions: suppressing weeds,
conserving soil moisture, moderating soil temperature, and slowly releasing
nutrients as the mulch breaks down. Materials that might otherwise be burned
or sent to landfill become valuable garden inputs.

### Biological Waste Treatment

Holmgren contrasts biological stormwater treatment using reed beds and
constructed wetlands with engineered solutions such as concrete litter traps.
The biological approach uses nature's services to treat waste, creating habitat
and beauty while purifying water. The engineering approach consumes non-renewable
resources and creates its own waste stream.

### Integrated Animal Systems

Animals can convert plant materials that humans cannot digest into food,
fertiliser, and other products. Chickens, for example, can process food scraps,
garden weeds, and insect pests while producing eggs and manure. Integrated
animal-plant systems turn potential waste streams into valuable products.

### Design for Longevity and Repair

Products and structures designed for durability, repairability, and eventual
recycling produce less waste than disposable alternatives. This connects to
[[use-and-value-renewable-resources]] and the broader critique of consumer
culture.

## The Non-Material Dimension

Holmgren emphasises that non-material well-being produces less waste than
material consumption. Activities that provide satisfaction without consuming
physical resources — walking rather than driving, playing with children rather
than buying toys, enjoying sunsets rather than consuming entertainment —
inherently produce no waste. Rising consumption in wealthy countries has not
improved well-being since the 1970s, suggesting that much of the waste
generated by modern economies is unnecessary.

## Population and Waste

The third permaculture ethic, [[permaculture-ethics-earth-care-people-care-fair-share]], addresses limits to consumption and reproduction. Every
additional person in a high-consumption society accelerates waste generation.
The principle of producing no waste thus connects to population questions and
the need for affluent societies to reduce per-capita consumption.

## Waste as Feedback

Waste can be understood as a form of negative feedback: it signals that a
system is not functioning efficiently. In [[apply-self-regulation-permaculture-principle]], responding to this feedback by redesigning systems to eliminate
waste is a form of self-regulation. Monitoring waste outputs provides valuable
information about system performance and design effectiveness.
