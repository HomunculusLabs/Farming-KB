# Alcohol and the Alchemy of Spirit

## Nostalgia for Paradise

History is the story of our unfocused agony over the loss of the perfect human world, and then of our forgetting it altogether, denying it and in so doing, denying a part of ourselves. It is a story of relationships, quasi-symbiotic compacts, with plants that were made and broken. The consequence of not seeing ourselves as a part of the green engine of vegetable nature is the alienation and despair that surrounds us and threatens to make the future unbearable.

All the natural narcotics, stimulants, relaxants and hallucinogens known to the modern botanist and pharmacologist were discovered by primitive man and have been in use from time immemorial. One of the first things that Homo sapiens did with his newly developed rationality and self-consciousness was to set them to work to find out a way to bypass analytical thinking and to transcend or temporarily obliterate the isolating awareness of the self.

## Alcohol and Honey

The great plant-drug complex that spans the cultural divide from partnership to dominator is alcohol. Alcohol has its roots in the deepest stratum of Archaic cultural activities. Ancient civilizations of the Near East were preoccupied with beer making. Very early in human culture, the intoxicating effects of fermented honey and fruit juices must have been noticed.

Honey is a magical substance — a medicinal substance in all traditional cultures. Mead, or fermented honey, seems to have been the recreational drug of the Indo-European tribes. The belief widely held in the classical world that bees were generated from the carcasses of cattle makes more sense if seen as an effort to connect bees as a source of honey and mead, the supplanting intoxicant, with cattle and the older mushroom cult.

## Wine and Woman

Fruits rich in seed, such as pomegranates and figs, appear from the earliest times as symbols of fecundity. The vine and its juice has a long history of religious significance. Wine played a central part in later Greek culture, so much so that the disturbing figure of ecstatic Dionysus was converted into the hairy-footed and lascivious wine-god Bacchus, the lord of orgy and drunken revelry carried on in the traditional dominator style.

Greek wines have always been somewhat puzzling. Their alcohol content could not have exceeded 14 percent. Yet Greek wines are sometimes described as requiring many dilutions before they could be drunk with comfort. This seems to suggest that Greek wines were more akin to extracts and tinctures of other plant essences. The practice of adding resin to wine in Greece to make retsina may well hark back to times when other plants, perhaps belladonna or Datura, also went into wine.

## Natural vs. Synthetic Drugs

Alcoholism as a social and community problem appears to have been rare before the discovery of distillation. Just as heroin addiction was the malignant flower that sprang from the relatively benign habit of opium use, so distilled alcohol changed the sacred art of the brewer and the vintner into a profane economic engine for the consumption of human hopes.

Alcohol can be distilled by using heat to vaporize it and separate it from its source, unlike alkaloids and indoles, which must be extracted using solvents. This fact made it possible for alcohol to be the first intoxicant to be chemically "isolated." The quality of being recaptured from its vaporous state gave rise to the practice of referring to distilled alcohol as "spirits."

## Alchemy and the Discovery of Distillation

In the West the discovery of distilled alcohol is alternately credited to Raymond Lully or Arnoldus de Villanova. Lully's search for the true elixir led him to the preparation of aqua vini, the first brandy. True to his alchemical roots, Lully made his universal panacea by fermenting wine in a double boiler of horse dung for twenty days before distilling it with a crude cold-water condenser.

Alchemy was a slowly evolving group of Gnostic and Hermetic theories concerning human origins and the dichotomy of spirit and matter. Its roots reached back deep into time, to at least Dynastic Egypt. The natural world had come to be seen, by late Roman times, as a demonic and imprisoning shell — the spiritual legacy of the destruction of the partnership model.

## Alcohol as the Dominator Drug

No other drug has had such a prolonged detrimental effect on human beings. Alcohol and slavery often went hand in hand across the economic landscape. Sugar and the alcohol that could be made from it became a European obsession that severely distorted the demographics of tropical regions.

Alcohol is the dominator drug par excellence. It has the effect of being libidinally stimulating at moderate doses at the same time that the ego feels empowered and social boundaries are felt to lose some of their restraining power. These fleeting effects are usually followed by a narrowing of awareness, diminishing ability to respond to social cues, and an infantile regression.

The alcohol abuse syndrome is symptomatic of the state of disequilibrium and tension existing between men and women and between the individual and society. The alcohol problem cannot be solved by prohibition but only by addressing the underlying cultural pathology.

## Key Points

- McKenna's analysis connects psychoactive plant use to fundamental patterns in human cultural evolution
- The stoned ape hypothesis, while controversial, raises important questions about the role of psychoactive substances in human cognitive development
- McKenna's critique of dominator culture draws on both anthropological evidence and contemporary political analysis
- The [[psychedelic-mushroom-history]] provides a broad context for understanding McKenna's specific claims

## Significance in Psychedelic Thought

McKenna's work represents one of the most ambitious attempts to construct a comprehensive theory of the role of psychoactive plants in human history and culture. While academic anthropologists have been skeptical of many specific claims, his ideas have been enormously influential in psychedelic culture and continue to inspire research into the relationship between psychoactive substances and human consciousness. The concept of an Archaic Revival — the recovery of ancient plant-based wisdom — has resonated with the growing interest in ethnobotany, ecological consciousness, and the therapeutic potential of psychedelic substances documented in [[psychedelic-therapy-current-research]].

## Related McKenna Topics

McKenna's analysis fits within his broader framework of the [[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-thesis]], which traces the relationship between humans and psychoactive plants from prehistory to the present. The themes explored here connect directly to his discussions of [[mckenna-dissolution-of-the-dominator-culture]] and the [[mckenna-archaic-revival]] as counter-movements to the suppression of plant-based ecstatic religion.

## Alcohol in Cross-Cultural Perspective
McKenna's analysis of alcohol gains additional depth when viewed through the lens of comparative ethnobotany. In societies that maintained access to [[psychedelic-plants-and-civilization]], alcohol typically played a subsidiary role as a social lubricant rather than a primary sacrament. Among Amazonian peoples who use ayahuasca, among Mazatec curanderos who use psilocybin mushrooms, and among Bwiti practitioners who use iboga, fermented beverages exist but are not elevated to the status of sacred entheogens. The elevation of alcohol to supreme religious significance appears to be a distinctive feature of cultures that had lost — or deliberately suppressed — access to more potent visionary plants.
In the context of the [[dominator-culture-and-partnership-society]], alcohol serves the dominator agenda in several ways. Unlike psychedelics, which tend to dissolve ego boundaries and promote feelings of interconnectedness, alcohol at moderate doses reinforces the ego and its desires while at higher doses simply produces unconsciousness. Alcohol does not typically catalyse mystical experiences, moral insight, or the sense of communion with nature that characterises the [[mckenna-dominator-vs-partnership-society-model]]. Instead, it produces a form of disinhibition that can facilitate social bonding within hierarchies while simultaneously dulling the critical faculties needed to question those hierarchies.
The contrast between Dionysian wine rituals in their original form and their later Roman appropriation illustrates this transformation. The original Dionysian rites, which likely involved the use of ergotised barley or other psychoactive additives, were ecstatic communal experiences that temporarily dissolved social hierarchies and allowed participants direct access to the divine. By the time these rites were absorbed into Roman culture as the Bacchanalia, the visionary component had been stripped away, leaving only the intoxicating and orgiastic elements — a hollow echo of the original sacramental experience. McKenna reads this devolution as a pattern: wherever the dominator culture encounters a genuine plant-based mystery religion, it preserves the form while evacuating the content, replacing genuine ecstatic experience with chemically induced oblivion.

## The Sugar-Alcohol-Slavery Complex
McKenna's treatment of alcohol is inseparable from his analysis of [[mckenna-sugar-slavery-and-dominance]]. The triangular trade that linked Europe, Africa, and the Americas was fundamentally driven by the demand for sugar and the rum that could be distilled from it. Sugar plantations in the Caribbean and Brazil consumed enslaved African labour at a rate that constituted one of the greatest demographic catastrophes in human history. The product of this suffering — refined white sugar and distilled alcohol — then flowed back to European cities, where it served as both a commodity and an instrument of social control.
Rum became a currency in the slave trade itself, used to purchase captives from African coastal kingdoms. The British Royal Navy distributed daily rum rations to sailors, a practice that ensured a compliant workforce for the maintenance of naval power. The connection between alcohol and military discipline — documented from the Roman army's posca to the modern soldier's canteen — reflects alcohol's unique capacity to provide temporary relief from the psychological stresses of dominator culture while simultaneously binding the individual more tightly to the dominator system.

## The Alchemical Metaphor
McKenna's use of the word "alchemy" to describe the distillation of alcohol is deliberately double-edged. In its original sense, alchemy was the art of spiritual transformation through material operations — the transmutation of base matter into gold understood as a metaphor for the transmutation of the human soul. The alchemist's laboratory was a workshop of spiritual development disguised as chemistry. The distillation of alcohol from wine preserves this metaphorical structure but inverts it: instead of extracting the spiritual essence from matter, distillation extracts a material substance that simulates and ultimately substitutes for genuine spiritual experience.
The term "spirits" for distilled alcohol is thus, for McKenna, a bitter irony. The genuine spirits of the [[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-thesis]] were the plant entheogens themselves — the mushrooms, the ergot, the iboga — that opened channels of communication with the divine mind. Distilled alcohol, called "spirits" because the volatile essence of the brew rises as vapour and is recaptured, is a counterfeit of this genuine spiritual technology. It provides the language of transcendence while delivering only intoxication and, ultimately, dependence. The alchemical project of spiritual transformation was thereby reduced to a chemical operation for the production of an addictive commodity.

## Fermentation as Proto-Shamanic Technology
Before the development of distillation, the fermentation process itself may have served as a proto-shamanic technology in early human societies. The transformation of fruit juice or grain mash into an intoxicating beverage through the action of invisible microorganisms is, in a sense, a miniature mystery — a demonstration that hidden forces can transform ordinary matter into something extraordinary. McKenna suggests that early brewmasters and vintners may have occupied a shamanic role in their communities, mediating between the human world and the invisible world of fermentation spirits (literally "spirits" in the animistic sense).
The mead halls of the Indo-European tribes, where fermented honey was consumed in communal rituals that reinforced social bonds and warrior solidarity, may have evolved from earlier mushroom ceremonies in which the psychoactive properties of the beverage were more pronounced. As the [[mckenna-vedic-soma-indo-european-mysteries]] lost access to its original psychoactive plant and replaced it with weaker substitutes, the ceremonial context of the drinking ritual was preserved while the visionary content was gradually diluted. The mead hall thus represents a transitional form — retaining the social and ritual structure of the original mushroom ceremony while the psychoactive content was reduced to the level of mild intoxication. The warrior ethos of the Indo-European mead hall, with its emphasis on courage, loyalty, and heroic death, can be read as a secularised version of the mushroom-inspired fearlessness that McKenna attributes to the original shamanic experience.

## The Gendered Dimensions of Alcohol in Dominator Culture
McKenna's analysis of alcohol includes a pointed examination of its gendered dimensions. In dominator culture, alcohol has historically been associated with male social spaces — the tavern, the mead hall, the officers' mess — while the preparation of fermented beverages was often women's work. This gendered division reflects the broader dominator pattern in which women's knowledge of plant medicines (including psychoactive plants) was systematically suppressed and replaced by male-controlled production systems focused on alcohol rather than visionary substances.
The connection between alcohol and gendered violence is a recurring theme in McKenna's analysis. Alcohol's pharmacological properties — disinhibition at moderate doses, impaired judgment, and aggression at higher doses — interact with the power dynamics of patriarchy to produce disproportionate rates of domestic violence, sexual assault, and other forms of gendered harm. McKenna argues that this is not an accidental side effect but a structural feature of the dominator culture's relationship with alcohol: a substance that reinforces male dominance and suppresses the feminine values of partnership, empathy, and ecological awareness is systematically promoted as the culturally normative intoxicant.
The [[mckenna-dissolution-of-the-dominator-culture]], in McKenna's framework, requires not only the recovery of suppressed plant knowledge but also the recovery of the partnership values that were displaced along with it. The [[mckenna-archaic-revival]] is, at its deepest level, a revival of the partnership between humans and plants, between masculine and feminine principles, and between humanity and the natural world — a partnership that alcohol, as the dominator drug, actively disrupts.

## Comparative Analysis: Alcohol vs. Psychedelic Plants
McKenna's most incisive analysis of alcohol comes through direct comparison with the [[psychedelic-plants-and-civilization]] that alcohol displaced. The contrast is structural and reveals the fundamental bias of dominator culture toward consciousness-suppressing rather than consciousness-expanding substances. Alcohol suppresses REM sleep, reducing dream activity and the psychological integration that dreams provide; psychedelics have been shown to increase the vividness and frequency of dreams long after the acute experience has ended. Alcohol narrows the visual field and impairs visual acuity; low-dose psilocybin, according to the [[terence-mckenna-stoned-ape-hypothesis]], enhances visual acuity and edge detection. Alcohol promotes aggressive, competitive social behaviour; psychedelics consistently promote empathic, cooperative social attitudes.
At the neurochemical level, alcohol enhances GABAergic inhibition and suppresses glutamatergic excitation, producing a net reduction in neural activity — literally turning down the volume of consciousness. Psychedelics, by contrast, enhance serotonergic signalling and increase the entropy (disorder, in the information-theoretic sense) of neural activity patterns, producing a net expansion of the range of states available to the brain. These opposite neurochemical profiles produce opposite experiential outcomes: alcohol narrows consciousness, while psychedelics broaden it. For McKenna, the cultural preference for alcohol over psychedelics is not a matter of taste or tradition but a reflection of the dominator culture's fundamental commitment to the restriction and control of consciousness rather than its expansion and liberation.
This comparative framework has practical implications for drug policy. If the goal of drug regulation is to minimise harm, then the drugs that should be most restricted are those that cause the greatest harm — a category in which alcohol ranks far above any psychedelic substance. The World Health Organization estimates that alcohol is responsible for approximately 3 million deaths per year worldwide, while the total number of deaths attributable to psychedelic substances is vanishingly small by comparison. McKenna argues that the actual function of drug prohibition is not harm reduction but the enforcement of a specific model of consciousness — one that values conformity, productivity, and consumer behaviour over creativity, insight, and ecological awareness.
