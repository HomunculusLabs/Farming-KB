# Aquaculture Basics

updated: 2026-04-18

Aquaculture -- the cultivation of aquatic organisms -- is one of the most productive and efficient food systems in permaculture. Water-based systems can produce more protein per unit area than terrestrial systems, while simultaneously providing water storage, wildlife habitat, fire protection, and microclimate moderation. In permaculture, aquaculture is integrated into the overall design as part of the water management and food production systems.

## Pond Design for Aquaculture

A productive aquaculture pond needs careful design:

### Depth and Zones

- **Deep zone** (minimum 3 feet/1m): Allows fish to survive winter without freezing; provides cool water in summer
- **Marginal shelf** (3-5 inches/7-12cm deep): Planting area for emergent aquatic plants; habitat for fry
- **Very shallow area** (2 inches/5cm deep): Refuge for the smallest fish and frog spawn; warmer water speeds growth of fry
- **Gently sloping edges**: Allow easy access for wildlife and safe exit for animals that fall in

### Siting

- Full sun for at least part of the day for plant growth and warmer water
- Away from deciduous trees -- falling leaves turn water sour as they rot anaerobically
- Visible from the house for monitoring and enjoyment
- Integrated with the overall water management system (swales, dams, irrigation)

### Sealing

Ponds can be sealed with:
- **Natural clay**: If sufficient clay is present in the subsoil, compacting it creates a natural seal
- **Gley technique**: Fresh manure or organic matter layered at the bottom and covered; anaerobic decomposition creates an impermeable layer
- **Rubber liners**: Durable and flexible; allow custom pond shapes
- **Fiberglass shells**: Quick to install but limit pond shape and size

## Fish Species

Fish selection depends on climate, pond size, and purpose:

- **Tilapia**: Fast-growing, warm-water fish; excellent for tropical and subtropical climates
- **Trout**: Cold-water fish requiring clean, well-oxygenated water; suitable for cooler climates
- **Carp**: Hardy, adaptable, and productive; the most widely cultured fish globally
- **Catfish**: Tolerant of poor water quality; bottom feeders that help clean the pond
- **Perch and bass**: Predator fish that help control populations of smaller fish

### Fish Management

- Provide shallow refuges where small fish can escape predation by larger fish
- Include brush piles and submerged structures for predator protection
- Manage stocking density to prevent overpopulation and stunting
- Consider a dry cycle for intensive ponds -- periodically drain and allow the bottom to dry and aerate
- Hay bales placed around pond edges encourage diatom growth, which supports the food chain

## Aquatic Plants

Aquatic plants serve essential functions in the pond ecosystem:

### Deep-Water Aquatics
Root at the bottom with leaves floating on the surface. They shade the water, reducing algae growth. Water lilies (Nymphaea) are the most important -- they reduce the sunlight reaching the water surface, starving algae of the light they need. Water hawthorn and water crowfoot are also excellent choices.

### Floating Plants
Float freely on the surface, further excluding light and providing habitat. Water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes) and water chestnut (Trapa natans) are effective floating plants. Use one floating plant per square yard/meter of water surface.

### Submerged Oxygenating Plants
Not decorative but essential to pond health. They absorb dissolved minerals and release oxygen through photosynthesis. Spiked water milfoil (Myriophyllum spicatum), Canadian pondweed (Elodea canadensis), and curly pondweed (Potamogeton crispus) are common choices. Plant one bunch per 2 square yards/meters of water.

### Marginal Plants
Grow in shallow water or boggy soil at the edges. They provide habitat, filter water, and create a transition zone between water and land. Sweet-scented rush (Acorus calamus), flowering rush (Butomus umbellatus), marsh marigold (Caltha palustris), iris, and primula are all good marginal plants.

## Water Quality Management

- **Algae control**: After filling, pond water may turn pea-green from rapid algae growth. Do NOT empty and refill -- this gives algae a fresh supply of mineral salts. Leave the water alone; as plants grow larger they exclude light, and as minerals are depleted the algae die and the water clears.
- **Blanket weed**: Remove slimy green blanket weed as soon as you see it. Regular removal prevents infestation.
- **Leaves**: Keep leaves out of the pond -- they turn water sour as they rot. Cover with netting in fall if the pond is near deciduous trees.
- **Plant thinning**: Thin oxygenating plants periodically by pulling out excess by hand. Return any animal life to the pond.

## Phosphorus Fixation

Mussels are superior phosphorus fixers in aquaculture systems. They filter feed, removing nutrients from the water column and incorporating them into their bodies and shells. Their presence helps maintain water quality and supports the overall nutrient cycling of the pond ecosystem.

## Pond Maintenance Through the Year

### Spring
- Clean out any debris that accumulated over winter
- Thin overgrown oxygenating plants
- Divide and replant marginal plants if needed
- Begin feeding fish as water warms

### Summer
- Monitor algae growth and remove blanket weed
- Keep water levels topped up during hot weather
- Ensure floating plants are providing sufficient surface coverage

### Fall
- Cover with netting if near deciduous trees
- Remove dead and dying foliage
- Stop feeding fish as water cools

### Winter
- Keep a hole open in ice for gas exchange (use a floating ball, not by breaking ice)
- Leave most vegetation in place for wildlife habitat
- Plan any maintenance or enlargement for spring
