# Tropical Food System Development

Tropical food system development in permaculture draws on diverse case studies that
demonstrate how degraded or barren landscapes can be rapidly converted into
productive food-producing ecosystems. The tropics offer unique advantages — abundant
sunlight, year-round growing seasons, and fast biomass accumulation — but also
present challenges including invasive weeds, wind exposure, and poor soils on
recent lava flows. Bill Mollison documents multiple proven approaches that combine
traditional polycultural knowledge with modern permaculture design.

## Case Study: Hawaii Lava Flow Establishment

One of the most dramatic examples of tropical food system development comes from
Hawaiian lava flow sites — essentially barren basalt with no organic matter. Through
the application of heavy mulch and irrigation, these seemingly lifeless landscapes
were transformed into productive food forests within a single year.

Key species established included mango (*Mangifera indica*), citrus (*Citrus* spp.),
macadamia (*Macadamia integrifolia*), and guava (*Psidium guajava*). The technique
relies on importing organic matter to kick-start soil biology, then using irrigation
to sustain rapid early growth. The lava rock itself, once colonized by roots and
microorganisms, begins to weather and contribute mineral nutrients. This approach
demonstrates that even the most unpromising substrates can support food production
when permaculture principles are applied consistently.

## Case Study: Molokai Chicken Tractor System

On Molokai, Dano Gorsich developed a chicken tractor system across 0.5 hectares
using five pens. Australorp chickens were employed as the primary land-management
tool, clearing weeds and grasses as they were rotated through pens. The chickens
feed on fallen papaya fruit and insects, creating a closed-loop system where the
food grown on-site partially sustains the livestock.

The system generates income from both eggs and papaya sales, sufficient to support
a family. This is an exemplary model of integrated animal-plant systems where the
animals perform essential work (weed control, pest management, manure fertilization)
while producing a marketable yield. The pen rotation ensures no single area is
overgrazed, allowing rest and recovery periods for the pasture and soil.

## Traditional Hawaiian Garden Systems

Traditional Hawaiian gardens represent centuries of refined polycultural design.
These systems typically combine staple and productive species including banana
(*Musa* spp.), papaya (*Carica papaya*), chili peppers (*Capsicum* spp.), and taro
(*Colocasia esculenta*) — often referred to as kalo in Hawaiian culture.

Pigs and chickens are integrated into these garden systems, contributing manure for
fertility, consuming waste and surplus, and performing tillage through their rooting
and scratching behavior. This traditional model demonstrates the permaculture
principle of integrating multiple yields and functions within a single system,
maximizing the productive use of available space and resources.

## Case Study: Kaiwaka NZ Instant Jungle Garden

Jim Kay Baxter's instant jungle garden at Kaiwaka, New Zealand, demonstrates that
food forests can be established remarkably quickly — in this case, transitioning
from open pasture to a functioning food forest in just 18 months. The approach
uses a structured layering strategy:

- **Windbreak layer**: Protects the developing system from desiccating winds,
  creating a sheltered microclimate essential for tender tropical species.
- **Ground covers**: Nasturtium (*Tropaeolum majus*), comfrey (*Symphytum* spp.),
  and sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*) rapidly cover bare soil, suppress weeds,
  retain moisture, and begin building organic matter.
- **Understory**: Banana (*Musa* spp.), tagasaste (*Chamaecytisus proliferus*),
  and taro (*Colocasia esculenta*) form the mid-layer, producing food and
  nitrogen fixation while providing shade.
- **Canopy**: Fruit trees form the upper layer, completing the forest structure.

The speed of this transformation underscores the importance of starting with
protective ground covers and windbreaks before introducing more demanding species.

## Weed Suppression Strategies

Weed management in tropical systems can be achieved through strategic plant
selection rather than mechanical or chemical intervention.

### Tagetes for Kikuyu Grass

Tagetes (*Tagetes* spp.), commonly known as marigolds, have proven effective at
suppressing kikuyu grass (*Cenchrus clandestinus*), one of the most aggressive
tropical pasture weeds. The allelopathic properties of Tagetes root exudates
inhibit kikuyu rhizome growth, gradually reducing its dominance.

### Choko for Lantana Suppression

Choko (*Sechium edule*) — also known as chayote — is a vigorous climbing vine
that can be deployed to shade out lantana (*Lantana camara*), a notoriously
invasive tropical shrub. Once established, the dense choko foliage denies lantana
the light it needs to survive. This strategy has been documented to yield
approximately $120 per week from choko harvest alone, demonstrating that weed
suppression and income generation are not mutually exclusive.

### Pigs as Attractors for Grass Clearance

Pigs serve as effective biological attractors for grass clearance. Their natural
rooting behavior thoroughly disturbs grass-dominated areas, breaking up rhizomes
and turning over soil. When managed in rotational systems, pigs can clear
persistent grasses while simultaneously depositing manure and preparing ground for
subsequent planting.

## Coastal Stabilization

Tropical coastlines face severe erosion pressure from storms, hurricanes, and sea
level rise. Permaculture approaches to coastal stabilization use hardy pioneer
species to anchor sediments and rebuild protective vegetation layers.

### Beach Convolvulus and Casuarina

Beach convolvulus (*Calystegia soldanella*) and casuarina (*Casuarina* spp.) work
together to stabilize beach systems. Convolvulus colonizes sand with its creeping
rhizomes, binding surface layers, while casuarina provides wind protection and
deeper root anchoring. Together they create stable beach profiles resistant to
normal wave and wind erosion.

### Mangroves for Post-Hurricane Recovery

Mangroves are critical for post-hurricane island recovery. Their dense root
networks absorb wave energy, trap sediment, and rebuild land. Mangrove systems
naturally regenerate after storm damage if seed sources remain available, making
them self-repairing coastal defenses that also provide habitat for fish and
shellfish.

## Windbreaks and Protective Hedges

### Erythrina and Pennisetum Windbreaks

Erythrina (*Erythrina* spp.) and Pennisetum (*Pennisetum* spp.) are used as
complementary windbreak species. Erythrina, as a nitrogen-fixing leguminous tree,
provides both wind protection and soil fertility. Pennisetum grasses fill in the
lower layers of the windbreak profile, ensuring ground-level wind reduction. This
combination creates effective multi-layer wind protection for sensitive crops and
livestock.

### Euphorbia Hedges

Euphorbia (*Euphorbia* spp.) hedges serve dual purposes: they act as dense
windbreaks and provide habitat for spiders. Spiders are among the most important
biological pest controllers in tropical systems, and supporting their populations
through habitat provision reduces the need for external pest management inputs.
Euphorbia hedges thus function as both physical infrastructure and biological
support systems within the broader food system design.

## Design Principles for Tropical Food Systems

The case studies collectively illustrate several key permaculture design principles
specific to tropical food system development:

1. **Rapid biomass accumulation** is both the primary challenge and the primary
   opportunity in the tropics. Mulch, ground covers, and fast-growing pioneer
   species are essential tools for converting solar energy into soil organic
   matter.
2. **Integrated animal systems** multiply yields and reduce labor by assigning
   essential functions — weed control, pest management, tillage, fertilization —
   to livestock rather than machinery or chemicals.
3. **Layered establishment** (windbreaks first, then ground covers, then
   understory, then canopy) ensures that each new layer is supported by the
   protection and resources provided by preceding layers.
4. **Weed suppression through productive species** converts a management problem
   into a yield-generating activity, as demonstrated by the choko-lantana system.
5. **Coastal and edge systems** require dedicated design attention using
   specialized pioneer species to stabilize and protect vulnerable transition
   zones.
