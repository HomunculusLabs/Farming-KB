# Hallucinogenic Mushroom Prevalence in Europe

## Overview

Use of hallucinogenic mushrooms lay relatively dormant in Europe from the late 1950s until availability and prevalence increased during the late 1990s and early 2000s. Data from multiple survey sources including general population surveys, school-based studies (ESPAD), and targeted clubbing surveys paint a consistent picture: lifetime prevalence is considerably lower than for cannabis but approaches or equals that of ecstasy among young people in several European countries.

## General Population Surveys (Ages 15 to 24)

Among young people aged 15 to 24 across 12 EU member states, lifetime prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use ranges from less than 1% to approximately 8%. The highest prevalence estimates were reported in:

- **Netherlands** (though data includes mescaline, psilocybin, 2C-B, ayahuasca, ketamine, and mushrooms combined)
- **Czech Republic** (data for 15 to 34 age group)
- **United Kingdom**
- **Germany**
- **Ireland**

The lowest prevalence was reported in Lithuania, Hungary, and France (though France's most recent adult general population survey at the time was over five years old). A more targeted 2003 survey of French 17 to 18 year olds reported 4.3% lifetime prevalence, 2.9% last-year prevalence, and 1% last-month prevalence, suggesting higher rates among younger cohorts.

Prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use is consistently and substantially lower than for cannabis, where lifetime prevalence among persons aged 18 to 36 ranges from 15% (Poland) to 45% (Denmark) across the EU.

The proportion of current users (last month) among those who have ever used is notably lower for hallucinogenic mushrooms than for both cannabis and ecstasy, suggesting that the effects of mushrooms limit the appeal of regular or continued use.

## School Surveys (ESPAD, Ages 15 to 16)

The European School Survey Project on Alcohol and Other Drugs (ESPAD) collected data in 2003 from 22 EU member states, Norway, and four candidate countries. Among students aged 15 to 16:

- Lifetime prevalence of hallucinogenic mushroom use ranged from 0% to 8%
- Highest estimates: Czech Republic, Netherlands, France, and Belgium
- Three countries reported zero lifetime prevalence: Cyprus, Finland, and Romania
- In **nine countries**, lifetime use of hallucinogenic mushrooms was equal to or higher than lifetime use of ecstasy

### Correlation with Other Drug Use

Analysis across 11 EU member states plus Norway and Croatia demonstrated that school students who had used hallucinogenic mushrooms were far more likely to have also used:

- LSD or other hallucinogens
- Ecstasy
- Amphetamines
- Cocaine

The association was much weaker with cannabis-only users or users of legal substances only. Male students generally reported higher prevalence rates than females, consistent with broader patterns of illegal drug use.

## Clubbing Surveys

Targeted surveys conducted in dance music and club settings reveal substantially higher prevalence than general population or school surveys:

| Location | Setting | Lifetime Prevalence |
|----------|---------|-------------------|
| France | 5 urban centres, electronic music | ~55% |
| Italy | Bologna, rave parade | ~38% |
| Czech Republic | Dance parties | ~35% |
| UK | Club music magazine readership | ~30% (2004) |
| Hungary | Budapest, 33 party settings | ~9% |

Within club populations, polydrug use clusters heavily in the same individuals. A 2000 survey of 760 clubbers from six nightclubs in south east England found:

- Overall lifetime prevalence for hallucinogenic mushrooms: 24%
- Among current drug users (used any drug in past month): 44%
- Among general population aged 16 to 19: 11%

## Trend Data

The only multi-year trend data available came from six consecutive annual readership surveys by a UK clubbing magazine (Mixmag, 2000 to 2005):

- Lifetime prevalence estimates increased between 2003 and 2004
- Last-month prevalence rose significantly from 2.4% in 2002 to 18% in 2004
- These figures require cautious interpretation due to non-probability sampling methodology

The average age for first use of hallucinogenic mushrooms was reported as 18 to 19 years. Among those who had used them, over half planned to take them again, with "Mexican" varieties ([[psilocybe-cubensis]]) being the most commonly used type.

## Perceived Availability

The ESPAD school surveys also measured perceived availability among 15 to 16 year olds:

- Rates of students perceiving mushrooms as "very" or "fairly easy" to obtain ranged from 4% to 28%
- Under 10% reported easy access in Cyprus, Finland, Greece, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Romania, and Turkey
- Over 20% reported easy access in the Czech Republic, Ireland, Italy, Poland, and the UK
- In the Netherlands, despite legal sales at the time, only 16% of school students reported easy access

A 2005 UK Mixmag survey, conducted shortly after the imposition of stricter controls, found 67% of respondents reporting that mushrooms were less available than previously.

## Telephone Helpline Data

The FESAT network of drug telephone helplines across 16 European countries found no significant increase in calls related to hallucinogenic mushrooms during 2004. Belgium reported approximately 1% of all inquiries to Druglijn Flanders were mushroom-related in 2003 and 2004. The Swedish Poisons Information Centre saw calls peak at 96 in 1998, remaining stable at 30 to 40 calls annually in subsequent years.

## Geographic Patterns

The geographic distribution of use correlates strongly with:

1. The presence of smartshop and retail markets (Netherlands, UK)
2. Cultural familiarity with recreational drug use in dance settings
3. Perceived ease of access
4. Wild mushroom foraging traditions (Poland, Ireland, Czech Republic)

## Data Limitations

Several important limitations affect the interpretation of prevalence data:

- Historically, surveys collected data on LSD or general "hallucinogens" rather than mushrooms specifically
- Survey methods differ between countries and over time
- Non-probability samples (club surveys) cannot be generalized to populations
- The recall period and question wording vary across surveys
- Underreporting is likely given the stigmatized and sometimes illegal nature of use
