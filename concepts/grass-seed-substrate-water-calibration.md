# Grass Seed Substrate Water Content Calibration

Water content is the single most critical variable in preparing grass seed
substrate for the [[mycelial-colonization]] or fail due to excessive dryness
or bacterial contamination from anaerobic conditions. Because different
grass seed varieties and even different batches of the same variety can
absorb water at markedly different rates, successful cultivation requires
calibration rather than rigid adherence to a fixed recipe.

## The Core Problem of Water Absorption Variability

The standard formula for 720 ml jars calls for 110 grams of grass seed and
180 ml of water. This ratio works well as a starting point but is subject to
significant variation depending on the specific seed used. The most commonly
employed grass seed is rye grass seed, Lolium perenne, though mixtures of
different species also produce acceptable results. The fundamental challenge
is that water absorption capacity varies between grass seed varieties and
between individual batches of the same variety.

When too much water is added relative to what the seed can absorb, the
result is a slimy, compacted clump of seed that cannot be shaken to
redistribute colonized kernels during the incubation phase. This clumping
has cascading negative effects: anaerobic pockets develop within the
substrate, bacterial contamination becomes likely, and the mycelium cannot
spread efficiently from inoculation points to the uncolonized portions of
the jar. When too little water is added, the substrate is too dry to support
robust mycelial growth, resulting in slow or stalled colonization and
reduced yields of mushrooms or sclerotia.

## The Overnight Soak Method

One technique for producing a more homogenous substrate is to soak the
grass seed in water overnight before loading it into jars. After soaking,
the saturated seed is packed into jars without adding additional water. This
method produces more uniform moisture distribution because the individual
seeds have had time to reach their maximum absorption capacity through slow
equilibration rather than the rapid imbibition that occurs when dry seed is
combined with water in the jar.

The overnight soak has several advantages over direct water addition. It
eliminates the risk of water pooling at the bottom of the jar, which can
create localized areas of excessive moisture even when the overall ratio is
correct. It also allows the cultivator to assess the water content visually
and tactilely before committing to the sterilization cycle. If the soaked
seed appears too wet or too dry, adjustments can be made at this stage
before the substrate is sealed into jars and sterilized.
