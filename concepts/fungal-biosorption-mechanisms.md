# Fungal Biosorption Mechanisms

Biosorption is the passive uptake of metal ions, organic compounds, and other molecules from solution by biological materials. In fungi, biosorption occurs primarily through interactions with cell wall components and does not require metabolic energy, making it effective with both living and dead biomass.

## Cell Wall Composition and Binding Sites

Fungal cell walls are complex matrices of polysaccharides with numerous metal-binding functional groups:
- **Chitin and chitosan:** Beta-1,4-linked N-acetylglucosamine polymers with amino groups that bind metal cations through coordination and electrostatic interactions. Deacetylation to chitosan increases amino group availability.
- **Glucans:** Beta-glucans with hydroxyl groups providing metal coordination sites
- **Mannans and galactans:** Polysaccharides with hydroxyl groups
- **Proteins:** Cell wall glycoproteins with carboxyl, amino, sulfhydryl, and imidazole groups
- **Melanins:** Dark pigments in some fungal cell walls with strong metal chelation capacity via phenolic hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amine groups

## Biosorption Mechanisms

**Ion exchange:** Metal cations exchange with counter-ions (H+, Na+, K+) on cell wall functional groups. This is often the dominant mechanism for heavy metal uptake. The process is rapid, reversible, and follows ion-exchange isotherms.

**Complexation/chelation:** Metal ions form coordination complexes with donor atoms (N, O, S) in cell wall polymers. Chitosan amino groups are particularly effective chelators. Stability constants vary: strong complexes with Cu2+, Fe3+; moderate with Zn2+, Ni2+; weaker with Cd2+, Pb2+.

**Electrostatic attraction:** Negatively charged cell wall surfaces attract positively charged metal cations. Surface charge depends on pH relative to the isoelectric point of the biomass.

**Physical adsorption:** Van der Waals forces contribute to non-specific binding, particularly at higher metal concentrations.

**Precipitation:** Metal ions may precipitate as hydroxides, oxides, or oxalates on or near the cell surface when local concentrations exceed solubility products.

## Factors Affecting Biosorption

**pH:** Most critical parameter. At low pH, H+ competes with metal cations for binding sites, reducing uptake. As pH increases, deprotonation of functional groups enhances metal binding. Optimal pH typically ranges from 4-6 for heavy metals.

**Biomass type:** Different fungal species and biomass preparations show vastly different biosorption capacities. Aspergillus niger, Rhizopus arrhizus, Mucor miehei, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae are widely studied.

**Pretreatment:** Physical (drying, autoclaving, grinding) and chemical (acid, alkali, CaCl2) pretreatments can enhance biosorption capacity by exposing additional binding sites.

**Metal concentration:** Uptake follows isotherm patterns (Langmuir, Freundlich) with saturation at high concentrations.

**Presence of competing ions:** In multi-metal systems, ions compete for binding sites based on affinity, concentration, and ionic radius. Binary and ternary systems show complex competitive interactions.

## Biosorption Reactor Systems

- **Batch stirred-tank reactors:** Simple, well-mixed systems for laboratory and small-scale applications
- **Continuous-flow stirred-tank reactors (CFSTR):** Steady-state operation for continuous treatment
- **Fixed packed-bed reactors:** Biomass packed in columns for continuous flow-through treatment
- **Immobilized reactors:** Biomass immobilized in alginate, polyacrylamide, or other matrices for enhanced mechanical stability and reuse

## Desorption and Regeneration

Biosorbed metals can be recovered through desorption using acid (HCl, HNO3) or chelating agents (EDTA). This enables biomass regeneration and metal recovery, improving process economics. Most biosorption-desorption cycles maintain 80-95% of initial capacity over multiple cycles.

## Quantitative Biosorption Capacities

Reported maximum biosorption capacities (qmax) vary considerably by fungal species, metal, and experimental conditions. Some representative values from the literature:
- **Aspergillus niger:** 15-90 mg/g for Pb2+, 12-35 mg/g for Cd2+, 20-65 mg/g for Cu2+, depending on pretreatment method
- **Rhizopus arrhizus:** 80-200 mg/g for Pb2+, 30-60 mg/g for U(VI), demonstrating exceptional affinity for lead
- **Mucor miehei:** 55-80 mg/g for Pb2+, 20-35 mg/g for Cr(VI)
- **Saccharomyces cerevisiae:** 10-25 mg/g for Cu2+, 5-15 mg/g for Cd2+ when using untreated biomass
- **Penicillium chrysogenum:** 100-290 mg/g for Pb2+ after alkali pretreatment, among the highest reported values

These values are typically determined from Langmuir isotherm fitting at optimal pH and temperature conditions. The wide ranges reflect the importance of biomass preparation, as chemical pretreatment with NaOH or CaCl2 can increase capacity by 2-5 fold by removing surface impurities and exposing additional binding sites.

## Live vs Dead Biomass

A fundamental distinction in fungal biosorption is between living and dead biomass. Living fungal cells can both biosorb (passive, metabolism-independent) and bioaccumulate (active, metabolism-dependent) metals. However, live biomass introduces complications: metabolic processes may be inhibited by toxic metals, nutrient requirements increase operating costs, and disposal of metal-loaded living biomass raises regulatory concerns.

Dead biomass offers several practical advantages for biosorption systems. It is not subject to metal toxicity, does not require nutrient media, can be stored for extended periods, and can be regenerated through desorption cycles. Industrial waste fungal biomass from fermentation industries (brewing, citric acid production, antibiotic manufacturing) provides a readily available and inexpensive source of dead biosorbent material. The choice between live and dead biomass depends on the specific application, with dead biomass preferred for most industrial wastewater treatment scenarios.

## Thermodynamics and Kinetics

Biosorption kinetics typically follow pseudo-second-order models, indicating that chemisorption is the rate-limiting step. Most biosorption processes reach equilibrium within 30-120 minutes, though some systems require up to 4 hours. The rapid initial uptake phase (first 5-15 minutes) is attributed to surface binding, while the slower subsequent phase involves intraparticle diffusion into the porous cell wall matrix.

Thermodynamic parameters (delta-G, delta-H, delta-S) generally indicate that biosorption is spontaneous (negative delta-G), with enthalpy values suggesting both exothermic and endothermic processes depending on the specific metal-biomass system. Most heavy metal biosorption is endothermic, meaning capacity increases with temperature, though excessive heat can denature cell wall binding sites and reduce capacity.

## Applications Beyond Heavy Metals

Fungal biosorption extends to:
- Organic pollutants (herbicides, phenols, dyes)
- Radionuclides (uranium, thorium, cesium, strontium)
- Cyanide-metal complexes
- Rare earth elements
