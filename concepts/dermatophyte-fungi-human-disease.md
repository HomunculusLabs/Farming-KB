# Dermatophyte Fungi and Human Disease

Dermatophytes are a group of fungi that infect the keratinized tissues of humans and other animals
— skin, hair, and nails. They are among the most common fungal pathogens, causing a range of
superficial infections collectively known as tinea or ringworm.

## Overview

Dermatophytes are classified in three genera:
- **Trichophyton** — infects skin, hair, and nails
- **Microsporum** — infects skin and hair
- **Epidermophyton** — infects skin and nails

Despite their medical importance, these fungi are often understudied relative to their impact.
Nicholas Money describes visiting a clinical mycology laboratory where the dermatophyte collection
was kept in a refrigerator — "a graveyard of old skin scrapings and nail clippings."

## Common Infections

### Athlete's Foot (Tinea Pedis)
- Most common dermatophyte infection in temperate regions
- Caused primarily by Trichophyton species
- Thrives in warm, moist environments (shoes, locker rooms, swimming pools)
- Affects the spaces between toes, causing itching, scaling, and cracking

### Ringworm (Tinea Corporis)
- Circular, raised, red lesions on the skin
- Not caused by a worm — the name comes from the ring-like appearance
- Can be transmitted by direct contact or contaminated objects (combs, towels)
- Common in children and wrestlers (where it is called tinea gladiatorum)

### Jock Itch (Tinea Cruris)
- Infection of the groin area
- Favored by tight clothing and perspiration

### Scalp Ringworm (Tinea Capitis)
- Primarily affects children
- Caused by Microsporum and Trichophyton species
- Can cause hair loss and scarring

### Nail Infections (Tinea Unguium / Onychomycosis)
- Invasion of fingernails and toenails
- Causes thickening, discoloration, and crumbling of nails
- Extremely difficult to treat due to poor nail penetration by antifungals

## Biology and Mechanism

Dermatophytes are keratinophilic (keratin-loving) fungi:
- They secrete keratinases — enzymes that break down keratin
- They do not invade living tissue — they grow only in the dead keratinized outer layers
- The immune response to fungal proteins causes the inflammation and itching
- Infections are generally superficial but can be persistent

## Epidemiology

- Dermatophytes are found worldwide
- Different species dominate in different regions (e.g., T. rubrum in temperate areas,
  T. mentagrophytes in tropical areas)
- Estimated 20-25% of the world population has a dermatophyte infection at any given time
- Transmission occurs through direct contact, fomites (contaminated objects), and occasionally
  from animals (zoophilic species)

## Reservoirs and Transmission

- **Anthropophilic species** — adapted to humans, person-to-person transmission
- **Zoophilic species** — normally found on animals, can infect humans (often more inflammatory)
- **Geophilic species** — normally found in soil, cause infections after soil contact

## Diagnosis

- Clinical appearance (ring-shaped lesions)
- Microscopic examination of skin scrapings (potassium hydroxide preparation)
- Fungal culture on specialized media (Sabouraud's agar)
- PCR-based molecular identification (increasingly used)

## Treatment

- **Topical antifungals** for mild skin infections (clotrimazole, terbinafine, miconazole)
- **Oral antifungals** for scalp and nail infections (terbinafine, itraconazole, griseofulvin)
- Nail infections may require months of oral therapy
- Treatment is complicated by potential drug interactions and side effects

## Historical Notes

- Dermatophytes have been recognized since antiquity — described in ancient Chinese, Greek,
  and Roman medical texts
- The fungal nature of ringworm was established in the 19th century
- The name "ringworm" persists despite the correct etiology being known for over 150 years
