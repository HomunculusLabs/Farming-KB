# Dermatophyte Fungi and Human Disease

Dermatophytes are a group of fungi that infect the keratinized tissues of humans
and other animals, including skin, hair, and nails. They are among the most
common [[bloomfield-fungal-pathogens-and-parasites]] worldwide, causing a range of superficial infections
collectively known as tinea or ringworm. Despite their prevalence, dermatophytes
are often understudied relative to their impact on human health.

## Overview

Dermatophytes are classified in three genera based on their tissue preferences:

- **Trichophyton** — infects skin, hair, and nails; the most common human pathogen
- **Microsporum** — infects skin and hair; known for causing scalp ringworm
- **Epidermophyton** — infects skin and nails; limited to two species

[[nicholas-money]] describes visiting a clinical mycology laboratory where the
dermatophyte collection was kept in a refrigerator, calling it "a graveyard of
old skin scrapings and nail clippings." This observation reflects the historical
neglect of these [[biodiversity-medically-important-fungi-isolation]] by the broader mycological community.

## Common Infections

### Athlete's Foot (Tinea Pedis)
The most common dermatophyte infection in [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]], caused primarily
by Trichophyton species. It thrives in warm, moist environments such as shoes,
locker rooms, and swimming pools. The infection affects the spaces between toes,
causing itching, scaling, and cracking of the skin. Chronic cases can lead to
secondary bacterial invasion requiring antibiotic treatment.

### Ringworm (Tinea Corporis)
Circular, raised, red lesions on the skin give this condition its misleading
name. The infection is not caused by a worm but by the ring-like appearance of
the expanding lesion. It is transmitted by direct contact or contaminated objects
such as combs and towels. It is common in children and wrestlers, where it is
specifically termed tinea gladiatorum.

### Jock Itch (Tinea Cruris)
Infection of the groin area favored by tight clothing and perspiration. This
form is more prevalent in males and often co-occurs with tinea pedis, creating
a cycle of reinfection between body sites.

### Scalp Ringworm (Tinea Capitis)
Primarily affects children and is caused by both Microsporum and Trichophyton
species. The infection can cause hair loss, scarring, and permanent alopecia
if untreated. Epidemics in schools are well documented worldwide.

### Nail Infections (Tinea Unguium / Onychomycosis)
Invasion of fingernails and toenails causes thickening, discoloration, and
crumbling. This form is extremely difficult to treat due to poor penetration
of antifungal drugs through the nail plate, often requiring months of oral
therapy with significant hepatotoxicity risks.

## Pathogenesis and Host Response

Dermatophytes are keratinophilic, specializing in utilizing keratin as a nutrient
source. They secrete keratinases, proteases, and other enzymes that [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]]
the structural proteins of skin, hair, and nails. However, dermatophytes do not
invade living tissue, remaining confined to the dead, keratinized outer layers.
The inflammation and itching are primarily caused by the [[fungal-virulence-factors-host-immune-evasion]] response
to fungal proteins. Anthropophilic species cause chronic, low-grade infections,
while zoophilic species from animals provoke more intense inflammatory responses.

## Keratinase Enzymes and Virulence

Keratinases are the primary virulence factors of dermatophytes. These serine
proteases and metalloproteases degrade the disulfide bonds and peptide linkages
in keratin, allowing the fungus to penetrate and colonize keratinized tissues.
Different species produce distinct profiles of keratinase isoforms, partially
explaining their different tissue preferences and infection patterns.

The regulation of keratinase production is controlled by environmental cues.
Gene expression is induced by keratin substrates and modulated by nutrient
availability, pH, and temperature. This substrate-sensing ability allows
dermatophytes to deploy their invasive machinery only when appropriate host
tissues are detected.
