# Compost Chemistry and the Soil Food Web

Composting is the controlled biological decomposition of organic materials into a stable, humus-rich product. Understanding the chemistry and biology of compost is fundamental to organic gardening and sustainable soil management.

## The Composting Process

### Mesophilic Phase (Room Temperature to ~40C)
- Initial colonization by mesophilic bacteria and fungi
- Rapid breakdown of simple sugars, proteins, and amino acids
- Temperature rises as microbial metabolism generates heat
- pH may drop initially due to organic acid production

### Thermophilic Phase (40C to 70C+)
- Thermophilic bacteria dominate (particularly Actinobacteria)
- Fungi like Scytalidium thermophilum play crucial roles in preparing substrata
- Breakdown of cellulose, hemicellulose, and some lignin
- Pathogens and weed seeds are killed at temperatures above 55C
- Regular turning maintains oxygen supply and distributes heat

### Cooling and Curing Phase
- Mesophilic organisms return as temperature drops
- Further decomposition of resistant materials
- Humic acids and fulvic acids form
- Mature compost has C:N ratio of approximately 20:1 or lower

## Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio

The C:N ratio is critical for successful composting:
- **Ideal starting ratio:** 25-30:1
- **Too high C:N (>30:1):** Decomposition slows; nitrogen becomes limiting
- **Too low C:N (<20:1):** Excess nitrogen lost as ammonia gas; unpleasant odors
- **Finished compost:** 15-20:1

### Common Materials and Their C:N Ratios
- Leaves: 40-80:1
- Straw: 80:1
- Wood chips: 400-700:1
- Grass clippings: 15-25:1
- Food scraps: 15-20:1
- Manure: 5-25:1 (varies by animal)
- Coffee grounds: 20:1

## The Soil Food Web

Compost feeds not just plants but the entire soil food web — the complex community of organisms that cycle nutrients in soil.

### Key Players
1. **Bacteria:** Primary decomposers; dominate in disturbed soils; fast nutrient cycling
2. **Fungi:** Slower decomposers; dominate in undisturbed soils; important for decomposition of woody materials
3. **Protozoa:** Eat bacteria; release plant-available nitrogen (the "microbial loop")
4. **Nematodes:** Both bacterial-feeding and fungal-feeding; regulate microbial populations
5. **Microarthropods:** Mites and springtails; fragment organic matter; graze on fungi
6. **Earthworms:** "Ecosystem engineers"; create burrows; mix organic matter into soil

### Nutrient Cycling
The microbial loop is central to soil fertility:
1. Soil organisms immobilize nutrients in their biomass
2. When organisms are eaten or die, nutrients are mineralized
3. Protozoa and nematodes excrete excess nitrogen as ammonium
4. This plant-available nitrogen is taken up by roots
5. Root exudates fuel further microbial activity near root zones (the rhizosphere)

## Humic Substances

The end product of decomposition includes:
- **Humic acids:** Large molecules that improve soil structure, increase CEC, and chelate micronutrients
- **Fulvic acids:** Smaller, more soluble molecules that enhance nutrient uptake
- **Humin:** Insoluble fraction that contributes to long-term soil carbon

## Practical Applications

### Building Compost
- Mix "greens" (nitrogen-rich) and "browns" (carbon-rich) materials
- Maintain moisture like a wrung-out sponge
- Ensure adequate oxygen (turn pile regularly)
- Monitor temperature (should reach 55C+ for pathogen kill)
- Cure for several weeks to months before use

### Using Compost
- Improves soil structure (sandy and clay soils alike)
- Increases water-holding capacity
- Provides slow-release nutrients
- Supports beneficial soil organisms
- Suppresses plant diseases (through competition and antibiosis)
- Buffers pH changes
