# Mycelium Inoculation Techniques

Inoculation is the process of introducing mushroom mycelium into a
prepared substrate. It is the most contamination-sensitive step in
mushroom cultivation because the substrate, now rich in nutrients and
moisture, is highly vulnerable to competitor organisms. Mastering
sterile technique during inoculation is the single most important
skill for achieving consistent cultivation success across multiple
grows and species.

## Types of Spawn

### Grain Spawn
Grain spawn consists of cereal grains (rye, millet, sorghum, wheat)
fully colonized by mushroom mycelium. Each grain kernel becomes an
independent inoculation point when mixed into a bulk substrate,
enabling rapid and even colonization. Grain spawn is the most
versatile and widely used form for indoor cultivation. It is typically
prepared in quart mason jars or autoclave bags with filter patches.
The choice of grain affects performance: rye holds moisture well and
has good nutrient content; millet provides many more individual
inoculation points per volume due to its small kernel size, which
significantly accelerates colonization; sorghum offers excellent
flowability for even distribution throughout bag culture systems.

### Liquid Culture
Liquid culture involves growing mycelium in a nutrient broth solution,
often with gentle agitation from a magnetic stir plate or orbital
shaker. It can be used to inoculate grain spawn (reducing the multi-
step process) or directly inoculate substrates for faster
colonization. Liquid culture offers considerably faster expansion than
spore inoculation and can be expanded through serial transfer across
many generations. The broth typically consists of 4% light malt
extract or honey dissolved in distilled water. However, liquid culture
has a shorter shelf life than grain spawn (1-2 months versus 2-4
months refrigerated) and requires careful handling to avoid
contamination. Visual inspection of liquid culture is more difficult
than grain spawn, making early contamination detection harder.

### Plug and Dowel Spawn
Wooden plugs or dowels colonized with mycelium are used primarily for
outdoor log cultivation. Hardwood dowels (typically oak, beech, or
maple) are inoculated in the laboratory and then shipped to growers
for outdoor installation. Plugs are inserted into drilled holes in
freshly cut hardwood logs and sealed with wax. This low-tech approach
is well suited to shiitake, maitake, and lion's mane cultivation on
logs and stumps. Plug spawn has lower contamination risk during
handling but significantly slower colonization compared to grain or
liquid inoculation methods.

### Spore Syringes and Prints
Spore-based inoculation is the slowest and least reliable method
because spores must germinate and undergo mating before robust
mycelium forms. Genetic variability is also a major concern: spores
from a single fruiting body may produce many different strains with
widely varying characteristics including growth rate, yield potential,
and fruiting quality. Spore inoculation is mainly used for
establishing new cultures from wild specimens or working with species
that are difficult to maintain vegetatively.

## Inoculation Rates

The inoculation rate (spawn-to-substrate ratio) significantly affects
both colonization speed and contamination resistance. Standard rates
range from 5-20% by volume. Higher rates (10-20%) provide faster
colonization and better contamination resistance because the
established mycelium can quickly outcompete any contaminant organisms
present. Lower rates save on spawn costs but extend the vulnerable
colonization period. For supplemented substrates, a minimum of 10% is
generally recommended. Commercial operations typically use 15-20% to
minimize turnaround time and maximize facility throughput.

## Sterile Technique Essentials

### The Still Air Box
A still air box (SAB) is the most accessible tool for home
cultivators. It is simply a transparent container with arm holes that
reduces air currents and creates a semi-sterile workspace. While not
truly sterile, it dramatically reduces the number of airborne
contaminants that can settle on exposed substrate during inoculation.
Wiping all surfaces with 70% isopropyl alcohol before work further
reduces contamination risk. The SAB should be allowed to settle for
15-30 minutes after placing items inside before beginning inoculation
work, allowing disturbed particles to settle.

### Laminar Flow Hoods
Laminar flow hoods provide the gold standard for sterile work in
mushroom cultivation. They use HEPA filters (typically 0.3 micron
rating) to create a continuous stream of particle-free air across the
work surface. With proper technique, contamination rates approach
zero. Flow hoods are essential for commercial operations and serious
hobbyists working with agar cultures, tissue cloning, and liquid
culture preparation. Proper maintenance includes regular HEPA filter
replacement according to manufacturer specifications and pre-run
sterilization of the work surface.

### Glove Boxes
Enclosed glove box systems provide additional physical protection but
can be cumbersome to work in for extended periods. Biosafety cabinets
combine HEPA filtration with physical containment. For most mushroom
cultivation work, a laminar flow hood offers the best balance of
protection, cost, and usability for repeated inoculation sessions.

## Contamination Monitoring

During the colonization period, cultivators should monitor containers
regularly for signs of contamination. Green molds (Trichoderma,
Aspergillus, Penicillium) appear as fuzzy green patches and are the
most common contaminants encountered at all scales of cultivation.
Bacterial contamination produces slimy textures and sour or rotten
odors that are unmistakable upon opening containers. Metabolic
byproducts from healthy mycelium may produce brownish or yellowish
droplets on surfaces, which should not be confused with contamination.
Early identification allows for removal of contaminated containers
before they sporulate and spread spores to neighboring substrates in
the incubation area.
