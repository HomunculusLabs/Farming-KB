# Mycelium Inoculation Techniques

Inoculation is the process of introducing mushroom [[mycelium]] into a prepared
substrate. It is the most contamination-sensitive step in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]
because the substrate, now rich in nutrients and moisture, is highly vulnerable
to competitor organisms. Mastering [[cultivator-sterile-technique-and-agar-culture]] during inoculation is the
single most important skill for achieving consistent cultivation success across
multiple grows and species.

## Types of Spawn

### Grain Spawn

Grain spawn consists of cereal grains (rye, millet, sorghum, wheat) fully
colonized by mushroom mycelium. Each grain kernel becomes an independent
inoculation point when mixed into a bulk substrate, enabling rapid and even
colonization. Grain spawn is the most versatile and widely used form for indoor
cultivation, typically prepared in quart mason jars or autoclave bags with
filter patches.

The choice of grain affects performance: rye holds moisture well with good
nutrient content; millet provides many more individual inoculation points per
volume due to its small kernel size, which significantly accelerates
colonization speed; sorghum offers excellent flowability for even distribution
throughout bag culture systems.

### Liquid Culture

Liquid culture involves growing mycelium in a nutrient broth solution, often
with gentle agitation from a magnetic stir plate or orbital shaker. It can be
used to inoculate grain spawn (reducing the multi-step process) or directly
inoculate substrates for faster colonization. The broth typically consists of 4%
light malt extract or honey dissolved in distilled water.

However, liquid culture has a shorter shelf life than grain spawn (1-2 months
versus 2-4 months refrigerated) and requires careful handling to avoid
contamination. Visual inspection of liquid culture is more difficult than grain
spawn, making early contamination detection harder. A single contaminated
syringe can ruin an entire batch of substrate.

### Plug and Dowel Spawn

Wooden plugs or dowels colonized with mycelium are used primarily for outdoor
[[comparison-mushroom-log-cultivation-vs-monotub-cultivation]]. Hardwood dowels (typically oak, beech, or maple) are inoculated
in the laboratory and then shipped to growers. Plugs are inserted into drilled
holes in freshly cut hardwood logs and sealed with wax to prevent desiccation
and contamination. This approach is well suited to shiitake, maitake, and lion
mane cultivation on logs and stumps.

### Spore Syringes and Prints

Spore-based inoculation is the slowest and least reliable method because spores
must germinate and undergo mating before robust mycelium forms. Genetic
variability is also a major concern: spores from a single fruiting body may
produce many different strains with widely varying characteristics including
growth rate, yield potential, and fruiting quality. [[mushroom-spore-inoculation-techniques]] is mainly
used for establishing new cultures from wild specimens.

## Inoculation Rates

The inoculation rate (spawn-to-substrate ratio) significantly affects both
colonization speed and contamination resistance. Standard rates range from 5-20%
by volume. Higher rates (10-20%) provide faster colonization and better
contamination resistance because the established mycelium can quickly outcompete
any contaminant organisms present. Lower rates save on spawn costs but extend
the vulnerable colonization period.

For supplemented substrates, a minimum of 10% is generally recommended.
Commercial operations typically use 15-20% to minimize turnaround time and
maximize facility throughput.

## Sterile Technique Essentials

### The Still Air Box

A still air box (SAB) is the most accessible tool for home cultivators. It is a
transparent container with arm holes that reduces air currents and creates a
semi-sterile workspace. While not truly sterile, it dramatically reduces the
number of airborne contaminants that can settle on exposed substrate. Wiping all
surfaces with 70% isopropyl alcohol before work further reduces contamination
