# Mescalero Apache Shamanism and Peyote Rejection

## Overview

The [[mescalero-apache]] of New Mexico present a unique and instructive case in the anthropological study of [[hallucinogenic-drugs]] and shamanism: they are a society that **adopted peyote** for use in shamanistic rituals and subsequently **abandoned and proscribed it** after it produced serious social conflict. This case demonstrates that the adoption of a hallucinogenic substance into religious practice is not irreversible, and that the social consequences of such adoption depend critically on the interaction between the drug's pharmacological effects and the pre-existing personality structure and cultural dynamics of the adopting society.

## Historical Background

The [[mescalero-indian-reservation]] was established in 1873. Its inhabitants include members of three Apache tribes, in descending order of numbers: Mescaleros, Chiricahuas, and Lipans. The Chiricahuas arrived as prisoners of war in 1886 after the capitulation of Geronimo and were freed in 1913. The Lipans, largely destroyed as functioning groups during the latter half of the nineteenth century, had their remaining members join the Mescaleros.

Nineteenth-century sources indicate that peyote was used in religious rites by the Mescaleros as early as 1867, the Chiricahuas by 1875, and the Lipans by 1885. Despite this documented history, the Apaches were excluded from early maps of peyote distribution in the United States and were listed as non-users in Bureau of Indian Affairs publications — an oversight that reflects the peyote practices' eventual suppression and the resulting cultural amnesia.

The Lipans appear to have obtained peyote from the [[carrizo-indians]], while the Mescaleros likely learned peyote rites from the Lipans, the Tonkawas, or other non-Apachean groups of northern Mexico, sometime before 1870. Modern informants affirm that peyote may be used for social purposes, but formerly it was ingested only during shamanistic ceremonies. By the period of intensive fieldwork (1958-1971), no confirmed peyote use could be documented on the reservation.

## Apache Religious Philosophy

Understanding why peyote was abandoned requires grasping the foundational religious concepts of the Apache peoples:

### The Nature of Power

The Apache conceive the world to be **permeated by supernatural power** (diyín) that has no intrinsic attribute of good or evil — its defining quality is its **potency**. Power approaches people through the agency of a plant, animal, or natural phenomenon, typically by means of a dream or other hallucinatory experience. Its acceptance is frequently accompanied by an ordeal. Ritual instruction may be received directly from the power itself or from other shamans.

### Universal Shamanic Potential

Any person is a potential recipient of supernatural power. Opler described the Mescaleros as "a tribe of shamans, active or potentially active." An individual might own any number of powers, and the same power can be used for either beneficial or harmful purposes.

### The Shaman-Witch Continuum

Apache religious thought does not draw a sharp distinction between shamans and witches:

- A person who uses power for purposes **oriented toward the common good** is accorded the status of **shaman**
- A person who uses power for purposes **not oriented toward the common good** is classified as a **witch**
- However, shamanism implicitly involves witchcraft, because a shaman who saves a life must then sacrifice either his own or that of a loved one — a cosmological rule that makes every healer potentially a killer

This inherent ambiguity creates an atmosphere of **jealousy, enmity, and suspicion**. Each shaman has private instructions concerning the use of power, and his rites are individually owned. Consistent with native concepts of leadership and authority, there has never been a chief shaman — each practitioner operates independently.

## The Peyote Period: Conflict and Disruption

### The Fundamental Incongruity

A critical factor in the failure of peyote among the Mescalero was a **fundamental incongruity** between the principles governing ordinary Apache shamanistic ceremonies and the rules applicable to peyote rites:

**In ordinary shamanistic practice:**
- A single shaman is the central figure
- The experiences of attendants are subordinate
- Religious ecstasy, visions, and communications with supernaturals are the **shaman's exclusive prerogatives**
- These experiences validate the shaman's unique power and efficacy

**In peyote ceremonies:**
- The hallucinogenic effects of peyote are shared by all participants
- The uniqueness of the shaman's experience disappears
- Multiple people simultaneously experience visions, perceptual distortions, and encounters with the supernatural
- The shaman's claim to privileged access to the spirit world is undermined

### From Ceremony to Conflict

The peyote meetings became arenas in which **shamanistic rivalries and witchcraft flourished**. Rather than producing cohesiveness through shared experience — as occurred in many other Native American peyote traditions — the meetings generated:

- Competition between shamans for dominance
- Suspicion and accusations of witchcraft
- Open hostilities that escalated to bloodshed
- Feuds that disrupted community life

This outcome stands in stark contrast to the usual pattern of "amiable, harmonious group sessions" of peyote use documented among other North American tribes, and to the similarly harmonious communal use of ayahuasca among Amazonian groups like the Cashinahua, Sharanahua, and Campa.

### Rejection and Proscription

The antagonisms became so severe that the peyote gatherings were ultimately abandoned. Peyote itself was identified as the cause of the disruption, and its ingestion became equated with the potential for witchcraft. Unlike many other Native American groups, the Mescaleros never became involved in the Peyote Religion (the [[native-american-church]]), and the use of peyote was not accompanied by the acceptance of Christian beliefs and practices.

**Peyote power is unique among the Mescaleros** in that it is uniformly considered to be **bad** — an exception to the general Apache principle that power has no intrinsic moral quality. Some Mescaleros believe that one other power, the owl, is also intrinsically evil, but opinions on the owl vary (it may be a witch, a ghost-inhabited creature, or the bearer of a human witch's power).

### Post-Peyote Shamanism

During 1959-60, there were thirteen accredited shamans on the reservation (Mescalero, Chiricahua, and Lipan) and approximately fifteen pseudoshamans who claimed supernatural power but were generally considered imposters. Only one shaman, called "Ancient One," was known to have participated in the original peyote camp. He and another shaman, "Black Eyes," were at times judged to be witches. They and two pseudoshamans were said to still use peyote in illicit witchcraft and love magic ceremonies.

The consequences of continued peyote use were understood to be punitive: while the legitimate shamans were not harmed by peyote power, the pseudoshamans suffered — one lost a leg in an accident and another had a close relative killed and another lose a limb. These outcomes were interpreted as evidence that peyote power had "turned back" on those who used it illegitimately.

## Apache Personality Structure

### Socialization Practices

The Boyer research team identified a pattern of child-rearing practices that profoundly shapes Apache personality:

**Maternal inconsistency**: Gross inconsistency characterizes the care of young children. The baby of the family may receive tender, loving care, but the mother periodically and impulsively abandons the infant to the supervision of others — sometimes children as young as four or five — for hours or days while she pursues narcissistic activities, commonly involving drinking. The husband's attention is no more constant.

**Abrupt weaning and displacement**: With the birth of a new baby (typically when the previous child is 18-24 months old), the older child is abruptly and often brutally displaced. The resultant sibling rivalry is intense but strongly culturally disapproved, leading to insecure repression.

**Consequences for personality development**: These practices stunt the development of basic trust (in Erikson's framework), producing marked ambivalence and suspiciousness as core features of Apache personality. Fear of loss of control — especially of hostile urges — is a dominant concern.

### The Typical Personality Configuration

L. B. Boyer, conducting psychoanalytically oriented interviews with 60 individuals ranging from age 4 to 65, found a consistent personality pattern:

- **Impulse-ridden** with fear of loss of control
- **Feebly repressed hostile urges** that are difficult to contain
- **Suggestible and phobic**
- **Avoidance of introspection** combined with seeking of outer controls and explanations for behavior
- **Suspicious and dependent** with unstable libidinal attachments
- **Men** caught between passive and aggressive urges, with insecure sexual identities

This configuration corresponds to the Western psychiatric diagnosis of **character disorder with hysterical and impulsive attributes**.

### Shamans vs. Pseudoshamans

The shamans were found to have personality configurations that concur with the typical Apache pattern, differing only in:

- Their greater success in employing **imposture** (the controlled use of performance and deception)
- Their greater **creative potential**

They are not, as some earlier anthropologists proposed, "autocultural deviants" who resolved serious psychopathology through assuming the shamanic role. The personality structure of the Apache shaman closely matches the psychoanalytic profile of the imposter.

The critical capacities for successful shamanism (and convincing imposture) are:

- **Capacity to regress in the service of the ego** (Kris, 1952)
- **Ego-controlled availability of primary process thinking** (Freud, 1915)

These capacities are related to creativity and showmanship. The pseudoshamans who were studied were found to lack both creative potential and the capacity for controlled regression — which explains their failure to achieve recognition as legitimate practitioners.

### The Peyote-Personality Interaction

The key insight of the Boyer et al. analysis is that the **physio-psychological effects of peyote** interacted destructively with the Apache personality structure:

- Apache adults already struggle to repress hostilities that are only feebly contained
- Peyote's effects — including perceptual liberation, reduced ego boundaries, and heightened emotional intensity — **made it impossible for many individuals to maintain their already fragile repression**
- The result was that pre-existing hostilities, rivalries, and suspicions erupted into open expression during peyote ceremonies
- Disruption, bloodshed, and feuds followed

This analysis suggests that the same pharmacological substance can produce dramatically different social outcomes depending on the personality structure of the users. Among cultures with stronger ego controls and less repressed hostility, peyote produced harmonious communal ceremonies. Among the Mescalero Apache, it became a catalyst for the expression of precisely those impulses that the culture strained to contain.

## Broader Implications

### The Culture-and-Personality Factor

The Mescalero case demonstrates that **pharmacology alone does not determine the social role of a hallucinogenic substance**. The same drug — peyote — that supported the development of the harmonious Native American Church among other tribes produced chaos and proscription among the Apache. The intervening variables are:

1. **Pre-existing personality structure** shaped by distinctive socialization practices
2. **Cultural framework** for interpreting and managing altered states of consciousness
3. **Social dynamics** of the shamanic system (competitive vs. collaborative)
4. **Economic and political context** (reservation confinement, cultural deprivation)
