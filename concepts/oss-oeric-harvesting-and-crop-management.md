# Oss & Oeric: Harvesting and Crop Management

The final active stage of the Oss & Oeric cultivation method
involves managing the fruiting process to maximize yield across
multiple flushes. The 1976 guide describes the progression from
primordia formation through mature harvest, including strategies
for handling aborted mushrooms, managing multiple flushes, and
dealing with common fruiting problems.

## Pin Formation and Development

After casing, the mycelium grows up into the soil, penetrating
just beneath the surface over 2-3 weeks. The mycelium may
occasionally break through to the surface, which is controlled
by daily fine-mist spraying to "knock down" the surface growth.

By the 14th to 20th day after casing, the mycelial network at
the soil-glass interface develops visible intersecting nodes that
differentiate into tiny white dots distributed along the jar
perimeter. These are mushroom primordia, which gradually enlarge
and take on the appearance of tiny mushrooms with squat, fat stems
and dark brownish heads, reaching the "pin" stage at approximately
1-2mm in length.

## Harvest Timing

Pins continue to enlarge and eventually thrust above the casing
surface, both at the sides and in the center of the jar. Once
young mushrooms have penetrated the surface, an additional 5-10
days is required for them to reach full maturity. Mature mushrooms
are several inches tall with caps ranging from one to several
centimeters in diameter.

The key harvest indicator is veil rupture. As the cap enlarges,
the partial veil connecting the cap margin to the stipe detaches,
forming an annulus (ring) on the stem. Mushrooms should be
harvested as soon as veil rupture occurs. For spore collection,
specimens should be allowed to mature further until the cap
flattens to an umbrella shape.

The authors recommend grasping the stalks firmly when harvesting
and removing any soil from the bottom of the stems.

## Aborted Mushrooms

A significant portion of mushrooms may grow to approximately half
size or less and then cease growing. These "aborts" can still be
plucked, dried, and consumed, though they are less aesthetically
appealing than fully mature specimens. With practice, growers can
learn to spot aborts early and remove them from the cultures.

Aborts left in place are susceptible to bacterial attack, which
quickly renders them "both ugly and unusable." Prompt removal of
aborts is therefore important for maintaining overall crop hygiene.

## Managing Bottom Primordia

Occasionally, mushroom primordia form toward the bottom of the
jar and begin to mature but cannot break through the casing soil
surface due to constriction by the glass. The authors suggest two
strategies for addressing this problem:

**Tinfoil Wrapping:** Wrapping jars with aluminum foil up to the
top of the casing soil can inhibit side and bottom pinning to some
extent by blocking light from reaching the lower portions of the jar.

**Jar Breaking:** If bottom pinning becomes excessive, the jar can
be carefully broken and the mycelium removed as a single block.
This allows primordia along the sides of the block, which would
otherwise abort due to glass constriction, to develop to maturity.
The authors caution that the jar must be broken carefully to avoid
bruising the fungus and causing personal injury.

## Multiple Flushes

The guide implies that cased jars can produce multiple flushes
of mushrooms over time. The daily watering regimen (2-3 squirts
per jar) is described as necessary to "maintain continuous
fruiting." The styrofoam box system allows jars at different
stages to be stored in chronological order for efficient management.

Cased jars maintained under proper conditions can continue
producing mushrooms for several flushes. Each flush is typically
smaller than the previous one as the substrate nutrients are
gradually depleted. The casing soil must be kept consistently
moist but never saturated throughout all flushes.

## Yield Expectations

The jar-based method typically produces between one and three flushes
per cased jar, with diminishing returns. The first flush is generally
the largest. Experienced cultivators could expect several grams of
dried material per jar over the culture's lifetime.

## Harvesting Best Practices

**Timing:** Harvest as the veil tears for maximum potency while
minimizing spore deposition. Spore-covered mushrooms are consumable
but may have slightly reduced potency.

**Technique:** Grasp the stalk firmly at the base and twist gently.
Cutting with a blade leaves a stump that can become a contamination
vector.

**Handling:** Minimize handling to reduce bruising (blue
discoloration from psilocin oxidation). Excessive bruising can
damage remaining primordia.

## Comparison with Modern Harvest Practices

Modern cultivators have developed additional techniques. "Twist and
pull" remains standard, while some prefer harvesting entire clusters
by cutting at the base for faster processing. Food dehydrators are
now preferred over gas ovens for precise temperature control.

## Common Problems During Harvest

Several issues may arise during harvest. Overlay, where mycelium
grows too thickly over the casing surface, can prevent pin
formation. Trichoderma (green mold) often appears during later
flushes as substrate nutrients are depleted. Affected areas should
be cut away and treated with a light salt solution.
