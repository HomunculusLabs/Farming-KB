A woodlot managed through coppicing provides a sustainable, renewable supply of firewood, [[natural-building-materials-guide]], and other forest products while improving biodiversity and forest health.

## What Is Coppicing

Coppicing is a traditional woodland management technique where trees are periodically cut back to ground level, stimulating rapid regrowth from the stump. This creates a continuous supply of usable wood in relatively short rotation cycles (7 years instead of 40+ for mature timber).

## How Coppicing Works

1. **Tree is cut** close to the ground at the point where the trunk begins to widen into the roots
2. **Each section** is cut at a 30-degree angle facing outward so rainwater runs off onto the ground
3. **The stump sprouts** multiple new shoots the following [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]
4. **New growth** provides usable wood in 7–25 years depending on species and desired product
5. **The cycle repeats**: Cut again, and the stump regrows again

## Benefits of Coppicing

- **Promotes biodiversity**: Letting light into the forest floor creates microclimates and allows undergrowth to flourish
- **Makes trees live longer**: Coppiced trees often outlive unmanaged trees of the same species
- **Faster production**: Wood is available every 7 years instead of waiting decades for mature timber
- **Easier harvesting**: Smaller trees are much easier to cut down than large timber
- **Continuous supply**: A managed coppice provides a steady, predictable wood supply each year
- **[[mycorrhizal-fungi-soil-carbon-sequestration-by-type]]**: Rapidly growing coppice shoots absorb significant carbon

## coppicing Species

Choose species based on their ability to regrow from stumps and grow quickly:

- **Maple**: Good all-round coppice species
- **Pawpaw**: Produces fruit alongside wood
- **Birch**: Fast-growing, good for small wood products
- **Beech**: Strong timber, good regrowth
- **Ash**: Excellent for tool handles and furniture
- **Oak**: Valuable timber, slow but reliable regrowth
- **Alder**: Nitrogen-fixing, grows well in wet areas
- **Willow**: Very fast growth, good for basketry and fencing
- **Hawthorn**: Good for hedging and wildlife
- **Persimmon**: Fruit production
- **Ginkgo**: Ornamental and medicinal value
- **[[permaculture-honey-locust-pod-fodder-system]]**: Nitrogen-fixing, animal forage
- **Black locust**: Extremely durable wood, nitrogen-fixing

## Woodlot Composition

Plant a mixture of:

### Polewood
- Used for fencing, outbuildings, and carpentry
- Fast-growing species on shorter rotation cycles

### High-Quality Timber
- Provides food and possible cash crops (walnut, bamboo)
- Longer rotation cycles for larger pieces

### Hedges and Shrubs
- Keep grass at bay
- Establish microclimates for trees to flourish
- Provide wildlife habitat and additional forage

## Sugars and Sap

The forest space can also grow trees for food and alcohol production:

- **Sugary sap trees**: Maple, birch, and others tapped for syrup production
- **Fruit trees**: Planted among other trees for fruit juice, which can also be distilled
- **Sugar cane or beets**: Can serve the same purpose as sap trees
- **Trees are particularly valuable** for sugar production because they require little care
- **Byproduct recycling**: Any waste from sap processing or distillation should be returned to the forest as mulch

## Harvesting Timeline

- **Minimum age at harvest**: 7 years
- **Maximum practical age**: Usually 25 years
- **General rule**: The longer you wait, the larger the timber
- **Annual yield**: You will be cutting down approximately 1/4 of the trees each year in a mature coppice
