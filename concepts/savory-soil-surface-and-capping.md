# Soil Surface and Capping

The condition of the soil surface is the single most critical factor
determining the health of all four ecosystem foundation blocks. In
Holistic Resource Management, soil surface management is central to
the management of succession, water cycle, mineral cycle, and energy
flow. An exposed, capped soil surface creates a cascade of degradation
that affects every aspect of ecosystem function.

## The Mechanism of Capping

On bare and exposed ground, the direct impact of raindrops destroys
any crumb structure present. The damage is governed by the size and
velocity of raindrops. Evidence shows up under tall trees where large
drops form on leaves and reach terminal velocity in about twenty-two
feet of fall, creating maximum impact below the canopy.

When raindrop impact breaks down surface crumb structure, it frees
organic and lightweight material to wash away while heavier fine
particles settle and seal, or cap, the soil. The importance of crumb
structure to water penetration is easily demonstrated: pour water on a
bowl of wheat grains and it soaks in; pour it on flour and it seals
the surface and runs off.

Capping is not always obvious on sandy soils but is almost always
present. Some soils develop caps so hard that only a sharp object can
break them. Tapping a severely capped soil with the fingers can produce
a hollow drum-like sound.

## Soil Cover: Two Forms

Soil cover generally comes in two forms. Erect growing plants intercept
rainfall so drops hit the ground with less energy. Dead, prone plant
material slows the flow of water across the land. Water can flow quite
fast between plants where no litter impedes it.

In nonbrittle environments, soil cover is seldom a problem because
plants are spaced closely and dead material stays in place. If bare
areas develop, they recover rapidly because active decay causes
surrounding plants to fall and provide litter quickly. The successional
process advances so rapidly that soil does not remain uncovered long.

None of this happens in brittle environments. Old plant material only
reaches the soil surface after long delay because decay occurs mainly
through slow oxidation and weathering. Much wider plant spacings allow
wind and water to carry litter away before it can protect the surface.

## The Chain Reaction of Degradation

The key to the health of the mineral cycle, like succession and the
water cycle, lies in the condition of the soil surface. An exposed,
capped surface creates a harsh microenvironment where biological
breakdown occurs slowly. The capped surface also limits air exchange
between soil and atmosphere. The chain reaction is relentless:

- As aeration decreases, life decreases
- As life decreases, organic material decreases
- As organic material decreases, soil structure deteriorates
- As soil structure decreases, aeration decreases further

This feedback loop ripples through the ecosystem. Fewer plants produce
less soil cover, creating more bare, capped soil. The process is
self-accelerating and extremely difficult to reverse without
intervention.

## Aeration and Plant Health

Plants must have both water and air in the soil. They grow best when
soil particles have air spaces between them. Four main factors govern
soil aeration:

1. Excessive compaction that reduces air spaces between particles
2. Lack of sufficient organic material to maintain crumb structure
3. An impervious clay or rock layer that inhibits drainage
4. A sealed or capped soil surface that reduces the soil's ability
   to breathe

A good crumb structure extending to the surface provides spaces large
enough to allow carbon dioxide to exit and oxygen to enter readily.
When soil is waterlogged, air is driven out of the spaces between
particles, and plant growth suffers dramatically.

## The Algal Crust Dilemma

In brittle environments, soil surfaces often develop communities of
algae, lichens, and mosses that can retard the advance of succession
for thousands of years once they encrust the surface. These crusts do
inhibit erosion to a degree on fairly flat ground, which has produced
debate about whether to disturb them.

Traditional American range management favors protecting algal crusts
and disparages any kind of trampling because breaking the crust
increases erosion in the short run. Savory argues that a much deeper,
long-term observation shows that heavy trampling over a short period
leads to establishment of plants that protect soil better than algae
ever can. In several national parks and monuments in the western United
States, despite decades of crust protection, the lack of grass plants
and more complex communities is most obvious.

## Organic Matter and Porosity

The porosity of soil both at the surface and below is increased by
organic material, which binds particles into larger structured pieces
with bigger spaces between them. Insects, worms, and small mammals
create additional space by burrowing and tunneling. Plant roots also
open channels for water movement. One Minnesota study showed that
earthworms added to cornfields increased absorption rates thirty-five
fold over control fields in only six weeks.

## Managing the Soil Surface

Management tools must be applied so that soil acquires and maintains
adequate organic content and animal activity. Tools that break up a
capped surface, increase organic content and crumb structure, and
create rough surfaces that slow water flow all improve soil surface
condition. In brittle environments, periodic animal impact through
herd effect provides the disturbance needed to maintain soil health.
