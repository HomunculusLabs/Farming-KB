# Protozoa and Nematodes — Nutrient Mineralizers

Protozoa and nematodes occupy the critical middle tier of the [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-types-amoeba-flagellate-ciliate]]:

**Amoebae** are the largest, constantly moving by pouring cytoplasm into false appendages called pseudopodia. Some have shell-like exoskeletons with predefined holes; others lack shells. They lack mouths and ingest bacteria by surrounding and engulfing them in gas bubbles containing digestive enzymes.

**Ciliates** are smaller than amoebae but still much larger than bacterial prey. Covered with rows of beating hairs that propel them toward food and create currents bringing bacteria into their mouth region. The familiar paramecium is a ciliate.

**Flagellates** are the smallest type, using one or two whip-like flagella to move about. Most are heterotrophic, obtaining nutrients from eating other organisms. A few, like Euglena, produce their own food via photosynthesis.

### Symbiotic relationships

Protozoa form symbiotic relationships, particularly with bacteria. Flagellates in termite guts digest wood fibers while nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the same guts provide fixed nitrogen — a triple symbiotic relationship. Some ciliates live in sand and "farm" bacteria colonies. Methane-generating bacteria inside ciliates are responsible for methane gas development during anaerobic respiration.

### Population dynamics and balance

Protozoa arrive at bacterial food sources in a consistent progression: first flagellates (smallest, can access tight soil pores), then ciliates, then amoebae. A single protozoan can eat 10,000 bacteria per day. The combined grazing pressure diminishes bacterial populations, triggering larger protozoa to eat smaller ones, which allows bacteria to stabilize.

Counterintuitively, increasing protozoa often increases bacterial populations: fewer bacteria means less competition for nutrients among survivors, so they multiply well-fed. Protozoa also keep bad-guy nematode populations in check by competing for the same food resources or by directly attacking them.

Why aren't all bacteria consumed? Bacterial slime is hard for protozoa to penetrate and lacks the oxygen they require. Bacteria can also hide in tiny soil pores.

### Moisture requirements and dormancy

Protozoa need moisture to live, travel, and reproduce. Hygroscopic water — the thin film on soil particle surfaces — provides this under normal conditions. When soils dry, most protozoa encyst in a protective cyst. Survival times vary by species; some withstand several years of drought.

**Predatory nematodes** feed on protozoa, algae, grubs, other nematodes, and small invertebrates — preventing overgrazing of bacteria and fungi and keeping destructive herbivore nematode populations down. Some were among the first [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], and are directly responsible for some decomposition.

### Mineralization by nematodes

Nematodes need less nitrogen than protozoa, so bacterivorous and fungivorous nematodes release even more previously immobilized nitrogen into the rhizosphere in ammonium form. If pH is below 7 (low nitrifying bacteria populations), mineralized nitrogen remains predominantly as ammonium.

Arguably, mineralization is the most important thing nematodes do for gardeners.

### Movement, soil requirements, and transport

Nematodes require porous soils to travel. Compacted or poorly textured soil blocks their movement, causing die-off or migration, and greatly diminishing nitrogen flow to plants. They locate food by sensing minute soil temperature variations or by tracking specific chemicals — acting like heat-seeking missiles.

Nematodes inadvertently transport bacteria and fungi to new areas as these organisms hitch rides on nematode skin. This is a great advantage to bacteria with extremely low soil mobility. Fungi too can hitch a ride — sometimes because the nematode is prey to a fungus attack and goes about its business while being eaten alive.
