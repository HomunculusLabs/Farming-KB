# Principle 10: Use and Value Diversity (Detailed Analysis)

From "Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability" by David Holmgren.

## Core Definition

The great diversity of forms, functions and interactions in nature and humanity are the source for evolved systemic complexity. The role and value of diversity in nature, culture and Permaculture is itself complex, dynamic, and at times apparently contradictory.

Diversity needs to be seen as a result of the balance and tension in nature between variety and possibility on the one hand, and productivity and power on the other. It is now widely recognised that monoculture is a major cause of vulnerability to pests and diseases. Polyculture is one of the most important applications of the use of diversity.

## Biodiversity Conservation

While Permaculture incorporates strategies to conserve biodiversity, it also seeks a more fundamental redesign of all we do, so that biodiversity becomes a valued and functional part of our world.

Nature is equally concerned with diversity and with power and productivity. Teaching of environmental science tends to ignore this aspect of nature in an effort to counter the obsession with prevailing economic measures of productivity and power. For a deeper understanding of the use of diversity, we need to see it in dynamic balance and complementary tension in all systems, at all levels.

## Specialization in Nature

One broad ecological classification is as generalist or specialist species. Generalists are jacks-of-all-trades, able to thrive in a range of habitats. Generalist plants make good pioneers, moderating and improving the environment for more sensitive specialists. Specialists are more like the master craftsperson, efficient in a particular habitat but not very flexible.

At a landscape scale, the diversity of ecosystems is mapped onto the underlying biophysical variation created by climate, landform and bedrock minerals. Different species and ecosystems are most efficient and powerful in using available resources in different places.

## Diversity Creating Stability

Diversity provides alternative pathways for essential ecosystem functions in the face of changing conditions. This makes sense from our understanding of traditional human systems, where diversity of crops and resources provides insurance against failure.

Where available resources are little differentiated into niches, a single generalist species tends to dominate, resulting in low diversity. Where resources are highly differentiated into niches, different species tend to prevail in different niches. Beyond the absence of competition, mutual and even symbiotic relationships can develop because of the different needs and outputs of those species.

Tropical rainforests, some of the most stable ecosystems, have high biodiversity. Large-scale climatic disturbance cycles periodically eliminate much of the biodiversity from temperate latitudes; in the tropics, evolving diversity can continue to accumulate.

## The Diversity-Stability Debate

The relationship between diversity and ecosystem stability has been a central question in ecology since the 1950s. Robert MacArthur's 1955 paper proposed that more diverse communities are more stable because the number of links in the food web increases, providing alternative pathways when one species is lost. This hypothesis was challenged in the 1970s by Robert May's mathematical models, which suggested that complex ecosystems with many species and interactions might actually be less stable. The ensuing debate generated decades of research.

The modern consensus, supported by large-scale experiments such as the Cedar Creek Biodiversity Experiment (running since 1982) and the BIODEPTH pan-European experiment, is that biodiversity generally enhances ecosystem stability through several mechanisms:
- **Portfolio effect:** Just as diversified investment portfolios are less volatile than single-stock investments, communities with multiple species show more stable productivity over time because species respond differently to environmental fluctuations.
- **Insurance effect:** Biodiversity provides a reservoir of species that can increase in abundance to compensate for declines in others when conditions change. This is directly relevant to climate adaptation, where diverse agricultural systems are more likely to contain species tolerant of new conditions.
- **Sampling effect:** More diverse communities are more likely to contain particularly productive or resilient species that dominate ecosystem function.

Holmgren's diversity principle draws on this ecological understanding while extending it to designed human systems. The principle acknowledges that diversity is not an end in itself but a means to achieving resilient, productive, and self-maintaining systems.

## Functional Diversity vs Species Diversity

A critical distinction in permaculture is between species diversity (counting the number of different species) and functional diversity (the range of different functions, roles, and niches occupied). A garden with 20 varieties of lettuce has high species diversity but low functional diversity. A polyculture with fruit trees, nitrogen-fixing shrubs, ground-cover herbs, root crops, and climbing vines has high functional diversity even with fewer total species.

Holmgren emphasizes that functional diversity is what matters most for system resilience. Each element should perform multiple functions, and each important function should be supported by multiple elements. This principle of redundancy ensures that if one element fails (disease, weather event, market disruption), other elements can partially or fully compensate. In practical design, this means choosing plants and animals that fill distinct ecological niches -- different root depths, canopy heights, flowering times, nutrient requirements, and growth habits -- rather than simply maximizing the species count.

## Diversity in Human Systems

Holmgren extends the diversity principle well beyond biological systems to encompass cultural, economic, and social diversity:
- **Economic diversity:** Local economies dependent on a single industry (mining towns, monocrop farming regions) are extremely vulnerable to market fluctuations, resource depletion, and policy changes. Diversified local economies with multiple income streams, local manufacturing, food production, and service sectors are more resilient to external shocks.
- **Cultural diversity:** The loss of indigenous knowledge systems, local languages, and traditional land management practices represents a contraction of humanity's collective adaptive capacity. Holmgren draws parallels between biological monocultures and cultural homogenization, arguing that both reduce the range of responses available to changing conditions.
- **Social diversity:** Communities with diverse ages, skills, backgrounds, and perspectives are better equipped to solve problems and adapt to change than homogeneous groups. Intergenerational knowledge transfer is particularly important in permaculture, where experienced practitioners and energetic newcomers each contribute essential perspectives.

## Examples from Permaculture Practice

Real-world applications of the diversity principle include:
- **Food forests:** Multi-strata perennial polycultures that mimic natural forest structure, typically including canopy trees, understory trees, shrubs, herbaceous plants, ground covers, root crops, and climbing vines. A mature food forest may contain 100+ species but derives its resilience from functional layering rather than species count alone.
- **Guild design:** Plant guilds group species with complementary relationships around a central element (usually a fruit or nut tree). The classic apple tree guild includes nitrogen fixers (comfrey, clover), dynamic accumulators (yarrow, chicory), pest confusers (garlic, nasturtium), pollinator attractors (dill, fennel), and mulch producers (comfrey, artichoke).
- **Integrated animal systems:** Combining multiple animal species (chickens, ducks, goats, bees) creates complementary grazing patterns, pest control functions, and nutrient cycling that no single species can provide alone. Chickens scratch and manure, goats browse woody weeds, ducks control slugs in wet areas, and bees pollinate crops -- each filling a distinct niche.
- **Seed saving and variety preservation:** Maintaining multiple varieties of each crop species preserves genetic diversity that may become critically important as climate conditions shift. Heritage and landrace varieties often possess resilience traits absent from modern commercial cultivars.

## Limits and Pitfalls of Diversity

Holmgren is careful to note that diversity has practical limits and costs:
- **Management complexity:** Highly diverse systems require more knowledge, observation, and management skill than simple systems. For beginners, a well-managed simple system outperforms a poorly managed complex one.
- **Diminishing returns:** Adding species beyond the carrying capacity of the site or the management capacity of the gardener provides no additional benefit and may create competition that reduces overall productivity.
- **Yield trade-offs:** Maximum total yield often comes from moderate diversity rather than maximum diversity. Pure monocultures of the highest-yielding variety typically outyield polycultures of the same crop area, though polycultures produce more total output when all species are counted and provide more consistent production across years.
- **Inappropriate diversity:** Introducing species unsuited to the local climate, soil, or ecosystem can create more problems than benefits. Diversity should arise from careful observation and design, not from indiscriminately adding species.

## Practical Applications

- Conduct a thorough site analysis before design, mapping zones, sectors, soil, water, and existing vegetation.
- Prioritize elements that serve multiple functions — every component should fulfill at least three roles.
- Design for energy efficiency by placing frequently-used elements closest to the zone of daily activity.
- Use biological resources (plants, animals, fungi) instead of mechanical or chemical alternatives wherever possible.
- Plan for succession — design initial plantings that will evolve into mature, self-maintaining systems.
- Create redundancy in critical systems to increase resilience against failure or disruption.
- Incorporate small-scale, slow solutions that can be tested and adjusted before large investments.
- Map and maximize edge effects between different ecosystems or landscape elements.
