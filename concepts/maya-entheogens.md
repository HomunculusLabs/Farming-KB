# Maya Entheogens in Plants of the Gods

Schultes and Hofmann present evidence that the Maya civilization employed
hallucinogenic plants in their ceremonial and religious practices, though the
archaeological and ethnographic record for Maya entheogen use is less complete
than for the Aztec tradition.

## Mushroom Cult Evidence

Several lines of evidence suggest Maya involvement with hallucinogenic
mushrooms. Mayan languages in Guatemala had terms for mushrooms named for the
underworld. Miniature mushroom stones, some 2,200 years old, have been found
in archaeological sites near Guatemala City, and stone mushroom effigies
buried with a Mayan dignitary have been postulated to suggest a connection
with the Nine Lords of the Xibalba described in the sacred book [[wasson-brough-soma-debate]].

Actually, more than two hundred mushroom stone effigies have been
discovered, the oldest dating from the first millennium BC. Although the
majority are Guatemalan, some have been unearthed in El Salvador and
Honduras and others as far north as Veracruz and Guerrero in Mexico. These
artifacts indicate the great antiquity of a sophisticated sacred use of
hallucinogenic mushrooms in the Maya region.

See also: [[mushroom-stone-artifacts]], [[mexican-mushroom-stones-archaeological-evidence]]

## Contemporary Maya Mushroom Use

A species of Psilocybe and possibly also Panaeolus are used today near the
classic Maya ceremonial center of Palenque. Hallucinogenic mushrooms have been
reported in use along the border between Chiapas in Mexico and Guatemala.
Whether these modern mushroom practices in the Maya region represent
vestiges of former use or have been recently introduced from Oaxaca remains
unclear.

## The Fly Agaric Among the Quiche-Maya

The Maya of highland Guatemala recognize [[amanita-muscaria-ethnobotany]] as
having special properties. They call it Kakulja-ikox ("lightning mushroom"),
relating it to Rajaw Kakulja, the Lord of Lightning, who directs the
operation of chacs (dwarf rain-bringers, now usually known by their Christian
designation, angelitos). The Quiche name Kaqulja refers to its legendary
origin, while Itzel-cox refers to its sacred power as "evil or diabolical
mushroom."

Schultes and Hofmann observe that thunder and lightning have been widely and
anciently associated with mushrooms in both hemispheres, especially with the
fly agaric. They conclude that "the Quiche-Maya are evidently well aware the
Amanita muscaria is no ordinary mushroom but relates to the supernatural."

## Water Lily -- Nymphaea in Maya Culture

Water lilies ([[blue-lotus-history]]) enjoyed an exceptionally prominent
place in the mythology and art of the Mayan world from the Middle Classical
period until the inception of the Mexican period. Nymphaea ampla and N.
caerulea are the species relevant to New World use, and their ritual
significance parallels Old World traditions involving these same plants.

## Highland Maya Practices

Highland Maya cultures in Guatemala maintain various traditions involving
psychoactive plants, though specific details in Schultes and Hofmann's
account are more limited than for lowland or Mexican groups. The Quiche-Maya
connection to the fly agaric represents the most clearly documented
entheogenic practice among the Maya in this source.

## The Quiche-Maya and the Supernatural

Schultes and Hofmann emphasize that the Quiche-Maya are "evidently well
aware the Amanita muscaria is no ordinary mushroom but relates to the
supernatural." This awareness reflects a broader Maya cosmology in which
plants, animals, and natural phenomena participate in a complex web of
supernatural relationships. The association of mushrooms with lightning and
rain deities places them within the most fundamental aspects of Maya
religious thought -- the agricultural cycle and the sustenance of life.

## The Popol Vuh and Underworld Mushroom Traditions

Several early sources have suggested that Mayan languages in Guatemala had
mushrooms named for the underworld. This linguistic evidence connects the
ethnobotanical record to the Popol Vuh, the sacred book of the Quiche-Maya,
which describes the Nine Lords of the Xibalba (the underworld). The
connection between mushroom stones and the Xibalba lords, while not
conclusively proven, suggests that hallucinogenic mushrooms may have served
as vehicles for underworld journeying in Maya ceremonial practice.

## Ololiuqui in Maya Regions

The morning glory seeds known as Ololiuqui, while most closely associated
with Aztec practice, have also been used in regions of southern Mexico and
Guatemala that fall within the broader Maya cultural area. The use of these
seeds, whose active constituents are lysergic acid amides, represents
another dimension of entheogenic practice that may have crossed cultural
boundaries between Maya and Aztec civilizations.

See also: Ololiuqui

## Limitations of the Record

Schultes and Hofmann acknowledge that the Maya entheogenic record is less
complete than for Aztec or other Mesoamerican cultures. The suppression of
indigenous practices following Spanish conquest was particularly effective in
Maya regions. Archaeological evidence continues to accumulate, but definitive
connections between specific Maya ceremonies and specific psychoactive plants
remain tentative for many species.

## Comparative Context

The Maya entheogenic tradition should be understood alongside the broader
Mesoamerican pattern documented by Schultes and Hofmann. The mushroom stone
artifacts found throughout Guatemala suggest a shared sacred mushroom complex
extending from central Mexico through the Maya highlands, part of a
continuum of pre-Columbian entheogenic practice that included the Aztec
Teonanacatl cult and the Oaxacan mushroom velada tradition.
