# Composting Methods and Compost Crops

Composting is an important way to recycle carbon, nitrogen, magnesium, sulfur, calcium, phosphorus, potash, and trace minerals — all elements necessary to maintain the biological cycles of life.

## Functions of Compost in the Soil

1. **Improves soil structure** — Makes soil easier to work, with good aeration, water retention, and erosion resistance.
2. **Provides nutrients** — Organic acids make nutrients in the soil more available to plants. Fewer nutrients leach out in soil with adequate [[vegetable-insect-and-disease-control]] attacks. Most insects look for sick plants to eat.
6. **Maintains fertility sustainably** — Once soil is brought to satisfactory fertility, it can be maintained with compost, good crop rotation, and recycling of plant residues.

## Humus: The Key to Soil Health

Humus is the living and dead bodies of microbial life. It acts as a site of nutrient adsorption and exchange for plants. The surfaces of humus particles carry a negative electric charge that attracts positively charged nutrients (calcium, sodium, magnesium, potassium, trace minerals). Plant roots "bargain" with humus, exchanging hydrogen ions for nutrient ions. Humus is the most reliable plant food — it feeds plants with nutrients they pick up on its surface and safely stores nutrients in forms not readily leached.

## Composting in Nature

Three natural forms of composting:

1. **Manures** — Plant and animal foods composted inside an animal's body (including earthworms), then aged outside. Earthworm castings are 5x richer in nitrogen, 2x richer in calcium, 7x richer in phosphorus, and 11x richer in potassium than the soil they inhabit.
2. **Surface decomposition** — Animal and plant bodies decay on and within soil.
3. **Root decay** — Roots, root hairs, and microbial life remain and decay beneath the surface after harvesting. One rye plant grows 3 miles of root hairs per day, 387 miles of roots in a season.

## GROW BIOINTENSIVE Compost Recipe

**By weight:**
- 1/3 dry vegetation (rehydrated as you water)
- 1/3 green vegetation (including kitchen wastes)
- 1/3 soil

**By volume:** approximately equal parts green and dry materials to 1/4 part soil.

This gives a carbon-nitrogen ratio of about 30:1, producing a hotter (thermophilic: 113-149°F) pile with faster-releasing cured compost (nutrients released over 3 months to 2 years).

**Cooler pile option (60:1 ratio):** 2 parts dry vegetation to 1/2 part green vegetation and 1/4 part soil. Produces slower-releasing compost (3 months to 5,000 years), especially with lignin-rich materials like corn and sorghum stalks. Much more cured carbon per unit added — potentially essential for sustainable soil fertility.

## Building the Pile

1. **Loosen the ground** underneath to a depth of 12 inches for drainage.
2. **Lay down roughage** (brush, corn stalks) 3 inches thick for [[oyster-mushroom-coffee-grounds-cultivation]], eggshells, and citrus rinds.

## Size, Location, and Timing

- **Minimum size:** 3' x 3' x 3' (1 cubic yard, ~1,000 pounds). In colder climates, 4' x 4' x 4' minimum.
- **Location:** Under a deciduous oak tree (ideal), any other deciduous tree (except walnut and eucalyptus), or any shady place. Keep 6 feet from tree trunk.
- **Timing:** Spring or autumn when biological activity is highest.
- **Curing:** Normally ready in 3 to 6 months. Ready when dark, rich looking, crumbles in hands, and smells like freshly plowed soil.

### Turning

Usually one turning at about the 3-week point, after temperature peaks and falls. A GROW BIOINTENSIVE pile should be ready about 2 months after turning.
