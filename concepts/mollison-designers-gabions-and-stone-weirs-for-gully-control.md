# Gabions and Stone Weirs for Gully Control

Gabions and stone weirs are critical structures in permaculture for
arresting gully erosion, trapping sediment, and restoring degraded
landscapes. Mollison devotes considerable attention to these low-cost,
locally-sourced interventions as primary tools for healing eroded
drylands and humid landscapes alike. They represent a cornerstone of
the [[mollison-arid-lands-design-and-erosion-control]] and [[mollison-designers-vetiver-grass-for-erosion-control]].

## The Problem of Gully Erosion

Gullies form when overgrazing, vehicle tracks, cattle paths, or poorly
built roads concentrate sheet flow into culverts that focus water energy.
Fragile soils, loose shaley ground, and recent sediments exposed to
sudden overland flows may gully in just a few downpours. In deserts,
this most frequently occurs where foothill flood plains are overgrazed,
where rabbits tunnel into loose subsoils, or where badly built roads
direct sheet flow to concentrated discharge points.

Once gullying has developed, Mollison notes that there may be no
alternative to allowing water to flow in the gully systems and creating
fields there, as extensive gullied badlands are too expensive to treat
with wholesale earthworks.

## Controlling Gullies: Principles

Controlling gullies is essentially a matter of:

- Relieving the causes, usually those of overuse and lack of water
  control; and
- Preventing further cut-back of the gullies.

Such areas need fencing out, light or no stocking of animals, pest
control, and above all, spreader banks for the water. In the valleys
above the gullies, stream flow can be diverted to flood spreader
channels, and a series of rip-lines drawn downhill from the gully bank
towards the ridges (sloping at 1:1000 or so).

## Gabion Construction and Placement

Stone-filled wire mesh cages (gabions) placed in stream beds
effectively check and stabilise soil erosion. A series of weirs
0.5 m high traps sediment so that tree establishment can stabilise the
run-off. Weirs can be made from a series of well-braced wire-mesh
fences or low stone bunds across gully floors, or stone-filled wire
baskets (gabions) placed to spread the flow of floodwater.

Key construction principles include:

- Gabions must always extend up the gully sides, well above any flood
  level
- Stone weirs are more easily maintained and restored than wire fences,
  and are more permanent
- All weirs need Vetiver grass or some tough plant barrier to hold
  erosion at the structure margins
- If weirs are higher than 0.5 m, a splash apron is necessary at the
  foot where plunge pools form
- All weirs are started at the gully outlet and built in sequence
  upstream

Where no local supply of boulder is available, the lower height of
weirs are built of wire mesh only. Where plentiful stones exist, stone
seepage weirs are very satisfactory and form roads across the gullies.

## Case Study: Cape Verde, Sahel

At projects such as that at Assomada, Cape Verde, terrace-building and
check dams built of stone-filled wire mesh gabions in the stream beds
have effectively checked and stabilised soil erosion. Both help to
absorb run-off water. A change from sugar cane cropping to tree and
vegetable culture also assists soil conservation and aids local
nutrition. Some 2,000 hectares have been reclaimed in two target
valleys.

Pigeon pea (Cajanus cajan) supplied food and ground cover, and
cooperatives formed to grow and market food, with credit provided for
crop production. Wells and galleries were dug to tap groundwater.

## Rock-Filled Mesh Weirs

Rock-filled mesh "baskets" (gabions) create silt fields behind them,
spreading water and allowing revegetation. Earth or concrete walls, or
gabions, hold silt fields and spread water across the landscape. Small
gullies can be bulldozed full, or interceptor drains made across them
to cut off flow. Larger gullies can also be filled or dammed if soils
are suitable.

As with many dryland techniques, gully retention banks need to be
small, frequent, and well-made to effectively spread flow and create
absorbent flats where water trapped in silts creates beneficial growing
media.

## Integration with Vegetation

Gabions and weirs work best when combined with [[mollison-designers-pioneer-species-for-exhausted-tropical-soils]] planting. Behind each wall, gabions allow silt to build up in the earlier stages. Once stabilised, the area can be planted to hardy shrubs and trees. The combination of structural control and biological re-establishment is fundamental to the permaculture approach.

Mollison emphasises that gully control weirs need splash aprons and
must be keyed into gully sides. Low fences of 0.5 to 1.0 m are more
likely to survive floods than taller structures. Where interceptor
banks can be made above the gully-head, and water diverted, the lower
volume of flow permits weirs of 1.0 to 1.5 m high without scour damage.

## Broader Applications

Beyond gully control, gabions are used for:

- [[mollison-designers-check-dam-types-and-landscape-placement]] and
  spillway protection
- Retaining walls for terraces on slopes
- Road foundations across unstable ground
- Stream bank stabilisation
- [[mollison-flood-retardation-by-trees-and-earthworks]] structures

The essential principle is that water energy must be dissipated
gradually, never allowed to concentrate, and that every structure must
be designed to trap and spread sediment while allowing vegetation to
re-establish. See also [[mollison-sediment-management-and-check-dam-systems]] for related techniques.
