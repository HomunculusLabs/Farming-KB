## Game Species Management and Capture Techniques

Managed [[wildlife-farming-and-game-management]] represents a productive alternative to conventional livestock, potentially integrating 40–80 species of birds, mammals, fish, and lower vertebrates in polyculture systems that exceed single-species yields while actually improving tree and prairie cover.

## The Case for Wildlife Farming

By the mid-1960s, wildlife researchers had developed good yield estimates under managed systems and could have devised excellent game farms. Pioneer farms of crocodile, emu, kangaroo, deer, and waterfowl already demonstrate [[holmgren-economic-and-social-succession]] benefits. These systems typically require less capital than conventional livestock operations and achieve better feed conversion.

The obstacle has been cultural rather than practical. Imported European cultures prevented the use of kangaroo and various antelope as farmed species, despite their status as preferred local foods. Redirecting capital from the production of fatty, chemicalised meats toward intelligent management of healthier rangelands and products would represent a far better use of resources.

## Capture Methods

The first priority in game management is developing harmless live-capture methods for each target species:

### Drift (Non-Return) Traps
Large funnel-shaped traps for migratory, moulting, or surface-driven species including jackrabbits, geese, deer, gazelle, and wildebeest (gnu). Animals are driven into the trap by people, dogs, or aircraft and cannot find their way back out.

### Yard Traps at Waterholes
Effective for species that regularly visit water: goats, horses, kangaroo. The trap encloses a water point and is closed when target animals enter.

### Baited Box Traps
Used for most rodent species. Bait attracts animals into a box that triggers a closing mechanism.

### Cage Traps
Employed for many bird species. Baited cages with trigger mechanisms capture birds unharmed.

### Set Nets
Nets placed in flyways or operated at night for birds and some mammals. Mist nets along known flight paths are highly effective for bird capture.

### Chemical Immobilisation
Fast-acting knockout drugs or chemical pellets for large or dangerous species: bear, rhino, elephant. This requires veterinary training and permits.

### Shooting
Large species are culled by shooting where essential, as sexes and ages are easily distinguished in the field.

Many capture methods operate at night when lights, traps, and nets are effective and [[heat-stress-cannabis]] on captured animals is reduced.

## Population Monitoring

Given a reliable capture and marking method, wildlife managers develop population estimates tied to multiple data sources:

- **Physical marks**: Dyes, collars, ear tags, leg bands, tattoos, ear notches
- **Indirect indicators**: Scat counts, browse effect on range vegetation, herd size observations, breeding success rates
- **Long-term data**: Natural mortality records from drought, predation, plant response, parasitism, and territorial behaviour enable prediction of natural losses

Pre-emptive culling can then target age/sex classes of animals that would otherwise perish in the field, converting inevitable losses into productive harvest.

## Habitat Enhancement for Wildlife

Free-range species are attracted to areas providing:

- **Special forage or crops**: Planted strips of preferred food species
- **Shade**: Trees and artificial shelters
- **Winter shelter**: Windbreaks, rock piles, brush piles, and loose [[compost]] mounds
- **Water points**: Constructed ponds, troughs, and wetlands

Seed and forage plants of particular value during periods of browse shortage can be scattered across depleted range to boost wildlife populations. These enhancements transform [[query-how-do-i-establish-a-food-forest-on-degraded-land]] into productive habitat while maintaining the [[fungal-melanin-biosynthesis-ecological-functions]] of the range.

## Deer Farming Lessons

Red deer and roe deer are now farmed in New Zealand, but Mollison criticises the approach: they are being raised on improved pasture with the conventional hay/concentrate system used for sheep. These animals are far better suited to managed montane tussock and tree browse systems, where they are most efficiently raised. The lesson is that game species should be farmed in environments matching their evolved [[ecological-niche]], not forced into conventional agricultural systems.

## The Broader Vision

True managed wildlife farming integrates up to 40–80 species in a system that:

1. **Exceeds monoculture yields** through complementary resource use
2. **Improves environmental condition** by increasing tree and prairie cover
3. **Reduces capital requirements** compared to conventional livestock
4. **Produces healthier products** from animals on natural diets
5. **Maintains ecological function** through species diversity

Only in recent years has intelligent management of game species in their preferred habitat resumed. The benefits are as yet scarcely realised, but in most cases exceed those of improved pasture with selected domestic breeds, at lower capital cost and with better feed conversion.
