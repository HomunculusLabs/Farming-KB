## C:N Effects on Oxygen Demand

High nitrogen availability relative to carbon leads to rapid
microbial growth and high oxygen demand. In compost tea brewing,
adding too much nitrogen-rich material without adequate carbon
can cause oxygen levels to plummet as bacteria bloom. This is one
of the reasons why the 0.5% to 3% molasses concentration range is
problematic: it provides enough carbon for rapid bacterial growth
but the nitrogen present allows the bloom to proceed unchecked,
driving oxygen levels down.

## Carbon Sources in Compost Tea

Carbon in compost tea comes from the compost itself and from added
food resources. Compost provides complex carbon compounds including
humic acids, cellulose, lignin, and other recalcitrant materials.
Added foods like molasses provide simple sugars. Fish hydrolysate
provides both carbon and nitrogen in a balanced form. The mix of
carbon sources determines which organisms are favored: simple
sugars favor bacteria while complex carbon sources favor fungi.

## Nitrogen Sources in Compost Tea

Nitrogen in compost tea comes primarily from the compost, with
supplemental nitrogen provided by fish hydrolysate or other
protein-rich additives. Fish hydrolysate is preferred over fish
emulsion because the hydrolysis process preserves more of the
amino acids and proteins that serve as food for diverse
microorganisms. The nitrogen source affects both the total biomass
produced and the composition of the microbial community.

## C:N in Compost Tea Brewing Water

The starting water used for compost tea has its own dissolved solids
that contribute to the overall C:N balance. Well water, municipal
water, and surface water all contain different levels of dissolved
minerals and organic compounds. These background levels affect how
much food can be added before the system becomes unbalanced. Water
with high mineral content leaves less room for added foods, while
very pure water may lack the micronutrients that organisms need.
Testing your water quality helps in formulating balanced recipes.

## Carbon Quality and Availability

Not all carbon is equally available to microorganisms. Simple sugars
like those in molasses are immediately available. Complex carbohydrates
like those in fish hydrolysate take longer to break down. Cellulose
and lignin from compost require specific enzymes produced by fungi.
Humic acids are recalcitrant and serve as long-term food sources.
The mix of carbon qualities in your tea determines the pattern of
microbial growth and the stability of the brew. More complex carbon
sources produce more stable teas with longer shelf life.

## Nitrogen Forms and Microbial Preference

Microorganisms in compost tea use nitrogen in different forms
depending on the species present. Bacteria prefer ammonium and
simple amino acids. Fungi can use more complex nitrogen compounds
including proteins. Protozoa obtain their nitrogen by consuming
bacteria. The form of nitrogen available in the tea influences which
organisms thrive. Fish hydrolysate provides nitrogen as amino acids
and short peptides, which are accessible to a wide range of
organisms. Synthetic nitrogen fertilizers are generally not
recommended because they provide only simple nitrogen forms.

## C:N Imbalance Symptoms

When C:N is out of balance in compost tea, several symptoms may
appear. Excess nitrogen relative to carbon causes rapid oxygen
depletion, foul odors, and loss of fungal and protozoan organisms.
Excess carbon relative to nitrogen results in slow organism growth,
low total biomass, and a tea that may not provide adequate
inoculation. Monitoring oxygen levels, organism counts, and tea
aroma provides early warning of C:N imbalance that can be corrected
by adjusting food resources in subsequent brews.
