## Macrofungal Species Richness Factors and Collection Methods

Understanding the factors that drive macrofungal species richness and employing rigorous collection methods are foundational to [[fungal-biodiversity]] studies. This concept page synthesizes key abiotic and biotic determinants of fungal diversity patterns alongside best practices for field collection, documentation, and preservation.

## Seasonality of Fruiting

Seasonal patterns in macrofungal fruiting are among the strongest drivers of observed species richness. The timing and intensity of fruiting seasons vary considerably depending on regional climate regimes.

### Temperate Regions with Summer Drought

In temperate regions characterized by summer drought (Mediterranean climates), **spring and fall** are the two principal fruiting seasons. Notably, the effective timing of "fall" fruiting shifts progressively later at lower latitudes within these regions, reflecting delayed onset of favorable moisture conditions. This latitudinal gradient means that scheduling standardized surveys requires careful attention to regional phenology rather than relying on fixed calendar dates. In Mediterranean-type ecosystems, the spring flush is often briefer and dominated by different taxa than the more diverse autumn assemblage.

### Temperate Regions with Summer Rain and High Elevations/Latitudes

Where summer rainfall predominates—whether in continental temperate zones or at higher elevations and latitudes—**summer becomes the most important fruiting period**. Surveys conducted only in spring or fall in such regions would systematically underestimate diversity. Alpine and boreal environments with short growing seasons concentrate fruiting into an even narrower summer window, making the timing of field visits especially critical.

### Weather Extremes and Anomalous Fruiting

Unusual weather events can trigger atypical fruiting patterns. For example, summer showers in regions with normally dry summers can stimulate out-of-season fruiting in species adapted to respond rapidly to moisture pulses. However, excess moisture can also **prevent fruiting** in some species, suggesting that optimal rather than maximal precipitation drives sporocarp production.

### Temperature and Precipitation Interactions

Temperature effects on fruiting are complex and operate on extended timescales. Ohenoja (1993) documented **year-round temperature effects** on fall fruiting patterns: warm summers increased mycorrhizal sporocarp production in spruce forests but not in pine forests, demonstrating that temperature interacts with both habitat type and ecological guild. At local scales, Vogt et al. (1999) found that **precipitation is a better predictor of species richness than vegetation type** for [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]]. This underscores the primacy of moisture availability in structuring fungal fruiting communities. The substantial variation observed among sites within a single forest zone means that sampling must include **multiple sites with similar host trees** to capture representative diversity.

## Vegetation as a Determinant of Fungal Diversity

### Habitat–Fungal Species Associations

Major vegetation types—grasslands, deserts, forests, and tundra—each harbor **characteristic macrofungal species assemblages**. Plant species composition directly influences fungal species numbers because plants serve as both habitat and energy source for fungi. All macrofungi display at least some degree of host or substratum specificity, ranging from obligate specialists to facultative generalists. The diversity of plant hosts available at a site is therefore a major determinant of the fungal species pool that can potentially occur there.

### Vegetation Zones as Sampling Frameworks

Vegetation zones and plant associations provide a practical framework for dividing landscapes into meaningful sampling units. Multiple studies have demonstrated strong correlations between ectomycorrhizal fungal distribution and forest type: Villeneuve et al. (1989), Bills et al. (1986), and Nantel and Neuman (1992) all found that **deciduous versus coniferous forest types** supported distinct ectomycorrhizal communities. These differences likely reflect both host-tree specificity and the distinct litter and soil conditions produced by different tree genera. Survey designs should therefore stratify by vegetation type to ensure comprehensive coverage.

## Geography and Large-Scale Diversity Patterns

### Habitat Diversity Over Latitude

Lodge et al. (1995) concluded that **habitat diversity, not geography per se, is the strongest influence** on fungal species richness at macroecological scales. This finding redirects attention from simple latitudinal gradients toward the complexity and heterogeneity of the environments fungi inhabit.

### European Diversity Patterns

Europe, despite having the most intensive and long-standing fungal collection history, **ranks lowest in fungal diversity** among major regions. This paradox is attributable to Pleistocene glaciation events, which dramatically reduced tree diversity and thereby limited the pool of available fungal hosts and substrates.

### Latitudinal Trends by Taxonomic Group

Different fungal lineages show contrasting latitudinal diversity patterns:
- **Cortinariaceae and discomycetes** reach peak diversity at middle to high latitudes
- **Saprobic Tricholomataceae ([[agaricales]])** are most diverse at low latitudes

These opposing trends caution against generalizing about fungal latitudinal gradients without considering taxonomic identity and ecological strategy.

## Succession in Fungal Communities

### Substrate Succession

Sporocarp succession occurs on particular substrata in predictable sequences. A classic example is the sequential fruiting of **herbivore dung fungi**, where different species appear consecutively as the dung ages and its chemical composition changes. Community composition shifts in response to substrate quality changes over time.

Hedger (1985) documented a striking example of **obligate succession**: *[[lepiota]]* species grow only on leaf litter that has already been partially decomposed by *Marasmius*, making the initial decomposer a prerequisite for the later one.

### Wood Decomposition Chronosequences

Fallen tree trunks support distinct assemblages of early and late decomposers. Lange (1992), Renvall (1995), and Heilmann-Clausen (2001) each demonstrated temporal partitioning among wood-inhabiting fungi, with early colonizers specializing in nutrient-rich sapwood and later arrivals exploiting progressively decayed wood. Heilmann-Clausen employed an innovative **chronosequence approach**, using a 30-year aerial photographic record of a Danish beech forest to divide 130 fallen logs into 7 age cohorts, enabling reconstruction of successional dynamics without long-term monitoring. This approach revealed that species turnover on decaying logs is both predictable and extensive, with few species persisting across all decay stages.

### Vegetation Succession and Disturbance

Broader vegetation succession at a site introduces new host taxa and alters the quality and quantity of organic matter, driving changes in fungal communities. Disturbance can also reshape fungal communities: Lodge and Cantrell (1995b) documented how canopy opening following Hurricane Hugo **extirpated mycelia of *Collybia johnstonii*** in Puerto Rico, illustrating the vulnerability of some species to microclimate disruption.

### Ectomycorrhizal Succession

Ectomycorrhizal fungal communities change across stand-age gradients. Some species are characteristic of young stands, others of mature or old-growth forests, and some occur across age classes (Mason et al. 1983, Dighton et al. 1986, Luoma et al. 1991). Effective sampling therefore requires **diverse microhabitats and a range of stand ages** among sites to capture the full spectrum of ectomycorrhizal diversity.

## Collection, Culture, and Preservation Methods

### Core Activities of Diversity Studies

The core of any fungal diversity study comprises a suite of interrelated activities: **collection, labeling, photography, culture preparation, spore printing, morphological description, and preservation**. Each step must be executed carefully to maximize the scientific value of specimens.

### Field Time Management

Balancing field collection time with laboratory processing time is critical. **Prioritize fragile or taxonomically important specimens first**, as they are most vulnerable to degradation. Not every specimen encountered needs to be collected—selection should be guided by specimen condition and the specific goals of the study.

### Developmental Stages and Search Radius

For many taxa, **all developmental stages** (buttons, expanding fruiting bodies, mature specimens) are needed to fully characterize morphological variation. When a target species is found, search should extend several meters around the initial collection point to locate additional individuals at different stages of development. Conversely, sporocarps that are too immature or too old to provide useful diagnostic characters should be left behind.

### Excavation and Substratum Collection

Complete excavation around the base of sporocarps is essential to reveal features that are critical for identification: **volva, rooting base, basal bulb, or sclerotium attachment**. The substratum (wood, litter, soil) should be collected along with the specimen, and any unidentified plant material in the vicinity should be included to aid in host identification.

### Recording Color Changes and Fragile Features

Color changes resulting from bruising or handling should be noted immediately. **Hygrophanous species** lose moisture rapidly and can change color dramatically within minutes of collection, so initial color observations must be recorded in the field at the time of collection. Equally important is the prompt recording of **fragile features**—such as veil material in *Lepiota* and *Psathyrella*—before they are damaged during handling or transport. Photographing specimens in situ, before disturbance, provides an invaluable record of these ephemeral characters and the collection context.

## Sampling Design Implications

The factors discussed above converge on several key principles for sampling design:
1. **Temporal replication** across seasons and years is essential to capture the full fruiting spectrum
2. **Multiple sites** with similar host trees must be sampled to account for within-zone variation
3. **Vegetation stratification** ensures representation of habitat-specific fungal assemblages
4. **Stand-age diversity** across sampling sites captures ectomycorrhizal and saprobic successional variation
5. **Microhabitat heterogeneity** within sites (dead wood of different decay stages, dung, litter layers, soil) should be deliberately sampled rather than left to chance
