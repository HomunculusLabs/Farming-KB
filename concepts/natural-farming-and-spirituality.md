# Natural Farming and Spirituality

Masanobu Fukuoka's [[natural-farming-philosophy]] is fundamentally a spiritual practice. While it produces practical agricultural results — high yields without chemicals — its deepest purpose is what Fukuoka called "the cultivation and perfection of human beings." Farming is the vehicle; awakening is the destination.

## The Original Experience

Fukuoka's entire body of work flows from a single spiritual experience on the morning of May 15, 1938. At age 25, working as a plant pathologist at the Yokohama Customs Bureau, he collapsed in exhaustion on a hillside overlooking the harbor. Between sleep and waking, he watched the sunrise through morning mist. A night heron cried and flew away. In that instant:

"All my doubts and the gloomy mist of my confusion vanished. Everything I had held in firm conviction, everything upon which I had ordinarily relied, was swept away with the wind."

The realization: "In this world there is nothing at all." Not nihilism, but the recognition that all concepts — including the concept of existence itself — are "empty fabrications." "Something one might call 'true nature' stood revealed."

He resigned the next day and spent the rest of his life demonstrating this insight through farming.

## Buddhist and Taoist Foundations

Though Fukuoka claimed no formal religious affiliation, his thought is deeply rooted in Mahayana Buddhism and Taoism:

### The Heart Sutra
He frequently quoted: "Form is emptiness, emptiness is form. Matter and the spirit are one, but all is void. Man is not alive, is not dead, is unborn and undying, without old age and disease, without increase and without decrease." He saw this not as abstract philosophy but as a description of reality directly observable in the rice field: "When rice is planted in the spring, the seed sends out living shoots, and now, as we are reaping, it appears to die. The fact that this ritual is repeated year after year means that life continues in this field and the yearly death is itself yearly birth."

### Life and Death as One
"People think that life is joy and death is sadness, but the rice seed, lying within the earth and sending out shoots in spring, its leaves and stems withering in the fall, still holds within its tiny core the full joy of life. The joy of life does not depart in death. Death is no more than a momentary passing." The same cycle occurs within the human body: "Day by day hair and nails grow, tens of thousands of cells die, tens of thousands more are born." To participate in this cycle daily is to experience something greater.

### Lao Tzu and Non-Action (Wu Wei)
"Lao Tzu spoke of non-active nature, and I think that if he were a farmer he would certainly practice natural farming." The Taoist concept of wu wei — action that arises spontaneously without forced effort — is the philosophical basis of do-nothing farming. "Lao Tzu, the Taoist sage, says that a whole and decent life can be lived in a small village. Bodhidharma, the founder of Zen, spent nine years living in a cave without bustling about."

### Gandhi
Fukuoka considered Gandhi's way — "a method-less method, acting with a non-winning, non-opposing state of mind" — akin to natural farming. "When it is understood that one loses joy and happiness in the attempt to possess them, the essence of natural farming will be realized."

## The Three Schools of Swordsmanship

Fukuoka used a martial arts metaphor to distinguish levels of farming:

- **Modern industrial farming** follows the "two-stroke school" — "believes that victory can be won by delivering the greatest barrage of sword strokes"
- **Organic farming** follows the "one-stroke school" — "seeks victory through the skilful, yet self-conscious application of technique"
- **Pure natural farming** is the "no-stroke school" — "It goes nowhere and seeks no victory. Putting 'doing nothing' into practice is the one thing the farmer should strive to accomplish"

The distinction between conscious technique and spontaneous harmony is the key: "The ultimate goal of farming is not the growing of crops, but the cultivation and perfection of human beings."

## The Inscription on the Hut

In the mountain huts where young people came to learn farming, Fukuoka left a pinewood plaque above the fireplace: "Right Food, Right Action, Right Awareness" — modeled on the Buddhist Eightfold Path. "The three cannot be separated from one another. If one is missing, none can be realized. If one is realized, all are realized."

## Farming as Sacred Work

### Serve Nature and All Is Well
"So for the farmer in his work: serve nature and all is well." Fukuoka saw farming as originally sacred work. When humanity fell away from this ideal, modern commercial agriculture rose. "When the farmer began to grow crops to make money, he forgot the real principles of agriculture."

### The Farmer's Original Freedom
"The one-acre farmer of long ago spent January, February, and March hunting rabbits in the hills." The New Year's holiday lasted three months, then shrank to two, one, and finally three days. "The dwindling of the New Year's holiday indicates how busy the farmer has become and how he has lost his easy-going physical and spiritual well-being."

### Work Is Not Work
"I do not particularly like the word 'work.' Human beings are the only animals who have to work, and I think this is the most ridiculous thing in the world. Other animals make their livings by living, but people work like crazy, thinking that they have to in order to stay alive." For human beings, "a life of such simplicity would be possible if one worked to produce directly his daily necessities. In such a life, work is not work as people generally think of it, but simply doing what needs to be done."

### Living in the Present
"Just to live here and now — this is the true basis of human life." "Whether autumn will bring wind or rain, I cannot know, but today I will be working in the fields." To break experience in half and call one side physical and the other spiritual "is narrowing and confusing."

## The Commune Movement

Seven or eight young people lived communally in huts on the mountain, learning farming skills to establish new villages. Many traveled to India, France's Gandhi Village, kibbutzim in Israel, or communes in the American West. "A community that cannot manage to produce its own food will not last long." Fukuoka saw this movement as "leading the way to a better time" where natural farming was "rapidly taking hold and gaining momentum."

## Why Religious People Are Drawn to Natural Farming

"Various religious groups have come to take up natural farming. In seeking the essential nature of man, no matter how you go about it, you must begin with the consideration of health." Fukuoka welcomed this but maintained no monopoly: "I do not belong to any religious group myself and will freely discuss my views with anyone at all. I do not care much for making distinctions among Christianity, Buddhism, Shinto, and the other religions."

## The Village Without War

In Fukuoka's spiritual vision, peace begins with farming. "The farmers of long ago were a peaceful people." War arises from the "empty distinctions of self/other, strong/weak, attack/defence" — the same discriminating mind that creates the illusion of understanding nature. "There is no other road to peace than for all people to depart from the castle gate of relative perception, go down into the meadow, and return to the heart of non-active nature. That is, sharpening the sickle instead of the sword." See [[fukuoka-view-of-nature-and-human-hubris]].
