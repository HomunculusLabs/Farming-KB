# Soil Foodweb Biology Overview

## Overview

## The Foodweb Concept
The soil foodweb is organized in a trophic hierarchy, similar to above-ground food
webs but operating at microscopic to macroscopic scales. The primary producers are
photosynthetic plants and algae, which fix carbon from the atmosphere. This carbon
flows into the soil through root exudates, litter fall, and dead plant material,
fueling the entire below-ground ecosystem. The organisms that process this organic
matter can be grouped into functional categories based on their feeding strategies
and ecological roles.

## Key Organism Groups

### Bacteria
Bacteria are the most numerous organisms in most soils, typically numbering in the
billions per gram. They are the primary decomposers of simple [[endophytic-fungi]] that colonize plant tissues. Pathogenic fungi cause plant diseases
but are normally held in check by competition and predation from other foodweb
organisms when the foodweb is balanced. Calcium availability is particularly
important for fungal growth — soils deficient in calcium tend to have suppressed

### Microarthropods and Earthworms
Microarthropods (springtails, mites) and earthworms are the larger organisms in the
soil foodweb, functioning as shredders and predators. They break down coarse organic
matter, creating surface area for [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] colonization, and their
burrowing activity creates channels that improve soil aeration and water infiltration.
Earthworms are particularly important in no-till systems, where their populations
can become very large and contribute significantly to soil structure and fertility.

## Functions of a Balanced Foodweb

### Disease Suppression
A healthy soil foodweb suppresses plant diseases primarily through competition and
predation rather than through antibiotic or toxic chemical production. When all
infection sites on plant surfaces are occupied by beneficial organisms, and when food
resources are consumed by beneficial organisms faster than pathogens can utilize them,
disease-causing organisms cannot establish. This is fundamentally different from a
pesticide approach — no toxic chemicals are involved, only ecological competition.
