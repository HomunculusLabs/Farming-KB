# [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] Biogeochemistry

## Overview

Nearly all land plants depend on symbiotic mycorrhizal [[mollison-designers-mycorrhizal-fungi-and-soil-microbiology]] fungi. These
associations are among the most important biological drivers of
biogeochemical cycling in terrestrial ecosystems, influencing carbon,
nitrogen, phosphorus, and metal dynamics. Mycorrhizal fungi not only
cycle major elements but can determine plant community composition.

## Types of Mycorrhiza

### Endomycorrhizas
The fungus colonizes the interior of host plant root cells. Major types:

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizas (AM)**: Most widespread; associated with the
  majority of plant species globally. Fungal hyphae form arbuscules
  (tree-like structures) within root cortical cells for nutrient exchange
- **Ericoid mycorrhizas**: Associated with plants in the Ericaceae family;
  important in acidic, nutrient-poor soils

### Ectomycorrhizas
The fungus is located outside the root cells of the host plant, forming:
- A **hyphal mantle** surrounding the root
- A **Hartig net** between root cortical cells (the primary nutrient
  exchange interface)
- **Extrametrical mycelium** extending into the surrounding soil for
  nutrient scavenging

## Mineral Weathering Mechanisms

### Organic Acid Exudation
Mycorrhizal fungi excrete low-molecular-mass carboxylic acids during
growth, including malic, succinic, gluconic, oxalic, citric, and formic
acid. This process, termed "heterotrophic leaching," dissolves soil
minerals through protonation and metal chelation.

In podzol E horizons under European coniferous forests, weathering of
hornblendes, feldspars, and granitic bedrock has been attributed to
organic acid excretion by ectomycorrhizal hyphae. Hyphal tips produce
micro- to millimolar concentrations of these acids at micropore
interfaces (3–10 μm) in weatherable soil minerals.

### Proton and Ligand Promoted Mobilization
Mycorrhizal fungi engage in proton-promoted and ligand-promoted metal
mobilization from mineral sources. This leads to:
- Enhanced weathering of soil minerals
- Metal cation release (Ca, K, Mg, Fe, Zn)
- Changes in the physicochemical characteristics of the root
  environment

### Mineral-Specific Responses
Ectomycorrhizal mycelia respond to different soil minerals by regulating
growth and activity. Carbon allocation within mycelium is significantly
greater when colonizing potassium feldspar patches compared to quartz
patches, indicating active resource targeting.

## Phosphorus Mobilization

Phosphorus mobilization is one of the most important functions of
mycorrhizal fungi. Key findings:

- **Zinc phosphate dissolution**: The ectomycorrhizal association of
  *Paxillus involutus* with Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*) dissolves zinc
  phosphate, acquiring phosphorus while accumulating zinc
- **Phosphorus status dependence**: Under phosphorus deficiency, zinc-
  tolerant ectomycorrhiza mobilize the highest zinc amounts; under
  phosphorus-replete conditions, they employ a metal avoidance strategy
- **Apatite dissolution**: *Suillus granulatus* and *P. involutus*
  release elements from apatite and wood ash, accumulating them in
  mycelia, especially rhizomorphs
- **Biotite weathering**: *Piloderma* extracts K and/or Mg from biotite,
  microcline, and chlorite, precipitating mycogenic calcium oxalate

## Metal Detoxification and Tolerance

Mycorrhizal fungi employ multiple strategies for metal tolerance:

- **Biosorption**: Metal immobilization on fungal cell walls and
  extracellular polymers
- **Accumulation**: Sequestration of metals within fungal biomass
- **Extracellular precipitation**: Formation of mycogenic metal oxalates
  (e.g., calcium oxalate crystals on hyphae)
- **Filtration**: Ectomycorrhizal hyphal sheaths filter toxic metals
  before they reach plant roots
- **Complexation**: Released organic acids complex and buffer metal
  toxicity

The outcome depends on the mycorrhizal status of the plant, the zinc
tolerance of the fungal strain, and the phosphorus status of the
environment.

## Environmental Applications

### Phytoremediation
Mycorrhizal associations have potential for:
- Enhancing plant growth on contaminated soils
- Remediation and removal of pollutants (phytoextraction)
- Re-vegetation and re-forestation of degraded land
- Rhizo-remediation strategies

### Soil Remediation Considerations
Fungal mineral-solubilizing activity may affect chemical remediation
technologies. Phosphate-induced metal stabilization (proposed for
cost-effective remediation) may be undermined by fungi that transform
the final mineral products. Long-term environmental assessments should
account for soil fungal communities.

### Limitations
Mycorrhizal interactions are variable and context-dependent, with
outcomes varying in response to biotic and abiotic factors. Most
efficient amelioration occurs when the mycobiont is metal-resistant.
