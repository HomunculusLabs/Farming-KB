## Condensation Strategies for Drylands

Where no piped water is available and water is in seriously short supply, trees and gardens require condensation strategies. The aim is to condense water from night air, from transpired moisture, or from weeds and trimmings, and return it to root level for reuse. These strategies bridge the gap between zero irrigation and full drip systems, often making the difference between plant survival and death in extreme environments.

## Shields of Plastic, Screen, or Metal

Small trees planted with a 3 or 4-stake frame over which a bottomless and topless bag is pulled, surrounded by loose weeds, live in a protected micro-environment. At night, soil moisture condenses on the inner bag surface and runs down to the roots. Alternatively, coastal ridge areas can condense water from night air using fine metal screens or fences placed crosswind.

Shields provide multiple simultaneous benefits:

- **Wind reduction**: Cutting wind speed dramatically reduces evaporation
- **Mulch retention**: Holding mulch in place around the plant base
- **Animal protection**: Shielding from small browsing animals
- **Water condensation**: Capturing night moisture and directing it to roots

Personal experience suggests an 80–90% difference in survival using shields compared to unprotected plants. Growth (and light availability) may double in tall clear plastic bag tubes around a small tree. This makes shields one of the most cost-effective survival strategies for dryland plant establishment.

## Organic Mulches

For very small areas or restricted tree sites, a variety of organic mulches provide enormous benefit. In extreme conditions, these need to be 45 cm or deeper to be effective, but in a drought summer they may mean the difference between survival and death of plants.

### Functions of Organic Mulch

- **Weed suppression**: Smothering competing vegetation
- **Condensation**: Releasing captured moisture back to soil
- **Wind protection**: Shielding soil surface from desiccating winds
- **[[drip-irrigation]] delivered through subsurface pipes under mulch
- Closed recycling for high-value crops
- Pit evaporators providing supplementary water for isolated trees

The principle is to create a layered defense against moisture loss, with each strategy addressing a different pathway of water escape — evaporation from soil, transpiration loss, wind desiccation, and lateral soil moisture movement. By stacking these strategies, even severely water-limited environments can support productive plant growth.

## Practical Considerations

- Shields require stakes and bag material — both low-cost and often scavengable
- Stone mulch is permanent but labor-intensive to establish
- Plastic sheets degrade in sunlight and need periodic replacement
- Organic mulches decompose and need replenishment
- Pit systems require regular input of fresh green material
- Closed systems need ventilation management to prevent disease

The selection of strategies depends on available materials, labor supply, crop value, and the severity of water limitation. In most dryland situations, combining stone mulch with organic matter and some form of shield provides the most robust and lowest-maintenance approach.
