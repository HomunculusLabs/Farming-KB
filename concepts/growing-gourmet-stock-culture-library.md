Storing cultures on agar slants at 35 degrees F (1-2 degrees C) is
recommended. The slant should be sealed with Parafilm or similar elastic
wax film to prevent desiccation and contamination. Under proper
conditions, cultures on slants can remain viable for several months to
over a year, depending on the species.

## Building Duplicates and Remote Storage

Every culture in the library should have duplicates. If the primary
culture becomes contaminated or lost, a backup is immediately available.
Stamets recommends keeping duplicates in remote locations -- a separate
refrigerator, or even a separate building -- so that a single
catastrophic event (power failure, contamination outbreak, fire) does
not destroy the entire genetic library.

## Maintaining Culture Health

To maintain the health and genetic integrity of stock cultures, the
cultivator should:

  1. Periodically transfer cultures to fresh media (typically every
     4-6 months for most species)
  2. Inspect cultures before each transfer for signs of contamination
     or senescence
  3. Keep careful records of P values, transfer dates, and any observed
     changes in growth characteristics
  4. Return to P1 stock when the P value of working cultures becomes
     too high (typically P5 or higher)
  5. Store at optimal temperatures and humidity

## Six Mycelial Growth Types

When examining cultures, cultivators should be familiar with the six
primary types of mycelial growth morphology. Recognizing these patterns
helps identify the species, assess culture health, and detect
contamination:

  1. Cottony: Fluffy, aerial mycelium that is diffuse and
     undifferentiated. Common with many Oyster species and some
     contaminant molds. Not ideal for spawn production as it indicates
     a less aggressive strain.

  2. Rhizomorphic: Strand-like, ropy growth with distinct linear
     strands. This is the most desirable growth type for cultivation as
     it indicates vigorous, fast-growing mycelium. Rhizomorphic growth
     is characteristic of healthy [[growing-gourmet-grain-spawn-generation]], growing gourmet sawdust spawn creation, and ultimately all mushroom crops trace their lineage back to the mother culture. If the mother culture is contaminated, senescent, or genetically degraded, every downstream product will suffer.

For this reason, mother cultures should be treated with the utmost care.
They should be stored separately from working cultures, handled with
strict sterile technique, and regularly monitored for signs of decline.
