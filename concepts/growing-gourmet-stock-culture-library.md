# Stock Culture Library and Strain Banking

A Stock Culture Library is a collection of mushroom strains preserved in
a format that maximizes longevity. These cultures serve as the genetic
bank from which all cultivation activities originate. Without a well-
maintained culture library, cultivators must repeatedly source strains
from the wild or from commercial suppliers, losing control over their
genetic resources.

## The P Value System: Tracking Generational Age

In the mushroom industry, a "P value" system is used to designate the
generational age of the mycelium. The first culture taken from a wild
mushroom or purchased from a culture bank is P1. Subsequent transfers
from this mother culture create P2, P3, P4, and so on. The higher the P
value, the greater the number of subcultures, and the more likely the
strain has undergone genetic changes.

Genetic stability is of paramount concern. As strains are serially
subcultured, they may lose vigor, change growth characteristics, or
accumulate mutations. The cultivator should track the P value of every
culture and maintain P1 master stocks as a hedge against senescence.

## Culture Slants: The Primary Storage Method

The most common method for long-term storage of mushroom cultures is the
agar slant. Slants are test tubes filled with a [[mushroom-agar-media-recipes]] that has been allowed to solidify at an angle, creating an inclined surface. Mushroom mycelium is grown on this surface and then stored under refrigeration.

Storing cultures on agar slants at 35 degrees F (1-2 degrees C) is
recommended. The slant should be sealed with Parafilm or similar elastic
wax film to prevent desiccation and contamination. Under proper
conditions, cultures on slants can remain viable for several months to
over a year, depending on the species.

## Building Duplicates and Remote Storage

Every culture in the library should have duplicates. If the primary
culture becomes contaminated or lost, a backup is immediately available.
Stamets recommends keeping duplicates in remote locations -- a separate
refrigerator, or even a separate building -- so that a single
catastrophic event (power failure, contamination outbreak, fire) does
not destroy the entire genetic library.

## Maintaining Culture Health

To maintain the health and genetic integrity of stock cultures, the
cultivator should:

  1. Periodically transfer cultures to fresh media (typically every
     4-6 months for most species)
  2. Inspect cultures before each transfer for signs of contamination
     or senescence
  3. Keep careful records of P values, transfer dates, and any observed
     changes in growth characteristics
  4. Return to P1 stock when the P value of working cultures becomes
     too high (typically P5 or higher)
  5. Store at optimal temperatures and humidity

## Six Mycelial Growth Types

When examining cultures, cultivators should be familiar with the six
primary types of mycelial growth morphology. Recognizing these patterns
helps identify the species, assess culture health, and detect
contamination:

  1. Cottony: Fluffy, aerial mycelium that is diffuse and
     undifferentiated. Common with many Oyster species and some
     contaminant molds. Not ideal for spawn production as it indicates
     a less aggressive strain.

  2. Rhizomorphic: Strand-like, ropy growth with distinct linear
     strands. This is the most desirable growth type for cultivation as
     it indicates vigorous, fast-growing mycelium. Rhizomorphic growth
     is characteristic of healthy [[growing-gourmet-strain-evaluation-28-features]] of Shiitake, many Oysters, and other aggressive decomposers.

  3. Linear: Growth that follows straight lines along the agar surface
     without much aerial branching. Often seen in slower-growing species.

  4. Aerial: Mycelium that lifts off the agar surface into the air
     space of the petri dish or slant. Can be a sign of excessive
     moisture or certain species characteristics.

  5. Matted: A dense, flat mat of mycelium pressed close to the agar
     surface. Can be difficult to transfer but is characteristic of some
     species.

  6. Powdery: A fine, powdery growth that may indicate a contaminant or
     a species with a distinctive growth pattern. Often seen with yeast
     contaminants.

## The Importance of the Mother Culture

The mother culture is the foundation of the entire cultivation operation.
All [[growing-gourmet-grain-spawn-generation]], growing gourmet sawdust spawn creation, and ultimately all mushroom crops trace their lineage back to the mother culture. If the mother culture is contaminated, senescent, or genetically degraded, every downstream product will suffer.

For this reason, mother cultures should be treated with the utmost care.
They should be stored separately from working cultures, handled with
strict sterile technique, and regularly monitored for signs of decline.

## Acclimating Mycelium to the End Substrate

A technique recommended by Stamets is to acquaint the mushroom mycelium
with the host substrate by introducing a small sample (1-2 grams) of the
end substrate (typically sawdust) directly into the liquid fermentation
vessels when creating [[growing-gourmet-grain-spawn-generation]]. Upon exposure, the mushroom mycelium generates enzymes and
acids to break down the proposed food source. Once acclimated, the
mycelium carries a genetic memory of the end substrate to which it is
destined.

With Shiitake, Enokitake, Maitake, and Reishi, Stamets is convinced
that this method empowers the mushroom mycelium. The lag period when
mushroom mycelium encounters unfamiliar components is significantly
reduced, and colonization prior to fruiting is greatly accelerated.
