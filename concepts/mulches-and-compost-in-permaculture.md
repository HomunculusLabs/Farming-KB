## Mulches and Compost in Permaculture

## Overview

Mulching and composting are the two primary organic methods for building and maintaining healthy soils in permaculture systems. Together, they supply nutrients, buffer temperature extremes, prevent erosion, promote [[crumb-structure]] develop, **only long periods of natural organic mulches stabilize nutrient supply and complex [[soil-life]]**. Results cannot be judged over one or two seasons — it can take **3–5 years** to create balanced soil under mulch from compacted or mined-out land. Even longer periods are needed for permanent crops where mulch must be produced on-site.

## Functions of Mulch

### Nutrient Addition
Mulch is an excellent nutrient delivery system. The "cool" decay pathway loses little nitrogen while stimulating soil life generally. As mulch breaks down, its mineral content becomes available to plants through the activity of soil organisms.

### Temperature Buffering
Under mulch, excessive [[soil-temperature]] ranges are moderated: soils stay **cooler by day** and **warmer at night or in winter**. This buffering effect improves conditions for seed germination and root growth across seasons.

### Water Conservation
Water absorption is significantly improved under mulch. Jeanette Conacher, reporting on extension trials in Nigeria (*Organic Gardening*, 1979), recorded **11% better water infiltration** on low- to no-tillage and mulched plots. Water needs are consistently reduced.

### Nutrient Retention
Over the long term, major nutrients (N, P, K) remain at satisfactory levels under mulch. Without mulch, bare soils lose nutrients through leaching, oxidation, and erosion.

### Soil Life Promotion
Only under mulch does the population of important soil organisms — particularly **earthworms** — increase significantly. Earthworms are keystone organisms for soil health, aerating soil, processing organic matter, and creating channels for water infiltration.

## Selecting Mulch Materials

Mulches should be selected for:
- **Minimal weed seed** content
- **Minimal residual biocides** (avoid pesticide-treated materials)
- **Best effect on specific crops** (test via row-by-row comparisons)

Leaf material cut and mulched green contains more nutrients than fallen leaves, though fallen leaves remain valuable for humus production.

### Plastic Mulches
Black plastic mulch provides heat and weed control; silver plastic repels aphids. However, plastic mulches have a limited role in permaculture:
- Often expensive or impractical in resource-poor settings
- Growers suspect many plastics release persistent chemical polymers with unknown effects on soil life
- They do not build soil structure or feed soil organisms

## Composting: Hot vs Cold Methods

### Hot Composting (Aerobic)
Hot compost heaps reach **50–60°C** and effectively kill weed seeds and pathogens. The downside is that nitrogen losses through ammonia volatilization can be severe. Adding **1% superphosphate** to a hot heap helps prevent ammonia escape.

### Cold Composting (Anaerobic/Pit)
Cold heaps (pitted or silage-style) do not lose nitrogen but neither do they kill weed seeds. They produce useful humus with minimal nitrogen loss.

### The Chinese Combined Method
Chinese scientists achieve the best of both worlds by:
1. Building an aerated heap with bamboo poles as air tunnels
2. Covering with mud so the heap heats to 55–60°C for a few days
3. Sealing all holes so the remaining decay proceeds anaerobically

This method kills weed seeds during the hot phase and preserves nitrogen during the cold phase.

### The UK Liquid Manure Method
In the UK, liquid manures sprinkled on straw in silos or tanks, combined with forced air draught, produce compost in about **one week**. Efficient open piles encased in straw need about 10 days. This system is well-suited to treating manurial sludge from biogas digesters (which is still full of seeds).

### Compost Heat Recovery
In severe winter areas, large compost piles of **10–50 cubic metres** can provide heat over long periods, significantly reducing glasshouse and house heating costs. The compost is best applied to soil in spring after the heat has been extracted.

## Soil Crumb Structure and Compost

Compost and mulch are critical to preserving soil crumb structure. The gums and gels produced by soil organisms create crumb structure, aerate soil, and darken it so it heats faster in spring. Humic acids from compost assist root development dramatically, even at levels as low as **60 ppm carbon**.

In taste tests, compost-grown vegetables show improved sugar content and flavour compared to chemically fertilized equivalents. Inorganic (chemical) and mechanical farming can just as easily destroy soil structure.

## Compost Materials Classification

Materials can be categorized by their best use:

1. **Deep mulch (>12 cm)**: Nut husks, shells, shredded paper, bark, woodchips, sawdust, natural-fiber carpets and bags. Least effort for best results; controls weeds, lessens labour.

2. **Manures on gardens**: Hay with seed heads, weeds in flower, roots of weeds. Good results obtained but moderate effort.

3. **Concentrates**: Sewage, liquid manure, urine, animal trimmings. Spread on intensive plots and seed beds. Most effort and most pollution risk, but effective.

4. **Biogas sludge**: Digester sludge and weed-free manures. Drill below surface or use in aquatic systems.

5. **Direct application**: Bird manures, blood, bone, feathers, seaweeds. Spray as liquid on foliage.

6. **Liquid teas**: Dissolved minerals, urine, seaweed and manure teas. Foliar spray or direct soil application.

## Toxic Contamination Risks

Even "natural" organic materials can introduce problems:

### Container Risks
Galvanized, copper, or brass containers can leach excess **zinc and copper** into manures or manurial teas. Clay, stainless steel, glass, or iron containers are preferable.

### Manure Risks
Manures from pigs, poultry, cattle, or sewage sludges may contain excessive **copper, zinc, nickel, boron, lead, or cadmium**, especially problematic in acid sandy soils. While initially helpful where such elements are deficient, heavy or constant application builds up toxic conditions.

### Feed-Related Contamination
Copper, arsenic, and other minerals are commonly added to stock feeds. Buying clean natural feeds from organic sources is the safest course.

### Worm Cast Limits
Handreck (*Organic Growing*, Autumn '87) recommends a limit of **10% worm casts** in potting mixes. Earthworm casts from contaminated pig, sheep, or domestic waste sources can concentrate zinc and copper to phytotoxic levels.

### The Safest Approach
After initial soil treatment, the safest long-term strategy is to **grow your own green manures** — foliage from leguminous hedgerows, windbreaks, or intercrops — and use these as mulch and compost materials. This closes the nutrient loop on-site and eliminates external contamination risks.
