## Overview

Eliot Coleman's vertical trellis system is a space-efficient, high-yielding method for
growing tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, and eggplants in unheated greenhouses. The core
principle involves training plants onto vertical supports using string trellises and
pruning them to single or double stems, then extending the productive life of
indeterminate varieties through a "lower-and-lean" technique that keeps the top 8 feet
of each plant in continuous production from spring through late fall.

This system is central to Coleman's approach to summer crop production in unheated
hoophouses, as detailed in *The [[coleman-claytonia-winter-harvest-cultivation]] Handbook*. It maximizes harvest per
[[comparison-biointensive-vs-square-foot-gardening]] of greenhouse floor space and allows a single planting to produce over
many months without replanting.

## Tomatoes

### String Trellis Setup

Tomato plants are supported by plastic twine that unrolls from spools attached to
wire frames. These wire frames hang from horizontal support wires that run the entire
length of the greenhouse. The horizontal support wires are positioned at approximately
8 feet above ground level.

Commercial trellis clips are used to secure the tomato stem to the twine every 12
inches as the plant grows upward. As the plant extends, additional clips are added
and the twine unspools from above, keeping the stem upright and well-supported.

### Single-Stem Pruning

All tomato plants are pruned to a single stem by removing every sucker (lateral
shoot) that emerges from the leaf axils. This concentrates the plant's energy into
one vertical leader, simplifies trellis management, improves [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]], and
produces larger, more uniform fruit compared to multi-stem approaches.

### Fruit Cluster Management

Fruit load is carefully managed to balance yield with fruit size and plant vigor:

- **Beefsteak varieties**: Limited to four fruits per cluster
- **Medium varieties**: Limited to five fruits per cluster
- **Cherry tomatoes**: Clusters are pinched back after the first fruit in each
  cluster ripens, redirecting energy to developing fruit

### Lower-and-Lean Technique

The hallmark of Coleman's system is the lower-and-lean method, which extends the
harvest period of spring-set tomato plants through late fall:

1. As the plant top reaches the 8-foot horizontal support wire, the lowest fruit is
   harvested and all lower branches are removed from the bare section of stem.
2. The twine is unrolled a turn or two from the spool above, and the plant top is
   lowered approximately 12 inches.
3. The wire frame holding the twine spool is slid horizontally along the support
   wire, moving the plant to a new position.
4. This process is repeated regularly as the plant continues growing upward.

The result is that paired rows of tomatoes come to resemble circular dry-cleaning
trolleys. The lower portions of the stems lie bare along the ground while the top
8 feet of each plant remain vertical and continue producing fruit. This technique
allows a single spring planting to remain productive for six months or more without
the need for [[query-what-is-succession-planting-and-how-do-i-plan-a-succession-planting-schedule]].

## Cucumbers

### Training System

Cucumbers are trained vertically using a modified approach that takes advantage of
their vigorous climbing habit. Plants are pruned to a single stem and trained upward
to a support bar positioned 7 to 8 feet above ground level. Once the growing tip
reaches the top of the support bar, the vine is directed back downward.

### Pruning Protocol

Cucumber pruning follows a strict protocol to ensure strong early [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]]:

- **Below 3 feet**: All suckers and all female flowers are removed. This forces the
  plant to establish a robust root system and vegetative framework before redirecting
  energy to fruit production.
- **At the support bar**: One sucker is allowed to develop, creating a second stem.
  Both the original leader and the new sucker trail over the bar and grow downward.
- **Fruit set**: One cucumber is left at each node on both descending vines.

### Harvest and Replacement

Cucumbers are picked daily to maintain production and prevent oversized fruit from
slowing the vine. Once the descending vines reach ground level and the final harvest
is complete, the entire plant is pulled and replaced with a new planting. Unlike
tomatoes, cucumbers do not benefit from a lower-and-lean approach due to their
shorter productive lifespan.

## Peppers and Eggplant

### Two-Stem Training

Peppers and eggplants are trained to two stems rather than the single stem used for
tomatoes and cucumbers. This reflects their different growth habits and branching
patterns. The two strongest stems emerging from the plant are selected and retained;
all others are removed.

### Fruit Management

One fruit is allowed to develop at every node on both stems. This moderate fruit
load is appropriate for the naturally lower productivity of peppers and eggplants
compared to tomatoes, and prevents the plants from becoming top-heavy or breaking
under excessive fruit weight.

## System-Wide Principles

Several principles are consistent across all crops in Coleman's vertical trellis
system:

- **Vertical space utilization**: The full height of the greenhouse (typically 8
  feet of productive canopy) is used rather than allowing plants to sprawl
  horizontally.
- **Pruning discipline**: Regular, attentive pruning to defined stem counts is
  essential for airflow, light penetration, and manageable harvest labor.
- **Extended season production**: The lower-and-lean technique for tomatoes
  demonstrates that with proper management, unheated greenhouses can sustain
  continuous harvest from a single planting across most of the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].
- **Labor efficiency**: All trellising uses commercially available clips, twine,
  and wire hardware, keeping the system practical for both market farms and home
  production.
