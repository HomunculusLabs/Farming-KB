# Quick Hoops and Low Tunnel Overwintering

## Overview

Quick hoops are low-cost, temporary low tunnel structures designed to protect
fall-planted hardy crops through winter for extra-early spring harvest. Developed
as an economical alternative to greenhouses, they are a key strategy for
maximizing year-round productivity on limited farmland.

## The Hidden Farm

The "hidden farm" is any section of land left empty when something productive
could be growing there. On small farms with limited acreage, unplanted ground
represents lost organic matter contribution (from root residues, outer leaves,
and stems) and lost financial return. The pursuit of eliminating the hidden farm
has led to cropping intensities where traditional winter green manures are
largely replaced by commercial crops grown so early and late that cover cropping
windows no longer exist.

## Double and Triple Cropping

Most outdoor fields are double- or triple-cropped each year. Rather than leaving
fields fallow between planned plantings, every gap is filled with an unrelated
fast-maturing crop. A field destined for carrots in June can produce lettuce,
spinach, or Asian greens in the intervening months. This succession planning has
achieved gross yields per acre nearly double conventional expectations.

## Close Spacing in Fields

Field spacing mirrors greenhouse techniques: twelve rows of baby leaf salads,
radishes, or carrots are sown on a single 30-inch (75 cm) bed. This close
in-row and between-row spacing dramatically increases per-acre production
compared to wider spacing typical of large-scale field operations.

## From Green Manures to Low Tunnels

Quick hoops evolved from the question of why uncovered fields should lie unused
through winter. Greenhouses are prohibitively expensive for merely wintering-over
hardy crops, and no winter green manures can establish after late-fall harvests
in northern climates. The solution distinguishes between crops harvested during
winter (requiring walk-in tunnels) and low-growing overwintered crops for early
spring harvest — which need only inexpensive, temporary low tunnel protection.

## Quick Hoop Construction

Quick hoops are built from readily available, low-cost materials:

- **Supports**: 10-foot lengths of 3/4-inch electrical conduit (plastic or
  metal), bowed into a half circle
- **Height**: Approximately 30 inches at the midpoint
- **Placement**: Each conduit end inserted ~10 inches into soil flanking two
  adjacent 30-inch beds with a path between them
- **Spacing**: One hoop every 5 feet along beds (typically 50–100 feet long)
- **Initial cover**: 10-foot-wide spun-bonded row cover, secured with sandbags
  every 5 feet along edges

Plastic conduit bends by hand during installation; metal conduit requires
pre-bending with a tubing bender.

## Seasonal Management

### Fall Setup

Hoops are placed over crops in mid-October with the row cover providing initial
protection against cold and wind.

### Winter Reinforcement (Late November/Early December)

A second layer of 10-foot-wide clear plastic goes over the row cover for
snow-proofing and improved thermal protection. Tightening is critical for wind
and snow resistance:

1. Drive a stake 4 feet from the last hoop at each tunnel end
2. Tie a rope to each end of the plastic sheet
3. Pull both ropes taut and secure to stakes (lengthwise tension)
4. Shift sandbags on top of the plastic edges (side-to-side tension)

### Sandbags vs. Buried Edges

Sandbags replace buried edges for practical advantages: much faster placement
and removal, and the ability to cover every pair of beds across a field without
digging trenches. While strong pre-freeze winds can dislodge covers, once the
ground freezes nothing moves.

## Crop Management

### Onions and Scallions

- **Planting**: Last week of August, five rows per bed
- **Scallion strategy**: Two intermediate rows harvested as scallions in spring,
  leaving space for the remaining three rows to bulb out
- **Key advantage**: Overwintered varieties mature by late June — five+ weeks of
  market availability before spring-planted onions are ready

### Spinach and Lettuce

- **Transplants**: Seed soil blocks October 1, transplant two weeks later
  (preferred since fall outdoor germination can be spotty)
- **Direct-seeding**: Slightly earlier planting date than transplants
- **Harvest timing**: Up to one month earlier than the best spring transplants
- **Variety selection**: Must be hardy enough to withstand winter stress

## Spring Ventilation

As spring brings sunny warm days, ventilation is essential:

1. Remove sandbags along the southern edge on sunny days
2. Insert notched props to hold up plastic and fabric edges for airflow
3. By late March, remove plastic layers entirely and store for next winter
4. Row cover fabric alone provides adequate remaining protection

## Economic Advantages

- **Cost**: Covering 1,000 sq ft (equivalent to a 22×48-ft greenhouse) costs
  only 1/20th of building an actual greenhouse
- **Reusability**: Hoops and covers disassemble and relocate for spring/summer
  field crop protection
- **Replacement**: Quick hoops have completely replaced curved wire wickets,
  offering superior durability and season-long versatility

## Future Exploration Potential

Quick hoops enable northern growers to adapt strategies common in warmer
climates. Key research questions: How early can fall-seeded crops be planted
without triggering premature bolting? At what growth stage are seedlings hardiest
for winter survival? Which crops can overwinter as seeds and germinate extra
early?

The most promising frontier is late-fall sowing of hardy crops (peas, carrots,
beets, arugula, onions) for mid-February to mid-March germination. This is
validated by December carrot sowings in cold houses that germinate within a
month. Temperatures inside double-covered quick hoops have reached 55°F on
sunny mid-February days, confirming that solar gain can drive germination even
in the far north.
