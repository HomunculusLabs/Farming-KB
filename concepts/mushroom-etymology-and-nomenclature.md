# Mushroom Etymology and Nomenclature

The words "mushroom" and "toadstool" carry centuries of linguistic and
cultural history. Understanding their origins illuminates how humans have
categorized -- and often misunderstood -- the fungal kingdom. These terms,
along with the broader challenge of fungal nomenclature, reveal fundamental
difficulties in classifying short-lived, irregular organisms (Ramsbottom,
1945).
## The Word "Mushroom"

The word mushroom has a long history with numerous spellings. In
fifteenth-century English it appears as "musseroun" and "muscheron." It is
presumably derived from the Old French "moisseron" (mousseron), itself derived
from "mousse" meaning moss -- reflecting the habitat where many mushrooms
appear. Over time, the term became restricted in English to members of the
genus Psalliota (now [[agaricus-species-guide]]), specifically the
Field Mushroom and Horse Mushroom and their near relatives. This narrowing is
a relatively modern
convention.
The linguistic journey of "mushroom" reflects a pattern common in many
European languages where the local word for fungus became specialized to refer
to the most commonly eaten species. In French, "champignon" originally meant
any mushroom but now primarily refers to cultivated Agaricus bisporus. In
German, "Pilz" serves as the general term while "Champignon" specifically
denotes the cultivated button mushroom, borrowed from French. See
[[mushroom-identification-resources]] for how these naming conventions affect
field
identification.
## The Word "Toadstool"

The word toadstool has an even longer history, appearing as "tadstole" and
"tode stole" in fourteenth-century English. An interesting variant is
"todyshatte." The derivation is straightforward: the fifteenth-century form
"paddocstol" (paddock-stool) directly recalls the modern Dutch "paddestoel,"
literally a stool for a toad -- reflecting the folk belief that toads sat upon
these
structures.
According to the [[mushroom-folklore-and-culture]],
both "mushroom" and "toadstool" were originally applied indiscriminately to
all umbrella-shaped fungi. The main definition of toadstool reads: "A fungus
having a round disk-like top and a slender stalk, a mushroom." Ramsbottom
(1945) argued it would be well to return to this original use, retaining
"mushroom" in its usually accepted sense as a member of the genus
Psalliota.
The toadstool concept became freighted with cultural anxiety about poisoning.
Folklore across Europe connected toads with toxic secretions, and the belief
that toads sat on poisonous fungi reinforced the edibility distinction. In
reality, toads have no special relationship with any mushroom species. The
connection is purely cultural, rooted in the medieval association of toads
with witchcraft and poison. See [[mushroom-poisoning-guide]] for scientific
approaches to
safety.
## The Edible/Poisonous Division

The primary division into edible and
[[ramsbottom-poisonous-fungi-toxins-and-symptoms]] species influenced the
classification of fungi until comparatively recent times. As long as only the
Field Mushroom with, at most, one or two of its near relatives were regarded
as edible, it was easy to define a mushroom as an edible fungus and a
toadstool as a poisonous one. If, however, edibility is the criterion, Britain
has hundreds of "mushrooms" and possibly only a dozen "toadstools" -- which
completely reverses the
picture.
This linguistic confusion had real consequences. Many edible species were
avoided because people believed they "looked poisonous," while genuinely toxic
species were consumed because they resembled known edibles. The false
dichotomy of mushroom vs. toadstool arguably contributed to more poisonings
than it prevented, by encouraging reliance on folk rules rather than careful
[[mushroom-identification]]. See [[mushroom-toxins-and-poisoning]] for a
modern understanding of fungal
toxicity.
## The Word "Fungus"

To many, the very word fungus suggests something mysterious, something morbid.
Some of the early herbalists, seeking a derivation for the word, found it in
the Latin "funus" (a funeral) and "ago" (to put in motion). John Ray, while
doubting its correctness, considered it appropriate. This folk etymology
reinforced the association between fungi and death that persists in popular
culture, as explored in  
[[mushroom-folklore-and-culture]]
.
The actual etymology of "fungus" is uncertain. It may derive from the Greek
"sphongos" (sponge), referring to the porous texture of some fungi. Pliny the
Elder used the Latin "fungi" to refer to edible mushrooms. The scientific
adoption of Fungi as a kingdom name came much later, reflecting the growing
understanding that these organisms constituted a fundamentally separate branch
of life from plants and animals. The kingdom Fungi now encompasses an
estimated 2.2 to 3.8 million species, of which only about 148,000 have been
formally described. See [[molecular-fungal-taxonomy]] for modern approaches to
fungal
classification.
## Challenges of Fungal Nomenclature

It was comparatively easy to describe herbs, shrubs, and trees clearly enough
for them to be recognized; indeed many old names are still used. But
toadstools are for the most part short-lived and irregular in occurrence, and
their distinguishing characters are not very easy to grasp. Consequently,
instead of clear descriptions of species known to be safe and those which are
dangerous, various [[classical-roman-mushroom-poisoning]] were given for
distinguishing the two groups. These rules were repeated by the herbalists and
many have a world-wide currency even today -- despite being entirely without
scientific
value.
Modern fungal nomenclature follows the International Code of Nomenclature for
algae, fungi, and plants (ICN), which governs the naming of fungi at all
taxonomic levels. DNA barcoding has revolutionized fungal identification,
revealing that many morphologically similar "species" are actually distinct
lineages, while some traditionally separate species are conspecific. See
[[fungal-dna-barcoding]] for the molecular revolution in fungal taxonomy and
[[fungal-species-recognition-genetics]] for how modern phylogenetics
reshapes our understanding of fungal
diversity.
## Classical References

The first known mention of fungi refers to poisoning: when the poet Euripides
(480-406 B.C.) was at Icarus, a woman with two grown-up sons and a married
daughter ate fungi gathered from the fields and were "destroyed by pitiless
fate in one day." Pliny's famous query -- "What great pleasure then can there
be in partaking of a dish of so doubtful a character as this?" -- was actually
a warning against [[agaricus-species-guide]], which was very
conveniently adapted for administering poisons. If fungi had not been commonly
eaten, why the frequent warnings? Many rich Romans valued them so highly that
they employed special
collectors.
The name [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] is the Greek "Amanitai," thought
to be derived from Mount Amanon in Cilicia. It was apparently the name for the
Field Mushroom, which grows abundantly in Greece and is still called
"Amanites" or more popularly "Manitari." When first used in a generic sense,
it was applied to several edible species including the Field Mushroom, but it
has had its present significance for well over a century. See
[[amanita-genus-morphology]] for the morphological characteristics that define
this important
genus.
