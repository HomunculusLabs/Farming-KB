# PCR and Molecular Methods for Assessing Fungal Diversity

Molecular methods enable [[monitoring-and-assessment-of-fungal-bioremediation]] biodiversity without the need for
culturing, fruiting, or direct microscopic observation. Rodriguez et al. (2004)
describe a suite of PCR-based techniques for discriminating morphologically
indistinct taxa, determining genetic relatedness, quantifying [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]]
in natural populations, monitoring species in complex substrata, and quantifying
specific gene activities in nature. These approaches are transforming our ability
to inventory [[biodiversity-fungi-soil-fungal-communities]] and understand their [[ecological-roles-fungi-ecosystems]].

## Arbitrarily Primed PCR (apPCR)

Arbitrarily primed PCR (apPCR), also known as RAPD (random amplified
polymorphic DNA) analysis, uses short oligonucleotide primers (10 to 20 bp)
that anneal to complementary DNA sequences. When complementary sequences occur
in opposite orientation on separate strands, a double-stranded product is
synthesized. The number of products generated ranges from fewer than 5 to more
than 20 per primer, so 10 to 20 primers may yield more than 100 genetic
markers. Primers composed of simple sequence repeats such as (AGG)5, (GACA)4,
and (GTC)5 are particularly effective. In fungi, 80 to 100% of DNA products
are shared among conspecific individuals, while individuals from different
species share only 0 to 20%, generating species-specific band patterns.

## Taxonomic Discrimination with apPCR

ApPCR can discriminate among [[undescribed-fungal-taxa]] with few distinguishing morphological
characters. Five Colletotrichum isolates that could not be identified
taxonomically because they had lost the ability to infect plants and produce
conidia were easily distinguished as five distinct species by apPCR (less than
10% band sharing). Distantly related basidiomycete congeners shared only 0 to
20% of bands, while conspecifics shared 80 to 100%. Species designations
require comparisons to apPCR patterns from known species, but an internet-
accessible library of known patterns is a realistic future goal.

## Dual-Primer PCR (dpPCR)

Many apPCR products represent species-specific DNA sequences that can be
verified by DNA hybridization analysis. Primers designed from the terminal
nucleotide sequences of species-specific bands are used for dpPCR to
specifically amplify the band(s) of interest. DpPCR detects target species DNA
even when mixed with four other species at ratios of 1:100. The technique is
so sensitive that as little as 25 picograms of DNA amplified with species-
specific primers can yield a product. DpPCR and npPCR require less accuracy
from thermocyclers than apPCR, making them easier to use and faster.

## Nested-Primer PCR (npPCR)

Nested-primer PCR increases sensitivity 100- to 1,000-fold over dpPCR through
a two-step procedure. The first step involves amplification of genomic DNA with
two species-specific primers. The second step reamplifies a small amount of
the first product with primers that anneal to sequences 10 base pairs or more
inside the terminal ends of the first product. This level of sensitivity allows
detection of a few fungal genomes in any complex sample. If greater sensitivity
is required, a second set of nested primers can be generated for a third
amplification, providing extremely high specificity for environmental
detection of target species.

## Genetic Diversity Assessment

The intraspecific variation in apPCR products allows assessment of genetic
diversity within and between populations. As the number of primers increases,
the probability of generating polymorphic markers also increases. For a measure
of genetic diversity to be meaningful, experiments suggest that at least 15
to 20 polymorphic markers are required. The sensitivity of apPCR is such that
small amounts of fungal material (0.5 cubic cm) are sufficient for hundreds to
thousands of genetic analyses. Clusters of 16 to 50 sporocarps of species
such as Cantharellus formosus, Cystoderma cinnabarium, and Laetiporus
sulfureus have been analyzed, revealing that adjacent sporocarps are often
nonclonal and represent distinct genotypes.

## DNA Extraction and Preservation

Tissue samples (approximately 0.5 cubic cm) collected from sporocarps are
placed in 1.5-ml microcentrifuge tubes containing 0.5 ml of DNA preservation
and extraction buffer (150 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 2.0% N-lauroylsarcosine).
Samples can be stored at ambient temperatures for at least 1 year without DNA
degradation. For extraction, samples are pulverized, incubated at 65 degrees C
for 30 minutes, and cell debris is removed by centrifugation. Nucleic acids and
proteins are precipitated with 0.7 volumes of PEG/NaCl (20% polyethylene
glycol molecular weight 8000 in 2.5 N NaCl), followed by ammonium acetate
precipitation and isopropanol extraction to purify the DNA.
