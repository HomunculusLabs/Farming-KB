# Phytochrome and Photoperiodism

## Overview

Phytochrome is a photoreceptor pigment protein that enables plants to measure the
duration of darkness and regulate critical developmental transitions, most
importantly the timing of flowering. Discovered through research on the Maryland
Mammoth tobacco strain, phytochrome functions as a reversible molecular switch that
alternates between two forms in response to red and far-red light. The phytochrome
system underlies photoperiodism—one of the most important biological timing
mechanisms in the plant kingdom—and allows plants to coordinate their reproductive
cycles with seasonal changes in day length.

## The Maryland Mammoth Discovery

In 1906, tobacco farmers in southern Maryland noticed a remarkable new strain of
tobacco that never seemed to stop growing. This strain, named Maryland Mammoth,
could reach four and a half metres in height and produce almost a hundred leaves,
stopping only when winter frosts set in. While its vigor seemed desirable, Maryland
Mammoth had a critical flaw: it rarely flowered, making it impossible for farmers
to harvest seed for the next year's crop.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of Agriculture
investigated this mystery. They planted Maryland Mammoth in two groups: one left
outside in the fields, and another moved to a dark shed every afternoon. Simply
limiting the amount of light the plants received was sufficient to cause Maryland
Mammoth to stop growing leaves and begin flowering. The plants flowered under short
days but continued vegetative growth under long summer days. This phenomenon, named
photoperiodism, provided the first strong evidence that plants measure how much
light they receive.

## Day Length vs Night Length

A key question following the discovery of photoperiodism was whether plants measure
the length of the day or the length of the night. Around the time of World War II,
scientists discovered they could manipulate flowering simply by turning lights on
and off briefly in the middle of the night. A short-day plant like the soybean could
be kept from flowering by a brief flash of light during the night, even during
naturally short days. Conversely, a long-day plant like the iris could be induced
to flower in midwinter by a brief nighttime light exposure.

These experiments proved conclusively that plants measure the length of the
continuous dark period, not the day length. The night break phenomenon became a
powerful tool for plant physiologists and remains commercially important today.
Chrysanthemum farmers, for example, keep their plants from flowering by briefly
illuminating them during autumn nights, then stop the night breaks two weeks before
Mother's Day to produce a synchronized burst of flowers.

## The Red/Far-Red Reversible Switch

Scientists investigating the color sensitivity of photoperiodism made a surprising
discovery: plants responded only to flashes of red light during the night. Blue or
green flashes had no effect on flowering, but just a few seconds of red light could
dramatically alter a plant's developmental trajectory.

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues made an even more remarkable
finding: far-red light (wavelengths slightly longer than bright red, barely visible
at dusk) could completely cancel the effect of red light. If irises were given a
pulse of red light in the middle of the night (inducing flowering), a subsequent
pulse of far-red light would negate this effect as if the red light had never been
given. A subsequent red pulse would restore flowering, and alternating red and
far-red pulses would toggle the plant's flowering response back and forth.

## The Phytochrome Molecule

By the early 1960s, Warren L. Butler and colleagues demonstrated that a single
photoreceptor protein was responsible for both the red and far-red effects. They
named it phytochrome (from Greek: "plant color"). Phytochrome exists in two
interconvertible forms:

- **Pr (P660)**: The inactive form that absorbs red light (660 nm peak). Absorption
  of red light converts Pr to the active Pfr form.

- **Pfr (P730)**: The active form that absorbs far-red light (730 nm peak).
  Absorption of far-red light converts Pfr back to the inactive Pr form.

In nature, this system operates as an elegant biological clock. The last light a
plant perceives at the end of the day is enriched in far-red wavelengths, converting
phytochrome to the inactive Pr form and signaling that night is beginning. In the
morning, red-rich sunlight converts phytochrome to the active Pfr form, initiating
daytime processes. The slow dark reversion of Pfr to Pr during the night provides
the plant with a built-in hourglass timer for measuring the duration of darkness.

## Site of Perception: The Leaf

Unlike phototropism, where the "eye" is located in the shoot tip, photoperiodic
perception occurs primarily in the leaves. Experiments in which different parts of
the plant were illuminated showed that shining light on any single leaf is sufficient
to regulate flowering in the entire plant. Conversely, if all leaves are removed
and only the stem and apex remain, the plant becomes completely blind to photoperiodic
cues.

When phytochrome in a leaf detects the appropriate light signal, it initiates the
production of a mobile signaling molecule (now known to include the protein FLOWERING
LOCUS T, or FT) that travels through the phloem to the shoot apex and triggers the
transition from vegetative growth to flowering. This leaf-to-apex signaling pathway
represents one of the most important long-distance signaling systems in plants.

## Classification of Photoperiodic Responses

Plants are classified by their flowering responses to day length:

- **Short-day plants** flower when the night length exceeds a critical minimum.
  Examples include chrysanthemums, soybeans, and poinsettias. They typically flower
  in late summer or autumn as days shorten.

- **Long-day plants** flower when the night length falls below a critical maximum.
  Examples include irises, barley, spinach, and lettuce. They typically flower in
  spring or early summer as days lengthen.

- **Day-neutral plants** flower regardless of photoperiod, responding instead to
  other cues such as plant age or temperature. Examples include tomatoes, rice,
  and maize.

Some plants have more complex requirements, needing a specific sequence of day
lengths or responding to both photoperiod and vernalization (cold exposure).

## Ecological and Agricultural Significance

The phytochrome system and photoperiodism are critical for plant fitness in natural
ecosystems. By synchronizing flowering with seasonal conditions, photoperiodism
ensures that pollination, seed development, and dispersal occur under favorable
environmental conditions. In agriculture, understanding photoperiodism has enabled
the manipulation of crop flowering and harvest schedules, the selection of
appropriate cultivars for different latitudes, and the development of greenhouse
lighting strategies to produce year-round harvests.

## Plants Measure Night, Not Day

For decades, photoperiodism was described in terms of day length. [[short-day-and-long-day-plant-classification]] plants were said to need short days to flower; long-day plants supposedly required long days.

But later experiments revealed a critical and counterintuitive truth: **plants actually measure the length of the night, not the day.**

This was proven through clever interruption experiments. If a long night was briefly interrupted by a flash of light in the middle, a short-day plant would not flower — as if the night had never happened. The plant was not counting hours of daylight; it was counting continuous hours of darkness.

This distinction is fundamental. A short-day plant is really a "long-night" plant. A long-day plant is really a "short-night" plant. The continuous dark period is what matters.

---

## Categories of Photoperiodic Response

Plants fall into three main photoperiodic categories:

**Short-day plants (long-night plants)** flower when the night exceeds a critical length. Examples include chrysanthemums, poinsettias, and soybeans. They typically bloom in late summer or autumn as nights lengthen.

**Long-day plants (short-night plants)** flower when the night is shorter than a critical threshold. Spinach, lettuce, and radishes fall into this group. They tend to bloom in spring and early summer.

**Day-neutral plants** flower regardless of day or night length. Tomatoes, rice, and cucumbers are day-neutral — they bloom based on maturity or other cues, not photoperiod.

This classification explains why Maryland Mammoth tobacco wouldn't flower during summer: as a short-day plant, it needed the long nights of autumn to trigger its reproductive cycle.

## The Red Light Flash

The next major breakthrough came when researchers asked a deeper question: what specific quality of light mattered? Was any light capable of interrupting the dark period?

Experiments showed that a brief flash of red light (around 660 nanometers) in the middle of the night was sufficient to prevent short-day plants from flowering and to induce long-day plants to flower. Red light alone could flip the switch.

But the truly remarkable discovery came next. If a flash of far-red light (around 730 nm) was administered immediately after the red flash, the effect was completely reversed. The short-day plant would flower as if no light interruption had occurred at all.

Red turns flowering off for short-day plants. Far-red turns it back on.

This could be repeated back and forth, endlessly. The plant's [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] always reflected only the **last** flash of light it received. It was a biological toggle switch.

---

## Harry Borthwick and Far-Red Reversal

The far-red reversal effect was discovered in the late 1940s and 1950s by Harry Borthwick and his colleagues at the USDA's Beltsville Agricultural Research Center.

Borthwick's team systematically tested different wavelengths of light and their effects on flowering. They constructed detailed action spectra showing exactly which wavelengths promoted or inhibited flowering in various plant species.

The finding that far-red light could completely undo the effect of red light was revolutionary. It meant plants possessed some kind of reversible pigment — a molecule that could exist in two interconvertible forms, toggled by different wavelengths.

Borthwick spent years characterizing this photoreversible system. He demonstrated that the last light quality perceived by the plant determined its physiological response, regardless of how many prior flashes [[phytochrome]] ived. The plant "remembered" only the most recent signal.

This elegant toggle mechanism pointed unmistakably to a single molecular switch, though its identity remained unknown for several more years.

## Warren Butler Identifies Phytochrome (1960)

In 1960, Warren Butler and his colleagues at the USDA finally identified the mysterious pigment responsible for the red/far-red reversible response. They named it **phytochrome** (from the Greek *phyton*, meaning plant, and *chroma*, meaning color).

Butler extracted phytochrome from dark-grown oat seedlings and demonstrated its unique photoreversible properties in vitro. The pigment could be switched between two stable forms: Pr, which absorbs red light, and Pfr, which absorbs far-red light.

When Pr absorbs red light, it converts to Pfr. When Pfr absorbs far-red light, it converts back to Pr. In darkness, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr over time.

It is this slow dark reversion that serves as the plant's biological hourglass. As long as Pfr persists, the plant "knows" it has recently seen light. Only when Pfr has fully reverted to Pr does the plant register that a sufficiently long, uninterrupted night has passed.

## Phytochrome as a Light-Activated Molecular Switch

Phytochrome operates as a molecular switch that links [[plant-photoreceptors-and-mechanism-of-light-perception]] to gene expression. The Pfr form — the active form produced by red light — migrates to the cell nucleus and directly interacts with transcription factors, altering which genes are turned on or off.

This is how a simple light signal is translated into the complex developmental decision to flower. The phytochrome molecule itself is a protein with a covalently bound chromophore — a light-absorbing molecule similar to the pigments found in our own retinas.

The system is remarkably sensitive. Even a brief flash of light lasting only a few minutes can convert enough Pr to Pfr to alter the plant's developmental trajectory. Yet the system is also robust, able to filter out transient fluctuations in light caused by passing clouds or brief canopy shading.

---

## One Leaf Controls the Whole Plant

One of the most fascinating aspects of phytochrome-mediated photoperiodism is that the "eye" for detecting day length is not a specialized organ. It is **any leaf** on the plant.

Experiments demonstrated that if even a single leaf is exposed to the appropriate light regime, the entire plant will flower. Conversely, if all leaves are kept in darkness while the stem tip is illuminated, the plant will not respond photoperiodically.

The leaf perceives the light signal through phytochrome, then produces a mobile signaling molecule — a hormone called **florigen** — that travels through the vascular system to the shoot apex. There, at the growing tip, florigen triggers the genetic program that converts vegetative growth into reproductive flowering.

This means that phytochrome in one small leaf can regulate flowering across the entire organism. It is an elegant example of how plants integrate localized sensory information into whole-plant responses.

## Commercial Applications

Understanding photoperiodism and phytochrome has had enormous practical significance for agriculture and horticulture.

Chrysanthemum growers, for example, have long exploited photoperiodic control to produce flowers year-round. Chrysanthemums are short-day plants that naturally bloom in autumn. By covering greenhouse plants with blackout cloth in the late afternoon to extend the dark period, growers can force flowering in any month.

Conversely, supplemental lighting can be used to prevent premature flowering or to keep long-day crops like spinach in a vegetative, leaf-producing state.

The red/far-red toggle has also been harnessed. Some commercial growers use specific light-emitting diodes (LEDs) tuned to red or far-red wavelengths to precisely manipulate phytochrome states, optimizing growth, flowering time, and plant architecture.

These applications demonstrate how fundamental research into plant sensing — beginning with a curious tobacco plant in Maryland — translates directly into agricultural practices that feed and beautify the world.

## Key Takeaways

- Photoperiodism was discovered through Maryland Mammoth tobacco in 1918 by Garner and Allard.

- Plants measure night length, not day length, to determine when to flower.

- Red light converts phytochrome to its active Pfr form; far-red light reverts it to inactive Pr.

- The last light signal perceived determines the plant's response — a true biological toggle.

- Warren Butler identified phytochrome in 1960 as the molecular basis of this system.

- A single leaf detecting light through phytochrome can regulate flowering across the entire plant.

- Commercial growers manipulate photoperiod and phytochrome to control flowering schedules.
