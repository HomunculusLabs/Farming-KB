# Phytochrome and Photoperiodism

Phytochrome is a photoreceptor pigment protein found in plants that enables
them to sense red and far-red light, forming the molecular basis of
photoperiodism -- the ability to measure [[coleman-winter-day-length-and-persephone-months]] regulate developmental
processes such as flowering in response to seasonal changes. The discovery of
phytochrome and photoperiodism represent milestones in plant biology that
transformed our understanding of how plants perceive light.

## The Discovery of Photoperiodism

The story begins with a remarkable tobacco strain called [[chamovitz-maryland-mammoth-photoperiodism-discovery]],
discovered by farmers in southern Maryland around 1906. This unusual cultivar
grew continuously to heights of 4.5 metres, producing nearly a hundred leaves,
and rarely flowered, preventing farmers from harvesting seed.

In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and Harry A. Allard at the US Department of
Agriculture planted [[garner-allard-maryland-mammoth-tobacco-experiment]] in pots and divided them: one group
remained in natural daylight, the other was moved to a dark shed each
afternoon. Plants with shortened days stopped growing and flowered, while
those in natural long days continued [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]]. This established
photoperiodism and categorized plants into short-day (chrysanthemums,
soybeans), long-day (irises, barley), and day-neutral plants.

## Measuring the Night, Not the Day

A crucial discovery around World War II showed that plants measure not the
length of the day but the length of the uninterrupted dark period. Scientists
could manipulate flowering by briefly interrupting the dark period with light.
A short-day plant could be kept vegetative by a few minutes of light at night;
a long-day plant could be induced to flower in winter with a brief night flash.

The continuous duration of darkness is the critical seasonal signal. This is
exploited commercially by chrysanthemum growers, who prevent flowering by
giving plants brief light interruptions each night, then stop two weeks
before desired harvest for synchronized flowering.

## The Color Specificity of Responses

Only red light (approximately 660 nm) was effective at interrupting the dark
period. Blue and green light flashes had no effect. This specificity pointed
to a dedicated red-light photoreceptor, distinct from the blue-light
phototropism system mediated by phototropins. Plants possess multiple, color-
specific light-sensing systems underscoring their perceptual sophistication.

## The Red-Far-Red Reversible Switch

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues discovered that far-red
light (approximately 730 nm, barely visible at dusk) could completely reverse
the effect of red light on flowering. Red light induced flowering; far-red
immediately after cancelled it. This was fully reversible -- only the last
color seen determined the response, with seconds of either color sufficient.
The plant effectively "remembers" the last color of light perceived.

## Phytochrome: The Molecular Switch

By the early 1960s, Warren L. Butler demonstrated that a single photoreceptor
was responsible for both red and far-red responses, naming it "phytochrome"
(Greek: phyton = plant, chroma = color). Phytochrome exists in two
interconvertible forms. The inactive form (Pr) absorbs red light at 660 nm
and converts to active (Pfr). Pfr absorbs far-red at 730 nm and reverts to Pr.

The ecological logic is elegant. Sunlight is red-rich; sunset light is far-
red-enriched because atmospheric scattering removes blue and red wavelengths.
Far-red at dusk converts phytochrome to inactive Pr, signaling night; morning
red activates Pfr, signaling day. Plants thus measure time since last seeing
red light and adjust growth accordingly.

## Where Phytochrome Senses Light

Unlike phototropism, where the "eye" is at the shoot tip, photoperiodic
sensing is distributed across the leaves. Illuminating any single leaf with
red light at night regulates flowering in the entire plant. If all leaves are
removed, the plant becomes completely blind to photoperiodic signals.

Phytochrome in leaves initiates a [[chamovitz-phytochrome-mobile-signal-leaf-to-flowering]] -- the protein FLOWERING
LOCUS T (FT) -- that travels through the phloem to the shoot apex and
triggers flowering. Leaves are distributed light sensors; the apex is the
response organ that produces flowers.
