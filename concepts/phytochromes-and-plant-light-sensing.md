# Phytochromes and Plant Light Sensing

## Overview

Phytochromes are a family of photoreceptor proteins unique to plants, bacteria,
and fungi that play a central role in detecting red and far-red light. They are
arguably the most important class of plant photoreceptors, governing an enormous
range of developmental processes from [[cannabis-seed-germination-methods]] to flowering. The
phytochrome system allows plants to sense not only the presence of light but
also its quality, duration, and the competitive threat posed by neighboring
vegetation.

## Discovery

Phytochromes were discovered in the 1950s through work on lettuce seed
germination by Sterling Hendricks, Harry Borthwick, and their colleagues at
the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Beltsville [[fukuoka-inductive-deductive-intuitive-reasoning-agricultural-research]] Center.
They found that lettuce seeds germinated in response to brief flashes of red
light (660 nm) but that this germination-promoting effect could be completely
reversed by a subsequent flash of far-red light (730 nm). This reversible
photoreversible response was unlike anything previously described in biology
and pointed to the existence of a novel pigment system.

## Structure and Photoconversion

Phytochromes are large proteins (approximately 125 kDa) consisting of:
- An **N-terminal photosensory domain** that covalently binds a linear
  tetrapyrrole chromophore called **phytochromobilin** (in plants) or
  heme-derived biliverdin (in bacteria).
- A **C-terminal output domain** containing a histidine kinase-related
  region involved in [[fungal-environmental-sensing-signal-transduction-read]].

The chromophore absorbs light and undergoes a conformational change, switching
the phytochrome between two stable states:

- **Pr (P_red)**: The inactive form that absorbs red light (peak absorption
  at approximately 660 nm). This is the form that phytochromes adopt in
  darkness.
- **Pfr (P_far-red)**: The biologically active form that absorbs far-red
  light (peak absorption at approximately 730 nm). This form is produced
  when Pr absorbs red light.

The conversion is fully reversible: red light converts Pr → Pfr, while
far-red light converts Pfr → Pr. In natural daylight, which contains both
red and far-red wavelengths, an equilibrium is established with a mixture
of both forms. In darkness, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr through a thermal
process called **dark reversion**.

## Phytochrome Families in Plants

Higher plants possess multiple phytochrome genes organized into two groups:

- **Phytochrome A (phyA)**: Light-labile; accumulates in darkness and is
  rapidly degraded upon conversion to Pfr. It is the primary phytochrome
  responsible for responses to very low fluence rates of light and for
  sensing continuous far-red light (which is enriched under vegetation
  canopies). phyA mediates the **far-red high-irradiance response** (FR-HIR)
  and the **very low fluence response** (VLFR).
- **Phytochromes B-E (phyB-phyE)**: Light-stable; these phytochromes
  accumulate in both light and dark conditions. phyB is the most
  extensively studied and is the primary mediator of **red/far-red
  reversible responses** and the **end-of-day far-red response**.

The existence of multiple phytochromes with overlapping but distinct
functions allows plants to integrate complex light information and
respond appropriately to diverse environmental conditions.

## Key Physiological Responses

### Seed Germination
Many seeds, particularly small-seeded species, require light for germination.
This is an adaptive mechanism that prevents germination when seeds are buried
too deeply in soil where light cannot penetrate. The phytochrome system acts
as a "[[phytochrome-ecological-function-dusk-dawn-red-far-red-light-switch]]": a brief flash of red light (converting Pr to Pfr)
promotes germination, while far-red light reverses this effect. In nature,
the red-to-far-red ratio at the soil surface promotes germination, while
deep burial in soil (where far-red light from the soil matrix may
predominate) inhibits it.
