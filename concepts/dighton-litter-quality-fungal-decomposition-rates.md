## Litter Quality Controls on Fungal Decomposition Rates

The chemical composition of plant litter is a primary determinant of
decomposition rates and the pattern of [[oyster-substrate-carbon-nitrogen-ratio]], lignin
content, polyphenol concentrations, and the ratio of lignin plus polyphenol to
nitrogen together predict how rapidly nutrients will be mineralized from
[[soil-edaphic-factors-microbial-communities]] (available
moisture and temperature), and colonization by appropriate saprotrophic
organisms (Cooke and Rayner, 1984). The input of different types of plant
litters varies with ecosystem type (Dickinson and Pugh, 1974; Cadish and
Giller, 1997).

The general consensus is that the carbon:nitrogen and lignin:[[fungal-nitrogen-mineralization-pathways]] rates are most strongly correlated with the (lignin
plus polyphenol):N ratio. Their regression analysis showed:

- C:N ratio: correlation of 0.74 with mass loss, 0.61 with N mineralization
- Lignin:N ratio: correlation of 0.68 with mass loss, 0.42 with N
  mineralization
- Polyphenol:N ratio: correlation of 0.54 with mass loss, 0.76 with N
  mineralization
- (Lignin plus polyphenol):N ratio: correlation of 0.77 with mass loss, 0.68
  with N mineralization

The combined ratio proved the best predictor, as it accounts for both the
structural resistance of lignin and the inhibitory effects of polyphenols on
microbial activity.

## Secondary Chemicals and Decomposition

Secondary chemicals produced by plants, particularly polyphenols and tannins,
independently inhibit decomposition rates by soil microorganisms (Harborne,
1997). Van Lauwe et al. (1997) showed that there was often little negative
relationship between nitrogen mineralization and either polyphenol:N or
lignin:N ratio alone. However, when these factors were combined, the
predictive power for decomposition rates improved substantially.

## Resource Quality and Fungal Community Structure

Differences in chemical composition of plant remains mean that not all
materials can be utilized by all fungal species. Decomposition is a product of
enzyme activity, where the types of enzymes required depend on the substrates
of the resource. The ability of different fungal species to produce specific
enzymes dictates the succession of fungi as they colonize resources (Sinsabaugh
and Liptak, 1997).

The initial colonizers of fresh litter typically utilize soluble carbohydrate
sources (sugars) and are later replaced by fungal species with greater
enzymatic competence capable of breaking down cellulose and lignin. This
succession from "sugar fungi" to basidiomycetes was formalized by Swift et al.
(1979).

## Cellulose:Lignin Ratio as a Control Point

Linkins et al. (1984) identified the cellulose:lignin ratio as a critical
control point for decomposition. Cellulose becomes unavailable for microbial
use when the cellulose:lignin ratio declines below 0.5. This threshold
represents the point at which lignin physically shields cellulose fibers from
enzyme attack, fundamentally altering the decomposer community and slowing
decomposition rates.
