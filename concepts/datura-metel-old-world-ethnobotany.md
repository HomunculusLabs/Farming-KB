# Datura metel: Old World Ethnobotany and Cultural History

Datura metel, known as dhatura and dutra in India, is an important Old World species of Datura whose narcotic properties have been known and valued since prehistory. This purple-flowered member of the deadly nightshade family (Solanaceae) has a long and complex history spanning India, China, the Middle East, and beyond, with connections to some of the most famous religious practices of the ancient world.

## Botanical Profile

Datura metel is a robust annual or short-lived perennial plant with large, ovate leaves and striking purple or white flowers. The plant produces spiny capsules containing numerous kidney-shaped seeds. All parts of the plant contain highly toxic tropane alkaloids, with the highest concentrations found in the seeds and leaves.

Datura metel is one of approximately 20 species of Datura distributed across both hemispheres. The genus belongs to the nightshade family, Solanaceae, and shares the characteristic tropane alkaloid chemistry of related hallucinogenic plants including [[belladonna-atropa-belladonna-ethnobotany]], [[henbane-hyoscyamus-niger-ethnobotany]], and [[mandrake-mandragora-officinarum-in-european-folklore]]. A white-flowered form, considered by some botanists to be a distinct species (D. fastuosa), is also widely cultivated.

## Indian Use Since Prehistory

The narcotic properties of Datura metel have been known in India since prehistory. The plant holds a significant place in Indian traditional medicine (Ayurveda) and has been used both as a medicine and as an intoxicant throughout recorded Indian history. The Hindu god Shiva is sometimes associated with datura, and the plant is offered at Shiva temples, particularly during the festival of Maha Shivaratri.

In many parts of Asia, even today, seeds of Datura are often mixed with food and tobacco for illicit use, especially by thieves for stupefying victims, who may remain seriously intoxicated for several days. This criminal application of datura has been documented across South and Southeast Asia for centuries and represents a darker aspect of the plant's cultural history.

## The Oracle of Delphi Connection

Some writers have credited Datura metel with being responsible for the intoxicating smoke associated with the Oracle of Delphi, one of the most important religious institutions of ancient Greece. The Pythia, the priestess who delivered prophecies at Delphi, was described by ancient sources as entering a trance state before pronouncing her oracles.

The hypothesis that datura or related tropane-containing plants contributed to the Pythia's trance remains debated. Geological studies at the Delphic site have identified natural emissions of ethylene and other hydrocarbons that could produce altered states of consciousness. However, the possibility that plant materials were also used to enhance or modulate the trance state cannot be ruled out.

## Chinese Tradition and Buddhist Connection

Early Chinese writings report a hallucinogen that has been identified with Datura metel. The plant was held sacred in China, where people believed that when Buddha preached, heaven sprinkled the plant with dew. This Buddhist connection elevated datura from a mere medicinal plant to a sacred substance imbued with divine significance.

The Chinese use of datura appears to have been primarily medical, with the plant employed in the treatment of asthma, coughs, and other respiratory conditions. However, the psychoactive properties of the plant were certainly known, and its use in shamanic or religious contexts may have been more widespread than surviving records indicate.

## Arabian Medicine: Avicenna and Jouzmathel

Avicenna, the great Arabian physician of the 11th century, mentioned Datura metel under the name jouzmathel in his Canon of Medicine. This influential medical text, which remained the standard reference in European and Islamic medicine for centuries, helped to transmit knowledge of datura from the East to the West.

Avicenna's description of jouzmathel focused primarily on its medical applications rather than its psychoactive properties, reflecting the medical tradition's tendency to emphasize therapeutic uses while downplaying intoxicating effects. However, the inclusion of datura in such an authoritative text ensured its continued availability in the Western pharmacopoeia.

## Use as an Aphrodisiac

The use of Datura metel as an aphrodisiac in the East Indies was recorded in 1578 by European observers. The tropane alkaloids in datura can produce a combination of disinhibition, sensory alteration, and physical effects that might be interpreted as aphrodisiac, though the plant's actual pharmacological effects on sexual function are complex and potentially dangerous.

The aphrodisiac reputation of datura parallels that of [[mandrake-mandragora-officinarum-in-european-folklore]] in European tradition, where the plant was similarly believed to possess potent properties related to love and fertility. These cross-cultural parallels suggest that the tropane alkaloids may have consistent effects on human psychology that are interpreted in culturally specific but convergent ways.

## Datura and Cannabis Combinations

Datura metel is commonly mixed with cannabis and smoked in Asia to this day. This combination, which may seem surprising given the very different pharmacological profiles of the two plants, has been documented across South and Southeast Asia. The tropane alkaloids of datura combined with the cannabinoids of cannabis produce a complex intoxication that differs significantly from either plant alone.

Leaves of the white-flowered form (D. fastuosa) are also smoked with cannabis or tobacco in many parts of Africa and Asia. This widespread practice of combining datura with other psychoactive plants reflects a common pattern in ethnopharmacology, where plant admixtures are used to modify, enhance, or extend the effects of primary intoxicants.

## Chemical Composition

The plant contains highly toxic alkaloids, the principal one being scopolamine. This hallucinogen is present in heaviest concentrations in the leaves and seeds. Scopolamine is found also in the New World species of Datura. Basically, all species of Datura have a similar chemical composition, with active principles mainly consisting of hyoscyamine and scopolamine, which are tropane alkaloids.

The differences among Datura species are chiefly in the relative concentrations of these various alkaloids. Datura ferox, a related Old World species not so widespread in Asia, is also valued for its narcotic and medicinal properties. The [[solanaceous-hallucinogens-of-medieval-european-witchcraft]] is characterized by the anticholinergic effects of these tropane alkaloids.

## Utilization Patterns: Old World versus New World

The utilization of Datura preparations in Asia entailed much less ritual than in the New World. In the Americas, Datura use was typically embedded in elaborate ceremonial contexts with specific mythological meanings, while in Asia the plant has been used more pragmatically — as a medicine, an intoxicant, a criminal tool, and occasionally a religious aid, but without the elaborate ritual frameworks found in New World traditions.

This difference in ritual context is part of the broader pattern distinguishing [[old-world-hallucinogenic-plants-overview]] from New World hallucinogen use. While New World cultures typically enveloped their psychoactive plants in complex religious and ceremonial frameworks, Old World cultures tended to use similar plants in more diverse and less ritualized contexts.

## Historical Context

Datura metel has a particularly rich history in Asian medicine and culture, where it has been used for millennia in Ayurvedic medicine, traditional Chinese medicine, and various South and Southeast Asian healing traditions. In India, the plant was known as "dhattura" and was considered one of the most important medicinal plants in the classical Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia, used both as a medicine and as a ritual intoxicant.

The plant's psychoactive properties were exploited in various cultural contexts, including the datura-based preparations used by the Thugs of India — the infamous "thagana" or "thuggee" method of administering tropane alkaloids to victims prior to robbery. While this use is historically notorious, it represents only a small fraction of the plant's extensive cultural history.

## Modern Significance

Datura metel remains important in traditional Asian medicine, where it continues to be used in the treatment of asthma, pain, and inflammatory conditions. Modern pharmacological research has validated some of these traditional uses by confirming the bronchodilatory and analgesic properties of the plant's tropane alkaloids.

The plant is also of interest to ethnopharmacologists studying the spread of psychoactive plant knowledge across Asia, as the different varieties and preparations of D. metel across the region reflect complex patterns of cultural exchange and botanical knowledge transmission.
