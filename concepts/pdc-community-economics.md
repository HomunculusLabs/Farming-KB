# PDC [[permaculture-community-scale-design]] Economics and Commonwork

In the 1981 Permaculture [[urban-permaculture-edge-effect-and-design-principles]] Course, Bill permaculture-design|Mollison presents two distinct but related economic frameworks: the "commonwork" model for large-scale investment (Pamphlet XV) and the community economics of permaculture design practice (Pamphlet VIII). Together they outline an alternative to both capitalism and traditional communism.

## Commonwork: Functional Stacking for Investors

In the "Permaculture for Millionaires" lecture (Pamphlet XV), Mollison describes his concept of commonwork:

"It is functional stacking. The original meaning of the word is to put one painting on top of another. It is like laying on colors. What we are laying on is functions."

### The Babassu Palm Model

Mollison's primary example is the Babassu palm system:
- Grows on "the worst conditions on the exposed coast of India"
- Produces "about ten to twelve thousand liters of fuel per acre annually"
- Can be "heavily intercropped" -- provides shade for under-crops
- For centuries has provided "building and thatch and carpeting material"
- Thatch is "absolutely water tight, low mass, and ideal for that climate"

In this model: "We produce a crop. We produce honey. We practice aquaculture. The investor can let all of these go. All the investor wants is to earn a return from the alcohol production capacity of the site. All the other people own these other capacities."

### The Institute Structure

Mollison proposes putting large permaculture projects "into the Permaculture Institute. Then everybody receives the eternal right to that part of production in which they are interested." The designer "starts stacking in" -- vanilla orchids on palm trees, honey production, aquaculture, cattle grazing, bean crops.

"You would be surprised how many non-interfering overlays you can get on a site -- overlays that will hold families in good health while maintaining an unending alcohol production."

### Addressing the Rich

Mollison's approach to the wealthy is pragmatic:
- "The rich don't have anybody to tell them what to do"
- "They are not immune from acid rain; they are not immune from environmental disaster"
- "There is a high capacity for information gathering very rapidly"
- "This group can have it checked in maybe four days and get a high impact statement that is absolutely frightening"

The key argument: "There is no opposition in high echelons." Unlike activists who face resistance, the permaculture designer faces "complete acceptance, acceptance of a real situation with a methodology to which the investor can link."

### Why Cooperate with the Powerful

Mollison justifies this approach bluntly:
- "It's war, or it's cooperation. For me it's going to be cooperation because war doesn't work"
- "Opposition doesn't work. War replaces one lot of oppressors with another lot of oppressors"
- "We haven't 10 years to sort it out"
- "It doesn't worry me if the investor doubles his money, providing we can go on doing what we are doing"

### The Implementation Problem

Mollison identifies a critical bottleneck: "It's exactly at the level of implementation that you strike trouble, because underlings are in the sort of desperate financial position where they are always looking out for their own corner." This is why "the principals, rather than the underlings, who must become involved in large scale permaculture conversion."

"We need thousands of qualified permaculture designers, capable of handling the implementation and managerial aspects."

## Designer Economics (Pamphlet VIII)

### Charging for Work

Mollison provides specific guidance on professional fees:
- **Tie rates to local landscape architects** ($35/hour in 1981)
- **Normal job** (up to 40 acres): one day on site + typed, documented, illustrated report (15-20 pages)
- **Large jobs** (over 100 acres): quote on time basis, pre-design report first
- **Design cost**: 1.5% to 2% of total investment client will make
- **Property finding**: 0.5% of purchase price plus 1.5% on purchase
- **Land management**: $2,000-$5,000/year per property

### Building a Practice

Mollison advises new designers to:
- Conduct weekend workshops ("from which jobs will come")
- Write articles for local journals
- Start modestly, gain experience before advertising
- Offer free work "in the context of a paid job" to reduce travel costs
- Build a network of resource people (horticulture, architecture, agricultural engineering, realty)

### The Scholarship System

- One or two scholarship students per course
- Half of design course participants must be women
- If a scholarship student starts charging for work, they pay the fee to those who trained them
- Contact indigenous groups to inform them about courses

### Real Estate Relationships

"I believe at least 80% of the real estate agents support what we are doing." Mollison recommends printing hand-outs for real estate agents about permaculture, finding property with water on site, and watching for "multi-titled" properties suitable for community development.

### Land Management as Service

For clients who own land they don't live on, permaculture designers can offer management services -- "frequent visit proposition where your fares are paid, at a charge of perhaps only $5,000 a year." However, Mollison warns: "As fast as we train designers, we lose designers. If we act as managers, we will never have enough designers in the district!"

## The Nuclear Approach

Mollison consistently argues for concentrated rather than dispersed development:

"One gross error that we have all made is to try to occupy too much land, and not really develop any of this land we have."

"A casual glance around this place here will show you that at least 90% of the resources are un-used, 10% partly used, and less than 1% used in any effective way."

"If you care for it foot by foot, you are high and dry long before anybody that tackles it on the broad scale."

"Two acres is enough. It contains quite a variety of elements, elements that make life worth living, like the patch of apples, and the eggs from our chickens."
