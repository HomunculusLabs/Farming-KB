# Organic Fertilizer Sources and Biofertilizers

Organic fertilizers are derived from plant and animal by-products as well as mineral rock deposits. Unlike [[teaming-with-nutrients-natural-vs-synthetic-fertilizers]], they feed [[the-soil-food-web]] — the community of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and other organisms that creates soil structure and cycles nutrients to plants. Nutrients in all but the most soluble natural fertilizers are only available when they are mineralized (broken down into ionic forms) by soil microorganisms.

## Phosphorus Sources

### Animal Bone Meal (N–P–K 3–15–0)

Bone meal is produced by steam-processing and grinding animal bones. The phosphorus it contains is very readily available and lasts 1 to 4 months in the soil. However, bone meal does not work well as a phosphorus source unless soil pH is below 7 — at higher pH values, phosphorus becomes locked up and unavailable to plants. Some brands list the percentage of elemental phosphorus while others list phosphate (the standard convention), so label reading is important. Bone meal may attract animals for 1 to 2 weeks after application until the odor dissipates.

### Bat Guano (N–P–K 3–10–1)

Guano — the feces and urine of cave-dwelling bats — comes in both high-nitrogen and high-phosphorus forms. The high-phosphorus variety lasts 1 to 4 months. Mycorrhizal fungi are the best releasers of phosphorus from guano, but excessive phosphorus actually limits mycorrhizal presence, creating a paradox. Gardeners should go easy on guano and ensure their [[solomon-soil-testing-laboratory-analysis-low-input-gardening]] knows they rely on mycorrhizal fungi when developing recommendations. Unless [[starting-a-new-garden]] without preparation time, guano should only be used when a testing laboratory specifically recommends it as a phosphorus source.

### Colloidal Rock Phosphate (N–P–K 0–2.5–0)

Also called soft rock phosphate, this material consists of clay particles surrounded by phosphate. The clay component improves the soil's cation and anion exchange capacities (CEC and AEC). This is a very slow-release source — it remains available for 3 to 5 years depending on rainfall and watering. Some gardeners report that colloidal rock phosphate actually performs better in its second and third years as the phosphorus gradually becomes available. Placement is critical: rock phosphates work best when positioned where plant roots will directly intercept the particles.

### Crab Shell Meal (N–P–K 2–3–0)

A valuable but sometimes hard-to-find phosphorus source. In addition to nitrogen and phosphorus, crab shell meal contains abundant calcium [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]]. It also contains **chitin**, which makes it an excellent fungal food. Chitin has the added benefit of helping control high populations of nematodes in the soil.

## Potassium Sources

### Greensand (N–P–K 0–0–7)

Also known as New Jersey Greensand or glauconite, this mineral formed on ancient sea beds and contains more than thirty elements, including calcium, magnesium, iron, and other micronutrients alongside potassium. It requires weathering to release nutrients, making it slow-acting with a effective lifespan of 2 to 3 years.

### Wood Ashes (N–P–K 0–1–3)

Wood ashes (historically called "pot ash") have been used for centuries as a potassium source. They are free for anyone with a fireplace, but care must be taken: ash from treated charcoals or pressure-treated woods may contain harmful chemical residues. Wood ashes are alkaline and will raise soil pH, so they should not be applied to soils that are already alkaline. They last approximately 1 to 2 months.

### Sulfate of Potash (N–P–K 0–0–2)

Often labeled as Sul-Po-Mag, this mineral salt is highly water-soluble and contains 23% sulfur, 22% potash, and 11% magnesium. Some organic gardeners do not consider it fully organic because it does not add bulk organic matter to soils. Its high solubility makes it quick-acting, lasting only about 2 months.

## Calcium Sources

### Calcitic Limestone

Sourced from sedimentary rock containing calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), powdered limestone breaks down when exposed to water and carbon dioxide (which form a weak acid). Gardeners can typically raise soil pH by only about one point per growing season, making autumn application ideal for the following growing season.

### Dolomitic Limestone

In addition to calcium, dolomite (CaMg[CO₃]₂) provides approximately 10% magnesium. However, excess magnesium binds soils and can impact the uptake of other nutrients. Soil testing should confirm a magnesium deficiency before applying dolomitic limestone.

## Micronutrient Sources

### Shrimp Shell Meal (N–P–K 5–8–15)

Made from ground shrimp heads and shells, this meal contains abundant trace elements from ocean sources, plus approximately 15% calcium and 20% chitin. It functions as a slow-release fertilizer lasting 3 to 6 months.

### Kelp Meal, Powder, and Liquid

Kelp (a type of seaweed) contains up to sixty elements, including all trace minerals plants need, plus natural plant growth hormones. **Kelp meal** becomes available 1–2 months after application and decays over 4–5 months. **Kelp powder** is more finely ground and soluble, making nutrients available to microbes within about 1 month. **Liquid kelp** (typically enzymatically digested to preserve growth hormones) provides instantly available micronutrients lasting 1–3 weeks. While effective for hydroponics, many kelp nutrients may already be present in garden soil — trace minerals are essential but needed only in minute quantities. Sustainable harvesting practices should be verified.

## Biofertilizers

Biofertilizers are living organisms that produce or facilitate the production of plant nutrients. While they do not fit the legal definition of fertilizers (because their N–P–K content cannot be reliably quantified), they play critical roles in plant nutrition.

### Nitrogen-Fixing Bacteria

**Rhizobia and Frankia** are symbiotic nitrogen fixers. Rhizobia are well-established for legume cultivation (soybeans, locust trees, wisteria), while Frankia were discovered more recently and their use is still developing. Both exhibit incredible specificity between plant species and microbial strain — this must be considered when employing them.

**Azotobacter and Azospirillum** are free-living (non-symbiotic) nitrogen fixers frequently used with cereal crops. Researchers are developing garden-specific mixtures.

### Phosphate-Solubilizing Microorganisms

Certain bacteria (*[[bacillus-megaterium]]* var. *phosphaticum*, *B. subtilis*, *B. circulans*, *Pseudomonas striata*) — called **phosphobacterins** — can free insoluble phosphorus and make it available to plants. Similarly, fungi (*Penicillium* species, *Aspergillus awamori*) release organic acids during their metabolic processes that break the bonds tying up phosphorus in soil.

### Mycorrhizal Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots, exchanging carbon from plant exudates for nutrients scavenged from soil. Their hyphae extend root surfaces up to hundreds of times, dramatically increasing nutrient interception. Research suggests mycorrhizae may deliver approximately **80% of a plant's phosphorus, 60% of its copper, 25% of its nitrogen, 25% of its zinc, and 10% of its potassium**. Over 90% of plant species form mycorrhizal relationships in nature. This is why trees in forests thrive with so little care — their mycorrhizal partners handle the bulk of nutrient acquisition for free, both economically and in terms of labor. Garden practices that disrupt mycorrhizal networks (excessive tilling, high-phosphorus fertilizers, fungicides) undermine this natural [[solomon-fertigation-bucket-drip-water-nutrient-delivery-system]].

### Plant Growth-Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPR)

PGPR such as *Pseudomonas fluorescens* colonize root zones and aid nutrient synthesis. Their major studied function is assisting mycorrhizal fungi with phosphorus acquisition, possibly by helping break phosphate bonds. Some PGPR produce **alkaline phosphatase**, an enzyme that breaks down phosphate bonds.

### Compost and Earthworm Castings

**Compost** is technically a soil amendment rather than a fertilizer, but it provides a rich microbial environment for nutrient cycling. It must be fully composted — partially composted material can tie up nitrogen as decomposition completes alongside plants. Many home compost piles never reach full completion. Compost does contain nutrients because organic matter and any clay in the compost will have an impact on its CEC. Ideally, compost should be tested for both nutrient content and microbial populations so the gardener understands what is being applied and how it might affect soil laboratory recommendations.

**Earthworm castings** concentrate nutrients dramatically: they contain 10× more potassium, 5× more nitrogen, 7× more phosphorus, 3× more magnesium, 1.5× more calcium, and 1.4× more humus than the source soil. The worms ingest organic matter but are primarily after bacteria, protozoa, and fungi — they digest some and process the rest into castings with higher concentrations of organic matter and minerals than the original material.

## Manures

Livestock manures are technically not fertilizers either, but they have been used for millennia. In the modern age, manures must be carefully assessed for antibiotics, hormones, and medicines — these often do not break down during composting. The use of manures can lead to the buildup of salts and heavy metals. If not composted properly, manures can also be a source of *E. coli*. In the home garden, manures must be completely composted for at least 72 hours at a minimum of 131°F (55°C) before use. Despite these caveats, well-composted manure remains a valuable source of organic matter and slow-release nutrients.

## Chilean Nitrate (Sodium Nitrate)

Chilean nitrate is a natural nitrogen source mined from desert deposits. It contains approximately 26% sodium, which is acceptable in small quantities but problematic in soils that already have high sodium content — a common issue in deserts and dry regions. Sodium can also react with clay particles, making it difficult to build soil structure and aeration. Unlike most organic fertilizers, Chilean nitrate does not improve soil tilth (structure), so it should never be the sole source of nitrogen in a garden. Its best use is limited to starting a new garden or at the beginning of the season, especially in gardens where soil takes a long time to warm up.

## Choosing Between Fertilizer Sources

Selecting the right organic fertilizer depends on several factors:

- **Nutrient needs**: Always base decisions on soil test results rather than guesswork.
- **Release speed**: Quick-acting sources (fish emulsion, liquid kelp) for immediate needs; slow-release sources (rock phosphate, greensand) for long-term soil building.
- **Soil pH compatibility**: Bone meal requires pH below 7; wood ashes raise pH and should be avoided in alkaline soils.
- **[[soil-food-web]] health**: Prefer fertilizers that feed microbial communities (compost, meals, molasses) over highly soluble mineral salts.
- **Sodium sensitivity**: Avoid Chilean nitrate and other high-sodium inputs in arid climates or heavy clay soils.
- **Mycorrhizal dependence**: Be cautious with high-phosphorus inputs that can suppress mycorrhizal colonization — these fungi may supply the majority of a plant's phosphorus needs if left undisturbed.

## Maximizing Nutrient Availability

The decomposition of organic fertilizers into bioavailable ionic forms depends primarily on the activity of bacteria, Archaea, and fungi at the base of the soil food web. Key factors include:

- **Soil temperature**: Microbes that release nutrients are more active at warmer temperatures. Cold spring soils slow nutrient cycling, which is why starter solutions and quick-acting fertilizers are valuable early in the season.
- **Soil pH**: The right pH zone maximizes the number of unencumbered ions available to plants. Each nutrient has an optimal pH range for availability.
- **Soil moisture**: Adequate (but not excessive) moisture supports microbial activity and nutrient diffusion to root surfaces.
- **Soil testing**: The single most important practice. Test before setting up a feeding program, retest at end of season or start of next, and thereafter every 2–3 years, adjusting programs accordingly. Without testing, even the best organic inputs can create imbalances.
- **[[cation-exchange-capacity]] (CEC)**: Soils with higher CEC (clay and organic matter) hold nutrients longer and release them more gradually, reducing leaching losses.
