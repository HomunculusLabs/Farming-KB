# Killing Sod and Starting a New Garden

## Overview

Starting a new [[aphid-management-vegetable-garden-solomon]] almost always means converting a piece of land currently growing grass (often mixed with other low-growing plants) into productive garden beds. Grass creates an especially dense network of tough roots called **sod** that holds soil together firmly. Eliminating this sod and preparing the soil for planting is the essential first step in creating any new garden.

## Understanding Sod

Sod is resilient. Simply turning grass over once with a shovel will not kill it — grass is a survivor that will attempt to regrow from any remaining root fragments. The soil beneath sod will not break up into a workable seedbed after a single turning; it requires the sod to die and decompose first. This means the gardener must deliberately work to kill the grass, not just disturb it.

## Timing: When to Start

### Climate Considerations

- **Warm climates** (Deep South US, most of California, Australia): Sod can be killed and gardens started at any time of year when soil moisture is right
- **[[query-best-kiwifruit-varieties-for-cold-climates]]**: The best season is **late summer or early autumn** — not spring when gardening enthusiasm peaks. Autumn preparation allows winter frost and decomposition to help [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]] the sod, so the garden is ready to plant as soon as ground can be worked in spring

### Advantages of Autumn Preparation

1. **Winter decomposition**: Frost action and microbial activity break down sod over winter
2. **Weed seed exhaustion**: Many weed seeds germinate in autumn after soil disturbance; they're killed when you dig again in spring
3. **More spring weeds germinate**: Then get killed by the spring digging
4. **Immediate spring readiness**: No waiting period between soil preparation and planting
5. **Resistant grass clumps can be removed**: Any surviving sod chunks are easily spotted and tossed onto the compost heap in spring

## Pre-Soil Preparation

### Step 1: Mow the Grass

Mow as close to the ground as possible — scalp it right down to the growing points if your mowing equipment can handle it without damage. Keep mowing until you expose patches of nearly bare soil.

- **Short clippings**: Leave them in place; they'll decompose
- **Tall grass** (requiring a weed whacker, scythe, or sickle): Rake up and remove clippings for compost — your shovel won't cut through thick grass easily, and long clippings will tangle rototiller tines

### Step 2: Spread Amendments

Spread your basic soil amendments over the area:
- Lime (or [[complete-organic-fertilizer]])
- Manure and/or compost
- Any other amendments recommended for your soil type

These will be incorporated during the digging/tilling process.

### Step 3: Check Soil Moisture — The Ready-to-Till Test

This is critical. Do not attempt to dig or rototill unless soil moisture is correct:

1. Take a handful of soil and squeeze it into a ball about golf-ball size
2. Cradle the ball in your palm and press firmly with your thumb
3. **If it breaks apart easily and crumbles** → soil is ready to work
4. **If it's pliable and your thumb merely dents it** → too wet — wait
5. **If it won't form a ball** → either too dry or has no clay

**Consequences of ignoring this test**:
- **Too dry**: The ground is so hard that digging is nearly impossible and creates excessive clods
- **Too wet**: Digging creates rock-hard clods that may persist for months. Seeds won't germinate well and plants won't grow properly in cloddy soil

## Method 1: Rotary Cultivation (Rototilling)

### Equipment Recommendations

- **Rent a rear-tined tiller** of at least 7–8 horsepower
- The tiller **must have a functioning drag bar** to control tine depth
- Do not attempt to use a front-tined tiller or an underpowered machine for [[starting-new-garden-sod-elimination]]

### Tilling Technique

#### First Pass
1. Start at one end and make a straight pass down the **long axis** of the plot (long way reduces turning)
2. Set the drag bar so tines cut only **1 inch (2.5 cm) below the surface** — deeper setting causes the machine to skip and jump
3. Hold the first pass as straight as possible

#### Repeated Passes
4. Turn around and make another pass in the **opposite direction** on the same row
5. After 3–5 passes on the same row, you should see some soil and the grass will look damaged
6. If no soil is visible, the sod is too tough — drop down one notch on the drag bar and try again

#### Widening the Tilled Area
7. Move over **half the width of the tines** to the side
8. Continue the pattern: pass in one direction, then the opposite, gradually widening
9. When you reach the edges, swing around and work the other side of the first row

#### Depth Expectations
- **Walk-behind tiller**: Realistically achieves 4–5 inches (10–12 cm) depth
- **Large tractor with heavy rotary cultivator**: Perhaps 7 inches (18 cm) with an experienced operator
- **Not as deep as it appears**: Freshly tilled soil is fluffy and expanded; after a few days of settling, the actual tilled depth will be less

### The Cross-Tilling Pass

After completing the initial long-axis passes, make a second set of passes **perpendicular** (if you tilled east-west, now go north-south):

1. Use the same center-outward pattern
2. If the soil seems softer below the grass root layer, lift the drag bar a notch
3. Aim for 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) deeper than the first set of passes
4. If the tiller handle swings side to side (the soil is getting hard to cut), offset your body to compensate

### The Wisdom of Stopping

**Solomon's strongest advice**: After the first tilling (and optionally the cross-tilling), **stop and wait 3–7 days.** Let the sun and weather kill off the damaged grass before disturbing the soil further. Reasons:

1. If you till deeper immediately, you'll relocate grass fragments into moist soil where they can regrow
2. The first tilling has already done most of the good work: killed most grass and broken up the hardest surface compaction
3. From this point, hand tools (spade/shovel) can finish the job with less total effort
4. Your hands and ears need a break

### Understanding Capillarity

After tilling, Solomon recommends observing the tilled surface the next morning:

- **Immediately after tilling**: All soil appears damp
- **After the sun dries the surface**: The tilled area turns a lighter color
- **Next morning, before sun hits the surface**: Look at your footprints from tilling — they will be damp while surrounding soil is dry

This moisture movement is called **capillarity** — water moves upward through the narrow spaces between soil particles, similar to how a wick draws oil in a lamp. Understanding capillarity is crucial for:

- [[solomon-sprouting-seeds-and-early-season-nutrition]] effectively (moisture must rise to seed depth)
- Watering efficiently (less frequent, deeper watering encourages deep capillary rise)
- Managing soil surface conditions (mulch interrupts capillary rise and reduces evaporation)

## Method 2: Hand-Digging (Solomon's Preferred Method)

### Why Hand-Digging Is Better

Despite requiring more time spread over more days, hand-digging offers critical advantages over rototilling:

1. **No [[plow-pan]]**: Rototillers create a compacted layer at the bottom of their reach (a "tiller pan"). Hand-digging breaks through any existing plow pan
2. **Full-depth soil loosening**: A shovel reaches 12 inches (30 cm) deep, compared to a tiller's 4–5 inches
3. **Better [[soil-tilth-and-seedbed-preparation]]**: The resulting soil is finer and more crumbly
4. **Quieter, no fuel, no rental costs**: Just a sharp shovel and effort
5. **Root access**: Vegetable roots can penetrate deeply into properly dug soil

### The Plow Pan Problem

Any garden on former farmland likely has a **plow pan** — a compacted layer created by years of moldboard plowing. The plow's "sole" slides across unplowed soil about 7 inches (18 cm) below the surface, pressing down at the same depth every time. After decades, this creates a hard barrier that:

- Prevents roots from penetrating below 7 inches
- May persist for **half a century** after plowing stops
- Is made worse, not fixed, by rototilling (which adds a shallower tiller pan on top)
- **Can only be eliminated by hand-digging** with a shovel or [[spading-fork]]

Many vegetables ideally develop root systems 4 feet (120 cm) deep. Restricting them to 7 inches of topsoil means dramatically reduced growth and nutrition.
