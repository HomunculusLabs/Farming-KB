## Cremation Versus Natural Burial

Fukuoka contrasted natural burial with cremation as a critique of humanity's tendency to
disrupt natural processes. Man, "the [[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-order-food-chain-unity]]," douses corpses
with heavy fuel oil and burns them rather than returning them to the soil. Much is made
of sulfurous gases from crematorium chimneys, but the polluting effects on smaller
animals and plants are greater than the effects on humans.

If one accounts for the fuel oil mined, transported, and burned, plus antipollution
treatment of stack gases, cremation is neither fast nor clean. Simple burial or open
burial (exposure to the elements) may seem primitive, but these are the most sensible
and complete methods of disposal — they allow the full microbial succession to operate.

## The Self-Governing Balance of Microbial Populations

Fukuoka emphasized the astonishing reproductive power of microorganisms. One bacterium
such as *Escherichia coli*, multiplying unchecked with adequate food, temperature, and
humidity, could leave a mass of progeny equivalent to the total mass of all living
organisms on Earth within two to three days. If the self-governing mechanisms that
regulate bacterial reproduction ceased for just several days, the earth would become
a morass of bacterial remains.

Yet this never happens. The balance between multiplication and destruction, the
equilibrium between production and consumption, has carried on harmoniously for
untold millions of years. Nature has seen to both the growth and propagation of
organisms and to the treatment of their wastes simultaneously, without the least
hitch. This, for Fukuoka, is the proper measure of comparison between the powers
of man and those of nature.

## The Natural Order: An Interconnected Food Web

All living things belong to an endless food chain. Plants are fed upon by birds and
beasts, which become prey to other animals or succumb to disease and age. Wastes and
remains are broken down by microorganisms that proliferate and die, returning nutrients
to the soil for uptake by plants. The struggle for survival among microorganisms mirrors
that of animals: fungi that wrap mycelia around prey and kill by dissolution, bacteria
that secrete fungicidal substances, bacteriophages that kill bacteria, and viruses that
kill both bacteria and fungi.

The broader ecosystem follows the same pattern: spiders kill rice borers and leafhoppers,
mites kill spiders, predaceous mites feed on those mites, ladybugs feed on predaceous
mites, earwigs feed on ladybugs, centipedes eat earwig eggs, swallows feed on centipedes,
snakes eat small birds, and kites and dogs kill snakes. Bacteria and viruses attack all
of these in turn. The [[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol]]:** Spider mites appear and crawl over the fungal growth.
