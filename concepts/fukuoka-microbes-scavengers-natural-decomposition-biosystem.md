# Fukuoka on Microbes as Scavengers: Natural Decomposition and the Biosystem

**Source:** [[masanobu-fukuoka]], *The Natural Way of Farming* (1985)

## Overview

Fukuoka devoted significant attention to the role of microorganisms as nature's primary
recycling agents. He argued that microbes — bacteria, fungi, [[slime-molds]], and yeasts —
perform the essential work of decomposition that keeps the earth's surface clean and
fertile, and that human attempts to improve upon or accelerate these natural processes
through composting, [[natural-farming-microbial-inoculants]], and fermentation promoters are not only
unnecessary but counterproductive.

## The Corpse and the Four Pallbearers

Fukuoka opens his discussion of microbial scavenging with a darkly humorous observation:
farmers dread being caught loafing because they will be told, "Don't think that you can
live all by yourself. There are days of darkness too. When you die, you'll need the
services of four people." But in reality, disposing of a corpse requires far more than
four people — it requires countless microbes and small animals engaged in an assembly-
line operation of dismantling, decomposition, rotting, and fermentation.

To completely return a human body to the soil, billions upon billions of microorganisms
appear one after another, each making their final service to a human being. A person's
cells live on in children and grandchildren, continuing to multiply, while the body
gradually breaks down. After death, the corpse is decomposed as food by bacteria, so
one's ultimate form may be that of a microbial cell. Since the last to offer incense
to the departed soul are probably lactic [[lactic-acid-bacteria-knf]], a person vanishes as a sweet,
acrid aroma through lactic fermentation.

## Cremation Versus Natural Burial

Fukuoka contrasted natural burial with cremation as a critique of humanity's tendency to
disrupt natural processes. Man, "the [[fukuoka-textdoc-man-sole-heretic-natural-order]] in the [[fukuoka-textdoc-natural-order-food-chain-unity]]," douses corpses
with heavy fuel oil and burns them rather than returning them to the soil. Much is made
of sulfurous gases from crematorium chimneys, but the polluting effects on smaller
animals and plants are greater than the effects on humans.

If one accounts for the fuel oil mined, transported, and burned, plus antipollution
treatment of stack gases, cremation is neither fast nor clean. Simple burial or open
burial (exposure to the elements) may seem primitive, but these are the most sensible
and complete methods of disposal — they allow the full microbial succession to operate.

## The Self-Governing Balance of Microbial Populations

Fukuoka emphasized the astonishing reproductive power of microorganisms. One bacterium
such as *Escherichia coli*, multiplying unchecked with adequate food, temperature, and
humidity, could leave a mass of progeny equivalent to the total mass of all living
organisms on Earth within two to three days. If the self-governing mechanisms that
regulate bacterial reproduction ceased for just several days, the earth would become
a morass of bacterial remains.

Yet this never happens. The balance between multiplication and destruction, the
equilibrium between production and consumption, has carried on harmoniously for
untold millions of years. Nature has seen to both the growth and propagation of
organisms and to the treatment of their wastes simultaneously, without the least
hitch. This, for Fukuoka, is the proper measure of comparison between the powers
of man and those of nature.

## The Natural Order: An Interconnected Food Web

All living things belong to an endless food chain. Plants are fed upon by birds and
beasts, which become prey to other animals or succumb to disease and age. Wastes and
remains are broken down by microorganisms that proliferate and die, returning nutrients
to the soil for uptake by plants. The struggle for survival among microorganisms mirrors
that of animals: fungi that wrap mycelia around prey and kill by dissolution, bacteria
that secrete fungicidal substances, bacteriophages that kill bacteria, and viruses that
kill both bacteria and fungi.

The broader ecosystem follows the same pattern: spiders kill rice borers and leafhoppers,
mites kill spiders, predaceous mites feed on those mites, ladybugs feed on predaceous
mites, earwigs feed on ladybugs, centipedes eat earwig eggs, swallows feed on centipedes,
snakes eat small birds, and kites and dogs kill snakes. Bacteria and viruses attack all
of these in turn. The [[fukuoka-natural-ecosystem-food-chain-interconnection]] is an incredibly complex array of interdependently
linked organisms — none live separately, none simply die and are done with.

Crucially, Fukuoka insisted this must not be seen as mere competition or "the strong
eating the weak," but as "a united family of many members that live together in a single
harmony."

## The Rice Straw Decomposition Succession

Fukuoka provided a detailed account of the natural decomposition of a single piece of
rice straw dropped onto the earth, as a demonstration of nature's composting method:

1. **Insect attraction:** Straw draws flies and other insects that lay eggs, producing
maggots and larvae.
2. **Fungal colonization:** Rice blast, leaf blotch, and rot-causing fungi already on
the rice leaves spread rapidly over the straw.
3. **[[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol]]:** Spider mites appear and crawl over the fungal growth.
4. **Secondary microbial colonization:** Yeasts, blue mold, bread mold, and
*Trichoderma* fungi destroy the pathogens and begin decomposing the straw.
5. **Faunal succession:** Nematodes feed on fungi, bacteria feed on nematodes, mites
consume bacteria, predaceous mites feed on those mites, and spiders, ground beetles,
earwigs, mole crickets, and slugs join the community.
6. **[[dighton-fungal-succession-disturbance]]:** When fibrin-decomposing fungi exhaust their food, they are
supplanted by lipoid- and lignin-decomposing bacteria.
7. **Anaerobic phase:** Parasitism and cannibalism set in among aerobic bacteria,
which are gradually replaced by anaerobic bacteria.
8. **Final fermentation:** Lactic acid bacteria complete the process with lactic acid
fermentation, at which point all trace of the straw disappears.

This entire succession occurs over several days for a single piece of straw, involving
hundreds of species in a precisely orchestrated sequence.

## Why Composting Efforts Are Misguided

Fukuoka recounted his own early experiments at the Kochi Prefecture Agricultural Testing
Center, where he isolated beneficial bacteria from refuse and animal droppings to
prepare compost from straw and brush. He cultured many bacteria, fungi, [[bloomfield-slime-molds-myxomycetes-plasmodial-protists]], and
yeasts, then inoculated straw with each to observe rotting rates.

Later, he realized this work was "really quite worthless." Rather than isolating beneficial
microbes and inoculating straw with a "fermentation promoter," all he needed to do was
scatter a handful of chicken droppings or clumps of soil over the straw. This was not
only the quickest method — it also produced the most completely rotted compost.

His conclusion: "There is no call for making a lot of fuss over 'microbial' and
'enzymatic' [[cho-poultry-natural-farming-methods]]." Nature's methods are faster, more complete, and more
perfect than anything human intervention can achieve. Anything man does just disturbs
the rapid and perfect natural processes.

## The Loquat Botrytis Incident: Pesticides Disrupting Natural Balance

Fukuoka described a local incident where loquat orchards suffered apparent cold damage
to blossoms. Upon inspection, he found the flower corollas rotted with *Botrytis* spores
— not cold damage at all. The village organized a pesticide spraying program that brought
the organism under control.

But Fukuoka asked the deeper question: why had the outbreak occurred in the first place?
His theory was that it was triggered by the sudden postwar application of new pesticides
intended for citrus [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]]. These broad-spectrum chemicals had eliminated the
natural predators and competing microorganisms that previously kept *Botrytis* in check,
allowing it to flourish on the loquat blossoms. The "solution" (more spraying) addressed
the symptom while the cause (pesticide disruption of the biosystem) went unrecognized.

## The Ripple Effects of Pesticides

Fukuoka used the biosystem concept to argue that pesticides cast the biggest "stones"
into the natural order. Their ability to destroy specific pests is accompanied by almost
total ignorance of their ripple effects on the rest of the biological world. A disruption
in nature gives rise to an unending series of repercussions — unlike a factory where
damage consists of a broken gear, in nature each disturbance cascades through
interconnected food webs.
