# Avalanche and Snow Instability

Avalanches are among the most destructive natural forces in mountain and
highland environments. Understanding snow instability — its triggers, movement
modes, ecological indicators, and prevention strategies — is essential for
[[permaculture-design]] in cold-climate regions. Mollison emphasizes that
[[avalanche-phenomenon]]-prone areas can be identified through observation and that prevention
through strategic land use and vegetation management is far more effective than
reaction.

## Conditions for Avalanche Risk

Avalanche risk arises wherever two fundamental conditions are met:

- **Slope angle exceeding 6°** — even gentle slopes can produce slides when
  other instability factors compound.
- **Snow accumulation of 15 cm or more** — deeper snowpacks create greater
  mass and potential energy for release.

These conditions commonly occur on slopes and in valleys of steep foothills.

## Avalanche Triggers

### Wind and Weather Triggers

- **Strong winds** exceeding 13–18 km/sec can break the snow crust and
  initiate movement.
- **Sudden freeze followed by thaw** — rapid temperature swings weaken the
  cohesive structure of the snowpack.
- **Warm winds** that cause snowmelt destabilize buried layers.
- **Normal spring thaw** produces widespread instability as meltwater
  penetrates deeper snow layers.

### Structural Weakness in the Snowpack

When meltwater percolates over layers of buried hoarfrost, ice, or earth, it
lubricates the boundary and weakens the entire snow mass until slides become
inevitable.

### Human and Animal Triggers

At critical instability, remarkably small disturbances can trigger an avalanche:
a **gunshot**, **collapse of a snow cornice**, or even a **skier**,
**antelope**, or **rabbit** moving across the snowpack.

### Moisture Condensation

Approximately **80% of all avalanches** occur where old compact or dry snow is
dislodged by condensation of moisture and rain from air with **>70% humidity**.
The remaining 20% result from gusty winds or meltwater lubrication (midday to
3 PM). **Sudden snowfalls of 50–69 cm followed by cloud-free weather** create
acute instability through rapid loading under clear-sky radiative cooling.

## Avalanche Effects and Impact

Avalanches pick up snow, mud, boulders, clay, and vegetation as they descend.
However, it is often the **high-pressure air wave** preceding and advancing
beyond the avalanche front that causes the most destruction — sometimes
carrying on up the opposite slope after the debris stops.

- **Compressed air blast** travels at approximately **330 m/sec**
- **Snowdust avalanches** produce waves of **10–100 m/sec at 35 atmospheres**
- **Direct impact** ranges from **4 to 54 tonnes per square metre**

## Snow Movement Modes

Snow movement exists on a spectrum of scale and speed:

| Mode | Speed | Description |
|------|-------|-------------|
| **Microcreep** | Few cm/day | Slow, imperceptible downhill drift |
| **Slip / Snow Boards** | Metres/day | Coherent slabs slide on underlying surfaces |
| **Snow Mound** | Variable | Accumulation from rocky uplands |
| **Snow Bodies** | Variable | En masse movement of large snow volumes |
| **Snow Slides** | 4–5 m/sec | Can be arrested by terraces |
| **Avalanches** | Up to 6.5 m/sec | Episodic catastrophic events, every 3–5 years |

Terracing can arrest slides at intermediate speeds, but full avalanches
require comprehensive prevention.

## Tree Indicators of Avalanche Paths

Avalanche chutes leave unmistakable signatures in vegetation:

- **Downslope lean** — trees bent downhill by repeated snow pressure and
  debris impact.
- **Lack of lower limbs** — branches stripped from the upslope side by
  passing avalanches.
- **Bare trains of pioneer species** such as **aspen** delineating chutes
  where climax forest cannot establish.
- **Blown-down forests** radiating outward from avalanche snouts at
  termination zones.

**Rock avalanches** produce similar effects, persisting **20–30 years** in
coniferous forests — providing a long-term record of past events.

## Prevention Strategies

Mollison advocates a prevention-first approach. Key strategies include:

- **Preservation of high-slope forests** — mature forests anchor snowpack,
  intercept moisture, and resist snow movement. The death of steep-slope
  forests through acid rain damage is a growing threat.
- **Tunnels** for essential services — routing roads and utilities through
  tunnels protects infrastructure and maintains access during slide events.
- **V-shaped barriers** — erected to split the avalanche front, dissipating
  its energy and reducing the force of the descending mass.
- **Snow barriers on plateaus** — placed at accumulation zones to prevent
  snow buildup from reaching [[critical-mass]] on steep slopes.
- **Prevention of settlement in high-risk areas** — the simplest and most
  effective measure.

Avalanche areas are often mapped, and warnings issued when conditions are
predictable locally. Integrating this knowledge into site selection is
fundamental [[permaculture]] practice.

## Permafrost

In high-latitude and high-altitude environments, [[permafrost]] adds complexity to
snow and land stability:

- **Extent** — occupies **47–50%** of Canada, Russia, and Greenland.
- **Peaty surfaces** insulate and prevent underlying permafrost from melting.
- **Disturbance** — clearing, fire, or overgrazing can cause rapid thaw to
  **2–45 m deep**, with severe consequences for stability and drainage.
- **Fossil permafrost** extends to **400 m thick** in some regions.
- **Thaw rates** — earth heat reduces thickness by ~**1 cm/year**, but
  **solar thaw is 5,000× more effective** when the protective peat is removed.

Preserving peat and vegetative cover is critical. Disturbance triggers a
positive feedback loop: thaw exposes dark soil, which absorbs more solar
radiation, accelerating further thaw and potentially catastrophic ground
failure.

## Design Implications

1. **Read the landscape** — tree form and species distribution reveal avalanche
   history.
2. **Preserve slope forests** — the primary natural defense against snow
   instability.
3. **Design for worst-case scenarios** — use tunnels, barriers, and terracing.
4. **Respect permafrost** — avoid disturbing insulating peat layers.
5. **Settle wisely** — never place permanent structures in avalanche paths or
   permafrost-thaw zones.
