# Grafting and Budding Fruit Trees

Grafting and budding are essential propagation skills in Bill
Mollison's Permaculture: A [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-animal-systems-in-permaculture]] for establishing and
maintaining diverse fruit tree systems. These techniques allow growers
to combine desirable fruiting characteristics (scion) with adapted
root systems (rootstock), enabling the replication of superior varieties
and the creation of multi-grafted trees that extend harvest seasons and
ensure pollination.

## Why Graft? The Role of Rootstock and Scion

Mollison explains that most comparison [[fukuoka-pruning-fruit-tree-natural-management]] do not grow "true to type" from
seed. A seed from a delicious apple will produce a tree with unknown
fruit quality. Grafting solves this by attaching a cutting (scion) from
a known variety to a rooted plant (rootstock). The rootstock provides:

- Adaptation to local lowenfels [[lowenfels-aerobic-anaerobic-soil-conditions]] (heavy clay, sandy, wet, dry)
- Disease and pest resistance (especially to soil-borne pathogens)
- Size control (dwarf, semi-dwarf, or standard tree)
- Precocity (earlier bearing in dwarf rootstocks)
- Cold hardiness or [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]]
- Anchorage and vigor

The scion determines [[old-fruit-variety-selection]], quality, flavor, ripening time,
and storage characteristics. The combination of rootstock and scion
allows permaculture designers to match trees precisely to their
 design,
allowing rapid conversion of seedling trees to named varieties without
waiting years for new plantings to bear.

## T-Budding

T-budding (shield budding) is the most widely used budding technique.
A T-shaped cut is made in the bark of the rootstock during active
growth (when bark slips easily). A single bud from the desired variety,
cut with a thin shield of bark and a sliver of wood, is inserted under
the bark flaps of the T-cut and wrapped securely. The bud remains
dormant through winter and grows out the following spring.

T-budding advantages include:

- High success rate (80-95% with proper technique)
- Efficient use of scion material (one bud per scion piece)
- Performed during summer when propagation work is otherwise slow
- Suitable for a wide range of fruit species

T-budding is typically done in mid-summer (June-August in village-development-in-permaculture projects.

## Chip Budding

Chip budding is similar to T-budding but uses a chip of wood with
the bud rather than a shield of bark. A rectangular chip of bark and
wood is removed from the rootstock, and a matching chip bearing the
bud from the scion is fitted into the space. This technique works
when bark is not slipping (early spring or late summer) and on species
with thin or brittle bark where T-budding is difficult.

Chip budding is Mollison's recommended technique for species like
walnut, pecan, and avocado that are challenging to graft by other
methods. It can be done over a longer season than T-budding.

## Rootstock Selection

Choosing appropriate rootstock is critical to tree performance.
Mollison emphasizes selecting rootstock adapted to local conditions:

- **Apple:** Malling and Malling-Merton series; MM106 for semi-dwarf
  trees, M9 for dwarf trees, MM111 for poor soils
- **Pear:** Quince rootstocks (Quince A for semi-dwarf, Quince C
  for dwarf); seedling pear for large trees and poor soils
- **Stone fruits:** Seedling rootstocks are common; specific clonal
  rootstocks exist for peach, plum, and cherry
