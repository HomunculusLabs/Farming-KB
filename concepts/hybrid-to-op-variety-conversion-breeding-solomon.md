## Why Convert Hybrids to Open-Pollinated Varieties

Commercial growers overwhelmingly prefer hybrids for perfect uniformity and higher yields. The economic equation is straightforward: higher seed cost is more than offset by [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]] field. The harvest from this field is then mislabeled with the heirloom variety name. This practice is why many "heirlooms" produced by mass-market growers have nothing in common with the original variety beyond a superficial resemblance. The home gardener buys what they believe is a treasured family variety, but receives a degraded or entirely different variety that may perform poorly.

## Commercial Seed Quality Factors

Four factors define high-quality seed, and Solomon argues all four are equally important for home gardening:

1. **Genetic purity**: The variety must be what it claims to be, not contaminated by off-types from cross-pollination or mechanical mixing during harvesting and processing.
2. **Genetic uniformity**: Plants should mature uniformly, hold well without bolting, and meet commercial grade standards consistently across the population.
3. **High germination percentage**: Seed must sprout rapidly and completely under normal conditions. Low germination indicates weak seed that produces inferior plants even when it does germinate.

He overwintered the hybrid carrot roots through the cold months and planted them out in spring, expecting them to flower and set seed normally. The flowers that formed produced not a single viable seed — the self-sterility had carried through completely, rendering the plants functionally sterile in isolation.

Was the anti-hybridist proven correct that hybrid seed represents a dead end for seed savers? Following Carol Deppe's suggestion, the next year Solomon again planted out the overwintered roots, but this time included a row of an OP carrot variety nearby to serve as a pollen source. The result was dramatically different: both rows set abundant good seed. The proximity of the OP pollen source overcame the self-sterility barrier by providing compatible pollen that the otherwise self-sterile plants could accept.

After a few generations of selection from this cross, Solomon had a quite uniform OP variety that was nearly as productive as the original hybrid Miracle carrot. He notes that a professional breeder with unlimited time could have developed a dozen rather similar varieties from those initial crosses, but even without professional resources, a dedicated home gardener can achieve meaningful results within four to eight generations of patient selection.

## Key Principles for Successful Variety Conversion

**Start with adequate plant numbers.** This is the single most common amateur mistake and the primary cause of variety failure. Working from too small a gene pool — too few parent plants — almost always results in variety degradation within two generations. A minimum population of 20 to 50 plants is recommended for most outcrossing species, and more for species with strong self-incompatibility mechanisms.

**Maintain rigorous selection pressure every generation.** Carefully evaluate each plant and remove off-types before they can contribute pollen or seed to the next generation. Select for vigor, disease resistance, earliness, yield, flavor, and any other traits the original hybrid was valued for. Inconsistent selection leads to rapid genetic deterioration.

**Allow sufficient generations for stabilization.** Converting a variety typically takes four to eight generations. The first two to three generations show the most dramatic changes as the self-sterility breaks down and genetic recombination sorts itself into stable combinations.

**Use an appropriate pollen source for the first generation.** When the F1 generation fails to set seed in isolation, grow it alongside a compatible OP variety. Choose one with complementary rather than competing traits.

**Document everything meticulously.** Keep records of which plants were selected, germination rates, and observed variability to guide future selection decisions.

## The Ethical Dimension: Misrepresented Hybrids

Solomon cautions that some seed sold as "hybrid" at premium prices may actually be OP seed. He observed this with Kolibri F1 purple kohlrabi: 100 plants produced seed that, when replanted, grew as uniform, productive, and tasty as the F1 — biologically impossible with true hybrid seed. Given that hybrid tomato seeds may cost twenty or more times what OP varieties cost, the economic incentive for deception is substantial.
