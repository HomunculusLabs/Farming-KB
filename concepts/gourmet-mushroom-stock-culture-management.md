# Gourmet Mushroom Stock Culture Library Management

The gourmet mushroom stock culture management is the foundation of any mushroom cultivation operation. It is the repository of pure mushroom strains from which all downstream production originates. Paul Stamets emphasizes that proper management of stock cultures is "the most critical element in the successful cultivation of mushrooms," as the genetic quality of the strain determines the potential of every crop that follows.

## Importance of Young Cultures

Spawn created from young cultures (low passage number) gives rise to more highly productive mycelium than spawn from old, extensively subcultured strains. The difference in productivity between a P2 culture (two transfers from origin) and a P20 culture can be dramatic. Maintaining a robust library of young backup cultures is essential for long-term commercial success.

## The Stamets P-Value System

The Stamets P-Value (SPV) is a simple arithmetic scale for measuring the expansion of mycelium through successive transfers from its origin. Each transfer of mycelium from one petri dish to the next represents one cell division event and is numbered sequentially:

- **P1**: First pure culture isolated from the wild specimen or spore germination
- **P2**: Mycelium transferred from P1 dish to a new dish
- **P3, P4, etc.**: Subsequent transfers

### How to Label Cultures

A typical culture dish label reads:
```
FVITC P2
11/16/92
C # 0825905
```
This means: Flammulina velutipes (Enoki), isolated from Telluride, Colorado, collection date August 19, 1990 (5th group found that day), currently at P2, inoculated onto medium on November 16, 1992.

### P-Value Guidelines

- **For commercial production**: Maintain cell lines within P1-P10 (10 successive transfers maximum)
- **Backup strategy**: Keep 10-20 backup culture slants at low P-values
- **Strain longevity**: Stamets has strains 10+ years old kept at P2 or P3 that still regenerate successfully
- **Species-specific sensitivity**: Morels are particularly susceptible to degeneration, losing ability to form micro-sclerotia in as few as 6-7 plate transfers. Many strains of Morels, [[stamets-cultivator-log-cultivation-shiitake-hericium]], and [[king-stropharia-wine-cap-cultivation]] express mutations when transferred beyond P10

## Mycelium Types and Characteristics

Each mushroom species produces recognizable types of mycelium. Understanding these types helps cultivators assess strain health and predict fruiting potential. The standard media is Malt-Yeast Agar (MYA), often fortified with peptone (MYPA).

### 1. Linear Mycelium

Arranged as diverging, longitudinal strands emanating from the center of the petri dish:
- **Species**: [[stamets-cultivator-log-cultivation-shiitake-hericium]] (Lentinula edodes), initially Oyster (Pleurotus ostreatus)
- **Morels**: Produce rapidly growing, finely linear mycelium that thickens with age; initially nearly invisible, detected only by tilting the dish to see fine strands on the agar's reflective sheen

### 2. Rhizomorphic Mycelium

Often called "ropey"; composed of braided, twisted strands of varying diameters:
- **Significance**: Rhizomorphic mycelium supports primordia formation. Its presence indicates a vigorous, fruiting-capable strain
- **Species**: [[medicinal-mushroom-guide-lions-mane]] (Hericium erinaceus), [[king-stropharia-wine-cap-cultivation]] (Stropharia rugoso-annulata), [[agaricus-bisporus-cultivation-biology]] (Agaricus spp.)
- **Selection tip**: Selecting rhizomorphic zones for transfer encourages continued rhizomorphic growth
- **Warning**: Disappearance of rhizomorphs indicates loss of vigor

### 3. Cottony (Tomentose) Mycelium

Looks like tufts of cotton, nearly aerial in growth:
- **Species**: Many Oyster strains (Pleurotus spp.), Shaggy Manes (Coprinus comatus), Hen-of-the-Woods (Grifola frondosa)
- **Significance**: When a rhizomorphic strain degenerates with age, cottony formations typically take over. This is a sign of declining vigor

### 4. Zonate Mycelium

Cottony mycelium showing concentric circles of dense and light growth:
- **Cause**: Natural circadian cycles of growth, even under constant temperature incubation
- **Species**: Commonly seen in [[ganoderma-lucidum-reishi-comprehensive-profile]], Hypholoma, and Hypsizygus
- **Pattern**: Newest mycelium (periphery) is light-colored; older mycelium (center) is strongly pigmented

### 5. Matted or Appressed Mycelium

Dense, compressed growth:
- **Species**: Characteristic of [[ganoderma-lucidum-reishi-comprehensive-profile]] (Ganoderma lucidum) after two weeks on 2% malt extract agar
- **Density**: So dense that a factory-sharpened surgical blade cannot cut through it; tears off in ragged sheaths
- **Warning sign**: Cultures that die mysteriously often have matted mycelium with a flat, highly reflective surface (different from healthy dense mycelium)

### 6. Powdered Mycelium

Best exemplified by Chicken of the Woods (Laetiporus sulphureus):
- **Characteristic**: Breaks apart with minimal disturbance
- **Hazard**: In front of a laminar flow bench, sterile wind can cause chains of hyphae to become airborne, causing cross-contamination

### 7. Unique Formations

Special structures on the mycelial mat surface:
- **Hyphal aggregates**: Ball-like or shelf-like structures. Often evolve into primordia. Favorable for selecting rapidly fruiting strains of [[stamets-cultivator-log-cultivation-shiitake-hericium]]
- **Coremia**: Broom-like bundles of spores (asexual structures). Typify Pleurotus cystidiosus, P. abalonus, and P. smithii. Often mistaken for contamination by mycologists unfamiliar with dimorphic Oyster species
- **Sclerotia**: Dense, spherical formations. Typical of Morchella angusticeps (brightly colored and abundant) and M. esculenta/M. crassipes (dull colored and sparse)

## Strain Degeneration

Healthy strains follow a predictable decline pattern:

1. **Healthy**: Rhizomorphic appearance, good growth rate, forms primordia readily
2. **Early decline**: Culture sectors, forming diverging "fans" of linear, cottony, and appressed mycelium
3. **Active mutation**: Aerial tufts of cotton-like growth; central zone sends forth mutation-ridden chains of cells
4. **Failure**: Loss of rhizomorphs, slowing growth, inability to fruit, susceptibility to parasitic bacteria, eventual death

### Warning Signs of Declining Strains

- Loss of ability to form volunteer primordia on agar media
- Growth rate deceleration
- Development of mycelium with aerial tufts
- Sectoring (diverging fans of different growth types)
- Anemic, slow growth incapable of fruiting
- Green mold contamination susceptibility

### Mitigation Strategies

- Keep cultures at low P-values through backup slants
- Add extracted end-substrates (sawdust, straw) to agar media to maintain enzymatic systems
- Only 1-3 grams of substrate per liter of nutrient medium recommended
- High-endospore substrates (manures, soils) should be boiled for at least one hour before adding to media
- Store backup slants in refrigeration
- Use cryogenic storage (liquid nitrogen at -302 F / -196 C) for long-term preservation

### Species-Specific Notes

- **Volvariella volvacea** (Paddy Straw Mushroom): Should NOT be chilled. Exposure below 45 F (7.2 C) causes drastic die-back. Store at minimum 50 F (10 C). Poor recovery from both refrigeration and liquid nitrogen storage
- **Morels**: Particularly susceptible to degeneration. M. angusticeps loses sclerotia-forming ability in 6-7 transfers
- **Lion's Mane**: Relatively slow mycelial growth on agar; may require liquid culture techniques for efficient spawn production

## Cryogenic Storage

Liquid nitrogen storage at -302 F (-196 C) is the best long-term preservation method:
- Cultures preserved in ampules or test tubes of liquid cryoprotectant
- For large strain collections, cryoprotectant storage is more space-efficient than grain media
- Most species recover well from cryogenic storage
- Exception: Volvariella volvacea should not be frozen
