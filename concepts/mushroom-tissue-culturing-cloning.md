## Mushroom Tissue Culturing and Cloning

Tissue culturing, commonly referred to as cloning, is the process of extracting a small sample of living tissue from a mushroom fruitbody and transferring it to an agar medium, where it reverts from its differentiated fruiting state back into its vegetative mycelial state. This produces a culture that is genetically identical to the parent fungus, allowing cultivators to propagate desirable strains with predictable characteristics.

## Selecting Tissue for Cloning

The best tissue for cloning comes from the **interior of the mushroom cap**, which is naturally protected from environmental contaminants. Exterior tissue is typically colonized by spores, molds, and bacteria that will contaminate cultures. When cracking a mushroom open from top to bottom, the cultivator should look for tissue that is:

- **Clean and fluffy** — showing healthy, viable mycelium
- **Relatively dry** — tissue that is too wet will not clone well
- **Free of holes or discolorations** — these may indicate insect infestation or contamination

Tissue density varies from the tip of the cap down to the base where it attaches to the substrate. Different areas should be inspected to identify the best-looking tissue for excision.

## Wet Tissue and Field Collections

If a mushroom found in the field is too wet for immediate cloning, it should be brought home and allowed to air-dry for a day or more. During this drying period, a [[spore-print]] should also be taken as a backup in case the tissue culture fails. Some mushrooms can be cloned up to a year after harvest by dehydrating the fruitbodies and refrigerating them, which maintains dryness while preserving tissue viability.

If water visibly comes to the surface when cutting or squeezing tissue, the specimen is too wet and will not clone successfully. Progress of drying can be checked by cracking the cap open slightly to inspect interior tissue.

## Handling Difficult or Dirty Specimens

When a mushroom is extremely small, deteriorating, or has little viable tissue, the cultivator must proceed with the best available sample, potentially employing [[challenge-microorganisms-microwave-surface-sterilization]]:

1. **Rinse** the specimen with clean water several times
2. **Soak** in 3% [[cervantes-hydrogen-peroxide-sterilization]] for 1–4 hours in a sealed plastic bag under refrigeration (which prevents rotting)
3. **Air-dry** in the lab to reduce water content
4. When almost dry but still fresh, **wipe the outer surface** (cap) with alcohol to prep before cracking open
5. **Excise** tissue in front of the [[laminar-flow-hood]]

The goal is to remove as much debris as possible outside the lab, cleaning and cutting the specimen into a fairly clean chunk that can be wiped down before making a transfer.

## Cloning Polypores and Nongilled Mushrooms

Polypores (bracket fungi and conks) generally lack good, viable interior tissue that readily transfers to agar in the way that fleshy-gilled mushrooms do. Strategies for these species include:

- Taking tissue from the **base of the mushroom** where it attaches to its substrate
- **Rinsing or soaking** brackets and conks in hydrogen peroxide for several hours before attempting to crack them open
- Cutting into partially colonized wood debris at the attachment point, which may contain viable mycelium

For particularly finicky nongilled mushrooms, an effective technique is to submerge the entire cap underwater (with only the attachment point exposed) in a sealed container. The lack of oxygen forces the mycelium to grow upward and out of the attachment point, searching for air. Once the mycelium has fuzzed up at the attachment point, it can be snipped and used for cloning.

Another approach for rotten or unsuitable tissue is to cut into the colonized deadwood the mushroom grew on, chop it into small bits, cover with 3% hydrogen peroxide in a sealed container, and refrigerate overnight before transferring to agar. This method carries higher contamination risk and requires daily monitoring.

## Transferring Tissue Samples to Agar

The steps for transferring tissue to agar are similar to spore streaking, with a few key differences:

1. Sterilize a scalpel or dental pick in the flame of an alcohol lamp
2. Dip the tool into agar to cool it
3. Use the tip to make a **small cut or depression** in the agar surface
4. **Insert the tissue sample into the cut or depression** — this maximizes surface contact with the medium and prevents the tissue from drying out as it reverts to its mycelial state
5. Proceed with standard plating procedures

Multiple tissue samples can be placed on a single plate (spread out) to increase chances of success and conserve plates, though this slightly raises contamination risk. The plates should be inspected frequently, and healthy mycelium can be sectored to fresh plates once it takes.

## Preventing Spore Contamination from Fresh Specimens

If a mushroom is fresh and actively producing spores, bringing it into the lab risks contaminating other cultures. Alternatives include:

- Excising tissue outside the lab and transferring it to a petri dish before bringing it inside
- Performing a **still-air transfer** outside the lab, which may produce some peripheral contamination from mold spores but carries less risk than introducing a potential spore source into the clean room

## Rescuing Cultures from Mold or Bacteria

### Mold Contamination

Molds appear as green, black, yellow, or brown circular colonies. To rescue mycelium from a contaminated plate:

1. Set up a **still-air workstation** (a glove box or room with no airflow) — never open contaminated plates near the laminar flow hood
2. Disinfect the entire work area including walls
3. Transfer tools and supplies (alcohol lamp, fresh petris in zippered bags, contaminated plate in its own bag) into the workstation
4. Slowly remove the lid from the contaminated plate and carefully retrieve healthy-looking mycelial tissue, avoiding all contamination
5. Transfer to a fresh agar plate, seal it, and place in a fresh ziplock bag
6. Dispose of the contaminated culture in a separate sealed bag
7. Monitor the rescue plate closely; additional rescue transfers may be needed

### Bacterial Contamination

Bacteria appear as irregularly shaped, cloudy, or clear growths that are smooth and shiny. They are less problematic than molds — contaminated plates can often be opened inside the lab with minimal risk. However, mycelium may purposefully harbor "hitchhiking bacteria" that help metabolize waste products in natural ecosystems.

The **[[antibiotic-sandwich]] technique** can purify bacteria-contaminated mycelial cultures:

1. Pour **antibiotic-supplemented agar plates**
2. Place the contaminated mycelium on one side
3. Cut a large wedge of antibiotic agar and flip it over on top, sandwiching the culture between agar layers
4. The mycelium drills through the agar while bacteria are left behind (unable to traverse the antibiotic-laced medium)
5. Purified mycelium fuzzes up on the outside and can be transferred to a fresh plate

### Antibiotic Agar Formula (Dr. Bob Blanchette, University of Minnesota)

**Base (autoclave 20 minutes, cool to 113–122°F / 45–50°C):**
- 1,000 ml distilled/deionized water
- 15 g malt extract
- 15 g agar
- 2 g [[marmite-yeast-extract-straw-substrate]]
- 0.06 g Benlate (50% wettable powder — a fungicide)

**Add after cooling (heat-sensitive):**
- 0.01 g [[streptomycin-sulfate]]
- 2 ml lactic acid

Cultures should be screened through one or two antibiotic transfers before switching back to antibiotic-free medium.

## Sectoring: Separating and Selecting Strains

When spore streak plates produce a "mixed" culture of the same species, different strains develop as islands of mycelium with distinct morphology, climate preferences, metabolic requirements, and growth rates. Key characteristics of strain separation:

- Mycelium will **not fuse** with non-identical mycelium — where colonies collide, they build walls and develop thickening lines of division
- Colonies compete like a race, with some surging ahead and others falling behind
- More aggressive colonies may make better candidates for expansion

**To sector a promising strain:** Excise a small bit of the outermost mycelium (hyphal tips) and transfer to a fresh agar plate. Once transferred, even and symmetrical growth is a strong indicator of successful strain purification.
