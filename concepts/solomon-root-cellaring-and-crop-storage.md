# Solomon on Root Cellaring and Crop Storage

From [[solomon-gardening-when-it-counts-index]] by Steve Solomon.

## Why Root Cellaring Matters

To keep a body in robust health you must feed it a sizeable amount of fresh food, preferably raw. The quality of canned and frozen foods has been massively reduced, especially if they were blanched during processing. In cold-winter climates, it is possible to store fresh vegetables and fruit in living condition for many months without electricity, using root cellaring.

Imagine having fresh vegetables throughout the winter: bins of root vegetables, your own cabbage, Brussels sprouts on the stalk in midwinter, or sprouting your own Belgian endive (an expensive delicacy when store-bought).

## Root Cellar Requirements

Most traditional homes had basements suitable for root cellaring. Today, options include:
- Wall off and highly insulate a part of an existing basement for a winter food-storage cellar
- Dig a cellar outside (requires permits only for structural safety)
- Recycle chest freezers into small root-storage compartments
- Use an unheated outbuilding (sufficient for potatoes and apples in moderate climates)

The key environmental factors:
- **Temperature**: Cool but above freezing; 32-40 degrees F (0-4 degrees C) is ideal for most root crops
- **Humidity**: High humidity (85-95%) prevents roots from shriveling; lower humidity is better for onions, garlic, and squash
- **Ventilation**: Some airflow prevents mold and ethylene buildup
- **Darkness**: Prevents potatoes from turning green and onions from sprouting

## Storage Methods by Crop Type

### Root vegetables (carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips, rutabagas)
- Store in slatted bins
- If the vegetable has a tendency to dry out, pack in damp sand, sawdust, or leaves
- Can also be housed in barrels or large trash bins with damp packing material
- Carrots in mild-winter climates (like Cascadia) can be stored in the ground under plastic sheeting and straw insulation

### Leafy crops (cabbage, Brussels sprouts, endive)
- Transplant into the cellar with roots in moist soil before hard freezes
- Brussels sprouts can be stored on the stalk in a cool cellar
- Cabbages and heads of endive blanch during storage, becoming milder and more tender

### Potatoes
- Need cool, dark, moderately humid conditions
- Avoid temperatures below 40 degrees F -- starch converts to sugar
- Store in slatted bins or mesh bags for airflow
- Keep in darkness to prevent greening (solanine formation)
- In mild climates, an unheated outbuilding may be sufficient

### Squash and pumpkins
- Need warmer, drier conditions than root vegetables: 50-55 degrees F, 50-70% humidity
- Cure winter squash for a few weeks after harvest to harden the skin
- Hang in mesh bags or arrange in single layers (not touching) on shelves

### Onions and garlic
- Need cool, dry, well-ventilated conditions: 32-40 degrees F, 60-70% humidity
- Hang in mesh bags or braided strings
- Any moisture causes rotting

## Overwintering Biennial Seed Stock

The only way people in snow country can make seed for most biennial crops is to overwinter the plants. This was done extensively on a commercial scale before the hybrid seed industry developed.

Process:
1. Dig up plants carefully in late autumn before hard freezes, preserving as much root as possible
2. Store in a bed of damp soil in the root cellar
3. In spring, transplant back outside
4. Plants will flower and set seed after experiencing the vernalization (cold treatment)

This method was standard for cabbage seed production in Denmark and other northern European regions. In milder winters, some biennials may survive unprotected if buried under enough soil and mulch.

## Recommended Resource

Root Cellaring by Mike and Nancy Bubel is an excellent book on this subject that Solomon recommends.

## Harvest Timing

- Dig root crops just before or after the first light frosts but before serious freezing begins
- Shake earth from roots; remove damaged or diseased specimens
- For leafy crops going into storage, transplant before the ground freezes

## The Broader Context

Solomon notes that he has spent most of his adult life in climates where winter storage was not essential -- in the tropics and subtropics, vegetables grow 12 months a year. But for self-sufficient homesteaders in temperate climates, root cellaring is one of the most important food preservation skills, requiring no electricity or processing and maintaining food quality far better than canning or freezing.
