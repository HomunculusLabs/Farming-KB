# Earth-Shaping and Water Management in the Tropics

Earth-shaping (earthworks) is a fundamental component of permaculture design in tropical environments, where the relationship between water, soil, and slope determines the success or failure of agricultural systems. The intense rainfall characteristic of humid and monsoon tropics creates both tremendous opportunities for water harvesting and significant risks of erosion and nutrient loss. Properly designed earthworks capture, store, and distribute water while building soil and preventing degradation.

## Swale Systems on Gentle Slopes

On level ground or gentle slopes (2–8°) in the wet-dry tropics, a series of large contour banks or swales has an excellent soil preservation effect. Swales are level trenches dug on contour, with the excavated soil piled on the downhill side to form a berm. When properly implemented, they provide multiple simultaneous benefits:

- **Soil preservation**: Contour swales intercept runoff water, allowing it to infiltrate into the soil rather than carrying topsoil downhill
- **Water retention**: Wet-season water is held in the landscape, available for dry-season use by plants
- **Terrace development**: Over time, swales facilitate the gradual development of productive terraces
- **Mulch production**: Hedgerows planted on swale berms provide continuous mulch material

### Integrated Swale Design

The swale system integrates with hedgerow plantings to create a stable, productive landscape. Between main hedges, mulch hedgerows and borders can be developed in crop, or terraces can be flooded seasonally for irrigated crops. The design creates a series of productive zones from valley to ridge:

1. **Wet terrace swales** (broad, water-retaining): Lowest position, hold maximum water
2. **Windbreak swales** (or terraces in humid areas): Mid-slope protection
3. **Production swales** with hardy trees (mango, olive, etc.): Main productive zone
4. **Ridge windbreak swales** or interswale zones: Upper slope protection
5. **Ridge access road** with runoff directed to adjacent swales
6. **Onward production swales** with wind-tender trees (avocado, citrus)
7. **Interswale deep mulch and forage crops**: Dolichos, pigeon pea, Acacia leucophloea, lupin, coprosma, lucerne, tree medic

## Raised Mounds and Ridges on Flat Sites

On very flat sites (less than 4° slope), earth-shaping strategies diverge depending on whether the goal is drainage or water retention:

### For Wet Areas (Drainage)

Where drainage is a problem and rainfall is intense, raised mounds or ridges operate to drain crops. This is essential for crops susceptible to waterlogging, such as cassava, yams, and cucurbits, which are mounded in areas where drainage is a problem and rainfall is intense.

### For Dry Areas (Water Retention)

In drier areas, the same raised earthworks serve to impound water for absorption. Pits can also be used where rainfall is less than 76 cm (30 inches) or where soil drainage is good. Pits retain mulch and moisture, functioning similarly to their use in desert areas.

## Slope-Based Design Considerations

### Log Barriers on Slopes

On deforested or degraded slopes, log barriers provide immediate erosion control while longer-term vegetation establishes. Logs are placed cross-slope to hold mulch until tree lines establish. The logs rot quickly in tropical conditions but mulch is renewed annually by banana clumps and other fast-growing vegetation. This technique, developed by Marjorie Spears in Queensland, Australia, uses rejected logs arranged across deforested slopes to create rough-wood terraces that support complex, rich gardens.

### Keyline Techniques

Keyline techniques of flood control are applicable in the wet-dry and monsoon tropics. These involve identifying the keypoint (where the valley floor starts to flatten) and the keyline (contour line through the keypoint), then plowing parallel to the keyline to spread water from valleys to ridges. This approach prevents valley erosion while distributing water across the full slope width.

### Low Bunds and Soakage Pits

Low bunds (small earth embankments) across slopes impound runoff and create conditions for water infiltration. Combined with soakage pits (small excavated pits that fill with water and drain slowly into the subsoil), these simple earthworks can dramatically improve water availability in dry-season tropical agriculture.

## Contour Banks and Hedgerow Integration

The combination of contour banks with mulch-producing hedgerows creates a self-reinforcing system:

- The contour bank captures water and sediment
- The hedgerow stabilizes the bank with its root system
- Prunings from the hedgerow provide mulch for crops between banks
- The mulch improves soil structure and water-holding capacity
- Improved soil supports more vigorous hedgerow growth

This positive feedback loop gradually transforms degraded slopes into productive, stable agricultural land.

## Seasonal Water Management

### Wet Season

During the wet season, earthworks must handle intense rainfall and prevent erosion:

- Swales and contour banks intercept and infiltrate runoff
- Terraces prevent downhill water concentration
- Log barriers provide immediate slope protection
- Flood plains and alluvial deposits are managed for seasonal inundation

### Dry Season

During the dry season, stored water supports crops:

- Swale-impounded groundwater remains available to deep-rooted trees
- Pits and mounds retain moisture around individual plants
- Irrigated terraces can be flooded from stored water
- Greywater from households can be directed to garden trenches

## Integration with Tropical Polyculture

Earth-shaping is not an isolated technique—it integrates with the full tropical polyculture system:

- **Tree crops** planted on swale berms and terraces have reliable water access
- **Annual crops** grown between earthworks benefit from improved soil moisture
- **Leguminous ground covers** on bunds and berms provide nitrogen and mulch
- **Aquatic plants** in swale ponds provide additional mulch material
- **Livestock** grazing between earthworks contribute manure to the system

## Adaptation to Specific Tropical Zones

### Wet Tropics

In the wet tropics, where rainfall is 152–328 cm (60–129 inches) with frequent thunderstorms, earthworks must prioritize rapid infiltration and erosion control. Swales should be broad and well-vegetated. The emphasis is on preventing the rapid water runoff characteristic of the landscape, where deeply weathered regolith over bedrock provides limited natural water retention.

### Wet-Dry Tropics

In the wet-dry tropics, earthworks serve a dual purpose: managing episodic flooding in summer while conserving water through the dry winter. Water storage (tanks, ponds, cisterns) becomes essential. Keyline techniques, low bunds, and soakage pits are particularly valuable in these seasonal environments.
