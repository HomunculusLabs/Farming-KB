# Plants for Many Purposes

In most conventional gardens, each plant is chosen for a single purpose: a maple for fall color, an oak for shade, daisies for accent. Nature never works this way. Natural plant groupings form through dynamic relationships among species and the environment, creating landscapes that harvest water, build soil, minimize disease, and support enormous diversity -- all while looking beautiful.

## The Many Roles of a Tree

A single white oak, even isolated in a lawn, performs dozens of functions:

**Water and Climate:**
- Transpires up to 2,000 gallons of water on a hot day, filtering groundwater through roots and exhaling pure water
- Up to half of rainfall over forested land comes from trees themselves
- Absorbs over 1/4 inch of rain before any reaches the ground
- Creates convection currents and cooling breezes through differential heating
- Blocks wind, substantially reducing heating bills when placed windward of a house
- Harvests dew and fog: on arid but foggy coasts, tree-harvested precipitation can triple average rainfall
- Roots extend the depth of moisture availability through burrowing

**Soil and Nutrients:**
- Roots loosen and aerate soil, build humus as they grow and die
- Etch minerals free from rocks with mild acid secretions
- Provide sugary root exudates that feed hundreds of soil organism species
- Concentrates diffuse nutrients from thousands of cubic yards of air and earth into a dense layer of leaf-mold topsoil
- Roots can graft with neighboring trees, exchanging nutrients and chemical insect-attack signals

**Air Quality:**
- 10 to 30 acres of leaf surface collect dust and pollutants from the air
- Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide and releases oxygen
- Fungal spores and bacteria from the tree's canopy seed clouds and aid rain formation

**Wildlife and Ecology:**
- Creates insect habitat with its temperature-layered canopy
- Supports swarms of insects: gall wasps, beetles, sucking and chewing bugs, and their predators
- Provides food (acorns, insects), shelter, and nesting sites for birds and mammals
- Mycorrhizal fungi beneath the tree secrete nutrients and antibiotics that feed and protect it
- Voles spread beneficial fungi between trees via their droppings

## Selecting Plants by Function

When we understand the roles a plant can play, we can place it to complement its neighbors. Each plant has positive or negative relationships with nearby plants, animals, structures, and limitations of soil, light, wind, and water.

### Example: Nitrogen-Fixing Shrubs

- **Siberian pea shrub** (Caragana arborescens): extreme cold tolerance, edible seeds
- **Wild lilac** (Ceanothus spp.): drought-tolerant, full sun
- **Indigo** (Indigofera anctoria): shade and drought tolerant, green manure, attracts insects
- **Sea buckthorn** (Hippophae rhamnoides): erosion control, hedges, edible berries packed with vitamin C

### Example: Maximilian Sunflower (Helianthus maximilianii)

One plant serving six functions in a single placement:
1. Deer barrier (coarse, fuzzy stems that deer avoid)
2. Ornamental (7-foot-tall, 4-inch yellow blooms in late fall)
3. Edible shoots (raw or cooked)
4. Bird seed source
5. Mulch production (copious trimmings)
6. Grass invasion suppression on slopes

### Example: Goumi (Elaeagnus multiflora)

A nitrogen-fixing shrub that provides edible fruit, attracts insects, tolerates poor soil, fixes nitrogen, and can be used as a hedge.

## The Roles Plants Play in the Ecological Theater

Plants serve many overlapping functions in ecological gardens:

- **Soil builders**: Nitrogen fixers, deep-rooted nutrient accumulators, mulch producers
- **Insect attractants**: Umbelliferous flowers (dill, fennel, yarrow, Queen Anne's lace) attract predatory wasps and pollinators
- **Pest repellents**: Strongly scented plants that confuse pest insects
- **Microclimate creators**: Shade trees, windbreaks, ground covers that modify temperature, humidity, and light
- **Nutrient cyclers**: Dynamic accumulators that mine subsoil minerals
- **Erosion controllers**: Deep-rooted and spreading plants that hold soil
- **Habitat providers**: Food, shelter, and nesting sites for wildlife
- **Food producers**: Fruits, nuts, greens, herbs, medicinal plants for people
- **Water managers**: Plants that harvest dew, slow runoff, pump water from depth

## Annuals vs. Perennials

Perennials offer significant advantages in ecological gardens because they:
- Develop deep, permanent root systems that build soil structure
- Provide year-round habitat and microclimate
- Require less replanting and soil disturbance
- Create more stable, long-term connections with other species

Annuals still play roles as quick soil-builders, intensive food producers, and succession pioneers.

## Nurses, Scaffolds, and Chaperones

Plants can help other plants establish:
- **Nurse plants**: Fast-growing species that shelter young, delicate seedlings from harsh sun and wind
- **Scaffolds**: Tall plants that provide physical support for climbing species
- **Chaperones**: Species that prepare soil conditions (nitrogen fixation, soil loosening) that later benefit other plants

## Design Implication

By understanding that plants don't stand alone but modify their environment in ways that support or inhibit what lies nearby, we can radically affect how we place features in our gardens. Each plant's outputs can become another's inputs, creating the interconnected web that characterizes a healthy ecological garden.
