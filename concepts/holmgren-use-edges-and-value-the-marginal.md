# Holmgren Use Edges and Value the Marginal

## The Principle of Edge

The icon of the sun rising over a horizon with a river in the foreground shows us a
world composed of edges. Within every terrestrial ecosystem the living soil —
perhaps only a few centimetres deep — is an edge or interface between non-living
mineral earth and the atmosphere. For all terrestrial life, including humanity,
this is the most important edge of all.

This principle reminds us to maintain awareness and make use of edges and margins
at all scales in all systems. At the edge of anything, system or medium, the most
interesting events take place. Design that sees edge as an opportunity rather than
a problem is more likely to be successful and adaptable. The negative connotations
associated with "marginal" should be discarded to see the value in elements that
only peripherally contribute to a function or system.

## Landscape Edges

### Coastlines and Estuaries
[[dighton-fungi-mangrove-coastal-ecosystems]] are diverse and productive interfaces between terrestrial and
oceanic domains. While deep oceans are ecological deserts, shallow coastal waters
fed by land nutrients support huge diversity and abundance. Tidal estuaries are
complex interfaces between land and sea, functioning as ecological trade markets
where fresh water rides over heavier saline water pulsing with daily tides,
redistributing nutrients and food for teeming life.

### Ecotones
An ecotone is the edge between two bioregions where species from both regions
overlap, creating greater biodiversity than in either region alone. Ecotones
generated by altitude may be narrow (less than 1 km); those from latitude or
continental versus maritime influence may be tens of kilometres wide.

The Towamba Valley on the far south coast of New South Wales is a three-way
ecotone between warm temperate east coast, cool temperate south coast, and cold
dry Monaro plateaus. Thirteen species of eucalypts from all three bioregions on
less than 75 hectares demonstrates the biodiversity concentration at edges.

### Land Systems
Changes in soil type, slope (break of slope) or aspect (ridges) create rapid
transitions in vegetation types over distances as small as a metre. Edges between
forest and grass, heath or swampland support more bird species than either
vegetation system because resources of both systems are available.

## Micro Scale Edge

Within the bodies of plants, animals and living soil, edges are where the action
happens. Lungs are fractal patterns of tissue allowing maximum gaseous exchange.
Plant roots have enormous surface area (edge) for osmotic absorption of water,
gases and dissolved nutrients. Clay and humus provide complex surfaces (indicated
by [[cation-exchange-capacity]]) that hold and release nutrients. A huge part of what
makes soil productive is open friable structure that amounts to maximum edge.

## Edge as a Systemic Property

Edges are dynamic and productive parts of all natural systems where exchange of
materials and energy takes place. They are the places where both cooperative and
competitive relationships between system elements and whole systems are played out.
Increasing edge is one important way to increase system intensity and productivity,
an alternative mode of [[cytokinin-plant-growth-and-development]] to gross system expansion. This
connects directly to the principle of [[holmgren-gigantism-intermediate-technology-and-small-scale]].

## Edge in Cultivated Landscapes

### English Hedgerows
Traditional English farm landscapes were patchworks of irregular fields bordered by
hedgerows, woods, villages, roads, streams and ponds — carefully maintained edges.
A hedgerow functions as a double forest edge intersecting crop and pasture land.
The change from small-scale intensive systems to large-scale monocultures
eliminated much landscape edge complexity, reducing [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]], wild food,
herbs, construction materials, and landscape amenity.

### Mediterranean Terracing
From the 14th to 16th centuries, terracing of stony hillsides increased
agricultural productivity through heroic construction and meticulous maintenance
of landscape edge. The pattern: initial clearing increases edge and productivity,
further clearing reduces edge and leads to degradation, then investment in designed
edge raises ecological values and productivity again.

### Indigenous Australian Landscapes
Aboriginal people actively managed landscapes with fire to create open pastoral
woodland with sharp transitional edges between vegetation types. The seasonal
movement of groups followed specific patterns for harvesting and management,
determined by precise synchronous cues in the natural environment.

Firestick farming created a pattern of localised degradation and enrichment:
nutrients lost from burnt areas (in smoke and water) were absorbed by adjacent
unburnt areas, which became more fertile over time. This use of edge between burnt
and unburnt areas increased total landscape diversity and productivity.

## Urban Examples

### Urban-Rural Fringe
The constant search for the edge between town and country drives urban sprawl.
Suburban landscapes that retain topographic constraints (escarpments, harbours)
maintain edge complexity. Progressive suburban design creates green space along
watercourses and nodes of denser pedestrian-dominated development.

### Shopfront as Edge
Shopfront is the interface between public street domain and private stores — the
edge across which information about goods is presented and economic exchange
occurs. Shopping malls captured and enclosed this edge, managing the shopfront
membrane in a fractal structure that unconsciously moves people through exchange
zones.

## Permaculture Design Applications

### Keyhole Gardens
Keyhole garden beds increase the accessible edge for a given area of path and
bed, maximizing growing area relative to access.

### Pond Design
Ponds built with sinuous edges of peninsulas, marshy flats and islands have
greater total biological productivity than those with simple shorelines. Greater
edge increases farmed fish yields by providing more habitat and feeding surface
for aquatic organisms.

### Revegetation Strategy
Linear (not necessarily straight) belts of trees, five or more rows deep,
optimise passive functions and yields without the disadvantages of fence-to-fence
reforestation. This is appropriate segregation — retaining the separate nature of
both crop/pasture and forest systems while maximizing the productive edge between
them.

### Effects of Intensity of Use
The complexity of edge that can be managed depends on scale. Hand-cut edges can be
sinuous and scalloped; mower edges need fewer direction changes; tractor slasher
or fenced edges must have much less variation. The imperative of small-scale
systems suggests always considering designs that reflect small site variations.
