# Fungal-Faunal Interactions: Synthesis and Conclusions

Fungi interact with animals across virtually all ecosystems through a spectrum of relationships ranging from mutualism to parasitism. These interactions are fundamental drivers of ecosystem processes, influencing nutrient cycling, decomposition rates, plant community structure, and population dynamics of both fungi and fauna.

## Fungi as Food Resources

### Vertebrate Mycophagy
Fungi serve as important food sources for numerous vertebrate animals. Mushroom collecting and consumption has deep cultural and culinary traditions in many European countries, and commercial mushroom cultivation is a multimillion-dollar industry in the United States alone. For many vertebrates, fungal consumption is especially critical during periods of food scarcity. In Arctic regions, reindeer rely on lichens as their primary food source when snow covers vascular plants. Similarly, small mammals worldwide incorporate fungi into their diets when other food sources are depleted or during seasonal fungal abundance peaks.

Hypogeous (underground) fungi, such as truffles, have evolved an obligate dependence on small mammals for spore dispersal. The fruiting bodies of these fungi are consumed by mammals, and viable spores are deposited in feces at foraging sites and middens. This relationship is so tight that the entire reproductive strategy of hypogeous fungi depends on mammalian vectors.

### Invertebrate Mycophagy
For invertebrates, both fruiting bodies and mycelial networks serve as food bases with high nutritional value. Soil arthropods such as collembola (springtails) and mites are particularly dependent on fungal mycelia for growth and development. Fungivorous nematodes also rely heavily on fungi, and selective feeding on specific fungal species can significantly influence nematode population demographics, including sex ratios, fecundity, and subsequent population sizes (Ruess and Dighton, 1996; Ruess et al., 2000).

## Grazing Effects on Fungal Communities and Ecosystem Processes

### Comminution and Inoculum Transport
Animals that graze on fungi directly affect fungal-mediated ecosystem processes, particularly decomposition. Soil fauna contribute to decomposition through:
- **Comminution**: Physical breakdown of leaf litter into smaller fragments, increasing surface area for fungal colonization
- **Inoculum transport**: Active movement of fungal spores and hyphal fragments into new substrates (Ponge, 1990, 1991)

### Compensatory Growth and Grazing Intensity
The relationship between grazing intensity and fungal growth is nonlinear. Intense grazing suppresses hyphal growth, but moderate grazing can stimulate compensatory growth, actually increasing total fungal biomass and facilitating colonization of new resources. This compensatory response has important implications for decomposition dynamics and nutrient mineralization.

### Selective Grazing and Community Structure
Like humans, animals are selective feeders. Invertebrates choose preferred fungal species based on nutritional quality or avoidance of toxic secondary metabolites. Importantly, many fungi that are poisonous to humans are preferred food sources for invertebrates, which consume them without apparent harm.

Selective grazing creates asymmetric selection pressures on fungal communities. A classic example involves the collembolan *Onychiurus armatus* preferentially grazing *Marasmius androcaceus* over *Mycena galopus* in a Sitka spruce stand, which resulted in both fungal species being displaced from their optimal habitat on the forest floor (Newell, 1984a,b). Such grazing-mediated shifts in decomposer community composition can alter rates of leaf litter decomposition and nutrient mineralization.

However, field observations suggest that collembola spend considerable time grooming and "nibbling" at fungal hyphae rather than consuming large quantities at once. This raises questions about the true magnitude of their grazing impact, especially since severing a single hyphum is sufficient to disrupt its translocatory function.

## Fungal Propagule Dispersal by Animals

### Ectomycorrhizal Inoculum
The ability of animals to transport fungal propagules—either externally on their bodies or internally through their gut—is critical for spreading inoculum through ecosystems. Fungal spores can survive passage through the digestive tracts of earthworms and soil arthropods, enabling dispersal to new resources including:
- Fresh organic substrates for saprotrophic fungal communities
- Root zones for mycorrhizal fungal communities
- Bare ground during primary or secondary succession

Earthworms may serve as delivery systems for pathogenic fungi to sites where soil pests reside, contributing to biological control (Doube et al., 1994a,b; 1995).

### Specialized Dispersal Mechanisms
Some fungal groups have evolved specialized dispersal strategies:
- **Hypogeous fungi** (truffles): Rely entirely on small mammal consumption and fecal deposition
- **Phallales** (Gasteromycetes): Produce sticky, malodorous spore masses that attract flies for dispersal

## Mutualistic Fungus-Agriculture

### Ant and Termite Fungiculture
The evolutionary association between animals and fungi has, in some cases, become so intimate that the partners are inseparable. Several ant and termite species actively cultivate preferred fungal species within their colonies:
- Workers maintain fungal gardens by selective grazing
- The most nutritious fungal material is preferentially fed to developing larvae
- Colonies are actively cleaned of contaminating fungi that are less palatable or compete against the preferred species

### Bark Beetle-Fungal Symbiosis
Bark beetles carry fungi in specialized structures called **mycangia** on their legs. These fungi serve multiple functions:
- Decompose wood in the phloem of host trees
- Translocate nitrogen from surrounding nitrogen-rich plant material into the nutrient-poor wood
- Make the resource more palatable and nutritious for developing beetle larvae

These systems can involve complex multispecies interactions, as demonstrated by the interplay between bark beetles, phoretic mites, and two competing fungi (Lombardero et al., 2000). The full complexity of such multispecies interactions in nature remains poorly understood.

## Marine Fungal-Faunal Interactions

### Wood Decomposition Mutualisms
In marine ecosystems, the low resource quality of wood encourages particularly tight linkages between fungi and fauna. Wood-boring marine mollusks preferentially settle and feed on wood that has been previously colonized and partially decomposed by fungi. The wood-boring crustacean *Limnoria tripunctata* (gribble) shows increased longevity on fungus-colonized wood and is incapable of reproduction without marine fungi in its diet, likely due to the enhanced availability of proteins, essential amino acids, and vitamins provided by fungal activity.

### Marine Fungal Pathogens
Fungi are major pathogens of marine animals (Hyde et al., 1998). Mitosporic fungi cause disease in crustaceans (Smolowitz et al., 1992), damage corals (Raghukumar and Raghukumar, 1991), and infect juvenile clams (Norton et al., 1994). Oomycetes are highly destructive pathogens of finfish, mollusks, and shellfish. While few fungi have been reported as pathogens of marine plants, many marine algae are susceptible to chytrid and oomycete pathogens.

### Marine Gut Symbionts
Like terrestrial arthropods, marine isopods contain gut fungi to assist in plant food breakdown. Cafaro (2000) documented trichomycetes of the genus *Palavascia* in Patagonian intertidal isopods. Black fly larvae also harbor trichomycete endosymbionts, with new recruits rapidly becoming colonized by *Harpella melusinae*, suggesting strong dependence on this gut symbiont (Beard and Adler, 2002).

## Fungal Pathogens as Population Regulators

### Entomopathogenic Fungi
Almost all animals are subject to attack by pathogenic fungi. Entomopathogenic fungi are particularly important as natural regulators of insect populations. Their effects follow density-dependent patterns, with maximal impact when host populations peak (Kamata, 2000). Under favorable environmental conditions, these pathogens can cause devastating population collapses.

### Biocontrol Applications
Many entomopathogenic fungi are being investigated as biocontrol agents:
- **Nematode-trapping fungi** can be introduced through cattle feces to reduce populations of parasitic gut nematodes, improving livestock yield
- **Beauveria bassiana** has been used to reduce grasshopper populations
- Fungal pathogens show potential for mosquito control

### Amphibian Declines
Fungal pathogens of frogs have caused major population declines globally, particularly in tropical biodiversity hotspots. Given climate change scenarios that may produce conditions more favorable for fungal growth, the importance of fungal diseases of animals is likely to increase.

## Key Knowledge Gaps

Several critical questions remain about fungal-faunal interactions:
- How important is fungal grazing in natural ecosystems compared to laboratory and greenhouse studies conducted at unrealistic animal densities?
- Can selective grazing of mycorrhizal fungi differentially affect plant species enough to alter plant community structure?
- How many multispecies interaction scenarios exist in nature similar to the bark beetle-phoretic mite-competing fungi system?
- How will climate change alter the balance between fungi and their animal hosts or predators?

The challenge of extrapolating from controlled studies to natural ecosystems remains a fundamental problem in fungal ecology, as interactions observed under contrived conditions may not reflect those actually occurring in nature.
