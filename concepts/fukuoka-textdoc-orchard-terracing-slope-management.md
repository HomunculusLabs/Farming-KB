## Overview

Masanobu Fukuoka describes a specific timeline and sequence for establishing
a natural orchard on sloping land, beginning with the chaotic early years of
weed and brush competition and culminating in a terraced, clover-covered
productive landscape. His approach diverges sharply from conventional orchard
establishment by embracing rather than fighting the natural succession of
vegetation, only gradually imposing human-scale order through minimal
earthwork and selective planting. This page details the three-stage process
of orchard management on hillside terrain as practiced on his own farm.

## The First Years: Chaos and Neglect

When Fukuoka planted his orchard, he dug large holes among the stumps of
felled trees and planted unpruned saplings and fruit seed over the site,
leaving these unattended much as one would leave alone a reforested stand
of trees. Suckers grew from the cut tree stumps and weeds and low brush
flourished abundantly. During this early period, orchard management consisted
primarily of coming in twice a year to cut the weeds and underbrush with a
large sickle. There was no attempt to create orderly rows, control specific
weed species, or establish ground cover crops. The fruit trees grew amid
this tangle of competing vegetation, receiving whatever light and moisture
filtered through the dense undergrowth.

The philosophy behind this approach was fundamentally one of patience and
non-interference. Rather than imposing order from the start, Fukuoka
allowed the land to express its own natural patterns of revegetation. The
diverse mix of pioneer species that colonized the cleared hillside served
as a living mulch, protecting the soil from erosion and beginning the slow
process of soil building that would eventually support productive fruit
trees. This period of apparent neglect was, in fact, a deliberate strategy
of allowing nature to prepare the ground for cultivation.

## Correcting Tree Form in the Wild

As the young trees struggled upward through the underbrush, some
intervention became necessary. A young transplanted sapling may experience
dieback at the tip or lose too much of its root system during planting,
causing an unnaturally large number of suckers to emerge and the branches
to become entangled. When a young tree lies in the shadow of a larger tree,
it tends to become leggy, and the lower branches often die back. Left to
itself, such a tree acquires an unnatural form that results in years of
unending labor for the grower. To hasten the tree's approach to a more
natural form, shoots and buds emerging from unnatural places must be nipped
off as soon as possible.

Trees that show normal, steady growth right from the start assume a nearly
natural form and can thereafter be left alone. Cutting the first one or two
shoots is therefore very important, as how well this is done can determine
the shape of the tree over its entire lifetime. It is often hard to tell
which shoots to leave and which to pinch off. The grower may decide, often
prematurely, which branches are to be the primary scaffold branches and
which the secondary scaffolds when the tree is still very young, only to
find later that these branches have tangled under unanticipated growth
conditions. Early pruning can turn out to be unnecessary and even harmful
when done unwisely.

Fukuoka emphasizes that it is a mistake to assume a tree grown in a natural
state will more easily acquire a natural form. It is not through abandonment
that a cultivated tree takes on a natural form, but only through the most
careful attention and protection. This paradox lies at the heart of natural
farming: the greatest care is often indistinguishable from apparent neglect.

## Weed Dynamics in the Young Orchard

Fukuoka was especially interested in the growth and control of other trees
and weeds in a natural orchard. Four to five years after planting fruit
trees, he found eulalia and other weeds growing thickly among the brush
and assorted trees. Weeding was not easy and sometimes it was even hard to
locate the fruit trees. Although the growth of fruit trees among this other
vegetation was irregular and yielded poor harvests in some cases, there was
very little damage from disease and insects. Fukuoka found it hard to
believe that, with the odd assortment of trees in his orchard and some of
the fruit trees even growing in the shadow of other trees, these were
spared attack by diseases and pests.

Later, with continued cutting back of the underbrush, the non-fruit trees
receded and weeds such as bracken, mugwort, and kudzu grew up in their
place. This marked a natural succession from woody pioneer species to
herbaceous perennials and climbing vines. At this stage, Fukuoka was able
to control or suppress weed growth by broadcasting clover seed over the
entire orchard. The clover gradually established itself as the dominant
ground cover, outcompeting the remaining weeds without the need for
chemical herbicides or intensive cultivation.

## Building Terraces at the Bearing Stage

Five to six years after planting, when the trees begin to bear fruit,
Fukuoka recommends digging up the earth on the uphill side of the fruit
trees with a hoe and constructing terrace-like steps and a road on the
orchard slope. This earthwork is not performed at the outset of orchard
establishment, as in conventional hillside farming, but only after the
trees have proven their viability and the soil has begun to recover from
the initial clearing. The terraces serve multiple purposes: they reduce
soil erosion on steep slopes, create level planting areas that retain
moisture more effectively, and provide access roads for harvesting and
management operations.

Once these terraces have been built and the original weeds replaced, first
with soft weeds such as chickweed, knotweed, and crabgrass, then with
clover, the orchard begins to look like an orchard. The transformation
from wild hillside to productive agricultural landscape is gradual and
follows the natural succession of the land rather than imposing an
artificial order from the beginning. The terraces themselves are not
engineered structures but simple earthworks that follow the contour of the
slope, shaped by hand with a hoe rather than by heavy machinery.

## The Three-Dimensional Vision

The ultimate goal of this terracing and management process is the creation
of a natural three-dimensional orchard in which full use is made of
vertical space. Deciduous fruit trees grow alongside evergreen fruit trees,
interplanted with green manure trees such as acacias, myrtle, alder, and
podocarpus. Climbing fruit vines including grapevine, akebia, and Chinese

plants and herbs enrich the orchard soil as undergrowth, while forage
