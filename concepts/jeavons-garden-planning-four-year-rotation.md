# Garden Planning and Four-Year Rotation

John Jeavons provides detailed garden plans that progress over four years,
allowing gardeners to develop their skills incrementally while improving the
soil. The approach begins with a simple mini-garden and expands to include
permanent plantings, protein crops, and fiber crops as soil health improves.

## The Simple Mini-Garden

The starting point is a 100 to 140+ [[comparison-berry-growing-vs-fruit-tree-pruning]] can be planted as early as possible in spring.

The simple mini-garden teaches fundamental skills: starting seedlings in flats,
pricking out seedlings, transplanting at the correct time, and maintaining beds.
Seedlings are started in flats 5 weeks before the last spring frost for
lettuce, parsley, and similar crops, while warm-season crops like peppers and
tomatoes are started 10 weeks before the last frost.

## The One-Person Mini-Garden: Four-Year Progression

### Year One

The first year plan covers 100 square feet with easy-to-grow crops. The focus
is on building basic gardening skills and improving soil through double-digging
and compost application. Leaf lettuce matures sooner than head lettuce, so
planting both ensures continuous harvest. Half the tomatoes should be an early
variety (65 days) for continuous production.

### Year Two

The square footage doubles to approximately 200 square feet. More difficult
crops are added as the gardener's skills improve. Wheat and other grain crops
may be introduced to begin building carbon for compost production.

### Year Three

Trees, herbs, strawberries, and asparagus are included as permanent plantings.
These are placed in soil that has now been worked and improved for two years. A
third bed is added, bringing the total cultivated area to approximately 300
square feet.

### Year Four

After three or four years, with improved gardening skills, the vegetable crop
area can be condensed from 200 square feet back to 100 square feet, leaving 100
square feet of improved soil for protein crops (wheat, rye, peanuts, lentils,
soybeans, and rice), fibers (cotton or flax), or special interest crops
(chicken, goat, or [[comparison-nut-trees-vs-grain-farming-for-caloric-staple-production]]).
