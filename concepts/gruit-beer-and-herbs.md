# Gruit Beer and Herbs

Gruit (also grut) is the historical term for the mixture of herbs used to flavor and preserve beer before the widespread adoption of hops. For thousands of years before hops became dominant, European beers were flavored with complex blends of bitter, aromatic, and often [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]. Yarrow contains a complex mixture of bitter principles, flavonoids, and [[query-how-do-i-build-a-medicinal-herb-drying-rack-and-optimize-drying-conditions]]. It is astringent, diaphoretic, and anti-inflammatory. The name Achillea comes from the Greek hero Achilles, who was said to have used yarrow to treat wounds. In gruit mixtures, yarrow contributed not only flavor but also medicinal properties that would have been valued in a daily beverage.

### Wild Rosemary (Ledum palustre)

Wild rosemary, also called marsh rosemary or marsh Labrador tea, is a shrub that grows in boreal and subarctic regions. It has a strong, aromatic fragrance reminiscent of both rosemary and Ledum species. Wild rosemary contains ledol, a compound with sedative properties, as well as various terpenes and flavonoids.

The flavor contribution of wild rosemary is distinctive -- herbal, slightly medicinal, with woody and floral notes. In larger quantities it can be overpowering. Some researchers have suggested that the psychoactive properties of wild rosemary, combined with those of sweet gale, may have contributed to the potent effects sometimes attributed to gruit ale.

## The Gruit Tax

The history of gruit is closely tied to medieval economics and politics. In many parts of medieval Europe, the right to produce and sell gruit was controlled by local authorities -- often the Church or feudal lords. Brewers were required to purchase their gruit mixture from authorized gruit merchants, creating a lucrative monopoly.

The gruit tax was a significant source of revenue for medieval towns and monasteries. This economic structure partly explains the eventual shift to hops: hops could be grown by individual brewers and farmers, bypassing the gruit monopoly. ## Brewing with Gruit Today

Modern brewers interested in gruit ales should note that gruit herbs behave differently from hops in the brewing process:

- Gruit herbs are typically added during the boil, but unlike hops they can also be added during fermentation (a "dry herb" technique) for aromatic extraction
- The preservative properties of gruit herbs are generally weaker than hops, so gruit ales may have a shorter shelf life
- Gruit herbs often require different quantities and timing than hops to avoid excessive astringency or overpowering flavors
- Starting with a simple blend of sweet gale and yarrow is recommended before adding wild rosemary or other herbs
- Some gruit herbs are best used as a tea or tincture added to the fermenter rather than boiled with the wort

## Legal and Safety Considerations

Some traditional gruit herbs, particularly wild rosemary (Ledum palustre), contain compounds that may be toxic in large quantities. Modern brewers should research each herb carefully and use them in moderation. Sweet gale also contains compounds that should not be consumed in excessive amounts, particularly by pregnant women.
