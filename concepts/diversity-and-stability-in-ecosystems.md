# Diversity and Stability in Ecosystems

Diversity and stability are foundational concepts in [[permaculture-design]] and ecological science. [[bill-mollison]]'s treatment of these topics emphasizes that the common assumption "more diversity equals more stability" is overly simplistic, and that the *quality* of connections between system components matters far more than the sheer number of components.

## Definitions: Stress and Harmony

In [[permaculture]] systems theory, two key terms frame the discussion of how systems function:

- **Stress** is defined as either the prevention of natural function, or the imposition of forced function. Stress disrupts the self-regulating capacity of an ecosystem.
- **Harmony** is defined as the integration of chosen and natural functions, combined with the supply of essential needs. A harmonious system functions with minimal external intervention.

Understanding stress and harmony at the system level helps designers identify when a design is working against natural processes rather than with them.

## Diversity: What It Is and Isn't

Diversity, strictly speaking, is the number of different components or constructs within a system — an enumeration of elements and parts. Mollison is explicit that diversity:

- Has **no inherent relationship** to connections between components
- Has **little bearing** on the function or self-regulating capacity of real systems (within the bounds of too few or too many components)
- Does **not by itself** guarantee either stability or yield

This is a critical departure from naive ecological thinking. A garden with many species is diverse, but unless the species are functionally connected — each supplying the needs and accepting the products of others — the diversity is merely cosmetic and requires ongoing human maintenance to persist.

## The Role of Human Effort in Maintained Diversity

Few species grown by people persist beyond the lifetime of those who tend them if the situation is left alone. Mollison cites the Australian experience: towns built in forested areas for mining or port operations, when abandoned, are obliterated by forest within 30–80 years. Only a few hardy "survivor" species — dates, mulberries, figs in savannah or isolated dryland locations — persist. These survivor species are important markers for planning long-term stability in their respective regions.

The implication is clear: maintained diversity in cultivated systems is not a natural state — it is a product of deliberate human effort. A permaculture designer must understand that diversity without functional connections is fragile and unsustainable.

## Beneficial Connections Over Component Count

The central principle of stability in permaculture states:

> **It is not the number of diverse things in a design that leads to stability, it is the number of beneficial connections between these components.**

Adding a technology or living species "just to have it there" has no sense. Adding it to supply a need or consume an otherwise wasted resource — to do something useful — makes a great deal of sense. This shifts the design focus from species lists to functional analysis: what does each component need, what does it produce, and how can its outputs serve as inputs for other components?

## Information as Critical Potential Resource

Mollison identifies information as the critical potential resource: "It becomes a resource only when obtained and acted upon." In the real world, resources are energy storages; in the abstract world, they are useful information or time. A diversity of beneficial functional connections is a resource in itself. The complexity arising from powerful interconnections between species is what designers seek in food systems — expressed in practice as polycultures and guilds of plants and animals that assist each other.

## Richness, Diversity, and Evenness

Peter Moon (writing in *New Scientist*, 28 February 1985) differentiated three related but distinct measures:

- **Richness**: the number of species per unit area
- **Diversity**: the relative abundance of species
- **Evenness**: how species contribute to the total biomass

These distinctions matter for design. Richness in plants may decrease as systems age (shade and competition reduce annuals and weaker species), but richness in animals such as decomposers may increase due to a greater range of niches and microclimates developing. More animals live in ungrazed or uncut grasslands, but fewer plant species survive.

Tree species richness has been correlated to the energy use of the plant community, as measured by evapotranspiration (*New Scientist*, 22 October 1987). Species-rich regions are essentially linked to the basic productivity of the region, rather than to latitude, glaciation history, or fire. Local niches and altitude variation can create additional diversity within productive regions.

## The Stress Mosaic: Moderate Disturbance as Design Tool

Some disturbance or "moderate stress" — such as that achieved in gardens — provides the richest environment. Designers can actively create a **stress mosaic**:

- Allow some undisturbed (low-stress) islands of vegetation
- Mow or dig in other areas (high-stress zones)
- Actively manage plant and animal maintenance, increasing or decreasing grazing pressures to manage species abundance locally

This approach gets "the best of both worlds" in terms of species richness, drawing from ecological edge theory where transitions between disturbance regimes create the greatest niche diversity.

## Stability as Self-Regulation

Stability in ecosystems is not the stability of a concrete pylon — it is the process of constant feedback and response, comparable to riding a bicycle. It is self-regulation rather than climax (end-point) stability. Nothing in nature remains forever; soil, hills, and forests all change. Tribal peoples have maintained dynamic life-support systems for thousands of years through constructive regulation.

The concept of climax states — endpoints toward which systems supposedly evolve — is itself uncertain. Inexorable climatic change, fire, nutrient leaching, and invasion deflect systems from apparent endpoints. Very old systems may actually be fragile, having been long in a state of maintenance, subject to sudden or slow collapse.

[[john-seymour]] noted the slow loss of nutrients in an old stable dune system at Cooloola, Australia, where climax is a passing phase as virgin dunes lose nutrient status to fire and water filtration to depths where nutrients become unavailable to trees. [[daniel-goodman]] documented "wild fluctuations" in tropical forests and savannah grasslands, where pathogen epidemics can severely reduce species numbers.

## Key Takeaways for Design

- Design for beneficial connections, not species counts
- Use information actively — it is only a potential resource until acted upon
- Create stress mosaics for maximum richness
- Understand that stability is dynamic self-regulation, not a fixed state
- Recognize that very old systems can be fragile, not robust
- Note survivor species as indicators of long-term regional resilience
