## Core Concept

Sweet potatoes ([[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]. Soil temperature below
50°F (10°C) damages roots in the ground. They are typically grown as a
spring crop where summers are long, hot, and humid, and dug before cool
weather arrives.

### Soil Requirements
Sweet potatoes prefer light, well-drained soils — sandy loam is ideal. Heavy
clay soils produce misshapen roots and reduce yield. The soil should be
loosened to at least 8-10 inches deep. Good drainage after heavy rains is
essential. Raise beds 6-8 inches above narrow footpaths between rows to
ensure drainage. Excessive fertility, especially nitrogen, produces
excessive vine growth at the expense of root development. A moderate
fertility regime is ideal.

## Planting

### Seed Selection
Choose seed roots that are 1.5 to 2.5 inches (4-6 cm) in diameter with
smooth skins, pleasant appearance, and no sign of insect damage or disease.
Select from hills that produced an abundant "nest" of potatoes — vigorous
production indicates good genetics.

### Producing Slips (Shoots)
**Small-scale method**: Suspend a sweet potato in a container of water,
covering half of it. Sprouts will develop from the exposed portion. This
works for producing a few plants.

**Larger scale method**: Place presprouted sweet potatoes on a bed of sand
and cover with 2 inches of moist soil. Maintain temperature between 70-75°F
(21-24°C). Sprouts will be ready for transplant at 10-12 inches (25-30 cm)
tall with stout stems. Cut sprouts 2 inches above the soil with a sharp
knife. Plant almost horizontally about 2 inches deep, ensuring two leaves
remain above ground. Take care not to damage the terminal bud.

### Commercial Options
Buying certified virus-free slips or tissue-culture clones is recommended.
Virus-free starts increase yields by up to a third. Sweet potatoes accumulate
virus diseases like Irish potatoes and also have a tendency to mutate.
Commercial starts are usually only a few generations from pure, virus-free
tissue-culture clones.

### Spacing and Layout
- Rows four feet (120 cm) apart on center
- Plants 15 inches (40 cm) apart within the row
- Alternatively, three seedlings per hill with hills on four-foot centers
- Proper drainage is essential — raise beds 6-8 inches in heavy soils

## Harvest

### Timing
Sweet potatoes continue to size up rapidly during the last few weeks before
maturity but their eating quality declines when overly large. Monitor closely
— harvest when roots are sizeable but not excessively so. There will come a
point when all roots have reached good size and should be dug promptly.

### Harvest Technique
Dig gently to minimize "skinning" — scraped roots don't keep well. If soil
is dry at digging, light irrigation a day before softens soil and reduces
damage. However, if soil is too wet, potatoes may crack after harvest or rot
in the ground. Do not allow harvested roots to sit in direct sun for more
than one hour or they may scald. Do not harvest after frost — roots damaged
by soil temperatures below 50°F (10°C) will rot.

### Yield Expectations
Given good soil, moderate fertility, and warm growing conditions with warm
nights, each plant can produce 8-12 good-sized sweet potatoes. In northern
climates, yield may drop to about one pound per row foot.

## Curing and Storage

### Curing Process
After harvest, sweet potatoes must be cured to heal skin abrasions and
develop proper sweetness. Ideal curing conditions: 85-90°F (29-32°C), 90%
humidity, with reasonable ventilation. Home method: air-dry roots for several
hours away from direct sunlight, then place in vented boxes in a warm, humid
location for 10-14 days.

### Storage Conditions
After curing, store at 55-60°F (13-16°C) with moderate humidity. Do not
refrigerate — temperatures below 50°F (10°C) cause chilling injury and rot.
Wait one month after curing for full sweetness to develop. Properly cured and
stored sweet potatoes last 4-6 months.

## Nutritional Profile

Sweet potatoes are exceptionally rich in beta-carotene (provitamin A),
vitamin C, and complex carbohydrates. Orange-fleshed varieties provide more
beta-carotene; white-fleshed varieties are higher in dry matter and starch.
They are one of the most calorie-dense vegetables a home gardener can produce,
making them an important food security crop.
