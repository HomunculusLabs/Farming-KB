## The Centrifugal Model: Scientific Farming

Scientific farming operates on a centrifugal, divergent trajectory. It moves
outward from nature, expanding endlessly through human intervention:

- New technologies are constantly introduced to solve problems created by
  previous technologies.
- Each addition of knowledge or equipment makes the system more complex.
- Labor and expense increase geometrically rather than linearly.
- The farmer becomes increasingly dependent on external inputs: chemical
  fertilizers, pesticides, machinery, petroleum, and purchased seeds.

Fukuoka described this as an "outward expansion that cannot be stopped." Like a
spinning top that must keep accelerating or fall over, scientific farming must
keep innovating or collapse. Every problem it solves creates two new problems:
pesticides kill pests but also kill beneficial insects, requiring more
pesticides; fertilizers boost yields but degrade soil, requiring more
fertilizers.

## The Centripetal Model: Natural Farming

Natural farming operates on a centripetal, convergent trajectory. It moves
inward toward nature, simplifying and reducing human intervention:

- Unnecessary practices are eliminated one by one.
- The farmer asks not "what can I add?" but "what can I remove?"
- Labor decreases over time as the system approaches self-sufficiency.
- External inputs are eliminated: no fertilizers, no pesticides, no machinery
  beyond the simplest tools.

Fukuoka described his own journey as progressive simplification. Over thirty
years, he reduced the work of growing rice and barley to two actions: sowing
seed and spreading straw. Everything else was handled by nature.

## Energy Efficiency Comparison

Fukuoka quantified the difference in energy terms:

| System | Energy Output per Man-Day | Relative Input |
|--------|--------------------------|----------------|
| Natural farming | ~200,000 kcal | 1x (none) |
| Traditional farming (horses/oxen) | ~20,000 kcal | 10x |
| Small-scale mechanization | ~10,000 kcal | 20x |
| Large-scale mechanization | ~5,000 kcal | 40x |

Natural farming produces roughly 100 times a farmer's daily caloric need per
man-day of labor, with zero external energy input. As mechanization increases,
the farmer must work off-farm to pay for equipment and inputs, trading outdoor
labor for factory work. The "efficiency gains" of mechanization are illusory.

## The Paradox of High-Yield Technology

Fukuoka identified a central paradox: high-yield practices do not actually
increase yields above what nature produces natively. They merely restore
productivity lost through destructive practices:

1. Man clears forest, burns vegetation, plows soil — destroying soil structure
   and killing microbes.
2. The degraded soil produces poor crops.
3. Chemical fertilizers are applied to compensate.
4. Yields improve, and fertilizer is credited with the increase.
5. In reality, fertilizer has only partially restored the productivity that
   existed before human intervention.

Fukuoka compared this to a man who breaks his own legs, then congratulates
himself on inventing crutches. The "advance" of agricultural science is largely
an advance in repairing damage that science itself caused.

## The Buddhist Parallel

Fukuoka linked this model to Buddhist philosophy. Natural farming is "the
methodless method of nature, the unmoving way of Bodhidharma." The centrifugal
model corresponds to the restless ego, constantly seeking to control and
possess. The centripetal model corresponds to "Mu" (nothingness) — not
emptiness as deprivation, but the fertile void from which all things arise.

## Why the Centrifugal Model Persists

Given its drawbacks, why does scientific farming dominate? Fukuoka offered
several explanations:

- **Pride**: Admitting "doing nothing" works better means conceding expertise
  is counterproductive.
- **Fear**: Trusting nature creates anxiety. Chemical controls feel secure.
- **Inertia**: The entire agricultural infrastructure — research institutions,
  suppliers, manufacturers — is built around the centrifugal model.
- **Misunderstanding**: Most people assume natural farming means low-yield
  primitivism, when it can match or exceed conventional yields.

## The Inevitable Convergence

Fukuoka believed centrifugal expansion could not continue indefinitely. Resource
depletion, soil exhaustion, and rising costs would eventually force a reversal.
Natural farming, with its centripetal logic of simplification, offered the
alternative — not a step backward into primitivism, but forward into a wiser
relationship with the natural world.
