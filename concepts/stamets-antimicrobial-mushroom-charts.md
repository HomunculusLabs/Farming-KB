# Stamets' Antimicrobial Mushroom Charts

Paul Stamets compiled extensive data on the antimicrobial properties of mushrooms, documenting which species show activity against specific pathogenic microbes. These charts represent a significant contribution to the evidence base for using mushrooms as sources of novel antibiotics and antimicrobial agents.

## The Mushroom Versus Microbes Chart

Stamets' primary chart catalogs mushroom species found to have specific antimicrobial effects against corresponding pathogenic microbes. Most listed microbes are pathogens to both animals (humans) and mushrooms.

### Key Species with Broad Antimicrobial Activity

- **Ganoderma lucidum** (reishi): active against multiple bacteria and fungi. One of the most extensively studied medicinal mushrooms.
- **Ganoderma applanatum** (artist conk): broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, active against multiple categories of pathogens.
- **Coprinus comatus** (shaggy mane): notable antimicrobial activity across several microbial categories.
- **Fomes fomentarius** (tinder conk): antimicrobial properties documented.
- **Fomitopsis officinalis** (agarikon): both antimicrobial and antiviral properties, including activity against pox viruses.

### Species with Documented Antiviral Activity

- **Psilocybe cubensis**: shows activity against specific viruses
- **Psilocybe cyanescens and allies**: antiviral properties documented
- **Psilocybe semilanceata**: antimicrobial activity against specific bacteria
- **Agaricus brasiliensis** (almond portobello): multiple antimicrobial activities
- **Ganoderma species**: broad antiviral activity

## The Antibacterial Assay Results

Stamets presents data from cold-water extracts of mycelial exudates (secondary metabolites) tested against *Escherichia coli* O157:H7 and *Staphylococcus aureus*. The charts show colony-forming units (CFUs) per gram plummeting from more than 100,000,000 to the 1,000-10,000 CFU range in 48-72 hours, equivalent to more than 99.99% inhibition. The three most antibacterially active species were:

1. **Oyster mushroom** (*Pleurotus ostreatus*)
2. **Birch polypore** (*Piptoporus betulinus*)
3. **Agarikon** (*Fomitopsis officinalis*)

These results were replicated across both bacterial targets, suggesting broad-spectrum antibacterial potential.

Key research findings supporting the chart:
- Suzuki et al. (1990): discovered antiviral water-soluble lignin in shiitake mycelium extract
- Collins and Ng (1997): identified polysaccharopeptide from turkey tail inhibiting HIV type 1
- Wang and Ng (2000): isolated ubiquitin-like glycoprotein from oyster mushroom inhibiting HIV
- Piraino and Brandt (1999): derivatives of *Cortinarius caperatus* (gypsy mushroom) inhibit varicella zoster (shingles), influenza A, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)
- Eo et al. (1999, 2000): antiviral activity in methanol-soluble fractions of reishi (*Ganoderma lucidum*) selectively inhibiting herpes simplex 1 & 2 and vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV)

## Specific Antibiotics Isolated from Mushrooms

Stamets catalogs notable antibiotic compounds discovered in mushrooms:

- **Calvacin** from giant puffballs (*Calvatia gigantea*)
- **Armillaric acid** from honey mushrooms (*Armillaria mellea*)
- **Campestrin** from meadow mushrooms (*Agaricus campestris*)
- **Coprinol** from inky caps (*Coprinus* species)
- **Coriolin** from turkey tail mushrooms (*Trametes versicolor* = *Coriolus versicolor*)
- **Cortinellin** from shiitake (*Lentinula edodes*)
- **Ganomvein** from reishi (*Ganoderma lucidum*)
- **Agaricin** from agarikon (*Fomitopsis officinalis*)
- **Sparassol** from cauliflower mushrooms (*Sparassis crispa*)

A Merck team in Spain published an extensive survey, "Screening of Basidiomycetes for Antimicrobial Activities" (Suay et al. 2000), assessing the antibacterial activities of 204 mushroom species, confirming that many mushrooms target specific species of bacteria.

## Polyporus umbellatus and Malaria

Lovy et al. (1999) found that Stamets' cultivated strain of zhu ling (*Polyporus umbellatus*) was 100% effective in vitro against the malarial parasite *Plasmodium falciparum*. This polypore mushroom, used in traditional Chinese medicine, could have significant applications in malaria-endemic regions. Mycomulching with this species around infected swamps could reduce background parasite populations.

## The Mechanism of Antimicrobial Action

Mushrooms produce antimicrobial compounds as part of their competitive strategy in microbially rich environments. Their mycelia secrete unique mixes of extracellular enzymes and acids, along with specific antibiotics, to create favorable conditions for their own growth while suppressing competitors. This is particularly important for saprophytic species that must defend their decomposition territory against bacterial colonization.

The mycelial cell surface "sweats out" antibiotics known as exudates or secondary metabolites. These antibiotics stop microbial parasites. Mushrooms resist bacterial and fungal rot until they release spores, age, and die. Stamets hypothesizes that mushrooms select those bacteria that ultimately favor their fungal lineage and that these interrelationships are critical to ecosystem health and fungal evolution.

## Three Lines of Defense Against Infectious Disease

Stamets argues that mushrooms offer protection from infectious diseases in at least three ways:

1. **Direct antimicrobial agents** (antibiotics) that kill or inhibit pathogens
2. **Immune system activation** -- boosting the host-mediated response, what physicians call the host-mediated response (Stamets 2003b)
3. **Mycofiltration membranes** -- custom-constructed mycelial mats that reduce infection risk from environmental sources such as sewage from feedlots and slaughterhouses

The key is matching the mushroom with the pathogen.

## Implications for Medicine

Stamets argues that old-growth forests harbor many undiscovered antimicrobial compounds. He draws a parallel to Fleming's discovery of penicillin from a moldy cantaloupe in 1941 -- a serendipitous finding from a common fungus that saved millions of lives and helped win WWII. With two-thirds of pharmaceuticals still originating from nature, mushroom-derived antibiotics represent a largely untapped pharmacological resource.

Stamets also highlights concerns about viral threats: the 2003 SARS epidemic, factory farms as potential bioterrorism launching platforms, and the need for a readily available, inexpensive, broad-spectrum antiviral antidote.

### Agarikon as a Biodefense Asset

Extracts from agarikon (*Fomitopsis officinalis*) mycelium showed potent activity against vaccinia pox and cowpox viruses through the NIH/USAMRIID Biodefense BioShield program. Stamets argues that this old-growth forest species, restricted to an endangered habitat in rapid decline, represents a potential defense against weaponized viruses -- making forest preservation a matter of national security.

## Mushroom Carcasses as Fertilizer

The antimicrobial and nutritional properties of mushrooms extend beyond their living phase. Dying and rotting mushrooms are quickly consumed by other organisms. As mushrooms decompose, the ecosystem benefits from the cycling of phosphorus, zinc, potassium, and other essential minerals back into the nutritional bank. Mushrooms have a preselecting influence on the bacteria sharing their habitat (Tornberg et al. 2003), and bacteria beneficial to trees regulate inputs and outputs of nitrogen.
