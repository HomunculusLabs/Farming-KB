## pH Indicator Plants and Site Assessment

## Plant pH Preferences in the Garden

Understanding which plants prefer acid or alkaline conditions is essential for garden planning and site assessment. While [[soil-ph]] conditions.

## Alkaline-Intolerant Plants (pH 4.5-6.0)

These plants thrive in acidic to slightly acidic soils and will struggle or fail in alkaline conditions:

- **Blueberry** — Perhaps the most famously acid-loving fruit; requires pH 4.5-5.5
- **Chicory** — Deep-rooted bitter salad green; indicates or prefers acid conditions
- **Chestnut** — Large nut tree; struggles in alkaline or limestone soils
- **Endive** — Leafy salad green related to chicory
- **Potato** — Tuber crop; more susceptible to scab disease in alkaline soils
- **Fennel** — Aromatic herb and vegetable
- **Tea** (*[[compost]], virtually all plants thrive in high-[[humus]] soils supplied with some lime at modest levels. The humus acts as a buffer, moderating pH extremes and making nutrients available across a wider range.

### Perennial Species Need More Care

While annual vegetables are relatively forgiving when grown in well-mulched beds, perennial species may need more careful site selection, particularly in alkaline areas. Perennials cannot be relocated as easily as annuals, so matching them to appropriate pH conditions from the outset is important.

### Soil Acidification Trends

Almost all pollutants and many fertilisers tend to make soils acid over time, as does continued cropping or over-grazing. This means that regular liming is generally necessary to maintain pH balance in productive gardens. Acid-loving plants may naturally find suitable conditions in older, established gardens. Monitoring pH periodically is worthwhile, even in well-managed systems.

## Wild Plant pH Indicators

Beyond cultivated plants, wild species provide reliable pH indication:

- **Sorrel (*Rumex*)** and **oxalis** in pastures indicate compact or acid conditions.
- **Fen species** and **limestone flora** establish in alkaline areas.
- **Large snails and dense snail populations** occur only over alkaline soils or in alkaline water. No snails or only minute species are found in acid water (pH < 5.0).

## Complementary Site Assessment Methods

### Soil Depth Assessment
Observe a single tree species (e.g., [[acacia]], [[prosopis]], honey locust) across a range of sites. Height and spread differences reveal areas of deeper soils where the largest specimens grow, versus shallow soils where the same species appears dwarfish.

### Water Reserve Indicators
Deep-rooted trees requiring reliable water (large nut trees, candlenuts) indicate water-lines not associated with valleys and often stand over springs or aquifer discharge areas. In sandy landscapes, clumps of deep-rooted shrubs and trees indicate clay bases at 1-2 m depth.

### Fire Frequency Reading
East-west ridges show abrupt species changes at fire boundaries. Cross-sectional cuts of trees reveal fire scars as gum pockets or charred sections that can be counted to determine fire frequency. If tree stem sections are marked for compass directions before sampling, fire direction can also be determined.

### Frost Line Detection
Frost-susceptible species planted down a hill profile will reveal frost intensity zones. Tomatoes, bananas, and potatoes are all frost-sensitive and useful for mapping frost-lines in subtropics and deserts.

### Drainage Indicators
- **Poorly-drained soils**: Mosses, sundews, fine-leaved heaths.
- **Well-drained soils (2+ m depth)**: Large trees such as chestnuts.

### Mineral Deposit Indicators
Specific plants concentrate minerals from underlying deposits:

| Plant | Indicates |
|-------|-----------|
| Rue of Violets | Zinc |
| Catchfly | Cobalt |
| Astragalus | Selenium |
| Milk Vetch | Selenium and uranium |
| Russian Thistle | Boron |
| Alyssum | Nickel |
| Honeysuckle | Silver and gold |
| Horsetail | Gold and silica |

Leaves or humus from birch, cherry, honeysuckle, St. John's Wort, wormwood, juniper, and heather indicate lead, copper-molybdate, tungsten, and tin deposits.

### Overgrazing Indicators
- High grasshopper and pasture grub activity.
- Patches of poisonous, inedible, thorny, and unpalatable plants (Sodom apple, oxalis, capeweed).

### Macrofauna Effects
Animal activity sites — sea-bird rookeries, rabbit warrens, goose nests, owl pellet piles, large carcass decay sites, termite mounds, harvester-ant colonies — cause sudden, often long-term vegetation change. These sites indicate soil drainage quality (rabbits choose good drainage), nutrient hotspots (guano on seabird rookeries), and natural tree establishment patterns.
