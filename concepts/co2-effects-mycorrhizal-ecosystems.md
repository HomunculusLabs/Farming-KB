## CO2 Effects on Mycorrhizal Ecosystems

Elevated atmospheric CO₂ concentrations are a defining feature of anthropogenic global change,
with cascading consequences for belowground fungal communities and the soil ecosystems they
sustain. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]—arbuscular mycorrhizae (AM) and ectomycorrhizae (ECM) alike—sit at
the critical interface between plant carbon fixation and [[soil-nutrient-cycling]], making them both
mediators and responders to rising CO₂. This concept page synthesizes key findings from
Dighton's *Fungi in Ecosystem Processes* (Chapter 6) on how elevated CO₂ reshapes mycorrhizal
function, belowground carbon allocation, microbial community dynamics, litter decomposition,
and broader ecosystem-level carbon storage.

## Elevated CO₂ and Mycorrhizal Colonization

One of the most consistently observed responses to elevated CO₂ is increased mycorrhizal
colonization of host plant roots. Knight et al. (1989) demonstrated that vesicular-arbuscular
mycorrhizal colonization of western wheatgrass increased under elevated CO₂, particularly when
soil solution phosphorus was limited, suggesting enhanced belowground carbon allocation to
fungal symbionts as a plant strategy to overcome nutrient constraints. Klironomos et al. (1998)
further showed interspecific differences in arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal responses to elevated
CO₂ with *Artemisia tridentata*, indicating that CO₂ enrichment does not uniformly benefit all
fungal taxa and may shift competitive hierarchies within mycorrhizal communities.

The species-specific nature of fungal responses has important implications for plant community
composition. Fungal species more responsive to enhanced carbon supply may preferentially benefit
their host plants, potentially altering competitive outcomes and driving long-term vegetation
change. CO₂ effects on mycorrhizal function cannot be predicted from colonization metrics
alone—taxonomic identity and functional traits of the fungal partner matter substantially.

## Belowground Carbon Allocation and Root Exudation

Under elevated CO₂, plants frequently allocate a greater proportion of photosynthate belowground,
mediated in part through mycorrhizal symbioses. McMurtrie et al. (2000) developed a modeling
analysis demonstrating that changes in litter quality/quantity and root exudation under
elevated [CO₂] have significant consequences for forest growth and carbon storage. Root
exudates serve as both an energy source for mycorrhizal fungi and saprotrophs, and as chemical
signals shaping rhizosphere microbial community assembly.

The stimulation of root exudation under elevated CO₂ creates a positive feedback loop: greater
exudation fuels mycorrhizal hyphal growth, enhancing nutrient foraging capacity, which supports
continued elevated photosynthesis. Jastrow et al. (2000) documented long-term effects of
elevated CO₂ on belowground biomass and soil organic matter transformations in grasslands,
showing that enhanced belowground carbon inputs accumulate over time and contribute measurably
to soil carbon pools.

## Soil Microbial Community Responses

Soil microbiome responses to elevated CO₂ are heterogeneous and context-dependent. Hungate
et al. (2000) examined soil microbiota in two annual grasslands and found microbial community
responses to elevated CO₂ varied between sites, suggesting that baseline soil properties, plant
composition, and climate mediate microbial sensitivity. Kandeler et al. (1998) investigated
soil microorganism and root responses to elevated CO₂ and temperature in a terrestrial model
ecosystem, finding that combined treatments produced non-additive effects.

Jastrow (1996) demonstrated that [[soil-aggregate-formation]] and accrual of particulate and
mineral-associated organic matter are enhanced under elevated CO₂, with mycorrhizal hyphae
playing a key structural role in stabilizing aggregates. This aggregation physically protects
organic carbon from decomposition, creating an additional pathway for long-term carbon
sequestration.

## Fungal-Arthropod Interactions Under Elevated CO₂

Klironomos and colleagues examined how elevated CO₂ alters trophic interactions between
mycorrhizal fungi and soil arthropods. Klironomos et al. (1997) documented fungal-arthropod
responses to *Populus tremuloides* grown under enriched CO₂ in field conditions, finding that
CO₂-induced fungal community shifts propagated through to higher trophic levels. Klironomos
and Ursic (1998) demonstrated density-dependent grazing on *Glomus intradices* extraradical
hyphae by the collembolan *Folsomia candida*, with elevated CO₂ modifying these grazing
dynamics. Klironomos and Kendrick (1996) established that microfungal palatability to soil
arthropods relates directly to arbuscular mycorrhizal functioning, with grazing potentially
limiting nutrient delivery to host plants.

These trophic cascades illustrate that CO₂ effects on mycorrhizal ecosystems cannot be
understood in isolation from the broader [[soil-food-web]]. Changes in fungal community
composition alter food resources for fungivorous arthropods, which feed back on fungal
function and plant nutrition.

## Litter Quality, Decomposition, and Nutrient Cycling

Elevated CO₂ often reduces foliar litter nitrogen concentration while increasing C:N ratios,
producing litter that decomposes more slowly. Johnson et al. (2000) examined CO₂ and nitrogen
fertilization effects on decomposition and N immobilization in ponderosa pine litter, finding
that CO₂-enriched litter decomposed more slowly and immobilized more nitrogen during early
decay. This partially offsets greater litter production under elevated CO₂, creating a complex
net effect on ecosystem carbon balance. The CO₂–nitrogen interaction is critical: where
nitrogen is limiting, decomposition slowdown could promote soil carbon accumulation while
tightening nitrogen constraints on plant and microbial activity.

## Nitrogen Deposition and Mycorrhizal Community Change

Atmospheric nitrogen deposition independently alters mycorrhizal communities and interacts with
CO₂ effects. Johansson (2000) showed ammonium nitrate deposition reduced ericoid mycorrhizal
colonization of *Calluna vulgaris*, suggesting nitrogen-saturated conditions reduce plant
dependence on the symbiosis. Kären and Nylund (1997) demonstrated that ammonium sulphate
deposition shifts ectomycorrhizal community structure and reduces biomass in Norway spruce,
with sensitive species replaced by nitrogen-tolerant taxa. The combined effects of elevated CO₂
(increasing carbon supply) and nitrogen deposition (reducing plant dependence on mycorrhizae)
create opposing pressures whose net outcome depends on site-specific driver strength.

## Boreal Forest Carbon Cycling and Wildfire

Jonsson et al. (1999a, b) provided critical insights into ectomycorrhizal communities in
late-successional Swedish boreal forests and their wildfire responses. They documented ECM
continuity in self-regulating boreal *Pinus sylvestris* forests by comparing mycobiont diversity
on seedlings and mature trees, showing multi-generational persistence of mycorrhizal networks
is essential for stand resilience. Following wildfire, mycorrhizal community composition shifts
dramatically, with fire-sensitive species eliminated and post-fire colonizers dominating—
potentially altering carbon cycling trajectories for decades. In boreal systems, where cold
temperatures already slow decomposition, mycorrhizal fungi are primary agents of organic
matter breakdown; changes in fire regime interacting with CO₂ effects could fundamentally
restructure boreal carbon storage.

## Modeling Forest Responses to Global Change

Integrating the diverse effects of elevated CO₂ on mycorrhizal ecosystems requires modeling
frameworks capturing feedbacks between productivity, belowground allocation, nutrient cycling,
and decomposition. Hunt et al. (1991) developed early simulation models for climate change
effects on temperate grasslands, incorporating microbial and soil organic matter dynamics.
McMurtrie et al. (2000) extended these to forests, modeling how changes in root exudation and
litter quality cascade through soil carbon pools. These models highlight that mycorrhizal
mediation of belowground carbon flow is a critical—often underrepresented—component of
ecosystem carbon cycle projections.

## Synthesis and Key Interactions

Elevated CO₂ effects on mycorrhizal ecosystems operate through multiple interacting pathways:
(1) enhanced belowground carbon allocation stimulates mycorrhizal growth and shifts community
composition; (2) altered root exudation reshapes [[rhizosphere-microbial-communities]]; (3) reduced
litter quality slows decomposition and promotes soil carbon accrual; (4) trophic interactions
with fungal-feeding arthropods modify mycorrhizal function strength; and (5) interactions with
nitrogen deposition and disturbance regimes create context-dependent outcomes varying among
ecosystems and mycorrhizal types.
