# Mollison: Aquaculture Harvest and Fish Nutrition

Source: Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (Chapter 13, Sections 13.9 and 13.5)

## Fish Nutrition Requirements

The same care must be paid to the nutrition of fish and shellfish as to that of land species. Vitamin supply from fresh greens, fruits, and algae are essential. High-protein foods with a modest admixture of starchy foods (all in fresh condition) are requisite for healthy growth, as fertiliser is for plants. There is no substitute for fresh live food to produce high quality fish.

## Feeding Rates

Fish maintain growth by eating about 1 percent of their body-weight daily, and gain most weight (at the least waste of food) when fed at 3 percent of body-weight daily. Trials of feeding are needed for specific species and size groups, but a good rule of thumb is to feed out as much food as will be completely eaten in 15 minutes, then check on food wasted and establish a growth curve from samples taken as the fish grow.

Demand feeding, where fish themselves trigger a food supply when hungry, has advantages but may necessitate pelleted foods purchased at some 60 to 70 percent of total expense. Sub-samples of netted fish can be weighed and a weighed ration fed at 3 percent of total, allowing a 10 to 12 percent mortality in fingerlings and 40 to 60 percent in fry.

## Integrated Feeding Systems

Fish such as Tilapia and carp are commonly fed on starchy foods from adjacent crops: pumpkin, banana, sweet potato, yam, taro, beans and bean flours, grains and doughs. They also eagerly eat fallen insects, seed, and fruit from fringing vegetation, along with selected water weeds.

Tilapia, in particular, eat many fruits and edible leaves from garden weeds and vines. For this reason, market gardens and fish ponds belong together. Fish-pond water is also of good nutrient value to gardens, so the relationship is enhanced. Crop production from well-fertilised ponds may be as much as twice that from reservoir irrigation.

## Grass Carp Integration

Grass carp neatly trim fringing vegetation such as Dolichos species and save encroachment of weeds into taro, while not only mixing quite well with Macrobrachium prawns but actually increasing prawn yields without feeding them artificial food. Like Tilapia, they appreciate garden waste, plants, and fruits.

Barry Costa-Pierce has polycultures in Hawaii where ponds keeping grass carp with prawns have neatly-trimmed edges (bitten by the fish, not lawn mowers), while the prawns grow as well on grass carp fecal detritus as they do on chicken pellets in nearby monoculture ponds.

## Bringing In the Harvest

### Local Supply

There are very few areas of the western world where fresh fish is easily available locally (unless living near a city fish-market). Modest aquacultural ponds can change that, as fish of known quality and species can be locally supplied. At present, affluent nations eat about 10 percent (6.1 kg) of fish and 90 percent red meats (60.1 kg) (USA figures, 1979).

### Processing

Processing of fish on farm has many advantages. Drying, smoking, freezing, salting, and reducing to pastes and sauces are all small-scale activities. It is critical to protein production in the tropics that rapid processing is achieved given the potential for spoilage. Up to 40 percent of a catch can be lost to spoilage and damage by rodents and birds.

Ferment of fish wastes (as practiced in Indonesia) produces a protein-rich, salt-stabilised liquid sauce of great value to people on rice diets. Processing also increases on-farm economics and rural employment potential.

### Catch-and-Restock Systems

For non-carnivorous species, catch-and-restock systems can greatly increase yields over batch or total removal. Many permanent ponds are run on this basis, or that of natural breeding in the case of crayfish (when a maximum size limit is used to return large, fast-growing breeders). It is always wise to consider returning or keeping the largest fish, and eating the slightly smaller grades.

### People's Markets

It helps greatly if regional growers can establish a "people's market" on the model of organic growers, where even live fish can be offered. Variety can be built in if growers allot species to specific members, or if complex polycultures are established.

### Disease Prevention

The disease problems that build up in aquacultures are due to over-emphasis on one species or class of food and high yields, and also to a neglect of shade, wild foods, and the beneficial effects of plants on water quality. True aquacultural planning should always include diverse plant products, with emphasis on local staples, with fish as accessory to a broad product base.
## Water Quality Monitoring in Polyculture Systems

Maintaining optimal water quality is essential for healthy fish growth and disease prevention. Key parameters include dissolved oxygen (ideally above 5 mg/L for warmwater species), pH (6.5-8.5 for most freshwater fish), ammonia and nitrite levels (both should remain near zero in well-managed systems), and water temperature. Regular monitoring allows the aquaculturist to detect problems before they become crises. In [[permaculture-designers-manual-water-quality]], the emphasis is on designing systems that maintain good water quality through biological processes rather than mechanical intervention. Floating aquatic plants such as duckweed absorb excess nutrients, shade the water surface to reduce temperature fluctuations, and provide supplemental food for herbivorous fish. The integration of [[mollison-duck-and-waterfowl-integration]] with fish ponds creates a mutualistic system where duck manure fertilises phytoplankton and zooplankton that feed the fish, while the ducks control aquatic weeds and insect pests.
## Seasonal Feeding Strategies

Feeding rates and food types must be adjusted seasonally to match fish metabolic rates and natural food availability. In spring, as water temperatures rise above 15 degrees Celsius, fish metabolism increases and feeding should be gradually stepped up. During peak summer temperatures, fish may feed voraciously but oxygen levels in warm water are lower, requiring careful management to avoid overfeeding and water quality degradation. In autumn, fish should be fed heavily to build fat reserves for winter, then feeding is gradually reduced as temperatures drop below 10-12 degrees Celsius and fish metabolic rates decline. In cold climates where ponds freeze over, feeding typically stops entirely during the coldest months. Understanding the [[mollison-pdc-trellis-systems-climate-control]] helps the designer plan feeding regimes that work with seasonal rhythms rather than against them.
## Species Selection for Polyculture

Choosing complementary species for polyculture is critical to maximising pond productivity while minimising inputs. Mollison emphasises selecting species that occupy different ecological niches within the pond: surface feeders, mid-water feeders, bottom feeders, and detritivores. This niche separation reduces competition and ensures that available food resources are fully utilised across all pond zones. [[permaculture-designers-manual-fish-species-guide]] recommend combining species with different feeding strategies: for example, Tilapia (omnivorous surface and mid-water feeder), common carp (bottom detritivore), silver carp (phytoplankton filter feeder), and grass carp (macrophyte browser). The addition of prawns or crayfish as benthic scavengers further increases the productivity of the lower pond zones without competing directly with fish for food.
## Record Keeping and Growth Tracking

Mollison stresses the importance of systematic record keeping for successful aquaculture. Monitoring growth rates through regular sampling -- netting a sub-sample of fish, weighing them individually, and calculating average weights -- allows the manager to adjust feeding rates, detect disease problems early, and predict harvest timing. A simple logbook recording daily feeding amounts, water temperature, weather conditions, mortality events, and periodic growth measurements provides the data needed to optimise management over successive growing seasons. This systematic approach connects to the broader [[mollison-professional-design-practice]] of observation, assessment, and adaptive management that applies to all aspects of a permaculture system.
## Duck and Waterfowl Integration

Mollison gives particular attention to the integration of ducks with aquaculture systems, describing them as one of the most productive and low-maintenance livestock additions to a fish pond. Ducks feed on aquatic weeds, snails, insect larvae, and other organisms that might otherwise become pests, converting them directly into eggs and meat while their manure fertilises the pond. A system that combines fish ponds with [[mollison-duck-and-waterfowl-integration]] can produce eggs, meat, and fish simultaneously with minimal external inputs. Mollison recommends one duck per 10-20 square metres of pond surface, with duck houses positioned on the pond bank where duck manure is washed directly into the water by rainfall. The [[mollison-geese-grass-control-and-integrated-poultry]] model extends this concept, using different waterfowl species to manage different aspects of the pond and surrounding landscape ecosystem.
## Small-Scale Aquaculture for the Home Garden

Not all aquaculture needs to be large-scale commercial production. Mollison emphasises that even a small backyard pond of a few square metres can produce significant quantities of fish and aquatic vegetables for household consumption. A small pond stocked with a few dozen fingerlings of an appropriate species, combined with duckweed cultivation on the surface for supplemental fish food, can produce several kilograms of fish per year from a system that requires only minutes of weekly maintenance. The [[mollison-pdc-small-pond-frog-habitat-design]] principles show how even the smallest water body can serve multiple functions: fish production, wildlife habitat, water storage for irrigation, fire protection, and aesthetic enjoyment. The key is matching the scale of the aquaculture system to the available resources and the manager's capacity for observation and maintenance.

## Waste Recycling in Integrated Systems

One of the most valuable aspects of Mollison's approach to aquaculture nutrition is the emphasis on recycling wastes within the farm system rather than importing external feed. Kitchen scraps, garden waste, and [[mollison-domestic-small-animals-zone-one]] can all be converted into fish food through intermediate biological processing. Black soldier fly larvae grown on food waste provide an extremely high-quality fish feed that can replace expensive commercial pellets. Duckweed cultivated on nutrient-rich pond water or wastewater provides a protein-rich feed supplement for Tilapia and carp. Azolla, a nitrogen-fixing aquatic fern, serves simultaneously as a fish feed, a nitrogen source for rice paddies, and a biological mulch. The [[mollison-biogas-and-methane-production-from-sewage]] that Mollison describes produce nutrient-rich effluent that can fertilise both pond phytoplankton and adjacent crops, closing the nutrient loop between human waste, fish production, and food crops. This integrated approach to waste recycling transforms what conventional systems treat as disposal problems into valuable productive inputs.
## Water Quality and Disease Management

Water quality directly impacts fish health and the incidence of disease in aquaculture systems. Mollison's approach to disease management in fish ponds emphasises prevention through system design rather than treatment after outbreaks occur. Key preventive measures include maintaining adequate dissolved oxygen through aeration and photosynthesis, avoiding overstocking that stresses fish and degrades water quality, ensuring that pond edges have adequate vegetation to provide shade and refuges, and maintaining diverse plant communities in and around the pond that support the biological filtration processes that keep water clean. The [[mollison-water-filtration-methods]] described by Mollison use sand beds, biological filters, and constructed wetlands to maintain water quality without chemical inputs. When disease does occur, Mollison recommends removing affected individuals, reducing stocking density, improving aeration, and adding salt at low concentrations -- a remedy that connects directly to the [[jadam-sea-salt-and-mineral-solutions]] approaches used in plant agriculture. The fundamental principle is that healthy fish in clean water with adequate nutrition rarely succumb to disease, making system design for health more effective than disease treatment.
## Aquaculture in Permaculture Design Courses

Mollison's aquaculture teachings form a core component of the [[pdc-aquaculture-basics]] and [[pdc-aquaculture-water-systems]] modules. Students learn to integrate fish production with vegetable gardens, livestock systems, and water management in ways that exemplify the permaculture principle of multiple functions for every element. The hands-on exercises typically include designing a small integrated aquaculture system for a home garden, calculating feeding rates and expected yields, and planning the integration of duck and fish production with vegetable growing. These practical exercises give students the confidence to begin small-scale aquaculture on their own properties, starting with simple systems that can be scaled up as experience and resources allow.
## Species Selection for Different Climates

Mollison's approach to fish species selection is pragmatic and regionally adapted, emphasising species that are locally available, environmentally appropriate, and nutritionally valuable. In tropical and subtropical climates, Tilapia species dominate permaculture aquaculture due to their rapid growth, tolerance of poor water quality, and willingness to feed on a wide range of organic materials. In temperate climates, species such as carp, goldfish, catfish, and perch are more appropriate, with each offering different advantages in terms of growth rate, temperature tolerance, and feeding behaviour. The [[permaculture-designers-manual-fish-species-guide]] in the Designers' Manual provides detailed selection criteria for different climate zones, including temperature ranges, pH preferences, oxygen requirements, and compatibility in polyculture systems. Mollison consistently warns against introducing exotic species without careful consideration of their potential ecological impact, advocating instead for native species that are adapted to local conditions and pose minimal risk of becoming invasive if they escape into natural waterways.
## Processing and Value Addition

Mollison encourages aquaculturists to process their fish harvest on-site rather than selling whole live fish to middlemen, as processing captures more of the value chain and provides additional products from each fish. Smoking, drying, pickling, and fermenting are traditional preservation methods that add value while extending shelf life without refrigeration. Fish offal -- heads, bones, and viscera -- can be converted into fish meal, fish emulsion fertiliser, or compost, ensuring that no part of the harvest is wasted. The [[cotter-mushroom-products-and-value-added-applications]] approach transforms a simple fish harvest into a diverse product range that commands higher prices, serves more market segments, and creates additional on-farm employment. For commonwork-scale operations, the processing facility becomes another node in the functional stacking model, providing processing services for multiple ponds and potentially for neighbouring farms as well.
## Harvest Timing and Techniques

Mollison's guidance on harvest timing reflects the permaculture principle of obtaining a yield, but with the important caveat that the yield should be taken at the point of maximum net benefit rather than maximum gross biomass. Harvesting fish when they are still growing rapidly may produce a smaller individual fish but a higher total biomass yield per unit of feed input, because larger fish convert feed less efficiently as they approach their maximum size. The harvest scheduling approach involves monitoring fish growth rates and feed conversion ratios, then harvesting when the marginal return on additional feeding begins to decline. Selective harvesting -- removing the largest individuals while leaving smaller ones to continue growing -- is often more efficient than draining the entire pond for a single harvest, as it maintains continuous production and reduces the disruption to the pond ecosystem. Mollison also notes that harvest timing should coordinate with seasonal cycles, market demand, and weather conditions to maximise both the biological and economic efficiency of the aquaculture system.
