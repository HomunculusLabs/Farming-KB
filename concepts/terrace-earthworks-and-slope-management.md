## Terrace Earthworks and Slope Management

Terracing is one of the most fundamental earth-shaping techniques in permaculture for soil conservation on slopes. Almost every slope benefits from some form of earth-shaping, whether through simple contour banks or elaborate terrace systems that have sustained agriculture for millennia.

## Principles of Terrace Design

Hand-made slope terraces need to be narrower than machine-made systems, typically 3.5–6.5 meters (12–15 feet) wide. On very steep humid slopes, garden terraces must be kept narrow and arranged in sets of 6–8 terraces running downslope; exceeding this number risks instability. Borders between terrace sets should remain vegetated with trees to anchor soil and provide mulch material.

## Broad Contour Banks Evolving to Terraces

A natural progression in slope management begins with broad contour banks. Over time, the slow erosion of crop soils builds up behind tree-banks to form stable contour terraces. Trees serve as permanent stabilizers in this system, making it both productive and self-reinforcing.

## Mounds, Ridges, and Pits

Specialized earth-shaping techniques serve different crops:

- **Mounds** increase yield of yams significantly
- **Ridges** benefit cassava and sweet potato production
- **Pits** suit taro, arrowroot, and mulch grasses

Each technique creates a micro-environment optimized for the specific crop"s root structure and water needs.

## Wet Terrace Systems (Padi Culture)

The classical wet rice and taro terrace system continuously feeds water into the top terrace of a series. Each terrace has a drain and sump to regulate water level. Fish can be grown in the deeper sections of such terraces, adding a protein yield to the system.

In stable clay or clay-loam soils, terraces hold and infiltrate water while permitting mulch application with minimal leaching losses. Where no streams exist, **dry terraces** hold soil against erosion in cropped areas, with deep mulch keeping terrace soils moist. Where stream water is available, wet crops such as rice, taro, watercress, kangkong, and water chestnuts can be cultivated in water-level controlled paddies — the rich wet terrace culture of Asia and Oceania.

## Mulch and Tree Integration

About one-half to one-third of the total terraced area should be devoted to mulch-producing tree crops providing fodder for livestock or direct leaf and branch mulch. The ideal allocation includes:

- **Upper one-third of hills** (slopes of 30° or greater)
- **Terrace side-borders**
- **Outer faces and crowns of bunds** (walls)

This approach has sustained terrace systems for up to 5,000 years, as demonstrated by the wet terrace cultures of the [[ifugao-people]] of the Philippines.

## Specific Growing Situations on Terraced Lands

Terraces offer multiple micro-sites for cultivation:

1. **Banks and bunds** — the rim of terraces and stepped bunds for tree crops
2. **Slope faces and walls** — often planted with clumping grasses or trailing vines
3. **Trellis structures** extending out from bunds
4. **Ditches and drains** — edges planted with wet-tolerant species
5. **Steep unterraced slopes** — held by contour grass strips
6. **Flat terrace surface** — primary crop area

## Dry Terrace Crop Species

Crops suited to dry terrace cultivation include:

- **Grains:** Millet (summer), dryland rice (spring-summer), barley, wheat, rye (winter), amaranth (summer), quinoa (summer), rape/mustard (winter oils)
- **Legumes:** Lentils, peas, grams, and pulses as intercrops and nitrogen fixers
- **Fodders:** Tagasaste, [[banna-grass]], comfrey, Leucaena, crop wastes and straw
- **Garden crops near homes:** Banana, papaya, melons, chilies, peppers, cucurbits, maize, beans, sugar cane, cultivated greens, edible chrysanthemum, rosella, moringa, coconut, and mango
- **Vine crops off bund faces:** Chayote, cucurbits, beans, passionfruit, kiwifruit

## Slope Stability with Grass Strips

Contour strips of [[cymbopogon-citratus]]*), and banna grass (*Pennisetum*) combined with tree legumes can replace contour ridges entirely. These strips trap soil particles, provide mulch for trees and intercrops, and represent a cost-effective way to "terrace" even on steep slopes. Both Vetiver and lemongrass clump readily, produce few seeds (reducing invasiveness), and provide substantial mulch material.

## Essentials and Variations

Key considerations for successful terrace systems include:

- Borders and uphill steep-slope forest crops selected for mulch value and fodder
- Animal sheds (ducks, pigeons, poultry, pigs, bees) over top terraces with manure washdown systems
- In-crop mulches such as beans, Azolla, and clovers
- Staggered short terrace sets for steep slopes and high rainfall versus longer continuous series for winter-dry irrigated terraces
- Deep areas in terraces for fish, crayfish, or shellfish refuges
- Vines over terraces to aid crops like taro
- Bunds planted in clover, beans, comfrey, or lemongrass
- Splash stones or plates for falling water to prevent erosion
- Border drains to keep soil dry for mid-season crop rotation
