# Palmer on Mushroom Drying and Preservation Methods

## The Need for Preservation

Palmer gathered mushrooms fresh in summer and dried his superfluous
stock for winter use. He considered dried fungus a natural substitute
for meat, noting that neither its odor when cooking nor the gravy it
produces resembles any form of vegetable food. This was, he argued,
natural since the plant absorbs oxygen from the air after the manner
of animals, making its nutritional character closer to flesh than
to plant matter.

## Chinese Dried Fungus Tradition

Palmer spoke from personal acquaintance with Chinese culinary habits,
acquired by residence in [[shipibo-san-francisco-ceremonial-center-ucayali]], Honolulu, and Hong-Kong. The
Chinese made dried fungus a very general article of diet. He regarded
their example as proof of concept for preservation, noting that dried
mushrooms maintained their meat-like qualities and could be stored
indefinitely. This cross-cultural observation gave him confidence
that drying was the most practical method for home preservation.

## Drying Methods

Palmer dried mushrooms by simple air-drying, spreading them in a warm,
dry place. The fairy-ring champignon ([[marasmius-oreades]]) was
particularly amenable to this method, expanding again the moment it
was put in water, as if fresh-picked. He noted that mushrooms should
be gathered in the afternoon when dry, rather than in the morning
when dew was upon them. This practical advice about [[cannabis-harvest-timing-visual-cues]]
served the dual purpose of improving both immediate eating quality
and the success of subsequent drying.

## General Principles of Mushroom Freshness

Palmer insisted that mushrooms are best gathered in the afternoon when
dry. The popular notion that mushrooms must be picked in the morning
with the dew on them was, in his view, erroneous. Dew adds moisture
that accelerates [[decomposition]]. Mushrooms decay very rapidly: in a
short time a fair, solid fungus becomes a mass of maggots, honeycombed
with cells charged on warm days with the vapor of decomposition. Any
preservation method presupposes starting with the freshest possible
material.

## The Problem of Chemical Change in Stored Mushrooms

Palmer documented how mushrooms undergo chemical changes that can
render esculent varieties dangerous. His case of the Boletus
sud-tomentosus (olive-red boletus) illustrated this principle. The
very specimens by which he had eaten bushels later made him ill,
simply from being in a state of acidulous fermentation when heavily
charged with water from dew or rain. The spongy boletus substance
became acidic, exhibiting brilliant blue when pressed, cut, or
broken. The lesson was that only young specimens with substance too
solid to admit of fermentation should ever be eaten, whether fresh
or preserved.

## Sweet-Oil as a Preservative and Antidote

Palmer believed the Italians ate many dangerous fungi with impunity
because, when fresh, their properties were changed by sweet-oil
([[staycare-fungal-treatment-of-olive-oil-mill-wastewater]]). When preserved, such fungi were neutralized by pickle.
Either element rendered harmless the peculiar alkali to a
superfluity of which mushrooms owe their noxious qualities. He
prescribed sweet-oil liberally in case of mistake, recommending two
wineglasses of whiskey and sweet-oil in equal parts to neutralize
poison. He suggested omitting the whiskey if desired, or
substituting vinegar for spirits, but always taking sweet-oil
liberally.

## Pickling as a Preservation Method

Pickling served dual purposes in Palmer's system: preserving mushrooms
for long-term storage and neutralizing potentially harmful alkaloids.
He noted that preserved fungi were neutralized by pickle. This
observation aligned with his broader theory that acidic agents could
counteract the alkaline poisons found in certain mushrooms. The
combination of vinegar or other acid with the mushroom's natural
alkaloid created a compound that was harmless to the human system.

## Vinegar and the [[amanita]] Alkaloid

Palmer's experiments revealed a paradox: if an Amanita was cut in
sections and laid in vinegar, the fungus could be eaten without
danger. However, on a small dose of the infused vinegar alone, death
would follow more speedily than eating the whole toadstool raw. One
stage of absorption had been completed, making the vinegar more
potent than the mushroom itself. In one documented case, a family
member escaped death by putting vinegar on her toadstool before
eating, neutralizing the alkaloid sufficiently to cause only
unusual bowel action. Yet when vinegar was given to a poisoned dog,
symptoms worsened because the acid aided absorption of the poison.
Sweet-oil then saved the animal.

## Canned and Imported Mushrooms

Palmer was often asked about the safety of French mushrooms sold in
cans. These were the common variety in button form and perfectly
safe, although there had been cases of nettle-rash from their use.
Delicious Boleti had also been imported, stamped "Cepes a l'Huile"
and "Cepes au naturel," which Palmer considered a great addition to
any table. This commercial preservation method, while different from
home drying, demonstrated the viability of industrial mushroom
preservation.

## Palmer's General Preservation Philosophy

Palmer's approach to preservation was guided by several principles.
First, freshness at the point of harvest was paramount. Second,
understanding the chemistry of each species was essential, as
chemical changes during storage could render safe varieties harmful.
Third, acid-based preservation (vinegar, pickle) could neutralize
alkaloid poisons but required careful handling. Fourth, oil-based
methods provided both preservation and safety. His constant use of
many varieties of mushrooms for food, with daily drying of surplus
stock, had developed in him an extreme sensitiveness to the
toxicological properties of fungi. The system did not become
hardened to noxious elements; contrariwise, it repelled them and was
repelled by them in quantities a stranger to such diet would not
notice.
