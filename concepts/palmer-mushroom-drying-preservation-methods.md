# Palmer on Mushroom Drying and Preservation Methods

## The Need for Preservation

Palmer gathered mushrooms fresh in summer and dried his superfluous
stock for winter use. He considered dried fungus a natural substitute
for meat, noting that neither its odor when cooking nor the gravy it
produces resembles any form of vegetable food. This was, he argued,
natural since the plant absorbs oxygen from the air after the manner
of animals, making its nutritional character closer to flesh than
to plant matter.

## The Problem of Chemical Change in Stored Mushrooms

Palmer documented how mushrooms undergo chemical changes that can
render esculent varieties dangerous. His case of the Boletus
sud-tomentosus (olive-red boletus) illustrated this principle. The
very specimens by which he had eaten bushels later made him ill,
simply from being in a state of acidulous fermentation when heavily
charged with water from dew or rain. The spongy boletus substance
became acidic, exhibiting brilliant blue when pressed, cut, or
broken. The lesson was that only young specimens with substance too
solid to admit of fermentation should ever be eaten, whether fresh
or preserved.

## Sweet-Oil as a Preservative and Antidote

Palmer believed the Italians ate many dangerous fungi with impunity
because, when fresh, their properties were changed by sweet-oil
([[staycare-fungal-treatment-of-olive-oil-mill-wastewater]]). When preserved, such fungi were neutralized by pickle.
Either element rendered harmless the peculiar alkali to a
superfluity of which mushrooms owe their noxious qualities. He
prescribed sweet-oil liberally in case of mistake, recommending two
wineglasses of whiskey and sweet-oil in equal parts to neutralize
poison. He suggested omitting the whiskey if desired, or
substituting vinegar for spirits, but always taking sweet-oil
liberally.
