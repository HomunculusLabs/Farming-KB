# Humidity Chamber Designs for Mushroom Cultivation — Jarrold Method

## Overview

The Jarrold boil-a-bag method for mushroom cultivation requires a humidity chamber (fruiting chamber) for the fruiting stage, where colonized substrate bags are opened and exposed to conditions that trigger mushroom formation. Jarrold describes three distinct humidity chamber designs, ranging from a simple passive setup to an actively heated and humidified system for cold-climate cultivation. These designs are notable for their use of inexpensive, readily available materials and their suitability for small-scale home cultivation. The emphasis throughout is on accessibility — all components can be obtained from hardware shops, pet stores, and car accessory shops.

## Design 1: Passive Humidity Chamber (Basic Fish Tank)

The simplest humidity chamber described by Jarrold uses a standard glass fish tank as the growing enclosure. This design relies on passive humidity generation from a water reservoir at the bottom of the tank.

### Materials

- **Enclosure**: 4-foot by 2-foot glass fish tank (standard aquarium size)
- **Humidity base**: Layer of soaked hydroton (expanded clay pellets) covering the bottom of the tank. Hydroton is chosen for its excellent water retention and release properties — the clay pellets absorb water and slowly release humidity into the chamber through evaporation.
- **Sealing**: Plastic sheet or wrap placed over the top of the tank, creating a seal that retains humidity and prevents flying insects from entering the growing area.

### Operation

The passive humidity chamber requires minimal active management:

- Fill the hydroton layer with water, ensuring the pellets are thoroughly soaked but not submerged
- Place colonized substrate blocks (with bags cut down the sides) onto the hydroton base
- Seal the top with plastic sheeting
- Fan fresh air into the tank twice daily by briefly lifting the plastic seal and waving fresh air in with a piece of cardboard or similar. This provides the gas exchange (carbon dioxide removal and oxygen introduction) that developing mushrooms require
- Monitor for signs of mushroom pinning (small primordia forming on the substrate surface)
- Maintain humidity by adding water to the hydroton as needed — the hydroton will change color as it dries, providing a visual indicator

### Advantages

- Extremely low cost and simple construction
- No electrical components required
- Easy to monitor progress through the transparent glass walls
- Hydroton provides consistent, slow-release humidity that is difficult to over-saturate

### Limitations

- Humidity depends on ambient temperature and may fluctuate
- No active air circulation, which can lead to CO₂ buildup between fanning sessions
- The sealed environment can promote bacterial or mold growth if contamination is present
- Not suitable for cold environments where ambient temperatures fall below fruiting range

## Design 2: Self-Humidifying Chamber with Air Pump

The second design adds active air circulation and humidification using a small aquarium air pump, providing more consistent environmental conditions than the passive system.

### Materials

- **Enclosure**: Similar fish tank or custom plastic container
- **Water reservoir**: Small container of water inside the tank
- **Air pump**: Small aquarium air pump positioned outside the tank
- **Air stone**: Connected to the air pump via tubing, submerged in the water reservoir
- **Air delivery**: Plastic tubing from the pump, fed through a small hole near the top of the tank, connected to the air stone in the water reservoir
- **Drip lids**: Inner angled drip lids made from 2mm plastic sheet, shaped to direct condensation back into the water reservoir rather than onto the growing mushrooms

### Operation

The air pump continuously pushes air through the submerged air stone, creating bubbles that release humidified air into the chamber. This provides more consistent humidity, better gas exchange, and reduced need for manual fanning compared to the passive system.

### Construction Details

The angled drip lids are made from 2mm plastic sheet formed into V-shaped panels, positioned above the growing area to catch condensation from the tank lid. Without drip lids, water droplets fall unpredictably onto mushrooms, potentially causing waterlogging. The angled design channels condensation back into the water reservoir.

### Advantages

- More consistent humidity and reduced manual maintenance
- Better air quality through continuous gas exchange
- Protection from condensation damage via drip lids

### Limitations

- Requires electricity for the air pump (low power but not silent)
- Still dependent on ambient temperature
- Air pump noise may be a consideration for discreet growing

## Design 3: Warm Humid Air Delivery Chamber

The most advanced design described by Jarrold adds active heating, making it suitable for cold-climate cultivation or winter growing when ambient temperatures are too low for mushroom fruiting.

### Materials

- **Humidity generator**: Separate container filled with a few inches of water, fitted with a lid and a main input fan
- **Heating element**: Submersible water heater with timer control
- **Distribution ducts**: Small connection ducts that serve as outlets for the humidified warm air
- **Main input duct**: One large duct that serves as the primary air input to the humidity generator
- **Growing chambers**: Connected to the humidity generator via the distribution ducts

### Operation

The warm humid air delivery system works by actively heating water in the humidity generator and blowing the resulting warm, humid air through ducts into the growing chambers. The system operates as follows:

1. The water heater raises the temperature of the water in the generator to the desired level
2. The input fan draws ambient air into the generator, where it passes over the heated water surface
3. The air absorbs moisture and heat from the water, becoming warm and humid
4. The warm humid air is distributed through the connection ducts to the growing chambers
5. Multiple small ducts can serve multiple growing chambers simultaneously from a single generator

### Timer Setup

A critical aspect of the warm humid air system is the timer configuration for the water heater. Jarrold notes that "it may take you a few hours to set the water heater's timer to get the desired humidity temperature," indicating that the system requires careful calibration. The goal is to maintain the growing chamber at the optimal fruiting temperature and humidity for the species being cultivated. For most cultivated mushroom species, this means temperatures of 70–78°F (21–26°C) and relative humidity of 85–95%.

### Advantages

- Enables year-round cultivation regardless of ambient temperature
- Provides precise control over both temperature and humidity
- Can service multiple growing chambers from a single humidity generator
- Most consistent environmental conditions of the three designs

### Limitations

- Most complex and expensive design
- Requires electricity for both the water heater and the fan
- Higher power consumption than passive or air-pump designs
- Requires careful calibration and monitoring
- Potential for overheating if the timer or thermostat malfunctions

## Bag Preparation for Fruiting

When colonized substrate bags are transferred to the humidity chamber, the bags must be prepared to allow mushroom formation:

1. Cut the bags down the sides to expose the colonized substrate surface
2. Remove as much of the bag material as needed to expose the substrate while leaving at least 2cm of the bag bottom intact
3. The 2cm bottom remnant allows the substrate block to stand upright in the humidity chamber without sitting directly in the hydroton or water, preventing waterlogging of the substrate base
4. Place the exposed substrate blocks into the humidity chamber on the hydroton base or on a clean rack

## Monitoring and Maintenance

All three chamber designs require regular monitoring:

- **Pin formation**: Check twice daily for the first signs of mushroom primordia (small white dots or bumps on the substrate surface). Once pinning begins, the mushrooms will grow rapidly and will be ready for harvest within a few days.
- **Fresh air exchange**: Even the active systems benefit from periodic manual fanning. Twice-daily fanning is recommended for all designs.
- **Humidity monitoring**: While Jarrold's original design does not include hygrometers, modern practitioners should add a digital hygrometer to any fruiting chamber for accurate monitoring.
- **Contamination watch**: Monitor for signs of mold (colored patches on substrate) or bacterial contamination (slimy or foul-smelling areas). Remove contaminated blocks immediately to prevent spread.
- **Water level**: Check water reservoirs and hydroton moisture levels daily. Evaporation rates will vary with ambient conditions.
