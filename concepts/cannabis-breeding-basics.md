# Cannabis Breeding Basics

## Overview

Cannabis breeding is the deliberate crossing of cannabis plants to combine desirable traits from parent strains into new varieties. Whether you are creating [[marijuana-strain-guide-hybrid|hybrid]] strains, preserving [[marijuana-strain-guide-sativa|sativa]] or [[marijuana-strain-guide-indica|indica]] landrace genetics, or developing unique expressions for [[cannabis-seed-banks-and-genetics|seed bank]] distribution, understanding breeding fundamentals is essential.

## Cannabis Plant Sex and Reproduction

Cannabis is a dioecious species, meaning individual plants are either male or female (with rare hermaphroditic exceptions). Understanding sex expression is fundamental to breeding.

### Female Plants

Female plants produce the cannabinoid-rich flowers (buds) that are consumed. In commercial and personal grows, females are the desired output. They are identified by their white, hair-like pistils that emerge at branch nodes.

### Male Plants

Male plants produce pollen sacs rather than flowers. They are essential for breeding but undesirable in sinsemilla (seedless) production. Males are typically culled in commercial grows unless specifically retained for breeding programs.

### Hermaphrodites

Plants that develop both male and female sex organs. Usually caused by stress (light leaks, temperature extremes, nutrient problems). Hermaphrodites are generally undesirable as they can pollinate an entire crop. Some breeders use induced hermaphroditism (rodelization) as a breeding technique.

## Pollen Collection and Storage

### Collecting Pollen

Pollen collection requires careful timing and technique:

1. Isolate male plants in a separate space before pollen sacs open
2. Place a collection container (glass jar or paper bag) over flowering branches
3. Gently shake branches to release pollen
4. Collect pollen from the container
5. Dry pollen for 24-48 hours in a cool, dark place
6. Sift through a fine mesh (micron screen) to remove plant debris

### Storing Pollen

Properly stored pollen remains viable for extended periods:

- Short-term: Store in a cool, dark, dry location
- Long-term: Mix with flour (1:1 ratio) as a desiccant
- Optimal storage: Glass vials in the refrigerator (up to 1 month) or freezer (up to 6 months)
- Avoid moisture exposure — pollen is highly sensitive to humidity

## Making a Cross

The basic process of crossing two cannabis strains:

### Step 1: Select Parent Plants

Choose a male and female with complementary traits. The male contributes 50% of the genetic material and influences traits like vigor, pest resistance, branching structure, and terpene production. The female contributes the other 50% and determines bud structure, flowering time, and resin production.

### Step 2: Timing the Cross

The male should begin releasing pollen roughly 2-3 weeks before the female is fully receptive. This ensures pollen is available when the female's pistils are white and fully formed (most receptive for pollination).

### Step 3: Pollination

- Isolate the female plant (or individual branch) to prevent unwanted pollination
- Use a small brush or cotton swab to apply pollen to selected pistils
- Alternatively, tie a paper bag containing pollen over a branch
- Pollinated pistils turn brown within 24-48 hours
- Seeds mature in 4-6 weeks after successful pollination

### Step 4: Seed Harvest

Harvest seeds when they are dark brown or grey with dark stripes. Immature seeds are white or green and will not germinate reliably. Allow seeds to dry for 2-4 weeks before storage.

## Backcrossing

Backcrossing (BX) is the practice of crossing a hybrid offspring back to one of its parent plants. This reinforces specific traits from that parent.

### BX1 (First Backcross)

Cross the F1 hybrid back to the original parent. For example, if Strain A (female) is crossed with Strain B (male) to create F1, backcrossing the F1 to Strain A creates BX1.

### BX2, BX3, and Beyond

Each subsequent backcross to the same parent further reinforces that parent's genetics. After 3 backcrosses (BX3 or "cubing"), the offspring will express approximately 87.5% of the recurring parent's genetics.

### Purpose of Backcrossing

- Stabilize specific traits (flavor, potency, growth characteristics)
- Introduce a desirable trait into an established genetic line
- Create feminized seed lines through repeated backcrossing

## Stabilizing Genetics

Creating a stable, true-breeding strain requires multiple generations of selective breeding.

### Generations Explained

- **P (Parental):** The original parent strains
- **F1 (First Filial):** First cross between two different parents. Uniform but not stable — selfing F1s produces wide variation
- **F2:** Crossing two F1 plants together. High variation — this is where phenotype hunting begins
- **F3, F4, etc.:** Continued inbreeding with selection for desired traits
- **IBL (Inbred Line):** After many generations of selection, lines stabilize and breed true

### Phenotype Hunting

In F2 and subsequent generations, significant genetic variation appears. Phenotype hunting involves:

1. Growing many seeds (50-100+ for serious programs)
2. Documenting each plant's characteristics throughout its life
3. Selecting the best individuals expressing desired traits
4. Cloning selected plants for further evaluation
5. Using selected plants as parents for the next generation

## Feminized Seeds

Feminized seeds produce only female plants, eliminating the need to identify and remove males.

### Colloidal Silver Method

1. Apply colloidal silver solution (50-80 PPM) to female plant branches daily for 2-3 weeks
2. Treated branches develop male pollen sacs (hermaphroditic response)
3. Collect "feminized" pollen from treated branches
4. Use this pollen to pollinate another female plant
5. Resulting seeds are ~99% female (XX chromosomes only)

### Rodelization

A natural technique where a female plant is left unpollinated past its normal harvest window. In response, some plants produce male pollen sacs. This pollen contains only X chromosomes and produces female-only seeds. Less reliable than colloidal silver.

## Naming Conventions

Cannabis strain naming follows loose conventions:

- Descriptive names based on aroma, flavor, or effects (Sour Diesel, Blueberry, Pineapple Express)
- Geographic references (Hindu Kush, Thai, Afghani, Durban Poison)
- Honoring breeders or figures (Jack Herer, DJ Short Blueberry, Bruce Banner)
- Creative or humorous names (Gorilla Glue, Girl Scout Cookies, Trainwreck)
- Lineage references (cross names like Triangle Mints #23)

## Ethical and Legal Considerations

Before beginning a breeding program:

- Check [[cannabis-regulations-by-state|local regulations]] regarding seed production and plant counts
- Respect breeder intellectual property — many strains are protected
- Document your breeding program thoroughly
- Consider sharing genetics with the community through open-source initiatives
- Maintain proper [[cannabis-testing-and-quality|testing]] protocols for any distributed genetics
