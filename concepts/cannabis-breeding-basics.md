# Cannabis Breeding Basics

## Overview

Cannabis breeding is the deliberate crossing of cannabis plants to combine desirable traits from parent strains into new varieties. Whether you are creating [[marijuana-strain-guide-hybrid|hybrid]] strains, preserving [[marijuana-strain-guide-sativa|sativa]] or [[marijuana-strain-guide-indica|indica]] landrace genetics, or developing unique expressions for [[cannabis-seed-banks-and-genetics|seed bank]] distribution, understanding breeding fundamentals is essential. At its core, breeding is about manipulating gene frequencies — the ratio of desired genes within a population. The higher the frequency, the more uniform and true-breeding the population becomes. A trait is considered "fixed" when its gene frequency reaches 100%.

## Cannabis Plant Sex and Reproduction

Cannabis is a dioecious species, meaning individual plants are either male or female (with rare hermaphroditic exceptions). Understanding sex expression is fundamental to breeding.

### Female Plants

Female plants produce the cannabinoid-rich flowers (buds) that are consumed. In commercial and personal grows, females are the desired output. They are identified by their white, hair-like pistils that emerge at branch nodes.

### Male Plants

Male plants produce pollen sacs rather than flowers. They are essential for breeding but undesirable in sinsemilla (seedless) production. Males are typically culled in commercial grows unless specifically retained for breeding programs.

### Hermaphrodites

Plants that develop both male and female sex organs. Usually caused by stress (light leaks, temperature extremes, nutrient problems). Hermaphrodites are generally undesirable as they can pollinate an entire crop. Some breeders use induced hermaphroditism (rodelization) as a breeding technique.

## Pollen Collection and Storage

### Collecting Pollen

Pollen collection requires careful timing and technique:

1. Isolate male plants in a separate space before pollen sacs open
2. Place a collection container (glass jar or paper bag) over flowering branches
3. Gently shake branches to release pollen
4. Collect pollen from the container
5. Dry pollen for 24-48 hours in a cool, dark place
6. Sift through a fine mesh (micron screen) to remove plant debris

### Storing Pollen

Properly stored pollen remains viable for extended periods:

- Short-term: Store in a cool, dark, dry location
- Long-term: Mix with flour (1:1 ratio) as a desiccant
- Optimal storage: Glass vials in the refrigerator (up to 1 month) or freezer (up to 6 months)
- Avoid moisture exposure — pollen is highly sensitive to humidity

## Making a Cross

The basic process of crossing two cannabis strains:

### Select Parent Plants

Choose a male and female with complementary traits. The male contributes 50% of the genetic material (vigor, pest resistance, branching, terpenes). The female contributes the other 50% (bud structure, flowering time, resin production). The male should release pollen 2-3 weeks before the female is fully receptive.

### Pollination and Seed Harvest

Isolate the female (or individual branch), apply pollen with a brush or paper bag. Pollinated pistils turn brown within 24-48 hours. Seeds mature in 4-6 weeks. Harvest when dark brown/grey with dark stripes; dry 2-4 weeks before storage.

## Backcrossing and Cubing

Backcrossing is where you breed an individual (your special clone) with its progeny. The terminology follows a specific progression:

1. **First backcross (BX1):** Cross the F1 offspring back to the original parent
2. **Squaring (BX2):** Cross the BX1 progeny back to the same parent (now grandparent)
3. **Cubing (BX3):** Cross the BX2 progeny back to the same parent (now great-grandparent)

### The Mathematics of Cubing

Cubing works on mathematical probabilities with respect to gene frequencies. With each backcross, a larger proportion of the gene pool matches the recurring parent:

| Generation | Gene Pool Match to P1 Parent |
|---|---|
| F1 cross | 50% |
| BX1 (1st backcross) | 75% |
| BX2 (squaring) | 87.5% |
| BX3 (cubing) | 93.75% |

These numbers are calculated by averaging between the two parents: BX1 = (100% + 50%) / 2 = 75%, and so on.

### Dominant vs Recessive Traits in Cubing

The outcome of cubing depends critically on whether the trait being fixed is dominant or recessive in the P1 parent:

**Heterozygous dominant trait (e.g., Pp where P = desired):**
- Without selection: ~72% express the trait after cubing, only ~22% are true breeding
- Even with infinite backcrossing, gene frequency maxes at 50% — you never fully eliminate the recessive
- Selecting against homozygous recessive offspring before each backcross improves this to ~95% expressing and ~35% true breeding
- **Key insight:** Cubing a heterozygous source will never produce a fully true-breeding strain

**Homozygous recessive trait (e.g., pp where p = desired):**
- Without selection: ~88% express the trait AND are true breeding after cubing
- Gene frequency reaches ~94%
- This is far more effective than cubing a dominant trait

**Multi-gene traits:** Multiply probabilities for each gene. For a trait influenced by 2 recessive genes and 1 heterozygous dominant gene: 87.5% × 87.5% × 71.9% = 55%. As the number of genes increases, cubing success drops rapidly without selective pressure.

### Applying Selective Pressure

The most important factor in breeding success is the degree of selection applied:

- **Full-sib cross:** Both male and female parents are selected for the desired trait
- **Half-sib cross:** Only females are selected (males chosen randomly, common when males don't express the trait)
- Full-sib breeding to F5 with selection achieves 96% expressing a dominant trait with 80% gene frequency
- Half-sib breeding to F5 only achieves 87% expressing with 63.5% gene frequency
- For recessive traits: full-sib achieves 100% by F3; half-sib achieves 87.5% by F5

**Gene linkage warning:** Cannabis has several thousand genes on just 10 chromosome pairs. Each chromosome carries hundreds of linked genes that travel together. Selecting for one trait may unintentionally select against hundreds of others on the same chromosome. This is why experienced breeders work on one or two traits at a time.

## Generational Inbreeding (Creating an IBL)

Unlike backcrossing to a single parent, generational inbreeding crosses individuals from the same generation to increase homozygosity across the entire population.

### Generational Progression

| Generation | What Happens |
|---|---|
| **P (Parental)** | Original parent strains |
| **F1** | First cross — uniform but not stable; selfing produces wide variation |
| **F2** | Cross two F1 plants — high variation appears; phenotype hunting begins |
| **F3–F5** | Continued inbreeding with selection for desired traits |
| **IBL** | After many generations of selection, lines stabilize and breed true |

### Phenotype Hunting

In F2 and subsequent generations, significant genetic variation appears:

1. Grow many seeds (50-100+ for serious programs)
2. Document each plant's characteristics throughout its life
3. Select the best individuals expressing desired traits
4. Clone selected plants for further evaluation
5. Use selected plants as parents for the next generation

### Progeny Testing

When you cannot directly observe a trait in males (e.g., flowering duration, potency), use progeny testing: breed each candidate male to several test females, grow out the offspring, and evaluate which male passed on the most desirable genes. This is essential for traits that can only be measured in female flowers.

### When the Desired Trait is Recessive

Recessive traits are actually easier to fix through generational inbreeding:

- The individual showing the recessive trait must be homozygous (pp)
- In the F2 generation, 25% of offspring will express the recessive trait
