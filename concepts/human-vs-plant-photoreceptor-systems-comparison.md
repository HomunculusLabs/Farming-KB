## Human vs Plant Photoreceptor Systems Comparison

## Defining Vision

Daniel Chamovitz's "What a Plant Knows" opens with a provocative claim: "plants
see you." While plants don't see in pictures the way humans do, they possess
sophisticated light-sensing systems that merit the term "vision." Chamovitz
constructs an analogy: imagine a person born totally blind being given first the
ability to discriminate between light and shadow, then colour. Each step would
be considered rudimentary sight. By this reasoning, plants—which can detect light
intensity, direction, colour, and duration—possess a form of vision.

## Human Photoreceptors

The human retina contains approximately 125 million rods and 6 million cones,
all in an area about the size of a passport photo. This gives the human eye an
effective resolution of about 130 megapixels—far exceeding common digital cameras
(8 megapixels) and the highest-resolution outdoor LED displays (about 10,000 LEDs
per square metre).

### Rods
Rods are sensitive to all visible light and enable vision in low-light conditions
([[medicinal-mushrooms-biological-response-modifiers]].

### Phototropins (Blue Light Sensors)
Blue light is the primary colour that induces phototropism—bending toward light.
Julius von Sachs discovered in 1864 that plants respond specifically to blue light
for directional growth. Phototropins are the photoreceptors responsible for this
response. Unlike human vision, where blue light activates both rods and blue cones,
plants use dedicated phototropin proteins that respond exclusively to blue
wavelengths for directional orientation.

### Phytochromes (Red/Far-Red Sensors)
Phytochrome, meaning "plant colour," is the photoreceptor responsible for
measuring the length of darkness (photoperiodism). It functions as a light-
activated molecular switch:
- **Red light** (~660 nm) converts phytochrome to its active form (Pfr)
- **Far-red light** (~730 nm) converts phytochrome to its inactive form (Pr)
- Only a few seconds of either colour are sufficient to trigger the switch

The last light a plant sees at the end of the day is far-red (present at dusk),
signalling the plant to "turn off." Morning red light "wakes" the plant. In this
way, phytochrome measures time since last seeing red light.
