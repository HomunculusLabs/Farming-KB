## Serpentine Soil Ecology

Serpentine soils, also called ultramafic soils, develop from the weathering
of ultramafic igneous rocks rich in magnesium and iron silicate minerals,
primarily serpentine group minerals (antigorite, chrysotile, lizardite).
These soils create harsh growing conditions that drive unique ecological
communities and have produced some of the most remarkable plant adaptations
known to science.

## Chemical and Physical Properties

Serpentine soils are characterized by several extreme properties. They have
high magnesium-to-calcium ratios (often exceeding 10:1, compared to typical
soil ratios of 2:1), low concentrations of essential macronutrients
(particularly nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium), elevated concentrations
of heavy metals including nickel, chromium, and cobalt, and low calcium
availability. Physical properties include shallow, rocky profiles with poor
water retention and high erosion potential.

These combined stressors create a selective environment that excludes most
plant species. In California, serpentine outcrops support 10-15% of the
state's endemic plant species while covering less than 1.5% of its land
area, making them biodiversity hotspots of global significance.

## Plant Adaptations

Serpentine-tolerant plants (serpentinophytes) have evolved multiple
strategies to cope with the challenging conditions. Nickel tolerance
involves enhanced root efflux, vacuolar sequestration, and chelation by
histidine and organic acids. Calcium-efficient species can maintain growth
at low calcium availability, sometimes by upregulating calcium transport
proteins or modifying root architecture to access calcium-rich microsites.
