# What Lies Beneath — Mycelial Architecture and Subterranean Networks

Beneath every forest floor and every patch of lawn lies an intricate
network of fungal threads — the mycelium — that constitutes one of the
largest and most poorly understood biological systems on Earth. These
subterranean networks connect trees, decompose organic matter, and
channel nutrients through ecosystems in ways that challenge conventional
understanding of how nature functions.

## The Scale of Mycelial Networks

Individual mycelial networks can extend for hectares. In the Blue
Mountains of Oregon, a single specimen of [[mycelial-network-resource-sharing-plants]] is not purely altruistic. The fungal network
mediates competitive interactions as well as cooperative ones.
Dominant trees may use the network to suppress competitors, and fungi
themselves can preferentially allocate resources to host plants that
provide more carbohydrates. The network is a marketplace, not a
commune, though the metaphor of interconnection remains apt.

## Soil Structure and Mycelial Contributions

Mycelium plays a critical role in soil formation and stabilization.
Hyphal threads bind soil particles together, creating stable aggregates
that resist erosion and improve water infiltration. The sticky
polysaccharide glues secreted by hyphae, called glomalin, contribute
significantly to soil organic carbon pools and can persist in soil for
decades.

In degraded soils, mycelial networks are often the first biological
structures to reestablish. Pioneer fungi colonize bare mineral surfaces,
initiating the process of soil development that will eventually support
plant communities. The role of fungi in ecological succession — the
gradual transformation of barren land into complex ecosystems — is
disproportionate to the attention it receives.

## Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling

The primary ecological function of saprotrophic mycelial networks is
decomposition. Fungi are the principal decomposers of lignin and
cellulose, the two most abundant organic polymers on Earth. Without
fungal decomposition, the world's forests would be buried beneath
mountains of undecayed wood, and the carbon cycle would grind to a halt.

White-rot fungi like [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], associated
with grasses and many crop plants, penetrate root cells and form
treelike structures called arbuscules within them. [[ericoid-and-orchid-mycorrhizae]] are essential
for orchid seed germination.

Each type represents a distinct evolutionary solution to the problem
of mutualistic nutrient exchange. In all cases, the fungus provides
mineral nutrients and water to the plant in exchange for carbohydrates
produced by photosynthesis. The specificity of these associations
varies enormously, from highly generalist fungi that associate with
hundreds of plant species to highly specialist fungi that associate
with only one.
