# Bloomfield Chapter 3: What Lies Beneath — Mycelial Architecture, Hyphal Biology, and the Subterranean Fungus

## Overview

Chapter 3 of *Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard*, titled "What Lies Beneath," is Nicholas Money's exploration of the hidden world of fungal mycelia—the vast networks of microscopic filaments that constitute the feeding phase of fungi. Opening with an epigraph from Einstein ("Something deeply hidden had to be behind things"), Money reveals that the most dramatic activities of fungi occur not in the visible mushrooms above ground but in the invisible labyrinth of hyphae beneath the surface. The chapter provides a detailed tour of hyphal cell biology, mycelial architecture, and the astonishing scale of subterranean fungal organisms—including the Armillaria individuals that rank among the largest living things on Earth.

## Invasive Growth: The Defining Fungal Process

Money begins with a provocative claim: "Like no other organisms, fungi flourish by burrowing into solid substances and transforming them into food." This **invasive growth** by filamentous hyphae is a uniquely fungal capability. Fungi penetrate the toughest leaves, woody plant tissues, skin, bone, and every other animal part. Some species even grow in granite. To illustrate the power of fungal penetration, Money notes that a rice blast fungus (*Magnaporthe*), when deliberately starved in the laboratory, will "punch holes through bulletproof vest material in its futile search for nourishment."

## Hyphal Cell Architecture

### The Eukaryotic Interior

Money provides a detailed description of hyphal cell biology, emphasizing the structural similarities between fungal and animal cells:

- **Nuclei**: Hyphae are eukaryotic cells with chromosomes housed in nuclei, just like human cells
- **Ribosomes**: Billions of these protein-manufacturing engines stud the **endoplasmic reticulum**, a membrane system connected to the nuclear envelope. Proteins forming on ribosomes are extruded into membrane pouches, "snipped and decorated in preparation for their individual functions"
- **Mitochondria**: Sinuous organelles that function as furnaces, "crumbling food molecules through the controlled chemical burn referred to as respiration." Energy is captured as smaller molecules that fuel all chemical reactions in the hypha
- **Cytoskeleton**: The cytoplasm is "suffused with a skeleton of protein strands that resembles a spider web in its multitude of interconnections and sensitivity to disturbance." Some strands act as rails for transporting new cellular materials to growing hyphal tips

### The Plasma Membrane: The Hypha as a Microscopic Battery

The cytoplasm is contained by a **plasma membrane** studded with integral proteins that function as channels and pumps. The transport of charged ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, H⁺) across this membrane **electrifies it**, turning each hypha into a "microscopic battery." This energized condition is essential for:

- Nutrient uptake against concentration gradients
- pH regulation of the extracellular environment
- Generation of electrical signals that may coordinate hyphal behavior
- Driving the turgor pressure that powers invasive growth

### The Cell Wall: Protection and Pressurization

The outer surface of the plasma membrane is sheathed with sugar-based **polysaccharides** woven into a cell wall. This wall serves three critical functions:

1. **Protection**: Shields hyphal contents from abrasion and screens out poisons
2. **Pressurization**: By restricting inflation of the cytoplasm, allows the cell to become highly pressurized
3. **Shape determination**: Determines the cylindrical form of the hypha and the location of branch points

The cell wall is not a static container but a dynamic structure that is continuously remodeled by enzymes. New wall material is deposited at the growing tip, while older regions may be selectively softened or reinforced. This plasticity allows the hypha to change direction, branch, and penetrate solid substrates.

## Mycelial Architecture: River Deltas of Fungal Filaments

Fungi grow by extending the tips of their hyphae and by branching to initiate new filaments. As the organism expands, it forms "a maze of filaments whose pattern bears some resemblance to a series of river deltas." This network is the **colony** or **mycelium**—the feeding phase of the fungus.

### Growth Patterns

When food is plentiful, the mycelium tends to adopt a **torus** (doughnut) shape by spreading outward from its origin, leaving an ever-widening circle of dead and dying cells at its core. This growth pattern is directly responsible for **fairy rings** in pastures and lawns, where annual flushes of mushrooms appear above the active perimeter of the expanding mycelium. As nutrients are exhausted at the center, the living margin advances outward like a slow-motion wave.

### Submerged and Surface Mycelia

Most often, hyphal growth is invasive, so mycelia develop *inside* their food source (substrate). In mushroom-forming basidiomycetes, the mycelium is submerged in soil or rotting wood beneath the fruiting bodies; in pathogens, mycelia sink themselves in host tissues. The individual mycelium can range from microscopic (a ringworm fungus digesting a single hair) to colossal (the Armillaria clones described below).

## Armillaria: The World's Largest Organisms

The chapter's most dramatic passages describe the giant **Armillaria** (honey mushroom) mycelia discovered in North America:

- A Michigan individual spread over **15 hectares (37 acres)** and equaled the mass of a blue whale
- A monster Armillaria in the Blue Mountains of eastern Oregon covers more than **2,200 acres** (approximately 8.9 km²)
- Based on its current rate of expansion and size, the Oregon fungus is estimated to be between **2,400 and 7,200 years old**
- Armillaria currently holds the title of **World's Largest Organism**, "soundly beating its nearest competitor, the quaking aspen"

Money notes that aspen groves can also spread as colonies, with hundreds of clones sprouting from a single tree, some patches potentially 10,000 years old. But aspens are "outclassed by the fungus in terms of living mass, because dead wood accounts for much of the incredible bulk of an aspen grove."

### Tracking Invisible Giants

Because different fungi can mingle in the same area, it is difficult to track individual mycelia by examining soil samples. Geneticists solved this problem by collecting mushrooms above ground, which duplicate the genetic makeup of their subterranean parents. By analyzing the DNA of mushrooms from different locations, researchers could map the otherwise invisible expansion of the mycelium and determine that widely separated fruiting bodies belonged to the same genetic individual.

Money adds a wry footnote: "Similar molecular genetic methods tracked the movements of O.J. Simpson in 1994, suggesting that he had not spent a quiet evening eating hamburgers with that winsome servant whose name we have all forgotten."

## Mycelial Foraging Strategies

Fungal mycelia are not passive absorbers but active foragers that deploy sophisticated strategies to locate and exploit food sources:

- **Resource capture at distance**: Mycelia can detect [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]] compounds released by decomposing material and direct their growth toward nutrient-rich patches, even from centimeters away. This chemotropic growth allows fungi to "home in" on food sources before physically contacting them.
- **[[dighton-fungal-nutrient-translocation-element-redistribution]]**: When a mycelium encounters a nutrient-rich patch, it can transport carbon and minerals through the hyphal network to support growth in nutrient-poor areas. In *Armillaria*, rhizomorphs (dense, cord-like aggregations of hyphae) function as specialized conduits for long-distance [[mycelial-network-nutrient-transport-imaging-gadd]], allowing the fungus to bridge gaps between food sources.
- **Selective resource allocation**: Mycelia can redirect resources from older, less productive regions to the actively growing margin. This "self-pruning" behavior is analogous to the way trees shed branches that no longer receive sufficient light.
- **Cross-feeding**: In wood decay, different fungal species may colonize different layers of a log, each specialized for different chemical components (cellulose, lignin, hemicellulose). Their metabolic products can become substrates for other species, creating a decomposition cascade.

## The Mycelium as a Distributed Intelligence

Money's description of mycelial architecture raises a question that has fascinated mycologists: does a mycelium constitute a single organism or a collection of semi-independent cells? The answer lies somewhere between. Septate mycelia allow cytoplasmic streaming and nuclear movement between compartments, creating a degree of physiological integration. Yet individual hyphal tips can respond independently to local stimuli—growing toward nutrients, away from toxins, or forming reproductive structures when conditions warrant.

This combination of integration and autonomy has led some researchers to describe mycelia as **distributed processing systems** analogous to neural networks. The mycelium has no central control center, yet it can coordinate complex behaviors such as the simultaneous formation of thousands of fruiting bodies across a large area. The mechanism likely involves the diffusion of signaling molecules (including calcium ions, cyclic AMP, and small proteins) through the cytoplasm, creating waves of information that propagate through the network.

## Mycelial Construction: Septate and Coenocytic Types

Mycelia are constructed in two fundamentally different ways:

1. **Septate mycelium**: The colony is divided into compartments by cross-walls (septa) that contain central pores. Cytoplasm and even nuclei can flow through these pores, creating a partially interconnected network. Nutrients can be transported rapidly through the colony, and damage to one compartment does not necessarily kill the entire mycelium. Most basidiomycetes and ascomycetes have septate hyphae.
2. **Coenocytic (aseptate) mycelium**: The hyphae lack regular septa, forming a continuous multinucleate tube. Cytoplasmic streaming distributes nutrients throughout the colony, but there are no barriers to limit the spread of damage. Zygomycetes typically have coenocytic hyphae.

In many septate fungi, the septal pores can be rapidly plugged with specialized structures ([[fungal-woronin-bodies-and-septal-pore-organization]] in ascomycetes) to seal off damaged compartments—a self-sealing mechanism that limits the consequences of physical injury or predation. Basidiomycetes use a different mechanism called **clamp connections** (see [[bloomfield-basidiomycete-mating-systems-tetrapolar-incompatibility-dikaryon-genetics]]) to maintain the dikaryotic condition while allowing cytoplasmic continuity.

## The Significance of the Subterranean World

Money's central message is that the visible mushroom is merely the "tip of the iceberg"—a brief reproductive structure erected by an organism whose true life is lived in darkness. The mycelium is where feeding, growth, competition, and most [[king-stropharia-ecological-interactions-permaculture]] occur. By revealing the architecture of hyphae and the scale of [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]], Chapter 3 establishes the foundation for all subsequent discussions of fungal ecology, pathology, and reproduction in the book.

The chapter also serves a broader philosophical purpose: to shift the reader's attention from the conspicuous to the hidden, from the macroscopic to the microscopic. Money suggests that Einstein was right—something deeply hidden *is* behind things—and that understanding the subterranean fungal world is essential to understanding terrestrial ecosystems as a whole.
