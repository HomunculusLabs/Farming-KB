# Fukuoka Pest Balance Without Chemicals

Updated: 2026-04-19

Masanobu Fukuoka demonstrates that pest problems in crops arise from imbalanced growing conditions and can be eliminated without chemical pesticides through his natural farming methods in *The Natural Way of Farming*.

This page details Fukuoka's practical experience and philosophical reasoning about pest management, connecting to his broader principles in [[fukuoka-four-principles-natural-farming]] and [[fukuoka-five-principles-natural-farming]].

## The Root Cause of Pest Problems

Fukuoka argues that insects are not the cause of crop damage but the symptom: "the appearance of a large number of insects or a massive weed growth is evidence that something is fundamentally wrong with the farming operation." Insect pests and diseases are signs that the crop is unhealthy, just as a cough is a sign of internal illness.

He frames this within his broader view in [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]]: although a thousand diseases attack plants in the fields and forests, nature strikes a balance; there never was any need for pesticides. Man grew confused when he identified these diseases as insect damage; he created with his own hands the need for labor and toil.

## How Chemicals Create Pests

Chemical fertilizers and pesticides create the very problems they claim to solve:

- Chemical fertilizers weaken crops, making them more susceptible to insects

- Pesticides kill natural predators alongside target pests, creating rebound explosions

- The cycle of pesticide application creates dependency and resistant pest populations

- Only the efficacy of fertilizers is emphasized in research; adverse effects on soil and crops are almost never reported

Fukuoka elaborates: "By killing the soil with plow and chemical fertilizer, and rotting the roots through prolonged summer flooding, farmers create weak, diseased rice plants that require the nutritive boost of chemical fertilizers and the protection of pesticides." Healthy rice plants have no need for chemicals. This is detailed in [[fukuoka-fertilizer-critique-detailed]].

## Thirty Years Without Pesticides

After thirty to forty years of farming without pesticides, Fukuoka states: "I have come to believe that, while people need doctors because they are careless about their health, crops do not indulge in self-deception. Provided the farmer is sincere in his efforts to grow healthy crops, there will never be any need for pesticides."

There have been massive local outbreaks of leafhoppers on two or three occasions over thirty years, but as the record of the Kochi Prefecture Agricultural Testing Station bears out, no ill came of a lack of control measures. The natural balance of predators and prey kept damage within acceptable limits.

## The Duck-Rice-Fish Solution

The most elegant pest management solution in Fukuoka's system is the [[fukuoka-duck-rice-fish-polyculture]]. From the standpoint of natural farming, releasing ten ducklings per quarter-acre onto the field when rice seedlings are established provides simultaneous weed control, insect control, soil aeration, and fertilization.

This is the preferred natural approach over even the chicken manure applications described in the [[fukuoka-rice-barley-step-by-step-method]].

## Natural Rice Resistance

Strong, healthy rice plants are more resistant to insect pests. Fukuoka's system produces vigorous plants through:

- Natural soil fertility rather than chemical forcing

- Proper water management (intermittent flooding, never standing water)

- Genetic diversity from seed-grown rather than grafted/cloned plants

- Ecological balance from the clover-barley-rice ground cover system

The connection to [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] and [[fukuoka-soil-building-approach]] is clear: healthy soil produces healthy plants that resist pests naturally.

## Connection to Food Chain

Fukuoka's holistic view extends to the food chain: "where there is an abundance of insects, there will also be an abundance of birds, frogs, and spiders." Rather than eliminating insects, the goal is creating conditions where natural predator-prey relationships keep pest populations in check.

He describes his own field in vivid terms: "As I walk through the paddy field, spiders and frogs scramble about, locusts jump up, and droves of dragonflies hover overhead. Whenever a large outbreak of leafhoppers occurs, the spiders multiply too, without fail. No matter that there are insect pests here. As long as their natural enemies are also present, a natural balance asserts itself."

## Weed-Pest Interactions

A field with many different types of weeds growing in the presence of grasses, legumes, and other plants is protected by a balance maintained naturally. When the farmer wipes out weeds with herbicides, insect pests that depended on the weed ecosystem lose their habitat but the crop pest species may expand into the ecological vacuum.

This connects to [[fukuoka-weed-ecology]] and the principle that all plants serve a function in the ecosystem. See also [[fukuoka-ground-cover-strategy]] and [[fukuoka-white-clover-ground-cover]].

## The Natural Balance Threshold

Fukuoka observes that while some insect damage always occurs in any field, this does not constitute a "pest problem." In his natural system, insect feeding is part of the natural cycle and does not significantly reduce yields. The threshold between "normal insect presence" and "pest outbreak" depends entirely on crop health and ecosystem balance.

He notes that twenty-percent crop damage by rice borers does not necessarily mean a twenty-percent decline in harvested grain. Yields may actually rise in spite of pest damage.

## The Research Gap

Fukuoka critiques agricultural scientists for focusing exclusively on pesticide efficacy while ignoring adverse effects: "No research is conducted on adverse effects — although some reports suggest they exist — or on the possibility that the action of chemicals may cause the creation of an environment favorable for microorganisms that attack cultivated plants."

## Nature Is Neither Violent Nor Peaceful

Fukuoka places his pest philosophy in the broader context of his view of nature: "Nature is neither living nor dead. Nor is it small or large, weak or strong, feeble or thriving. It is those who believe only in science who call an insect either a pest or a predator and cry out that nature is a violent world of relativity and contradiction in which the strong feed on the weak."

Notions of right and wrong, good and bad, are alien to nature. These are only distinctions invented by man. See [[fukuoka-what-is-nature]] and [[fukuoka-view-of-nature-and-human-hubris]].
