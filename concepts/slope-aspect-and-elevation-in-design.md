### D: Plains and Lowlands

The plains are the most open to **wind erosion** but the most resistant to water erosion. However, great damage can occur here through **salting**. Red and dusty rains and plagues of locusts result from delinquent use of the plough, heavy machinery, clean tillage, removal of trees and hedgerows, and conversion to extensive monocultures of grazing and grain cropping.

It is on the plains that water is most cheaply stored — in soil and in large surface dams — where no-tillage crops, copses, and hedgerows are desperately needed. This is where broadscale revolutions in technique can be implemented to improve soil health, reduce wind and water losses, and produce healthy foods.

## The Power of Slope

### Water Flow and Thermosiphon

Slope determines the **unpowered flow of water** from source to use point. Slope and elevation permit placement of hot air or hot water collectors **below** their storages, where the **[[thermal-belt]]** of the house site, make a remarkable difference to midslope climate and soil temperatures. Walking uphill toward a forest on a frosty night reveals the warm down-draught from high forests. If these forests are above Zones 1 and 2, they present little or no fire danger while providing erosion control and water retention.

Downslope, **reflection from dams** adds warmth. Solar collectors placed here transmit heat via air or water circulating by thermosiphoning alone, assisting house, glasshouse, or garden to function more efficiently.

### Settlement Placement

The easy, rounded ridges of non-eroded lower slopes and their foothill pediments are **prime settlement sites**. They allow:

- Filtration of wastes through lowland forest and lake
- Conversion of wastes into useful timber, trees, fruits, and aquatic life
- Access to multiple ecological zones

## Aspect and Orientation

### Aspect

**Aspect** describes the orientation of a slope relative to the sun. A slope facing the sun receives considerably more direct solar radiation than a slope facing the shade side (south in the southern hemisphere, north in the northern hemisphere). The shaded side of hills may delay thaw and thus moderate frost effects in vegetation.

In mid-latitudes, designers seek the **sunny aspect** of slopes to achieve maximum sunlight absorption for settlements and gardens.

### Sun Angle

**Sun angle** describes the arc of the sun during summer and winter months, determining:

- Eave and sill placement of windows
- Areas of shade
- Reflection or absorption angles of surfaces

In every situation (even hot deserts), some part of the system should remain open to the sun for its energy potential.

### Orientation

The final act in site planning is to **orient all buildings and structures correctly** — to face mid-sky, the sun, or the wind systems they reference, or to shelter them from detrimental factors such as cold winter winds or late afternoon sun.

## The Junction of Two Ecologies

There is hardly a viable traditional human settlement that is not sited on the **critical junctions of two natural ecologies** — whether on the boundary between foothill forests and plains, on the edge of plain and marsh, land and estuary, or some combination. Planners who place housing on a plain or plateau may have "plain planning" advantages but abandon inhabitants to failure if transport fuels dry up, leaving them dependent on a monocultural landscape for varied needs.

Successful and permanent settlements have always been able to draw from the resources of at least two environments. Similarly, any settlement that fails to preserve natural benefits — for example, by clearing all forests — is bent on eventual extinction.

## Access Roads

With zones, sectors, and slopes all analyzed, access roads can be placed:

- **High access roads** for heavy cargo or mulch delivery
- **Low access roads** for fire control

Provision for attached glasshouse, hot air collectors, reflection pond, solar pond, and shadehouse should be made at all homestead sites where climatic variation is experienced.

## Summary

Slope, aspect, and elevation transform a flat zone-sector plan into a three-dimensional design that works with gravity, thermal dynamics, and ecological gradients. The S-curve landscape profile provides a template for placing forests, agriculture, water storage, and settlement in their optimal positions. Keypoint water conservation, thermosiphon systems, thermal belts, and settlement at ecological junctions all emerge from careful slope analysis — yielding designs that are more productive, more energy-efficient, and more resilient than those imposed on flat plans alone.
