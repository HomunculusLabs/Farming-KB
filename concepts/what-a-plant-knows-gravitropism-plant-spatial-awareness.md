## Overview

Plants possess a remarkable ability to sense gravity and orient their growth accordingly. Roots grow downward (positive gravitropism) while shoots grow upward (negative gravitropism). This ability, called gravitropism (or geotropism), allows plants to maintain proper orientation even when displaced—whether a seedling is buried at an angle, a branch is bent by wind, or a potted plant [[plant-memory-and-spatial-awareness]] physiologist Haberlandt, working independently. They observed that root cap cells of plants contained specialised starch-filled organelles called amyloplasts (or statoliths) that settled to the bottom of the cell under the influence of gravity.

### How It Works

The mechanism operates as follows:

1. **Statolith settling**: In specialised cells called columella cells (in root caps) or endodermal cells (in shoots), dense amyloplasts sediment to the lowest point of the cell under gravity. These starch grains are 1.5-3 times denser than the surrounding cytoplasm.

2. **Signal detection**: The settling of statoliths against the lower cell wall or endoplasmic reticulum triggers a chain of biochemical events. The leading theory is that the pressure of statoliths on membrane-bound structures (possibly mechanosensitive ion channels or the endoplasmic reticulum) initiates a [[plant-phototropism]] hormone auxin (indole-3-acetic acid, IAA). In roots, auxin accumulates on the lower side; in shoots, auxin also accumulates on the lower side but produces the opposite growth effect.

## Evidence for the Statolith Hypothesis

### Starchless Mutants

Strong genetic evidence comes from starchless mutants of [[fungal-tropisms-and-gravitropic-responses]]. However, they still grow, just without orientation to gravity—roots and shoots grow in random directions. This shows that while gravity sensing can be disrupted, it is not essential for cell division or basic growth.

## The Auxin Redistribution Pathway

The movement of auxin in response to gravity involves a sophisticated polar auxin transport system:

- **PIN proteins**: Transmembrane proteins called PIN-FORMED (PIN) efflux carriers control the direction of auxin flow. In response to gravistimulation, PIN proteins rapidly relocalise within the cells, redirecting auxin flow to one side of the organ.

- **Auxin transporters**: AUX1/LAX influx carriers and ABCB efflux transporters work alongside PIN proteins to regulate auxin distribution.

- **The TIR1/AFB pathway**: Auxin binds to its receptor TIR1 (Transport Inhibitor Response 1), which promotes the degradation of Aux/IAA repressor proteins, thereby releasing ARF transcription factors to activate gravity-responsive gene expression.

The entire signal chain from statolith settling to visible bending occurs within minutes to hours, depending on the species and conditions.

## Root Cap: The Gravity-Sensing Organ

### The Role of Columella Cells

In roots, gravity sensing occurs primarily in the root cap, a thimble-shaped structure at the root tip. The central column of the root cap contains columella cells, each housing 10-30 amyloplasts. These are the statocytes—the gravity-sensing cells.

### Root Cap Removal

If the root cap is surgically removed, the root loses its ability to respond to gravity. However, the root cap regenerates within a few days, and gravitropic sensitivity is restored. This regeneration capacity demonstrates the robustness of the system.

### Gravity-Sensing Zone vs. Bending Zone

An important distinction in gravitropism is that gravity is sensed at the root tip but the bending response occurs in the elongation zone, which is several millimetres behind the tip. The signal must therefore travel from the sensing organ to the responding tissue—this is achieved through auxin redistribution.

## Gravitropism in Shoots

### Endodermal Statocytes

In shoots, the gravity-sensing cells are located in the endodermis—the innermost layer of the cortex, surrounding the vascular bundle. Endodermal cells contain amyloplasts that function as statoliths, analogous to those in root columella cells.

The endodermis is strategically positioned: it encircles the stem and is in direct contact with both the cortical tissue (where growth occurs) and the vascular tissue (through which auxin is transported). This anatomical arrangement allows rapid communication between gravity sensing and growth response.

## Interactions with Other Tropisms

Gravitropism does not operate in isolation. Plants constantly integrate multiple directional cues:

- **Light vs. gravity**: When light comes from the side and gravity pulls straight down, plants must integrate both signals. Young seedlings prioritise light (negative gravitropism is overridden by positive phototropism) to reach light as quickly as possible.
- **Touch vs. gravity**: Mechanical stimulation can temporarily override gravitropic responses.
- **Water vs. gravity**: Roots will bend toward moisture (hydrotropism) even when this means growing against gravity.

The integration of these multiple signals allows plants to optimise their orientation in complex natural environments.
