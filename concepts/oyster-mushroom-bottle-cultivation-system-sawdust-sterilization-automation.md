# Oyster Mushroom Bottle Cultivation System

## Overview

Bottle cultivation is the most modern and technologically intensive method for producing oyster mushrooms (*Pleurotus* spp.). Developed primarily in East Asia, the system uses heat-resistant polypropylene bottles filled with [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] substrate that is sterilized, inoculated under laminar flow, and fruited in controlled environments. The method enables year-round production with predictable yields but requires significant capital investment and advanced sterile technique knowledge.

## Container Selection

Polypropylene bottles are the standard container because they are heat-resistant and autoclavable, endurable and reusable across multiple growing cycles, and easy to handle mechanically. The system is not practical for beginning growers or those using pasteurized bulk substrates and composted materials that are not appropriate for bottling. Small particle-sized growth media such as sawdust, spent grains, and grain hulls are the favored substrate types for bottle systems.

Standard bottle sizes for oyster mushroom production range from 850 mL to 1,100 mL, with the 850 mL size being the most commonly referenced in the literature. Bottles are typically filled with 500 to 550 grams of wet substrate material. The narrow mouth of the bottle helps maintain high humidity around the developing fruiting bodies while restricting contamination entry. Bottles should be inspected before each use for cracks, clouding, or deformation that could compromise the sterilization seal or harbor residual contaminants in surface scratches.

## Floor Plan and Facility Design

Commercial large-scale bottle cultivation operations typically maintain two separate complexes: one for [[substrate-preparation-and-pasteurization]] another for growing. The ideal floor plan is designed to minimize the length of pathways between workstations, a design feature that directly reduces contamination risks. [[oyster-mushroom-non-sterilized-substrate-cultivation]] in bottles harbors neither harmful nor [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]], meaning the first organism to colonize the substrate occupies it entirely. This vulnerability to airborne contaminants demands the highest levels of hygiene throughout the operation.

Key design elements include double-door autoclaves that separate the sterilization area from the clean cooling room, skate wheel conveyors for moving bottles between stations, laminar flow hoods for inoculation, and air showers at entry points to sensitive work areas. Visitors and workers should remove shoes and wear clean clothing when entering the [[pf-tek-inoculation-and-incubation]] areas.

## Substrate Preparation

### Material Selection

[[growing-gourmet-substrate-materials-wood-types]] should be in particles small enough to run smoothly into the bottle during the filling process. Sawdust from hardwood or broadleaf trees such as poplar, alder, and cottonwood are preferred. Sawdust from softwood trees like Douglas fir can be used after a three to four month outdoor fermentation process during which phenol compounds are dissipated. Growers are advised to use mature sawdust but not overly aged material that may contain heat-resistant bacteria and substances unfavorable for mycelial growth.

### Formulation

The same substrate preparation recipes used for mushroom bag culture can be applied to bottle preparation. Rice or wheat bran, corncob, or other cellulosic materials can be supplemented to promote mycelial growth. Although optimal [[chen-maitake-substrate-formulation]] varies among strains, generally four parts basal ingredient and one part supplement are mixed, and the final moisture content should be 65%. A practical rule of thumb among growers is that one or two droplets should be released when squeezing the mixture in the palm of the hand. Some growers add lime (calcium carbonate) to improve physical structure and lower acidity.

## Bottling Process

Prepared and moisture-conditioned sawdust mixture is loaded into a bottle feeder. Through the feeder, bottles are filled with a preset quantity of mixture. Once filled, compactors press the mixture down to the pre-set height and hole-makers create vertical channels through the compacted substrate. Proper compaction gives the substrate high density, which means more nutrients are available to mycelia and thus produces a higher yield. Vertical holes in the bottle permit even distribution of mushroom spawn to the bottom, which allows for fast, even colonization. Fast depletion of nutrients in the substrate leads to early fruiting.

## Sterilization

Filled bottles are loaded into an autoclave. Commercial-scale autoclaves have double doors: one for entry and another for exit. This design virtually eliminates post-sterilization contamination risk since sterilized bottles are removed through the exit-only door into a well-controlled, dust-free cooling room. Bottles should be sterilized at 121°C (15 psi) for 60 to 90 minutes, counting from the point at which temperature or pressure reaches the target value. Growers must ensure the autoclave has enough water and fuel so that sterilization will not be interrupted. Protective gloves should be worn and sawdust litter removed from bottle surfaces that could act as [[mushroom-contamination-vectors]].

## Cooling and Inoculation

When removed from the autoclave, bottles should be cooled to 20°C in the cooling room. Slow cooling is advisable because condensation occurs when hot bottles are abruptly exposed to cool air. Some operations use a pre-cooling room before the main cooling room. Bottles ready to inoculate are moved from the cooling room through a small window into the clean bench on the skate wheel conveyor.

Before inoculation, the inside of the laminar flow hood must be disinfected with an ultraviolet lamp and 70% alcohol. The floor should be mopped with 10% bleach solution. Some large-scale farms have an air shower before entry to the inoculation area. The highest sanitation standards are required since contaminated bottles can grow weed and disease fungi.

## Spawn Run

Inoculated bottles are moved to an incubation room where [[greg-green-temperature-and-humidity-control]] are maintained at 17–18°C and 65–70% respectively. The spawn run is strain-dependent but usually takes 20 to 25 days. Ventilation time and frequency vary depending on room temperature, humidity, and the number of bottles. Growers can determine ventilation requirements by measuring CO2 concentration, with the maximum upper limit for mycelial growth being 3,000 ppm.

During incubation, it is critical to perform close examination of all bottles and look for any contamination. When unnoticed, contaminated bottles can ruin all the work involved in substrate preparation, inoculation, and incubation. A single contaminated bottle can release millions of spores that spread to neighboring bottles through the ventilation system or during routine handling. Contaminated bottles should be removed immediately and either autoclaved again before disposal or moved far from the growing facility. Before fruiting, some growers opt for removing aged mycelia on the top part of the bottle to reduce the risk of contamination and expose fresh mycelium for primordial formation.

## Fruiting

When 90% of the substrate in the bottle is colonized, bottles are brought to a growing room or exposed to a lower temperature if incubation and fruiting occur in the same space. Fruiting is induced by low temperature or high humidity as the mycelia shift from vegetative to reproductive growth. Growth parameters for mushroom development are the same as with mushroom bag or shelf [[gourmet-medicinal-mushroom-oyster-cultivation-methods]]. For *P. ostreatus*, scratching the surface of the bottle is advised for primordial induction. Light exposure of 50 to 150 lux for 8 to 10 hours daily promotes normal [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] and prevents etiolation (leggy, pale mushrooms).

Temperature shock — a sudden drop of 5 to 8°C — is commonly used to trigger the fruiting response. Relative humidity should be maintained at 85 to 95% during pinning and gradually reduced to 80 to 85% as fruiting bodies mature to minimize bacterial blotch and other moisture-related diseases. Air exchange is critical during fruiting: CO2 levels above 1,000 ppm can cause abnormal cap formation and elongated stipes, particularly in *P. eryngii* which is naturally selected for this trait but can become excessively deformed under poor ventilation.

## Emptying and Bottle Reuse

After harvest, bottles are loaded into an emptying machine (de-bottler) that first removes spent substrate and then washes the emptied bottle with air or water. The emptying area should be located far from growing facilities since [[fao-spent-mushroom-substrate-composting-recycling]] may harbor spores of weed or disease mold. Cleaned bottles are inspected, sorted, and returned to the filling station for reuse.

## Advantages and Limitations

### Advantages

- **Labor efficiency**: The system automates the entire production process, from filling through emptying
- **Year-round production**: Mushrooms can be produced continuously in microclimate-controlled rooms
- **Predictable cash flow**: Consistent, scheduled harvests enable stable business planning
- **High yield per unit area**: Bottles can be densely stacked for maximum space utilization
- **Spawn self-production**: The same system can produce both spawn and fruiting mushrooms

### Limitations

- **High initial investment**: Capital costs for autoclaves, conveyors, filling machines, and climate control systems are prohibitive for most small-scale growers
- **Requires specialized knowledge**: Skilled sterile techniques and strict hygiene practices are essential
- **Complex operation**: The multi-step process demands experienced operators who understand each stage
- **Vulnerability to contamination**: Any breach in sterile protocol can cascade through the entire production cycle
