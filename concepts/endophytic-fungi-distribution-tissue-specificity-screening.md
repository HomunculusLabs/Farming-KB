# Endophytic Fungi — Distribution Patterns, Tissue Specificity, and Screening Methods

[[endophytic-fungi]] inhabit healthy plant tissues without causing apparent disease symptoms. Their
distribution, tissue specificity, and screening methods are fundamental topics in fungal ecology.

## Spatial and Temporal Distribution

### Within-Leaf Distribution Patterns

Different distribution patterns occur within individual leaves. Carroll and Carroll (1978) first
documented that species composition varies between the leaf blade and the midvein, indicating that
endophyte assemblages are not uniformly distributed across a single leaf. Johnson and Whitney
(1989) further demonstrated that both growth stage and canopy position affect endophyte
distribution in woody hosts, adding vertical and developmental dimensions to spatial variation.

### Community Structure and Consistency

Foliar endophyte assemblages typically form a relatively consistent cohesive group dominated by a
few common species (Carroll 1995). In *Sequoia sempervirens*, endophyte composition remained
remarkably constant over a broad north-to-south distributional range (Rollinger and Langenheim
1993), suggesting strong host-driven selection that overrides regional environmental differences.

However, surveys also produce long lists of incidental species — many represented only once or
twice in several hundred samples. The number of rare or incidental species recorded is proportional
to sampling intensity. Petrini (1986) found that the distribution of rare species is influenced
more by site than by host, and that variation among sites is largely attributable to recovery of
incidental species with disjunct distributions. Bills and Polishook (1991, 1992) reported
differences in species richness among sites but observed that a core group of taxa was maintained
in constant proportions across locations. Species richness tends to increase at a constant rate
with additional sampling, eventually approaching an asymptote. The number of species per isolate
is comparable to that found in soil habitats.

### Tropical Forests

In tropical forests, host specificity is considerably harder to demonstrate than in temperate
systems, and overall species richness is markedly higher (Arnold et al. 2000). The sheer diversity
of both hosts and fungi in the tropics complicates efforts to resolve host–endophyte relationships.

### Seasonal Variation

Seasonal differences in endophyte communities have been documented in several temperate systems.
Halmschlager et al. (1993), working on *Quercus petraea*, observed that *Discula quercina*
decreased in frequency from May through September while other endophyte taxa increased over the
same period. Wilson and Carroll (1994) found that *Q. garryana* leaf midveins were colonized more
heavily by *D. quercina* than were leaf blades, and that infection frequency peaked in May–June
before declining through the growing season.

In contrast, Rodrigues (1994) found relatively small seasonal differences in endophyte
assemblages of *Euterpe oleracea* in Amazonian Brazil, suggesting that tropical aseasonal climates
may dampen temporal fluctuations in endophyte community composition.

## Tissue Specificity

### Leaves vs. Twigs

Distinct differences in endophyte assemblages have been found between leaves and twigs on the same
host species. This pattern has been documented in *Acer macrophyllum* (Sieber and Dorworth 1994),
*Alnus rubra* (Sieber et al. 1991), and *Quercus petraea* (Halmschlager et al. 1993).

### Outer Bark vs. Xylem

Comparisons between outer bark and xylem tissues reveal markedly different endophyte communities.
Studies on *Alnus* (Fisher and Petrini 1990), *Castanea* (Bissegger and Sieber 1994), *Quercus
ilex* (Fisher et al. 1994), *Salix fragilis*, and *Q. robur* (Petrini and Fisher 1990) all show
tissue-specific assemblages. Petrini and Fisher (1988) observed that in a mixed stand of *Pinus
sylvestris* and *Fagus sylvatica*, the most common endophytes occurred on only one host species
even when trees were adjacent. *Verticicladium trifida*, generally a conifer-associated species,
was dominant on *P. sylvestris* and only occasionally recovered from *F. sylvatica*, illustrating
strong host fidelity despite physical proximity.

### Inner Bark vs. Outer Bark

Inner bark supports lower endophyte diversity than outer bark. Outer bark harbors many species with
general host distributions that lack specificity. Inner bark shows greater host specificity; its
colonists have been termed "phellophytes" (Kowalski and Kehr 1992), with characteristic taxa
including *Tryblidiopsis pinastri* and *Phialocephala scopiformis*.

### Xylotropic Endophytes

Chapela (1989) described xylotropic endophytes — quiescent sapwood colonists found deep within
sound wood. These fungi have been documented in 55–60-year-old *Picea abies* stems (Roll-Hansen
and Roll-Hansen 1979–1980b; Huse 1981), demonstrating that endophytes can persist in woody tissue
for decades without causing visible decay.

## Effect of Tissue Age

A consistent trend across studies is that infection frequencies increase with the age of host
organs and tissues. This pattern is best observed in evergreen plants or long-lived foliage, though
it is also apparent in deciduous species and annuals.

In Douglas fir, *Rhabdocline parkeri* infection densities increase at a constant rate with needle
age, ranging from 0.2 to over 30 infections per mm². Stone (1987) demonstrated that this increase
is caused by repeated infection events over time rather than extended colonization by an initial
infection. In the palm *Euterpe oleracea*, tissue age strongly influences colonization frequency
(Rodrigues 1994). Similarly, age affects colonization of gorse (*Ulex*) stems (Fisher et al. 1986).

In *Sequoia sempervirens*, both species diversity and total infection frequency increase with
foliage age, while species evenness remains high among age classes (Espinosa-Garcia and Langenheim
1990). Barklund and Kowalski (1996) documented a sequential pattern of bark endophyte species in
Norway spruce internodes: *T. pinastri* colonizes younger and upper internodes, while *P.
scopiformis*, *M. cinerea*, *T. livido-fusca*, and *G. serpens* are found in older and lower
internodes — suggesting successional replacement over time.

## Screening Grasses for Asymptomatic Clavicipitaceous Endophytes

The most rapid method for detecting clavicipitaceous endophytes in grasses is to screen herbarium
collections. These preserved specimens provide access to large numbers of samples from diverse
localities and offer a good indication of geographic distribution patterns.

After preliminary herbarium screening, fresh collections should be made for isolation and culture
examination. Endophytes must ultimately be isolated and grown in culture to assess morphological
features or to obtain material for molecular identification.

## Screening Herbarium Specimens

### Tissue Selection and Staining

Either culm (stem) or seed tissues may be examined for endophytic mycelium. Stains include
nonacidified aniline blue (diluted for thick preparations) or rose bengal. A green interference
filter enhances contrast (Saha et al. 1988). Care should be taken to minimize damage to specimens.

### Seed Examination Protocol

1. Select 1–10 seeds and soften in concentrated HNO₃ at 60°C for 40–60 seconds.
2. Pour into 1 L cold water to halt digestion.
3. After 15–30 minutes, place on a slide in aniline blue stain.
4. Squash and examine for blue-stained mycelium associated with aleurone cells.

**Overdigestion warning:** Excessive acid causes abnormally swollen aleurone cells. Reduce
time if this occurs; smaller seeds need shorter durations. An alternative uses 5% NaOH for
8 hours at room temperature, then rinse in running tap water for 20–30 minutes.

### Culm Tissue Examination Protocol

1. Cut a 1–2 cm culm segment and split longitudinally.
2. Remove the upper half to expose the parenchyma.
3. Moisten exposed parenchyma with aniline blue or rose bengal stain.
4. Scrape stained tissue onto a slide and macerate.
5. Examine at 40× for nonbranching endophytic mycelia.

### Documentation

Each herbarium sheet examined should be labeled with: tissue examined, infection status, notable
mycelium characteristics, date, and investigator name.
