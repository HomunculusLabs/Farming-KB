## The Mexican Conjugation Zone

Mexico occupies a unique biogeographical position as a zone where northern
and southern mycobiotas converge. Northern species such as *P. caerulipes*
reach northeastern Mexico through the same type of vegetation (mesophytic
forest with *Fagus*), while southern species like *P. yungensis* reach
Mexico through mesophytic forests with *Alnus*. This conjugation explains
Mexico's extraordinary species richness.

## Notable Absences and Undercollection

The distribution data is heavily biased toward areas where mycologists are
active. Guzmán notes that "the mushrooms occur in abundance wherever
mycologists abound." Significant gaps include:

- No *Psilocybe* records from Korea, Malaysia (except Java and Sumatra),
  or Hawaii
- No records from several US states: Arizona, Colorado, Illinois,
  Maryland, Vermont, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania
- No records from southeastern Russia, Mongolia, Arabia, or Turkey
- *P. semilanceata* is unknown in Mexico despite being widespread
  throughout the temperate Northern Hemisphere

These gaps likely reflect undercollection rather than true absence, as
every intensive study of new regions has yielded previously unknown species.

## Taxonomic Identification Challenges

The authors note persistent problems with identification that affect
distribution data. Misidentification is common, and some chemical studies
were conducted on mixed collections of multiple species. The bluing
reaction remains the primary field indicator for psilocybin-containing
species, though it is not universal among neurotropic fungi and some
non-neurotropic species also blue.

The degradation of psilocybin over time complicates chemical
confirmation. Specimens analyzed 20-60 days after collection may show
no trace of indoles. Mexican indigenous users wisely discard dried
specimens older than one year, recognizing their reduced potency.
