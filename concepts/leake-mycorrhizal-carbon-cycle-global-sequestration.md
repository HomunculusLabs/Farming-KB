# Mycorrhizal Fungi and the Terrestrial Carbon Cycle

## Overview

Jonathan Leake's chapter in *Fungi in the Environment* examines
the critical but often underappreciated roles [[query-what-are-the-different-types-of-mycorrhizal-fungi]]
in global carbon cycling and sequestration. Mycorrhizal symbioses
represent one of the largest sinks for photosynthetically fixed
carbon in terrestrial ecosystems, with profound implications for
understanding carbon budgets, climate change mitigation, and plant
[[dighton-aquatic-hyphomycete-conidia-community-dynamics]]. The mycorrhizal mycelium constitutes a
significant and often dominant component of [[soil-microbial-biomass-measurement-ecological-significance]].

## Scale of Mycorrhizal Carbon Flux

Mycorrhizal fungi receive 10-20% (sometimes up to 30%) of total
plant photosynthate — carbon that would otherwise be available for
plant growth or release to the atmosphere through respiration. Given
that mycorrhizal fungi associate with the vast majority of terrestrial
plants, this represents an enormous carbon flux. In forest ecosystems,
the mycorrhizal mycelium can comprise the largest single pool of soil
biomass, exceeding even bacterial and invertebrate contributions.

## Carbon Pathways Through Mycorrhizal Networks

Photosynthate flows from plant to fungus through multiple pathways:

1. **Immediate respiration:** A significant portion of plant-derived
   carbon is rapidly respired by the fungal partner, returning CO2
   to the atmosphere
2. **Biomass incorporation:** Carbon is incorporated into growing
   mycelial networks, representing medium-term carbon storage
3. **Exudate production:** Fungi release carbon compounds into soil
   as exudates that feed other soil organisms and contribute to
   [[knf-leaf-mold-humus]] formation
4. **Necromass contribution:** When fungal hyphae die, their
   carbon-rich biomass contributes to stable soil organic matter
   pools with relatively slow turnover rates

## Ectomycorrhizal vs. Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Carbon Dynamics

The two major mycorrhizal types differ significantly in their carbon
processing:

**Ectomycorrhizal fungi (ECM):**
- Associated primarily with forest trees (Pinaceae, Fagaceae,
  Betulaceae)
- Produce extensive, long-lived [[mycelial-networks-and-intelligence]] cord
  systems that persist for months to years
- Generate substantial amounts of necromass with relatively
  resistant cell walls
- Contribute significantly to stable soil carbon pools
- Their mycelial networks can interconnect multiple trees,
  facilitating carbon transfer between individuals (the "wood
  wide web")

**[[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF):**
- Associated with the majority of herbaceous plants and many
  tropical tree species
- Produce less extensive, shorter-lived mycelial networks
- Carbon turnover is generally faster than in ECM systems
- Despite shorter residence times, the sheer ubiquity of AMF
  associations makes their total carbon contribution globally
  significant

## Mycorrhizal Contributions to Stable Soil Carbon

Mycorrhizal [[query-how-do-fungi-contribute-to-carbon-sequestration]] long-term carbon sequestration
through several mechanisms:

- **Melanin and chitin:** Fungal cell walls contain recalcitrant
  compounds (melanin, chitin) that decompose slowly, contributing
  to stable soil organic matter pools
- **[[singh-fungal-roles-in-soil-aggregate-formation]]:** Mycelial networks physically bind soil
  particles into aggregates that protect organic carbon from
  microbial decomposition
- **Glycoprotein production:** Glomalin-related soil protein (GRSP)
  produced by AMF is highly resistant to decomposition and can
  persist in soil for decades, contributing substantially to
  stable carbon pools
