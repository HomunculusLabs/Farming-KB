## The Gothic Arch Structure

Coleman's winter-harvest cold houses are standard, plastic-covered,
gothic-style hoop houses. The gothic arch profile is distinguished from
simple Quonset or hoop shapes by its pointed peak, which allows snow to
slid [[plutarch-and-cicero-on-the-eleusinian-mysteries]] on the roof.

This is a critical advantage in Maine winters where heavy snow loads
can collapse structures with inadequate roof pitch. Snow accumulates on
flat or low-pitch roofs, adding significant weight that can exceed the
structural capacity of lighter hoop houses. The gothic shape directs
snow to slide cleanly off the steeper upper sections before it can build
up to dangerous levels.

The pipe-frame construction uses galvanized metal hoo [[allegro-christianity-as-a-fertility-cult]] s or fans as a heat-storage medium,
and no buried insulation around the perimeter. The economic cold house
is the end result of a quest begun in 1970 for simple, low-cost,
user-friendly winter production.

## Dimensions and Scale

The primary production houses at Four Season Farm are 30 feet wide by
96 feet long, providing 2,880 square feet of growing area per house.
This size was chosen as the most efficient scale for commercial winter
vegetable production after Coleman's early experiments with smaller
prototypes.

The shift to larger greenhouses was driven by the need for commercial
viability. More growing area per structure means lower per-square-foot
costs for infrastructure, covering, and management.

### First Generation: 30 x 96 Feet

When Coleman first built these movable 30-by-96-foot houses in 1996,
he overengineered them, having never moved houses of that scale before.
Each rib was reinforced with a crossbar and truss support. Since the
houses are 96 feet long and Coleman wanted to ventilate without fans,
they were equipped with continuous roof ventilation.

Both the extra strength of the trusses and the roof vents were sound
decisions and the houses work well, but they cost more than Coleman
considers necessary for a winter-harvest greenhouse. Based on that
experience, subsequent houses were built to lighter specifications.

### Second Generation: 22 x 48 Feet

Newer houses are only 48 feet long by 22 feet wide (1,056 square feet).
The shorter length was partially dictated by available building sites,
but also by the decision to use end-wall ventilation rather than
continuous ridge vents.

The rule for adequate passive ventilation through end walls in the
coastal Maine climate is that tunnel length should be no more than
three times its width. Beyond that ratio, dead-air spots develop in
the center of the house where air stagnates. A third reason for
choosing shorter houses was to make them easier to move.
