## The Gothic Arch Structure

Coleman's winter-harvest cold houses are standard, plastic-covered,
gothic-style hoop houses. The gothic arch profile is distinguished from
simple Quonset or hoop shapes by its pointed peak, which allows snow to
slid [[permaculture-integrate-rather-than-segregate]] r than accum [[plutarch-and-cicero-on-the-eleusinian-mysteries]] on the roof.

This is a critical advantage in Maine winters where heavy snow loads
can collapse structures with inadequate roof pitch. Snow accumulates on
flat or low-pitch roofs, adding significant weight that can exceed the
structural capacity of lighter hoop houses. The gothic shape directs
snow to slide cleanly off the steeper upper sections before it can build
up to dangerous levels.

The pipe-frame construction uses galvanized metal hoo [[primary-metabolic-precursors-to-the-shikimate-pathway]] hored to
the ground, creating a structure that is both strong and relatively
inexpensive compared to glass or rigid-panel greenhouses. The entire
approach follows Coleman's minimalist philosophy:

- Unheated (no fuel consumption)
- No fans (passive ventilation only)
- No complicated machinery or space-age materials
- Soil itself serves as the heat-storage medium

These are completely passive structures. There is no heating system,
no water or stone ballast with [[allegro-christianity-as-a-fertility-cult]] s or fans as a heat-storage medium,
and no buried insulation around the perimeter. The economic cold house
is the end result of a quest begun in 1970 for simple, low-cost,
user-friendly winter production.

## Dimensions and Scale

The primary production houses at Four Season Farm are 30 feet wide by
96 feet long, providing 2,880 square feet of growing area per house.
This size was chosen as the most efficient scale for commercial winter
vegetable production after Coleman's early experiments with smaller
prototypes.

The shift to larger greenhouses was driven by the need for commercial
viability. More growing area per structure means lower per-square-foot
costs for infrastructure, covering, and management.

### First Generation: 30 x 96 Feet

When Coleman first built these movable 30-by-96-foot houses in 1996,
he overengineered them, having never moved houses of that scale before.
Each rib was reinforced with a crossbar and truss support. Since the
houses are 96 feet long and Coleman wanted to ventilate without fans,
they were equipped with continuous roof ventilation.

Both the extra strength of the trusses and the roof vents were sound
decisions and the houses work well, but they cost more than Coleman
considers necessary for a winter-harvest greenhouse. Based on that
experience, subsequent houses were built to lighter specifications.

### Second Generation: 22 x 48 Feet

Newer houses are only 48 feet long by 22 feet wide (1,056 square feet).
The shorter length was partially dictated by available building sites,
but also by the decision to use end-wall ventilation rather than
continuous ridge vents.

The rule for adequate passive ventilation through end walls in the
coastal Maine climate is that tunnel length should be no more than
three times its width. Beyond that ratio, dead-air spots develop in
the center of the house where air stagnates. A third reason for
choosing shorter houses was to make them easier to move.

Each hoop of the newer houses has a crossbar with a vertical support,
but these are lighter than the crossbar trusses of the first houses.
This lighter construction is supplemented by diagonal braces at each
corner of the house. The crossbar on each hoop was retained because
it is considered necessary in a mobile house to keep the hoops from
splaying during moves. The crossbars also provide overhead structure
for trellised summer crops such as tomatoes or cucumbers.

## East-West Orientation

All houses are aligned on an east-west axis. This orientation maximizes
light capture during the winter months when the sun tracks low in the
southern sky. An east-west alignment ensures that the broad south-facing
side of the gothic arch receives maximum solar exposure throughout the
short days of winter.

Since light is the limiting factor for plant growth during winter (more
so than temperature, as Coleman repeatedly emphasizes), maximizing solar
gain is essential for warming the soil and driving whatever plant growth
occurs during the cold months.

## Covering and Light Transmission

For the most part, the cold houses need only a single-layer covering of
UV-resistant plastic, whereas heated greenhouses benefit from two layers
that are air-inflated to minimize heat loss. Coleman deliberately chose
the single-layer approach to maximize light input—each additional layer
of plastic cuts out approximately 10 percent of ambient light.

In a cold climate where light is already severely limited by short
winter days, preserving every possible photon is critical. Coleman favors
greenhouse plastics with anti-drip coatings that cause condensed moisture
to form a thin film rather than individual droplets. These coated covers
not only transmit more light, but the thin moisture film also reflects
back heat waves radiating from the soil at night, helping to maintain
warmer interior air temperatures.

Growers in southern states where cold is less intense may prefer plastics
designed to block infrared input to prevent overheating. For the critical
sowing period from December 15 to February 15, when new crops are being
started in the coldest part of winter, Coleman double-covers those cold
houses to accelerate seedling establishment, even though it sacrifices
some light transmission.

## Ventilation Design

Ventilation is a critical concern for these structures, both in summer
(when overheating can damage crops) and in winter (when excess humidity
must be managed). Coleman normally begins to vent when the daytime
temperature under the inner layer reaches 70°F.

### Ridge Vents (Original 96-foot Houses)

The original long houses were equipped with continuous roof ventilation
along the ridge, allowing fan-free ventilation despite the 96-foot
tunnel length. These ridge vents were effective but quite expensive.
