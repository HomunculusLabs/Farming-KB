# Permaculture in Urban Areas

Urban [[permaculture]] applies the same fundamental [[aact-brewer-design-principles-aeration-systems-ingham]] used on broadacre properties to the confined spaces of cities and towns. While the scale changes dramatically, the core approach—maximizing [[component-analysis-and-functional-connections]] between elements, working with natural forces, and creating productive yields from every available niche—remains constant. [[comparison-backyard-food-forest-vs-urban-permaculture]] demands perhaps even more design skill than rural work, because the constraints are tighter and every square foot matters.

## Vertical Space and Microclimate

In urban settings, the primary strategy is exploiting vertical dimension. Trellises become essential infrastructure, transforming walls and fences into productive growing surfaces. Where buildings create shade situations, reflecting surfaces such as old mirrors or aluminum foil can redirect light into dark corners. One practitioner acquired hundreds of mirror strips from a mirror shop, turning a tiny house and yard into a highly productive growing space by bouncing light into otherwise shaded areas.

Greenhouses within roofs, though primarily heating systems rather than growing spaces, can produce some crops. They require active ventilation with fans and benefit from heat storage beneath the roof. Under-floor spaces, under-stair cavities, and other built-in voids offer additional microclimate opportunities. Roof turfing can support herbs and small crops on modest structures.

Ivy (*Hedera* species) serves a dual function in urban areas: as a shade plant and as external insulation. On brick or masonry buildings, a thick ivy covering can prevent approximately 70% of heat gain during spring and autumn seasons in variable climates. While less effective at preventing radiant heat loss (approximately 40%), its primary value is checking the rapid lateral flow of cold winds along building walls. Where evergreen hedges are impractical, ivy on walls provides effective wind protection.

## Plant Selection for Small Spaces

Space constraints demand careful plant selection. Slow-bearing and low-yielding plants like bush peas and bush beans should be abandoned in favor of high-producing, long-yielding crops such as lettuce, zucchini, and peppers. Glassed-in areas provide greater continuity and controllability of production. Wind shielding is critical in urban microclimates—boxed trellises that stop wind from walls significantly improve growing conditions.

## Optimal Lot Sizes

Research indicates that suburban lots between approximately one-quarter and one-half acre represent optimal sizes for food production. Lots smaller than a quarter acre tend not to produce gardens at all, while lots larger than a half acre are typically devoted to lawns rather than food production. A quarter-acre lot can produce 28% more food than the equivalent area of rural cropland, owing to the intensive management that small urban spaces invite.

Poultry can be integrated into quarter-acre suburban lots, though roosters and peacocks should be avoided due to noise concerns. The quarter-acre lot represents what Mollison considers the ideal size for home food production.

## Social Strategies for Urban Land Access

Many city residents lack direct access to land, making social strategies as important as physical design. Several proven systems address this challenge:

### The Allotment System

Common in Britain and available through community gardening organizations in America, allotments typically provide one-eighth acre plots. Britain has a particularly effective post office-based matching system: post offices maintain listings of people seeking land opposite listings of people who have land. The system has successfully matched many young gardeners with older residents no longer able to maintain their allotments. Typically, gardeners using someone else’s land provide vegetables in lieu of rent.

### Food Parks and Community Land Reclamation

In Australia, land frequently reverts to local authorities when absentee owners fail to pay rates and taxes. [[bill-mollison]] identify these neglected lots and work with local councils to transform them into community food parks. The process involves bringing in trucks for heavy planting, installing paths, water systems, sprinklers, and hoses, then formally donating the completed garden to surrounding residents in a ceremony—often attended by the mayor and press.

These donated gardens remain on council land but are effectively given to residents. After three to four years, such projects have proven sustainable. The strategy works particularly well in low-income industrial neighborhoods where residents lack resources for initial landscaping.

### Garden Clubs

Garden clubs operate as cooperatives where a large group purchases a farm through formally issued shares—often $1,000 shares with $100 down and the remainder on time. Critical requirements include public transportation access (train or bus) and overnight camping facilities with central toilet amenities. Members typically spend weekends at the farm, maintaining sheds or small caravans salvaged from urban materials.

### Farm Link

[[farm-link]] connects a small grower with 20 to 30 urban families on a personal basis. The grower guarantees to try meeting the families’ production needs, and the families guarantee to buy the crop at better-than-wholesale prices (but below retail). The group plans the entire year together, scheduling harvest times so families can prepare for processing (freezing peas, receiving apricots). Urban families often assist with labor at critical periods—planting, hilling potatoes, harvesting—and earn credit for their work. Some relationships have evolved to include small shelters for family holidays on the farm.

### WWOOF (Working Weekends on Organic Farms)

Originating in Britain and now operating in many countries, WWOOF allows city residents to pay for the experience of working and learning on organic farms. Approximately 68% of people surveyed express desire to leave urban areas but feel uncertain about their abilities. WWOOF provides hands-on experience with fencing, firewood, crop planting and harvesting, and equipment maintenance.

## The City as a Farm

A [[psilocybin-as-catalyst-for-paradigm-shift]] in urban permaculture is viewing the city itself as an existing farm with vast untapped resources:

### Urban Fruit and Nut Harvesting

Melbourne and San Francisco each contain approximately half a million citrus trees in private back yards, with most fruit falling to the ground unused. One entrepreneur in Melbourne identified thousands of chestnut trees by surveying the city, then offered owners wholesale prices ($2 per pound) for their excess chestnuts. He sold $70,000 worth of chestnuts retail in his second year, used the proceeds to buy a farm, and developed superior grafting techniques—discovering that chestnuts must be grafted according to nut color (dark brown, light tan, medium tan) for successful unions. He now sells grafted trees at $15 each and operates a seed business worth $30,000 annually.

### Urban Sheep Grazing

In Adelaide, one man runs 9,000 sheep as a suburban mowing service. Each sheep rents at $6 per week, providing fire hazard reduction on properties where professional mowing would be prohibitively expensive. The operator owns a suburban shearing shed, pickup truck, and trailer, processing sheep in lots of 100 to 200. Demand exceeds supply.

## Cooperative Processing

Group cooperative processing units allow communities to share expensive equipment. Greek and Italian immigrant communities in Australia have installed shared wine presses and fermentation vats. Members weigh in grapes and receive either juice back or a proportional share of finished wine.

In Yugoslavia, every small town hall houses a two-story brass still for community use. Residents bring fruit for weighing and receive brandy in proportion to their contribution. Town councils operate these facilities as public services.

## City Farms

City farms serve as demonstration and education sites. The 18-acre Melbourne city farm, designed by permaculturists and built by an unemployed group, demonstrates herb gardens, attached greenhouses (frames constructed from salvaged factory materials), ponds, picnic areas, and a retrofitted house. It serves as a retailer of [[permaculture-hardware-manufacturing-and-communications]]—stock ponds, windmills, and related equipment.

British city farms (46 in total) occupy deprived urban areas where children have never visited farms. They maintain milk cows, goats, geese, rabbits, and worm beds under ordinary conditions. While they approach self-sufficiency, they require a manager’s wage and many rely on voluntary staffing.

## Design Implications for Consultants

Urban permaculture consultants work more on strategy than on physical design. Key activities include:

- **Locating land**: Always available, though it requires investigation and negotiation
- **Stimulating nursery functions**: Getting early clients to establish plant supply businesses (bamboo, aquatics, quail, pigeons, guinea fowl)
- **Creating supplier networks**: Within 12 to 24 months of starting consultancy, clients should have access to all needed species locally
- **Establishing non-competitive operations**: Encouraging each client to specialize in what grows best on their property while referring other crops to neighbors
- **Regional planning**: Designing complementary, non-competitive systems across a district where each specialist has a local market

The 1979 German conference on future agricultural trends identified three key findings: a return to small specialized farms (half acre to fifteen acres), increasing demand for self-pick and wayside sales, and the prediction that organically produced products would be the only products in future demand. This direction aligns precisely with permaculture’s urban-rural linkage strategies.
