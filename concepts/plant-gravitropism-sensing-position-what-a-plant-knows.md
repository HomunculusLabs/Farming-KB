# [[plant-cell-membrane-transport-proteins-channels-carriers-and-pumps|Plant]] Gravitropism: How Plants Sense Gravity and Know Their Orientation

> **Source:** *[[what-a-plant-knows-plant-vision-and-light-perception|What]] a Plant Knows* by [[daniel-chamovitz]] — Chapter 5: "How a Plant Knows Where It Is"
>
> Gravitropism is one of the most fundamental senses in the plant kingdom, enabling plants to orient themselves relative to gravity even without eyes, ears, or a nervous system.

---

## Overview

Every plant must answer a deceptively simple question: which way is down? Unlike animals, plants cannot move to find favorable conditions — they must grow toward them. Gravitropism is the growth response to gravity that ensures roots grow downward into the soil (positive gravitropism) and shoots grow upward toward light (negative gravitropism).

As Chamovitz explores in Chapter 5 of *What a Plant Knows*, this ability represents a sophisticated form of [[plant-gravitropism-and-spatial-awareness]] that rivals many animal sensory systems in its precision and reliability. The fact that a seedling emerging from total darkness can immediately orient its root downward and its shoot upward speaks to how deeply gravity sensing is embedded in plant biology.

## The Gravity Sensors: Statoliths and Statocytes

### Statoliths (Amyloplasts)

The primary gravity sensors in plants are specialized organelles called **statoliths** — dense, starch-filled amyloplasts that act as microscopic lead weights inside gravity-sensing cells. Because of their high density relative to the surrounding cytoplasm, statoliths sediment to the lowest point of the cell within minutes of a change in orientation. This sedimentation is the physical signal that tells the plant which direction gravity is pulling.

The physics is straightforward: amyloplasts are roughly 1.7 times denser than the cytoplasm, giving them enough mass to overcome the resistance of the cytoskeletal network and settle under gravitational force. In a typical statocyte, this sedimentation covers a distance of only 10–20 micrometers — less than the width of a human hair — but this tiny movement is enough to trigger a complete reorientation of the plant's growth axis.

Mutant plants that cannot synthesize starch (and therefore lack functional statoliths) show significantly impaired [[fungal-tropisms-and-gravitropic-responses]], confirming the central role of these organelles. However, the fact that starchless mutants still exhibit some residual gravitropism indicates that statoliths, while primary, are not the only mechanism at work.

### Statocyte Cells

Statoliths reside within specialized cells called **statocytes**, which are located in two key positions:

- **Root cap columella cells:** The root cap is the protective tip of the root, and the central column of cells (the columella) contains statocytes that detect gravity and direct root growth downward. These cells are arranged in tiers, with the highest tier showing the most rapid and sensitive gravitropic response.
- **Shoot endodermis:** In shoots, the endodermal layer surrounding the vascular [[plant-tissue-systems-meristematic-ground-vascular-and-dermal|tissue]] contains statocytes that detect gravity and direct upward growth. The endodermis also contains the Casparian strip, a suberin-based barrier that may play a role in auxin regulation during the gravitropic response.

The spatial separation of these sensing tissues — statocytes at the root tip versus the endodermis along the shoot — reflects the distinct developmental strategies of roots and shoots and allows each organ to respond independently to gravity. This decentralized arrangement means that a single plant can simultaneously direct its roots down and its shoots up, maintaining proper body plan orientation at all times even as the plant grows and its geometry changes.

## From Sedimentation to Bending: The Signaling Cascade

### Statolith Sedimentation Triggers Auxin Redistribution

When a plant is tilted, statoliths settle to the new "bottom" of the statocyte within 1–2 minutes. This physical displacement is detected by the cell and converted into a biochemical signal. The prevailing model holds that statolith sedimentation interacts with the **cytoskeleton** — particularly actin microfilaments — which may anchor or tether statoliths, transmitting mechanical information to membrane-bound receptors or directly influencing vesicle trafficking pathways.

Disruption of the actin cytoskeleton with pharmacological agents can either enhance or inhibit gravitropic bending, depending on the specific agent and timing. This suggests that the cytoskeleton plays a complex modulatory role in gravity [[fungal-environmental-sensing-signal-transduction]] rather than a simple tethering function.

### PIN Proteins and Auxin Transporter Redistribution

The signal from statolith sedimentation rapidly triggers the **lateral redistribution of auxin efflux carriers**, primarily the PIN-family proteins:

- **PIN3** in root columella cells relocates from a symmetric distribution to the lower side of the cell within minutes of gravistimulation, redirecting auxin flow laterally.
- **PIN7** plays a complementary role in the shoot endodermis, redirecting auxin flow to create the asymmetric distribution needed for upward bending.
- **AUX1** (an auxin influx carrier) is also redistributed, fine-tuning auxin accumulation on the lower flank of the organ and ensuring a steep enough gradient for a robust bending response.

This rapid repolarization of PIN proteins creates an auxin gradient across the organ — higher auxin concentration on the lower side — which then drives differential cell elongation and the characteristic bending response.

## The Cholodny-Went Model

The **Cholodny-Went model** (proposed independently by Nikolai Cholodny and Frits Went in the 1920s) remains the foundational framework for understanding gravitropic bending. Though the molecular details have been refined considerably since its original formulation, the core logic has stood the test of nearly a century of experimental validation:

1. **Auxin redistributes** to the lower side of a gravistimulated organ via repositioned PIN and AUX transporters.
2. **In shoots:** The elevated auxin concentration on the lower side **promotes cell elongation**, causing the shoot to bend upward against gravity.
3. **In roots:** The elevated auxin concentration on the lower side **inhibits cell elongation**, causing the root to bend downward toward gravity.

The same hormone produces opposite effects in roots versus shoots because the two organs have different sensitivities and transcriptional responses to auxin concentration — a difference rooted in their distinct developmental programs and tissue-specific auxin response factors (ARFs).

### Root vs. Shoot Mechanisms

While the Cholodny-Went model applies broadly, roots and shoots differ in important mechanistic details:

- **Roots** respond faster (bending begins within minutes) and rely primarily on the columella statocytes. The root cap can be surgically removed and the root will temporarily lose gravitropic sensitivity until the cap regenerates, directly proving the cap's role as the gravity-sensing organ.
- **Shoots** respond more slowly and depend on endodermal statocytes. Shoot gravitropism involves a longer signal transduction chain and is more strongly modulated by other stimuli such as light, mechanical touch, and hormonal cross-talk with gibberellins and ethylene.

Additionally, the two organs differ in their dose-response curves to auxin: roots are far more sensitive, with inhibitory effects beginning at relatively low concentrations, while shoots require higher auxin levels for growth promotion. This biphasic response is central to how a single hormone can drive opposite bending directions in different parts of the same plant.

## Evidence from Space: ISS Experiments

Some of the most compelling evidence for gravity sensing comes from growing plants aboard the **International [[space-station-closed-system-aseptic-access-sterilization]]** and earlier spacecraft:

- Plants grown in microgravity fail to orient properly — roots do not reliably grow downward and shoots do not grow upward, instead growing in random directions.
- Mutant plants lacking functional statoliths are even more disoriented in space, confirming the statolith pathway's importance in normal gravity conditions.
- However, some residual orientation still occurs in microgravity, pointing to **statolith-independent gravity sensing mechanisms** that operate alongside the primary statolith pathway.
- Space experiments have also revealed that plant cell development proceeds largely normally without gravity, but spatial organization of tissues can be disrupted — particularly in the arrangement of amyloplasts and the patterning of vascular tissue.

## Statolith-Independent Gravity Sensing

Beyond statolith sedimentation, plants appear to sense gravity through additional mechanisms:

- **Protoplast pressure:** The weight of the entire cell contents pressing on the lower plasma membrane may activate mechanosensitive ion channels, triggering calcium signaling cascades.
- **Membrane tension:** Changes in tension across the plasma membrane and endoplasmic reticulum could serve as a gravity signal, similar to how animal cells detect mechanical stress.
- **Cytoskeletal strain:** The actin and microtubule networks may deform under the weight of the protoplast, triggering downstream signaling through associated proteins.

These mechanisms likely work alongside statoliths, providing redundancy and sensitivity across a range of gravitational environments — from the full 1g on Earth to the microgravity of space. Understanding these complementary pathways is an active area of research, with implications both for fundamental plant biology and for growing plants in extraterrestrial environments as part of long-duration space missions.
