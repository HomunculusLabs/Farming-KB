# Tropical House Design and Cooling Strategies

Designing houses for tropical and subtropical climates requires understanding human comfort thresholds and applying a suite of passive cooling strategies. Unlike temperate housing (where providing heat is the primary concern) or equatorial housing (where reducing heat is the sole problem), subtropical houses must handle both significant winter cold and very humid, hot summers.

## Human Comfort Parameters

Optimum comfort levels occur at dry-bulb temperatures of 20°C (68°F) in still air during winter and 25°C (77°F) in summer. Above 40% relative humidity, each additional percentage point effectively adds to perceived temperature. In wet-dry tropics where summer humidity commonly exceeds 50% and long periods of 70% humidity occur, sensible temperatures can exceed 30°C (86°F), producing heat stress.

A wet-bulb thermometer — where the mercury bulb is kept dampened by a cotton wick drawing from a water beaker — serves as a useful indicator. Below 18°C (65°F) wet-bulb temperature, people can remain fairly comfortable.

## Factors Affecting Perceived Temperature

- **Nearby radiant surfaces:** A wall or pavement exceeding 38°C (100°F) adds 1°C per degree radiated to perceived air temperature
- **Air movement:** Each meter per second of airflow above 1 m/s subtracts 1°C from perceived temperature
- **Evaporative cooling** greatly reduces heat in dry air, but high tropical humidity limits the effectiveness of sweating

Since humid tropical conditions prevent effective evaporative cooling through sweating, design must focus on dehumidifying air (primarily through cooling), casting shade, developing cool surfaces, and above all inducing cool air currents within houses.

## Five Essential Cooling Strategies

### 1. Shade

Light and heat are both excluded as incoming radiation under shade. Shade is particularly critical on massive walls or over water tanks close to homes. Over-shading trees, attached shadehouses, and wide eaves are essential in both equatorial and subtropical climates.

### 2. Transpiration

Plants assist cooling by transpiring moisture into the air. Partial shade helps this factor in understory species, except in extremely wet conditions where transpiration may be limited.

### 3. Cool Bodies

Large heat reservoirs used as water tanks, and relatively cool blocks of shaded stone, concrete, or mud brick absorb heat from the air and from warm bodies. Conversely, hot radiant bodies (unshaded masonry, asphalt) adversely affect comfort. White exterior and interior surfaces maximize reflectivity and minimize heat absorption.

### 4. Air Flows

Even low air flows from shaded areas greatly aid both transpiration and evaporative cooling. Creating air flows requires developing both relatively hotter and colder air sources and providing a cross-flow airway. Even a simple fan stirring the air aids human comfort significantly.

### 5. Removing Heat Sources

All massive cook-stoves and hot-water systems are best placed in a semi-detached kitchen in the tropics. These are commonly reached via a vine-covered shade area, are themselves shaded by palms or trees, and have wide eaves and ceiling vents for hot air escape.

## Cross-Ventilation Design

Heat can be harnessed via metal roof areas, hot-water storages, attached glasshouses, or solar chimneys to vent hot air and create updraught. This updraught acts as a heat engine drawing in cool replacement air. The essentials of good cross-ventilation are:

- Flowing air must have a **simple pathway** with no unnecessary corners
- **Large vents** must allow good volume of air through workrooms and storage areas
- Vented ceiling slopes allow hot room air to escape while cool air enters at lower levels
- Vertical shutters or sashes act as air scoops to direct breezes into rooms

## Earth-Cooled Tunnels and Pipes

The most effective cooling systems in tropical houses use a hot roof or metal chimney to draw cold air from earth-cooled underground tunnels or pipes. Cool dense air naturally flows downhill or sinks to lower levels. This cool air can be drawn into houses via positive exhaust or actively fanned into rooms.

Construction requirements for an effective cool air tunnel:

- A trench **1 meter deep** and **15–20 meters long** in the earth
- Condensed water must be drained off (ideally self-draining to a lower slope)
- A sloping floor in the trench or pipe
- The intake end should be box-screened to exclude mice and shaded by plants
- Outlets can be floor grills or a louvred cupboard opening to the pipe system

This system can open first into a pantry area or a half-buried cool store for root and fruit crops, providing food storage benefits alongside comfort cooling.

## The Subtropical House

The subtropical house must both heat and cool. For heating, it needs an insulated slab floor or Trombe wall. For cooling, it requires induced or forced cross-ventilation from a cool or shaded area to an updraught area. The design demands more careful planning than either purely equatorial or purely temperate housing.

## Storage Challenges in Humid Climates

Beyond human comfort, high humidity causes secondary problems ranging from the merely annoying (salt will not pour) to serious effects (clothes, food, film, and books mildew). Houses in humid climates therefore need both cool and warm storage areas — both of which must be dry. The cool air tunnel system can serve double duty by opening into a pantry or root cellar, while separate dry, warm storage is needed for grains and other moisture-sensitive goods.

## Shadehouse-Greenhouse Combinations

A shadehouse can be added to the poleward (cooler) side of a house and cross-ventilated to a well-vented greenhouse on the sun side. This creates a thermal-driven airflow where the greenhouse heats and vents upward, drawing cooler air through the shadehouse and living spaces — a purely passive cooling system requiring no external energy input.
