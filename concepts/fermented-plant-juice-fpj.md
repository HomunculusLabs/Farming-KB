# Fermented Plant Juice (FPJ)

Fermented Plant Juice (FPJ) is a core [[plant-tissue-systems-meristematic-ground-vascular-and-dermal]] while preserving their biological activity. The result is a dark amber liquid that contains:

- **Plant growth hormones** — auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, and ethylene precursors that regulate cell division, elongation, and differentiation.
- **Enzymes** — including amylases, proteases, and various oxidoreductases that support metabolic processes in the target crop.
- **Vitamins and minerals** — water-soluble vitamins (B-complex, C) and trace minerals drawn from the source plant tissue.
- **Organic acids** — lactic acid and other fermentation byproducts that can slightly acidify the leaf surface or soil micro-environment, favoring beneficial microbial activity.

FPJ is not a fertilizer in the conventional sense. It does not supply large quantities of NPK. Instead, it acts as a biostimulant — enhancing the plant's own ability to take up and use nutrients that are already present in the soil.

## Role in the Nutritive Cycle

FPJ is closely tied to the [[permaculture-plants-aquatic-and-wetland-species]] systems), and various leguminous plants (nitrogen-fixing species produce especially rich FPJ). The key is always freshness and vigor — wilted or stressed plant material will produce inferior FPJ.

## How FPJ Is Made

### Materials

- Fresh plant material (tips and young leaves)
- Brown sugar or crude sugar (jaggery/molasses can substitute)
- A clean ceramic, glass, or food-grade plastic container
- A weight or press to compress the mixture

### Sugar Ratio

The standard ratio is **1:1 by weight** — equal parts plant material and brown sugar. This ratio is critical. The sugar draws moisture out of the plant tissue through osmotic pressure, creating a liquid extract while simultaneously providing the carbohydrate substrate that microorganisms need for fermentation. Too little sugar and the plant material may rot rather than ferment; too much sugar can overly slow microbial activity and produce a syrup that is difficult to dilute properly.

Brown sugar is preferred over refined white sugar because it contains trace minerals (particularly magnesium, potassium, and calcium) that support microbial activity during fermentation. Jaggery or muscovado sugar are excellent choices. Avoid chemically refined or bleached sugars.

### Fermentation Process

1. **Preparation**: Collect plant material in the early morning. Weigh it and measure an equal weight of brown sugar. Have all materials ready before starting to minimize delays.
2. **Mixing**: Alternate layers of plant material and brown sugar in the container, finishing with a layer of sugar on top. This ensures even distribution and contact between plant surfaces and sugar crystals.
3. **Pressing**: Press the mixture down firmly to eliminate air pockets. Oxygen exposure promotes spoilage organisms rather than the desired lactic acid fermentation. Fill the container to roughly two-thirds capacity to leave room for expansion.
4. **Covering**: Place a breathable cloth or paper over the container opening. Do not seal airtight — the fermentation produces gases that must escape. Weight the cover down to keep insects out.
5. **Fermentation**: Leave the container in a cool, shaded location for **7 days**. Fermentation is faster in warm weather (5–7 days) and slower in cold weather (up to 10–14 days). The mixture should produce a brownish liquid as the sugar extracts plant sap. Gentle bubbling is normal and indicates active fermentation.
6. **Extraction**: After 7 days, strain the liquid through a clean cloth or fine sieve. This liquid is the FPJ. The residual plant material can be added to compost as a nutrient-rich amendment.
7. **Storage**: Store FPJ in a glass or plastic container in a cool, dark place. When properly stored, FPJ can last 6–12 months. Refrigeration extends shelf life. Sediment settling at the bottom is normal and harmless — simply shake before use.

The fermentation relies on naturally occurring microorganisms — particularly lactic acid bacteria — present on the plant surfaces. This is the same principle behind [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] (lush but weak), reduce FPJ application. If growth is sluggish, increase frequency slightly.
- **Storage and quality**: Discard FPJ that develops an off-odor (putrid rather than sweet-sour), mold on the surface, or unusual coloration. Properly made FPJ has a pleasant sweet-sour smell and a dark amber to brown color. The liquid should be clear to slightly cloudy, never murky or opaque. Label each batch with the source plant species and harvest date for future reference.
