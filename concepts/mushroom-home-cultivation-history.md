## Mushroom Home Cultivation History

The development of reliable methods for cultivating [magic mushrooms at home was
one of the key factors in their global dissemination. From the first laboratory successes of [[roger-heim]] to the revolutionary techniques of [Terence McKenna and the notorious

## Early Cultivation: Roger Heim and Albert Hofmann

[[roger-heim]], Professor of Mycology at the Musee National d'Histoire Naturelle in Paris, was
the first person ever to culture [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-emerging-trend-europe]] in the laboratory. He
accompanied [Gordon Wasson to Mexico in 1956 to classify and describe the species
used in Mazatec ceremonies. Attempts by his own laboratory and by American pharmaceutical companies (Merck;
Smith, Kline and French) to isolate the active ingredients had failed, prompting him to approach

## The McKenna Brothers and the Colombian Discovery

The breakthrough in home cultivation came from [Terence McKenna (1946-2000) and his brother
Dennis. In the early 1970s, Terence was travelling in the Colombian Amazon when he learned of a local
hallucinogenic mushroom, [[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]], and collected spore prints. The species
grew abundantly on cow dung in the tropical lowlands, making it far easier to cultivate than the Mexican
highland species that had previously dominated scientific attention.

After a messy break-up with Dennis left him depressed and plagued by migraines, all thought of mushrooms was
put on hold. Then one day he went to clear out the greenhouse, expecting it to be overrun with detritus and
mould. Instead, he discovered rows of cubensis mushrooms sprouting merrily from the abandoned experiments.
"I was neck deep in gold!" he wrote. "The elf legions of hyperspace had ridden to my rescue again."

## Psilocybin: Magic Mushroom Grower's Guide (1976)

Working with Dennis at the Botanical Garden in Berkeley, Terence McKenna adapted a technique for growing
mushrooms on sterilised rye grains. The McKennas published their method -- together with Terence's then wife
Kat Harrison and detailed photographs by Jeremy Bigwood -- as Psilocybin: Magic Mushroom Grower's Guide in
1976, under the pseudonyms O. T. Oss and O. N. Oric. The book has been reprinted eight times and by 1981
had sold one hundred thousand copies.

The McKennas estimated that there must be thousands of people worldwide following the method and growing
mushrooms at home. The method had three stages: spores were collected and germinated in Petri dishes on
agar jelly; healthy mycelium was transferred to jars filled with sterilised rye grains; and the grain was
"cased" with sterilised soil. Casing -- depriving the mycelium of oxygen while keeping it moist --
quadrupled the yield from a single jar. The brothers claimed it was only marginally more complicated than
making jam.

## Steven Pollock

The McKenna book was followed by similar methods put forward by [Steven Pollock in 1977.
Pollock was a medical doctor who became fascinated with psychedelic mushrooms after encountering them in
Colombia. He developed his own cultivation techniques and discovered several new species, including the
potent Psilocybe stuntzii. His growing operation eventually attracted the attention of the authorities. He
was arrested and charged with manufacture of psilocybin, receiving six months' imprisonment and three years'
probation. His case became a cause celebre among mushroom enthusiasts.

## Paul Stamets and The Mushroom Cultivator

The exhaustive guide to growing mushrooms of all kinds, The Mushroom Cultivator, was published in 1983 by
Paul Stamets and Jeff Chilton. This remains the bible for today's magic mushroom farmer.
Stamets went on to become perhaps the world's most prominent mycological writer, publishing extensively on
both edible and psychedelic mushroom cultivation. His work bridged the gap between illicit underground
growing and mainstream mycology.

## The PF Tek and Psilocybe Fanaticus

Robert McPherson (1947-), better known as Psilocybe Fanaticus, developed the so-called
"PF Tek" technique that revolutionised home cultivation. A jazz-blues guitarist and Haight-Ashbury hippy,
McPherson obtained Pollock's spores and began growing in the 1970s. He realised that the problem with the
cased-grain method was the likelihood of contamination and wondered whether spores could be inoculated
directly onto grain.

His solution was elegant: inject a solution of spores down into the medium, then cover the rye with a layer
of dry vermiculite to protect it from contamination. Vermiculite -- an inert substance that absorbs water,
does not shrink on drying, and keeps the substrate moist and aerated -- forced the mycelium to stretch
through pores in search of more nutriment, exactly mimicking the natural environment.
