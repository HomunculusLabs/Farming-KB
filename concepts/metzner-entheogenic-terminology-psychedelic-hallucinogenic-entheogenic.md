# Entheogenic Terminology: Psychedelic, Hallucinogenic, and Entheogenic

## Overview

The words used to describe consciousness-altering substances
shape how societies understand, regulate, and integrate them.
Metzner's work draws attention to the terminological
landscape surrounding [[psychedelic-compounds-reference]], arguing that
the choice of label is never neutral — each term carries
implicit assumptions about the nature of the experience,
its validity, and its relationship to religion, medicine,
and culture. The three dominant terms — hallucinogenic,
psychedelic, and entheogenic — each emerged from distinct
historical moments and carry distinct connotations that
influence clinical practice, public policy, and personal
meaning-making.

## The Problem of Naming

Naming a phenomenon is never merely descriptive.
The word selected to describe [[tryptamine]] and ergoline
experiences performs ideological work: it locates the
experience within a particular framework of meaning.
A hallucination implies a perceptual error — seeing
what is not there. A [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]] implies
mind-manifesting — revealing what is already present
in consciousness. An [[pineal-gland-dmt-entheogenic-experience]] implies
encounter with the divine — accessing realities that
transcend ordinary perception. Each framing opens
certain doors of understanding while closing others.

## Hallucinogenic: The Clinical Frame

The term "hallucinogenic" emerged from mid-twentieth
century psychiatric and pharmacological research.
It locates the substance experience firmly within
a pathology framework — the altered perception is
understood as a departure from consensual reality,
a form of misperception or sensory distortion. This
terminology served institutional needs: it positioned
psychedelic compounds as objects of medical study and
regulation, consistent with the disease-model orientation
of mid-century psychiatry. The hallucinogenic frame
privileges the visual dimension — seeing things —
and implicitly casts those visions as unreal.

## Psychedelic: The Countercultural Frame

Osmond coined "psychedelic" in 1956 — mind-manifesting
or soul-revealing. The term reflected the growing
recognition among researchers that these substances
did not merely distort perception but appeared to
reveal dimensions of consciousness ordinarily hidden.
Huxley's "doors of perception" metaphor captured
this framing: the compounds opened awareness to
realms that were always present but filtered out.
The psychedelic label was adopted enthusiastically
by the 1960s counterculture, which embraced the
implication that expanded consciousness was liberating
rather than pathological. However, the association
with counterculture ultimately contributed to the
backlash and scheduling that suppressed research.

## Entheogenic: The Sacred Frame

The term "entheogenic" — generating the divine within
— was proposed by scholars including Ruck, Wasson,
and Hofmann in the late 1970s. It deliberately
situated these substances within the history of
religious practice, arguing that the visionary
experiences they produce are best understood as
spiritual or mystical events rather than psychiatric
symptoms or recreational distortions. Metzner
embraces this framing, noting that indigenous
cultures universally understood these plants as
sacred medicines — teachers and healers rather
than drugs. The entheogenic label restores the
dimension of reverence and intentionality that
clinical language systematically stripped away.
