# Water Filtration and Settling Techniques

Water purification in [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-aquaculture-and-water-systems|systems]] relies on a diverse suite of natural and low-energy treatment methods. Rather than depending solely on industrial chemical processes, [[bill-mollison]]'s *[[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual*.

## Overview of Water Treatment Categories

Mollison identifies several broad families of water treatment, each addressing different classes of contaminants:

- **Aeration** — Introduces dissolved oxygen to support aerobic biological activity and volatilise dissolved gases.
- **Settling** — Allows suspended particles to gravitationally settle in quiescent conditions.
- **Skimming and Sieving** — Physically removes floating debris and coarse particulates.
- **Filtration** — Passes water through porous media (sand, charcoal, soil, plant roots) to trap fine particles and pathogens.
- **Coagulation and Flocculation** — Causes fine colloidal particles to clump together so they can settle or be filtered out.
- **Biological Removal** — Uses living organisms (bacteria, phytoplankton, higher plants) to metabolise or sequester pollutants.
- **pH Adjustment** — Modifies water chemistry with calcium or sulphur compounds to create conditions favourable for downstream treatment or to precipitate dissolved metals.

## Aeration Methods

Aeration can be achieved through several means, each suited to different scales and contexts:

- **Wind-powered aeration** — Windmills or wind-driven devices agitate the water surface or inject air.
- **Mechanical aeration** — Pumps, paddle wheels, or venturi injectors force air into the water column.
- **Trickle columns** — Water is distributed over porous media (gravel, slag) and allowed to cascade, maximising air-water contact.
- **Vegetative aeration** — Root zones of emergent aquatic plants oxygenate surrounding water through radial oxygen loss.
- **Phytoplankton** — Photosynthetic algae produce oxygen in well-lit surface waters during daylight hours.
- **Injected air** — Compressed air delivered through diffusers at the bottom of ponds or tanks.

## Settling Techniques

Still-water ponds and rush beds provide the simplest form of clarification. When water velocity drops below a critical threshold, suspended sediments settle under gravity. Rush beds (planted with *[[juncus]]* or *[[scirpus]]* species) add filtration and biological uptake on top of purely physical settling, making them a dual-purpose treatment stage.

## Sand Filters

The classical slow sand filter remains one of the most reliable low-technology water treatment systems. Britain historically adopted them widely, typically followed by chlorination.

### Design Specifications

- Bed depth: **1.2 metres** of fine sand layered over coarse sand and gravel.
- Grading: Fine-over-coarse arrangement prevents short-circuiting.
- Maintenance: The top 1 cm of sand, which accumulates a biological schmutzdecke (biofilm), is periodically swept off, removed, dried, and roasted before reuse.

The schmutzdecke is not a defect — it is the active biological layer where much of the pathogen removal actually occurs.

## Activated Charcoal

Activated charcoal adsorbs dissolved organic compounds, colours, odours, and some chemical contaminants. In permaculture contexts, it can be produced from:

- **Bones** — Bone char is especially effective at removing fluoride.
- **Willow** — Fast-growing willow provides a renewable feedstock.
- **Coconut husks** — Produce a highly microporous carbon with excellent adsorption capacity.

Activated charcoal columns are typically placed downstream of sand filters as a polishing step.

## Trickle Filters

Trickle (or percolating) filters pass water through beds of sand and gravel that support resident bacterial communities. These bacteria metabolise dissolved organic matter and nutrients as the water trickles over the media surfaces. The system depends on maintaining aerobic conditions within the filter bed.

### Carbon for Nitrogen Removal

Carbon is essential for the denitrification process, where bacteria convert nitrate to nitrogen gas. In systems where natural carbon is insufficient, it can be supplemented as carbohydrates:

- **Methanol**
- **Ethanol**
- **Acetic acids derived from plant residues**

Adding these carbon sources enables bacterial populations to drive the denitrification reaction to completion.

## Pond Bank Stabilisers

Vegetation around pond margins serves critical water-quality functions beyond erosion control. Roots filter runoff, shade suppresses algal blooms, and leaf litter provides habitat for detritivorous organisms. Mollison recommends the following species:

- **Water lilies** — Shade the surface, reducing light penetration and temperature.
- **Azolla** — A floating fern that fixes atmospheric nitrogen; also shades the water.
- **Water hyacinth** — Vigorous nutrient uptake (use with caution — invasive in many regions).
- **Juncus and Scirpus** — Rush species that filter and oxygenate.
- **Grasses and clovers** — General bank stabilisation and nitrogen fixation.
- **Phyla nodosa** — A mat-forming perennial for erosion-prone margins.
- **Bamboo and pampas grass** — Deep-rooted species for structural bank reinforcement.

## Light Exclusion and Surface Stabilisation

Reducing light penetration into water bodies directly lowers turbidity by suppressing phytoplankton blooms. Floating and emergent vegetation, combined with surface-stabilising plants, create a layered canopy that limits photosynthesis in the water column. This is a key strategy for managing eutrophic waters.

## Lime Flocculation

Lime causes fine colloidal particles to aggregate (flocculate) and settle out. Suitable lime sources include:

- **Crushed marble and limestone**
- **Burnt lime (quicklime)**
- **Shells** — Crushed mollusc shells provide a slow-release lime source.

Lime flocculation also raises pH, which can precipitate dissolved metals and improve conditions for subsequent biological treatment stages.

## Multi-Stage Filtration in Series

Mollison describes a practical sequence of treatment stages that can be assembled for thorough water purification:

1. **Trickle filters of pebbles** — Bacterial communities absorb dissolved nutrients from the water as it percolates through.
2. **Sand filter** — Removes bacterial pollution and fine suspended solids.
3. **Water mussels as biological monitors** — Each mussel filters approximately **100 L/day**. They are highly sensitive to water quality: they die at pH 5.5 and are sensitive to pesticides such as **Dieldrin**, serving as living indicators of contamination.
4. **Watercress** — Absorbs dyes and nitrates from the water passing through planted beds.
5. **Active carbon and silicon dioxide column** — Final polishing stage for dissolved contaminants.

## Sewage Treatment: Natural Pond Systems

Large-scale sewage treatment can be accomplished entirely through constructed pond systems. The **Werribee sewage farm** in Melbourne, Australia, serves as a landmark example:

- **Population served:** 2.7 million people
- **Total area:** 1,500 hectares of sewage lagoons
- **Land ratio:** Approximately **1 hectare per 1,800 inhabitants**
- **Pond series:** Anaerobic → facultative → aerobic ponds in sequence

The anaerobic ponds handle the heaviest organic loads; facultative ponds allow both aerobic surface and anaerobic bottom zones; aerobic ponds complete the polishing through photosynthetic oxygenation and further biological uptake.

### Pond Sizing for Small Communities

For a community of **1,000 townspeople**, Mollison provides two design options:

- **Settling pond:** 270 m² at 1 metre depth
- **Compact digester:** 3 m × 30 m × 3 m deep

### Sealed and Agitated Digesters

Enclosed digesters that are mechanically agitated prevent scum formation on the surface, ensuring consistent contact between organic matter and methanogenic bacteria. These systems produce valuable **biogas** as a by-product, composed of:

- **Methane** (CH₄) — The primary combustible component
- **Carbon dioxide** (CO₂)
- **Ammonia** (NH₃) — Can be stripped and recovered as fertiliser

## Coagulation and Flocculation Agents

Beyond lime, other coagulants include chemical additives such as common salt, ferric sulphates, and organic bacterial gels produced by certain soil and water microorganisms. The choice of coagulant depends on the specific contaminants present and the desired end-use of the treated water.

## Key Principles

- **Use multiple stages in series** — No single treatment removes all contaminants; a chain of complementary processes is most effective.
- **Leverage biological systems** — Bacteria, plants, and animals provide continuous, self-renewing treatment capacity.
- **Monitor with living indicators** — Organisms like water mussels provide real-time feedback on water quality.
- **Design for low energy input** — Gravity flow, wind, and photosynthesis can replace pumps and chemical dosing in many contexts.
- **Integrate treatment with production** — Treated water and nutrient by-products (ammonia, sludge) can fertilise crops, aquaculture, or energy systems.
