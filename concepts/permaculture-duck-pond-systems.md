# Permaculture Duck Pond Systems

Duck pond systems in permaculture integrate water management, animal husbandry, and plant production into a single productive element. Unlike chickens, ducks are waterfowl that thrive with access to open water, and their pond can become the center of a productive aquatic ecosystem that yields food, fertilizer, and wildlife habitat.

## Benefits of Ducks Over Chickens

- Better pest control in wet areas (slugs, snails, aquatic insects)
- Manure is higher in nitrogen and less likely to burn plants when diluted in water
- Do not scratch up gardens or damage soil structure
- More cold-hardy in many breeds
- Forage more effectively from water plants and insects
- Can be integrated directly into rice paddies and wetland gardens

## Pond Design

### Sizing
A general guideline is 5-10 square feet of water surface per duck. For a small flock of 6-10 ducks, a pond of 50-100 square feet (approximately 8x8 to 10x10 feet) is sufficient. Depth should range from shallow edges (6-12 inches) to a deeper center (2-3 feet).

### Shape and Edge
Irregular, curved edges maximize the shoreline — the edge is where biological activity and diversity are highest. Create gently sloping banks for easy duck access and plant establishment. Avoid steep drop-offs which can trap ducklings.

### Construction
- Dig to desired depth, creating shelves for marginal plants at 6-12 inch depths
- Line with clay (if naturally clay soil) or a durable pond liner
- Cover liner with soil or sand to protect it and provide planting substrate
- Create an inlet (overflow from swale, roof catchment, or spring) and an outlet (controlled spillway)
- The outlet should drain to an irrigation channel, garden, or additional wetland cell

### Location
- Place downhill from water sources (roofs, swales) for gravity-fed filling
- Near zone 1-2 for easy daily access
- In full sun for warm water (promotes plant growth and duck health)
- Avoid placing directly under large trees (leaf fall clogs the pond)

## Water Filtration with Plants

Aquatic plants are essential for maintaining water quality in duck ponds. They absorb nutrients from duck manure, filter sediment, provide shade, and produce duck forage.

### Submerged Plants
- **Elodea** — oxygenator, absorbs dissolved nutrients
- **Hornwort** — oxygenator, tolerates low light
- **Eelgrass** — deep water oxygenator

### Floating Plants
- **Duckweed** (Lemna spp.) — extremely productive, high protein duck food, rapid nutrient absorption
- **Water hyacinth** (where legal) — aggressive nutrient uptake, duck forage
- **Water lettuce** — ornamental and functional nutrient filter

### Marginal/Emergent Plants
- **Cattails** (Typha spp.) — nutrient filter, edge stabilizer, multiple yields (food, thatch, fiber)
- **Iris** — beautiful, filters nutrients, rhizomes stabilize banks
- **Pickerelweed** — ornamental, wildlife habitat, nutrient uptake
- **Watercress** — edible, high nutrient demand, excellent biofilter
- **Sweet flag** (Acorus calamus) — bank stabilizer, traditional medicinal
- **Rushes and sedges** — bank stabilization, wildlife habitat

### Multi-Cell Filtration
For larger systems, create a chain of ponds or wetland cells:
1. **Duck pond** — primary water body where ducks live
2. **Settling pond** — slower water allows sediment to drop out
3. **Constructed wetland** — densely planted cell that absorbs remaining nutrients
4. **Clean water outlet** — to irrigation, stream, or recharge

## Duck Forage Systems

Ducks are excellent foragers and can obtain a significant portion of their diet from pond and surrounding systems.

### Aquatic Forage
- Duckweed (can provide 30-40% of diet when abundant)
- Water snails and aquatic insects
- Algae (ducks eat some species)
- Rice in paddies (ducks eat pests and weeds while fertilizing rice)

### Terrestrial Forage
- Slugs and snails (ducks are voracious slug hunters — excellent for gardens)
- Grass and clover (short grass paddocks around the pond)
- Insects in mulch and compost areas
- Fallen fruit in orchards
- Kitchen scraps and garden waste

### Supplementary Feed
- Mixed grain (wheat, oats, barley, corn)
- Layer pellets (for egg production)
- Oyster shell or limestone grit for calcium
- Kitchen scraps from [[permaculture-food-waste-cycling]]

## Egg Production

Duck eggs are larger, richer, and have a longer shelf life than chicken eggs due to thicker shells. They are excellent for baking.

### Egg-Laying Breeds
- **Khaki Campbell** — 300+ eggs per year, excellent foragers
- **Indian Runner** — 250-300 eggs per year, active foragers, upright posture
- **Welsh Harlequin** — 240-330 eggs per year, calm, good foragers
- **Ancona** — 210-280 eggs per year, hardy, excellent foragers
- **Magpie** — 220-290 eggs per year, lightweight, active

### Nesting
Ducks prefer ground-level nesting in secluded spots near the pond. Provide covered nest boxes with straw bedding. Collect eggs daily to prevent broodiness and reduce predation risk.

## Predator Protection

- Secure the pond area with fencing (4-6 foot height with buried bottom edge)
- Provide floating platforms or islands where ducks can retreat from land predators
- Lock ducks in a secure coop at night (ducks are vulnerable to raccoons, foxes, owls)
- Net over the pond if aerial predators (hawks, herons) are a concern
- Ensure the coop is ventilated but draft-free

## Integration with Other Systems

- Pond water can be pumped or gravity-fed to irrigate gardens, carrying dissolved nutrients from duck manure
- Overflow from [[water-management]] swales can feed the pond
- Duck manure-enriched water is an excellent fertilizer for [[permaculture-guild-design]] plantings
- Combined with [[permaculture-animal-integration]] strategies for multi-species systems
- Duck feathers can be used in compost or as mulch material
