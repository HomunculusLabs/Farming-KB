## Permaculture Site Design and Zones

Permaculture site design is the most common application of [[permaculture-design-principles]]. The "site" concept refers to a limited parcel of land, often focused on a central point that is generally a dwelling or other building. Site design uses the tools of zone, sector, and slope to organise information about the land into useful patterns and provide a starting point for an overall concept plan.

## The Site-Based Approach

The site-based approach to [[permaculture-design]] has several advantages:

- It encourages **small-scale, nucleated development** that mimics the cellular growth patterns of organisms
- It is well suited to **residential sites**, the primary concern of Permaculture designers
- It encourages **distinct systems** that reflect the particular nature of the site
- It provides a **focal point** for [[permaculture-zones-and-sectors]]

### Site Design as Cellular Design

A Permaculture-designed garden (Zones 1 and 2) functions as a human rural settlement cell. There is a limit to efficient garden size before a jump to a more complex production system is required. Successful gardens do not keep expanding — they provide a surplus of plant stock and knowledge that help establish new gardens.

The dwelling and household are analogous to the cell nucleus, providing control, management, and information for reproduction. Households "reproduce" when children leave home and establish their own. There is an upper limit beyond which household size doesn"t work well — ironically, Western nuclear families are too small to be efficient in food production and preparation. The traditional extended family of five to fifteen members was large enough to efficiently support many self-reliance functions.

## The Five Permaculture Zones

Permaculture zones are more-or-less concentric areas of intensity of use around a focal point. The closer to the centre, the more efficient and intensive the land use; the further away, the more the system relies on self-maintaining elements requiring little input.

| Zone | Description | Scale | Characteristics |
|------|-------------|-------|-----------------|
| **Zone 0** | House/building | — | The nucleus; centre of activity and management |
| **Zone 1** | Intensive gardens | 10–1000 m² | Daily attention; vegetables, herbs, small animals, compost |
| **Zone 2** | Orchards and extensive gardens | 1000 m²–1 ha | Regular attention; fruit trees, small livestock, poultry |
| **Zone 3** | Field crops and sown pastures | 1–100 ha | Occasional attention; broadacre crops, main dams, large livestock |
| **Zone 4** | Managed forests and rangeland | 100–1000 ha | Minimal attention; timber, foraging, grazing |
| **Zone 5** | Wilderness | Variable | No management; ecological reference and conservation |

## "Starting at the Back Door"

The admonition to "start at the back door" reminds designers not to fall into the common mistake of "overreach" when developing a site. Scattering a few vegetable seeds, water, and compost across a grass paddock gives no yield; the same effort and resources can create a small but productive food garden in one corner.

Although zones are conceived as concentric, this is never so in practice. Slope, soil, aspect, and infrastructure all cause particular zones to shrink or expand. Even the idea that each zone forms a continuous band enclosing the inner zone does not necessarily work on the ground.

## Zones and Scale

Each consecutive Permaculture zone is not only further from the house but larger in area by roughly an order of magnitude. The specific nature of zones varies with environment and context, but the scale ranges are instructive:

- **Home garden (part-time)**: 10–100 m²
- **Local subscription organic garden (full-time)**: 100–1000 m²
- **Organic market garden**: 1–10 ha
- **Broadacre cropping and grazing**: 10–1000 ha
- **Rangeland pastoralism and forestry**: 1000–10,000+ ha

Gardening covers as large a scale range as farming. The strategies, methods, and technology relevant at one order of magnitude are not necessarily useful at another. For example, weed-control strategies on a 10-hectare market garden would not apply to a 100-hectare cropping property — the farmer's crops may well be the market gardener's weeds.

## Linear Versus Aerial Thinking

A common failure in understanding scale is thinking linearly rather than in terms of area. A garden of 10 × 10 metres is only three times longer and wider than a 3.3 × 3.3 metre garden, yet it is **ten times larger in area**. In most production systems, total area is the critical determinant of management energy required and yields likely to result.

On large scales (1000 m² and larger), it is rare for the whole area to be managed intensively. Traditional orchards, animal runs, and low-input gardening systems are properly considered Zone 2, which can extend garden agriculture to 10,000 m² (1 hectare).

## Optimum Scales of Production

For any particular production system and set of techniques, there is an optimum scale for best productivity, stability, and simplicity of management. A significant increase (or decrease) in scale requires a jump to a different production system and new techniques.

This development process follows a roller coaster or pulsing pattern: heavy going up the hills of transition, followed by a breezy ride down into the next valley of optimum operation. Some hills prove too steep, and the designer turns around to roll back into the last valley. This pattern is observable throughout natural and human systems wherever surplus energy exists to reach the next level.

## Sectors

Sectors radiate from the site focal point, defining the direction from which external wild energies enter the site:

- **Sun sectors**: The most important in temperate latitudes, varying between summer and winter but precisely definable from solar charts
- **Wind sectors**: Based on local observation and regional data
- **Fire sectors**: Important in fire-prone landscapes
- **Rain and flood sectors**: Influenced by topography and drainage patterns

Each bioregion has a generalised sector layout, modified by topography, microclimate, and land use for each site. Using sectors to understand and account for wild energies allows designers to make best use of those energies and diffuse or deflect their destructive aspects.

## Slope

On a slope, even a very gentle one, gravity acts to move things downhill. Designing to take advantage of this force while minimising work against it creates a more energy-efficient system:

- **Siting tanks and dams high** enables gravity-fed water without pumping
- **Primary access from above** makes importation of bulk materials easier
- **Terraces, swales, and contour tracks** slow water and soil movement downslope

## Design Tools

Beyond zones, sectors, and slope, Permaculture designers use a range of tools:

- Aerial photos, overlays, scale plans, and models
- Mud maps and concept plans for rapid design recording
- Cognitive maps and brainstorming to understand the designer's view of the site
- Scoring or rating of key development options and siting choices
- Flow charts to explore complex development processes

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.

## Research Directions
Current research explores the intersections between traditional knowledge and modern scientific understanding.
Comparative studies across different bioregions provide valuable insights into generalizable principles.
Long-term monitoring and documentation continue to build the evidence base for these approaches.
Collaborative research networks facilitate knowledge exchange and accelerate innovation.

## Key Considerations
Context-specific implementation requires attention to local ecology, climate patterns, and community needs.
Integration with existing systems often yields better results than complete replacement strategies.
Monitoring and adaptive management are essential for long-term success and continuous improvement.
