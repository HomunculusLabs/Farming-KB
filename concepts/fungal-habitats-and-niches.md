# Fungal Habitats and Niches
Fungi occupy virtually every habitat on Earth where organic matter is available.
Their ecological versatility far exceeds that of plants or most animal groups,
and many fungal species are adapted to extreme or specialized environments that
harbor unique assemblages of undescribed taxa. Understanding habitat diversity
is essential for estimating [[fungal-biodiversity-estimates]].

## Soil and Leaf Litter

Soil is the single largest reservoir of fungal diversity. A single gram of
forest soil can contain hundreds of fungal species, most detectable only
through [[environmental-dna-metabarcoding-fungi]]. Soil fungi include
decomposers of cellulose and lignin, mycorrhizal mutualists, plant pathogens,
nematode-trapping predators, and opportunistic yeasts.
Vertical stratification -- from the organic litter layer down through mineral
horizons -- creates distinct fungal communities at each depth. Leaf litter
hosts specialized ascomycete and basidiomycete communities that drive
decomposition of plant material. The succession of fungi on decaying leaves
follows predictable patterns: early colonizers are often generalist
saprotrophs, while late-stage decomposers specialize in recalcitrant compounds
like lignin and cutin.

## Wood and Woody Debris

Dead wood supports a distinct fungal flora including polypores, corticioid
fungi, and wood-inhabiting ascomycetes. The fungal community on a fallen log
changes dramatically as decomposition progresses, with different species
specializing on specific stages of wood decay -- white rot, brown rot, and
soft rot.
The volume and diversity of coarse woody debris in old-growth forests
supports fungal communities that may take centuries to fully develop. Standing
dead trees (snags) provide a different succession trajectory, favoring fungi
that colonize heartwood while the tree is still upright.

## Plant Endophytes

Every healthy plant tissue examined to date harbors fungal endophytes --
fungi that live asymptomatically within plant cells or intercellular spaces.
Leaf endophytes are especially diverse in [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]], where a single
leaf can host dozens of species.
Many endophytes appear to be latent pathogens or saprotrophs that switch
lifestyles when their host dies, blurring the boundary between mutualism,
commensalism, and parasitism. Some [[cryptic-fungal-species]] are known only
from endophyte surveys and have never been observed in a reproductive state.
Some endophytes confer [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]], herbivore resistance, or disease
protection to their hosts, making them of significant interest for
agriculture.

## Root-Associated Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic associations with the roots of most
terrestrial plants. [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] fungi (Glomeromycota) associate
with a broad range of hosts, while ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes and
ascomycetes partner primarily with trees in temperate and boreal forests.
Dark [[dark-septate-endophytes]] other root-colonizing fungi add further complexity.
The specificity and diversity of these associations remain poorly
characterized, particularly in the tropics. Orchid mycorrhizae represent an
especially specialized relationship, where the fungal partner is required for
seed germination.

## Aquatic and Marine Habitats

Freshwater fungi colonize submerged wood, leaf packs, and detritus in streams,
rivers, and lakes. These [[aquatic-hyphomycetes-and-ingoldian-spores]] produce specialized conidia
adapted for dispersal in flowing water. Marine fungi inhabit sediments,
driftwood, mangrove roots, and living algae, with many species found only in
specific salinity and temperature regimes.
The deep sea remains virtually unexplored for fungal diversity, though
molecular surveys have revealed novel lineages from hydrothermal vent
environments and cold-water corals. [[freshwater-fungal-habitats-conservation-urgency]]
has been increasingly recognized as fungal communities in aquatic systems face
threats from pollution, water abstraction, and habitat modification.

## Extreme Environments

Fungi colonize habitats that are inhospitable to most eukaryotes. These
extreme environments harbor specialized fungal communities with unique
physiological adaptations:

### Rock-Inhabiting and Desert Fungi

Rock-inhabiting fungi (also called microcolonial fungi or black fungi) grow on
bare rock surfaces in deserts and polar regions, tolerating extreme UV
radiation, desiccation, and temperature fluctuations. These melanized fungi
can survive years of desiccation and resume [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] within hours
of rehydration. They contribute significantly to rock weathering and soil
formation in arid landscapes. Antarctic cryptoendolithic communities -- fungi
living within porous rocks -- represent some of the most extreme terrestrial
fungal habitats known.

### Thermal Environments

Thermal fungi thrive in geothermal soils at temperatures exceeding 50C,
with some species capable of growth above 60C. Thermophilic fungi in compost
piles drive the decomposition process at temperatures lethal to most
organisms. Geothermal springs and volcanic soils host specialized assemblages
that include both thermophilic and thermotolerant species. These fungi are of
considerable biotechnological interest due to their heat-stable enzymes.

### Cold Environments

Psychrophilic species fruit on snow banks and glacial ice, while cold-tolerant
fungi dominate the soil microbiome of arctic and alpine tundra ecosystems.
Permafrost soils harbor viable fungal propagules that have been dormant for
thousands of years. The polar regions are among the most understudied fungal
habitats, yet molecular surveys suggest they harbor substantial undescribed
diversity.

### Deep Subsurface

Deep-subsurface fungi have been recovered from mine shafts kilometers below
the surface. These organisms survive in complete darkness under enormous
pressure, obtaining energy from geological sources rather than photosynthetic
products. The deep terrestrial subsurface and deep ocean sediments together
may represent one of the largest reservoirs of fungal biomass on Earth.

### High-Radiation Environments

Fungi have been found growing inside the damaged reactor at Chernobyl,
utilizing melanin to convert gamma radiation into chemical energy for growth.
Radiotrophic fungi have also been detected in other high-radiation
environments, suggesting that ionizing radiation may serve as an energy source
in ecosystems previously considered abiotic.

## Anthropogenic Habitats

Human activities have created novel fungal habitats. Compost piles, landfills,
and polluted sites harbor distinct fungal assemblages. Indoor environments
support communities of allergenic and toxigenic species including Aspergillus,
Penicillium, and Stachybotrys. Agricultural soils under intensive management
develop distinct fungal communities shaped by tillage, fertilization, and
pesticide application. Each anthropogenic habitat creates selection pressures
that favor certain fungal groups while suppressing others.
