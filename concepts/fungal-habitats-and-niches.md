# Fungal Habitats and Niches

Fungi occupy virtually every habitat on Earth where organic matter is
available. Their ecological versatility far exceeds that of plants or most
animal groups, and many fungal species are adapted to extreme or specialized
environments that harbor unique assemblages of undescribed taxa. Understanding
habitat diversity is essential for estimating total fungal biodiversity.

## Soil and Leaf Litter

Soil is the single largest reservoir of fungal diversity. A single gram of
forest soil can contain hundreds of fungal species, most detectable only
through molecular methods. Soil fungi include decomposers of cellulose and
lignin, mycorrhizal mutualists, plant pathogens, nematode-trapping
predators, and opportunistic yeasts. Vertical stratification — from the
organic litter layer down through mineral horizons — creates distinct fungal
communities at each depth.

Leaf litter hosts specialized ascomycete and basidiomycete communities that
drive decomposition of plant material. The succession of fungi on decaying
leaves follows predictable patterns: early colonizers are often generalist
saprotrophs, while late-stage decomposers specialize in recalcitrant
compounds like lignin and cutin.

## Wood and Woody Debris

Dead wood supports a distinct fungal flora including polypores, corticioid
fungi, and wood-inhabiting ascomycetes. The fungal community on a fallen log
changes dramatically as decomposition progresses, with different species
specializing on specific stages of wood decay — white rot, brown rot, and
soft rot. The volume and diversity of coarse woody debris in old-growth
forests supports fungal communities that may take centuries to fully
develop. Standing dead trees (snags) provide a different succession
trajectory, favoring fungi that colonize heartwood while the tree is still
upright.

## Plant Endophytes

Every healthy plant tissue examined to date harbors fungal endophytes — fungi
that live asymptomatically within plant cells or intercellular spaces. Leaf
endophytes are especially diverse in tropical forests, where a single leaf
can host dozens of species. Many endophytes appear to be latent pathogens or
saprotrophs that switch lifestyles when their host dies, blurring the
boundary between mutualism, commensalism, and parasitism. Some endophytes
confer drought tolerance, herbivore resistance, or disease protection to
their hosts, making them of significant interest for agriculture.

## Root-Associated Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic associations with the roots of most
terrestrial plants. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (Glomeromycota) associate
with a broad range of hosts, while ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes and
ascomycetes partner primarily with trees in temperate and boreal forests.
Dark septate endophytes and other root-colonizing fungi add further
complexity. The specificity and diversity of these associations remain poorly
characterized, particularly in the tropics. Orchid mycorrhizae represent an
especially specialized relationship, where the fungal partner is required for
seed germination.

## Aquatic and Marine Habitats

Freshwater fungi colonize submerged wood, leaf packs, and detritus in streams,
rivers, and lakes. These aquatic hyphomycetes produce specialized conidia
adapted for dispersal in flowing water. Marine fungi inhabit sediments,
driftwood, mangrove roots, and living algae, with many species found only in
specific salinity and temperature regimes. The deep sea remains virtually
unexplored for fungal diversity, though molecular surveys have revealed
novel lineages from hydrothermal vent environments and cold-water corals.

## Extreme and Specialized Habitats

Fungi colonize habitats that are inhospitable to most eukaryotes. Rock-inhabiting fungi (also called microcolonial fungi or black fungi) grow on
bare rock surfaces in deserts and polar regions, tolerating extreme UV
radiation, desiccation, and temperature fluctuations. Deep-subsurface fungi
have been recovered from mine shafts kilometers below the surface. Thermal
fungi thrive in geothermal soils at temperatures exceeding 50°C, while
psychrophilic species fruit on snow banks and glacial ice.

Other specialized habitats include the guts of herbivorous insects (where
fungi assist in digesting plant material), human-built environments (indoor
fungi including allergenic species), and the rhizosphere of agricultural
crops. Each of these niches harbors potentially unique fungal communities
that are severely undersampled.

## Anthropogenic Habitats

Human activities have created novel fungal habitats. Compost piles, landfills,
and polluted sites harbor distinct fungal assemblages. [[mycoremediation]]
exploits the ability of certain fungi to degrade pollutants including
petroleum hydrocarbons, heavy metals, and even explosive compounds. Building
materials support characteristic indoor mold communities, with implications
for human health. Agricultural soils, shaped by tillage, fertilization, and
pesticide application, represent dramatically altered fungal landscapes
compared to their undisturbed counterparts.
