# [[essential-plant-elements-hydrogen-boron-carbon-nitrogen|Essential]] Soil Macronutrients

The essential macronutrients form the chemical foundation of soil fertility and plant health. Derived from parent rock minerals, atmospheric inputs, and biological cycling, these elements determine the capacity of soils to support plant and animal life. Understanding their roles, sources, and toxicity thresholds is fundamental to sustainable land management and [[permaculture-design]].

## Hydrogen (H)

Hydrogen is an extremely mobile and reactive gaseous element. The concentration of free hydrogen ions (H⁺) in solution, balanced against hydroxyl (OH⁻) ions, determines the **pH** of soils and water. This acid-alkaline balance is one of the most critical factors governing nutrient availability to plants.

### Chemical Roles

Hydrogen combines with several elements and organic substances to form acids. It combines with oxygen as hydroxyl (OH⁻) or water (H₂O) — the basic gaseous and liquid elements of life. Combined with carbon as **methane (CH₄)**, hydrogen is emitted by decaying [[humus]] in anaerobic (airless) environments such as under water, in compacted soils, or in boggy ground.

### Practical Soil Indicators

Soils producing methane typically display a **mottled profile** and may appear bluish, yellow, or contain iron stains and nodules. These visual indicators signal wet-season waterlogging — critical information for plant placement in [[permaculture]] designs. A sulphurous smell in subsoils is a reliable guide to seasonal waterlogging and should be noted for plants intolerant of stagnant water.

Methane is a component of marsh gas and of biogas produced by digesters, usually associated with carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide — the characteristic gases of decomposition. Some plants, especially algae and rushes, can transpire hydrogen and in doing so break down halogenated hydrocarbons used in pesticides and herbicides, offering a biological remediation pathway.

## Lithium (Li)

Lithium, the lightest metal, is found in plant and animal tissue. Medically, lithium salts are prescribed for hyperactive and disturbed individuals, as the element may moderate nerve impulse transmission across synapses. While not a major agricultural concern, its presence in biological systems hints at the complex relationship between trace elements and neurological function.

## Boron (B)

Boron is a **trace element** essential to brassicas and beets. It plays roles in sugar transport within the plant, pollen formation (hence fertility), and cell wall structure — functions it shares with calcium.

### Toxicity and Pollution

Boron pollution can build up in gardens because many detergents contain boron. In drylands especially, citrus and grains can be boron-poisoned during dry seasons when concentrations intensify. Using soap instead of detergents is recommended, particularly in arid regions.

- **Ideal soil level**: 0.75 ppm
- **Toxic threshold**: 1.0 ppm for plants
- **Toxicity risk**: Highest in dry years on soils derived from marine sediments

### Agricultural Application

Borax at 160 g/ha is used on beet and brassica crops (cauliflower, turnip) where soil boron levels are low. Borax is also poisonous to seed and insects — used with dilute honey as an ant and cockroach poison. Sea sediments and seawater contain high boron levels. Surplus boron can cause anaemia in people.

## Carbon (C)

Carbon is the basic building block of all life forms, combining with hydrogen and oxygen as hydrocarbons. It is added to soils as humus, compost, and mulch.

### Soil Structure

- **10–20% humus** ensures good structure in both clays and sands
- **More than 50% humus** inhibits uptake of polluting heavy metals (such as lead) in city soils
- **Less than 7% humus** may not improve soil structure unless calcium is also added

Pitted or buried wastes need nitrogen or oxygen (therefore air) for decomposition — compacted anaerobic conditions stall the carbon cycle.

### Carbon Content Assessment

Carbon constitutes approximately **58% of organic matter** (combustible fraction) in soils, existing mostly as colloids. To estimate soil carbon content, assess nitrogen levels and multiply by 20.

### The Greenhouse Effect

Carbon combines with oxygen to form carbon dioxide gas, released from agriculture, forest felling, and industry. This creates the greenhouse effect and subsequent earth heating — identified by [[mollison-designers-humus-building-and-soil-organic-matter|Mollison]] as potentially critical for human survival. The permaculture response is clear: **plant trees, avoid bare fallow, and add humus to soil.**

## Nitrogen (N)

Nitrogen constitutes 80% of the atmosphere and is a major plant nutrient. Legumes and many non-legumes (alder, *Casuarina*) that have root-associated fungi and bacteria will fix atmospheric nitrogen if molybdenum is present as a catalyst to convert nitrogen to ammonia.

### Plant Functions

Nitrogen is part of all amino acids (hence proteins), chlorophyll, and enzymes. It is highly mobile in plants and has a dominant effect on other nutrient uptake. Plants absorb nitrogen as nitrates or ammonium, then convert it to ammonia to create proteins.

### The Nitrate Danger

In water with bacteria, nitrogen forms nitrates which are beneficial at low levels but **lethal to young animals and children at high levels** (more than 80 ppm). Mouth bacteria and sewage bacteria convert nitrates to nitrites, hence nitrosamines, which are cancer-associated. Nitrates are building up in all soil waters below agricultural land and occur naturally in many desert bore waters.

**Key guidance**: Fertilise garden plants at minimum nitrogen levels. Use modest legume interplant rather than heavy nitrogen fertiliser. Constantly measure nitrate levels in leaves, water, and soil.

### Nitrite/Nitrate Levels (ppm)

| Level | Source |
|-------|--------|
| 0–50 | Safe for human consumption |
| 500 | Spinach grown from compost |
| 2,000–3,000 | Chemical fertiliser-grown spinach |

### Environmental Impact

Excess animal manure releases surplus nitrogen. Ammonia from cow dung alone reaches 115,000 tonnes/year in some intensive dairy regions, accounting for 30% of acid rain from those sources. In humid areas, approximately 70% of human nitrate intake comes from vegetables and 21% from water. The European standard of 50 mg/L (60–80 ppm) is being exceeded in many agricultural areas.

**Permaculture strategy**: Reduce cattle, run them on pastures rather than feedlots, dispose of sewage in forests to aid tree growth. Do not inflict high nitrate levels on malnourished populations.

## Oxygen (O)

Oxygen is necessary for respiration and energy release. Soils also need oxygen — requiring good open structure and soil pores constituting **12–30% of soil bulk**.

### Achieving Soil Aeration

Soil aeration is achieved through:
- Calcium amendments
- Tree root penetration
- Earthworm activity
- Soil ripping
- Humus addition
- Blowing air through sub-surface irrigation pipes

**Waterlogging** reduces pore space and root oxygen. Crops such as walnuts, oranges, chestnuts, and potatoes require deep loose soil and plenty of oxygen. Pear trees and marsh grasses (Yorkshire fog, *Imperata* grass) require much less oxygen and tolerate compacted or wet soils.

### Deep Soil Requirement

Soils need good open pore spaces to a depth of **1–2 metres (3–6 feet)**. Large soil pore spaces are only achieved by [[soil-life]] and perennial crops — annual monocultures on compacted soils cannot maintain adequate aeration.

## Fluorine (F)

Fluorine is beneficial in small amounts (as in seaweed fertiliser) but a **serious poison at high levels**. It is commonly found in bore water and in downwind pollution plumes from metal processing works (zinc, aluminium, copper), causing bone deformation in animals, stunted growth, and serious metabolic disturbance.

### Safe Levels

- **Normal growth requirement**: approximately 1 mg/day
- **Sufficient water concentration**: 1 mg/L (1 ppm)
- **Seafood levels**: 5–10 ppm fluorides (fish, oysters)

### Toxicity Problems

Problems of excess fluorine occur where:
- Mineral processing dusts fall on vegetation
- "Hard" desert waters contain more than 1 ppm (actual levels can reach 14 ppm)
- Diet is mainly seafood

Bones, spine, and ligaments can become calcified with excessive fluorine. Teeth may be "case-hardened" by fluorine, showing little outer sign of decay while the tooth itself rots internally. **Check bore water for high fluorine levels and use tanks for drinking water.**

## Sodium (Na)

Combined with chlorine as common salt, sodium is necessary at low levels for plant and animal cell health and nervous system function. At high levels, however, sodium causes **collapse of soils** and loss of pore space, displaces calcium, and disperses colloids.

### Sources and Problems

Beets and some crops need adequate sodium levels, likely absent only in wet tropical uplands. Elsewhere, sufficient sodium arrives with rain as salt crystals in raindrop nuclei. Real problems occur when soil salts are mobilised by:

- Irrigation
- Potash fertiliser
- Soil flooding in dryland areas

### Health Effects

Excessive salt causes circulatory and kidney problems in people, especially those in sedentary occupations. Salt in vegetable cooking water blocks uptake of some heavy metals on plant proteins — so if lead pollution is suspected, adding salt to cooking waters can be protective.
