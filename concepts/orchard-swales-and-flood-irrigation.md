## Orchard Swales and Flood Irrigation

[[water-management]] on orchard and agricultural land can be achieved through a variety of earthworks, channels, and flood-based techniques. These methods leverage gravity, contour, and seasonal rainfall patterns to move, store, and distribute water with minimal energy input.

## Earth Tanks

Earth tanks are excavated ponds where the soil dug out is piled up on the down-sun side to create a **sun trap**. The berm protects the water surface from wind and reflects warmth back onto the pond, extending [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] for marginal species. Earth tanks serve multiple purposes:

- **Plant habitats**: Depths of 3–6 feet support aquatic and riparian plants, providing habitat and forage.
- **Kettle holes for fish**: Deeper excavations within the tank create protected pockets where fish can survive and breed.
- **Stock water**: Open water for livestock on rangeland.
- **[[holzer-climate-zone-microclimate-creation]]**: The piled soil warms quickly in spring, enabling early planting on the berm itself.

## Lock Pipes and Flags

A **lock pipe** is a pipe laid through a dam wall at a set height, sealed with a removable flag (a simple plug). When the flag is pulled, water flows from the dam into an irrigation channel or [[diversion-drain-mollison]]. This is the simplest possible irrigation valve — no mechanical parts, no cost beyond the pipe itself. Flags allow controlled release from storage into downstream systems on demand.

## Floodgate Irrigation

Floodgate irrigation uses a system of **side channels** running alongside a field, with **plug barriers** and **2-inch pipes** set at intervals along the channel bank. To irrigate:

1. Open the lock pipe or head gate to fill the side channel.
2. Pull a plug barrier to let water flow through a pipe into one section of the field.
3. That section floods; once saturated, replace the plug and open the next one downstream.
4. Proceed section by section across the entire field.

This method floods fields in discrete paddocks or strips, soaking the ground thoroughly. It works on gentle slopes and requires no pumps — just gravity and removable plugs. It is simple to construct and maintain, making it ideal for smallholders and broadacre operations alike.

## Reverse Siphon Systems

On hillside orchard sites, a **reverse siphon** pipe carries water from a higher [[swale]] or dam down to a lower system. The pipe is buried and runs downhill, then curves back up to the surface at the next swale or planting zone below. Water flows through the pipe by gravity, and the upward exit end is lower than the entry point, so the siphon is self-priming once filled. This eliminates the need for pumps or open channels across steep or erodible slopes.

## Swale-Based Orchard Management

Swales are shallow, broad ditches dug **on contour** to intercept runoff and allow it to infiltrate into the soil. In orchard systems, swales are fundamental:

- **Construction**: Minimum 4 feet wide, dug level along the contour. In **clay soils**, compacted clay sections in the swale bottom hold standing ponds for slow infiltration. In **sandy soils**, the swale is widened to spread water over a larger area for faster absorption.
- **White clover**: Plant white clover on the swale mound as a nitrogen-fixing ground cover that tolerates foot traffic and mowing.
- **Grass mowing**: Mow grass from the inter-tree alleys and throw it onto the swale mound, building [[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] mulch in place.
- **Regrading soil**: Over time, soil and organic matter migrate downslope from the swale mound toward the tree line. Periodically regrade — shovel the accumulated soil and [[humus]] back upslope to the trees, maintaining fertility where the roots need it.
- **Staggering tree species**: Place **narrow-leaf deciduous trees** (peaches, apricots, almonds) on the **uphill side** of the swale, and **broad-leaf species** (apples, pears, plums) on the **downhill side**. Narrow-leaf trees cast less shade and allow more light penetration to the broad-leaf trees below, while the downhill species benefit from deeper moisture reserves in the soil below the swale.

## Flat Land Flood Irrigation

On nearly flat terrain, broad shallow flood irrigation is an ancient and effective technique. The **Turkish and Afghan method** uses two people and a rope-pulled shovel to shape low bunds across a field, creating a series of broad, shallow basins. Water is released at the high end and floods across the entire surface slowly and evenly. The bunds are only a few inches high — enough to hold water long enough for infiltration, but low enough to allow overflow into the next basin when full. This requires no machinery and can cover large areas with minimal labor.

## Keyline Dual Function

The **keyline** system provides a single drain that serves two opposing seasonal functions:

- **Winter**: Excess rainfall is intercepted and carried away to prevent waterlogging, erosion, and dam overflow.
- **Summer**: The same drain, fed from storage dams via lock pipes, distributes irrigation water across the landscape following the same contour channels.

This dual-purpose infrastructure halves the earthwork required and ensures that every contour channel contributes to both flood control and irrigation.

## Contour Dams

Contour dams are built across very gentle slopes or on flat land by simply pushing up earth across the contour with a bulldozer. They are **cheap and no-fuss** — no core trench, no compaction engineering beyond what the machinery provides. They hold water in broad, shallow ponds that follow the land's natural shape. Contour dams are ideal for flat or gently undulating country where deeper excavated dams would be unnecessary or impractical.

## Urban Swale Systems

Swale principles apply at the urban scale. **Davis, California** is a widely cited example:

- Streets have **no gutters or curbs**; instead, swales run alongside the road surface.
- **Double rows of houses** are placed between parallel swales, so roof runoff and street runoff all flow into the swales.
- Native and adapted plantings in the swales filter and absorb water.
- This system increased local **water absorption from 40% to 100%** of annual rainfall, eliminating stormwater runoff entirely.
- The swales also provide green corridors, reduce urban heat island effects, and create habitat within the residential fabric.

## Bamboo on Dam Banks

Bamboo planted on dam banks provides [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]], windbreak, and useful material. Two growth forms matter:

- **Monopodial (clumping) bamboo**: Stays in a tight clump, produces large edible shoots, does not invade the dam or surrounding land. Preferred for food production and controlled growth.
- **Sympodial (runner) bamboo**: Spreads aggressively via rhizomes, producing many small culms. Good for arrow shafts, stakes, and erosion control on large banks where spreading is acceptable.

Choose monopodial varieties near dams and water features to avoid root invasion of the structure; use sympodial types on broader embankments where containment is not a concern.

## Integration

These systems are not standalone — they connect. A lock pipe feeds a diversion drain, which fills a side channel for floodgate irrigation. Swales above an orchard feed reverse siphons that irrigate the rows below. Keyline drains move winter floodwater into contour dams, whose lock pipes release it back through the same drains in summer. The design principle is always the same: use gravity, contour, and simple mechanical valves to move water from where it is abundant to where it is needed, with no pumps and minimal maintenance.
