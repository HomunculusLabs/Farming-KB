## Species Diversity

Mexico's neurotropic mycota spans all four groups defined by Guzman et al. (1998):

- **Group 1 (Psilocybin-containing):** 44 Psilocybe taxa plus species of
  [[panaeolus-subbalteatus-profile]], Panaeolina, Conocybe,
  Gymnopilus, Inocybe, Pluteus, and Hypholoma
- **Group 2 (Ibotenic acid-containing):**
  [[claviceps-purpurea-chemistry-and-water-soluble-ergot-alkaloids]], C. paspali
- **Group 4 (Sacred fungi without chemical study):** Dictyophora indusiata,
  Lycoperdon spp., Vascellum spp.

## Notable Psilocybe Species

Key Mexican Psilocybe species include:

- **P. mexicana** — The type species for Section Mexicanae, among the most
  well-documented ceremonial species. First studied by
  [[psychedelic-mushroom-set-and-setting]]
- **P. caerulescens** — Known as "derrumbes" (landslides), found in cloud
  forests. Two varieties: var. caerulescens and var. ombrophila
- **P. zapotecorum** — Named after the Zapotec people of Oaxaca, found in
  subtropical forests. Belongs to Section Zapotecorum, related to
  P. aucklandii from New Zealand
- **P. aztecorum** — Grows at high elevations (up to 4,000 m) in subalpine
  and alpine habitats. Two varieties: var. aztecorum and var. bonetii.
  Related to P. baeocystis (Pacific NW) and P. quebecensis (Canada) in
  Section Aztecorum
- **P. cubensis** — The most widely distributed neurotropic species globally.
  In Mexico called "san isidros" after San Isidro, patron saint of agriculture
- **P. hoogshagenii** — Two varieties: var. hoogshagenii and var. convexa
- **P. muliercula** — An endemic Mexican species
- **P. wassoniorum** — Named in honor of R. Gordon and Valentina Wasson

## Habitat and Distribution Patterns

Of the 42 neurotropic Psilocybe species documented in Guzman's 1983 monograph:

- **34 species** occur in mesophytic (cloud) forests
- **4 species** in tropical forests
- **4 species** in coniferous forests

Despite coniferous forests being more thoroughly mycologically surveyed,
mesophytic forests harbor far greater diversity. This reflects the preference of
most [[wasson-mexico-expeditions]] of the 1950s brought international attention
to Mexican mushroom ceremonies, leading to the identification of psilocybin by
Albert Hofmann in 1958. Maria Sabina, a Mazatec curandera, became one of the
most famous figures in the history of ethnobotany as a result
of her role in introducing Wasson to velada ceremonies.

## Conservation Concerns

Several Mexican neurotropic species face habitat loss due to deforestation of
cloud forests and mesophytic woodlands. Climate change may also threaten the
specific humidity and temperature conditions these species require. The growing
popularity of psychedelic tourism in regions like Oaxaca raises
additional concerns about sustainable harvesting and respect for indigenous
ceremonial traditions.
