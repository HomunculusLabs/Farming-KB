# Holzer Farming Philosophy

## Core Principles

[[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]]'s farming philosophy centres on working *with* nature rather than against it. At his Krameterhof farm in Lungau, Austria (1,100-1,500m altitude), he demonstrates that productive farming is possible in marginal climates without chemical inputs, irrigation, or specialised equipment. His approach was developed independently through decades of experimentation and observation, predating the formal permaculture design system.

## Nature as Teacher

Holzer argues that the belief that nature can be improved upon is a fundamental mistake. When an imbalance emerges -- such as a pest outbreak -- the farmer must identify the root cause rather than treat symptoms. Conventional agriculture, in his view, creates problems by suppressing natural cycles and then selling the chemicals to fix them.

A healthy ecosystem regulates itself. The farmer's role is to create conditions where this self-regulation can occur, not to intervene at every sign of trouble. This requires patience, observation, and a willingness to let natural processes take their course.

## Against Specialisation

Specialisation makes farmers dependent on subsidies, cooperatives, and agrochemical companies. A diverse farm with many income streams can react flexibly to market changes and unforeseeable events. The Chernobyl disaster wiped out Holzer's mushroom income overnight, teaching him that specialisation creates only risk and dependence.

Holzer warns that farm subsidies create a dangerous dependency. Once a farmer adapts their operation to subsidy criteria, they lose the ability to make independent decisions. The subsidy system rewards monoculture and specialisation, further eroding farm resilience.

## The Rebel Farmer Approach

Holzer advocates courage and independence in decision-making. What neighbours are growing is already abundant and therefore uninteresting commercially. He encourages farmers to find their own niches using intuition and careful observation rather than following recipes or patterns from agricultural schools.

This independence extends to every aspect of the farm. Holzer does not follow planting calendars, fertiliser schedules, or pruning guides from agricultural authorities. Instead, he observes the land, the weather, the plants, and the animals, and makes decisions based on what he sees. He trusts his own experience over expert opinion -- not out of arrogance, but because decades of direct observation have given him knowledge that no textbook contains.

## Past Mistakes: A Critique of Modern Agriculture

Holzer documents the damage caused by decades of conventional land management: in the name of agriculture, perceived imperfections in the terrain are corrected and unwanted water is drained. Rocks and cliffs are blasted to make fields suitable for mechanised farming. Wetlands are drained and dense spruce monocultures planted. Hedges and orchards are grubbed out, rivers and streams are straightened.

The result is already well known: floods, landslides, and storm damage are becoming more prevalent. Valuable biomass and fertile humus are being lost. Soil is losing its capacity to store water -- entire areas are acidifying and turning to desert. Pesticides and fertilisers will poison the groundwater. Biodiversity is being seriously threatened. Humans disturb the balance of nature and then fight against the "pests" and "weeds" for which they have only themselves to blame.

Holzer's own experience mirrors this larger pattern. As a young farmer trained in conventional methods, he applied chemical fertilisers, harsh pruning, vole poisoning, and all the textbook recommendations to his trees and his customers' trees. The result was devastation. Only when he abandoned these methods and returned to observation-based farming did his trees recover. This personal failure became the foundation of his philosophy.

## Key Differences from Conventional Farming

- No pesticides, chemical fertilisers, or herbicides
- Polycultures replace monocultures
- Animals integrate with crops rather than being confined
- Terraces and raised beds replace flat-field monoculture
- Water is retained on the land through ponds and ditches
- Old varieties and seed saving replace hybrid seeds
- Deep ploughing is avoided to protect soil life
- Green manure is left standing in autumn rather than cut
- Mulch is spread unshredded for slow nutrient release
- Trees are not pruned -- branches retain resilience against snow and wind
- Rootstocks from vigorous seedlings are preferred over dwarf varieties for independence

## The Problem-Solving Mindset

Holzer approaches every farming problem as a symptom of an underlying imbalance:

- Pests indicate weakened plants or disrupted predator populations
- Weeds indicate soil conditions (compaction, acidity, excess nitrogen)
- Disease indicates stress from wrong location, wrong variety, or soil degradation
- Erosion indicates poor water management and lack of ground cover

Rather than fighting symptoms, he addresses root causes. Slugs are managed by creating habitat for their natural predator (toads) through water landscapes, not by spreading slug pellets. Aphids are managed by providing habitat for ladybirds and lacewings through structured ground cover. Voles are managed by providing decoy plants (Jerusalem artichoke, black salsify) rather than by poisoning them -- the voles then aerate the soil and spread plants to new locations through their tunnels.

## Economic Independence

The Krameterhof operates profitably as a full-time farm across 45 hectares. By eliminating input costs (fertilisers, pesticides, irrigation) and creating diverse revenue streams (fruit, livestock, mushrooms, fish, aquatic plants, tours, training courses), the farm remains financially independent. Holzer demonstrates that ecological farming is not just environmentally sound but economically superior when properly designed.

The financial case is straightforward: when you stop buying inputs, your costs drop dramatically. When you produce diverse outputs, your market risk drops. When your soil improves each year, your yields increase over time. This is the opposite trajectory of conventional farming, where costs rise, risk concentrates, and soil degrades.

## Relationship to Permaculture

While Holzer's work is often labelled permaculture, he arrived at his methods independently through childhood observation and decades of experimentation. His approach predates and in some ways diverges from the formal permaculture design system -- he emphasises intuition, direct observation, and working at large scale with heavy machinery (excavators) where appropriate.

Holzer also differs from some permaculture practitioners in his use of old domesticated breeds rather than purely wild species, his willingness to use mechanical earthworks at scale, and his focus on commercial viability alongside ecological principles. He does not reject technology outright -- he rejects dependency on it. An excavator is a tool; a chemical fertiliser company is a chain.

## Courage and Observation

Two qualities Holzer returns to repeatedly are courage and careful observation. Farmers must be willing to experiment, to fail, and to learn from failure. The natural world provides constant feedback -- the farmer need only pay attention. This is not passive farming; it requires active, daily engagement with the land, the plants, the animals, and the weather. The reward is a farm that becomes more productive and resilient over time rather than degrading.
