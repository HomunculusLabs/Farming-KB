# Use Small and Slow Solutions — Permaculture Principle 9

Use Small and Slow Solutions is the ninth of [[david-holmgren]]'s twelve
permaculture-design-principles. It advocates for systems and organisations
that are smaller in scale and slower in pace than those typical of industrial
culture. The principle recognises that small and slow systems are more
manageable, more adaptable to local conditions, and more likely to endure
through rapid change than large, fast, and centralised systems.

## The Beam Balance Metaphor

Holmgren uses the image of a beam balance to explore the asymmetrical and
dynamic balance between fast and slow systems in industrial and sustainable
cultures. Industrial culture heavily weights the fast side: fast transport, fast
food, fast communication, fast returns on investment. Sustainable culture
requires a shift toward the slow side: slow food, slow transport, slow
building, slow investment returns. The principle is not about rejecting speed
entirely but about restoring balance.

## Small Is Beautiful

The principle draws on E.F. Schumacher's influential work "Small Is Beautiful,"
which argued that appropriate scale is essential for economic and ecological
sustainability. Schumacher challenged the assumption that bigger is always
better, showing that small-scale, locally appropriate technologies and
organisations often outperform large-scale, centralised alternatives in
efficiency, resilience, and human satisfaction.

The Intermediate Technology Development Group (ITDG), founded in 1965 and now
known as Practical Action, has demonstrated the effectiveness of small-scale
[[appropriate-technology]] in development contexts. These technologies are
maintainable, affordable, and adapted to local conditions, unlike large-scale
industrial solutions that create dependency on external expertise and inputs.

## Agriculture and Animal Husbandry

### Industrial Dairy versus Community Dairy

Holmgren contrasts industrial dairy operations, which maximise output and
slaughter animals after a few years of productive life, with the approach of
Hare Krishna communities that care for all animals over their natural lifespan.
The community model maintains approximately seven milking cows in a herd of
eighty to ninety animals. While less "efficient" in narrow output terms, the
community approach is more resilient, ethically grounded, and sustainable
over the long term.

### Childhood Development

The principle extends to human development. Waldorf/Steiner education, based
on Rudolf Steiner's spiritual science, deliberately delays academic instruction
to allow natural developmental processes to unfold. Teaching reading too young
is seen as creating imbalance, analogous to forcing fruit before it is ripe.
The slow approach allows children to develop physical, social, and creative
capacities before focusing on abstract intellectual skills.

### Nutrition and Concentrated Foods

Holmgren critiques concentrated and processed foods as expressions of the fast
culture. Fruit juice, for example, is a concentrate that delivers sugar without
the fibre, vitamins, and eating experience of whole fruit. Over-nutrition from
concentrated foods is causing early maturation in children and chronic disease
in adults. Whole foods, eaten in their natural state, represent the slow
solution.

## The Slow Food Movement

The Slow Food movement, with 60,000 members worldwide at the time of writing,
represents a cultural expression of this principle. Founded in Italy in response
to the spread of fast food culture, Slow Food celebrates local food traditions,
seasonal eating, and the pleasure of shared meals prepared with care. The
movement connects food culture to environmental sustainability and social
justice.

## Cities and Scale

Newman and Kenworthy's research on cities demonstrates that smaller, more
compact cities with slower transport modes are more energy-efficient and have
higher quality of life than sprawling car-dependent cities. The permaculture
preference for decentralised, human-scale settlement patterns aligns with this
research. Village-scale communities where most daily needs can be met on foot
or by bicycle represent the small and slow ideal.

## Information Economy

The acceleration of the information economy, while creating new possibilities,
often ignores the value of what is being communicated. Faster communication
does not necessarily mean better understanding. The principle suggests that
slower, more reflective communication and decision-making may produce better
outcomes than rapid response driven by market competition.

## Why Small and Slow Works

Small systems are easier to understand, manage, and modify. When something goes
wrong, the consequences are contained and the cause is easier to identify. Small
systems can be replicated and adapted to diverse local conditions. They are less
vulnerable to catastrophic failure because they lack the tight coupling that
creates cascading failures in large systems.

Slow systems have time to respond to feedback, allowing [[apply-self-regulation-permaculture-principle]] to function. Fast systems outrun their own feedback
mechanisms, making them prone to overshoot and collapse. In nature, the most
persistent and productive systems are typically slow-growing: old-growth
forests, mature grasslands, stable coral reefs.

## Balance, Not Dogma

The principle does not demand that everything be small and slow. Some functions
benefit from scale and speed. The point is to restore balance in a culture that
overwhelmingly favours the large and fast. [[permaculture-design]] seeks
systems where small and slow approaches are the default, with large and fast
solutions deployed only when their benefits clearly outweigh their costs and
risks.

## Connection to Energy Descent

In an era of energy-descent, the arguments for small and slow solutions
become more compelling. As fossil fuel energy becomes scarcer and more
expensive, large-scale centralised systems will become increasingly difficult
to maintain. Small, local, slow systems powered by renewable energy will be
more resilient and adaptable. The principle is not merely an aesthetic
preference but a strategic response to energy and resource constraints.
