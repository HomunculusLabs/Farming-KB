# Mushroom Cloning Technique and Strain Capture

The surest method of starting a mushroom strain is by
cloning. Cloning means that a piece of pure, living flesh
is excised from the mushroom and transferred into a sterile
nutrient medium in a clean room. This process captures the
unique genetic characteristics of the parent mushroom.

## Cloning vs. Spore Culture

A culture arising from cloning is fundamentally different
from one originating from spores. When spores are germinated,
many different strains are created, some incompatible with
one another. A cultivator will not know what features will
be expressed until each strain is grown out to mushroom
production. This form of genetic roulette results in very
diverse strains.

By cloning, you capture the phenotype, the expressed
characteristics of the mushroom. Later, under proper
cultural conditions and barring mutation, these same
features are expressed in subsequently grown mushrooms.
Spores offer the most diverse source of genetic
characteristics, far more than the phenotypic clone.

## Best Candidates for Cloning

Several factors determine the success of mushroom cloning:

- **Age**: Young mushrooms, preferably in "button" form,
  are better candidates than aged specimens. Young
  mushrooms are in a state of frenzied cell division.
  Clones from young mushrooms tend to be more vigorous.
  Older mushrooms have a higher contamination risk and
  are slower to recover from the shock of transfer.

- **Freshness**: The more recently picked, the better.
  Dried specimens cannot be cloned.

- **Quality**: The best-looking specimen should be chosen,
  showing no signs of disease, insect damage, or
  deterioration.

## Optimal Tissue Collection Sites

Two locations on the mushroom result in the highest number
of successful clones:

1. **Directly above the gills**: This genetically rich area
   gives rise to the gills and their associated spore-
   producing cells, the basidia.

2. **The interior tissue at the base of the stem**: This
   location has the disadvantage of being in direct
   contact with the ground, often the entry point through
   which larvae tunnel, carrying other microorganisms.

For this reason, the area directly above the gills is
generally preferred for its combination of genetic richness
and reduced contamination risk.

## Cloning Procedure

The by step procedure for cloning a mushroom:

1. Choose the best specimen possible and cut away any
   attached debris
2. Using a damp paper towel, wipe the mushroom clean
3. Lay the specimen on a new sheet of paper towel
4. Flame-sterilize a sharp scalpel until it is red hot
5. Cool the scalpel tip by touching the nutrient [[agar]]
   medium in a petri dish. This same dish will receive
   the mushroom tissue
6. Carefully tear the mushroom apart from the base, up the
   stem, and through the cap
7. Cut a small section ("square") of flesh about the size
   of a kernel of grain from the tissue above the gills
8. Quickly transfer the excised tissue to the nutrient-
   filled petri dish
9. Insert the tissue partway into the agar medium rather
   than resting it on the surface for maximum contact with
   nutrients
10. Each time a clone is taken, the scalpel is re-sterilized,
    cooled, and then the tissue is transferred into a
    separate petri dish

## Sterile Workflow Prioritization

When working in front of a laminar flow hood, every movement
is prioritized by degree of cleanliness:

- The cleanest articles remain upstream, nearest to the
  micron filter
- The next cleanest downstream in second position
- The cultivator's hands are usually furthest downwind
  from the media and cultures

The hot scalpel tip and freshly poured media plates should
be kept upstream of the mushroom being cloned or the
[[mycelium]] being transferred. One should always presume
hands are replete with contaminants, no matter how many
times they have been disinfected.

## Avoiding Cross-Contamination

Some use a "cooling dish" into which the hot scalpel tip
is inserted before touching the living flesh of a mushroom.
Repeatedly cooling the scalpel tip into the same medium-
filled petri dish before each inoculation is not
recommended. A mistake with any inoculation could cause
contamination to be re-transmitted with each transfer.

Only one cooling dish should be used for each transfer,
the same dish that receives the cloned tissue. In this
fashion, at least one potential cross-contamination vector
is eliminated.

## Number of Repetitions

When cloning a mushroom for the first time, a minimum of
5 repetitions is recommended. If the mushroom specimen is
rare, cloning into several dozen dishes is recommended. As
the specimen dries out, viable clones become increasingly
less likely.

## Preserving Genetic Diversity

Stamets strongly believes that the future health of the
planet may well depend upon the strains we preserve this
century. A culture can be preserved for decades, probably
centuries, all the while kept within a few thousand cell
divisions from the original culture. Since we still live in
an era of relatively rich growing gourmet global, the time is now
to preserve as many cell lines from the wild as possible.
