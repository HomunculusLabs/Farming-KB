# Living Fences and Hedgerows

## Overview

A living fence (also called a hedge, live fence, or fedge) is a line of closely planted shrubs and trees that serves as a barrier, boundary marker, windbreak, and wildlife corridor. Unlike conventional post-and-wire fencing, living fences are self-repairing, long-lived, productive, and increase in value over time as they mature. They represent one of the oldest forms of land division, with hedgerow systems in Britain and Europe dating back thousands of years.

## Functions of Living Fences

### Livestock Containment and Exclusion

Properly established living fences can contain cattle, horses, sheep, and goats, though the required density and thorniness varies by animal. Cattle are generally contained by dense hedges 1.5–2 m tall. Goats are the most challenging to contain and may require supplementary electric fencing until the hedge is fully mature. Hedges also exclude deer, rabbits, and ground-dwelling pests from garden areas when designed with appropriate height and density.

### Wind Protection

Living fences reduce wind speed on the leeward side by 40–60%, with effects extending 10–15 times the height of the hedge. This creates sheltered microclimates for crops, livestock, and buildings, reducing heating costs, water loss through transpiration, and physical damage from wind. Unlike solid walls, permeable living fences deflect wind upward rather than creating turbulent eddies on the lee side.

### Wildlife Habitat and Corridors

Hedgerows serve as wildlife corridors connecting habitat patches across agricultural landscapes. They provide nesting sites for songbirds, habitat for pollinators and predatory insects, travel corridors for small mammals and amphibians, and food sources through berries, seeds, and nectar. Studies in the UK have found that hedgerows support over 600 plant species, 1,500 insect species, 65 bird species, and 20 mammal species.

### Erosion Control

Root systems of hedge plants stabilize soil on slopes, ditch banks, and field margins. Above ground, the physical barrier of stems and branches slows overland water flow, reducing sheet erosion and trapping sediment. On contour-planted living fences, this function is particularly valuable for preventing gully formation on hillsides.

## Establishment Methods

### Direct Planting

The most common approach: plant bare-root or container seedlings 30–60 cm apart in a single or double row. For a double-row hedge, stagger plants 30–40 cm between rows with 60–80 cm between plants within each row. Prepare the planting strip (60–100 cm wide) by removing sod, loosening soil to 30 cm depth, and incorporating compost. Mulch heavily (10–15 cm) to suppress weeds during establishment. Water regularly for the first 2 growing seasons.

### Live Stakes

Many species establish readily from hardwood cuttings (live stakes). Willows, poplars, black locust, and osage orange can be planted as 30–50 cm cuttings pushed directly into moist soil in early spring, with 2–3 buds above ground. This is the fastest and cheapest establishment method for suitable species. Survival rates of 70–90% are typical with willow stakes. For osage orange and black locust, soak cuttings for 24–48 hours before planting to improve rooting success.

### Plashing and Pleaching

Traditional European hedge-laying technique where stems are partially cut at the base and bent over at 30–45 degrees, woven together and staked. The bent stems continue growing and root along the bent section, creating a dense, stock-proof barrier. This is typically done on established hedges 5–10 years old during the dormant season. Regional styles of hedge-laying vary across Britain and Europe, each adapted to local conditions and livestock types.

### Instant Hedgerow from Pre-Planted Panels

Pre-grown hedgerow panels (1–2 m tall) grown in woven willow or biodegradable containers can be planted as instant barriers. More expensive but provide immediate function for gardens and high-visibility sites.
