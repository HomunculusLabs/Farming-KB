# Native US Plants Containing DMT and Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors

N,N-Dimethyltryptamine (DMT) is a potent short-acting hallucinogenic tryptamine that occurs naturally in a surprising variety of plants growing within the United States. Unlike the well-known Amazonian ayahuasca brew, which combines DMT-containing plants with monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) to achieve oral activity, the North American sources of these compounds are far less recognized despite their widespread distribution across the continent. John Halpern's 2004 review in Pharmacology and Therapeutics documented the principal North American botanical sources and their pharmacological context.

## DMT Pharmacology and Routes of Administration

DMT produces rapid and intense hallucinogenic effects when smoked, inhaled, or injected, with peak effects achieved within 2 to 5 minutes and resolution over the following 20 to 30 minutes. The typical smoked dose is 40 to 50 milligrams, though anecdotal reports describe use ranging from 20 to 100 milligrams. The experience is characteristically intense, often described as involving contact with seemingly autonomous entities, geometric visual landscapes, and profound alterations in the sense of self and reality.

The effective intravenous dosage range has been estimated at 0.1 to 0.4 milligrams per kilogram of body weight based on Rick Strassman's clinical research. At 0.6 milligrams per kilogram, the experience was described by two research participants as "too much" and difficult to recollect, suggesting an upper threshold beyond which the experience becomes ineffable. DMT has been called the "businessman's trip" because of its short duration, which was noted as early as the 1960s.

Orally, DMT is essentially inactive because monoamine oxidase enzymes in the gut and liver rapidly deaminate the molecule before it can reach the central nervous system. This is the fundamental pharmacological challenge that ayahuasca preparations solve by combining DMT-containing leaves with MAOI-containing plants to produce a 3 to 4 hour intoxication peaking within the first hour. The MAOI temporarily disables the gut's enzymatic defense, allowing DMT to reach systemic circulation and cross the blood-brain barrier.

## Phalaris Species: Reed Canary Grass

The most accessible North American source of DMT is the genus Phalaris, commonly known as reed canary grass. Three species contain appreciable amounts of DMT: Phalaris arundinacea, Phalaris aquatica, and Phalaris tuberosa. These grasses are found throughout the United States in fallow fields, cracks in sidewalks, lawns, pastures, and disturbed areas. Their ubiquity and weedy nature mean that DMT-containing plant material can be obtained from plants growing in virtually any American neighborhood, a fact that has significant implications for drug enforcement policy.

DMT content in Phalaris species is highly variable, influenced by genetics, growing conditions, plant age, and time of harvest. Inheritance studies by Barker and Hovin demonstrated that alkaloid concentration is a heritable trait in reed canary grass, and high-percentage strains have been developed through selective breeding. These improved strains are available through specialty seed suppliers and via Internet-based companies. The variability makes generalizations about potency unreliable and complicates any attempt to standardize dosing from wild-harvested material.

Simple juice extraction from Phalaris grass can yield appreciable amounts of DMT. The grass is macerated, pressed or squeezed, and the resulting juice can be reduced through evaporation to produce a crude extract. Mathematical models of DMT production in Phalaris seedlings have been developed, though these remain largely of academic interest given the legal status of the compound.

## Desmanthus Illinoensis: Prairie Bundleflower

Desmanthus illinoensis, the prairie bundleflower, is a leguminous perennial native to the central and southeastern United States. It has a wide American distribution documented by the USDA plants database and is sometimes cultivated as a forage crop or ornamental. The root bark of Desmanthus illinoensis has been found to contain approximately 0.34% DMT by dry weight, making it a moderately concentrated source.

The prairie bundleflower is notable because the DMT is concentrated in the root bark rather than in above-ground tissues, requiring excavation and processing that is more labor-intensive than simply harvesting grass tops. The plant's status as a native prairie species means it can be found in remnant prairies, along roadsides, and in undisturbed meadow habitats across its range. Research on the indolealkylamines of Desmanthus illinoensis has also documented growth inhibition activity in certain insect pests, suggesting an ecological role for these compounds beyond their psychoactive properties.

## North American MAOI Sources

The key to rendering orally ingested DMT active is co-administration with a reversible type A monoamine oxidase inhibitor. While Amazonian ayahuasca uses Banisteriopsis caapi as its MAOI source, two plants growing in the United States contain sufficient MAOI activity to serve the same pharmacological function.

Peganum harmala, known as Syrian rue, was introduced to the western United States and has now naturalized and is found growing wild. It contains 2 to 4% beta-carboline MAOIs by dry weight, including harmine, harmaline, and tetrahydroharmine. This concentration is comparable to or exceeds that found in Banisteriopsis caapi, making Syrian rue an effective and convenient MAOI source for ayahuasca-analog preparations. The harmala alkaloids contained in Peganum harmala range from 0.05% to 1.95% by dry weight.

Passiflora incarnata, the common passionflower, grows throughout the southeastern United States and much of the Midwest. It contains approximately 1% MAOI content by dry weight and is widely used in herbal preparations for its sedative properties. It bears an edible fruit and is familiar to many Americans as an ornamental vine, making it a readily available if less potent alternative to Syrian rue.

## Non-Traditional Ayahuasca Analogs

The existence of both DMT-containing plants and MAOI-containing plants within the United States means that ayahuasca-analog brews can theoretically be prepared entirely from North American botanical sources. Recipes combining Phalaris extract with Peganum harmala or Passiflora incarnata have circulated on the Internet since the late 1990s, and self-reports confirm that these preparations produce subjective effects similar to traditional ayahuasca.

Halpern noted that these non-traditional brews are "not theoretical" and that recipes can be found on websites such as Erowid and Trout's Notes, with self-reports confirming activity. The Internet has played a critical role in disseminating knowledge about these combinations, connecting indigenous Amazonian pharmacological knowledge with the North American flora in ways that would have been unlikely through traditional ethnobotanical channels alone. However, the nausea and vomiting routinely produced by MAOIs, combined with the variable DMT content of North American plant sources, make these preparations considerably less predictable than traditional ayahuasca.

## The Harmala Alkaloids: Pharmacology Beyond MAO Inhibition

The beta-carboline alkaloids found in Peganum harmala, particularly harmine and harmaline, are themselves psychoactive substances with a distinct pharmacological profile. They produce mild sedative and hallucinogenic effects at therapeutic doses and have been the subject of pharmacological research since the 1950s, when Pennes and Hoch first documented their psychotomimetic properties.

A typical 100 milliliter dose of ayahuasca has been reported to contain approximately 24 milligrams of DMT, 107 milligrams of tetrahydroharmine, 20 milligrams of harmaline, and 170 milligrams of harmine. The harmala alkaloids contribute their own subjective effects to the overall experience, including visual phenomena, mild sedation, and a characteristic perceptual sharpening. Research by Naranjo, Turner, and others has explored the unique psychotherapeutic potential of these beta-carbolines independent of their MAOI function.

## Legal and Enforcement Implications

DMT is a Schedule I controlled substance in the United States, and the plants that contain it are technically subject to the same controls as any material containing a scheduled substance. However, the widespread natural distribution of Phalaris grass and the legal availability of many DMT-containing plant species create enforcement challenges of a fundamentally different nature than those posed by synthetic drugs or imported botanicals.

Halpern observed that law enforcement measures might not be capable of controlling DMT use if these common local plants become popular as manufacture sources. The legal status of the MAOI-containing companion plants is less clear. Peganum harmala and Passiflora incarnata are not specifically scheduled, though extraction of their active alkaloids for human consumption would likely fall under drug manufacturing statutes.
