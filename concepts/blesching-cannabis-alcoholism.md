## Evidence Base (4 Studies)

The four studies reviewed represent a growing but still preliminary body
of evidence:

- **Substitution surveys**: Multiple studies found that 30-50% of medical
  cannabis patients reported substituting cannabis for alcohol, with many
  reporting decreased alcohol consumption
- **Epidemiological data**: Population-level studies in medical cannabis
  states showed associations between cannabis availability and reduced
  alcohol-related hospitalizations and mortality
- **Clinical observations**: Small clinical samples suggested that
  cannabis-using individuals in alcohol treatment had comparable or
  better outcomes than non-users on some measures
- **Preclinical evidence**: Animal models demonstrated that cannabinoid
  administration reduced voluntary alcohol consumption and alcohol-
  seeking behavior

## Comparative Harm Profile

A central argument for cannabis substitution in AUD is the comparative
harm profile:

- Alcohol is directly toxic to multiple organ systems (liver, brain,
  cardiovascular, gastrointestinal)
- Alcohol overdose (alcohol poisoning) can be fatal; cannabis overdose
  cannot
- Alcohol withdrawal can be lethal; cannabis withdrawal is mild by
  comparison
- Alcohol is associated with approximately 88,000 deaths annually in
  the U.S. alone; cannabis-associated mortality is extremely rare
- Alcohol is a significant contributor to violent behavior and accidents;
  cannabis's association with these outcomes is substantially lower

## Dosing Considerations

- CBD-dominant preparations may be preferable during acute withdrawal
  to manage symptoms without intoxication
- Balanced CBD:THC preparations may help with craving management
- Higher-THC products should be used cautiously due to their own
  dependence potential
- Consistent dosing schedules may be more effective than as-needed use
  for craving prevention
- Integration with conventional AUD treatment (counseling, support
  groups, naltrexone, acamprosate) should be maintained

## Risks and Limitations

- Cannabis substitution does not address the underlying psychological
  and social factors driving alcohol dependence
- Some individuals may develop cannabis use disorder as a replacement
  for alcohol dependence
- The evidence base is insufficient to recommend cannabis as a
  standalone treatment for AUD
- Combined use of cannabis and alcohol may increase impairment beyond
  either substance alone
- Medical supervision is essential, particularly during acute alcohol
  withdrawal which can be medically dangerous

## Harm Reduction Perspective

Blesching frames cannabis in the context of a harm reduction approach:

- The goal is not to replace one addiction with another but to provide
  a less harmful tool for individuals already struggling
- Abstinence-based approaches have high relapse rates; cannabis may
  serve as a bridge or adjunctive support
- Patient autonomy and informed choice are central to harm reduction
- Any reduction in alcohol consumption, even without complete
  abstinence, produces meaningful health benefits
