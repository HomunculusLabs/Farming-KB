# Permaculture Two Introduction and Design Principles

Permaculture Two, published by Bill Mollison in 1979, extends the
framework established in Permaculture One (1978) toward practical
application in both urban and rural settings. The book's subtitle,
"Practical Design for Town and Country in Permanent Agriculture,"
signals its core mission: making permaculture accessible and
actionable for any landholder regardless of scale.

## Definition and Scope

Mollison defines permaculture as a "consciously designed agricultural
system" that combines landscape design with perennial plants and
animals to achieve sustainable yields with minimal energy inputs.
Unlike conventional agriculture, which Mollison characterises as a
"destructive system," permaculture seeks to model natural ecosystems
where multiple species interact to create self-maintaining,
self-fertilising, and productive landscapes.

The book opens with the observation that aboriginal "crop-masters"
functioned as living computers who orchestrated tribal permaculture
through tree-based ecology and food taboos. These indigenous systems
demonstrated that sustainable food production requires deep knowledge
of ecological relationships rather than brute-force energy inputs.

## Seven Reasons for Designing Plant Systems

Mollison identifies seven compelling reasons to consciously design
plant systems rather than allowing them to develop haphazardly:

1. Save energy in system operation
2. Cope with external energies (sun, wind, fire, flood)
3. Arrange plants to assist each other through guilds and
   companion planting
4. Achieve optimal placement of units via zonation
5. Suit the specific climate and site conditions
6. Integrate food production with human society and settlement
7. Provide for all human necessities from the local landscape

## Critique of Ornamental and Status-Based Landscaping

Mollison offers a sharp critique of landscape design driven by status
rather than function. He cites the Taj Mahal lawns, British hedgerows
maintained for aesthetic tradition, and suburban front gardens planted
in cosmetic flowers as examples of "delinquent landscape" that wastes
productive capacity. The suburban plot under lawn, the urban blight
around cities, and the desperate misuse of frontier land all represent
a system that is not sustainable in any future terms.

## Von Thunen's Zonation as Unconscious Design

Mollison references Von Thunen's analysis of pre-industrial northern
European settlements, where functions were ranged around human
settlements in concentric zones based on transport cost and
perishability. While this represents a form of zonation, Mollison
considers it "unconscious rather than designed" -- a pattern that
emerged from economic forces rather than intentional ecological
planning. Permaculture seeks to make this zonation conscious and
optimal.

## The Golden Rules of Placement

Two fundamental energy-conserving rules emerge from Permaculture Two:

- "No placement without the element (plant, animal or structure)
  serving at least two or more functions"
- "Every function (water collection, fire protection) served in two
  or more ways"

These principles demand that every element in the design perform
multiple roles and that every essential function be supported by
multiple elements, creating redundancy and resilience.

## Slope-Based Design Principles

Mollison emphasises that slope gives "immense planning advantages"
and that hardly a viable human settlement exists that is not
positioned to draw resources from at least two environments. The
broad landscape profile, from high plateau to plains, dictates
specific strategies for permanent use:

- Ridge-top storage dams for gravity-fed water
- Keyline water systems developed from Yeomans' work
- Thermal belts created by uphill forests protecting lower
  settlements
- Solar collectors and reflection ponds on favourable slopes
- Fire protection through strategic placement of forests and
  water bodies

## Edge Effect and Diversity

The "edge effect" is identified as an important factor: the
interface between two ecosystems represents a third, more complex
system combining both. Gross photosynthetic production is highest at
interfaces. Estuaries and coral reefs show the highest production per
unit area of any major ecosystem. A landscape with complex edge is
both more productive and more beautiful, forming what Mollison calls
"the basis of the art of landscape design."

## Plant Stacking and Multi-Tier Agriculture

The book introduces the concept of plant stacking through an evocative
quote describing traditional Middle Eastern agriculture: palm above,
then olive, then fig, then pomegranate and vine, then grain and
vegetables. This vertical arrangement uses shade cast by taller plants
to protect lower layers rather than competing with them. Three
strategies can increase the number of plants in an area: preventing
water loss through high shade and windbreaks, introducing
drought-resistant species, and using vertical moisture barriers.

## The Interaction of Plants and Animals

Mollison draws on Atsatt and O'Dowd's research on "plant defense
guilds" to outline three categories of plant interaction: plants that
breed predators for pest control (insectaries like Phacelia in
orchards), plants that repel browsers through physical or chemical
means, and attractant or decoy plants that draw pests away from crops.
The conclusion is that "a little powerful diversity" of the right
kind is the key component of stability.

## Philosophy of Individual Responsibility

The book closes with a strong statement about moral obligation:
stabilisation and utilisation of landscape is a moral issue with
global implications. If one cannot maintain or improve a system, one
should leave it alone. Settlements should always include total food
provision, or else risk "the double jeopardy of sterile city and
delinquent landscape." The permaculture system, integrated with human
settlement, provides an "inexhaustible resource" when properly
designed.
