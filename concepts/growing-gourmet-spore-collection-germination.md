    12|# Spore Collection and Germination for Mushroom Cultivation
    13|
    14|Collecting and germinating mushroom spores is a fundamental
    15|skill for cultivators seeking to capture genetic diversity
    16|from wild specimens. While cloning captures the phenotype of
    17|a known mushroom, spore germination creates an array of new
    18|strains with varying characteristics.
    19|
    20|## Making Spore Prints
    21|
    22|Mushroom spores are collected by taking a spore print. The
    23|procedure is straightforward:
    24|
    25|1. Sever the cap from the stem
    26|2. Place the cap, gills down, upon a piece of typing paper
    27|   or glass
    28|3. Cover the mushroom cap with a bowl or plate to lessen
    29|   evaporation and disturbance from air currents
    30|4. Within 24 hours, spores fall in a beautiful pattern
    31|   according to the radiating symmetry of the gills
    32|
    33|A single mushroom can produce from tens of thousands to
    34|one hundred million spores. The [[mushroom-spore-printing-and-storage]] can be
    35|sealed, stored, and saved for future propagation.
    36|
    37|## Glass Spore Booklets
    38|
    39|A preferred method for long-term spore storage uses glass
    40|plates approximately 6 x 8 inches. The procedure:
    41|
    42|1. Wash glass with soapy water, wipe dry
    43|2. Clean with rubbing alcohol (isopropanol)
    44|3. Join two pieces of glass with a length of duct tape
    45|   to create a binding
    46|4. Lay mushrooms on the cleaned, open surface
    47|5. After 12-24 hours, remove the contributing mushroom,
    48|   dry it, and store for reference
    49|6. Tape the remaining edges to create a glass-enclosed
    50|   "Spore Booklet"
    51|
    52|Spore booklets can be stored at room temperature for years.
    53|Spores are easily removed from the smooth glass surface for
    54|future use and can be observed without increasing the
    55|likelihood of contamination.
    56|
    57|## Germinating Spores on Agar
    58|
    59|To germinate spores, an inoculation loop, sterilized needle,
    60|or scalpel is brought into contact with the spore print.
    61|The recommended procedure:
    62|
    63|1. Flame-sterilize an inoculation loop until red hot
    64|2. Immediately cool it in a petri dish filled with
    65|   sterilized nutrient medium. The tip sizzles as it cools
    66|3. The tip becomes covered with a moist, adhesive layer of
    67|   media to which spores easily attach
    68|4. Touch the cooled tip to the spore print, picking up
    69|   hundreds of spores
    70|5. Streak the spore-laden tip in an "S" pattern across the
    71|   surface of another media dish
    72|
    73|With heavy spore prints, the "S" streaking technique may not
    74|sufficiently disperse the spores. In this case, the scalpel
    75|or inoculation loop should be immersed into a sterile vial
    76|holding 10 cc. (ml.) of water. After shaking thoroughly,
    77|one drop is placed onto the surface of the nutrient medium
    78|in each petri dish.
    79|
    80|## Managing Germination
    81|
    82|Five days later, spores may be seen germinating according
    83|to the streaking pattern. Colonies of germinating spores
    84|are subcultured into more petri dishes. Afmyceliummycelium
    85|has grown away from the subculture site, a small fragment
    86|of pure mycelium is again subcultured into more petri
    87|dishes.
    88|
    89|If these cultures do not sector, then backups are made for
    90|storage and future use. This last transfer usually results
    91|in individual dikaryotic strains which are labelled. Each
    92|labelled strain is then tested for productivity. Mini-culture
    93|experiments must be conducted prior to commercial-level
    94|production.
    95|
    96|## Contamination Risks
    97|
    98|When a concentrated mass of spores is germinated, the
    99|likelihood of bacteria and weed fungi infesting the site
   100|is greatly increased. Bacteria replicate faster than
   101|mushroom spores can germinate. As a result, germinating
   102|spores become infected. Mycelium arising from such
   103|germinations is frequently associated with a high
   104|contamination rate, often not experienced until the
   105|mycelium is transferred to grain media.
   106|
   107|However, if the spore prints are made correctly,
   108|contamination is usually not a problem. Once inoculated,
   109|petri dish cultures should be taped with an elastic film
   110|(such as Parafilm) to protect the incubating mycelium
   111|from intrusive airborne contaminants.
   112|
   113|## Spore Mass Inoculation
   114|
   115|The ultimate shortcut for culturing mushrooms is via spore
   116|mass/liquid inoculation directly into bulk substrates.
   117|Primarily used in China, this technique works well with
   118|oyster and Shiitake mushrooms but is also applicable to
   119|all wood or straw saprophytes.
   120|
   121|The process parallels the brewery industry in cultivating
   122|yeasts. Large fermentation vessels are filled with sugar
   123|broth, inoculated with pure spores, and incubated with
   124|aeration via air compressors. Each Spore-Mass Master can
   125|inoculate 100 times its mass, with functional expansion
   126|ratios of 1:25 to 1:200.
   127|
   128|## Advantages of Spores
   129|
   130|Spores offer several advantages over cloning:
   131|
   132|- Can be sealed and stored, even sent through the mail,
   133|  with little ill effect
   134|- Offer the most diverse source of genetic characteristics
   135|- For the traveler, spore prints are an easy way to send
   136|  back potential new strains to the home laboratory
   137|- Create entirely new strain combinations not available
   138|  through cloning alone
