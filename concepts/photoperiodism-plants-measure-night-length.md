# Photoperiodism — How Plants Measure the Length of Night

Photoperiodism is the physiological response of organisms to the length of day or night. In plants, it is the primary mechanism by which flowering and other developmental processes are synchronized with the changing seasons. Discovered in 1920 by Wightman Garner and Harry Allard, photoperiodism was the first strong evidence that plants can actively measure the passage of time through light perception.

## The Discovery: Maryland Mammoth Tobacco

The story of photoperiodism begins with a remarkable tobacco plant. In 1906, farmers in southern Maryland noticed a new tobacco strain that grew continuously, reaching 4.5 meters in height with nearly a hundred leaves, and stopping only when winter frosts set in. While its vigorous growth seemed desirable, the plant — named Maryland Mammoth — rarely flowered, making it impossible to harvest seed for the next season.

In 1918, Garner and Allard at the USDA investigated by growing Maryland Mammoth in pots under controlled conditions. One group was left in the field with natural long summer days. The other group was moved to a dark shed every afternoon, artificially shortening the day. Only the plants with artificially shortened days flowered, revealing that Maryland Mammoth needed short days to trigger flowering.

This discovery — that plants measure day length to time their reproduction — revolutionized botany and agriculture. It showed that plants possess an internal biological clock coupled to light-sensing mechanisms, allowing them to anticipate seasonal changes.

## Plants Measure Darkness, Not Light

A crucial refinement came in the 1940s when scientists discovered that photoperiodic responses are actually controlled by the length of continuous darkness, not the length of light. This was demonstrated through night-break experiments:

- A short-day plant (e.g., soybean, chrysanthemum) kept in short days will flower normally
- If given a brief flash of light in the middle of the long night, it will not flower — as if the night had been shortened
- A long-day plant (e.g., iris, barley) kept in short days will not flower
- If given a brief flash of light in the middle of the long night, it will flower — as if the night had been shortened

The critical insight is that plants do not count hours of light; they count hours of uninterrupted darkness. The "long-day" and "short-day" terminology is technically misleading — the plants are really "short-night" and "long-night" plants.

## Classification of Photoperiodic Responses

Plants are classified by their flowering response to day/night length:

### Short-Day Plants (Long-Night Plants)

These flower when the continuous dark period exceeds a critical length. Examples include:

- Chrysanthemum ([[chrysanthemum-morifolium]]) — flowers in autumn when nights exceed ~12 hours
- Soybean (Glycine max) — flowers as days shorten in late summer
- Poinsettia ([[euphorbia-pulcherrima]]) — requires long nights to develop its characteristic red bracts
- Cannabis (Cannabis sativa) — most photoperiodic cultivars flower under 12-hour dark periods

### Long-Day Plants (Short-Night Plants)

These flower when the continuous dark period is shorter than a critical length. Examples include:

- Iris (Iris spp.) — flowers in spring and early summer
- Barley ([[hordeum-vulgare]]) — flowers as days lengthen in spring
- Spinach ([[spinacia-oleracea]]) — bolts and flowers under long days
- Arabidopsis ([[arabidopsis-thaliana]]) — the model organism flowers rapidly under long days

### Day-Neutral Plants

These flower regardless of photoperiod, relying instead on other cues such as plant age, temperature, or accumulated heat. Examples include tomato, rice, and cucumber.

## The Role of Phytochrome

The molecular mechanism of photoperiod sensing depends on phytochrome, the red/far-red photoreceptor:

- During daylight, red light continuously converts phytochrome to its active Pfr form
- After sunset, Pfr gradually reverts to the inactive Pr form through dark reversion over several hours
- If the night is long enough, all Pfr reverts to Pr — the plant registers "long night"
- If dawn arrives before all Pfr has reverted, residual Pfr signals "short night"
- A flash of red light during the night converts remaining Pr to Pfr, resetting the clock
- Far-red light immediately reverses the red flash, restoring the original night-length reading

The phytochrome system thus acts as a biochemical hourglass, with Pfr as the "sand" that gradually drains away during darkness.

## Darwin and Phototropism: The Precursor

Before photoperiodism was understood, Charles Darwin and his son Francis laid the groundwork for plant light sensing through their studies of phototropism (the bending of plants toward light). In their 1880 book "The [[darwin-power-of-movement-in-plants]] in Plants," they demonstrated through elegant experiments with canary grass seedlings that:

- The "eyes" of the plant for phototropism are at the shoot tip (coleoptile tip)
- The bending response occurs in the stem below the tip
- The tip perceives light direction and sends a signal downward to the stem
- Covering the tip with a lightproof cap eliminated the bending response
- Covering the tip with a transparent cap allowed normal bending

Julius von Sachs had previously established that blue light is the primary color inducing phototropism. Plants are effectively blind to green light for this purpose. This blue-light phototropism system (mediated by phototropins) is entirely separate from the red/far-red phytochrome system used for photoperiodism — demonstrating that plants possess multiple, independent light-sensing systems.

## The Mobile Signal: Florigen

A key question in photoperiodism research was how the leaf-generated signal reaches the shoot apex to induce flowering. This mobile signal was hypothesized for decades and named "florigen" by the Russian plant physiologist Mikhail Chailakhyan in 1936.

The molecular identity of florigen was finally established in 2005 when the FT (FLOWERING LOCUS T) protein was identified as the primary component. FT is produced in leaves in response to photoperiodic signals and travels through the phloem to the shoot apical meristem, where it activates flowering genes.

## The Circadian Clock Connection

Photoperiodism is intimately linked to the plant circadian clock — an internal 24-hour oscillator that persists even in constant conditions. The circadian clock does not directly measure day length; instead, it provides the temporal framework within which external light signals are interpreted.

The circadian clock consists of interconnected feedback loops of gene expression involving genes such as CCA1, LHY, TOC1, and ELF3 in Arabidopsis. These oscillations create "gates" — windows of sensitivity during which external light signals can most effectively influence downstream responses. The clock ensures that the plant's photoperiodic measurement is consistent from day to day and can distinguish gradual seasonal changes from transient weather fluctuations.

## Photoperiodism in Cannabis

Cannabis presents a particularly interesting case for photoperiodism because:

- Most commercially grown cultivars are short-day plants that flower when nights exceed approximately 12 hours
- Indoor growers exploit this by maintaining 18 hours of light during vegetative growth, then switching to 12 hours of light to trigger flowering
- The transition typically takes 1-2 weeks, during which the plant redirects energy from vegetative growth to flower (bud) production
- Autoflowering cultivars exist that carry mutations in photoperiod pathway genes (often related to the CO/FT module), allowing them to flower based on age rather than day length
- The interaction between photoperiodism and cannabinoid production is commercially significant, as the flowering stage is when THC, CBD, and terpenes are produced in greatest quantities

## Quantitative Aspects

The photoperiodic response can be described quantitatively:

- **Critical night length** — the minimum continuous dark period required to induce flowering in short-day plants (typically 8-12 hours depending on species and cultivar)
- **Critical day length** — the minimum day length required to induce flowering in long-day plants (typically 12-16 hours)
- **Qualitative response** — some plants show an all-or-nothing flowering response (obligate photoperiodism)
- **Quantitative response** — others show a gradual acceleration or delay of flowering (facultative photoperiodism)
- **Sensitivity range** — the range of night lengths over which the flowering response changes from maximum to minimum

## Practical Applications

Photoperiodism knowledge has numerous agricultural applications:

- **Seasonal flower production** — chrysanthemums are kept from flowering by night-break lighting in autumn, then allowed to flower on schedule for Mother's Day
- **Geographic adaptation** — understanding photoperiod requirements allows breeders to develop varieties adapted to specific latitudes
- **Greenhouse cultivation** — supplemental lighting extends the effective day length, enabling year-round production of long-day crops or prevention of flowering in short-day crops
- **Cannabis cultivation** — indoor growers use 18/6 (vegetative) and 12/12 (flowering) light cycles to control the transition from growth to flowering, exploiting the plant's photoperiodic response
