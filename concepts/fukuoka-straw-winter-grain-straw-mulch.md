# Winter Grain Growing in Straw Mulch

## The Rice/Barley Cropping Tradition

Japanese farmers have long practiced the continuous rotation of rice with
barley. This has enabled them to reap the same harvest year after year
indefinitely, something which they have always regarded as perfectly natural.
Yet this type of rotational cropping is an extraordinary method of farming
that has taken hold nowhere else in the world.

The reason rice and barley can be grown in continuous succession each year is
that the rice is grown in paddy fields, the soil fertility of which has been
built up by a superior method of irrigation. About seventy percent of the
nitrogenous components absorbed by rice and barley are supplied directly by
the soil, while about thirty percent are furnished artificially by
fertilization. If all the straw and chaff from the threshed grain were returned
to the fields, farmers would only have to apply at most fifteen percent of the
nitrogenous components required by the plants.

## Natural Rice and Barley/Wheat Cropping

Fukuoka adopted the standpoint of natural farming early on, and discontinuing
the transplantation of rice, sought his own method of rice and barley direct
seeding. In the process, he gradually approached a unified technique of
direct-seeding naked barley and rice without tilling. He began a method of
cultivation involving direct seeding and straw mulching as the basic pattern
for natural farming.

This method was studied at a large number of agricultural testing stations
throughout Japan. In almost every instance, researchers found there to be no
basic problem with the no-tillage, succession cropping of rice and barley
using straw mulch. Fukuoka called this the "clover revolution" in rice and
barley cropping to voice his opposition to modern scientific farming with its
use of chemicals and large machinery.

## The Complete Annual Cycle

### Fall (October-November)

In early or mid-October, clover seeds are sown over the standing heads of
rice. About two weeks before harvesting the rice, barley seed is sown. The
rice is harvested while treading over the young barley seedlings. After
threshing and cleaning, the straw is immediately scattered uncut over the
entire field. Chicken manure or decomposed organic matter is applied.

Rice seed enclosed in clay pellets is scattered over the field in mid-November
or later. This completes the sowing of rice and barley for the coming year.

### Spring

A thick layer of clover grows at the foot of the maturing barley, and beneath
the clover, rice seedlings begin to emerge. The clover is cut together with
the barley during harvest, but this does not interfere with the harvesting
work.

### Late May (Barley Harvest)

When the barley is cut, the rice seedlings are perhaps an inch or two high.
After leaving the barley on the ground to dry for three days, it is gathered
into bundles, threshed and cleaned. The barley straw is scattered uncut over
the entire field, and chicken manure spread over this. The trampled rice
seedlings emerge through the barley straw and the clover grows back.

### Early June

When the rich growth of clover appears about to choke out the young rice
seedlings, the levees are plastered with mud and water held in the field for
four to seven days to weaken the clover. After this, the field is
surface-drained to grow hardy plants. During the first half of the rice
growing season, irrigation is not strictly necessary, but water may be passed
briefly over the field once every week to ten days.

## The Revolutionary Importance of Straw

### Why Straw Matters

Straw promotes seed germination, holds back winter weeds, and enriches the
soil. All of the straw and chaff obtained when harvesting and threshing the
rice should be scattered uncut over the entire surface of the field. Barley
straw too should be returned to the field after the harvest, but this must be
done as soon as possible following threshing because once dried barley straw
is wet by rain, it becomes more than five times as heavy and very difficult
to transport.

### The Bold Step of Fresh Straw

Although it may appear to be crude and backward, spreading fresh straw on a
rice field is really quite a bold and revolutionary step in rice farming. The
agricultural technician has always regarded rice straw as nothing but a source
of rice diseases and pests, so the common and accepted practice has been to
apply the straw only when fully decomposed as prepared compost. That rice straw
must be burned as a primary source of rice blast disease is virtually gospel
in some circles, as illustrated by the burning of rice straw on an immense
scale in Hokkaido.

Fukuoka deliberately called composting unnecessary and proposed that all the
fresh rice straw be scattered over the field during barley cultivation and all
the barley straw be spread over the field during rice cultivation. But this is
only possible with strong, healthy grain.

### The Fate of the Land

Straw produced on Japanese rice fields is of great importance as a source of
organic fertilizer and for protecting the fields and enriching the soil. Yet
today the practice of burning such invaluable material is spreading throughout
Japan. Are there no farmers, scientists, or agricultural administrators out
there who see that whether or not we spread straw over our fields may decide
the fate of our national lands? It is from just such a small matter that shall
emerge the future of Japanese agriculture.

## Fertilization in the Natural System

Following the rice harvest, spread 650-900 pounds of chicken manure per
quarter-acre either before or after returning the rice straw to the fields.
An additional 200 pounds may be added in late February as a top dressing
during the barley heading stage. After the barley harvest, manure again for
the rice. When high yields have been collected, spread 450-900 pounds of dried
chicken manure before or after returning the barley straw to the field.
