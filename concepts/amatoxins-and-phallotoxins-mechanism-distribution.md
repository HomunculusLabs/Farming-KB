# Amatoxins and Phallotoxins: Mechanism of Action and Distribution

Amatoxins and phallotoxins are two families of cyclic peptide toxins produced primarily by
species in the genus Amanita, notably A. phalloides (the death cap), A. virosa (the destroying
angel), A. verna (the fool's mushroom), and several Galerina and Lepiota species. These toxins
are responsible for the vast majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide, with mortality
rates of 10-60% depending on the dose ingested and the speed of medical intervention. The
death cap alone is estimated to cause more fatalities than all other [[palmer-poisonous-mushrooms-deadly-species]] combined,
accounting for over 90% of mushroom-related deaths globally.

## Amatoxins: Structure and Chemistry

The amatoxin family consists of at least nine characterized compounds, with alpha-amanitin,
beta-amanitin, gamma-amanitin, and epsilon-amanitin being the most significant toxicologically.
Amatoxins are bicyclic octapeptides with a molecular weight of approximately 900 Da. They share
a common structural core: a macrocyclic ring formed by eight amino acid residues, bridged by a
sulfoxide-containing tryptophan derivative called 2'-hydroxy-2-(3'-hydroxy-3'-methylglutaryl)
tryptophan. This bridged bicyclic structure confers remarkable chemical stability, making
amatoxins resistant to heat, acid, and [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] — which is why cooking, drying, or
freezing contaminated mushrooms does not neutralize the toxins.

The individual amatoxins differ in the side chains of specific amino acid positions. Alpha-amanitin
and beta-amanitin are the most toxic, with alpha-amanitin having an LD50 in mice of approximately
0.1 mg/kg body weight when administered intraperitoneally. The structural differences between
amatoxins and the related phallotoxins are subtle but pharmacologically crucial: amatoxins
contain a 6'-hydroxy-tryptophan bridge, while phallotoxins contain a dihydroxyproline bridge,
and this single difference determines their distinct mechanisms of action and toxicity profiles.

## Amatoxin Mechanism of Action

Amatoxins exert their lethal effects by inhibiting RNA polymerase II, the enzyme responsible
for transcribing protein-coding genes in eukaryotic cells. The toxins bind with extraordinary
affinity to RNA polymerase II with a dissociation constant (Kd) in the nanomolar range, forming
an essentially irreversible complex that blocks the translocation step of transcription. This
means that while the enzyme can still initiate RNA synthesis, it cannot elongate the growing
mRNA strand beyond approximately 10-15 nucleotides.

The consequences of RNA polymerase II inhibition are catastrophic for the cell. Without new
mRNA production, protein synthesis cannot be sustained as existing mRNAs are degraded through
normal turnover processes. Within hours of exposure, cellular protein levels begin to decline,
affecting all cellular functions from metabolism to structural maintenance. The half-life of
different proteins varies considerably, which determines the timing of organ dysfunction. Cells
with high metabolic rates and short protein half-lives are affected first.

Amatoxins show differential toxicity among cell types due to variations in their uptake
mechanisms. Hepatocytes (liver cells) are particularly susceptible because they express specific
organic anion transporting polypeptides (OATPs, particularly OATP1B1 and OATP1B3) that
actively transport amatoxins across the plasma membrane. This transporter-mediated uptake
concentrates amatoxins in the liver to levels far exceeding those in the bloodstream, explaining
the severe hepatotoxicity that characterizes [[bloomfield-amatoxin-poisoning-mechanism]]. Renal tubular cells also
express OATPs and are the second most affected cell type, accounting for the acute kidney injury
that often accompanies liver damage in severe poisoning cases.

## Phallotoxins: Structure and Mechanism

The phallotoxin family includes phalloidin, phallacidin, phallin, phallisacin, and phallisin.
Phallotoxins are also bicyclic heptapeptides (seven amino acids, unlike the octapeptide
amatoxins) with molecular weights around 800-900 Da. They share the characteristic
bicyclic structure but differ from amatoxins in their bridge composition and biological activity.

Phallotoxins primarily target the actin cytoskeleton. They bind to F-actin (filamentous actin)
with high affinity and stabilize the filamentous form, preventing depolymerization into G-actin
(globular actin). This disrupts the dynamic equilibrium between F-actin and G-actin that is
essential for cell motility, cytokinesis, vesicle trafficking, and maintenance of cell shape.
The stabilization of F-actin by phalloidin is so specific and tight that fluorescently labeled
phalloidin derivatives are widely used in cell biology laboratories to stain and visualize
actin filaments in fixed cells.

Despite their potent cytoskeletal effects in vitro, phallotoxins are considerably less toxic
than amatoxins when ingested. This is because phallotoxins are poorly absorbed from the
gastrointestinal tract and are rapidly degraded by proteases and bile acids in the digestive
system. Their oral bioavailability is negligible compared to amatoxins, which pass through
the intestinal lining and enter the bloodstream efficiently. For this reason, phallotoxins are
not considered significant contributors to the clinical picture of human [[amanita-poisoning-identification-antidotes-palmer]],
though they may contribute to the severe gastrointestinal symptoms observed in the early phase.

## Clinical Course of Amatoxin Poisoning

The clinical presentation of amatoxin poisoning follows a characteristic biphasic course that
has been recognized since antiquity:

**Phase 1 (6-24 hours post-ingestion):** A severe gastrointestinal phase characterized by
abdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting, profuse watery diarrhea, and sometimes dehydration and
electrolyte imbalances. This phase can be so severe that it mimics acute gastroenteritis or
food poisoning, leading to misdiagnosis. Patients may temporarily improve after this phase,
creating a dangerous false sense of recovery.

**Phase 2 (24-72 hours):** A deceptive asymptomatic or latency period during which the patient
appears to recover. However, during this phase, amatoxins are actively destroying hepatocytes.
Liver enzymes (ALT, AST) begin to rise, and coagulation parameters may start to deteriorate.
This quiet period is clinically treacherous because patients may be discharged from emergency
care before the liver damage becomes apparent.

**Phase 3 (72-96 hours):** The hepatorenal phase, marked by rapidly progressive liver failure
with jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, elevated transaminases (often exceeding 5,000 U/L),
coagulopathy, hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, and acute kidney injury. In severe cases, this
progresses to fulminant hepatic failure, cerebral edema, multi-organ dysfunction, and death.
Survivors of severe poisoning may require liver transplantation.

**Phase 4 (survivors):** Recovery over weeks to months, with potential long-term liver damage.

## Treatment Approaches

Current treatment for amatoxin poisoning relies on multiple strategies: aggressive
gastrointestinal decontamination (activated charcoal, gastric lavage), intravenous
administration of high-dose penicillin G and silibinin (the active compound from milk thistle,
[[silybum-marianum]]), which competitively inhibit OATP-mediated hepatic uptake of amatoxins.
N-acetylcysteine (NAC) may provide antioxidant protection to hepatocytes. In cases of fulminant
liver failure, emergency liver transplantation remains the only definitive life-saving intervention.
Early recognition and prompt initiation of therapy are critical for survival.

## Species Distribution

Beyond Amanita, amatoxins have been detected in several genera including Galerina (particularly
G. marginata and G. autumnalis), Conocybe filaris, and Lepiota species (L. helveola, L.
brunneoincarnata). The ability to produce amatoxins has evolved independently in these
phylogenetically distant lineages, suggesting convergent evolution of this potent defense
mechanism. The biosynthetic pathway involves a prolyl oligopeptidase (POPB) gene that has
been identified through comparative genomics of toxic and non-toxic Amanita species. The
presence of the alpha-amanitin biosynthetic gene cluster (specifically the MSDIN gene family
and POPB) reliably predicts amatoxin production and may be useful for rapid toxin screening
of mushroom species through [[biodiversity-of-fungi-pcr-molecular-methods-fungal-diversity]].
