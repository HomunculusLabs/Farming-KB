# Amatoxins and Phallotoxins: Mechanism of Action and Distribution

Amatoxins and phallotoxins are two families of cyclic peptide toxins produced primarily by
species in the genus Amanita, notably A. phalloides ([[query-how-to-identify-and-avoid-the-death-cap-amanita-phalloides]]), A. virosa (the destroying
angel), A. verna (the fool's mushroom), and several Galerina and Lepiota species. These toxins
are responsible for the vast majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide, with mortality
rates of 10-60% depending on the dose ingested and the speed of medical intervention. The
death cap alone is estimated to cause more fatalities than all other [[enzymatic-degradation-in-mycoremediation]] — which is why cooking, drying, or
freezing contaminated mushrooms does not neutralize the toxins.

The individual amatoxins differ in the side chains of specific [[cho-fish-amino-acid-preparation]] positions. Alpha-amanitin
and beta-amanitin are the most toxic, with alpha-amanitin having an LD50 in mice of approximately
0.1 mg/kg body weight when administered intraperitoneally. The structural differences between
amatoxins and the related phallotoxins are subtle but pharmacologically crucial: amatoxins
contain a 6'-hydroxy-tryptophan bridge, while phallotoxins contain a dihydroxyproline bridge,
and this single difference determines their distinct mechanisms of action and toxicity profiles.

## Amatoxin Mechanism of Action

Amatoxins exert their lethal effects by inhibiting RNA polymerase II, the enzyme responsible
for transcribing protein-coding genes in eukaryotic cells. The toxins bind with extraordinary
affinity to RNA polymerase II with a dissociation constant (Kd) in the nanomolar range, forming
an essentially irreversible complex that blocks the translocation step of transcription. This
means that while the enzyme can still initiate RNA synthesis, it cannot elongate the growing
mRNA strand beyond approximately 10-15 nucleotides.

The consequences of RNA polymerase II inhibition are catastrophic for the cell. Without new
mRNA production, protein synthesis cannot be sustained as existing mRNAs are degraded through
normal turnover processes. Within hours of exposure, cellular protein levels begin to decline,
affecting all cellular functions from metabolism to structural maintenance. The half-life of
different proteins varies considerably, which determines the timing of organ dysfunction. Cells
with high metabolic rates and short protein half-lives are affected first.

Amatoxins show differential toxicity among cell types due to variations in their uptake
mechanisms. Hepatocytes (liver cells) are particularly susceptible because they express specific
organic anion transporting polypeptides (OATPs, particularly OATP1B1 and OATP1B3) that
actively transport amatoxins across the plasma membrane. This transporter-mediated uptake
concentrates amatoxins in the liver to levels far exceeding those in the bloodstream, explaining
the severe hepatotoxicity that characterizes [[amanita-poisoning-identification-antidotes-palmer]],
though they may contribute to the severe gastrointestinal symptoms observed in the early phase.

## Clinical Course of Amatoxin Poisoning

The clinical presentation of amatoxin poisoning follows a characteristic biphasic course that
has been recognized since antiquity:

**Phase 1 (6-24 hours post-ingestion):** A severe gastrointestinal phase characterized by
abdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting, profuse watery diarrhea, and sometimes dehydration and
electrolyte imbalances. This phase can be so severe that it mimics acute gastroenteritis or
food poisoning, leading to misdiagnosis. Patients may temporarily improve after this phase,
creating a dangerous false sense of recovery.

**Phase 2 (24-72 hours):** A deceptive asymptomatic or latency period during which the patient
appears to recover. However, during this phase, amatoxins are actively destroying hepatocytes.
Liver enzymes (ALT, AST) begin to rise, and coagulation parameters may start to deteriorate.
This quiet period is clinically treacherous because patients may be discharged from emergency
care before the liver damage becomes apparent.

**Phase 3 (72-96 hours):** The hepatorenal phase, marked by rapidly progressive liver failure
with jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, elevated transaminases (often exceeding 5,000 U/L),
coagulopathy, hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, and acute kidney injury. In severe cases, this
progresses to fulminant hepatic failure, cerebral edema, multi-organ dysfunction, and death.
Survivors of severe poisoning may require liver transplantation.

**Phase 4 (survivors):** Recovery over weeks to months, with potential long-term liver damage.
