# Cold Frames and Season Extension

Cold frames are the simplest, least expensive, and most time-tested tool for modifying the microclimate around growing plants. They represent the foundational technology of season extension—the practice of creating conditions that allow crops to be grown outside their natural outdoor season. A cold frame is essentially a bottomless box with a transparent top (traditionally glass, now often plastic or polycarbonate) that captures solar energy to create a warmer, more sheltered growing environment.

## How Cold Frames Work

A cold frame works by trapping solar radiation. Sunlight passes through the transparent cover and warms the soil and air inside the frame. The cover then prevents this heat from escaping as quickly as it would in the open air. The result is a microclimate that is consistently warmer than the surrounding environment—approximately equivalent to moving the growing area 1.5 USDA hardiness zones to the south, or about 500 miles southward along the East Coast of the United States.

In practical terms, a standard cold frame will typically prevent the crops inside from freezing until the outdoor temperature drops below approximately 25°F (-4°C). This level of protection is sufficient to keep cold-hardy crops alive through much of the fall and winter in temperate climates.

## The Traditional Cold Frame Design

After extensive experimentation with different designs, the standard old-time cold frame remains the most effective:

- **Construction**: A bottomless box made of 2-inch-thick wooden planks
- **Dimensions**: 8 to 12 inches high at the back, 6 to 8 inches high at the front (the sloped design allows maximum sunlight capture and rain runoff)
- **Cover**: Glass panels, historically known as "Dutch lights"—approximately 30 by 60 inches set in a lightweight wooden frame
- **Orientation**: The frame faces south (in the Northern Hemisphere) to maximize solar gain

The Dutch light design, which Eliot Coleman adopted after studying cold frame systems in Holland during the 1970s, was simpler to construct than the French models and proved highly effective. The key insight from comparative studies of different cold frame designs is that simplicity is an advantage—basic materials and construction have proven more durable and effective than high-tech alternatives like Styrofoam insulation or reflective covers.

## The Double-Layer Breakthrough

The most significant innovation in cold frame technology came from the simple act of placing cold frames inside an unheated greenhouse. This "double layer" system was discovered somewhat accidentally in the winter of 1980, when Coleman built an unheated greenhouse in the Dutch style (2×4 structural frame covered with Dutch lights) and placed cold frames inside over the winter crops.

The results were transformative. The two layers of protection moved the effective climate an additional 500 miles to the south—the equivalent of USDA Zone 8—while also providing a snow-shedding roof. This double-layer principle became the foundation of the entire [[winter-harvest-system]].

### The Conceptual Shift

The double-layer discovery led to two important conceptual realizations:

1. **Not fighting the cold**: The system does not actively battle against winter cold, as a heated greenhouse does for warm-weather crops. Instead, it maintains a protected microclimate sufficient for the needs of naturally cold-hardy plants. The analogy is the difference between sitting by a fire on a cold day versus going outside with enough layers of clothing to stay comfortable.

2. **Extending the harvest, not the growing season**: After mid-November, most cold-hardy crops enter a state of semi-dormancy. They are not actively growing at summer speed but are "idling their engines," remaining alive and harvestable. The goal is not to keep plants growing vigorously through winter but to preserve them in a harvestable state.

## From Cold Frames to Row Covers

While cold frames remained the inner layer of protection during the experimental years, the shift to commercial production in the early 1990s necessitated a more efficient inner layer. Cold frames, though effective, are costly to build and require daily manual venting to prevent overheating on sunny days—a significant labor burden at commercial scale.

The replacement was **floating row cover** (spun-bonded polypropylene or polyester fabric) held about a foot above the soil on flat-topped wire wickets. Row covers offered several advantages:

| Feature | Cold Frames | Row Covers |
|---------|------------|------------|
| Cost | High (wood, glass, hardware) | Low (fabric and wire) |
| Ventilation | Manual (must open/close daily) | Self-venting (porous fabric) |
| Weight | Heavy | Light |
| Installation/removal | Labor-intensive | Quick and easy |
| Frost protection | Greater (~25°F threshold) | Slightly less |

Although row covers did not provide quite as much climatic protection as cold frames, the trade-offs strongly favored them for commercial operations. The self-venting property alone eliminated hours of daily labor during sunny winter days.

## Movable Greenhouses

Another key evolution from the cold frame concept was the development of movable greenhouses. Rather than keeping a greenhouse permanently in one location (which eventually leads to soil problems from continuous cultivation), the greenhouse structure is moved between two or more sites on a rotational basis.

Several movable greenhouse designs were developed and tested:

1. **Wooden skid design**: Greased wooden skids sliding on wooden rails
2. **Ball caster design**: Rails on the bottom of the structure rolling along ball casters set into the tops of support posts
3. **Sled runner design**: Runners sliding directly along the ground like a sled
4. **Flanged wheel design**: Flanged wheels on the bottom of each hoop rolling along pipes on the ground

The metal sled-runner design proved most practical for commercial-scale houses (30 feet wide by 96 feet long), pulled by a tractor and secured with ground anchors once in position.

## Practical Applications

### Getting Started with Cold Frames

For home gardeners and small-scale growers, cold frames remain an excellent entry point into season extension:

1. **Site selection**: Choose a south-facing location with good drainage and protection from strong winds
2. **Construction**: Build a simple box from 2-inch lumber with a south-sloping lid
3. **Use cases**: Start seedlings in early spring, harden off transplants, extend fall harvest of cold-hardy crops, overwinter perennial herbs
4. **Management**: Prop the lid open on sunny days (above 40°F) to prevent overheating; close it before sunset to trap heat

### Scaling to Commercial Production

For growers moving beyond cold frames:

- **High tunnels / hoop houses**: 30×96-foot unheated structures provide the outer layer
- **Inner layer**: Row cover on wire wickets for ease of management
- **Mobility**: Movable structures allow soil rotation and rest periods
- **Succession planting**: Multiple planting dates from August through October ensure continuous winter harvest
