## Fungal Decomposition and Leaf Litter Dynamics

Dighton (2003) provides a comprehensive treatment of how fungi drive leaf litter
decomposition, emphasizing the succession of fungal species on decomposing
substrates, the role of litter chemistry in governing decomposition rates, and
the interactions between fungi and other soil organisms. The decomposition
process is a cascade mediated by fungal enzymatic activity, [[fungal-nitrogen-mineralization-pathways]]. Where C:N or
lignin:N ratios are high, decomposition rates are reduced. Herbaceous leaf
litter ranges from 15:1 to 160:1 in C:N ratio, while tree leaf litter ranges
from 20:1 to 300:1. Secondary plant metabolites, particularly polyphenols and
tannins, also inhibit decomposition rates by soil microorganisms.

Vanlauwe et al. (1997) showed that decomposition (mass loss) and nitrogen
mineralization rates are most strongly correlated to the (lignin +
polyphenol):N ratio of the resource. The C:N ratio alone explains 74% of
variation in litter mass loss, while lignin:N explains 68%. Cellulose becomes
unavailable for microbial use when the cellulose:lignin ratio declines below
0.5, a threshold identified by Linkins et al. (1984) as critical in
determining whether decomposition can proceed.

## Fungal Succession on Leaf Litter

Colonization of leaf litter follows a predictable successional pattern driven
by changing resource chemistry and the enzymatic competence of fungal species:

**Stage 1 — Initial colonizers (sugar fungi):** Species present on the leaf
at abscission (e.g., Lophodermium, Ceuthospora, and Lophodermella on Pinus
sylvestris needles) utilize soluble carbohydrates. Ponge (1990, 1991)
characterized this initial phase by browning of the leaf and decomposition of
relatively available resources. Phylloplane fungal species act as saprotrophs
on freshly fallen leaf litter.

**Stage 2 — Secondary colonizers:** Microfungi such as Verticicladium and
basidiomycetes such as Marasmius and Collybia invade leaf tissue as cellulose
and lignin decomposition proceeds. The C:N ratio decreases from approximately
200:1 to 30:1. Frankland (1992, 1998) described this pattern on Pteridium
aquilinum litter, from lesion-forming Rhizographus and Aureobasidium through
basidiomycete colonization related to cellulose and lignin loss.

**Stage 3 — Late-stage colonizers:** Mycorrhizal fungi enter along with soil
arthropods, exploiting more recalcitrant materials. The close association
between mycorrhizal fungi and decomposing organic matter has been shown for
