# Fungal Decomposition and Leaf Litter Dynamics

Dighton (2003) provides a comprehensive treatment of how fungi drive leaf litter
decomposition, emphasizing the succession of fungal species on decomposing
substrates, the role of litter chemistry in governing decomposition rates, and
the interactions between fungi and other soil organisms. The decomposition
process is a cascade mediated by fungal enzymatic activity, resource quality,
and competitive interactions among species.

## Resource Quality and Decomposition Rate

The chemical composition of leaf litter is the primary determinant of its
resistance to decomposition. The C:N and lignin:N ratios serve as key
predictors of decomposition rate and nitrogen mineralization. Where C:N or
lignin:N ratios are high, decomposition rates are reduced. Herbaceous leaf
litter ranges from 15:1 to 160:1 in C:N ratio, while tree leaf litter ranges
from 20:1 to 300:1. Secondary plant metabolites, particularly polyphenols and
tannins, also inhibit decomposition rates by soil microorganisms.

Vanlauwe et al. (1997) showed that decomposition (mass loss) and nitrogen
mineralization rates are most strongly correlated to the (lignin +
polyphenol):N ratio of the resource. The C:N ratio alone explains 74% of
variation in litter mass loss, while lignin:N explains 68%. Cellulose becomes
unavailable for microbial use when the cellulose:lignin ratio declines below
0.5, a threshold identified by Linkins et al. (1984) as critical in
determining whether decomposition can proceed.

## Fungal Succession on Leaf Litter

Colonization of leaf litter follows a predictable successional pattern driven
by changing resource chemistry and the enzymatic competence of fungal species:

**Stage 1 — Initial colonizers (sugar fungi):** Species present on the leaf
at abscission (e.g., Lophodermium, Ceuthospora, and Lophodermella on Pinus
sylvestris needles) utilize soluble carbohydrates. Ponge (1990, 1991)
characterized this initial phase by browning of the leaf and decomposition of
relatively available resources. Phylloplane fungal species act as saprotrophs
on freshly fallen leaf litter.

**Stage 2 — Secondary colonizers:** Microfungi such as Verticicladium and
basidiomycetes such as Marasmius and Collybia invade leaf tissue as cellulose
and lignin decomposition proceeds. The C:N ratio decreases from approximately
200:1 to 30:1. Frankland (1992, 1998) described this pattern on Pteridium
aquilinum litter, from lesion-forming Rhizographus and Aureobasidium through
basidiomycete colonization related to cellulose and lignin loss.

**Stage 3 — Late-stage colonizers:** Mycorrhizal fungi enter along with soil
arthropods, exploiting more recalcitrant materials. The close association
between mycorrhizal fungi and decomposing organic matter has been shown for
both ectomycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal associations.

## The Swift et al. (1979) Model

The classic decomposition model proposes that initial carbohydrate utilization
occurs until the C:nutrient ratio approaches that of the decomposer organism
(approximately 15:1 for P and 6:1 for N in fungi). Only then does net
conversion of organic nutrient to inorganic nutrient (net mineralization)
occur. Initial resource structure is chemically heterogeneous, supporting a
variety of fungal species. As decomposition proceeds, recalcitrant chemicals
remain that can be degraded only by fungi capable of producing the necessary
enzymes. Diversity thus decreases through the decomposition cascade.

## Fungal Enzyme Systems

Different enzyme systems degrade specific plant compounds. Sinsabaugh and
Liptak (1997) compiled a comprehensive list:

- **Lignin**: Lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, glucose oxidase,
  cellobiose oxidase, arylalcohol oxidase, glyoxaloxidase, laccases
- **Cellulose**: Exo-1,4-beta-glucanase, endo-1,4-beta-glucanase,
  1,4-beta-glucosidases
- **Hemicellulose**: Endo-1,4-beta-xylanases, endo-1,4-beta-mannases,
  xylosidases, mannosidases, arabinosidases, glucuronidases, galactosidases
- **Pectin**: Polygalacturonases, pectinlysases, pectinesterases

The ability of different fungal species to produce specific enzymes dictates
the succession of fungi as they colonize resources. In addition to enzymatic
competence, relative growth rates, production of antibiotic secondary
metabolites, and environmental constraints influence colonization outcomes
(Cook and Rayner, 1984; Lockwood, 1992; Wicklow, 1992).

## Interactions with Bacteria

Fungal-bacterial interactions during leaf litter decomposition are not always
synergistic. Moller et al. (1999) showed that the cellulolytic fungus Humicola
sp. caused double the carbon utilization from beech leaves compared to a
combined fungal-bacterial inoculum. This increase was positively related to
greater beta-N-acetylglucosaminidase and endoexocellulase activity of the
fungus alone. The competitive balance between fungi and bacteria depends on
resource chemistry and environmental conditions.

## Faunal Interactions

Grazing of fungal hyphae by soil animals affects both colonization of resources
and rates of nutrient mineralization. Newell (1984a, 1984b) showed that
collembola grazing altered the vertical distribution of competing mycelia of
Mycena galopus and Marasmius androcaceous in a spruce forest floor.
Preferential grazing of Marasmius restricted its growth to lower depths. In
forests with high faunal densities, relief of grazing pressure increased
decomposition, while in agricultural soils with low faunal densities, faunal
reduction decreased decomposition.

## Spatial and Temporal Patterns

Leaf litter patches on the forest floor vary in size and quality depending on
understory stem density. Dighton et al. (2000) showed that patch size was
dependent upon ericaceous stem density, and resource quality within patches
depended on patch size. Microscopic FT-IR spectroscopy studies (Mascarenhas
et al., 2000; Dighton et al., 2001) reveal real-time microscale (100 x 100
micrometer) changes in leaf surface carbohydrate chemistry caused by fungal
colonization. Scaling these observations from the leaf litter patch scale to
ecosystem-level nutrient cycling remains an unresolved challenge.

In tropical forests, approximately 7% of total leaf litter fall is trapped in
the canopy by fungal hyphae and rhizomorphs of Marasmius and Marasmiellus
species, forming aerial "soil" that influences canopy fertility and
throughfall chemistry (Hedger et al., 1993).

## Ecosystem Seral Context

During seral succession from herbaceous to forest communities, the quality of
litter inputs changes. Early seral stages contribute high-quality resources
(high C:N, low lignin), favoring sugar fungi, yeasts, ascomycetes, and
mitosporic fungi. Following forest canopy closure, woody resources and
recalcitrant leaf litters dominate, favoring basidiomycetes and increasing
the importance of fungal-fauna interactions. This shift in dominant fungal
groups parallels changes in the chemical composition of plant residues.
