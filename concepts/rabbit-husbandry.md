# Rabbit Husbandry

Rabbits are the most space-efficient meat animal for the homestead. A single
breeding trio (1 buck, 2 does) can produce 80-100+ lbs of meat per year in
a footprint smaller than a garden shed. They are quiet, reproduce rapidly,
and produce lean, mild-tasting meat. Their manure is an outstanding garden
fertilizer that can be applied directly without [[composting]].

## Breed Selection

### Meat Breeds
- **New Zealand White** — The standard meat rabbit. 9-12 lbs mature, 8-10
  babies per litter, 65-day finish. Calm, good mothers.
- **Californian** — Similar to NZW, slightly hardier. 8-10.5 lbs, good
  mothers, good meat-to-bone ratio.
- **Silver Fox** — Heritage breed, 9-12 lbs. Excellent meat quality,
  beautiful pelt. Good mothers. Listed as threatened.
- **Champagne d'Argent** — Heritage, 9-12 lbs. Excellent meat, silvered
  pelts. Good mothers.
- **Flemish Giant** — 14-20+ lbs. Can be bred with NZW for fast-growing
  crosses. Eats more but produces larger fryers.
- **Satin** — 8-11 lbs. Fine-boned, high meat-to-bone ratio. Pelts have
  unique sheen. Good mothers.
- **Cinnamon** — Heritage, 8-11 lbs. Good meat, attractive rust-colored
  pelts. Good disposition.

### Dual-Purpose (Meat + Fur)
- **Rex** — 7-10 lbs. Plush velvet-like fur, good meat quality. Popular
  for pelts.
- **Palomino** — 8-11 lbs. Good meat, golden-colored pelts.
- **Standard Chinchilla** — 5.5-7 lbs. Smaller but efficient, beautiful
  pelts. Good mothers.

### Fur Breeds (Less Meat)
- **Angora** — Bred for wool fiber, not ideal for meat production. Require
  significant grooming.

## Housing (Hutches and Colonies)

### Individual Hutch System
- Standard wire cage: 24x30 inches for a single doe, 30x36 for a buck or
  doe with litter.
- 1x2 inch welded wire floor (allows droppings to fall through). Some
  homesteaders prefer solid floors with bedding to prevent sore hocks.
- Hutch height: 18-24 inches.
- Wire cages should have a resting board (wood or plastic) to prevent sore
  hocks.
- Hang hutches at waist height for ease of management and predator
  protection.
- All-wire construction with a wooden or metal frame. Protect from wind and
  rain with a roof and windbreaks.
- Automatic waterers (nipple type) or heavy crocks that cannot be tipped.
- J-feeders attached to cage wire for dry feed.

### Colony System
- Rabbits live in a group on the ground in a large enclosure.
- More natural, less labor-intensive, allows natural behaviors.
- Challenges: aggression, difficult to track breeding, higher disease
  transmission, predator vulnerability.
- Provide multiple hiding places and nesting boxes. Underground burrows are
  ideal.
- Best suited to hardy heritage breeds with strong maternal instincts.

### Housing Requirements
- Temperature: rabbits tolerate cold (down to 0°F with shelter) but are
  extremely heat-sensitive. Above 85°F causes heat stroke. Provide frozen
  water bottles, fans, or misters in summer.
- Ventilation: essential. Ammonia from urine damages respiratory tracts.
- Protection: hutches must be predator-proof. Rats, weasels, raccoons,
  foxes, and dogs all prey on rabbits. Use 1/2 inch hardware cloth on lower
  portions.

## Feeding

### Pellets
- High-quality commercial rabbit pellet (16-18% protein) as the base ration.
- Feed 1-1.5 oz per lb of body weight daily for adults.
- Lactating does and growing fryers: free-choice pellets.
- Avoid pellets with animal protein or excessive fillers.

### Hay
- Free-choice grass hay (timothy, orchard grass) is essential for digestion.
- Provides long fiber critical for gut motility.
- Alfalfa hay is good for growing fryers and lactating does (higher protein
  and calcium). Too rich for adult maintenance.

### Fresh Feed
- 10-20% of diet can be fresh greens: [[dandelion]], plantain, comfrey,
  raspberry leaves, clover, parsley.
- Avoid iceberg lettuce (causes diarrhea), cabbage family in excess,
  potatoes, rhubarb.
- Introduce new greens gradually to prevent digestive upset.
- Apple branches and willow provide both food and dental wear.

### Water
- Clean, fresh water at all times. Rabbits drink more than most people expect
  — 1-3 cups daily depending on temperature and feed.

## Breeding

- Sexual maturity: small breeds at 4-5 months, large breeds at 6-8 months.
- Does are induced ovulators — they ovulate in response to mating.
- Take the doe to the buck's cage (never the reverse — bucks are territorial).
- Breeding: multiple successful matings observed. Remove doe after breeding.
- Gestation: 28-32 days (average 31 days).
- Provide nest box at day 27 with clean straw. Doe will pull fur to line
  the nest.
- Litter size: 6-12 kits (average 8 for NZW).
- Rebreed doe 2-4 weeks after kindling for maximum production (4-6 litters
  per year). Or allow longer recovery for doe health.

## Kindling

- Kindling usually occurs in the early morning.
- Most does kindle without assistance. Do not disturb unless a problem is
  suspected.
- Check the nest after kindling: remove any dead kits, ensure remaining kits
  are warm and fed.
- Foster kits between does if one doe has a very large or very small litter.
  Rub kits with the foster doe's fur to transfer scent.
- Kits are born hairless and blind. Eyes open at 10-14 days.
- Weaning: 4-6 weeks. Offer pellets and hay from 3 weeks.
- Kit mortality: aim for under 15%. Common causes: chilling, doe stepping on
  kits, failure to nurse, enteritis.

## Processing

- Ideal harvest weight: 4-5 lbs live weight (8-12 weeks of age).
- Older rabbits are still edible but tougher ("stewers"). Best at young age.
- Dispatch methods: cervical dislocation (quick, requires skill), or
  the broomstick method. Ensure unconsciousness before processing.
- Skin by pulling hide from hind legs forward (rabbit skin is thin and
  pulls off easily with practice).
- Gutting: small incision below the sternum, remove entrails carefully.
  Save liver, heart, kidneys.
- Chill carcass for at least 4 hours before cutting or freezing.
- Yield: approximately 60% dress-out (live to hanging weight).
- See [[animal-harvest-and-processing]] for detailed processing procedures.
