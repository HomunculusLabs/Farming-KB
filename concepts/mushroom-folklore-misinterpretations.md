# Mushroom Folklore: The Hildesheim Doors and Other Misinterpretations

The history of mushroom interpretation is littered with cases where images that appear to depict mushrooms turn out to represent something entirely different. Andy Letcher uses several examples in Shroom: A Cultural History of the Magic Mushroom to illustrate the dangers of interpretation based on visual similarity alone.

## The Hildesheim Cathedral Bronze Doors

The most instructive example comes from Hildesheim, Germany, where a magnificent Gothic cathedral features a pair of cast bronze doors commissioned by Bishop Bernward around 1015 CE.

### The Mushroom Claim
One panel on the right-hand door seems to show human figures dwarfed by what looks extremely like a giant Liberty Cap. Various writers have suggested this is evidence for a medieval magic mushroom cult persisting secretly despite Christian oppression.

### The Reality
The doors contain eight descending panels showing the fall of Man and eight ascending panels showing redemption through Christ. Every image on one door appears paired with its matched antithetical opposite on the other. Given the coherent theological structure and the significance of every element, "it seems improbable that a magic mushroom could have been surreptitiously slipped in without anyone noticing."

The image is simply a stylized representation of "the most biblical of plants, the fig-tree."

### The Lesson
"Clearly it is not enough to identify an image as a representation of a magic mushroom on the basis of homology alone. The problem with prehistoric art is that very often we have only the image, and not the contextual evidence that would tip the interpretation one way or another."

## The Stonehenge Axe/Mushroom Debate

Laser scans revealed previously undiscovered Bronze Age carvings on the sarsen stones of Stonehenge. Some look remarkably like mushrooms in cross section, leading to speculation that:
- The carvings depict magic mushrooms
- Notch-like marks represent gills
- The entire monument was designed to resemble a fairy ring

The more widely accepted interpretation identifies the carvings as Bronze Age axes, "their significance unknown."

## The Tassili "Bemushroomed Shaman"

See [[rock-eating-fungi-and-mineral-weathering]]. The widely circulated image is not a photograph but a drawing by Kat Harrison, informed by her own mushroom experiences. In the original rock art, "it is not quite so emphatic: they could be mushrooms or, say, arrows."

## The Scandinavian Bronze Age Mushroom Motif

See [[amanita-muscaria-guide]]. Reid Kaplan's 1975 identification of a mushroom-shaped motif on Scandinavian artifacts has been challenged by alternative readings: ship's sail, tree, hatchet, or axe. "There is no sure way of telling which, if any, is the correct interpretation."

## The Pattern of Misinterpretation

Letcher identifies a consistent pattern:
1. An ambiguous prehistoric or medieval image is discovered
2. A modern observer with knowledge of psychoactive mushrooms sees a resemblance
3. The mushroom interpretation is proposed and circulated
4. It gains currency through repetition in popular and academic literature
5. Closer examination reveals alternative, more plausible interpretations
6. The mushroom interpretation persists in popular culture despite being debunked

## Why Mushroom Interpretations Are Seductive

Several factors make mushroom interpretations particularly appealing:
- Mushrooms have a distinctive, immediately recognizable form
- The modern Western cultural association between mushrooms and altered consciousness predisposes observers to see "psychedelic" imagery
- The desire to find ancient precedents for modern practices creates confirmation bias
- Ambiguous art allows multiple valid interpretations, making mushroom readings impossible to definitively disprove
- The romance of "secret mushroom cults" makes for compelling narratives

## Implications for Prehistoric Mushroom Use

These cases of misinterpretation do not prove that prehistoric people never used psychoactive mushrooms. They do demonstrate that the visual evidence is far less conclusive than enthusiasts often claim. Letcher's overall argument is that "the absence of evidence cannot be taken as evidence of absence" — but neither can ambiguous images be taken as evidence of use.
