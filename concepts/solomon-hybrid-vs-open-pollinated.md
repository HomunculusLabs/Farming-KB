## Solomon Hybrid vs Open Pollinated Varieties

## The Anti-Hybrid Movement

A movement exists against hybrid vegetables, claiming that OP varieties taste better, are more nutritious, and are necessary for seed saving. Solomon acknowledges the philosophical appeal but warns against romanticizing: many modern hybrids were bred specifically for home garden conditions and may outperform heirlooms in disease resistance and yield. The anti-hybrid stance should be based on evidence, not ideology. Much of the anti-hybrid information in circulation is spread by people who want to profitably sell low-quality open-pollinated seed, and while some criticisms are valid, the propaganda often contains significant half-truths.

## Self-Pollinating Species

For species that self-pollinate -- where the flowers are structured in such a way that they pollinate themselves before they can be cross-pollinated by insects -- hybrid seed is produced by hand, flower by flower. This is labor-intensive and expensive.

When it comes to the solanums (tomato, pepper, eggplant), these are self-pollinating species. Hybrid seeds must be produced painstakingly by hand. However, each pollination results in 100+ seeds in one fruit. Hybrid tomato seed wholesales for close to $1,000 per pound. Hybrid peppers are only slightly cheaper.

There are minor advantages to hybrid solanums -- they may be a bit more productive and can resist certain diseases better than any known OP variety. Hybrid peppers and eggplants are actually quite useful, maybe essential, in maritime climates where conditions are so marginal for the species that OP varieties are not productive. Elsewhere, most gardeners have no need of them. The existence of hybrids within these species is not threatening the continued availability of OP varieties.

## Outcrossing Species

In species that naturally outcross (brassicas, corn, cucurbits), breeders have worked out clever methods of mass-producing hybrid seeds. So it is true on average that modern hybrids are not as nutritious or tasty as old-fashioned OP heirlooms. But it is also true that modern OP varieties developed since 1870 are not either. And much of what passes for "heirloom" OP varieties these days is really just commercial varieties used before WWII or relatively modern varieties that have been misnamed by unethical primary growers producing cheap garden seed.

## Solomon's Practical Advice

1. Be cautious about betting the ranch on heirlooms -- a lot of breeding has gone into modern commercial varieties that resist bolting and disease
2. For outcrossing species, don't try to save seed from too few plants -- the variety will be ruined within two generations
3. For self-pollinating species, seed saving is much easier and safer
4. For your main crop, use quality modern seed; trial heirloom/OP varieties alongside
5. If an OP variety proves worthy in your trials, switch to it in your second year
6. For staple crops like field corn, do your own trials and begin saving seed from the best performer

## Economic Considerations

The hybrid-vs-OP debate is ultimately about whether the gardener values short-term convenience or long-term self-reliance. Hybrid seed must be purchased every year — the cost accumulates over a lifetime of gardening. Open-pollinated seed, once purchased, can be saved indefinitely, with the seed-saving investment paying for itself within 2-3 growing seasons for a serious gardener.

Solomon notes that for gardeners growing primarily for home consumption rather than market sale, the yield difference between hybrids and well-adapted OP varieties is often negligible. The hybrid advantage is most pronounced in commercial settings where uniformity, transport tolerance, and maximum yield per acre are economically critical. For a [[greenhouse-cool-climates-mollison]]; Brandywine, Cherokee Purple, and Amish Paste for flavor; and Roma VF for processing. Hybrid tomatoes like Big Beef and Early Girl offer disease resistance and uniformity but are not necessary for most home gardens
- **Sweet corn**: This is the crop where hybrids dominate most. OP sweet corns lose sweetness rapidly after harvest (the su gene converts sugar to starch quickly). If growing corn for fresh eating, hybrids with the se (sugar enhanced) or sh2 (supersweet) genes provide superior eating quality. For cornmeal or polenta, OP field corns like Bloody Butcher or Reid's Yellow Dent are excellent and easy to save
- **Brassicas**: Modern hybrid broccoli like Green Valiant and Packman is hard to beat for uniformity and yield. However, Tim Peters' Umpqua OP demonstrates that good OP broccoli is achievable. For cabbage, OP varieties like Early Jersey Wakefield and Golden Acre remain competitive
- **Peppers**: Hybrid peppers offer genuine advantages in marginal climates -- earlier maturity, better set in cool conditions, and disease resistance. In favorable climates, OP peppers like California Wonder (bell), Jalapeno, and Ancho are productive and easy to save
- **Lettuce**: OP lettuce varieties are abundant and perform excellently. Solomon recommends selecting for your own conditions, as lettuce adapts rapidly to local growing environments

## Seed Saving Techniques

For gardeners committed to saving their own seed, Solomon offers these practical guidelines:

- **Self-pollinating species** (tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, beans, peas): These are the easiest to save seed from. Select the best plants, allow fruits or pods to fully mature, extract and dry seeds. Isolation is not strictly necessary but prevents rare cross-pollination. Tomato seeds benefit from a brief fermentation (3-4 days in their own juice) to remove the gel coating that inhibits germination
- **Outcrossing species** (brassicas, corn, carrots, beets, squash): These require careful isolation to maintain varietal purity. Save seed from the minimum number of plants to maintain genetic diversity: at least 20-30 plants for most species, more for small-population varieties at risk of inbreeding depression

## Cross-Pollination Isolation Distances

Maintaining varietal purity when saving seed from outcrossing species requires knowledge of isolation distances:

- **Corn**: Wind-pollinated, with pollen capable of traveling 1 mile or more. A minimum of 1/4 mile isolation is recommended for home seed saving. Alternatively, hand-pollinate individual ears and cover with bags before and after silks emerge
- **Brassicas**: Insect-pollinated. Most sources recommend 1/2 mile isolation between varieties of the same species. Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, and kohlrabi all belong to *Brassica oleracea* and will cross with each other. Turnips, rutabagas, and some Asian greens are different species and will not cross with B. oleracea
- **Squash and pumpkins**: Insect-pollinated, with isolation distances of 1/2 mile recommended. Note that crosses occur within species groups: *C. pepo* (zucchini, pumpkins, acorn squash), *C. maxima* (hubbard, buttercup), and *C. moschata* (butternut) do not cross with each other, but varieties within each species will
- **Beets and chard**: Wind-pollinated with moderate isolation needs (approximately 1/4 mile). Beets and Swiss chard are the same species (*Beta vulgaris*) and will cross freely
- **Carrots**: Insect-pollinated, requiring 1/4 to 1/2 mile isolation. Wild Queen Anne's Lace (*Daucus carota*) will cross with cultivated carrots, so wild populations should be eliminated from the isolation zone
