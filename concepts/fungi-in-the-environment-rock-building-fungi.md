# Rock-Building Fungi and Mycogenic Mineral Formation

Fungi do not merely degrade rocks -- they also construct them. Through the precipitation of secondary minerals, fungi actively contribute to soil formation, rock cementation, and the creation of new mineral phases. This process of mycogenic mineral formation represents a critical link between the biosphere and the lithosphere, with implications for carbon sequestration, soil stability, and metal immobilization.

## Overview of [[mycogenic-mineral-precipitation]]

The formation of secondary organic and inorganic minerals by fungi occurs through both metabolism-independent and metabolism-dependent processes. Precipitation, nucleation and deposition of crystalline material on and within cell walls are influenced by environmental pH and the composition of cell walls, including factors such as the presence of chitin, glucans, melanin and other wall components.

This process is important in soil because precipitation of carbonates, phosphates and hydroxides increases soil aggregation. Cations such as Si4+, Fe3+, Al3+ and Ca2+ (released through dissolution mechanisms) stimulate precipitation of compounds that act as bonding agents for soil particles. Hyphae enmesh soil particles, alter alignment and release organic metabolites that enhance aggregate stability. The combination of physical enmeshment and chemical cementation by fungal precipitates creates stable soil aggregates that resist erosion.

## Carbonate Formation

Microbial carbonate precipitation coupled with silicate weathering provides an important sink for CO2 in terrestrial environments. In limestone, fungi and [[lichens]] are important agents of both mineral deterioration and secondary mineral formation. Many near-surface limestones (calcretes), calcic and petrocalcic horizons in soils are secondarily cemented with calcite (CaCO3) and whewellite (calcium oxalate monohydrate, CaC2O4.H2O).

Key aspects of fungal carbonate formation include:

- The presence of fungal filaments mineralized with calcite, together with whewellite, has been reported in limestone and calcareous soils from many localities worldwide
- Calcium oxalate can be degraded to calcium carbonate in semi-arid environments, where such a process acts to cement pre-existing limestones
- During decomposition of fungal hyphae, calcite crystals act as sites of further secondary calcite precipitation, creating a self-reinforcing cementation cycle
- Chitin, the major component of fungal cell walls, serves as a substrate on which calcite readily nucleates
- Fungal precipitation of secondary calcite, whewellite and glushkinskite (MgC2O4.2H2O) has been demonstrated experimentally
- Calcified fungal filaments have been documented in the petrocalcic horizon of soils in Beauce, France, demonstrating the deep pedogenic significance of these processes

## Oxalate Mineral Formation

Fungi produce metal oxalates with a variety of metals and metal-bearing minerals. The range of metals immobilized as oxalates includes:

- **Essential metals**: Ca, Mn, Zn, Co, Fe
- **Toxic metals**: Cd, Cu, Pb, Ni, Sr

Calcium oxalate dihydrate (weddellite) and the more stable calcium oxalate monohydrate (whewellite) are the most common forms in nature, associated with various ecophysiological groups of fungi. Depending on physicochemical conditions, biotic fungal calcium oxalate exhibits varied crystalline forms:

- Tetragonal
- Bipyramidal
- Plate-like
- Rhombohedral
- Needles

Precipitation of calcium oxalate acts as a reservoir for calcium in the ecosystem and influences phosphate availability. The formation of toxic metal oxalates provides a mechanism for fungal tolerance of environments containing high concentrations of toxic metals. Beauveria caledonica, an oxalate over-excreting fungus, was able to transform cadmium, copper, lead and zinc from a variety of toxic metal minerals into oxalates and remained tolerant to all tested minerals.

Oxalate excretion by fungi is modulated by environmental factors:

- Enhanced with NO3- as a nitrogen source (versus NH4+)
- Stimulated by the presence of HCO3- and Ca2+
- Increased by some toxic metals (Cu, Al) or minerals (pyromorphite, zinc phosphate)
- Environmental chemistry therefore directly regulates the rate and extent of mycogenic mineral formation

## Reductive and Oxidative Mineral Precipitation

Reduced forms of metals and metalloids are precipitated within and around fungal cells by many fungi, representing an important detoxification mechanism. The reductive ability manifests as characteristic colony colouration:

- **Silver (Ag)**: black colonies from elemental silver precipitation
- **Selenium (Se)**: red colonies from elemental selenium precipitation
- **Tellurium (Te)**: black colonies from elemental tellurium precipitation

These transformations can immobilize otherwise mobile and toxic metal species, converting them into less bioavailable forms.

Desert varnish, an oxidized metal layer a few millimetres thick found on rocks and in soils of arid and semi-arid regions, is believed to be of microbial origin with fungal involvement. Fungi oxidize manganese and iron in minerals such as siderite (FeCO3) and rhodochrosite (MnCO3) and precipitate them as oxides. They also form dark Fe(II)- and Mn(II)-patinas on glass surfaces. These oxide patinas represent a visible record of long-term fungal geochemical activity in arid environments.

## Other Mycogenic Minerals

A specific combination of biotic and abiotic factors leads to deposition of diverse secondary minerals associated with fungi. The diversity of minerals formed reflects the wide range of geochemical conditions under which fungi operate and the variety of metabolites they produce:

- **Oxides and hydroxides**: birnessite, MnO, FeO, ferrihydrite, goethite, todorokite
- **Carbonates**: calcite, hydroserussite
- **Organic metal compounds**: iron gluconate, calcium formate, moolooite (copper oxalate)
- **Silicates**: forsterite, halloysite, montmorillonite

Precipitation immobilizes metals in the soil environment, limiting bioavailability. The formation of these secondary mineral phases represents a permanent or semi-permanent sink for metals, with implications for long-term metal cycling and contaminant sequestration.

## Role in Soil Formation and Stabilization

The rock-building activities of fungi have major environmental consequences that span local pedogenesis to global biogeochemical cycling:

- Carbonate precipitation couples with silicate weathering to sequester atmospheric CO2, providing a biological carbon sink
- Fungal filaments mineralized with calcite contribute to cementation of limestone and calcrete horizons
- Oxalate precipitation creates calcium reservoirs and influences phosphate cycling in soils
- Hyphal enmeshment combined with mineral precipitation enhances soil aggregate stability, reducing erosion
- Secondary mineral formation contributes to long-term carbon storage in soils through stable mineral-organic associations
- Mycogenic minerals act as nucleation sites for further mineral growth, creating positive feedback loops for cementation
- These processes are especially significant in arid, semi-arid and karst environments where biological activity is concentrated
- The diversity of mycogenic minerals demonstrates the broad geochemical influence of fungi across terrestrial ecosystems
