## Mycoremediation Applications and Advanced Fungal Research

[[mycoremediation]] -- the use of fungi to degrade, sequester, or otherwise remove environmental contaminants -- represents one of the most promising applications of mycology in environmental science. This field spans from classroom demonstrations to college-level research and engineering, with fungi demonstrating remarkable abilities to break down pollutants, control erosion, filter water, and even contribute to biofuel production. Beyond remediation, advanced fungal research explores bacterial-fungal interactions, novel metabolite discovery, and the development of fungal-based consumer products.

## Soil Erosion Control with Mycelium

Mycelium networks physically bind soil particles together, making fungi effective agents for erosion control. In experimental setups, students construct small biological erosion units in trays using varying combinations of ground covers, plants, and fungi in a standardized soil base. A "water run" -- a long trough or channel angled at the desired slope -- is used to simulate site runoff by adding a known volume of water at the upper end and collecting it at the base.

### Experimental Design

Units are measured for flow rate using clean water before testing, since water filtration and site runoff effectiveness depends on retention and contact time between the water and mycelium. The rate of flow reveals how long water interacts with the mycelium treatment. Different erosion treatments are compared: bare soil (control), soil covered with erosion fabric, shredded wheat straw, and myceliated mushroom substrate. Local landscaping companies or departments of transportation can provide professional advice on reinforcement strategies.

For the mushroom substrate treatment, mycelium must be allowed to recover and thread into the soil before testing, which helps bind soil particles together. Levels of silt or soil erosion are measured by dry weight after each run. The ideal treatment minimizes or eliminates silt runoff entirely. Trials should be run in replicates, with data recorded and averaged to compare treatment efficiency. Students can also calculate the cost-effectiveness of each treatment by comparing material costs against performance.

### Contaminated Water Filtration

For testing contaminated water containing diluted herbicides, fertilizers, or other environmental pollutants, water is collected and examined for each run, noting retention time. Safety precautions -- gloves and safety glasses -- are mandatory when working with chemicals. Contaminant levels can be tested using commercial test kits or low-cost bioassays such as the dilution "bean test" or "worm assay." Results from all modules are graphed and analyzed to determine the best treatments and the variables contributing to both erosion prevention and water remediation.

## Oil Spill Remediation

Fungi can remediate oil spills using extracellular enzymes to break apart hydrocarbon molecules into smaller units more easily biodegraded by other fungi and bacteria. Hydrocarbons are chemically similar in architecture to lignin -- the woody component of wood that fungi decompose naturally -- so fungi adapt quickly, typically beginning to decompose oils within just a few weeks.

### Classroom Oil Spill Simulation

A mock oil spill can be set up using a small aquarium, plastic bins, shredded dried grass (hay or straw), vegetable oil, and [[oyster-mushroom]] biomass. The biomass consists of sheets of soaked cardboard colonized with oyster mushroom mycelium prepared two weeks before the experiment. Motor oil would be more realistic but is classified as hazardous waste requiring special permits.

The procedure involves filling the aquarium half full with water, pouring a measured amount of oil onto the surface, and adding shredded dried grass which floats and absorbs the oil. The oiled grass is removed and layered between colonized cardboard sheets in small bins, packed tightly. Each bin receives the same amount of oiled plant matter. Bins are covered with lids that have holes drilled for gas exchange.

Students inspect the bins weekly by gently peeling back layers to photograph and observe the mycelium. After a few weeks, large yellow metabolite droplets appear on the myceliated surface -- the mycelium exuding enzymes needed to break down the oil. After approximately one month, red wiggler worms can be added to bins to test whether the treated material supports life. Multiple bins can be set up to add worms at weeks four, six, eight, ten, and twelve, making this a long-term experiment that should be started early in the academic year.

## Screening Fungi for Mycoremediation Potential

The basic life cycle of fungi -- taking hold, eating, surviving, and developing biomass capable of producing spores -- means that any fungus able to do this in the presence of a contaminant shows remediation promise. Students prepare "traps" containing enriched substrate samples with baseline levels of a specific toxic soil contaminant. These traps are exposed to air, sometimes at known contaminated sites, and monitored to see which naturally occurring fungi develop into viable mycelium.

### Training Fungi for Remediation

Colonizing mycelium is subcultured and soil contaminant levels gradually increased to "train" the fungi -- genetically speaking -- to express traits allowing them to break down the contaminant and thrive in the contaminated substrate. Many molds appear during this process, which is appropriate since all are fungi and all contribute to the biodegradation effort. The goal is to find organisms that devour the main contaminant and continue thriving.

Students can also collect fungi at known contaminated sites and clone them to reproduce strains already acclimated to the contaminant, site conditions, and surrounding ecology. These field-collected strains are compared against lab-trained samples for mycoremediation effectiveness. Native strains can be further screened and "improved" to select favorable mutations that enhance remediation potential, then reintroduced at the original site for field studies.

## Bacterial-Fungal Interactions and Metabolite Discovery

Contact with bacteria can stimulate fungi to release various metabolites that encourage or limit bacterial growth, based on fungal signaling pathways. Bacterial partners can be identified as indispensable players in triggering fungi to manufacture unique chemical compounds. Students create behavioral plates -- petri dishes subplated with two or more species separated by space -- to study interactions between bacteria and fungi.

### Isolating Novel Metabolites

Fungi function as assembly-line factories, manufacturing metabolites in response to environmental triggers. Students dose fungal cultures with particular chemicals or biological organisms, harvest the resulting metabolites, and compare them against control cultures to identify which metabolites are produced in response to which trigger. Testing metabolites back against the biological or chemical trigger reveals whether the fungal culture aims to eradicate or promote the trigger.

A practical example involves stimulating mycelial biomass with a particular bacterium, harvesting the metabolites, adding them to an agar formula, and culturing the original bacterium on the agar to observe inhibition rates. Testing with different dilutions helps determine effective concentrations. This process mirrors the original discovery of penicillin from *Penicillium* interacting with *Staphylococcus*.

## Fungal Biofuel Production

Fungi are well known as decomposers but are also molecular builders. Their enzymes perform molecular disassembly of complex substrates and reorganize the elements as new molecules. Small-scale assays can reveal fungal metabolic pathways and substrate preferences based on by-products produced, especially gases. Continuously sampling carbon dioxide generation, for example, reveals the progression of [[lignin-degradation]] in substrate, helping students understand bioavailability of by-products during degradation.

### Sequential Fungal-Bacterial Systems

Matching and isolating fungal strains native to carbon sources, while also isolating bacteria that perform collaborative metabolic processes, can enable alcohol and biofuel production. Additional experiments can encourage fungal enzyme expression up to a specific point -- such as primary lignin degradation for nutrient availability rendering -- then "kill" the fungal biomass to end the primary metabolic sequence. The mass with its component enzymes is then added to an anaerobic digester or bioreactor utilizing bacteria known to produce biofuel compounds, such as gut bacteria from termites or mammals that produce methane, convertible to methanol. Similar sequences can produce ethanol and butanol using limitless combinations of fungi and bacteria.

## Fungal-Based Consumer Products

Dense mycelial mats can be manufactured into various consumer products including textiles, packaging, building materials, and insulation. Students can test different fungal mycelium for properties such as tensile strength, antibiotic activity, insect-repelling capability, and water resistance, with the goal of replacing existing products with known negative environmental impacts. Prototypes of biodegradable packaging, insulation, building materials, and biotextiles are created by cultivating mycelia and molding them into required shapes for specific applications. Mycelium can also convert waste paper into packaging material, and campus-wide projects using mushrooms to divert biodegradable material from waste streams can be organized at the college level.

## Engineering Mycoremediation Prototypes

Interdepartmental collaborations between engineering and microbiology create powerful alliances. Engineering students apply data to real-world problems, designing methods, systems, and equipment to produce results. Microbiology students explore data, ask questions, and test hypotheses in biology and cell function. Together, they identify pollution sites, design and test mycoremediation methods for contaminated soil and water, and progress from data collection through prototype testing to construction and implementation of full filtration systems.
