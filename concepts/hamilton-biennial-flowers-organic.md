# Biennial Flowers Organic

Biennials are plants that are sown one year and flower the next, after
which they die. Some, such as pansies, are really perennials but are
best grown as biennials because they flower better in the first year.
If carefully chosen, biennials provide a fine display of flowers
throughout spring and summer. They include some of the true cottage-
garden plants like sweet Williams and Canterbury bells.

## Choosing Biennial Flowers

Biennials grow in most soils and are valued for filling gaps in the
ornamental border between the main seasons of perennial and annual
display. They grow in most soils, making them versatile and easy to
incorporate into any garden plan. Among the most popular biennials are
sweet Williams ([[digitalis-purpurea]]) add dramatic height to
the border. Forget-me-nots (Myosotis alpestris) are excellent for
filling spaces at the front of the border with their delicate blue
flowers.

When choosing biennials for your garden, consider how they fit into
the overall planting scheme. Spring-flowering biennials will bloom
until early summer, when they can be pulled up, composted, and
replaced with clumps of summer-flowering annuals. This succession
planting ensures continuous color throughout [[query-how-do-cover-crops-suppress-weeds]].

After flowering, pull up the spent plants and add them to the compost
heap. Biennials that set seed can sometimes self-sow, producing
volunteer seedlings the following year. Forget-me-nots are
particularly good at this. Simply pull up a seeded plant and shake it
over the border to disperse seeds naturally. This is an easy way to
maintain a supply of biennials without having to sow them every year.

## Propagating Biennials

The most economical way to propagate biennials is from seed. Collect
seed from healthy plants at the end of the flowering season. Store in
a cool, dry place until sowing time in early summer. Sow thinly in
drills in the seed bed and cover lightly with soil. Water gently and
keep the bed free from weeds. Thin the seedlings as they grow and
transplant to their temporary positions when large enough to handle.
