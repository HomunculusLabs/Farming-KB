# Garden Mushroom Growing

Incorporating gourmet and medicinal mushrooms into vegetable gardens using companion planting strategies. From Chapter 12 of *Mycelium Running* by [[paul-stamets]].

## Why Grow Mushrooms in Gardens?

- **Soil building** — mycelium decomposes organic matter, creating humus
- **Water retention** — mycelial networks act as sponges, reducing irrigation needs
- **Pest control** — some species kill nematodes and attract/trap pest insects (see [[fungal-pest-control]])
- **Nutrient cycling** — fungi break down complex molecules into plant-available forms
- **Food production** — harvest gourmet mushrooms alongside vegetables
- **Disease resistance** — plants with mycorrhizal partners resist disease better
- **Erosion prevention** — mycelium binds soil particles together

## Best Garden Mushroom Species

### Stropharia rugoso-annulata (Garden Giant / Wine Cap)
- The premier garden mushroom — easy, vigorous, productive
- Loves bacteria-rich environments; grows faster with microbial contact
- Produces massive burgundy-capped mushrooms
- Can grow as both primary and secondary decomposer
- Excellent for wood chip beds
- Edible at all stages; burgundy color fades as it matures

### Pleurotus ostreatus (Oyster Mushroom)
- Fast-growing, aggressive colonizer
- Grows on straw, corncobs, coffee grounds, paper, cardboard
- Excellent for filtering bacteria and breaking down toxins
- Medicinal: antiviral (HIV), cholesterol-lowering (natural lovastatin), anticancer (pleuran)
- Multiple flushes from a single inoculation

### Hypsizygus ulmarius (Elm Oyster)
- Promotes plant growth as a companion
- Grows well on straw and wood chips
- Good edible mushroom with almond-like flavor
- One of the species Stamets recommends for crop enhancement

### King Stropharia as a Bridge Species
- Functions as intermediary between primary and secondary decomposition
- First digests fresh debris, then continues thriving as complex microbial communities develop
- Perfect for transitioning raw organic matter into rich soil

## Companion Planting Strategies

### The HUG Bed (Hypsi-Ulmarius-Garden)
A tiered growing system combining plants and fungi:

1. **Bottom layer**: Elm oyster (Hypsizygus ulmarius) inoculated straw/wood chips
2. **Middle layer**: Garden soil with vegetable plants
3. **Top layer**: Mulch with Stropharia spawn

The elm oyster mycelium interacts with plant roots, enhancing growth while producing mushrooms at the base of plants.

### Wood Chip Pathways
- Line garden paths with 4-6 inches of wood chips inoculated with Stropharia spawn
- Mycelium colonizes within months
- Mushrooms fruit along pathways after rains
- Runoff from paths carries beneficial fungi into garden beds
- Paths become spongy, erosion-resistant walkways

### Straw Bale Inoculation
- Place straw bales in the garden, soak thoroughly
- Inoculate with oyster mushroom spawn by stuffing spawn into holes
- Keep moist; mushrooms fruit from the bales
- After mushroom production, use spent straw as mulch — already partially decomposed and enriched with mycelium

### Beneath Raised Beds
- Line the bottom of raised beds with inoculated cardboard and wood chips
- Mycelium processes the organic layer, creating nutrients that wick up into the soil above
- Acts as a biological drainage layer

## Simple Inoculation Methods (No Lab Needed)

### Stem Butt Transfers
1. Buy fresh garden giant or oyster mushrooms
2. Remove stems, bury stem butts in wood chips or straw
3. Keep moist
4. Mycelium grows from the stem butts into the surrounding material

### Spore Slurries
1. Collect mature mushroom caps
2. Blend with water in a blender
3. Add a pinch of salt to deter bacteria
4. Pour over wood chips or straw
5. Less reliable than spawn but very low-cost

### Cardboard Inoculation
1. Soak corrugated cardboard in water
2. Peel apart to reveal the fluted inner layer
3. Sprinkle sawdust spawn between layers
4. Stack and keep moist
5. Once colonized (white mycelium visible), use as inoculum for larger beds

## Crop Benefits Documented

- Garden giant mycelium enhances growth of Brassicas, corn, and many garden vegetables
- Elm oyster mycelium associated with increased plant vigor
- Oyster mushrooms reduce nematode populations in soil
- Mycorrhizal partnerships extend effective root zone, increasing nutrient uptake

## Integration with No-Till

[[no-till-gardening-method]] and garden mushrooms are natural allies:
- Mycelium decomposes crop stubble in place
- No tilling preserves the mycelial network
- Sheet mulching with inoculated material builds soil while filtering runoff
- Glomalin from mycelium binds soil particles, reducing erosion
