# Gibberellin Discovery Period in Fukuoka's Life

The years Masanobu Fukuoka spent at the Yokohama Customs Bureau Plant
Inspection Division, working under Professor Eiichi Kurosawa, represent a
critical formative period that shaped both his scientific expertise and his
eventual disillusionment with [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture]]. During this period, Fukuoka was immersed in plant pathology
research at the very moment when gibberellin, one of the most important
plant hormones, was being discovered. This experience gave him an insider's
understanding of agricultural science that would later inform his powerful
critique of it.

## The Yokohama Customs Laboratory

At age twenty-five, Fukuoka worked for the Yokohama Customs Bureau in the
Plant Inspection Division. His primary job was inspecting incoming and
outgoing plants for disease-carrying insects. The position afforded him
considerable free time, which he spent in the research laboratory pursuing
investigations in his specialty of plant pathology.

The laboratory was located next to Yamate Park on a bluff overlooking
Yokohama harbour, with the Catholic Church directly in front and the
Ferris Girls' School to the east. Fukuoka describes it as very quiet and
the perfect environment for carrying on research. This idyllic setting
contrasts with the intensity and eventual crisis that unfolded within it.

## Eiichi Kurosawa and the Gibberellin Discovery

Fukuoka's mentor in the laboratory was Eiichi Kurosawa, the pathology
researcher. Although Kurosawa remained largely unknown in the Japanese
academic world, he made a discovery of enormous significance: he isolated
and raised in culture the fungus that causes bakanae (foolish seedling)
disease in rice. From this fungal culture, Kurosawa became the first person
to extract the plant growth hormone gibberellin.

Gibberellin has a peculiar dual effect on rice plants. When a small amount
is absorbed by young rice plants, it causes them to grow abnormally tall,
producing the characteristic spindly, weak-stemmed appearance of bakanae
disease. When given in excess, however, it brings about the opposite
reaction, retarding the plant's growth entirely. This paradoxical dose-
response relationship foreshadowed themes that would become central to
Fukuoka's later [[fukuoka-fallacies-scientific-understanding]].

## International Reception and Neglect in Japan

No one in Japan took much notice of Kurosawa's gibberellin discovery.
However, overseas it became a topic of active research. Soon after, an
American researcher made use of gibberellin in developing the seedless
grape, one of the first commercial applications of plant growth hormones.

This pattern of Japanese innovation being ignored domestically while
exploited abroad resonated with broader themes in Fukuoka's critique of
Japanese agricultural institutions. The [[fukuoka-breakdown-japanese-agriculture]], as he saw it, was partly due to an inability
to recognize and value insights that did not fit conventional frameworks.

## Fukuoka's Research on Citrus Diseases

Under Kurosawa's guidance, Fukuoka built a dissection microscope and
devoted himself to research on decay-causing resin diseases in the trunk,
branches, and fruit of American and Japanese citrus trees. He observed
fungus cultures, crossbred various fungi, and created new disease-causing
varieties. The work was so absorbing that he sometimes fell unconscious
from overwork in the laboratory.

Looking through the microscope, Fukuoka was struck by how similar the
minute world of fungi was to the great world of the infinite universe. This
experience of the micro-world, combined with his evening social life in the
port city of Yokohama, created a vivid contrast between the realms of
nature and human society that would inform his later [[fukuoka-how-nature-should-be-perceived]].

## The Pneumonia Crisis and Spiritual Awakening

The combination of intense laboratory work and an active social life in
Yokohama eventually led to a physical collapse. Fukuoka contracted acute
pneumonia and was placed in the pneumothorax treatment room on the top
floor of the Police Hospital. It was winter, and through a broken window,
swirls of snow blew around the room. He felt he had been put out in the
bitter cold and plunged into a world of solitude and loneliness, face to
face with the fear of death.

This hospitalization triggered the spiritual crisis that would ultimately
lead Fukuoka away from scientific research and toward [[fukuoka-spiritual-philosophy-farming]]. His subsequent [[fukuoka-personal-journey]]
from confident young scientist to doubting seeker to natural farming
prophet begins here. The question of what he had placed his confidence in,
and the nature of his complacency, became an agony of doubt about the
nature of life and death.

## Significance for Understanding Fukuoka's Critique

The gibberellin period is essential context for understanding Fukuoka's
critique of agricultural science because it demonstrates that his
criticism comes from within the scientific establishment. He was not an
outsider attacking something he did not understand; he was a trained
plant pathologist who had firsthand experience with the limitations and
paradoxes of the scientific approach.

The gibberellin story itself illustrates a key Fukuoka theme: the same
substance that causes disease (abnormal elongation in rice) can, at a
different dose, produce a commercially valuable product (seedless grapes).
This ambiguity, where the boundary between beneficial and harmful is
not fixed but depends on context and quantity, anticipates his later
argument that [[fukuoka-textdoc-no-good-or-evil-in-nature]], only human judgments imposed upon natural
processes.

## The Irony of Creating Disease

Fukuoka's work creating new disease-causing fungal varieties carries a
deep irony in retrospect. The young scientist who spent his days
engineering new plant diseases would go on to develop a farming system
based entirely on not interfering with natural processes. This
transformation from creator of disease problems to cultivator of natural
health represents one of the most dramatic intellectual reversals in
the history of agricultural thought.
