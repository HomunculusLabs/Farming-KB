## Economic and Social Succession

Models of [[ecological-succession]] are not limited to biological systems — they provide powerful frameworks for understanding micro-economic and social change, as well as large-scale human cultural patterns. [[david-holmgren]] applies ecological succession theory to explain social phenomena ranging from back-to-the-land movements and gentrification to generational wealth cycles and the macro-dynamics of fossil-fuel-powered capitalism.

## Back-to-the-Land Pioneers

### Social Pioneer Plants

The term "pioneer plants" derives from the original meaning of "pioneer" — one who first settles a region, opening it for occupation and development by others. Since the 1970s, back-to-the-land migrants leaving cities for autonomous rural life have acted as pioneers in changing the social and economic environment of depressed rural areas.

### Succession Dynamics

The first generation of settlers created the foundations for more mainstream rural resettlement and tourism:
- Restored old buildings
- Arts and craft workers
- Cafes and health-food shops
- Permaculture properties and alternative buildings
- Small-scale organic farms
- Conserved patches of forest and wetland
- Alternative schools and health practitioners

This social infrastructure attracted a second phase of new settlers and tourists, countering the intensifying hold of big cities over national culture and economy.

### The Irony of Pioneer Displacement

The pioneers rarely view arriving newcomers with equanimity, seeing them as unappreciative consumers who bring dysfunctional urban attitudes. While there is truth in this, understanding social succession reveals the pattern: pioneers make the social environment favorable for like-minded people, but over time collectively create conditions that suit another class or sector of society.

## Gentrification

### The Process

Gentrification is a recognized social succession process in inner-city areas:
1. **Pioneer phase**: Students, artists, and trendsetters are attracted to working-class inner-city areas that are physically rundown but socially dynamic
2. **Renovation phase**: Pioneers make the area more attractive to owner-renovators, who raise rental and resale values, pushing out original working-class occupants
3. **Investor phase**: Investor owners rent out fancy townhouses to upwardly mobile young professionals
4. **Decline phase**: The dynamic community that attracted original bohemians is dead. Absent and disinterested owners, increasing crime, and declining values create new opportunities for the poor

Over the last several decades, this process has transformed old inner suburbs of most Western cities. The later stages look like a pulsing or even four-phase ecosystem cycle.

## Nouveau Riche Succession

### Generational Cultural Succession

Cultural succession occurs within families through a classic pattern that rides the wave of fossil-fuel-based affluence, visible across all cultures as a truly global social process:

1. **First generation**: Traditional rural peasants connected to place, driven away by grinding poverty, oppression, or dispossession (or lured by hoped-for riches)
2. **Second generation**: Migrant urban workers
3. **Third generation**: Successful small business persons
4. **Fourth generation**: Educated professionals

### Limits of Affluence as Climax

Like rapid succession of gentrification, evidence suggests the transition to affluence is not a final stable climax state but a **pulse of consumption** not sustained over generations. The common maxim — "the first generation creates the wealth, the second spends it, and the third loses it" — reflects widespread understanding of affluence"s limits.

While aristocracy and the rich have maintained affluent lineages for many generations, this has been achieved in contexts where their distinction from the masses and small numbers help maintain their culture. If everyone were equally rich, elites could not maintain distinctive culture.

### Signs of Affluence's Declining Returns

Affluent Western industrial societies have managed generations of moderately functional affluence for the majority, but only in a global context where the masses remain poor. Mounting problems indicate the costs of affluence are catching up:
- Rising health and welfare costs
- Loss of work culture
- Addictive behaviors
- Crisis in the education system

## Socio-Political Cycles as Pulsing Systems

### The Consumption of Social Capital

Neo-classical economic theory and its attendant political beliefs, which dominated mainstream public policy in the last two decades of the 20th century, can be understood as an intense pulse of economic activity based on consuming social and economic capital built up over the preceding four decades.

### The Building Phase (1940s–1970s)

Following the disasters of the Great Depression and World War II, Keynesian economics and social democratic politics prevailed. A mix of factors contributed to accumulation of public infrastructure and assets:
- Work ethic and culture of frugality
- Faith in societal structures and values
- Rising wages and working conditions
- Strong public policy controlling capitalism's worst excesses
- Direction of societal wealth for the common good

### The Release Phase (1980s–2000s)

The current phase of laissez-faire capitalism began with Margaret Thatcher and Ronald Reagan in the early 1980s, signaling the demise of Keynesian economics. By the 1970s, oil crises, the Cold War, and other factors impacted the capacity to support continually rising standards of living. The agents of the elites sold the message that public bureaucracy and economic control inhibited efficient markets.

### Results of the Consumption Pulse

After 20 years, much of the so-called productivity was built by cashing in public assets:
- Public physical assets sold off and dismantled
- Knowledge and skills of people in large organizations discarded in favor of "animal cunning driven by the bottom line"
- Commonwealth of shared scientific knowledge became intellectual property to be plundered
- Goodwill and credibility of NGOs, built by decades of voluntary action, valued in millions of dollars

Like the farmer marveling at crops growing on the ashes of a burnt forest, many mistakenly believe this phase of intense economic consumption can continue indefinitely.

## The Large-Scale Pulse of Fossil Fuels

### Energetic Context

The rebuilding of social and cultural capital must now occur within a context of declining net energy availability. The easy half of total likely conventional oil reserves has been consumed, opportunities for major electricity expansion are contracting, and the world's population has more than tripled.

### Reframing Capitalism's Destruction

Global capitalism has been like a fire converting green forests to ashes, but it has likewise released potential and information from the constraints of cultural norms and institutions that were hopelessly inappropriate for dealing with a world of declining energy. The ashes of the consumed forest provide opportunities for pioneering species to reform the forest in ways that better reflect large-scale realities.

### Permaculture as Reorganisation Phase

If global capitalism is viewed as releasing the earth's accumulation of renewable and non-renewable resources according to Holling's Four-Phase Cycle, then Permaculture represents the new potential of the **Reorganisation phase** — the critical juncture where open niches and released energy can be captured by new, more productive systems.

### Generational Responsibility

In developing a post-affluent culture, it is not necessary to denigrate what parents, grandparents, or ancestors did as ignorant, shortsighted, or anti-nature. The ground on which we stand has been prepared for us by those who have gone before. A positive view of generational change enables constructive action in the face of immense opportunities from energy descent.
