# Incremental Design and Permaculture Ethics

Incremental design and the ethical framework of [[permaculture]] together address the temporal and moral dimensions of land use planning. While zone, sector, and slope analyses provide the spatial logic of design, incremental design provides the temporal methodology — how designs evolve over time — and the ethical framework provides the justification for why we design the way we do.

## Incremental Design

### The Principle of Adaptive Evolution

Almost all successful engineering design is based on small changes to existing designs, gradually refining them until some ultimate limit in efficiency or performance is reached. The entire process can take centuries, and the end result can be mass-produced when stable. Kevin Lynch, in his 1982 book *Site Planning*, describes this as **design by incremental adaptation of already-existing designs** — following physical systems that have been shown to work.

Lynch maintains that the best site planning of the past resulted from this process, and that it works very well unless some external and important condition — such as market changes or shifts in land ownership — disrupts the established pattern. He also warns that fine-tuning a successful design for a specific climate and purpose can be totally inappropriate if transferred to a different culture, climate, or applied to a different purpose.

### Known Effective Design Units

The incremental approach relies on **known effective design units and specifications** — whether of roads, culverts, houses, garden beds, or technologies — that have been subjected to long tests and evolved from trials (or prototypes) to working, reliable standards. Even if considered "old-fashioned" (like overshot water wheels), they may represent a simplicity and efficiency hard to beat without considerable increase in expense and complexity.

Such continual adaptation is the basis of **feedback in systems undergoing establishment**, where designers make additions or changes to houses or plant systems based on observed performance. Each season of observation informs the next cycle of modification.

### Limitations of Incremental Design

However, incremental design alone is insufficient for complex systems. A private home and garden must satisfy a complex set of priorities that go beyond simple efficiency. Incremental adaptation does not cope well with:

- **Changing futures**: new information, shifting values, evolving social needs
- **Self-reliance requirements**: the need for systems to function independently of external inputs
- **Self-governance**: the capacity of inhabitants to manage and modify their own systems

Incremental design is most powerful as a **refinement tool** after initial strategic placement and energy conservation have been adequately addressed through the zone-sector-slope framework. It handles the details; it does not establish the foundation.

## Zoning of Information and Ethics

### The Ethical Gradient

Mollison extends the zone concept beyond physical management into an ethical and informational framework. Zones represent a gradient between an ecosystem managed primarily for people (Zone 1, the home garden) and the wilderness, where all things have their right to exist and humans are only visitors.

This reframing has profound implications:

- **Zone 1** is where we are information developers. We tend species selected by and dependent on mankind. All animal species tend their "home gardens," and an interdependency arises not unlike the parasite-prey relationship. The garden is our classroom and our responsibility.
- **Zone 2** is where nature begins to complicate our situation. We start to learn from species other than our people-dependent selections. The need for control diminishes and the capacity for observation increases.
- **Outer zones** progressively reduce our person-orientation and increase our understanding of the necessity of all life forms. We do not "need" to exploit most species. We in fact need and use only a few species of the hundreds of thousands that exist.
- **Wilderness** is where we are visitors or strangers. We have neither need nor right to interfere or dominate. We should not settle there and leave wastelands at our back. In wilderness we may learn lessons basic to good design, but we cannot improve on the information already available there.

### Three Fundamental Understandings

1. **Everything is of use.** It is not necessarily needed by people, but it is needed by the life complex of which we are a dependent part.
2. **We cannot order complex functions.** They must evolve of themselves.
3. **We cannot know a fraction of what exists.** We will always be a minor part of the total information system.

Thus, Mollison concludes, we are **teachers only in our home gardens, and learners elsewhere**. Nowhere do we create. Everything we depend on we have evolved from what is already created, and that includes ourselves. Thoughtful people need wilderness as schools need teachers.

### The Ethical Argument Against Wilderness Destruction

The core ethical proposition is that we have **no right and no ethical justification for clearing land or using wilderness while we tread over lawns, create erosion, and use land inefficiently**. Our responsibility is to put our own house in order first.

If we do so, there will never be any need to destroy wilderness. Most farmers can become stewards of forest and wildlife — as they will have to become in any downturn in the energy economy. **Unethical energy use** is what destroys distant resources for short-term convenience.

Mollison targets the hypocrisy of the "ecologist" with large lawns and no food garden, or the "environmentalist" drinking from aluminum cans and buying newspapers to read about destructive exploits. Both occupations exploit wilderness and people while claiming virtue.

## Type 1 Error

Mollison formalizes the ethical error of wilderness settlement as **Type 1 Error**: when we settle into wilderness, we are in conflict with so many life forms that we have to destroy them to exist. The prescription is simple: **keep out of the bush. It is already in good order.**

This error thesis operates as a cascading principle — such errors, once made, lead into increasing problems. The initial decision to settle wild land creates a chain of destructive consequences: clearing, erosion, species loss, and ultimately the degradation of the very resources that attracted settlement in the first place.

## Permaculture vs. Conventional Design

Mollison contrasts the two approaches in a structured comparison:

**[[permaculture-design]]** is stimulated by a perceived social problem, subject to values of energy conservation, self-reliance, and harmonious human occupancy. It is mediated by consideration of long-term biosocial factors, achieved through research and consultation with clients, and refined by allowing space, finance, and feedback to adjust activity. It leads to a dynamic, healthy area inhabited by people with the power and understanding to make necessary changes — the stability of dynamic local adjustment.

**Conventional design** is stimulated by a drive to erect monuments or make money, subject to economic considerations and the desire for profit. It is mediated by purely functional values for short-term cost and material factors, achieved through external funding with little consultation or education. It is refined by selling off and not taking responsibility for results. It leads to a dislocated population, powerless to effect change easily — the instability of perceived individual powerlessness.

## The Bio-Social Approach

Site designing needs not a specialist approach but rather a **multi-disciplinary and bio-social approach** that takes into account the effects the environment has on its intended occupants. Mollison references the "Bruce effect" — where rats subjected to breathing the same airstream as their fellows experienced severe instability, physiological stress, and consequent pathologies akin to crowding stress — and notes that this effect may apply equally well to people crammed into multi-story office buildings with no opening windows and only a single airstream.

## Summary

Incremental design provides the methodology for evolving designs over time through observation, feedback, and adaptation. The ethical framework provides the moral compass: put your own house in order before reaching into wilderness, understand that we are learners in nature and teachers only in our gardens, and recognize that complex functions cannot be ordered but must evolve. Together, these principles ensure that permaculture designs are not only efficient and productive but also ethical and sustainable across generations.
