# Fungal Biosorption of Cadmium

Cadmium (Cd) is one of the most toxic [[fungal-biosorption-of-heavy-metals-by-filamentous-fungi]] in the environment,
ranking alongside mercury and lead in terms of its environmental and
health impacts. Fungal biosorption has emerged as a promising
cost-effective technology for cadmium removal from aqueous solutions
and contaminated soils. Cadmium is given high consideration in biosorption
research due to its strong toxic environmental effects, its persistence,
and its accumulation in food chains.

## Cadmium Toxicity and Environmental Significance

Cadmium enters the environment through industrial discharge, mining
activities, battery manufacturing, and phosphate fertilizers. It is a
non-essential metal that causes severe health effects including kidney
damage, bone demineralization, and carcinogenesis. The persistence of
cadmium in the environment leads to a quest for new remediation
technologies. Cadmium and its compounds are classified as Group 1
carcinogens by IARC, making its removal from water and soils a critical
environmental priority.

## Biosorption Mechanisms for Cadmium

Fungal biosorption of cadmium involves several mechanisms operating
simultaneously. Surface binding occurs through functional groups on the
fungal cell wall, including carboxyl, amino, hydroxyl, and phosphate
groups. Intracellular accumulation involves energy-dependent transport
systems and subsequent sequestration in vacuoles. The cell wall of
filamentous fungi, composed primarily of chitin, glucans, and proteins,
provides abundant binding sites for cadmium ions. Fungal melanins of
filamentous fungi also contribute to cadmium removal through interactions
with metal ions.

## Cadmium Uptake Capacities by Filamentous Fungi

Quantitative data from Singh's comprehensive review demonstrate the
impressive cadmium biosorption capacities of various fungal species.
Mucor rouxii shows 8.46 mg/g live biomass and 20.31 mg/g dead biomass
at initial Cd(II) concentrations of 10 mg/l. Aspergillus terreus
achieves 70% removal of Cd(II) at 10 mg/l concentration over 13 days.
Penicillium chrysogenum displays 11 mg/g Cd(II) uptake capacity.
Rhizopus oryzae demonstrates 0.28 mM/g, while R. oligosporus achieves
0.35 mM/g and A. oryzae 0.40 mM/g of cadmium removal. Cladosporium
cladosporioides shows 63% removal efficiency under similar conditions.
The biosorption efficiency depends on pH, temperature, initial metal
concentration, and biomass characteristics.

## Cadmium Biosorption by White-Rot Fungi

White-rot fungi exhibit notable cadmium biosorption capabilities and
additional enzymatic mechanisms for cadmium interaction. Trametes
versicolor is an excellent cadmium biosorbent, removing all Cd(II) ions
within 2 hours at a rate of about 2 mg Cd(II) per gram of mycelial dry
weight. This appears to be an energy-independent surface binding process.
Extracellular laccase activity in T. versicolor is significantly
stimulated when Cd(II) ions are added to 10-day-old cultures, suggesting
a dual mechanism of cadmium removal involving both passive biosorption
and enzymatic transformation. Pleurotus ostreatus accumulates 20% of
Cd(II) intracellularly in the presence of 150 ppm Cd(II) in the medium.

## Cadmium Effects on Fungal Morphology and Physiology

Heavy metals cause a reduction in growth followed by changes in
morphology across all groups of fungi. Cadmium adsorption to
Schizophyllum commune leads to the formation of aerial hyphae, loops,
and the development of connective filaments. Stereum hirsutum produces
a yellow-orange pigment in mycelium and extracellularly in the presence
of 0.25 mM or more Cd(II). [[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]] produces a brown pigment
when grown on Cd(II)-containing medium. These morphological changes
serve as visual indicators of cadmium stress and may reflect the
activation of defense mechanisms within fungal cells.

## Cadmium Biosorption by Yeasts

Yeasts also demonstrate significant cadmium biosorption potential.
Saccharomyces cerevisiae ATCC 834 shows 37.30 mg/g dry weight Cd(II)
uptake at 100 mg/l concentration, attributed to its thicker mannan
layer and larger surface area. The thicker mannan layer provides more
binding sites for cadmium ions. During Cd(II) biosorption by deactivated
protonated yeast, the amount sorbed at equilibrium is directly
proportional to the initial concentration divided by the biomass. FTIR
spectra of Ni(II)-saturated yeast show that biosorption occurs primarily
in the sugar and nucleic acid regions, involving carboxyl and amino
groups.

## Metal Ion Resistance and Cadmium Tolerance

Some fungi have evolved the ability to survive in environments containing
excessive concentrations of cadmium. Metallothioneins (MTs), which are
gene-encoded cysteine-rich metal-binding proteins, play a crucial role in
cadmium tolerance. Cd(II) stress triggers the biosynthesis of both MTs
and phytochelatins (PCs) followed by an increase in glutathione (GSH)
levels in resistant species like Heliscus lugdunensis and Verticillium
cf. alboatrum. Schizosaccharomyces pombe serves as a model system for
the chelation and transport of cadmium. The gene hmt1, encoding a
vacuolar membrane protein belonging to the ATP-binding cassette (ABC)
transporter family, has been isolated from S. pombe for heavy metal
resistance. Cadmium tolerance in S. pombe requires two purine
biosynthetic enzymes.

## Cadmium in Multimetal Systems

In real environmental scenarios, cadmium rarely occurs alone. In binary
and ternary metal systems, the biosorption capacity of an individual
metal ion decreases in the presence of other metal ions. Using Mucor
rouxii biomass, sorption capacities in multimetal systems are lower
than in single-metal systems. Aspergillus niger biomass displays the
adsorption of heavy metals in the preferential order lead > copper >
cadmium. Cadmium adsorption is more sensitive to the presence of both
copper and lead. [[staycare-phanerochaete-chrysosporium-in-bioremediation]] shows competitive
biosorption of Cd(II), Pb(II), and Cu(II) with adsorption capacities of
7.80 mg for Cd(II), 16.91 mg for Pb(II), and 7.57 mg for Cu(II) per
gram of dry [[bioremediation-fungal-biomass-biosorbent-material]].

## Future Perspectives

The development of metal-resistant species through [[fukuoka-textdoc-genetic-engineering-critique-warning]]
holds promise for enhanced cadmium biosorption. With identification of
target genes in model organisms, sequences can be modified for
heterologous or homologous expression in fungi with high biosorption
capacity. Computer models based on metal interaction and mass transfer
could optimize biosorption process design. Interdisciplinary research
integrating physicochemical processes with biological processes should
achieve the full potential of fungal cadmium biosorption technology.
