# Permaculture Designers Manual - Marine Resources

updated: 2026-04-18

Marine and coastal resources are treated primarily in Chapter 13 (Aquaculture) and Chapter 11 (Dryland and Island Strategies), with additional references to coastal systems in tropical and arid climate chapters. Mollison presents coastal zones as among the most productive biological systems on earth, while cautioning that their exploitation has historically been destructive and short-sighted.

## Coastal Productivity

Mangrove swamps, marshes, and estuaries produce sometimes prodigious biomass of great complexity. Mollison notes that attitudes to these systems have been "typically destructive" since the invention of the bucket dredge and bulldozer. Many marshes, estuaries, saltflats, and ponds have been drained or deeply flooded to suit private purposes, often in disregard of their total yields and values.

The intimate link between the health of a river and the health of its catchment means that pollution most rapidly permeates landscapes via water. Wherever biocides are used in a catchment, the effects reach rivers and seas offshore as far as reef areas. The death of coral reef areas is closely correlated with inland use of 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T.

## Traditional Coastal Polycultures (Section 13.10)

The antiquity of some terrace and riverside cultures demonstrates their sustainability. Key systems include:

- Chinampas of Mexico: the Aztec system at Lake Tenochtitlan, possibly the most productive polyculture yet devised, combining chinampa crop, waterfowl, reedbed, and fish culture
- The flood cultures of the Nile: probably 17,000 years old
- The Ifugao terrace system of the Philippines: 3,000+ years of sustainable rice-fish production
- Hawaiian ohana synthesis: island cultures neatly combining water and earth resources
- Euphrates and Lake Titicaca: whole cultures based on reed beds deriving forage, boats, housing, bread, and meat from Typha, Phragmites, Cyperus

## Seaweed and Marine Plants

There are 20-30 species of water plants in constant culture with varietal selection. Many species are still wild-gathered or derived from unselected wild stock. Potential exists for:
- Wetland forestry
- Craft products from reeds and rushes
- Specialised forage assemblies for bees, ducks, waterfowl, and fish
- Seasonal swamp forage for pigs
- Lagoon and shallow water browsing by cattle
- Seagrass and seaweed browsing by sheep, turtles, and marine iguanas
- Samphire pastures for geese, swan, and fish species at varying tide levels

## Shellfish and Benthic Systems

The manual references the importance of mussels as the major phosphate reservoir of ponds and rivers, conveying phosphates to the mud via their anal siphons. Fertilised pond mussels yield increases from 52.8 kg (unfertilised) to 1,012 kg (fertilised, whole weight; meats are 35% of total).

Oyster and mussel shell provide slow-release calcium in tanks, small ponds, intake filter systems, and upper canal sections. Shellfish aquaculture is presented as potentially more productive per unit input than almost any terrestrial protein system.

## Marine Constructs

The manual references artificial reef systems as a design element for coastal enhancement, providing habitat for fish, molluscs, and crustaceans. Such systems can restore degraded coastal zones and provide sustainable harvest.

Coral cays and low islands require special planting strategies for food and shelter trees. Coconut, pandanus, beach almond (Terminalia catappa), and Casuarina are key species for atoll and cay systems.

## Coastal Zone Design Principles

- Preserve mangrove and estuarine habitat as the foundation of marine productivity
- Design pond systems to flow into marine zones without carrying pollutants
- Use catchment-wide planning to prevent biocide and nutrient runoff to coastal waters
- Integrate freshwater aquaculture with brackish and marine systems through gradients
- Protect reef areas through inland land-use practices
- Utilise tidal energy for water exchange in coastal pond systems
- Design for multiple species at multiple trophic levels
- Recognise that island cultures have always combined water and earth resources most efficiently

## Production Costs Comparison

Mollison cites Bardach's production cost analysis (kcal per ounce):
- Wild-caught lobster: 21,801
- Wild-caught shrimp: 16,953
- Intensive silo rearing of perch: 15,167
- Pond catfish (USA): 3,941
- Pond polyculture of carp, mullet, tilapia (Israel): 184
- Sewage/stream culture of carp in cages: less than 8

This analysis shows why distant-water fishing is collapsing and why aquaculture is likely to eclipse terrestrial protein production, especially intensive feedlot systems.

## Ecological Limits and Sustainability

Mollison warns that aquaculture is no more valid as a high-energy-use monoculture than terrestrial monocultures. It is at its most enjoyable, convivial, and socially valuable as community culture, and at its most depressing as 100-hectare intensive prawn or catfish farms. The attitude throughout stresses sensible yield and procedure while discouraging the "maximum yield of one species" outlook.
