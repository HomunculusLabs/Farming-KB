# Cannabis Zinc (Zn)

## Overview

Zinc is a [[cannabis-indoor-soil-mix-and-preparation]] classified as **mobile** in cannabis plants. Zinc is the most common micronutrient found deficient in arid climates and alkaline soils. Deficiencies are most common in soils with a pH of 7 or more. Along with [[query-how-to-identify-and-fix-cannabis-iron-deficiency]] and [[cannabis-manganese-nutrient]], zinc is one of the three most common micronutrient deficiencies that plague cannabis grow rooms.

## Role in Plant Biology

Zinc works with [[cannabis-manganese-nutrient]] and [[cannabis-magnesium-nutrient]] to promote the same enzyme functions. It is a key structural component of many enzymes and plays a central role in the synthesis of the plant hormone auxin (indole-3-acetic acid), which regulates stem elongation and leaf expansion. Key roles include:

- Cooperates with other elements to help form chlorophyll and prevent its demise
- Essential catalyst for most plant enzymes and auxins
- Crucial for stem growth
- Vital part in sugar and protein production
- Works with manganese and magnesium to promote enzyme functions
- Required for synthesis of tryptophan, a precursor to auxin
- Involved in membrane integrity and protein structure stabilization

## Mobility in Soil

Zinc is classified as **mobile** within the cannabis plant, meaning it can be translocated from older tissue to newer growth when supplies are limited. In the soil, zinc availability is strongly influenced by pH. Zinc is most available in acidic soils (pH 5.0-6.0) and becomes increasingly unavailable above pH 6.5, with severe lockout above pH 7.0. Zinc also binds readily to clay particles and organic matter, becoming less available in heavy soils. High levels of phosphorus can further reduce zinc availability by forming insoluble zinc-phosphate compounds. In arid climates with alkaline water and soil, zinc deficiency is the single most common micronutrient problem.

## Interactions with Other Nutrients

Zinc's most important interaction is with cannabis-phosphorus|phosphorus. Zinc is necessary for proper utilization of phosphorus, and excessive phosphorus is the most common cause of zinc deficiency symptoms. Zinc also interacts closely with [[query-how-to-identify-and-fix-cannabis-iron-deficiency]] -- excess zinc interferes with iron's ability to function properly and causes an iron deficiency. Conversely, iron excess can compete with zinc uptake. Along with manganese and iron, zinc is one of three micronutrients that commonly become deficient together when pH rises above 6.5. Excess [[cannabis-copper-nutrient]] can also interfere with zinc uptake.

## Organic Sources

[[organic-fertilizer-sources-guide]] of zinc include:

- Well-rotted manures (cow, horse, chicken)
- Compost (variable content)
- Seaweed and kelp extracts
- Zinc sulfate (inorganic but commonly used)
- Rock dusts (some varieties contain zinc)

Natural chelates such as humic acid and citric acid can be added to organic soil mixes to improve zinc availability. Roots and bacteria also exude natural chelates to promote the uptake of metallic elements.

## Chelated Zinc

A chelate (Greek for "claw") is an organic molecule that forms a claw-like bond with free electrically charged metal particles. This property keeps metal ions such as zinc, iron, and manganese soluble in water, and the chelated metal's reactions with other materials is suppressed. Roots take in the chelated metals in a stable, soluble form that is used immediately.

Different chelate types work in different pH ranges:

- **DTPA** -- Most effective in a pH below 6.5
- **EDDHA** -- Effective up to a pH of 8.0
- **EDTA** -- Chelate is slow to cause leaf burn

Natural chelates such as humic acid and citric acid can be added to organic soil mixes. Roots and bacteria also exude natural chelates to promote the uptake of metallic elements. Chelates decompose rapidly in low levels of ultraviolet (UV) light, including light produced by HID bulbs and sunlight. Keep chelates out of the light to protect them from rapid decomposition.

## Deficiency

Zinc is the most common micronutrient found deficient. These symptoms are often confused with a lack of manganese or iron, but when zinc deficiency is severe, new leaf blades contort and dry out.

### Symptoms

- Interveinal chlorosis of young leaves
- New leaves and growing tips develop small, thin blades that contort and wrinkle
- Leaf tips and later margins discolor and burn
- Burned spots on leaves could grow progressively larger
- Often confused with manganese or iron deficiency
- When severe, new leaf blades contort and dry out
- Flower buds also contort into odd shapes, turn crispy dry, and are often hard
- Lack of growth including buds

### Deficiency Progression Timeline

1. Interveinal chlorosis of young leaves
2. New leaves develop thin, wispy leaves
3. Leaf tips discolor, turn dark, and die back
4. New growth contorts horizontally
5. New bud and leaf growth stops

### Treatment

- Flush the growing medium with a diluted mix of a complete fertilizer containing chelated trace elements, including zinc, iron, and manganese
- Add a quality-brand hydroponic micronutrient mix containing chelated trace elements
- Zinc is also necessary for proper utilization of cannabis-phosphorus|phosphorus
- Because all three (iron, zinc, manganese) often occur concurrently, treat with a chelated dose of all three

## Toxicity

Zinc is **extremely toxic in excess**. Severely toxic plants die quickly.

### Symptoms

- Excess zinc interferes with [[query-how-to-identify-and-fix-cannabis-iron-deficiency]]'s ability to function properly
- Causes an iron deficiency
- Plants can die quickly in severe cases

## Common Mistakes

- Treating only zinc deficiency while ignoring concurrent iron and manganese deficiency
- Over-applying "super bloom" fertilizers (high phosphorus), which locks out zinc
- Not using chelated forms of zinc in alkaline conditions (unavailable to plants)
- Storing chelated micronutrients in clear containers exposed to light (causes decomposition)
- Confusing zinc deficiency with iron or manganese deficiency (all cause interveinal chlorosis in young leaves)
- Using zinc sulfate without pH adjustment in alkaline media
