# Amanita Muscaria -- Fly Agaric

The fly agaric (Amanita muscaria) is perhaps the most recognizable mushroom in the world, with its bright scarlet cap dotted with white warts. Despite its popular reputation as the most poisonous toadstool, it never causes death in healthy people. Its history of ritual use, psychoactive properties, and cultural prominence make it one of the most studied fungi in ethnobotany.

## Description

This beautiful fungus is well known, featuring in nursery pictures, table decorations, films such as Fantasia, and serving as a model for children's toys. The cap is scarlet or orange-red, slightly viscid, shiny and dotted with thick white or yellowish wart-like patches often arranged more or less concentrically.

It expands from roundish to flat with a striate edge. The crowded gills are white or tinted with yellow. The stem is white, stuffed then hollow, often scaly, with a bulbous base encircled by several zones of white or yellowish squamules.

The specific epithet muscaria refers to the fact, first mentioned by Albertus Magnus in the thirteenth century, that the fungus broken up in milk kills flies. In country districts on the Continent the method is still used, though in Poland and Czechoslovakia sugar solution replaces milk. In Romania the fungus is placed on window-sills to discourage flies.

It was formerly employed in England and Sweden for getting rid of bugs, accounting for the alternative name "Bug Agaric."

## Psychoactive Properties

Among the Koryak tribes of northeast Siberia, the fungus is eaten to produce a state of excessive emotion on ceremonial occasions. For over two centuries it has been known that the stimulant is eliminated by the kidneys, allowing the effects to be recycled by drinking the urine of intoxicated individuals.

According to Scandinavian tradition, the Vikings ate Amanita muscaria to go berserk. This connection between the fly agaric and the legendary berserker rage has been debated by historians but remains a persistent element of the mushroom's folklore.

The visual hallucinations induced by the mushroom are thought to be due to muscimol and ibotenic acid, compounds which respectively excite and sedate the nervous system by binding to certain brain receptors. The frequently disoriented world described in Lewis Carroll's Alice in Wonderland (1865) probably owes much to contemporary accounts of fly agaric inebriation by the mycologist [[fungal-inventory-methods]].

## Poisoning Profile

Though popularly regarded as deadly, Amanita muscaria never causes death in healthy people. Usually one to three hours after a meal there is a period of delirium and hallucinations, sometimes accompanied by intestinal disturbances. After a few hours this is followed by intense stupor and awakening to complete forgetfulness.

The poison resides principally in the skin of the cap, but there is considerable variation in the amount present. The fungus is eaten in some regions apparently without ill effect. The price on the barren Steppes -- three or four reindeer for a single specimen -- suggests considerable potency in those populations.

The first poison isolated was muscarine (mycomuscarine), present in small amounts, with well-marked effects that are not those characteristic of Amanita muscaria poisoning. A second alkaloid, mycetoatropine (muscaridine), has effects on the central nervous system similar to atropine. A third alkaloid, choline, occurs in fairly large amounts and is probably responsible for gastrointestinal symptoms.

## Historical Treatments

[[mushroom-foraging-safety-guide]] classified Amanita muscaria among poisonous fungi but noted the absence of fatal outcomes in healthy adults. The treatments recommended in the mid-twentieth century included emetics, purgatives, and the administration of atropine or morphine to control symptoms.

The variation in toxin concentration between specimens and populations makes dose-response relationships difficult to establish. This variability is one of the factors that makes the fly agaric both less dangerous than popularly believed and less predictable than careful users would wish.

## Ecological Role

Like other Amanita species, the fly agaric forms mycorrhizal associations with trees, growing mostly under birches but also occurring under pines. Its bright coloration serves as a warning to potential predators, though as with [[mushroom-toxins-and-poisoning]], the psychoactive compounds were probably not "designed" for this purpose. Their actual function, if any, for the fungus remains unknown.

When conditions are damp, the cap may slip through the volva without tearing it and is then without the characteristic white spots. This spotless form has caused confusion with edible species and is one of several reasons why the fly agaric, while rarely fatal, remains a source of poisoning incidents.

# Ramsbottom's 1945 Guide -- Lesser Poisonous Fungi

John Ramsbottom's 1945 Penguin handbook Poisonous Fungi provided detailed descriptions of every mushroom species known to be dangerous in Britain at that time. Beyond the notorious Amanita species, Ramsbottom documented a range of fungi whose toxicity varied from potentially lethal to merely indigestible. The work remains a landmark in [[european-mushroom-poisoning-history]].

## Lepiota Species

Small species of Lepiota, brownish with an admixture of pink, were recognized as potentially dangerous. Lepiota helveola (poisonous lepiota) causes symptoms resembling mild [[mushroom-toxins-and-poisoning]] poisoning.

It has a rounded then flattened cap, initially covered with an ochre-brown cuticle that becomes flushed with pink or lilac, especially in damp weather, tearing into numerous scales. The flesh is white, often becoming pinkish when cut, with a pleasant smell and sourish taste. The combination of pleasant taste and potentially fatal toxicity makes Lepiota helveola particularly treacherous.

Lepiota cristata (crested lepiota), with its concentric circles of small scales on a campanulate cap, has an unpleasant taste and strong disagreeable smell. Despite its suspect appearance, Ramsbottom concluded it was not actually poisonous. The difficulty of distinguishing among small Lepiota species makes the genus one of the more treacherous for amateur collectors.

## Russula Species

The genus Russula contains many brightly colored species, most of which are edible. Those with acrid taste are usually considered poisonous, though whatever irritant substance they contain is dispersed in cooking.

Russula emetica (the sickener) has a bright red cap, pink under the skin, with a very acrid and persistent taste. Russula fragilis is similar but smaller with a wider colour range and an immediately acrid taste. Russula fellea (bitter russula) is pale ochraceous with a taste described as "so bitter as to be unforgettable."

## Inocybe Species

In the genus Inocybe, most species are small and none is known to be edible. Inocybe patouillardi is at first wholly creamy white, then becomes pinky yellow and finally vermilion or brownish red, staining red immediately on handling.

It caused a fatality in Surrey in 1937; others who partook more moderately had giddiness, profuse sweats, and vomiting. The poisoning is of the Amanita muscaria type, affecting the nervous system rather than causing the delayed liver destruction characteristic of amatoxins.

Inocybe geophylla (earthy inocybe) has a conical cap with silky fibrils, varying in colour from white and yellowish to lilac or blue. Its lilac form (var. lilacina) loses colour in darkness but regains it with light exposure. It is often said to be poisonous but Ramsbottom noted no definite information.

## Boletus Species

Boletus satanas (devil's bolete) is the most handsome of the poisonous boletes, with a dirty white or greyish cap, yellow tubes turning bright crimson, and a stem covered with a close network of bright red veins. When first described over a century earlier it had caused sickness, and its powers were "much overrated" according to Ramsbottom.

It is probably very indigestible rather than truly dangerous, and is eaten in Czechoslovakia and parts of Italy. The dramatic color changes -- tubes turning blue then crimson when cut -- make it one of the most visually distinctive fungi in European forests.

Boletus felleus (bitter bolete) has a taste "so bitter as to have been described as unforgettable." Though not poisonous, a single specimen will ruin a meal. Its identification challenge lies in confusion with Boletus edulis when young and the tubes still white.

Boletus luridus and B. erythropus are both edible despite their tendency to turn vivid blue when bruised. This rapid colour change is responsible for the widespread but mistaken belief that they are poisonous.

## Entoloma and Hebeloma

Entoloma lividum (leaden entoloma) has a tawny or greyish cap, hard and compact, with a mealy smell and taste that is pleasant at first but not lasting. It causes violent sickness and occasional death, known around Dijon as "le grand empoisonneur de la Cote d'Or."

Hebeloma crustuliniforme (crustlike hebeloma) grows in rings in grassy places, looking like "a row of rolls coming from a baker's oven." It is very indigestible, and the persistent taste of radish after cooking makes it objectionable.

## Species Now Known to Be Harmless

Ramsbottom's guide also documented species formerly considered deadly that were subsequently found to be harmless. Amanita mappa (false death cap) was "now known to be harmless, though worthless because of its offensive flavour."

Volvaria gloiocephala (sticky volvaria), "formerly regarded as deadly poisonous," is harmless and much eaten in Portugal and Algeria. These reclassifications illustrate the evolution of [[mushroom-toxins-and-poisoning]] and the danger of relying on outdated mushroom-identification|field guides.
