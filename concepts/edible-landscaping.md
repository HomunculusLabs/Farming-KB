# Edible Landscaping

Edible landscaping replaces or supplements ornamental plants with food-producing species while maintaining aesthetic appeal. It integrates [[permaculture-design]] design principles with traditional landscape architecture, creating productive spaces that look intentional and beautiful.

## Core Design Principles

- **Function meets form**: Every plant should serve at least two purposes — food production plus beauty, shade, [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]], or [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]]
- **Right plant, right place**: Match species to site conditions (sun, soil, moisture, hardiness zone)
- **Layer vertically**: Use canopy trees, understory shrubs, herbaceous plants, ground covers, and vines to maximize production per square foot
- **Year-round interest**: Plan for sequential bloom, foliage color, structure, and harvest windows
- **Start small, expand gradually**: Convert one area at a time rather than ripping out the entire landscape

## Edible Ornamentals

These plants blur the line between ornamental and edible:

- **Blueberries**: Spring flowers, summer fruit, brilliant red fall foliage, winter twig color
- **Elderberry**: Lacy white flower clusters (elderflower), dark berries, attractive form
- **Cherry trees**: Spectacular spring blossoms, summer fruit, fall color
- **Currants and gooseberries**: Attractive shrubs with tart berries, colorful fruit
- **Grape vines**: Ornamental on arbors and fences, productive fruit
- **Rosemary**: Evergreen shrub, culinary herb, blue flowers attractive to bees
- **Chives**: Purple globe flowers, edible leaves and blossoms
- **Kale and ornamental cabbage**: Dramatic foliage color in cool weather
- **Daylilies (Hemerocallis)**: [[comparison-scented-geraniums-vs-edible-flowers]], buds, and tubers; carefree landscape plant
- **Nasturtiums**: Edible leaves, flowers, and seed pods; vibrant colors, pest-repellent companion plant

## Food Forests in Suburban Settings

A suburban food forest layers productive perennial plants to mimic a forest ecosystem:

### Canopy Layer
- Standard fruit trees (apple, pear, plum, persimmon, pecan)
- Chestnut, walnut, mulberry

### Understory Layer
- Semi-dwarf or dwarf fruit trees, pawpaw, serviceberry
- Nut bushes (hazelnut, filbert)

### Shrub Layer
- Berry bushes (blueberry, raspberry, blackberry, currant, elderberry)
- Nitrogen-fixing shrubs (sea buckthorn, goumi, Siberian pea shrub)

### Herbaceous Layer
- [[perennial-vegetables]] (asparagus, rhubarb, sorrel)
- [[culinary-herbs-cultivation-and-kitchen-garden-guide]] (thyme, oregano, mint in containers)
- Annual vegetables interplanted in gaps

### Ground Cover Layer
- [[ground-cover-plants]] like creeping thyme, strawberry, clover
- Nitrogen fixers (white clover, vetch)

### Root Layer
- Jerusalem artichokes, groundnuts, horseradish (confine aggressively)

### Vine Layer
- Hardy kiwi, grapes, passionflower, hops, malabar spinach

### Design Considerations for Small Lots
- Use espalier or cordon-trained fruit trees against fences and walls
- Select dwarf and semi-dwarf rootstocks for small spaces
- Multi-graft trees provide multiple varieties on one tree
- Utilize [[comparison-vertical-gardening-vs-container-gardening]] on fences, arbors, and trellises
- Replace foundation plantings with edible shrubs and herbs

## Front Yard Gardens

Many municipalities restrict or regulate food gardening in front yards. Key strategies:

- **Check local ordinances** before converting front lawn to food production
- **Design for curb appeal**: Use structured beds, clean edging, pathways, and ornamental interplanting
- **Raised beds** with finished lumber or stone look intentional and tidy
- **Mix ornamentals with edibles** to maintain a traditional landscape feel
- **Replace turf incrementally** — start with a border bed along the front walk, then expand
- **Community engagement**: A beautiful edible front yard often inspires neighbors rather than complaints

## Planning an Edible Landscape

1. **Assess the site**: Sun exposure (track throughout the day/seasons), soil type, drainage, existing plants
2. **Identify your goals**: Primary crops, preserving surplus, feeding family year-round
3. **Create a base map**: Draw existing structures, trees, hardscape, and utilities
4. **Design in zones**: High-frequency crops (herbs, salad greens) near the kitchen; lower-maintenance perennials further away
5. **Select plants**: Choose species matching site conditions and your eating preferences
6. **Plan infrastructure**: Irrigation, pathways, compost area, tool storage
7. **Install hardscape first**: Paths, raised beds, trellises, irrigation before planting
8. **Plant perennials first**: Fruit trees, berries, and [[perennial-vegetables]] take years to produce
9. **Fill in with annuals**: Use annual vegetables and herbs while perennials mature

## Maintenance Considerations

- Edible landscapes need more active management than traditional ornamental landscapes
- Plan for seasonal harvest and preservation time
- Pest management requires vigilance — avoid spraying food crops with non-organic treatments
- Mulch heavily to [[query-how-do-cover-crops-suppress-weeds]] and retain moisture
- Compost on-site to close nutrient loops
