## Shamanism, Death, and Rebirth

Shamanism is widely regarded as the oldest indigenous spiritual practice on Earth, with roots extending back tens of thousands of years. Archaeological evidence, including cave paintings depicting transformational figures and mushroom-iconography dating to the Paleolithic era, suggests that shamanic practices involving altered states of consciousness are among the most ancient forms of human religious expression. At its core, shamanic practice revolves around the death-and-rebirth experience — a psychospiritual ordeal in which the practitioner undergoes a symbolic or experiential dissolution of the ego, confronts mortality, and returns transformed with heightened insight and healing capacity. This initiatory process is not merely metaphorical; in traditional contexts, it is often facilitated by the ritual consumption of **entheogenic plants**, which induce profound altered states of consciousness that mimic or enact the experience of dying and being reborn.

## Entheogenic Plants and the Shamanic Death Experience

Entheogenic (literally "generating the divine within") plants — including psychoactive fungi, cacti, vines, and herbs — have been central to shamanic practice across virtually every continent. Substances such as [[banisteriopsis-caapi]]* and DMT-containing plants), **peyote** (*Lophophora williamsii*), **iboga** (*Tabernanthe iboga*), and various other preparations were used by shamans to deliberately induce the death-and-rebirth vision. In these altered states, practitioners reported experiences of ego dissolution, encounters with spiritual entities or ancestors, journeys through underworld or celestial realms, and a fundamental restructuring of their understanding of self and reality.

The experiential "death" produced by these substances was not seen as a literal ending but as a necessary passage — a dying of old identity structures, habitual patterns, and limited perceptions. The "rebirth" that followed was understood as an awakening to a broader, more integrated consciousness. By confronting and conquering the fear of death in a controlled ritual context, shamans claimed to gain power over it, both for themselves and for the communities they served. The shaman who has "died" and returned is considered to have access to realms of knowledge unavailable to the uninitiated — a living bridge between the world of the living and the world of spirits.

Ethnographic accounts from Siberia, the Amazon, Mesoamerica, Africa, and Oceania consistently describe the shaman's initiatory crisis as involving a genuine experience of dismemberment, consumption by spirits, or descent into an underworld, followed by reconstruction and re-emergence. These accounts parallel the phenomenology of high-dose psychedelic experiences, reinforcing the hypothesis that entheogenic plants were the primary technology for inducing the death-and-rebirth ordeal in traditional societies.

The role of the shaman within the community extended far beyond personal spiritual development. Shamans served as healers, diviners, psychopomps (guides of souls), and mediators between the human and spirit worlds. Their ability to "die" and return gave them authority to diagnose illness, retrieve lost souls, negotiate with spirits on behalf of the living, and guide the dying through the passage of death itself. In many traditions, the shaman's initiatory death was understood not as a one-time event but as a recurring practice — periodic journeys into the underworld or celestial realms to renew their power and bring back knowledge for their people.

The biological and psychological mechanisms by which entheogens produce the death-and-rebirth experience have become clearer through modern neuroscience. Functional brain imaging studies have shown that psychedelics such as psilocybin dramatically reduce activity in the **default mode network (DMN)** — a set of interconnected brain regions associated with self-referential thought, autobiographical memory, and the maintenance of the ego. The suppression of DMN activity correlates strongly with subjects' reports of ego dissolution and the sense of dying, suggesting a neurobiological basis for what shamans have described in spiritual terms for millennia.

## Shamanism as the Cornerstone of Organized Religion
