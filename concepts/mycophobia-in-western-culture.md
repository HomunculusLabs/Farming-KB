# Mycophobia in Western Culture

The concept of [[mycophilia-mycophobia-european-mushroom-history]] -- an irrational fear of
mushrooms -- was coined by [[gordon-wasson-mushroom-discovery]] and his wife Valentina to explain the
striking cultural differences in attitudes towards fungi between nations. Wasson argued that mycophobia and
its opposite, mycophilia, were "residual and half-forgotten cultural memories" of ancient religious
prohibitions placed upon the eating of mushrooms. Andy Letcher subjects this influential theory to rigorous
and devastating critique in Shroom, demonstrating that it rests upon discredited intellectual foundations and
contradicts empirical evidence.

## Wasson's Mycophobia-Mycophilia Thesis

The Wassons noticed striking differences between cultures in their relationship with fungi. The paucity of
names for mushrooms in the English language compared to Russian was one marker. They divided the nations of
the world along a mycophobic-mycophilic axis, placing Russia (and much of Eastern Europe) in the mycophilic
camp and Britain firmly in the mycophobic camp.

Wasson's more radical claim was that these attitudes represented the last surviving cultural traces of an
ancient mushroom-worshipping religion. He proposed that European ancestors had consumed magic mushrooms --
probably the [[amanita-muscaria-ancient-wine-question]], but perhaps later psilocybin mushrooms -- as part of an
archaic form of [[mckenna-psychedelic-shamanism]]. As this loosened and gradually became institutionalised, mushroom consumption
was restricted to a powerful priesthood that placed a terrible taboo upon its profane usage. The cult spread
widely but eventually gave way to what would become the world's major religions. Nevertheless, the taboo
persisted in vestigial fashion so that fear of, or fascination with, the mushroom lastingly determined
people's attitudes.

## Intellectual Foundations: Frazer and Cultural Evolution

Wasson's thesis was modelled explicitly upon the foundational works of anthropology. He borrowed from James
George Frazer's The Golden Bough (1890) both the emphasis on taboo -- which he considered a "lasting and
deep-seated emotional attitude" -- and the notion of "survivals": "fossil meanings, fossil sayings,
fossilised folkloric bits of our various languages." From Robert Graves he took the belief
that myths, however fantastical, have their origins in actual historical events.

This intellectual framework -- known as cultural evolution -- had reached its peak in the early twentieth
century. Thinkers such as Edward Burnett Tylor, Herbert Spencer, and Auguste Comte proposed that human
societies evolved through fixed stages from savagery to civilisation. Frazer's comparative method -- amassing
evidence from as many different sources as possible -- was perfectly suited to Wasson's purposes. Frazer's
work, despite being rejected within anthropology, proved titillating to the general public and exerted a
lasting influence upon popular imagination.

## The Mycophobia Thesis as Mushroom Cult Origin Story

Wasson imagined the following scenario. Our European ancestors had consumed magic mushrooms as part of an
original and archaic form of shamanism. As this loosened, mushroom consumption was restricted to a powerful
priesthood that placed a terrible taboo upon its profane usage. The cult spread widely but eventually gave way
to the world's major religions. The magic mushroom, wrote Wasson, "must have been instilled with mana, an
object of awe, of terror." The taboo persisted in vestigial fashion so that fear of, or fascination with,
the mushroom lastingly determined people's attitudes.

At first sight Wasson's extraordinary thesis seems incredible, more like something from the novels of H.
Rider Haggard than a serious ethnographic proposition. But what made Wasson's conclusions plausible was that
he modelled his theoretical and methodological approach explicitly upon the foundational works of
anthropology, and consequently a diverse range of distinguished academics were persuaded that Wasson was
absolutely correct.

## The Critique of Mycophobia

Letcher identifies several fundamental problems with Wasson's mycophobia thesis:

**The term itself is misleading.** "Phobia" implies an overwhelming and irrational panic reaction, a force
bearing down from within the unconscious. Yet anxieties about mushrooms are wholly rational. In the absence
of reliable methods for distinguishing edible from poisonous species, and given the high price of getting the
decision wrong -- a slow and painful death in the case of the Death Cap -- blanket
avoidance of all mushrooms is the most sensible and reasonable option. Fear of mushrooms is actually fear of
being poisoned by mushrooms, and the way this expresses itself is no more irrational than our aversion to
other life-threatening dangers such as snakes.

**Empirical evidence contradicts the thesis.** A long-term study in Colorado (nominally mycophobic, with no
widespread custom of gathering wild mushrooms) found only about six cases of accidental poisoning per year
requiring hospital treatment. Fatalities were so rare as to be all but non-existent. By contrast, in the
Polish province of Poznan (nominally mycophilic, with a similar population but a long-standing folk tradition
of mushroom-picking), approximately fifty incidents per year required treatment, of which around ten per cent
proved fatal. The risk of being killed by mushrooms was substantially higher in the mycophilic culture. This
runs counter to Wasson's thesis and suggests that folk wisdom regarding mushroom identification is
worryingly unreliable.

**Mycophagy patterns are not fixed.** During the nineteenth century, wild mushrooms were commonly sold at
British markets, but fear of poisoning meant this had died out by the twentieth. During the Second World War,
the British government encouraged people to learn to identify edible mushrooms safely. Wild mushroom
consumption duly increased until after the war, when commercial mushroom production made foraging unnecessary.
This is a far more complex situation than can ever be encompassed by Wasson's blanket designation of Britain
as a mycophobic nation.

**Cultural evolution was discredited.** By the time Wasson was developing his thesis, the arguments about
cultural evolution and the Frazerian comparative method had long been rejected within anthropology. Franz Boas
and later critics demonstrated that the comparative method produced a distorted picture by stripping evidence
of context and reflecting the biases of the scholars. Later, by the 1970s, revisionist work specifically
demolished the idea that British folk customs were pagan survivals.

**Taboos are not what Wasson thought.** Later anthropologists discovered the importance of "rituals of
rebellion" in structuring both traditional and modern societies, challenging Frazer's notion of taboo as an
enduring, binding structure, silently obeyed by all. Taboos have always been about breaking. Declaring that
a hostile reaction to mushrooms is evidence that it must have been taboo at some earlier cultural development
is, as Letcher argues, an unwarranted inductive leap.

## The Frazerian Comparative Method

The principal objection to the Frazerian comparative method, and to Wasson's use of it, is that it attempts
to make data fit theory rather than vice versa. Tylor, Frazer, Graves and Wasson all attempted to prove their
preconceived hypotheses by accumulating large amounts of apparently supporting evidence while ignoring
contradictory material. The selection process inevitably reflected the class, gender, and cultural biases of
these scholars -- a point seized upon by later Marxist, feminist, and postmodernist critics.

## The Real Roots of Mushroom Avoidance

Rather than a deep cultural memory of suppressed mushroom worship, Letcher suggests that Western avoidance
of wild mushrooms is a pragmatic response to genuine danger, reinforced by the absence of a strong folk
tradition of safe mushroom-picking. The contrast with countries like Russia or Poland reflects not ancient
religious history but practical factors: the relative abundance of safe edible species, the strength of folk
traditions of identification, and the availability of alternatives. The term "mycophobia" should be retired,
along with the theory it supports.
