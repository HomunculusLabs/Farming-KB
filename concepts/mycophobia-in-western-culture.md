## Wasson's Mycophobia-Mycophilia Thesis

The Wassons noticed striking differences between cultures in their relationship with fungi. The paucity of
names for mushrooms in the English language compared to Russian was one marker. They divided the nations of
the world along a mycophobic-mycophilic axis, placing Russia (and much of Eastern Europe) in the mycophilic
camp and Britain firmly in the mycophobic camp.

Wasson's more radical claim was that these attitudes represented the last surviving cultural traces of an
ancient mushroom-worshipping religion. He proposed that European ancestors had consumed [[mckenna-psychedelic-shamanism]]. As this loosened and gradually became institutionalised, mushroom consumption
was restricted to a powerful priesthood that placed a terrible taboo upon its profane usage. The cult spread
widely but eventually gave way to what would become the world's major religions. Nevertheless, the taboo
persisted in vestigial fashion so that fear of, or fascination with, the mushroom lastingly determined
people's attitudes.

## Intellectual Foundations: Frazer and Cultural Evolution

Wasson's thesis was modelled explicitly upon the foundational works of anthropology. He borrowed from James
George Frazer's The Golden Bough (1890) both the emphasis on taboo -- which he considered a "lasting and
deep-seated emotional attitude" -- and the notion of "survivals": "fossil meanings, fossil sayings,
fossilised folkloric bits of our various languages." From Robert Graves he took the belief
that myths, however fantastical, have their origins in actual historical events.

This intellectual framework -- known as cultural evolution -- had reached its peak in the early twentieth
century. Thinkers such as Edward Burnett Tylor, Herbert Spencer, and Auguste Comte proposed that human
