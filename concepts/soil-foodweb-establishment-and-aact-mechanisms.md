## Soil Foodweb Establishment and AACT Mechanisms

Establishing a healthy soil foodweb is the primary goal of [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]] (AACT) applications. The *Field Guide for Actively Aerated Compost Tea* outlines a systematic approach to assessing, building, and monitoring soil biology, along with the mechanisms by which AACT improves plant and soil health. This page covers the procedures for establishing beneficial biology and the scientific mechanisms through which AACT achieves its effects.

## Establishing What Biology You Have

The first step in any biological soil management program is assessment. Before applying AACT, practitioners must determine the current state of soil biology through direct microscopy examination of soil samples. This baseline assessment reveals:

- **Total bacterial biomass**: The total amount of bacterial material in the soil, indicating the soil's capacity for decomposition and nutrient retention
- **Total fungal biomass**: The amount of fungal material, critical for nutrient cycling in perennial systems and soil structure formation
- **Active versus total organisms**: The proportion of organisms that are currently metabolically active versus dormant
- **Protozoan populations**: Numbers of flagellates, amoebae, and ciliates, which indicate nutrient cycling capacity
- **Nematode community**: The types and numbers of nematodes, which reveal the structure of the soil foodweb and potential pest pressures
- **Mycorrhizal colonization**: The degree to which plant roots are associated with beneficial mycorrhizal fungi

Different soil types, crop types, and management histories produce characteristic biological profiles. Disturbed soils (recently tilled, chemically fertilized, or compacted) tend to be bacterial-dominated with low overall biomass. Mature perennial systems and forests are typically fungal-dominated with high total biomass.

## Balancing the Soil Foodweb

Once the current biological state is understood, the goal is to shift the foodweb toward a desired profile. The target biology depends on the crop or ecosystem being managed:

- **Annual crops and vegetables**: Generally benefit from bacterial-dominated soils with moderate fungal biomass. The rapid nutrient cycling provided by bacteria supports fast-growing annual plants.
- **Perennial crops, orchards, and pastures**: Benefit from fungal-dominated soils where fungi decompose complex organic matter and make nutrients available over longer timeframes.
- **Forests and woody systems**: Require strongly fungal-dominated soils with significant mycorrhizal colonization.

Balancing biology involves applying AACT with the appropriate microbial composition. Compost teas can be brewed to be more bacterial or more fungal depending on the compost used, the food sources added during brewing, and the brewing duration. Fungal teas typically use compost rich in fungal biomass, longer brewing times, and food sources that favor fungal growth (such as humic acids or fish hydrolysate).

## Balancing Biology and Chemistry

Soil biology and soil chemistry are deeply interconnected. Microbial activity influences pH, nutrient availability, and soil structure, while chemical conditions (pH, salinity, nutrient levels) affect which organisms can thrive. The field guide recommends balancing biology first, since healthy biology can correct many chemical imbalances:

- **pH buffering**: Microbial metabolism produces organic acids and bases that buffer soil pH toward neutral
- **Nutrient availability**: Organisms solubilize mineral nutrients (phosphorus, calcium, potassium) that would otherwise be locked in unavailable forms
- **Salt reduction**: Bacterial and fungal populations can help remediate saline soils by binding salts and improving leaching
- **Organic matter accumulation**: As organisms grow and die, they build soil organic matter, which improves cation exchange capacity and nutrient holding

The field guide provides specific guidance on how to sequence biological and chemical interventions, emphasizing that adding chemical amendments without addressing biology often wastes money and can damage soil health further.

## Monitoring the Foodweb Over Time

Soil biology is dynamic, responding to seasonal changes, management practices, and environmental conditions. Regular monitoring — typically quarterly or at key management points — allows practitioners to track changes and adjust their AACT applications accordingly.

The monitoring protocol involves collecting soil samples from representative locations, preparing slides for direct microscopy, and counting organism types and biomass. Consistent sampling methods and record-keeping are essential for meaningful comparisons over time. The field guide includes data collection sheets designed to standardize this process.

## Mechanisms of Impact from AACT Applications

The field guide identifies several specific mechanisms through which AACT improves plant and soil health. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for explaining AACT's value to clients and for troubleshooting when results are not as expected.

### AACT Does Not Kill Anything

A fundamental principle emphasized throughout the field guide is that AACT works through competition and ecological processes, not through antibacterial or antifungal action. AACT does not kill disease organisms directly. Instead, it creates conditions where beneficial organisms outcompete pathogens for resources.

This distinction is important for regulatory and marketing reasons — AACT is not a pesticide or fungicide — and for practical reasons. Understanding that AACT works ecologically, not chemically, guides proper application timing, rates, and expectations.

### Outcompeting Disease Organisms

The primary mechanism of disease suppression is competitive exclusion. When AACT is applied to soil or plant surfaces, the beneficial organisms it introduces:

1. **Occupy physical space**: Bacterial biofilms and fungal hyphae cover root surfaces and leaf surfaces, leaving no room for pathogens to establish
2. **Consume food resources**: Beneficial organisms rapidly consume the sugars, amino acids, and other exudates that pathogens need, starving potential invaders
3. **Create hostile microenvironments**: Metabolic byproducts of beneficial organisms (organic acids, enzymes, antibiotics) make the local environment less favorable for pathogens
4. **Activate plant defenses**: Some beneficial organisms trigger [[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]] in plants, priming their immune responses against future pathogen attack

### Food as a Control Point

The second major control point for disease management is food — the resources available to soil and plant surface organisms. AACT applications are most effective when combined with practices that feed beneficial biology:

- **Compost applications**: Adding organic matter provides ongoing food for soil organisms
- **Cover cropping**: Living roots exude sugars and amino acids that feed the soil foodweb
- **Mulching**: Organic mulches provide habitat and food for surface-dwelling organisms
- **Reduced tillage**: Minimizing soil disturbance preserves existing fungal networks and organism habitat

### Nutrient Retention and Cycling

Bacteria and fungi in the soil foodweb play critical roles in nutrient management:

- **Nutrient retention**: Microbial biomass acts as a biological buffer, holding nutrients in organic forms that resist leaching. When organisms die or are consumed by predators, these nutrients are released in plant-available forms.
- **Nutrient solubilization**: Fungi produce acids and enzymes that dissolve mineral phosphorus, calcium, and other nutrients from rock particles, making them available to plants.
- **Nitrogen cycling**: Bacterial decomposition of organic matter releases ammonium, which is then converted to nitrate by nitrifying bacteria. Protozoa and nematodes further regulate nitrogen availability through the microbial loop.
- **Micronutrient access**: Mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective root zone, accessing nutrients beyond the reach of plant roots alone.

### Pathogenic Fungi and Calcium

The relationship between calcium availability and fungal disease susceptibility is well-established. Adequate calcium in plant cell walls strengthens physical barriers against fungal invasion. AACT can improve calcium availability by:

- Increasing microbial activity that solubilizes calcium compounds
- Improving root health and calcium uptake efficiency
- Supporting mycorrhizal associations that enhance nutrient uptake

### Yeasts and Molds

Yeasts (unicellular fungi) play important roles in both AACT and soil ecology. In compost tea, yeast populations contribute to disease suppression on plant surfaces by competing with pathogenic fungi for sugars and other resources. On leaf surfaces, yeasts can rapidly colonize and occupy infection sites, preventing pathogen establishment.

### Tannins, Terpenes, and Phenolic Compounds

Many plants produce secondary metabolites — tannins, terpenes, and phenolic compounds — that inhibit microbial growth. These compounds can be both beneficial (suppressing pathogens) and problematic (inhibiting beneficial organisms too). Understanding which plant materials contain these compounds helps practitioners:

- Select appropriate compost ingredients that avoid excessive antimicrobial compounds
- Choose food sources for AACT brewing that support rather than suppress beneficial biology
- Explain to clients why certain plant residues decompose slowly or produce phytotoxic effects

### Streptococcus and Anaerobic Concerns

The presence of Streptococcus and other facultative anaerobes in compost tea indicates brewing problems — insufficient aeration, excessive food additions, or poor compost quality. These organisms can be harmful to plants and indicate that the tea may contain other undesirable anaerobic biology. Regular microscopy monitoring during brewing helps catch these problems before application.

## Typical Application Timing and Rates

The field guide provides guidance on typical amounts and timing of inputs for AACT programs:

- **Compost quality**: Only mature, biologically active compost should be used as the starter material. Compost should be tested by direct microscopy before use.
- **Brewing duration**: Most AACT is brewed for 24 hours, though fungal teas may require 24–48 hours.
- **Application timing**: Apply AACT during cool, cloudy conditions or in the evening to minimize UV damage to organisms. Soil drenches can be applied at any time.
- **Application frequency**: Initial programs may require monthly applications, with frequency reducing as soil biology establishes.
