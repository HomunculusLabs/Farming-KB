# Convection, Thermosiphons, and Heat Transfer in Design

Understanding how heat is stored and transmitted is essential for building and garden design in permaculture. Heat flows from warmer to colder bodies through three primary mechanisms — conduction, convection, and radiation — each with distinct properties, efficiencies, and design applications that can be harnessed to create comfortable and productive living systems.

## Convection Loops and Thermosiphons

### The Principle of Convection

Convection transmits low-grade heat through the movement of air and water over slightly heated surfaces. It is this effect that operates in valley climates and creates valley winds. Cool air is heavier (more dense) than heated air; the same factor holds true for water, other liquids, and gases generally. This density differential is the driving force behind all convective circulation.

### Thermosiphon Design

If heated air or water is contained in pipes or ducts, a closed-loop circulation can be set up by applying heat to the lower part of that loop, provided that at least a **40 cm (about 18 inches) height difference** is built into the loop. Any greater height is also effective in producing the thermosiphon effect. This is the same principle used in refrigerators driven by flames or heat sources — no mechanical pump is needed.

### Practical Applications

- **Solar ponds and flat plate collectors:** Transferring heat from collectors to home radiators or hot water tanks through thermosiphon circulation.
- **Self-regulating systems:** Heat collectors should wherever possible be sited 0.5 m (1.6 feet) below the storage or use points so that they are self-regulated thermosiphons requiring no external pumping energy.
- **Valley winds:** In the atmosphere, columns of heated air over land ascend as an "[[overbeck-jet]]," and at the top of this column condensation and rain may occur as the air is cooled in the upper atmosphere. Such convective rains are responsible for the mosaic of rainfall that patterns the deserts.

### Limitations of Convection

Convection loops will not occur in closed rooms where hot air (at 8–10°C or 15–18°F higher temperature) sits in a quiet or stratified layer below ceilings. As air is difficult to heat and stores little heat, air convection is not an efficient way to heat building interiors, although it is the main engine of atmospheric circulation in the global sense.

## Three Grades of Heat Transfer

### Low-Grade Heat: Conduction

Conduction is heat transmitted by direct solid-to-solid or fluid-to-fluid contact. It is in this way that we heat an entire floor or wall by heating it in one place — the basis of the efficiency of the slab-floored house where the floor is previously insulated from surrounding earth. Pipes buried in hot solid masses have heat conducted to their contents, and hot water pipes conduct heat to slab floors in which they are buried. Such heating is most efficient in homes.

In open (uninsulated) systems, conduction effects are local because heat is fairly rapidly radiated from solids or soil surfaces. Insulation is effective in trapping conducted heat.

### Medium-Grade Heat: Convection

Convection transmits heat by the movements of fluids or gases — as in air, wind, or water. The key principle is that heated fluids rise. Draught-proofing conserves convective heat losses in buildings. While inefficient for interior heating, convection drives the global atmospheric system that distributes heat across the planet.

### High-Grade Heat: Radiation

Radiation is heat travelling by straight-line radiation across space — the effect transmitted across space by the sun. Radiant heaters (electric, gas-fire, or wood-heated mass stoves) affect air temperature very little but heat other solids and liquids like our bodies directly. Thus we can keep very warm even in a draughty or cool room by the use of radiant heat sources — these are very efficient space heaters.

As radiation crosses space and is non-directional, focused radiation can produce very intense local heat. Radiant heat can only be conserved by reflective (dust-free) mirrors.

## Solar Energy and Atmospheric Attenuation

The sun provides approximately **2 Langleys per minute** (gram calories per square centimetre) to the outer atmosphere. The common heat unit is that needed to raise one gram of water from 14.5°C to 15°C. The quantity of heat received on Earth is greatly affected by latitude and season (depth of atmosphere), the angle of slopes (affecting reflection and absorption), and the amount of ice, water vapour, dust, or cloud in the air above.

Even when the sun is directly overhead on a clear day, only **22%** of the radiant energy penetrates the atmosphere (1 atmosphere depth). In polar areas where the slanting sun at 5° elevation passes obliquely through at a distance of 11 atmospheres, as little as **1%** of incoming energy is received. Slope has similar profound effects, so slopes facing towards the poles receive even less energy from radiation.

Most homes receive enough sunlight on their sun-facing areas to heat the water and space of the house, provided we arrange to capture this heat and store it.

## Advected Heat and Cold

Winds travelling from warmer to cooler regions (or the opposite) bring **advected** (exotic, out-of-area) warmth and cold to local regions. This produces advection fogs where cold air comes inshore from coasts, and advection frosts when cold air flows down mountain slopes to pool in hollows and flat areas.

The invasion of cool areas by warm advected air causes moisture condensation, which is critical to precipitation in forests but a nuisance in enclosed buildings. Designers should attempt to bring only dry warm air into wooden houses, or provide ways to direct condensation moisture to the house exterior.

## Plants as Biological Heaters

Most or all Arum lilies and species such as *Philodendron selloum* store fats which are "burnt" to create heat, so that the flowers actually heat up. Philodendrons may register 46°C (115°F) when the surrounding air is only 4°C (39°F) — a remarkable 42°C differential. Crocuses heat up to 15°C (27°F) above ambient air temperature. The warmth generated is probably used to attract flies and heat-seeking insects to the pollen.

Some plants, however, may use their heat more strategically. **Skunk cabbage** (*Symplocarpus foetidus*) may use its heat to melt a hole in the spring snow, protecting blooms from cold. These thermogenic plants demonstrate that biological systems can serve as active heat sources in garden design, potentially moderating microclimates for neighbouring frost-sensitive species in ways that designers can exploit.

## Ecological Significance
The principles described in relation to convection thermosiphons heat transfer
are fundamental to understanding how natural systems maintain equilibrium.
Forests and vegetation play critical roles in water cycling, temperature
regulation, and atmospheric processes that sustain regional climate patterns.

## Practical Applications
These concepts apply directly to permaculture design, agroforestry systems,
and watershed management. Understanding the interactions between vegetation,
water, and atmosphere enables designers to create more resilient and productive
landscapes that work with natural processes rather than opposing them.
