## The Attine Ant-Fungus Mutualism

The attine ants include the leaf-cutter ants (genera Atta
and Acromyrmex) and numerous less derived genera
(Apterostigma, Cyphomyrmex, Trachymyrmex, and others).
All attine ants cultivate fungi in the family Lepiotaceae
(primarily Leucoagaricus) as their food source. The ants
provide the fungus with substrate (fresh leaves in the
case of leaf-cutters, or various plant and insect debris
in lower attines), maintain optimal growing conditions,
and protect the garden from competitors and parasites.

Higher attines, particularly Atta and Acromyrmex, cultivate
a very low diversity of ant, often a
single clonal cultivar that is transmitted vertically from
parent to daughter colonies. This monoculture is maintained
in an environment where the ants actively weed out
contaminating fungi and defend the garden against parasites.

## Escovopsis: The Primary Garden Parasite

Escovopsis is a specialized ascomycete parasite that grows
only on the cultivated fungus of attine ants. It was first
recognized as a significant pathogen of attine gardens and
subsequently described as a new genus. Escovopsis species
are horizontally transmitted, unlike the vertically
transmitted cultivar, and represent a persistent
evolutionary threat to the mutualism.

### Biology and Pathogenicity

Escovopsis grows as a dense mycelial mat over the surface
of the fungal garden, outcompeting the cultivar for
resources. The parasite can rapidly destroy a garden if
left unchecked, leading to colony starvation and death.
Escovopsis aspergilloides, a rediscovered hyphomycete from
leaf-cutter ant nests, produces specialized conidia and
mycelial structures adapted for growth on the cultivar
fungus.
