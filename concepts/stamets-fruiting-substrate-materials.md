# Stamets Fruiting Substrate Materials and Formulations
#mushroom-cultivation #substrate-preparation #materials #sustainable-agriculture

Updated: 2026-04-18

Source: Paul Stamets, Growing Gourmet and Medicinal Mushrooms (1993), Ch. 6 "Materials for Formulating a Fruiting Substrate"

## Overview

The potential for recycling organic wastes with fungi seems unlimited. Many mushrooms thrive on base materials alien to their natural habitat. Although Oyster mushrooms are generally found on deciduous woods, they grow well on cereal straws, corn cobs, seed hulls, coffee wastes, sugar cane bagasse, paper and pulp by-products, and numerous other materials. Success increases if the base material is modified for optimal structure and moisture, and heat-treated before inoculation. See also [[bulk-substrate-preparation]], [[oyster-mushroom-substrate-preparation]], [[stamets-non-composted-substrates]].

## Raw Materials

### Wood Wastes
Preferred primary substrate component. Deciduous woods that decompose quickly are best -- they have greater ratios of starch-enriched sapwood to heartwood. These sugars encourage rapid initial growth.

**Best hardwoods:** Alder, cottonwood, poplar (fast-rotting, less disease-resistant)

**Also suitable:** Oak, maple, ironwood, beech, ash, chestnut, chinkapin, birch, hornbeam, larch, sweetgum, tanoak, walnut, elm, willow

**Softwoods:** Douglas fir and hemlock work. Most other pines, cedars, and redwood NOT recommended (anti-rotting compounds). Aromatic hardwoods like eucalyptus not recommended.

**Important warnings:**
- Avoid wood chips from trees along busy roadways (automobile exhaust and asphalt leachate contaminate with lead, aluminum -- mushrooms bioconcentrate heavy metals)
- County road chips with little traffic are less prone to contamination
- Obtain from larger-diameter trees; sawmills and pulp chip companies provide cleanest sources
- Heavy metal concentrations in mushrooms are below US government standards for fish
- Analysis of mushrooms from China showed highest aluminum, mercury, and lead; California next; Washington's Olympic Peninsula (less industrialized) the least
- Environmental service companies analyze for $50-125 US

### Substrate Matrix Concept (50:50 Sawdust/Chips)
A simple 50:50 mixture by volume of sawdust and chips, of varying particle sizes:
- **Smaller particles:** stimulate quick growth ("leap-off" from spawn)
- **Larger particles:** encourage thick, cord-like rhizomorph formation
- **Largest chips:** become nutritional bases and fruiting platforms, giving rise to super-large mushrooms
- This concept was pioneered with King Stropharia, producing 5 lb. specimens

### Cereal Straws
Best for Oyster mushroom cultivation:
- Wheat straw: preferred (inexpensive, preserves well, ideal shaft diameter, few competitors)
- Rye straw: similar but coarser
- Oat and rice straw: finer
- Chopped to 1-4 inch lengths, pasteurized in hot water (160F/71C) for 1-2 hours
- Wetted and turned on cement slab 2-4 days, then steam-pasteurized 160F for 2-4 hours
- Coarser straws create looser substrate; finer straws create denser substrate
- Target: 20-25 lbs per cubic foot wetted straw

### Paper Products (Newspaper, Cardboard, Books)
- Particularly attractive for tropical islands and desert communities where sawdust limited
- Paper is pulped wood (lignin-cellulose fibers) supporting wood-decomposing mushrooms
- Modern printing uses soybean-based inks, reducing toxic residues
- Free substrate from newspaper recycling programs
- See [[stem-butt-cardboard-cultivation]]

### Other Agricultural Wastes
- Coffee waste and tea leaves
- Sugar cane bagasse
- Banana fronds
- Corncobs
- Seed hulls (cottonseed, oil-rich seeds)
- Hulls: almonds, walnuts, sunflower, pecans, peanuts
- Soybean meal, roughage (Okara), soy waste
- Artichoke waste
- Cactus waste: saguaro, prickly pear, yucca, agave (Pleurotus opuntiae is native to prickly pear/agave/yucca)

## Suitable Tree Species List

Comprehensive list compiled from trials and reports by Stamets, Pegler (1973), San Antonio (1981), Farr (1983), Gilbertson & Ryvarden (1986), Chang & Miles (1989), Przybylowicz & Donoghue (1989), and Krieger (1992):

### Hardwoods
Acer (Maples), Alnus (Alders), Betula (Birches), Carpinus (Hornbeams), Carya (Hickories, Pecan), Castanea (Chestnuts), Castanopsis (Chinkapins), Cornus (Dogwoods), Fagus (Beeches), Fraxinus (Ashes), Juglans (Walnuts), Larix (Larches), Liquidambar (Sweetgums), Liriodendron (Tulip Poplar), Lithocarpus (Tanoaks), Ostrya (Ironwoods/Hophornbeams), Populus (Cottonwoods, Poplars, Aspens), Prosopis (Mesquite), Quercus (Oaks), Robinia (Black Locust), Rhus (Sumac), Salix (Willows), Taxus (Yews), Ulmus (Elms)

### Softwoods
Abies (Firs), Tsuga (Hemlocks), Pseudotsuga (Douglas Fir)

### Conifer-Degrading Mushroom Species
Ganoderma (G. oregonense, G. tsugae), Hypholoma (H. capnoides), Pleurotus (P. pulmonarius), Psilocybe (P. cyanescens and allies), and Stropharia (S. rugoso-annulata) grow naturally on firs. These conifer-degrading species can also be cultivated on most hardwoods, but few hardwood-native species will fruit on conifers.

## Sustainability Considerations

- Do not cut trees solely as substrate source
- Follow sustainable forest practices
- Wood materials should ideally be a "waste" product from other activities
- Mixed wood sources from mills that don't separate species are NOT recommended
- Disease-free logs selected from forest in winter or early spring for log culture
- Freshness of sawdust/chips counts -- competitors may already have taken hold in aged material
- Lumber mills, pulp mills, furniture manufacturers generate usable waste

## Supplement Options

Base substrate is supplemented with carbohydrate- and protein-rich additives to enhance yields:
- Rice bran (10-20% by dry weight)
- Wheat bran
- Corn meal
- Soy meal
- Millet
- Rye grain

Supplements increase biological efficiency but also increase contamination risk. Higher supplement levels require stricter sterilization protocols. See [[stamets-compost-preparation]], [[stamets-phase-ii-composting]].
