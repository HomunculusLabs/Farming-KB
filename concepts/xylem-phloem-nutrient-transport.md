## Xylem and Phloem Nutrient Transport

Plants move water, nutrients, sugars, proteins, and signaling molecules through two
specialized vascular systems: the **[[xylem]]** and the **[[phloem]]**. Together they form a
distribution network that routes materials from roots to shoots and back again with
remarkable precision.

## Spring Sap Flow: The Maple/Birch Freeze-Thaw Mechanism

In early spring, before leaves emerge, maples and birches produce a pressurized sap
flow through their xylem driven by a physical **freeze-thaw cycle**:

1. **Night (freezing):** CO₂ and other gases dissolve into sap as temperatures drop,
   reducing internal pressure and drawing water in from surrounding tissues.
2. **Morning (thawing):** Dissolved gases come out of solution and expand. Since
   xylem vessels can only stretch so far, internal pressure builds and pushes sap
   upward.

Once leaves and buds form in later spring, the xylem stops carrying sugars and the
**phloem** takes over sugar transport.

## Xylem Ion Concentration and Transpiration

Nutrient ion concentration in xylem sap varies with **[[transpiration]] rate**:

- **High transpiration (hot conditions):** Rapid water flow dilutes ion concentration.
- **Spring:** Mineral uptake is generally higher than during summer heat.

This means plants take up more minerals per unit of water in cooler conditions.

## Hormonal Signaling Through the Xylem

The xylem also transports **hormones** that regulate plant physiology:

- **Stomatal regulation:** Chemical signals trigger stomata to close when roots sense
  insufficient water.
- **Growth signals:** Hormones coordinating growth are distributed through xylem.

As few as **one or two hormone molecules** arriving in a cell can trigger a complete
physiological response—a communication system of extraordinary sensitivity.

## Phloem Transport of Sugars, Starches, and Synthesized Proteins

Plants take fourteen nutrients in ionic form, plus carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, and
synthesize everything else. Short-distance transport between adjacent cells occurs via
the **symplastic pathway**, but long-distance delivery relies on the **phloem**.

Phloem sap is water loaded with sugars, synthesized proteins, amino acids, organic
acids, ATP, potassium ions, and hormones. Carbon fixed by photosynthesis must be
distributed to all regions where it forms proteins, lipids, and other compounds.

## Phloem Sap Composition

Phloem sap composition varies by species, growth stage, season, and time of day:

- **Sugars:** ~90% of compounds are sugars in **complex forms**, not glucose. Glucose
  is found in very low concentrations or absent entirely. Some species transport
  sugar-alcohols (e.g., sorbitol) instead of simple sugars.
- **pH:** Typically **alkaline (7.5–8.5)**, contrasting with acidic vacuolar and xylem
  saps. The exception is perennials in spring, whose phloem sap is acidic.
- **Nitrogen-based molecules:** Can constitute up to **50% of sap solids** in new
  seedlings, reflecting enormous nitrogen demand for cellular synthesis.

Different phloem tubes carry different loads: smaller veins may transport only
carbohydrates, while larger feeder veins accommodate amino acids, ATP, hormones,
organic acids, and even viruses.

## Messenger RNA in the Phloem

Modern techniques have revealed **messenger RNA (mRNA)** in phloem sap, forming a
sophisticated long-distance signaling system with several roles:

- **Virus defense:** Coordinating systemic responses to viral attacks
- **Stress response:** RNA levels change in response to environmental stress
- **Gene silencing:** Regulating gene expression across the plant body
- **Nutrient allocation:** Influencing where nutrients are directed
- **Developmental regulation:** Coordinating growth between distant organs

The phloem is not merely a sugar-delivery pipe but a **communication network**
integrating information across the entire plant.

## Root Exudates Traveling Through the Phloem

[[root-exudates]] are plant-synthesized compounds released into the rhizosphere to attract
beneficial microbes and facilitate nutrient acquisition. They travel to roots through
the phloem and include lipid-, [[carbohydrate]]-, and amino acid–based molecules.

Key exudate acids—**citric, oxalic, tartaric, malonic, piscidic, acetic, and lactic
acids**—are synthesized from nutrient ions and released in response to specific needs,
particularly for uptake of **phosphorus, zinc, iron, and manganese**. Each acid has
different abilities to dissolve specific mineral compounds.

Hydrophilic carbohydrates and amino acids require transport proteins and energy to
cross the plasmalemma, while small nonpolar lipids can diffuse through membranes.
Larger lipids may require facilitative or active transport.

## Partitioning: How Plants Distribute Resources

**Partitioning** is the process of dividing photosynthate, proteins, and hormones
among competing organs. Sugar itself functions as a **regulatory signal**:

- Leaf photosynthesis produces sugar that flows to roots (the "sink"), stimulating
  new root cell growth and supporting more leaf nutrient supply—a positive feedback.
- **Negative feedback:** If roots don't demand sugar, it accumulates in leaves,
  photosynthesis slows or stops, and root growth ceases.

Practical implication: if a plant loses leaves, roots must adjust to reduced sink
demand—overwatering can be harmful. Flowers and seeds develop only after sufficient
resources exist for their synthesis.

## Seasonal Nitrogen Remobilization

In autumn, plants execute a nutrient recovery program:

1. **Protein breakdown:** Leaf proteins are enzymatically dismantled.
2. **Nitrogen translocation:** Liberated nitrogen moves via phloem to stems and roots
   for winter storage.
3. **Carbohydrate storage:** Carbohydrates are also moved to storage tissues.

This remobilization allows perennials to survive winter and fuel rapid spring growth
without relying solely on soil nutrients.

## Summary

| Feature | Xylem | Phloem |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Direction | Primarily upward (unidirectional) | Bidirectional |
| Primary cargo | Water, mineral ions, hormones | Sugars, proteins, amino acids, mRNA |
| Driving force | Transpiration pull; freeze-thaw (spring) | Pressure flow (source to sink) |
| pH | Typically acidic | Typically alkaline (7.5–8.5) |
| Signaling | Hormone transport, stomatal regulation | mRNA signaling, gene regulation, virus defense |
