# Phytochrome Distributed Leaf Perception vs Localized Tip Phototropism

## Overview

One of the most counterintuitive findings in [[daniel-chamovitz]] in "What a Plant Knows," is that
plants use **fundamentally different anatomical strategies** for two
light-sensing tasks: directional phototropism is detected at the
shoot tip, while photoperiodic measurement is sensed by any leaf
on the plant. This architectural distinction reveals how plants
have evolved specialized sensory systems for different ecological
challenges.

## Tip-Localized Phototropism

The directional [[julius-von-sachs-blue-light-phototropism-discovery]] (1864) further established that **blue light** is
the primary wavelength inducing phototropism, mediated by phototropin
receptors concentrated at the shoot apex.

## Distributed Leaf Photoperiodism

In contrast to the tip-localized phototropism system, the
photoperiodic response (measuring night length to time flowering)
uses a **distributed sensing architecture**. The phytochrome
receptors responsible for photoperiod detection are located
throughout the leaves, not concentrated at any single point.

The experimental evidence for distributed perception:

- Illuminating **any single leaf** in the middle of the night is
  sufficient to regulate flowering across the entire plant
- Pruning all leaves, leaving only stem and apex, renders the
  plant completely insensitive to light flashes
- Even when the entire plant is illuminated, if no leaf

### Phototropism: Rapid Directional Response

Phototropism requires fast, directional information about light
source position. The shoot tip, as the highest point, receives
the least filtered light and can most accurately determine light
direction. A single localized sensor is sufficient because light
direction is essentially uniform across the plant (the sun is far
away). The response — auxin redistribution and differential cell
elongation — needs to be coordinated from the growing tip anyway.

### Photoperiodism: Integrated Seasonal Measurement

Photoperiodism requires measuring the duration of continuous
darkness, which is an integrating function over time. Distributed
leaf sensors provide several advantages:

1. **Redundancy**: If one leaf is shaded by a neighboring plant
   or damaged, other leaves continue monitoring light conditions

2. **Averaging**: Multiple sensors reduce noise from brief light
   fluctuations (clouds, passing animals) that shouldn't trigger
   a flowering response

3. **Canopy integration**: Leaves at different positions in the
   canopy experience different light environments. Distributed
   sensing allows the plant to integrate information from across
   its full light-gathering surface

4. **Historical recording**: Each leaf accumulates phytochrome
   state over its lifetime, providing a record of recent light
   conditions that informs the flowering decision

## The Mobile Signal

Both systems use mobile signals to communicate light information
from the sensor location to the responding tissue:

**Phototropism**: Auxin (indole-3-acetic acid) is redistributed
from the lit side to the shaded side of the stem, causing cells
on the shaded side to elongate more and the stem to bend toward
light. The signal travels a short distance from tip to bending
zone (a few centimeters in seedlings).

**Photoperiodism**: The mobile signal from leaves is a different
compound (identified as FT protein in Arabidopsis) that travels
through the phloem from leaves to the shoot apical meristem, where
it triggers the transition from vegetative to floral development.
This signal travels much greater distances — from leaves that may
be meters from the growing tip in large plants.

## The Red-Far-Red Switch Mechanism

The phytochrome system in leaves operates as a molecular switch
activated by specific wavelengths:

- Red light (~660nm) converts phytochrome from Pr to Pfr (active
  form), signaling "lights on"
- Far-red light (~730nm) converts Pfr back to Pr (inactive form),
  signaling "lights off"
- A few seconds of either wavelength is sufficient for switching
- The plant effectively "remembers" the last color it saw

Ecologically, this makes sense: the last light at dusk is far-red
(deactivating phytochrome), while morning light is red (activating
it). The duration of continuous Pfr-to-Pr conversion (night
length) determines whether short-day or long-day plants flower.
