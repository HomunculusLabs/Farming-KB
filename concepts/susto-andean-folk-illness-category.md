# Susto and Andean Folk Illness Categories

## Overview

Susto is one of the most widespread folk illness categories in Latin America, particularly common throughout Peru. In the context of Peruvian mestizo folk healing, susto belongs to a system of culture-bound syndromes that include daño, pulsario, and mal de ojo.

These illness categories form the primary diagnostic framework used by ayahuasqueros (ayahuasca healers) in urban Peruvian settings like Iquitos and Belén. Understanding these syndromes is essential for understanding how ayahuasca is used therapeutically in mestizo healing contexts.

The following descriptions are drawn from Marlene Dobkin de Rios's chapter "Curing with Ayahuasca in an Urban Slum" in [[urban-ayahuasca-iquitos-healing]].

## Susto (Soul Loss / Fright)

- A common illness found throughout Peru and Latin America

- Profound alteration of metabolism or nervous disorder

- Intense psychic trauma provoked by an emotion of fear

- Symptoms include lack of appetite and lack of energy

- Caused by the loss of the sick person's soul (animistic framework)

- One of the most frequent types of illness treated by Peruvian folk healers

- Believed that a sudden fright can cause the soul to leave the body

Within the mestizo healing system documented by Dobkin de Rios, susto represents a fundamental category of illness rooted in the separation of spirit from body. The healer's task is to locate and recover the wandering soul, often through [[hallucinogens-and-shamanism-harner-anthology]].

## Daño (Sorcery / Harm)

- Caused by envy (envidia) or feelings of vengeance (despecho) toward the victim

- Recognizes the role of interpersonal strife and stress in generating illness

- Can manifest as hemorrhaging, muscular pain, loss of consciousness, suffocation, tumors, or consistent bad luck (saladera)

- Crucial distinction: it is the CAUSE (magical harm), not the physical manifestation, that matters

- Caused by a powerful potion slipped into a drink, or thrown across a doorstep

- A witch (brujo) may be paid to cause magical harm using [ayahuasca

- Some witches and ayahuasqueros control special spirits or familiars

- Belief that a chonta (thorn) carrying disease-producing substances can be shot through the air

- Imperative that a sick person seek a healer to neutralize the harm before it becomes fatal

Daño occupies a central place in the folk etiology of illness among Iquitos mestizos. The concept encodes social tensions and interpersonal conflict into a medical framework, allowing patients to articulate suffering that might otherwise remain inexpressible. ## Pulsario (Anxiety Lump)

- Marked by restlessness, hyperactivity, and free-floating anxiety

- Described as a ball located at the mouth of the stomach

- Painful and prevents normal digestive action

- Attacks mainly women

- Associated with irritability and general unhappiness

- The lump may also represent repressed pain, sorrow, or anger

Pulsario illustrates the somatic idiom of distress common in Andean folk medicine, where emotional and psychological states are experienced and communicated through bodily sensation.

## Mal de Ojo (Evil Eye)

- Disease syndrome found throughout Peru

- Symptoms include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fever, loss of weight, insomnia, and sadness

- Caused by the magical action of one person's glance upon another

- Not necessarily with evil intentions

- Can be motivated by envy

- Very frequent among children whose personal attraction may catch the evil eye

- Mothers place charms or amulets on children's wrists or necks

- Daily exorcism with tobacco smoke to ward off the disaster

Mal de ojo demonstrates the pervasive belief in the power of interpersonal gaze and intentionality as vectors of illness in mestizo Andean culture.

## The Role of the Ayahuasquero

- The healer must determine the origin of the illness before treatment

- Must bring to light the cause without merely focusing on external symptoms

- Both healer and patient work together to analyze the visionary content of the ayahuasca experience

- The sickness is typically ascribed to a specific bewitcher

- Therapeutically effective: transforms generalized, incapacitating anxiety into solid fear placed on an evildoer

- Hallucinations and shamanic guidance reinforce the patient's belief in the healer's power

- In some cases, ayahuasqueros go beyond diagnosis to actively punishing those who caused the harm, believing they can leave their bodies during intoxication to inflict incurable disease upon enemies

- These beliefs parallel those of traditional [European witchcraft

The ayahuasquero serves simultaneously as diagnostician, therapist, and spiritual protector. The ritual use of Banisteriopsis caapi and admixture plants, whose [pharmacology supports visionary experience, is structured around identifying and resolving these folk illness categories.

## Cultural Context

- Slum-dwellers of mixed Indian-white ancestry (mestizos) in Iquitos ascribe illness to supernatural causation when Western medicine fails

- The inclination to employ an ayahuasquero is reinforced by the high cost of formal medical consultation

- Healing sessions always occur at night in forest clearings

- Healers carefully select patients, eliminating those with severe psychotic disorders

- Dose varies by body weight; optimal dose found to be 7 mgm/kg

- Healers give larger doses to persons suffering from psychosomatic illness

Dobkin de Rios observed that the mestizo population of the Belén district turned to ayahuasca healing not out of purely traditional allegiance but as a pragmatic response to the limitations and expense of biomedicine. The folk illness categories of susto, daño, pulsario, and mal de ojo provide a coherent explanatory model that addresses both physical symptoms and their social-emotional roots.
