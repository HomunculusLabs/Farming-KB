# Falconer Mushroom Cellar Management

Falconer devoted his second chapter entirely to cellar-based mushroom
cultivation, declaring that "the cellar is everybody's mushroom house"
and providing detailed guidance for every type of cellar installation.

## Why Cellars

Mushrooms require uniform moderately low temperature and moist
atmosphere, and will not thrive where drafts or sudden fluctuations
of temperature or moisture prevail. Underground cellars were the best
of all structures for this purpose. Many market gardeners who had never
grown a mushroom instinctively recognized that the first step in the
business was a cellar.

## Dedicated Mushroom Cellars

A cellar wholly devoted to mushroom-growing should be made as warm as
possible with double windows and double doors (where the entrance was
from outside). A chimney-like shaft rising from the ceiling served as
the ventilator in winter, since side ventilation during bearing was
precarious. An indoor stairway from a building above was essential, as
was an easy way to bring in fresh materials and remove spent material.

### Interior Arrangement

The simplest layout had beds 3-4 feet wide around the inside walls,
with beds 6 feet wide and pathways 2-2.5 feet wide running parallel
along the middle. Above the floor beds, shelf-beds in tiers of one,
two, or three were formed according to ceiling height, always leaving
2.5-3 feet between the bottom of one bed and the bottom of the next.

### Heating

Fire heat was not absolutely necessary in a snug, warm cellar.
Capital crops could be grown without furnace heat by using a larger
body of material (enough to maintain steady warmth). However, this
was wasteful -- no more mushrooms grew in a two-foot bed than in a
one-foot bed. In unheated cellars, mushrooms grew large and solid but
came less quickly and in smaller numbers. A little artificial warmth
dispelled the cold, raw, damp air peculiar to pent-up cellars and
purified the atmosphere by assisting ventilation.

Where fire heat was used, the furnace itself should be boxed off by a
thin brick wall from the main cellar, with only pipes introduced,
eliminating dust, noxious gas, and parching heat.

### Condensation Drip

One chief trouble with flat-roofed mushroom cellars was the drip from
condensed moisture rising from beds. This was worse in unheated than
heated cellars. Oiled paper or calico strung along above the upper
beds protected them; whatever fell on passageways did no harm.

## Dwelling House Cellars

The cellar of a dwelling house was a capital place for mushroom beds,
usable in whole or part. For families wanting only a few mushrooms,
a portion could be partitioned off with boards. Beds could be made on
the floor (flat, ridged, or banked against the wall), 10-12 inches
deep in warm cellars and 15-20 inches in cool ones, about 3 feet wide.

### Boxing Method

Beds were enclosed in boxing of any available boards, built 6-10
inches higher than bed tops for headroom. The lid could be hung on
hinges or straps, made of half-inch boards. When shut, mushrooms were
secure from cold and vermin; when open, accessible for observation and
harvesting. The manure was prepared away from the house, spread thin
to cool and free from steam for two days, then brought in. Once
well-sweetened, it was cool, nearly odorless, and after a few days
could be spawned and earthed over at once. The loam coating prevented
escape of steam or odor.

Falconer cited Mr. Robinson's observation of Mr. Horace Cox at Harrow,
whose basement boiler room floor was covered with small fat mushroom
beds in active bearing, "without the slightest unpleasant smell."

## Notable Cellar Installations

### Gardner's Cellar (Jobstown, N.J.)
Sixty feet long, twenty-four feet wide, nine feet high. Earthen floor,
perfectly dry. Well supplied with window ventilators and doors, plus a
tall shaft ventilator through the roof center. The bed occupied the
entire cellar floor, made up in one day. A single row of boards served
as pathway, with pieces of slate for stepping while gathering.

### Denton's Cellars (Woodhaven, L.I.)
Two cellars under barns, 7.5 feet high inside, beds 5 feet wide and
9 inches deep, 2 feet apart. Three tiers of beds (floor plus two
shelves), 2.5 feet apart. Temporary structures of rough scantling and
hemlock boards. Heated by common iron stove with sheet iron smoke
pipes, which had a visible parching effect on beds. Cleaned out and
white-washed every summer.

### Van Siclen's Cellars (Jamaica, L.I.)
Similar to Denton's but using hot water heating apparatus and
greenhouse smoke flues rather than sheet iron pipes.

### The Dosoris Mushroom Cellar
Falconer's own installation, a purpose-built subterranean tunnel or
arch, 83 feet long overall (63 feet of actual mushroom cellar), 7
feet high in the middle, 8 feet wide within, with the arch top 2.5
feet below the soil surface. Brick walls and arch, perfectly drained
earthen floor. Originally fitted with three ventilators 16 feet apart
in the arch top -- a mistake, as condensation kept the areas beneath
them cold and wet. A single tall chimney-like shaft would have been
superior.

The Dosoris cellar held 756 square feet of bed surface (252 running
feet), renewed twice yearly for 1,512 square feet total. Average yield
was three-fifths to four-fifths of a pound per square foot. Only two
beds (one section) were made at a time, with a month between each
pair, ensuring steady supply from October through May rather than
flushes and scarcity. Each bed had a bearing period of approximately
two months.

### Heating at Dosoris

A Hitchings base-burner boiler with 134 running feet of 4-inch
hot-water pipe, providing one square foot of heating surface per
15 cubic feet of air space -- more than ample. In the severest winter
weather, the heater burned no more than a common hodful of coal in 24
hours. Temperature varied no more than three degrees (52-55 F), day
or night, mild or severe weather.

## Annual Maintenance

After the mushroom season closed, all manure was lifted out, shelving
boards cleaned, and the cellar thoroughly cleaned. Walls were
white-washed and woodwork painted with kerosene to destroy noxious
insects and fungi. No old compost or soil was ever reused.
