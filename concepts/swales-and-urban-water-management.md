## Swales and Urban Water Management

Swales are among the most critical technologies in [[permaculture]] for water harvesting, soil hydration, and landscape regeneration. [[bill-mollison]] positions them as foundational infrastructure — especially for winter-wet climates — and extends their application into urban design, forest systems, pond construction, and integrated slope management.

## Swale Design and Construction

A swale is a shallow blade trench cut on **true contour** with no water movement along the trench itself. Key design specifications:

- **Width**: At least 4 feet wide at the base
- **Depth**: Minimum 2 feet deep
- **Edges**: Gently shoaled (sloped) rather than vertical
- **Alignment**: Strictly on contour — water must not flow along the trench

The principle is simple: water entering a swale spreads evenly and soaks into the soil profile rather than running off. In clay-rich areas, swales may hold permanent ponds. In sandy soils, swales should be widened to maximize infiltration surface area rather than deepened.

Within **3–4 years**, swales can charge 17–20 feet of soil below and downslope, creating a fully hydrated soil profile that supports robust plant growth even through dry seasons.

## Urban Swale Systems: The Davis, CA Case Study

Mollison describes a village project in Davis, California as a model for urban [[water-management]] through swale integration:

- **No conventional drainage infrastructure**: No gutters, no curbs, no underground pipes
- **Houses positioned between double rows of swales**, capturing all roof and road runoff
- **All stormwater directed into swales** rather than municipal storm drains

### Infiltration Rates Over Time

The Davis project demonstrates a progressive improvement in water absorption as the swale system matures:

| Phase | Absorption Rate | Condition |
|-------|----------------|-----------|
| Initial | 40% | Newly constructed swales |
| Intermediate | 85% | Trees and soil biology establishing |
| Mature | 100% | Full tree canopy and root networks |

This progression illustrates a key [[permaculture-principle-1-observe-interact]]: **systems improve with time as biological components mature**. The trees planted alongside swales develop root networks that break up compacted soil, increase organic matter, and create macropores for water movement. The canopy reduces evaporative losses from the soil surface.

## Forest Swale Benefits

Swales integrated into forest systems provide multiple advantages:

- **Drought resilience**: Forests with swales remain alive and productive even when neighboring groundwater has flowed away during dry periods
- **Mulch production**: Swales serve as collection points for leaf litter and organic material, creating natural composting zones
- **Fire risk reduction**: The hydrated soil profile and lush vegetation along swales act as firebreaks, reducing forest fire vulnerability
- **[[holzer-climate-zone-microclimate-creation]]**: Moisture-retaining swales create cooler, more humid microclimates that benefit understory plants

### Specialized Cultivation

Mollison specifically highlights swales as ideal sites for:
- **[[ginseng-cultivation-and-medicinal-properties]]**: The moist, shaded, well-drained conditions along swale berms suit ginseng"s requirements
- **Blueberry production**: Blueberries thrive in the acidic, moisture-retentive soils that develop in swale systems

## Pond Sealing Techniques

When constructing ponds, ensuring water retention is critical. Mollison outlines several methods:

### Gleying

Gleying involves sealing pond bottoms with **green sappy material** (fresh vegetation, manure, organic matter) placed beneath a layer of soil. As the material decomposes anaerobically, it forms an impermeable biological seal. This is a low-cost, widely applicable technique.

### Bentonite Clay

Bentonite — a highly expansive clay — can be spread across the pond bottom. When wet, it swells to create a tight, waterproof barrier. Best suited where bentonite is locally available.

### Water Dynamite

Small controlled charges ("water dynamite") can seal cracks in rocky or fractured substrates by compacting the material and redistributing it into fissures. This is a specialized technique for difficult sites.

### Repurposing Dry Holes

Not every hole holds water. Mollison pragmatically suggests repurposing failed pond excavations as:
- Root cellars and barns
- Storage spaces
- Sheltered [[mushroom-growing-environments]]

## Biological Dam Structures

Beyond simple earthworks, Mollison describes complex biological dam structures that serve multiple functions:

- **Islands**: Created within ponds for [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]], wind protection, and additional growing area
- **Barrier islands**: Positioned to deflect and moderate wind across water surfaces
- **Constant-level marshes**: Designed to maintain stable water levels, supporting consistent wetland ecosystems
- **Mud-dammed shallows**: Shallow zones created with mud dams for specific plant and animal communities
- **Underwater stone structures**: Placed to provide habitat for aquatic wildlife and promote biological filtration

These structures transform simple water impoundments into rich, multi-functional ecological systems.

## Fencing and Road Integration with Water Systems

Mollison advocates for **aligning fences and roads with water systems** — swales, channels, and drainage lines. This approach:

- Reduces earthworks costs by combining functions
- Creates natural corridors for wildlife movement
- Prevents fencing from interfering with water flow
- Simplifies maintenance by concentrating infrastructure along contour lines

Roads following water systems can serve as access routes while also functioning as diversion drains during extreme rainfall events.

## Slope Advantages for Integrated Design

Slopes offer unique opportunities when water systems are integrated with built structures:

### Ponds as Multi-Function Elements

Ponds on slopes serve roles beyond water storage:
- **Fire barriers**: Open water stops fire spread
- **Light reflectors**: Water surfaces bounce sunlight into adjacent growing areas
- **Heat buffers**: Water moderates temperature extremes, absorbing heat during the day and releasing it at night

### Thermal Collection

Slopes allow strategic placement of:
- **Hot water collectors** — positioned to capture maximum solar exposure with gravity feed to points of use
- **Hot air collectors** — leveraging convective airflow patterns created by slope geometry

### Tank Placement and Structural Integration

Water tanks placed on slopes gain:
- **Gravity-fed distribution** to lower elevations without pumping
- **Structural integration** with buildings — tanks can serve as thermal mass, retaining warmth or coolth
- **Reduced pumping costs** throughout the system's lifetime

## Orchard Species Arrangement on Slopes

Mollison recommends a specific pattern for orchards on slopes:

- **Upper slope**: Narrow-leafed species (conifers, some natives) that reduce wind speed and intercept rainfall without excessive shade
- **Lower slope**: Broad-leafed species (fruit trees, deciduous species) that benefit from the reduced wind and increased moisture availability

This arrangement creates a **progressive filtration system**: wind is slowed and rainfall is gently distributed across the slope before reaching moisture-sensitive broad-leafed crops below.

## Gravity Flow Water Systems

Whenever possible, Mollison designs for **gravity-fed water movement**. This eliminates the need for pumps, reduces energy consumption, and increases system reliability. Swales, tanks, ponds, and [[magic-mushroom-retail-markets-and-commercial-distribution-channels]] are all positioned to exploit elevation differences naturally.

## The Maturity/Immaturity Reversal Principle

A deep design principle running through this material: **nature's tendency for maturity to exploit immaturity can be reversed by design**. In natural succession, mature systems draw resources from immature ones. In [[permaculture-design]], we intentionally position mature, stable elements (established swale forests, sealed ponds, integrated infrastructure) to support and accelerate the development of younger, less established systems. The Davis swale system exemplifies this: the mature water management infrastructure enables the rapid maturation of the biological systems it supports.

## Key Design Principles Summary

1. **Water is the primary design medium** — shape the land to capture, hold, and distribute it
2. **Systems improve over time** — biological maturation increases function
3. **Every element serves multiple functions** — swales store water, grow mulch, reduce fire risk, support crops
