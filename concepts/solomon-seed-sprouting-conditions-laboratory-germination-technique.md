# Seed Sprouting Conditions and Laboratory Germination Technique

## Overview

Steve Solomon devotes significant attention in *Gardening When It Counts* to the science of seed germination, drawing directly from laboratory seed-testing protocols to help home gardeners achieve reliable stand establishment. The core principle is straightforward: to maximize field germination, gardeners should replicate as closely as possible the ideal sprouting conditions that seed laboratories use. Solomon teaches the specific techniques germ labs employ and translates them into practical methods for the home garden.

## Laboratory Germination Protocol

### The Standard Method

Solomon describes how professional seed testing laboratories achieve accurate and reproducible germination results: "Laboratory germination is accurate and duplicatable: two different germ labs testing samples from the same bag of seed should come up with the exact same result."

The laboratory procedure involves these controlled variables:

1. **Sterile media** — Labs use sterile blotting paper or standardized soil mix to eliminate hostile microorganisms that kill seedlings in garden soil
2. **Precise moisture** — Blotting paper is saturated but not waterlogged; excess water is squeezed out before seeds are placed
3. **Controlled temperature** — Each species has an ideal germination temperature, maintained constant or varied on a cycle (e.g., 16 hours warmer, 8 hours cooler, mimicking daytime heating and nighttime cooling)
4. **Consistent duration** — Each species has a maximum allowed germination period; seeds emerging after the cutoff are considered too weak for field conditions

### The Petri Dish Setup

The specific lab equipment Solomon describes:

- A petri dish (shallow, airtight, clear plastic container approximately four inches in diameter)
- A blotting paper disk slightly smaller than the inside of the petri dish
- The blotting paper is saturated, then all surplus water is squeezed out
- Seeds are spaced on the moist paper and the dish is sealed
- The sealed dish maintains constant humidity throughout the test

### The Relationship Between Lab and Field Results

A critical insight from Solomon is that laboratory germination percentages always exceed field results, but the relationship is predictable: "The results of a test almost always show that the batch that germinates the quickest also germinates at the highest percentage."

The key finding is that high lab germination does not guarantee high field emergence. Field losses come from soil crusting, damping-off fungi, temperature fluctuations, moisture inconsistency, and soil insects. Seed vigor — the speed and completeness of germination — is the most important quality indicator.

Solomon's conclusion: "To get the best outdoor germination, start with vigorous seeds and then assist them by creating soil conditions that match as closely as possible the conditions that have been determined to give the best results in a laboratory."

## Chitting (Presprouting)

### Definition and Purpose

Solomon defines chitting clearly: "To chit means either to presprout seeds or to green-up seed potatoes so the shoots start emerging from the eyes." The technique initiates sprouting under nearly ideal conditions, then the sprouted seeds are planted in soil.

### Advantages of Chitting

1. **Eliminates germination uncertainty** — Seeds are observed actually sprouting before being committed to the ground
2. **Prevents wasted garden space** — Non-viable seeds are identified and discarded rather than occupying garden beds
3. **Accelerates harvest** — The sprouting phase happens on the kitchen counter rather than in cold spring soil
4. **Overcomes suboptimal soil conditions** — Especially valuable when planting early, before soil has fully warmed
5. **Confirms seed quality** — If you chit 100 seeds and only 10 sprout weakly, you know the seed lot is poor

### Chitting Beans and Peas

Solomon provides a detailed method for chitting legumes:

1. Soak seeds in tepid water for no more than 6–8 hours (excessive soaking drowns seeds)
2. Place seeds in a jar covered with plastic fly screen or cheesecloth
3. Drain water, rinse with tepid water, drain thoroughly
4. Place jar on its side near the kitchen sink
5. Ambient temperature should exceed 65°F (18°C)
6. Rinse and drain twice daily with tepid water
7. In 3–4 days, roots should emerge
8. Before roots become long and brittle, plant sprouted seeds about 1.5 inches deep with root pointed down

### Chitting Cucurbits (Squash, Melons, Cucumbers)

For heat-loving cucurbits, Solomon uses a different approach:

- Squash sprouts at 65°F (18°C) but prefers 75°F+ (24°C+)
- Melons and cucumbers need at least 70°F (21°C) and do best above 75°F
- Use a germination box or sealed plastic bag with a warm heat source
- Temperature must not exceed about 90°F (32°C) or seeds may die
- Check twice daily once germination begins
- Plant before roots exceed the length of the seed

## Watering and Sprouting

Solomon challenges a common gardening belief: overwatering sprouting seeds causes more problems than underwatering.

### Problems With Overwatering

1. **Temperature reduction** — Water is cold relative to soil; each watering drops soil temperature, slowing germination
2. **Damping-off promotion** — Cool, wet soil favors the fungus that causes damping-off, which "invades and kills the seedling before and after emergence"
3. **Oxygen deprivation** — Waterlogged soil deprives seeds of oxygen needed for respiration during germination

### Damping-Off Risk

Damping-off is a fungal disease that attacks seedlings at the soil line, causing stems to collapse. "This disease doesn't thrive in dryish soil." The same applies to powdery mildew on cucurbit seedlings in cool, damp conditions.

## Soil Conditions for Optimal Germination

### The Furrow Technique

For small seeds sown close to the surface, Solomon recommends pressing a hand-shaped depression into the bed about 0.5–1 inch deep, sowing seeds in the depression, and covering with humus-rich surface soil. Fine compost can be used instead of garden soil — it holds moisture better than clay and rarely forms a crust.

## Seed Vigor and Quality Assessment

### The Sprouting Test at Home

Solomon teaches that gardeners can perform their own germination tests using the petri dish method. This is especially valuable for:

- Old seed from previous seasons
- Seed from unknown sources
- Expensive or rare seed varieties
- Verifying mail-order seed quality before planting

### Interpreting Results

The speed and percentage of germination reveal seed quality:

- **Fast, uniform germination (80%+ in the expected timeframe)** — High vigor; suitable for direct sowing
- **Slow, uneven germination** — Reduced vigor; consider replacing or using [[solomon-seed-quality-and-germination-testing|chitting]] to compensate
- **Very low germination (<50%)** — Seed is likely too old or poorly stored; discard and obtain fresh seed

## Species-Specific Temperature Requirements

Different vegetable species have distinct optimal germination temperatures:

| Crop | Minimum Temp | Optimal Range | Notes |
|------|-------------|---------------|-------|
| Lettuce | 35°F (2°C) | 60–75°F | Cool-tolerant |
| Peas | 40°F (4°C) | 60–65°F | Very cold-hardy |
| Beans | 60°F (16°C) | 70–85°F | Cold-sensitive |
| Corn | 50°F (10°C) | 75–85°F | Slow below 60°F |
| Tomatoes | 50°F (10°C) | 70–85°F | Needs warmth |
| Cucumbers | 60°F (16°C) | 75–90°F | Heat-loving |
| Melons | 65°F (18°C) | 75–90°F | Very heat-demanding |
