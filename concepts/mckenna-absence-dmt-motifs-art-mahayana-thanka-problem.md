# Absence of DMT Motifs in Art History and the Mahayana Thanka Problem

## Overview
One of the most puzzling observations Terence McKenna raises in his 1983 Esalen
lecture is the total absence of DMT visual motifs in the entire history of human
art. McKenna had traveled to Konarak and Bubaneshwar, studied Hindu iconography,
and collected Mahayana Buddhist thankas. He found significant parallels between
his LSD experiences and the iconography of Mahayana Buddhism—in fact, it was LSD
experiences that drove him to collect Mahayana art. But when he searched for
corresponding representations of the [[mckenna-dmt-experience-and-hyperspace]] in any artistic tradition,
he found nothing. This absence is striking given the intensity and consistency
of the DMT experience and raises profound questions about the relationship
between visionary experience and cultural representation.

## The LSD-Art Correspondence

### Mahayana Buddhist Thankas
McKenna reports that his LSD experiences bore significant resemblance to the
visual iconography of Mahayana Buddhism. The mandalas, deity figures, celestial
palaces, and complex geometric patterns found in Tibetan thankas (painted
scrolls) correspond to visual experiences commonly reported under the influence
of LSD. This correspondence is not superficial; McKenna found it compelling
enough to actively seek out and collect Mahayana art, suggesting that the
visual language of Tibetan Buddhism encodes experiences accessible through
psychedelic states.

### Motivations for Art Collection
The fact that LSD experiences "drove" McKenna to collect Mahayana art suggests
a recognition effect: the art served as a cultural confirmation of experiences
that were otherwise difficult to articulate or validate within Western
frameworks. The thankas provided a visual vocabulary for experiences that McKenna
had encountered in the psychedelic state, creating a bridge between personal
experience and cultural tradition.

## The DMT-Art Disconnect

### Total Absence
"What amazed me was the total absence of the motifs of DMT. It is not there; it
is not there in any tradition familiar to me." This statement is remarkable for
its absoluteness. McKenna is not reporting a scarcity or a difficulty in finding
DMT motifs—he is reporting their complete absence from all known artistic
traditions. Given the vividness, intensity, and consistency of DMT experiences
across cultures and individuals, this absence demands explanation.

### The Contrast with LSD
The contrast between LSD and DMT in this regard is particularly telling. LSD,
a synthetic compound first synthesized in 1938, produced experiences that found
correspondences in artistic traditions dating back centuries or millennia. DMT,
an endogenous compound present in the human brain and used in shamanic
traditions for millennia, produced experiences that found no such artistic
echoes. This inversion—synthetic compound matching ancient art, natural compound
matching nothing—is paradoxical.

## Possible Explanations

### The Epistemic Black Hole
McKenna's own concept of the "black [[dmt-black-hole-effect-and-ineffability-of-tryptamine-experience]]" surrounding DMT offers one
explanation. DMT experiences are so alien, so far beyond the categories of
ordinary experience, that they resist representation in any artistic medium.
The "[[mckenna-cognitive-dissonance-translinguistic-object]]" produced by the encounter with translinguistic
objects means that the mind cannot successfully translate the experience into
visual or verbal form. Art requires some degree of cognitive mastery over its
subject matter; the DMT experience may simply exceed this capacity.

### The Secrecy Hypothesis
McKenna connects the DMT experience to what he calls "the Sect of the Phoenix,"
drawing on Jorge Luis Borges' short story of that name. In Borges' tale, there
is a secret that "is transmitted from generation to generation," that "is
sacred, but is always somewhat ridiculous," and whose performance is "furtive
and even clandestine." The adepts "do not speak of it," and "there are no
decent words to name it." If the DMT experience represents such a secret—known
across cultures but never openly depicted—this would explain its absence from
art history.

### Cultural Suppression
The destruction of indigenous shamanic traditions by colonial powers, Christian
missionaries, and modern states may have eliminated the artistic traditions
that once encoded DMT experiences. McKenna notes that the mushroom cults of
Mexico were driven into hiding by the Spanish Inquisition and survived only in
a few remote villages. Similar suppression may have occurred wherever DMT-
using traditions existed, destroying both the practices and their artistic
expressions.
