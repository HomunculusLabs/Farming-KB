# Winter Gardening and Season Extension

Updated: 2026-04-18

Winter gardening ranges from difficult to impossible depending on climate, but in many regions it can significantly extend the fresh food supply. Steve Solomon and the permaculture design course both provide practical strategies for growing food through the cold months.

## Climate Zones for Winter Gardening

Solomon defines four climate categories relevant to winter growing:

### Maritime Climates

Regions like coastal British Columbia, southern coastal Victoria, Tasmania, and the South Island of New Zealand:
- Soil rarely freezes solid in winter except at higher elevations.
- Winter gardening ranges from difficult to easy and productive.
- Cool summers mean certain crops are suited to winter gardening without protection.
- West of the Cascades is particularly suited to winter gardening -- many varieties offered by Territorial Seed Company are bred specifically for this.

### Warm Climates

Southern US states, eastern Australia north from Sydney:
- Soil never freezes solid; summers are long and hot.
- Winter gardening is productive without requiring protection.
- Brief winters may occasionally be frosty but rarely severe.

### Moderate Climates

Middle American states, east coast of Australia south of Sydney:
- Soil freezes solid at least 12 inches (30 cm) deep.
- Winter gardening requires protection (cold frames, row covers, greenhouses).
- Growing season is more than 120 days frost-free.

### Short-Season Climates

Northern US and Canada:
- Severe winters with deeply frozen soil.
- Winter gardening requires substantial structures (insulated cold frames, heated greenhouses).
- Focus shifts to stored crops rather than fresh harvesting.

## Crops for Winter Harvest

### Cold-Hardy Vegetables

- **Chicories** (endive, escarole, radicchio): remarkably cold-hardy salad greens. In cold climates, they can survive as long as temperatures don't stay below 10 degrees F (-12 degrees C) when the soil is not frozen. In freezing-soil climates, dig up heads with their carrot-like roots before the ground freezes and transplant them into beds or tubs in a root cellar.
- **Kale and Brussels sprouts**: flavor improves after frost. Brussels sprouts shrug off nighttime lows under 10 degrees F.
- **Winter radishes** (Black Spanish, Daikon): form large roots that cellar well.
- **Turnips and rutabagas**: excellent for winter. Rutabagas can stand in the garden through winter, remaining in good eating condition.
- **Carrots and parsnips**: can overwinter in the earth in mild climates. Where soil freezes, hilling soil over the crowns provides protection. Roots may be root-cellared in barrels of moist sand.
- **Leeks**: can withstand frosts and continue growing slowly.
- **Spinach**: when grown for autumn/winter harvest, is a low-demand crop because there is no urgency to push maximum growth.

### Overwintering Cover Crops

Cover crops planted in autumn protect soil through winter and provide green manure in spring:
- Winter rye
- Crimson clover
- Field peas
- Fava beans
- Hairy vetch

These prevent erosion, suppress weeds, fix nitrogen, and add organic matter when turned under in spring.

## Season Extension Methods

### Spun-Fabric Row Covers (Reemay)

- Provides a few degrees of frost protection.
- Enhances springtime and autumn growth by moderating temperatures.
- Provides protection against flying insect pests without needing to spray.
- Available in widths of 5-6 feet (150-180 cm).
- The entire perimeter must be carefully anchored with soil or stakes to keep insects out.
- As plants grow, they lift the almost weightless fabric so no supporting structure is needed.
- One downside: reduces light levels by about 25%, which is not desirable at summer's end when light is already diminishing.

### Cold Frames and Hot Beds

- Simple structures with a transparent lid over a sunken or raised bed.
- Cold frames capture solar heat and protect from wind and frost.
- Hot beds use decomposing manure beneath the growing bed to provide bottom heat.
- Eliot Coleman's work in Maine demonstrates that with proper cold frame management, winter salad production is possible even in severe climates.

### Greenhouses

- Provide the most control over growing conditions.
- Unheated greenhouses can extend the season significantly.
- Heated greenhouses allow year-round production in any climate.
- Solomon cautions that greenhouse-grown seedlings are always lush and succulent, making them vulnerable to transplant shock and pests.

## Pests and Diseases in Winter

- Overwintering pests survive in soil, plant debris, and protected locations.
- Sanitation is important: remove crop residues and destroy pest habitat.
- Some pests (like certain aphid species) overwinter as eggs on plant material.
- Fungal diseases can be more problematic in cool, damp conditions.
- Good air circulation and proper spacing reduce disease pressure in winter crops.

## The Economics of Winter Gardening

Solomon's perspective is practical:

- Winter gardening requires investment in infrastructure (row covers, cold frames, etc.).
- The question is whether the fresh food produced justifies the cost.
- In maritime climates, the answer is almost always yes -- the investment is minimal and the returns are substantial.
- In colder climates, root cellaring stored crops may be more efficient than trying to grow fresh food through deep winter.
- A combination approach works best: extend the autumn harvest as late as possible, store root crops for winter, and start early spring crops under protection.
