# Solomon's Complete Organic Fertilizer — Soil Science

The Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) is [[steve-solomon]]'s signature contribution
to home gardening. Rather than a simple recipe, COF embodies a philosophy of
soil nutrition based on providing all [[teaming-with-nutrients-essential-plant-nutrients]] in balanced,
slow-release organic forms. Understanding the soil science behind COF helps
gardeners adapt the formula to their specific conditions.

## The Problem with Incomplete Fertilization

Solomon identifies a critical flaw in both [[jadam-conventional-and-organic-pesticide-integration]] gardening:
most fertilizers address only NPK (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) while
ignoring the full spectrum of plant nutrition. This creates vegetables that
look good but are nutritionally deficient. His research suggests that modern
vegetables contain significantly lower concentrations of vitamins, minerals,
and enzymes compared to those grown decades ago on more mineral-rich soils.

[[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]] are designed for maximum bulk yield per acre, triggering
plants to produce more calories (starch and fiber) but not more protein,
vitamins, enzymes, or trace minerals. The consumer gets larger vegetables
with less nutritional value per unit of weight.

## The COF Philosophy

COF addresses this by providing all known essential plant nutrients in forms
that release slowly through microbial activity. The formula combines:

- **Nitrogen source**: Seedmeal (byproduct of oilseed pressing)
- **Phosphorus source**: Rock phosphate, bonemeal, or guano
- **Potassium source**: Kelpmeal or basalt dust
- **Calcium source**: Agricultural lime or gypsum
- **Magnesium source**: Dolomite lime
- **Trace minerals**: Kelpmeal (broad spectrum) or azomite

## Ingredient Science

### Seedmeal (Nitrogen Base)

Seedmeals are what remains after oil is pressed from seeds — linseed, soybean,
cottonseed, canola, sunflower, copra (coconut), and safflower are common.
They typically analyze around 6-4-2 NPK, though coprameal is about one-third
weaker.

Key considerations:
- Higher protein seedmeals contain more nitrogen
- Seedmeals also provide calcium, magnesium, and trace nutrients not
  reflected in their NPK label
- Protein content varies between seed types and between purchases
- Store seedmeal dry with a tight lid to prevent mold

### Rock Phosphate and Bonemeal (Phosphorus)

Two types of rock phosphate exist: "hard" rock phosphate (very slow release,
essentially geological time) and "soft" rock phosphate (moderately available).
Bonemeal provides more immediately available phosphorus but at higher cost.

Solomon notes that phosphorus is often oversupplied in gardens. A soil test
can determine whether additional phosphorus is needed. In many gardens,
phosphorus accumulates from repeated applications and eventually reaches
excessive levels that can inhibit micronutrient uptake.

### Kelpmeal (Trace Minerals and Potassium)

Kelpmeal is Solomon's preferred trace mineral source. Sea kelp absorbs the
full spectrum of minerals dissolved in ocean water, making kelpmeal a broad-
spectrum micronutrient supplement. It also provides potassium, growth hormones
(cytokinins), and compounds that may enhance plant stress resistance.

Basalt dust is an alternative that provides potassium and trace minerals from
volcanic rock. It is cheaper than kelpmeal but lacks the organic growth
regulators.

### Lime Types and pH Management

Three lime types are available, each with different mineral profiles:

1. **Agricultural lime**: [[peat-calcium-carbonate-perlite-casing-psilocybe-cubensis-miniculture]]. Raises pH, provides calcium.
2. **Dolomitic lime**: Calcium + magnesium carbonate. Use when soil needs
   both [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]].
3. **Gypsum**: Calcium sulfate. Provides calcium and sulfur without
   affecting pH. Essential for sodic soils.

Solomon emphasizes that lime selection should be based on soil test results,
not habit. Using dolomite year after year when your soil already has adequate
magnesium will cause imbalances. He also debunks the common myth that all
gardens need lime — only those with acidic soil benefit from liming.

## The Formula (By Volume)

The COF recipe is measured by volume, not weight:

- 4 parts seedmeal (any kind except coprameal, or 3 parts regular seedmeal
  + 1 part tankage for higher nitrogen)
- 1/2 part agricultural lime (best finely ground)
- 1/4 part gypsum (if not using gypsum, double the lime)
- 1/2 part dolomite lime
- 1 part rock phosphate, bonemeal, or high-phosphate guano
- 1 part kelpmeal or 1 part basalt dust

Blend uniformly and store in a sealed container.

## Application Rates

Solomon recommends applying COF at approximately 4-6 quarts per 100 square
feet of garden bed, worked into the top 2-3 inches of soil before planting.
For demanding crops like tomatoes, corn, and brassicas, a side-dressing of
additional COF can be applied mid-season.

## Why Not by Weight?

The COF ingredients have very different bulk densities. Measuring by weight
would produce an entirely different (and incorrect) ratio. Volume measurement
with scoops, buckets, or jars is the intended method, and Solomon notes that
small proportional errors are inconsequential — the formula is forgiving.

## Economics and Sourcing

COF ingredients are available from feed stores, garden centers, and online
suppliers. Buying in bulk significantly reduces cost. Solomon calculates that
the per-season cost of COF is modest compared to the improvement in vegetable
quality and nutritional value it provides. He argues that spending money on
quality soil inputs saves far more in health costs than it costs.
