# Psilocybe aztecorum Section Aztecorum: Alpine Neurotropic Fungi

Section Aztecorum of the genus Psilocybe represents a distinctive
biogeographic group of neurotropic fungi adapted to high-elevation,
subalpine, and alpine habitats. According to [[guzmán,-allen-&-gartz-(1998)]], this section includes species found at altitudes ranging from
2,000 to 4,000 meters above sea level, primarily in Mexico and extending
northward into the Pacific Northwest of North America and as far east as
Quebec, Canada. The alpine adaptation of these species distinguishes them
from the majority of neurotropic fungi, which are typically associated with
lowland tropical and subtropical forests.

## Species Composition

The Section Aztecorum, as documented in the Guzmán, Allen & Gartz survey,
includes the following principal species:

- **Psilocybe aztecorum** Heim: The type species of the section, occurring
  in two varieties: var. aztecorum and var. bonetii
- **Psilocybe baeocystis** Singer & Smith: The namesake of Section
  Baeocystis, closely related and sometimes included within a broader
  Aztecorum concept
- **Psilocybe quebecensis** Ola'h & Bigelow: The easternmost member of
  this group, found in Quebec, Canada

## Psilocybe aztecorum in Mexico

P. aztecorum var. aztecorum is one of the most culturally significant
neurotropic fungi, with an extensive publication history. The Guzmán, Allen &
Gartz survey documents references spanning from Wasson's original 1957
account through dozens of subsequent studies:

The species was first encountered by Wasson in 1957, with formal
documentation by Heim (1957a, 1957c), Heim & Hofmann (1958), and Heim &
Wasson (1958). Singer & Smith (1958) and Singer (1958, 1959, 1978) provided
early taxonomic treatments. Guzmán's own extensive work on the species
includes studies from 1959 through 1997.

P. aztecorum var. aztecorum is found at high elevations in central Mexico,
particularly on the slopes of Popocatépetl and other volcanic peaks near
Mexico City. The specific epithet "aztecorum" honors the Aztec civilization,
reflecting the deep ethnomycological significance of this species. The
species grows in association with Pinus (pine) forests at elevations
typically above 3,000 meters, where the cool, moist microclimate of the
subalpine zone provides suitable conditions.

P. aztecorum var. bonetii was described by Ott & Guzmán (1976) and
subsequently documented by Guzmán (1977a, 1983, 1995, 1997), Chilton
(1978), and Singer (1978). This variety represents a morphological variant
distinguished primarily by differences in pileus features and spore
morphology.

## Psilocybe baeocystis and the Pacific Northwest

P. baeocystis is found primarily in the Pacific Northwest of the United
States, particularly in Washington and Oregon states. The Guzmán, Allen &
Gartz survey documents an extensive publication history for this species:

First described by Singer & Smith (1958), it was subsequently studied by
Leung et al. (1965), Repke et al. (1977a), Chilton (1978), Stamets (1978,
1996), Beug & Bigwood (1981, 1982), and Guzmán (1983). Gartz (1996) also
confirmed its neurotropic properties. The species is associated with conifer
debris, woody substrates, and sometimes enriched soils in the Pacific
Northwest coniferous forest zone.

The biogeographic connection between P. baeocystis of the Pacific Northwest
and P. aztecorum of the Mexican highlands suggests a shared evolutionary
history along the western mountain ranges of North America, with alpine
habitats serving as corridors for distribution between tropical and
temperate zones. This pattern parallels the Mexico as a Northern and Southern Mycobiotic Convergence Zone concept.

## Psilocybe quebecensis: Eastern North American Alpine Fungi

P. quebecensis, documented by Ola'h & Bigelow, represents the most
eastern extension of the Section Aztecorum group, found in Quebec, Canada.
Its presence in eastern North America, far from both the Mexican highlands
and the Pacific Northwest, raises interesting biogeographic questions about
the historical distribution of alpine-adapted neurotropic fungi.

The Quebec population may represent:

1. A relict population from a formerly more widespread distribution during
   cooler Pleistocene climatic periods
2. A more recent long-distance dispersal event from western North American
   populations
3. Independent adaptation to high-elevation habitats from a common ancestor
   shared with the western species

## Alpine Habitat as a Distribution Corridor

The Section Aztecorum species collectively demonstrate that alpine and
subalpine habitats serve as important distribution corridors for neurotropic
fungi. While most Psilocybe species are found in tropical and
subtropical lowland forests, the alpine species occupy a distinct ecological
niche that allows them to extend into higher latitudes and different climate
zones.

This pattern is significant for understanding the broader biogeographic
framework proposed by Southern Hemisphere Origin Hypothesis for Neurotropic Fungi:

- The majority of Psilocybe species occur in tropical and subtropical
  regions, supporting a Gondwanan origin
- Alpine species like P. aztecorum represent secondary adaptations to
  cooler climates, made possible by the availability of high-elevation
  habitats
- The mountain ranges of western North America (Sierra Madre, Rocky
  Mountains, Cascades) provide a continuous chain of alpine habitats that
  could facilitate northward dispersal from tropical Mexico
- Similar alpine corridors may have operated in South America (Andes) and
  Asia (Himalayas), explaining the presence of neurotropic fungi at high
  elevations in those regions

## Comparison with Other Alpine Neurotropic Fungi

Beyond Section Aztecorum, several other neurotropic fungi are adapted to
high-elevation habitats:

- **Gymnopilus spectabilis**: Found across a wide range of elevations from
  sea level to mountain habitats in North America, Europe, and Asia
- **Gymnopilus purpuratus**: Documented in Chilean and German mountain
  regions
- **Amanita muscaria**: Extremely wide elevation range, from sea level to
  subalpine, found on every continent except Antarctica

However, the Section Aztecorum species are unique in their restriction to
alpine and subalpine zones, making them the most specialized high-elevation
neurotropic fungi.
