# Three Don Quixotes: The International Conferences

Fukuoka attended two international conferences during his
1985 American tour. These gatherings brought together
leading voices in alternative agriculture and revealed both
the promise and the limitations of the movement to transform
farming. See also [[fukuoka-european-travels-natural-farming]] and [[fukuoka-european-civilization-standstill]].

## The First Conference: Olympic College

The first conference took place at Olympic College in
Washington, on a campus thickly forested with large trees.
About 600 participants attended. The opening remarks were
given by a Native American professor wearing a feather
headdress and beautiful ceremonial costume, who asked what
human knowledge is while making reference to old American
Indian legends.

Three main presentations were given:

1. **Bill Morrison** advocated "permaculture" -- creating
   farms designed in the form of gardens using other plants,
   allowing farming to be continued permanently on the same
   land in contrast with modern agriculture that exploits
   the soil. He seemed to be raising quite a following of
   organic farming advocates throughout Australia.

2. **Wes Jackson** of the University of California promoted
   energy-saving agricultural methods. His main thrust was
   that unless farming methods which reduce the consumption
   of energy to an absolute minimum are widely adopted,
   farming has no future. He seemed to be searching for new
   methods while remaining basically supportive of science.

3. **masanobu-fukuoka|Fukuoka** was introduced as an advocate of natural
   farming that rejects the tenets of modern science,
   founded on the philosophy of "nothingness."

## The Donkey Sketch

At the end of a panel discussion the following day, Fukuoka
drew a picture of Don Quixote's donkey. Riding on the back
of the donkey was Morrison, who he depicted as blind. A
deaf Jackson was riding backwards, while Fukuoka was hanging
onto the donkey's tail, swinging back and forth.

He asked the audience: "Here are three Don Quixotes trying
to stop that donkey from plunging wildly over the cliff by
returning to nature, but their efforts are all in vain. What
would you do?"

Then he drew President Reagan standing on the donkey's neck
and dangling a carrot in front of its nose. "What is this
carrot?" he asked. One person replied: "Money." The host
laughed and said that this would be a shame if it were to
sum up the results of the day's meeting.

## The Second Conference: UC Santa Cruz

A week later, the second conference was held at the
agricultural department of the University of California at
Santa Cruz. The campus was built on a very large site that
had once been a ranch, with a broad meadow stretching as far
as one can see. The university buildings were located in a
large virgin forest of hemlock and redwoods more than a
hundred feet high, preserved in their original state.

The hall was packed with 800 people -- half backers of the
"nature movement" gathered from many countries, and half
local people connected with the school. Fukuoka was told to
expect both supporters and opponents of scientific
agriculture in the audience.

## Fukuoka's Philosophical Presentation

Fukuoka began by talking about what had led him as a young
man to become a proponent of [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-impact]]. He used
philosophical cartoons drawn on sheets of transparent paper
with a felt-tip marker, projected onto a large screen. He
also used slides and visual aids to explain the methods of
natural farming and where it now stands.

At the end, because the conference host had asked him to
prepare a closing message, he had a statement summarizing
his conclusions and expressing his hopes translated into
English and read to the audience. When the interpreter began
reading, the entire hall suddenly grew hushed.

## The Response

The first to respond was a man from India, who said that
Fukuoka's ideas were exactly like those of Gandhi. Adding
that ancient Indian texts mentioned that no-till farming
existed there long ago, he expressed support.

The next person was a very influential professor of religion
and philosophy at UC. They had disagreed the day before
over Western philosophy. The professor maintained that
Socrates was mistaken. Fukuoka had said that although he
could reject everything about the Western philosophers from
Descartes on down, he was unable to find the slightest thing
wrong with anything Socrates had said.

The professor stood up, turned his back to the podium, and
declared: "In Western philosophy, Descartes, Locke, Kant,
Hegel, and others have explained the process by which the
foundations of modern science have been built up. Fukuoka
rejects the tenets of Western philosophy and has even
succeeded in demonstrating the validity of his stance. This
is an astounding feat and modern scientific farming has no
choice but to accept that its foundations have been
overthrown. I welcome Fukuoka's natural philosophy and
farming as the foundation for a new age."

His speech was met by a thunderous ovation. Fukuoka felt
at last that he had been right to come to America a second
time. A load had been lifted from his shoulders. See also [[fukuoka-food-civilization-critique]] and [[fukuoka-one-straw-revolution-impact]].
