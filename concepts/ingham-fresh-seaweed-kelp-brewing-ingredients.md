# Fresh Seaweed and Kelp as AACT Brewing Ingredients

## Overview

Seaweed and kelp products are among the most versatile and beneficial ingredients
in [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] compost tea (AACT) brewing. Dr. Elaine Ingham's field guide
emphasizes that both fresh seaweed and processed kelp extracts serve critical
roles as microbial foods, mineral sources, and biostimulants in compost tea
recipes.

## Types of Seaweed Products in AACT

### Fresh Seaweed

Fresh seaweed collected from coastal areas represents the most complete form of
marine-derived compost tea ingredient. It contains living marine microorganisms,
alginates, and [[gibberellins-plant-growth-hormones]] that may be degraded during commercial processing:

- **Rinsing**: Fresh seaweed should be rinsed to remove excess salt. While some
  sodium is tolerated, excessive salt inhibits beneficial bacteria and fungi.
- **Chopping**: Should be chopped or blended to increase surface area and
  accelerate release of soluble compounds.
- **Quantity**: Modest amounts relative to other ingredients. Excess fresh
  material can create anaerobic conditions if not properly aerated.
- **Timing**: Can be added at the beginning of the brew cycle alongside compost
  inoculant and other food sources.

### Processed Kelp Products

Kelp meal and liquid kelp extracts offer convenience and consistency:

- **Kelp Meal**: Dried, ground kelp provides slow-release minerals, cytokinins,
  auxins, and amino acids. Typical rate: 0.25-1 cup per 5 gallons of tea.
- **Liquid Kelp**: Soluble extracts are immediately available to microorganisms,
  useful in shorter brew cycles where rapid microbial multiplication is desired.
- **Commercial products**: Some contain preservatives that may affect microbial
  activity. Ingham recommends checking labels for additives.

## Mineral and Trace Element Contributions

Marine algae concentrate minerals from seawater, providing a broad spectrum of
elements often deficient in terrestrial soils:

- **Major elements**: Nitrogen (protein forms), phosphorus, potassium, calcium,
  and magnesium in bioavailable forms.
- **Micronutrients**: Iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum, and
  cobalt in chelated organic forms that remain available to plants.
- **Rare elements**: Over 60 minerals [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]] serving as cofactors
  for enzymatic processes in soil microorganisms.

These minerals, delivered via compost tea, are already in biologically processed
forms. Unlike synthetic supplements, seaweed-derived minerals are chelated by
microbial metabolites, making them immediately available while avoiding leaching
and lockup problems.

## Plant Growth Regulators

Seaweed contains several classes of plant growth regulators that survive brewing:

- **Cytokinins**: Promote cell division and delay [[leaf-senescence-autumn-colour-change-active-plant-process]]. Applied as
  foliar sprays via compost tea, they extend productive leaf life.
- **Auxins**: Influence root development and enhance root systems when tea is
  applied as a soil drench.
- **Betaines**: Osmoprotectant compounds helping plants manage salt stress and
  drought. Also serve as food for certain beneficial bacteria.

## Fungal vs. Bacterial Food Considerations

### For Bacterial-Dominant Teas

Kelp products work well because soluble components are readily metabolized by
bacteria. Combined with molasses, kelp provides micronutrients and growth
factors allowing bacterial populations to reach optimal densities.

### For Fungal-Dominant Teas

Kelp serves more as a mineral supplement than primary food. Fungi derive energy
from complex carbon sources like humic acids and [[comparison-fish-hydrolysate-vs-fish-emulsion]]. Ingham
recommends including kelp at reduced rates in fungal teas to avoid stimulating
excessive bacterial growth that might outcompete target [[biodiversity-fungi-soil-fungal-communities]].
