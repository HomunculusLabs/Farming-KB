# Permaculture Sewage and Marsh Treatment

**Updated:** 2026-04-18
[[mollison-lawn-reform-and-alternatives-in-permaculture-design]], sewage is treated through biological marsh systems rather than mechanical treatment plants. Sewage flows through a sequence of holding ponds, marsh plants, and finally into productive ponds, creating an industrial resource base rather than a waste problem.

## Source
Bill [[mollison-permaculture-design-course-curriculum]] Course, Pamphwater-in-permacultureaculture)

## Core Principle

Sewage should be turned out into a marsh, NOT a pond. The marsh is planted with high-demand plants (mints, bog plants) that have phenomenal [[mycorrhizal-nutrient-uptake]] capacity. After passing through the marsh, the water contains no solids but still holds dissolved nutrients (mainly phosphates and nitrates) that can fertilize downstream systems.

## System Design

### Stage 1: Holding Pond
- Raw sewage enters a holding pond first
- Necessary because in winter, marsh plants are dormant and cannot purify water
- The holding pond stores sewage during dormant months
- Can be designed as a simple earth-embankment pond

### Stage 2: Treatment Marsh
- Sewage seeps through a planted marsh from the holding pond
- Marsh plants absorb nutrients and filter solids
- Key plants: [mints (especially black peppermint), rushes, bog plants
- Swiss research identified rush as the most efficient water cleanser
- The marsh should be sized to handle peak flow from the community

### Stage 3: Productive Pond
- After marsh treatment, water enters a pond system
- At this point, no solids remain but dissolved nutrients (phosphates, nitrates) persist
- This nutrient-rich water grows duckweed, algae, and supports fish
- Can be used for [aquaculture

## Case Study: Town of 8,000 People

Mollison designed a sewage disposal system for a town of 8,000 people that:
- Saved $30,000 per year in engineering costs
- Saved equivalent amount annually in fuel costs
- Turned sewage into an industrial base for the town
- Created an employment system through mint and bamboo processing
- Originally designed on 50 acres
- The town subsequently purchased 1,500 additional acres for expansion
- Successfully attracted vast numbers of black swans and teal

## Comparison: Marsh vs Mechanical Treatment

Mollison contrasted his marsh design with Canberra's $700,000 mechanical sewage plant:
- The mechanical plant produces poisonous water as output
- The marsh system produces productive ecosystems as output
- The marsh system generates income (mint oil, bamboo, waterfowl)
- The mechanical system requires ongoing energy inputs
- The marsh system requires only earthworks and planting

## Mint Marsh System

Black peppermint is an excellent sewage treatment plant:
- Mint has phenomenal sewage/nutrient demand
- Steam distillation produces menthol (valuable product)
- Good to grow mint if you are dairying and have dairy outwash
- From dairy outwash you get powerful blooms of mint
- Mint exhausts ponds after a few years; in [[permaculture-designers-manual-cold-climates]] it has a rest period for renewal
- Control mint's lateral invasiveness with dense bushes on either side

## Bamboo in Sewage Systems

- A good place for bamboo is back from treatment ponds
- Bamboo looks great around pond edges
- Bamboo can be harvested for structural material
- Combined with mint, creates a productive treatment landscape

## Siting Considerations

- Need a sloping site for gravity flow between stages
- A bulldozer and sloping site are the primary requirements
- Systems can be scaled from single household to town of 8,000+
- In cold climates, the holding pond must be sized for winter storage when marsh plants are dormant
