# Mycorrhizal Biodiversity, Function, and Drought Stress Response

## Overview

The diversity of ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungal species associated with individual trees and forest stands is not merely a consequence of random colonization but reflects functional differentiation that enhances ecosystem resilience. Different ECM species vary in their nutrient acquisition strategies, enzyme production capabilities, and responses to environmental stressors. Beyond nutrient acquisition, mycorrhizal associations play a critical role in plant-water relations and drought tolerance, which may be as important as their nutritional benefits for explaining the ubiquity and persistence of mycorrhizal symbioses.

## Species-Level Functional Differences

### Evidence from Field Studies

While most studies comparing ECM species effects are conducted under controlled conditions, field evidence supports significant functional differentiation:

**Phosphorus content in willow** (*[[salix-viminalis]]*): Jones et al. (1990) showed that soil type influenced ECM performance, but *[[laccaria-proxima]]* consistently induced higher tissue phosphorus content than *[[thelephora-terrestris]]*.

**Phosphorus influx in birch**: Dighton et al. (1990) injected radioactive inorganic phosphorus (³²P) into soil zones dominated by different ECM species under field-grown birch trees. Despite complexities of isotopic dilution and non-uniform canopy translocation, they found higher phosphorus influx into leaves in zones dominated by *Hebeloma* spp. compared to *Laccaria* spp. or *Lactarius* spp.

### Enzyme Production Variation

Significant differences exist among ECM fungal species in enzyme production capacity (Dighton, 1983; Antibus et al., 1992, 1997; Leake and Read, 1990b), and the availability of inorganic nutrients exerts negative feedback on enzyme production (Sinsabaugh and Liptak, 1997).

### The Case for Functional Plasticity

Individual forest trees can simultaneously maintain communities of many ECM fungal species (Zak and Marx, 1964; Gibson and Deacon, 1988; Palmer et al., 1994; Allen et al., 1995; Shaw et al., 1995). Given the demonstrated species-level functional differences, this suggests that the ECM community on root systems is functionally plastic — able to shift composition at local spatial and temporal scales to optimize resource utilization as environmental conditions change.

Tibbett (2000) emphasized that extraradical hyphae of both ericoid and ECM symbioses exhibit significant morphological and physiological plasticity (Bending and Read, 1995a, 1995b; Cairney and Burke, 1996), ideally suiting them for exploiting patchily distributed nutrient resources.

## Environmental Factors Shaping Mycorrhizal Communities

### Oligotrophic vs. Fertile Systems

Mycorrhizal function has a more dramatic effect on plant growth in oligotrophic systems than in fertile ones. Gehring and Whitham (1994) showed that ECM addition doubled pinyon pine growth on cinder soils (half the moisture, one-third available phosphorus, no mineralizable nitrogen compared to adjacent loam) but had a lesser effect on loam soil. Jumpponen et al. (1998) demonstrated enhanced lodgepole pine growth on glacial outwash soil through the dark septate mycorrhizal fungus *Phialocephala fortinii*, attributed to its phosphate acquisition ability in nutrient-poor conditions.

### Fire and Mycorrhizal Succession

Fire is a natural disturbance that maintains both plant and fungal diversity. Changes in ECM species composition following fire are well-documented (Visser, 1995; Jonsson et al., 1999a), suggesting a succession of mycorrhizal fungi during forest re-establishment (Frankland, 1992, 1998; Boerner et al., 1996).

The post-fire ECM community depends on the degree of damage to the pre-fire community:
- **Minimal soil damage**: Dying roots of former forest trees serve as inoculum sources, maintaining species diversity similar to the pre-fire community (Baar et al., 1999; Jonsson et al., 1999b)
- **Significant organic matter loss**: Changes in nutrient conditions and soil physical characteristics (increased heating from dark soil surfaces) affect relative survival and function of mycorrhizal species

For arbuscular mycorrhizae in dry sclerophyllous Australian shrub communities, fire effects on root colonization are more closely related to host plant density than direct fire impacts on the mycorrhizae (Torpy et al., 1999).

## Mycorrhizae and Plant-Water Relations

### The Drought Tolerance Hypothesis

Fitter (1985) proposed that the observed lack of consistent nutritional benefit from mycorrhizae in natural ecosystems suggests that other benefits — particularly drought tolerance — may be the primary rationale for maintaining mycorrhizal associations. This hypothesis has received substantial support.

### Mechanisms of Drought Protection

Augé (2001) reviewed the effects of AM colonization on plant water relations and concluded that root colonization by AM fungi improves water relations under both drought conditions and adequate water supply. The mechanisms include:

1. **Direct water transport**: Water flow through fungal hyphae provides an additional pathway for water uptake
2. **Improved phosphate nutrition**: Better P nutrition enhances root growth and osmotic adjustment
3. **Altered hormonal balance**: Mycorrhizal colonization modifies plant hormone levels, affecting stomatal regulation and root architecture

### Evidence from Tree Species

Michelsen and Rosendahl (1990) demonstrated synergistic benefits of AM inoculation and drought stress in tropical trees:
- *Leucaena leucocephala* showed the greatest AM benefit specifically under drought conditions
- The AM effect was greater than the effect of phosphorus addition for *Leucaena* under drought stress
- Both *Acacia nilotica* and *Leucaena* benefited from AM + P addition, but the interaction was strongest for *Leucaena*

Cruz et al. (2000) showed that in papaya, the decrease in leaf water potential during drought was less severe in AM plants than in non-mycorrhizal plants.

### Leaf Endophyte Effects on Drought

Drought tolerance conferred by fungal associations is not limited to mycorrhizal endophytes. Grass leaf endophytes produce loline alkaloids that act as osmoregulators, enhancing drought tolerance (Belesky and Malinowski, 2000). However, the effect is not universal — Cheplick et al. (2000) found no drought benefit from endophytes in *Lolium perenne* and observed reduced growth both under drought and normal conditions with endophyte presence, indicating that fungal endophyte effects are context-dependent.

### Saprotrophic Fungus Effects on Desiccation Tolerance

Osonubi et al. (1990) demonstrated that inoculation of the Nigerian pulpwood tree *Gmelina arborea* with the saprotrophic fungus *Chaetomium bostrychoides* increased both seed germination and tolerance to desiccating conditions:

| Timing | Plant Part | Inoculation | Droughted (g DW) | Undroughted (g DW) |
|--------|-----------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Before | Shoot | Seed | 1.35 | 0.99 |
| Before | Root | Seed | 0.72 | 0.48 |
| After | Shoot | Seed | 9.36 | 5.89 |
| After | Root | Seed | 4.47 | 2.65 |

Seed-inoculated plants consistently outperformed seedling-inoculated plants, and droughted plants showed greater biomass than undroughted controls after the drought event, suggesting a priming or compensatory growth effect.

## Ecological Implications

The combination of functional biodiversity within mycorrhizal communities and the multi-stress protection conferred by mycorrhizal associations has several important ecological consequences:

1. **Ecosystem resilience**: Diverse mycorrhizal communities provide functional redundancy, ensuring that some fungal partners can respond to changing conditions
2. **Plant community dynamics**: Differential drought tolerance among mycorrhizal types influences which plant species persist during drought periods
3. **Succession trajectories**: Post-disturbance recovery depends on mycorrhizal community survival and recolonization capacity
4. **Climate change adaptation**: Mycorrhizal-mediated drought tolerance may become increasingly important as climate change alters precipitation patterns
