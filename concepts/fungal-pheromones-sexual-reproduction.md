# Fungal Pheromones and Sexual Reproduction

Fungi employ an astonishing diversity of chemical signaling systems to
coordinate sexual reproduction, ranging from steroid hormones in water molds
to sesquiterpene pheromones in chytrids and carotenoid-derived trisporic
acid in zygomycetes. These chemical conversations govern mate recognition,
hyphal fusion, and the development of sexual structures across all major
fungal groups. The common challenge for all sexually reproducing fungi is
the fundamental problem of recognition: with whom should I attempt to fuse
today?

## Oomycete Sexual Communication

### Antheridiol and Oogoniol
Oomycete water molds (such as Achlya) secrete steroid hormones to coordinate
sexual development. The female mycelium releases antheridiol from the
lacework of its hyphae, which diffuses through the water and stimulates
nearby male hyphae to produce antheridia (male sex branches). As the
antheridia grow toward the antheridiol source, they release a second
pheromone, oogoniol, which the female recognizes as confirmation of male
proximity.

Sensing the success of her seductive behavior, the female forms oogonia (egg
sacs) and secretes more antheridiol to inflame every male in the
neighborhood. She soon becomes entangled in a cage of male sex branches that
clamber around until they pinpoint the antheridiol source. Once contact is
made with the oogonium, the tips of the antheridia flatten against its curved
surface, stimulating egg formation. Finally, stumpy hyphae protruding from
each antheridium penetrate the egg chamber and inject sperm nuclei,
fertilizing one or more eggs. When multiple males reach the oogonium
simultaneously, each egg in a clutch can be fertilized by a different male.

### Hormone Receptor Mechanism
In animals, steroid hormones enter target cells and bind to nuclear receptors,
functioning as transcriptional regulators that direct the expression of
specific genes controlling the cellular response. A similar mechanism likely
operates in Achlya, where antheridiol elicits changes in gene expression
that redirect hyphal growth from normal extension to antheridium formation.

Antheridia emerge as bumps close to the hyphal apex, unlike normal branches
that form further behind the tip, suggesting that the hormone causes
localized changes in cell wall plasticity rather than stimulating de novo
branch initiation.

### Endoglucanase and Wall Loosening
Thomas and Mullins (1967) discovered that antheridiol triggers secretion of
an endoglucanase enzyme, which clips polymers within the hyphal wall, causing
it to relax and allow branch emergence. Money and Hill (1997) confirmed a
strong correlation between endoglucanase activity and wall tensile strength
by measuring wall rupture pressure using a micropipette oil-injection
technique. A glass micropipet filled with transparent oil was inserted into a
growing hypha, pressure was raised until the wall ruptured, and tensile
strength was calculated from the critical pressure.

### Environmental Effects on Mating Strategy
Don Thomas compared the distribution of mating types of Achlya ambisexualis
in seasonal ponds that dry in summer and freeze in winter versus permanent
spring-fed ponds. In seasonal ponds, self-fertile hermaphrodites outnumber
mate-requiring heterothallic strains by 10 to 1. In permanent ponds,
heterothallic strains predominate.

Self-fertility is favored in unstable environments because it allows rapid
egg production without waiting for a mate, even at the cost of reduced
genetic recombination. The thick-walled eggs (oospores) serve primarily as
survival capsules, allowing the fungus to persist in drying mud like a
lungfish. The retention of egg-laying behavior by hermaphrodites that evolved
from heterothallic ancestors suggests that the primary advantage is the
provision of a durable survival structure rather than genetic mixing.

## Chytrid Pheromones: Parisin and Sirenin

Chytrid fungi use sesquiterpene-based pheromones rather than steroids. The
male sex cell releases parisin, and the female produces sirenin, named for
the mythological sirens whose songs lured sailors to their doom. When sperm
cells detect sirenin, they abandon random swimming and immediately begin
corkscrew paths toward the egg.

To avoid overstimulation, the sperm cell inactivates sirenin molecules as it
swims through the pheromonal cloud. This allows frequent directional readings
from the concentration gradient, a crucial activity for a cell competing with
many other gametes. Close approach involves a series of short dashes until
collision with the egg surface.

## Zygomycete Mating: Trisporic Acid

Zygomycete pheromones are manufactured from carotenoids, the class of
compounds that includes beta-carotene (precursor of vitamin A) and
photosynthetic plant pigments. Rather than releasing large quantities of
pheromones into the environment, zygomycetes employ cooperative biosynthesis:
each strain releases a minute precursor compound that is converted into the
active pheromone trisporic acid only upon receipt by the partner strain. An
individual strain lacks the necessary enzyme to process its own precursor.

In response to trisporic acid accumulation, asexual sporangium production
ceases in both strains, and aerial hyphae extend into the air and grow toward
one another in a cobra-like dance that climaxes in fusion and zygospore
generation. The resulting zygospore, covered with warts and melanin pigments,
is impervious to UV light, noxious chemicals, and heat, allowing it to
persist in rotting food or sleeping in the soil.

## Basidiomycete Sexual Systems

Mushroom-forming fungi lack visible sexual differentiation; all mycelia
appear as masses of androgynous tubes. However, a handful of genes determines
whether individual mycelia can fuse and produce spores. A single species of
ink-cap mushroom can encompass hundreds of different strains, so
basidiomycetes effectively have legions of sexes. Each mating-type gene can
exist in different alleles, and only certain combinations are compatible.

When incompatible strains meet and their hyphae fuse, the mingled cytoplasm
darkens and decays. On tree stumps, the pattern of compatible fusions versus
incompatible clashes creates dark zone lines mapping territories of competing
mycelia. Compatible strains fuse and leave no black line between them.

Armillaria gallica fruiting bodies have been found to contain nuclei from nine
or more individual mycelia, a finding that unsettled mycologists accustomed
to thinking in terms of pairwise matings. This multi-strain phenomenon may be
environmentally regulated: Massachusetts fruiting bodies collected before 1988
were genetic mosaics, while later mushrooms developed from single mycelia.

## Cryptococcus Sexual Communication

Cryptococcus neoformans exists as two mating types (a-strain and alpha-strain)
that look identical microscopically. Over 95 percent of human infections are
caused by the alpha-strain, partly because it can produce basidiospores
without mating, providing a shortcut for infectious spore production in
isolation. The sexual stage (Filobasidiella neoformans) has been found in
eucalyptus flowers and pigeon droppings, where creatinine-rich guano provides
an ideal nitrogen source.
