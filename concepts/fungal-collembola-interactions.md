# Fungal Nematode-Trapping Ecology

Fungi that capture and kill nematodes represent a fascinating example of predatory behavior in the fungal kingdom. Nematophagous fungi employ diverse trapping structures, including adhesive networks, adhesive knobs, constricting rings, and non-constricting rings, to capture nematodes that move through soil and decomposing organic matter. These fungi occupy an important position in soil food webs as predators that regulate nematode populations and contribute to nutrient cycling.

## Types of Nematophagous Fungi

Nematophagous fungi are classified into several functional groups based on their mechanisms of nematode capture. Endoparasitic fungi infect nematodes through spore adhesion and germ tube penetration, often after the spores are ingested by the nematode. These fungi, including species of Drechmeria, Harposporium, and Nematoctonus, complete their life cycle within the nematode body.

Predatory fungi, which are the most diverse and ecologically important group, produce specialized trapping structures on their hyphae that capture free-living nematodes. These trapping structures include adhesive networks (formed by species of Arthrobotrys and Monacrosporium), adhesive knobs (produced by species of Dactylella and Monacrosporium), constricting rings (formed by species of Arthrobotrys and Drechslerella), and non-constricting rings.

A third group includes opportunistic fungi that can infect and kill nematodes without specialized trapping structures, using their normal hyphal growth to penetrate the nematode cuticle. These fungi typically target weakened or dead nematodes rather than actively hunting healthy individuals.

## Trapping Structure Morphology

Adhesive networks consist of three-dimensional webs of hyphae covered with an adhesive secretion that entraps nematodes as they move through the network. When a nematode contacts the network, it becomes stuck to the adhesive surface. The fungus then produces an infection peg that penetrates the nematode cuticle, and hyphae grow into the body cavity, digesting the nematode tissues.

Adhesive knobs are swollen hyphal cells that secrete a sticky adhesive on their surface. They are typically produced on short stalks that project from the hyphae into the surrounding environment. Nematodes that contact the adhesive surface of a knob become immobilized, and the fungus penetrates and digests the nematode.

Constricting rings are among the most sophisticated trapping structures. They consist of three-celled rings formed on hyphal branches. When a nematode enters the ring and exerts pressure on the inner surface of the cells, the cells rapidly swell inward, constricting the ring and trapping the nematode. This mechanical trapping response occurs within fractions of a second and is triggered by the mechanical stimulus of nematode contact.

## Induction of Trap Formation

The production of trapping structures by nematophagous fungi is induced by the presence of nematodes or nematode-derived signals. Fungi growing in the absence of nematodes typically produce only vegetative hyphae, but when nematodes are present or when nematode exudates are added to the growth medium, the fungus switches to trap-producing mode.

The induction of trap formation involves specific chemical signals from nematodes, including small peptides, amino acids, and other metabolites present in nematode exudates. The specificity of these signals varies among fungal species, with some responding to a broad range of nematode species and others responding only to specific nematode groups.

The nutritional status of the fungus also influences trap formation. Fungi growing on nutrient-poor substrates are more likely to produce traps than those growing on rich substrates, suggesting that trap formation is a nutritional strategy triggered by nutrient limitation. The fungus responds to the presence of nematodes as a potential nutrient source when other nutrients are scarce.

## Nematode Digestion and Nutrient Acquisition

After a nematode is captured, the fungus penetrates the cuticle using specialized infection structures and produces hyphae that grow through the body cavity, digesting the nematode tissues. The digestion of nematode biomass releases nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients that are absorbed by the fungus. Nematode tissue has a favorable carbon-to-nitrogen ratio for fungal growth, making nematode capture a highly efficient nutritional strategy.

The capture of nematodes by fungi is particularly important in nitrogen-limited soils, where the nitrogen released from nematode digestion can significantly enhance fungal nitrogen status. Dighton and colleagues have discussed the role of fungal interactions with soil fauna, including nematodes, in the context of nutrient cycling and decomposition processes.

## Ecological Significance in Soil Food Webs

Nematophagous fungi are important regulators of nematode populations in soil ecosystems. Nematodes are among the most abundant and diverse animals in soil, with densities ranging from millions to billions per square meter in productive soils. Many soil nematodes feed on fungi and bacteria, and the predation of these nematodes by fungi creates a complex web of trophic interactions.

The fungal nematode-trapping community is part of a broader micro-food web in which energy flows from organic matter to bacteria and fungi, from bacteria and fungi to bacterial-feeding and fungal-feeding nematodes, and from these nematodes to predatory nematodes and nematophagous fungi. This micro-food web is a major pathway for nutrient mineralization in soil ecosystems.

## Biological Control Applications

Nematophagous fungi have been investigated as biological control agents against plant-parasitic nematodes, which cause significant crop losses worldwide. Species of Arthrobotrys, Monacrosporium, and Paecilomyces have been developed as commercial biocontrol products for the management of root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne species), cyst nematodes (Globodera and Heterodera species), and other plant-parasitic nematodes.

The effectiveness of nematophagous fungi as biocontrol agents depends on their ability to establish and persist in soil, their capacity to produce trapping structures in response to target nematodes, and their compatibility with other soil organisms and agricultural practices. The integration of nematophagous fungi into integrated pest management systems requires an understanding of their ecology and the environmental factors that influence their activity.

## Interactions with Other Soil Organisms

Nematophagous fungi interact with other soil organisms in complex ways. Collembola and other fungivorous soil animals can graze on nematophagous fungi, reducing their abundance and biological control efficacy. Conversely, the nematode-trapping activity of these fungi can influence the availability of nematodes as food for other predators, creating cascading effects through the soil food web.

The presence of other soil fungi can also affect nematophagous fungal activity through competitive interactions for space and resources. In some cases, the activity of nematophagous fungi is enhanced by the presence of other microorganisms that modify the soil environment or produce compounds that stimulate trap formation.
