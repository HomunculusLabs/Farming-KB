Fungi possess specialized intracellular molecules that bind and
sequester toxic metals, forming a critical line of defense against
metal toxicity. These metal-binding molecules include metallothioneins,
phytochelatins, glutathione, and associated peptide complexes. Their
ability to chelate metal ions with high affinity makes them relevant
both for understanding fungal metal tolerance and for developing
bioremediation technologies.

## Metallothioneins

Metallothioneins (MTs) are specific, low-molecular-weight (6,000 to
10,000 Da) cysteine-rich metal-binding proteins produced by animals,
plants, and microorganisms in response to toxic metals. In fungi,
metallothioneins are particularly important for copper homeostasis and
detoxification. The main function of [[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]] MT is
cellular copper regulation, and both induction and amplification of MT
genes leads to enhanced copper resistance in S. cerevisiae and
Candida glabrata.

Production of metallothioneins has been detected in both copper- and
cadmium-resistant strains of S. cerevisiae. However, S. cerevisiae
cannot rely on MT synthesis as a copper-resistance mechanism when
grown with cadmium, indicating functional specificity among different
metallothionein isoforms. Relatively little work has been carried out
on metallothioneins in filamentous fungi compared to the extensive
yeast literature, representing a significant gap in understanding.

Eukaryotic metallothioneins, including yeast MT, have been expressed
in Escherichia coli as fusions to membrane proteins such as LamB.
This in vivo expression of MTs provides a means of designing biomass
with specific and increased metal-binding properties. Expression of
yeast and mammalian MTs increased the ability of E. coli to bind
cadmium 15 to 20-fold, correlating with the number of metal-binding
centres contributed by the MT moiety.

## Phytochelatins and Related Peptides

Phytochelatins and related peptides containing glutamic acid and
cysteine at the amino terminus have been identified in plants, algae,
and several microorganisms. The general structure is (gamma-Glu-Cys)n
-Gly where n may be up to 11. Phytochelatins are also known as
cadystins and metal gamma-glutamyl peptides, with the chemical
structure (gamma-EC)nG being a more precise description.

Although phytochelatin induction has been reported with a wide variety
of metals including silver, gold, mercury, nickel, lead, tin, and
zinc, metal binding has been demonstrated primarily for cadmium and
copper. For cadmium, two types of complex exist in Schizosaccharomyces
pombe and Candida glabrata. A low-molecular-weight complex consists
of phytochelatin and cadmium, while a higher-molecular-weight complex
additionally contains acid-labile sulfide.

The sulfide-containing phytochelatin-cadmium-sulfide complex has
greater stability and higher cadmium-binding capacity than the
low-molecular-weight complex. It possesses a structure consisting
of a CdS crystallite core surrounded by an outer layer of glutathione
and phytochelatin peptides. This higher binding capacity confers
greater tolerance to cadmium. In S. pombe, subsequent vacuolar
localization of these complexes illustrates the link between
cytosolic sequestration and vacuolar compartmentation.

## Glutathione as Precursor and Protective Agent

Glutathione (gamma-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine) serves as the
biosynthetic precursor for phytochelatins and also functions directly
as a metal-binding and antioxidant molecule. The tripeptide provides
cysteine thiol groups that form coordinate bonds with soft metal ions
such as cadmium, copper, and mercury. Glutathione levels are tightly
regulated in fungal cells, and depletion of glutathione pools during
metal stress can sensitize cells to oxidative damage.

The glutathione-ascorbate cycle plays a dual role during metal stress:
it maintains the reduced glutathione pool needed for phytochelatin
synthesis while simultaneously detoxifying reactive oxygen species
generated by metal-catalyzed redox reactions. This integration of
metal detoxification with oxidative stress defense is a hallmark of
fungal metal tolerance.

## Phytochelatin Synthase and Genetic Engineering

The identification of genes encoding phytochelatin synthases has
provided molecular evidence for the role of phytochelatins in metal
tolerance. Heterologous expression of phytochelatin synthase genes
dramatically enhances metal tolerance in recipient organisms. Future
research may allow testing of phytochelatin synthase genes for
bioremediation applications, and plant metal tolerance may be improved
by transfer of fungal metal-resistance genes.

The yeast MT gene has been successfully transferred into cauliflower,
enabling selection of cadmium-tolerant transgenic plants that
accumulated more cadmium in leaves than non-transformed controls.
Metal-binding peptides with sequences such as Gly-His-His-Pro-His-Gly
and Gly-Cys-Gly-Cys-Pro-Cys-Gly-Cys-Gly have been engineered into
membrane proteins and expressed in E. coli, with surface display of
cysteine-rich peptides increasing cadmium-binding ability fourfold.

## Applications in Detection and Remediation

Beyond direct remediation, metal-binding molecules have applications
for detecting and measuring toxic metal ions in water. A rapid method
for metal quantification has been developed using chemically
synthesized phytochelatin as a mediator, exploiting the metal-binding
property of phytochelatins and quantification of thiol groups.
Phytochelatin-cadmium-sulfide complexes can behave as semiconductor
nanocrystallites, providing additional possibilities for industrial
applications including photocatalytic degradation of organic
pollutants under ultraviolet irradiation.

Other intracellular metal immobilization mechanisms include
precipitation by reduction, sulfide production, and association with
polyphosphate. The diversity of these intracellular strategies
complements extracellular immobilization mechanisms to provide fungi
with a comprehensive toolkit for surviving in metal-contaminated
environments.
