# Ingham — Chelation and Microbial Nutrient Processing

Elaine Ingham's view of chelation emphasizes that biological chelation is the primary natural mechanism by which mineral nutrients become mobile and available to plants. Synthetic chelation (e.g., EDTA) is an industrial substitute for a process that living organisms perform continuously.

## What Chelation Does

The chelation process removes the positive charge from metal cations (iron, calcium, zinc, etc.), allowing the neutral or slightly negatively charged chelated molecule to pass through cell membranes and leaf stomata more rapidly. Any biological cell has a net negative charge, so positively charged cations have difficulty entering without being neutralized first.

## Microbial Chelation

Bacteria and fungi are the primary biological chelators in soil. They:
- Need chelated minerals to move nutrients into their own cells
- Produce the proteins, amino acids, and organic acid molecules that perform chelation
- Provide the biological energy required to synthesize chelating compounds

Chelation is not something that "just happens" in soil — it requires active biological energy. EDTA, for example, is not found naturally in soil to any significant degree.

## Synthetic vs Organic Chelators

### Synthetic Chelators (EDTA)
- Neutralize charge effectively
- May leave undesirable residues in plants
- Not found naturally in soil
- Plant has mechanisms to deal with them but residues may be problematic
- Should be checked against organic certification standards (OMRI, IFOAM)

### Biological Chelators (proteins, amino acids, organic acids)
- Produced by soil microorganisms
- Readily metabolized by plants after mineral release
- No harmful residues — amino acids and proteins are used in cellular synthesis
- The natural mechanism plants evolved to work with

## Plant Processing of Chelated Molecules

Once a chelated molecule enters the plant:
1. Plant enzymes remove the protein, amino acid, or other neutralizing element
2. The mineral is placed where needed in cellular machinery
3. Carbon atoms and mineral atoms typically go to different locations
4. There is no requirement for them to stay together

## What Happens to Chelating Agents

- **Amino acids and proteins**: Used in cellular synthesis by the plant
- **Lignin**: A difficult compound — plant usually spits it back out
- **Synthetic chelators (EDTA)**: Probably excreted as waste by the plant

## Chelated Minerals in Practice

Chelated minerals are:
- Less likely to leach from soil
- Less likely to harm plants compared to non-chelated nutrients
- Beneficial when plant tissue tests confirm deficiency

However, nutrient levels in soil may appear adequate while plant tissue shows deficiency — this indicates the biological chelation mechanism is missing. The solution is restoring soil biology rather than applying chelated minerals.

## Implications for AACT

Compost tea delivers the organisms that perform biological chelation. When the soil food web is functioning:
- Microbes chelate minerals from rock dusts, organic matter, and mineral soils
- Protozoa and nematodes cycle these chelated nutrients through the food web
- Mycorrhizal fungi directly deliver chelated phosphorus and other minerals to plant roots
- The plant receives nutrients in biologically appropriate, cell-ready forms
