## Pond Fertilisation and Nutrient Management

Nutrient management in aquaculture ponds represents one of the most critical and rewarding aspects of [[permaculture]] water design. The difference between a poorly fertilised and a well-managed fertilised pond can be a factor of 2 to 10 times in yield. Understanding the dynamics of nutrient input, uptake, cycling, and loss enables the designer to create extraordinarily productive aquatic systems with minimal external inputs.

### The Three Major Recurrent Costs

J.E. Bardach identifies three factors that account for well over 90% of the energy or monetary production costs in fish culture:

1. **Food** — the primary input determining fish growth rates and final yields
2. **Fertiliser** — nutrients that stimulate the base of the aquatic food chain
3. **Fuel** — energy for water pumping, aeration, and harvesting

Labour and fuel energy are to some extent interchangeable, and while the utilisation of waste food represents a saving, pollutants represent a cost — sometimes a terminal one, as the water environment is highly susceptible to biocides and chemical contamination.

### Manure Integration Strategies

A hallmark of traditional Asian aquaculture is the tight integration of livestock and poultry with fish production. Multiple devices bring complex plant nutrients to ponds:

- **Duck houses** sited over or adjacent to pond water
- **Pig pens** positioned to drain directly into ponds
- **Chicken roosts** erected over water surfaces
- **Pigeon lofts** situated to deposit droppings into ponds
- **Perches for gulls, ducks, and land birds** erected over water
- **Martin and swift nest sites** placed in or over water
- **Bat roosts** (as used in Holland) positioned to contribute guano

Second-stage sewage effluent is also used productively in fish ponds. Pig, duck, and poultry manures are all proven inputs. The design principle is to create nutrient cycling loops where land-based animals contribute manure to water, pond water irrigates land crops, and each element of the cycle produces a yield.

### Fertiliser Types and Fish Food Preferences

Ponds are fertilised according to the food preferences of the target fish species. Three general categories exist:

**Heavily fertilised ponds** — intended for intensive algal growth to feed milkfish or prawns. These ponds receive high organic inputs and develop dense plankton blooms.

**Moderately fertilised ponds** — where higher vegetation is encouraged to feed grass carp or [[tilapia]]. These receive moderate inputs that stimulate macrophyte growth without excessive algal blooms.

**Clear-water ponds** — maintained for bass, perch, and salmonids (trout). These receive minimal fertilisation, as the target species require clean, well-oxygenated water.

In any polyculture system, a larger proportion of fish should be algal or plant feeders, with a usual ratio of approximately 5:1 herbivore to predator. This ratio ensures that the base of the food chain is efficiently converted through multiple trophic levels.

### Nutrient Dynamics in Water

Phosphates, potash, and minor elements are fixed and held in water within 12–14 hours in freshwater ponds, or 3–4 days in marine environments. This rapid uptake has critical implications for management — nutrients must be managed carefully to avoid both deficiency and excess.

Because fertiliser uptake is so rapid, fertile ponds need to be stocked with shrimp or large numbers of small forage fish while fingerling pan fish are growing. Without this "intermediate crop," the fertiliser stimulates plant and algal growth that dies and decomposes before it can be consumed by the target fish, effectively wasting the nutrient input.

Mussels play a particularly important role in phosphate cycling. They convey phosphates to the mud via their anal siphons and serve as the major phosphate reservoir of ponds and rivers. Swingle (1966) recorded a yield difference for mussels in the same pond of from 52.8 kg (unfertilised) to 1,012 kg (fertilised) in whole weight (meats are 35% of total), demonstrating the extraordinary impact of well-managed fertilisation.

### Managing Excess Nutrients

Too-high nitrate status can be filtered through several strategies:

- **Forage food ponds** — using crustaceans to filter excess nutrients from water before it reaches the main pond
- **Reed beds** — constructed wetlands that absorb nutrients from pond overflow
- **Downstream land crops** — relying on terrestrial crops downstream to remove excess nutrient from pond overflow

In cascading pond systems (flow-on designs), downstream land crops typically achieve a doubling in yield of species such as melons, tomatoes, or fuel forests. This makes fish ponds a key feature in raising land-based agricultural yields — the pond is not an isolated production unit but an integral part of the whole farm system.

### Lime and Minor Element Management

After testing waters, it is likely that minor elements and phosphates may need supplementation. Several important cautions apply:

- **Nitrate caution** — avoid adding nitrates to waters where natural manures are used, or in desert basins where water nitrate levels can already be high
- **Boron awareness** — avoid boron-containing detergents in or near fish ponds
- **Biocide prohibition** — avoid any biocides not specifically recommended for fish culture
- **Approved treatments** — salt, copper sulphate, weak formalin, and some antibiotics are used to control diseases or parasites without lasting harm

Phosphatic rock and granite dust can supply lime once selected nutrient levels have been achieved. These slow-release sources are preferable to rapid-release agricultural lime for ongoing maintenance.

### Shelter and Refuges for Productivity

The provision of shelter structures has a decisive effect on predation losses of young fish, crayfish, and species subject to predation by their own kind:

- **Stacks of pots and pipes** — create cavities for refuge
- **Tyre bundles** — durable artificial reef structures
- **Rope, reed, or brush bundles** — biodegradable shelter that also contributes organic material
- **Shallow weedy areas** — natural refuge zones for forage species and fry

Crab and crayfish culture benefits particularly from such refuges. Yield increases of 10–30% are recorded for crustaceans, and 20–100% for forage fish protected by shallows or weedy areas. Mortality of young and fry is always greater in ponds of simple design without refuges.

Cage culture is valuable not only as a high-yield system in itself, but because it enables the strategic separation of predator and prey (or adults and juveniles). By isolating breeding stock or vulnerable juveniles within cages, the designer gains precise control over population dynamics while maintaining overall pond productivity.

### Temperature Management for Enhanced Production

Higher temperatures stimulate plant growth and algae production, increasing overall biological turnover. It is advantageous to maintain winter temperatures at optimum levels for the selected species mix. Multiple strategies achieve this:

- **Solar ponds** — dark-bottomed ponds that absorb and store solar heat, incorporated within or adjacent to production ponds
- **Industrial waste heat** — using warmed water from power stations, salt works, or food processing plants
- **Glazed covers** — glazing over canals, refuges, or building raft columns insulated from earth and colder waters
- **Earth-covered pipes** — heat refuges as shaded canals or deep kettles (5 m or so) in shallow ponds
- **Solar thermal collectors** — pumping pond water over dark surfaces (roofs, rocks, cement, bitumen roads) at close to 100% solar heat absorption efficiency
- **[[thermosiphon]] systems** — passive collector systems using heat sources placed below the pond, with non-return valves preventing reverse flow
- **[[compost]] heat** — utilising the exothermic [[decomposition]] of compost piles to warm adjacent water
- **Glasshouses** — enclosing small ponds to raise water temperatures by 3°C or more

These strategies can extend fish growing periods by 30–40% and increase yields by 2 to 5 times. Heat pumps from canals can deliver heat to ponds, shortening maturation times of fish or plants to approximately one-third that of cool, open pond areas. The principle is straightforward: maintaining optimal temperatures for feeding activity and food growth translates directly into higher protein production per unit area.

Adapted local fish generally need little assistance to survive seasonal extremes, but selected stock or exotic species may require refuges during weeks of extreme weather. The investment in temperature management infrastructure is rapidly repaid through increased production and reduced mortality.
