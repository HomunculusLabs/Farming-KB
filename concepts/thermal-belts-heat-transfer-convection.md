# Thermal Belts, Heat Transfer, and Convection

## Overview

Understanding how heat moves through landscapes is fundamental to [[permaculture-design]]. Heat is transferred by three principal mechanisms—conduction, convection, and radiation—each operating at different scales and with distinct implications for site planning, building design, and agricultural strategy. The concept of the **[[thermal-belt]]** on hillsides, combined with knowledge of convection loops and thermosiphons, gives designers powerful tools for placing structures, gardens, and settlements to maximize thermal comfort and minimize energy input.

## Heat Transfer Mechanisms

### Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of heat through direct contact between solids or between fluids. It operates most efficiently when dense materials are in contact, and is the principle behind slab-floor heating systems where hot water pipes embedded in concrete distribute warmth throughout a building's floor. In uninsulated open systems, conduction effects are local, as heat is fairly rapidly radiated away from exposed solid surfaces.

Key design implications:

- **Insulated slab floors** are extremely efficient for home heating because conduction distributes heat evenly through the thermal mass of the floor.
- **Insulation** (solid foam, straw, trapped air) prevents conducted heat from escaping to surrounding earth or exterior surfaces.
- Pipes buried in hot solid masses conduct heat to their contents; this principle is used in radiant floor systems and heat exchange.

### Convection

Convection is the movement of heat by the circulation of fluids (air or water). Cool air is denser than heated air, so heated air rises—a principle that drives atmospheric circulation, valley winds, and building ventilation patterns.

**Convection loops (thermosiphons):** When heated air or water is contained in pipes or ducts, a closed-loop circulation can be established by applying heat to the lower part of the loop, provided a minimum height difference of approximately 40 cm (about 18 inches) is built into the loop. This self-regulating effect:

- Requires no pumps or mechanical energy
- Is used in refrigerators driven by heat sources
- Transfers heat from solar ponds or flat-plate collectors to home radiators or hot water tanks
- Works best when heat collectors are sited at least 0.5 m below the storage or use point

**Atmospheric convection:** Columns of heated air over land ascend as "Overbeck jets," and at the top of these columns, condensation and rain may occur as air cools in the upper atmosphere. Convectional rains create the mosaic of rainfall patterns seen in deserts.

**Important limitation:** Convection loops will NOT occur in closed rooms where hot air (at 8–10°C above ambient) stratifies in a quiet layer below ceilings. Air is difficult to heat and stores little heat, so air convection alone is not an efficient way to heat building interiors.

### Radiation

Radiation is the transmission of heat across space by electromagnetic waves. It is the mechanism by which the sun heats the Earth. Radiant heaters affect air temperature very little but directly heat solid objects and bodies, making them extremely efficient space heaters. Key characteristics:

- Radiation is non-directional and crosses space
- Focused radiation can produce very intense local heat
- Only reflective (dust-free) surfaces or mirrors can effectively conserve radiant heat
- Glass is less permeable to long-wave (heat) radiation, creating a greenhouse "trapping" effect

## The Thermal Belt

### Definition and Mechanism

The **thermal belt** is a zone on mid-slopes of hills and mountains that is significantly warmer than valley floors and hilltops. This phenomenon occurs because:

1. **Cold air drainage:** At night, cold air—being denser—flows downhill and pools in valleys and low-lying plains
2. **Hilltop exposure:** Hilltops are exposed to cold winds and lack the insulating effect of surrounding landmass
3. **Mid-slope position:** The thermal belt sits above the cold valley air and below the exposed, windy hilltops

### Temperature Inversions

In valleys and plains surrounded by mountains, cool air from the hills or cold air generated by rapid radiation loss from soils can become trapped below warmer air, creating a **temperature inversion**. In these conditions:

- Fog, smog, and pollution build up over cities in valleys
- Wind effect is minimal, preventing dispersion
- Sites must be carefully analyzed for potential pollutant accumulation

### Altitude and Elevation Effects

Temperature normally decreases approximately 1°C for every 100 m increase in altitude (3°F per 1,000 feet). The thermal belt therefore occupies different elevation ranges depending on the landscape:

| Landscape Type | Thermal Belt Elevation |
|---|---|
| Hill country and mountains | 1,000–5,000 m (3,280–16,400 ft) |
| Lower hill slopes | 100–200 m (330–650 ft) |
| Hot deserts (mesas) | 10–15 m (33–49 ft) above valley floor |

### Design Strategy

Siting houses on sun-facing slopes within the thermal belt is a **critical energy-conservation strategy** in all but tropical climates. In tropical climates, siting in shade or cooling coastal windstreams is preferred.

Information about local thermal belt positions can be gathered from:
- Local anecdotes and traditional knowledge
- Observation of existing vegetation patterns
- Trial plantings of frost-susceptible species

## Albedo and Heat Absorption

The **albedo** (reflectivity) of surfaces profoundly affects how much solar energy is absorbed versus reflected. Key albedo values include:

| Surface | Reflected (%) | Absorbed (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Clean fresh snow | 75–95 | 5–25 |
| White gravel | 50–93 | 7–50 |
| Dense white clouds | 60–90 | 10–40 |
| Calm water (low sun) | 50–80 | 20–50 |
| Sandy soils | 15–40 | 60–85 |
| Young pines | 14 | 86 |
| Dark soils | 7–10 | 90–93 |
| Fir forest | 10 | 90 |

## Radiation and Design Implications

### Community and Plant Health

Areas of severe radiation—where atmosphere is thin (mountains), albedo is high (snow, granite, white sand), or reflective surfaces dominate—can produce severe radiation burns, skin cancers, and temporary or long-term blindness. Plants must be screened against sunburn by partial shade and white paint on stems, especially when young.

### Heat Storage Principles

- **Water and stone** have high specific heat and are excellent thermal mass for heat storage
- **Air** has low specific heat and is a very poor conductor, making it an excellent insulator
- **Trees with high biomass** (mainly water) store significant heat in their mass
- **Dark evergreen trees** absorb and radiate heat effectively
- **White-barked or light-coloured trees** reflect heat—useful in cool districts where light is limiting

### Practical Design Applications

1. **Maximize solar radiation** in subtropical to cool climates by choosing appropriate house sites
2. **Shelter from excessive radiation** in hot and arid climates
3. **Convert light to heat** and store it below insulated slab floors
4. **Reflect heat** onto cool areas using reflective surfaces or light-colored walls
5. **Install solar chimneys** to draw cool air through cross-ventilation systems
6. **Use radiant heating** (massive stoves) rather than air convection for efficient space heating

## Advected Heat and Cold

**Advection** refers to heat or cold carried by wind from other regions. Advected fogs come inshore from coasts, and advection frosts occur when cold air flows down mountain slopes to pool in hollows. The invasion of cool areas by warm advected air causes moisture condensation—critical for forest precipitation but a nuisance in enclosed buildings where condensation management is essential.

## Plants as Heat Generators

Several plant species generate their own heat through metabolic processes:

- **Arum lilies** and **[[philodendron-selloum]]** store fats that are metabolically "burned" to create heat
- Philodendrons may reach 46°C (115°F) when ambient air is 4°C (39°F)
- **Skunk cabbage** (*Symplocarpus foetidus*) generates 20–25°C of extra heat to melt spring snow and protect blooms
- The first leaf of skunk cabbage creates a wind-driven vortex that carries pollen to lower flowers without insects

These "heater plants" can assist frost-tender species in understorey plantings, though many have unpleasant odors and should be used with caution.

## Temperature Extremes for Design

Understanding critical temperature thresholds is essential for site planning:

- **43°C (109°F):** Upper survival limit for poultry and many wild birds
- **36°C (97°F):** Soil temperatures above this cause transplant shock
- **0°C (32°F):** Frost-affecting temperature for many plants
- **Sustained sub-zero temperatures** will eliminate even hardy, established species

Black frosts that affect whole regions can be economically disastrous and must be documented during site analysis.
