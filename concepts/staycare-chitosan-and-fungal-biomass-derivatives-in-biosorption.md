## Binding Mechanisms

Metal binding to chitosan and fungal cell wall components involves
multiple mechanisms that may act individually or synergistically:

- **Ion exchange**: Metal ions displace protons or other cations
  from binding sites, as evidenced by the release of hydrogen ions
  during biosorption.
- **Coordination**: Metal ions form coordination complexes with
  lone pair electrons on amino, carboxyl, and phosphoryl groups.
- **Complexation**: Metal ions form complexes with multiple binding
  sites on the same or adjacent polymer chains.
- **Electrostatic attraction**: At low pH, protonated amino groups
  on chitosan attract anionic metal species through electrostatic
  interactions.
- **Precipitation**: In certain conditions, metal ions may
  precipitate on the biosorbent surface as hydroxides or other
  insoluble compounds.

## Processed Chitosan Biosorbents

Purified chitosan extracted from fungal biomass or crustacean shells has
been extensively studied as a biosorbent material. The amino groups of
chitosan can be further chemically modified to enhance metal binding
selectivity and capacity. Chemical cross-linking of chitosan with
agents such as formaldehyde, formaldehyde-urea mixtures, sulfone, and
divinylsulfone has been shown to increase metal uptake capacity by
10-30% compared to untreated biomass.

Chitosan-based biosorbents have demonstrated particular effectiveness
for the uptake of metal anions and complexes, including chromate,
platinum, palladium, and molybdenum species. The adsorption of these
anionic species is favored at low pH where the amino groups of chitosan
are protonated, enabling electrostatic attraction of anions.

## Fungal Pellet Biosorbents

The growth of fungi (Aspergillus, Rhizopus, and Penicillium species)
in pellet form through manipulation of culture conditions has been
investigated as a method for producing biosorbent materials with
favorable physical properties. Fungal pellets are self-immobilized
biomass aggregates that offer advantages including good settling
characteristics, reusability, and suitability for packed bed reactor
configurations. Despite promising early results with pelletized
biomass, recent research has focused more extensively on the use of
external immobilization matrices.

## Immobilization Techniques for Biosorbents

Three principal immobilization approaches have been applied to fungal
biosorbents for enhanced performance in continuous flow systems:

- **Biofilm growth on inert matrices**: Fungal mycelia are grown on
  support materials such as coal, sand, and foam particles. This
  approach offers simplicity but may suffer from poor biosorbent
  stability and limited reactor performance.
- **Entrapment in polymeric matrices**: Natural polymers (alginate,
  carrageenan) and synthetic polymers (polyacrylamide, polysulfone,
  polyvinyl alcohol, silica gels) are used to encapsulate fungal
  biomass in porous gel beads. Synthetic polymers provide greater
  stability but may reduce binding kinetics due to additional mass
  transfer resistance.
- **Chemical cross-linking**: Treatment with cross-linking agents
  increases structural stability and can enhance metal binding
  capacity by 10-30% compared to untreated cells.
