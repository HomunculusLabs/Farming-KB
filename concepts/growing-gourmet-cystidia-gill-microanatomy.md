Key diagnostic features include:

- **Presence or absence** of cheilocystidia and/or
  pleurocystidia
- **Shape**: Capitate (with a swollen head), lageniform
  (flask-shaped), fusoid-ventricose (spindle-shaped with
  swollen middle), or cylindrical
- **Size**: Measured in micrometers
- **Wall thickness**: Thin-walled vs. thick-walled
- **Contents**: Whether they contain crystalline or
  pigmented inclusions
- **Apex characteristics**: Narrow, bulbous, or forked

For example, the presence of abundant capitate pleurocystidia
precludes a species from being called [[agrocybe-aegerita-detailed]], where gills are gray at first and
become chocolate brown with spore maturity.

## Impact on Cultivation

Cystidia have practical implications for mushroom
cultivation:

- Species having pleurocystidia often have a high number
  of gills per millimeter of radial arc. More gills means
  more spores, and the survival of the species is better
  assured.

- As mushrooms mature, cystidia swell with metabolic waste
  products. Often an oily droplet forms at their tips. This
  process helps regulate humidity in the gill microclimate.

- The detection of cystidia features requires microscopy,
  making cultivated mushrooms difficult to differentiate
  without microscopic examination. This is particularly
  relevant in the Pleurotus and [[psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa]] versus non-forking types in
  related species.

## Clamp Connections and Gill Cells

Clamp connections, small semicircular bridges between cells,
are another microscopic feature found on the hyphae of many
gilled mushrooms. Their presence or absence, combined with
cystidia characteristics, provides a comprehensive picture
for species identification. Clamp connections are
particularly useful for cultivators determining whether
they have mated dikaryotic mycelium versus unmated
monokaryotic mycelium from a single spore.
