## Overview

Steve Solomon delivers a pointed critique of municipal composting programs, arguing that the finished product is nutritionally inadequate for [[companion-cultivation-of-mushrooms-with-garden-vegetables]].

### Low Total Nutrient Content

Municipal compost typically contains:

- **Nitrogen:** Below 1.0% (well under the 1.5% minimum for low-demand vegetables)
- **Phosphorus:** Often negligible
- **Potassium:** Low to moderate
- **Total N+P+K:** Under 3% (compared to the 6%+ needed for high-demand crops)

Solomon provides a comparison table showing that municipal compost ranks at the bottom of [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-trace-minerals]] in the proportions needed by vegetable crops. The woody feedstocks that dominate municipal inputs are inherently mineral-poor compared to leguminous green manures or animal manures.

## The Greenhouse Gas Paradox

Solomon raises a provocative environmental argument against municipal composting. Because the starting C:N ratio is so high, far more than half of the carbon in the starting materials is lost during the composting process. This carbon is released primarily as carbon dioxide and, under anaerobic pockets within the heap, as methane — both greenhouse gases.

The final weight of finished municipal compost represents only a tiny fraction of the starting weight of materials. Solomon contends that municipal composting, "portrayed as socially positive, actually is a huge contributor to greenhouse gases." While this claim is contentious and depends heavily on the alternative fate of the waste (landfilling produces methane), it highlights the thermodynamic inefficiency of composting high-carbon feedstocks.

## What Municipal Compost Is Good For

Solomon is careful to acknowledge that municipal compost is not worthless — it is simply not a vegetable fertilizer. Its appropriate uses include:

- **Ornamental mulch:** Spreading under trees, shrubs, and along roadsides
- **Soil structure amendment:** Adding organic matter to compacted or sandy soils
- **Park landscaping:** Municipal parks and public gardens where food production is not the goal
- **Moisture retention:** As a mulch layer to reduce evaporation
