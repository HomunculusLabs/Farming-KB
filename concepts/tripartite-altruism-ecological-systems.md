# Tripartite Altruism in Ecological Systems

Tripartite altruism is a concept from systems ecology, articulated by Howard T. Odum, describing how organisms and populations allocate their captured energy across three functional roles within a hierarchical system. David Holmgren uses this framework extensively in his discussion of self-regulation and feedback in permaculture design, applying it to both natural and human systems.

## The Three-Part Energy Distribution

According to Odum's model, any organism or population distributes its available energy in roughly equal thirds:

1. **Self-maintenance**: Approximately one-third of captured energy is required for metabolic self-maintenance — keeping the individual or population alive, growing, and reproducing.

2. **Feedback to lower-order providers**: One-third is fed back to maintain the lower-order system providers that supply the organism's needs. This includes contributions to soil health, habitat maintenance, and mutualistic relationships with organisms at lower trophic levels.

3. **Contribution to higher-order controllers**: One-third is contributed upward to higher-order system controllers — predators, parasites, diseases, or environmental feedback mechanisms that regulate the population and maintain ecosystem balance.

This is not a rigid formula but a general pattern observable across scales in nature. The model illustrates that apparent "waste" from one perspective is actually functional exchange within the larger system. These feedback payments and taxes help maintain the flow of energy and environmental stability for the organism itself, so they are not wasteful but essential to long-term survival. Organisms that have evolved to maintain all three feedback pathways tend to persist; those that have lost any one tend to be short-lived and subject to boom-and-bust cycles.

## The Rabbit Example

Holmgren illustrates tripartite altruism with rabbits. Rabbits eat grass to live, grow, and reproduce. Their manure fertilises the grass that feeds them (feedback to lower-order providers). The "sacrifice" of weak rabbits to predators helps keep the population fit and in balance (contribution to higher-order controllers).

If these feedback mechanisms fail, the rabbit population suffers. If rabbits deposit most of their manure under adjacent brambles to avoid predators, they may be progressively eliminated — both by overpopulation leading to overgrazing of declining pasture and by fertilised brambles shading out their food supply. The breakdown of tripartite altruism cascades through the system, demonstrating that the three allocations are interdependent. A population that captures all available energy for self-maintenance without paying feedback costs is behaving like a cancer — it may grow rapidly for a time, but it will eventually destroy its host system and collapse.

## Energy Hierarchy and the Trophic Pyramid

Tripartite altruism is embedded in hierarchical structures. The trophic pyramid (Figure 13 in Holmgren's book) illustrates these relationships: those at the base of the energy chain — producers — are numerous but have little individual power, while those at the top — consumers — are fewer but more powerful.

| Level | Role | Numbers | Individual Power |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | Primary producers (peasants) | Very many | Very low |
| Middle | Primary consumers (specialised trades) | Moderate | Moderate |
| Top | Top consumers (royalty, elite) | Very few | Very high |

In natural ecosystems the pyramid is maintained by the thermodynamic inefficiency of energy transfer between trophic levels: only about 10% of energy is passed from one level to the next. This thermodynamic constraint limits the number of top predators and keeps the pyramid stable. Industrial energy has temporarily overridden this constraint, allowing a historically unprecedented middle-class bulge.

The saying "too many chiefs and not enough Indians" describes an imbalance in this traditional structure. Industrial energy and affluence have made this imbalance possible on a grand scale, with "energy slaves" supporting a swollen middle class that has no historical precedent in terms of numbers or consumption levels.

## The Energy Hierarchy Curve

In low-energy pre-industrial societies, the distribution of energy use follows a hyperbolic curve — abundant producers on the left with modest individual consumption, and rare elite consumers on the right with very high individual consumption. Industrialisation has created a "normal distribution" curve with a great bulge of middle-class consumers. This bulge is, in Odum's framing, an artifact of the fossil fuel energy pulse moving up the hierarchy.

While statistics of the modern nation state may reflect law, culture and history, economic and ecological reality is now global. When we consider all people today as part of a global society and economy, the middle-class bulge is very modest: rural and industrial workers in poor countries far outnumber the billion or so middle-class consumers mostly in rich countries.

Transition from growth to descent requires rebuilding the trophic pyramid to suit local energy realities and social justice. The prevailing focus on economic rationalism is partly a recognition that we can no longer live by consumption and need to be more "productive," but economic measures of productivity are often ecologically irrational.

## Design Tension: Autonomy vs System Control

There is an inherent design tension between autonomy and higher-order system control. Each cell, organism, and population is as self-reliant as possible. This self-reliance at the smallest practical scale provides benefits to the larger system: internal selection for maximum power in cellular parts contributes to the general fitness of the larger system, and the failure of some parts due to external stress does not adversely affect resilience.

On the other hand, cells within an organism that grow and reproduce without control can be fatal. We call this cancer. Similarly, at all levels of nature, including ecosystems and the living planet, the larger system controls its constituent parts for the good of that system. We can think of a hierarchy of large-scale system controls that flow back down the energy hierarchy to constrain the exuberant life of smaller-scale systems.

The large-scale system controls are relatively harsh, and at times destructive. Natural disasters, predators, and parasites control plant and animal populations. The Gaia hypothesis has given us a scientific renewal of the idea of earth as a nurturing mother who maintains favourable conditions for the diversity and renewal of life, but is ruthlessly harsh to individual species in maintaining that balance. Understanding this harshness is essential for designers: it means that any system which relies on suppressing natural feedback rather than incorporating it will eventually face catastrophic correction.

## Self-Regulation as Evolutionary Achievement

One of the most important evolutionary responses of organisms to higher-order control is to develop internal self-regulation mechanisms, which control excessive growth or inappropriate behaviour before the harsher higher-order controls come into effect. For example, in a dam or pond the growth of fish and crustaceans is suppressed by their own wastes. This decreases the likelihood of disease or starvation killing all the fish.

Kangaroos and other marsupial herbivores can respond to seasonal conditions by slowing the development of embryos, and so partially regulate their numbers before poor feed favours diseases and predator control. These self-regulation mechanisms represent evolutionary sophistication.

Traditional societies had social and ethical constraints on population growth and resource use, which allowed communities and culture to persist over long periods without destroying the environment. Holmgren regards these self-controlling aspects of human culture as "the highest evolutionary development achieved by Homo sapiens." The ways in which we apply these abilities to controlling the excesses of growth and expansion over the next century will be the greatest test of our evolutionary sophistication.

## Application to Permaculture Design

The tripartite model has direct implications for designing productive systems:

- **Self-maintenance priority**: The first priority is survival — obtaining a yield from captured energy
- **Maintenance feedback**: The second priority is paying for what we get by maintaining future energy flows — planting, weeding, watering, fertilising
- **Contribution to the whole**: The third priority is contributing surplus to the wider system — giving away food and seed, maintaining seed-saving networks, supporting community

In garden design, this means choosing and organising elements that provide for their own needs rather than depending on constant inputs. Deep-rooted plants that access deep soil moisture, break open subsoil layers, and provide food for animals simultaneously fulfil all three roles of tripartite altruism. Dock grown in wet clay soils and eaten by goats both helps break up subsoils and feeds back energy to lower-order system providers.

## Personal Resource Allocation

Holmgren extends the tripartite model to guide personal and organisational resource allocation:

- One-third of time to providing for material needs
- One-third to self-development and reflection
- One-third to wider societal benefit

This provides a practical framework for individuals seeking to balance self-reliance with community contribution — a challenge that modern economic structures fail to address through appropriate feedback signals. The modern economy, despite its complexity, fails to provide critically important feedback signals to ensure appropriate behaviour.

## Failure of Self-Regulation by Elites

A recurring problem across human history is that elites begin to believe the whole social ecosystem exists for their benefit. This corrupts the process of acknowledging higher power and fails to provide functional feedback control to keep the system healthy. The breakdown cascades back through the social hierarchy until corruption at all levels leads to breakdown and reform.

The problem is exacerbated when energy abundance creates many hierarchical levels occupied by fallible humans who lack the wisdom their positions require. Permaculture could be described as "ecological rationalism" — it recognises the design rules and measures of value in energy descent and provides ethical pathways for embracing those rules.

## The Modern Economy as Failed Tripartite System

In modern economies people "earn a living" in diverse ways — salaried work, welfare, business, speculation. They use money to pay for goods and services, since they have nothing directly useful to give the producers or suppliers; and in the process, they pay some of that money in taxation to ensure the wider society continues to function. All these examples broadly reflect Odum's tripartite altruism.

However, the modern economy fails to provide critically important feedback signals to ensure appropriate behaviour. The questions it fails to answer include: how much is enough to consume? How much work is enough? How should we pay and how do we measure it? What should we contribute to the greater good?

With the progressive breakdown of many traditional and institutional functions, each person needs to consider how to answer these questions, since the current social and economic structures are clearly failing to provide appropriate and credible feedback and guidance. The tripartite model offers a starting framework for this self-examination.

## Connections to Permaculture Ethics

Tripartite altruism provides an energetic and ecological foundation for the three permaculture ethics. Care for the Earth corresponds to feedback to lower-order system providers — maintaining the soil, water, and biodiversity that sustain all life. Care for People corresponds to self-maintenance at the appropriate scale — providing for oneself and one's community. Fair Share corresponds to contribution to higher-order system controllers and redistribution beyond one's immediate circle.

This mapping is not exact but it is suggestive. The energetic reality is that systems which allocate their energy across all three functions tend to persist, while those that neglect any one function tend to decline. This provides an empirical, testable basis for ethical behaviour rather than relying solely on moral argument.

The more power available to a civilisation, the more critical ethics become in ensuring long-term survival. Holmgren treats the ethics not as abstract moral principles but as practical design constraints that emerge from energetic realities. This ecologically functional view of ethics makes them central to the development of a culture for energy descent.
