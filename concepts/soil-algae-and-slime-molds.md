## Algae in Soil

### Overview and Classification

Algae are a diverse group of photosynthetic organisms found across marine,
freshwater, and terrestrial environments. They are **photoautotrophic**, meaning
they manufacture their own food using sunlight, carbon dioxide, and water. Unlike
higher plants, algae possess no true roots, leaves, or stems — they are
structurally simple yet remarkably adaptable. Soil-dwelling algae colonize the
surfaces of soil particles, rock fragments, and plant litter, thriving in moist,
well-lit environments such as cultivated fields and recently disturbed soils.

### Diatoms

Diatoms are a major group of algae characterized by their intricate silica-based
cell walls (frustules). These glass-like shells persist long after the organism
dies, and over geological time scales, vast accumulations form **diatomaceous
earth** — a fine, porous sediment used in agriculture, filtration, and pest
control. In soil, living diatoms contribute to primary production and return
silica and other nutrients to the soil matrix upon death.

### Algae as Pioneer Organisms

Algae are among the first organisms to colonize bare rock and mineral surfaces,
earning them the designation of **pioneer organisms**. Through **carbonic acid
weathering** — algae release CO₂ during respiration, which dissolves in water to
form carbonic acid that slowly dissolves rock minerals — they break parent rock
into the mineral particles that become the foundation of soil. As algal cells die
and decompose, they also contribute the first layers of organic matter to
nascent soils.

### Lichens: Algae-Fungi Symbiosis

Lichens represent one of the most successful symbiotic partnerships in nature —
a close association between algae (or cyanobacteria) and fungi. The fungal
partner provides structure and mineral extraction, while the algal partner
supplies carbohydrates through photosynthesis. Lichens are powerful agents of
biological weathering: they secrete **organic acids** (such as oxalic acid) that
chemically dissolve rock surfaces far more aggressively than carbonic acid
alone. When cyanobacteria serve as the photosynthetic partner, lichens also
contribute **fixed nitrogen** to developing soil — a scarce resource on bare
substrates. Lichens can colonize extremely harsh environments, making them
indispensable pioneers in soil genesis.

### Blue-Green Algae (Cyanophyta)

Blue-green algae, more accurately known as **cyanobacteria**, are photosynthetic
bacteria that perform oxygenic photosynthesis. Their [[lichen-biology-and-ecological-significance]] is
enormous: they are the only photosynthetic organisms capable of fixing
atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) into ammonium (NH₄⁺), using the enzyme **nitrogenase**
within specialized cells called heterocysts. Cyanobacteria are particularly
important in rice paddies (contributing significant nitrogen) and in arid lands
where [[biological-soil-crusts]] dominated by cyanobacteria stabilize surface soils
against erosion.

### Soil Structure and Algal Polysaccharides

One of the most underappreciated contributions of soil algae is their role in
soil aggregation. Algal cells secrete **polysaccharide-rich mucilage** — a
sticky, gelatinous substance that coats soil particles and binds them into
stable aggregates. This mucilage enhances **soil structure** by gluing fine
particles into erosion-resistant crumbs, forms microscopic **air passageways**
that improve aeration and water infiltration, and retains moisture that creates
favorable microhabitats for other microorganisms.

### Algae in the Soil Food Web

As primary producers, algae form the base of several soil food chains. They are
grazed upon by **nematodes**, protozoa, and microscopic arthropods, converting
solar energy captured at the soil surface into animal biomass and, ultimately,
into nutrients available to plants through the microbial loop.

## Slime Molds in Soil

### Overview and Classification

Slime molds occupy a taxonomic position at the intersection of fungi and
protozoa. They are heterotrophic, consuming bacteria, yeasts, and organic
particles. Two major groups are found in soil:

- **Dictyosteliomycota (cellular slime molds)**: Individual amoeboid cells
  remain separate throughout most of their life cycle but aggregate into a
  mobile, multicellular structure when food becomes scarce.
- **Myxomycota (plasmodial slime molds)**: Individual cells fuse to form a
  single large, multinucleate mass called a **plasmodium** — a creeping,
  streaming structure that can cover considerable areas as it feeds.

### The Myxamoeba Stage and Swarming

Both groups begin life as **myxamoebae** — small, amoeba-like cells that crawl
through soil films and leaf litter, feeding on bacteria and organic debris.
When food is exhausted, cellular slime molds exhibit remarkable swarming
behavior: individual myxamoebae emit **chemical attractant signals** (cyclic AMP
in *Dictyostelium*), creating concentric trails that draw cells together. Up to
**125,000 individual cells** can aggregate into a single pseudoplasmodium or
plasmodium. This mass migrates as a coordinated unit toward light or heat,
eventually forming a **fruiting structure** (sporangium) that elevates spores
above the substrate for dispersal.

### The Physarum Genus and Ecological Roles

The genus *Physarum* includes some of the most visually striking slime molds.
*[[physarum-polycephalum]]*, the "many-headed slime mold," is famous for its
extensive vein-like network of protoplasmic streaming channels, its ability to
solve simple spatial problems and find optimal routes through mazes, and its
bright yellow coloration on decaying wood and leaf litter.

Despite their unusual biology, slime molds serve essential ecological functions:
- **Nutrient cycling**: By consuming vast numbers of bacteria, slime molds
  mineralize [[fungal-bacterial-biomass-ratio]] and release nitrogen and phosphorus in
  plant-available forms.
- **Soil particle binding**: As plasmodia creep through soil pores, their
  mucilaginous secretions bind particles together, contributing to aggregate
  stability much like algal polysaccharides.
- **Food web integration**: Slime molds serve as prey for nematodes, springtails,
  and other microfauna, linking bacterial production to higher trophic levels.

## Summary

| Organism Group | Key Roles in Soil |
|---|---|
| Green algae | Primary production, carbonic acid weathering, mucilage secretion |
| Cyanobacteria | Nitrogen fixation, [[singh-mycelial-mats-and-biological-soil-crusts]], [[lichens-biogeochemistry-and-pioneer-colonization]] |
| Diatoms | Silica cycling, primary production, [[diatomaceous-earth]] formation |
| Lichens | Rock weathering, nitrogen input, organic matter accumulation |
| Cellular slime molds | Bacterial grazing, chemical signaling, spore dispersal |
| Plasmodial slime molds | [[nutrient-mineralization]], soil particle binding, network formation |

Together, soil [[algae-and-slime-molds]] represent foundational but frequently
unrecognized members of the soil microbiome, contributing to everything from
initial rock weathering to ongoing nutrient cycling, structural stabilization,
and support of complex food webs.
