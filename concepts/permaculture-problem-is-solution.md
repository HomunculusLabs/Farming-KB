# Permaculture Problem Is the Solution

"The problem is the solution" is one of the most widely recognised permaculture maxims, expressing the idea that things are not always as they seem and that the elements we view negatively may have a positive aspect that is more important than, or at least compensates for, the predominant negative perception. david holmgren explores this principle extensively in [[permaculture-principle-observe-and-interact]] of Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability.

## Reframing Problems as Resources

The most common examples of "the problem is the solution" relate to weeds or other lifeforms regarded as pests, which may actually be positive. Weeds or pests may be environmental indicators of the need for management change, agents repairing damaged soil or other degraded conditions, or resources which, for economic or cultural reasons, we fail to value.

Pests can be viewed as the surpluses of nature, which need to be used rather than destroyed. This idea of pests as surplus natural abundance that we can use has been a major theme in Holmgren's work over many years. When we shift from seeing a pest as a problem to be eliminated to seeing it as an unused resource, entirely new design possibilities emerge.

## Environmental Indicators

Many so-called weed species are actually environmental indicators that reveal soil conditions, water patterns, or management deficiencies. Thistles often indicate compacted or overgrazed soil. Dandelions indicate low calcium and high potassium. Rushes indicate waterlogging. Rather than fighting the indicator species, the permaculture approach is to read the message they carry and address the underlying condition.

This reframing transforms a control problem into a diagnostic opportunity. The weed is not the problem; it is the messenger. Eliminating the messenger without addressing the underlying condition ensures the message will keep being delivered, often by more persistent messengers. This is a direct application of [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]].

## Agents of Repair

Many pioneer species that are regarded as invasive weeds are actually performing essential ecological repair work. Blackberries on disturbed stream banks are stabilising soil, filtering water, and building organic matter. Gorse and broom on degraded hillsides are fixing nitrogen and creating the conditions for more diverse plant communities to establish. Willows in modified waterways are capturing sediment and nutrients that would otherwise be lost to the system.

The riparian weedscapes of Hepburn Springs provided a case study where blackberry and gorse were recognised as successional pioneers that were building the conditions for a more diverse and stable forest community. Research confirmed that willows along Spring Creek were capturing forty times more sediment and ten times more phosphorus than eucalypts, making them extraordinarily effective water filtration systems.

## Cultural and Economic Barriers to Use

Often the real barrier to using so-called pests as resources is cultural and economic rather than technical. Many useful plants are ignored because they are considered weeds, or because processing them requires knowledge and skills that have been lost. The economic systems that drive modern agriculture have no mechanism for valuing the ecosystem services provided by pioneer species, only for the commodities they produce.

Holmgren notes that suggestions that the majority of the population may be addicted to using their cars are often dismissed, yet a complete social and environmental audit of car transport can show the damage is deep and long-term. The problem of car dependency is actually the solution: it reveals the degree to which our settlement patterns and economic systems need redesign.

## Elegant Solutions Are Simple and Invisible

In science, the simplest answer that explains all the facts is regarded as having more validity than a complex answer. Similarly in design, enormous complexity often indicates poor design. A really effective design solution may be remarkably simple. This simplicity may be inherent, or it may arise because self-organising living complexity that works without us understanding or controlling it is doing most of the job.

Really effective systems may work so well that we do not even notice them. This is common with free environmental services, such as purification of air and water or soil rehabilitation, and good design solutions that go unnoticed until they fail from abuse. The saying "we don't know what we've got till it's gone" is relevant here. Careful observation and a respect for humble lifeforms and processes is a partial antidote for this perennial problem.

## Make the Smallest Intervention Necessary

In attempting to adjust systems to fix problems, we need to be careful that we do not damage or disrupt other processes that are working perfectly. Because much effective design is invisible, large-scale interventions are likely to do more harm than good, and they require large amounts of energy and resources to implement.

Japanese natural farmer Masanobu Fukuoka has written eloquently about the value of doing nothing and the damage from large-scale intervention in nature. The Bradley method of bush regeneration, based on careful observation and minimal interventions, has been acknowledged as highly effective in conserving bushland from the spread of environmental weeds. Its focus on minimal disturbance to remove weeds from the most intact areas of native vegetation runs counter to the prevailing approach of attacking weeds head-on with herbicide and other high-impact methods.

## Avoiding Too Much of a Good Thing

When we experience a positive result from an action, there is almost always a powerful temptation to repeat the action, working on the often misguided idea that if some is good, more must be better. In sustainable societies and nature, limited resources often acted as a natural constraint on such actions, but in the modern world these temptations are everywhere.

Any gardener or farmer who has had a great harvest following use of a fertiliser knows the temptation to use more next time. The slowing in growth of world food yields, despite increases in fertiliser use, shows the problem is widespread. Ethical constraints on greed are part of the antidote to the problems created by too much of a good thing.

## Success or Function in One System May Represent Failure in Connected Systems

Many of these statements that caution against bold and impulsive action reflect a general observation: that success or function in any system may represent failure in connected systems that are larger or smaller in scale. This systemic perspective is fundamental to permaculture thinking. A weed-free garden may represent failure in the larger system if it required herbicides that contaminate waterways. A high-yielding crop may represent failure if the yield was achieved by mining soil fertility.

## Open Inquiry Versus Urgent Solutions

An open, inquiring attitude to problems is almost always more fruitful than an urgent demand for solutions. The latter is often driven by fear and an unquestioned consensus about the nature of the problem. Taking time to understand the problem deeply, to see it from multiple perspectives, and to recognise the resources it contains, leads to more creative and effective solutions.

Another aspect of this maxim is that the best solutions to problems can be found in places and cultures where the problem is extreme. In these situations, co-evolution over time will have developed the best responses. In places where the design problem is less severe, people often ignore it or come up with solutions that use more effort or resources to overcome the problem than necessary.

## From Problem to Design Opportunity

The ultimate expression of "the problem is the solution" in permaculture design is the transformation of perceived problems into the organising principles of a design. A steep, erosion-prone slope is not a problem to be terraced at great expense; it is an opportunity to use gravity for water distribution through keyline-design|keyline systems. A weed-infested property is not a liability; it is evidence of active soil-building processes that can be guided toward productive ends. A limited water supply is not a constraint; it is a design parameter that leads to more efficient and resilient systems.

This reframing is not mere optimism. It is a rigorous application of [[permaculture-reading-landscape]] skills and ecological understanding. The problems we perceive are real, but they are also information. They tell us something about the system we are working with. The permaculture designer's task is to read that information and use it as the foundation for design rather than fighting against it.

## Incremental Adjustment and Learning

Planning and design processes, like life in general, often involve incremental adjustment in response to experience. When working with complex natural systems we need to remember that we do not understand, let alone control, all the factors, and that cause and effect are often a loop or a web rather than a linear chain. When you try some action, do not assume you were the reason for any success. Conduct small trials and think about other possible causes for success or failure.

This humility in the face of complexity is essential for applying "the problem is the solution" effectively. It requires the patience to observe before acting, the willingness to be surprised, and the discipline to accept feedback even when it contradicts our expectations.
