# Molecular Fungal Taxonomy

## Overview

Molecular methods have revolutionized fungal taxonomy and systematics, providing tools for identifying species, resolving phylogenetic relationships, and discovering cryptic diversity invisible to morphological approaches. DNA-based methods are now essential for fungal biodiversity studies, complementing but not replacing traditional morphological taxonomy.

Fungal molecular systematics has relied on PCR amplification and characterization (restriction digestion, sequencing) of discrete genomic DNA targets. Molecular characters are especially important for arthropod-associated fungi where morphological convergence and lack of known teleomorphs obscure phylogenetic relationships. The sensitivity of PCR allows molecular characters from minute quantities of non-culturable types.

## Nuclear Genome Markers

Fungal nuclear genomes are large, ranging from approximately 1.5 x 10^7 bp to 820 x 10^7 bp, representing thousands of genetic loci containing both single-copy and repetitive DNA. The development of PCR has increased sensitivity so that genetic diversity can be assessed using very small tissue samples, greatly decreasing the need for lethal sampling or culturing. This has been transformative for studying [[fungal-speciation-evolution]] and [[fungal-biodiversity-measurement-methods]] in ecological contexts where destructive sampling is undesirable.

## Arbitrarily Primed PCR and RAPD Methods

Arbitrarily primed PCR (apPCR), also known as Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA (RAPD) analysis, uses short oligonucleotide primers (10-20 bp) that anneal to complementary DNA sequences at low stringency. A panel of 10-20 primers can generate 100 or more genetic markers in a single experiment, providing a rapid fingerprinting approach.

In fungi, 80-100% of DNA products are shared among conspecifics (individuals of the same species), while different species share only 0-20%. This stark contrast enables unequivocal taxonomic identification in many cases, making RAPD a useful screening tool for [[fungal-molecular-identification]].

However, significant reproducibility issues exist. RAPD results are sensitive to thermocycler accuracy -- some machines never reach their programmed denaturing temperatures, leading to inconsistent banding patterns between laboratories. Standardization of reaction conditions and equipment calibration are essential for reliable inter-study comparisons.

## rRNA/rDNA Region Comparisons

Ribosomes are present in all cellular organisms and share a common evolutionary origin. Some rRNA sequences are sufficiently conserved to serve as alignment reference points across broad phylogenetic distances, while other regions are variable enough to discriminate among closely related taxa. These properties make rRNA/rDNA the cornerstone of [[fungal-dna-barcoding]] and [[fungal-phylogeny-systematics]].

### 25S/28S rRNA

The large subunit rRNA (25S in some yeasts, 28S in filamentous fungi) was first used for yeast systematics by Bicknell and Douglas (1970). It provides intermediate phylogenetic resolution and remains widely used in multilocus phylogenetic studies.

### 18S and 26S rRNA

The small subunit (18S) and large subunit (26S) rRNA genes are now preferred over 5S for phylogenetic analysis due to their richer information content. They provide reliable resolution across a broad range of taxonomic depths, from kingdom-level relationships to family and genus-level classification within the [[fungal-kingdom-classification]].

### 5S rRNA

The 5S rRNA gene is conservative and small (~120 nucleotides). In Ascomycetes, 5S sequences resolve three major groups: (1) Schizosaccharomyces and Protomyces, (2) budding yeasts, and (3) filamentous fungi. While historically important, its limited length and information content have reduced its utility relative to 18S and 26S for modern phylogenetics.

### 5.8S rRNA

The 5.8S rRNA is approximately 160 nucleotides in length and, like the 18S and 26S genes, contains modified nucleotides. It is located between the two ITS spacers and is sometimes included in multilocus phylogenetic analyses.

### ITS Region

The Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) region, comprising ITS1, the 5.8S gene, and ITS2, is used extensively for species-level identification across the fungal kingdom. It has been proposed as the universal DNA barcode for fungi due to its high interspecific variability and broad PCR amplification success.

### D1/D2 Domains of 26S

The D1 and D2 domains at the 5-prime end of the 26S large subunit rDNA are particularly useful for species discrimination in yeasts. Sequencing these domains has become a standard method for yeast identification in clinical and ecological settings.

## RFLP Analysis

Restriction-fragment-length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis exploits the fact that rDNAs occur in multiple copies within fungal genomes, lending themselves to restriction digestion and pattern comparison. RFLP analysis of rDNA can recognize individual species and strains, and was widely used before direct sequencing became routine. It remains useful for rapid screening of large sample sets in [[soil-fungal-diversity]] surveys and [[fungal-inventory-methods]].

## Mitochondrial DNA Analysis

Fungal mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is relatively small (20-80 kb) compared to the nuclear genome. mtDNA is transmitted maternally (in most fungi) and evolves at a different rate than nuclear DNA, making it useful for resolving phylogenetic differences between closely related species. The combination of nuclear and mitochondrial markers provides complementary perspectives on fungal [[fungal-genetics]] and evolutionary history.

## DNA Barcoding

### Ribosomal RNA Genes

The nuclear ribosomal DNA (rDNA) region is the most widely used molecular marker for fungal identification and phylogeny. It contains the small subunit (SSU or 18S), internal transcribed spacer (ITS), 5.8S, and large subunit (LSU or 28S) regions. The ITS region has been proposed as the universal DNA barcode for fungi because it offers the best species-level resolution across the kingdom.

### Genes and Regions in Common Use

- **18S rDNA (SSU)**: Broad phylogenetic resolution; useful for higher-level classification across all fungi
- **ITS (ITS1 + 5.8S + ITS2)**: Standard barcode region; best species-level discrimination for most fungal groups
- **28S rDNA (LSU)**: Intermediate resolution; useful for order-to-family level phylogenetics
- **Beta-tubulin**: Protein-coding gene; useful for species-level resolution in some groups
- **Translation elongation factor 1-alpha (TEF1)**: Protein-coding gene; useful for species delimitation
- **RNA polymerase II subunits (RPB1, RPB2)**: Useful for deep phylogenetic studies

## Applications in Fungal Taxonomy

### Species Delimitation

Molecular data have revealed extensive cryptic speciation in fungi. Morphologically identical isolates from different hosts or geographic regions often represent genetically distinct species. Conversely, morphologically variable fungi may represent a single widespread species. For arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, selective rDNA primer sequences in PCR reactions have been used to delimit new genera (Archaeospora, Paraglomus) based on sequence divergence, refining our understanding of the [[fungal-species-concept]].

### Phylogenetic Reconstruction

Molecular phylogenies have reshaped our understanding of fungal evolution. Key findings include:

- Chytridiomycota as basal fungi, consistent with a flagellated ancestor
- Basidiomycota and Ascomycota forming a monophyletic crown group
- Traditional Zygomycota being polyphyletic rather than monophyletic
- Glomales (AM fungi) representing a distinct lineage separate from other Zygomycota
- Some Acaulospora and Glomus species being more closely related to Geosiphon pyriformis than to other AM fungi

### Environmental DNA and Metabarcoding

Culture-independent methods using environmental DNA (eDNA) allow detection of fungi that cannot be cultured or observed directly. Soil DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing of ITS amplicons reveal fungal diversity far exceeding that captured by traditional methods. This approach has been particularly important for documenting soil fungal communities, [[endophytic-fungal-diversity]], and root-associated fungi.

## In Situ Molecular Methods

In situ PCR -- DNA amplification on 5-10 mm sections of fixed, embedded material -- shows great potential for molecular analysis of endosymbiotic fungi. This technique allows researchers to identify fungi within host tissues without extracting DNA, preserving spatial relationships between the fungus and its host.

## DNA Extraction Challenges

Molecular systematics faces practical challenges in DNA extraction, particularly for arthropod-associated fungi. A recommended protocol involves crushing 3-5 thalli dry between silanized microscope slides, collecting the homogenate in sterile water, and using it directly in PCR. However, coamplification of insect host DNA may require cloning of PCR products to isolate the fungal sequences of interest. For some groups, such as melanized spores, standard extraction protocols may fail entirely.

## Limitations and Challenges

- **Incomplete taxonomic reference databases**: Most fungal species lack sequence data in public databases, limiting identification
- **Intraspecific variation**: ITS sequences may vary within species, complicating species delimitation
- **Fungal DNA extraction difficulties**: Some fungi (e.g., melanized spores) resist standard extraction protocols
- **Non-orthologous ITS copies**: Some fungi harbor divergent ITS paralogs within a single genome
- **Uneven taxon sampling**: Fewer than 800 fungi have been included in any single comprehensive phylogenetic reconstruction -- less than 1% of described species
- **Reproducibility of PCR-based methods**: Thermocycler variation and reaction conditions can affect consistency across laboratories

## Integration with Traditional Taxonomy

Molecular data are most powerful when integrated with morphological, ecological, and cultural data. DNA studies have validated many traditional taxonomic decisions but have also required reclassification of numerous taxa. All taxonomic decisions are hypotheses that are tested repeatedly and sometimes refuted by new molecular evidence.

## Future Directions

Expanded sequencing efforts, improved reference databases, and cheaper high-throughput methods will continue to transform fungal taxonomy. The gap between described species (approximately 74,000-120,000) and estimated total diversity (1.5 million+) means molecular methods have enormous potential for discovery. Standardized barcoding protocols will enable large-scale comparative studies across geographic regions and ecosystem types.
