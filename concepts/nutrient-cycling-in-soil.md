## Nutrient Cycling in Soil

updated: 2026-04-18

Nutrient cycling is the continuous process by which nutrients are converted
between organic and inorganic forms, moved through the soil, taken up by
plants, and returned to the soil through decomposition. Understanding these
cycles is essential for the organic gardener, because the

temporary nitrogen deficiency in plants. The solution is to either compost

## The Nitrogen Cycle in Organic Systems

The nitrogen cycle is perhaps the most complex and consequential nutrient
cycle in soil. Understanding it is critical because nitrogen is the nutrient
most often limiting plant
growth.
**[[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]] fixation by
[[actinorhizal-symbiosis-frankia]] bacteria. Rhizobia form symbiotic
relationships with legumes (soybeans, clover, alfalfa, peas, beans), forming
nodules on roots where they convert atmospheric nitrogen into ammonium. The
plant provides sugars to the bacteria in exchange. Up to 20 percent excess
nitrogen is produced and moves into the surrounding soil. Frankia perform a
similar function with non-legume plants like alder and
bayberry.
Free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria such as Azotobacter and Azospirillum also
contribute, though they produce less nitrogen than the symbiotic forms. About
50 to 60 percent of available nitrogen is produced by biological systems, with
the rest coming from industrial fixation through the Haber-Bosch process,
which requires 5 percent of the world's natural gas
production.
**Ammonification:** When plants die and decompose, the nitrogen in their
proteins and nucleic acids is converted to ammonium (NH4+) by bacteria and
fungi. Some of this ammonium is taken up by plants
directly.
**Nitrification:** Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrite (NO2-) and
then to nitrate (NO3-). This process is temperature dependent -- the microbes
involved are active between 41F (5C) and 95F (35C), with peak production
