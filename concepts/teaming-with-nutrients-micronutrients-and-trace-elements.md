## Micronutrients and Trace Elements

Although plants require only trace amounts of micronutrients, these eight elements are every bit as essential as the macronutrients. Per Von Liebig's Law of the Minimum, if even the least-used nutrient is absent, no amount of the others will compensate. The micronutrients are boron (B), chlorine (Cl), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), zinc (Zn), molybdenum (Mo), and nickel (Ni).

## Boron (B)

Boron connects carbohydrate chains in cell walls and is necessary for pollen tube formation. It balances sugar versus starch levels, translocates both throughout the plant, and helps transport potassium ions across cell membranes to regulate stomata. Boron is the only mineral nutrient that does not need to be ionic to enter plant cells -- it is taken up as boric acid (B[OH]3) using diffusion, facilitative transport, and [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]], as the conversion of atmospheric nitrogen and nitrate relies on iron-containing enzymes. Plants have developed a system to release ions into soil to lower pH and prevent iron from becoming unavailable. Iron is immobile in plants -- deficiency causes chlorosis (yellowing) in young leaves while veins remain green. Iron is common in most soils but precipitates as insoluble oxides at neutral to alkaline pH.

## Manganese (Mn)

Manganese frees oxygen during photosynthesis by accepting electrons from water and works with enzymes to break apart carbohydrates. The water-splitting complex at photosystem II contains a cluster of four manganese atoms essential for extracting electrons from water. Manganese is not mobile in plants. Deficiency shows interveinal chlorosis (yellow leaves with green veins). Availability is strongly pH-dependent, becoming unavailable above pH 6.5.

## Zinc (Zn)

Zinc is a component of many enzymes involved in oxidation-reduction reactions. It helps produce auxins (main growth hormone), synthesizes chlorophyll and carbohydrates, activates enzymes for RNA/DNA synthesis, and helps cells withstand cold temperatures. Zinc is mobile in plants -- deficiency causes slow/no growth, lack of stem elongation, and yellowing. Classic symptoms include "little leaf" in younger leaves. Excessive phosphorus can induce zinc deficiency.

Soil microbes produce siderophores — high-affinity iron-chelating compounds — that solubilize iron from mineral oxides and make it available to both the microorganisms themselves and to plant roots. This biological iron acquisition system is far more efficient than chemical iron supplementation in alkaline soils, where iron rapidly precipitates as insoluble hydroxides and oxides. ## Micronutrient Toxicity

While deficiency receives more attention, micronutrient toxicity can be equally damaging. Boron toxicity causes leaf tip necrosis and is common in arid regions where irrigation water contains high boron concentrations. Copper toxicity is a concern in soils with a history of copper fungicide application, particularly in vineyards and orchards — it can persist in soil for decades and suppress beneficial microbial activity. Manganese toxicity in acid soils causes oxidative damage to plant tissues and is a significant limitation on crop production in tropical and subtropical regions. Nickel toxicity occurs naturally on serpentine soils, supporting unique plant communities adapted to high heavy-metal concentrations.
