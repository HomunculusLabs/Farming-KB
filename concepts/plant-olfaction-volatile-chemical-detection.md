## Plant Olfaction — How Plants Detect Chemical Signals

Plants possess a sophisticated ability to detect [[plant-olfaction-volatile-chemical-perception]] signals in
their environment, a capacity that [[daniel-chamovitz]] explores in Chapter 2 of
"What a Plant Knows." Plant olfaction, while fundamentally different from
animal smell, enables plants to perceive neighboring organisms, respond to
herbivore attacks, coordinate ripening, and engage in complex ecological
interactions through airborne [[chamovitz-plant-scent-communication-volatile-organic-compounds]] (VOCs) through receptor proteins in
their cell membranes. When a volatile molecule binds to these receptors, it
triggers intracellular signaling cascades that alter gene expression and
physiological responses. Unlike animals, which have specialized olfactory
organs, plants detect smells across their entire surface — leaves, stems, and
roots all possess chemosensory capabilities.

The plant equivalent of an olfactory receptor is typically a membrane-bound
protein that undergoes a conformational change when it binds a specific
[[reactive-oxygen-species-and-oxidative-stress]],
and phytohormones like ethylene, [[jasmonic-acid-plant-defense-signaling]], and [[comparison-jasmonate-vs-salicylic-acid-defense-signaling]].

## Key Examples of Plant Olfaction

### Fruit Ripening Coordination

One of the most well-documented examples of plant olfaction is the role of
ethylene in fruit ripening. When one fruit begins to ripen, it releases
ethylene gas, which is detected by neighboring fruits. This triggers a
cascade of ripening in the surrounding fruit, ensuring synchronized
ripening. This is why placing a ripe banana in a paper bag with unripe
avocados accelerates the avocado ripening process — the avocado is
"smelling" the ethylene from the banana.

### Herbivore Detection

When a plant is attacked by herbivores, it releases specific volatile
compounds that serve dual functions. These compounds can directly repel the
herbivore, but they also serve as airborne signals to neighboring plants.
Undamaged plants that detect these volatile cues activate their own defense
responses preemptively, producing defensive compounds before the herbivore
reaches them. This was first convincingly demonstrated in sagebrush and
tobacco plants, where clipped sagebrush neighbors showed increased
herbivore resistance compared to control plants.

### Parasitic Plant Host Detection

Perhaps the most dramatic example of plant olfaction is found in parasitic
plants like dodder (Cuscuta spp.). Dodder seedlings cannot photosynthesize
and must locate a host plant within days of germination. They do this by
"sniffing out" the volatile profiles of potential hosts, growing preferentially
toward plants that emit attractive compounds. Dodder can distinguish between
different host species and even between healthy and stressed plants, choosing
the most suitable host based on its chemical scent alone.

## Root-Level Olfaction

Plant roots also detect chemical signals in the soil environment. Root
exudates serve as chemical signals that mediate plant-plant interactions,
plant-microbe communication, and belowground ecological relationships:

- **Strigolactones**: Released by roots to attract [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], but
  paradoxically also trigger germination of parasitic witchweed seeds.
- **Allelochemicals**: Some plants release compounds that inhibit the
  growth of competing species through root-level chemical detection.
- **Microbe signaling**: Roots detect [[fungi-in-the-environment-symbiotic-relationships]].

## Molecular Basis of Volatile Detection

Research has identified several families of plant proteins involved in
volatile detection:

- **Ethylene receptors**: Members of the two-component [[gadd-two-component-signalling-histidine-kinase-fungi]]
  family that bind ethylene and regulate ripening, senescence, and stress
  responses.
- **Jasmonate receptors**: The COI1-JAZ co-receptor complex detects
  jasmonic acid and its volatile derivative jasmonyl-isoleucine,
  mediating defense against herbivores and necrotrophic pathogens.
- **Unknown VOC receptors**: Many plant volatile responses are clearly
  demonstrated but the specific receptor proteins remain unidentified,
  representing an active area of research.
