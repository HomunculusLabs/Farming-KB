# Plant Nutrients and Mineral Sources

## Overview

The health of plants, animals, and the environment depends on the balance of elements available in soils. Understanding primary nutrients, their sources, and how they move through soil profiles is essential for sustainable food production and land management. Permaculture designers must understand both natural mineral cycles and practical strategies for maintaining soil fertility.

## Primary Nutrients for Plants

### Phosphorus (Phosphates)

Soils in heavily leached areas are often deficient in phosphates. Sources include:

- **Guano**: Bird manures from dry islands; a concentrated phosphate source
- **Sedimentary rock deposits**: Phosphatic rock containing 8-15% phosphorus in various combinations with oxygen or water (hydrated)
- **Bird manures**: Phosphate concentrated by seed-eating birds can be locally produced if birds are plentiful and their manures are used

**Warning**: Phosphates mined from marine guano may contain concentrated levels of cadmium and uranium, taken up by oceanic fish and shellfish used by marine bird colonies. Continual heavy use of such resources is likely to become polluting to soils. The ethical strategy is to use just enough and conserve locally.

### Potassium (Potash)

Potash occurs in the mineral kainite, formed in areas of evaporated waters. Desert salts usually contain 20-25% potash. Sources include:

- **Burnt plant material**: Ash from wood fires
- **Rotted plants and compost**: Potash released through decomposition
- **Mineral deposits**: Large reserves exist in common minerals like orthoclase (a major constituent of granite)
- **Evaporite deposits**: Bitterns (see below)

### Nitrogen

Nitrogen can be supplied by several biological mechanisms:

- **Rhizobia-inoculated plants**: Water or land plants inoculated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria
- **Algae and water plants**: Species such as Scirpus or Azolla fix atmospheric nitrogen
- **Nitrogen-fixing trees**: Acacia, Albizzia, and Eleagnus are notable examples

Much higher levels of nitrogen than previously thought are fixed by land plants in a series of zones extending from the roots. Even after nitrogenous plants such as Acacia, Albizzia, and Eleagnus are cut, the root zone continues to release nitrogen for up to 6 years.

This means pioneer legumes or nitrogenous trees serve as cover crops for subsequent trees, releasing nitrogen during their lifetimes and for some years after. Legumes may not be needed in older forests and typically die out under canopy. Only a few larger leguminous trees (Samanea, Acacia melanoxylon) persist as forest trees in mixed forests.

## Mineral Sources for Soils

### The Sea

As soils are leached and made mineral-poor, these minerals become concentrated in the sea, in marine organisms, or in inland saltpans. Seaweeds, seagrasses, and fish residues have always been part of agricultural fertilisers.

As seawater evaporates, minerals separate in sequence:
1. **First**: Calcite and dolomite—used for soil conditioning and pH adjustment
2. **Next**: Gypsum and anhydrite—used to restore soil crumb structure
3. **Then**: Rock salts—common nutrient, though only wet tropical uplands may actually lack this
4. **Finally**: Potash, magnesium salts, and minor elements remain

### Bitterns

The liquid remaining after common salt deposition is a rich source of minor minerals and trace elements, sold commercially as "bitterns" (bitter oily fluids). Applications include:

- Dilution and incorporation in crop soils
- Foliar sprays at strengths of 1-20 litres per hectare (diluted 100-500:1)
- Source of bromine, early periodic table elements, and rare minerals

**Handling precautions**: Bitterns are very corrosive and must be held and distributed only via non-corrosive vessels and pipes (polyethylene pipes and drums).

Bitterns are cheap and easily transported to leached areas, but their effects must be established by local trials on specific crops. Because evaporites are so easily dissolved, they are also those most likely to be carried to sea in rains.

### Rocks and Rock Dusts

- **Granites**: Contain felspars yielding potash or sodium salts
- **Limestone and dolomite**: Yield calcium and magnesium
- **Mineral deposits/ores**: Provide traces of basic minor elements

#### Priority of Mineral Needs

1. **Calcium** (in all but highly calcareous areas): Most commonly needed
2. **Dolomite** (except where magnesium is already in high ratio): Next priority
3. **Phosphates and felspars**: Follow calcium needs
4. **Trace elements**: In small quantities (as low as 5-7 kg per 100 hectares for zinc, copper, cobalt, and molybdenum)

#### Effectiveness of Rock Dusts

Field trials have established that cheap ores, finely ground, are as effective as more refined sulphates or oxides. Minerals can be given to animals as salt licks, in molasses, in water, or as slow-release pellets (cobalt "bullets" that lodge in the rumen). Foliar sprays are more rapid-acting and effective than soil application for trace elements.

#### Specific Rock Dusts

- **Basalt dusts**: Helpful on leached tropical soils
- **Rock phosphate**: Contains 8-15% phosphorus but is very slow to release nutrient; may be absorbed completely onto leached clays and clay-loams
- **Super-acid phosphate**: May be necessary added to compost or compost plants under highly leached conditions

**Caution**: Rock dust as an unselective category can do as much harm as good on soils, adding excessive or poisonous nutrients in some cases, or excessive micronutrients. Always match rock dust selection to local soil deficiencies.

## Distribution of Elements in the Soil Profile

The concentration of elements or nutrients through the soil profile (the vertical column from surface to approximately 2 metres deep) is determined by two important factors:

### Water Penetration

Water is a universal solvent that enables compounds to dissociate into ions and transports them to various deposits at microsites in the soil. This effect works in three dimensions:

1. **Infiltration**: Water travels from the surface downward, soaking in at varying depths
2. **Capillary rise**: Water can rise from the soil water table upward by capillary action
3. **Lateral movement**: Water moves sideways through soil layers of different permeability

### Soil Catena

Soil types change predictably with position in the landscape:

- **Ridge tops**: Shallow, well-drained, often acidic soils with low organic content
- **Upper slopes**: Thinner soils subject to erosion
- **Mid-slopes**: Deeper, more developed soils
- **Lower slopes and valleys**: Deep, alluvial soils with higher fertility and moisture

Erosion and leaching create predictable soil sequences down slopes—understanding the catena helps designers select appropriate plant systems and management strategies for each position in the landscape.
