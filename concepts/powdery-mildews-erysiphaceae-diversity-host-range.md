# Powdery Mildews - Erysiphaceae Diversity and Host Range

The Erysiphaceae, commonly known as powdery mildews, is a
family of fungal-biodiversity|Ascomycetes whose species
are obligate parasites on the above-ground parts of higher
plants. They are among the most recognizable and economically
important groups of plant-parasitic fungi, producing a
characteristic white powdery growth on leaf and stem surfaces
that makes them easily identifiable even by non-specialists.

## Biology and Life Cycle

Powdery mildews are nourished by haustoria -- specialized
feeding structures that penetrate host cells. They develop a
white, dusty conidial stage on the surface of the host. The
life cycle includes both asexual and sexual phases:

- **Asexual reproduction** -- conidia are produced
  continuously on conidiophores erupting through the leaf
  surface, enabling rapid epidemic development during
  favorable conditions
- **Sexual reproduction** -- cleistothecia (non-ostiolate
  perithecia) form on older, senescing tissue, producing
  ascospores for overwintering and genetic recombination

Immature cleistothecia are yellowish to orange, while mature
fruiting bodies are black, globose, and less than a
millimeter in diameter. They are ornamented with various
types of hyphal appendages that may be simple or branched
with hooks, which are important taxonomic characters for
generic delimitation.

## Taxonomic Overview

Depending on the authority consulted, there are from 19 to 22
currently accepted genera and an estimated 400 species, the
majority of which are in the genus Erysiphe. The genera are
delimited based on features of the cleistothecial appendages
plus the number of asci per cleistothecium. Because many
species are host-specific, correct host identification is
essential for species determination.

## Host Range and Geographic Distribution

Hirata (1986), in his massive worldwide treatise, documented
9,838 angiosperm host species dispersed among 44 orders, 169
families (roughly half of the known angiosperm families), and
1,617 genera. Key patterns of host association include:

- 93% of hosts are dicotyledons
- Of the 662 known monocotyledonous hosts, 634 are in the
  Gramineae (grasses)
- No powdery mildews are known on gymnosperms,
  pteridophytes, or lower plants such as mosses and
  liverworts
- Host ranges vary from large to small, but species
  concepts have become narrower and more restricted in
  recent years

## Geographic Patterns

Hirata (1986) divided the world into 10 geographic regions
and analyzed host species numbers per region. Key findings:

- North-temperate regions have far larger numbers of host
  species than tropical, subtropical, arctic, or subarctic
  regions
- The areas of greatest abundance and greatest damage are
  California and Israel, both characterized by warm,
  rain-free summers and intensive agriculture
- Whether powdery mildews truly are more species-rich in
  temperate areas remains uncertain pending tropical surveys

## Host Habit and Infection Patterns

Hirata calculated that the number of herbaceous host species
(8,250) hosting powdery mildews far exceeded the number of
tree hosts (2,645). This reflects the growth habit of the
fungus, which is generally limited to leaves and succulent
shoots. The restricted host range to above-ground, herbaceous
tissues distinguishes powdery mildews from many other foliar
[[fungal-parasites]]. For comparison with downy mildews, see [[downy-mildew-biology-diversity-peronosporales]].

## Species-to-Host Ratio

Hirata (1986) calculated that the ratio of powdery mildew
species to angiosperm species is approximately 4.5%, based
on an estimated total of 220,000 angiosperm species. This
ratio provides a useful baseline for estimating total powdery
mildew diversity and suggests that many species remain
undescribed, particularly in tropical regions where host
plant diversity is highest but mycological survey effort has
been lowest.

## Recognition and Field Identification

Powdery mildews can be recognized in the field by the white,
dusty, conidial state coating leaf and small stem surfaces.
Infected tissues may be stunted or chlorotic. Key field
characteristics include:

- White to grayish powdery coating on leaf surfaces
- Stunting or yellowing of infected tissue
- Conidial states develop first and may be the only stage
  on young infections
- Cleistothecia more likely on older, well-established
  infections on senescing leaves
- Dark, globose, mature fruiting bodies visible with a hand
  lens scattered over the mycelium

## Collection Techniques

For collection and identification of powdery mildews:

- If possible, collect some dark, mature fruiting bodies
  along with the conidial state
- Because species are often host-specific, correct host
  identification is essential
- If the diseased host is unidentified, healthy foliage and
  flowers or fruit should be collected at the same time
- Voucher specimens should include both infected and healthy
  tissue

Hirata (1986) listed close to 4,000 publications on powdery
mildews, including regional monographs that are essential
references for species-level identification.
