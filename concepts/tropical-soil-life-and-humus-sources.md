# Tropical Soil Life and Humus Sources

[[soil-life]]—particularly termites and ants—plays a foundational role in tropical ecosystem development, while a diverse array of organic materials provides [[humus]] for building and maintaining tropical soils. Both topics are explored in depth in Chapter 10 of *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*.

## Termites and Ants as Ecosystem Engineers

Research by Harris (1971) documented that leaf-cutter ants in South America and termite mounds in Uganda actively assist forest spread and establish vegetation islands within grasslands. This phenomenon has been observed as far afield as granite country in Hyderabad, India and acid peatlands in Tasmania, demonstrating the near-universal role of these soil engineers across tropical and subtropical environments.

Ant and termite mounds serve as protected microsites that shield soils from fire, waterlogging, and poor aeration—three of the most persistent challenges in tropical landscapes. The mounds themselves are enriched in calcium and potash, exhibit better aeration than surrounding soils, and support faster water infiltration, making them exceptionally fertile planting sites.

## Succession on Mounds

Termite and ant mounds initiate a predictable succession sequence that moves from open grassland toward forest canopy:

1. **Tall grasses** — *Pennisetum*, *Eragrostis* colonize first
2. **Shrubs** — castor oil bean, *Prosopis*, thorny legumes establish
3. **Understory and forest** — tamarind, *Vitex*, *Sapium*, and palms develop into closed canopy

This succession pathway provides a model for deliberate reforestation: by working with mound-building insects rather than against them, designers can accelerate the transition from degraded grassland to productive forest systems.

## Agricultural Use of Mounds

A variety of crops can be cultivated directly on ant and termite mounds, including sisal, cotton, and tobacco. Remarkably, palms and coffee can have their outer bark removed by termites without any loss of production—a relationship that suggests careful niche gardening is possible even in termite-active zones.

Beyond mound cultivation, termites provide a broader service by assisting the primary breakdown of logs, coarse stems, and hard leaf material that would otherwise persist as unproductive mulch. Their digestive capacity converts tough lignified material into accessible organic matter far faster than microbial decomposition alone could achieve.

## Niche Gardening with Soil Insects

Mollison recommends a systematic observational approach to niche gardening in termite and ant territory:

- **Crops left alone** — identify and propagate these as core species
- **Crops attacked but still productive** — these may be worth tolerating for net yield
- **Crops that benefit from association** — seek out species that gain protection or nutrient access from proximity to insect activity

This triage system allows designers to compose productive polycultures even in areas with heavy insect pressure.

## Sources of Humus for Tropical Soils

Tropical soils require constant replenishment of organic matter. A wide range of humus sources is available to the permaculture designer:

### Logs and Branches

Logs and branches form one of the most substantial humus sources. They can be placed cross-slope on bare clay, eroded areas, and atolls to create instant planting sites with moisture-retaining woody mass. In some strategies, logs are deliberately rotted in wet terraces before being spread.

A traditional Hawaiian strategy involves placing kukui tree (*Aleurites moluccana*) logs in shallow taro terrace water. Edible fungi colonize the submerged wood, and once the log has partially decomposed, the remaining material is crushed and spread across the terrace as rich organic amendment.

Marjorie Spears developed a related approach using roughwood terraces on deforested slopes, building retaining structures from rejected logs that simultaneously stabilize soil and generate humus as they decay.

### Detritus from Woody Plants

Fallen material from bamboo, pines, and *Casuarina* provides a steady stream of organic matter. Palms, bamboo, *Casuarina*, and *Acacia* yield silica-rich mulch that is particularly valuable for reducing pH in alkaline island soils—a common constraint in Pacific and Caribbean atoll environments.

### Aquatic Weeds

Aquatic weed mats serve as both humus source and nitrogen input:

- **Azolla** — a small floating fern that fixes atmospheric nitrogen through its symbiotic relationship with *Anabaena* cyanobacteria, making it a dual-purpose mulch and fertilizer
- **Pistia** (water lettuce) — fast-growing surface cover that can be harvested and applied to garden beds
- **Eichhornia** (water hyacinth) — prolific biomass producer useful for bulk organic matter
- **Algae** — collected from ponds and waterways as nutrient-dense amendment

### Crop Wastes and Manures

Everyday organic residues constitute reliable humus inputs: corn husks, kitchen wastes, bones, animal manures, cardboard, and newspaper. These materials are universally available and should be cycled back into the soil system rather than discarded.

### Hedgerow and Forage Plants

Hedgerow species serve dual functions as windbreaks and humus producers. Recommended hedgerow plants include *Hibiscus*, *Casuarina*, banana grass, palms, *Gliricidia*, *Acacia*, and *Prosopis*. Regular pruning of these species generates a continuous supply of branch and leaf mulch.

Lemongrass and comfrey are recommended as a combined kikuyu barrier and mulch crop—lemongrass's dense root mat suppresses the invasive kikuyu grass while both species produce abundant cuttable biomass.

### Green Mulch and Ground Cover

Soft legumes provide fast-cycling green manure and living ground cover. Key species include *Sesbania*, vetch, cowpea, lablab, soya bean, *Desmodium*, *Siratro*, and *Centrosema*. These can be intercropped, used as relay covers, or grown in dedicated green-manure beds before incorporation.

## Managing Difficult Mulch

Some mulch materials—particularly resprouting weeds and persistent rhizomatous species—resist conventional decomposition and may re-establish if simply laid on the soil surface. Mollison outlines several strategies for handling these materials:

- **Route through animal systems** — pass difficult weeds through poultry or cattle pens where trampling, digestion, and manuring break them down
- **Shred for biogas** — mechanical shredding followed by anaerobic digestion in a biogas unit converts problem vegetation into energy and digestate
- **Solar "cooking"** — bag material in plastic and leave in full sun; the heat kills viable plant parts
- **Water pit immersion** — submerge in covered water pits for anaerobic breakdown over weeks to months

Each strategy converts a management liability into a productive input.

## Integration

The overarching principle is that tropical soil fertility depends on continuous biological activity. Termites and ants serve as primary decomposers and soil builders, while a diverse portfolio of humus sources—from massive logs to fast-growing legumes—ensures that organic matter is always being added to the system. The designer's task is to observe, select, and combine these elements into self-reinforcing cycles that reduce external inputs and build long-term soil health.
