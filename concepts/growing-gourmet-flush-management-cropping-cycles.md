    16|# Flush Management and Cropping Cycles
    17|
    18|Mushroom cultivation proceeds in distinct cropping cycles called
    19|"flushes" or "breaks." Each flush represents a wave of mushroom
    20|production followed by a rest period. Managing flushes effectively
    21|is one of the most important skills a cultivator can develop,
    22|directly impacting total yield, crop quality, and the economic
    23|viability of a mushroom growing operation.
    24|
    25|## Understanding Flush Patterns
    26|
    27|The first, second, and third crops (or flushes) are usually the
    28|best, with each successive flush decreasing in yield. For indoor
    29|cultivators concerned with optimizing yield and crop rotation from
    30|each growing room, maximizing yield indoors may incur unacceptable
    31|risks. As the mycelium declines in vigor after several flushes,
    32|contaminants begin to flourish. Future runs are quickly imperiled.
    33|
    34|If growing on sterilized sawdust, it is recommended to remove the
    35|blocks after the third flush to a specially constructed, four-sided,
    36|open-air, netted growing room. This overflow or "yield recapture"
    37|environment is simply fitted with an overhead nozzle misting system.
    38|Natural air currents provide plenty of circulation. These recapture
    39|buildings give bonus crops and require minimum maintenance.
    40|
    41|## Timing Between Flushes
    42|
    43|An important feature of any mushroom strain is the time between
    44|breaks or flushes. The shorter the period, the better. Strains
    45|characterized by long periods of dormancy between breaks are more
    46|susceptible to exploitation by insects and molds. By the third
    47|flush, a cultivator should have harvested 90% of the potential
    48|crop. The sooner these crops can be harvested, the sooner the
    49|growing room can be rotated into another crop cycle.
    50|
    51|The rapid cycling of younger batches poses less risk of
    52|contamination. With [[growing-gourmet-shiitake-cultivation]]
    53|cultivation on supplemented sawdust, flushes typically occur 10-14
    54|days apart after the first harvest, which comes 25-35 days from
    55|inoculation.
    56|
    57|## Shiitake Flush Management in Detail
    58|
    59|The first flush from white sawdust blocks is unique and calls for
    60|a strategy totally different from subsequent flushes. Timing is
    61|critical. During incubation, the outer surface of the myceliated
    62|sawdust appears as a smooth flat plane. After 20-25 days, the
    63|blocks start "buckling," an irregular blister-like surface
    64|topography forms. These formations are the precursors to primordia.
    65|
    66|Several days after this surface topography forms, temperatures
    67|are dropped and small brown spots form at the peaks of the
    68|blisters. Primordia can form overnight, measuring 1-3 mm in
    69|diameter. The cultivator must assess the maturity of the primordia
    70|population and expose the sensitive mycelium to the growing room
    71|environment precisely at the right time by stripping the bags from
    72|the blocks.
    73|
    74|Since these events occur rapidly and the window of opportunity is
    75|so narrow, all the skills of the cultivator come into play. The
    76|humidity must be maintained at 100% under fog-like conditions until
    77|the desired number of primordia form. Once six to a dozen mushrooms
    78|form, relative humidity is lowered and air turbulence is increased
    79|to affect greater evaporation.
    80|
    81|## Post-Flush Recovery and Re-Soaking
    82|
    83|After the first flush, the fruiting blocks must dry out. Blocks
    84|may weigh only 3-4 lbs of their original 6-7 lb weight. The
    85|humidity is lowered to 30-50% and maintained around 70 degrees F.
    86|After 7-10 days of dormancy, the now-browning blocks are submerged
    87|in non-chlorinated water for 24-48 hours. If the water temperature
    88|is 45-55 degrees F, 48 hours is recommended. If above 60 degrees F,
    89|blocks should not be submerged for more than 24 hours.
    90|
    91|When blocks are removed from the soak tank, they should be placed
    92|directly back into the growing room onto open-wire shelves. If the
    93|humidity is raised to 100% at this point, disaster soon results.
    94|Green molds flourish. The constant and at times drastic fluctuations
    95|in humidity improve crop quality but discourage contamination.
    96|
    97|Three days from soaking, white star-shaped fissures break through
    98|the outer brown surface. The blocks are wafted with water every
    99|8-12 hours. One week after soaking, the crop cycle begins with
   100|picking of the first mushrooms. After the harvest is completed,
   101|the blocks are dried out for 7-10 days, after which the re-soaking
   102|process begins anew.
   103|
   104|## Determining Block Exhaustion
   105|
   106|This cycle can be repeated several more times. After five flushes,
   107|with an accumulated yield of 2-3 lbs of fresh mushrooms per 6-7 lb
   108|block, the mycelium can produce at most rapidly maturing miniature
   109|mushrooms, few and far between. This is a sure sign that maximum
   110|yields have been achieved.
   111|
   112|Another way of determining whether the block is spent is to drop
   113|it from waist level to a cement floor. A block with good yield
   114|potential will strike the surface and not break apart. An expired
   115|block will burst upon impact: a direct measure of mycelial
   116|fortitude. At completion, blocks are half to one-third of their
   117|original size and are often blackish brown in color.
   118|
   119|The spent blocks can be recycled by pulverizing them back into a
   120|sawdust-like form. The expired substrate is then re-sterilized for
   121|the sequential cultivation of Oyster, [[growing-gourmet-maitake-cultivation]],
   122|Zhu Ling, or [[growing-gourmet-reishi-cultivation]] mushrooms.
   123|
   124|## Straw-Based Flush Management
   125|
   126|On pasteurized straw, two to three flushes seem most practical.
   127|Straw-grown crops tend to have a shorter productive lifespan than
   128|sawdust-grown blocks. Two flushes 10-14 days apart are typical for
   129|many [[growing-gourmet-tree-species-guide-mushroom-cultivation]] species grown on straw. The first flush
   130|often produces the largest yield, with each subsequent flush
   131|declining. The timing between flushes on straw is influenced by
   132|species, strain, substrate quality, and environmental management.
   133|
   134|## Spore Load and Harvest Timing
   135|
   136|Once spores have been released in mass, the mushroom soon
   137|decomposes. Hence, strains that are not heavy spore producers at
   138|the time of harvest are attractive to cultivators. Young mushrooms
   139|are more flavorful, tighter fleshed, often more colorful, and ship
   140|and store longer than older ones. Crop rotation, with much less
   141|associated spore load, is likewise accelerated through the harvest
   142|of adolescent forms. Diseases are less likely and consistency of
   143|production is better assured.
   144|
   145|The massive release of spores, particularly by Oyster mushrooms,
   146|is an environmental hazard to workers within the growing rooms and
   147|is taxing on equipment. Spores from Oyster mushrooms can actually
   148|clog and stop fans running at several hundred RPM, ruining their
   149|motors.
