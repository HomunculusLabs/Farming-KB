# Mushroom Cultivation Troubleshooting Guide

Most failures in mushroom cultivation result from errors in basic technique rather than from insurmountable problems. This troubleshooting guide organizes the most common cultivation problems by growth stage, listing symptoms, causes, and solutions. The guide is based on the systematic approach presented in Paul Stamets' The Mushroom Cultivator, adapted for both home and commercial cultivators.

See [[mushroom-contamination-control-strategies]] for identifying specific contaminants, [[mushroom-sterile-technique]] for laboratory hygiene, and [[mushroom-primordia-formation-triggers]] for pinning problems.

## Stage 1: Agar Culture Problems

### Media Fails to Solidify

Cause: Insufficient quantity of agar, or the agar was not thoroughly mixed before pouring. Agar must be brought to a boil and stirred to fully dissolve before pouring into plates.

Solution: Thoroughly mix media before autoclaving and pouring. Increase agar concentration if the medium remains too liquid after cooling.

### Contamination Before Inoculation

Cause: High contaminant spore load in the laboratory, or the pressure cooker was opened before cooling sufficiently. Contaminated pressure cookers can harbor bacteria that survive between cycles.

Solution: Clean and paint the laboratory. Install a laminar flow hood. Allow the pressure cooker to cool in a sterile environment before opening. Sterilize contaminated pressure cookers at 15 psi for 24-48 hours.

### No Growth from Spores or Tissue

Cause: Wrong type of media, incorrect pH, old or dehydrated spores, inoculation tool too hot, or sugar in the media caramelized from excessive sterilization temperature.

Solution: Verify media recipe and pH. Rehydrate old spores in sterile water for 12-24 hours. Allow inoculation tools to cool before contacting spores or tissue. Lower sterilization temperature for sugar-containing media.

### Contamination Around Transfer Point

Cause: Inoculum is contaminated, or inoculation tools are not sterile. Wild spores or tissue samples may carry contaminants that are not apparent until they grow out on the nutrient media.

Solution: Obtain cleaner spores or take tissue culture from a fresher specimen. Inoculate many plates simultaneously and save only the uncontaminated ones. Autoclave tools, soak in alcohol, and flame sterilize before use.

### Mycelium Becomes Cottony and Slow

Cause: Strain senescence (aging), mutation, or media that has caramelized during sterilization. Insufficient agar can also cause subsurface growth that appears cottony.

Solution: Retrieve stock cultures and reactivate a vigorous strain. Alternate media types to prevent genetic selection by a limited chemical matrix. Add more agar and mix thoroughly before pouring.

## Stage 2: Grain Culture Problems

### Jars Break in Pressure Cooker

Cause: Pressure cooker cooled too rapidly, creating thermal shock. Jars packed too tightly without room for expansion.

Solution: Allow pressure cooker to descend to room temperature gradually. Leave headspace in jars and do not pack grain too densely. Check jars for cracks before use.

### Grain Spontaneously Contaminates Before Inoculation

Cause: Contaminant spores entered during cooling, or bacterial endospores survived sterilization.

Solution: Cool jars in a sterile environment or in front of a laminar flow hood. Pre-soak grain for 24 hours before autoclaving to germinate bacterial endospores, making them vulnerable to heat.

### No Growth After Agar Wedge Transfer

Cause: Grain too hot when inoculated, grain too dry, mycelium not distributed, wrong incubation temperature, or incorrect pH.

Solution: Allow grain to cool to room temperature before inoculating. Adjust moisture content per recommended recipes. Shake jars vigorously after transfer and again 3-5 days later. Verify incubation temperature matches species requirements. Buffer pH with calcium carbonate.

### Grain Jars Difficult to Shake

Cause: Too much grain or too much water relative to the container size.

Solution: Fill jars to only two-thirds capacity. Follow recommended grain-to-water ratios precisely. Calibrate measuring cups if results are inconsistent.

## Stage 3: Spawn Running Problems

### Slow or Incomplete Colonization

Cause: Substrate too dry, spawn rate too low, temperature incorrect, or competing organisms present.

Solution: Verify substrate moisture content is within the recommended range. Increase spawn rate to at least 10% by volume. Check incubation temperature against species parameters. Ensure pasteurization was adequate to eliminate competitors.

### Compost Overheating During Spawn Run

Cause: Excessive microbial activity in under-composted substrate, or compost filled too deeply.

Solution: Reduce fill depth. Improve air circulation around containers. Ensure Phase I composting was adequate to break down readily available nutrients.

### Spawn Running But Contamination Appears

Cause: Insufficient pasteurization, contaminated spawn, or poor hygiene during spawning.

Solution: Verify pasteurization temperatures and duration. Check grain spawn for contamination before use. Sterilize tools and work surfaces before spawning. Use higher spawn rates to outcompete potential contaminants.

## Stage 4: Casing Problems

### Overlay (Mycelium Completely Covers Casing Surface)

Cause: Too high temperature, too high CO2, casing surface too dry, or casing applied too early before full substrate colonization.

Solution: Lower air temperature to the fruiting range. Increase fresh air exchange. Maintain casing moisture at optimal levels. Patch with moist casing to restore surface porosity. Ensure casing is applied only after substrate is fully colonized.

### Mycelium Does Not Grow Through Casing

Cause: Casing pH too high or too low, casing too dry, or mycelium weakened from poor spawn run.

Solution: Adjust casing pH to species-specific range (typically 7.0-7.5). Increase casing moisture gradually. Verify that spawn run was complete and vigorous before casing.

## Stage 5: Pinning and Cropping Problems

### No Primordia Form

Cause: Temperature not lowered, CO2 too high, humidity too low, or insufficient light for photosensitive species.

Solution: Initiate the full pinning sequence: lower temperature to fruiting range, increase fresh air exchange to reduce CO2 below species-specific threshold, maintain humidity at 95%, and provide 12-hour light cycle for photosensitive species.

### Mushrooms Have Long Stems and Small Caps

Cause: High CO2 concentration or insufficient light. Both conditions produce similar symptoms.

Solution: Increase fresh air exchange. Verify lighting is adequate for the species. Position light directly above the growing surface.

### Bacterial Blotch on Caps

Cause: Pseudomonas tolaasii infection favored by wet conditions and stagnant air.

Solution: Reduce humidity slightly, increase air circulation, avoid watering caps directly, and improve overall air movement in the growing room. Remove affected mushrooms to prevent spread.

### Aborted Pins (Dead Primordia)

Cause: Temperature fluctuations, excessive watering during pin development, CO2 too high, or insufficient nutrients in substrate.

Solution: Maintain stable temperatures. Avoid watering until pins reach button size. Increase fresh air exchange. Ensure substrate had adequate nutrition and spawn rate.

## General Principles

When troubleshooting, change only one variable at a time so that the effect of each change can be observed. Follow established, proven procedures before experimenting with new techniques. Keep detailed records of every variable at each stage of cultivation so that problems can be diagnosed retrospectively. Most cultivation problems have multiple possible causes, and systematic elimination is the most reliable path to a solution.
