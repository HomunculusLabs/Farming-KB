## Green Manures and Cover Crops

Green manures and [[cover-crops]] are interchangeable terms for growing something whose sole purpose is enriching the soil. A well-managed cover crop program is perhaps the strongest proof of a skilled gardener — even more so than large, healthy vegetable plants. However, cover cropping can be tricky and even hazardous if not done correctly.

## Why Cover Crop?

### The Fundamental Principle

If a plot of land is put to work growing as much biomass as possible during every day of the year that biomass will grow, and if almost all that biomass is recycled back into the land, then that bit of earth will maintain a relatively stable and high organic matter content. In that condition of maximum organic matter content, the soil will have good tilth and grow good crops.

### The Garden's Problem

In [[query-how-do-i-plan-and-start-a-vegetable-garden-from-scratch]], the opposite occurs. Just at the time when plants would be growing their fastest (midsummer), we harvest them, and the ground is left bare. By the time new plants are growing and their leaves form a "crop canopy" that thickly covers the ground, much of [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] has already passed.

Cover cropping means making sure the ground produces biomass by covering it with a crop canopy for as much of the growing season as possible.

## The Downsides (Start Here)

### Scale and Strength

Green-manuring practices filtered down to vegetable growers from farmers. Farmers use big tractors and heavy plows. Gardeners use shovels. A six-foot-tall (180 cm) stand of woody-stalked grain rye can overwhelm a home gardener trying to handle it by hand.

**Rule**: If we gardeners are going to green-manure, we had best use types within our strength.

### The Timing Trap (Critical!)

This is the single most important caution about cover cropping. Timing is everything:

| When Turned In | Material State | C:N | Decomposition Time | Wait Before Planting |
|----------------|---------------|-----|-------------------|---------------------|
| Before flowering | Tender, green, low C/N | ~12:1 | ~2 weeks | 2 weeks |
| At flowering | Starting to woody | ~20:1 | 3-4 weeks | 3-4 weeks |
| After seed formation | Woody, high C/N | ~30:1+ | 4-5+ weeks | 4-5+ weeks |

### The CO₂ Problem

When a great deal of decomposition is going on in the soil, microorganisms breathe in heaps of oxygen and exhale heaps of CO₂. The soil atmosphere becomes so filled with CO₂ that seeds cannot germinate and transplanted seedlings are suffocated. This settles back to normal as decomposition finishes.

### The Spring Disaster Scenario

Imagine: heavy soil, a thriving overwintered cover crop of rye grass or winter wheat, and a wet spring. You can't dig when the soil is too wet (you'll make a mess of clods). When the soil dries enough, the grass is waist-high and forming seeds. After digging it in, you face an additional 4-5 week wait. That land might not be ready to plant until your cool-weather crops for autumn.

### Allelopathy

Some crops put residues into the soil that harm the growth of the crop that follows. The cover crop you select could prevent some of your garden crops from growing well — the opposite of the effect you hoped for.

### Moisture Competition

In semi-arid conditions, a green-manure crop might suck so much moisture out of the soil before you turn it in that the ground will be deprived of the moisture you need to get seeds sprouted.

## The Upsides

If all goes well:

- **Soil structure**: The soil is thickly filled with rapidly decomposing roots, leaving land loose, airy, and totally open to rapid penetration by crops
- **[[beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]]**: Provide overwintering habitat
- **Nitrogen fixation**: If the cover crop was a legume, it adds nitrogen to the soil
- **Nutrient capture**: Overwintering crops incorporate nutrients that might otherwise leach out during winter rains
- **[[dighton-fungal-roles-soil-erosion-prevention]]**: Cover crops protect soil from wind and water erosion
- **Weed suppression**: Dense cover crop canopy suppresses weed germination

## Specific Cover Crops

### Buckwheat (Summer) — The Safest Choice

The only summer cover crop recommended without hesitation. It works almost anywhere and won't get any gardener into trouble.

- Grows best during early summer; local varieties are adapted to your situation
- Sown thickly before the solstice, grows waist-high in less than five weeks, then begins blooming
- Stalks are tender and brittle — easily dug in without tangling in tiller tines
- Decomposes in just one week if dug into moist soil before the first flowers appear
- Puts the soil into magnificent, fine-textured condition — a perfect seedbed
- Scatter seeds about 1 inch (2.5 cm) apart and chop in shallowly with a hoe

**Warning**: Use seed grown at a latitude similar to where you live. Seed from very different latitudes may go to bloom prematurely.

### Southern Peas (Summer)

In southern climates, southern peas function much like buckwheat but have the additional advantage of being a legume (nitrogen fixation). Promptly till in or yank out the crop when seeds begin forming.

### Garden Peas (Spring/Summer)

You can fill whole beds with ordinary garden peas as a green-manure crop. Benefits:
- Fills the freezer with peas
- If beds are needed sooner, the green manure can be composted anytime
- Peas leave the soil in the most beautiful condition

### Brassicas (Autumn)

Fodder kale, oilseed radish, tyfon (turnip × [[growing-chinese-cabbage]] hybrid), rape, and field turnips make excellent autumn green manures:
- In cold climates they will certainly freeze out — no spring problem
- Where winters are milder, they may overwinter and make lush spring growth
- Their leaves protect the ground in autumn
- Their huge taproots open up the soil
- Sow at least 40 days before the end of autumn growing season

**Caution**: In maritime climates with mild winters, don't overwinter brassicas on clayey soils unless you can yank them out by hand when blooming begins.

### Annual Legumes (Overwintering)

Annual legumes are the mainstay for overwintering anywhere they won't freeze out:
- **Crimson clover**: Popular in the middle states of the US
- **Hairy vetch**: Widely grown and popular
- **Lupins**: Small-seeded types are being experimented with

**Caution**: Beware of perennial legumes — they grow more slowly the first year and may be difficult to get rid of. This is particularly true of red clover.

### Cereal Rye (Winter)

Rye is a classic winter cover crop:
- Very hardy — survives severe winters
- Produces massive biomass
- Can become overwhelming (6+ feet tall) — consider "no-till transplanting" method
- Decomposing rye releases a chemical that blocks weed seed sprouting
- If using no-till method, sprinkle strong nitrate source (COF or chicken manure) on the earth before cutting to prevent nutrient tie-up

### The No-Till Rye Method

A technique where rye (sometimes mixed with hairy vetch) is grown right on the site:
1. Rye is cut off at ground level after forming seed heads
2. Dead vegetation remains as a thick mulch
3. Transplants are set directly into the mulch
4. The decomposing rye releases chemicals that suppress weeds

**Caution**: Unless a strong nitrate source is sprinkled on the earth before cutting, the woody rye residue may tie up nutrients.

## Practical Guidelines

1. **Work it out for yourself** — cover cropping takes a few years to develop intuitive sense
2. **Consult local experts** — your local agricultural extension office will have information about what works in your area
3. **Buy seed locally** — farm suppliers are probably the best source, not mail-order garden seed companies
4. **Use autumn-sown, winter-killed crops** if you have harsh winters — their roots decompose in early spring, giving you fine, loose seedbeds
5. **Start small** — don't commit your entire garden to cover crops until you've gained experience
