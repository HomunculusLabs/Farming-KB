# Maitake Synthetic Log Growth Parameters

**Grifola frondosa** (maitake / hen of the woods) is a polypore mushroom prized for its culinary value and studied extensively for medicinal properties. Cultivating maitake on synthetic logs requires careful management of temperature, humidity, CO₂, and light across three distinct growth phases. The entire production cycle from inoculation to harvest takes approximately **3.5 months** (Stamets, 1993; Royse & Guardino, 1997).

## Overview of the Cultivation Cycle

Synthetic log cultivation of maitake involves inoculating sterilized supplemented substrate (typically hardwood sawdust with bran) inside filter-patch bags. The sealed bag environment allows precise control over gas exchange and moisture during the spawn run. After full colonization, the mycelium is induced to form primordia through a dramatic shift in environmental conditions, followed by a fruiting phase where the characteristic overlapping brackets develop.

The three major phases are:

1. **Spawn run** — vegetative colonization of the substrate
2. **Primordia initiation** — induction of pin formation
3. **Fruiting body development** — growth to harvestable maturity

Each phase has distinct environmental requirements that must be met for reliable yields.

## Phase 1: Spawn Run

The spawn run is the longest phase, lasting **4–5 weeks**. During this period the mycelium must fully colonize the synthetic log without being triggered to fruit prematurely.

| Parameter | Target Range | Notes |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| Temperature | 20–25°C (68–77°F) | Optimal for vigorous mycelial growth |
| Humidity | High (maintained inside sealed bag) | No external humidification needed; filter patch allows gas exchange |
| Light | ~50 lux | Very low light or darkness; ambient room light is sufficient |
| CO₂ | Elevated (sealed bag environment) | Naturally accumulates inside bag; no active management required |
| Duration | 4–5 weeks | Substrate should be fully white with dense mycelium |

The synthetic log bags should remain sealed throughout the spawn run. The filter patch provides sufficient gas exchange for respiration while maintaining the high-CO₂, high-humidity environment that favors vegetative growth over fruiting. Bags are typically stored on shelving in a clean, temperature-controlled room. Light should be minimized — around 50 lux or less — as even moderate light during spawn run can cause premature primordia formation in some strains (Chalmers, 1994).

## Phase 2: Primordia Initiation

Once the substrate is fully colonized, a significant environmental shock is required to shift the mycelium from vegetative to reproductive mode. This cold-shock and humidity induction phase lasts **5–10 days**.

| Parameter | Target Range | Notes |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| Temperature | 10–15.6°C (50–60°F) | Cold shock triggers primordia formation |
| Humidity | 95% RH | Critical — surface must not dry out |
| Light | 100–500 lux | Low to moderate indirect light |
| CO₂ | 2000–5000 ppm | Moderate elevation; some gas exchange required |
| Duration | 5–10 days | Small white pins appear at substrate surface |

The bag is opened or cut at this stage to expose the colonized substrate surface. Maintaining 95% relative humidity is essential; a humidity tent or misting system may be needed if the fruiting chamber cannot sustain this level. CO₂ must be kept in the 2000–5000 ppm range — significantly lower than the sealed-bag environment of spawn run, but still elevated compared to ambient air (~400 ppm). This moderate CO₂ level appears to be important for proper primordia density and morphology in maitake.

Light levels of 100–500 lux (diffuse fluorescent or LED) provide the phototropic cue needed for primordia orientation without causing the desiccation or stress that higher intensities might induce at this sensitive stage.

## Phase 3: Fruiting Body Development

After primordia are established, conditions are adjusted to promote rapid, healthy fruiting body growth. This phase has two sub-stages: early development (4–8 days) and maturation to harvest (10–14 days).

| Parameter | Target Range | Notes |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| Temperature | 10–15.6°C or 13–16°C (up to 18°C) | Strain-dependent; see regional differences below |
| Humidity | 85–90% RH | Lower than initiation to reduce bacterial risk |
| Light | 500–1000 lux | Moderate indirect light for normal morphology |
| CO₂ | <1000 ppm | Fresh air exchange is critical |
| Duration | 4–8 days development + 10–14 days to maturity | Total ~14–22 days |

**Early development (4–8 days):** Primordia enlarge and begin differentiating into the characteristic bracket-like lobes. Temperature, humidity, and CO₂ must be closely monitored.

**Maturation (10–14 days):** The fruiting body expands, individual caps develop, and spore production begins near maturity. Harvest is typically timed when the margins of the caps are still slightly inrolled and before significant spore drop.

Maintaining CO₂ below 1000 ppm requires active fresh air exchange (FAE). Inadequate ventilation at this stage produces elongated, abnormal fruiting bodies with reduced quality. Growers should provide multiple air exchanges per hour, balanced against humidity maintenance.

## Regional Differences: Chinese vs. North American Practices

There are notable differences in cultivation practices between Chinese and North American growers, largely driven by strain genetics and commercial priorities.

### Chinese Practices

Chinese commercial cultivation often employs strains adapted to slightly higher temperature ranges for primordia initiation. Primordia may be induced at temperatures up to 18°C (64°F), which reduces cooling costs in subtropical and temperate Chinese growing regions. Chinese strains also tend to produce larger, more compound fruiting bodies and may have shorter spawn runs. Chinese growers frequently use larger-format synthetic logs and may adjust supplementation rates to favor yield over speed.

### North American Practices

North American strains (particularly those derived from wild isolates of eastern North American *G. frondosa*) generally require the lower end of the primordia temperature range, around 10–13°C (50–55°F). These strains may fail to initiate or produce sparse primordia if temperatures exceed 16°C during the induction phase. North American growers typically emphasize fruiting body quality — compact, well-formed clusters with good color — over total yield, and may extend the cold-shock period accordingly (Royse & Guardino, 1997).

Growers working with imported strains should verify the optimal temperature range for their specific isolate, as a mismatch between strain origin and cultivation conditions is a common source of failure.

## Synthetic Log Cultivation Parameters for Home Growers

The following table summarizes practical environmental targets for home-scale synthetic log cultivation of maitake:

| Stage | Temp (°C) | Temp (°F) | RH (%) | Light (lux) | CO₂ (ppm) | Duration |
|-------|-----------|-----------|--------|-------------|-----------|----------|
| Spawn run | 20–25 | 68–77 | High (sealed) | ~50 | Elevated (sealed) | 4–5 weeks |
| Primordia initiation | 10–15.6 | 50–60 | 95 | 100–500 | 2,000–5,000 | 5–10 days |
| Fruiting development | 10–15.6 | 50–60 | 85–90 | 500–1,000 | <1,000 | 4–8 days |
| Fruiting maturation | 13–16 (≤18) | 55–61 (≤64) | 85–90 | 500–1,000 | <1,000 | 10–14 days |

**Total production timeline: approximately 3.5 months** from inoculation to first harvest (Stamets, 1993).

## Key Cultivation Considerations

### Ventilation and CO₂ Management

CO₂ concentration is the single most critical parameter distinguishing the three growth phases. During spawn run, the sealed bag naturally maintains CO₂ at levels well above 5000 ppm, which suppresses fruiting. At primordia initiation, CO₂ must drop to the 2000–5000 ppm range — this requires opening the bag and introducing limited air exchange. During fruiting body development, CO₂ must fall below 1000 ppm, necessitating active fresh air exchange. Home growers can achieve this with timed exhaust fans, passive venting, or manual fanning, while monitoring with a portable CO₂ meter.

### Light Requirements

Maitake is not a photosynthetic organism, but light serves as an important environmental cue at each stage:

- **Spawn run (~50 lux):** Near-darkness prevents premature fruiting.
- **Primordia initiation (100–500 lux):** Low light provides directional cues.
- **Fruiting (500–1000 lux):** Moderate light ensures normal cap development and coloration.

Excessive light at any stage is not directly harmful but can contribute to substrate drying, particularly during the humidity-sensitive primordia phase.

### Humidity Management

The transition from sealed-bag humidity during spawn run to open-air fruiting is a critical management point. Rapid desiccation of the substrate surface during primordia initiation is a common cause of crop failure. Humidity tents, ultrasonic humidifiers, or misting systems are recommended for home growers.
