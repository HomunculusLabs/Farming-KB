# Mycorrhizal Mineral Weathering and Nutrient Acquisition

Mycorrhizal fungi are among the most important biological agents of mineral
weathering in terrestrial ecosystems. Through their ability to exude organic
acids, produce siderophores, and deploy [[suillus-granulatus]] and Paxillus
involutus released elements from apatite and wood ash (K, Ca, Ti, Mn, Pb) and
accumulated them preferentially in rhizomorphs (Wallander et al., 2003).

## Organic Acid Exudation Mechanisms

The primary mechanism by which mycorrhizal fungi weather minerals is the
exudation of low-molecular-weight organic acids. These acids chelate metal
cations bound in mineral crystal lattices, effectively dissolving the mineral
structure and releasing nutrients into the soil solution. Oxalic acid is
particularly important because it forms strong complexes with calcium, iron,
and aluminum, the dominant cations in most silicate and phosphate minerals.

Different fungal species produce distinct organic acid profiles. Some
ectomycorrhizal species specialize in oxalic acid production, which is
especially effective at dissolving calcium-bearing minerals like apatite and
calcite. Others produce primarily citric or malic acid, which are more
effective at mobilizing phosphorus from iron and aluminum phosphates. This
specialization means that fungal community composition directly influences
which nutrients are most available to plants in a given soil environment.

The regulation of organic acid exudation is tightly linked to plant carbon
supply and nutrient demand. When plants are deficient in phosphorus or
micronutrients, they allocate more photosynthate to mycorrhizal partners,
which respond by increasing [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]], carbon allocation, and substrate acidification.
Rosling et al. (2004a, 2004b) demonstrated that [[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]]/Pinus
sylvestris ectomycorrhizas responded to the presence of specific minerals by
adjusting hyphal growth direction and intensity. When minerals containing
limiting nutrients were detected, hyphae proliferated toward the mineral
source, forming dense exploration networks.

This directed growth represents a significant carbon investment by the host
plant. The fungal mycelium acts as an extended sensory and absorptive organ,
reaching far beyond the root depletion zone to access mineral nutrients in
bulk soil. In forest ecosystems, the total hyphal length of ectomycorrhizal
mycelium can exceed several kilometers per gram of soil, creating an
enormous interface for mineral weathering and nutrient uptake.

## Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Contributions

While ectomycorrhizal fungi have received more attention for mineral
weathering, arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi also contribute significantly
to nutrient acquisition from mineral sources. AM fungi enhance phosphorus
acquisition by extending hyphae into soil micropores that roots cannot
access, effectively increasing the soil volume explored for available
phosphorus. AM fungi also produce glomalin, a glycoprotein that influences
soil aggregation and may indirectly affect mineral weathering rates by
altering water movement and gas exchange in soil.
