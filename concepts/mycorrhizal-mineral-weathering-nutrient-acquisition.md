Mycorrhizal Fungi in Mineral Weathering and Nutrient Acquisition

Mycorrhizal fungi are among the most important biological agents of mineral
weathering in terrestrial ecosystems. Through their ability to exude organic
acids, produce siderophores, and deploy extracellular enzymes at the soil-
root interface, mycorrhizal fungi significantly enhance the release of
essential nutrients from mineral sources, driving plant nutrition and soil
development.

Ectomycorrhizal Weathering of Soil Minerals

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi dissolve soil weatherable calcium-bearing minerals
through proton- and ligand-promoted mechanisms (Callot et al., 1985; Lapeyrie
et al., 1990, 1991). ECM hyphal tips produce micro- to millimolar
concentrations of low-molecular-mass carboxylic acids including malic, succinic,
gluconic, and oxalic acids, contributing to "heterotrophic leaching" of
minerals.

In podzol E horizons under European coniferous forests, the weathering of
hornblendes, feldspars, and granitic bedrock has been attributed to organic
acid excretion by ectomycorrhizal hyphae. ECM hyphal tips are associated with
micropores (3-10 micrometers) in weatherable soil minerals, where they create
localized zones of mineral dissolution (van Breemen et al., 2000; Smits et al.,
2005).

The ECM fungus Piloderma extracted K and/or Mg from biotite, microcline, and
chlorite to satisfy nutritional requirements and precipitated mycogenic
calcium oxalate crystals on hyphae (Glowa et al., 2003). Suillus granulatus
and Paxillus involutus released elements from apatite and wood ash (K, Ca,
Ti, Mn, Pb) and accumulated them preferentially in rhizomorphs (Wallander
et al., 2003).

Resource-Specific Hyphal Responses

ECM mycelia respond to the presence of different soil minerals by regulating
growth, colonization patterns, carbon allocation, and substrate acidification.
Rosling et al. (2004a, 2004b) demonstrated that Hebeloma crustuliniforme/Pinus
sylvestris ectomycorrhizas showed significantly greater carbon allocation within
the mycelium when colonizing potassium feldspar patches compared to quartz
patches. This differential carbon investment suggests active sensing and
response to mineral nutrient availability.

Mycorrhizal dissolution of phosphate minerals is particularly important for
plant phosphorus nutrition. Paxillus involutus/Scots pine associations
dissolved zinc phosphate, acquiring phosphorus while accumulating zinc in
fungal tissues. Under phosphorus-replete conditions, zinc-tolerant ECM strains
limited zinc entry into cells, protecting plant tissues from excessive zinc
accumulation. Under phosphorus deficiency, zinc-tolerant ECM mobilized and
accumulated the highest zinc amounts, demonstrating context-dependent
mineral weathering strategies (Fomina and Gadd, unpublished data).

Mechanisms of Mycorrhizal Mineral Weathering

Mycorrhizal fungi employ multiple mechanisms for mineral dissolution:

1. Proton extrusion: Active transport of protons into the rhizosphere lowers
   pH and promotes acidolysis of mineral surfaces. Proton extrusion is
   coupled to cation uptake and ammonium assimilation.

2. Organic acid excretion: Low-molecular-weight carboxylic acids (oxalate,
   citrate, malate, succinate) provide both protons for acidolysis and
   chelating ligands for complexolysis. Organic acids solubilize minerals
   by forming stable metal-organic complexes.

3. Siderophore production: Iron-chelating siderophores released by
   mycorrhizal fungi mobilize iron from mineral sources and may also
   chelate other metals.

4. Enzyme production: Phosphatases, proteases, and other extracellular
   enzymes mobilize organic forms of nutrients from mineral-associated
   organic matter.

5. Physical penetration: Hyphal growth into mineral pores and along
   crystal boundaries creates direct contact with mineral surfaces.

Mycorrhizal Metal Immobilization

Mycorrhizal fungi immobilize metals through biosorption to biomass (cell
walls, pigments, extracellular polysaccharides), intracellular accumulation,
and extracellular precipitation of mycogenic metal oxalates. ECM mycobionts
filter toxic metals in the hyphal sheath or Hartig net by sorption, restrict
metal mobility due to hydrophobicity of the fungal sheath, and complex
metals through released organic acids.

Ericoid mycorrhizal and ectomycorrhizal fungi dissolve cadmium-, copper-,
zinc-, and lead-bearing minerals including metal phosphates (Leyval and
Joner, 2001; Martino et al., 2003; Fomina et al., 2004, 2005b). Mobilization
of phosphorus is generally regarded as one of the most important functions of
mycorrhizal fungi in mineral weathering.

Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Contributions

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi also contribute to mineral weathering,
particularly in grassland and tropical forest ecosystems. AM fungi excrete
organic acids from arbuscule-containing root cells and extraradical hyphae.
Their extensive hyphal networks explore large soil volumes, accessing
mineral nutrients beyond root depletion zones.

AM fungi reduce toxic metal uptake by plants (Christie et al., 2004), partly
through hyphal filtration and partly through altered rhizosphere chemistry.
In metal-contaminated soils, AM fungi can enhance plant establishment and
growth while limiting metal accumulation in plant tissues.

Ecological Significance

Mycorrhizal mineral weathering has several important consequences:
- Enhances plant access to rock-derived nutrients (P, K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn)
- Drives long-term soil development through mineral transformation
- Influences soil solution chemistry and nutrient availability patterns
- Affects carbon cycling through organic acid production and CO2 release
- Contributes to soil aggregation through oxalate and polysaccharide production
- Creates microscale heterogeneity in soil chemistry around hyphal tips
- Modifies the fate of toxic metals through immobilization and
  translocation processes
