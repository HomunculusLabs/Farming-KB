# Companion Planting and Polycultures

Companion planting is the practice of growing different plants together for mutual benefit. A polyculture is a system where multiple species are grown together in the same space, mimicking natural plant communities. Both strategies reduce pests, increase yields, and build healthier soil compared to monocultures.

## How Companion Planting Works

Companion plants help each other through several mechanisms:

- **Pest control**: Some plants repel pests with aromatic compounds (alliums, marigolds, herbs). Others attract pest predators (dill attracts parasitic wasps that attack caterpillars). Trap crops lure pests away from valued plants (nasturtiums attract aphids away from vegetables).

- **Nutrient sharing**: Legumes fix nitrogen that nearby plants can access. Deep-rooted plants bring up minerals from subsoil that become available to shallow-rooted neighbors when leaves drop or plants are cut.

- **Physical support**: Tall plants provide shade, wind protection, or trellising for climbers (corn supports beans in the Three Sisters).

- **Space optimization**: Plants with different root depths, mature heights, or growth habits can occupy the same space without competing (root layer, ground cover, canopy).

- **Soil improvement**: Some plants accumulate specific nutrients (comfrey for potassium/calcium, yarrow for phosphorus). When cut as mulch, these nutrients become available to neighboring plants.

## Plant Communication: The Science Behind Companion Planting

Modern research (Chamovitz, [[plant-sensing-communication]]) reveals that companion planting works through sophisticated chemical signaling systems:

### Volatile Organic Compound (VOC) Signaling
- When a plant is attacked by herbivores, it releases **green leaf volatiles (GLVs)** — the "fresh cut grass" smell is actually a distress signal
- **Methyl jasmonate** is an airborne defense hormone: attacked plants release it, and neighboring plants detect it and upregulate their own defenses (tannins, alkaloids, protease inhibitors) before attack arrives
- **Methyl salicylate** acts as a systemic defense signal within and between plants
- Some plants release VOCs that attract predators of their attackers — corn releases volatiles attracting parasitic wasps when attacked by caterpillars
- This explains why interplanting aromatic herbs (basil, rosemary, thyme) can prime neighboring crops' defenses even without direct pest contact

### Root Exudate Signaling and Allelopathy
- Plants release chemical compounds through roots that influence neighbors — this is the mechanism behind both beneficial and antagonistic companion relationships
- **Kin recognition**: Plants can detect root systems of competitors vs. kin, and adjust growth accordingly (compete less with siblings)
- **Allelopathy** (negative chemical interactions):
  - Black walnut (*Juglans nigra*) releases juglone, which is toxic to tomatoes, potatoes, and many other plants
  - Fennel inhibits growth of most garden plants
  - Sunflowers produce allelopathic compounds that suppress weed growth nearby
  - Rye and sorghum residues release allelopathic chemicals that suppress weeds when used as cover crop mulch
- **Positive root signaling**: Some root exudates attract beneficial mycorrhizal fungi and nitrogen-fixing bacteria, creating mutualistic underground networks

### Mycorrhizal Network Communication
- Mycorrhizal fungi ([[mycorrhizal-networks]]) connect multiple plants underground, transferring chemical signals and even nutrients between them
- "Mother tree" effects: larger plants can share carbon and defense signals with smaller neighbors through common mycorrhizal networks
- This underground communication system means companion planting effects can extend beyond above-ground interactions

## Classic Companion Planting Combinations

### Three Sisters (Native American)
Corn + beans + squash. Corn provides a trellis for beans. Beans fix nitrogen for corn and squash. Squash shades soil and suppresses weeds with large leaves. This is the classic polyculture.

### Apple Tree Guild
- Apple tree (center)
- Comfrey (dynamic accumulator, mulch producer)
- Clover (nitrogen fixer, ground cover, bee forage)
- Chives/garlic (pest deterrent -- repels apple scab)
- Dill or fennel (attracts beneficial insects)
- Nasturtiums (trap crop for aphids)
- Yarrow (attracts predatory wasps, accumulates phosphorus)

### Tomato Companions
- Basil (repels tomato hornworm, improves flavor)
- Borage (repels tomato hornworm, attracts pollinators)
- Marigold (repels root-knot nematodes)
- Carrots (mutual benefit -- tomatoes shade carrots, carrots aerate soil)
- Alliums (repel spider mites)

### Avoid Near Tomatoes
- Fennel (inhibits tomato growth)
- Walnuts (juglone toxicity)
- Cabbage family (both are heavy feeders and compete)

### Insectary Plants (Attract Beneficial Insects)

Plant these throughout the garden to support predatory insects:

- **Dill, fennel, cilantro** (umbellifers): Attract parasitic wasps, ladybugs, lacewings
- **Yarrow**: Attracts ladybugs, hoverflies, parasitic wasps
- **Alyssum**: Tiny flowers attract hoverflies (aphid predators)
- **Buckwheat**: Attracts beneficial insects, bees; fast [[cover-cropping]]
- **Phacelia**: One of the best overall beneficial insect attractors
- **Marigold**: Repels root-knot nematodes, attracts hoverflies
- **Sunflower**: Attracts pollinators, provides bird habitat
- **Clover**: Nitrogen fixer AND excellent bee forage

### Nitrogen-Fixing Companions

Use these near heavy-feeding crops:
- **Clover** (living mulch under everything)
- **Beans/peas** (interplant with corn, squash)
- **Vetch** (overwinter with grains, terminate before planting vegetables)
- **Lupine** (ornamental nitrogen fixer)
- **Sea buckthorn, goumi** (shrubs for food forest edges)

## Designing Polycultures

A polyculture is more than just companion planting -- it is a designed community of plants that occupy different niches:

1. **Choose a center element** (fruit tree, or main crop)
2. **Add nitrogen fixers** (legumes beneath or nearby)
3. **Add dynamic accumulators** (comfrey, yarrow)
4. **Add pest deterrents** (alliums, aromatic herbs)
5. **Add pollinator attractors** (flowers with varied bloom times)
6. **Add ground covers** (clover, thyme, strawberry)
7. **Add root crops** in deeper layers (radish, jerusalem artichoke)
8. **Add vines** on vertical surfaces

## Practical Tips

- Interplant fast-maturing crops (radish, lettuce) between slow-maturing ones (tomatoes, peppers)
- Always have something flowering for pollinators from early spring through late fall
- Plant aromatic herbs (rosemary, thyme, oregano, sage) throughout the garden
- Let some plants go to seed -- dill, cilantro, and fennel flowers attract huge numbers of beneficial insects
- Use [[cover-cropping]] with clover as a living companion under taller crops
- Ducks are the best companion for slug control -- let them patrol the garden in late afternoon
- Avoid large monoculture plantings -- mix varieties and species everywhere
