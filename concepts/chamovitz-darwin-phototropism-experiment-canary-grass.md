# Darwin's Phototropism Experiment with Canary Grass

## Overview

Daniel Chamovitz's "What a Plant Knows" opens with Charles Darwin's
pioneering experiments on phototropism, conducted with his son Francis
and published in Darwin's final book, *The Power of Movement in Plants*
(1880). These experiments demonstrated that plants possess a form of
vision — the ability to perceive light direction and translate that
information into a growth response. The work established foundational
principles of plant [[plant-sensory-biology-smell-touch-hearing]] that continue to influence research.

## Darwin's Observation

Darwin wrote: "There are extremely few [plants], of which some part
… does not bend towards lateral light." In modern terms: almost all
plants bend towards light. This universal behaviour, known as
phototropism, is readily observed in houseplants that bow towards
window light, but the mechanism behind it was unknown until Darwin's
experiments.

## The Canary Grass Setup

### Species
Canary grass (*Phalaris canariensis*) was chosen as the experimental
subject — a common grass with easily observable growth responses.

### Growing Conditions
- A pot of canary grass was grown in a **totally dark room** for
  several days to eliminate any pre-existing light response
- This dark adaptation ensured that any subsequent bending response
  was purely due to the experimental light source

### The Light Source
A very small gas lamp was placed **twelve feet (3.5 metres)** from the
pot. The lamp was kept so dim that Darwin and his son "could not see
the seedlings themselves, nor see a pencil line on paper." Despite
this extremely low light intensity, after only **three hours** the
plants had obviously curved towards the light.

### Key Observation
The curving always occurred at the same part of the young plant —
approximately an inch (two centimetres) below the tip. This localised
response pointed to a specific sensory mechanism rather than a general
light effect on the entire plant.

## The Classic Five-Seedling Experiment

The Darwins designed an elegant experiment to determine which part of
the plant perceived light and which part executed the bending response.
They tested five conditions:

### Seedling A: Untreated Control
The first seedling received no treatment. It demonstrated that the
experimental conditions (dim light, dark room) were conducive to
phototropism. **Result: Bent towards light** — confirmed baseline.

### Seedling B: Tip Removed
The tip (apex) of the seedling was pruned off entirely before light
exposure. **Result: No bending** — the plant went "blind."

### Seedling C: Lightproof Cap on Tip
The tip was covered with an opaque, lightproof cap that prevented
any light from reaching the apex. **Result: No bending** — confirmed
that the tip requires light exposure.

### Seedling D: Clear Glass Cap on Tip
The tip was covered with a transparent glass cap that allowed light
to pass through. **Result: Bent towards light** — the plant could
still "see" through the glass.

### Seedling E: Lightproof Tube on Middle
The middle section of the seedling (below the tip) was covered with
a lightproof tube, while the tip remained exposed. **Result: Bent
towards light** — blocking light from the bending region had no
effect.

## Darwin's Conclusion

The experiments proved that phototropism results from a two-stage
process:

1. **[[plant-vision-and-light-perception-mechanisms]] occurs at the shoot tip** — this is the
   plant's "eye"
2. **The bending response occurs in the midsection below the tip**
   — this is the plant's "muscle"

The tip perceives the light and sends a signal downward telling the
stem to bend in that direction. This was the first demonstration of
rudimentary sight in plants.

## Significance for Plant Biology

### Separation of Perception and Response
Darwin's key insight was demonstrating that light perception and the
growth response occur at different locations on the plant. This
implied a signalling mechanism — some mobile factor that travels from
the tip to the bending region. This [[chamovitz-phytochrome-mobile-signal-leaf-to-flowering]] was later identified
as auxin (indole-3-acetic acid), a plant hormone that redistributes to
the shaded side of the stem, causing cells on that side to elongate
more than cells on the lit side, producing the bend.

### Not Photosynthesis
Darwin explicitly showed that the bending response was not due to
photosynthesis (the process of converting light to chemical energy)
but to an independent light-sensing capability. The extremely dim
light in the experiment was insufficient for photosynthesis but
perfectly adequate to trigger phototropism.

### Foundation for Photoreceptor Discovery
The experiment laid the groundwork for the later discovery of specific
plant photoreceptors. In 1864, [[chamovitz-julius-von-sachs-blue-light-phototropism]] Sachs (Darwin's
contemporary) had already established that blue light is the primary
colour inducing phototropism, while plants are generally insensitive
to other colours. The identification of phototropins (blue light
receptors) in the late 20th century confirmed the molecular basis
of what Darwin observed at the organismal level.

## The Power of Movement in Plants (1880)

This book was Darwin's final published work and represented twenty
years of research on plant movement. It covered not only phototropism
but also gravitropism (response to gravity), thigmotropism (response
to touch), and other plant movements. The phototropism experiments
with canary grass remain the most cited work from this book and
represent one of the most elegant experimental designs in the history
of plant biology.
