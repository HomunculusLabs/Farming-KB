# MDMA and the Drug Terror of the 1980s: Crack Cocaine Economics

The 1980s represented a pivotal decade in the history of psychoactive
substances, marked by the emergence of MDMA (Ecstasy) as a therapeutic
tool and its immediate criminalization, alongside the devastating rise
of crack cocaine.
For McKenna, these developments were not coincidental but reflected a
deeper pattern in which substances that expand consciousness are
suppressed while substances that reinforce social pathology are
tolerated or even tacitly enabled by the economic structures they
serve.

## MDMA: Promise and Suppression

MDMA appeared in significant amounts during the 1980s and showed
considerable promise when used in directed psychotherapy.
Unlike the classical psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, mescaline), MDMA
produced a state of empathic openness and emotional clarity without
the full-blown visual hallucinations that made clinical use of earlier
psychedelics challenging.
Therapists found it uniquely valuable for treating post-traumatic
stress, relationship difficulties, and terminal illness anxiety.

Synthetic amphetamines such as MDA had been sporadically available
from the early 1970s, but MDMA represented a qualitative advance in
the [[metzner-ayahuasca-therapeutic-potential]] of empathogenic substances.
However, like the psychedelics before it, MDMA was quickly made illegal
and forced underground before it could achieve any general impact on
society.
McKenna viewed this as part of a recurring pattern: any substance that
shows genuine potential for expanding human consciousness or
facilitating psychological healing is rapidly criminalized.

## The Crack Cocaine Epidemic

While MDMA and other consciousness-expanding substances were being
suppressed, the drug terror of the 1980s was crack cocaine — a
substance whose effects were almost perfectly antithetical to those of
the psychedelics.
Crack cocaine produced intense but short-lived euphoria, followed by
crashing depression, paranoia, and compulsive redosing.
It created addiction, destroyed communities, and generated enormous
profits for the illicit drug trade.

McKenna argued that crack cocaine's economic profile and high risk for
addiction made it ideal in the eyes of the already established
infrastructure for supplying the ordinary cocaine market.
The same distribution networks that had been built for powdered cocaine
could be repurposed for crack, and the drug's addictive properties
ensured a reliable and expanding customer base.
The social devastation caused by crack was, from this perspective, not
an unintended consequence but a feature that served the economic
interests of the drug trade.

## The Hidden Issue Behind Criminalization

McKenna identified what he considered the hidden issue that makes
governments unwilling to consider legalization: the unmanaged shift
of consciousness that legal and available drugs, including plant
psychedelics, would bring is extremely threatening to a dominator,
ego-oriented culture.
The costs of drug education and drug treatment are small relative to
routine military expenditures and could be contained.
What cannot be contained are the effects that psychedelics would have
in shaping the cultural self-image if all drugs were legal and
available.

This argument frames the [[mckenna-consciousness-expansion-and-drug-war]] not as a public health initiative but
as a [[powell-cultural-defense-criticism-psychedelic-use]] mechanism.
A society organized around hierarchy, competition, and the suppression
of non-ordinary [[mckenna-food-gods-designer-states-of-consciousness]] has a vested interest in
preventing widespread access to substances that dissolve ego
boundaries and reveal the constructed nature of social reality.

## The Suppression Timeline

The pattern McKenna identified can be traced through the twentieth
century.
LSD was criminalized in October 1966, putting an end to promising
research into its [[psilocybin-therapeutic-applications]] for pain, addiction,
alcoholism, and depression during terminal illness.
Psychiatry's promising new uses of LSD were put on indefinite hold.
The research could not be repeated because of its illegality.

It fell to the humble science of botany to advance understanding of
hallucinogenic plants after the suppression of the psychedelic
subculture.
[[richard-evans-schultes]] and his students at Harvard continued the work
of ethnobotany, but their findings remained largely academic and could
not be translated into therapeutic practice.

## An Interrupted Symbiosis

McKenna's broader argument was that an interrupted psychophysical
symbiosis between humans and visionary plants is the unrecognized
cause of the alienation of modernity.
A worldwide attitude of fear toward drugs is fostered and manipulated
by [[mckenna-dissolution-of-the-dominator-culture]] and its propaganda organs.
Vast illicit fortunes continue to be made while government wrings its
hands.
This represents the most recent effort to profiteer from and frustrate
the species' deeply instinctual need to make contact with what McKenna
called the [[powell-gaian-mind-and-entheogenic-flora]] of the living planet.

The distinction McKenna drew between "drugs" and "plant hallucinogens"
was central to his argument.
He preferred to separate the vision-producing plants from the popular
notion of drugs, arguing that the global drug problem has to do with
the fates of nations and mega-dollar criminal syndicates, while the
relationship between humans and psychoactive plants is a matter of
consciousness, healing, and the recovery of lost dimensions of human
experience.

## Toward Decriminalization

McKenna advocated for decriminalization of drugs, mass education, and
shamanism as an interdisciplinary and professional approach to the
realities of psychoactive substance use.
He rejected both the "Just Say No" approach as asinine and feel-good
philosophies that saw unbridled hedonism as the answer.
The shaman, he argued, is a healer of souls, and it is our souls that
have become ill when we abuse drugs.

Such measures would not immediately solve the general drug problem, but
they would preserve the sorely needed pipeline to the spirit necessary
to restructure society's attitude toward plant and substance use and
abuse.
