# Inselbergs and Fold Mountains in Desert Systems

## Overview

Inselbergs (rock domes) and fold mountains represent two of the most significant desert landform types, each offering distinct opportunities for water harvesting, settlement, and productive land use. While simpler than scarp-and-wadi systems, they are among the most extensive and strategically valuable features in arid landscapes.

## Inselbergs and Residual Domes

### Characteristics

Inselbergs are massive isolated or grouped granitic domes or metamorphosed sandstone massifs that rise steeply from the desert plain. Compared to wadi and scarp systems, they are relatively simple:

- **Rare deep caves** but some rock shelter available
- **Rare pronounced valley entries**—most sides dip steeply
- **Loose sandy soils** around the base
- **Very solid run-off areas**, some spanning many tens of hectares

Large trees and good humus soils develop at the base of cliffs on the shade side. A few shallow mulch-filled valleys exist on these massifs, with soil that is mainly humus, capable of supporting trees, yams, and other vegetation.

### Water Harvesting Design

The fundamental ratio for inselberg cultivation is **1 hectare of field to 20 hectares of rock run-off**. Water is led via fluming or gutters to fields where both crops and fruit trees or palms can be grown.

Design approaches include:

- **Concrete or stone gutters** on bare domes leading run-off to walled fields
- **Simple concrete or rock slab gutters** gathering water from rock dunes for infiltration to fields or storage in cisterns
- **Small tanks** with access ramps for pigeon, quail, small mammals, and frogs
- **Thatch covers** to retard evaporation and exclude large animals

### Occupancy Guidelines

The larger systems (100 hectares or more) have historically been occupied as base camps for long-range foraging after rains. Each inselberg has a special character requiring sensitive and restricted occupancy—developing and protecting natural resources rather than attempting extensive systems.

Very small rock slabs (1–10 hectares) are useful for a gutter and tank system, a hectare or two of trees, and a wayside house. Many have been guttered using rock walls, slab on edge, or concreted drains to serve houses or steam trains for water in remote locations. Wildlife ramp-tanks for desert birds and small mammals are a valuable addition.

## Fold Mountains

### Geological Formation

Fold mountains are the most extensive features in many deserts, with characteristics combining those of inselbergs and scarps but generally far more complex. They form through:

- **Continental drift** creating fold series inside coastal ranges
- **Synclines** (down-flexed sediments) that may erode to great canoe shapes with river gorges cutting their way out
- **Anticlines** that may form great whalebacks, eventually developing long valleys in the hills as rivers form on their spines
- **Basin and range topography** evolving from folded series over geological time

### Water Storage Opportunities

Fold mountains offer the greatest dam potential of any desert landform:

1. **Exit dams:** Dams at the exit of rivers from ranges create large ovoid freshwater lakes. The ratio of run-off area to storage area must be 20–30:1 in deserts. Modest, deep storages are more likely to hold water year-round than large shallow ones.

2. **Foothill dams:** Where massive ranges back the fold mountains, foothill dams hold water from snow melt and rain run-off. Settlements and roads must be well off flood paths.

3. **Modest dams:** Narrow rock-walled exit valleys, foothill diversion dams, and complex freshwater dam systems led off low-slope wadi-like streams flowing to the plains.

4. **Permanent rockholes and streams:** A characteristic of fold systems is that they occur in large series with correspondingly large catchments, often maintaining some permanent water.

### Palisade Rock Formations

All fold mountains of sedimentary or metamorphic rock show more or less marked palisades of harder rock series running along the slopes like sub-contours. These are ecologically significant:

- Vegetation is **far taller, greener, and denser** on the upslope side of palisades because run-off is slowed and forced to infiltrate
- Some are **wide-spaced, near-contour**, project well above the surface, and develop striking tree-lines
- While not as effective as purpose-built swales, they assist water infiltration
- **Horizontal bores** along these palisades often tap sweet water trapped in rock strata

### Headwater Stream Diversion

A shallow, fast dry-stream system develops out of the upper foothill regions, typically 4–15 metres wide with banks 0.5–2 metres high. The bed is rock-filled, and flood detritus along banks shows the extent of flood levels. Floods are violent and sudden—boulders and smashed tree branches attest to this.

**Key strategy:** Rather than damming these violent flows directly (which would fill with boulder and silt in a few rains), the approach is to **bleed off the torrent** and divert it to a more peaceful contour flow, then to a dam. An oversized, wide, shallow contour trench allows silt to settle before reaching the dam. The trench itself serves as a planting site and an easily cleaned silt trap.

### Engineering Considerations

Dams in fold mountains require:
- Careful hydrological assessment
- Professional engineering
- Awareness that the opportunity for successful freshwater dams is greater here than in other desert landforms
- Positioning settlements well below dams to avoid catastrophic consequences from burst dams in war or earthquake
