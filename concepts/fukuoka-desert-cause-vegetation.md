# Fukuoka Desert Cause and Vegetation

## Overview

Fukuoka's analysis of desertification goes beyond the conventional focus on water scarcity. He identifies the root cause as the destruction of vegetative diversity by human action, and he proposes a radical solution: large-scale aerial seed scattering as the primary intervention, reversing the conventional approach of building dams and irrigation systems first.

## The Root Cause Chain

Fukuoka traces a clear causal chain:

1. Man cuts down trees and his livestock feeds on grasses
2. The varieties of plant life invariably dwindle
3. The simplified vegetative cover is easily destroyed
4. If the land is taken over by yellow grasses such as foxtail, the increased reflection of sunlight raises the temperature of the ground
5. This upsets weather patterns and causes the accelerated evaporation of ground moisture
6. The land becomes desert

"The land does not become a desert because the water disappears. The water disappears because of the fundamental conditions preceding and underlying its appearance -- namely, the perishing of other plants."

## The Temperature Data

Fukuoka's investigation on abandoned American farmland revealed striking temperature differences:

- Soil temperature on exposed land with dry grasses: normally 20-30 degrees Celsius higher than areas covered with green grasses or near woods
- A spot adjacent to lush vegetation: 30 degrees Celsius
- Temperature on a stretch of yellow grasses: rises to 60 degrees Celsius

State forestry bureau researchers were themselves astonished at these figures. They had suspected reflected heat from arid land affected surrounding areas, but the data confirmed the devastating feedback loop.

## Against Conventional Approaches

Since everyone assumes vegetation disappears because of a lack of water, revegetation plans always begin by drawing in water. Dams and irrigation systems are constructed. Fukuoka considers this approach fundamentally flawed:

- It addresses results rather than causes
- It is only marginally effective
- Countless examples in Egypt and elsewhere demonstrate frequent failure
- Irrigation often ends in failure on account of [[cervantes-salt-buildup-leaching-management]] in irrigated fields
- It represents the "throwaway" use of land

## The Pivot Farm as Symbol

The pivot farm is described as a fitting symbol of [[fukuoka-american-agriculture-fields-of-death]]'s destructive approach: a mammoth sprinkler system a half-mile long rotates about a circular field, continuously sprinkling water drawn from depths of hundreds of feet. After 5-6 years of [[jadam-salt-accumulation-in-soil]] on the soil surface, the system is moved to adjoining land and the abandoned land becomes a true desert.

## The Real Solution: Seed Scattering

Fukuoka's approach begins with a large-scale [[clay-seed-pellet-composition-and-aerial-seeding-method]]:

1. Scatter the seeds of many different kinds of grasses, trees, and vegetables for greening the deserts from airplanes
2. Mix together the seeds of many different vegetables and green manure plants, and scatter the mixture over the land
3. Even if 99 percent fail, some seeds will sprout somewhere -- this germination gives precious clues
4. Continue seeding with the full expectation of failure -- if anything shows even a slight survival rate, seed it again the second year
5. Start out with a three-year plan, expecting to fail about three years, and just concentrate on sowing seed over the entire area, as far as the eye can see

## The Sequence of Natural Recovery

If cactuses grow on arid land, then other succulents will grow there as well. Stonecrop, amaranthus, and wormwood can take hold at an early stage. Plants of the lily family such as wild rocambole, leek, and garlic may also be able to grow. The progression continues naturally if given the chance.

## The Acacia Tree Lesson

The seeds that fall below a single old [[fukuoka-black-wattle-fertilizer-tree-method]] tree number in the hundreds of thousands, even millions. But very often no more than one or two saplings will grow. The chances for survival are perhaps one in ten thousand, or one in a hundred thousand. Most seeds disappear -- eaten by birds, stashed away by mice, or carried off by ants. Yet this is not waste in the eyes of nature. The survival of just those few seedlings is part of the natural ecological process.

## Man and Domestic Animals as Enemies of the Land

Fukuoka's observation from decades on his farm: "Allowing any animals larger than chickens -- such as cows, horses, goats, or sheep -- to graze freely on a farm of up to five acres depletes the soil. Allowing too many chickens to range over the land is also harmful. Strictly speaking, even living self-sufficiently in a small mountain hut on the land has a negative impact, no matter how religiously all human wastes and ash from the hearth are returned to the soil."

"The quickest way to enrich the land is to scatter green manure and vegetable seeds beneath the fruit trees and leave the place uninhabited."
