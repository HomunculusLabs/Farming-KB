# Saprobic Soil Fungi — Colony Selection, Community Fractionation, Baiting, and Basidiomycete Isolation

## Selecting Colonies from Isolation Plates

The practice of arbitrarily selecting only "different-looking" colonies from isolation plates is strongly discouraged. Instead, investigators should follow a **predetermined sampling pattern**, gathering all isolates and ranking them by abundance (see Fig. 13.1 in the source text). This systematic approach offers several critical advantages:

1. **Forces comprehensive evaluation.** All fungi are assessed, not merely the conspicuous or immediately interesting ones. Species that appear uncommon at first glance often prove to be common, compelling investigators to grapple with taxonomically difficult groups that might otherwise be ignored.

2. **Reduces investigator bias.** Less experienced researchers tend to overselect pigmented and heavily sporulating colonies — such as *Epicoccum nigrum*, *Stachybotrys chartarum*, and *Aspergillus terreus* — while overlooking dingy, less distinctive colonies of *Acremonium*, *Fusarium*, and featureless basidiomycetes that may be ecologically significant.

3. **Enables pattern detection.** Recurring ecological and distributional patterns are detectable and quantifiable only when systematic, abundance-ranked data are collected.

4. **Produces revisitable datasets.** Species lists lacking relative commonness information rarely establish patterns that can be revisited, tested, or compared across studies.

The **quantity of unidentified material** should also be carefully recorded. High proportions of unidentified isolates may indicate taxonomic uniqueness or regional endemism — a signal that warrants further investigation rather than dismissal.

### Dissecting Microscopy

A dissecting microscope with **epistage and substage lighting** and variable magnification (up to 45×, preferably 80–120×) is essential for colony examination and dissection. Higher magnification is critical for distinguishing fine morphological features and for hyphal transfer work.

## Fractionating the Soil Fungal Community

Standard isolation methods rapidly yield many isolates of readily cultured, fast-growing species. However, the high propagule densities of common species can mask or physically interfere with the isolation of rare and slow-growing fungi. Several **fractionation techniques** have been developed to address this bias.

### Density-Gradient Centrifugation

- **Paden (1967)** demonstrated that large ascospores could be preferentially isolated using sucrose density gradients, selectively enriching for fungi producing larger propagules.
- **Okuda et al. (1995)** employed Percoll density-gradient centrifugation, recovering more taxa than standard suspension plating, though no entirely novel groups were detected.
- **Miller et al. (1994)** used sucrose density gradients to separate basidiospores and sclerotia from *Pinus contorta* soils, providing targeted access to these otherwise underrepresented propagule types.

### Differential Centrifugation

Differential centrifugation has been applied specifically for isolating **predatory nematophagous fungi** (discussed in detail in Chapter 19 of the source text), exploiting differences in propagule size and density.

### Partial Killing of the Fungal Population

Deliberately reducing the viable fungal population with dry heat, steam, or chemical solvents shifts the recovered community toward ascomycetes and basidiomycetes that possess resistant propagules — including ascospores, thick-walled chlamydospores, and sclerotia.

#### Ethanol Pasteurization

- Introduced by **Warcup and Baker (1963)**, ethanol pasteurization combined with mild heat stimulates ascospore germination while killing more sensitive propagules.
- **Mahoney (1972)** found that 44 of 250 taxa from the Galapagos Islands were isolated *only* after ethanol pasteurization — a striking demonstration of its power to extend species lists.
- Ethanol pasteurization alone performs approximately as well as ethanol combined with heat (Mahoney 1972). It lowers recovery of yeast species and thin-walled conidia, while increasing isolation of Eurotiales, *Chaetomium*, *Xylaria*, and coprophilous fungi.

#### Other Chemical Pasteurizations

- **Phenol pasteurization** (Furuya and Naito 1980; Udagawa and Uchiyama 1998) provides an effective variation for ascomycete isolation.
- **Formalin pasteurization** (Warcup 1951b) represents another established approach to selectively enrich for resistant propagule-bearing fungi.

## Baiting

Baiting involves placing an organic substratum — enriched nutritionally — in direct contact with or mixed into soil, allowing target organisms to preferentially colonize it. This technique arose primarily from the need to assay for difficult-to-isolate plant pathogens.

Baiting is **not widely used in broad soil fungal surveys**. Notable exceptions include:

- **Cellophane** strips for cellulolytic fungi
- **Hair** baits for keratinolytic fungi
- **Insect** baits for entomopathogenic fungi
- **Chitin, cellulose, keratin, and pollen** baits for Chytridiomycetes and other zoosporic fungi (the group for which baiting is most extensively employed)

## Sclerotia

Sclerotial populations are assessed primarily during **autoecological studies** of root-associated or pathogenic fungi (Coley-Smith and Cooke 1971; Singleton et al. 1992). Their location in soil is guided by knowledge of associated plant hosts, ectomycorrhizae, or the positions of fruiting bodies.

### Separation Techniques

- **Wet-sieving** (Trappe 1969; Adams 1979)
- **Elutriation** (Porter and Steele 1983)
- **Density-gradient centrifugation** (Miller et al. 1994)
- **Direct observation** on the soil surface (Cotter and Bills 1985)

**Miller et al. (1994)** surveyed both burned and unburned *Pinus contorta* stands in Wyoming for sclerotia of *Cenococcum geophilum* and *Morchella* species, demonstrating the utility of targeted sclerotial surveys in fire ecology contexts.

## Selective Isolation of Saprobic Basidiomycetes

Surveys relying on standard culture-based isolation markedly **underestimate saprobic basidiomycete diversity** (Warcup 1959; Warcup and Talbot 1962; Thorn et al. 1996). Two principal factors explain their rarity in isolation studies:

1. Basidiomycete propagules are vastly outnumbered by Zygomycete and mitosporic fungal spores in most soils.
2. Even when basidiomycetes germinate on isolation media, they are rapidly overgrown by faster-growing competitors.

### Warcup's Methods

**Warcup (1950, 1955, 1957, 1959)** developed two pioneering approaches:

- **Soil plates** — small amounts of soil are directly embedded in agar medium, allowing in situ fungal growth.
- **Hyphal fragment plating** — soil is dispersed in water and individual hyphal fragments are plated onto agar.

These methods demanded considerable skill and effort, which deterred widespread adoption by other investigators.

### Particle Filtration with Benomyl-Containing Medium

**Thorn et al. (1996)** introduced a more accessible approach combining particle filtration with a selective medium:

- **Benomyl** is toxic to most ascomycetes but relatively benign to basidiomycetes, suppressing competition.
- **Alkali lignin** is added to encourage ligninolytic fungi.
- **Guaiacol** serves as a colorimetric indicator of laccase or peroxidase activity — development of a dark brownish-red oxidation zone signals a positive result.

Plates are scanned at low magnification (40–100×). Putative basidiomycetes are selected while undesired mitosporic fungi (e.g., *Alternaria*, *Trichoderma*) are avoided. Using this approach, **Thorn et al. (1996) obtained 67 basidiomycete isolates from 64 soil samples across 35 plots**. Additional species were recovered from fresh soils using Drechsler or Warcup soil plates.

### Direct Hyphal Transfer

Some basidiomycetes produce characteristic **lax white cottony colonies** among soil crumbs on isolation plates. These hyphae can be picked with a fine needle or forceps and transferred to fresh media, bypassing the need for spore germination entirely.

## Isolation Techniques for Soil Chytridiomycetes

### Collection Strategy

Both **fresh and air-dried soils** should be studied, because chytrid taxa differ in their tolerance of desiccation. Chytrids produce either thin-walled sporangial stages, thick-walled resting spores, or both; some species do not form resting spores at all. Consequently:

- **Ephemeral taxa** lacking resting spores may not be recoverable from dried soils and require fresh material.
- **Drying reduces competitors**, meaning some resting-spore chytrids are best isolated from air-dried samples (Whisler 1987).

A robust sampling protocol calls for **multiple soil samples (at least 14)** collected across a range of sites, with sampling repeated at least once during each major season to capture temporal variation.

### Identification

Chytrid identification presents special challenges:

- Many taxa possess distinctive, stable morphologies, but others — producing only simple spherical sporangia with unornamented walls and rhizoids — are exceedingly difficult to identify.
- Some species exhibit **morphological plasticity** that crosses generic boundaries (Powell and Koch 1977a,b).
- Because most chytrids do not grow by hyphal tip elongation, few can be isolated using standard hyphal fungus strategies (Willoughby 1984).

Instead, **zoospore motility is exploited** using baits of chitin, cellulose, keratin, and pollen to attract and trap motile zoospores.

#### Modern Classification

Modern chytrid taxonomy is based on **zoospore ultrastructure** (Barr 1980), which provides stable, reliable, and conservative characters. This has created parallel classification systems — morphological versus ultrastructure-based — that sometimes conflict. For example, the genus *Phlytochytrium* was split into four genera on the basis of zoospore ultrastructure alone (Barr 1980).

Reference works include the most recent comprehensive monograph by **Sparrow (1960)**, subsequent generic monographs (e.g., *Septosperma* — Blackwell and Powell 1991), and revisions by **Karling (1977)**.

For large-scale identification work, maintaining chytrids in pure culture may be impractical. Instead, **single thalli are transferred to enriched water-bait cultures** (Johnson 1973, 1975). However, description of new genera requires enriched or pure culture to determine zoospore architecture.

### Enumeration

Quantifying chytrid propagules per unit soil weight is technically difficult. **Willoughby (1984)** proposed mixing released zoospores with polycell wallpaper paste and counting resulting thalli. In practice, chytrids are typically recorded as **present or absent**, with abundance expressed as **percent frequency**:

> **Percent frequency** = (collections containing the taxon / total collections) × 100

This metric does not distinguish truly rare species from those that are locally abundant but patchily distributed. Abundant species nonetheless achieve higher frequency across multiple samples.

Other enumeration approaches:

- **Most probable number (MPN) method** (Ulken and Sparrow 1968) — shown to be unreliable for soil chytrids (Booth and Barrett 1976).
- **Distribution intensity index (DII)** = percent occurrence × √(percent frequency) (Booth and Barrett 1976) — a composite metric integrating both spatial occurrence and frequency.

Typical chytrid diversity ranges from **2 to 35 species per habitat** (Willoughby 1961; Booth and Barrett 1976). **Biodiversity** is quantified as total species per site, while **ecological prominence** is assessed using the DII.

## Summary

Effective study of saprobic soil fungi requires disciplined colony selection, strategic community fractionation to overcome competitive exclusion, targeted baiting and sclerotial recovery, and specialized methods for basidiomycetes and chytridiomycetes that standard plating techniques underrepresent. Together these approaches yield a more complete and less biased picture of soil fungal diversity.
