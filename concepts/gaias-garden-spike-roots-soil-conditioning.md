## Key Spike Root Species

### Deep Taproot Plants

- **Daikon radish** (Raphanus sativus) -- also called Japanese radish or
  tillage radish; enormous taproot can penetrate 3-4 feet into compacted
  soil; edible root can be pulled with one mighty tug rather than dug;
  if left unharvested, the blossoms attract beneficial insects and the fat
  root adds humus as it rots
- **Chicory** (Cichorium intybus) -- renowned lengthy taproot that seeks
  out potassium, sulfur, calcium, and magnesium; perennial, so roots
  grow larger each year; blue flowers attract pollinators; edible roots
  and greens
- **Comfrey** (Symphytum spp.) -- deep, aggressive root system breaks up
  compacted soil; produces enormous biomass for
  [[gaias-garden-nitrogen-fixers-guild-design]];
  requires reasonably fertile soil to establish well
- **Fava beans** (Vicia faba) -- strong root system breaks up surface
  compaction; also fixes nitrogen; edible beans and greens

## Two Strategies for Using Spike Roots

Hemenway describes two approaches for incorporating spike-rooted plants
into garden design:

### Strategy 1: Pre-Planting Soil Conditioning

Sow spike-rooted species into a future orchard or garden area one or two
years before the final planting. Let the roots work the soil for a full
growing season (or two), then cut the plants down and proceed with the
intended planting. The soil will be significantly improved with better
drainage, aeration, and organic matter content.

### Strategy 2: Intercropping Among Established Plants

Interplant spike-rooted species among existing beds or under trees to
continually break up soil. Daikon radish seeds can be sprinkled in open
spots among perennial plantings. The long roots can be pulled when
needed, or left to decompose in place. Chicory and comfrey can be
maintained as permanent understory plants beneath fruit trees.

## The Daikon Radish Technique

Hemenway provides a specific technique for using daikon radish in heavy
soil:

1. Sow daikon seed in compacted garden beds in late summer or early fall
2. Allow the radishes to grow through the fall and into winter
3. The roots will penetrate deep into the soil, breaking up compaction
4. Snap the radishes off at ground level (do not pull them out, to
  leave the root channels intact)
5. Each daikon leaves a forearm-sized load of organic matter in the soil
  as it decomposes
6. The root channels improve drainage and aeration for subsequent crops
