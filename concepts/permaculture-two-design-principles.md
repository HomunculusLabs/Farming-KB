# Permaculture Two Design Principles

updated: 2026-04-18

Permaculture Two: Practical Design for Town & Country in Permanent Agriculture (Tagari Books, 1979) by Bill Mollison establishes the core design framework that distinguishes permaculture from gardening or farming. This page summarizes the foundational principles unique to Permaculture Two. See [[permaculture-design-principles-beginners]] for the broader principles from both books.

## What Permaculture Is

"Permaculture is primarily a consciously designed agricultural system -- a system which, if we follow the lessons of nature, will provide all the necessities of life for people living in it." Permaculture Two is about design, not gardening or livestock per se but as elements in a system intended to serve man, and the ends of good ecology.

## The Seven Reasons for Design

1. Save energies -- reduce external energy inputs
2. Cope with external energies -- sun, wind, fire
3. Mutual plant assistance -- guilds and companion planting
4. Optimal placement of all units -- zonation for efficiency
5. Suit climate and site -- species matched to conditions
6. Integrate with man/society -- reduce heating, cooking fuel, labor
7. Provide wide range of necessities achievable by every person

## The Stacking Principle

Williams' description of multi-tier agriculture: palm tree above, olive beneath, fig under the olive, pomegranate and vine below the fig, then grain and vegetables. Stacking intensity is determined by environment -- rainfall sets the main limitation. A well-occupied system resists invasion by rampant forms. At about 60 years for long-lived elements, systems begin to simplify and need intervention.

## Edge Effect as Design Tool

The interface between two ecosystems represents a third, more complex system. Gross photosynthetic production is higher at interfaces. Estuaries and coral reefs show the highest production per unit area. A landscape with complex edge is interesting, beautiful, and more productive.

## Slope-Based Zonation

- Ridge-top: Storage dams, fire protection, high forests
- Keyline: Water distribution system following contour
- Midslope: Gardens, dwellings, small dams
- Lower slope: Reflection from dams, solar collectors
- Access: Integrated drainage, fire control, cargo access

## The Crop-Master Concept

From Chapter 1 on Aboriginal ecology: "The crop-master as the most important person in the tribe -- the living computer, one of a long line of accurate memories, who orchestrated the taking of food, who arranged the taboos and the prohibitions, the feasts and celebrations." Mollison observes: "We lack crop-masters today."

Brother trees (stringy bark, ironbark, native cherry, cider gum) defined tribal range in Aboriginal ecology. The crop-master managed the relationship between people and their food landscape through accumulated generational knowledge.

## How Much Land

"Admire a large estate if you like: but farm a small one." -- Virgil

The concept of being "land poor": men with too much land become poorer as holdings increase. The ideal plot is about 750-1000 m2 for the home garden in annual agriculture. In Russia, 4% of land around peasant houses produces 60% of the food.

## Design for Community

Permaculture Two extends beyond individual sites to community design:

- Every dwelling needs a tank, dry toilet, small glasshouse, insulated space, and garden
- Community ownership of land and public resources (life leases on homes and gardens)
- Groups limited to 300-3000 people with at least 5 locations per group
- Regional groups choose specialized manufactures suited to local resources
- Global federations of specialized groups in travel and trade

## The Ethical Framework

The ethics underlying Permaculture Two:

- Care of the earth
- Absolutely no waste products (hence no "unemployed")
- World citizenship and membership of spaceship earth
- Global communication and education
- Self-reliance balanced with aid to others to establish self-reliance
- Adoption of the most sophisticated environmental principles known
- A moratorium on freeways, arms, centralized power, and non-principled energy export

## Design as Integration

"It only remains for the homesteader to gather these warps of plants and wefts of animals into a logical weave." The plants should be arranged to hedge and shield the buildings, permit observation, and reduce losses from exposure to sun, wind, rain, and predators. Salt marsh may suggest more geese, wetlands more ducks, and deserts more quail or pigeon in the species mix.
