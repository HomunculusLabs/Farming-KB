# Four Factors of Evaporative Demand in Mushroom Tropism

## Overview

Badham (1982) proposed that the primary mechanism driving stipe curvature in
*Psilocybe cubensis* is differential evaporative demand across the stipe surface.
This evaporative demand is a function of four interacting environmental factors:
wind, humidity, light, and temperature. The combined effect of these factors
determines the rate of water loss from the stipe surface, which in turn modulates
cell expansion rates and produces directional growth.

## The Evaporative Demand Model

The central hypothesis is that cells on the side of the stipe experiencing greater
evaporative demand elongate more slowly than cells on the side with lower
evaporative demand. This differential growth rate produces curvature toward the
side of greater demand (into the wind). The four factors that determine
evaporative demand are:

### 1. Wind

Wind is the primary factor redistributing the boundary layer around the stipe.
In still air, the boundary layer (a thin layer of relatively still, humid air
adjacent to the stipe surface) is uniform. When wind flows past the stipe:

- The boundary layer becomes thicker on the leeward (downwind) side, where
  recirculation and eddies trap moist air.
- The boundary layer remains thinner on the windward (upwind) side, where
  the flowing air continuously removes water vapor.
- The result is asymmetric evaporative demand: greater on the windward side,
  lower on the leeward side.

Badham measured wind velocity at 9 ± 3 cm/sec in his wind tunnel experiments
and demonstrated that this was sufficient to direct stipe growth.

### 2. Humidity

Relative humidity directly determines the vapor pressure gradient between the
stipe surface and the surrounding air:

- At high humidity (near saturation), the vapor pressure gradient is small,
  and evaporative demand is low regardless of wind speed.
- At lower humidity, the gradient steepens, amplifying the effect of wind on
  differential water loss.
- Badham maintained humidity at 94 ± 3% RH in his wind tunnel—high enough to
  prevent desiccation but low enough to maintain a meaningful vapor pressure
  gradient.

Humidity also affects the boundary layer itself, as the moisture content of the
air flowing past the stipe influences the rate at which water vapor can diffuse
away from the surface.

### 3. Light

Light influences evaporative demand through two potential mechanisms:

- **Heating effect**: Light absorbed by the stipe surface raises tissue
  temperature, increasing the vapor pressure of water at the surface and thus
  the rate of evaporation. Under strong illumination, the light-facing side
  of the stipe could experience greater evaporative demand than the shaded
  side, producing phototropic curvature.

- **Hormonal trigger**: Light may trigger hormonal responses that modulate
  cell wall extensibility or growth rate independently of the purely physical
  effects of evaporative demand. In *P. cubensis*, light is necessary for
  basidiocarp initiation and spore production, suggesting at least two distinct
  light-sensitive pathways.

Badham noted that the light flux density in his experiments was approximately
1/10 of natural field conditions, suggesting that light was not the dominant
factor in his wind tunnel studies. However, he acknowledged that if small
quantities of light trigger hormonic responses, the situation could be
entirely different.

### 4. Temperature

Temperature affects evaporative demand through vapor pressure (higher temperature
increases saturation vapor pressure, steepening the gradient), metabolic rate
(enzyme activity, membrane fluidity, turgor maintenance), and boundary layer
properties (temperature gradients create convective currents). Badham maintained
24.5 ± 1.5°C to isolate wind and humidity as primary variables.

## Interaction Effects

The four factors interact in complex ways:

- **Wind × humidity**: Wind's boundary layer effect is modulated by ambient
  humidity. At very high humidity, even strong wind produces minimal
  differential evaporation.
- **Light × temperature**: Light absorption increases tissue temperature,
  coupling both effects on evaporative demand.
- **Temperature × humidity**: Temperature increase at constant relative
  humidity changes absolute moisture content and evaporative demand.
- **All four**: Under natural conditions, all four vary simultaneously.
  Net evaporative demand at any stipe surface point integrates all gradients.

## Boundary Layer Mechanics

The boundary layer is central to understanding how evaporative demand becomes
asymmetric around the stipe:

- **Laminar vs. turbulent flow**: At low wind speeds, flow around the stipe
  is laminar, producing a predictable boundary layer profile. At higher speeds,
  turbulence creates eddies that can enhance mixing and modify the effective
  boundary layer thickness.

- **Reynolds number considerations**: The Reynolds number for wind flow around
  a mushroom stipe (roughly 1 cm diameter at 10 cm/sec) is in the laminar-to-
  transitional regime, where both laminar and turbulent effects may coexist.

- **Stipe surface properties**: The texture and wettability of the stipe
  surface affect how readily water evaporates and how the boundary layer
  adheres to the surface. The partial veil (velum) in young basidiocarps may
  further modify boundary layer dynamics.

## Comparison with Phycomyces

The model draws support from *Phycomyces blakesleeanus* work:

- Johnson and Gamow (1971) demonstrated wind and water vapor necessity for
  the avoidance response in *Phycomyces*.
- The sporangiophore bends away from objects, consistent with boundary layer
  redistribution.
- Ethylene involvement in *Phycomyces* suggests additional volatile signals
  may contribute alongside water vapor.
