# Stone Cellar Construction

Stone cellars represent the most permanent and durable approach to crop
storage in Sepp Holzer's permaculture system. Built on the [[krameterhof-holzer-model-farm]] specifically for storing fruit and vegetables, these
structures are designed to last for generations. While more labour-intensive
to build than [[holzer-roundwood-shelters-earth-cellars]], stone cellars
provide unmatched longevity and stable storage conditions. They complement
the broader traditions of [[sepp-holzer-earth-cellars-and-philosophy]] and
[[earth-cellar-construction]].

## Comparison with Roundwood Shelters

Stone cellars have the same basic characteristics as a cellar made from
wood, only stone cellars are meant to last forever. This means that building
one requires a great deal more work. The choice between stone and roundwood
construction depends on the resources available, the intended lifespan, and
how much labour can be invested in the project.

Roundwood shelters can be built in a single day with an excavator and one
additional person, at a cost of 700 to 800 euros (if timber is already
available). Stone cellars require significantly more time, labour, and
skill, but they will outlast any wooden structure by centuries.

## Storage Conditions

The optimal storage room for all types of crops maintains humidity between
80 and 90 percent and temperature between 8 and 10 degrees Celsius, using
just the warmth of the soil and no additional energy. These conditions are
critical for successful long-term storage of most fruits and vegetables.

Modern houses with cellars containing heating systems and concrete floors
are disastrous for storing fruit and vegetables. The heating means that the
humidity is usually so low that apples put into storage wither and wrinkle
in the shortest amount of time. The high humidity and steady temperature of
earth-insulated cellars is ideal for the majority of crops.

This connects to the broader [[permaculture-food-storage-and-preservation]]
philosophy of using passive, energy-free methods wherever possible.

## Ventilation Design

Good air circulation is essential for successful cellar storage. The
ventilation pipes must be large enough to provide the room with the required
amount of oxygen. Holzer's stone cellar design includes a sophisticated
ventilation system:

### Air Inlet Pipe
The air goes through a ten-metre-long underground pipe into the stone
cellar. The pipe reaches to a depth of around one metre. On its way through
the pipe, the air is brought to the same temperature as the soil. If the
temperature of the air coming in was not the same as the air inside, there
could be an unwanted temperature drop in the cellar. The result would be a
build-up of condensation. The dripping water could make the crops begin to
rot or moulder.

The air inlet pipe must be at least ten metres long so that the incoming air
has time to adjust to the temperature of the soil. If the air inlet pipe is
sloped slightly downwards, it can also be used as a drain pipe when the
cellar is being cleaned.

### Air Outlet Pipe
The air outlet pipe should be put in at the highest point in the room. This
also helps to prevent condensation from building up. Warm, moist air rises
naturally and exits through the outlet, drawing fresh air in through the
inlet pipe.

### Pipe Sizing
Even the diameter of the air inlet and outlet pipes must be adjusted
according to the size of the room. For a 1x3 metre earth cellar, Holzer uses
ventilation pipes with a diameter of at least 15cm. Larger cellars require
proportionally larger pipes.

## Floor Design

Gravel is laid on the floor for drainage. This ensures that any condensation
or water that enters the cellar can drain away rather than pooling around the
stored crops. A compacted earth floor, rather than concrete, helps maintain
the high humidity levels that are essential for crop storage.

The gravel floor also allows air to circulate beneath the stored crops,
preventing the build-up of stagnant air that can promote mould and decay.
This connects to [[root-cellar-design-and-construction]] principles used in
many traditional root cellar designs.

## Condensation Prevention

Preventing condensation is one of the most critical aspects of cellar
design. Condensation occurs when warm, moist air comes into contact with
cool surfaces, causing water droplets to form. In a storage cellar, this can
lead to rot and mould that destroys the stored crops.

The ten-metre underground air inlet pipe is the primary defence against
condensation. By tempering the incoming air to match the cellar temperature
before it enters, the system prevents the thermal shock that causes
condensation. The placement of the outlet pipe at the highest point ensures
that moist air is efficiently removed from the cellar.

## Structural Durability

Stone cellars are built to be permanent structures. Using locally sourced
stone, they can be constructed without imported materials. The thermal mass
of the stone walls provides excellent temperature stability, buffering
against both summer heat and winter cold. The stone also acts as a moisture
buffer, absorbing excess humidity when levels are high and releasing it when
the air becomes too dry.

This permanence reflects the [[sepp-holzer-method]] principle of designing
systems that endure. While roundwood shelters may last 30 years with larch
or robinia, a well-built stone cellar will last for centuries with minimal
maintenance.

## Integration with Farm Systems

Stone cellars can be integrated into the broader farm infrastructure in
several ways:

- **Crop storage**: Holding fruit, vegetables, and root crops through winter
  without energy inputs
- **Seed storage**: Providing cool, stable conditions for [[holzer-seed-saving-and-variety-preservation]]
- **Grafting scion storage**: Keeping scions dormant in wet sand until spring
  for [[holzer-grafting-fruit-tree-propagation]]
- **Mushroom cultivation**: The cool, humid environment can support certain
  types of [[holzer-mushroom-cultivation-outdoor-beds]]
- **Processing**: Providing a cool workspace for making juices, preserves,
  and other processed products

## Climate Adaptation

Building roundwood shelters and earth cellars is both possible and practical
all over the world. Holzer has had good results with all of his projects. If
you make use of the balancing effect of the soil, you can create a pleasant,
cool retreat in hot places and a warm one in cold places. You just have to
understand how to make use of nature's resources properly.
