# Falconer Mushroom Marketing and Economics of the 1890s
Falconer opened his treatise by declaring mushrooms "a healthful and
grateful food" that, "when successfully grown, become a most profitable
crop." He believed America could have "the best market in the world for
[[mushroom-cultivation-in-kenya]]," with prices more than double those
paid in London. Chapter XVII provided detailed guidance on [[falconer-gathering-and-marketing-mushrooms]] and marketing.

## The American Market Advantage

America offered uniquely favorable conditions for mushroom cultivation:
- Abundant supply of horse [[falconer-manure-for-mushroom-beds]] in cities (before the automobile era)
- Insatiable winter demand at good prices
- A population "alert to every profitable, legitimate business"
- Prices more than double London's
- The vicinity of New York was both the great mushroom-growing center and
  the best mushroom market in the country

## Economics for Different Grower Classes

### Market Gardeners

Mushrooms were a winter crop filling the gap when outdoor operations were
at a standstill. Manure used first for mushrooms was thoroughly rotted
and ideal for subsequent spring crops of tomatoes, lettuce, cabbages, and
cauliflowers -- making the [[falconer-mushroom-cultivation-victorian-methods]] enterprise doubly profitable.
Key economic facts:
- The crop's only cost was labor in making the beds and the price of spawn
- Manure was essentially free (city stable manure, often gratis)
- There was always a demand at good price
- If the crop turned out well it was "nearly all profit"
- Even a complete failure cost very little: "it must be a bad failure that
  will not yield enough to pay for its cost"
- Market gardeners guarded everything about mushroom-growing as trade
  secrets, even from lifelong friends

### Florists

A 100-foot greenhouse could yield 500 square feet of bed producing 250
pounds at fifty cents per pound net ($125), with the additional expense
being only labor and spawn price. Mushrooms grew in the otherwise
worthless spaces under greenhouse benches.

### Private Gardeners

Mushroom growing was routine duty in gentlemen's gardens. For farmers'
wives, mushroom growing provided pin-money: with "an insatiable market
demand for mushrooms all winter long, at good prices, no farmer's wife
need care whether the eggs and poultry bring good prices or not."

### Village People and Suburban Residents

Could grow mushrooms in boxes, barrels, or frames in any cellar, shed,
or spare room.

### Farmers

Could grow mushrooms in any shed or cellar, with minimal investment.

## Prices and Yields

### Prices

- Net price: approximately fifty cents per pound ($0.50/lb)
- London prices were less than half American prices
- Mushrooms were a luxury food item commanding premium prices

### Yields

- Falconer's Dosoris cellar: 3/5 to 4/5 pound per square foot of bed
  surface
- Standard estimate: 1/2 pound per square foot
- Mr. Gardner marketed 1,700 to 2,500 pounds of mushrooms per season
- Mr. Denton of Woodhaven marketed 1,000 to 2,000 pounds per season
- A 100-foot greenhouse: 250 pounds from 500 square feet of bed
- Mr. Barter of London: 21,000 pounds per year from ridge beds

### Manure Costs

- City stable manure: often free or very cheap (agents handled supply
  efficiently)
- Baled manure: 350-500 lbs per bale, sometimes gratis (freight only)
- One grower at White Plains got his manure from the city in bales at
  no cost beyond freight
- Selected pure horse manure from agents: moderate price
- Baled manure particularly useful for small growers who needed only a
  few bushels

### Spawn Costs

- Brick spawn: sold by weight, standard brick = 1 pound
- [[falconer-mushroom-spawn-preparation]] on spawn
- A gardener at Jamaica, L.I., bought 1,000 lbs of brick spawn at once
- French flake spawn cost about three times as much as English brick
  spawn

## Gathering Mushrooms

### When to Pick

- For market: plump and fresh, just before the frill connecting cap to
  stem breaks apart
- For soups: button state (marble-sized), before gills burst -- they
  retain white appearance and don't discolor soup
- For home cooking (baking, stewing, broiling): full size, frills burst
  but before cap opens flat or gills turn brown
- Never let mushrooms blow open before gathering
- Never let them stay ungathered until tomorrow to grow larger -- this
  gave an unfavored, unequal lot

### Pulling vs. Cutting

Falconer strongly advocated pulling mushrooms out by the root rather
than cutting:
1. **Benefited the bed**: Cut stumps decayed, producing fluffy/spongy
   substance that poisoned surrounding mycelium and destroyed nearby
   small mushrooms. Pulled roots left clean holes.
2. **Kept fresh longer**: Pulled mushrooms always kept fresher than cut.
3. **Increased weight and price**: Mushrooms were sold by weight. Retaining
   full stems increased both weight and price. "If part of the stems
   had been cut off the weight would have been reduced, and, in like
   proportion, the price."
In cases where pulling would disturb neighboring small mushrooms, cutting
was acceptable. A practiced hand could extract full-grown mushrooms from
thick bunches with "a twitch and a pull" without disturbing the multitude
of small buttons around them.

## Marketing Methods

### Direct Sales

Most market growers around New York City sold direct, delivering to
hotels, restaurants, and fancy fruiterers.

### Commission Merchants

Growers at considerable distance from the city sold through commission
merchants in New York, who dealt out in quantities to suit customers.
