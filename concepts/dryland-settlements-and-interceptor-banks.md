# Dryland Settlements and Interceptor Banks

## Overview

Dryland [[permaculture]] presents unique challenges: extreme temperature fluctuations, water scarcity, high evaporation, saline soils, and wind exposure. Mollison's global survey of dryland strategies reveals a consistent pattern of solutions drawn from traditional knowledge and adapted modern techniques, centered on earth-sheltered buildings, underground food systems, and interceptor banks to combat soil salinization.

## Earth-Sheltered and Underground Buildings

Dryland cultures worldwide have independently developed earth-sheltered construction, using the ground's thermal mass to buffer temperature extremes. These structures exploit the principle that soil temperature at depth remains relatively constant regardless of surface conditions.

### Underground Orchards

In Fresno, California, hand-dug caves open out to shaded pits containing fig, grape, citrus, and pomegranate plantings. Looking up from underground, the sky is framed by fruit-laden branches. The root systems grow in pits that stay naturally cool and can be easily mulched. The ground surface above is shaped into a funnel to catch and direct rainwater to the plant containers, maximizing every drop of precipitation.

This technique turns the dryland disadvantage of high evaporation into an advantage: the underground environment maintains humidity and reduces transpiration losses, while the funnel-shaped surface captures water that would otherwise run off.

### Traditional Buried Structures

In Greenock, South Australia, traditional buried dairy sheds keep milk and food cool, with vine, earth, or tree shade providing additional cooling. At Village Homes in Davis, California, earth-sheltered houses with solar hot water, turf roofs, and slab floors achieve good comfort control without mechanical air conditioning.

The ice plant and herb roof systems at Village Homes provide active cooling through transpiration and shade, demonstrating that living roofs can significantly reduce cooling loads in dryland climates.

### Desert-Specific Building Strategies

Several desert plants serve dual functions as building materials and food sources:

- **Ocotillo** (*Fouquieria splendens*): Cultivated for fencing material, it can be live-set to grow gated fences and is widely used as reinforcement or base for mud wall construction. It serves as a useful crop plant for desert structures while producing flowers that attract pollinators.
- **Euphorbia** species: Used extensively in tropical and subtropical drylands for live fencing. When cutting Euphorbia, protective goggles are essential due to the caustic milky sap, and skin should be covered if allergic.

## Desert Plant Strategies

### Nebka Formation by Desert Trees

Gidgee (*Acacia cambagei*) creates desert mounds or nebkas approximately 1.5 meters high and 12 meters across. Many sessile or weeping trees in deserts cause dust accretion in this way. In the Kalahari, Acacia species perform the same function, providing deep absorbent soils and mulch for vines, other trees, and burrowing animals.

This natural process of wind-borne soil deposition around vegetation creates fertile microsites in otherwise impoverished desert landscapes. The nebkas capture organic matter, increase water infiltration, and provide shelter for establishing additional plants.

### Opuntia (Prickly Pear) as a Multi-Use Plant

At Las Palmas in the Canary Islands, Opuntia serves multiple functions simultaneously:

- Fruit production (*tuna* — the edible cactus fruit)
- Vegetable food (nopales — young pads)
- Cattle forage
- Living hedge and boundary marker
- Host plant for cochineal scale insects, used locally as a food dye

This multi-functional approach is central to dryland permaculture: every plant must justify its water requirement through multiple yields.

### Volcanic Cinder Mulching

Apple and pear crops on the Canary Islands are sustained by a deep (0.3 meter) mulch of *enarenado* (volcanic cinder). This technique produces fruit, potato, and other crops in areas receiving mainly night fog and condensation rather than rainfall. The cinder mulch captures atmospheric moisture from fog events, suppresses weed growth, moderates soil temperature, and slowly releases mineral nutrients as it weathers.

### Desert Poultry Forage Systems

Many desert Acacia species provide abundant poultry seed, as do *Lycium* and *Solanum* species. *Solanum* species in particular provide abundant fruit and seed suitable for chicken forage, while many woolly *Solanum* species serve as dryland spinach or pot herbs.

## Vine Screens for Cooling

At St. Theresa Mission in Central Australia, house verandahs are screened with *Bryonia* (a cucurbit), lab-lab bean, and tomato vines. These living screens cool west and sunward walls and are sustained by waste wash-water and greywater, turning a waste product into a productive cooling system.

This technique is applicable wherever buildings face hot afternoon sun. The vines provide shade through their leaf canopy and additional cooling through transpiration, while producing food as a byproduct.

## Salting and Interceptor Banks

### The Salination Problem

Deforestation, overgrazing, and wheat cultivation in dryland areas produce widespread secondary salinization, a process where rising saline groundwater or throughflow brings salts to the surface, killing vegetation and collapsing soil structure. In Western Australia's wheat belt, this has created extensive salt pans from once-forested marshes.

Indicators of soil collapse include:
- **Sea barley grass** (*Hordeum maritimum*): An indicator species of saline, collapsing soils
- **Spike rush** (*Juncus* species): Indicates imminent soil collapse, surface flooding, and anaerobic conditions
- Visible seepage lines where salty water reaches the surface

### Interceptor Bank Design

Interceptor banks are contour-parallel earthworks designed to divert surface water and throughflow off agricultural fields, isolating soil blocks for rain leaching and preventing the "cascade" effect of salty overland flow moving downhill.

The classic interceptor bank design observed at Quairading, Western Australia:
- **Depth**: 1.5 meters
- **Width**: 4 meters (top)
- **Spacing**: Not more than 100 meters apart, or 1 meter of vertical separation on slopes
- **Construction**: A bulldozer rams subsoil on the downhill wall of the bank for compaction
- **Termination**: Banks end on the streamline as marked by distant trees
- **Orientation**: Run along the contour, ending on natural drainage lines

### Evidence of Effectiveness

Soil pits below interceptor banks show no throughflow and no rising salted groundwater, demonstrating that the banks successfully prevent the upward movement of saline water. At Beermullah, Western Australia, interceptors have prevented soil collapse by flooding, with rushes growing on the upslope side indicating successful [[water-management]] while protecting crops below the bank.

A series of interceptor banks in wheatfields can isolate soil blocks from both overland flow and salting effects, creating manageable units for leaching and rehabilitation.

## Key Principles for Dryland Settlement

1. **Minimize evaporation**: Use earth-sheltering, underground construction, and mulching
2. **Maximize water capture**: Funnel surfaces, swales, and interceptor banks direct every available drop to productive use
3. **Use multi-functional plants**: Species that provide food, forage, shelter, and building materials simultaneously
4. **Manage salinity proactively**: Interceptor banks prevent the cascade of salt-laden water across the landscape
5. **Leverage microclimates**: Nebkas, vine screens, and underground spaces create protected niches within harsh environments
6. **Utilize waste water**: Greywater sustains productive vine screens and garden plantings
7. **Respect traditional knowledge**: Many of the most effective dryland strategies come from indigenous and traditional practices refined over centuries
