# Mimosa pudica and Sensitive Plant Experiments

## Overview

The *[[plant-communication-volatile-organic-compounds]] Plants* documents numerous experiments involving
**Mimosa pudica**, the "sensitive plant" or "touch-me-not," as a key
subject for investigating plant sensitivity, electrical signalling, and
perception. Mimosa pudica's dramatic visible response to touch, its
leaflets collapsing when disturbed, made it an ideal candidate for
early [[plant-electrophysiology]] research and later for investigations into
the [[cleve-backster-plant-perception-experiments]]
later expanded these investigations into the realm of consciousness and
perception.

## Byrd's Instrumentation Experiments

In May 1973, researcher Byrd began instrumenting the tiny leaves of
Mimosa pudica using a thin wire barely touching a leaf, connected through
a special amplifier to detect minute changes in voltage or resistance.
Byrd used one of the world's finest chart recorders, a West German Siemens
device that shot out more than three feet of recording paper per second
with patterns recorded by a jet of ink only a few microns wide.

The goal was to pick up plant reactions that had hitherto gone unnoticed
by conventional equipment. Byrd also planned to work with Acetabularia
cremulata, a primitive marine alga only two inches long but made of a
[[fungal-single-cell-protein-production]], to determine whether removing its nucleus would eliminate
response capability.

## Bose's Pioneering Mimosa Research

The book extensively documents Sir Jagadis [[jagadis-chandra-bose-plant-research]]'s work with
Mimosa pudica in the early 1900s. Bose demonstrated several remarkable
parallels between mimosa responses and animal nervous system function:

- **Electrical excitation**: When Bose electrically shocked the stem or
  touched it with a hot wire, the base of the nearest petiole collapsed
  within seconds, followed by the folding of leaves at its end.
  Connecting a galvanometer to the petiole recorded an electrical
  disturbance between these two reaction points.

- **Reflex arc**: Bose showed that mimosa possesses the same kind of
  reflex arc that causes humans to withdraw fingers from a hot stove
  before pain is consciously felt. When Bose touched the tip of one leaf
  on a three-leaf petiole, the leaflets of the disturbed leaf gradually
  closed from the tip, then the petiole collapsed, and lastly the other
  two leaves closed from the base upward.

- **Nerve-like conduction**: Bose interpreted these actions as due to
  electrical excitation producing mechanical responses, identical to the
  nerve-muscle unit in animals where the nerve carries the impulse and
  the muscle contracts in response.

## Bose's Optical Lever and Crescograph

To magnify suspected contractions in plants that did not show visible
movement, Bose designed an **optical lever** and later the **crescograph**,
capable of magnifying plant movement up to one hundred million times. The
optical lever used a movable lever attached to mirrors that reflected a
beam of light onto continuous paper driven by clockwork.

With this instrument, Bose demonstrated that:
- The skins of grapes, tomatoes, and other fruits behaved similarly to
  animal skins.
- Plant digestive organs in insectivorous plants were analogous to
  animal stomachs.
- Responses to light in leaves paralleled those in animal retinas.
- Plants became fatigued by continuous stimulation just like animal
  muscles, whether hypersensitive mimosas or undemonstrative radishes.
