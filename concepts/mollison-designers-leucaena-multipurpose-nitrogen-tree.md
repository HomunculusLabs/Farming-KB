# Leucaena Multipurpose Nitrogen Tree

Leucaena leucocephala is one of the most widely used multipurpose trees in
tropical permaculture and agroforestry systems. Bill Mollison described leucaena
as a keystone species for tropical land restoration and food production, noting
its exceptional nitrogen-fixing ability, rapid growth, palatable foliage for
livestock, and versatility as a source of fuelwood, mulch, and shade. Despite
its invasive potential in some contexts, leucaena remains an invaluable tool
in the permaculture designer's repertoire for tropical and subtropical systems.

## Botanical Characteristics

Leucaena is a fast-growing leguminous tree in the Fabaceae family, native to
Central America but now naturalised throughout the tropics. It can reach heights
of 10 to 20 metres but is typically managed as a coppiced shrub at two to four
metres for forage production. The tree has a deep taproot system that accesses
subsoil nutrients and moisture, and it forms [[mollison-designers-mycorrhizal-fungi-and-soil-microbiology]] that
enhance nutrient uptake.

The foliage of leucaena is high in protein, typically 20 to 30 percent crude
protein in young leaves, making it one of the most nutritious tropical forages.
The wood is dense and burns well, making it a valuable [[mollison-designers-fuelwood-forestry-coppice-pollard]] species. The
tree produces abundant seeds that can be used for human food in some varieties.

## Nitrogen Fixation

Leucaena forms symbiotic associations with Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules,
fixing atmospheric nitrogen at rates of 40 to 100 kilograms per hectare per
year under favourable conditions. This nitrogen becomes available to companion
plants through leaf litter decomposition, root exudation, and when the tree is
coppiced and the foliage is used as [[mollison-designers-living-mulches-ground-covers]] or cut-and-carry forage.

The [[mollison-designers-legume-tree-inoculation-and-nitrogen-distribution]] process is important for establishing leucaena on
new sites. Native Rhizobium strains may not be effective, so inoculation with
tested strains at planting ensures vigorous nitrogen fixation. Mollison
recommended treating leucaena seeds with the appropriate Rhizobium inoculant as
part of the [[mollison-designers-seed-pelleting-and-pretreatment]] process.

## Forage Production

Leucaena is primarily valued as a forage tree for ruminant livestock including
cattle, goats, and sheep. It can be managed as a [[mollison-designers-coppicing-management-of-legume-trees]] that is cut
every six to twelve weeks during the growing season, producing high yields of
nutritious leaf material. In [[mollison-designers-silvopasture-integrated-tree-livestock]], leucaena rows are interplanted
with pasture grasses, with livestock browsing the trees directly or receiving
cut forage.

The main limitation of leucaena as forage is the presence of mimosine, a
non-protein amino acid that can cause hair loss, goitre, and reproductive
problems in livestock at high intake levels. Ruminants that have been gradually
adapted to leucaena develop rumen microorganisms that degrade mimosine, but
monogastric animals such as pigs and poultry are more sensitive. Feeding
leucaena as part of a mixed diet rather than the sole forage source minimises
mimosine-related problems.

## Alley Cropping with Leucaena
The [[mollison-designers-alley-cropping-and-contour-hedgerow-systems]] system uses leucaena hedgerows along the contour with
food crops planted in alleys between. Hedgerows are periodically coppiced and
cut foliage laid as mulch or green manure. This suppresses weeds, conserves
moisture, adds organic matter and nitrogen, and reduces erosion. Over time,
leucaena mulch significantly improves soil fertility. The contour farming
principles apply directly to hedgerow placement.

## Shade and Microclimate

Leucaena provides valuable shade in tropical systems. When planted as an
overstory for [[mollison-designers-shade-grown-coffee-and-cacao-systems]], leucaena reduces temperature stress and
evapotranspiration while contributing nitrogen to the understory crop. The
dappled shade from a managed leucaena canopy is ideal for many understory crops
that require protection from full tropical sun.

The [[mollison-designers-microclimate-creation-frost-protection]] potential of leucaena extends to frost protection in
subtropical areas, where a dense leucaena windbreak can reduce radiative frost
damage by several degrees.

## Windbreak and Erosion Control

Leucaena makes an effective [[mollison-designers-windbreak-configurations-for-crops-and-orchards]] component in tropical and subtropical
areas. Its rapid growth and dense foliage provide quick wind protection for
sensitive crops. On slopes, leucaena hedgerows reduce runoff velocity and trap
sediment, contributing to [[mollison-designers-tropical-terrace-design-and-earth-shaping]] formation over time as soil
accumulates behind the hedgerows.

The [[mollison-designers-vetiver-grass-for-erosion-control]] system can be combined with leucaena hedgerows for enhanced
erosion control on steep slopes, with vetiver providing dense ground-level
barriers and leucaena adding above-ground biomass and nitrogen.

## Invasive Potential and Management

Leucaena has become invasive in several tropical regions, particularly in
Australia, parts of the Pacific Islands, and some areas of Southeast Asia. The
tree produces abundant seed that can germinate readily and establish in disturbed
areas. Mollison acknowledged this risk and recommended several management
strategies:

- Use sterile or low-seeding varieties where available.
- Manage leucaena intensively through frequent coppicing to prevent seed
  production.
- Avoid planting leucaena near waterways or conservation areas where it could
  spread into native vegetation.
- Monitor plantings regularly and remove volunteer seedlings before they
  establish.

The designer should assess the invasive potential of leucaena for the specific
site and region before including it in the design. In areas where leucaena is
prohibited, alternative nitrogen-fixing trees such as [[mollison-designers-casuarina-windbreak-erosion-control]], [[mollison-designers-moringa-and-multipurpose-tropical-trees]], or
calliandra can fill similar roles.

## Propagation and Establishment

Leucaena is propagated from seed, which requires scarification to break seed
coat dormancy. The [[mollison-designers-seed-dormancy-and-germination-treatments]] involves soaking seeds in hot water
or nicking the seed coat with a file. After treatment, seeds germinate within
three to seven days. Seedlings can be raised in the [[mollison-designers-plant-nursery-propagation-bed-design]] and transplanted
at the beginning of the rainy season.

Direct seeding is also possible in areas with reliable rainfall. Seeds are
planted at a spacing of 10 to 50 centimetres within hedgerow rows, depending on
the intended use. Closer spacing produces denser hedgerows for erosion control,
while wider spacing allows more light penetration for alley crops.

## Integration with Other Systems

Leucaena integrates with many permaculture elements:

- **[[mollison-designers-pig-tractor-systems-and-land-preparation]]**: Leucaena foliage provides supplementary forage for
  pigs in rotational grazing systems.
- **[[mollison-designers-rabbit-housing-and-manure-management]]**: Leucaena leaf meal can be incorporated into rabbit rations
  at moderate levels.
- **[[mollison-designers-charcoal-production-biochar-soil-amendment]]**: Leucaena wood produces high-quality charcoal for
  biochar soil amendment.
- **[[mollison-designers-compost-toilet-humanure-systems]]**: Leucaena leaf litter provides carbon-rich material for
  composting.
- **[[mollison-designers-goat-management-and-browsing-systems]]**: Goats readily browse leucaena and can be used to control
  hedgerow height in silvopastoral systems.
