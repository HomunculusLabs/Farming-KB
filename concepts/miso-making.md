
## What Is Miso

Miso is a traditional Japanese fermented soybean paste. It is one of the most complex and time-intensive ferments in the culinary world, requiring months to years of aging to develop its characteristic deep umami flavor. Miso serves as the base for soups, marinades, glazes, dressings, and pickling brines.

The essential components are: cooked soybeans, koji (rice or barley inoculated with [[aspergillus-oryzae]]), salt, and time.

## Koji Preparation

Koji is the foundational ingredient in miso making. It is steamed rice or barley inoculated with [[aspergillus]] oryzae, a filamentous fungus that produces enzymes (amylase and protease) that break down starches into sugars and proteins into amino acids.

### Making Koji from Scratch

1. Soak short-grain rice (or barley) for several hours or overnight
2. Steam the grain — it should be cooked through but not mushy
3. Cool to approximately 85F (30C)
4. Sprinkle koji-kin (A. oryzae spores) evenly over the grain while tossing to distribute
5. Transfer to a shallow tray or wooden koji box
6. Cover with a damp cloth and incubate at 85-90F (30-32C) with high humidity
7. Monitor temperature — the koji will generate its own heat as the mold grows
8. After 12-18 hours, stir the grain to redistribute heat and moisture
9. Continue incubating for 36-48 hours total
10. Koji is ready when the grain is covered in white, fragrant mycelium with a sweet aroma

### Buying Koji

For beginners, purchasing prepared koji rice or koji starter (koji-kin) is recommended. Many Japanese grocery stores and online suppliers carry both. Making koji from scratch requires careful temperature and humidity control.

## Soybean Preparation

1. Rinse and sort dried soybeans, removing any debris or damaged beans
2. Soak the beans in plenty of water for 12-18 hours (they will roughly triple in volume)
3. Drain and cook the beans:
   - Boiling: simmer for 3-4 hours until beans crush easily between fingers
   - Pressure cooker: 45-60 minutes at pressure
4. Cook beans until very soft — undercooked beans create hard pockets in the miso
5. Reserve some of the cooking liquid (called bean broth) for adjusting texture

## Salt

Salt serves two critical roles: inhibiting unwanted bacteria and controlling the fermentation rate.

- Use non-iodized sea salt or kosher salt
- Iodized salt can inhibit fermentation
- Salt-to-bean ratios determine fermentation speed and final character:
  - 10-12% salt: slower, longer fermentation, more complex flavor (traditional)
  - 6-8% salt: faster fermentation, sweeter, milder miso
- Dissolve salt in the reserved bean broth or mix directly with koji before combining

## The Miso Making Process

### Ingredients (1 kg batch)

- 500g dried soybeans (yields roughly 1.2 kg cooked)
- 500g koji rice (or barley koji)
- 150-200g sea salt (adjust based on desired salt percentage)
- Reserved bean cooking liquid as needed

### Steps

1. Mash the cooked soybeans while still warm — use a potato masher, food processor, or mortar and pestle. Some texture is fine; a few whole beans add character.
2. Cool the mash to below body temperature (below 95F / 35C) — heat kills koji enzymes
3. Mix salt with the koji rice, rubbing it in thoroughly to distribute evenly
4. Combine the salted koji with the mashed soybeans
5. Add bean broth as needed to achieve a paste-like consistency (thicker than peanut butter)
6. Mix thoroughly — every soybean should be coated with koji

### Packing and Fermentation

1. Pack the miso tightly into a fermentation vessel to eliminate air pockets
2. Traditional vessels: wooden miso tubs (kioke), ceramic crocks, or glass jars
3. Press the surface flat and smooth
4. Sprinkle a thin layer of salt on the surface (prevents mold)
5. Place a weight (clean rocks, a sealed bag of water, or a plate) on top to keep the miso submerged
6. Cover with breathable material (cloth or paper) secured with string
7. Store in a cool, dark place — ideal temperature is 50-68F (10-20C)

### Monitoring and Maintenance

- Check the miso every few weeks initially
- If surface mold appears (white, yellow, or pink), scrape it off and re-salt the surface — this is common and not dangerous
- Green, blue, or black mold with a strong off-smell indicates spoilage — discard
- After the first month, the miso can be mostly left alone
- Some makers stir the miso periodically (age-miso technique) to redistribute enzymes and promote even fermentation

## Types of Miso

### By Grain Type

- Rice koji miso (kome miso) — most common, well-balanced flavor
- Barley koji miso (mugi miso) — earthier, slightly sweeter, popular in Kyushu
- Soybean-only miso (hatcho miso) — no grain, very concentrated, dark, and intense

### By Fermentation Length

- Shiro miso (white miso) — 1-3 months, low salt (5-7%), sweet and mild, made with more koji
- Shinshu miso (yellow/light miso) — 3-6 months, moderate salt, balanced flavor
- Aka miso (red miso) — 6-12 months, higher salt, deep umami, reddish-brown color
- Dark miso / aged miso — 1-3+ years, very concentrated, complex, salty

### By Region

- Sendai miso — dark red, long-aged, from northern Japan
- Saikyo miso — sweet white miso from Kyoto, low salt
- Miso from other traditions: doenjang (Korea), jiang (China), tauchu (Southeast Asia)

## Storage and Use

- Active fermenting miso: keep in a cool, dark place
- Finished or store-bought miso: refrigerate to slow further fermentation
- Aged miso develops deeper flavor over years
- Do not boil miso — add to soup after removing from heat to preserve probiotics and flavor compounds
- Miso keeps refrigerated for a year or more
- Freeze in small portions for long-term storage
