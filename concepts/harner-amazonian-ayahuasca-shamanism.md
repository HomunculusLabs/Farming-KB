# Amazonian Ayahuasca Shamanism

The upper Amazon rain forest is one of the last remaining areas where hallucinogenic drugs are used under essentially aboriginal conditions. The drink, commonly called **yage** or **yaje** in Colombia, **ayahuasca** (Quechua: "vine of the dead") in Ecuador and Peru, and **caapi** in Brazil, is prepared from *Banisteriopsis* species (Malpighiaceae), a genus of tree-climbing forest vines.

## Botanical and Pharmacological Basis

The distribution of native *Banisteriopsis* use extends from northwestern Colombia to lowland Bolivia, both east and west of the Andes, and eastward into the upper Orinoco area.

- The primary alkaloids identified in *B. caapi* are **harmine**, **harmaline**, and **d-tetrahydroharmine** (beta-carbolines)
- *B. rusbyana* contains significant amounts of **N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT)**
- A common admixture, *Psychotria viridis*, also contains DMT
- The beta-carbolines in *Banisteriopsis* serve as MAO inhibitors, making orally ingested DMT psychoactive
- The four known alkaloids are structurally related indole derivatives, related to mescaline, psilocybin, and LSD

## Preparation

Typically only one other plant is added to the *Banisteriopsis* brew, depending on the tribe. The Cashinahua combine the vine with *Psychotria* leaves. The vine segments are pounded and boiled with water for about an hour, then ladled off to cool. Participants drink about a pint each and may drink again after fifteen minutes.

## Social Patterns of Use

Different tribes use ayahuasca in characteristically different ways:

- **Jivaro**: Individualistic and feuding; prefer to take the drug alone, using it to cope supernaturally with enemies. The brew is called *natem*.
- **Cashinahua and Sharanahua**: Gregarious; participate together in the hallucinogenic experience, sharing revelatory content portending the common good.
- **Campa**: Join together for sessions where the shaman plays a directorial role as leader of ceremonies.

In all four groups, hallucinogenic experiences are deeply integrated into the supernatural life and total culture.

## Shamanic Functions

*Banisteriopsis* is typically taken by South American Indian shamans of the tropical forest to:
- Perceive the supernatural world
- Contact and affect the behavior of particular supernatural entities
- Locate and withdraw supernatural objects from bewitched patients
- Achieve supernatural power
- Accompany a shaman in a curing or other ritual

The use of *Banisteriopsis* makes it possible for virtually anyone to achieve the trance state necessary for the practice of shamanism. Among the Jivaro, approximately one in four men is a shaman, a remarkably high proportion attributable partly to the presence of the drug.

## The Jivaro "Inside View" of Reality

The Jivaro believe the normal waking life is simply a "lie" or illusion, while the true forces that determine daily events are supernatural and can only be seen and manipulated with the aid of hallucinogenic drugs. They go further than the distinction between "ordinary" and "non-ordinary" reality: they believe there is only one reality, the supernatural.

## Urban Ayahuasca Healing

Among mestizo healers (*ayahuasqueros*) in Peruvian Amazon cities like Iquitos and Pucallpa, ayahuasca has received its greatest cultural elaboration as an adjunct to folk healing. Healers gather groups of 5-25 patients in forest clearings for nighttime sessions. The healer distributes the potion, reciting orations and whistling to protect each person. Healing sessions involve:
- Visionary diagnosis of illness
- Sucking out magically introduced objects (spines, thistles)
- Counseling and ritual exorcism
- Dietary proscriptions (no salt, lard, or sweets for 24 hours before/after)
- Sexual abstinence before sessions
