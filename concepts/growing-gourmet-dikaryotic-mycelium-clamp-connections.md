## From Haploid to Dikaryotic

Each [[cotter-mushroom-life-cycle-mycelium-biology]] extends.
Hyphae continue to reproduce mitotically. Two hyphae, if
compatible, come together, fuse, and combine genetic
material. The resulting mycelium is then described as
being binucleate and dikaryotic.

After this union of genetic material, the dikaryotic
mycelium accelerates in its growth, again reproducing
mitotically. Mated mycelium characteristically grows
faster than unmated mycelium arising from single spores.

## Mating Systems

The mating of compatible hyphae is genetically determined.
Most of the gourmet species are governed by two
incompatibility factors (A and B). As a result, only
subsets of spores are able to combine with one another.
When spores germinate, several strains are produced.
Incompatible strains grow away from each other, while
compatible strains mate and form dikaryotic mycelium.

This biphasic mating system creates genetic diversity
within populations of the same species. The diversity
within a species is critical to preserving its ability to
adapt to changing ecological landscapes.

## Clamp Connections as Diagnostic Features

When two spores come together and mate, the downstream
mycelium produces bridges between the cells, called clamp
connections. These small, semicircular, hollow structures
connect adjacent cells and ensure the proper distribution
of the two genetically distinct nuclei.

Clamp connections are especially useful for cultivators
who want to determine whether or not they have mated
mycelium. Key distinguishing features:

- **Dikaryotic mycelium** (mated): Has clamp connections;
  capable of producing fertile mushrooms
- **Monokaryotic mycelium** (from single spore): Lacks clamp
  connections entirely; incapable of producing fertile
  mushrooms

This distinction is critical during strain isolation and
evaluation. If a culture fails to produce mushrooms, one
diagnostic step is to check for the absence of clamp
connections, indicating an unmated monokaryon.

## The Vegetative Phase

While running through a [[cotter-mushroom-life-cycle-mycelium-biology]]. The substrate will
continue to be colonized until physical boundaries prevent
further growth or a biological competitor is encountered.

When vegetative colonization ceases, the mycelium enters
into temporary stasis. Heat and carbon dioxide evolution
decline, and nutrients are amassed within the storage
vestibules of the cells. This resting period is usually
short-lived before entering into the next phase of
primordia formation.

## Mycelial Network Architecture

As the mycelium expands, a web of cells is formed,
collectively called the mycelial network. The arrangement
of these cells is designed to optimally capture an
ecological niche. Species differ in the manner by which
the mycelial mat is projected.

Most saprophytic mushrooms produce a thick mycelial mat
after spore germination. These types of mycelial mats are
characterized by many cross-overs between the hyphae,
typified by the presence of clamp connections.

## Mycelial Byproducts

As the mycelial network extends, several byproducts are
produced during colonization:

- **Heat**: Generated in significant quantities as
  metabolic activity proceeds
- **Carbon dioxide**: Produced in enormous quantities. One
  study (Zadrazil, 1976) showed nearly 50% of the carbon
  base in wheat straw is liberated as gaseous CO2 during
  decomposition by Oyster mushrooms
- **Proteins**: Approximately 20% of the straw carbon was
  converted to proteins
- **Volatile compounds**: Including alcohols, ethylenes,
  and other gases
- **Secondary metabolites**: Complex acids and other
  compounds that forestall competition from other fungi
  and bacteria
