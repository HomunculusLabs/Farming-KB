# Casing Soil Preparation: Peat-Vermiculite-Marl Sterilized Recipe

## Overview

The GUILD cultivation guide specifies a particular casing soil recipe for
grass seed-based mushroom cultivation. Notably, the authors advocate for
full sterilization of casing soil rather than the more common pasteurization
approach, directly contradicting authors who recommend pasteurization to
preserve beneficial microorganisms. This page documents the recipe, preparation
method, and the authors' rationale for the sterilization approach.

## Recipe

The casing soil formulation uses three ingredients in dry form:

| Component | Parts | Role |
|-----------|-------|------|
| Peat | 10 | Primary organic matrix, moisture retention, pH buffer |
| Vermiculite | 5 | Aeration, water retention, structure |
| Limestone (Marl) | 2 | pH adjustment, calcium/magnesium source |

The 10:5:2 ratio produces a casing with good water-holding capacity,
adequate aeration, and appropriate pH for mushroom fruiting. Marl, a
calcareous mudstone containing clay and calcium carbonate, serves as both
a pH buffer and a source of essential minerals.

## Preparation Method

### Dry Mixing

All three ingredients are combined in dry form and thoroughly mixed. This
ensures even distribution of the limestone throughout the peat-vermiculite
matrix, preventing localized pH hotspots that could inhibit mycelial
colonization or promote contaminant growth.

### Hydration

While stirring, water is gradually added. The guide notes that the amount
of water depends on the moisture content of the peat, which varies
considerably between sources and batches. The target moisture level is
described as "as much water as possible without turning it into mud." This
field-expert description translates to approximately 60-70% water-holding
capacity — the casing should hold its shape when squeezed but release only
a few drops of water.

If the casing becomes too wet, additional dry ingredients are added to
correct the moisture level. This iterative approach requires some
experience to judge correctly.

### Packaging for Sterilization

The moistened casing soil is filled into:
- **Oven bags** (nylon)
- **Autoclave bags** (polypropylene, PP)
- **Glass jars**

Any of these containers works for the pressure cooker sterilization step.

### Sterilization

The casing soil is sterilized for one hour in a pressure cooker at 15 psi
(121°C). After cooling to room temperature, it is ready for use.

## The Sterilization vs. Pasteurization Debate

### The GUILD Position: Full Sterilization

The guide authors explicitly reject pasteurization of casing soil:

> "We know that some authors advise against sterilisation of casing soil
> because it would kill all the beneficial organisms. We however have had
> only bad experiences with untreated or pasteurised casing soils."

Their position is purely empirical — they tried both approaches and found
that sterilized casing produced consistently better results in their
specific context of grass seed substrate cultivation in disposable trays.

### The Conventional Position: Pasteurization

Many established cultivation texts (Stamets, 1983; van Griensven, 1988)
recommend pasteurization (60-70°C for 1-2 hours) rather than full
sterilization for casing soil. The rationale is that pasteurization kills
most pathogenic organisms and competitor spores while preserving beneficial
bacteria such as *Pseudomonas putida* that:

- Suppress competitor molds through antibiosis
- Stimulate pinning through metabolic byproducts
- Improve casing structure through microbial aggregation

### Possible Explanations for the Discrepancy

Several factors may explain why the GUILD authors found sterilization
superior:

1. **Clean room technique**: Their sterile inoculation procedures and
   clean incubation may reduce the need for biological casing protection
2. **Grass seed substrate**: The fully sterilized grain substrate may be
   more vulnerable to casing-introduced competitors than partially
   sterilized bulk substrates
3. **Species specificity**: *Psilocybe mexicana* and *P. tampanensis*
   may respond differently to casing microbiology than *Agaricus bisporus*
4. **Small scale**: In small tray cultivation, contamination from a single
   competing organism can destroy an entire tray, making the risk-reward
   calculus favor sterilization

## Casing Application Technique

The guide provides specific instructions for applying the casing to
colonized grass seed:

1. Colonized grain is shaken loose from the jar and poured into a 1-litre
   disposable plastic tray
2. Lumps are broken up using the clean rim of the jar
3. The grain surface is levelled evenly
4. A thin layer (1.5-2.0 cm) of casing soil is applied using a large
   spoon and fork
5. The casing surface is kept intentionally rough, with small valleys and
   hills, while maintaining overall evenness
6. The tray is covered with tin foil and placed at 20-25°C

The rough surface texture is important — it creates micro-environments with
varying moisture and gas exchange conditions that promote more even
primordia formation across the casing surface.

## Moisture Monitoring During Use

After casing application and during fruiting, the casing must never dry out.
The misting protocol is responsive rather than scheduled: cultivators
observe the surface and mist when it begins to lose its sheen. Post-flush
watering is increased because maturing mushrooms extract significant
moisture from the casing layer.
