# Compost Thermal Stages

[[compost|Composting]] progresses through three distinct temperature phases, each driven by a different community of microorganisms. Understanding these stages helps gardeners and farmers manage their piles effectively, producing finished compost that is rich in nutrients and free of pathogens and weed seeds.

## Overview of the Three Stages

| Stage | Temperature Range | Primary Activity |
|-------|------------------|------------------|
| Mesophilic | 68–104°F (20–40°C) | Initial breakdown of organic matter |
| Thermophilic | 104–150°F (40–65°C+) | Rapid decomposition, pathogen kill |
| Maturation | Cooling back down | Lignin decay, humus formation |

---

## Mesophilic Stage (68–104°F / 20–40°C)

The mesophilic stage is the opening act of composting. [[bacteria|Bacteria]] begin the work of decomposing organic matter by breaking down cellulose chains into glucose through a process called [[depolymerization]]. This is the chemical dismantling of long-chain molecules into simpler, consumable forms.

Key microbial players in this stage include:

- **Brown rot fungi** ([[basidiomycetes]]) — break down resistant structural materials
- **Bacillus spp.** and **Heliospirillum spp.** — attack tough organic compounds
- These organisms produce [[endospores]] that are highly resistant to heat and chemicals, allowing them to survive the intense temperatures of the next stage and re-colonize afterward

As decomposition proceeds, larger organisms join in, physically tearing apart organic matter and increasing surface area for microbial attack. The collective metabolic activity of all these organisms steadily raises the pile temperature to approximately 104°F, at which point the thermophilic community takes over.

### The Role of Psychrophilic Bacteria

In cold climates, [[psychrophilic]] bacteria can initiate decomposition even from frozen starting conditions. These cold-adapted organisms work at temperatures as low as 32°F (0°C), generating enough metabolic heat to gradually warm the pile until mesophilic conditions are reached. Without psychrophiles, composting in winter would be nearly impossible to start.

---

## Thermophilic Stage (104–150°F / 40–65°C+)

The thermophilic stage is the high-heat phase where the most rapid and dramatic decomposition occurs. Complex carbohydrates are fully broken down, some proteins are decomposed, and hemicelluloses are degraded.

### Temperature Milestones

A properly constructed [[compost]] pile follows a predictable heating curve:

- **135°F** reached within 24–72 hours
- **150°F** reached within approximately 3 days
- **140–150°F** sustained for several days ensures pathogen elimination
- **150°F** destroys most weed seeds
- **155°F+** — *danger zone*; never allow the pile to exceed this threshold, as carbon is burned off and valuable organic matter is lost

### Additional Microbial Contributors

During the thermophilic stage, new bacterial species join the decomposition effort:

- **Arthrobacter** — common soil bacteria involved in nutrient cycling
- **Pseudomonas** — versatile decomposers of organic compounds
- **[[actinomycetes|Streptomyces]]** — filamentous bacteria that degrade complex organics

Fungi also remain active during this phase, though their diversity narrows as temperatures climb.

### Troubleshooting a Cold Pile

If the pile is not reaching thermophilic temperatures, several interventions can help:

- **Turn the pile** to introduce fresh oxygen
- **Add fresh green materials** (grass clippings, kitchen scraps)
- **Incorporate newspaper or fruit pulps** as readily available carbon
- **Apply a commercial inoculum** to boost microbial populations

### Cooling an Overheated Pile

If temperatures exceed 155°F, take immediate action:

- **Turn the pile** to release trapped heat and introduce cooler air
- **Add water** to increase evaporative cooling
- **Mix in brown materials** (leaves, wood chips) to dilute the heat-generating mass

## Maturation Stage

As readily available proteins and carbohydrates are consumed, metabolic activity declines and the pile begins to cool. This signals the start of the maturation stage, where the most recalcitrant materials in the pile are finally broken down.

### Return of the Mesophiles

[[mesophilic]] organisms that survived the thermophilic phase as protected [[endospores]] now reassert themselves, recolonizing the cooling pile and continuing decomposition at moderate temperatures. After the thermophilic run-up, the ideal temperature range for maturation is 104–131°F.

### Lignin Decay

[[lignin]] — the toughest molecular bonds in the entire pile — is broken down during maturation. This is the final frontier of compost decomposition. Two key groups drive this process:

- **[[actinomycetes|Actinomycetes]]** — chain-like bacteria that physically resemble fungi. They attack lignin, cellulose, chitin, and residual proteins. Actinomycetes are responsible for the characteristic earthy smell of finished, high-quality compost.
- **[[basidiomycetes|Basidiomycetes]]** — fungi that continue working on resistant materials throughout maturation

### The Physical Decomposer Community

A diverse community of macroscopic organisms supports the microbial team during maturation:

- Nematodes, springtails, and centipedes
- Earthworms, ants, and beetles
- Snails, slugs, mites, and sow bugs

Earthworms play a particularly important role: they coat organic particles with mucus as they pass through their digestive tracts, binding soil aggregates together and improving the physical structure of the finished compost.

### Knowing When Compost Is Finished

Compost is complete when you can no longer recognize the original materials. The product should be dark, crumbly, uniform in texture, and carry a pleasant earthy aroma.

## Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio

The [[carbon-nitrogen-ratio]] (C:N ratio) is one of the most critical factors influencing composting success. The ideal range for active composting is **25:1 to 30:1**.

### Imbalance Problems

- **Too much carbon** — nitrogen is consumed rapidly, leaving the microbial community starved. Decomposition slows dramatically or stalls entirely.
- **Too much nitrogen** — excess carbon is vented as gases (ammonia, CO₂) or washed away in leachate. Nutrients are lost, and the pile may develop foul odors.

### Brown vs. Green Materials

| Category | Examples | C:N Ratio | Microbial Preference |
|----------|----------|-----------|---------------------|
| Brown (carbon) | Leaves, bark, wood chips, sawdust, paper | 40:1–500:1 | Supports [[fungi]] |
| Green (nitrogen) | Grass clippings, weeds, kitchen scraps | ~19:1 | Supports [[bacteria]] |

### Common Material C:N Ratios

| Material | C:N Ratio |
|----------|-----------|
| Sawdust | 500:1 |
| Paper | 170:1 |
| Tree leaves | 40:1–80:1 |
| Grass clippings | 19:1 |

Achieving the right balance of browns and greens from the start ensures the pile moves smoothly through all three thermal stages and produces finished compost in the shortest possible time.
