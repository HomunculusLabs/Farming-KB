## Overview

Oxidative addition is an elementary organometallic reaction in which a metal complex inserts into a covalent bond and formally increases
both its oxidation state and coordination number. It is paired with reductive elimination as one of the defining transformations of
transition-metal catalysis.

A simple representation is M + X-Y to X-M-Y, where the metal gains two ligands and is oxidized by two units in the formal electron-counting
model. The real electronic change can be more distributed, but the formalism is extremely useful for following catalytic cycles.

Oxidative addition explains how comparatively inert bonds such as H-H, C-H, C-X, Si-H, B-H, and H-X become attached to a metal center. Once
attached, the fragments can migrate, insert, exchange, or recombine to make new chemical bonds.

## Requirements for the Metal Center

The reaction is favored when the metal center is electron-rich, coordinatively unsaturated, and capable of accessing a higher oxidation
state. Low-valent d8 and d10 complexes of palladium, platinum, iridium, rhodium, nickel, and related metals commonly participate.

A vacant coordination [[holmgren-site-design-as-cellular-design|site]] or a ligand that can dissociate is usually needed. Four- coordinate square-planar complexes and five-coordinate
complexes are common starting points because they can accept additional ligands without severe steric or electronic penalty.

Electron-donating ligands promote oxidative addition by increasing metal basicity and back-donation into antibonding orbitals of the
substrate. Bulky ligands can accelerate the reaction by creating open space, but excessive steric hindrance can prevent substrate approach.

The reverse process, reductive elimination, is favored when the newly formed X-Y bond is strong and the two groups are cis on the metal.
Catalytic design often requires balancing the conditions that promote oxidative addition against those that later promote reductive
elimination.

## Concerted Pathways

Nonpolar substrates such as hydrogen often add by a concerted pathway. The metal first interacts with the sigma bond to form a sigma
complex, then electron donation and back-donation weaken the substrate bond until two metal-ligand bonds are formed.

Addition of H2 to Vaska's complex is a classic example. Iridium(I) is converted to iridium(III), the coordination number rises, and two
hydride ligands are produced. The reaction is reversible, illustrating the energetic relationship between oxidative addition and reductive
elimination.

Concerted oxidative addition preserves stereochemical information in many systems because both atoms of the substrate engage the same metal
center in a single organized transition state. The ligands produced are usually cis before any later isomerization.

C-H activation can follow a related pattern through agostic or sigma-complex intermediates. The metal coordinates a C-H bond, back-donates
into sigma-star, and forms a metal hydride plus a metal carbon bond.

## SN2 and Polar Pathways

Alkyl halides can undergo oxidative addition by an SN2-like route, especially when the metal complex is nucleophilic and the carbon
electrophile is accessible. The metal attacks carbon while halide leaves, producing inversion at carbon when stereochemistry is tracked.

The first product of an SN2-type addition may be a cationic metal-alkyl complex with a separated halide counterion. Halide coordination can
then complete the formal oxidative addition product.

Polar oxidative additions are sensitive to solvent polarity, leaving-group ability, and substrate structure. Benzyl, allyl, methyl, and
primary halides are often more suitable than hindered secondary or tertiary substrates.

Aryl halides often add by pathways that are not simple [[nucleophilic-substitution-sn1-sn2-reactions]] at carbon. Palladium(0) oxidative addition into aryl bromides, iodides,
chlorides, and triflates depends strongly on ligand electronics and on the strength and polarity of the aryl- leaving group bond.

## Radical and One-Electron Pathways

Not every oxidative addition is a clean two-electron event. Some reactions proceed by single-electron transfer, radical formation, halogen-
atom abstraction, or radical recombination before the same formal product is reached.

Nickel chemistry frequently uses one-electron manifolds. Nickel(0), nickel(I), nickel(II), and nickel(III) states can participate in cross-
electrophile coupling, photoredox-assisted catalysis, and reactions of alkyl halides that would be difficult for palladium.

Radical pathways can be diagnosed by radical clocks, stereochemical scrambling, inhibition by radical traps, paramagnetic intermediates, or
rate patterns inconsistent with concerted or polar mechanisms.

The existence of one-electron oxidative addition is a reminder that oxidation state bookkeeping is not a mechanism. Formal electron counts
summarize starting and ending structures, while kinetic and spectroscopic evidence are needed to identify the pathway.

## Role in Catalysis

Oxidative addition is often the entry step in catalytic cycles that convert an organic electrophile into an organometallic intermediate.
Cross-coupling reactions such as Suzuki, Heck, Negishi, Stille, Kumada, and Sonogashira chemistry all depend on related activation events.

In a typical palladium cross-coupling cycle, Pd(0) adds into an aryl halide bond to form an aryl-palladium(II) halide. Transmetalation
introduces a second organic group, and reductive elimination releases the coupled product while regenerating Pd(0).

Hydrogenation and hydrofunctionalization also use oxidative addition. H2, silanes, boranes, or hydrides can add to a metal, creating metal
hydride species that transfer hydrogen or hydride equivalents to unsaturated substrates.

C-H activation chemistry expands the concept by using oxidative addition-like steps to functionalize bonds that lack conventional leaving
groups. This can shorten syntheses, but it requires exquisite control over site selectivity and competing pathways.

## Thermodynamics and Kinetics

The driving force depends on the strength of the bond being broken, the strength of the new metal-ligand bonds, ligand reorganization,
solvent stabilization, and the redox accessibility of the metal center. Strong H-H and C-H bonds require especially favorable metal-
substrate interactions.

Kinetic barriers can be tuned with ligand design. Electron-rich phosphines, N-heterocyclic carbenes, and pincer ligands often accelerate
addition into aryl chlorides or otherwise sluggish substrates by making the metal more reducing and nucleophilic.

Steric effects are dual. Bulky ligands can open a low-coordinate site by discouraging ligand association, yet they can also block approach
of a large substrate or destabilize the higher-coordinate oxidative-addition product.

Solvent and additives influence halide abstraction, ion-pair separation, ligand dissociation, and aggregation. Apparent rates in catalytic
reactions may reflect pre- equilibria rather than the intrinsic oxidative-addition step alone.

## Stereochemical Consequences

Concerted additions to H2, Si-H, or some C-H bonds tend to put the two new ligands cis on the metal. Subsequent ligand rearrangement,
hydride migration, or isomerization can obscure this initial geometry.

SN2-like additions to alkyl electrophiles can invert configuration at carbon. Radical pathways can racemize stereocenters or produce
rearranged products, making stereochemical probes powerful mechanistic tools.

In cross-coupling, oxidative addition sets the geometry and oxidation state for later transmetalation and reductive elimination. If the
oxidative-addition complex is too stable, catalyst turnover can stall despite rapid substrate activation.

## Practical Considerations

Air and moisture sensitivity are common because many low-valent organometallic precatalysts and ligands are easily oxidized. Reactions may
require glovebox handling, Schlenk techniques, degassed solvents, or in situ generation of the active species.

Aryl chlorides are attractive industrial substrates because they are cheap and available, but their stronger C-Cl bonds make oxidative
addition harder than for aryl bromides or iodides. Ligand design was crucial for making aryl chloride coupling broadly useful.

Competing beta-hydride elimination, ligand oxidation, metal black formation, and catalyst poisoning can all prevent productive catalysis
even when oxidative addition occurs. Mechanistic studies therefore track every step of the catalytic cycle, not only substrate activation.
