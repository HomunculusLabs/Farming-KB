Among [[masanobu-fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy]]'s contributions to agriculture, one of the most remarkable was his
development of a rice variety he named "Happy Hill." Created through decades of natural
selection on his farm in Ehime Prefecture, Japan, Happy Hill rice is notable for combining
the genetic hardiness of wild rice variants with some of the highest grain yields of any
rice strain in the world. Fukuoka described the variety as having been created "together
with the insects in my fields," emphasizing that its development was not the product of
laboratory breeding programs or genetic manipulation but of a process of natural
cultivation in which the rice plants were allowed to evolve and adapt to local conditions
without the protection of pesticides, [[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]], or artificial selection.

The genetic foundation of Happy Hill rice lies in what Fukuoka called "the blood of wild
variants." Unlike the highly bred, commercially developed rice varieties that dominate
modern agriculture, which Fukuoka regarded as weak and dependent on chemical inputs for
their survival, Happy Hill inherited traits from wild rice that made it exceptionally
resilient. Wild rice possesses a natural vigor that allows it to survive under conditions
that would destroy cultivated varieties: poor soil, irregular water supply, competition
from weeds, and attack by insects and disease. By allowing his rice plants to grow under
natural conditions over many years, without the artificial protections of conventional
farming, Fukuoka effectively selected for precisely those traits of hardiness and
independence that characterize wild rice. The insects in his fields, rather than being
enemies of the rice, played a role in this process by eliminating weaker plants and
allowing only the most vigorous to survive and reproduce.

Happy Hill rice belongs to what Fukuoka described as the "panicle weight type" of rice,
as distinguished from the "panicle number type." The panicle weight type produces fewer
but heavier heads of grain, resulting in a more concentrated energy store per plant.
Fukuoka's research into the ideal form of rice led him to believe that this type was
superior for natural cultivation because it maintained an orderly shape receptive to
sunlight, matured well, and was resistant to disease and pest attack, even in very dense
stands. The ideal rice plant, as he described it, is short-stalked and robust, with
leaves that are short, wide, and erect. The weight of the unhulled grain is 150 to 167
percent that of the straw, compared with less than 70 percent for ordinary rice. When a
dried stalk of ideal rice is balanced on a fingertip, the point of equilibrium is close
to the neck of the panicle, indicating the heavy grain load, whereas in ordinary rice
this point is located near the center of the stalk.

The yields achieved by Happy Hill rice under [[cho-loess-powder-in-natural-farming]] conditions were substantial.
On his farm, Fukuoka generally observed about 250 heads of grain per square yard in his
paddy fields, with an average of 200 grains per head, producing a harvest of
approximately 33 bushels for every quarter-acre. He reported consistently harvesting close
to 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre, with a goal of eventually achieving 33 bushels.
Through [[fukuoka-textdoc-sparse-seeding-natural-high-yield-rice]] and careful cultivation, he was able to obtain yields of over one
ton per quarter-acre with naturally grown rice. These figures are remarkable considering
that they were achieved without any chemical inputs, without plowing, and with minimal
labor — essentially just sowing seed and spreading straw.

What made Happy Hill rice particularly significant in Fukuoka's vision was its potential
as a tool for addressing global food scarcity. In the preface to "The Natural Way of
Farming," Fukuoka proposed an extraordinary scheme for distributing Happy Hill rice to
food-scarce nations [[fungi-around-the-world-species-distribution]]. He calculated that if a single head of Happy Hill
rice were sent across the sea to a country where food is scarce and there sown over a ten-
square-yard area, a single grain would yield 5,000 grains in one year's time. There would
be grain enough to sow a half-acre the following year, fifty acres two years hence, and
7,000 acres in the fourth year. This handful of grain could, in Fukuoka's vision, become
the seed rice for an entire nation, opening up the road to independence for a starving
people.

The arithmetic of this exponential expansion reflects the extraordinary fecundity of the
rice plant. A single head of rice typically contains several hundred grains, and each
grain, when planted and cultivated, can produce a new plant that bears multiple heads.
Under the conditions [[four-principles-of-natural-farming]], where the seed is protected in clay pellets and
the soil is enriched by clover and straw mulch, the germination rate and survival of
seedlings are high enough to sustain this rate of multiplication. Fukuoka believed that
the hardiness of Happy Hill rice, derived from its wild rice genetics, would allow it to
thrive even in the poor soil conditions and challenging climates found in many food-scarce
regions of the world, without requiring the expensive chemical inputs that make
conventional high-yield rice varieties impractical for subsistence farmers in developing
nations.

Fukuoka expressed urgency about the distribution of this seed rice, writing that it must
be delivered as soon as possible. He emphasized that even one person can begin the process
of propagation, and stated that he could be no happier than if his humble experience with
natural farming were to be used toward this end. The vision of Happy Hill rice as a means
of alleviating world hunger was consistent with Fukuoka's broader belief that the natural
farming experience could be of some help, however small, in revegetating the world and
stabilizing food supply. He saw in the variety a demonstration of the principle that
nature, when allowed to function without human interference, is capable of producing
abundance far exceeding what [[critique-of-scientific-agriculture]] achieves through costly and
destructive interventions.

The development of Happy Hill rice also illustrates Fukuoka's broader critique of
scientific plant breeding. While [[fukuoka-inductive-deductive-intuitive-reasoning-agricultural-research]] institutions invested enormous
resources in developing new high-yield strains through controlled crosses and laboratory
selection, Fukuoka achieved comparable or superior results through a process of natural
selection that required no laboratory, no budget, and no specialized knowledge beyond an
understanding of natural principles. The variety stood as living proof of his conviction
that the full productivity of nature far exceeds what [[fukuoka-critique-of-scientific-agriculture]] can achieve
through human manipulation, and that the path to true agricultural abundance lies not in
increasing human intervention but in reducing it.

Fukuoka's approach to developing Happy Hill rice was deeply connected to his philosophy of
[[korean-natural-farming-and-jadam]] his understanding of the ideal form of rice. He had long searched for
the true form of rice and asked what healthy rice is. When he grew rice, barley, and
clover together, he found that rice ripening over a thick cover of clover is short-stalked,
robust right down to the bottom leaf, and bears fine golden heads of grain. After observing
this, he tried seeding the rice in the fall and winter, and learned that even rice grown
under terrible conditions on arid, depleted soil gives surprisingly high yields. This
experience convinced him of the possibilities of growing high-yielding rice on continuously
untilled fields. He eventually found what he thought to be the ideal form of high-yielding
rice: a short-stalked dwarf rice of robust appearance, with only three or four nodes above
the ground, having a sturdy, masculine, panicle weight type shape rather than a feminine
form with a high head-to-body ratio. Happy Hill rice embodied this ideal form.

The implications of Happy Hill rice for global food security extended beyond the variety
itself. Fukuoka envisioned it as part of a larger system [[fukuoka-four-principles-of-natural-farming]] that could be
adopted anywhere in the world without requiring expensive inputs, large machinery, or
specialized training. The combination of a hardy, high-yielding variety with the no-tillage,
no-chemical methods of natural farming offered a path to food self-sufficiency for nations
that could not afford the petroleum-dependent agriculture of the developed world. In this
vision, a single head of rice carried the potential not merely to feed a village but to
transform the food economy of an entire nation, provided only that the principles of
natural farming were followed in its cultivation and propagation.
