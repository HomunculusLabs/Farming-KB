# Homestead Canning Recipes

Canning preserves homestead harvests for year-round use. Two methods exist: water-bath canning (high-acid foods, pH below 4.6) and pressure canning (low-acid foods). Botulism risk makes strict adherence to tested recipes non-negotiable.

## Equipment

- Pressure canner (not a pressure cooker -- must have a dial gauge or weighted gauge)
- Water-bath canner with rack
- Mason jars (regular or wide-mouth) and two-piece lids
- Jar lifter, headspace tool, bubble remover, funnel
- Clean towels and cutting boards

Always inspect jars for chips and cracks. Use new lids each time; rings are reusable.

## Pressure Canning: Green Beans

1. Wash and trim fresh green beans; pack raw into hot jars
2. Add 1/2 tsp canning salt per pint (optional)
3. Cover with boiling water, leaving 1-inch headspace
4. Remove air bubbles, wipe rims, apply lids and rings fingertip-tight
5. Process pints at 11 psi (dial gauge) or 10 psi (weighted) for 20 minutes; quarts for 25 minutes
6. Adjust pressure for altitude: +1 psi per 2,000 ft above 1,000 ft

## Pressure Canning: Corn

1. Husk corn, remove silk; blanch 3 minutes in boiling water
2. Cut kernels from cob (do not scrape -- that changes density)
3. Pack loosely into hot jars, leaving 1-inch headspace
4. Do not add liquid -- corn creates its own
5. Process pints at 11 psi for 55 minutes; quarts for 85 minutes

Whole-kernel corn is one of the longest-processing vegetables due to its density.

## Pressure Canning: Tomatoes (Water-Bath or Pressure)

Tomatoes border on the acidity threshold. Modern varieties are less acidic than heirlooms. Add 2 tbsp bottled lemon juice or 1/2 tsp citric acid per quart (half for pints).

Water-bath method:
1. Dip tomatoes in boiling water 30-60 seconds, then ice bath
2. Peel, core, and quarter
3. Pack raw into jars, pressing to release juice
4. Add acid (lemon juice or citric acid) and 1 tsp salt per quart
5. Leave 1/2-inch headspace
6. Process pints 35 minutes, quarts 45 minutes in boiling water bath

Crushed tomatoes: heat tomatoes to boiling, simmer 5 minutes, pack hot.

## Pressure Canning: Soups and Stews

Use only approved recipes (USDA Complete Guide or Ball Blue Book). Rules:
- Do not add noodles, rice, pasta, or thickeners (they interfere with heat penetration)
- Do not add dairy
- Fill solids to 1/2 jar volume, then cover with broth or water
- Process pints at 11 psi for 60 minutes; quarts for 75 minutes

Example vegetable soup: diced carrots, green beans, corn, peas, potatoes, onions. Pack 1/2 solids, fill with hot broth, 1-inch headspace.

## Pressure Canning: Meat

Meat must be pressure canned -- no exceptions.

Raw pack (beef, pork, venison):
1. Trim excess fat and gristle
2. Cut into 1-inch cubes or strips
3. Pack raw into hot jars, leaving 1-1/4-inch headspace
4. Add 1 tsp salt per quart (optional)
5. Do not add liquid
6. Process pints at 11 psi for 75 minutes; quarts for 90 minutes

Hot pack (better quality): brown meat, pack hot, add hot broth to 1-inch headspace. Same processing times.

Ground meat: brown and drain fat, pack loosely, add hot water. Process same as raw pack.

Poultry: bone-in or boneless. Process pints 65 min, quarts 75 min at 11 psi.

## Safety Essentials

- Never use recipes from blogs, social media, or older pre-1990 canning books
- Always adjust pressure for altitude
- Let the canner depressurize naturally -- never force-cool
- Wait 10 minutes after removing weight before opening lid
- Check seals after 12-24 hours (lid should be concave and not flex)
- Remove rings for storage; label with date and contents
- Store in cool, dark, dry place; use within 12-18 months
- If a jar did not seal: refrigerate and use within 3 days, reprocess within 24 hours, or freeze

## Common Mistakes

- Using a pressure cooker instead of a pressure canner (insufficient volume)
- Omitting acidification for tomatoes
- Overpacking jars (prevents proper heat circulation)
- Using old canning recipes that haven't been tested for safety
- Storing jars with rings on (can mask seal failure)
