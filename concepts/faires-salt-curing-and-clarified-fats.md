## Salting Meat

Salting is one of the oldest and most reliable methods of meat
preservation. While smoking produces delicious results, it requires a
smokehouse and several weeks of constant vigilance. Salting is practical
and efficient with very little effort or equipment.

### Step-by-Step Process

1. **Prepare the meat:** Clean the meat and cut off anything
   undesirable. Save the fat for clarified fat production. Dry the meat
   with a clean cloth and cut into smaller strips to ensure the salt
   penetrates to the center. Rub spices into the strips, then rub salt
   in generously until the meat cannot accept any more.

2. **Coat and hang:** Cover the strips in a layer of salt. Hang in a
   place that stays consistently 59°F (15°C) for at least three weeks,
   checking often for spoilage. A basement or cold storage is ideal.
   The meat should stay edible for several months.

3. **The science of salt curing:** Salt dissolves into the water in the
   meat, preventing bacterial growth if the balance exceeds 3.5% salt
   to water. Ideally the concentration should be over 10%. By rubbing
   in as much salt as the meat will accept, this threshold is reliably
   achieved.

4. **Before cooking:** Wash off the salt thoroughly. You may need to
   soak the meat briefly to remove all salt from the surface and outer
   layers.

### Tips and Considerations

- Use coarse [[jadam-sea-salt-and-mineral-solutions]] or kosher salt
  rather than fine table salt, which contains anti-caking agents
- Avoid commercial curing salts containing sodium nitrite unless
  specifically desired
- Add whole peppercorns, bay leaves, garlic, or juniper berries for
  flavor variety
- Ensure good [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]]
  around the hanging meat to prevent mould and mildew
- Label each batch with the date and meat type for rotation

## Clarified Fats

Fat from homegrown meat or dairy is fundamentally different from
factory-processed fats. Saving and clarifying fat turns what many
consider waste into a valuable cooking resource that lasts for years.

### Making Clarified Fat (Lard)

Save all fats from butchering and store in the freezer until ready to
process. Cut the fat into small pieces and place in a saucepan. Simmer
on low heat for several hours until fully liquefied. For flavored fat,
add onions, carrots, leeks, turnips, herbs, and spices along with salt
and pepper — this creates a ready-to-use base for soups and stews.

Once liquefied, pour through cheesecloth or a fine mesh strainer into
clean containers. Allow to cool and solidify. Properly clarified fat
stored in a cool, dark place will last for a year or more. In the
refrigerator, it keeps indefinitely.

### Making Ghee (Clarified Butter)

Ghee has been a staple in Indian cooking for thousands of years and has
exceptional shelf stability — it can sit at room temperature for months
without spoiling. To make ghee, melt butter at very low temperature
until completely liquid. Do not stir. Raise heat slightly until the
butter steams gently but does not brown. The milk solids will rise to
the surface — skim these off carefully. Continue until the butter is
golden and completely clear. Pour into a clean container and allow to
solidify. Once cool and solid, seal tightly.

Ghee is ideal for cream sauces, frying, and any application where
butter flavor is desired without the milk solids burning.

### Uses for Clarified Fats

- Frying and sautéing (higher smoke point than unclarified fats)
- Pie crusts and pastry (produces flakier results)
- Seasoning cast iron cookware
- Soap making (rendered fat is the base for traditional soap)
- Bird food (mix with seeds in winter)
- Leather conditioning (neatsfoot oil from cattle fat)

## Comparison with Other Preservation Methods

Salt curing sits alongside drying, fermentation, and cold storage as
one of the four pillars of traditional food preservation. Each method
has distinct advantages depending on climate, available resources, and
the type of food being preserved.

- **Salt curing** excels for meat and fish in humid climates where air
  drying alone is unreliable
- **Drying** requires less equipment but depends on consistent heat and
  low humidity
- **Fermentation** adds probiotic benefits but requires controlled
  temperature and careful monitoring
- **Cold storage** preserves the most natural flavour but demands energy
  or specific climate conditions

A diversified homestead uses multiple preservation methods in parallel.
Salt curing is particularly valuable as a no-energy method that works
year-round, making it an excellent complement to seasonal techniques
like freezing or canning. See [[faires-dairy-processing-cheese-and-butter]]
for related dairy preservation approaches.

## Historical and Cross-Cultural Context

Salt curing has been practised across virtually every human culture with
access to salt deposits. Roman soldiers were partially paid in salt
(salarium, the origin of the word "salary"), underscoring its
importance. In Nordic countries, salt cod (klippfisk) became a staple
trade commodity that sustained long sea voyages. Chinese salt-cured
meats (la rou) remain culturally significant today, particularly around
Lunar New Year celebrations.

The choice between dry salting and brine curing (immersion in salt
solution) varies by tradition and application. Dry salting draws
moisture out more aggressively and produces firmer, drier results.
Brine curing produces more evenly seasoned meat with better moisture
retention. Both methods rely on the same fundamental principle: salt
concentration above 3.5% in the tissue water prevents microbial growth.

## Integration with Homestead Systems

Clarified fats connect salt curing to broader homestead cycles. Fat
rendered during butchering provides cooking oil, soap-making stock,
leather conditioner, and candle material. The salt-cured meat itself
generates brine that can be diluted and used as a mineral supplement
for livestock or compost tea.

On a well-designed homestead, nothing is wasted. The bones remaining
after meat curing become valuable for bone broth. The salt that has
absorbed moisture from curing meat can be dried and reused for less
demanding applications like cleaning or preserving hides. This
closed-loop approach is central to [[natural-farming]] principles.
