# Tribal Pattern Knowledge Systems

Tribal peoples worldwide have developed extraordinarily sophisticated pattern-based knowledge systems that encode vast quantities of information in apparently simple forms — tattoos, carvings, sand drawings, songs, dances, and woven textiles. These systems constitute valid literatures and accurate recording methods that Western cultures have largely dismissed.

## Pattern as Literature

Tribal art, including Celtic and ancient engraving, displays pattern complexity that carried important meanings to their peoples. To call such peoples "illiterate" is only possible if we ignore their patterns, songs, and dances as valid literature and accurate recording systems. Having evolved number and alphabetical symbols, modern education has abandoned pattern learning — which Mollison considers a gross error, because simple patterns link so many phenomena that learning even one significant pattern is akin to learning an underlying principle applicable to specific data and situations.

## Maori Genealogical Patterns

The Maori of New Zealand use tattoo and carved patterns to record and recall genealogical and saga information. These are not decorative — they are functional memory systems that encode complex lineage data and historical narratives in visual form accessible to trained readers.

## Polynesian Navigation Charts

Polynesians used **pattern [[maps]]** that lacked scale, cartographic details, and trigonometric measures, yet sufficed to locate 200–2,000 island specks across the vastness of the Pacific Ocean. These maps were constructed from:

- **Sticks and flexed strips** representing wave interference patterns
- **Cowrie shells** marking island positions and ocean current interactions
- **Song cycles** that served as keys to unlock the map's meaning

The maps were linked to star sets and ocean currents, encoding navigational information that Western cartography would require extensive instruments and calculations to replicate.

## Pitjantjatjara Sand Pattern Maps

Australian Pitjantjatjara people sing over sand patterns and can "sing" strangers to a single stone in an apparently featureless desert. Their designs accurately reflect:

- **Lobular shapes** and elaborate micro-elevations of the desert terrain
- **Vegetation changes** richly portrayed in what Westerners perceive as abstract art
- **Fire patterns**, pollen distributions, and flowering stages of individual plants
- **Rain tracks** and cloud streets
- **Hunting, saga, and climatic data**

## Song as Knowledge Encoding

Children of many tribes are taught hundreds of simple chants whose words hide deeper, secondary meanings about medicinal, sacred, or navigational knowledge. This becomes meaningful only when the initiate receives the decoding system — or discovers it through personal revelation (intuition). A pattern map may have little meaning without its song keys.

### Nootka Navigation Songs

The Nootka Indians of British Columbia (documented by Anne Cameron in *Daughters of Copper Woman*, 1981) used song navigation: "There was a song for goin' to China, and a song for goin' to Japan, a song for the big island and a song for the smaller one. All she [the navigator] had to know was the song and she knew where she was."

The navigation songs recorded "the streams and creeks of the sea" — ocean currents, headlands and bays, star constellations, and "ceremonies of ecstatic revelation." Song stanzas functioned as highly accurate timers, reproducible at any time, with content recording observations from prior voyages.

### Song Duration as Measurement

The steerswomen used the accurate rhythm of song duration to time both current speed and boat speed through the water. Current speed was timed between headlands, and boat speed was measured against a log or float. The song duration was, in fact, an accurate timing mechanism — one that any person can use today.

## Dance and Muscular Memory

Dances involving muscular learning and memory, coupled with chants, carry accurate long-term messages, saga details, and planting knowledge. Many dances and chants evolved from work and travel movements. Particularly significant are **dance-imitations of animal species**, which interpret postural meanings of those species in a non-verbal, universally transmittable way.

Many formal attitudes of prayer and submission are basic imitations of primate postures. Even the chair enables humans to maintain the postures of baboons — and baboons were revered as gods and embalmed by Egyptian chair-makers. Humans can remember hundreds of songs, postures, and chants, but little of prose and even less of tabulated data.

## Interspecies Pattern Communication

People who can call the deer (Paiute wise men), the dolphin (Gilbert Islanders), the kangaroo (tribal Tasmanians), and other species to present themselves demonstrated profound behavioral, interspecific "pulser" pattern understanding. The Eskimo navigated in fog by listening to quail dialects specific to certain headlands. Similar insights are available to anyone whose ear for bird dialect is trained, so that song and postural signals from other species form a rich encyclopedia of a world unnoticed by those lacking pattern knowledge.

## The Anasazi Spiral Calendar

The [[anasazi-spiral]] provides perhaps the most striking example of information density in a simple pattern. This apparently simple spiral, inscribed on a rock surface near the top of a mesa in the desert southwest USA, contains:

- **19 intercept points** on its "horizon" line
- **Three carefully balanced rock slabs** shaped as gnomons
- **Moon-shadow casting** that indicates the 19-year (actually 18.6-year) lunar cycle caused by the sway of Earth's axis
- **Solar cycle indicators** for equinoxes and solstices via vertical daggers of sunlight

One simple spiral thus records lunar and solar cycles for regulating planting, timing ceremonies, and predicting the 18.6-year cycle of drought and flood. Scientists have often doubted tribal peoples' capacity to pattern such long-term and complex events, yet this single pattern encodes what would take a small library to express in modern notation.

## Shamanic Pattern Understanding

Tribal shamans who attempt to foresee the future and control dreams through sensory deprivation, or who read fortunes by smoke, entrails, water, or the movement of serpents, employ pattern-recognition strategies not fundamentally different from modern statistical methods. By subjecting themselves to isolation, danger, and stress, practitioners report passing "across the folds of time" to scan present and future — a phenomenon described by J.W. Dunn in *An Experiment with Time* (1921) and experienced by participants in Shoshone sun dances.

## Implications for Modern Education

Mollison argues that pattern, song, and dance could be of great assistance to education and great relevance to life — they are among the easiest things to accurately reproduce. The abandonment of pattern-based learning in favor of purely numerical and alphabetical systems represents a significant loss of human knowledge capacity.

The challenge is to revive meaningful geometries, songs, and dances that gave people and their work meaning — to reintegrate art with science and with the functional needs of society.
