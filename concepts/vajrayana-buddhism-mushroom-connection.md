## The Guru-Disciple Hierarchy

Arthur offers a critical perspective on the hierarchical structure of Vajrayana Buddhism, noting that the system of a guru determining a student's readiness for higher teachings creates inherent limitations. The limits of the teacher's knowledge become the limits of the student's experience. Teachers are "never willing to admit they are not qualified to teach," so they claim to be all-knowing instead.

Absolute and total devotion to the guru is described as an integral part of Buddhism, with refuge taken in the dharma and the guru's interpretation of it. Arthur points out that this hierarchical order is "flawed by its very concept of withholding anything from anybody." The hope of any student to find a teacher worthy of devotion depends on the amount of knowledge that particular teacher possesses, and those who do not know about the mushroom connection are "somewhere on the path to finding it out."

This critique connects to the broader theme of knowledge suppression across religious traditions, where hierarchical systems control access to information and use that control to maintain authority. The Vajrayana claim of "lightning-fast" enlightenment, when divorced from its entheogenic basis, becomes an empty promise that keeps practitioners engaged in a system that cannot deliver the advertised results without the mushroom sacrament that has been hidden at its core.

## Criticism and Context

It should be noted that Arthur's interpretation of Vajrayana Buddhism is not accepted by Buddhist scholars or practitioners. Traditional Buddhism, including Vajrayana, does not require or endorse the use of entheogenic substances. The dark retreat practice, while producing powerful experiences, is understood within the tradition as a meditation technique rather than a method for stimulating pineal DMT production.

However, the remarkable parallels between Vajrayana symbolism and mushroom characteristics, the emphasis on death-and-rebirth experiences, and the lightning-fast enlightenment claims do raise questions about the tradition's origins that merit further investigation. The possibility that entheogenic practices were historically present in Buddhist traditions before being symbolized and eventually replaced by meditation-only practices is consistent with the broader pattern of entheogenic-to-symbolic substitution that Arthur traces across all major world religions.
