# Garden Microclimates

Whether perennial-vegetables|perennial or annual, a plant will thrive only under the right growth conditions. A drought-loving rosemary that will drown too close to a downspout will perk up in a hot, sunny corner. A sweet woodruff that is withering in the sunny site will sigh in relief when shaded by some nurturing shrub. Each plant requires a certain range of soil type, acidity, temperature, light, moisture, and other factors. Microclimate gardening is the practice of identifying, creating, and managing these conditions to support the plants we want.

## What Creates Microclimates

The big force that creates most microclimates is heat transfer -- the movement of energy from one place to another. Heat transfer occurs when the sun sends radiant energy to the earth, when warm ground radiates heat to the sky, or when wind mixes hot air with cold.

Few microclimates exist in the open, where wind constantly mixes air and evens temperatures. Open ground experiences the full force of sun, wind, and weather with no moderation. Microclimates form when trees, walls, and other structures block air mixing, reduce heat loss, and cast shade. Understanding these mechanisms lets gardeners deliberately create the conditions their plants need.

## The South Wall Effect

The warm spot on the south side of a house, where snow melts soonest, exists because of heat transfer. Without the house, the sun would still heat the ground, but most warmth would simply radiate back to the open sky, and the rest would be swept away by the wind.

When a south-facing wall enters the picture, the situation changes dramatically. Sun heats the ground just as in the open, but now the sunlight also warms the south-facing wall. The wall's warmth then bounces onto the ground, warming the earth further. The heat radiating from the ground, which in the open would disappear skyward, is partially blocked by the wall, and cannot escape. A vertical wall blocks up to two-thirds of heat loss from a nearby radiant body (the ground in this case). The result is a significantly warmer zone that extends several feet from the wall.
