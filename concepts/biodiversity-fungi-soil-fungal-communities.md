# Soil Fungal Communities

Soil fungal communities represent one of the most diverse and ecologically
significant components of terrestrial ecosystems. They drive
[[fukuoka-natural-farming-soil-ecology-principles]], interacting with
virtually all other soil organisms in intricate food webs.

## Structure and Composition

All major [[coprophilous-fungi-taxonomic-groups-distribution]] of fungi are encountered in soils. Among
saprobic filamentous forms, prevalent groups include the Saprolegniales,
Pythiales, Mucorales, Eurotiales, Microascales, Hypocreales, Sordariales,
Onygenales, Leotiales, Pezizales, and a vast array of mitosporic fungi
(almost exclusively anamorphs of ascomycete or basidiomycete species).
Basidiomycetes, once described as "the missing link in soil mycology,"
account for approximately 60% of total living microbial biomass in the
litter and humus of deciduous forests but remain under-represented in
most surveys due to technique-based bias.

In grassland soils, organic matter is enriched throughout the soil
profile, especially from annual, finely divided grass roots whose
biomass far exceeds above-ground biomass. Forest soils are enriched by
debris from massive above-ground biomass plus substantial mycorrhizal
inputs. Organic matter generally decreases downward through the soil
profile, with deep forest horizons containing primarily highly stable,
polyphenolic compounds hundreds to thousands of years old.

## Factors Affecting Community Composition

The composition of soil fungal communities is regulated strongly by
climatic and [[soil-edaphic-factors-microbial-communities]], and ecological similarity among habitats
supersedes geography. An experienced taxonomist reading a list of the
10 to 20 most abundant fungi in a soil sample can guess with great
accuracy the prevailing vegetational and climatic regimens.

### Soil Type and Vegetation

Distinctive assemblages of soil fungi characterize different vegetation
types. Antarctic, arctic, alpine, and boreal soils are dominated by
Cylindrocarpon, Chrysosporium, Mortierella, Oidiodendron, certain
Penicillium species, and sterile fungal strains. Beech forest soils of
[[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] and Europe show striking similarities in principal
saprobic fungal species. Agricultural soils are frequently dominated
by Acremonium, Fusarium, and Phoma species, with reduced Mucorales
diversity. Desert soils have low propagule densities but high species
diversity, enriched with melanized fungi adapted to stress.

### Depth and Moisture

Species diversity generally decreases with soil depth as organic matter
declines. In humid tropical forests, the litter-soil interface is often
abrupt, with species-rich litter overlying depauperate mineral soil.
Water potential strongly affects soil fungal activity, with soil
moisture influencing the rate of organic matter decomposition and
shifting community composition seasonally.

### pH and Chemistry

Soil pH, ion content, and temperature influence fungal distributions
and community dynamics. Chytridiomycetes show species diversity
increasing along a gradient from higher latitudes toward equatorial
regions, with distributions sensitive to moisture, pH, and temperature.
Biotic interactions, including competition and grazing by microfauna,
may equal or exceed abiotic factors as determinants of community
composition.

## Succession Patterns

Species richness in soil fungal communities increases through both
primary and secondary succession in plant communities. Structural change
in the community, such as increased equitability as densities of
principal species diminish, is a parameter distinct from species
richness. Primary factors promoting high diversity include microhabitat
heterogeneity, intermingling of decomposers at different successional
stages, faunal grazing, persistence of organic matter promoting niche
partitioning, temporal changes in climate and vegetation, and the
individualistic behavior of fungal cells and mycelia.

## Agricultural Impacts

Disturbance of soil fungal communities through agriculture, logging, or
habitat conversion generally has quantitative rather than qualitative
effects on species composition. Cultivation typically reduces overall
fungal biomass and shifts community composition toward rapidly
colonizing opportunistic species at the expense of slow-growing
specialists. Soil compaction, tillage, and chemical inputs alter the
physical and chemical environment that shapes fungal community structure.

## Methods for Studying Soil Fungi

[[fukuoka-direct-observation-method]] of soil fungi in situ is limited to indistinct
mycelia. Most surveys rely on indirect isolation techniques in which
fungal propagules are dispersed on artificial media or trapped on
organic baits. No complete inventory of soil fungi has been undertaken
for any single geographic region, and inventories using a single-
isolation method detect only a portion of organisms present. Molecular
approaches using species-specific probes and nucleic acid analyses are
increasingly important but complement rather than replace cultural
methods. Comprehensive guides to soil fungi, especially tropical soil
fungi, remain a high priority in fungal systematics.

## Species Richness Estimates

Estimates of species richness vary widely with survey intensity and
methodology. Surveys from temperate forests have recovered 90 to more
than 470 taxa per vegetation type. Tropical soil fungal diversity
appears comparable to that of temperate forests, challenging the
assumption that [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]] harbor significantly more fungal species.
However, tropical soils remain less thoroughly studied, and many genera
appear to be primarily tropical in distribution.
