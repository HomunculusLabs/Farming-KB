## Fungal Accumulation of Radionuclides

Fungi play a remarkably important role in the uptake, accumulation, and cycling of radionuclides in terrestrial ecosystems. Their extensive hyphal networks, efficient ion-exchange mechanisms, and diverse trophic strategies make them among the most effective biological accumulators of radioactive contaminants, particularly radiocesium (¹³⁷Cs) and radiostrontium (⁹⁰Sr).

## Uptake Mechanisms

Radionuclide uptake by fungi occurs through several mechanisms:

- **Ion-exchange sites on cell walls**: Fungal hyphal walls possess charged sites that can adsorb radionuclides from soil solution. When expressed on a hyphal surface area basis, uptake values range from 0.1 to 2.5 nmol m⁻² hyphal area per hour.
- **Potassium replacement**: Radiocesium behaves similarly to potassium in biochemical pathways. In *Fusarium solani*, both Rb and Cs can replace K, while in *Candida utilis*, only Rb (not Li, Na, or Cs) substitutes for K — demonstrating species-specific uptake mechanisms.
- **Calcium-mimicking behavior**: Strontium behaves similarly to calcium in fungal metabolism. The wood decay fungus *Resinicium bicolor* can utilize strontium from strontianite sand, translocate it through mycelial cord systems, and deposit it in calcium oxalate crystals, suggesting a pathway for ⁹⁰Sr movement through decomposer fungi.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi and Radionuclides

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi

Ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes are particularly effective radionuclide accumulators. In a boreal coniferous forest ecosystem in Sweden, facultative mycorrhizal species contained 230 kBq kg⁻¹ dry weight of ¹³⁷Cs, obligate mycorrhizal species 120 kBq kg⁻¹, and saprotrophic fungi 140 kBq kg⁻¹ — far exceeding levels in leaf litter (26 kBq kg⁻¹), lichens (36 kBq kg⁻¹), mosses (19 kBq kg⁻¹), and angiosperms (8 kBq kg⁻¹).

The distribution of fungal mycelia in upper soil horizons correlates with radiocesium accumulation in surface soil and reduced downward leaching, due to immobilization in fungal tissue. This suggests that mycorrhizal fungi could serve as a barrier preventing radionuclide migration into groundwater.

Dighton and Horrill (1988) found that up to 92% of radiocesium in mycorrhizal basidiocarps in the UK was derived from pre-Chernobyl weapons testing fallout, demonstrating the remarkable long-term retention capacity of these fungi.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi

The role of arbuscular mycorrhizae (AM) in radionuclide uptake is more complex and sometimes conflicting:

- AM inoculation of sweet clover and Sudan grass showed slight, statistically insignificant increases in ¹³⁷Cs and ⁶⁰Co uptake.
- AM colonization of soybeans by *Glomus mosseae* significantly increased ⁹⁰Sr uptake.
- Conversely, AM symbiosis in sheep grass (*Festuca ovina*) reduced radiocesium uptake into shoots.

### Ericoid Mycorrhizal Fungi

In ericoid mycorrhizal heather (*Calluna vulgaris*), influx of radiocesium was lower in mycorrhizal than nonmycorrhizal plants. However, internal redistribution within mycorrhizal plants allowed a greater proportion of Cs to be translocated to shoots, especially when plants were pre-incubated in high-potassium environments.

## Saprotrophic Fungi

Saprotrophic (decomposer) fungi in grassland soils have extraordinary potential for radionuclide immobilization. Based on laboratory uptake studies estimating an average influx rate of 134 nmol Cs g⁻¹ dry weight of mycelium, combined with field measurements of hyphal biomass (approximately 6 g dry weight cm⁻³ of soil), Dighton et al. (1991) estimated that the fungal community in two upland grassland ecosystems in northern England could take up between 350 and 804 nmol Cs m⁻² h⁻¹. Given pore water concentrations at the micromolar level, these fungi could accumulate a large percentage of total fallout radiocesium.

On a weight basis, saprotrophic species showed higher accumulation rates than mycorrhizal species (Clint et al., 1991), suggesting they may be the primary immobilizers of radionuclides in many soil environments.

## Translocation Within Fungal Thalli

Translocation of radionuclides through fungal hyphae differs from that of essential nutrients:

- **Carbon and phosphorus** translocate through hyphae of *Rhizopus*, *Trichoderma*, and *Stemphylium* primarily by diffusion.
- **Radiocesium** translocation through *Schizophyllum commune* hyphae is slower than diffusion, suggesting an active accumulation mechanism.
- Preferential transport of radiocesium occurs toward developing fruit bodies, which act as nutrient sinks.

Gray et al. (1995, 1996) demonstrated, using quantitative autoradiography, that translocation of radiocesium through fungal thalli of *Armillaria* spp. and *Schizophyllum commune* was significantly reduced compared to a diffusion model, confirming active accumulation rather than passive movement.

## Bioremediation Potential

The ability of fungi to accumulate radionuclides has practical implications for environmental cleanup:

### Industrial Applications

- White and Gadd (1990) developed air-lift bioreactor systems with live fungal cultures for biosorption of radiothorium.
- *Rhizopus arrhizus* and *Aspergillus niger* were more efficient absorbers than *Penicillium italicum* and *P. chrysogenum*.
- Mycelial fungi can be palletized to make them physically similar to commercial ion-exchange materials.

### Ecosystem Restoration

Ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes offer particular promise for terrestrial ecosystem restoration because their large, harvestable fruiting structures (mushrooms) provide a potential means of physically removing accumulated radionuclides from contaminated sites. The concept involves:

1. Establishing mycorrhizal trees on contaminated sites
2. Allowing fungi to accumulate radionuclides from soil
3. Harvesting fruit bodies to remove radionuclides from the ecosystem

### Enhanced Phytoremediation

Entry et al. (1994) demonstrated that mycorrhizal-enhanced uptake of radionuclides into trees can be 3–5 times greater than in nonmycorrhizal seedlings. In ponderosa and Monterey pine seedlings:

- Mycorrhizal plants accumulated significantly more ⁹⁰Sr than uninoculated controls
- *Laccaria laccata* showed the highest concentration ratios in ponderosa pine (162× soil specific activity)
- *Pisolithus tinctorius* showed the highest uptake in Monterey pine

### Potassium Fertilization

Berreck and Hasselwandter (2001) investigated potassium fertilization as a method to reduce Cs uptake by mycorrhizal grass (*Agrostis tenuis*). While mycorrhizal development did reduce Cs uptake at moderate nutrient levels — likely through sequestration in extraradical hyphae — adding potassium provided no additional benefit.

## Trophic Transfer

Radionuclides accumulated in fungi enter food webs through consumption:

- **Reindeer** in Norway derive significant radiocesium from lichens, which form about 40% of their annual food intake. Lichen ¹³⁷Cs levels ranged from 6,700 to 24,000 Bq kg⁻¹ between 1986 and 1988.
- **Roe deer** in Sweden showed high radiocesium intake (up to 3,000 Bq day⁻¹) associated with consumption of fungal fruit bodies during autumn.
- **Fungal-feeding beetles** accumulated mercury at 17.5 mg kg⁻¹ dry weight, compared to 2–2.5 mg kg⁻¹ in saprophagous, phytophagous, and zoophagous genera, indicating biomagnification through fungal-based food chains.
- In the UK, the average consumer radiation dose from fungal-derived radiocesium is estimated at less than one microsievert — well below the IAEA annual limit, though this figure may be higher where wild mushroom consumption is more widespread.
