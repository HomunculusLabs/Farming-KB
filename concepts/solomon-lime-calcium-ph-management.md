# Lime, Calcium, Magnesium and Soil pH Management

Understanding lime and its role in garden soil is essential for anyone
using Solomon's [[solomon-complete-organic-fertilizer-guide]] system. Lime provides calcium and magnesium -- two critical
secondary nutrients -- and also influences soil pH. However, Solomon's
approach to pH management differs significantly from conventional advice.

## Types of Lime

There are three types of lime, all of which are ground natural rock:

1. **Agricultural lime**: Relatively pure calcium carbonate. Provides
   calcium and raises soil pH.
2. **Dolomitic lime**: Contains both calcium and magnesium carbonates,
   usually in roughly equal amounts.
3. **Gypsum**: Calcium sulfate. Provides calcium and sulfur without
   significantly affecting soil pH.

Solomon warns never to use quicklime, burnt lime, hydrated lime, or other
chemically processed limes. These are too harsh and can damage soil biology.

If you have to choose only one kind of lime, agricultural lime is probably
the best single choice. However, you will get a far better result using a
mixture of the three types. All are inexpensive when bought in large sacks
from agricultural suppliers.

## Solomon's Radical View on Soil pH

Most gardening books advise that the acidity or pH of soil should be
corrected by liming. Solomon takes a contrarian position: he advises that
you forget about pH. His reasoning:

- Liming to adjust soil pH may be useful in broadacre farming but is not
  of primary concern in an organic garden.
- The concept of soil pH is controversial. Solomon recommends reading the
  papers of William Albrecht (cited in the book's bibliography) for those
  interested in the scientific debate.
- If a soil test shows your garden's pH is low and you are advised to
  lime to correct it -- do not. Instead, each year just add what Solomon
  recommends in his soil improvement program: compost/manure, lime(s), and
  COF.

## How pH Corrects Itself

Over time, soil pH will correct itself, more because of the increased
organic matter than from adding calcium and/or magnesium directly. If your
soil test shows the pH is acceptable, use Solomon's full recommendation.
If pH is low, add lime anyway because vegetables still need calcium and
magnesium, and in the right balance.

If you routinely garden with COF, there will be no need to separately lime
the garden. COF is formulated so that when applied at the recommended
rate, it automatically distributes about 4 pounds of lime per 1,000
square feet per year. See
[[solomon-complete-organic-fertilizer-guide]]
for the exact COF recipe.

## Calcium and Magnesium Balance

Vegetables need both calcium and magnesium, and the balance between them
matters. In areas with high rainfall (where soils may already contain
significant levels of calcium and sometimes excessive magnesium), and
where clay soils are present, Solomon suggests caution about adding any
forms of lime in excess of the minimal amounts in COF. You might be
advised to use gypsum instead of lime.

Gypsum is particularly useful where:

- The soil already has adequate or high magnesium (common in clay soils).
- You want to add calcium without raising pH.
- The soil is heavy clay that could benefit from improved structure
  (gypsum helps flocculate clay particles).

## Regional Considerations

### High-Rainfall Regions

In areas receiving more than 40 inches (1,000 mm) of rainfall per year,
soils may already contain significant calcium and sometimes excessive
magnesium. Solomon suggests caution about adding lime in excess of COF's
built-in content. You might use gypsum instead of agricultural lime.

### Low-Rainfall and Arid Regions

In drier regions, soils tend to be alkaline. Adding lime is usually
unnecessary and may be harmful. Gypsum helps improve structure without
raising pH.

### Clay Soils

Heavy clay benefits from calcium in lime and gypsum, which causes clay
particles to aggregate (flocculate), improving drainage and root
penetration. Digging in compost or manure along with lime is especially
important for clay.

## Lime in the COF Recipe

Solomon's COF recipe includes lime as a standard ingredient:

- 1 part ordinary agricultural lime (best finely ground)
- Or 1 part dolomite lime (if soil needs magnesium)
- 1/4 part gypsum (if you do not use gypsum, double the quantity of
  agricultural lime)

The COF recipe also calls for phosphorus sources that are rich in trace
elements: rock phosphate (hard or soft), bonemeal, or high-phosphate
guano. These provide calcium along with phosphorus and a broad spectrum of
micronutrients.

## Trace Elements and Micronutrients

Solomon emphasizes that the complete organic fertilizer provides calcium,
magnesium, and trace nutrients in balanced proportions. The kelp meal in
COF is especially important as a source of trace minerals. The nutritional
quality of vegetables depends directly on the mineral content of the soil
they grow in. See
[[solomon-vegetable-nutritional-decline-trace-minerals]].

## Blossom-End Rot

A common calcium-deficiency symptom in tomatoes, peppers, and eggplant is
blossom-end rot. If your subsoil offers sufficient calcium, a few years of
light applications of lime will provide enough. Solomon notes that blossom
end rot often fades away as overall soil health improves through his
fertilizer program.
