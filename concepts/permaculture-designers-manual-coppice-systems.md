## Principles of Coppicing

### How Coppice Works
A forest managed for coppice will yield first coppice (small-diameter regrowth from cut stumps), then pole timbers, and eventually honey, fruit, nuts, bark, and plank timber as the forest matures. Coppice relies on the ability of many tree species to regrow from the stump after cutting, producing multiple straight stems suitable for a variety of uses.

### Rotation Periods
Firewood production typically operates on a 2-7 year rotation, where one-half to one-seventh of the plants are cut annually. Depending on the culture, firewood can be cut as coppice or stickwood, or grown to 4-10 cm log size. In most cases, firewood species are chosen for persistent coppice (regrowth from stump) and good fuel value (calories per tonne).

### Species Selection
Tagasaste, Acacia species, Casuarina, and eucalypt species are examples of good fuelwoods for coppice systems, but almost any vigorous tree can be used. Species are chosen based on:
- Persistent regrowth from cut stumps
- Good fuel value (calories per tonne)
- Fast growth rate
- Multiple useful products
- Adaptation to local site conditions

## Coppice for Legume Mulch Systems

### Avenue Cropping
Avenue cropping with regularly coppiced legume trees is a key strategy for tropical and subtropical systems. The essentials are:
- Correct spacing to shade the ground fully but allow cropping between the tree legumes
- Lopping to leave stems 0.5-1.0 m above ground (suitable species include tagasaste or Leucaena)
- Sowing of crop just before rain or irrigation, to beat the growth of weeds
- Possible two-crop sequence, with mulch also returned from crop wastes

This system is used in Africa with Gliricidia, and in New Zealand with tagasaste as the green crop. A parallel system is successful in establishing small trees that otherwise perish from frost or open grass competition.

### Problems with Sustained Coppicing
At least two problems arise in sustained coppicing of legume trees:
- Coppice should be confined to warm wet periods, allowing mature leaf to carry over into dry, cool, or frosty periods
- Constant coppice weakens trees over 5-8 years, and replanting is necessary

Very few legume trees will sustain constant coppicing, and other strategies are called for:
- Perennial thin-crowned leguminous trees can be spaced throughout the orchard and garden
- Fast-growing and short-lived legumes can be allowed to grow and die, or ring-barked or felled on a 2-5 year cycle
- A carefully-planned system of mixed non-coppiced tall legumes (Erythrina, Tipuana tipu) providing seasonal leaf drop, combined with non-legumes for ground mulch

### Non-Legume Mulch Under Legume Cover
With permanent high legume cover, mulch can be obtained all year from non-legume hedge and understory species:
- Nicotiana, Echium fastuosum, Lantana
- Cinnamon, clumps of daisies, wild ginger
- Lemongrass, Vetiver grass, Pennisetum
- Crop wastes from maize, Sesbania, soft ground legumes, or comfrey

This reduces or eliminates the need to coppice susceptible legume species themselves.

## Management Considerations

### Spacing and Trimming
With shrubs and small trees, 0.5 m apart is best for foliage production, with a trimming height of 0.5-1.5 m recommended. For shelter purposes, trees can be more widely spaced for root nitrogen, seed production for poultry and bees, and for in-crop shelter. Flowering and seed production is better at 2-20 m spacing depending on tree size.

### Seasonal Timing
Only in frost-free tropics can trimming occur all year (4-5 cuts). Wherever cold is a seasonal problem, two months of growth must be allowed to harden the plant before winter, or it will weaken as shoots are cold-killed. Trees in drought can be part-trimmed.

### Forage Safety
There is some danger that young coppice shoots will have higher levels of metabolic poisons than 2-3 year old shoots. If stock do not thrive, this factor should be assessed before widespread coppicing for fodder.

### Replacement
Although many trees will coppice for 4-30 years, any sign of loss of vigour should indicate the need to replant. Replacement for small shrubs may be necessary every 2-3 years, while some shrubs and ground covers are annuals or become annuals in cold-season areas.

## Products from Coppice Systems

### Fuelwood and Charcoal
Coppice systems provide the primary feedstock for both direct fuelwood use and [[permaculture-designers-manual-timber-construction]]. Special uses of fast polewood from Chinese elm, poplar, or similar species include chipping for wallboard or fuel bricks, crushing for fibre or cellulose production, fermenting for stock feed or alcohol distillation, and chipping for distillation to oils, resins, and chemical products.

### Willow Coppice
Willow coppice is specifically highlighted as a multi-product system providing:
- Medicinals and forage
- Stickwood for radiant (mass) heaters
- Active charcoal filters
- Artist's charcoal
- Basketry material
- Windbreak and shelter functions

Willows are pollarded for propagation cuttings at nurseries, and strict-form willows (e.g., Matsudana) provide minimal space-loss windbreaks for crops.

### Mulch and Soil Building
Coppice systems produce large quantities of leaf and branch material for mulch. In the Seychelles, cinnamon leaves and branches from pollarded stumps are considered an excellent mulch for vegetable crops, and the bark is a valuable spice.

### Nitrogen Yield
To assess total nitrogen yield from coppice legume systems, both soil nitrogen from mycelia (approximately 200 kg/ha/year) and leaf and slash nitrogen per hectare must be considered. In crops such as Tephrosia, yielding 13.5 t/ha in 4 cuts, leaf nitrogen is about 20-30 kg/t, or 1,000-1,500 kg/ha/year, which is several factors higher than root nitrogen yield alone.
