## Marine Fungi and Salt Marsh Decomposition

Marine ecosystems harbor a diverse array of fungal species that should be regarded as an ecological rather than strictly taxonomic group. These fungi have evolved specialized physiological adaptations that allow them to function as critical decomposers in some of the planet's most extreme and productive environments.

### Diversity and Taxonomic Composition

The ascomycete order Halosphaeriales is well represented in marine ecosystems by approximately 43 genera and 133 species. A lower diversity of basidiomycete and mitosporic fungi are found in marine environments, and species distribution is often related to environmental conditions. Jones (1993) reports on fungal communities restricted to tropical and subtropical marine systems that are distinct from cosmopolitan species. Many of these tropical species are associated with mangrove ecosystems.

The majority of marine fungal species are saprotrophs, particularly involved in decomposition of wood and plant litter derived from mangrove swamps. All marine fungi show physiological adaptations that allow survival under the stress of high saline environments, especially in their regulation of osmotic potential. Remarkably, fungi even exist in the hypersaline conditions of the Dead Sea at total salt concentrations of 340 g L⁻¹, where 55 species have been isolated.

### Ecological Strategies of Marine Decomposers

Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer (1979) characterize marine fungi by habitat and function:

- **Arenicolous (sand-inhabiting) fungi**: Mainly decomposers utilizing algae, leaves, and detritus as resources
- **Lichen fungi**: Form associations with algae with ecological functions similar to terrestrial lichens, including soil formation
- **Parasitic fungi**: Range of ascomycete fungi that are true parasites of seaweeds, causing effects from minor discoloration to gall formation that limits photosynthetic function

Newell (1996) identified four basic strategies adopted by marine decomposers (organo-osmotrophs):

1. **Maximized surface area with high substrate affinity**: Allowing enzymes to diffuse into solid particles, particularly seen in prokaryotes
2. **Tunneling and surface erosion**: Penetration of decomposing resources by chains or series of single cells, common in bacteria and labyrinthulids
3. **Ectoplasmic nets and rhizoids**: Adopted by true chytrids, thrasusochytrids, and hyphochytrids
4. **Self-extending tubular reactors**: Networks of chitin-laminarin or cellulose-laminarin tubes signifying mycelial eumycotic fungi and oomycete protoctists

### Wood Decomposition in Marine Ecosystems

Wood represents a major resource for fungi in marine ecosystems. Wooden breakwaters, jetties, and piers provide substrates for saprotrophic fungal communities whose decomposing effects cause significant economic repercussions. Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer (1979) cataloged 107 fungal species isolated from decomposing wood in marine habitats, including 73 Ascomycotina, 2 Basidiomycotina, and 29 mitosporic species.

Although fungi compete with marine borers as wood decomposers, their ability to survive in sediments of low oxygen tension gives them a competitive edge. The low resource quality of wood encourages tight linkages between fungi and fauna for its decomposition. Wood-boring marine mollusks preferentially settle and feed on wood that has been previously colonized and partially decomposed by fungi rather than invading fresh wood. The wood-boring crustacean the gribble ([[limnoria-tripunctata]]) has increased longevity when feeding on fungus-colonized wood and is incapable of reproduction on any substrate unless marine fungi are included in its diet, likely due to the enhanced availability of proteins, essential amino acids, and vitamins provided by fungal colonization.

### Salt Marsh Decomposition Dynamics

In salt marsh ecosystems, decomposition of the salt marsh grass Spartina relies on a community of fungi that decompose leaves separately from those decomposing roots and rhizomes, which constitute more than half of the plant biomass. Live fungal biomass is low (less than 20 mg g⁻¹ substrate), and the fungal strategy is rapid growth within the substrate with nutrient immobilization into fungal biomass. Fungi in this environment can contain 75–100% of the total nitrogen of decaying cordgrass leaves.

Leaves are mainly decomposed by ascomycetes, with Phaeosphaeria spartinicola as the dominant species. Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer (1979) report a range of both ascomycete and mitosporic fungi involved in salt marsh vegetation decomposition, but only a single basidiomycete species, [[nia-vibrissa]].

### Anaerobic Decomposition Capabilities

In salt marsh sediments, aerobic conditions quickly give way to anaerobic conditions with increasing depth. Mansfield and Bärlocher (1993) found that fungal biomass, measured as ergosterol content, was negatively related to redox potential in Spartina salt marshes, showing rapid decline of fungal activity with increasing sediment depth.

However, when balsa wood panels were buried in anaerobic salt marsh conditions, they were colonized by fungi within 12 weeks. Many colonizing species could not grow in entirely anaerobic conditions but were able to grow down from aerobic to anaerobic zones over distances of 5–10 mm in 15 days from resources in the aerobic zone. This evidence demonstrates that fungi can conduct oxygen from aerobic regions through hyphae to advancing mycelial fronts that remain physiologically active in decomposition within anaerobic zones.

### Mangrove Swamp Fungal Communities

Mangrove swamps are the tropical equivalent of salt marsh habitats, occurring in more sheltered areas away from direct wave action. The litter from mangrove ecosystems is more diverse than that of salt marsh systems, and high rates of primary production produce copious detritus supporting large populations and diversity of detritivore fungi, bacteria, and fauna.

Mangrove fungi are almost exclusively saprotrophic, consisting of approximately 23 ascomycete species, 17 mitosporic species, and 2 basidiomycetes. Fungal biomass in decaying mangrove leaves is much lower than in decaying salt marsh vegetation (less than 1 mg g⁻¹ compared to 60–85 mg g⁻¹, respectively), and bacteria represent only a small proportion of decomposers (0.7 mg g⁻¹).

Newell and Fell (1992) showed significant changes in fungal biomass during decomposition of red mangrove leaves. Mangrove leaves rapidly accumulate a large population of oomycete fungi (Halophytophthora spp.), but ascomycete species dominated by [[lulworthia-grandispora]] may comprise 50% of the fungal community. Ergosterol content increases from less than 1 mg g⁻¹ in live or senescent leaves to 19 mg g⁻¹ at 30% mass loss (red-brown stage) and 85 mg g⁻¹ at 40–60% mass loss (black, submerged stage).

### Mangrove Detritus Export and Fungal Dispersal

Export of plant detritus from mangrove ecosystems to oceans is an important contribution to oceanic nutrient loading. Outwellings from mangrove swamps can transfer 60–260 tonnes per year of carbon, exported mainly as dissolved organic carbon (DOC), representing a process in which fungi play a major role.

Fungi are also exported from mangrove ecosystems through viviparous seedlings of [[rhizophora-mangle]]. These seedlings develop on the tree, fall into water, and are carried by currents. The drifting seedlings serve as vehicles for marine fungal dispersal. Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer (1979) documented instances where [[keissleriella-blepharospora]] and Lulworthia spp. were transported from the tropics to the coast of North Carolina by the Gulf Stream.

### Fungal Biomass and Invertebrate Nutrition

Fungal biomass in decomposing material within salt marsh ecosystems is important for sustaining invertebrate herbivore populations. The amphipod Ulorchestia spartinophila has complex dietary requirements and grows best and produces the most offspring when fed on decaying leaves containing high fungal biomass, demonstrating the critical link between fungal decomposition activity and higher trophic levels in marine and estuarine food webs.
