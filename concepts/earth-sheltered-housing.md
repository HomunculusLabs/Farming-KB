## Earth-Sheltered and Underground Housing

Underground and earth-sheltered housing represents one of the most effective and time-tested approaches to creating comfortable dwellings in desert and arid environments. From the ancient cave complexes of Cappadocia to the modern opal-mining dugouts of Coober Pedy, Australia, underground living has been independently developed across cultures in Asia, India, the Middle East, the Mediterranean, and the Americas.

## Advantages of Underground Housing

Underground dwellings offer several compelling benefits in arid climates:

- **Temperature stability**: Internal temperatures fluctuate only about 5°C annually, remaining fairly constant at approximately 25°C in central Australian deserts
- **Low maintenance**: Cool, clean, and requiring minimal upkeep
- **Scalability**: Easy casual extension without major structural work
- **Aesthetic potential**: Excellent sculptural qualities with domed ceilings and natural stone walls
- **Cost efficiency**: Even when sealed and tiled, underground houses cost about 50% of equivalent surface dwellings (based on 1985 figures)
- **Security**: Protection from extreme weather, flooding, sun, and pests

## Construction Methods

### Modern Mechanical Construction (Coober Pedy Model)

Australia"s opal mining industry has made large-bore drilling equipment readily available for cave construction:

1. **Site preparation**: A bulldozer cuts a cliff face 3–6 m high into a selected hillside of soft stone
2. **Drilling**: Large-bore drills (1–1.5 m diameter, such as the "Caldwell") cut corridors, rooms, and storage caves to approximate size, plus deep wells for toilets and water cisterns
3. **Ventilation**: Smaller diameter drills cut vents, light shafts, and skylights at strategic positions
4. **Finishing**: Rooms are hand-trimmed smooth with axes, floors sealed with level concrete, and electrical conduits and plumbing laid before pouring floors
5. **Spoil removal**: Large blowers (vacuum pumps) remove spoil as drills cut shafts
6. **Sealing**: After drying for several months, Bondcrete® or polyvinylacetate sealer is sprayed on stone to prevent dust

### Traditional Hand Construction

Where machinery is unavailable, the wheelbarrow, pick, and shovel suffice. Site selection is enhanced when the proposed dugout is in soft rock capped with [[compost]], down-draught, and solar chimneys; last for years but should be occasionally pumped out to establish trees around the site
- **Soakage beds**: Where water is sufficient, low-flush toilets lead to large shallow leach-fields with thick top mulch, where large trees rapidly transpire water and convert nutrients

### Water Cisterns

- Cisterns can be opened from the upper surface and deliver water at head to the lower cave
- Rendered for waterproofing
- Sections of hill slope above the cave can be sealed with concrete as a water runoff area, simultaneously preventing seepage into the cave and strengthening the strata above rooms

## Earth-Sheltered Surface Housing

Where soils or stone will not support caves, surface housing with earth banks built up to the eaves (and optionally over the roof) effectively duplicates cave conditions without the risk of soil collapse or water seepage. Where stable clay-fraction soils are available, a bulldozer can quickly consolidate a "turkey"s nest" dam above ground; when formed and roofed, it becomes a cool desert house with variety of interior wall treatments and safety from severe surface flooding.

### Intermediate Forms

The compact, notch-sheltered pueblo housing of the Great Basin desert (USA) and analogous sites in China and North Africa represent intermediates between excavated and surface housing. Large rock-shelters at the base of scarps or cliffs are "built in" with mud brick, providing cool and secure dwellings safe from flooding, rain, and sun. Even when abandoned, these pueblos last for hundreds of years in good condition.

## Notable Examples

- **Coober Pedy, South Australia**: Entire town built underground in opal-mining dugouts, with decorative facades, modern amenities, and stable 25°C temperatures year-round
- **Forestieri House, Fresno, California**: Underground house covering approximately 2.8 ha with bathrooms, fishponds, and a large citrus garden—only the treetops visible on the surface, calcrete forming the ceiling
- **Canary Islands**: Houses where only the facade stands out, the rest hidden underground, with facades shaded by grape trellis
- **North Africa**: Large courtyard pits (20 × 20 m) cut into the ground with caves built off them, featuring trellised walls and top openings
