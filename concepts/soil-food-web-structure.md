# Soil Food Web Structure

## Overview

The soil food web is the complex community of organisms living in soil, from microscopic bacteria and fungi to visible earthworms and beetles. Unlike a simple food chain, it is a web of interconnected relationships where organisms at any level may feed on or be fed upon by organisms at multiple other levels. Plants are at the center, initiating and controlling the system through [[root-exudates]].

## The Web Members

### Bacteria (Foundation Level)

Bacteria are the smallest and most numerous organisms in soil -- 100 million to 1 billion in a single teaspoon. They are primary decomposers that break down simple organic matter (sugars, simple carbohydrates) and retain nutrients in their bodies. They produce slime that binds soil particles together into microaggregates, forming the foundation of soil structure. A good garden soil may contain 20,000 to 30,000 different species of bacteria.

### Fungi (Foundation Level)

Fungi are the second primary decomposer group. Fungal hyphae (thread-like structures) travel through soil particles, binding them together into macroaggregates. Fungi specialize in breaking down more complex organic materials like lignin and cellulose that bacteria cannot digest. Fungal hyphae can extend hundreds of yards in a teaspoon of forest soil.

### Protozoa (First Predator Level)

Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic organisms that eat bacteria (and occasionally fungi). They come in three types: flagellates (smallest), ciliates (medium), and amoebae (largest). A single protozoan can eat 10,000 bacteria per day. When they consume bacteria and fungi, they release the nutrients held within those organisms back into the soil in plant-available form (mineralization). See [[protozoa-in-soil]].

### Nematodes (Second Predator Level)

Nematodes are nonsegmented roundworms that are the second most dominant form of animal life after [[soil-arthropods|arthropods]]. A teaspoon of good soil averages about 40-50 nematodes. They are classified by feeding habit: bacterial-feeders, fungal-feeders, predators, omnivores, and root-feeders. The bacterial and fungal feeders are crucial mineralizers. See [[nematodes-in-soil-ecology]].

### Microarthropods (Third Predator Level)

Microarthropods are tiny animals with segmented bodies and jointed appendages. They include mites, springtails, and small insects. They feed on fungi, nematodes, protozoa, and each other. Their movement through soil creates passages for air and water.

### Earthworms and Larger Organisms (Top Level)

Worms, insect larvae, moles, and burrowing animals move through soil creating pathways for air and water exchange. They shred organic matter, opening it up for bacterial and fungal decay. Earthworms concentrate nutrients and create humus.

## How the Web Functions

### Nutrient Cycling (Immobilization and Mineralization)

1. Plants produce [[root-exudates]] (sugars, proteins) that attract bacteria and fungi to the rhizosphere
2. Bacteria and fungi consume these exudates and retain nutrients in their bodies (immobilization)
3. Protozoa and nematodes eat the bacteria and fungi
4. These predators digest what they need and excrete the excess nutrients (especially nitrogen as ammonium) as waste -- right in the rhizosphere where plant roots can absorb them (mineralization)

As much as 80% of the nitrogen a plant needs comes from the wastes produced by bacteria- and fungi-eating protozoa.

### Soil Structure Formation

- Bacterial slime binds soil particles into microaggregates
- Fungal hyphae bind microaggregates into macroaggregates
- Worms and arthropods create pores and tunnels for air and water movement

### Disease Suppression

A healthy, diverse food web controls pathogens through competition for food resources, space, and infection sites on plant surfaces. A diverse community of 20,000+ bacterial species in a teaspoon of soil keeps pathogens in check the way a crowded market deters thieves.

## Why a Web, Not a Chain

Every member of the soil food web has its place. Each plays a specific role. Elimination of even one group can drastically alter the soil community. Birds spread protozoa on their feet; mammals provide dung for beetles. A healthy soil food web won't allow one set of members to get so strong as to destroy the web.

## Key Points

- Plants control the food web through root exudates
- Nutrients are retained in microbial biomass and released through predation
- Soil structure is built by the combined activities of all web members
- Disease is suppressed through competition and predation
- Chemical fertilizers bypass the web, leaching through soil; organic inputs feed the web
- Compaction, tilling, and chemicals damage the web; compost, mulch, and [[compost-tea-recipe-guide|compost tea]] restore it
