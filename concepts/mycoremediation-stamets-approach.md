## Contaminant Targets

Mushroom mycelia can break down a surprisingly wide range of toxins that have chemical bonds similar to those in wood:

- **PAHs (polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons)**: in Battelle tests, oyster mushroom mycelium degraded 97% of the PAHs from diesel-contaminated soil after 8 weeks
- **Pentachlorophenol (PCP)**: a common wood preservative, degraded by acclimated oyster mycelium
- **BTEX compounds**: benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene from petroleum contamination
- **PCBs (polychlorinated biphenyls)**: found widely in industrial pollution; targeted by Phanerochaete chrysosporium and other white rot species
- **Dioxins**: among the most recalcitrant toxins, attacked by the suite of lignin-degrading enzymes
- **Chemical warfare agents**: DMMP and surrogates for VX and sarin nerve agents

## Spent Mushroom Compost for Toxin Reduction

Field trials with spent oyster mushroom compost demonstrated significant toxin reduction (Eggen and Sasek, 2002):

- Anthracene: 87% reduced to 50% after second treatment at 15 weeks
- Fluorene: 99% reduced to 87% after second treatment at 15 weeks

A second treatment of spent oyster mushroom substrate was added 15 weeks after the initial inoculation. Stamets theorized that this second inoculation would have been more effective had researchers added unsterilized wood chips and covered the mycelia with cardboard and straw to protect and feed them. Introduced mycelium can become a launching platform for bacteria, and oftentimes toxins stall the biology until the fungal community is well established.

## Heavy Metal Hyperaccumulation

Some mushroom species concentrate heavy metals to extraordinary degrees relative to their substrate. Key hyperaccumulators include:

- **Paxillus atrotomentosus**: 1180x concentration
- **Macrolepiota procera**: 230x
- **Morchella species**: 70-100x
- **Pleurotus ostreatus**: 65-140x

Mercury remediation uses organomercury lyases that break down organomercury compounds, with selenium bonding playing a key role. Approximately two dozen known hyperaccumulator species have been identified.

## Plurality of Species Principle

Each mushroom species secretes a unique suite of extracellular enzymes and acids. Using multiple species in combination creates a synergistic effect for more complete degradation of complex contaminants than any single species could achieve alone. This is analogous to how bacteria feast on newly available nutrients after fungi break down large molecules -- the tandem approach of using both fungi and bacteria outperforms either alone. Nature loves communities; when one species is suddenly introduced, the population dynamics shift in response. The art of mycoremediation lies in selecting the right species combination and timing their introduction, appealing to or redirecting the appetite of [[mycorestoration-ecology]], and many real-world applications combine both functions.
