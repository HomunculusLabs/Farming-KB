# Community Asset Categories

## Overview

In *[[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual*, [[bill-mollison]] presents a framework for understanding
[[informal-and-formal-community-economics]] through five distinct categories of assets. This classification provides
communities with a diagnostic tool for evaluating where their wealth is directed and whether
their expenditures build long-term resilience or erode their resource base.

The central insight is that a healthy community economy must balance all five asset categories,
with deliberate emphasis on those that conserve and create lasting wealth. This framework
connects ecological thinking directly to economic planning, treating financial flows with the
same attention a permaculture designer gives to water [[ozier-pdc-composting-and-nutrient-cycles]] in the landscape.

## The Five Asset Categories

### 1. Degenerative Assets

Degenerative assets inevitably decay, rust, wear out, or depreciate over time. They include
buildings, roads, bridges, vehicles, furnishings, appliances, and most manufactured goods.
Every community requires some degenerative assets, but an economy over-invested in this
category impoverishes itself long-term.

These assets demand continuous inputs of energy, labor, and materials simply to maintain
their function. Each cycle of maintenance or replacement draws down resources without
generating new wealth. Mollison warns that societies over-invested in degenerative assets
become trapped in consumption cycles that drain community wealth and create dependence on
external supply chains for replacement materials and energy.

### 2. Generative Assets

Generative assets are the tools and systems that manufacture or process raw materials into
useful products: huskers, grinders, lathes, furnaces, mills, looms, presses, and other
productive equipment. A defining characteristic is their capacity for mutual repair — a
community with lathes, forges, and skilled operators can maintain its own productive capacity
indefinitely as tools repair tools within workshop systems.

However, generative assets alone cannot sustain a community. Without raw materials from
procreative assets and knowledge from informational assets, productive tools sit idle or
consume imported resources, creating dependence on distant supply chains. They are necessary
but not sufficient for community self-reliance.

### 3. Procreative Assets

Procreative assets are living systems that grow, reproduce, and yield ongoing returns: trees,
forests, wildlife, fish, invertebrates, mammals, and domestic livestock. These are the only
assets that literally create wealth from sunlight, soil, water, and atmospheric nutrients
through biological processes.

Maximizing procreative assets provides raw materials — timber, fiber, food, fertility — that
feed generative tools and justify modest investments in degenerative infrastructure. A community
rich in procreative assets possesses orchards, grazing systems, aquaculture, timber stands, and
diverse wildlife populations forming a self-renewing economic foundation. No amount of machinery
can replace the wealth-creating capacity of healthy living systems.

### 4. Informational Assets

Informational assets encompass education, data, applied intelligence, practical skills,
traditional knowledge, books, and databases. Mollison observes that seeds carry extraordinarily
high information content, each encoding genetic knowledge of generations of environmental
adaptation. Similarly, skilled practitioners and community memory represent accumulated
intelligence that optimizes the use of every other asset category.

A community with strong informational assets knows how to manage forests sustainably, repair
tools, design efficient buildings, and adapt to changing conditions. Information multiplies
the value of all other investments — without it, even abundant physical resources are wasted.

### 5. Conservative Assets

Conservative assets guard, store, and recycle resources for future use: insulation, dams,
water catchments, seed banks, cold storage, grain elevators, money recycling systems, and
strategic forest reserves. They do not produce directly but prevent waste and ensure surpluses
are available during lean times.

Conservative assets represent a community's commitment to intergenerational stewardship. Without them, even abundant resources are squandered through leakage and short-term thinking.

## The Wealth Conservation Principle

The critical lesson is directional: **expenditure on categories 3, 4, and 5 (Procreative,
Informational, and Conservative) conserves and creates wealth over time.** These investments
compound as living systems mature, knowledge accumulates, and stored resources appreciate.

**Over-investment in categories 1 and 2 alone consumes wealth.**
Societies directing resources primarily toward physical infrastructure will pollute and
extinguish their resource base — extracting faster than they regenerate, importing more
than they produce, accumulating liabilities faster than productive assets.

## Money as Transport and Shaper of Trade

Mollison extends this analysis to the nature of money itself, drawing an analogy to water in
the [[holzer-permaculture-landscape-design]]. Money functions as the transport and shaper of trade — it carries
value from where it is generated to where it is spent, and its flow patterns determine which
activities are nourished and which wither.

Just as [[bill-mollison]] shape water flows through swales, dams, and keyline systems
to maximize retention, communities must shape monetary flows to prevent leakage and ensure
wealth circulates locally. When money flows outward — paying distant corporations, importing
locally-producible goods, or servicing external debt — it drains the community's economic
lifeblood. Ethical financial systems redirect flows inward, creating virtuous cycles of
local production, employment, and reinvestment.

## Financial Institutions and Community Governance

Mollison argues that financial institutions should belong to and be governed by the communities
they serve. This is an ecological necessity: money shapes land use, production decisions, and
[[mycelial-foraging-resource-allocation]]. When financial power is concentrated in distant institutions with no local
stake, money flows are shaped by abstract profit rather than community wellbeing.

This requires **ethical charters** that bind banks and credit systems to community welfare:

- Restrictions on speculative investment detached from productive activity
- Requirements for local lending supporting procreative and conservative assets
- Mandates accounting for ecological and social outcomes
- Limits on interest extraction that drains community capital

Community-owned banks, credit unions, and cooperative financial structures keep decision-making
local and align capital flows with community needs.

## Preventing Wealth Leakage

Wealth leakage occurs when community resources flow outward without equivalent return. Common
pathways include external ownership extracting profits, imported goods replacing local
production, financial systems channeling savings to distant centers, and degraded ecosystems
no longer producing locally.

The asset framework provides a diagnostic: if spending flows primarily to degenerative assets
produced elsewhere, wealth is leaking. Redirecting expenditure toward local procreative,
informational, and conservative assets plugs these leaks and builds self-reinforcing economies
where money circulates multiple times before leaving.

## Summary

| Category | Direction | Effect on Community Wealth |
|----------|-----------|---------------------------|
| Degenerative | Consumptive | Decreases — demands continuous inputs |
| Generative | Productive | Neutral without procreative base |
| Procreative | Creative | Increases — foundation of all wealth |
| Informational | Multiplier | Amplifies every other category |
| Conservative | Protective | Preserves wealth across generations |

Community economics must be designed with the same intentional care applied to ecological
systems. By classifying assets by their long-term effects and directing investment toward
those that create, inform, and conserve, communities can build economies that sustain
themselves and future generations indefinitely.
