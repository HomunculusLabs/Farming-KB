# Fungal Genetic Diversity Assessment
Molecular approaches that allow investigators to assess fungal
biodiversity directly are being developed and refined rapidly.
These approaches are essential for determining the species
diversity of fungi in ecosystems, which historically has been
complicated by our inability to culture many fungi, the ephemeral
nature of sporocarp production, and intraspecific variation in
fungal morphology and biochemistry.
## Impediments to Understanding Fungal Diversity
Several major challenges confront the study of fungal biology
and diversity. Developmental stages of many fungi occur in
complex substrata such as soil, wood, plants, or animals, making
them difficult to monitor. Lifestyle habits are poorly defined.
Morphological traits are limited in number, impeding taxonomic
identification. Many fungi are difficult to culture. The
morphological and biochemical variation of fungi in natural
environments is undefined. Physical and chemical requirements
for sporocarp development are poorly understood. Genetic and
biochemical interactions between fungi in situ are poorly
defined.
## Nuclear Genome Analysis
The nuclear genomes of fungi contain the genetic information
that defines species and controls the expression of developmental
cycles. Nuclear genomes are very large (1.5 x 10 to the 7th to
820 x 10 to the 7th base pairs), represent thousands of genetic
loci, and contain both single-copy and repetitive DNA. Nuclear
DNA is therefore excellent for measuring genetic diversity and
deriving molecular fingerprints for taxonomic purposes.
The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has so increased the
sensitivity and resolution of genetic analyses that genetic
diversity may be assessed using very small amounts of starting
material. Samples can be collected from individuals in situ
without impacting community structure.
## Arbitrarily Primed PCR (apPCR)
apPCR analysis (also known as RAPD) can discriminate among
fungal taxa with few distinguishing morphological characters.
Short oligonucleotide primers (10 to 20 base pairs) anneal to
complementary DNA sequences to produce double-stranded DNA
products. The number and size of amplified products depend on
primer hybridization site frequency and distribution.
In fungi, 80 to 100 percent of DNA products generated from
apPCR amplification are shared among individuals of the same
species for any given primer 15 to 16 base pairs long and
composed of simple sequence repeats. Individuals from different
species share only 0 to 20 percent of DNA products. This has led
to species-specific apPCR band patterns for unequivocal
taxonomic identification.
A major source of nonreproducibility in apPCR is the inaccuracy
of thermocyclers. Some machines never come within 10 degrees C
of the programmed denaturing temperatures, causing incomplete
DNA denaturation and unreliable primer annealing.
## Genetic Diversity Assessment
The intraspecific variation in apPCR products allows investigators
to assess genetic diversity among individuals within and between
populations. As the number of primers increases, the probability
of generating polymorphic genetic markers also increases. At least
15 to 20 polymorphic markers are required for accurate measures
of genetic diversity.
The sensitivity of apPCR is such that small amounts of fungal
material (0.5 cubic cm) are sufficient for hundreds to thousands
of genetic analyses. This allows analysis of population structure
without disturbing sporocarps or underground mycelia.
Studies of [[biodiversity-basidiomycete-diversity-taxonomy]]
populations have shown that clusters of sporocarps occurring in
close proximity often represent genetically distinct populations
composed of closely related but nonclonal individuals. Many
adjacent sporocarps are nonclonal, indicating high within-population
genetic diversity.
## Species Monitoring
Species-specific band patterns from apPCR can be verified by DNA
hybridization. Primers generated from species-specific sequences
enable dual-primer PCR (dpPCR) specifically to amplify target
bands. The dpPCR process can detect the DNA of a target species
even at ratios of 1:100 when mixed with other species. As little
as 25 picograms of DNA can yield a product.
Nested-primer PCR (npPCR) provides additional sensitivity for
detecting species in complex substrata. These techniques allow
in situ monitoring of temporal and spatial occurrence of
individual species, which is essential for understanding fungal
population dynamics in ecosystems.
## Ribosomal RNA/DNA Comparisons
Ribosomal RNA/DNA sequence comparisons are particularly valuable
for assessing yeast diversity
and for phylogenetic studies. Isolation and characterization of
rRNAs and rDNAs allow estimates of relatedness between taxa.
Reliability of phylogenies inferred from rRNA gene trees depends
on appropriate methods of analysis.
## Mitochondrial Genome Analysis
Mitochondrial genomes are relatively small (20 to 80 kb), are
transmitted maternally, and function similarly in all fungi.
Mitochondrial DNA represents a single genetic locus, although
low levels of recombination have been observed in some systems.
Analysis of mtDNA is particularly useful for determining
phylogenetic differences between closely related fungal species.
## Applications to Biodiversity Studies
These molecular techniques have been applied to assess genetic
structure and diversity of both macrofungal and microfungal
populations. They provide critical tools for:
- Discriminating morphologically indistinct taxa
- Determining relatedness between taxa
- Quantifying genetic diversity in natural populations
- Monitoring species in complex substrata
- Assessing functional roles of fungi in ecosystems
