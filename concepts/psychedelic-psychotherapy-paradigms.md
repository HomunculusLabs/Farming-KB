# Psychedelic Psychotherapy Paradigms

> From Ralph Metzner, "Hallucinogenic Drugs and Plants in Psychotherapy and Shamanism" (1998)

---

## Historical Context: LSD Discovery

LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide) was first synthesized at Sandoz Laboratories in Switzerland during World War II.

Albert Hofmann discovered its psychoactive properties accidentally in 1943, recognizing its effects through their similarity to his own childhood mystical experiences.

Initially, researchers framed LSD as "psychotomimetic" — a substance that mimicked psychosis. Later it was reclassified as "psycholytic," meaning "psychic loosening," reflecting a more nuanced understanding of its capacity to open psychological material rather than merely distort perception.

Military and intelligence agencies became interested in LSD as a potential tool for mind control and espionage during the Cold War.

However, LSD's inherent tendency to produce mystical, expansive states of consciousness made it poorly suited as a weapon, ultimately undermining these applications.

## Two Paradigms of Psychedelic Psychotherapy

### Psycholytic Therapy (European Model)

The psycholytic approach emerged in Europe during the 1950s and 1960s. Its name derives from the German *seelische Auflockerung*, meaning "psychic loosening" or "psychic opening."

In this model, neurotic patients received LSD in a series of sessions at gradually increasing doses. The therapeutic framework was rooted in Freudian psychoanalysis, with the drug used to facilitate analytic interactions between patient and therapist.

The core rationale was straightforward: by loosening psychological defenses, patients could become directly aware of unconscious emotional dynamics that had previously been inaccessible through ordinary talk therapy alone.

### Psychedelic Therapy (Anglo-American Model)

The psychedelic therapy model, developed primarily in Anglo-American contexts, employed one or a small number of high-dose sessions. These produced vivid, often overwhelming hallucinatory imagery leading to profound insight and personal transformation.

Humphrey Osmond coined the term "psychedelic," meaning "mind-manifesting," to describe this approach.

It was used notably for treating alcoholism, theorizing that a single overwhelming experience could simulate a "bottoming out" moment — catalyzing a dramatic shift in self-understanding and behavior.

## Grof's Perinatal Matrices

Working within the psycholytic tradition, Stanislav Grof made a landmark discovery. As patients moved through successive low-dose sessions, he observed that deeper layers of the unconscious opened beyond Freudian dynamics — revealing birth and pre-birth memories previously unknown to clinical psychology.

Grof identified four "perinatal matrices" — fundamental experiential patterns related to the biological process of birth. Each matrix corresponds to a distinct stage of the birth process and carries its own characteristic emotional and somatic qualities.

These matrices formed the foundation for Grof's later development of holotropic breathwork and his broader theoretical framework of transpersonal psychology.

## Leary, Harvard, and "Set and Setting"

Timothy Leary's arrival at Harvard in the early 1960s marked a pivotal shift in psychedelic research.

Leary introduced the concept of "consciousness expansion" — positioning psychedelics not as treatments for pathology but as tools for exploring the full potential of human awareness.

Leary and his colleagues founded the *Psychedelic Review*, a journal dedicated to publishing research and philosophical reflections on the therapeutic and consciousness-expanding potential of these substances.

His most enduring contribution was the "set and setting" hypothesis. This framework holds that the primary determinants of a psychedelic experience are:

- **Set**: the internal state of the individual — including intention, expectation, personality structure, and current emotional condition.

- **Setting**: the external context — the physical environment, the presence and demeanor of guides or therapists, and broader social and cultural expectations.

This insight fundamentally reframed psychedelic therapy, shifting attention from pharmacology alone to the critical importance of context, preparation, and interpersonal relationship.

## Five Shared Principles

Despite their differences, both major paradigms share five foundational principles of psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy:

1. **Profoundly expanded consciousness**: Psychedelics produce states of awareness that are qualitatively different from ordinary waking consciousness, granting access to material usually beyond reach.

2. **Set and setting are paramount**: The therapeutic outcome depends far more on psychological preparation, intention, and environmental context than on the specific substance or dose administered.

3. **Amplifier analogy and microscope metaphor**: Psychedelics function as amplifiers of mental contents — intensifying whatever thoughts, emotions, or perceptions are already present in the mind. They also act as a microscope for the psyche, revealing fine-grained structures of experience that normally remain below the threshold of awareness.

4. **Therapist's personal experience is essential**: Clinicians who have undergone their own psychedelic experiences are better equipped to guide others through these states with empathy, understanding, and appropriate grounding.

5. **Access to transcendent and transpersonal dimensions**: Beyond personal psychological material, psychedelic states frequently open into experiences of unity, archetype, cosmic consciousness, and connection with dimensions that transcend the individual self.

## The Transpersonal Dimension

Grof's later work expanded far beyond the birth matrices into what he called the "transpersonal dimension" of human experience.

He described "holotropic states" — states oriented toward wholeness — in which consciousness moves beyond the boundaries of the personal biography.

In holotropic states, individuals may encounter ancestral memories, past-life experiences, archetypal visions, and experiences of cosmic unity.

These phenomena resist explanation within conventional psychological frameworks and point toward a fundamentally expanded understanding of the psyche.

---

## Empathogens: MDMA and Shulgin's Discoveries

A significant development beyond the classical psychedelics was the identification of the "empathogen" class of substances.

The chemist Alexander Shulgin synthesized and bioassayed hundreds of novel psychoactive compounds, including MDMA, MDA, and 2C-B.

Empathogens expand heart-level awareness and interpersonal empathy with minimal perceptual or hallucinatory effects.

MDMA, in particular, was used successfully in psychotherapy during the 1970s and 1980s to facilitate relationship communication, resolve interpersonal conflict, and support trauma healing by reducing fear and defensiveness while enhancing trust and openness.

---

## Summary

The history of psychedelic psychotherapy reveals two distinct yet complementary clinical paradigms — the European psycholytic approach and the Anglo-American psychedelic model — each producing profound therapeutic insights.

Grof's discovery of perinatal matrices and holotropic states expanded the map of the psyche far beyond Freudian bounds.

Leary's set and setting framework remains the cornerstone of all contemporary psychedelic-assisted therapy.

Together, these traditions laid the groundwork for the current renaissance in psychedelic research and clinical application.
