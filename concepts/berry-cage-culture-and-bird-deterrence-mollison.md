# Berry Cage Culture and Bird Deterrence

Mollison presents two main approaches to commercial and homestead berry
production that address the critical problem of bird predation: intensive
cage culture and field-based systems with hawk kite deterrence. These
strategies are essential for anyone serious about berry production in cool
humid climates, where fruit-eating birds can devastate unprotected crops.

## The Bird Problem

Production of blueberries, strawberries, and raspberries attracts
fruit-eating species such as blackbirds, thrushes, starlings, and parrots.
Without protection, crop losses can be near-total at peak ripeness. The
challenge is to protect fruit without excluding pollinators or creating
management difficulties.

## Cage Culture

Cage culture is the most effective approach to bird protection for
high-value berry production. Growers of sweet cherries, blueberry, and
table-quality raspberry all report economic advantage in completely caging
over the area of crop. Such an advantage gives yields of up to 30 times that
of open field conditions.

### Cage Specifications

Typical cages are about 20 m by 10 m by 2.4 m high, using 10 mm mesh.
This mesh size is small enough to exclude all fruit-eating birds while
allowing pollinators (bees, hoverflies) free access. Within these cages,
a polyculture of small fruit plus espalier fruits (nectarine, peach) is
safe from birds and even large moth species.

### Internal Design

One or two such cages of piping plus mesh will support a grower aiming for
high quality markets. Ground preparation may involve:

- Ridging for species such as blueberry (ridges of 1.5 m wide, 0.6 m high,
  with a drainpipe at base is ideal)
- Fitting of drip irrigation
- A deep mulch for pH adjustment and soil moisture conservation
- A careful schedule of liquid manures selected for crop

### Biological Pest Control in Cages

It is beneficial in cage culture to use species such as tree frogs, small
lizards, and insectivorous birds (such as quail) in the cages for pest
control, and to select plants of high fruit yield. Given such care, a family
livelihood can be obtained from a single cage structure.

Cage walls may support trellis of bramble fruits (youngberry, boysenberry),
effectively using the vertical surfaces of the cage as growing space.
Maintenance is mainly that of pruning and adding mulch as needed to maintain
soil health.

## Hawk Kite Deterrence

For field-grown berries where full cage enclosures are not economically
viable, tethered predator hawk kites above the crop at critical ripening
periods provide effective bird deterrence. Such kites imitate local hawks or
eagles and are very effective bird repellents.

Key management notes for hawk kites:

- Kites should be realistic in silhouette and mounted on flexible poles that
  allow them to move naturally in the wind
- They must be removed after the crop is picked so that birds do not get
  accustomed to them (habituation is the primary failure mode)
- Commercially available hawk kites (especially in Australia) have been
  validated through extensive field use

## Field Culture and Self-Pick Systems

In field conditions, losses to birds are unavoidable but may be minimised
using the hawk kite approach. As much of the berry crop cost is in picking,
it is preferable to plant for self-pick sales where farms are within 15-20
km of town markets.

For self-pick operations, berries (currant, gooseberry, raspberry,
strawberry) are best planted in double rows on contour mounds 1-1.5 m high,
with grassed paths and a wider spacing of mounds or ridges than in cage
culture, so that pickers have easy access to crop. Self-pick farms need
parking and weigh-out centres, buckets for pickers, and produce tubs for
crop.

## Berry Ecology: Natural Succession Patterns

Berries demand little but a humus-rich and somewhat acid soil, high ammonia
nitrogen (provided by birds and rodents in the wild as urea), and a thick
mulch to discourage grasses. Berries pioneer for, and protect, seedling
trees, so that advancing forest edges often develop bramble and cane
thickets. In these thickets oak, chestnut, plum, apple, and birch thrive.

Brambles, in particular, protect and nourish young fruit trees, and on farms
bramble clumps (blackberry or one of its related cultivars) can be used to
exclude deer and cattle from newly set trees. As the trees (apple, quince,
plum, citrus, fig) age, and the brambles are shaded out, hoofed animals come
to eat fallen fruit, and the mature trees (7 plus years old) are sufficiently
hardy to withstand browsing.

This natural succession pattern — bramble nursery to fruit tree orchard —
may represent how our forest ancestors developed orchards, assisted by
indigenous birds and mammals.
