## Why Sawdust Spawn for Wood Decomposers

Wood-decomposing mushroom species have evolved enzyme systems optimized for breaking down lignin and cellulose — the primary components of wood. While these species can colonize grain spawn, they transition more efficiently onto woody substrates when started on sawdust-based spawn. The mycelium is already adapted to the chemical environment of wood and can begin aggressive colonization immediately upon inoculation.

Sawdust spawn also has practical advantages for [[sterilization-techniques-mushroom-cultivation]] in the form of resistant spores.

The sawdust should be of medium to fine texture — coarse chips do not pack well and leave air gaps that slow colonization, while extremely fine dust compacts too tightly and restricts gas exchange.

## Perlite Spawn Formulation

An alternative to the sawdust/bran combination is perlite spawn, which uses expanded perlite as the structural base.

The perlite spawn formulation is: 40 grams perlite, 50 grams wheat bran, 1.5 grams calcium carbonate, and 160 ml water. Perlite is vitreous rock heated to 1000°F and exploded like popcorn. The thin flakes of bran readily coat the lightweight perlite particles, creating a well-aerated, moisture-retaining medium.

Perlite spawn has excellent aeration properties due to the porous nature of the perlite. It colonizes quickly and is easy to shake and distribute. However, it provides less nutritional buffering than sawdust-based spawn and may not sustain mycelial vigor as long during storage.

## Sterilization Requirements

Sawdust/bran spawn must be thoroughly sterilized to eliminate competitor organisms. The standard protocol is autoclaving at 15 PSI (121°C / 250°F) for 90-120 minutes. The longer time (compared to the 60-90 minutes typical for grain spawn) is necessary because sawdust is denser and more difficult to penetrate than grain.

For bags, autoclaving at 15 PSI for 2 hours is standard. Some cultivators use a two-stage sterilization: 60 minutes, rest for 24 hours, then another 60 minutes. This "tyndallization" approach kills organisms that survive the first sterilization by allowing them to germinate during the rest period, then killing them in the second run.

After sterilization, bags or jars must be allowed to cool to below 90°F before inoculation to avoid heat-killing the mushroom mycelium.

## Inoculation and Incubation

Sawdust spawn is inoculated under [[comparison-mushroom-log-cultivation-vs-monotub-cultivation]], sawdust spawn is packed into inoculation holes drilled into logs. Each hole receives approximately one tablespoon of spawn, which is then covered with wax or a foam plug to prevent desiccation and contamination.

For supplemented sawdust blocks (the standard method for shiitake, maitake, and other species), a small amount of sawdust spawn (typically 3-5% of the substrate weight) is mixed into the sterilized substrate before bagging.

For [[outdoor-mushroom-bed]] cultivation, larger quantities of sawdust spawn are used to inoculate wood chip beds or bunker stacks.

A fully colonized bottle or bag of sawdust/bran spawn can also serve as the "mother culture" for expanding to larger quantities of sawdust spawn, creating a cascade of increasing volume from a single agar plate.

## Spawn Quality Assessment

Good sawdust spawn should be fully colonized with a uniform white mycelium that has a pleasant mushroomy smell. Any discoloration (green, blue, black, or pink patches) indicates contamination and the spawn should be discarded.

The mycelium should be firm and cohesive — when the bag is opened, the spawn should hold together as a block rather than crumbling apart. Loose, crumbly spawn may indicate incomplete colonization or excessive moisture.

Properly made sawdust spawn, stored at cool temperatures (35-40°F), remains viable for 2-4 months. Beyond this, viability gradually declines as the mycelium consumes available nutrients and begins to senesce.
