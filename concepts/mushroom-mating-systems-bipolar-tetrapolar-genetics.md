# Mushroom Mating Systems: Bipolar and Tetrapolar Genetics

Mushroom fungi (Basidiomycota) employ complex genetic mating systems that govern
sexual reproduction, [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]], and the transition from vegetative mycelium
to [[fungal-fruiting-body-formation-environmental-triggers]]. Understanding these systems is essential for mushroom
breeding, strain improvement, and commercial cultivation.

## Overview of Basidiomycete Mating

Unlike plants and animals, fungi do not have distinct male and female sexes.
Instead, they possess mating types controlled by specific genetic loci. When two
compatible mycelia encounter each other, they undergo plasmogamy (cytoplasmic
fusion) to form a dikaryotic mycelium capable of producing fruiting bodies.
Compatibility is determined by alleles at one or more mating-type loci.

In nature, the mating system determines how much genetic recombination occurs
during each reproductive cycle. Systems with more loci generate greater allelic
diversity, producing more adaptable populations.

## Bipolar Mating Systems

In bipolar mating systems, compatibility is controlled by a single mating-type
locus, designated the **A locus**. This locus encodes homeodomain transcription
factors arranged in two sub-loci (Aα and Aβ), each with multiple alleles in the
population. Mating succeeds when two individuals carry different alleles at this
single locus — essentially a "one-factor" system.

### How Bipolar Mating Works

The A locus proteins function as transcriptional regulators. When two haploid
mycelia with different A alleles fuse, the resulting heterodimeric proteins
activate dikaryon-specific gene expression including clamp [[clamp-connection-formation-and-nuclear-migration]],
coordinated nuclear division, and fruiting body primordia development.

In a population with n different A alleles, approximately 1/n of random pairings
will be compatible.

### Species with Bipolar Mating

Several commercially important mushrooms use bipolar systems:

- **[[paddy-straw-mushroom-volvariella-volvacea-cultivation]]** ([[comparison-king-stropharia-vs-paddy-straw-mushroom]]) — commercially significant in
  tropical Asia, well-studied bipolar species
- **[[flammulina-velutipes]]** (enoki) — important cultivated species in East Asia
- **[[pholiota-nameko]]** (nameko) — popular in Japanese cuisine
- Some **Pleurotus** species, though many are tetrapolar
- **[[stamets-cultivator-coprinus-comatus-shaggy-mane-cultivation]]** (shaggy mane) — a bipolar coprinoid species

### Breeding Implications

The bipolar system simplifies breeding — only one locus needs consideration when
planning crosses. However, less genetic recombination per generation may limit
diversity available to breeders.

## Tetrapolar Mating Systems

Tetrapolar mating is the most common system among Basidiomycota, found in an
estimated 65% of species. Compatibility is controlled by two unlinked loci,
the **A locus** and the **B locus**. Both must be heteroallelic (carrying
different alleles between the two mates) for full compatibility.

### The A Locus: Nuclear Pairing and Clamp Connections

The A locus controls nuclear pairing, clamp connection formation, and
coordinated cell division. Homeodomain genes (HD genes) at this locus form
heterodimeric proteins from products of different alleles. When the A locus is
heteroallelic, these heterodimers activate the dikaryon-specific genetic program,
triggering the formation of clamp connections at each septum — the hallmark of
the dikaryotic state in most Basidiomycota.

The A locus is subdivided into Aα and Aβ sub-loci, each with multiple HD genes.
Full A-function requires heteroallelism at both sub-loci.

### The B Locus: Nuclear Migration and Cell Fusion

The B locus encodes pheromone receptors and pheromone genes. When heteroallelic,
signaling triggers septal dissolution at hyphal contact points, allowing nuclei
from one mate to migrate through the other's mycelium. This controls both cell
fusion (anastomosis) and intercellular nuclear migration.

The B locus typically contains multiple pheromone and receptor genes. In
**[[schizophyllum-commune]]**, the B locus contains up to 9 receptor and 8 pheromone
genes, generating enormous numbers of possible B specificities.
