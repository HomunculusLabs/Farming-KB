# Sinicuichi (Heimia salicifolia): Auditory Hallucinogen of Mexico

Sinicuichi (Heimia salicifolia) is a poorly understood but fascinating auditory hallucinogen from central Mexico. Unlike most known psychoactive plants, which primarily produce visual hallucinations, sinicuichi is reported to cause distinctive auditory effects — altered hearing, auditory hallucinations, and sounds that seem to come from a distance. This unusual profile makes it one of the most unique hallucinogenic plants known to ethnobotany.

## Botanical Profile

Heimia salicifolia is a shrub that ranges in the highlands from the southern United States to Argentina. With the closely related H. myrtifolia, it represents an American genus of three hardly distinguishable species that belong to the loosestrife family, Lythraceae. This family includes ornamental plants such as purple loosestrife and crepe myrtle, but Heimia is the only genus known to contain hallucinogenic principles.

The plant is also known by several other names in Mexico, where sinicuichi is applied to multiple intoxicating species. Other plants called sinicuichi include Erythrina, Rhynchosia, and Piscidia, but Heimia salicifolia commands the greatest respect and is considered the true sinicuichi. The plant has interesting uses in folk medicine, and only in Mexico is the hallucinogenic use important.

## Preparation and Effects

The preparation of sinicuichi follows a specific traditional method. The leaves, slightly wilted, are crushed and soaked in water. The resulting juice is put in the sun to ferment into a slightly intoxicating drink. The fermentation process is considered important and distinguishes the preparation from a simple cold-water extract.

The reported effects of sinicuichi are distinctive and unusual among hallucinogenic plants. They include giddiness, darkening of the surroundings, shrinkage of the world, and drowsiness or euphoria. Either deafness or auditory hallucinations may result, with voices or sounds distorted and seeming to come from a distance. Partakers claim that unpleasant after-effects are rare, but excessive drinking of the intoxicant can be quite harmful.

The auditory effects are particularly noteworthy, as most hallucinogenic plants produce primarily visual phenomena. The sensation of sounds seeming to come from a distance or being distorted is relatively unique among documented psychoactive plant effects. Some users also report a yellowing or darkening of visual perception, a characteristic that has led to the alternative name "sun opener" in some contexts.

## Chemistry

The presence of hallucinogenic principles in the loosestrife family was unknown until chemists examined Heimia salicifolia. Six alkaloids have been found in the plant, belonging to the quinolizidine group. One, cryogenine (also called vertine), appears to be the most active.

However, the hallucinogenic effects following ingestion of the total plant have not yet been duplicated by any of the alkaloids isolated thus far. This provides a notable example of the often appreciable difference between the effects of drugs taken as natural products and the effects of their purified chemical constituents. The total plant extract may contain additional active compounds, or the various alkaloids may act synergistically to produce effects that no single compound can replicate.

## Traditional and Modern Context

Sinicuichi has a long history of use in Mexican folk medicine and as an intoxicant. The Aztecs may have known and used the plant, though documentary evidence for pre-Columbian use is less abundant than for better-known Mexican hallucinogens like peyote and ololiuqui. The plant continues to be used in rural areas of central Mexico, where it occupies a modest but persistent place in the local ethnopharmacopoeia.
