# Tropical House Design and Cooling Strategies

Designing houses for tropical and subtropical climates requires understanding the
interplay between temperature, humidity, radiant heat, and air movement. Mollison's
permaculture approach integrates building design with landscape strategies to
achieve comfort without reliance on energy-intensive mechanical systems.

## Optimum Comfort Levels

Human comfort in tropical and subtropical zones is defined by measurable
environmental factors:

- **Dry-bulb temperature targets:** 20°C in winter, 25°C in summer
- **Humidity correction:** Perceived temperature rises approximately +1°C for
  every 44% increase in relative humidity above 40%
- **Wet-bulb thermometer:** A wet-bulb reading below 18°C indicates comfortable
  conditions; above 18°C wet-bulb, conditions feel oppressively muggy
- **Radiant heat surcharge:** For every degree a radiant source exceeds 38°C,
  perceived temperature increases by roughly +1°C
- **Airflow cooling:** Air movement above 1 m/s provides an effective −1°C
  perceived cooling

These five variables form the basis of passive cooling design in the tropics.

## Evaporative Cooling Limitations

Evaporative cooling works well in arid climates but is severely limited in humid
tropics. When ambient humidity is already high, the air cannot absorb additional
moisture, so evaporation rates drop and cooling effect is negligible. The focus
in humid tropics shifts to **de-humidification strategies** instead.

## De-humidification Strategies

Removing moisture from indoor air is the primary path to comfort in the humid
tropics:

1. **Cooling** — Lowering air temperature reduces its capacity to hold moisture,
   causing condensation that can be drained away
2. **Shade** — Preventing solar gain reduces both radiant heat and moisture
   released from heated materials
3. **Cool surfaces** — Dense, shaded masonry or earth-contact surfaces act as
   condensation plates that pull moisture from passing air
4. **Cool air currents** — Drawing air across shaded ground or through earth
   tubes delivers de-humidified air to living spaces

## Landscape and Structural Cooling Elements

### Overshading Trees
Large-canopy trees planted east and west intercept morning and afternoon sun.
Deciduous species allow winter sun through in subtropical zones where seasonal
heating matters.

### Shadehouses
A shadehouse on the poleward side provides a transitional zone buffering the
house from hot exterior air. When cross-ventilated to a greenhouse on the
equatorial side, it receives transpiration-cooled air, creating a thermosiphon
breeze through the house.

### White Surfaces
Painting roofs and walls white maximises solar reflection and minimises radiant
heat absorption, reducing surface temperatures by 10–15°C versus dark finishes.

### Breezeways
Open corridors allow prevailing winds to penetrate deep into the building,
preventing stagnation and hot spots.

## Comfort Factors in Tropical Design

Holistic factors beyond measurable variables contribute to tropical comfort:

- **Shade** from vegetation and structures
- **Transpiration** from surrounding plants cools ambient air
- **Cool bodies** — water features, shaded earth, and dense masonry act as
  thermal reservoirs, absorbing daytime heat and releasing it slowly at night
- **Air flows** designed into the building and landscape for continuous movement
- **Detached kitchens** — separating cooking removes the largest internal heat
  source, keeping living and sleeping areas cool

## Cross-Ventilation Design

Effective cross-ventilation is the cornerstone of passive tropical cooling.

### Vented Ceiling Slopes
Sloped ceilings with high vents (ridge vents, gable vents, cupolas) allow hot
air to rise and escape. The stack effect draws cooler air through lower openings.

### Solar Chimneys
A dark, sun-heated vertical shaft creates a strong updraft that pulls air
through the house from low-level inlets, driving continuous ventilation without
fans.

### Hot Roof Systems
A roof designed to get very hot creates an upward draft that draws cool air
from earth-cooled tunnels or shaded lower storeys, exhausting it at the ridge.

## Earth-Cooled Tunnels

Earth-cooled tunnels (ground-coupled air tubes) use stable subterranean
temperature to pre-cool ventilation air:

- **Depth:** ~1 metre below grade, buffered against daily temperature swings
- **Length:** 15–20 metres for sufficient contact time
- **Drainage:** Must be self-draining to prevent humidity and pathogens
- **Intake:** Screened against insects/rodents; placed in shaded, vegetated area
- **Material:** Smooth-walled pipes (PVC or concrete) to minimise friction

Air enters at roughly 18–22°C year-round — a dramatic improvement over peak
ambient temperatures of 35–40°C.

## Air Scoops for Coastal Zones

On subtropical coasts with reliable trade winds, **air scoops** (fixed or
adjustable vanes) capture and redirect prevailing winds into the house. Combined
with roof vents or solar chimneys, they amplify natural draw.

## The Continental Subtropical Challenge

Continental subtropical locations face cold winters and humid hot summers.
Houses must accommodate both:

- **Heating:** Insulated slab floor absorbs winter sun; a trombe wall (glazed
  masonry on the equatorial side) provides passive solar heating
- **Cooling:** Induced cross-ventilation, earth tubes, and shade manage summer
  heat through the same openings
- **Reversible design:** Thermal mass that stores winter warmth must not become
  a summer liability, managed through adjustable shading and ventilation

## Storage and Moisture Control

High humidity damages clothes, food, photographic film, and books. Design
responses include ventilated storage rooms with separate airflow paths,
de-humidified closets with desiccants, elevated food storage, and avoiding
unventilated cupboards on exterior walls where condensation forms.

## Integrated Design Summary

A well-designed tropical or subtropical house combines: orientation and shading
to minimise solar gain while capturing breezes; thermal mass and earth coupling
to stabilise temperatures; cross-ventilation pathways (vents, chimneys, tunnels,
scoops) driven by stack effect and prevailing winds; landscape integration with
trees, shadehouses, and water features; and zoned heat management with detached
kitchens and moisture-controlled storage. The goal is a house that breathes with
its environment, using natural forces rather than mechanical energy to maintain
comfort year-round.
