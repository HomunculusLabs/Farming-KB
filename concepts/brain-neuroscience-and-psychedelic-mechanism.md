# Brain Neuroscience and Psychedelic Mechanism

Simon G. Powell dedicates Chapter 5 of "The [[powell-psilocybin-solution-ecological-cognition-theory]]" to a detailed exploration of the neuroscience underlying [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]]. Titled "The Mushroom and the Synapse," the chapter investigates the neuronal architecture of the brain to comprehend the nature and potential dynamics of consciousness, examining how psilocybin and related compounds interact with the brain's information-processing systems.

## The Neuron and the Nervous System

The human nervous system consists of the body's entire network of nerve cells, or neurons. Unlike other cells designed to form tissues and organs, neurons exist solely to transmit information in the form of discrete signals or impulses. Humans possess a vast network of these neurons which manage to continually process and communicate information both about the external state of the world and the internal state of the body.

Neuropsychology, the scientific discipline Powell draws upon, studies how the brain's particular neurons function to produce thinking and behaviour. Since psychoactive substances affect the way brain neurons process information, neuropsychology has made headway into understanding the chemistry of the brain and the actual way in which psychoactive substances work.

## Serotonin: The Key Neurotransmitter

Psilocybin is believed to cause its effects by acting upon nerve cells within the brain that utilise a substance named serotonin. Serotonin is a chemical messenger, or neurotransmitter, which allows individual neurons to communicate with one another so that information can be transmitted and processed.

Powell emphasises that the neurotransmitters employed by brains have evolved over millions of years and are determined by the chemicals available in the environment, particularly from raw materials available in food. Serotonin has emerged as a key neurotransmitter because it can be produced from these environmental raw materials. You cannot have just any chemical compound acting as a neurotransmitter; it must have arisen through evolution under deterministic constraints set by the laws of chemistry and the further constraints of food availability.

This means that serotonin, and by extension consciousness itself, is bound up with the chemistry of the environment. If the chemical constitution of the natural environment were radically different, Nature would have evolved completely different neurotransmitters. In this sense, Powell argues, we are what we eat — consensus reality is a "popular serotonergic hallucination."

## The Serotonergic Homeostatic System

Serotonin is employed in several brain structures controlling functions like sleep, mood, and general arousal. One critical structure is the raphe system at the base of the brain, whose serotonergic neuronal axons project to all other major areas of the brain, notably the limbic system (controlling emotional responses) and areas of the visual system.

According to neuroscientist G.K. Aghajanian, the serotonin-using raphe system has a homeostatic function with two primary effects. In the waking state, it enhances the activity of motor neurons governing muscular movement. More significantly, during the waking state this same serotonergic system suppresses sensory systems — those systems relaying information about the external world.

This second effect serves to screen out distracting sensory cues. Aghajanian's homeostatic model suggests that this screening function maintains a kind of "balance" of consciousness in which we perceive reality in a "steady" way, almost as if the serotonergic raphe system were a balancing stick enabling us to walk the "tightrope" of normal perceptual awareness.

## Psilocybin's Action on the Raphe System

Research conducted primarily in the 1960s, using rat brains, cat brains, and isolated rat neurons, revealed that LSD and psilocybin depress the action of serotonin neurons in precisely the raphe system — a neuronal system shared by rats, cats, and humans.

The usual activity of the serotonergic neurons which psilocybin and LSD depress is inhibitory, meaning their normal firing serves to dampen or suppress activity in other parts of the brain with which they synapse. Psilocybin's dampening effect on these serotonergic neurons therefore facilitates an increase in neuronal firing in areas of the brain in contact with the raphe system, including the visual and limbic/emotion systems.

It was this enhancement of neuronal activation that was believed to correlate with the entheogenic experience itself. Chemically dismantling the raphe system's screening effect would admit the entry of latent information into consciousness — precisely the mechanism Huxley had intuited with his reducing valve theory.

## The 5-HT2 Receptor Discovery

However, the raphe theory proved incomplete. Mescaline, another classic entheogen, induces the full spectrum of visionary phenomenology but is not known to significantly affect the raphe system. This anomaly led to the discovery that there are distinct kinds of serotonin receptors — sub-type receptors — within the brain, and that different psychedelic drugs have differential effects upon them.

One particular serotonin receptor, the 5-HT2 type, appears to represent a common site of action for both psilocybin and mescaline. 5-HT2 receptors are found throughout the cortex and in abundance in the locus coeruleus, a brain system situated at the base of the brain that processes so many sensory inputs it is considered a "novelty detector" capable of influencing one's state of arousal.

## The Locus Coeruleus and Novelty Detection

The locus coeruleus monitors the constant surge of electrochemical traffic passing through it and can detect changes in data flow, alerting other parts of the brain to novel stimuli. According to Aghajanian, both psilocybin and mescaline bind to 5-HT2 sites in the locus coeruleus and thus alter the functioning of this system, ultimately raising levels of alertness and arousal.

Once again, the evidence points toward entheogens functioning by making more information available to the experiencer.
