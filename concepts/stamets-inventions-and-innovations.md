# Stamets' Inventions and Innovations

Paul Stamets has developed several novel technologies and methods that apply fungal biology to environmental, agricultural, and defensive challenges. His innovations bridge the gap between academic mycology and practical applications.

## Mycopesticide Patent: US Patent 6,660,290 (2003)

Stamets' most commercially significant invention is his patented method for controlling insect pests using pre-sporulating fungal mycelium. The key insight was that presporulating (white wedge) *Metarhizium anisopliae* mycelium, which has not yet produced spores, is carried by carpenter ants back to their nests without triggering alarm behavior. Once inside the colony, the mycelium sporulates and kills the nest. This turns insect social behavior into a delivery system for biological pest control.

The patent covers the general principle of using pre-sporulating entomopathogenic fungal mycelium as a carrier that target insects transport to their colonies. Applications extend beyond carpenter ants to termites and other social insects.

### The Trojan Horse Discovery

The invention originated from a carpenter ant infestation in Stamets' farmhouse. He observed that parasitic fungal mycelium attracted ants as a "Trojan horse" before sporulation -- the ants would carry the mycelium back to their colony without recognizing the threat. When the fungus eventually sporulated inside the nest, it killed the colony. The moldy ant carcasses then created a protective shield against re-invasion, as new ants encountering the dead colony avoided the area. This observation of natural fungal entomopathogenic behavior became the basis for the patent.

### The Fungus Gnat-Phorid Fly Matrix

Stamets discovered that certain mycelia attract fungus gnats, and mycelia also attract phorid flies -- a known predator of fire ants (*Solenopsis invicta*). Some entomopathogenic mycelia can emit both a fragrance that attracts phorid flies and another that kills fire or leaf-cutting ants. A matrix of 2 fungal species could be "mycoengineered" for a double-pronged treatment -- one species attracts the predator while another kills the pest. This is one example of hundreds of tactical predator-prey combinations described in Stamets' mycopesticide patents.

### Termite Economics

New Orleans loses approximately $300 million per year to termite damage, illustrating the economic scale of the pest problem these inventions address.

## Nerve Agent Degradation: US Patent Application 09/259,077

Stamets filed a patent application for using fungi to degrade chemical warfare agents. Working with Battelle Pacific Northwest Laboratories, his team identified mushroom strains capable of breaking down DMMP (dimethyl methylphosphonate), a key surrogate for VX and sarin nerve agents.

### The Battelle Collaboration

Battelle, an approved Department of Defense test facility, had well-established programs for researching defenses against chemical and biological weapons. Stamets trained their chemists in tissue culture of mycelium, showing them how to introduce a non-native wood substrate to agar media to familiarize mushroom strains to new substrates early in their life cycle. This "naturalization" approach was then expanded: instead of wood, toxins were used as added nutrients in the agar media. He provided Battelle with a select library of 26 of his most aggressive mushroom strains for testing.

### Strain Adaptation Protocol

The team incrementally increased concentrations of various toxin surrogates while decreasing other natural nutrients until starving mycelia digested toxins as food. Several strains adapted and grew when toxins became their sole source of nutrition. Stamets sought strains with enzymes for making and breaking phosphorus bonds -- the critical bonds holding nerve gas toxins together. Once the enzymes broke the phosphorus bonds through dephosphorylation, the neurotoxin molecules unraveled into nontoxic forms.

### The Psilocybe Connection

Stamets notes that psilocybin mushrooms (*Psilocybe azurescens*, *P. cyanescens*) absorb phosphorus from their surroundings to synthesize the psilocybin molecule (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine), which can constitute 2% of the mushroom's mass. He hypothesizes that with the right enzymes, Psilocybe mycelium could extract phosphorus from DMMP. Non-fruiting strains could be used for cleaning environments contaminated with DMMP-like neurotoxins. However, he raises the question: if these "magic" mushroom species proved effective against VX, would we choose not to use them since they are controlled substances?

### Proof of Concept

A Battelle report dated July 14, 1997 (with Stamets listed as coauthor) -- "Adaptation of mycofiltration phenomena for wide-area and point-source decontamination of chemical warfare and biological warfare agents" -- gave results of tests against chemical warfare agents and *Escherichia coli* (used as a surrogate for anthrax). Two of Stamets' strains neutralized "very close surrogates of chemical weapons such as sarin, soman, and the VX family of compounds" (Jane's Information Group 1999). The joint study suggests mycoremediation of chemical warfare agent sites is more effective and less expensive than conventional methods.

## Acclimated (Naturalized) Spawn

While not formally patented, Stamets' development of "acclimated spawn" represents a significant practical innovation. Rather than using sterile commercial spawn that struggles in outdoor environments, he discovered that mushroom farm mycelium exposed to wild microbes over time develops superior survival characteristics. This "naturalization" process is analogous to vaccination -- the mycelium develops natural antibiotics that preselect helpful microbes and create a defensive shield. This concept makes [[mycoremediation-of-pahs]] projects more practical and affordable.

## Mycofiltration Membranes

Stamets developed practical methods for constructing mycofiltration membranes using readily available materials:

- Bark and crude wood chips inoculated with mycelium
- Burlap bags filled with myceliated substrate ("bunker spawn"), stacked like sandbags
- Sheet mulch beds 6 inches to 2 feet thick
- Spore slurries applied to contaminated sites

These membranes can filter pathogens, silt, and chemical toxins from water. The Skookum Inlet experiment demonstrated hundredfold coliform reduction using garden giant mycelial beds.

## Spored Oils for Chainsaws

Stamets proposes inoculating chainsaw bar oil, chipper lubricants, and other cutting equipment oils with mushroom spores. When trees are cut, the oil delivers spores directly to the cut surfaces, jump-starting decomposition by beneficial saprophytes and denying entry to parasitic pathogens. This is one of the [[mycoforestry-principles-stamets]].

## Rhizomorphic Mycelium Applications

Stamets documented the extraordinary tensile strength of rhizomorphic mycelium. A single rhizomorphic thread of a caerulescent *Psilocybe* weighed 0.002 grams but held dowels weighing 6.079 grams -- supporting 3,029 times its mass. When 90% of the rhizomorph was cut away, it still supported the dowels, meaning it could hold more than 30,000 times its mass. This property has implications for biodegradable packaging and structural materials.

### Mycelium Density

A cubic foot of spawn contains more than 14,000 miles of mycelial threads. This extraordinary density creates vast surface areas for enzyme secretion, filtration, and structural applications.

## Cold Composting for Munitions Destruction

Stamets recommends "cold composting" for munitions destruction -- using mushroom species that metabolize at very cold temperatures. Natural advantages: they continue secreting enzymes in near- or below-freezing conditions, and the temperatures they generate are below the danger threshold. Examples include enoki (*Flammulina velutipes*) and waxy cap (*Hygrophorus camarophyllus*).

## Agarikon Antiviral Extracts

Working through the Biodefense BioShield program (NIH and USAMRIID), Stamets discovered that extracts from agarikon (*Fomitopsis officinalis*) mycelium showed potent activity against vaccinia pox and cowpox viruses. While not a "device" invention, this discovery of a natural antiviral from an old-growth forest mushroom has significant implications for biodefense.

## Ecological Design Principles

Beyond specific patents, Stamets' broader innovation is the conceptual framework of [[mycorestoration-ecology]] itself -- the systematic use of fungi across four domains (filtration, forestry, remediation, pest control) to restore damaged environments. His vision of "customized mycological landscapes" populated with targeted fungal communities represents a new paradigm for ecological engineering.
