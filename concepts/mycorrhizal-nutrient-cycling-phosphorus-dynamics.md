## Mycorrhizal Nutrient Cycling and Phosphorus Dynamics

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are central to [[nutrient-cycling-in-soil]] terrestrial ecosystems, with phosphorus (P) representing one of the most critical nutrients mediated by these symbiotic associations. The research literature reveals a complex picture of how different [[mycorrhizal-types]] access, transport, and redistribute phosphorus and other nutrients between soil, fungi, and host plants.

## Phosphorus Uptake Mechanisms

### Depletion Zone Extension

Non-mycorrhizal plants rely solely on root interception, mass flow, and diffusion for [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-phosphorus-acquisition]]. Because phosphorus diffuses very slowly in soil, a depletion zone rapidly forms around roots. Mycorrhizal fungi overcome this limitation by:

- Extending hyphae far beyond the root depletion zone, accessing P from bulk soil
- Transporting P along hyphae back to the host plant (Jakobsen, 1995; Jakobsen et al., 1992a, b)
- Exploiting the phosphorus depletion zone in mycorrhizal white clover in calcareous soil (Li et al., 1991a)

### Phosphatase Activity

Both ectomycorrhizal (ECM) and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi produce acid phosphatase enzymes that liberate phosphate from organic compounds:

- Root surface phosphatase activities in field-collected gray birch and red maple roots correlate with P uptake (Antibus et al., 1997)
- ECM fungi produce phosphatases at the soil-hyphae interface, decreasing pH and enhancing P solubility (Li et al., 1991b)
- Phosphatase activities of external hyphae have been documented in AM fungi (Joner and Johansen, 2000)
- The effects of organic and inorganic phosphorus concentration on acid phosphatase activity vary among ECM species (Kroehler et al., 1988)
- ECM mats in Douglas-fir forests show elevated nitrogen chemistry and nitrogen cycle transformation rates (Griffiths et al., 1990)

### Phosphorus Solubilization

Some mycorrhizal fungi can solubilize otherwise unavailable phosphorus sources:

- Plants inoculated with mycorrhizal fungi and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria can utilize rock phosphate in alkaline soils (Azcon et al., 1976)
- ECM fungi demonstrate phosphate-solubilizing activity in vitro (Lapeyrie et al., 1991)
- Phosphatase production by mycorrhizal fungi has been documented since early studies (Dighton, 1983)

## Organic Nitrogen Utilization

### Protein and Peptide Utilization by Ectomycorrhizae

ECM fungi have a remarkable ability to access organic nitrogen sources that are unavailable to most plants:

- ECM fungi can utilize peptides and proteins as nitrogen sources (Abuzinadah and Read, 1986a)
- *[[hebeloma-crustuliniforme]]* enables utilization of proteins by birch, spruce, and pine (Abuzinadah and Read, 1986b)
- Different ECM fungi vary in their ability to utilize peptides by birch (Abuzinadah and Read, 1989)
- ECM fungi can mobilize nitrogen from protein–polyphenol complexes (Bending and Read, 1996)
- Nitrogen mobilization from exploited litter by ECM mycelial strands has been demonstrated (Bending and Read, 1995a, b)

### Variation in Nitrogen Source Utilization

Considerable physiological variation exists within and between mycorrhizal fungal species:

- Pisolithus isolates show variation in nitrogen source utilization in axenic culture (Anderson et al., 2001)
- Intraspecific physiological variation has implications for understanding functional diversity in ECM fungi (Cairney, 1999)
- Physiological heterogeneity within fungal mycelia is an important concept for understanding ECM symbiosis (Cairney and Burke, 1996)

## Nutrient Transfer Between Plants

### Carbon and Phosphorus Translocation

One of the most significant discoveries in mycorrhizal ecology is the ability of fungal hyphae to interconnect different plants and facilitate nutrient transfer:

- 14C-labelled carbon can be translocated between plants interconnected by a common ECM mycelium (Finlay and Read, 1986a)
- Phosphorus is taken up and distributed by mycelial strands interconnecting host plants (Finlay and Read, 1986b, c)
- Links between roots by AM hyphae were demonstrated in early studies (Heap and Newman, 1980a, b)
- The specificity of interplant phosphorus cycling depends on the mycorrhizal association (Eason et al., 1991)
- Cycling of nutrients from dying roots to living plants involves mycorrhizal mediation (Newman and Eason, 1989)

### Translocation in Rhizomorphs

Solute translocation occurs in both ECM and saprotrophic rhizomorphs (Cairney, 1992), and rhizomorphs play a special role in water transport (Duddridge et al., 1980). The mycelial cord-forming fungi that produce rhizomorphs are well-adapted to [[fungal-foraging-strategies-heterogeneous-environments]] (Boddy, 1999).

## Mycorrhizae and Soil Development

### Weathering and Mineral Dissolution

Mycorrhizal fungi contribute to soil development through mineral weathering:

- Lichen colonization induces rock weathering through physical and chemical processes (Chen et al., 2000)
- Rhizospheric microflora associated with mycorrhizae can weather micas and promote plant growth (Berthelin and Leyval, 1982)
- [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-and-carbonate-precipitation]] accumulation in mats of hypogeous fungi contributes to soil weathering (Cromack et al., 1979)

### Litter Decomposition Interactions

The relationship between mycorrhizae and litter decomposition is complex:

- Mycorrhizal roots of *Pinus radiata* can suppress litter decomposition (Gadgil and Gadgil, 1971, 1975)
- Litter quality influences ECM community structure and root surface acid phosphatase activity (Conn and Dighton, 2000)
- Interactions between tree roots, mycorrhizae, saprotrophic fungi, and decomposition of organic substrates occur in microcosms (Dighton et al., 1987)
- 14C-labelled substrates are decomposed by ECM fungi in association with [[douglas-fir]] (Durall et al., 1994)

## Mycorrhizae in Specialized Habitats

### Salt Marshes

Mycorrhizal associations occur in salt marsh environments:

- Mycorrhizae have been documented in coastal salt marsh plants (Cooke and Lefor, 1990, 1998; Hoefnagels et al., 1993)
- Vertical distribution of AM fungi in salt marsh grass roots varies with soil saturation (Cooke et al., 1993)
- Spatial and temporal dynamics of mycorrhizae in salt marsh halophytes follow tidal patterns (Brown and Bledsoe, 1996)
- Semi-aquatic grasses show AM colonization along wide hydrological gradients (Miller, 2000)
- AM fungi mediate uptake and translocation of P and Zn by wheat in calcareous soil (Goh et al., 1997)

### Arid and Stressed Environments

- [[biological-soil-indicators]] fix nitrogen in arid environments of southeast Utah (Belnap, 2002)
- Microphytic crusts in pinyon-juniper woodlands contribute potential carbon (Beymer and Klopatek, 1991)
- AM fungi help plants cope with salinity stress (Allen and Cunningham, 1983; Juniper and Abbott, 1993; Johnson-Green et al., 2001)
- Water relations, drought, and AM symbiosis interact to affect plant performance (Auge, 2001)
