# Food Preservation and Canning

Food preservation is the practice of treating and storing food so it remains safe and nutritious long after harvest. For homesteaders and gardeners, preservation transforms seasonal abundance into year-round food security. Methods include water bath canning, pressure canning, fermentation, drying, freezing, and root cellaring. Each method is suited to different types of food and has specific safety requirements.

## Water Bath Canning

Water bath canning (boiling water canning) processes jars of high-acid foods in boiling water (212°F / 100°C). The high acidity (pH below 4.6) prevents the growth of Clostridium botulinum — the organism that causes botulism.

### Suitable Foods

- Fruits (apples, peaches, pears, berries, cherries)
- Jams, jellies, preserves, marmalades
- Tomatoes (with added acid — lemon juice or citric acid)
- Pickles (vegetables preserved in vinegar with proper acidity)
- Relishes, chutneys, salsas (with adequate vinegar/acid)
- Fruit juices, fruit butters

### Equipment

- Water bath canner (or a large pot deep enough to cover jars with 1-2 inches of water)
- Mason jars (regular or wide mouth) with new two-piece lids (flat lid + screw band)
- Jar lifter, funnel, headspace tool, bubble remover, magnetic lid wand
- Clean kitchen towels

### Process

1. **Prepare jars**: Wash jars in hot, soapy water. Keep hot until ready to fill (in simmering water or the dishwasher).
2. **Prepare food**: Follow a tested recipe from a reliable source (USDA, Ball Blue Book, NCHFP). Do not improvise with canning recipes — safety depends on specific acid levels, processing times, and ingredient ratios.
3. **Fill jars**: Pack food into hot jars. Leave the recommended headspace (space between food and jar lid) — typically 1/4 to 1/2 inch depending on the recipe.
4. **Remove air bubbles**: Run a non-metallic spatula or bubble remover around the inside of the jar to release trapped air.
5. **Clean rims**: Wipe jar rims with a clean, damp cloth. Any food on the rim prevents a proper seal.
6. **Apply lids**: Place flat lids on jars, then screw on bands fingertip-tight (do not over-tighten — air must escape during processing).
7. **Process**: Place jars on a rack in the canner. Ensure jars are covered by 1-2 inches of water. Bring to a rolling boil. Start timing when the water reaches a full boil. Process for the time specified in the recipe.
8. **Cool**: Turn off heat, remove the lid, wait 5 minutes. Remove jars with a jar lifter and place on a towel. Do not retighten bands. Let cool undisturbed for 12-24 hours.
9. **Check seals**: Press the center of each lid. If it is firm and does not flex, the jar is sealed. Remove bands, wipe jars, label with contents and date, and store in a cool, dark place.

### Storage

Properly sealed water bath canned goods are safe for at least 1 year. Store at 50-70°F in a dark, dry place.

## Pressure Canning

Pressure canning is the ONLY safe method for canning low-acid foods (pH above 4.6). Low-acid foods include vegetables, meats, poultry, seafood, soups, stews, and beans. The high temperatures (240-250°F / 116-121°C) achieved in a pressure canner are necessary to destroy C. botulinum spores that can survive boiling water temperatures.

### Equipment

- **Pressure canner**: A heavy pot with a locking lid and pressure gauge (dial gauge or weighted gauge). Must be specifically designed for pressure canning — pressure cookers are NOT a safe substitute (they do not maintain adequate pressure for the required time).
- Same jar and accessory equipment as water bath canning.

### Process

1. **Prepare jars and food**: Same as water bath canning. Follow tested pressure canning recipes.
2. **Fill jars**: Pack hot food into hot jars. For most vegetables and meats, use the hot pack method (pre-cook the food before packing).
3. **Add liquid**: Add boiling water, broth, or juice as specified. Maintain recommended headspace.
4. **Process**: Place jars on the canner rack. Add 2-3 inches of water (or amount specified for your canner). Lock the lid. Vent steam for 10 minutes. Close the vent or put on the weight. Allow pressure to build to the specified PSI.
5. **Maintain pressure**: Process at the recommended PSI for the specified time. Adjust for altitude:
   - **Dial gauge canner**: Increase PSI by 1 for every 2,000 feet above sea level.
   - **Weighted gauge canner**: Use 15 PSI for altitudes above 1,000 feet.
6. **Cool naturally**: Turn off heat. Let pressure return to zero naturally (do not force-cool). Wait 10 minutes after pressure reaches zero, then open the lid away from you.
7. **Check seals**: Same as water bath canning.

### Critical Safety Notes

- **Always follow tested recipes**: The National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) and the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning are the gold standards.
- **Never alter ingredient proportions**: Adding more vegetables, reducing vinegar, or thickening with flour or starch can make a recipe unsafe.
- **Never pressure-can dairy, pasta, rice, or thickeners**: These ingredients interfere with heat penetration.
- **Check your canner gauge**: Dial gauges should be tested annually at your county extension office.
- **Weighted gauges are more reliable**: They don't need calibration and automatically regulate pressure.

## Fermentation

Fermentation preserves food through the action of beneficial bacteria and yeasts. It is one of the oldest preservation methods and produces probiotic-rich foods with enhanced nutritional value.

### Lacto-Fermentation
Uses lactic acid bacteria (LAB) to produce acid, which preserves the food.

**Common fermented foods**: Sauerkraut, kimchi, pickles, kombucha, kefir, yogurt.

**Basic sauerkraut process**:
1. Shred cabbage finely.
2. Mix with salt (2-3% by weight — approximately 2 tablespoons per medium head of cabbage).
3. Massage or pound the cabbage until it releases its juices.
4. Pack tightly into a fermentation vessel (glass jar, crock, or fermentation crock). Ensure the cabbage is submerged below the liquid.
5. Weight down with a fermentation weight, cabbage leaf, or ziplock bag of water.
6. Cover loosely (not airtight — gases must escape). Ferment at 65-72°F for 1-4 weeks.
7. Taste periodically. When it reaches your desired flavor, move to cold storage (refrigerator or root cellar).

**Safety**: Fermentation is inherently safe because the acidic environment inhibits pathogens. Trust your senses — if it smells off or shows mold colors other than white kahm yeast, discard it.

## Dehydration (Drying)

Removing moisture from food to levels that prevent microbial growth (typically below 10-15% moisture).

### Methods
- **Electric dehydrator**: Most consistent and reliable. Temperature control. Recommended for beginners.
- **Oven drying**: Possible but less efficient. Set to the lowest temperature (140-170°F). Prop the door open slightly for airflow.
- **Air/sun drying**: Traditional method. Works for herbs, peppers, tomatoes. Requires consistent warm, dry weather.

### Suitable Foods
- Herbs (dried at 95-110°F)
- Fruits: apples, bananas, berries, apricots, peaches
- Vegetables: tomatoes, peppers, kale, zucchini
- Jerky (meat dried at 160°F)
- Fruit leathers

### Storage
Store dried foods in airtight containers in a cool, dark place. Properly dried foods last 6-12 months. For long-term storage, vacuum-seal with oxygen absorbers.

## Freezing

Freezing preserves food quality by stopping microbial activity and enzymatic reactions.

### Best Practices
- **Blanch vegetables**: Briefly immerse in boiling water (2-5 minutes), then immediately plunge into ice water. This destroys enzymes that cause off-flavors and texture loss during frozen storage.
- **Freeze quickly**: Spread food in a single layer on a baking sheet before transferring to bags. This prevents clumping.
- **Remove air**: Use freezer bags, squeeze out air, or vacuum-seal. Air causes freezer burn.
- **Label everything**: Contents, date, and any preparation notes.
- **Storage life**: Most frozen vegetables and fruits: 8-12 months. Meat: 4-12 months.

## Root Cellaring

Root cellaring uses natural cool temperatures and high humidity to store certain crops through winter without processing.

### Ideal Conditions
- Temperature: 32-40°F (0-4°C)
- Humidity: 85-95% for root vegetables; 60-70% for onions, garlic, squash
- Darkness: Light causes potatoes to green and onions to sprout
- Ventilation: Some airflow to prevent mold and gas buildup

### Suitable Crops
- **Root vegetables**: Carrots, beets, parsnips, turnips, rutabagas — store in damp sand, sawdust, or leaves
- **Potatoes and sweet potatoes**: Dark, cool (not as cold as roots — 45-50°F for sweet potatoes)
- **Onions and garlic**: Cool, dry conditions (lower humidity than root vegetables)
- **Winter squash and pumpkins**: Cool, dry conditions (50-55°F)
- **Apples and pears**: Cool, slightly humid conditions. Store away from root vegetables (apples emit ethylene gas that causes sprouting)
- **Cabbage**: Can be stored in root cellars, often hung by the root
