# Hofmann's Discovery of LSD: The Bicycle Day Account

The discovery of [LSD by Swiss chemist [[albert-hofmann]] on April 19, 1943, is one of the most celebrated accidents in the history of pharmacology. His firsthand account in LSD: My Problem Child provides a vivid, detailed narrative of both the accidental exposure and the deliberate self-experiment that followed -- events now commemorated annually as "Bicycle Day."

## Background: The Fifth Cycle of Research

LSD-25 (lysergic acid diethylamide, the 25th compound in a series) was first synthesized on November 16, 1938, as part of a systematic program investigating derivatives of lysergic acid at Sandoz Pharmaceuticals in Basel, under Professor Arthur Stoll. The compound arose from Hofmann's research into [[claviceps-purpurea]] ergot life cycle, which he had persuaded Stoll to let him resume despite Stoll's warning: "I must warn you of the difficulties that such research entails."

The compound was tested pharmacologically and found "relatively uninteresting" -- it was set aside for five years. During this period, Hofmann continued productive work on the chemical structure of ergot alkaloids, eventually correcting the American-proposed structural formula for lysergic acid in 1949 and achieving the total synthesis of ergotamine in collaboration with Dr. Albert J. Frey and Dr. Hans Ott.

## The Peculiar Presentiment: Spring 1943

Five years after the first synthesis, Hofmann experienced what he calls a "peculiar presentiment -- the feeling that this substance could possess properties other than those established in the first investigations." This intuition led him to produce LSD-25 once again. This was highly unusual; experimental substances were normally "definitely stricken from the research program if once found to be lacking in pharmacological interest."

## The Accidental Exposure: April 16, 1943

During the final step of the synthesis -- purification and crystallization of lysergic acid diethylamide tartrate -- Hofmann was interrupted by "unusual sensations." He sent a report to Professor Stoll:

> "Last Friday, April 16, 1943, I was forced to interrupt my work in the laboratory in the middle of the afternoon and proceed home, being affected by a remarkable restlessness, combined with a slight dizziness. At home I lay down and sank into a not unpleasant intoxicated-like condition, characterized by an extremely stimulated imagination. In a dreamlike state, with eyes closed (I found the daylight to be unpleasantly glaring), I perceived an uninterrupted stream of fantastic pictures, extraordinary shapes with intense, kaleidoscopic play of colors. After some two hours this condition faded away."

Hofmann surmised that a trace of LSD solution had contacted his fingertips during crystallization and been absorbed through the skin. Given the known toxicity of ergot substances, he maintained "meticulously neat work habits," making accidental absorption through carelessness unlikely. If LSD-25 was indeed the cause, it "must be a substance of extraordinary potency."

## The Deliberate Self-Experiment: April 19, 1943

Concluding that "there seemed to be only one way of getting to the bottom of this," Hofmann planned a carefully controlled self-experiment. Exercising extreme caution, he chose the smallest dose expected to produce any effect based on known ergot alkaloid activity: 0.25 mg of lysergic acid diethylamide tartrate. His laboratory journal entry reads:

> "4/19/43 16:20: 0.5 cc of 1/2 promil aqueous solution of diethylamide tartrate orally = 0.25 mg tartrate. Taken diluted with about 10 cc water. Tasteless."
> "17:00: Beginning dizziness, feeling of anxiety, visual distortions, symptoms of paralysis, desire to laugh."
> "Supplement of 4/21: Home by bicycle. From 18:00- ca.20:00 most severe crisis."

The dose of 0.25 mg was in fact enormous -- approximately 250 times the threshold dose later established. Hofmann notes that "no known substance had displayed even the slightest psychic effect in fraction-of-a-milligram doses" at that time, making his miscalculation understandable.

## The Bicycle Ride

By 5:00 PM, the effects were overwhelming. Hofmann asked his laboratory assistant to escort him home. Because of wartime restrictions on automobile use, they traveled by bicycle. On the way home, his condition "began to assume threatening forms":

> "Everything in my field of vision wavered and was distorted as if seen in a curved mirror. I also had the sensation of being unable to move from the spot."

Despite his perception of paralysis, his assistant later reported that they "had traveled very rapidly."

## The Crisis at Home

The experience at home was terrifying. Hofmann's account is remarkably candid about his fear:

> "Even worse than these demonic transformations of the outer world, were the alterations that I perceived in myself, in my inner being. Every exertion of my will, every attempt to put an end to the disintegration of the outer world and the dissolution of my ego, seemed to be wasted effort. A demon had invaded me, had taken possession of my body, mind, and soul. I jumped up and screamed, trying to free myself from him, but then sank down again and lay helpless on the sofa. The substance, with which I had wanted to experiment, had vanquished me."

> "I was seized by the dreadful fear of going insane. I was taken to another world, another place, another time. My body seemed to be without sensation, lifeless, strange. Was I dying? Was this the transition?"

He also experienced the experience with remarkable self-awareness, observing himself "as an outside observer" and contemplating the irony of dying from "this lysergic acid diethylamide that I myself had brought forth into the world." He drank more than two liters of milk as a nonspecific antidote. The neighbor woman who brought it became "a malevolent, insidious witch with a colored mask."

## The Doctor's Visit

When the family doctor arrived, the climax of the crisis had passed. The doctor could detect no abnormal symptoms beyond extremely dilated pupils; pulse, blood pressure, and breathing were all normal. "He saw no reason to prescribe any medication" and instead conveyed Hofmann to bed.

## The Recovery and Revelation

As the terrifying effects subsided, Hofmann began to experience the positive aspects that would define LSD's significance:

> "Now, little by little I could begin to enjoy the unprecedented colors and plays of shapes that persisted behind my closed eyes. Kaleidoscopic, fantastic images surged in on me, alternating, variegated, opening and then closing themselves in circles and spirals, exploding in colored fountains, rearranging and hybridizing themselves in constant flux."

> "It was particularly remarkable how every acoustic perception, such as the sound of a door handle or a passing automobile, became transformed into optical perceptions. Every sound generated a vividly changing image, with its own consistent form and color."

The next morning he awoke "refreshed, with a clear head, though still somewhat tired physically." Walking into his garden after a spring rain, "everything glistened and sparkled in a fresh light. The world was as if newly created. All my senses vibrated in a condition of highest sensitivity, which persisted for the entire day."

## Significance of the Discovery

Hofmann identified several remarkable properties of LSD from this single experience:

- **Extraordinary potency**: No other known substance evoked such profound psychic effects in such extremely low doses
- **Complete recollection**: The conscious recording function was not interrupted, even at the climax; "for the entire duration of the experiment, I had even been aware of participating in an experiment"
- **No hangover**: Despite the intensity, the next day brought excellent physical and mental condition
- **Synesthesia**: Sounds were transformed into visual images with consistent form and color
- **Inescapability**: Despite full awareness of his condition, "I could not, with every exertion of my will, shake off the LSD world"

## Aftermath and Verification

Hofmann wrote to Professor Stoll and the pharmacological director Professor Rothlin describing his experience. Their first reaction was incredulous: "Are you certain you made no mistake in the weighing? Is the stated dose really correct?" Both repeated the experiment at one-third the dose with "extremely impressive, and quite fantastic" results. All doubts about Hofmann's report were eliminated.
