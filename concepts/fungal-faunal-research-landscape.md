# Research Landscape of Fungal-Faunal Interactions

## Overview

The study of fungal-faunal interactions spans multiple ecological disciplines, from soil microbiology to marine ecology, and from population dynamics to ecosystem-level processes. This page synthesizes the major research themes and key contributors based on the extensive literature on fungal-animal relationships, drawing from foundational studies in mycology, soil ecology, entomology, and ecosystem science.

## Soil Microbial-Faunal Interactions

A central theme in fungal-faunal research concerns how soil animals interact with fungal communities in decomposition and nutrient cycling:

- **Anderson and Ineson (1984)** explored nutrient flux pathways between microorganisms and soil invertebrates in forest ecosystems, establishing frameworks for understanding trophic interactions in soil
- **Beare et al. (1992)** investigated microbial and faunal interactions and their effects on litter nitrogen and decomposition in agroecosystems, demonstrating how animals modify decomposition rates
- **Lussenhop (1992)** provided a comprehensive review of mechanisms in microarthropod-microbial interactions in soil
- **Wardle et al. (1998)** developed predictive frameworks for responses of soil micro-food-web trophic relationships to changing global environments

## Mycophagy and Spore Dispersal by Mammals

Research on mammalian consumption of fungi has revealed important ecosystem functions:

- **Fogel (1976)** conducted ecological studies of hypogeous fungi, documenting sporocarp phenology in [[douglas-fir]] stands
- **Fogel and Trappe (1978)** synthesized evidence of fungus consumption (mycophagy) by small animals, establishing mycophagy as a significant ecological interaction
- **Claridge and May (1994)** surveyed mycophagy among Australian mammals, revealing the geographic breadth of this interaction
- **Cork and Kenagy (1989a, 1989b)** quantified the nutritional value of hypogeous fungi for forest-dwelling squirrels and measured rates of spore passage and retention through rodent digestive tracts
- **Trappe and Maser (1976)** demonstrated that spores of *Glomus macrocarpus* could germinate after passage through rodent digestive tracts, confirming the viability of animal-mediated [[spore-dispersal]]
- **Mangan and Adler (2002)** documented seasonal dispersal of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] by spiny rats in neotropical forests

## Collembola-Fungal Interactions

Collembola (springtails) are among the most studied fungal grazers, with extensive research on their selective feeding:

- **Newell (1984a, 1984b)** conducted seminal studies on interactions between decomposer basidiomycetes and collembola under Sitka spruce, demonstrating how selective grazing could alter fungal distribution and litter decomposition
- **Shaw (1985, 1988, 1992)** documented consistent hierarchies in fungal feeding preferences of *Onychiurus armatus* and developed frameworks for understanding fungal food webs
- **Hiol Hiol et al. (1994)** showed that the feeding preference of mycophagous collembola varies with ectomycorrhizal symbiont species
- **Klironomos and Ursic (1998)** demonstrated density-dependent grazing on the extraradical hyphal network of the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus *[[glomus-intraradices]]* by the collembolan *Folsomia candida*
- **Klironomos et al. (1999)** showed reproductive significance of feeding on saprobic versus arbuscular [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] for collembola
- **Parkinson et al. (1979)** demonstrated effects of collembolan grazing on fungal colonization of leaf litter

## Entomopathogenic Fungi

Fungi that attack insects have been extensively studied both as natural population regulators and as biocontrol agents:

- **Hajek and St. Leger (1994)** reviewed interactions between fungal pathogens and insect hosts, providing a comprehensive framework for understanding entomopathogenic relationships
- **Charnley (1997)** assessed entomopathogenic fungi and their role in pest control
- **Johnson and Goettel (1993)** demonstrated reduction in grasshopper populations following field application of *Beauveria bassiana*
- **Pickford and Riegert (1964)** documented the fungal disease caused by *Entomophthora grylli* and its effects on grasshopper populations in Saskatchewan
- **Onofre et al. (2001)** tested pathogenicity of entomopathogenic fungi against the bovine tick *Boophilus microplus*

## Earthworm-Fungal Interactions

Earthworms serve as both dispersal agents for fungi and modifiers of fungal communities:

- **Brown (1995)** reviewed how earthworms affect microfloral and faunal community diversity
- **Gange (1993)** demonstrated translocation of mycorrhizal fungi by earthworms during early succession
- **Moody et al. (1995, 1996)** showed selective consumption of decomposing wheat straw by earthworms and documented the fate of fungal spores passing through earthworm guts
- **Doube et al. (1994a, 1994b, 1995)** investigated earthworms as delivery systems for beneficial soil microorganisms and biocontrol agents of root disease
- **Reddell and Spain (1991)** confirmed earthworms as vectors of viable propagules of mycorrhizal fungi

## Ant and Termite-Fungal Symbioses

The fungus-growing insects represent some of the most complex fungal-animal mutualisms:

- **Cherrett et al. (1989)** reviewed the mutualism between leaf-cutting ants and their fungi, documenting the sophistication of this agricultural symbiosis
- **Bass and Cherrett (1996)** analyzed fungus garden structure in the leaf-cutting ant *Atta sexdens*
- **Wood and Thomas (1989)** characterized the mutualistic association between Macrotermitinae termites and *Termitomyces* fungi
- **Korb and Linsenmair (2001)** investigated spatial patterning of mounds of fungus-cultivating termites
- **Ba et al. (2000)** found yeasts in mound soil of the red imported fire ant

## Nematode-Fungal Interactions

Research on nematodes and fungi encompasses both grazing relationships and biological control:

- **Chen and Ferris (1999)** studied the effects of nematode grazing on nitrogen mineralization during fungal decomposition
- **Ruess and Dighton (1996)** conducted cultural studies on soil nematodes and their fungal hosts
- **Laakso and Setälä (1999)** examined population and ecosystem-level effects of predation on microbial-feeding nematodes
- **Larsen et al. (1997)** reviewed biological control of gastrointestinal nematodes in livestock

## Aquatic Fungal-Faunal Interactions

Research in aquatic systems has revealed important parallels with terrestrial interactions:

- **Bärlocher and Kendrick (1975)** established the role of hyphomycetes as intermediates of energy flow in streams
- **Suberkropp (1992)** reviewed interactions between aquatic hyphomycetes and invertebrates
- **Gessner et al. (1997)** compared decomposition of plant litter by fungi in marine and freshwater ecosystems
- **Graca et al. (1993)** demonstrated the importance of fungi in the diet of aquatic detritivores (*Gammarus pulex* and *Asellus aquaticus*)

## Key Synthesis Works

Several major reviews and syntheses have shaped the field:

- **Wilding et al. (1989)** - *Insect-Fungus Interactions*: A landmark volume covering the breadth of insect-fungal relationships
- **Coleman and Hendrix (2000)** - *Invertebrates as Webmasters in Ecosystems*: Comprehensive treatment of invertebrate roles in ecosystem processes
- **McGonigle (1997)** reviewed fungivores in *The Mycota IV*, providing a systematic overview
- **Wall and Moore (1999)** discussed interactions underground, emphasizing soil biodiversity, mutualism, and ecosystem processes

## Research Gaps and Future Directions

The literature reveals several persistent gaps: extrapolation from laboratory studies to field conditions remains problematic; the density-dependent nature of many fungal-faunal interactions makes field quantification difficult; and the roles of fungi in marine and freshwater ecosystems are considerably less studied than terrestrial systems. Climate change impacts on these interactions represent an emerging and critically understudied area.
