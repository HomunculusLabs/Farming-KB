# Compost Tea Brewing Guide

Updated: 2026-04-18

Actively aerated compost tea (AACT) is a liquid concentrate of beneficial
aerobic microbes extracted from [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-biology]]. It
contains the same set of microorganisms as the compost from which it derives,
but in higher concentrations -- bacterial populations grow from 1 billion per
teaspoon of compost to 4 billion per teaspoon of finished tea.

## AACT vs Leachate vs Extract vs Manure Tea

Do not confuse AACT with other preparations:

- **Compost leachate**: Liquid that oozes out when compost is pressed or water
  runs through it. Has some nutrient value and color but does little to impart
  microbial life -- bacteria and fungi are attached to organic matter with
  biological glues and don't simply wash off.

- **Compost extract**: Compost soaked in water for weeks, producing an
  anaerobic soup. Loss of aerobic microbial diversity and risk of anaerobic
  pathogens and alcohols make it not worth the effort.

- **Manure tea**: Manure suspended in water for weeks, also anaerobic.
  Virtually assures the presence of E. coli. Not safe or advisable.

- **AACT**: Made by pumping air into a mixture of compost, dechlorinated water,
  and microbial nutrients for 24-36 hours. Remains aerobic throughout. The
  beneficial aerobic microorganisms are the ones you want.

## DIY 5-Gallon Brewer

You can easily build an AACT brewer:

1. A 5-gallon plastic bucket
2. An aquarium air pump (the biggest you can afford)
3. An air stone (or a 2-foot length of 3/4-inch soaker hose coiled on the
   bucket bottom)
4. About 4 feet of plastic tubing
5. Tape the air stone/hose to the bucket bottom, connect to pump

Use a double-outlet pump if possible. Sufficient aeration is critical -- if
the tea smells bad, it is going anaerobic. Keep air stones and tubing clean
to prevent clogging from bacterial slime. Use 3% hydrogen peroxide or 5%
baking soda solution to clean dried slime.

## Ingredients

**Water**: Must be chlorine-free. Run air bubbles through chlorinated water
for 1-2 hours before brewing. Carbon filters and reverse osmosis also work.

**Compost**: Use good-quality, finished compost. Avoid "almost compost,"
anaerobic compost, or overheated compost that killed beneficial microbes.
Vermicastings are an excellent substitute (especially fresh ones, which are
very bacterial). Use about 4 cups per 5-gallon brew.

**Bacterial foods** (to boost bacterial populations):
- Molasses (nonsulfured): 2 tablespoons per 5 gallons
- Cane syrup, maple syrup, fruit juices
- Fish emulsion, more complex sugars

**Fungal foods** (to boost fungal populations):
- Kelp (Ascophyllum nodosum)
- Humic and fulvic acids
- Phosphate rock dusts (provide attachment surfaces)
- Fish hydrolysate (enzymatically digested fish with bones)
- Yucca and zeolites (support fungi, not bacteria)
- Aloe vera extract (without preservatives)
- Fruit pulps (oranges, blueberries, apples)

## Fungal Activation

Bacteria multiply rapidly in tea, but fungi only grow bigger during the short
brew time. To get sufficient fungi, activate them in compost before brewing:

1. Several days before brewing, mix compost with a fungal food (soybean meal,
   powdered malt, oatmeal, oat bran, or powdered baby oatmeal) at 3-4
   tablespoons per cup of compost
2. Ensure adequate moisture (a drop can be squeezed from a fistful)
3. Place in a warm (80F/27C), dark location
4. After ~3 days, visible white mycelia should cover the compost
5. Use this activated compost for brewing

## Brewing Process

- Brew for 24-36 hours (DIY brewers may take longer than commercial units)
- Keep at room temperature; avoid direct sunlight (UV kills microbes)
- Good tea smells sweet and earthy; bad-smelling tea has gone anaerobic
  -- discard it
- Add mycorrhizal fungi at the very end of the brew cycle (they are too
  fragile to survive the full brewing process)
- Use within 4 hours of manufacture; lasts 3-5 days if refrigerated or kept
  aerated

## Application

AACT can be applied as a soil drench or sprayed on leaf surfaces (strain
first if using a sprayer). It puts beneficial microbes exactly where needed --
both in the rhizosphere and on the phyllosphere.
