# Mycopesticides — Fungi as Natural Insect Control

Using entomopathogenic fungi to control insect pests — from termites and carpenter ants to fire ants, flies, and agricultural pests. Pioneered by [[paul-stamets]]' discovery of pre-sporulating mycelium as an insect attractant. From *Mycelium Running* (2005).

## The Discovery: Pre-Sporulating Mycelium as a Trojan Horse

### Origin Story

[[paul-stamets]]' farmhouse on the Olympic Peninsula was being destroyed by carpenter ants (Camponotus modoc). After discovering that wood-digesting fungi preceded insect invasions, he obtained a culture of Metarhizium anisopliae — a green mold fungus used to kill termites:

1. Cultured the mold on malt-enriched agar
2. Noticed white wedge sectors emerging from green sporulating colonies
3. Subcultured these presporulating sectors through multiple generations
4. Isolated a nonsporulating mycelial phenotype — white, lacking the repellent spore smell
5. Placed the white mycelium on rice in a doll dish next to ants' sawdust pile
6. **Result**: Ants picked up the myceliated rice kernels and carried them back to the nest — each ant became a distribution vector for the lethal fungus
7. Within 2 weeks: house was free of carpenter ants and never reinvaded

### Key Innovation

The parasitized insect carcasses became moldy from repellent spores, creating a resident protective shield that ward off future invasions.

## Entomopathogenic Fungi: Biology

### How They Kill

1. Spores (or mycelium) contact the insect
2. Hyphal pegs bore through the exoskeleton using chitin-dissolving enzymes
3. Other portals: respiratory tract, anus, mouth
4. Mycelium forks through internal organs
5. Interferes with metabolism, causes malaise, necrosis, and death in a few days
6. Insect becomes mummified with fuzzy mycelium
7. Carcass becomes a launching platform for spore production

### Fungal Dimorphism

Some entomopathogenic fungi express two forms:
- **Mold state**: Metarhizium anisopliae (green mold) — pathogenic to ants, termites, locusts, mosquitoes, mites
- **Mushroom state**: Cordyceps species — fruit from dead insect carcasses

Cordyceps lloydii infects carpenter ants and compels them to climb to the canopy, lock mandibles into a leaf, and die — ensuring wide spore dispersal.

### Major Genera

| Genus | Form | Primary Targets |
|-------|------|----------------|
| Metarhizium anisopliae | Green mold | Termites, ants, locusts, mosquitoes, flies |
| Beauveria bassiana | White mold | Wide range of insects |
| Paecilomyces spp. | White mold | Various agricultural pests |
| Hirsutella spp. | Various | Specific host insects |
| Cordyceps spp. | Mushroom | Ants, caterpillars, other insects |

## The Presporulating Mycelium Discovery

### Why It Works

Insects have evolved sophisticated defenses:
- Social insects (ants, termites) maintain sentry systems guarding queens
- Infected individuals are recognized, killed, and placed in graveyards away from the nest
- Spore-carrying workers are refused entry at multiple tiers of guards
- Free-flying or soil-borne spores alert insects to danger

The breakthrough: **presporulating mycelium lacks the repellent spore smell and actually contains attractants and feeding stimulants.**

### Texas A&M Research Results

Under Dr. Roger Gold at Texas A&M Entomology Department:

- **Formosan termites** (Coptotermes formosanus): Attracted to mycelium, fed upon it, carried fragments to nest, died within 2-3 weeks
- **Eastern subterranean termites** (Reticulitermes flavipes): Similar results
- **Fire ants** (Solenopsis invicta): Workers enthroned the queen on a bed of mycelium, where she expired along with her subjects
- **Choice tests**: Termites preferred mycelium to wood
- **Arrestant behavior**: Termites burrowed to mycelium location and stopped — did not penetrate further
- **Recruitment effect**: Termites that contacted mycelium recruited others, compounding the effect

### Mechanism of Action

- Mycelium kills large protozoa in termite digestive tracts
- Without protozoa, termites cannot metabolize cellulose → they starve
- Insects sicken and become susceptible to both internal and external fungal infection

## Economic Context

- US pesticide industry: $9 billion total market
- Biopesticide market share: $450 million (5%), growing 15% per year
- Termite damage (native subterranean): exceeds $10 billion/year in the US
- Formosan termite damage: exceeds $1 billion/year
- New Orleans alone loses $300 million/year to termites
- About 5% of insect species (4-6 million total) are economic pests

## Patents

- **U.S. Patent 6,660,290**: Approved December 9, 2003 — attracting and killing Formosan termites, eastern subterranean termites, and carpenter ants
- **Second patent**: Approved October 17, 2006 — controlling all social insects (~200,000 species)
- **Expanded CIP patents**: Pending divisional patents covering additional species

## Carriers for Deployment

Presporulating mycelium can be projected via many carriers:
- Wood chips
- Sawdust
- Paper
- Cardboard
- Biodegradable fabrics
- Agricultural waste products

This enables "landscapes customized with mycelial matrices" targeting specific insects.

## Additional Targets

Recent trials show effectiveness against:
- Fungus gnats (phorid and sciarid species)
- Blowflies (Calliphora species)
- This strongly suggests applicability to non-social insects as well

## Beauveria bassiana: Additional Discovery

Stamets also found that Beauveria bassiana produces attractants prior to sporulation. Other genera likely to show similar activity:
- Hirsutella
- Mucor
- Paecilomyces
- Aspergillus
- Nomuraea
- Species in the order Entomophthorales

## Advantages Over Chemical Pesticides

- Does not infect plants, mammals, fish, or bees
- EPA has encouraged study of Metarhizium anisopliae as biopesticide
- Insects recruit others to their own destruction
- After treatment, spores repel future invasions (long-term protection)
- Uses genome as indigenous source — limits tolerance buildup
- Minimizes harm to nontargeted insects
- Reduces need for termite-resistant tropical hardwoods from rain forests
- Spent mycelium becomes a soil amendment after remediation

## Historical Context

- **1834**: Agustino Bassi noticed Beauveria bassiana spores causing muscardine disease in silkworms — credited with conceiving "germ theory" before Pasteur (1858)
- **1990s onward**: Multiple patents awarded on entomopathogenic fungi
