# Fungi in Aquatic and Marine Faunal Interactions

[[fungal-biodiversity-marine-ecosystems-coastal-deep-sea]] range
from mutualistic associations that enhance animal nutrition to pathogenic
relationships causing disease. Fungi condition leaf litter to improve
its palatability for shredder invertebrates, form obligate gut symbioses
with aquatic insects, and serve as essential dietary components for
marine wood-boring crustaceans. These interactions are critical for
energy flow and [[freshwater-fungi-aquatic-hyphomycetes-ascomycetes]] as intermediaries in
energy flow in stream ecosystems. The amphipod Gammarus increased in
weight faster relative to food ingested when provided with leaves
colonized by fungi including Humicola, Anguillospora, Clavariopsis,
Tricladium, or Fusarium, compared to uncolonized elm or maple leaves.
The biochemical changes in leaf chemistry caused by fungal attack
improve availability of essential nutrients, particularly nitrogen, and
readily assimilable energy sources.

## Selective Feeding on Fungal Colonizers

Aquatic shredder species show selective feeding among fungal species
colonizing leaf litter. The amphipod Gammarus pseudolimnaeus grew
better on leaves colonized by four specific fungal species than six
others. Trichopteran larvae showed significantly improved growth rates
on leaf litter colonized by fungi compared to uncolonized leaves.
Suberkropp (1992) suggests it is not the fungus per se but rather
the synergistic benefit of the combination of fungal-modified leaf
tissue that elicits greater productivity. Fungi make up 90 to 95% of
microbial biomass on decaying leaves, suggesting their dominance in
initial leaf decomposition stages.

## Mass Loss Partitioning

Hieber and Gessner (2002) showed that in alder and willow leaf
decomposition in streams, the shredder community accounted for 64%
and 51% of leaf mass loss respectively, fungi for 15% and 18%, and
bacteria only 7% and 9%. Within this community, fungi composed 95
to 99% of total microbial biomass. The fungal contribution to
respiration accounted for 1 to 57% of total carbon respired from
fungal-colonized leaves, with fungal biomass alone being insufficient
to account for the increased respiratory carbon loss, suggesting
conditioning effects on leaf chemistry are primary mechanisms.

## Marine Wood-Boring Associations

Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer (1979) documented 107 fungal species isolated
from decomposing wood in marine habitats, including 73 Ascomycotina,
two Basidiomycotina, and 29 mitosporic species. Evidence suggests that
wood-boring marine mollusks preferentially settle and feed on wood
previously colonized by fungi. The association between the wood-boring
crustacean gribble (Limnoria tripunctata) and marine fungi is so
tight that the gribble has increased longevity when feeding on
fungus-colonized wood and is incapable of reproduction unless marine
fungi are included in its diet, likely due to enhanced protein,
amino acid, and vitamin availability.
