## Digester Design and Sizing

### Scale and Dimensions
The sewage of 1,000 townspeople and their associated industries needs as little as 270 metres square of settling pond 1 m deep. This can be configured as:
- A long pond 3 m wide x 90 m long
- Three side-by-side ponds 30 m long and 3 m wide
- A compact pond 3 m deep x 3 m wide x 30 m long

We can halve the length by doubling the depth to 2 m, or treble the depth and condense the area. Such a long and narrow pond is easily made totally anaerobic by fitting water seals and a weighted cover over the top (plastic, metal, butyl rubber, or fibreglass).

### Sealed and Agitated Systems
For these deeper digester ponds, artificial agitation of the sludge is necessary (using pumped biogas to stir it), otherwise it settles and becomes inactive. Sludge is active only in contact with the semi-liquid inputs of the sewer; thus when we stir up the sludge, the better we break down the sewage to biogas.

Another critical benefit in sealed and agitated digesters is that no scum forms on the pond surface, which can slow the breakdown process and cause acid conditions.

## Digester Performance

### Conversion Efficiency
Over a period of 20 days and with a temperature of 25-30°C (77-86°F), a very high percentage of the total dissolved solids entering the digester is transferred into methane. A small proportion passes on to other ponds as living cells (bacteria or algae). As methane forms, the oxygen demand of the effluent falls. About a cubic metre of methane generated removes about 2.89 kg of solids, reducing biological oxygen demand (BOD) accordingly.

### Pathogen Destruction
In the digester:
- 90-94% of worm eggs are destroyed
- Many harmful bacteria are eliminated
- Copper is removed 97% (78% anaerobically)
- Cadmium is removed 70% (all anaerobically)
- Zinc is removed 97% (83% anaerobically)
- Nickel is removed 65%
- Lead is removed 95% (90% anaerobically)
- Chromium is removed 87%
- Iron is removed 85%

### Self-Regulating Equilibrium
The more sludge in an anaerobic pond, the more active the pond. A self-regulated equilibrium condition soon establishes where input balances gas output. If sludge is removed, the process slows or stops. This is a clear case of leaving well alone, of active sludge becoming its own solution; rather than being a problem, it generates a resource (methane).

## Energy Recovery

### Gas Utilisation
The useful energy generated can be used at that location to:
- Run a motor for electricity generation
- Compress gas for cooking or machinery
- Supply heat for the digester process itself
- Drive any motors needed for both aeration and gas compression

The motor both supplies the heat for the digester process and also compresses the gas for digester agitation and for energy supply. Methane will drive any motors needed for both aeration and gas compression, creating a self-sustaining energy loop.

### Seasonal Variation
Half as much gas (as methane) is given off in winter as in summer (or in heated digesters). In winter, the cooling water of methane-powered engines can provide the essential heat to digesters via a closed-loop pipe.

### Temperature Management
Methane production is temperature-dependent. At 25-30°C, optimal conversion occurs. For year-round production in cold climates, waste heat from methane-powered engines is routed through closed-loop pipes to maintain digester temperature. Solar heating of digesters can supplement this in warmer months.

## Feedstock Materials

### Sewage
Raw sewage provides the primary feedstock for large-scale systems. The influent contains:
- Total dissolved solids (TDS): 1,200 mg/L
- Biological oxygen demand (BOD): 170-570 mg/L
- Nutrients including nitrogen (ammonia 5-32 mg/L), phosphorus (7-24 mg/L), and potassium (9-56.2 mg/L)
- pH near neutral (6.9)

Of the total sewage input, 45-60% of the volume builds up as sludge in settling ponds.

### Alternative Feedstocks
For biogas applications, septic tank effluent, weeds, and aquatic crops can serve as feedstock. An aquatic crop for biogas feedstock followed by a leach field represents a complete small-scale waste-to-energy system. Flowthrough systems for methane production take little maintenance.

## The Three-Stage Pond System

### Stage 1: Anaerobic (Methane-Producing) Ponds
- Raw sewage enters settling ponds where sludge creates anaerobic conditions
- These are the smallest but deepest ponds
- Biogas is produced and captured
- Sulphur-loving bacteria (Thiosporallum, Chromatium, Rhodopseudomonas) produce elemental sulphur which binds heavy metals
- About 1.8-2.0 mg/L of heavy metals are precipitated as sulphides at 1.0 mg/L of elemental sulphur

### Stage 2: Facultative Ponds
- Part methane-producing, part oxygen-producing
- Algae blooms develop (Euglena, Chlamydomonas, Chlorella, Scenedesmus)
- Phytoplankton (both algae and bacteria) thrive
- Zooplankton appear: rotifers, cladocerans, copepods, ostracods

### Stage 3: Aerobic Ponds
- Totally oxygen-producing
- Further nutrient removal and pathogen destruction
- Algal and bacterial production for fish food or harvest
- Water suitable for irrigation or discharge

## Sludge Utilisation

Sludge from digesters and weed-free manures provide excellent fertiliser. The anaerobic process not only produces energy but stabilises the organic material, reducing pathogen load and weed seed viability.
