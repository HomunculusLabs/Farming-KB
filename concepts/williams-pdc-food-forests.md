# Food Forests

Food forests are gardens modeled after the three-dimensional
structure of natural forests, using vertical layering to
produce diverse food yields on far less surface area than
conventional gardening, combining multiple [[permaculture]]
principles into one harmonious food system.

## The Concept of Food Forests

We can apply the examples of natural forests to our garden
design, vastly increasing our food production. Food forests
are gardens modeled after the three dimensional aspect of
forests. They take advantage of the vertical dimension and
combine many [[permaculture-principles]] into one harmonious
food system. Modeled after a forest, a food forest can have
up to seven layers of production.

The concept ties in exactly with the concept of density
and complexity central to Arcology. It is also an excellent
application of the permaculture principle of using edges and
valuing the marginal, since food forests utilize empty three
dimensional space that is typically marginalized instead of
used. In essence food forests are what happens when the
concept of Arcology meets the concept of an orchard.

## The Seven Layers

Instead of mature large trees like Doug Firs or Oaks, the
canopy of the food forest will be fruit and nut trees.
Shorter fruit or nut bearing trees fill in the next layer,
and one level down from those can be shrubs with berries.
Growing around their trunks can be herbs or other small
plants, sometimes called kneelers because you have to kneel
down to harvest them. Spreading from there can be ground
covering food plants, and then root plants such as carrots
or potatoes taking advantage of the production space below
ground. Climbing vines such as grapes or hops can grow up
through all the layers, completing the vertical stacking.

## Efficiency Through Vertical Stacking

In modern thinking each food bearing plant would typically
be planted in its own area: trees here, lettuce over there,
carrots down yonder, and berry bushes out there somewhere.
This type of gardening takes up a large amount of surface
area. By using the three dimensional stacking of food
producing plants, we get the same amount of food grown on
far less surface area, leaving the rest of the area open to
just be wild.

This is the essence of understanding density, and why
permaculture is not about making designs that reduce our
impact but rather increase it in positive ways. The food
forest produces more food per unit area while simultaneously
providing habitat, [[building-soil]], and supporting ecological
health.

## Food Forests and Plant Guilds

Food forests rely heavily on the concept of plant guilds,
where plants are grouped in mutually beneficial communities.
Each layer of the food forest contains plants that support
one another through nutrient cycling, pest management, shade
provision, and other ecological services. The lynch pins of
food forests are well designed guilds that create
self-maintaining plant communities requiring minimal human
intervention over time.

## Urban Food Forests

Urban forestry can be combined with food forest concepts to
create urban food forests. We can plant indigenous fruit and
nut trees that line roads and delineate property lines.
Around the bases of the trees we can recreate the layers of
the forest, planting shrubs and ground covers. Urban food
forests provide cooling, increase air quality, block wind,
naturally clean runoff water, reduce storm water runoff, and
provide habitat for migrating birds and smaller animals.

## Ecological Benefits

Food forests provide far more than just food production.
They build soil through leaf litter and root decay, create
habitat for beneficial insects and wildlife, sequester
carbon, moderate local temperatures, and increase
biodiversity. Over time a mature food forest becomes
increasingly self-sustaining, requiring less and less human
input while producing more and more yield, approaching the
stability and resilience of a natural forest ecosystem.

## Designing a Food Forest

When designing a food forest, begin by observing the existing
patterns on your site. Consider sun exposure, slope, water
flow, existing vegetation, and soil conditions. Choose your
canopy trees first, selecting species appropriate to your
climate that will produce food while creating the micro-
climate conditions needed by the understory layers. Then
work downward through each layer, selecting plants that
thrive in the conditions created by the layers above.

Remember that food forests take time to establish. Start
with the canopy and understory trees, then gradually add
the lower layers as the microclimate develops. Patience is
essential. A food forest is a long-term investment that
increases in productivity and self-sufficiency with each
passing year. The designer should plan for succession,
understanding that the food forest will evolve over time
and designing for those changes from the beginning.

A well designed food forest is one of the highest
expressions of [[permaculture-design]], demonstrating how
human food needs can be met within systems that mimic and
support natural ecological processes.
