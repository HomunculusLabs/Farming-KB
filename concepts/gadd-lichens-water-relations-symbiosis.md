# Water Relations in Lichens: Symbiotic Adaptation to Environmental Extremes

## Overview

Chapter 9 of *Fungi in the Environment* (Rosmarie Honegger, University of Zurich) provides
a comprehensive examination of how lichens—symbiotic associations between fungi (mycobionts)
and photosynthetic partners (photobionts)—manage water relations. Lichens are poikilohydric
organisms that lack mechanisms for active water regulation, making their water economy
fundamentally different from vascular plants. This chapter explores the structural, physiological,
and ecological adaptations that allow lichens to colonize some of Earth's most extreme
environments.

## The Lichen Symbiosis

Lichens represent a dual (or sometimes tripartite) organism where:

- **The mycobiont** (usually an Ascomycete fungus) provides structure, protection from
  desiccation and UV radiation, mineral acquisition, and [[cervantes-green-algae-growing-medium]] and/or cyanobacteria) provides photosynthate (sugars) to
  the fungal partner
- **The cyanobiont** (when present as a third partner) fixes atmospheric nitrogen,
  enabling lichens to colonize nitrogen-poor substrates

The mycobiont controls the relationship and determines lichen morphology (crustose, foliose,
or fruticose), [[fungal-hyphae]].

## Poikilohydry: Life Without Water Regulation

Unlike homoiohydric plants that maintain relatively constant internal water content through
stomatal regulation and root uptake, lichens are poikilohydric—their internal water content
equilibrates with atmospheric humidity. Key characteristics include:

- **No stomata or vascular tissue**: Water uptake occurs across the entire thallus surface,
  primarily through the medullary hyphae (loosely woven fungal tissue between cortex layers)
- **Rapid equilibration**: Thallus water content can change from fully saturated to air-dry
  within minutes in response to changing atmospheric conditions
- **Metabolic suspension**: When dry, lichens enter a state of anhydrobiosis—metabolism
  effectively ceases, and the organism can survive for months or years in a dormant state
- **Rapid reactivation**: Upon rehydration, photosynthesis and respiration resume within
  minutes, though full recovery may take hours depending on the severity and duration of
  prior desiccation

## Water Uptake Mechanisms

### Atmospheric Humidity Absorption
Lichens absorb water vapor directly from the atmosphere. Most species require a minimum
relative humidity of 80-95% (corresponding to water potential of approximately -5 to -10 MPa)
before significant [[capillary-action-in-soils]].

### Liquid Water Uptake
Rain, dew, fog, and meltwater provide liquid water that lichens absorb rapidly. The upper
cortex, though hydrophobic in many species (due to hydrophobin proteins), allows water
penetration through pores and cracks. Some lichens have specialized water-conducting
structures called cephalodia or cyphellae.

### Hydrophobin-Coated Hyphae
The mycobiont produces hydrophobins—small secreted proteins that self-assemble into
hydrophobic coatings on hyphal surfaces. These coatings serve multiple functions:
- Creating a hydrophobic upper surface that repels excess water (preventing prolonged
  saturation that could favor algal overgrowth)
- Channeling water to specific uptake zones
- Maintaining gas exchange pathways even when the surface is wet

## Photosynthetic Response to Hydration

The photosynthetic response of the photobiont upon hydration follows a characteristic
pattern:

1. **Initial burst**: A rapid spike in CO2 exchange (respiration exceeds photosynthesis)
   immediately upon wetting, as both partners resume metabolic activity
2. **Recovery phase**: Photosynthetic rate gradually increases as the photobiont repairs
  damage from desiccation (reactivation of Calvin cycle enzymes, repair of thylakoid
   membranes)
3. **Steady state**: Net photosynthesis stabilizes at a rate determined by light, temperature,
   CO2 concentration, and water content
4. **Supersaturation depression**: At very high water contents, CO2 diffusion through the
   thallus becomes limiting, causing photosynthetic depression even though water is abundant

This pattern is critical for understanding lichen productivity in fog-dependent ecosystems
