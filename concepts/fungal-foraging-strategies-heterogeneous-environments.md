# Fungal Foraging Strategies in Heterogeneous Environments

## Overview

Saprotrophic cord-forming basidiomycetes face the same fundamental challenge as plants and animals: resources are heterogeneously distributed in space and time. Unlike most fungi that are restricted to individual resource units (disseminated only as spores), cord-formers are **non-resource-unit restricted** — they can grow out of one resource and actively search for others. This capability has led to the evolution of distinct **foraging strategies** that parallel those of plants and animals, characterized by differences in mycelial morphology, fractal dimension, and responsiveness to environmental cues.

## The Challenge of Environmental Heterogeneity

Natural environments differ profoundly from laboratory conditions:

| Feature | Laboratory | Natural Environment |
|---|---|---|
| **Nutrient distribution** | Homogeneous, superabundant | Patchy, sparse, or locked in recalcitrant material (e.g., lignin) |
| **Biological interactions** | Sterile | Bacteria, other fungi, invertebrate grazers |
| **Microclimate** | Relatively constant | Temporally and spatially variable (temperature, moisture, gases) |

Fungi have evolved two complementary approaches to coping with this heterogeneity:

1. **"Sit and wait"**: A large mycelial network waits for resources to land on it, then actively colonizes them. Responses may occur at distant parts of the network.
2. **Active search**: Mycelia grow outward, exploring soil and litter for new resources, with an array of responses when resources are found.

Both strategies involve continuous remodelling of the mycelial network through growth, branching, hyphal fusion, and regression.

## Fractal Analysis of Mycelial Morphology

### Fractal Dimension as a Foraging Metric

Fractal geometry provides a quantitative description of mycelial pattern formation through the **fractal dimension (D)**:

- **D = 1**: Linear structure
- **D = 2**: Completely plane-filled (two dimensions)
- Values between 1 and 2 describe the degree of space-filling

Two useful fractal dimensions are distinguished:
- **Mass fractal dimension (D_M)**: Describes whether the system has gaps in its interior.
- **Surface/border fractal dimension (D_S)**: Describes whether the system is plane-filled except at boundaries.
- **Mass fractal systems**: D_S = D_M (gaps in interior).
- **Surface fractal systems**: D_S < D_M (completely filled except at edges).

### Box-Count Method

The most common method for determining D uses **box-counting** (designated D_BM and D_BS for mass and border dimensions, respectively). This method reveals:
- Developmental shifts between diffuse, assimilative growth and corded, distributive growth.
- Subtle morphological differences that may be missed by simple observation.

## Two Foraging Strategies

### Long-Range Foragers (Mass Fractal)

Species with mass fractal mycelia include: *Agrocybe gibberosa*, *[[coprinus-picaceus]]*, *[[phallus-impudicus]]*, *Phanerochaete velutina*, and *Resinicium bicolor*.

**Characteristics:**
- Open systems with well-defined, rapidly extending cords (high-conductivity channels formed by aggregation and differentiation of hyphae).
- Lower fractal dimension — less space-filling.
- Would be unsuccessful at capitalizing on homogeneously supplied nutrients.
- Successfully discover **large, sparsely distributed resources** (fallen branches, trunks, stumps).
- *A. gibberosa* utilizes graminaceous litter; the others interconnect woody resources with little leaf litter colonization.

### Short-Range Foragers (Surface Fractal)

Species with surface fractal mycelia include: *Hypholoma fasciculare* and *Stropharia caerulea*.

**Characteristics:**
- Diffuse, slowly extending search fronts.
- Higher fractal dimension — more space-filling.
- Search areas intensively.
- Likely to discover **abundant, relatively homogeneously distributed resources**.
- *Stropharia* species commonly found on nutrient-rich sites (compost heaps, rhizomes, small woody and non-woody litter).
- *H. fasciculare* colonizes stumps but frequently colonizes leaves, small twigs, and cupules.

## Temporal Dynamics

Mycelial patterns are not static — they change over time:

- **Initial phase** (several months): Surface fractal species remain space-filling; mass fractal species extend cord networks.
- **Long-term shift**: Even initially surface fractal mycelia become increasingly **mass fractal** (more open) over time.
- **Mechanism**: Regression of individual hyphae and minor cords leaves a persistent network of thick cords behind the foraging margin.
- **Resource recycling**: Regressing mycelium allows redistribution of biomass and nutrients to support further exploration by foraging fronts.
- **Nutrient translocation**: Radiotracer studies confirm nutrients are scavenged at foraging fronts and translocated from the resource base to support growth.

## Environmental Modification of Foraging Patterns

### Resource Effects

- **Larger inocula**: Most species produce mycelia with greater space-filling from larger resources.
- **Higher nutrient concentration**: *S. caerulea* and *R. bicolor* produce greater space-filling; higher D often correlates with higher extension rates (space-filling is not at the expense of extension).

### Soil Nutrient Status

- **Short-range foragers** (*H. fasciculare*, *S. caerulea*) respond to elevated soil nitrogen or phosphorus by **increasing branching**, enhancing nutrient uptake capacity.
- **Long-range foragers** (*Phal. impudicus*) are **less responsive**; elevated soil nutrients have little apparent effect on morphology.
- *Phan. velutina* (long-range) is usually less responsive but sometimes fills more space with a plethora of soil nutrients.

### Non-Nutrient Factors

- **Temperature**: Can dramatically affect morphology (extension, branching, cord production, space-filling), though mediating mechanisms are unclear.
- **Soil pH**: *Phan. velutina* had much higher D at pH 7 than pH 4.4; *C. picaceus* often failed to grow at pH 4.4 but produced similar biomass at all pH values due to greater space-filling at lower pH.
- **Water potential**: *S. caerulea* D_BS decreased from ~1.7 to ~1.35 as water potential increased (wetter soil), with greater cord aggregation at very low water potential.
- **Fluctuating conditions**: In nature, constantly varying temperature and moisture affect mycelial development, nutrient uptake, and movement.

## Parallels with Macroorganisms

### Plants: Phalanx vs. Guerrilla Strategies

The fungal dichotomy mirrors clonal plant foraging:

| Plant Strategy | Fungal Equivalent | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Phalanx** | Short-range foraging | Tight aggregated structures; intensive local search |
| **Guerrilla** | Long-range foraging | Loosely aggregated, fast-growing branches; extensive exploration |

Plant root system traits — extensive development (high-scale), concentration in rich patches (high-precision), rapid proliferation (high-rate) — apply equally to mycelial foraging.

### Animals: Army Ant Foraging

Trail patterns of foraging army ants (*Eciton* species) are remarkably similar to mycelial systems:

- *E. hamatum* (widely dispersed prey) → resembles long-range fungal foraging.
- *E. burchelli* (evenly distributed prey) → resembles short-range foraging.
- *E. repax* (intermediate) → intermediate pattern.

Ant trails are **self-organizing**: pioneers make slow progress through rough terrain, creating easier routes for followers who reinforce trails with pheromones (positive feedback). Where there is less use, negative feedback reduces trail formation. However, ant trail patterns change with prey distribution rather than reflecting different search strategies — a key difference from the innate fungal strategies.

## Response to Resource Discovery

When foraging mycelia encounter new resources, dramatic responses occur:

- **Thickening of resource-connected cords**
- **Regression of non-resource-connected mycelium**
- **Cessation or slowing of radial extension**
- **Subsequent outgrowth from the newly colonized resource**

These responses depend on the size and quality of both the original inoculum and the newly encountered resource, the spatial distribution of the new resource, soil nutrient status, and fungal species.
