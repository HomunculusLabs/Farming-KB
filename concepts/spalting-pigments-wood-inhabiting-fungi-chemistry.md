# Spalting Pigments and Wood-Inhabiting Fungi Chemistry

## Introduction

Spalting is any form of wood coloration caused by fungi. Woodworkers and artisans have prized spalted wood for centuries for its dramatic dark lines, colorful zones, and unique visual patterns. Beyond aesthetics, the pigments and [[hofmann-lsd-chemical-modifications-derivatives]] produced by spalting fungi represent a rich area of natural product chemistry with applications in wood science, biomaterials, and potential pharmaceutical or industrial uses.

## Types of Spalting

### Pigmenting (Staining)

Pigmenting occurs when fungi deposit colored metabolites into wood cell walls. The pigments are typically extracellular [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] that diffuse through the wood's porous structure, creating zones of color that follow the moisture gradients and fungal growth patterns. Colors range from blues and greens to reds, yellows, purples, and pinks, depending on the fungal species and chemical conditions.

### White Rot (Bleaching)

White-rot fungi degrade lignin preferentially, leaving behind the lighter-colored cellulose and creating contrasting bleached zones. This is the most common form of spalting and is produced primarily by basidiomycetes in the Polyporales and Agaricales. The visual effect is a mottled pattern of light and dark areas.

### Zone Lines

Dark demarcation lines form where incompatible fungal individuals or species meet. These lines are composed of melanized fungal tissue and represent "no man's land" boundaries where competing fungi deploy defensive pigments (primarily melanin) to protect their territories. Zone lines can be black, dark brown, or dark blue-green, and they create the striking patterns most associated with high-value spalted wood.

## Key Spalting Fungi and Their Pigments

### Scytalidium cuboideum (Red Pigment)

*S. cuboideum* produces a vivid red pigment identified as **dramada** (a naphthoquinone derivative). This pigment is water-soluble and diffuses readily through wood, creating pink-to-red zones. The pigment shows:

- **Color range**: Pink to deep crimson depending on concentration and wood species
- **Stability**: Light-fast when bound to wood; fades in direct UV exposure in solution
- **Solubility**: Water-soluble, methanol-soluble, poorly soluble in non-polar solvents
- **Antimicrobial activity**: Moderate activity against Gram-positive bacteria

### Scytalidium ganodermophthorum (Yellow Pigment)

*S. ganodermophthorum* produces a yellow-orange pigment that creates golden zones in wood. The pigment chemistry is distinct from the red pigment of *S. cuboideum* and involves different naphthoquinone or anthraquinone derivatives.

### Chlorociboria aeruginosa and C. aeruginosa (Green Pigment)

*Chlorociboria* species produce the distinctive blue-green pigment **xylindein**, a quinonoid pigment that stains wood a characteristic teal-to-emerald green. Xylindein has attracted particular scientific interest:

- **Chemical structure**: A bis-naphthoquinone derivative with extensive conjugation, responsible for both its color and its electronic properties.
- **Color stability**: Extremely light-fast and stable; green-stained wood from historic artifacts (16th–17th century) retains its color today.
- **Historical use**: Green-stained wood (primarily oak and beech colonized by *Chlorociboria*) was valued for intarsia and marquetry in Renaissance Europe, particularly in Italian and Flemish workshops. The green wood was used as sky, foliage, and water in pictorial inlays.
- **Electronic properties**: Xylindein is a semiconductor with potential applications in organic electronics, organic photovoltaics, and bio-derived optoelectronic devices.
- **Biosynthesis**: Produced in the hyphal cell walls and extruded into the surrounding wood matrix.

### Trametes versicolor (Turkey Tail)

*T. versicolor* produces a zone-line-forming white-rot fungus that creates dramatic patterns. The concentric zone pattern on its own [[comparison-fruiting-body-vs-mycelium-extracts-medicinal-mushrooms]] (resembling a wild turkey's tail feathers) reflects the same competitive interaction chemistry that produces zone lines in wood. The fungus produces several bioactive polysaccharides (PSK, PSP) and sterols in addition to its wood-modifying enzymes.

## Chemistry of Zone Line Formation

### Melanin as a Defense Pigment

Zone lines are primarily composed of **melanin** — a heterogeneous polymer of oxidized phenolic and indolic compounds. Fungal melanin serves multiple functions:

- **Physical barrier**: Melanin's rigid, cross-linked structure creates a physical barrier that impedes hyphal penetration by competing fungi.
- **Chemical defense**: Melanin scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and hydrolytic enzymes produced by competitors.
- **UV protection**: Melanin absorbs UV radiation, protecting the fungal tissue beneath zone lines.
- **Metal chelation**: Melanin binds metal ions, potentially depriving competitors of essential micronutrients.

### DHI and DHN Melanin Pathways

Most wood-inhabiting fungi produce melanin through the 1,8-dihydroxynaphthalene (DHN) pathway:

1. Acetyl-CoA → malonyl-CoA → polyketide chain (via polyketide synthase)
2. Cyclization to 1,3,6,8-tetrahydroxynaphthalene (T4HN)
3. Sequential reduction and dehydration steps to produce scytalone, vermelone, and finally DHN
4. Oxidation and polymerization of DHN to form melanin

Some fungi use the DOPA-melanin pathway (L-DOPA → dopachrome → 5,6-dihydroxyindole → melanin), which is biochemically distinct from the DHN pathway.

## Enzymatic Wood Modification

### Lignin-Degrading Enzymes

White-rot spalting fungi produce three [[ascomycota-phylogeny-major-classes-lineages]] of lignin-modifying enzymes:

- **Laccases** (EC 1.10.3.2): Blue multicopper oxidases that oxidize phenolic lignin subunits. Laccases also catalyze the polymerization of melanin precursors in zone line formation.
- **Lignin peroxidases** (LiP, EC 1.11.1.14): Heme-containing peroxidases that oxidize non-phenolic lignin through a veratryl alcohol-mediated radical mechanism.
- **Manganese peroxidases** (MnP, EC 1.11.1.13): Oxidize Mn²⁺ to Mn³⁺, which then diffuses into the wood matrix to oxidize phenolic compounds.

### Cellulose and Hemicellulose Modification

While white-rot fungi preferentially degrade lignin, some cellulose and hemicellulose degradation occurs, particularly in advanced stages of spalting. This can reduce wood density and strength — a tradeoff between aesthetic value and structural integrity.

## Controlled Spalting for Woodworking

### Methods

Artisans and wood scientists have developed methods for controlled spalting:

- ** inoculation**: Introducing selected fungal cultures onto freshly cut wood under controlled moisture and temperature conditions.
- **Air-drying**: Maintaining wood at 25–30% moisture content for 2–8 weeks to allow fungal colonization and pigment development.
- **Killing the fungus**: Once desired spalting is achieved, the wood is dried below 20% moisture content (often kiln-dried at 50–60°C) to halt fungal activity.

### Challenges

- **Timing**: Under-spalted wood lacks visual impact; over-spalted wood loses structural integrity (particularly from white-rot degradation).
- **Species specificity**: Not all wood species spalt equally. Maple, birch, beech, and oak spalt well; dense tropical hardwoods and resinous softwoods resist fungal colonization.
- **Unpredictability**: The exact pattern cannot be predicted — fungal growth is influenced by moisture gradients, temperature fluctuations, wood chemistry, and competing microorganisms.
- **Toxicity concerns**: Some spalting fungi produce [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]] compounds (VOCs) during colonization. Adequate ventilation during processing is recommended.

## Applications Beyond Woodworking

### Biomaterials

Spalted wood pigments are being explored as natural colorants for textiles, paper, and bioplastics. Xylindein's semiconductor properties have generated interest in bio-derived electronic materials.

### Wood Protection

The antifungal properties of some spalting pigments (particularly the red and yellow naphthoquinones) suggest potential applications as natural wood preservatives.

### Bioremediation

The lignin-degrading enzyme systems of spalting fungi are the same systems used in [[staycare-composting-as-a-fungal-bioremediation-strategy]] of environmental pollutants (PAHs, dyes, pharmaceuticals). Understanding pigment production in the context of these enzyme systems provides insight into fungal metabolic regulation.
