# Mollison on Granitic Landscapes

**Source:** Bill Mollison, Permaculture Design Course, Pamphlet VI (Permaculture on Granitic Landscapes), 1981

## Overview

Mollison addresses the special challenges of granitic landscapes: deep permeable sands, rock outcrops, limited water storage, and the strategies for productive design on these difficult sites. Granitic landscapes present a paradox -- they appear barren and challenging, yet with appropriate design they can become remarkably productive. This material connects to [[permaculture-site-analysis]] and [[permaculture-earthworks]].

## The Granitic Landscape Problem

- May have 100 feet of permeable sand; very slight water storage potential

- All organic materials wash down between sand particles; mulch disappears in 6 months

- Example: entire Perth plain of Australia -- 200 foot depth of clean sand

- Water bores go ever deeper and saltier (fresh water flows over stratified salt layers)

- As fresh water pumped off, bore deepens and water gets more expensive/salty

## Essential Strategies

1. **Tree crops**: trees do well despite difficulty explaining why; place big accent on tree crop replacing annual crop

2. **No lawns**: takes 90 inches/year to maintain lawn on siliceous sands; "total disasters"

3. **Small gley ponds, tanks**: very modest bore water use

4. **Plastic-lined garden**: line garden area, then mulch; humic acid reaches roots

5. **Treat like desert**: even if area is reasonably humid

## Rock Microclimates

- Granite reflectivity creates light and heat benefits

- Microclimates allow range from dates to strawberries on same property

- Incorporate rock mass into buildings: under glass, in shade houses, evaporative cooling

- Dig rock out and incorporate into house; good heat stores

- Woman in Sydney designed own house with rocks coming through bedroom and living room walls; evaporative cooling systems with moss and ferns

- Small keylines running all over rock surfaces; little run-off systems into pockets

- Mulch right on top of rocks and beside them; granite flakes off into usable sheets

- One friend: 4 acres granitic soil became "slowly becoming far more productive" than 4 acres of "promising agricultural soil"

## Species for Granitic Soils

- Nearly all palms, many fruits, figs, grapes -- all do well with minimal work

- Olives, date palms, grapes, raspberries, strawberries, marigolds

- Direct little runnels of water to planting pockets among rocks

- Piles of rocks = niches for tomatoes, cucumbers, vines, strawberries, banks, shaded areas

## Below-Garden Gley System (Experimental)

- Dig off earth, put green mat layer in, return earth

- Mollison: "I don't know whether it would work. It might."

- Worth experimenting where other water retention strategies fail

## Design Approach

- Accept natural rock features as part of design (don't fight them)

- Rocks are "amazingly efficient heat stores, very cheap"

- "There is nothing like an old rocky river bed or a pile of stones to work in"

- A pile of big logs creates "a huge amount of niches, and a great potential"

- "Just start people cleaning up the country"

## Water Management on Granitic Soils

Water management is the central challenge and the central design opportunity on granitic landscapes.

- **Permeability problem:** Deep sand profiles (50-200 feet) allow water to drain rapidly beyond the root zone of most plants. Traditional irrigation methods are extremely wasteful on these soils. The solution is to create contained, localized water retention zones rather than trying to irrigate broad areas.

- **Plastic-lined gardens:** Mollison's recommendation to line garden beds with plastic and then add mulch and soil is practical and effective. The plastic barrier prevents water and dissolved humic acids from leaching below the root zone. The humic acids -- which normally wash away in weeks on granitic sand -- remain available to plant roots.

- **Gley ponds:** Small, sealed ponds (using the gley technique of compacted organic matter under anaerobic conditions) provide water storage that doesn't leak into the sand. These ponds can serve as irrigation reservoirs and as [[aquaculture-in-permaculture]] habitat.

- **Rock catchment systems:** Granite outcrops and rock surfaces can serve as water catchment areas. Runnels carved into rock surfaces direct rainfall to planting pockets and small reservoirs. Even small rock surfaces can collect meaningful quantities of water.

- **Keyline adaptations:** Traditional [[keyline-design]] relies on soil's ability to hold and slowly release water. On granitic soils, keylines must be adapted with sealed channels or lined swales that prevent rapid infiltration into the sand profile.

## Rock as Thermal Mass and Building Material

Mollison emphasizes the exceptional thermal properties of granite for both passive heating and cooling applications.

- **Heat storage:** Granite has high thermal mass, meaning it absorbs heat slowly and releases it slowly. Rock walls, rock floors, and rock features incorporated into buildings moderate temperature fluctuations, keeping interiors warm at night and cool during the day.

- **Evaporative cooling:** When water flows over or through rock surfaces, evaporation provides cooling. Mollison describes a Sydney woman's house where rocks penetrate the bedroom and living room walls, with moss and ferns growing on the rock surfaces and water flowing over them for evaporative cooling. This is a natural air conditioning system requiring no energy input.

- **Shade house integration:** Rock mass can be incorporated into shade house structures, with the rock absorbing daytime heat and releasing it during cool nights, extending the growing season for frost-sensitive plants.

- **Construction material:** Granite flakes off into usable sheets that can serve as building stone, paving, and retaining wall material. Digging rock out for building simultaneously creates foundations and thermal mass while producing landscape depressions for water collection.

## Microclimate Creation with Rocks

Rock outcrops create microclimates that dramatically expand the range of species that can be grown on granitic landscapes.

- **Heat reflection:** Light-colored granite reflects sunlight onto adjacent planting areas, increasing light intensity and warmth for sun-loving species.

- **Heat absorption:** Dark granite absorbs solar radiation and re-radiates it at night, creating warm microsites that protect frost-sensitive plants.

- **Wind shelter:** Rock outcrops, rock piles, and boulder fields provide wind shelter for tender plants, reducing transpiration and physical damage.

- **Moisture pockets:** The interface between rock surfaces and soil creates moisture pockets where water accumulates. Planting at the base of rocks takes advantage of these concentrated moisture zones.

- **Species diversity:** Mollison notes that microclimates around rock outcrops allow everything from dates (requiring heat and drought) to strawberries (requiring cool, moist conditions) to grow on the same property. This is extraordinary diversity for a single site.

## Soil Building Strategies

Building productive soil on granitic sand requires targeted strategies that account for rapid leaching.

- **Composted mulch in pockets:** Rather than mulching broad areas (which disappears in 6 months), concentrate compost and mulch in planting pockets among rocks. The rocks protect the mulch from wind and rapid decomposition, while the contained volume retains moisture and nutrients.

- **Nitrogen-fixing pioneer species:** Plant nitrogen-fixing trees and shrubs (acacias, casuarinas, tagasaste) as the first step in building soil. Their leaf litter contributes organic matter, their roots break up compacted layers, and nitrogen fixation adds the nutrient most limiting on granitic soils.

- **Biochar incorporation:** Biochar from [[mollison-dry-distillation-bioenergy]] systems, incorporated into planting pockets, provides long-term carbon that resists leaching on sandy soils and improves nutrient retention.

- **Chicken tractoring:** Mobile chicken coops concentrated on small areas deposit manure and scratch organic matter into the sand surface. Rotated frequently, chickens build soil fertility one small area at a time.

## Economic Opportunities

Granitic landscapes offer unique economic opportunities when designed with permaculture principles.

- **Specialty crops adapted to sandy soils:** Many Mediterranean crops (olives, grapes, figs, dates, lavender, rosemary) are naturally adapted to well-drained granitic soils and command premium market prices.

- **Rock products:** Granite building stone, crushed rock for landscaping, and decorative stone provide income from the very material that makes agriculture challenging.
