# Symbiotic Rice-Barley-Clover Succession Cropping

## Overview

Fukuoka's signature [[boil-a-bag-mushroom-cultivation-method]] — direct-seeded, no-tillage, winter grain
and rice succession in a living clover cover — represents the most complete
practical expression of his natural [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy-and-practice]]. Through this system, he
demonstrated that two staple grain crops could be grown annually on the same land
without plowing, without [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]], without pesticides, and with a
fraction of the labor required by [[microwave-sterilization-versus-conventional-methods-comparison]]. The clover cover performs
the functions that [[fukuoka-critique-modern-agriculture]] assigns to machinery and chemicals: nitrogen
fixation, weed suppression, moisture retention, and soil structure maintenance.

## The Annual Cycle

### Fall: Broadcasting Clover and Winter Grain

The cycle begins in autumn, while the rice crop still stands in the field.
Fukuoka broadcasts a mixture of clover seed and winter grain seed — typically
barley — directly over the ripening rice heads. The seeds fall among the standing
rice stalks and settle onto the soil surface, where they germinate with the
autumn rains. No tilling or soil preparation precedes this seeding; the seeds
lodge in the natural unevenness of the field surface and in the mulch of
decomposing rice leaves.

This timing is critical. By seeding before the rice harvest, Fukuoka eliminates
the need for any separate seeding operation later. The winter grain and clover
establish themselves while the rice completes its maturation, so that by the
time the rice is harvested, a green carpet of young shoots is already emerging.

### Fall Harvest: Treading and Straw Return

When the rice is ready, harvest proceeds by hand. The farmer walks through the
field cutting rice heads, deliberately treading on the young winter grain shoots
in the process. Far from damaging the emerging crop, this treading actually
presses the young plants more firmly into contact with the soil and helps
anchor their root systems. The winter grain is resilient enough to survive the
foot traffic and resumes growth within days.

After cutting, the rice is dried for approximately three days. The threshed
grain is collected, and then — in a step essential to the system's fertility —
**all rice straw is scattered back over the field uncut**. This straw mulch
serves multiple functions: it suppresses weed germination by blocking light,
retains soil moisture by reducing evaporation, and as it decomposes returns
[[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] mineral nutrients to the soil. Optionally, chicken droppings
may be scattered over the straw for additional phosphorus and potassium.

### Winter: Pellet Seeding and Rest

Before the New Year, Fukuoka forms clay pellets containing rice seed — using the
same technique he developed for [[fukuoka-desert-revegetation-clay-pellets]] — and scatters them across
the field. The pellets protect the rice seeds through the winter months, holding
them dormant until conditions favor germination in spring. This winter seeding
eliminates the need for spring transplanting, the most labor-intensive operation
in conventional rice cultivation.

After pellet scattering, there is nothing left to do until the winter grain
harvest. The entire winter period — months of growing season — requires no
active management whatsoever. The clover continues to grow, fixing nitrogen and
building soil. The winter grain matures on its own schedule. The labor of one or
two people is sufficient to manage a quarter-acre field through this phase.

### Late Spring: Winter Grain Harvest and Rice Emergence

In late May, the winter grain — barley — is ready for harvest. At this point,
three things are happening simultaneously across the field: the barley is
ripening for harvest, the clover cover is growing luxuriantly, and rice shoots
are beginning to emerge from the clay pellets scattered the previous winter.
The farmer harvests the barley, allows it to dry, threshes it, and once again
scatters all straw uncut over the field surface.

The timing creates a natural transition. As the barley straw returns to the
field, the clover is at its peak of vegetative growth and the rice seedlings
are establishing themselves within the clover matrix. The system has no bare
soil moment — the ground is continuously covered by living plants or fresh mulch.
