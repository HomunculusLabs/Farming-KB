## Magical Illness Categories in Peruvian Amazon Folk Medicine

## Overview

In the folk medical system of the Peruvian Amazon — particularly among the mestizo population of the Belén slum near Iquitos — diseases are classified into two fundamental categories: **simple illnesses** ( naturales ) and **magical illnesses** ( sobrenaturales ). The magical illness framework is not merely a relic of pre-modern belief; it functions as a culturally coherent system for diagnosing and treating psychosocial distress, interpersonal conflict, and emotional suffering within a community marked by extreme deprivation.

This taxonomy of illness is documented in Michael J. Harner's *Hallucinogens and Shamanism* (1973), drawing on ethnographic fieldwork in the Belén district. The framework is notable for its psychological sophistication — Harner observes that Amazonian drug-healing traditions represent an established method of dealing with psychological problems that predates Freud by centuries.

## The Belén Context

Understanding magical illness requires understanding the social environment in which it thrives. Belén is a riverside slum that serves as the primary receiving area for migrants arriving from the forest interior. The district is characterized by:

- **High unemployment** and chronic economic insecurity
- **Malnutrition**, especially among children
- **Family breakdown**, including abandonment and domestic instability
- **Prostitution** and associated social pathologies
- **Vandalism** and community violence
- **Chronic illness** resulting from unsanitary, overcrowded conditions

### Living Conditions

Belén's residents live in houses built on balsa log supports or floating rafts along the riverbank. For roughly four months of each year, the area is flooded, forcing inhabitants to live with water at or above floor level. This annual inundation compounds health risks from contaminated water, rotting structures, and disrupted sanitation. Fishing, once a primary subsistence activity, has been severely depleted.

### Economic Structure

The local economy is shaped by two key roles:

- **Rematistas** — wholesalers who purchase forest products (rubber, animal skins, timber) from indigenous collectors and resell them at a markup
- **Regatones** — river traders who travel into remote forest communities by motor boat, trading manufactured goods for forest products, often at exploitative exchange rates

These economic relationships extract wealth from the forest while concentrating disadvantage in slum areas like Belén, creating the material conditions in which magical illness flourishes.

## Two Categories of Disease

### Simple Illnesses (Enfermedades Naturales)

These are "God-given" afflictions that arise from natural causes: colds, skin disorders, injuries, and infections. They are treated with herbal remedies, practical first aid, and rest. Simple illnesses have straightforward etiology and respond to conventional folk treatments.

### Magical Illnesses (Enfermedades Sobrenaturales)

When simple treatments fail — when an illness persists, worsens, or appears without clear natural cause — the diagnostic framework shifts. The illness is reclassified as magical, meaning it is attributed to one of two supernatural causes:

1. **The malice of another person** — deliberate sorcery (bewitchment) or the involuntary effects of envy and hostility
2. **The punishment of a natural spirit** — incurred through violation of a taboo

#### Taboo Violations

The Amazonian world is populated by powerful natural spirits (genios) who enforce behavioral codes. Violating these taboos offends the spirits and provokes illness. A classic example: a menstruating woman who bathes in the river before three days have passed risks hemorrhage, because the blood pollutes the water and offends the forest spirit who dwells there. The spirit's response — hemorrhage — is not random but symbolically appropriate: blood in the water offends the spirit, so the spirit causes blood to flow from the woman.

#### Bewitchment (Brujería)

In cases of suspected bewitchment, an enemy is believed to have consulted a witch ( brujo ) who prepares a harmful substance — often a magical preparation ( preparado ) — and introduces it into the victim's food or drink. The resulting illness may present as chronic pain, wasting, organ failure, or psychological disturbance. The social dimension is critical: bewitchment accusations always index existing interpersonal conflicts and community tensions.

## The Four Major Magical Illnesses

Harner identifies four principal categories of magical illness recognized in Belén's folk medical system. Each has a distinct etiology, symptom profile, and social meaning.

### Susto (Fright Illness or Soul Loss)

**Etiology:** Susto is caused by intense psychic trauma — a sudden fright, a terrifying experience, or a shocking event. The trauma is so severe that it causes the soul ( alma ) to flee the body.

**Symptoms:**

- Loss of appetite and progressive weight loss
- Lethargy and lack of energy
- Apathy and social withdrawal
- Sleep disturbances
- A general "wasting away" appearance

**Mechanism:** The concept of soul loss is central. The soul, frightened by the traumatic event, partially or fully separates from the body. Without the soul's animating presence, the physical body deteriorates. Recovery requires a shamanic ritual (often involving ayahuasca) to locate the wandering soul and coax it back into the body.

**Social context:** Susto frequently occurs in environments of chronic stress and sudden danger — precisely the conditions of Belén. Children are especially vulnerable.

### Daño (Harm or Sorcery Illness)

**Etiology:** Daño is caused by human malice, specifically through **envidia** (envy) or **despecho** (vengeance). Someone who harbors resentment toward the victim — perhaps a jealous neighbor, a disgruntled associate, or a former lover — either performs sorcery directly or employs a witch.

**Symptoms:**

- Internal hemorrhaging (unexplained bleeding)
- Severe muscular pain
- Tumors or growths
- **Saladera** — a condition of pervasive bad luck affecting work, relationships, and health simultaneously

**Social context:** Daño diagnoses invariably reflect real or perceived interpersonal conflicts. The accusation of daño is a socially sanctioned way of naming hostility and envy within a community where direct confrontation may be dangerous. Saladera is particularly significant because it extends the illness beyond the physical body into the victim's entire social and economic life.

### Pulsario (Anxiety/Restlessness Illness)

**Etiology:** Pulsario has a complex etiology that blends somatic sensation with emotional repression. It is primarily associated with women and is linked to unexpressed or repressed emotional states — particularly pain, sorrow, and anger.

**Symptoms:**

- Restlessness and inability to sit still
- Hyperactivity and nervous energy
- Free-floating anxiety
- A characteristic somatic sensation described as a "ball at the mouth of the stomach" (a feeling of tightness or pressure in the epigastric region)
- Insomnia

**Social context:** Pulsario can be understood as a culturally shaped expression of what Western psychiatry might diagnose as generalized anxiety disorder, somatization disorder, or depression with anxious features. The "ball at the mouth of the stomach" is a culturally specific body metaphor for emotional distress that has no acceptable social outlet. In Belén's patriarchal social structure, women who cannot express anger or grief directly may somaticize these emotions as pulsario.

### Mal de Ojo (Evil Eye)

**Etiology:** Mal de ojo is caused by the glance of a person who possesses the power to inflict harm simply by looking — whether intentionally or inadvertently. The person casting the evil eye may not even be aware of their power.

**Symptoms:**

- Nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea
- Fever
- Progressive weight loss (wasting)
- Insomnia
- Sadness and irritability (especially in children)

**Social context:** Mal de ojo is most frequently diagnosed in children, who are considered especially vulnerable to the envious or admiring glances of adults. A stranger who comments on a child's beauty or health, for example, may inadvertently cast the evil eye. Protective measures — amulets, specific rituals, keeping children out of sight — are common preventive practices.

## Psychological and Cultural Significance

Harner argues that the magical illness framework, far from being mere superstition, represents a sophisticated indigenous psychology. Several features support this interpretation:

1. **Psychosomatic integration:** The categories explicitly link emotional states (envy, fear, grief, anger) to physical symptoms, anticipating the psychosomatic medicine of the twentieth century.

2. **Social diagnosis:** Magical illness diagnoses embed individual suffering within a social context. Susto names trauma; daño names interpersonal hostility; pulsario names emotional repression; mal de ojo names the dangers of envy and admiration. Each diagnosis is simultaneously a medical and social statement.

3. **Ritual treatment:** Treatment involves ayahuasca-based shamanic ceremonies that provide cathartic experience, community support, and symbolic resolution of the underlying conflict. The ayahuasca experience itself serves as a form of abreactive therapy — a concept that Western psychotherapy would not develop until the late nineteenth century.

4. **Cultural coherence:** The system provides a complete explanatory framework that accounts for illness, assigns responsibility, and prescribes treatment, all within a worldview that is internally consistent and socially functional.

## Comparison with Western Psychiatry

| Magical Illness | Approximate Western Parallel |
|-----------------|------------------------------|
| Susto | PTSD, acute stress disorder, dissociation |
| Daño | Psychosomatic illness secondary to interpersonal conflict |
| Pulsario | Generalized anxiety, somatization, masked depression |
| Mal de Ojo | Factitious illness by proxy, anxiety in caregiving relationships |

The comparison is imperfect — the magical categories carry cosmological implications (soul loss, spirit punishment, sorcery) that have no Western equivalent. However, the pragmatic overlap in symptom profiles and therapeutic approaches (catharsis, social support, symbolic meaning-making) is striking.
