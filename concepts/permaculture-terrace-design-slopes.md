# Terrace Design for Slopes

Terracing is a foundational earthworks strategy in permaculture for managing sloped land. By converting gradients into a series of level platforms, terraces enable productive polyculture, control erosion, and harvest water and nutrients. Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual* treats terracing as one of the most important tools for stabilising and enriching sloping landscapes.

## Core Benefits of Terracing

- **Easy crop access** — Level surfaces allow planting, weeding, and harvesting without negotiating steep ground.
- **Controlled irrigation** — Water can be directed precisely across each terrace rather than running off downslope.
- **Minimal soil loss** — Terraces interrupt the velocity of overland flow, preventing the wholesale removal of topsoil.
- **Nutrient gain** — Dissolved nutrients in run-off and leaf fall from upslope are captured on each successive terrace, building fertility over time.

## Slope Considerations

Terracing is **most effective on moderate slopes**, ideally between **10–18°**. On gentler slopes the cost-benefit may not justify the earthworks; on steeper slopes, construction becomes significantly more labour-intensive and erosion risk increases.

### Moderate Slopes (10–18°)

This is the optimal range for full terrace systems. Construction proceeds methodically:

1. Begin on the **lowest terrace** first.
2. Strip and **stockpile topsoil** from the terrace area before shaping the bund.
3. Form the level platform, then redistribute the saved topsoil over the surface.
4. Continue uphill, using material excavated from the next terrace to help build the bund below.

### Steep Sites (20°+)

On steep terrain, Mollison recommends a more conservative approach:

- Create only **4–6 narrow production terraces** rather than a continuous system.
- Maintain **permanent vegetated slopes** between the terraces to anchor soil and slow runoff.
- Use **hand-cut terracing exclusively** — machinery is inappropriate and potentially destructive on steep grades.

## Design Principles

### Bund Stability

The bund (the riser or retaining wall of each terrace) must be stable over the long term. Two primary approaches:

- **Rock-walled bunds** — Durable, permeable, and well-suited to rocky sites. Stones are laid to create a stable retaining face that allows drainage while holding the terrace platform.
- **1:3 earthen slope bunds** — Where rock is unavailable, bunds can be shaped from compacted earth at a slope ratio of 1 vertical to 3 horizontal. Grass or groundcover is essential to prevent slumping.

### Trees on Bunds

Mollison emphasises that **trees planted on bunds should form 40–60% of the landscape** in a terraced slope system. Tree roots reinforce the bund structure, canopy intercepts rainfall reducing splash erosion, and leaf litter contributes organic matter to the terrace below. This tree component transforms a simple structural terrace into a productive polycultural system.

### Terrace Proportions

The area devoted to terracing should be kept within ecological limits:

| Climate | Maximum Terrace Area |
|---------|---------------------|
| Tropical / humid | ≤ 30% of total slope area |
| Dryland / arid | ≤ 5% of total slope area |

In **humid areas**, **10–20%** of the terrace system can be designated as **wet terraces** devoted to **fish-plant polyculture** — integrating aquaculture with crop production for maximum yield per unit area.

## Polycultural Terraces

Terraces are ideal platforms for polyculture. The level surface allows intercropping of:

- **Wet crops** (in low, irrigated pockets or dedicated wet terraces)
- **Dry crops** (on well-drained upper portions)
- **Tree crops** (on bunds and upper terrace edges)

This layered approach maximises productive use of space, light, and water while the terrace structure itself manages the hydraulic gradient across the slope.

## Construction Methods

### Sequential Bottom-Up Construction

1. Survey the slope and mark contour lines for each terrace.
2. Begin excavation at the **lowest bench**, cutting into the slope and using the spoil to form the bund on the downhill side.
3. **Remove and stockpile topsoil** before subsoil is disturbed.
4. After the terrace platform is shaped, **replace the topsoil** evenly across the surface.
5. Plant bunds immediately with deep-rooted trees and groundcovers.
6. Repeat the process moving **uphill**, with each successive terrace feeding material to the one below.

### Hand-Cut vs. Machine Construction

- **Hand-cut** terracing is preferred on slopes above 20° and in sensitive environments where soil compaction or damage to existing vegetation must be minimised.
- **Machinery** may be appropriate on moderate slopes (10–18°) with access, but operators must take care to avoid compaction of the terrace platform and bund.

## Earth Constructs

Beyond agricultural terraces, Mollison extends the principle of shaped earth to a wide range of functional constructs:

### Shelter and Structures

- **Raised banks** for shelter and windbreaks around settlements and animal systems.
- **Compacted earth walls** for houses and barns — a low-energy, thermally massive building material.
- **Earth ramps and stands** for elevated viewpoints, animal handling, or loading.

### Water Management

- **Polders and levees** for flood and tide control in low-lying coastal or riparian zones.
- **Wind and water deflection patterns** — shaped earth forms that redirect prevailing winds or channel water flow for passive irrigation or frost protection.
- **Tracks in marshes** — raised earthen paths that provide dry passage through wetland areas.

### Boundary and Safety

- **"Ha ha" fences** — a below-grade ditch with a vertical retaining wall on the house-side, creating a barrier to livestock without interrupting the visual continuity of the landscape. The name derives from the expression of surprise when encountering the sudden drop.
- **Containments for inflammable fluids** — earth berms and bunds designed to contain spills around fuel storage.
- **Noise deflectors** — earthen mounds placed to absorb or redirect sound from roads, machinery, or other sources.

### Infrastructure Grading

- **Rail, canal, and road grades** — carefully designed earthworks that maintain appropriate gradients for transport and water movement across varied terrain.

## Integration with Broader Design

Terrace systems do not exist in isolation. They should be integrated with:

- **Swales and diversion drains** upslope to manage incoming water before it reaches the terraces.
- **Access paths** that follow terrace contours for easy movement.
- **Zone planning** — intensive terrace crops belong in Zone 1–2, while tree-cropped bunds may extend into Zone 3.
- **Water storage** — each terrace can incorporate small ponds or soakaways for passive irrigation.

## Key Summary

| Factor | Guideline |
|--------|-----------|
| Optimal slope | 10–18° |
| Steep slope strategy | 4–6 narrow terraces with vegetated inter-slopes |
| Bund types | Rock-walled or 1:3 earthen slope |
| Tree cover on bunds | 40–60% of landscape |
| Max terrace area (tropical) | 30% |
| Max terrace area (dryland) | 5% |
| Wet terrace allocation (humid) | 10–20% for fish-plant polyculture |
| Construction direction | Bottom-up, topsoil stockpiled first |
| Construction method (steep) | Hand-cut only |
