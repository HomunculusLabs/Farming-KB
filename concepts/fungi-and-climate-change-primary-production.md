## Fungi and Climate Change Effects on Primary Production

Climate change — driven by rising atmospheric CO₂ concentrations and associated temperature increases — profoundly affects fungal communities and their [[fungi-in-the-environment-symbiotic-relationships]] with plants. Understanding these interactions is critical because fungi mediate nutrient cycling, decomposition, and carbon storage processes that determine ecosystem responses to global change.

## The Carbon Sink Challenge

Rising atmospheric CO₂ from industrialization, fossil fuel combustion, and deforestation has prompted international efforts to increase terrestrial carbon sinks through forest plantations:

- US land-based carbon sinks: 0.3–5.8 × 10¹⁵ g C (Pacala et al., 2001)
- China's mean annual carbon accumulation: 0.021 × 10¹⁵ g C over 30 years
- Chinese planted forests alone sequestered 0.45 × 10¹⁵ g C

The critical question is how carbon allocation within forest structure involves fungal components, both as mycorrhizae and saprotrophs.

## Model of Climate Change Effects on Mycorrhizae

Dighton and Jansen (1991) proposed three scenarios for how elevated CO₂ and temperature affect tree–ectomycorrhizal symbiosis:

### Scenario A: Elevated CO₂ Only
Increased photosynthesis provides larger carbohydrate pools to support:
- Enhanced mycorrhizal development and fruiting
- Greater community diversity
- Expanded extraradical hyphal exploitation of soil
- Increased C:N ratio of plant litter, making mycorrhizal saprotrophic function more important

### Scenario B: Elevated Temperature
Warming stimulates saprotrophic microbial communities, increasing:
- Litter decomposition rates
- [[nutrient-mineralization]]
- Available nutrient pool sizes

This "fertilizer effect" reduces plant dependence on mycorrhizae, potentially declining [[mycorrhizal-diversity-and-ecosystem-function]] soil exploitation.

### Scenario C: Combined CO₂ and Temperature with Changed Litter Chemistry
Enhanced nitrogen content in leaf litter improves resource quality, forcing mycorrhizal fungi to compete with saprotrophs for mineralized nutrients by increasing diversity and extraradical hyphal soil exploitation.

## Predicted Impacts on Mycorrhizal Types

The model suggests climate change affects mycorrhizal types differently:

- **Ectomycorrhizal and ericoid mycorrhizal communities** (dominating cool and boreal ecosystems) are more strongly affected, as the greatest NPP increases are predicted distant from the Equator and in cool-adapted plants.
- **Arbuscular mycorrhizal communities** (dominating tropical and grassland ecosystems) are less affected by these predicted changes.
- As plants colonize new climatic zones, the role of mycorrhizal fungi in preventing pathogens and accessing scarce nutrients will increase in importance, while fungal pathogens may reduce populations of displaced plant species.

## Mycorrhizal Responses to Elevated CO₂

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizae

Research has documented significant responses:

- A Mediterranean *Bromus madritensis* monoculture at 700 μmol mol⁻¹ CO₂ showed: 31% root biomass increase, 88% root length increase, 42% [[soil-microbial-biomass-measurement-ecological-significance]] increase, 20% soil fungal hyphal length increase, and 57% increase in total AM colonization.
- Five to six times greater carbon translocation to mycorrhizal vs. non-mycorrhizal root systems in *Carrizo citrange* seedlings.
- Mycorrhizal *Panicum* plants showed 20% increased carbon exchange rate, 45% increased photosynthate storage in leaves, and more than doubled sink activity to roots.

### Ectomycorrhizae

Species-dependent responses have been documented:

- *[[laccaria-laccata]]* with oak showed more-than-additive effects on stem height, diameter, and lateral root formation under elevated CO₂.
- *[[suillus-bovinus]]* (nitrogen-poor environment specialist) translocated extra carbon below ground into root and fungal biomass, yielding plant nitrogen contents twice as high as *L. laccata*-colonized trees.
- *Laccaria laccata* (nitrogen-rich environment specialist) respired additional carbohydrate rather than incorporating it into biomass.
- Douglas fir seedlings showed increased total mycorrhizae under elevated CO₂ and increased diversity with elevated temperature, with *Rhizopogon* and *Cenococcum* morphotypes dominating.

### Biomass Enhancement

Oak and pine seedlings produced 56–66% more biomass under doubled CO₂ due to enhanced mycorrhizal colonization, with increased photosynthesis offsetting the carbon drain imposed by greater mycorrhizal colonization.

## Below-Ground Carbon Allocation

### Root Respiration and Carbon Flux

Root systems and their mycorrhizal components represent major carbon sinks that are frequently overlooked:

- Nearly two-thirds of soil respiration in temperate mixed hardwood forests comes from root activity.
- Root litter decomposition contributes 70–80% of total soil respiration across forested ecosystems.
- Coniferous trees allocate approximately twice as much carbon to roots as deciduous species — a trade-off related to nitrogen acquisition, as conifer leaf litter has low quality and low N mineralization rates.

### Root Contributions to Litter and Nutrients

In heathland ecosystems, *Molinia* root turnover contributed:
- 67% of total litter production
- 87% of litter nitrogen loss
- 84% of total litter phosphorus loss

### Mycorrhizal Carbon Flow

The carbon drain to sustain mycorrhizae is substantial but offset by functional benefits:

- Photosynthesis is enhanced in mycorrhizal plants.
- Between-plant carbon transfer occurs through mycorrhizal bridges, optimized from source plants (full sun) to sink plants (shade), supporting whole-community resource conservation rather than purely competitive interactions.
- This transfer occurs both within and between tree species in field conditions.

## Inconsistency in Below-Ground Responses

The response of soil organisms to elevated CO₂ is notably inconsistent:

- Increased CO₂ and temperature: increased microbial carbon but decreased metabolic quotient.
- Temperature alone: variable responses.
- A review of 47 publications on soil C and N cycling showed 80–800% coefficients of variation between life forms.
- General patterns: greater plant growth, more below-ground carbon input, greater microbial [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] — but magnitude and direction vary case-by-case.

## Importance of Below-Ground Indicators

Vogt et al. (1993) and O'Neill (1994) emphasized that soil organisms are sensitive to environmental stress and may show responses before observable above-ground symptoms. Fine root growth, turnover, and mycorrhizal status and function are among the most useful early indicators of environmental change.
