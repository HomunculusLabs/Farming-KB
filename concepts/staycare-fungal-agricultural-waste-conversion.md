# Fungal Conversion of Agricultural Wastes

Fungi play a central role in converting agricultural and agro-industrial by-products into valuable products through [[fungal-bioremediation]]. Recognition of biological materials as renewable resources has revived interest in the ancient technology of fungal solid-state fermentation (SSF), which remains widely practised in the Orient for miso, sake, soy sauce, and tempe production.

## Solid-State Fermentation (SSF) on Agricultural Wastes

SSF involves microbial growth on a moist, solid substrate in the absence of free water. Conditions resemble the natural growth habitat for filamentous fungi, and the process utilizes cheap raw materials and residues from agricultural food industries. Compared with submerged culture, SSF offers:

- Better product quality
- Higher product yield and concentration
- Cheaper processing of solid feeds
- Less wastewater and off-gas production
- Simpler cultivation equipment and lower operating costs

## Five Categories of Fungal Bioconversion

The SSF process on agricultural wastes can be divided into five main types:

1. **Degradation of cellulose and starch** for production of protein-rich animal feed
2. **Degradation of lignin** in lignocellulose to give cellulases access to cellulose for ruminant feed, saccharides, and ethanol production
3. **Degradation of organic matter** for production of pure enzymes
4. **Production of specific biochemicals** such as organic acids and saccharides
5. **Composting**: conversion of mixed organic waste into stable organic product through [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-biology]]

## Key Extracellular Enzymes

Fungal conversion of agricultural wastes involves secretion of different exoenzymes:

### Amylases
[[aspergillus-niger-in-fungal-bioleaching]] and [[aspergillus-niger-in-fungal-bioleaching]] secrete considerable quantities of amylases used extensively in the fermentation industry. A. oryzae is a commercial source of alpha-amylase and glucoamylase used for producing high-glucose syrups. A. foetidus produces high levels of extracellular alpha-amylase from rice starch and can treat starch-containing wastewaters.

### Cellulases
Cellulases consist of three major activities: endoglucanase, exocellulase (cellobiohydrolase), and glucosidase. True cellulolytic fungi include *Sclerotium rolfsii*, [[singh-cytochrome-p450-fungal-bioremediation]], [[ingham-trichoderma-biocontrol-fungi-aact]], and [[aspergillus-niger-in-fungal-bioleaching]] and penicillium entity species. Genetically improved strains of T. reesei are used for rapid cellulase synthesis.

### Xylanases
Complete xylan degradation requires endo-1,4-beta-xylanase, beta-xylosidase, beta-glucuronidase, and acetylesterase. Applications include bioconversion of lignocelluloses, juice clarification, plant oil extraction, improvement of silage and green feed nutritional value, and pulp bleaching. [[aspergillus-niger-in-fungal-bioleaching]] produces cellulase-free xylanase on sugar cane bagasse, wheat bran, and corn cob.

### Pectinases
Pectinases degrade pectic substances in plant cell walls. They are used for fruit juice extraction and clarification, wine clarification, plant oil extraction, and linen manufacture. Production is 4-6 times higher in SSF than submerged fermentation for [[aspergillus-niger-in-fungal-bioleaching]].

### Lignin-Degrading Enzymes
See [[fungal-ligninolytic-enzyme-systems-overview]]. White-rot fungi produce lignin peroxidases (LiPs), manganese peroxidases (MnPs), and laccases. [[stamets-pleurotus-oyster-cultivation]] and P. eryngii degrade lignin preferentially in wheat straw and cotton stalks without LiP, using aryl alcohol oxidase for H2O2 production necessary for MnP action.

## Agro-Industrial Waste Substrates

### Lignocellulosic Wastes
Comprising crop residues and forestry wastes, lignocelluloses contain cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Biological delignification of straw by white-rot fungi, particularly [[pleurotus-tuberregium-sclerotia-cultivation]] species, is the most promising approach for improving digestibility for ruminant feed.

### Corn Cobs
Used for xylanase production (A. tamarii, Thermomyces lanuginosus) and glucose production (T. viride). P. ostreatus grown on corn cobs increased dietary fibre content up to 78% and significantly prevented colon cancer development in rats.

### Sugar Cane Bagasse
An inexpensive, abundant energy source for xylitol production (Candida guilliermondii), single-cell protein (Candida blankii), and xylanase production. About 10^8 tonnes of roots are produced annually in the EU.

### Coffee Pulp
One of the most abundant and hardest-to-handle agricultural wastes. A. niger under SSF conditions improves nutritional quality for animal feed by increasing amino acid content and reducing caffeine. Five Aspergillus strains and two Penicillium strains can degrade almost 100% of caffeine.

### Sugar Beet Pulp
Low lignin content enhances suitability for biotransformation. Used for protein production (thermophilic microorganisms) and nutritional upgrading by white-rot fungi.

### Cotton Stalks
India produces 10 million tons annually. Anaerobic preservation produces silage suitable for commercial [[pleurotus-tuberregium-sclerotia-cultivation]] cultivation up to 9 months after harvest.

### Okara
Soybean residue from tofu production. Fermentation by Rhizopus oligosporus and A. oryzae improves nutritional qualities as a high-fibre, low-energy foodstuff with cholesterol-lowering and antioxidant properties.

## Role of Fungi in Composting

When wastes cannot serve as pure-culture substrates, composting is the treatment of choice. The process has three phases:

1. **Mesophilic phase** (5-37°C): Bacteria dominate initially, temperature rises
2. **Thermophilic phase** (45-70°C): Thermophilic fungi (Talaromyces emersonii, Thermomyces lanuginosus, Chaetomium thermophilium) degrade lignocellulose and produce laccase
3. **Cooling phase**: Mesophilic and thermotolerant fungi reappear

### Compost Maturity and Biocontrol
Humification during composting involves polymerization of aromatic compounds and lignin modifications by fungal laccase. Mature compost builds antagonistic microorganism populations that suppress soilborne plant pathogens. [[mushroom-contamination-control-strategies]] species and Gliocladium virens are effective biocontrol agents in compost-amended substrates. Compost must be of consistent quality for successful biological control of horticultural crop diseases.
