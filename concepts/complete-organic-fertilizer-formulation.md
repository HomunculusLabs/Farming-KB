# Complete Organic Fertilizer Formulation

## Overview

Complete Organic Fertilizer (COF) is a precisely balanced, homemade organic fertilizer blend developed for food gardens that prioritizes [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] of produce over maximum bulk yield. Unlike commercial fertilizers that push potassium levels to inflate crop weight at the expense of protein, vitamin, and mineral content, COF provides a balanced ratio of nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium, magnesium, and trace elements that produces nutrient-dense vegetables. The formulation is designed to be mixed by the gardener from bulk agricultural ingredients, making it economical for serious home food production.

## The Nutritional Quality Problem

Research compiled from USDA data over the 20th century shows a steady decline in the vitamin and mineral content of commercially grown fruits and vegetables. Analyses demonstrate that the mineral content of table vegetables has declined approximately 2 to 25 percent across most nutrients over the past 25 years.

Most commercial growers, whether chemical or organic, push soil potassium to high levels because potassium increases bulk yield measured as weight. However, the increased yield comes almost entirely in the form of starch and fiber (calories), while protein, vitamins, enzymes, minerals, and other micronutrients actually decrease proportionally.

This nutritional dilution means that eating modern produce provides less nourishment per calorie than the same vegetables grown decades ago. COF is formulated to reverse this trend by prioritizing the nutritional density of food over its weight. A garden fertilized exclusively with COF was found to produce vegetables with dramatically higher nutritional content compared to commercially grown equivalents.

## Core Formula

The standard COF formula is measured by volume (scoops, bucketfuls, jarfuls) rather than weight, which makes home preparation practical. Proportions varying plus or minus 10 percent will work out to be close enough. Do not attempt to make this formula by weight. The blend consists of the following components mixed uniformly:

### Nitrogen Base (choose one option)

**Option A: Standard nitrogen blend**
- 4 parts any kind of seedmeal except cottonseed meal
- 1 part "tankage" (sometimes called blood-and-bone or meatmeal)

**Option B: Leafy crop spring blend**
- 3 parts any seedmeal except cottonseed meal
- 2 parts tankage (slightly higher nitrogen for leafy spring crops)

**Option C: Economy blend**
- 4 parts cottonseed meal (one third weaker in NPK than other seedmeals, but coconut-derived so it is a sustainable byproduct)
- 1 part tankage to boost nitrogen content

Seedmeal is the byproduct remaining after oil is pressed from oilseeds such as soybeans, canola, linseed (flax), sunflower, cottonseed, and safflower. Most seedmeals analyze at approximately 6-4-2 NPK when chemically analyzed, though cottonseed meal is about one third weaker.

The general rule for selecting seedmeal is to buy whichever provides the most protein per unit cost. All seedmeals provide other nutritional components besides NPK, including calcium, magnesium, and trace nutrients. Because seedmeal is a secondary product of the oil pressing industry, it is inexpensive and available in bulk.

Tankage, a slaughterhouse byproduct made from the whole animal minus the prime cuts, analyzes at approximately 10-4-0 and provides a potent nitrogen boost. The recommended form of tankage should be kept dry and protected from moisture with a tight lid. Stored properly, it remains viable for more than 20 years. If the tankage appears overly rich in nitrates, slightly more of it may be needed to achieve equivalent results.

### Mineral Base (blend with chosen nitrogen base)

- 1 part ordinary agricultural lime (finely ground, relatively pure calcium carbonate)
- 1/4 part gypsum (calcium sulfate) — if gypsum is not used, double the quantity of agricultural lime
- 1/2 part dolomite lime (provides both calcium and magnesium in roughly equal amounts)
- 1 part phosphorus source: finely ground rock phosphate (hard or soft), bonemeal, or high-phosphate guano
- 1/2 part kelpmeal or 1/2 part basalt dust

## Understanding the Lime Components

Three types of lime are included in COF, each serving a distinct nutritional purpose:

- **Agricultural lime** provides relatively pure calcium carbonate as a source of calcium
- **Dolomite lime** supplies both calcium and magnesium in roughly equal proportions
- **Gypsum** (calcium sulfate) contributes calcium and sulfur without affecting soil pH

The deliberate combination of all three types distributes calcium and magnesium in the right balance for vegetable nutrition. Quicklime, burnt lime, hydrated lime, and other chemically processed limes should never be used. If forced to choose only one lime type, agricultural lime is the safest single choice, but results are far better when all three are combined.

Limes are inexpensive when purchased in large sacks from agricultural suppliers. The recommended total application of lime materials is approximately 50 pounds per 1,000 square feet (25 kilograms per 100 square meters) per year when used as part of the COF system.

## Phosphorus Sources

Rock phosphate (either hard or soft), bonemeal, and high-phosphate guano all serve as phosphorus sources in the COF formula. Rock phosphate is the most economical choice for large-scale gardeners and is available in bulk from agricultural suppliers. There are two kinds of rock phosphate: "hard" and "soft," with soft rock phosphate being more readily available to plants. Bonemeal provides faster [[soil-ph-management]] ment. Rather than testing and adjusting pH through targeted liming, the COF system provides calcium, magnesium, and organic matter in the correct proportions.

Over time, the pH self-corrects, driven more by the accumulation of organic matter than by the direct addition of calcium and magnesium. If a soil test shows low pH, the recommendation is not to lime specifically to correct it, but rather to continue with the standard COF application schedule. The soil improving materials in COF will gradually bring pH into an appropriate range.

This approach reflects the controversial nature of the soil pH concept in [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]], where the focus shifts from chemical pH values to the biological health and [[holmgren-mineral-balance-soil-fertility]] of the soil ecosystem. If a soil test shows acceptable pH, the full COF recommendation should still be followed because vegetables still need calcium and magnesium in the right balance regardless of pH readings.

## Economic Advantages

The primary economic advantage of COF is that all ingredients can be purchased in bulk sacks from agricultural supply and grain dealers at a fraction of the cost of equivalent products from garden centers. A complete 100-square-meter garden can be fertilized with COF for roughly one quarter to one third of what equivalent commercial [[hamilton-organic-fertilizers-and-plant-nutrition]] would cost.

The long-term health savings from consuming nutrient-dense produce further multiply the economic benefit. When calculating the true cost of food production, the nutritional value delivered per dollar spent makes COF one of the most cost-effective approaches to home food production available.

## Complementary Soil Management Practices

COF works best as part of an integrated soil management strategy rather than as a standalone input. Several complementary practices enhance its effectiveness.

### Compost integration

COF is designed to be used alongside modest amounts of compost or manure, not as a replacement for organic matter. Compost provides humus that improves soil structure, water-holding capacity, and biological activity, while COF supplies the specific mineral nutrients that compost alone cannot provide in balanced proportions. The combination produces results superior to either input used alone.
