## What Are Soil Horizons

A soil profile is a vertical cross-section of the soil, revealing distinct layers called horizons. These layers form over time through the combined action of weathering, organic matter deposition, and biological activity. Soil scientists assign letter designations to each horizon, allowing consistent description of soils worldwide.

For gardeners and farmers, the top horizons -- the O and A -- are the most important because they contain the majority of organic matter, microbial life, and root activity. Understanding your soil profile helps determine how deep your topsoil extends, whether compaction layers exist, and what management practices are needed.

## The O Horizon (Organic Layer)

The O horizon sits on top of the mineral soil and consists primarily of organic material in various stages of decomposition. It is subdivided into three layers based on the degree of decomposition:

- **Oi horizon (fibric)**: The least decomposed layer. Plant materials are still recognizable -- you can identify specific leaves, twigs, and other debris. This layer is actively being colonized by fungi and bacteria.
- **Oe horizon (hemic/humic)**: Partially decomposed organic matter. Materials are clearly plant-derived but individual species cannot be identified. Decomposition is well underway with significant fungal and bacterial activity.
- **Oa horizon (sapric)**: The most decomposed layer. Organic material has broken down so completely that its original origin is indistinguishable. This material approaches the state of humus.

The O horizon is crucial for the soil food web. It is where most surface decomposition occurs, where leaf litter is broken down by fungi, bacteria, springtails, mites, and other detritivores. The thickness of the O horizon is a direct indicator of soil health -- thin O horizons suggest rapid decomposition or disturbance, while thick O horizons indicate steady organic matter accumulation.

## The A Horizon (Topsoil)

The A horizon lies directly beneath the O horizon and is what most people think of as topsoil. Here, humus particles accumulate as water percolating through the O horizon carries dissolved and suspended organic materials downward. The A horizon has the highest organic matter content of any mineral soil layer and the highest biological activity -- this is where most plant roots grow and where the [[soil-food-web]] is most active.

Key characteristics of the A horizon:
- Dark color from accumulated humus
- Granular or crumb structure from biological activity
- High populations of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes
- Active nutrient cycling driven by root exudates and microbial metabolism
- Good aggregation from biological glues including [[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]]

In disturbed soils (conventionally farmed or heavily tilled), the A horizon is often thin or absent entirely. Topsoil erosion removes the A horizon, exposing less fertile subsoil. Building and maintaining a deep A horizon is one of the primary goals of [[soil-food-web]] gardening.

## The E Horizon (Eluviation Layer)

The E horizon, when present, is a light-colored zone where minerals and organic matter have been leached downward (eluviated) by water movement. Clay particles, iron, aluminum, and dissolved organic compounds are stripped from this layer and carried to the B horizon below. The E horizon is common in forested soils but may be absent in many agricultural and garden soils.

## The B Horizon (Subsoil)

The B horizon is the zone of accumulation (illuviation). Materials leached from the A and E horizons -- particularly clays, iron oxides, and dissolved organic compounds -- accumulate here. The B horizon typically has:
- Higher clay content than the A horizon
- Distinctive colors from iron oxide accumulation (reddish, yellowish, or brownish)
- Blocky or prismatic structure
- Lower organic matter content
- Reduced biological activity compared to the A horizon

Tree roots and deep-rooted perennials may extend into the B horizon, accessing minerals that have been transported from surface layers.

## The C Horizon (Parent Material)

The C horizon consists of weathered parent rock or unconsolidated geological material. It has undergone minimal soil development and provides the mineral foundation from which the upper horizons develop. Plant roots rarely penetrate this layer, though deep taproots of certain species may reach it.

## The R Layer (Bedrock)

The R layer is the underlying unweathered bedrock. It is not considered part of the true soil but represents the geological substrate from which the soil ultimately derives.

## How Soil Horizons Form

Soil horizon development (pedogenesis) results from four main factors:

1. **Parent material**: The underlying rock or geological deposit determines the initial mineral composition of the soil.
2. **Climate**: Temperature and precipitation drive weathering rates, organic matter accumulation, and leaching intensity. Warm, wet climates develop deeper soils faster than cold, dry climates.
3. **Organisms**: Plants, animals, and microorganisms contribute organic matter, create channels, and drive nutrient cycling. Plant roots physically break up parent material and deposit organic compounds. Burrowing animals mix layers. Microbial activity creates humus from organic debris.
4. **Topography**: Slope position affects water movement, erosion, and deposition. Valley bottoms accumulate material; ridges and slopes lose it.
5. **Time**: Soil development is measured in centuries to millennia. Young soils have weakly developed horizons; old soils have strongly differentiated profiles.

## Human Impact on Soil Horizons

Conventional agricultural practices dramatically alter soil profiles:

- **Tillage** mixes the O and A horizons, destroying the natural stratification that supports distinct microbial communities. Repeated tillage accelerates organic matter oxidation, thinning both the O and A horizons over time.
- **Erosion** removes the A horizon entirely, exposing less fertile subsoil. The United States has lost roughly one-third of its original topsoil since European settlement.
- **Compaction** from heavy equipment creates artificial hardpans within the A or B horizon, restricting root penetration and water movement.

## The Rhizosphere and Soil Horizons

The [[rhizosphere-fungal-community-analysis-rrna-rdna]] -- the zone of microbial activity immediately surrounding plant roots -- is concentrated in the A horizon. Root exudates fuel bacterial and fungal populations, which in turn cycle nutrients back to the plant. The health of the A horizon directly determines the productivity of the rhizosphere.

## Soil Horizons and the Soil Food Web

Different horizons support different components of the soil food web:
- The O horizon supports decomposer fungi, bacteria, springtails, mites, and earthworms that break down surface litter.
- The A horizon hosts the most diverse microbial community, including nitrogen-fixing bacteria, mycorrhizal fungi, protozoa, and bacterial-feeding nematodes.
- The B horizon has reduced biological activity but may contain anaerobic bacteria and specialized fungi.

Understanding your soil profile helps you manage each layer appropriately. Building a thick O horizon through mulching, maintaining a biologically active A horizon through compost and compost tea, and avoiding compaction of the B horizon are all practices that support a thriving soil food web.

## Reading Your Soil Profile

Gardeners can assess their soil profile by digging a small pit (about 30-60 cm deep) and observing the layers. Key things to look for:
- Depth of dark, organic-rich topsoil (A horizon)
- Presence or absence of surface organic layer (O horizon)
- Color changes indicating different horizons
- Root penetration depth
- Signs of compaction or hardpan layers
- Evidence of earthworm activity

A healthy garden soil should have at least 15-20 cm of dark, crumbly topsoil with visible organic matter incorporation and active root penetration throughout.
