# Permaculture Windbreak Design

updated: 2026-18-04

Windbreak design in permaculture uses strategic placement of trees, shrubs, and structures to modify wind speed, reduce evaporation, protect crops and livestock, and create beneficial microclimates. Chapter 5 of Mollison's Designers' Manual provides extensive guidance on windbreak species, placement, and establishment.

## Windbreak Functions

Windbreaks serve multiple functions in permaculture design:
- Reduce wind chill around houses, saving 20-30% on heating fuels in cold winters
- Reduce ground temperatures up to 15 degrees C in deserts by blocking advected heat
- Protect livestock from blizzards and heat stress (unsheltered sheep weigh 15% less)
- Reduce crop evapotranspiration and physical damage
- Create sheltered microclimates for gardens and orchards
- Direct and funnel winds for ventilation or wind power
- Provide wildlife habitat, forage, timber, and mulch

## Design Principles

The basic strategy is a directional, patterned windbreak of earthbank and trees. On sites where severe winds may come from any direction (some deserts), impose a close rectangular or network pattern of windbreak.

For any windbreak, buildings, gardens, and animal shelters can be arranged to face the sun and benefit from solar impact on the lee (sheltered) side.

## Species Selection

Essential criteria for windbreak species:
- Good pioneer species (easily grown)
- 40-50% penetrability in the front line or as dominants
- Resistance to desiccation and sandblast

Common adaptive features of wind-resistant plants:
- Fibrous stems (palms)
- Fleshy leaves (aloes, agaves, Euphorbias)
- Hard, needle-like leaves or stems (pines, tamarisks, Casuarinas, Acacias)
- Hairy (tomentose) or waxy leaf covers (Coprosma, eucalypts)

Many fire-resistant plants are also wind resistant, and some drought-resistant but fire-prone species (pines) will withstand wind. Local lists of species for windbreak are often available from forestry and agricultural departments.

## Placement and Density

Windbreak effectiveness extends approximately 10-15 times the height of the windbreak downwind. Maximum protection occurs at 3-5 times the height. Optimal density is 40-50% penetrability -- solid barriers create turbulence on the lee side, while too-open barriers provide insufficient protection.

On coasts and in deserts, it is not until after the fourth or even fifth tree row that wind-prone fruit or nut trees will yield. Windbreak is the first priority for gardens in these situations. Windward rows will be heavily wind-flagged; taller species should be placed in their lee.

Small openings of 6-9 m in windbreak forests near coasts provide garden shelter while admitting light.

## Establishment Methods

Initial protection for young windbreak plantings:
- Individual open-ended plastic bags around stakes (common and effective)
- Earth mounds or side-cast earth banks longer than the tree line
- Brush fences, wire-mesh fences, or staked fences with 40% penetrability
- Tussock or tough unmown grass to windward (leave if present)

Ongoing establishment needs:
- Periodic or trickle irrigation to reduce desiccation
- Anchoring by stones or mulch
- Good pioneer species selection

## Earthbank Windbreaks

Earth mounds can be better streamlined than fences, being less sensitive to windthrow. The hollow from which earth is taken to make the mound can hold water or protect young plants. Earthbanks plus shelterbelt, combined with sun-facing aspect, is a critical design strategy in cold areas.

## Shelterbelt Effects on Livestock

Blizzards kill livestock and newborn lambs. Fences must not form downwind or downslope traps where herds pile up and smother. All moorland and high plateau fences should allow easy downwind escape to woodlots or sheltered valleys. Shelterbelt is one of the most effective ways to increase livestock production and conserve rations.

## Multi-Function Windbreaks

Windbreaks should serve multiple functions: timber production, wildlife corridors, forage systems, mulch production, nitrogen fixation, and bee forage. A matrix of shelterbelt species 10-16 m in height across croplands can increase cotton yields by 17.4% within five times the height of the shelterbelt, while general crop yields increase 18.5% in shelterbelt systems.
