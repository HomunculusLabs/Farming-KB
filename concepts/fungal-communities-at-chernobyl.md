# Fungal Communities at Chernobyl

The 1986 Chernobyl nuclear disaster created an unprecedented natural laboratory for studying how fungal communities respond to intense, sustained radiation exposure. Research over the subsequent decades has revealed remarkable fungal resilience, adaptive radiotropism, and significant shifts in community structure [[singh-fungal-biofilms-in-contaminated-environments]].

## Community Structure Changes

### Diversity Reduction

Monitoring of fungal communities around Chernobyl over 15 years resulted in the isolation and culturing of approximately 2,000 strains encompassing 180 species from 92 genera. Zhdanova et al. (1994) demonstrated that microfungal community structure in soil varied in relation to the degree of radionuclide exposure:

- **Community complexity (diversity) decreased** with increasing radioactive dose
- **Proportion of melanized species increased** with dose rate
- Community structure was represented diagrammatically by "pleiads" — constellations of points representing fungal species, with connecting lines indicating linkage complexity. The number of linear connections decreased as radiation exposure increased, reflecting simplified community structure.

### Dominance of Melanized Fungi

Melanin-containing (pigmented) fungal species increasingly dominated communities at higher levels of radioactivity. Key melanized indicator species identified for high radionuclide contamination in woodland ecosystems include:

- *[[chaetomium-aureum]]*
- *[[paeciliomyces-lilacinus]]*

The melanin pigment in hyphal cell walls is believed to provide protection against ionizing radiation damage, analogous to the UV-protective role of melanin in lichen fungi (Gauslaa and Solhaug, 2001).

## Reactor Room Fungi

Perhaps the most striking evidence of fungal radioresistance comes from the walls of the reactor room at the Chernobyl Atomic Electric Station itself. Zhdanova et al. (2001) isolated 37 species from 19 genera growing under conditions of 1.5 to 800 mR h⁻¹ in the presence of multiple alpha- and beta-emitting isotopes (²³⁹Pu, ²⁴⁰⁺²⁴¹Pu, ²⁴¹Am, ²⁴⁴Cm) and primarily the gamma-emitting ¹³⁷Cs.

### Most Frequently Isolated Species

The frequency of fungal isolation generally increased with radiation intensity:

| Fungal Species | 1.5–25 mR h⁻¹ | 40–220 mR h⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| *Cladosporium sphaerospermum* | 73.7% | 99.9% |
| *[[alternaria-alternata]]* | 40.2% | 44.4% |
| *Aureobasidium versicolor* | 26.8% | 55.5% |
| *[[aureobasidium-pullulans]]* | 20.1% | 44.4% |
| *Cladosporium herbarum* | 20.1% | 44.1% |
| *Acremonium strictum* | 33.5% | 22.2% |
| *[[cladosporium-cladosporioides]]* | 26.8% | 11.1% |

*Cladosporium sphaerospermum* was the most consistently isolated species, appearing in nearly 100% of samples at higher radiation levels. The sustained radiation has also led to shifts in genetic composition within some fungal species (Mironenko et al., 2000).

## Radiotropism

A particularly intriguing finding is the development of **radiotropism** — the ability of certain fungi to actively direct their growth toward sources of radioactivity:

- Fungi isolated from areas of high radioactivity directed growth toward collimated radiation sources in culture
- The same species isolated from areas of low radioactivity did **not** exhibit this behavior
- This suggests a behavioral adaptation that evolved under selective pressure from radiation exposure

This phenomenon, documented by Vember et al. (1999) and Zhdanova (1994), raises fundamental questions about the triggers and physiological mechanisms underlying the response. It implies that some fungi may actually benefit from or utilize radiation as an energy cue for growth direction.

## Decomposition of Radioactive Debris

Many of the microfungi isolated from Chernobyl have demonstrated the ability to grow into and decompose carbon-based radioactive debris from the reactor — so-called "hot particles":

- *Cladosporium cladosporioides* and *Penicillium roseo-purpureum* could overgrow hot particles with activity less than 1,147 Bq
- These fungi destroyed the particles within 50 to 150 days
- This capacity for biodegradation of radioactive organic material has significant implications for long-term containment and cleanup strategies

## Lichen Radionuclide Accumulation

The fungal component of the lichen symbiosis is responsible for the elevated radionuclide content observed in lichens following Chernobyl:

### Accumulation Levels in Austria

Heinrich et al. (1999) reported that ¹³⁷Cs levels in Austrian lichens rose from approximately 0.4 kBq kg⁻¹ pre-Chernobyl to over 50 kBq kg⁻¹ after the disaster. In *Pseudovernia furfuracea*, ¹³⁷Cs concentration exceeded natural ⁴⁰K activity by 430 times.

### Biological Half-Lives

The retention time of radionuclides in lichens varies by species and growth form:

| Lichen Species | Growth Form | ¹³⁷Cs Half-Life |
|---|---|---|
| *Pseudovernia furfuracea* | Foliose, arboreal | ~1.3 years |
| *Cetraria islandica* | Terricolous | ~2.5 years |
| *[[evernia-prunastri]]* | Foliose | Similar to *P. furfuracea* |
| *Hypogymnia physoides* | Foliose | Similar to *P. furfuracea* |
| ⁹⁰Sr in *P. furfuracea* | — | 1.2–1.6 years |

The relatively long biological half-lives (1.3–2.5 years) allow for sustained transfer to animal grazers over extended periods.

### Radionuclide Content in Norwegian Lichens (1986)

Gaare (1990) documented extraordinarily high radiocesium levels in Norwegian lichens:

| Lichen Species | ¹³⁷Cs (Bq kg⁻¹) |
|---|---|
| *Pseudephebe pubescens* (rock) | 54,080 |
| *Umbilica deusta* (rock) | 34,756 |
| *Umbilicia hyperborea* (rock) | 31,767 |
| *Cetraria nivalis* (soil) | 24,286 |
| *Alectoria ochroleuca* (soil) | 24,005 |

Rock-dwelling species generally showed higher accumulation than soil-dwelling species, possibly due to differences in growth form and moisture dynamics affecting ion uptake.

## Ecological Indicators

Fungal bioindicator traits have been characterized for different levels of radioactive contamination:

- **High contamination**: *Chaetomium aureum*, *Paeciliomyces lilacinus*, melanized species dominance
- **Medium contamination**: Mixed community with moderate melanization
- **Low contamination**: More diverse communities with lower proportion of melanized species

These bioindicators provide a practical tool for assessing and monitoring radioactive contamination levels in terrestrial ecosystems.

## Trophic Transfer and Ecological Impact

### Wildlife Exposure

- Reindeer in Norway at densities of ~4 per hectare derive about 40% of annual food intake from lichens during winter, resulting in significant radiocesium body burdens
- Roe deer in Sweden showed seasonal peaks in radiocesium intake (3,000 Bq day⁻¹ in August–September) associated with fungal fruit body consumption
- Fungi transfer radionuclides to higher trophic levels, with accumulation in the organic horizon of steppe soils serving as a reservoir

### Human Exposure

Barnett et al. (2001) estimated that fungal consumption delivers less than one microsievert of radiation dose to the average UK consumer — well below the IAEA annual limit. However, this figure varies significantly with regional mushroom-eating practices and the specific fungal species consumed.

## Summary of Fungal Radiation Resistance

Several factors contribute to fungal resilience in radioactive environments:

1. **Melanin pigmentation** in cell walls provides physical shielding against ionizing radiation
2. **Efficient DNA repair mechanisms** allow survival despite genetic damage
3. **Extensive hyphal networks** enable rapid colonization of new substrates
4. **Radionuclide accumulation capacity** may actually be utilized rather than merely tolerated
5. **Phenotypic plasticity** allows adaptation to extreme conditions
6. **Behavioral adaptation** (radiotropism) optimizes growth patterns in radiation fields
